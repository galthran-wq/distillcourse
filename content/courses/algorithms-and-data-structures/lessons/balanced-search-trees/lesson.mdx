A binary search tree degrades to O(n) operations whenever insertions arrive
in sorted order; a balanced search tree prevents that by restoring a height
invariant after every insertion or deletion using a constant number of local
rotations. <Ref lesson="binary-search-trees">The previous lesson</Ref>
established that every BST operation costs $O(h)$ and that the arrival order,
not the key set, picks $h$ anywhere between $\lceil\lg(n+1)\rceil - 1$ and
$n - 1$, with the routine orders (ascending IDs, timestamps) picking the
worst. This lesson takes that choice away. It builds the machinery in full on
the AVL tree, the simplest balancing scheme, and then presents the red-black
tree, the one every mainstream library ships, through the correspondence
that explains its rules. What remains open afterward is cost, not
correctness: the heap of the next lesson keeps far less order for a smaller
constant factor.

The route: the rotation and the one thing it preserves; the AVL invariant
and the two-line proof that it forces $h = O(\log n)$; the insertion fixup,
one rebalance of at most two rotations; red-black trees as 2-3 trees in
binary costume; and the module scoreboard updated.

## Rotation: three pointer writes that reorder nothing

The flaw was never the BST invariant, which licenses one-comparison discards
in a tree of any shape; the flaw is that nothing constrains the shape. A fix
therefore needs two parts: a **shape invariant** strong enough to force
$h = O(\log n)$, and a **repair operation** cheap enough to run at every
update. The repair operation is under a hard constraint of its own. The
in-order sequence of the tree *is* the stored sorted order
(<Ref lesson="binary-search-trees" id="order-restored-the-in-order-walk-is-sorted">the
in-order theorem</Ref>), so a legal repair may change depths but never the
left-to-right order of the keys.

Exactly one such operation exists on a local scale, and it is the *rotation*.
Take an edge between a node $y$ and its left child $x$. A **right rotation**
at $y$ pivots the edge: $x$ comes up, $y$ goes down to $x$'s right, and $x$'s
old right subtree $B$ switches parents to hang off $y$'s left. Reading the
figure left to right, the same rotation run backward is a **left rotation**
at $x$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/rotation.svg"
  alt="Before and after of a rotation: y with left child x and subtrees A, B, C becomes x with right child y; B changes parent; the in-order strip A x B y C is identical under both"
  caption="A rotation pivots one edge. Subtrees A, B, C keep their in-order positions: the sequence A · x · B · y · C reads the same on both sides."
/>

Three pointer writes implement it: $x.\mathit{right} \gets y$,
$y.\mathit{left} \gets B$, and the parent's child pointer $\gets x$. Cost
$O(1)$, independent of the sizes of $A$, $B$, $C$. Correctness is the
figure's bottom strip: both trees read $A, x, B, y, C$ in order, and every
subtree keeps keys in its legal range ($B$'s keys lie between $x$ and $y$,
so $B$ may hang right of $x$ or left of $y$ equally well). The BST invariant
survives, so search, insert, and delete work identically on the rotated
tree.

<Example title="right rotation at the root of the running tree">
The previous lesson's running tree holds ten keys with 41 at the root, 20
heading the left subtree, and 65 heading the right. Rotate right at 41: here
$y = 41$, $x = 20$, $A = \{11\}$, $B = \{26, 29, 32\}$ (rooted at 29), and
$C$ is 41's entire right subtree $\{50, 65, 72, 91\}$.

The writes: $20.\mathit{right} \gets 41$, $41.\mathit{left} \gets 29$, and
the root pointer $\gets 20$. In-order output before and after:
$11, 20, 26, 29, 32, 41, 50, 65, 72, 91$. Unchanged, as the strip demands.

The height is not preserved: 72, at depth 3 under the old root, sits at
depth 4 under the new one, so the rotation moved the tree *away* from
balance, height 3 to height 4.
</Example>

The example's last line is the point to keep. A rotation is direction-blind:
it shortens one side by exactly the amount it lengthens the other. It is a
steering wheel, not a stabilizer, and the invariant of the next section is
what decides where and which way to turn it.

<Solution>
Here $y = 65$, $x = 50$, $A$ and $B$ are both empty (50 has no children),

The right subtree becomes the chain $50 \to 65 \to 91$ with 72 below 91:
in-order $11, 20, 26, 29, 32, 41, 50, 65, 72, 91$, unchanged. The right
subtree's height grows from 2 to 3, so the tree's height grows from 3 to 4.
The transferable point: rotating an already-shallow side downhill digs it
deeper. The empty $B$ is the boundary case of the schema, and nothing in the
three writes needs adjusting for it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Aside>
Rotations are not just order-preserving; they are universal. Any BST can be
transformed into any other BST on the same keys by $O(n)$ rotations: rotate
the first tree into the all-right chain (each right rotation at the deepest
available spot pushes one node down; at most $n - 1$ of them are needed),
then run the second tree's chain-making sequence backward.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Rotation's monopoly cuts both ways: no other operation restructures a BST
locally while preserving the in-order sequence, and none is needed, since
$O(n)$ rotations reach every legal shape (the construction in the Aside
above). A balancing scheme never has to ask whether a good shape exists,
only how to steer toward it cheaply, and the steering rule is the entire
difference between the schemes of this lesson.
</KeyFact>

## The invariant: sibling subtrees within one level of each other

With the repair operation fixed, the design question is what the invariant
should demand. The natural target is the floor itself: keep the tree at
minimum height, every level full but the last. That demand turns out to
forbid cheap repair. A complete tree's shape is a function of $n$ alone, so
the BST condition dictates exactly which key occupies which node: the
$k$-th node in in-order position must hold the $k$-th smallest key. Perfect
balance is the sorted array wearing pointers, and it inherits the array's
insertion bill: adding the key 8 to the complete tree on $0, \dots, 7$
forces six of the eight existing nodes to change keys, and in general one
insertion relabels $\Theta(n)$ nodes. An invariant that can be repaired by
a few rotations near the changed leaf has to buy that locality with slack.

The classical slack is one level, proposed by the Soviet mathematicians
Adelson-Velsky and Landis in 1962 as the first balancing scheme.

<Definition title="balance factor; AVL tree">
For a node $x$ in a binary tree, the **balance factor** is
$$
\mathrm{bf}(x) = \mathrm{height}(x.\mathit{right}) - \mathrm{height}(x.\mathit{left}),
$$
with the height of an empty subtree taken as $-1$ (so a leaf has
$\mathrm{bf} = 0$). An **AVL tree** is a BST in which every node satisfies
$\mathrm{bf}(x) \in \{-1, 0, +1\}$.
</Definition>

Four instances fix the boundary. A perfect tree has $\mathrm{bf} = 0$
everywhere: the invariant includes the floor. A two-node tree has
$\mathrm{bf} = \pm 1$ at its root: single children are legal. The
maximally tilted trees of the figure below lean at every node and are still
AVL: the invariant is a local condition, and local tilts compound. The
near-miss is the three-node chain: its top node has one subtree of height 1
beside an empty subtree of height $-1$, $\mathrm{bf} = \pm 2$, violating
exactly the one clause there is.

### The height bound: minimum-size trees

Whether local slack still forces logarithmic height is a counting question:
how *few* nodes can an AVL tree of height $h$ have? Let $N(h)$ be that
minimum. The stingiest height-$h$ tree has a root, one subtree of height
$h-1$ (forced, to reach height $h$), and the other as small as the invariant
tolerates, height $h-2$:

$$
N(0) = 1, \qquad N(1) = 2, \qquad N(h) = 1 + N(h-1) + N(h-2).
$$

<Figure
  src="./figures/min-avl.svg"
  alt="The smallest AVL trees of heights 1 through 4, with 2, 4, 7, and 12 nodes; the height-4 tree is shown as a root joining the height-3 tree (7 nodes) and the height-2 tree (4 nodes)"
  caption="Minimum-size AVL trees: N(h) = 1 + N(h−1) + N(h−2), giving 2, 4, 7, 12, … nodes. Task: build N(5) from the last two counts before reading on; it should come to 20."
/>

<Theorem title="AVL height bound (MIT 6.006, Lecture 7)">
An AVL tree with $n$ nodes has height $h \le 2\lg n$.

<Proof>
$N(h) = 1 + N(h-1) + N(h-2) \ge 2\,N(h-2)$, and $N(0) = 1 = 2^0$,
$N(1) = 2 \ge 2^{1/2}$, so by induction $N(h) \ge 2^{h/2}$. A tree of
height $h$ therefore has $n \ge N(h) \ge 2^{h/2}$ nodes; taking $\lg$ of
both sides gives $h \le 2 \lg n$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The halving in the exponent is the whole proof: allowing a tilt of one
level means the tree can thin out at worst every *other* level, so height
at most doubles against the perfect tree. The constant 2 is not tight. The
recurrence for $N(h) + 1$ is the Fibonacci recurrence, and following it
through (the Fibonacci exercise at the end of the lesson) sharpens the
bound to
$h \le \log_\varphi n \approx 1.44 \lg n$, where
$\varphi = (1+\sqrt 5)/2$ is the golden ratio. At $n = 10^6$: the floor is
19, the AVL ceiling is 28, and the plain BST ceiling is 999,999.

<KeyFact>
One level of local slack costs at most 44% of height globally: an AVL tree
on $n$ keys has height at most $\approx 1.44 \lg n$, against the
unmaintainable perfect $\lceil\lg(n+1)\rceil - 1$. Every $O(\log n)$
guarantee in this lesson is this inequality plus the $O(h)$ costs of the
previous lesson.
</KeyFact>

## Insert fixup: walk back up, rebalance once

The invariant is worthless unless every insert and delete restores it, and
restoring it needs the balance factors to be readable. Recomputing a height
from scratch costs $\Theta(n)$, so each node instead *stores* its height,
an extra integer maintained like any other field: a node's height is one
plus the max of its children's stored heights, an $O(1)$ update, and only
the changed node's ancestors (plus the two or three nodes relinked by a
rotation) ever need updating. This store-a-subtree-summary trick is called
*augmentation*, and it returns in the priority-queue and order-statistics
material later in the course.

An insertion hangs a new leaf exactly as in
<Ref lesson="binary-search-trees">the previous lesson</Ref>, then walks the
root-ward path updating stored heights. Each ancestor's height changes by
at most 1, so no balance factor can pass $\pm 2$; the fixup finds the
*lowest* ancestor with $|\mathrm{bf}| = 2$ and repairs there. Take
$\mathrm{bf}(z) = +2$ (right-heavy; the $-2$ cases are mirror images), and
let $y = z.\mathit{right}$:

- **Outside case** ($\mathrm{bf}(y) \ge 0$: the extra weight is on the
  outer, right-right line): one left rotation at $z$.
- **Inside case** ($\mathrm{bf}(y) = -1$: the weight hangs in the bend
  between $z$ and $y$): a single rotation fails, and a right rotation at
  $y$ followed by a left rotation at $z$ succeeds.

These are the four textbook cases RR, RL and their mirrors LL, LR: two
genuine shapes, each with a reflection.

<Figure
  src="./figures/double-rotation.svg"
  alt="Top row: the inside case 3-1-2, where a single right rotation at 3 produces 1-3-2, still unbalanced. Bottom row: rotating left at 1 straightens the bend into 3-2-1, and a right rotation at 3 then balances to 2 over 1 and 3"
  caption="The inside case defeats a single rotation: the bend key 2 just flips sides. Straighten the bend first (left at 1), then rotate at the violated node."
/>

The top row of the figure shows the failure mechanism: the single rotation moves
the imbalance from $-2$ at node 3 to $+2$ at node 1 because the middle key
2 is *inside* the pivoting edge, and the pivot merely hands it to the other
side. The first rotation of the double exists to move the middle key onto
the outside line, where the second rotation can do its work.

<Theorem title="one rebalance per insertion">
After an insertion into an AVL tree, one rebalance (a single or double
rotation, so at most two rotations) at the lowest violated node restores
the invariant at every node of the tree.

<Proof>
Let $z$ be the lowest violated node and $H$ the height of $z$'s subtree
before the insertion. The insertion raised that subtree to $H + 1$; that is
the only way $z$'s balance factor moved. Checking the two cases: in the
outside case the rotation makes both former endpoints balanced and returns
the subtree to height $H$; in the inside case the two rotations lift the
bend node to the top with balance factor 0 and likewise return height $H$
(the case arithmetic is three lines per case; MIT 6.006 Lecture 7 writes
the heights out in full). Since the subtree now has its pre-insertion
height, every ancestor sees exactly the height it saw before the insert,
so every stored height and balance factor above $z$ returns to its old,
legal value, including any ancestor the insertion had pushed to $\pm 2$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Deletion is the same walk with a weaker ending. A rebalance after a
deletion can *shrink* the subtree below its pre-deletion height, which can
newly violate the parent, which rebalances and can shrink again: the repair
can cascade the whole way up, $O(\log n)$ rotations rather than at most
two (the deletion exercise at the end of the lesson builds a cascade). Keep the two quantities separate: for
both operations the fixup *walk* is $O(\log n)$; the *rotation count* is
what differs, $\le 2$ for insert, up to $O(\log n)$ for delete. Either
way, every dictionary operation on an AVL tree is $O(\log n)$ worst case.

its claim to the standard libraries.
</KeyFact>

## Red-black trees: a 2-3 tree in binary costume

The AVL tree settles the module's question, so the reason a second scheme
exists is engineering, not necessity. The red-black tree gives a slightly
weaker height bound in exchange for constant structural work on *every*
update, deletions included. Stated as rules it looks arbitrary; derived
from the 2-3 tree it is close to inevitable, so the 2-3 tree comes first.

### 2-3 trees: grow at the root, not at the leaves

A *2-3 tree* relaxes node width instead of balance: a node holds either
one key with two children (a *2-node*) or two keys with three children (a
*3-node*, whose middle child holds the keys between its two), and **every
leaf sits at the same depth**. Search generalizes the BST walk: compare
against the one or two keys in the node, follow the one interval that can
contain the target.

Insertion is where the design pays. The new key always joins a *leaf*
node: if the leaf was a 2-node it becomes a 3-node and the shape is
untouched; if it was a 3-node, the node briefly holds three keys, and it
**splits**: the middle key moves up into the parent and the outer two
become 2-nodes. The parent absorbs the promoted key the same way, possibly
splitting too, and if the split cascades to the root, the root's middle
key becomes a new root.

<FigureSequence
  name="two-three-split"
  alt="Five frames: N is inserted into a 2-3 tree whose middle leaf and root are both 3-nodes; the leaf overflows and splits, N promotes into the root, the root overflows and splits, and N becomes a new root with every leaf dropping one level together"
  caption="The split cascade in full: an overflowing node sends its middle key up, and when the cascade reaches the root the tree gains its new level at the top."
/>

<Task>
Advance the frames, predicting before each one which node overflows next.
Watch the leaf level throughout: no leaf ever gains a child, and the only
depth change is in the last frame, where every leaf drops one level at
once because a new root appeared above all of them.
</Task>

From the chain disaster one would expect growth at
the bottom to be the danger; a 2-3 tree makes bottom growth structurally
impossible. Leaves never gain children: the tree grows only when the root
splits, adding one level *at the top* with every leaf dropping together,
which is why all leaves stay level forever. Height therefore sits between
$\log_3 n \approx 0.63 \lg n$ (all 3-nodes) and $\lg n$ (all 2-nodes):
guaranteed logarithmic, for every insertion order. Widening nodes further,
to as many keys as fit a disk page, gives the B-tree that file systems
and databases are built on; the second course takes it up.

The catch is the code. Two node types, three-way comparisons, and the
split cases make direct implementations long and slow with no
compensating asymptotic gain, so the direct form is rarely shipped. What
is shipped is an encoding.

### Red links are glued 3-nodes

Encode each 3-node as two ordinary binary nodes joined by a *red* link,
the smaller key hanging as the larger's left child; every real 2-3 edge is
a *black* link. One color bit per node (the color of its parent link)
records which is which. The result is a plain BST, searchable by the
previous lesson's unmodified `Tree-Search`, whose black links form a
perfectly balanced 2-3 skeleton.

<Figure
  src="./figures/rb-23.svg"
  alt="A red-black BST with red links drawn horizontally (E glued to J, A to C, S to X), and below it the same keys as a 2-3 tree after hoisting each red node into its parent; every 2-3 leaf sits at depth 2"
  caption="One structure, two drawings. Hoisting each red node into its parent turns the red-black BST into a 2-3 tree; the amber keys are the glued (red) halves of 3-nodes."
/>

Read the standard rules off the encoding. A red-black tree is a BST in
which:

1. every node is red or black;
2. the root is black (it is a real node of the encoded tree, not glue);
3. every nil is black;
4. a red node has only black children (a red node is glue, so it must
   hang directly off the black core of its cluster; a red child of a red
   node would chain glue onto glue and fuse unboundedly many binary nodes
   into a single node);
5. every root-to-nil path has the same number of black nodes (black links
   are the encoded tree's real edges, and its leaves all sit at the same
   depth).

These five properties do not quite say "this BST is a diagram of a 2-3
tree". Property 4 caps a glued cluster at one black node with at most two
red children, and that is up to *three* keys with four subtrees, a
*4-node*. Properties 4 and 5 therefore encode the **2-3-4 tree**, the
sibling structure whose nodes hold one, two, or three keys; it balances
by the same split-and-promote mechanism, and it is the variant CLRS
maintains directly, with red links hanging on either side. Pinning the
encoding to the 2-3 tree of the figure takes one more rule, Sedgewick's
**left-leaning convention**:

6. red links lean left, and no node has two red children.

Forbidding two red children outlaws the 4-node cluster, and forcing the
glue leftward makes each 2-3 tree's encoding unique. The height bound is
indifferent to the choice: properties 4 and 5 alone force it, in either
convention.

<Theorem title="red-black height bound (CLRS Lemma 13.1)">
A red-black tree with $n$ internal nodes has height at most
$2\lg(n+1)$.

<Proof>
Write $\mathrm{bh}(x)$ for the *black-height* of $x$: the number of black
nodes on any path from $x$ (exclusive) down to a nil, well defined by
property 5. Induction on height shows the subtree at $x$ has at least
$2^{\mathrm{bh}(x)} - 1$ internal nodes: a nil has $\mathrm{bh} = 0$ and
$2^0 - 1 = 0$ nodes, and an internal $x$ has two children of black-height
at least $\mathrm{bh}(x) - 1$, giving
$n(x) \ge 2\,(2^{\mathrm{bh}(x)-1} - 1) + 1 = 2^{\mathrm{bh}(x)} - 1$.
By property 4 at least half the nodes on any root-to-nil path are black,
so $\mathrm{bh}(\mathrm{root}) \ge h/2$, hence
$n \ge 2^{h/2} - 1$ and $h \le 2\lg(n+1)$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The factor 2 has the same meaning as the correspondence: a path alternates
red and black at worst, so no path is more than twice as long as the
all-black paths, which have length $\le \lg(n+1)$. It is the AVL argument
with "thin out every other level" replaced by "go red every other link".

### Constant rotations: why libraries default to red-black

Insertion and deletion maintain the rules by simulating the 2-3 (in CLRS's
variant, 2-3-4) operations on the encoding. Adding a key to a 2-node or
splitting a 3-node becomes a **color flip** (repaint the links around one
node) plus at most a couple of rotations to point the red links the right
way. The consequences:

- an insertion performs at most **2 rotations**;
- a deletion performs at most **3 rotations**;
- a split cascade can recolor $O(\log n)$ nodes, but recoloring is a bit
  write, and averaged over any operation sequence the recoloring work is
  $O(1)$ per update (the split-counting exercise at the end of the lesson
  proves the 2-3 version of this by the aggregate method).

That deletion line is the differentiator: the AVL delete can rotate at
every level, the red-black delete restructures a bounded neighborhood and
otherwise only recolors. The price is the height constant, 2 versus
AVL's 1.44, so reads pay slightly more so that writes pay a bounded
amount. Measured on realistic loads the price is smaller than the bound
suggests: Sedgewick reports typical search paths of about
$1.0 \lg n$. This trade is why the red-black tree backs `std::map` and
`std::set` in C++, `TreeMap` and `TreeSet` in Java, and the
[Linux kernel's rbtree](https://docs.kernel.org/core-api/rbtree.html)
(which schedules processes with it in the Completely Fair Scheduler);
AVL trees appear where reads dominate updates.

The scheme and the name date to 1978, when
[Guibas and Sedgewick](https://doi.org/10.1109/SFCS.1978.3) published
"A Dichromatic Framework for Balanced Trees", showing that the known
balancing schemes could all be expressed as BSTs with one extra bit per
node, drawn as two link colors.

### A height bound as a contract

The bound has been tested in court, and the case closes an account this
module left open. A telephone company contracted a database provider for a
real-time customer database, built on red-black search and insert with
<Ref lesson="binary-search-trees">Hibbard deletion</Ref>, the
successor-splicing scheme of the previous lesson, which performs no
red-black fixup at all. The implementers capped tree height at 80,
comfortable if the theorem holds, since $2\lg n \le 80$ covers
$2^{40} \approx 10^{12}$ keys, and wired the cap to an error-recovery
process. Unrepaired deletions let the height creep past 80; the recovery
process triggered; the phone service suffered an extended outage; the
telephone company sued. The expert-witness testimony was the theorem read
into the legal record: "if implemented properly, the height of a red-black
BST with $n$ keys is at most $2 \lg n$." The height bound is not a
performance nicety; it is the load-bearing clause of the structure's
contract, and it binds only if *every* mutating operation restores the
invariant, deletion included.

<KeyFact>
A red-black tree is a 2-3-4 tree drawn as a BST, and under the
left-leaning convention exactly a 2-3 tree: red links glue multiway
nodes, black-path equality is their level leaves. It holds
$h \le 2\lg(n+1)$ with at most 2 rotations per insert and 3 per delete,
the bounded-write guarantee that makes it the default ordered container
in C++, Java, and the Linux kernel.
</KeyFact>

## The scoreboard: every ordered operation, O(log n), guaranteed

The module set out to restore what hashing destroyed without giving up
updates, and the goal is now met with worst-case guarantees.

The columns are not rivals so much as answers to different questions: if
no query ever mentions order, hashing's expected $O(1)$ is unbeatable;
the moment minimum, successor, or a range enters the workload, the
balanced BST is the structure that serves it without a full scan, at
logarithmic cost per operation with no assumptions about keys, arrival
order, or deletions.

What is established: a rotation restructures in $O(1)$ while provably
preserving the in-order sequence; the AVL invariant (sibling heights
within 1) forces $h \le 1.44 \lg n$ and is restored after an insertion by
one rebalance of at most two rotations; the red-black rules are the 2-3
tree's perfect leaf-balance transcribed onto a binary tree, force
$h \le 2\lg(n+1)$, and are maintained with a constant number of rotations
per update, which is why libraries default to them. The module's next
structure answers a cheaper question: a priority queue never asks for
search, successor, or range, only "hand me the minimum", and
<Ref lesson="binary-heaps-and-priority-queues">the binary heap</Ref>
serves exactly that with no pointers, no rotations, and a weaker
invariant on a complete tree packed into an array. The closing exercise
walks to its doorstep.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
The 2-3 tree $T$ has root $(G\ P)$ with three children: the leaf
$(A\ C)$, the leaf $(K)$, and the leaf $(S\ U)$. (a) Draw the left-leaning
red-black BST that encodes $T$. (b) Verify the five red-black properties
and the left-leaning rule on your drawing. (c) Give the height of $T$, the height of the encoding,
and check the encoding against the $2\lg(n+1)$ bound.

<Solution>
(a) Encode each 3-node as (larger key, black) with the smaller key as its
red left child. Root: $P$ black with red left child $G$. $G$'s children
are the encodings of the subtrees left of $G$ and between $G$ and $P$:
left, $C$ black with red left child $A$; right, $K$ black. $P$'s right
child covers keys above $P$: $U$ black with red left child $S$.

(b) Property 1: every node colored. Property 2: the root $P$ is black.
Property 3: nils black by convention. Property 4: the red nodes $G$, $A$,
$S$ each have only black (or nil) children; no red-red edge. Property 5:
count black nodes below the root on each path: through $A$:
$C, \text{nil}$: 2 ($G$ and $A$ are red); through $K$: $K, \text{nil}$:
2; through $S$: $U, \text{nil}$: 2. Equal on every path. Left-leaning
rule: every red node ($G$, $A$, $S$) hangs as a left child, and no node
has two red children.

(c) $T$ has height 1 (root to leaves). The encoding has height 3 (path
$P \to G \to C \to A$): each level of the 2-3 tree can cost up to two
binary levels, one black link plus one red. With $n = 7$ keys the bound
is $2\lg 8 = 6 \ge 3$. The transferable check: black links alone
reproduce the 2-3 edges, so the black count 2 in (b) is the 2-3 height
plus one nil step.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "AVL and red-black trees both force height $O(\log n)$, so every
red-black tree satisfies the AVL invariant, just with different
bookkeeping." Exhibit a legal red-black tree (the five-property CLRS
convention, no rule 6, so red links may hang on either side) that is not
an AVL tree, verify both claims about it, and
name what each invariant actually bounds.

<Solution>
Take: root 10 black; left child 5 black (a leaf); right child 20 **red**
with black children 15 and 30; and 35 red as 30's right child.

Red-black legality: root black; the red nodes 20 and 35 have only black
or nil children; black counts below the root: through 5: $5, \text{nil}$
= 2; through 15: $15, \text{nil}$ = 2 (20 is red); through 35:
$30, \text{nil}$ = 2; through 30's left nil: $30, \text{nil}$ = 2. All
paths agree, so property 5 holds.

AVL check at the root: left subtree (node 5) has height 0, right subtree
($20 \to 30 \to 35$) has height 2, so $\mathrm{bf}(10) = +2$. Not an AVL
tree.

The step that fails in the claim is the substitution of one bound for
the other. Property 5 equalizes only the *black* count per path, and
property 4 then caps a path at twice its black count: red-black bounds
the **ratio of path lengths** (longest over shortest $\le 2$). The AVL
invariant bounds the **difference of sibling subtree heights** at every
node. Both imply $h = O(\log n)$, with constants 2 and 1.44, but neither
implies the other; the tree above has a legal 2:1 path ratio that the
AVL difference condition forbids.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The lesson used $N(h) \ge 2^{h/2}$ and cited $1.44 \lg n$ as the true
constant. Derive it: (a) show $N(h) = F(h+3) - 1$, where $F(1) = F(2) =
1, F(k) = F(k-1) + F(k-2)$ are the Fibonacci numbers; (b) show
$F(k) \ge \varphi^{\,k-2}$ for $k \ge 2$, where
$\varphi = (1 + \sqrt 5)/2$; (c) conclude
$h \le \log_\varphi(n + 1) - 1 \approx 1.44\lg(n+1)$, and evaluate the
bound at $n = 10^6$ against the lesson's figure of 28.

<Solution>
(a) Set $M(h) = N(h) + 1$. The recurrence $N(h) = 1 + N(h-1) + N(h-2)$
becomes $M(h) = M(h-1) + M(h-2)$, exactly Fibonacci's. Bases:
$M(0) = 2 = F(3)$ and $M(1) = 3 = F(4)$, so by induction
$M(h) = F(h+3)$, i.e. $N(h) = F(h+3) - 1$. Check against the figure:
$F(4)-1, \ldots, F(7)-1 = 2, 4, 7, 12$.

(b) Induction using $\varphi^2 = \varphi + 1$. Bases:
$F(2) = 1 = \varphi^0$ and $F(3) = 2 \ge \varphi$. Step:
$F(k) = F(k-1) + F(k-2) \ge \varphi^{k-3} + \varphi^{k-4} =
\varphi^{k-4}(\varphi + 1) = \varphi^{k-4}\varphi^2 = \varphi^{k-2}$.

(c) A height-$h$ AVL tree has
$n \ge N(h) = F(h+3) - 1 \ge \varphi^{h+1} - 1$, so
$\varphi^{h+1} \le n + 1$ and
$h \le \log_\varphi(n+1) - 1$. Since
$\log_\varphi x = \lg x / \lg \varphi$ and $\lg \varphi \approx 0.694$,
the constant is $1/0.694 \approx 1.44$. At $n = 10^6$:
$\log_\varphi(10^6) = 6 \ln 10 / \ln \varphi \approx 13.816 / 0.481
\approx 28.7$, so $h \le 27.7$, i.e. height at most 27, matching (and
slightly beating) the figure's rounded 28.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Deletion's rotations cascade where insertion's stop. Build the
minimum-size AVL tree of height 4 on the keys $1, \ldots, 12$ (left
subtree always the deeper one), delete the key 12, and run the fixup:
identify every node that becomes violated, the rotation that repairs it,
and the total rotation count. Then state the general pattern this example
illustrates.

<Solution>
Construction ($T_h$ = min tree of height $h$, root's left subtree
$T_{h-1}$, right $T_{h-2}$, keys assigned by in-order rank): root 8; left
subtree on $1..7$: root 5 with left $T_2$ (3 over $(2, 4)$, 2 with left
child 1) and right $T_1$ (7 with left child 6); right subtree on
$9..12$: root 11 with left $T_1$ (10 with left child 9) and right leaf
12.

Delete 12 (a leaf: unlink). Node 11: left height 1, right $-1$,
$\mathrm{bf} = -2$; left child 10 has $\mathrm{bf} = -1$: outside case,
**right rotation at 11**, subtree becomes 10 over $(9, 11)$. Its height
fell from 2 to 1, and that shrinkage is what insertion never produces.
Now the root 8: left height 3, right height 1, $\mathrm{bf} = -2$; left
child 5 has $\mathrm{bf} = -1$: outside case, **right rotation at 8**.
Final tree: root 5; left 3 over $(2(1, \cdot), 4)$; right 8 over
$(7(6, \cdot), 10(9, 11))$. All balance factors in $\{-1, 0\}$; the
tree's height fell from 4 to 3. Total: **two rotations** from one
deletion, at two different levels.

Pattern: in a minimum-size (Fibonacci) tree, every node is tilted, so a
deletion on the shallow side violates its parent, and the repairing
rotation shrinks that subtree and passes the violation up; deleting the
shallowest leaf of $T_h$ triggers a rebalance at every other level,
$\Theta(h) = \Theta(\log n)$ rotations. This worst case is exactly what
the red-black tree's $\le 3$-rotation deletion avoids, and the practical
reason update-heavy libraries prefer it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
In a 2-3 tree, an insertion's split cascade can run all the way to the
root, $\Theta(\log n)$ splits in the worst case. Prove that over any
sequence of $n$ insertions into an initially empty 2-3 tree, the *total*
number of splits is at most $n$, so the amortized split count per
insertion is at most 1. Name the analysis method, and the earlier
structure whose analysis had the same shape.

<Solution>
Count 3-nodes. A split happens only to a node that was a 3-node and
received one key more; the split replaces it by two 2-nodes (destroying
one 3-node) and promotes its middle key. So **every split destroys
exactly one 3-node**. Where are 3-nodes created? Only when a 2-node
receives a key, which happens at most once per insertion: the cascade
ends either at the first 2-node ancestor, which becomes a 3-node (one
creation, cascade over), at the root splitting (the new root is a
2-node: zero creations), or at a 2-node leaf if there was no split at
all (one creation). Every step before the cascade's end destroys a
3-node and creates none. Hence over the whole history,
$$
\#\text{splits} = \#\text{3-nodes destroyed} \le \#\text{3-nodes created} \le n .
$$
Total split work over $n$ inserts is $O(n)$: at most one split per
insertion on average, even though a single insertion can suffer
$\Theta(\log n)$.

The method is the **aggregate method** of
<Ref lesson="dynamic-arrays-and-amortized-analysis">the amortized-analysis
lesson</Ref>, and the shape is the doubling array's: an expensive event
(split, resize) must be paid for by many cheap events that set it up, so
the total telescopes. Via the correspondence, this is also why a
red-black tree's recoloring cascades cost $O(1)$ amortized: a color flip
is the encoded split.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
An event scheduler performs exactly two operations: insert a timestamped
event, and remove-and-return the earliest event. (a) Give the cost of
each on an AVL tree, including how remove-earliest finds its target.
(b) List the capabilities the AVL invariant maintains that this workload
never invokes. (c) The workload's shape needs are weak enough for the
one shape rotations exist to avoid: a *complete* tree, which this
lesson showed is a function of $n$ alone and stores in an array with no
pointers. Propose the weakest per-node ordering condition on a complete
tree that still makes the earliest event readable at the root in $O(1)$,
and state what an insertion would have to repair.

<Solution>
(a) Insert: $O(\log n)$, the ordinary fixup. Remove-earliest: the
minimum is the leftmost node, an $O(\log n)$ walk down left links
(<Ref lesson="binary-search-trees">previous lesson</Ref>), plus an
$O(\log n)$ delete with its possible rotation cascade.

(b) The full sorted order: search for an arbitrary key, successor and
predecessor of any element, range reports, in-order traversal. The
scheduler asks only ever for one end of the order. Maintaining
comparability of *every* pair of keys, which is what the BST invariant
plus balance buys, is wasted on it.

(c) It suffices that **every node's key is $\le$ both children's keys**:
then the root is a minimum by induction along any downward path. Nothing
constrains siblings, which is precisely the order given up. A complete
tree's shape is determined by $n$, so an insertion appends at the one
legal new slot (constant-time to locate in the array layout) and then
must repair only the ordering along the *single root-to-leaf path*
through that slot, $O(\lg n)$ comparisons, no rotations, since the shape
is already correct by construction. This structure is the binary heap,
and the repair walk is the sift of
<Ref lesson="binary-heaps-and-priority-queues">the next lesson</Ref>,
which also shows the surprise that building it from scratch costs
$O(n)$, not $O(n \log n)$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

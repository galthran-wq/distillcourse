Merge sort is one instance of a general pattern — split a problem into
smaller instances of itself, solve them recursively, and combine the
results — and the same pattern designs algorithms for problems that look
nothing like sorting. Until now the course has analyzed algorithms it was
handed: the analysis module built the pricing tools, and the sorting
module spent them on merge sort and quicksort, naming their shared shape
<Ref lesson="comparison-sorting-merge-and-quicksort">divide-and-conquer</Ref>.
This lesson opens the design-paradigms module, where the direction
reverses: the pattern becomes a method for producing an algorithm that
does not exist yet. The proving ground is integer multiplication, a
problem with no sorting in it anywhere; the finding is that the split
by itself contributes nothing, the first honest attempt landing back on
$\Theta(n^2)$, and that the combine step is where a divide-and-conquer
design is won or lost. The route: the pattern restated as a four-decision
checklist, the checklist run on the Tower of Hanoi as a warm-up, then on
multiplication, where a 1960 algebraic trick rescues the failed design;
last, the same trick at matrix scale, and the boundary where the paradigm
stops working.

## The template: four decisions and the recurrence they induce

The sorting module's two algorithms, re-read as a sequence of decisions
rather than a pair of programs, answer the same four questions. Stated
once, generally:

1. **Split.** How is an instance cut into smaller instances of the same
   problem? (Merge sort: at the midpoint, blind to the values.)
2. **Return contract.** What exactly does a solved subproblem hand back?
   (Each half comes back sorted; the recursion is trusted for this,
   never re-checked.)
3. **Combine.** How do the sub-answers become the whole answer, and at
   what cost? (The two-finger merge, $\Theta(n)$.)
4. **Recurrence.** The three previous answers fix $a$, $b$, and $f(n)$
   in $T(n) = a\,T(n/b) + f(n)$, and the
   <Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">Master theorem</Ref>
   prices the design before a line of it is coded. (Merge sort:
   $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n) = \Theta(n \lg n)$.)

<Figure
  src="./figures/template-card.svg"
  alt="A four-row design card listing the split, return contract, combine, and recurrence decisions of a divide-and-conquer algorithm, each row filled in with merge sort's answer"
  caption="The design card, filled in for the known instance. A new design fills the same four slots."
/>

The fourth row is the reason the checklist is worth writing down. An
iterative algorithm must be built before its loop can be counted; a
divide-and-conquer design is priced **at the design stage**, because the
recurrence depends only on the three choices, not on any implementation.
A designer who fills in the first three slots and gets an unacceptable
fourth row throws the design away without coding it; this lesson does
exactly that once.

Two boundary notes on the pattern, both established in
<Ref lesson="comparison-sorting-merge-and-quicksort">the sorting
lesson</Ref> and load-bearing here. The subproblems must be *disjoint*
instances of the same problem: the clause recursive Fibonacci violates,
and the final section returns to. And recursion into a *single* smaller
instance (binary search discarding one half) is conventionally called
*decrease-and-conquer*: the template covers it with $a = 1$, but with one
call there is nothing to combine, and none of this lesson's design
tension exists there.

## Tower of Hanoi: the combine can be trivial

Before multiplication, one run of the checklist on a problem where three
of the four slots are almost empty: recursion in its purest form. The
*Tower of Hanoi* is a physical puzzle published in 1883 by the French
mathematician Édouard Lucas: $n$ disks of decreasing size are stacked on
one of three pegs, the whole stack must move to another peg, and the
rules allow moving only one disk at a time and never placing a disk on a
smaller one.

Every disk blocks the ones below it, so the largest disk can move only
when the other $n-1$ are stacked, in order, on the spare peg. That
observation is the entire algorithm.

<FigureSequence
  name="hanoi"
  alt="Three frames of the recursive Tower of Hanoi move: the top n minus 1 disks are parked on the spare peg by recursion, the largest disk moves to the destination in one move, and the parked disks are re-stacked onto it by a second recursion"
  caption="The recursive move: park n−1 disks by recursion, move the largest, re-stack the n−1 by recursion."
/>

<Task>
Step the three frames and write the recurrence before reading on: count
one $T(n-1)$ per recursive frame and $1$ for the middle frame.
</Task>

```text
Hanoi(n, src, dst, tmp):
  if n > 0
    Hanoi(n−1, src, tmp, dst)    ▷ park the n−1 smaller disks
    move disk n from src to dst  ▷ one move
    Hanoi(n−1, tmp, dst, src)    ▷ re-stack them on top
```

The checklist: the split peels one disk off ($n \to n-1$, twice); the
return contract is "the $n-1$ smaller disks now sit, legally stacked, on
the named peg"; the combine is nothing at all: the second recursive call
*is* the rest of the answer. The move count obeys

$$
T(n) = 2\,T(n-1) + 1, \qquad T(0) = 0,
$$

and unrolling gives $T(n) = 1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots + 2^{n-1} = 2^n - 1$
(check: $T(1)=1$, $T(2)=3$, $T(3)=7$; induction confirms the pattern in
an exercise below, and no algorithm does better).

The recurrence is deliberately outside the $a\,T(n/b) + f(n)$ form: the
instance shrinks by subtraction, not by a constant factor, so the
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">Master theorem</Ref> does
not apply. Summing levels still prices the tree: depth $i$ holds $2^i$
calls of constant work each, the same doubling trend as any
leaf-dominated tree. But subtraction makes the tree $n$ levels deep
instead of $\lg n$, with $2^n$ leaves instead of $n^{\log_b a}$. Two
recursive calls are exponential or cheap depending entirely on how fast
the instance shrinks.

<KeyFact>
Lucas's promotional legend has monks moving $64$ golden disks by this
algorithm; $2^{64} - 1 \approx 1.8 \times 10^{19}$ moves at one per
second is about **585 billion years**, roughly forty times the age of
the universe. Two calls on $n-1$ cost $2^n$; two calls on $n/2$ cost
$n \lg n$. The split geometry, before anything else, decides polynomial
against exponential.
</KeyFact>

<Exercise>
In a run of `Hanoi(n, ...)`, how many times does disk $k$ move
($k = n$ the largest, $k = 1$ the smallest)? Derive a formula, then
check it by summing over all disks. Write the recurrence for the count
before opening the solution.

<Solution>
Strategy: the same unrolling as for $T(n)$, tracked per disk.

Let $m_k(n)$ be the number of moves of disk $k$. Disk $n$ moves exactly
once, at the middle line, and no recursive call touches it: $m_n(n) = 1$.
For $k < n$, disk $k$ moves only inside the two recursive calls, each a
full run on $n-1$ disks: $m_k(n) = 2\,m_k(n-1)$. Unrolling the doubling
from $m_k(k) = 1$ gives
$$
m_k(n) = 2^{\,n-k}.
$$
The smallest disk moves $2^{n-1}$ times, every second move of the whole
run. Check by summation:
$\sum_{k=1}^{n} 2^{\,n-k} = 2^{n-1} + \cdots + 2 + 1 = 2^n - 1 = T(n)$,
the total already derived. The transferable move: a per-element count in
a recursion obeys its own recurrence, read off the same code.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Multiplication: an ancient problem and a failed split

<Figure
  src="./figures/treviso-1478-multiplication.png"
  alt="Two multiplication worksheets in 15th-century type: on the left, 934 times 314 set out as long multiplication with the partial products 3736, 934 and 2802 summing to 293276, a cross of casting-out-nines check digits beside it; on the right, the same product in a three-by-three lattice with 9 3 4 along the top and 3 1 4 down the right side, each cell split by a diagonal and the digits of 293276 read off the left and bottom edges"
  caption="Two ways to multiply 934 × 314, printed in 1478: the long form with a casting-out-nines check, and the lattice."
  float="right"
  width="m"
/>

The second run of the checklist is on multiplication: given two $n$-digit
numbers, compute their product. The digit-by-digit method a schoolchild
still learns, shown in the figure in its 1478 typeset form, computes
every product of a digit of $x$ with a digit of $y$: $n^2$ single-digit
multiplications, $\Theta(n^2)$ time.

The cost is real. Modern cryptography and computer algebra multiply
integers thousands of digits long, and at $n = 10^6$ digits a
$\Theta(n^2)$ method performs on the order of $10^{12}$ digit products:
<Ref lesson="asymptotic-notation">the wall-clock ladder's</Ref> minutes,
for one multiplication.

### The obvious split: four quarter-size calls

Merge sort halved its input and turned $\Theta(n^2)$ sorting into
$\Theta(n \lg n)$; the precedent suggests halving the numbers should cut
multiplication's cost the same way. Split each factor into a high and a
low half of $m = n/2$ digits:

$$
x = 10^m a + b, \qquad y = 10^m c + d,
$$

so that $x = 1234$ splits as $a = 12$, $b = 34$. Multiplying out:

$$
x \cdot y \;=\; 10^{2m}\,ac \;+\; 10^m\,(ad + bc) \;+\; bd.
$$

The identity is a complete divide-and-conquer design. Split: cut each
number's digit array in half, $\Theta(n)$. Return contract: each of the
four sub-products $ac$, $ad$, $bc$, $bd$ is a genuine half-size instance
of the same problem, computed recursively. Combine: multiplying by
$10^{2m}$ or $10^m$ is a digit shift, and the additions are
digit-by-digit, $\Theta(n)$ in total. The fourth row of the card:

$$
T(n) = 4\,T(n/2) + \Theta(n).
$$

This recurrence was solved in
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">the recurrences lesson</Ref>:
the watershed is $n^{\log_2 4} = n^2$, the driving function is
polynomially smaller, and case 1 of the Master theorem gives
$T(n) = \Theta(n^2)$, leaf-dominated, with $n^2$ leaves doing the work.
The design is discarded at the fourth row, exactly as the checklist
allows: recursion reproduced the grade-school cost because its $n^2$
leaf-level single-digit products are the same $n^2$ digit pairs the
lattice computes directly. **Splitting alone bought nothing.** The
precedent from sorting misled: merge sort's win was never the split
(quicksort wins with all its work in the divide, merge sort with all of
it in the combine); the win was that two half-size calls cost less than
one full-size problem, and here the split produced four.

## Karatsuba: three multiplications instead of four

What the failed design needs is fewer recursive calls, and the count of
calls is set by the combine's algebra: the combine needs the three
coefficients $ac$, $ad + bc$, and $bd$, and the obvious way to get the
middle one costs two multiplications. In 1960 Andrei Kolmogorov,
conjecturing that $\Theta(n^2)$ was optimal, posed exactly this problem
to a seminar at Moscow State University; within a week a 23-year-old
student, Anatoly Karatsuba, brought back the counterexample below, and
Kolmogorov, after presenting the result to the seminar himself,
terminated the seminar. The observation, in the clean form later given
by Knuth:

$$
ad + bc \;=\; ac + bd - (a - b)(c - d).
$$

Expanding the right side checks it in one line: $(a-b)(c-d) = ac - ad -
bc + bd$, and subtracting that from $ac + bd$ leaves $ad + bc$. The
consequence: the middle coefficient comes **free** from the two
coefficients already needed, plus *one* new half-size multiplication,
$(a-b)(c-d)$. Three recursive calls, not four.

<Example title="Karatsuba on 1234 × 5678, every digit">
Split at $m = 2$: $a = 12$, $b = 34$, $c = 56$, $d = 78$.

**Three products** (each a half-size multiplication):
$$
ac = 12 \cdot 56 = 672, \qquad
bd = 34 \cdot 78 = 2652,
$$
$$
(a-b)(c-d) = (12 - 34)(56 - 78) = (-22)(-22) = 484.
$$

**Middle coefficient by subtraction**, no fourth multiplication:
$$
ad + bc = 672 + 2652 - 484 = 2840.
$$
(Direct check, using the two products the algorithm never computes:
$ad + bc = 12 \cdot 78 + 34 \cdot 56 = 936 + 1904 = 2840$.)

**Recombine with shifts:**
$$
1234 \times 5678
= 672 \cdot 10^4 + 2840 \cdot 10^2 + 2652
= 6{,}720{,}000 + 284{,}000 + 2{,}652
= 7{,}006{,}652.
$$

Three two-digit multiplications and a handful of additions and shifts
replaced four two-digit multiplications; recursing into the three
sub-products (rather than calling them primitive) repeats the same trade
at every level.
</Example>

<Exercise>
Compute $3141 \times 2718$ by one level of Karatsuba: give the split,
the three products, the middle coefficient, and the recombination. One
of the intermediate values is negative this time; carry its sign through.

<Solution>
Split at $m = 2$: $a = 31$, $b = 41$, $c = 27$, $d = 18$.

Three products: $ac = 31 \cdot 27 = 837$; $bd = 41 \cdot 18 = 738$;
$(a-b)(c-d) = (31 - 41)(27 - 18) = (-10)(9) = -90$.

Middle coefficient: $ad + bc = 837 + 738 - (-90) = 1665$; the negative
product is *subtracted*, so it adds. (Check: $31 \cdot 18 + 41 \cdot 27
= 558 + 1107 = 1665$.)

Recombination:
$837 \cdot 10^4 + 1665 \cdot 10^2 + 738 = 8{,}370{,}000 + 166{,}500 +
738 = 8{,}537{,}238$, which is $3141 \times 2718$. Unlike the worked
example, here exactly one of $a-b$, $c-d$ is negative, so their product
is negative; the identity needs no case analysis, only honest signed
arithmetic.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### The recurrence: one deleted call moves the exponent

The combine gained two additions and a subtraction, still $\Theta(n)$,
and the call count dropped from four to three. The fourth row of the
card changes accordingly:

<Figure
  src="./figures/four-vs-three.svg"
  alt="Two recursion trees side by side: the four-call tree whose level totals double, summing to order n squared, and the three-call tree with one subtree deleted, whose level totals grow by three halves per level and sum to order n to the 1.585"
  caption="One recursive call deleted at every node. Level totals grow ×2 per level on the left, ×3/2 on the right; the leaves, and with them the exponent, shrink from n² to n^lg 3."
/>

<Theorem title="Karatsuba's algorithm (Karatsuba–Ofman 1962)">
Two $n$-digit numbers can be multiplied in
$\Theta(n^{\lg 3}) = O(n^{1.585})$ time.

<Proof>
The algorithm: split as above, compute $ac$, $bd$, and $(a-b)(c-d)$ by
three recursive calls, obtain $ad + bc$ from the identity, recombine
with shifts and additions. Correctness is induction on $n$: the base
case is a single-digit product, and the identity, verified above by
expansion, makes the combine exact whenever the three sub-products are.
The differences $a - b$ and $c - d$ have at most $m$ digits (plus a
sign, handled by multiplying the signs separately), so all three calls
are genuine half-size instances. The cost recurrence is
$T(n) = 3\,T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$: watershed $n^{\log_2 3}$, with
$\lg 3 = 1.5849\ldots$, and $f(n) = \Theta(n) = O(n^{\lg 3 - \epsilon})$
for $\epsilon = 0.5$ is polynomially smaller. Case 1 of the
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">Master theorem</Ref> gives
$T(n) = \Theta(n^{\lg 3})$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The recurrences lesson's closing exercise priced this pair of
recurrences blind, before either algorithm existed on the page: the
ratio $n^2 / n^{\lg 3} = n^{0.415}$ is a factor of about **300 at a
million digits**. That arithmetic now has an algorithm attached to each
side.

<Remark title="where the crossover sits in practice">
The three-call recursion spends more on additions and bookkeeping per
level than the grade-school method, so for short numbers the
grade-school method wins; the asymptotic gain must first pay off the
constant. CPython's integer multiplication draws the line concretely:
`longobject.c` multiplies grade-school style until the smaller operand
exceeds 70 of its internal 30-bit digits (roughly 600 decimal digits)
and switches to Karatsuba above that. A designer who benchmarks only
below the crossover would conclude the "better" algorithm is worse,
which is <Ref lesson="asymptotic-notation">the first lesson's</Ref>
crossover phenomenon operating inside a production runtime.
</Remark>

<Aside>
Karatsuba's algorithm opened a race that is now closed, at least in
theory. Splitting into $k$ pieces instead of two, with cleverer
identities (Toom and Cook, 1963–66), gives $O(n^{1+\epsilon})$ for any
$\epsilon > 0$; Fourier-transform methods (Schönhage–Strassen, 1971)
reached $O(n \log n \log\log n)$; and in 2019 David Harvey and Joris
van der Hoeven closed the problem with an $O(n \log n)$ algorithm,
published in the *Annals of Mathematics* in 2021: sixty-one years from
Kolmogorov's conjecture to the (conjecturally optimal) exponent. The
practical frontier is far more conservative: production big-integer
libraries run Karatsuba, Toom variants, and FFT methods in ranges chosen
by benchmark, and the 2019 algorithm is not implemented anywhere;
its constants pay off only past astronomical sizes.
</Aside>

## The design rule: the win lives in the combine

The two multiplication designs differ in nothing but the combine's
algebra, and the difference generalizes. In any leaf-dominated
recurrence (a design whose split and combine are both linear is
leaf-dominated whenever $a > b$) the answer is
$\Theta(n^{\log_b a})$: the *exponent* is set by
the call count $a$ and the shrink factor $b$, and the driving function
is invisible in it. <Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">The
recurrences lesson</Ref> put it as "where the cost lives is where
optimization pays": at the leaves, the only lever is $a$. Hence the
design rule: **count the recursive calls first.** Before refining a
split or tuning a combine, compute $\log_b a$ and compare it against the
cost of the method to be beaten; if the exponent is not already an
improvement, no amount of engineering in $f(n)$ will make one.

The move that saved multiplication, spending cheap operations in the
combine to delete an expensive recursive call, is a repeatable design
device, and its most famous second use is matrix multiplication. The
standard method multiplies two $n \times n$ matrices in $\Theta(n^3)$
scalar operations. Partition each matrix into four $n/2 \times n/2$
blocks, and the block formula for the product needs two block products
per output block: eight half-size multiplications,
$T(n) = 8\,T(n/2) + \Theta(n^2) = \Theta(n^{\lg 8}) = \Theta(n^3)$, the
same null result as the four-call split, for the same reason. In 1969
Volker Strassen produced seven combinations of block sums whose products
reconstruct all four output blocks:

$$
T(n) = 7\,T(n/2) + \Theta(n^2) = \Theta(n^{\lg 7}) = O(n^{2.81}),
$$

the recurrence already solved among
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">the recurrences
lesson's</Ref> exercises. The seven products themselves are an
unilluminating page of algebra (CLRS §4.2 lists them); what transfers is
the shape of the move, which is Karatsuba's exactly: extra $\Theta(n^2)$
additions in the combine, one recursive call deleted, exponent moved.

<Figure
  src="./figures/strassen-blocks.svg"
  alt="Two by two block partitions of matrices A and B multiplying into C, annotated with the naive count of eight half-size block products and Strassen's seven, and the resulting exponents lg 8 equals 3 versus lg 7 about 2.81"
  caption="The same deletion at matrix scale: 8 block products → 7, exponent 3 → lg 7 ≈ 2.81."
/>

<KeyFact>
The count-calls rule is conditional on leaf domination. In a
root-dominated recurrence the total is $\Theta(f(n))$ and the call
count does not appear in it at all, so deleting a call changes nothing
and the only lever is the combine; in a balanced recurrence (merge
sort's), a deleted call buys at most the $\lg n$ factor. Classify the
recurrence first; hunt for a call-deleting identity only when the
leaves dominate.
</KeyFact>

## ★ Closest pair: a combine that prunes

Multiplication's combine won by algebra; a second classical design wins
by geometry, and is sketched here because its combine illustrates the
other standard way to make one cheap: pruning. Given $n$ points in the
plane, find the pair at minimum distance; comparing all pairs costs
$\Theta(n^2)$.

Split by a vertical line through the median $x$-coordinate; recurse on
each half; let $\delta$ be the smaller of the two answers. The combine
must check whether some *cross* pair, one point on each side, beats
$\delta$, and there are $\Theta(n^2/4)$ cross pairs, which would make
the combine quadratic and the recurrence $\Theta(n^2)$. Two prunings rescue it. First, a cross pair closer than
$\delta$ must have both points within distance $\delta$ of the line, so
only a vertical *strip* of width $2\delta$ matters. Second, inside the
strip, sorted by $y$: two points on the same side are at distance
$\ge \delta$, so any $\delta \times 2\delta$ rectangle of the strip
holds at most 8 points, and a point's partner, if it beats $\delta$,
must be among the next 7 points in $y$-order. The combine drops to a
sorted walk with 7 comparisons per point, $\Theta(n)$ given
presorting, and

$$
T(n) = 2\,T(n/2) + \Theta(n) = \Theta(n \lg n),
$$

merge sort's recurrence on a problem with no order relation in sight.
The full proof of the packing bound and the presorting bookkeeping are
in Kleinberg–Tardos §5.4; nothing later in this course depends on them.
What the sketch adds to the lesson is the pattern: when the combine
looks quadratic, the design question is what structure (here, the
minimum $\delta$ already in hand) proves most of the work unnecessary.

## Where the paradigm stops: overlapping subproblems

Every design in this lesson leaned on the disjointness clause of
<Ref lesson="comparison-sorting-merge-and-quicksort">the
divide-and-conquer definition</Ref>. Halves of an array, halves of a
digit string, blocks of a matrix, sides of a line: in each case the
subproblems share nothing, so each is solved once and the tree's cost is
the honest total. The clause is not decoration. Recursive Fibonacci,
the definition's near miss, splits $F(n)$ into $F(n-1)$ and $F(n-2)$,
two smaller instances of the same problem whose subproblem trees overlap
almost entirely: $F(n-2)$ is recomputed inside $F(n-1)$, and its
subproblems again inside both, so the recursion tree holds exponentially
many copies of a *linear* number of distinct subproblems. An exercise
below counts the copies exactly, and the count is the doorstep of the
module's third lesson: when subproblems overlap, the repair is not a
better split but a memory (solve each distinct subproblem once, store
the answer, and reuse it), which is dynamic programming.

What is established: the four-slot design card (split, return contract,
combine, recurrence), priced by the Master theorem before any code
exists; Tower of Hanoi at $T(n) = 2T(n-1)+1 = 2^n - 1$, exponential
because the instance shrinks by subtraction; the four-call
multiplication split discarded at $\Theta(n^2)$ because splitting alone
buys nothing; Karatsuba's identity deleting one call for
$\Theta(n^{\lg 3}) = O(n^{1.585})$, and Strassen's $8 \to 7$ repeating
the move at matrix scale for $O(n^{2.81})$; and the design rule that in
leaf-dominated territory only the call count moves the exponent.
<Ref lesson="greedy-algorithms">The next lesson</Ref> takes up the
paradigm at the opposite pole from recursion: the greedy algorithm,
which builds a solution by committing to the locally best choice and
never revisiting it, and the exchange argument that certifies when that
commitment is optimal. <Ref
lesson="dynamic-programming-i-one-dimensional">The module's third
lesson</Ref> takes up the case divide-and-conquer cannot price
honestly, overlapping subproblems, and builds the paradigm that solves
each subproblem exactly once.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
The lesson guessed $T(n) = 2^n - 1$ for the Tower of Hanoi from the
first values. (a) Prove it by induction, in the style of
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">the substitution
method</Ref>. (b) Prove the matching lower bound: *every* correct
solution, algorithmic or not, makes at least $2^n - 1$ moves. State the
recurrence the lower bound obeys and where the rules of the puzzle
enter.

<Solution>
**(a)** Claim: $T(n) = 2^n - 1$ for all $n \ge 0$, where
$T(n) = 2T(n-1) + 1$, $T(0) = 0$. Base: $T(0) = 0 = 2^0 - 1$. Step:
assume $T(n-1) = 2^{n-1} - 1$; then
$T(n) = 2(2^{n-1} - 1) + 1 = 2^n - 2 + 1 = 2^n - 1$. Exact equality,
with no constant to choose: unlike the asymptotic bounds of the
substitution method, this hypothesis is a closed form and reproduces
itself directly.

**(b)** Let $M(n)$ be the minimum number of moves of any correct
solution. Consider the first time the largest disk moves. At that
moment every other disk must be off both the source peg (they started
above the largest disk) and the destination peg (the largest disk may
not land on a smaller one), so all $n-1$ smaller disks sit, legally
stacked, on the single spare peg. Getting them there is a full
$(n-1)$-disk transfer: at least $M(n-1)$ moves. After the largest
disk's final move (at least 1 move in total), those $n-1$ disks must
travel from the spare peg onto the destination: another full transfer,
at least $M(n-1)$ moves. Hence $M(n) \ge 2M(n-1) + 1$ with
$M(0) = 0$, so $M(n) \ge 2^n - 1$ by the induction of part (a). The
algorithm attains the bound, so it is optimal. Both stacking rules
were used, and the "no disk on a smaller one" rule is what forces the
smaller disks onto one peg.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Refute this design memo: "Karatsuba gains by splitting in half; splitting

<Solution>
Which kind of problem: a Master-theorem classification hiding inside a

another $\Theta(n^2)$ null result, and the failing step is "shrink

splitting changes nothing by itself; only deleting calls moves the
exponent.

To beat Karatsuba, a three-piece split must get the call count below the
value that matches him: $n^{\log_3 a} < n^{\lg 3}$ requires
$\log_3 a < \lg 3 \approx 1.585$, that is, $a \le 5$ (since
$\log_3 5 \approx 1.465 < 1.585 < \log_3 6 \approx 1.631$). Five is
achievable: Toom's 3-way scheme (Toom–Cook multiplication, 1963–66)
computes the five needed coefficients from five multiplications of
$n/3$-digit pieces, giving
$T(n) = 5\,T(n/3) + \Theta(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_3 5}) =
O(n^{1.47})$, the next rung on the ladder the lesson's Aside describes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A teammate implements Karatsuba from Karatsuba's original identity,
$ad + bc = (a+b)(c+d) - ac - bd$, instead of the lesson's
$ac + bd - (a-b)(c-d)$, and reports "same three multiplications, same
recurrence." The code returns correct answers. (a) Verify the identity
and check it on the worked example's numbers. (b) Diagnose the
inaccuracy in "same recurrence": what is different about the operands of
the third multiplication, and what does the honest recurrence look like?
(c) Judge the claim's conclusion: does the difference change the
asymptotic answer?

<Solution>
**(a)** $(a+b)(c+d) = ac + ad + bc + bd$; subtracting $ac$ and $bd$
leaves $ad + bc$. On $1234 \times 5678$:
$(12+34)(56+78) = 46 \cdot 134 = 6164$, and
$6164 - 672 - 2652 = 2840$, the same middle coefficient as the
subtractive form.

**(b)** The operands grew. $a - b$ and $c - d$ are at most $m$ digits
(plus sign), but $a + b$ and $c + d$ can *carry*: $46$ and $134$; the
latter is three digits from two-digit halves. The third call is on
$(m{+}1)$-digit numbers, so the honest recurrence is
$T(n) = 2\,T(n/2) + T(n/2 + 1) + \Theta(n)$, not three clean calls on
$n/2$.

**(c)** The conclusion survives: a $+1$ in a subproblem size never
changes a growth rate (the same domain-transformation fact that lets
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">the recurrences
lesson</Ref> drop floors and ceilings), so both versions run in
$\Theta(n^{\lg 3})$. The diagnosis is about the *argument*, not the
answer: "same recurrence" is false, "same $\Theta$ class" is true, and
the subtractive form, Knuth's simplification, exists precisely
so that the clean recurrence is the honest one.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design a divide-and-conquer algorithm from scratch, running the lesson's
card. An *inversion* in an array $A$ is a pair $i < j$ with
$A[i] > A[j]$, a measure of how unsorted the array is (sorted: $0$;
reverse-sorted: $n(n-1)/2$). Checking all pairs costs $\Theta(n^2)$.
(a) Fill in the four slots: split, return contract, combine, recurrence.
The productive contract makes each recursive call return something
*more* than the count. (b) Run your algorithm on $[\,5, 2, 7, 1\,]$.
(c) State the running time.

<Solution>
**(a)** Split: halves at the midpoint, as in merge sort. Return
contract: each call returns its half's inversion count **and the half
in sorted order**, the strengthening that makes the combine cheap.
Combine: inversions are within the left half, within the right, or
*cross* pairs; the recursive calls count the first two, and cross pairs
are counted during a merge of the two sorted halves: when the merge
copies right-half element $A[j]$ while left-half elements remain, that
element is smaller than **all** of them, so add the number of elements
remaining in the left half. Sorting does not change cross-inversion
counts (each pair keeps its membership in left and right), so the merge
counts them exactly, in $\Theta(n)$. Recurrence:
$T(n) = 2\,T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$.

**(b)** $[\,5, 2\,] \to$ sorted $[\,2, 5\,]$, 1 inversion. $[\,7, 1\,]
\to [\,1, 7\,]$, 1 inversion. Merge $[\,2,5\,]$ with $[\,1,7\,]$: copy
$1$ with two left elements remaining ($+2$ cross), copy $2$, copy $5$,
copy $7$ ($+0$). Total $1 + 1 + 2 = 4$: the pairs $(5,2), (5,1), (2,1),
(7,1)$.

**(c)** Merge sort's recurrence: $\Theta(n \lg n)$, by Master case 2.
The transferable move is the strengthened return contract: the count
alone cannot be combined in $\Theta(n)$, and the sorted order is
carried not because sorting was asked for but because the combine needs
it, the same reason Karatsuba's calls return full products rather than
digits.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Strassen's scheme rests on multiplying $2 \times 2$ block matrices with
7 multiplications. (a) If someone found a $2 \times 2$ scheme with 6
multiplications (block additions free, as usual), what running time
would it give, and would it beat Strassen? (b) That search is closed:
Winograd proved in 1971 that 7 is optimal for $2 \times 2$. Given that,
state where faster-than-Strassen algorithms must come from, and check
the claim against the recurrence's structure.

<Solution>
**(a)** $T(n) = 6\,T(n/2) + \Theta(n^2)$: watershed
$n^{\lg 6} = n^{2.585}$, driving function polynomially smaller, case 1:
$\Theta(n^{\lg 6}) = O(n^{2.59})$, beating Strassen's $n^{\lg 7} =
n^{2.81}$.

**(b)** With the $2 \times 2$ base blocked at 7, the parameters left in
$n^{\log_b a}$ are $a$ *and* $b$: partition into $k \times k$ blocks
($b = k$) and beat $\lg 7$ via $\log_k a < \log_2 7$, i.e. find
$k \times k$ block-multiplication schemes using fewer than
$7^{\lg k} = k^{\lg 7}$ multiplications. That is exactly where the
post-Strassen algorithms live: enormous base schemes (and heavier
machinery in the same spirit) have pushed the exponent below $2.38$,
though every algorithm past Strassen is purely theoretical: the
crossover sizes dwarf any real matrix. The structural check: the
combine stays $\Theta(n^2)$, polynomially below any watershed above
$n^2$, so these recurrences all stay leaf-dominated and the base
scheme's count alone sets the exponent, which is the lesson's design
rule with $b$ promoted to a design variable.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Count what recursion costs when the subproblems are *not* disjoint.
Naive Fibonacci computes $F(n)$ by recursing on $F(n-1)$ and $F(n-2)$,
with $F(0) = 0$, $F(1) = 1$ returned directly. Let $C(n)$ be the total
number of calls (including the outermost). (a) Write the recurrence for
$C(n)$ and tabulate it up to $n = 10$. (b) How many of those calls have
argument $1$? Find the pattern in the table. (c) How many *distinct*
subproblems does the run on $n$ have? Compare with (a) and name the
clause of the divide-and-conquer definition that fails.

<Solution>
**(a)** A call on $n \ge 2$ makes two subcalls:
$C(n) = 1 + C(n-1) + C(n-2)$, with $C(0) = C(1) = 1$. Tabulating:
$1, 1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25, 41, 67, 109, 177$; computing $F(10) = 55$
takes $177$ calls, and the sequence grows by the same rule as Fibonacci
itself: exponentially, like $\varphi^n$ with
$\varphi \approx 1.618$.

**(b)** Calls with argument $1$: reading off subtrees,
$0, 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, \ldots$; the count of $1$-calls in the run on
$n$ is exactly $F(n)$ (each contributes $1$ to the final sum, and the
sum of the base cases is the answer). Computing $F(30) = 832{,}040$
recomputes the subproblem $F(1)$ over eight hundred thousand times.

**(c)** The distinct subproblems are $F(0), F(1), \ldots, F(n)$: just
$n + 1$ of them, against exponentially many calls. The failing clause
is **disjointness**: $F(n-2)$ is a subproblem of both branches, so the
recursion tree contains the same work many times, and the recurrence
prices every copy. Storing each of the $n+1$ answers the first time it
is computed and returning the stored value afterwards collapses the
tree to $\Theta(n)$ work: that repair is *memoization*, the opening
move of
<Ref lesson="dynamic-programming-i-one-dimensional">dynamic
programming</Ref>, which the module's third lesson builds into a
paradigm.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

A dynamic array supports appends past its current capacity by allocating a
larger block and copying every element into it, an O(n) operation that
nonetheless makes appending cost O(1) on average over any sequence of
appends. The two previous lessons left exactly one flaw standing on the
module's scoreboard: the ‡ capacity wall, the fact that a full
<Ref lesson="arrays-and-linked-lists">array</Ref> cannot grow in place, and
that the naive repair, growing by one slot per append, costs about $n^2/2$
copies over $n$ appends. The
<Ref lesson="stacks-and-queues">two-stack queue</Ref> ended on the same
unfinished note: a structure whose totals were plainly cheap while its worst
single operation was not, with the averaging left informal. This lesson
supplies the missing tool, *amortized analysis* by the aggregate method, and
uses it to knock the wall down. The route: the resize and its schedule; the
full price of seventeen appends; the sum that bounds any $n$ appends by
$3n$; the aggregate method and what "amortized" does and does not promise;
why the growth factor must be a factor; and the shrink threshold that keeps
the guarantee when deletions join in.

## Growing on demand: the append that rebuilds the array

The <Ref lesson="arrays-and-linked-lists">arrays lesson</Ref> established
why the wall exists: the bytes adjacent to an array's block belong to other
data, so a full block cannot extend in place, and the only continuation is
to allocate a larger block and copy all $n$ elements across. A *dynamic
array* builds that continuation into the append operation itself:

```text
Append(A, x):
  if n = capacity:
    allocate a new block of 2 · capacity slots    // the growth schedule
    copy the n elements into it; free the old block
    capacity ← 2 · capacity
  A[n] ← x
  n ← n + 1
```

Cost is counted in **slot-writes**: writing one element into one slot
costs 1, so an append costs $1$ for the new element plus $n$ more if it
triggered a resize. This is the same element-move accounting the
arrays lesson used to price shifting. Allocation and freeing cost
no more than the copying they bracket, so slot-writes track the true
running time to within a constant.

<Definition title="dynamic array">
A **dynamic array** stores $n$ elements in the first $n$ slots of a backing
array of $\text{capacity} \ge n$ slots, and appends by writing into slot
$n$, first replacing the backing array with one twice as large and copying
all $n$ elements whenever $n = \text{capacity}$. The first append
into an empty structure allocates a block of 1 slot.
</Definition>

The one free choice in the definition is the line marked "growth schedule":
the new block could instead be larger by a fixed increment
($\text{capacity} + c$) or by a different factor. Doubling is the choice to
justify; the schedule turns out to carry the entire guarantee, and a later
section prices the alternatives.

## Doubling priced: n appends cost less than 3n

An append is expensive exactly when it lands on a full array, and doubling
makes fullness rare in a precise pattern: the resize at capacity $2^j$
banks $2^j$ free slots, which absorb the next $2^j$ appends at cost 1 each.
Seventeen appends from empty cross that resize point five times, at
capacities 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16.

<Example title="seventeen appends, every write counted">
Start empty and append 17 elements. A resize fires when the array is full,
which happens at append $i$ exactly when $i - 1$ is a power of 2, so the
cost of append $i$ is

$$
c_i \;=\;
\begin{cases}
i & \text{if } i - 1 \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16\} \quad (\text{copy } i-1 \text{, then write}),\\[2pt]
1 & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
$$

The expensive appends are $i = 2, 3, 5, 9, 17$, costing $2, 3, 5, 9, 17$.
Every other append costs 1. The total:

$$
\underbrace{17}_{\text{one write each}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16}_{\text{copies at the five resizes}}
\;=\; 17 + 31 \;=\; 48 .
$$

Average per append: $48 / 17 \approx 2.8$. The single worst append cost 17,
yet the run as a whole paid under 3 per operation.
</Example>

### The sum: copies form a geometric series

The trace generalizes without change. Over $n$ appends the resizes copy
$1, 2, 4, \ldots, 2^{\lfloor \lg (n-1) \rfloor}$ elements: each copy is
twice the previous one, and each is at most $n - 1$ because a resize copies
only elements already appended.

<Theorem title="total cost of n appends">
Starting from an empty dynamic array with doubling, any $n$ appends cost
fewer than $3n$ slot-writes: $n$ writes of new elements plus fewer than
$2n$ copy-writes.

<Proof>
The plan: separate writes from copies, then sum the copies as a geometric
series. Every append writes its own element once: $n$ slot-writes. Copies
occur only at resizes, and the resize at append $i$ copies $i - 1$
elements where $i - 1$ is a power of 2. Writing $k = \lfloor \lg (n-1)
\rfloor$ for the largest power of 2 below $n$, the copy total is

$$
\sum_{j=0}^{k} 2^{\,j} \;=\; 2^{\,k+1} - 1 \;<\; 2 \cdot 2^{\,k} \;\le\; 2(n-1) \;<\; 2n ,
$$

the second step because a geometric series with ratio 2 is one less than
twice its largest term. Total: $n$ writes plus fewer than $2n$ copies —
fewer than $n + 2n = 3n$ slot-writes, with the inequality strict, so the
bound is never attained. ∎
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The structure of the bound is worth keeping separate from its constant: the
$3n$ is $n$ mandatory writes plus a copy bill that is really a bill for the
*last* resize twice over: all earlier resizes together cost less than the
final one. Doubling the spacing of the spikes at the same rate as their
height is the entire mechanism.

## The aggregate method: bound the sequence, divide by its length

The number $48/17$ is an average over one particular run. The claim worth
having is stronger: *no* run of $n$ appends, however chosen, averages worse
than 3. The theorem above already delivers it, since its bound holds for
every $n$ and every run, and the analysis pattern has a name.

<Definition title="amortized cost, aggregate method">
Let a data structure start empty, and suppose every sequence of $m$
operations costs at most $T(m)$ in the worst case. The **aggregate method**
assigns each operation the **amortized cost** $T(m)/m$. For the dynamic
array, $T(n) < 3n$ gives an amortized cost per append of less than 3
slot-writes: $O(1)$.
</Definition>

The bound is **worst-case over sequences**: no probability enters
anywhere. Average-case analysis assumes a distribution over inputs;
expected time (the hash tables of
<Ref lesson="hash-tables">the next lesson</Ref>) averages over random
choices the structure itself makes; amortized analysis assumes nothing and
holds against an adversary choosing the worst possible sequence.

  caption="Three guarantees that all read O(1). Worst-case bounds every bar; amortized bounds the total — the spikes are certain and their positions known in advance; expected bounds an average over the structure's own random choices, and only there can bad luck raise the bill."
/>

<Aside>
**Three kinds of guarantee (Morin, ODS §1.5).** Morin's financial
comparison separates the three. A \$120,000
house on a 120-month mortgage costs \$1,200 per month: a **worst-case**
bound, paid every month. Buying it outright averages \$1,000 per month over
the same decade: an **amortized** bound, cheaper in total, but one month
bore the entire cost. Fire insurance priced from actuarial tables costs
\$15 per month against an **expected** loss of \$10: the average is over
randomness, and a bad draw costs the full \$120,000. Amortized and expected
bounds are both averages; only one of them can be voided by bad luck.
</Aside>

### What amortized O(1) does not promise

Amortized $O(1)$ append is a guarantee about totals: any $m$ appends cost
$O(m)$. A common reading claims more: that amortized $O(1)$ means each
individual append is fast. It does not, and the difference is not
pedantry. The spikes in the cost profile are real operations that really
execute: at $n = 2^{20}$, the next append copies 1,048,576 elements before
writing one. The worst-case cost of one append remains $\Theta(n)$ under
every growth schedule; what doubling buys is that the expensive appends
are geometrically rare, never that they are gone.

The correct model is a throughput guarantee without a latency guarantee. A
batch job appending ten million elements runs in linear time and cannot be
made to care which appends were slow; a real-time audio callback that must
finish in 5 ms can be killed by a single resize it had no way to see
coming. Where individual deadlines matter, engineering answers exist
(pre-sizing past the largest expected $n$, or structures that spread each
copy across many operations), but they are answers to a different question
than the one amortized analysis poses.

<KeyFact>
The definition's from-empty clause is a second non-promise: amortized
bounds price whole histories, so they say nothing about a sequence
detached from the operations that built its starting state. One append
into a full array of $n$ elements costs $n + 1$ by itself — the $3n$
theorem never claimed otherwise, because that array's history already
contains the $n$ appends that must be billed alongside it.
</KeyFact>

The aggregate method is not specific to arrays; it applies wherever
per-operation worst cases overstate a sequence's total. The
<Ref lesson="stacks-and-queues">two-stack queue</Ref> was one instance
before the method had a name.

<Exercise>
A stack supports `Push(x)` and `MultiPop(k)`, which pops $\min(k, n)$
elements at cost equal to the number popped. A single `MultiPop` costs up
to $n$, so $m$ operations cost $O(m^2)$ by per-operation accounting. Use
the aggregate method to prove the tight bound: any $m$ operations starting
from an empty stack cost $O(m)$. State the invariant that caps the total
pop count before opening the solution.

<Solution>
Strategy hint: bound pops by pushes, not by inspecting any single
`MultiPop`.

The invariant: an element can be popped at most once, and only after being
pushed. A sequence of $m$ operations contains at most $m$ pushes, so all
`MultiPop`s together pop at most $m$ elements: the total cost of every
`MultiPop` in the sequence, summed, is at most $m$. Adding at most $m$ for
the pushes themselves, $T(m) \le 2m$, so the amortized cost per operation
is at most 2: $O(1)$, despite the $\Theta(n)$ worst case of one operation.
The transferable move is the same one the dynamic array used: find the
resource each expensive operation consumes (popped elements, banked free
slots) and observe that cheap operations are the only source of it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## The growth factor is load-bearing

Doubling looks like a detail; it is the theorem. Replace the schedule with
a fixed increment, growing to $\text{capacity} + c$ on overflow for a
constant $c$, and the guarantee collapses. With increment $c$, a resize
fires every $c$ appends, and the $j$-th resize copies about $jc$ elements,
so $n$ appends pay

$$
c\,(1 + 2 + \cdots + n/c) \;\approx\; c \cdot \frac{(n/c)^2}{2}
\;=\; \frac{n^2}{2c}
$$

copy-writes: $\Theta(n^2)$ total for any constant $c$, an amortized
$\Theta(n)$ per append. The final exercise of
<Ref lesson="arrays-and-linked-lists">the arrays lesson</Ref> computed the
$c = 1$ case at $n(n-1)/2$ copies; a larger $c$ divides that bill by a
constant and changes nothing about its shape. The arithmetic series grows
quadratically because every resize copies nearly everything and the resizes
never spread out: banking a *constant* number of free slots buys a constant
number of cheap appends, while the next copy has grown by $c$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/fixed-increment.svg"
  alt="Side-by-side bar charts of the same 17 appends: doubling shows five spikes over a flat floor totalling 48; the plus-one schedule shows a solid triangle of rising bars totalling 153"
  caption="Same 17 appends, two schedules: doubling totals 48; growing by one slot totals 153, and the triangle's area grows as n²."
/>

Any *factor* strictly greater than 1 works. Growing by $g > 1$ makes the
copy sizes a geometric series with ratio $g$, whose total is under
$\frac{g}{g-1} \cdot n$: about $2n$ for $g = 2$, $1.5n$ for $g = 3$, $3n$
for $g = 1.5$. The choice of $g$ trades copy work against wasted space (a
just-resized array is $1/g$ full), and 2 is the customary compromise, not
a law.

<KeyFact>
Growth must be *geometric*: multiplying capacity by any constant factor
$g > 1$ gives amortized $O(1)$ appends, and adding any constant increment
gives amortized $\Theta(n)$. What separates the two is whether each resize
banks free slots proportional to the copy bill it just paid.
</KeyFact>

<Aside>
**Growth factors in deployed libraries.** The mechanism is universal; the
constant is not. GCC's C++
`std::vector` doubles; Microsoft's grows by 1.5. Java's `ArrayList`
grows by 1.5 (`oldCapacity + (oldCapacity >> 1)` in the OpenJDK source).
CPython's `list` over-allocates by about 12.5%, a factor of roughly 1.125,
betting that most lists stop growing early. All are factors greater than
1: every mainstream growable array accepts the geometric-growth argument
and haggles only over $g$.
</Aside>

## Shrinking: halve at one-quarter full, never at one-half

Appends alone never shrink the array, and a structure that grew to a
million elements and then deleted most of them holds a million slots for
thousands of entries. The repair is contraction: copying into a *smaller*
block when the array gets empty enough. The natural-looking rule is
the mirror image of growth, halving the capacity when the array falls to
half full. That rule is wrong, and it is wrong adversarially, not
marginally.

<Example title="the thrash: alternating at the boundary (Sedgewick–Wayne §1.3)">
Take capacity 8 holding $n = 5$ under grow-at-full, halve-at-half, and run
`pop, push, pop, push, ...`:

- **pop** → $n = 4 = 8/2$: halve to capacity 4, copying 4 elements, cost 5.
  The shrunken array is exactly full.
- **push** → full: double to capacity 8, copying 4, writing 1, cost 5. The
  state is exactly the starting one.
- **pop** → the same shrink again; **push** → the same grow again, forever.

Every operation in the alternation copies all $\approx n$ elements: $2k$
operations cost about $5k$ slot-writes here, and $\Theta(kn)$ in general.
The amortized cost per operation is $\Theta(n)$: the resizing array with
the wrong shrink rule is no better than the fixed-increment schedule.
</Example>

### Hysteresis: the shrink threshold sits strictly below half

The diagnosis from the frames: under halve-at-half, a resize can land the
array *on* a threshold (a grow lands within one pop of the shrink
trigger, a shrink lands exactly on the grow trigger), so no cheap
operations need separate expensive ones. The repair separates the
thresholds: keep growing at full, but **halve only when the array falls to
one-quarter full**, landing the shrunken array at one-half full. Now every
resize, in either direction, leaves the array near half full, a state from
which any sequence needs many operations to force another resize. The
design rule in general form: the shrink threshold must sit strictly below
the grow threshold, and every resize must land the fill level strictly
between the two.

<Theorem title="mixed appends and deletes (aggregate; after Morin, ODS §2.1)">
With grow-at-full to double capacity and shrink-at-quarter to half
capacity, any sequence of $m$ appends and deletes-from-the-back, starting
empty, costs $O(m)$ slot-writes: amortized $O(1)$ per operation.

<Proof>
The plan: show every resize is preceded by proportionally many operations,
then charge each resize to the operations since the previous one.

**Resizes land at half full.** A grow at capacity $k$ produces capacity
$2k$ holding $k$ (plus the append that fired it): within one element of
half. A shrink at capacity $k$ fires at $n = k/4$ and produces capacity
$k/2$ holding $k/4$: exactly half.

**Every resize is far from the previous one.** Consider a resize firing at
capacity $k$, and the state just after the *previous* resize (or the empty
start): the array held within one element of $k/2$ (capacity does not
change between resizes, so it was $k$ then too). If the new resize is a
grow, $n$ climbed from $\approx k/2$ to $k$: at least $k/2 - 1$ appends
occurred in between. If it is a shrink, $n$ fell from $\approx k/2$ to
$k/4$: at least $k/4 - 1$ deletes occurred. The resize copies at most $k$
elements (a grow copies $k$, a shrink $k/4$), so in both cases

$$
\text{copies at this resize} \;\le\; 2 \cdot (\text{operations since the previous resize}) + 2 .
$$

**Sum over the sequence.** Each of the $m$ operations lies between exactly
one pair of consecutive resizes, so summing over all $r \le m$ resizes
gives total copies $\le 2m + 2r \le 4m$. Counting one more unit per
operation for the operation itself, $T(m) \le 5m$. ∎
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The same two thresholds bound the memory. Between resizes the array stays
**between one-quarter and completely full**, so the allocated block never
exceeds $4n$ slots: total space stays $\Theta(n)$, which was the point of
shrinking at all. Sedgewick and Wayne's
[Java accounting](https://algs4.cs.princeton.edu/13stacks/) makes it
concrete: their resizing-array stack of $N$ items occupies between
$\approx 8N$ bytes when full and $\approx 32N$ bytes just above
one-quarter full.

<KeyFact>
The design rule generalizes beyond arrays: an expensive rebuild costing
$k$ is harmless whenever the structure's rules force $\Omega(k)$ cheap
operations between consecutive rebuilds. The ¼ rule engineers that
property for deletes; the doubling schedule engineers it for appends; the
<Ref lesson="hash-tables">hash table</Ref> reuses it for its bucket
array.
</KeyFact>

What stands at the end of this lesson: the ‡ footnote is off the
scoreboard. A doubling dynamic array appends in amortized $O(1)$, with
the total for $n$ appends under $3n$ ($n$ writes plus under $2n$ copies).
The guarantee requires geometric growth, fails for any fixed increment,
and extends to mixed append/delete sequences exactly when the shrink
threshold (one-quarter) sits strictly below the grow threshold (full).
The aggregate method (bound the worst-case total of any sequence, divide
by its length) is the named form of the argument, now applied three times:
dynamic array, MULTIPOP stack, two-stack queue. This is the
only amortized-analysis technique this course uses; the accounting and
potential methods, which assign per-operation charges instead of bounding
totals, are the general tools and belong to the advanced sequence.
<Ref lesson="hash-tables">The next lesson</Ref> builds the hash table on
this result: its bucket array resizes on a load-factor threshold by
exactly the argument proved here, layered under an expected-time analysis
of collisions: the two kinds of average, amortized and expected, working
in the same structure without blurring.

<Exercise>
A doubling dynamic array receives $n = 1{,}000{,}000$ appends from empty.
(a) Compute the exact copy total and the exact overall total. (b) Compute
both again for $n = 2^{20} + 1 = 1{,}048{,}577$ appends. (c) The bound
$3n$ is loose in (a) and nearly tight in (b): state exactly which $n$ make
the copy total approach $2n$, and why no $n$ reaches it.

<Solution>
(a) Resizes copy $1, 2, 4, \ldots, 2^{19}$ ($2^{19} = 524{,}288$ is the
largest power of 2 below $10^6$; the resize at append $2^{19}+1$ is the
last one, producing capacity $2^{20} > 10^6$). Copy total:
$2^{20} - 1 = 1{,}048{,}575$. Overall: $10^6 + 1{,}048{,}575 =
2{,}048{,}575 \approx 2.05n$, comfortably under $3n$.

(b) One more resize fires at append $2^{20} + 1$, copying $2^{20}$. Copy
total: $2^{21} - 1 = 2{,}097{,}151$. Overall: $1{,}048{,}577 +
2{,}097{,}151 = 3{,}145{,}728$, which is $3n - 3$: within 3 slot-writes of
the bound.

(c) The copy total is $2^{k+1} - 1$ where $2^k$ is the largest power of 2
below $n$. It approaches $2n$ exactly when $n = 2^k + 1$: one past a
power of two, immediately after the biggest possible resize with the
smallest possible $n$. It never reaches $2n$ because the series
$1 + 2 + \cdots + 2^k$ falls one short of $2 \cdot 2^k$, and
$2^k \le n - 1$. The general lesson: aggregate bounds are worst-case over
all $n$, and the worst $n$ sit just past the resize points.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Re-run the doubling analysis for a growth factor of 3: on overflow the new
block has $3 \times$ the capacity, starting from capacity 1. (a) Determine
which appends are expensive and derive the copy total for $n$ appends as a
sharp bound of the form $\alpha n$. (b) State the total-cost bound and
compare it against doubling's $3n$. (c) Tripling copies less; name the
price it pays, with the worst case quantified.

<Solution>
(a) Resizes fire at appends $2, 4, 10, 28, \ldots$ (when $i - 1 =
3^{\,j}$), copying $1, 3, 9, \ldots, 3^{\,k}$ where $3^{\,k}$ is the
largest power of 3 below $n$. The geometric series with ratio 3 sums to
$\tfrac{3^{\,k+1} - 1}{2} < \tfrac{3}{2} \cdot 3^{\,k} < \tfrac{3}{2} n$:
copy total under $1.5n$.

(b) Adding $n$ element-writes: total under $2.5n$, an amortized cost under
2.5 against doubling's 3. In general, factor $g$ gives copy total under
$\tfrac{g}{g-1} n$: the series is dominated by its last term, and the
domination sharpens as $g$ grows.

(c) Space. Just after a resize the array is $1/3$ full, so the block can
hold $3n$ slots for $n$ elements: waste up to $2n$ slots, against at most
$n$ for doubling (and the mixed append/delete invariant weakens the same
way). The strategy choice is a genuine trade: copy work scales as
$\tfrac{g}{g-1}$, worst-case waste as $g - 1$; no $g$ wins both, which is
why deployed libraries scatter across $1.125 \le g \le 2$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute or repair: "Amortized cost 3 per append means any $m$ append
operations on a dynamic array cost at most $3m$." Produce the strongest
counterexample you can, name the hypothesis of the aggregate bound that it
violates, and state the repaired claim.

<Solution>
Counterexample: hand the adversary an array that already holds
$n = 2^{20}$ elements at exactly full capacity, legitimately built by
$2^{20}$ earlier appends, and let the "sequence" be the single operation
$m = 1$: one append. It resizes and costs $2^{20} + 1$ slot-writes, which
is not at most $3 \cdot 1$.

The violated hypothesis is *starting from the empty structure*: the
aggregate bound prices whole histories, and this sequence's history was
amputated. The repaired claim: any $m$ appends **starting from empty**
cost under $3m$; equivalently, for a sequence beginning at an arbitrary
reachable state of $n$ elements, the cost is at most $3(m + n)$: charge
the build of the starting state, and the bound is honest again. The
failure mode is worth recognizing in the wild: quoting a per-operation
amortized cost while discarding the prefix of the sequence that banked
the structure's current state is the standard way to misuse an amortized
bound.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

(a) Halve-at-half. `push`: full, double to 16, copy 8, write, cost 9,
$n = 9$. `pop`: $n = 8 = 16/2$, halve to 8, copy 8, cost 9. `push`: full
again, double, copy 8, cost 9. `pop`: cost 9. `push`: cost 9. `pop`:
cost 9. Total **54** for six operations: every operation resized.

(b) Halve-at-quarter. `push`: cost 9 (the grow is identical), $n = 9$,
capacity 16. `pop`: $n = 8$, and the shrink threshold is $16/4 = 4$: no
resize, cost 1. `push`: $n = 9$, a free slot exists, cost 1. `pop`, `push`,
`pop`: cost 1 each. Total **14**, and the next resize is at least 4
operations away in either direction.

(c) Under (a) the grow lands at 9 of 16, one pop from the shrink
threshold of 8, and the shrink lands exactly full: each resize deposits
the array on a boundary, so the next operation can resize again. Under (b)
the grow lands at 9 of 16, five pops and seven pushes from the nearest
thresholds. The totals differ by a factor that grows with $n$:
$\Theta(n)$ versus $O(1)$ amortized, from moving one threshold.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A $k$-bit binary counter supports one operation, `Increment`, whose cost
is the number of bits it flips: the trailing block of 1s flips to 0, then
one 0 flips to 1 (so incrementing $0111$ costs 4). A single `Increment`
can cost $k$. Prove by the aggregate method that $n$ increments from zero
cost fewer than $2n$ bit-flips (amortized cost under 2) by counting how
often each bit position flips.

<Solution>
Bit 0 flips on every increment: $n$ flips. Bit 1 flips on every second
increment (when bit 0 carries): $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ flips. In general
bit $i$ flips exactly $\lfloor n/2^i \rfloor$ times, once per completed
block of $2^i$ increments. Total flips:

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \left\lfloor \frac{n}{2^i} \right\rfloor
\;<\; n \sum_{i=0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^i} \;=\; 2n .
$$

Amortized cost per increment: under 2 bit-flips, against a worst case of
$k$. The counter is the dynamic array's argument inverted: the array's
copies doubled in size and halved in frequency going up the series; the
counter's flips halve in frequency going up the bit positions, and the
same geometric series with ratio $\tfrac12$ caps the total. (This is CLRS
§16.1's second aggregate example, and the structure of the sum recurs in
<Ref lesson="binary-heaps-and-priority-queues">build-heap's</Ref> $O(n)$
bound later in the course.)
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The <Ref lesson="stacks-and-queues">circular buffer</Ref> ran a queue on a
fixed array; a *growable* circular buffer doubles like a dynamic array,
copying all $n$ resident elements (unwrapping them to start at slot 0 of
the new block) when an enqueue finds it full. Dequeues never shrink it.
Prove that any $m$ enqueue/dequeue operations from empty cost $O(m)$, and
state why the wraparound (residents possibly spanning the array boundary)
changes nothing in the argument.

<Solution>
Cost model: each operation costs 1, plus $n$ copies if an enqueue resizes.
A resize fires at capacity $k$ only when $n = k$ residents exist, and the
previous resize (or empty start) left at most $k/2$ residents (a resize
doubles from a full array of $k/2$). Since only enqueues raise $n$, at
least $k/2$ enqueues occurred since the previous resize: copies at this
resize $\le 2 \cdot (\text{operations since the previous one})$. Each
operation is charged once, so total copies $\le 2m$, and $T(m) \le 3m$:
amortized $O(1)$.

The wraparound is irrelevant because the argument never inspects *where*
residents sit, only *how many* there are: the copy cost is $n$ moves
whether the occupied window is contiguous or split across the boundary,
and $n$ is all the accounting uses. The resident count is governed by
enqueues alone, exactly as the dynamic array's $n$ was governed by
appends; dequeues, like the two-stack queue's dequeues, only ever make
the next resize farther away.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A hash table (next lesson's structure) stores $n$ keys in an array of $m$
buckets and resizes like a dynamic array: when $n$ reaches $m$, it
allocates $2m$ buckets and re-inserts all $n$ keys, at a cost of 1 per
re-inserted key. Assume each ordinary insert also costs 1. Show that $n$
inserts from an empty 1-bucket table do $O(n)$ total work, and state the
one respect in which this analysis is *not* yet the next lesson's.

<Solution>
The resize costs are identical to the dynamic array's: re-insertions of
$1, 2, 4, \ldots, 2^{\lfloor \lg(n-1)\rfloor}$ keys, totalling under $2n$,
plus $n$ ordinary inserts: under $3n$ work, amortized $O(1)$ per insert.
Nothing about the argument used what the array's slots hold: the aggregate
bound prices the *resizing policy*, not the contents.

What it does not yet cover: the assumption that an insert and a re-insert
cost 1. In a hash table, an insert's cost depends on collisions (how many
keys share the target bucket), and bounding it requires the expected-time
analysis over hash-function randomness that
<Ref lesson="hash-tables">the next lesson</Ref> supplies. There the two
averages stack: expected $O(1)$ per operation from hashing, amortized
$O(1)$ from this lesson's resize argument, and the load-factor threshold
($n/m$ crossing a constant) is exactly a grow-at-nearly-full rule. You
have just proved the half of the hash table's guarantee that needs no
probability.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

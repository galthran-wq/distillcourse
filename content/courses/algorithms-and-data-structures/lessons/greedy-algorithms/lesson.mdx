A greedy algorithm builds a solution by repeatedly making the choice that
looks best right now, never reconsidering it — a strategy that is wrong for
most problems and provably optimal for a specific, recognizable few. This
lesson builds one greedy algorithm that is provably optimal, exhibits three
plausible ones that are silently not, and extracts the proof technique, the
exchange argument, that separates the two cases.

The design-paradigms module opened with
<Ref lesson="divide-and-conquer-design">divide-and-conquer design</Ref>, where
the effort of a design sat in choosing the split and the combine, and the
recurrence priced the result before any code existed. Greedy design inverts
that balance. The code is the cheapest in this course, a sort followed by one
linear sweep, and it runs and returns a feasible answer whether or not the
ordering criterion is right. All of the effort moves into the correctness
argument, so this lesson is mostly about proof: the obligation a greedy design
carries, and the schema that discharges it when it can be discharged. Problems
the schema cannot rescue, those whose choices require comparing subproblem
answers, pass to
<Ref lesson="dynamic-programming-i-one-dimensional">dynamic programming</Ref>,
the module's next lesson.

The route: the template and one problem where it provably works;
counterexample hunting against three other criteria; the exchange argument
carried in full and abstracted into a reusable schema; the two properties that
mark a problem greedy-solvable; and knapsack, rod cutting, and coin change,
where the same instinct fails by measurable margins.

## Interval scheduling: the template on its best-known problem

A proof obligation needs a concrete problem to attach to, and the paradigm's
standard first problem supplies it: one conference room, eight booking
requests, each a half-open time interval $[s_i, f_i)$; the room serves one
booking at a time, and the goal is to grant as many requests as possible. The
requests, already listed in order of finish time:

| $i$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $s_i$ | 1 | 3 | 0 | 4 | 2 | 7 | 8 | 12 |
| $f_i$ | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 9 | 10 | 14 | 16 |

<Definition title="interval scheduling">
An instance is a set of $n$ intervals $a_i = [s_i, f_i)$ with $s_i < f_i$. Two
intervals are **compatible** if they do not overlap: $a_i$ and $a_j$ are
compatible when $s_j \ge f_i$ or $s_i \ge f_j$. The problem asks for a
maximum-size subset of pairwise compatible intervals.
</Definition>

Three cases fix the definition's boundary. Intervals $a_1 = [1,4)$ and
$a_4 = [4,7)$ are compatible: the half-open convention makes a meeting that
starts the instant another ends legal, with no gap required. A nested pair
such as $[2,9)$ and $[4,7)$ is incompatible even though neither "crosses" the
other; overlap, not crossing, is the test. And the near-miss: asking for the
subset covering the most total *time* is a different problem with different
optima; the objective clause, maximum *count*, is part of the definition.

### The template: order, sweep, keep what fits

The greedy template has three lines, none of which mention the problem:

1. **Order** the candidates by a single, locally computed criterion.
2. **Sweep** once through that order, taking each candidate that keeps the
   partial solution feasible.
3. **Never revisit** a decision: nothing is removed, nothing is reconsidered.

For interval scheduling, take the criterion to be *earliest finish time*: the
intuition is that finishing early leaves the room free for the most remaining
requests. Sorting costs $O(n \log n)$, the sweep costs $O(n)$, and the
feasibility test is one comparison, justified by a loop invariant in the
closing exercises:

```text
GreedySelect(a₁ … aₙ, sorted by finish time):
    A ← {a₁};  last ← 1
    for m ← 2 to n:
        if s[m] ≥ f[last]:        # compatible with everything selected
            A ← A ∪ {aₘ};  last ← m
    return A
```

<Example title="the sweep on the eight-request instance">
Selected set $A$ and the finish time of the last selection, step by step.
Take $a_1 = [1,4)$; the frontier is $f = 4$. Next $a_2 = [3,5)$: starts at
$3 < 4$, rejected. $a_3 = [0,6)$: starts at $0 < 4$, rejected.
$a_4 = [4,7)$: starts at $4 \ge 4$, taken; the frontier moves to $7$.
$a_5 = [2,9)$: $2 < 7$, rejected. $a_6 = [7,10)$: $7 \ge 7$, taken; frontier
$10$. $a_7 = [8,14)$: $8 < 10$, rejected. $a_8 = [12,16)$: $12 \ge 10$,
taken.

Result: $A = \{a_1, a_4, a_6, a_8\}$, four bookings. The theorem of this
lesson certifies that no compatible subset of this instance has five.
</Example>

<KeyFact>
A wrong greedy does not crash and does not warn. For most orderings the sweep
still returns a feasible answer, only a smaller one, so the paradigm's entire
risk is silent suboptimality: an unproven greedy is a bug that no test suite
reliably catches, because every output looks like a valid schedule.
</KeyFact>

## Three plausible criteria, and how counterexamples kill two

Nothing in the template forces the earliest-finish criterion, and at least
three alternatives sound equally reasonable: *earliest start time* (put the
room to use as soon as possible), *shortest interval* (small commitments block
the least), and *fewest conflicts* (take the request that collides with the
fewest others). The first tool to apply to any proposed criterion is not a
proof but a hunt: a single instance on which the criterion loses settles the
question permanently, and it is far cheaper to find than a proof.

### Two criteria die on three intervals

Shortest-interval-first dies on three intervals. Take $[1,4)$, $[3,5)$, and
$[4,7)$: the short middle interval straddles the boundary between the two long
ones, so taking it blocks both, and the tally is one against
earliest-finish-first's two.

<Figure
  src="./figures/counterexample-pair.svg"
  alt="The same three intervals under two criteria: shortest-interval-first picks the short straddling interval and ends with one; earliest-finish-first picks the two long compatible intervals and ends with two"
  caption="Three intervals, two criteria: the short interval straddles both long ones, and taking it costs a booking."
/>

The same motif is embedded in the running instance: $a_2 = [3,5)$ is its
shortest interval, and starting the sweep there yields
$\{a_2, a_6, a_8\}$, three bookings against the four above. One planted
interval was enough to drag a plausible criterion below optimal on an
instance of eight.

### Fewest conflicts: no small instance kills it, and it is still wrong

Fewest-conflicts-first resists this treatment longer: no three- or
four-interval instance kills it, and it survives most instances a tester
would type in. It is still wrong, and the smallest standard counterexample,
eleven intervals arranged so that the least-conflicted interval is a trap, is
the fewest-conflicts exercise at the end of the lesson.

## The exchange argument: earliest finish first proved optimal

Counterexamples and proofs are asymmetric: one instance refutes a criterion,
but no number of surviving instances validates one, since the claim
quantifies over every instance and every competing schedule. What must be
shown: on every instance, the sweep's output is as large as every compatible
subset. The method: take an arbitrary optimal schedule and morph it, one
interval at a time, into the greedy schedule, showing that no step of the
morph loses an interval.

  caption="The exchange argument: each swap replaces the optimal schedule's next divergent choice with greedy's, never losing an interval."

### The lemma, then the induction

The proof splits into a lemma about the first choice and an induction that
repeats it. Both are direct arguments; the only inequality either one uses is
"greedy's pick finishes no later."

<Theorem title="the greedy first choice is safe (Cormen et al., Thm. 15.1)">
Let $g$ be an interval with the earliest finish time in the instance. Some
maximum-size compatible subset contains $g$.

<Proof>
Let $X$ be any maximum-size compatible subset. If $g \in X$, done. Otherwise
let $j$ be an earliest-finishing member of $X$; the claim is that
$X' = (X \setminus \{j\}) \cup \{g\}$ is compatible and maximum-size.

**Feasibility.** Take any $x \in X \setminus \{j\}$. Compatibility of $x$ and
$j$ means $s_x \ge f_j$ or $s_j \ge f_x$. The second case is impossible: it
would give $f_x \le s_j < f_j$, making $x$ finish strictly before $j$ and
contradicting the choice of $j$ as earliest-finishing in $X$. So
$s_x \ge f_j$. Since $g$ has the earliest finish time in the whole instance
and $j$ is in the instance, $f_g \le f_j \le s_x$: every remaining member of
$X$ starts at or after $g$ finishes, so $X'$ is compatible.

**Size.** $g \notin X$, so the exchange removes one interval and adds one:
$|X'| = |X|$, and $X'$ is maximum-size. $X'$ contains $g$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The lemma covers greedy's first selection. The theorem repeats the exchange
along the whole sweep; the proof mode is induction on the position of the
first disagreement, and the roadmap is the figure above: locate the first
difference, exchange, recurse on the suffix.

<Theorem title="earliest finish first is optimal (Erickson §4.2)">
On every instance, `GreedySelect` returns a compatible subset of maximum
size.

<Proof>
Let $G = \langle g_1, \dots, g_k \rangle$ be the greedy schedule in sweep
order. Among all maximum-size compatible subsets, choose one,
$X = \langle x_1, \dots, x_m \rangle$ sorted by finish time, that agrees with
$G$ on the longest possible prefix: $x_1 = g_1, \dots, x_{j-1} = g_{j-1}$
with $j$ as large as possible.

**The prefix is everything.** Suppose $j \le \min(k, m)$, so $x_j \ne g_j$.
Both $x_j$ and $g_j$ are compatible with the shared prefix
$g_1, \dots, g_{j-1}$, and $g_j$ is by construction the earliest-finishing
interval in the instance compatible with that prefix: this is exactly what
the sweep selects after its first $j-1$ selections. Hence
$f_{g_j} \le f_{x_j}$. Every later $x_i$ with $i > j$ is compatible with
$x_j$ and finishes no earlier (the argument from the lemma's feasibility
step, applied inside $X$), so $s_{x_i} \ge f_{x_j} \ge f_{g_j}$. Replacing
$x_j$ by $g_j$ therefore keeps $X$ compatible and keeps $|X| = m$, but
lengthens the agreeing prefix, contradicting the choice of $X$. So no
disagreement exists below position $\min(k,m)+1$: $X$ agrees with $G$ on its
first $\min(k, m)$ entries.

**No optimal schedule is longer.** Suppose $m > k$. Then $x_{k+1}$ exists and
is compatible with $x_k = g_k$, so $s_{x_{k+1}} \ge f_{g_k}$. When the sweep
reached $x_{k+1}$, its frontier was the finish time of some $g_i$ with
$i \le k$, and $f_{g_i} \le f_{g_k} \le s_{x_{k+1}}$: the sweep would have
selected $x_{k+1}$, contradicting $|G| = k$. So $m \le k$, and since $G$ is
itself compatible, $m = k$: the greedy schedule is maximum-size.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### The schema, stated once for reuse

Strip the intervals out of the proof and the following remains, applicable
to any greedy algorithm:

1. **Assume** some optimal solution differs from the greedy one, and locate
   the *first* difference along the greedy order.
2. **Exchange** the optimal solution's choice at that position for the
   greedy choice, and show the swap preserves feasibility and does not
   worsen the objective; the greedy criterion supplies exactly the
   inequality this step needs.
3. **Induct**: repeated exchange morphs the optimal solution into the greedy
   one with no loss, so the greedy solution is optimal.

<Definition title="exchange argument">
An **exchange argument** is a proof of greedy optimality of this shape: any
optimal solution can be transformed, one choice at a time, to agree with the
greedy solution, without ever decreasing its objective value.
</Definition>

<KeyFact>
An exchange argument never shows the greedy solution beats an optimal one;
it shows an optimal one can be bent to agree with it at no cost. The same
three moves, with "interval" replaced by "edge crossing a cut," certify
Kruskal's and Prim's algorithms in
<Ref lesson="minimum-spanning-trees">minimum spanning trees</Ref>; the
closing cut-property exercise has you run that instance of the schema now.
</KeyFact>

*latest start time first*, which scans the intervals in decreasing order of

<Solution>
Strategy hint: mirror the lemma, exchanging "finishes earliest" for "starts
latest" throughout.

**Lemma.** Let $g$ be an interval with the latest start time. Some
maximum-size compatible subset contains $g$. *Proof.* Let $X$ be
maximum-size with $g \notin X$, and let $j$ be a latest-starting member of
$X$. For any $x \in X \setminus \{j\}$, compatibility means $s_j \ge f_x$ or
$s_x \ge f_j$; the second would give $s_x \ge f_j > s_j$, contradicting the
choice of $j$. So every other member finishes by $s_j$, and
$s_g \ge s_j \ge f_x$: swapping $j$ for $g$ preserves compatibility and
size. ∎

**Theorem.** Induction exactly as in the body, with the sweep order
reversed: among optimal schedules agreeing with greedy's on the longest
prefix (in decreasing start order), a first disagreement could be exchanged
away because greedy's pick starts no earlier, and a leftover interval beyond
greedy's last pick would have been taken by the sweep.

The two criteria are mirror images: reversing the time axis maps every
interval $[s, f)$ to $[-f, -s)$, turning latest start into earliest finish,
so the result also follows by applying the body's theorem to the reversed
instance. Either argument is complete on its own.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Two properties, and the fork to dynamic programming

The proof succeeded for reasons worth naming, because they are the reusable
diagnosis. Two properties carried it, and textbooks state both as the
preconditions of the paradigm.

<Definition title="greedy-choice property">
A problem has the **greedy-choice property** when some optimal solution
makes the locally best first choice; equivalently, when the greedy first
choice is contained in at least one optimal solution. The exchange argument
is the standard way to prove it.
</Definition>

<Definition title="optimal substructure">
A problem has **optimal substructure** when an optimal solution contains,
within it, an optimal solution to the subproblem that remains after its
first choice.
</Definition>

Interval scheduling has both. The lemma is precisely the greedy-choice
property. Optimal substructure is the cut-and-paste step hidden inside the
induction: once $g_1$ is chosen, what remains is a fresh interval-scheduling
instance, the intervals starting at or after $f_{g_1}$, and any improvement
to the rest of the schedule would improve the whole, so the rest must be
optimal for the residual instance.

The fork between the module's paradigms sits exactly here. Dynamic
programming, next lesson, needs only optimal substructure: it solves *every*
subproblem a choice could leave behind, then makes each choice by comparing
the subproblems' computed answers. A greedy algorithm is the degenerate case
in which the comparison is a foregone conclusion: the greedy-choice property
says the winner of that comparison is known before any subproblem is solved,
so nothing needs to be tabulated and one sweep suffices. When the property
fails, the comparison is genuinely necessary, and the problem is dynamic
programming's.

## Knapsack twice: one criterion, optimal and 27% off

The fork is invisible in a problem statement until it is tested, and the
sharpest demonstration is a pair of problems that differ by one clause. In
the *fractional knapsack* problem, a knapsack of capacity $W$ pounds is to
be filled from $n$ items, item $i$ weighing $w_i$ with value $v_i$, and
items are divisible: any fraction of an item may be taken, with
proportional value. The *0/1 knapsack* problem is identical except each
item is taken whole or not at all.

### The fractional case: density order, proved by a shift

The plausible criterion is value density $v_i / w_i$: take the most
valuable-per-pound item first. The standard three-item instance, capacity
$W = 50$:

| item | weight | value | \$/lb |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 10 | \$60 | 6 |
| 2 | 20 | \$100 | 5 |
| 3 | 30 | \$120 | 4 |

<Example title="fractional knapsack, greedy by density">
Take all of item 1: 10 lb used, \$60. Take all of item 2: 30 lb used,
\$160. Item 3 no longer fits whole; take the fraction that fits,
$20/30$ of it, worth $\tfrac{20}{30} \times 120 = \text{\textdollar}80$. The knapsack is
full at 50 lb and the value is $60 + 100 + 80 = \text{\textdollar}240$.
</Example>

Density order is provably optimal here, by an exchange in the schema above:
if a filling puts weight on a lower-density item while a higher-density item
has supply left, shifting a small amount $\delta$ of weight from the low
item to the high one changes the value by
$\delta\,(v_h/w_h - v_l/w_l) \ge 0$, and repeating the shift morphs any
optimal filling into the greedy one. The closing exercises carry this proof
in full.

### The 0/1 case: the same numbers, and the shift is illegal

On the 0/1 problem the same criterion fails on the same three items. Greedy
takes item 1, then item 2, and stops: item 3 needs 30 lb and only 20 remain.
The load is \$160 with a fifth of the knapsack empty. The optimum skips the
densest item entirely: items 2 and 3 fill all 50 lb for \$220.

<Figure
  src="./figures/knapsack-panels.svg"
  alt="Three capacity bars: the fractional greedy fill reaching $240 with a 20-pound slice of item 3; the 0/1 greedy fill reaching $160 with 20 pounds empty; the 0/1 optimum of items 2 and 3 reaching $220"
  caption="Same items, same criterion: divisibility is the entire difference between provably optimal and 27% below optimal."
/>

The diagnosis lands exactly on the schema's second step. The exchange that
proved the fractional case moves $\delta$ pounds between items; in the 0/1
problem no such move is legal, because a fraction of an item is not a
solution. Removing item 1 and inserting "20 pounds of item 3" is not an
available swap, so the greedy-choice property has no proof, and the
counterexample confirms it has no truth. Optimal substructure still holds:
whether to include item 1 depends on comparing the best load *with* it
against the best load *without* it, two subproblems that must actually be
solved. That comparison is the dynamic program that
<Ref lesson="dynamic-programming-ii-two-dimensional">the 0/1 knapsack
table</Ref> computes.

<KeyFact>
What licenses the density criterion is the feasible set, not the values:
divisibility is what makes "shift $\delta$ pounds between items" a legal
solution, so the exchange step has a move to make. Freezing each item to
all-or-nothing destroys that move: what dies is the proof step, not the
intuition, which reads identically in both problems — exactly why the
failure is silent.
</KeyFact>

## Where the paradigm hands off

Two more failures close the lesson, chosen because the next two lessons
solve both problems exactly. They are the seam between this lesson and
dynamic programming, stated with numbers.

### Rod cutting: the best ratio yields 32, the optimum is 33

A rod of length 7 is cut into integer-length pieces, and a
piece of length $\ell$ sells for $v(\ell)$:

| $\ell$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $v(\ell)$ | 1 | 10 | 13 | 18 | 20 | 31 | 32 |

The greedy instinct, cut the piece with the best value per unit length
first, selects length 6 at $31/6 \approx 5.2$ per unit, leaves a remnant of
length 1 worth 1, and totals $31 + 1 = 32$. The optimum is $2 + 2 + 3$ for
$10 + 10 + 13 = 33$. No piece in the optimal cut has the best ratio; the
value sits in a combination that no single-piece criterion sees, and the
proof obligation could never have been met.

### Coin change, and the seam forward

Coin change has the same shape: with denominations $\{1, 3, 4\}$ and target
6, taking the largest coin first gives $4 + 1 + 1$, three coins, while the
optimum is $3 + 3$, two. The coin-change exercise below dissects it,
together with the reason the same greedy happens to be correct for US
coinage.

What is now established: the greedy template costs a sort and a sweep; its
output is feasible always and optimal only when proved so; the exchange
argument is the proof schema, and it works exactly when the problem has the
greedy-choice property on top of optimal substructure. Rod cutting, coin
change, and 0/1 knapsack have only the latter, and
<Ref lesson="dynamic-programming-i-one-dimensional">dynamic programming
I</Ref> opens by solving the first two with a one-dimensional table,
computing the 33 and the two-coin answer that greed missed;
<Ref lesson="dynamic-programming-ii-two-dimensional">dynamic programming
II</Ref> settles 0/1 knapsack. The paradigm itself returns later with its
proofs attached: Dijkstra's algorithm
(<Ref lesson="shortest-paths-dijkstra">shortest paths</Ref>) finalizes
nodes greedily, and Kruskal's and Prim's algorithms
(<Ref lesson="minimum-spanning-trees">minimum spanning trees</Ref>) are
exchange arguments over cuts. Huffman's optimal compression code is the
other classical member of the family, proved by the same exchange schema;
Cormen et al. §15.3 carries that proof.

<Solution>
Strategy hint: build a four-interval optimal chain, then plant a trap
interval that bridges the middle of the chain while everything else piles
conflicts onto the chain's own members.

The instance (11 intervals): a chain $A_1 = [0,4)$, $A_2 = [4,8)$,

$[11,13)$; and the trap $X = [7,9)$.

Conflict counts: $X$ overlaps only $A_2$ and $A_3$: 2 conflicts. $A_1$ and

Fewest-conflicts-first therefore takes $X$ first, which eliminates $A_2$
and $A_3$. Whatever it does next, the schedule can contain at most one
interval from $\{A_1, [3,5)\text{'s}\}$ and one from
$\{A_4, [11,13)\text{'s}\}$: tally at most 3. The chain
$\{A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4\}$ has size 4, and earliest-finish-first finds a
size-4 schedule by the theorem.

optimal schedule's first choice for $X$ is not loss-free, since $X$'s two
conflicts sit in the *middle* of the time axis and destroy two chain
positions at once. Low conflict count bounds neither the finish time nor
the damage a selection does to what remains.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

still needed." Use denominations $\{1, 3, 4\}$ with target 6. Then explain
what the correctness of largest-coin-first on US denominations
$\{1, 5, 10, 25\}$ (a true fact, not proved here) does and does not imply
about the criterion.

<Solution>
On $\{1,3,4\}$ with target 6, largest-coin-first takes 4, leaving 2, then
$1 + 1$: three coins. The optimum is $3 + 3$: two coins. The claim's error
is the inference "covers the most now $\Rightarrow$ fewest coins later";
covering 4 leaves a remnant, 2, that the denomination set handles badly,
while two mid-sized choices leave no remnant at all. It is rod cutting's
failure in miniature: the best single step strands the remainder.

The US-coin fact shows that greedy correctness is a property of the
*instance family*, here the denomination set, not of the criterion: the
same rule is optimal for every target on $\{1,5,10,25\}$ and wrong on
$\{1,3,4\}$ at target 6. Surviving every test on one family is therefore
no evidence for another, and only a proof for the family at hand closes
the question. The general problem, minimum coins for an arbitrary
denomination set, is solved by the table of
<Ref lesson="dynamic-programming-i-one-dimensional">dynamic programming
I</Ref>, which compares the subproblem answers greedy refuses to compute.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A problem this lesson has not treated. One machine, $n$ jobs; job $j$ needs
$t_j$ consecutive minutes and carries a deadline $d_j$. Every job must run,
back to back from time $0$ with no idle gaps, so a schedule is an ordering of
the jobs; if job $j$ finishes at time $C_j$, its lateness is
$\max(0,\, C_j - d_j)$, and the objective is to minimize the *maximum*
lateness over all jobs. Design the algorithm: propose an ordering criterion,
hunt counterexamples against it and against at least one rival criterion, and
settle the survivor's status — an exchange lemma if you can prove one, a
counterexample if you cannot.

<Solution>
Three criteria present themselves: shortest job first ($t_j$ ascending),
smallest slack first ($d_j - t_j$ ascending), earliest deadline first
($d_j$ ascending).

Shortest-first dies on two jobs: $t_1 = 1, d_1 = 100$ and
$t_2 = 10, d_2 = 10$. It runs job 1 first, so job 2 finishes at $11$,
lateness $1$; the reverse order finishes job 2 at $10$ and job 1 at $11$,
both on time, maximum lateness $0$. Smallest-slack-first dies on
$t_1 = 1, d_1 = 2$ (slack $1$) and $t_2 = 10, d_2 = 10$ (slack $0$): it runs
job 2 first and job 1 finishes at $11$, lateness $9$, while the reverse
order's maximum lateness is $1$ (job 2 at $11$ against deadline $10$).

Earliest deadline first survives the hunt, and the exchange lemma closes it —
step 2 of the schema, instantiated as an adjacent swap. Call an *inversion* a
consecutive pair out of deadline order: job $i$ directly before job $j$ with
$d_i > d_j$. Swapping the two changes no other job's finish time. Job $j$
moves earlier, so its lateness cannot increase. Job $i$ now finishes exactly
where $j$ used to, at some time $f$, and
$\max(0, f - d_i) \le \max(0, f - d_j)$ because $d_i > d_j$: job $i$'s new
lateness is at most job $j$'s old one. The swap therefore never increases the
maximum lateness. Any ordering other than earliest-deadline order contains an
adjacent inversion, and each swap removes one inverted pair, so finitely many
swaps morph any optimal schedule into earliest-deadline order at no cost
(neighbors with equal deadlines may sit in either order; swapping them
changes neither job's bound). Some optimal schedule is in earliest-deadline
order: the greedy-choice property holds, and the criterion is provable, not
merely unrefuted. The problem and this proof are Kleinberg and Tardos's
§4.2, *scheduling to minimize lateness*.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Carry the fractional-knapsack exchange argument in full: prove that taking
items in nonincreasing density order $v_1/w_1 \ge v_2/w_2 \ge \cdots$, each
to the largest extent possible, maximizes the value of the load. Represent
a solution as amounts $x_i \in [0, w_i]$ with $\sum_i x_i \le W$, and name
the exact sentence of your proof that fails for the 0/1 problem.

<Solution>
Assume $\sum_i w_i > W$, otherwise every solution takes everything and the
claim is trivial; then the greedy load $g$ fills the knapsack:
$\sum_i g_i = W$, and any optimal load $x$ may be assumed to fill it too,
since raising any $x_i$ never lowers the value.

Suppose $x \ne g$ and let $i$ be the smallest index with $x_i \ne g_i$.
Greedy takes the maximum legal amount of item $i$ given full agreement on
items $1, \dots, i-1$, so $x_i < g_i$. Since both loads total $W$ and agree
before $i$, some $j > i$ has $x_j > g_j$. Let
$\delta = \min(g_i - x_i,\; x_j - g_j) > 0$ and shift $\delta$ pounds from
item $j$ to item $i$: the new load $x'$ is feasible ($x'_i \le g_i \le
w_i$, $x'_j \ge g_j \ge 0$, total unchanged) and its value changes by
$$
\delta \left( \frac{v_i}{w_i} - \frac{v_j}{w_j} \right) \ge 0,
$$
by the density ordering. So $x'$ is still optimal and agrees with $g$ on a
longer prefix (either $x'_i = g_i$, or $x'_j = g_j$ and a later shift will
finish index $i$). Each shift strictly reduces
$\sum_i |x_i - g_i| > 0$ by $2\delta$, and the quantity cannot decrease
forever below zero, so finitely many shifts transform $x$ into $g$ with no
value lost: $g$ is optimal.

The sentence that fails for 0/1 is the definition of the shift: "$x'$ is
feasible" uses $x_i$ ranging over the continuum $[0, w_i]$, and with
$x_i \in \{0, w_i\}$ the amount $\delta$ is forced to be a whole item,
which need not fit. On the three-item instance of the lesson, exchanging
item 1 for $\delta = 10$ pounds of item 3 is exactly the illegal move.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The sweep tests each new interval against only the *last* selected one,
yet claims compatibility with the whole selected set. Justify the shortcut
with a loop invariant, in the three-part form of
<Ref lesson="loop-invariants-and-correctness">loop invariants and
correctness</Ref>: state a claim that holds before every iteration of
`GreedySelect`'s loop, discharge initialization, maintenance, and
termination, and state precisely which property of the algorithm your
invariant does and does not establish.

<Solution>
**Invariant.** Before the iteration with counter $m$: (i) $A$ is a
pairwise-compatible subset of $\{a_1, \dots, a_{m-1}\}$; (ii)
$\texttt{last} \in A$ and $f_{\texttt{last}} = \max\{f_i : a_i \in A\}$.

**Initialization.** Before $m = 2$: $A = \{a_1\}$ is trivially compatible,
$\texttt{last} = 1$, and $f_1$ is the maximum finish in $A$.

**Maintenance.** Suppose the invariant holds before iteration $m$. If
$s_m < f_{\texttt{last}}$, nothing changes and the invariant carries over
with $m+1$. If $s_m \ge f_{\texttt{last}}$, then for every $a_i \in A$,
part (ii) gives $f_i \le f_{\texttt{last}} \le s_m$, so $a_m$ is
compatible with each of them and (i) holds for
$A \cup \{a_m\}$. Setting $\texttt{last} = m$ restores (ii): the input is
sorted by finish time, so $f_m \ge f_i$ for every $i \le m$, in particular
for every member of the new $A$.

**Termination.** The loop exits with $m = n + 1$, and (i) instantiated
there says $A$ is a pairwise-compatible subset of the whole instance: the
output is *feasible*, and the one-comparison test never admitted a
conflict.

What the invariant does not establish is optimality. Feasibility is a
property of each state of the loop, which is what invariants certify;
maximality is a comparison against every other schedule, which is what the
exchange argument certifies. The lesson's guarantee is the conjunction of
the two proofs, and neither substitutes for the other.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the exchange schema one module ahead. A connected weighted graph is a
set of nodes joined by edges, each edge with a distinct positive weight; a
*spanning tree* is a subset of edges that connects all nodes and contains
no cycle, and a *minimum* spanning tree has the smallest total edge weight.
Let $(S, V \setminus S)$ be any split of the nodes into two nonempty sets,
and let $e$ be the lightest edge with one endpoint in each set. Prove that
some minimum spanning tree contains $e$.

<Solution>
Strategy hint: this is step 2 of the schema with "interval" replaced by
"edge crossing the split"; the swap partner is found on a cycle.

Let $T$ be a minimum spanning tree, and suppose $e = (u, v) \notin T$.
Since $T$ connects all nodes, it contains a path from $u$ to $v$, so
$T \cup \{e\}$ contains a cycle through $e$. Walking that cycle from $u$
(in $S$) to $v$ (in $V \setminus S$) and back, the walk changes sides at
least twice, so the cycle contains a second edge $e'$ with one endpoint in
each set. Exchange: $T' = (T \setminus \{e'\}) \cup \{e\}$.

*Feasibility.* $T'$ has $|T|$ edges. Removing $e'$ from the cycle of
$T \cup \{e\}$ breaks no connectivity, because the rest of the cycle still
joins $e'$'s endpoints, so $T'$ is connected; a connected graph on $n$
nodes with $n-1$ edges is acyclic, so $T'$ is a spanning tree.

*No loss.* $e$ is the lightest edge crossing the split and $e'$ crosses
it, so $w(e) < w(e')$ (weights distinct), giving
$w(T') = w(T) - w(e') + w(e) < w(T)$. That contradicts $T$'s minimality,
so the supposition fails: $e \in T$ for every minimum spanning tree, in
particular for some.

The statement just proved is the *cut property*, the single lemma from
which <Ref lesson="minimum-spanning-trees">minimum spanning trees</Ref>
derives the correctness of both Kruskal's algorithm (sort edges by weight,
sweep, keep what creates no cycle) and Prim's (grow one tree by its
lightest outgoing edge). Both are the greedy template of this lesson with
the exchange already done.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Quickselect's expected linear time collapses to O(n^2) against an
adversarial pivot sequence; choosing the pivot as the median of medians of
groups of five restores a guaranteed 30-70 split and makes selection
worst-case linear. The sorting module closed with
<Ref lesson="binary-search-and-order-statistics">quickselect</Ref>:
expected $\Theta(n)$ for any rank, worst case $\Theta(n^2)$, and its final
exercise walked into an apparent circle: a deterministic escape needs a
pivot guaranteed to be near-central, a near-central element is
approximately a median, and finding medians is the very selection problem
the pivot was meant to solve. This chapter, off the course's main line,
breaks the circle: the pivot need only be approximately central, and an
approximate median can be computed by recursion on an array one fifth the
size. Nothing later in the course depends on this chapter.

The route: the specification a pivot rule must meet; the construction,
run in full on 25 concrete elements; the counting argument that
guarantees the 30-70 split; the recurrence
$T(n) \le T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + O(n)$ and why fractions summing below one
force linear time; why groups of five and not three; and the honest
accounting against the randomized algorithm.

## The specification: a guaranteed-central pivot, found in linear time

The flaw to be repaired was quantified in
<Ref lesson="binary-search-and-order-statistics">the order-statistics
lesson</Ref>: quickselect's cost is governed by
$T(n) \le \max_{0 \le \ell \le n-1} T(\ell) + O(n)$, where $\ell$ is the
size of the surviving side. A pivot sequence that always picks an
extreme element gives $T(n) = T(n-1) + O(n) = \Theta(n^2)$, the
subtractive recurrence of
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">the recurrences
lesson</Ref>. Random pivots make that sequence improbable; no input can
force it, but no run is protected from it either.

### Two demands in tension

A worst-case guarantee therefore needs a deterministic pivot rule with
two properties, each pulling against the other:

- **rank control**: the pivot's rank is guaranteed, for every input, to
  lie a constant fraction inside both ends, so every partition discards
  a constant fraction of the array;
- **linear cost**: the rule itself runs in $O(n)$, or the pivot search
  swallows the time it was meant to save.

### Lowering the demand: the exact median of a sample

An exact median satisfies the first property perfectly and, as far as
the course has shown, costs a sort. The repair is to lower the demand:
compute the exact median of a small sample whose structure forces it to
be an approximate median of the whole, and compute it by calling the
selection algorithm itself on the sample.

The repair was found in 1973 by Manuel Blum, Robert Floyd, Vaughan
Pratt, Ronald Rivest, and Robert Tarjan, in the paper that first proved
selection needs no sorting
([*Time Bounds for Selection*](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-0000(73)80033-9)).
The sample has size $n/5$: small enough that the extra recursion leaves
the total work linear. The next three sections deliver each promise in
turn.

## The construction: medians of five, then the median of those

### Group, take medians, recurse

The sample is built in one pass. Split the array into
$\lceil n/5 \rceil$ groups of five consecutive elements (the last group
may be smaller). Find each group's median by brute force: five elements
are a constant-size problem, and a fixed comparison network settles the
median of five in at most 6 comparisons.

Collect the $\lceil n/5 \rceil$ group medians into an array $M$, and
call the selection algorithm recursively on $M$ to get their median: the
*median of medians*, written $\mathit{mom}$. Then proceed exactly as
quickselect: partition the full array around $\mathit{mom}$ and recurse
into the one side containing rank $k$.

```text
MomSelect(A[1..n], k):                ▷ k-th smallest of n distinct keys
  if n ≤ 25
    sort A and return A[k]            ▷ constant-size base case
  m ← ⌈n/5⌉
  for i ← 1 to m
    M[i] ← median of A[5i−4 .. 5i]    ▷ brute force, ≤ 6 comparisons each
  mom ← MomSelect(M[1..m], ⌈m/2⌉)     ▷ recursion 1: choose the pivot
  r ← Partition(A[1..n], mom)         ▷ mom lands in its true rank r
  if k = r        return A[r]
  else if k < r   return MomSelect(A[1..r−1], k)      ▷ recursion 2
  else            return MomSelect(A[r+1..n], k − r)  ▷ recursion 2
```

### Two recursions, two jobs

The procedure recurses for two different purposes, and keeping them
separate is the key to reading it. **Recursion 1** steers: it computes
the pivot, on an array of size $m \approx n/5$. **Recursion 2**
searches: it is ordinary quickselect descent, on the surviving side of
the partition, which the next section bounds by $7n/10$ plus a constant.
One recursion is being used to guarantee the good behavior of another.

## The guarantee: three-tenths eliminated on each side, unconditionally

The example's split (13 against 11) was comfortable; the claim to prove
is that no input produces an uncomfortable one. The argument is a
counting argument on a picture.

### The grid: count ranks, not positions

Draw the array as a $5 \times (n/5)$ grid, one group per column, each
column sorted small to large, and the columns arranged left to right by
their medians. The algorithm never performs this arrangement; the grid
exists only to count ranks, and rank counts do not depend on where
elements physically sit.

<Figure
  src="./figures/grid.svg"
  alt="Seven columns of five dots, each column sorted top to bottom, columns ordered by their amber median dots; the pivot x sits at the central median; a blue shaded region covers the top three rows of the left four columns, at least 3n/10 elements known to be at most x, and a green region covers the bottom three rows of the right four columns, at least 3n/10 elements known to be at least x"
  caption="The grid argument: half the columns have medians ≤ x, and each such column contributes three elements ≤ x. Recreated from CLRS Figure 9.3."
/>

<Theorem title="the elimination bound">
Let $n$ be a multiple of 5, let $g = n/5$, and let $\mathit{mom}$ be the
median of the $g$ group medians. Then at least $3n/10$ elements of the
array are $\le \mathit{mom}$ and at least $3n/10$ are
$\ge \mathit{mom}$. Consequently each side of the partition around
$\mathit{mom}$ contains at most $7n/10$ elements, whichever side the
recursion enters.

<Proof>
$\mathit{mom}$ is the $\lceil g/2 \rceil$-th smallest of the $g$
medians, so $\lceil g/2 \rceil$ medians (counting $\mathit{mom}$ itself)
are $\le \mathit{mom}$: in the grid, the median dots of the left half of
the columns. Each such median sits above two smaller elements of its own
column, and those two are $\le$ their median $\le \mathit{mom}$. Each of
the $\lceil g/2 \rceil$ columns therefore contributes three elements
$\le \mathit{mom}$, giving
$3\lceil g/2 \rceil \ge 3g/2 = 3n/10$ elements $\le \mathit{mom}$: the
shaded upper-left region of the figure.

Symmetrically, $\lfloor g/2 \rfloor + 1$ medians are
$\ge \mathit{mom}$, each with two larger column-mates, giving
$3(\lfloor g/2 \rfloor + 1) \ge 3g/2 = 3n/10$ elements
$\ge \mathit{mom}$: the lower-right region.

The partition's low side contains only elements strictly smaller than
$\mathit{mom}$, so it excludes the $\ge 3n/10$ elements that are
$\ge \mathit{mom}$: at most $n - 3n/10 = 7n/10$ elements remain.
The same count bounds the high side.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### A floor on elimination, not a prediction

Against the worked example: $n = 25$, $g = 5$, and
$\lceil g/2 \rceil = 3$ medians (22, 29, 44) are $\le 44$, contributing
the nine elements $\{9, 15, 22\}$, $\{3, 12, 29\}$, $\{18, 26, 44\}$.
The guarantee says each partition side holds at most $25 - 9 = 16$; the
actual sides held 13 and 11. The bound is a floor on elimination, not a
prediction of the split.

<Remark title="floors, ceilings, and the partial last group">
Exact bookkeeping perturbs every count by a constant, not by a growth
rate: $\lceil n/5 \rceil$ groups, a last group of fewer than five, and
the $\lceil m/2 \rceil$-th median shift the guarantee to
$3n/10 - O(1)$ eliminated and $7n/10 + O(1)$ kept (at most six extra
elements under one standard accounting). The recurrence with these
constants still solves to $O(n)$, by the same license for brushing
floors, ceilings, and lower-order terms that
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">the recurrences lesson</Ref>
established. Implementations differ only in where they hide the slop:
CLRS trims the input to a multiple of five with a $\Theta(n)$
preprocessing loop; Erickson pads the last group with $+\infty$
sentinels.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The 30-70 split is unconditional: it is a counting fact about ranks,
true for every input and every adversary, where the randomized
guarantee of <Ref lesson="binary-search-and-order-statistics">the
previous lesson</Ref> is an expectation that an unlucky run of pivot
draws can void. That difference in kind, not the constants, is what
this algorithm exists to supply.
</KeyFact>

## The recurrence: two fractions that sum below one

A good pivot alone does not finish the analysis, because MomSelect pays
for it with a second recursive call. Each invocation on $n$ elements
does $O(n)$ local work (group medians and partition), plus a steering
call on $n/5$ elements, plus a searching call on at most $7n/10$
elements:

$$
T(n) \;\le\; T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + cn .
$$

### Outside the Master theorem's shape

This recurrence has two subproblems with different shrink factors, so
no single $b$ in $aT(n/b) + f(n)$ fits it. It belongs to the
unequal-split family
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">the recurrences lesson</Ref>
handled with the recursion-tree method, which never needed the Master
theorem's hypotheses, only levels to sum.

The decisive quantity is the total size handed to the two calls:
$n/5 + 7n/10 = 9n/10$. Every node of the recursion tree passes at most
$9/10$ of its own input down to its children, so the level totals decay
geometrically, and the root's $cn$ dominates the whole tree.

<Figure
  src="./figures/mom-tree.svg"
  alt="The recursion tree for T(n) = T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + cn: a root doing cn work with children cn/5 and 7cn/10, grandchildren cn/25, 7cn/50, 7cn/50, 49cn/100, and a level-sum column showing cn, nine-tenths cn, nine-tenths squared cn, totaling at most 10cn"
  caption="Level sums shrink by 9/10 per level: a geometric series, total ≤ 10cn."
/>

### The geometric series, made a theorem

<Theorem title="worst-case linear selection (Blum–Floyd–Pratt–Rivest–Tarjan 1973)">
MomSelect finds the $k$-th smallest of $n$ elements in $\Theta(n)$ time
in the worst case.

<Proof>
The proof mode is a recursion-tree bound on
$T(n) \le T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + cn$; floors, ceilings, and the additive
constant in $7n/10 + O(1)$ are suppressed under the license above.

A node processing $s$ elements does $cs$ local work and hands its two
children a total of $s/5 + 7s/10 = (9/10)\,s$ elements. By induction on
depth, the total input size across all nodes at depth $t$ is at most
$(9/10)^t\, n$, so the total work at depth $t$ is at most
$c\,(9/10)^t\, n$. The tree is lopsided (the steering branch bottoms out
after $\log_5 n$ levels, the searching branch only after
$\log_{10/7} n$), but that is immaterial: branches that have already
bottomed out contribute zero to deeper levels, which only helps the
bound. Summing the geometric series,

$$
T(n) \;\le\; \sum_{t \ge 0} c \left(\tfrac{9}{10}\right)^{t} n
\;=\; 10\,cn \;=\; O(n).
$$

The matching $\Omega(n)$ is immediate: the first call reads all $n$
elements. A substitution proof certifies the same bound and handles the
exact constants (CLRS Theorem 9.3).
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<KeyFact>
The linearity test for any recurrence of this shape is one addition:
with recursive calls on fractions $a_1, \ldots, a_j$ of the input and
linear local work, the level sums decay geometrically exactly when
$a_1 + \cdots + a_j < 1$, and the total is $cn / (1 - \sum_i a_i)$.
MomSelect sits at $\frac{1}{5} + \frac{7}{10} = \frac{9}{10}$. The next
section shows what happens at exactly 1.
</KeyFact>

## Why five: every cheaper group size crosses the line

### Groups of three: the same guarantee, a worse recurrence

Every ingredient of the construction seems to survive a cheaper group
size. Groups of three have brute-force medians at 3 comparisons instead
of 6, and the same grid argument still eliminates a constant fraction:
half the $n/3$ medians are $\le \mathit{mom}$, each bringing one smaller
group-mate, so $2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{n}{3} = n/3$ elements
are settled on each side.

From that, one would expect the same $O(n)$ result with smaller
constants. The recurrence says the opposite. With groups of three, the
steering call costs $T(n/3)$ and the searching call keeps up to
$n - n/3 = 2n/3$:

$$
T(n) \;\le\; T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + cn ,
\qquad \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{2}{3} = 1 .
$$

The fractions sum to exactly one: each node hands its children the same
total size it received, so level sums never decay.
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">The recurrences lesson</Ref>
solved exactly this recurrence: every complete level totals $cn$, the
tree has $\Theta(\log n)$ levels, and the answer is $\Theta(n \log n)$.
Selection with groups of three is no faster than sorting; the entire
construction returns nothing.

  src="./figures/three-tree.svg"
  alt="The recursion tree for T(n) = T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + cn: the same tree layout as the previous figure, but the level-sum column reads cn at every level, totaling Theta(n log n) over Theta(log n) levels"
  caption="Groups of 3: fractions sum to exactly 1, level sums stay at cn, and the total is Θ(n log n) — no faster than sorting."
/>

### The criterion: fractions that sum below one

The comparison of the two trees locates the design constraint
precisely. Elimination is not the criterion; both group sizes eliminate
a constant fraction. The criterion is that the two recursive fractions
**sum below one**.

The group size controls both fractions at once: smaller groups make the
steering call bigger ($n/3$ against $n/5$) and eliminate less per group
(2 elements against 3). Groups of seven also pass, with fractions
$\frac{1}{7} + \frac{5}{7} = \frac{6}{7}$; an exercise below carries
the count.

### Even sizes: four sits exactly at the line

An even group has no middle element; the standard convention is the
*lower median*, the $\frac{g}{2}$-th smallest of a group of $g$. With
it, the count for groups of four runs as before. Sort a group as
$a \le b \le c \le d$, median $b$: half the $n/4$ group medians are
$\le \mathit{mom}$, each bringing one smaller group-mate, so
$2 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{n}{4} = n/4$ elements are settled
below $\mathit{mom}$.

The guarantee is now asymmetric. A group whose median is
$\ge \mathit{mom}$ contributes $b$, $c$, and $d$: three elements, hence
$3n/8$ settled above. The searching call keeps up to $n - n/4 = 3n/4$
in the bad direction, the steering call costs $T(n/4)$, and
$\frac{1}{4} + \frac{3}{4} = 1$: the same stalled tree as groups of
three, $\Theta(n \log n)$.

Groups of six do pass the test — the fractions come to
$\frac{1}{6} + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{11}{12}$, and an exercise below runs
the count — but $11/12$ decays slower than five's $9/10$ and a
brute-force median of six costs more than one of five. Five is the
smallest group size, odd or even, whose fractions sum below one, and
that arithmetic is the entire content of the constant.

<KeyFact>
The general test, for odd groups of $g = 2t+1$: the steering fraction
is $\frac{1}{g}$, the elimination is $\frac{(t+1)}{2g}n$ per side, and
the two fractions sum to $1 + \frac{1-t}{2g}$ — below one exactly when
$t \ge 2$, that is, $g \ge 5$. The constant five is not tuned for
speed; it is the smallest value at which a one-line inequality turns
true.
</KeyFact>

## The price of the guarantee: 16n against 4n

### Counting the constant

The worst-case bound is bought with large constants, and this section
prices them honestly. Per invocation on $n$ elements, the group medians
cost $6 \cdot n/5 = 6n/5$ comparisons, and partitioning adds only about
$2n/5$ more: the grid argument has already settled $3n/10$ elements on
each side of the pivot without further comparisons.

The comparison recurrence is $C(n) \le C(n/5) + C(7n/10) + 8n/5$, and
the same geometric series gives $C(n) \le 16n$ worst-case comparisons;
the first exercise derives this. Randomized quickselect makes about
$4n$ comparisons in expectation, by a sharper analysis than
<Ref lesson="binary-search-and-order-statistics">the previous
lesson's</Ref> generation argument, which proved the cruder $8n$.

The deterministic guarantee therefore costs roughly a factor of four in
comparisons, and more in practice: the group-median pass touches memory
in a pattern caches serve poorly.

### The crossover with sorting: 65,536 elements

Against full sorting the constant bites too. Sorting and reading off
rank $k$ costs about $n \lg n$ comparisons, so $16n$ undercuts it only
once $\lg n > 16$: past $n = 2^{16} = 65{,}536$ elements. Below that
size, the guaranteed-linear algorithm loses even to the
$\Theta(n \log n)$ method it exists to beat.

  src="./figures/crossover.svg"
  alt="Comparisons per element against input size on a log scale: horizontal lines at 4 (randomized quickselect, expected) and 16 (MomSelect, worst case), and a rising lg n line for full sorting that crosses the 16 line at n = 2 to the 16, 65,536; the quickselect line lies below both everywhere"
  caption="Comparisons per element: 16n undercuts n lg n only past n = 2¹⁶ = 65,536, and randomized quickselect's 4n sits below both at every size."
/>

<Aside>
**The comparison-count frontier.** $16n$ prices this construction under
this analysis, not the selection problem itself; the original 1973
paper already tuned its algorithm's constant well below the textbook
account. For the median, Schönhage, Paterson, and Pippenger (1976) got
down to $3n + o(n)$ comparisons with a heavier machinery of merging
"factories", and [Dor and
Zwick](https://doi.org/10.1137/S0097539795288611) brought the constant
under $2.95n$; the best known lower bounds sit just above $2n$ (also
Dor and Zwick). The exact comparison cost of the median remains open
half a century after linearity itself was settled.
</Aside>

### What survives in practice: introselect

So the algorithm's value is not in replacing randomized quickselect on
typical data; it does not, and standard-library implementations reflect
that. David Musser's 1997 *introselect* (the selection half of
[introsort](https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1097-024X(199708)27:8%3C983::AID-SPE117%3E3.0.CO;2-%23),
behind C++'s `std::nth_element` in common toolchains) runs plain
quickselect and switches to a median-of-medians fallback only when the
recursion depth reveals a run of bad pivots: expected-case constants,
worst-case guarantee, each supplied by the algorithm that owns it.

The same division answers "which one" directly: randomized quickselect
where the workload is typical and a slow run costs only latency; the
deterministic pivot where the input is adversarial or a hard deadline
makes $\Theta(n^2)$ an outage. The construction's other export is the
technique itself: a recursion used to **steer** another recursion, with
the recurrence discipline (fractions summing below one) that makes the
combination affordable.

What is established: selection of any order statistic is $\Theta(n)$ in
the worst case in the comparison model, by a pivot rule whose 30-70
split is a counting guarantee rather than an expectation, at the price
of a $16n$ comparison constant that keeps randomized quickselect the
default in practice. The module's price list is now complete, with its
selection row upgraded from "expected" to "guaranteed".

No later lesson uses this chapter; the core path resumes with
<Ref lesson="divide-and-conquer-design">divide-and-conquer design</Ref>,
where recurrence shapes like the ones compared here become tools for
designing algorithms rather than analyzing them.

single step is cheap.

The claim smuggles in "constant fraction eliminated $\Rightarrow$

fractions must sum strictly below one. At exactly one, selection by
this construction is no faster than sorting.

With $g = n/7$ groups, $\mathit{mom}$ is the median of the $g$ group
medians, so at least $g/2$ medians are $\le \mathit{mom}$, and each
brings the three smaller elements of its own sorted group of seven:
$4 \cdot \frac{g}{2} = \frac{4}{2} \cdot \frac{n}{7} = \frac{2n}{7}$
elements eliminated on each side. The searching call keeps at most
$n - 2n/7 = 5n/7$, and the steering call runs on $n/7$ medians:

$$
T(n) \le T(n/7) + T(5n/7) + cn,
\qquad \tfrac{1}{7} + \tfrac{5}{7} = \tfrac{6}{7} < 1 .
$$

The fraction test passes, so the level sums decay with ratio $6/7$ and
$T(n) \le cn \sum_t (6/7)^t = 7cn = O(n)$. The decay constant 7 beats
the groups-of-5 tree's 10, but $c$ itself grows: a brute-force median
of seven costs more comparisons per group than a median of five, and
the per-level total shifts accordingly. Any odd group size of at least
five works; the fraction test, not elimination, is what small sizes
fail.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The body ran the even-size count for groups of four. Run it for groups
of six: take the group median to be the lower median (the 3rd smallest
of six), count how many elements a contributing group settles on each
side of $\mathit{mom}$, write the recurrence for the worse side, and
decide whether groups of six give linear selection. If they do, compare
the decay constant against the groups-of-5 tree's 10.

<Solution>
With $m = n/6$ groups, half the group medians are $\le \mathit{mom}$.
A group sorted $a_1 \le \cdots \le a_6$ has lower median $a_3$, so a
contributing group settles $a_1, a_2, a_3$: three elements, giving
$3 \cdot \frac{m}{2} = \frac{n}{4}$ elements below $\mathit{mom}$.

The other direction is stronger: a group whose median is
$\ge \mathit{mom}$ settles $a_3, a_4, a_5, a_6$, four elements, so
$4 \cdot \frac{m}{2} = \frac{n}{3}$ sit above. The worse surviving side
is $n - n/4 = 3n/4$, and the steering call costs $T(n/6)$:

$$
T(n) \le T(n/6) + T(3n/4) + cn,
\qquad \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{11}{12} < 1 .
$$

The test passes: groups of six are linear, with decay constant
$\sum_t (11/12)^t = 12$ against five's 10 — a slower-shrinking tree —
and a costlier brute-force median (six elements per group instead of
five). Even sizes from six up work; none beats five. What sinks four,
like three, is the sum landing exactly on one, not the evenness itself.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Use MomSelect as a black box to make quicksort's worst case
$O(n \log n)$ (CLRS Ex. 9.3-3). Specify the pivot rule, write the
resulting recurrence, solve it with the tools of
<Ref lesson="recurrences-and-master-theorem">the recurrences
lesson</Ref>, and explain why production sorts obtain the same bound a
different way.

<Solution>
Pivot rule: at each quicksort call on a subarray of size $s$, run
$\texttt{MomSelect}(A, \lceil s/2 \rceil)$ to find the subarray's true
median in $\Theta(s)$, and partition around it. Both sides then hold at
most $\lceil s/2 \rceil$ elements, so

$$
T(n) \le 2\,T(n/2) + \Theta(n)
$$

(the $\Theta(n)$ covers selection plus partition). This is merge sort's
recurrence: Master theorem case 2, $T(n) = \Theta(n \log n)$, now as
quicksort's worst case, since no input can produce an unbalanced split.

Production sorts decline this construction because the $\Theta(n)$
hides MomSelect's $16n$-scale constant at every level of the sort. C++
standard libraries instead ship introsort: random-flavored quicksort
that monitors its own recursion depth and, past $\approx 2\lg n$,
abandons quicksort for heapsort, whose
<Ref lesson="heapsort-and-the-sorting-lower-bound">$O(n \log n)$ worst
case</Ref> costs nothing extra to invoke. Same guarantee, cheaper
insurance: the fallback only ever runs on the rare bad path.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Four recurrences, four fates. For each, first name the applicable tool,
then solve:
(a) $T(n) = T(n/5) + T(7n/10) + cn$;
(b) $T(n) = T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + cn$;
(c) $T(n) = 4\,T(n/2) + cn$;
(d) $T(n) = 3\,T(n/2) + cn$.
For each, compute the per-level growth ratio of the recursion tree's
level sums and classify: decaying, constant, or growing.

<Solution>
The ratio is $\sum_i a_i \cdot (\text{fraction}_i)$: the total size
handed down per unit of input.

**(a)** Ratio $\frac{1}{5} + \frac{7}{10} = \frac{9}{10} < 1$: decaying,
root-dominated, $T(n) = \Theta(n)$ (this lesson).

**(b)** Ratio $\frac{1}{3} + \frac{2}{3} = 1$: constant level sums,
$\Theta(\log n)$ levels, $T(n) = \Theta(n \log n)$ (the recurrences
lesson's unequal split).

**(c)** Master-shaped: ratio $4 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 2 > 1$: level sums
double, the leaves dominate, and the total is the leaf count,
$T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_2 4}) = \Theta(n^2)$ (Master case 1).

**(d)** Ratio $3 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{2} > 1$: still
leaf-dominated, $T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_2 3}) = \Theta(n^{1.585})$.

The step from (c) to (d), cutting four half-size recursive calls to
three, moves the exponent from 2 to 1.585 while the local work stays
linear. That move, applied to multiplying $n$-digit numbers, is
Karatsuba's algorithm, the opening result of
<Ref lesson="divide-and-conquer-design">the divide-and-conquer design
lesson</Ref>: you have just computed its payoff before meeting the
algorithm.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

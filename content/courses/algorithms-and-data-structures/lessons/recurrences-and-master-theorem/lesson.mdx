A divide-and-conquer algorithm's running time satisfies a recurrence — T(n)
written in terms of T applied to smaller inputs — and solving that
recurrence, not tracing the recursion by hand, is how its asymptotic running
time is found. <Ref lesson="asymptotic-notation">The first lesson</Ref>
fixed what a running time is (a word-RAM step count, compressed by $O$,
$\Omega$, $\Theta$ to a growth rate), and every count so far came from
loops, where per-line costs multiply by iteration counts.
<Ref lesson="loop-invariants-and-correctness">The previous lesson</Ref>
added the correctness tool and ended at the toolkit's gap: a recursive
algorithm's cost statement is an equation in $T(n)$, not a product, because
the cost of a call includes the cost of further calls. This lesson supplies
the solving technique and completes the module. The route: read the
recurrence off merge sort's code, solve it with a recursion tree, classify
every such tree by the shape of its level sums, state that classification
as the Master theorem, certify guesses by induction, and solve the
recurrences the theorem does not cover.

## From code to equation: reading the recurrence off merge sort

The specimen is *merge sort*: the algorithm whose $50\,n \lg n$
instruction count beat a thousandfold-faster machine running a $2n^2$ sort
in <Ref lesson="asymptotic-notation">the first lesson</Ref>. That
comparison took the $n \lg n$ count on faith; this lesson is where it
comes from. The algorithm sorts by recursion:

```text
MergeSort(A[1..n]):
  if n ≤ 1  return              ▷ Θ(1)
  m ← ⌊n/2⌋                     ▷ Θ(1)
  MergeSort(A[1..m])            ▷ T(⌊n/2⌋)
  MergeSort(A[m+1..n])          ▷ T(⌈n/2⌉)
  Merge(A[1..m], A[m+1..n])     ▷ Θ(n)
```

`Merge` combines two sorted halves into one sorted array by repeatedly
moving the smaller of the two front elements into place: at most $n$
moves and $n - 1$ comparisons, so $\Theta(n)$. The procedure itself, with
its loop invariant, arrives in
<Ref lesson="comparison-sorting-merge-and-quicksort">the sorting
lesson</Ref>; here it is an $\Theta(n)$ combine step, and that is all the
analysis needs.

### Two kinds of cost

The pseudocode's costs split in two. The two recursive calls are
not steps of this call: each is a whole subproblem of half the size,
priced by the same function $T$ being defined. Everything else (the
test, the split, the merge) is work this call performs itself,
$\Theta(n)$ in total. Adding the two kinds:

$$
T(n) = T(\lceil n/2 \rceil) + T(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor) + \Theta(n)
\quad \text{for } n \ge 2, \qquad T(1) = \Theta(1).
$$

<Definition title="divide-and-conquer recurrence">
A **recurrence** is an equation that defines $T(n)$ in terms of $T$ on
smaller arguments, together with a base case. The divide-and-conquer form
is
$$
T(n) = a\,T(n/b) + f(n),
$$
where the constant $a \ge 1$ counts the recursive calls, the constant
$b > 1$ is the factor by which the input shrinks, and the **driving
function** $f(n)$ is the cost of one call's own body: everything except
the recursive calls.
</Definition>

### What the form covers

Merge sort is the canonical instance: $a = 2$, $b = 2$, $f(n) =
\Theta(n)$. The form also covers recursion with a single call: the
halving loop proved correct at the end of
<Ref lesson="loop-invariants-and-correctness">the previous lesson</Ref>
does $\Theta(1)$ work (one comparison of the middle element) and then
continues on half the range, which read recursively is $T(n) = T(n/2) +
\Theta(1)$ with $a = 1$, $b = 2$. Two near-misses mark the definition's
boundary, each violating one clause: $T(n) = T(n-1) + \Theta(n)$ shrinks
by subtraction, not by a factor ($b > 1$ fails), and $T(n) = T(n/3) +
T(2n/3) + \Theta(n)$ makes two calls whose sizes differ (no single
$b$). Both are genuine running-time recurrences (the last section of
this lesson solves them), but the machinery built for the
$a\,T(n/b) + f(n)$ form will not apply.

<Remark title="two standing conventions (CLRS §2.3, Erickson §1.7)">
First: base cases are omitted from here on. Below any constant threshold
$T(n) = \Theta(1)$ (a real program on a constant-size input takes
constant time), and the choice of threshold never affects the growth
rate. Second: floors and ceilings are dropped, so merge sort's recurrence
is written $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$. This is legitimate, not sloppy:
a change of variable (Erickson's *domain transformation*) converts any
solution of the clean recurrence into one for the floored version at the
cost of a constant, and this course asserts that fact once here rather
than reproving it per recurrence. The $\Theta(n)$ inside the equation is
the anonymous-function usage from
<Ref lesson="asymptotic-notation">the first lesson</Ref>: some function
of class $\Theta(n)$, details deliberately forgotten.
</Remark>

An equation that defines $T$ in terms of $T$ cannot be read off like a
formula; something must remove the self-reference. Plugging in a specific
size (computing $T(1000)$ by working down to the base case) yields one
number, and a growth rate is a claim about all $n$ at once, the same
obstacle that made <Ref lesson="loop-invariants-and-correctness">an
unbounded loop</Ref> unprovable by finite case-checking. The finite
handle this time is a picture.

## The recursion tree: total work, summed level by level

A *recursion tree* has one node for every subproblem the recursion ever
creates. The value written at a node is the work its call does itself:
the driving-function part only, since the recursive part is what the
children are for. $T(n)$ is then the sum of all node values, and the sum
is organized by summing each level and adding the level totals.

<Example title="solving merge sort's recurrence by recursion tree">
Solve $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + cn$, writing $c$ for the constant the $\Theta(n)$
hides and taking $n$ a power of $2$ so the picture is exact.

**Level totals.** Depth $i$ holds $2^i$ nodes, one per subproblem of size
$n/2^i$, each doing $c \cdot n/2^i$ work: the level totals
$2^i \cdot cn/2^i = cn$. The doubling of node counts and the halving of
node costs cancel exactly, at every depth.

**Number of levels.** The subproblem size at depth $i$ is $n/2^i$, which
reaches the base-case size $1$ at $i = \lg n$ (the halving count from
<Ref lesson="asymptotic-notation">the first lesson</Ref>, with $\lg$
denoting $\log_2$). So the tree has internal levels at depths $0$ through
$\lg n - 1$, plus a leaf level of $n$ base cases costing $c$ each.

**Sum.** $\lg n$ internal levels of $cn$, plus $cn$ at the leaves:
$$
T(n) = cn \lg n + cn = \Theta(n \lg n).
$$

**Check at $n = 8$**, against the recurrence directly with $T(1) = c$:
$T(2) = 2c + 2c = 4c$, $T(4) = 8c + 4c = 12c$, $T(8) = 24c + 8c = 32c$;
and the formula gives $c \cdot 8 \cdot 3 + 8c = 32c$. Exact agreement,
not just asymptotic.
</Example>

This settles the module's opening debt: merge sort costs
$\Theta(n \lg n)$ on any machine, and the $50\,n\lg n$ figure of the
first lesson was this solution with one machine's constant filled in.

### The general tree: aᵗʰ powers against bᵗʰ shrinkage

The same drawing works for any $T(n) = a\,T(n/b) + f(n)$, and only two
quantities change. Depth $i$ holds $a^i$ nodes of size $n/b^i$, so its
total is $a^i f(n/b^i)$; and sizes reach the base case at depth
$\log_b n$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/leaves-count.svg"
  alt="A branching diagram with 1, then a, then a-squared nodes per level, ending at a to the power log-b of n leaves, which equals n to the power log-b of a"
  caption="Node counts multiply by a per level for log_b n levels; the leaf count is a^(log_b n) = n^(log_b a)."
/>

The leaf level holds
$a^{\log_b n}$ nodes, and the exponent identity $a^{\log_b n} =
n^{\log_b a}$ (take $\log_b$ of both sides: each is $\log_b a \cdot
\log_b n$) rewrites that count as a power of $n$. For merge sort,
$n^{\log_2 2} = n$ leaves; for $a = 4$, $b = 2$, already $n^{\log_2 4} =
n^2$ of them.

<KeyFact>
The tree for $T(n) = a\,T(n/b) + f(n)$ has $n^{\log_b a}$ leaves, and
this single quantity, not $a$ or $b$ separately, is what the driving
function will be measured against. Since each leaf costs $\Theta(1)$, no
divide-and-conquer algorithm of this form beats $\Omega(n^{\log_b a})$,
whatever its $f$.
</KeyFact>

<Exercise>
Solve $T(n) = 4T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$ by recursion tree: give the level
totals as a function of the depth $i$, the number of levels, the leaf
count, and the sum. Write the level totals for depths $0$, $1$, $2$
before opening the solution.

<Solution>
Strategy: run the worked example's three steps with $a = 4$, $b = 2$,
$f(n) = cn$.

**Level totals.** Depth $i$ holds $4^i$ nodes of size $n/2^i$, each doing
$cn/2^i$ work: the total is $4^i \cdot cn/2^i = 2^i cn$. Depths $0, 1,
2$ total $cn$, $2cn$, $4cn$: the totals double per level instead of
staying flat, because four subproblems at half size do twice the combined
work of their parent.

**Levels and leaves.** Sizes halve, so there are still $\lg n$ internal
levels; the leaf count is $4^{\lg n} = n^{\log_2 4} = n^2$.

**Sum.** The internal levels form an increasing geometric series:
$$
\sum_{i=0}^{\lg n - 1} 2^i cn
= cn\,(2^{\lg n} - 1) = cn(n-1) = \Theta(n^2),
$$
and the $n^2$ leaves add $\Theta(n^2)$ more. So $T(n) = \Theta(n^2)$.

The transferable move: when level totals grow geometrically, the whole
sum is within a constant factor of its last term. The answer is the leaf
level, and the leaf count $n^{\log_b a}$ is the answer's growth rate.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Three shapes of level sums: root-dominated, balanced, leaf-dominated

Two trees are now on the table: merge sort's, where every level totals
the same $cn$, and the paired exercise's, where totals double per level
and the leaves decide. From $\lg n$ levels one might expect every
divide-and-conquer answer to carry a $\log$ factor; the two examples
already show otherwise, and there is exactly one more possibility.

The trend of the level totals is a ratio. One level's total is
$a^i f(n/b^i)$, the next is $a^{i+1} f(n/b^{i+1})$, and for a polynomial
driving function $f(n) = n^k$ the ratio between them is $a/b^k$: a
constant, the same at every depth, so the level totals always form a
geometric series. Three shapes, by whether that constant sits below, at,
or above $1$:

<Figure
  src="./figures/three-shapes.svg"
  alt="Three panels for T(n)=2T(n/2)+f(n) with f equal to n-squared, n, and 1: level-total bars that shrink (root-dominated, Theta of n squared), stay equal (balanced, Theta of n log n), and grow (leaf-dominated, Theta of n)"
  caption="One tree shape, three driving functions. The trend of the per-level totals, not the depth, decides the answer."
/>

- **Root-dominated** (totals shrink by a constant factor): the whole sum
  is at most a constant times the root's term, by the geometric-series
  bound $w(1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 + \cdots) < w/(1-\alpha)$ for
  $\alpha < 1$. Answer: $T(n) = \Theta(f(n))$. For
  $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n^2$ the totals are $n^2, n^2/2, n^2/4, \ldots$ and
  the sum stays under $2n^2$: the entire recursion beneath the root is
  cheaper than the root's own work.
- **Balanced** (totals equal): the sum is one level's total times the
  number of levels. Answer: $T(n) = \Theta(f(n) \log n)$, merge sort's
  case and the only shape where the depth enters the answer.
- **Leaf-dominated** (totals grow by a constant factor): the sum is
  within a constant of the last term, the leaf level. Answer: $T(n) =
  \Theta(n^{\log_b a})$. For $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + 1$ the totals are $1, 2,
  4, \ldots, n$, summing below $2n$: $\Theta(n)$, with no $\log$ anywhere.

The vocabulary is CMU 15-210's, which teaches the classification as the
"brick method"; the three names are worth adopting because they say
where the cost lives.

<KeyFact>
Where the cost lives is where optimization pays. Cheapening the driving
function improves a root-dominated algorithm in full and a balanced one
by at most the single $\log$ factor; in a leaf-dominated one it buys
nothing: there the only lever is $a$, the number of recursive calls.
</KeyFact>

Which shape a recurrence takes is a comparison between two growth rates
in disguise. The ratio $a/b^k$ is below $1$ exactly when $\log_b a < k$,
that is, when $n^{\log_b a}$ grows slower than $f(n) = n^k$; above $1$
exactly when it grows faster. So the trend of the level sums is decided
by racing $f(n)$ against the leaf count $n^{\log_b a}$: whichever end of
the tree wins the race is where the cost concentrates, and a tie is the
balanced case. Stated as a theorem, that comparison is the next section.

## The Master theorem: the classification stated once

The three shapes were established by drawing trees. The Master theorem is
the same trichotomy packaged as a test on $f(n)$, so that recurrences of
the standard form can be solved by one comparison, with no tree drawn.
The quantity $n^{\log_b a}$, the leaf count, is called the *watershed
function*, since which side of it $f$ falls on determines everything.

<Theorem title="Master theorem (CLRS Theorem 4.1, simplified)">
Let $a \ge 1$ and $b > 1$ be constants, let $f(n)$ be nonnegative for
large $n$, and let $T(n) = a\,T(n/b) + f(n)$ (floors or ceilings in the
argument do not change the conclusion). Then:

1. If $f(n) = O(n^{\log_b a - \epsilon})$ for some constant $\epsilon >
   0$, then $T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_b a})$.
2. If $f(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_b a})$, then $T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_b a}
   \lg n)$.
3. If $f(n) = \Omega(n^{\log_b a + \epsilon})$ for some constant
   $\epsilon > 0$, and additionally $a f(n/b) \le c f(n)$ for some
   constant $c < 1$ and all large $n$, then $T(n) = \Theta(f(n))$.
</Theorem>

The cases are the shapes: case 1 is leaf-dominated, case 2 balanced,
case 3 root-dominated. The proof is the level-sum argument of the
previous two sections made careful about floors, ceilings, and
non-power-of-$b$ sizes; CLRS §4.6–4.7 carries it out, and this course
takes it as read.

  src="./figures/master-cases.svg"
  alt="Decision diagram: compare f(n) with the watershed n to the log-b of a; three branches lead to case 1 leaf-dominated, case 2 balanced, and case 3 root-dominated, each with a worked instance"
  caption="The theorem as a decision procedure: one watershed computation, one comparison, one worked instance per branch."
/>

<Remark title="the fine print, once">
Three technical clauses, dealt with here so they never interrupt again.
*Polynomial separation:* cases 1 and 3 require $f$ to differ from the
watershed by a factor $n^\epsilon$, not merely to differ: $f(n) = n \lg
n$ against watershed $n$ is bigger but not polynomially bigger, and falls
into a gap between cases 2 and 3 where the theorem as stated says
nothing (an exercise below solves exactly this recurrence by tree, and
CLRS's full case 2 covers it by allowing $f = \Theta(n^{\log_b a} \lg^k
n)$ with answer $\Theta(n^{\log_b a}\lg^{k+1} n)$). *Regularity:* the
condition $a f(n/b) \le c f(n)$ in case 3 forbids driving functions that
oscillate their way around geometric decay. For every polynomial $f(n) =
n^k$ in case-3 territory, $a f(n/b) = (a/b^k) f(n)$ with $a/b^k < 1$, so
the condition holds automatically; for this course's recurrences it is
never an obstacle. *Base of the logarithm in* $\lg$: irrelevant inside
$\Theta$, per <Ref lesson="asymptotic-notation">the first lesson</Ref>.
</Remark>

<Example title="four recurrences through the theorem">
**Merge sort, $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$.** Watershed:
$n^{\log_2 2} = n$. The driving function is $\Theta(n)$, the same order.
Case 2: $T(n) = \Theta(n \lg n)$, agreeing with the tree.

**Halving search, $T(n) = T(n/2) + \Theta(1)$.** Watershed:
$n^{\log_2 1} = n^0 = 1$. The driving function is $\Theta(1)$, the same
order. Case 2: $T(n) = \Theta(\lg n)$: the binary-search bound, now
derived rather than counted.

**Four half-size calls, $T(n) = 4T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$.** Watershed:
$n^{\log_2 4} = n^2$. Here $f(n) = n = O(n^{2 - \epsilon})$ with
$\epsilon = 1$: polynomially smaller. Case 1: $T(n) = \Theta(n^2)$,
agreeing with the paired exercise's tree. This recurrence is what a
first attempt at divide-and-conquer multiplication of $n$-digit numbers
produces (splitting each number in half generates four half-size
multiplications), and $\Theta(n^2)$ is no better than the grade-school
method it tried to beat.
<Ref lesson="divide-and-conquer-design">The design lesson</Ref> takes up
what to do about that.

**Heavy combine step, $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n^2)$.** Watershed: $n$.
Here $f(n) = n^2 = \Omega(n^{1+\epsilon})$ with $\epsilon = 1$:
polynomially larger. Regularity: $2f(n/2) = 2(n/2)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}n^2
\le \tfrac{1}{2} f(n)$, so $c = \tfrac{1}{2}$ works. Case 3: $T(n) =
\Theta(n^2)$; the recursion adds only a constant factor on top of the
root's own combine step.
</Example>

## Substitution: guess, then prove it by induction

The tree arguments leaned on simplifications: floors vanished, $n$ was a
power of $b$, and level sums were read off as geometric. For this
course's recurrences the simplifications are safe, but a method that
certifies an answer without any of them exists and costs half a page.
The *substitution method* guesses the bound, then proves it by induction
on $n$, exactly the machinery
<Ref lesson="loop-invariants-and-correctness">the previous
lesson</Ref> used on loops. The tree supplies the guess; induction makes
it a theorem.

<Example title="certifying merge sort's upper bound by substitution">
Claim: if $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$, then $T(n) = O(n \lg n)$.

**Setup.** Let $c_2$ be a constant with the $\Theta(n)$ term at most
$c_2 n$ for all large $n$ (such a $c_2$ exists by the definition of
$\Theta$). The inductive hypothesis is the bound *with an explicit
constant*: $T(m) \le c\,m \lg m$ for all $2 \le m < n$, where $c$ is
chosen below.

**Inductive step.** The recurrence hands each half to the hypothesis:
$$
T(n) \;\le\; 2 \cdot c\,\frac{n}{2} \lg\frac{n}{2} + c_2 n
\;=\; cn(\lg n - 1) + c_2 n
\;=\; cn \lg n - (c - c_2)\,n
\;\le\; cn \lg n,
$$
where the last step holds as soon as $c \ge c_2$. The hypothesis
reproduces itself at $n$, exactly as a loop invariant's maintenance step
reproduces the invariant one iteration later.

**Base.** $T(2)$ and $T(3)$ are constants; enlarging $c$ to
$\max(c_2,\, T(2)/2,\, T(3)/(3\lg 3))$ makes $T(m) \le c\,m\lg m$ true
outright there, grounding the induction. Hence $T(n) \le cn\lg n$ for
all $n \ge 2$: $T(n) = O(n \lg n)$. The matching lower bound
$T(n) = \Omega(n \lg n)$ runs the same computation with the inequalities
reversed and a lower-bound constant for the $\Theta(n)$ term.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/substitution-slack.svg"
  alt="Bar diagram of the inductive step: the hypothesis applied to both halves yields cn lg n minus cn, the call's own cost adds back at most c2 times n, and the result stays under the target cn lg n whenever c is at least c2"
  caption="The inductive step as a budget. The hypothesis delivers cn lg n with a whole cn to spare; the Θ(n) cost spends c₂n of that slack, and c ≥ c₂ keeps the result under the target."

### The pitfall: asymptotic notation has no place in the hypothesis

The inductive hypothesis must be an exact inequality with a named
constant ($T(m) \le c\,m\lg m$), never an asymptotic claim. The
tempting shortcut is a documented trap (CLRS §4.3 devotes a subsection
to it), and it looks like this:

$$
T(n) \le 2 \cdot O(n/2) + \Theta(n) = O(n). \qquad \text{(wrong)}
$$

By this reasoning merge sort would be linear. The step fails because
$O(n)$ is a set membership, not a quantity: each level of the induction
is licensed to use its own hidden constant, and stacked over
$\lg n$ levels the constants grow without bound, which is the very thing
a single fixed $c$ exists to forbid. Redo the argument with the constant
named and the failure is visible: from $T(m) \le c\,m$ the recurrence
gives $T(n) \le cn + c_2 n$, and $cn + c_2 n \le cn$ holds for no
positive $c_2$. The step is not false; it is unprovable, the same
verdict the previous lesson issued for a
<Ref lesson="loop-invariants-and-correctness">non-inductive
invariant</Ref>, and the repair is the same in spirit: prove the exact
statement, not a paraphrase of it. A second repair, strengthening a
correct guess by subtracting a lower-order term, is run in the
exercises.

## Where the standard form breaks: unequal splits and subtraction

The definition's two near-misses are still unsolved, and both matter:
algorithms produce them. The Master theorem's hypotheses exclude them
(no single $b$, or no $b$ at all), but the recursion tree never needed
those hypotheses, only levels to sum.

### An unequal split: T(n) = T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + Θ(n)

This recurrence arises when a partitioning step splits its input into a
one-third and a two-thirds piece: a quicksort whose pivot reliably
lands in the middle third behaves exactly so, a fact
<Ref lesson="comparison-sorting-merge-and-quicksort">the sorting
lesson</Ref> uses. The tree is lopsided: subproblem sizes shrink by
$3\times$ along the left edge and only by $1.5\times$ along the right,
so different root-to-leaf paths have different lengths.

<Figure
  src="./figures/unbalanced-split.svg"
  alt="The unbalanced recursion tree for T(n) = T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + cn, with the shortest path reaching size 1 after log base 3 of n levels and the longest after log base 3/2 of n levels; every level totals at most cn"
  caption="An unbalanced tree still sums by levels: at most cn per level, between log₃ n and log₃/₂ n levels."
/>

Level sums still decide the total. As long as a level is complete, its
nodes' sizes add to exactly $n$, so it totals exactly $cn$; once the
shallow left branches start bottoming out (below depth $\log_3 n$),
levels lose nodes and their totals drop below $cn$. The depth is set by
the slowest-shrinking path: the rightmost, which multiplies sizes by
$2/3$ per level and reaches the base case after $\log_{3/2} n$ levels.
Two bounds follow: at most $cn$
per level for at most $\log_{3/2} n = \Theta(\lg n)$ levels gives $T(n)
= O(n \lg n)$, and the $\log_3 n$ complete top levels at exactly $cn$
each give $T(n) = \Omega(n \lg n)$. So $T(n) = \Theta(n \lg n)$: the
imbalance moved the constant, not the class. An uneven split costs
nothing asymptotically as long as both pieces are a constant fraction of
the whole.

<Aside>
A theorem does exist for multi-way and unequal splits: the Akra–Bazzi
method (1998) solves $T(n) = \sum_i a_i T(n/b_i) + f(n)$ by finding the
exponent $p$ with $\sum_i a_i / b_i^p = 1$. For the leaf-counting
recurrence $L(n) = L(n/2) + L(n/3)$, for instance, it gives $L(n) =
\Theta(n^p)$ for the $p \approx 0.788$ solving $(1/2)^p + (1/3)^p = 1$.
It needs calculus this course does not assume, and belongs to the
advanced sequence; the tree-plus-substitution pair covers everything this
course meets.
</Aside>

### Shrinking by subtraction: T(n) = T(n−1) + Θ(n)

Recursing on all but one element gives the other excluded shape. The
"tree" is a path: level $i$ holds one node doing $c(n-i)$ work, there
are $n$ levels, and the level totals $cn, c(n-1), c(n-2), \ldots$ form
an arithmetic series, not a geometric one. No shape test applies, but
none is needed, because direct summation finishes it:
$$
T(n) = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} c\,(n - i) = c \cdot \frac{n(n+1)}{2} =
\Theta(n^2).
$$
Halving-style recursion turned $\Theta(n)$ of per-level work into
$\Theta(n \lg n)$ total; peeling one element at a time turns the same
per-level work into $\Theta(n^2)$. This recurrence is quicksort's worst
case (a pivot that repeatedly draws the smallest element), and the gap
between the two answers is the central comparison of
<Ref lesson="comparison-sorting-merge-and-quicksort">the sorting
lesson</Ref>. (The word *recurrence* returns with a different referent in
<Ref lesson="dynamic-programming-i-one-dimensional">dynamic
programming</Ref>, where equations of this shape range over solution
values rather than running times; until then, a recurrence prices
recursion.)

## The toolkit against a production sort

The module closes by reading a deployed system with all three of its
tools. CPython's `list.sort` is Timsort, Tim Peters's 2002 merge sort
variant, since adopted by Java's object sort and V8's `Array.sort`; its
design notes
([listsort.txt](https://github.com/python/cpython/blob/main/Objects/listsort.txt))
document the engineering the recurrence's terms absorb.

### The merge policy is the balanced shape

Timsort merges runs whose lengths roughly double from a minimum size up
to $n$; each doubling generation moves every element at most once, which
is the balanced shape read from the leaves upward: $\Theta(n)$ per
generation, $\Theta(\lg n)$ generations, worst case $O(n \lg n)$, the
case-2 answer.

<Figure
  src="./figures/timsort-generations.svg"
  alt="Four rows of runs: eight minrun-sized runs at the bottom are merged pairwise into four runs, then two, then one sorted run of length n; each row moves about n elements in total"
  caption="Timsort's merge generations. Run lengths double per generation and every generation moves ≈ n elements: merge sort's balanced shape, read from the leaves upward."
/>

At the bottom, runs shorter than a cutoff *minrun*
(chosen between 32 and 64) are sorted by the insertion sort proved
correct in <Ref lesson="loop-invariants-and-correctness">the previous
lesson</Ref>, run with a binary search for the insertion point — still
$\Theta(n^2)$, and quadratic cost is irrelevant at constant size. That
cutoff is the recurrence's base-case convention
turned into a tuning knob: $T(n) = \Theta(1)$ below a threshold, with
the threshold chosen for cache behavior and merge balance.

<KeyFact>
Moving a production sort's insertion-sort cutoff moves its constant
factor and can never move its $\Theta(n \lg n)$: the base-case threshold
$n_0$ appears in the recurrence's solution only inside the constants the
notation absorbs.
</KeyFact>

### What the recurrence cannot see

The recurrence prices the worst case, and Timsort spends most of its
design on inputs that are not worst case: on an already-sorted array it
detects one run and finishes in
$n - 1$ comparisons, $\Theta(n)$. That adaptivity is invisible to the
$a\,T(n/b) + f(n)$ form, which fixes the split and the combine cost in
advance; it is a genuine limit of the tool, not an error in it, and the
worst-case guarantee the recurrence proves is what the adaptive
machinery is not allowed to break.

The module's toolkit is now complete: asymptotic notation to state costs,
loop invariants to prove correctness, and recurrences (tree for the
answer, substitution for the certificate, Master theorem for the
standard form) to price recursion. The headline results:
$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n) = \Theta(n \lg n)$ and its two neighbors
$\Theta(n^{\log_b a})$ and $\Theta(f(n))$, decided by one watershed
comparison. <Ref lesson="arrays-and-linked-lists">The next lesson</Ref>
turns from analyzing algorithms to the structures they run on, starting
with the two ways of storing a sequence; the recurrences return the
moment the course meets its next recursive algorithm.

<Exercise>
For each recurrence, first name the applicable tool (Master theorem,
recursion tree, or direct summation) and say what rules the others out;
then solve. (a) $T(n) = 3T(n/9) + \Theta(\sqrt{n}\,)$; (b) $T(n) =
T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$; (c) $T(n) = T(n-1) + \Theta(1)$; (d) $T(n) =
7T(n/2) + \Theta(n^2)$.

<Solution>
**(a) Master, case 2.** Standard form with $a = 3$, $b = 9$: watershed
$n^{\log_9 3} = n^{1/2}$, and $f(n) = \Theta(\sqrt n)$ matches it.
$T(n) = \Theta(\sqrt n \, \lg n)$.

**(b) Master, case 3.** Watershed $n^{\log_2 1} = 1$; $f(n) = n =
\Omega(n^{0+\epsilon})$, polynomially larger, and regularity holds:
$1 \cdot f(n/2) = n/2 = \tfrac12 f(n)$. $T(n) = \Theta(n)$. The tree
view: level totals $n, n/2, n/4, \ldots$ halve: root-dominated, sum
under $2n$. Note the contrast with binary search's $T(n) = T(n/2) +
\Theta(1) = \Theta(\lg n)$: same recursion, different driving function,
different shape.

**(c) Direct summation.** Not the standard form ($b > 1$ fails: the
input shrinks by subtraction). The path-shaped tree has $n$ levels of
$\Theta(1)$ each: $T(n) = \Theta(n)$, a recursive linear scan.

**(d) Master, case 1.** Watershed $n^{\log_2 7} = n^{2.807\ldots}$;
$f(n) = n^2 = O(n^{\log_2 7 - \epsilon})$ for any $\epsilon \le 0.8$.
$T(n) = \Theta(n^{\log_2 7}) \approx \Theta(n^{2.81})$. (This is the
recurrence of Strassen's 1969 matrix-multiplication algorithm: seven
half-size multiplications instead of eight beat the $\Theta(n^3)$
watershed of the obvious method.)
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The boundary of case 2: solve $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n \lg n)$. First
show that no case of the Master theorem as stated in this lesson
applies, naming the failing hypothesis for each; then solve by recursion
tree.

<Solution>
**No case applies.** The watershed is $n^{\log_2 2} = n$. Case 2 needs
$f = \Theta(n)$; but $n \lg n \ne \Theta(n)$. Case 3 needs $f =
\Omega(n^{1 + \epsilon})$ for a fixed $\epsilon > 0$; but $\lg n =
O(n^\epsilon)$ for every $\epsilon > 0$, so $n \lg n$ is larger than $n$
only by a sub-polynomial factor: the polynomial-separation hypothesis
fails. Case 1 needs $f$ smaller; it is larger. The recurrence sits in
the gap between cases 2 and 3.

**Tree.** Depth $i$: $2^i$ nodes of size $n/2^i$, level total
$2^i \cdot c\,(n/2^i)\lg(n/2^i) = cn(\lg n - i)$. The totals decrease
linearly rather than geometrically: neither root- nor leaf-dominated,
and "balanced" only approximately. Summing over the $\lg n$ internal
levels:
$$
\sum_{i=0}^{\lg n - 1} cn(\lg n - i) = cn \sum_{j=1}^{\lg n} j =
cn \cdot \frac{\lg n\,(\lg n + 1)}{2} = \Theta(n \lg^2 n),
$$
and the $n$ leaves add only $\Theta(n)$. So $T(n) = \Theta(n \lg^2 n)$.

This is exactly what CLRS's full case 2 predicts: for $f =
\Theta(n^{\log_b a} \lg^k n)$ with $k \ge 0$, $T(n) =
\Theta(n^{\log_b a} \lg^{k+1} n)$ (here $k = 1$). The transferable move:
a driving function on the watershed up to $\log$ factors adds one more
$\log$ to itself.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "Merge sort's tree has $\lg n$ levels because the input halves
each level. Any recurrence that halves its input therefore costs its
per-level work times $\lg n$; in particular $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(1)$
(constant work per call) solves to $\Theta(\lg n)$." Name the step
that fails and give the correct solution, then state a recurrence for
which the quoted conclusion is right.

<Solution>
The failing step is "per-level work times $\lg n$": multiplying one
level's total by the level count is valid only when every level totals
the same, the balanced shape. Here the totals are not equal. Depth $i$
holds $2^i$ nodes doing $\Theta(1)$ each, so the totals run $1, 2, 4,
\ldots, n$: an increasing geometric series, leaf-dominated, summing to
less than $2n$. Correct solution: $T(n) = \Theta(n)$, also by Master
case 1, since $f = \Theta(1) = O(n^{1-\epsilon})$ against watershed
$n^{\log_2 2} = n$. (The claim also misreads "per-level work" as
"per-call work"; even in the balanced case what multiplies by the depth
is a level's total.)

The conclusion is right for $T(n) = T(n/2) + \Theta(1) = \Theta(\lg n)$:
with $a = 1$ there is one call per level, the level totals really are
all $\Theta(1)$, and the sum is the level count. One recursive call
versus two — invisible in the phrase "halves its input" — is the entire
difference between $\Theta(\lg n)$ and $\Theta(n)$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The substitution repair the lesson deferred. For $T(n) = 2T(n/2) +
\Theta(1)$ (solution $\Theta(n)$, by the previous exercise): (a) attempt
a substitution proof of $T(n) = O(n)$ with the hypothesis $T(m) \le cm$
and show the inductive step gets stuck, even though the bound being
proved is true; (b) repair it with the hypothesis $T(m) \le cm - d$,
finding the constraint on $d$; (c) explain why subtracting a term
helps where enlarging $c$ does not.

<Solution>
**(a)** Let $d_0$ bound the $\Theta(1)$ term. From $T(m) \le cm$ for
$m < n$:
$$
T(n) \le 2 \cdot c\,\frac{n}{2} + d_0 = cn + d_0,
$$
and $cn + d_0 \le cn$ fails for every $c$, since $d_0 > 0$. No choice of
$c$, however large, absorbs the additive $d_0$: the step is unprovable
as stated, the same verdict as the lesson's $O$-in-the-hypothesis
pitfall, but for an honest guess.

**(b)** Hypothesis $T(m) \le cm - d$ with $d \ge 0$ to be chosen:
$$
T(n) \le 2\left(c\,\frac{n}{2} - d\right) + d_0 = cn - 2d + d_0 =
(cn - d) - (d - d_0) \le cn - d
$$
whenever $d \ge d_0$. The base case needs $c$ large enough that
$T(m) \le cm - d$ holds for the constant-size starts, which one
enlargement of $c$ arranges. Hence $T(n) \le cn - d = O(n)$.

**(c)** The hypothesis is applied once per recursive call, so whatever
is subtracted comes back doubled: $-d$ becomes $-2d$, leaving slack
$d - d_0 \ge 0$ to swallow the driving cost. A larger $c$ cannot create
that slack, because $c$ multiplies $n/2$ twice and returns exactly
$cn$ with nothing to spare. A stronger claim is easier to prove: the
same strengthening move the previous lesson used when a loop invariant
could not reprove itself.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Insertion sort, recursively. <Ref lesson="loop-invariants-and-correctness">
The previous lesson</Ref> proved insertion sort correct as a loop; it can
be phrased as a recursion instead: to sort $A[1..n]$, recursively sort
$A[1..n-1]$, then insert $A[n]$ into the sorted prefix by shifting larger
elements right. (a) Write the recurrence for the worst case, stating what
the driving function costs and which input realizes it. (b) Solve it.
(c) Write and solve the best-case recurrence, and name the input class
that realizes that one.

<Solution>
**(a)** The insertion step shifts every prefix element greater than
$A[n]$; in the worst case (the prefix entirely greater, as when the
input is reverse-sorted) it shifts all $n - 1$ of them: $\Theta(n)$.
The recurrence is $T(n) = T(n-1) + \Theta(n)$.

**(b)** Subtract-and-conquer, so direct summation (the Master theorem's
$b > 1$ hypothesis fails): $T(n) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \Theta(k) =
\Theta(n^2)$, the same $\Theta(n^2)$ the loop analysis gave, as it must
be: the recursion performs the identical shifts in the identical order,
and only the bookkeeping changed.

**(c)** On an already-sorted input the insertion step compares once and
shifts nothing: $\Theta(1)$. Best case $T(n) = T(n-1) + \Theta(1) =
\Theta(n)$. Same algorithm, two recurrences: worst and best case are
separate functions of $n$, per
<Ref lesson="asymptotic-notation">the first lesson</Ref>, and each gets
its own equation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Splitting an $n$-digit multiplication into halves can be arranged to
need four half-size multiplications plus $\Theta(n)$ digit additions,
or, with a cleverer combination step, only three:
$$
T_4(n) = 4T_4(n/2) + \Theta(n), \qquad T_3(n) = 3T_3(n/2) + \Theta(n).
$$
(a) Solve both. (b) Grade-school multiplication costs $\Theta(n^2)$;
state what each scheme gains over it. (c) For $n = 10^6$ digits, estimate
the ratio of the two solutions' growth functions.

<Solution>
**(a)** Both are case 1 (leaf-dominated): the watershed exceeds
$f(n) = n$ polynomially in each. $T_4$: watershed $n^{\log_2 4} = n^2$,
so $T_4(n) = \Theta(n^2)$. $T_3$: watershed $n^{\log_2 3}$ with $\log_2
3 = 1.585\ldots$, so $T_3(n) = \Theta(n^{1.585})$.

**(b)** The four-call scheme gains nothing: recursion reproduced
grade-school's $\Theta(n^2)$, because $n^2$ leaves do as much work as
the $n^2$ digit-pairs of the direct method. The three-call scheme breaks
below $n^2$: dropping one recursive call moved the exponent from
$\log_2 4 = 2$ to $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$. In a leaf-dominated
recurrence the exponent comes straight from the branching factor, so
the only way to speed it up is to make fewer calls.

**(c)** $n^2 / n^{\log_2 3} = n^{2 - \log_2 3} = n^{0.415}$; at $n =
10^6$ this is $10^{6 \times 0.415} \approx 10^{2.5}$, roughly a factor
of $300$.

The three-multiplication combination step actually exists: it is
Karatsuba's algorithm, constructed in
<Ref lesson="divide-and-conquer-design">the divide-and-conquer design
lesson</Ref>; this exercise's arithmetic is the entire reason that
construction is worth having.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design the measurement that checks this lesson's headline. You implement
merge sort and run the doubling experiment of
<Ref lesson="asymptotic-notation">the first lesson</Ref>: time it at
$n, 2n, 4n, \ldots$ and take ratios of consecutive times. (a) Derive the
ratio $T(2n)/T(n)$ that $T(n) = \Theta(n \lg n)$ predicts, and evaluate
it at $n = 2^{15}$ and $n = 2^{20}$. (b) State what ratios $\Theta(n)$
and $\Theta(n^2)$ would predict, and which competitor is hard to
distinguish from $n \lg n$. (c) Propose a better plot than raw ratios
for confirming the $\lg$ factor, and state what result would count as
agreement.

<Solution>
**(a)** For $T(n) = c\,n \lg n$:
$$
\frac{T(2n)}{T(n)} = \frac{2cn\,(\lg n + 1)}{cn \lg n} = 2 +
\frac{2}{\lg n}.
$$
At $n = 2^{15}$: $2 + 2/15 \approx 2.13$. At $n = 2^{20}$: $2 + 2/20 =
2.10$. The prediction is a ratio slightly above $2$, drifting down
toward $2$ as $n$ grows.

**(b)** $\Theta(n)$ predicts ratios converging to exactly $2$;
$\Theta(n^2)$ predicts $4$. The quadratic is unmistakable after two
doublings; the linear competitor differs from $n \lg n$ only by that
slow drift ($2.13$ versus $2.00$ at $n = 2^{15}$), within the noise of
casual timing: the first lesson's warning that the doubling signature
cannot see logarithmic factors, now quantified.

**(c)** Plot $T(n)/n$ against $\lg n$. Under $T = cn\lg n + c'n$ this is
$c \lg n + c'$: a straight line, gaining a fixed increment $c$ per
doubling. Agreement: the points fall on a line with positive slope
(within measurement scatter), and the slope estimates the constant $c$
of the recurrence's $\Theta(n)$ term. A horizontal line would falsify
the $\lg$ factor ($T = \Theta(n)$); upward curvature would indicate a
polynomial exponent above 1. This turns a factor the ratio test cannot
see into the slope of a line: the counted model predicting the
stopwatch, which is the standard of the first lesson's doubling
experiment.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

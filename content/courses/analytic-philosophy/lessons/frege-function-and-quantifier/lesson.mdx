Every student of mathematics writes 'for every ε there is a δ' without a second thought, and the step from 'every boy loves some girl' to 'some girl is loved by a boy' looks too obvious to need a rule. Yet in 1878 the logic in general use could state neither sentence, let alone license the step: for two thousand years a sentence had been parsed as a subject and a predicate, and a sentence with two 'every'/'some' words in it has no single subject. The question is what a sentence has to be taken apart into for such inferences to become mechanical.

The logic in question is Aristotle's syllogistic, written in Athens in the fourth century BCE and still the logic taught in European universities in the 1870s. It handles one sentence shape: a quantity word, a subject term, a *copula* (the 'is' that joins the two terms) and a predicate term, as in 'every man is mortal' or 'some swan is not white'. Inside that shape it is complete and correct. From 'every man is mortal' and 'every Greek is a man' it derives 'every Greek is mortal', and every two-premise argument built from the four sentence forms (every, no, some, some…not) with a term shared between the premises is sorted by it into valid and invalid. What it cannot do is look inside a predicate. 'Loves some girl' has a quantity word of its own, and to the syllogistic it is one more term, as opaque as 'mortal'.

The fix came from mathematics. In 1879 a mathematician at the university of Jena, a town in Thuringia in central Germany, published a short book whose title, *Begriffsschrift*, means "concept-script": a written notation for logic, modelled on the formulas of arithmetic. The quantifier notation you already read fluently descends from it, and each of its parts was the answer to a specific failure of the subject–predicate form.

## The sentence with no subject

Take 'every boy loves some girl' and ask what it says. It can be heard two ways, and the two are different claims.

- **(A)** Each boy loves at least one girl, possibly a different girl for each boy.
- **(B)** There is one girl whom every boy loves.

(B) is the reading of 'some girl is loved by every boy'. Written in the notation you already use, with $Bx$ for '$x$ is a boy', $Gy$ for '$y$ is a girl' and $Lxy$ for '$x$ loves $y$':

$$
\text{(A)}\quad \forall x\,\bigl(Bx \to \exists y\,(Gy \wedge Lxy)\bigr)
\qquad\qquad
\text{(B)}\quad \exists y\,\bigl(Gy \wedge \forall x\,(Bx \to Lxy)\bigr)
$$

The two formulas contain the same symbols. They differ only in which quantifier stands outside the other.

The conclusion of the opening's step, 'some girl is loved by a boy', is a third sentence, and it differs from (B) in one word: 'a boy' where (B) has 'every boy'. It claims only that at least one boy loves at least one girl, and the opening's step runs from (A) to it:

$$
\text{(C)}\quad \exists y\,\bigl(Gy \wedge \exists x\,(Bx \wedge Lxy)\bigr)
$$

### Two readings, one model

<Example title="a model that separates (A) from (B)">
Take three boys $b_1, b_2, b_3$ and three girls $g_1, g_2, g_3$.

**Model 1.** Each $b_i$ loves $g_i$ and nobody else. Check (A): for $b_1$ the witness is $g_1$, for $b_2$ it is $g_2$, for $b_3$ it is $g_3$; every boy has one, so (A) is true. Check (B): it needs a single girl loved by all three boys. $g_1$ is loved only by $b_1$, $g_2$ only by $b_2$, $g_3$ only by $b_3$. No candidate survives, so (B) is false.

**Model 2.** All three boys love $g_2$, and $b_1$ also loves $g_3$. (B) is true with witness $g_2$. (A) is true as well, and it has to be: once $g_2$ is loved by everyone, every boy has a girl he loves.

Model 2 shows why (B) brings (A) with it; Model 1 shows that (A) does not bring (B). (C) is true in both models, since $b_1$ loves a girl in each.
</Example>

### Forcing the sentence into subject and predicate

What can the syllogistic write down for (A)? Its one shape is quantity word, subject, copula, predicate, so the natural parse takes 'every boy' as the subject and 'loves some girl' as the predicate: every boy is a girl-lover. That is a well-formed syllogistic sentence, but the form the logic reads in it is 'every $B$ is $P$', the form of 'every boy is mortal'. The words 'some girl' survive only as letters inside the term $P$. Nothing in the form says that for each boy there is a girl.

(B) and (C) have to be parsed with 'some girl' as the subject, and each gets its own opaque predicate:

| | subject–predicate rendering | the form the syllogistic reads |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (A) | every boy is a girl-lover | every $B$ is $P$ |
| (B) | some girl is loved-by-every-boy | some $G$ is $Q$ |
| (C) | some girl is loved-by-a-boy | some $G$ is $R$ |

This is the sense in which the syllogistic could not state these sentences. The syllogistic can write a sentence for each reading, but only with the second quantifier sealed inside an opaque term, so what separates (A) from (B), which quantifier stands inside the other, is written in neither. The inferences between them cannot be written either. (B) and (A) share no term in the right-hand column, and a syllogistic inference runs only through a term its sentences share, so the valid step from (B) to (A) is not a step the logic can express.

The opening's step, from (A) to (C), also runs between rows with no term in common, and trying it shows where the logic stops. The syllogistic reads 'every boy is a girl-lover' as implying that there are boys, so it may pass to 'some boy is a girl-lover', and since a 'some' sentence may have its two terms swapped, to 'some girl-lover is a boy'. There it stops. The conclusion wanted, 'some girl is loved by a boy', has 'girl' as its subject, and in every sentence the syllogistic can reach, 'girl' is a fragment of the sealed term 'girl-lover'. No rule takes a fragment out of a term and makes it a subject.

The same gap shows in a far simpler case. From 'John loves Mary' you may infer 'something loves Mary' and also 'John loves something'. In subject–predicate logic these are two unrelated moves. The first generalises the subject. The second reaches inside the predicate 'loves Mary' and replaces a part of it, which no rule of the syllogistic can do.

## Function and argument: a sentence can be cut anywhere

What would a form have to look like for 'John' and 'Mary' to stand on a par, so that one rule could generalise either? Mathematics already had such a form. In $2^2 = 4$, the expression $(\ )^2$ is a function with an empty place, the number $2$ is its argument, and $4$ is its value. Nobody asks whether $2$ is the "subject" of $2 + 3$. The expression $(\ ) + (\ )$ has two places, and either can be filled or left open.

The concept-script takes this form over for every sentence. Take 'hydrogen is lighter than carbon dioxide'. Remove 'hydrogen' and what is left, '$(\ )$ is lighter than carbon dioxide', is an expression with a gap: a function, of which 'hydrogen' is the argument. Remove 'carbon dioxide' instead, and 'hydrogen is lighter than $(\ )$' is the function and carbon dioxide the argument. Grammar would rather say 'carbon dioxide is heavier than hydrogen' to make carbon dioxide the subject, but the content is the same either way. Remove both names and you have a function of two places.

No cut is privileged, and that is the point. A subject–predicate parse makes one name special; a function–argument analysis lets you choose which place to leave open, and a place left open is what can later be generalised over. In $L(j, m)$, 'John loves Mary', the two names are arguments of one two-place function on equal terms. The book's preface makes the claim in one sentence:

<Quote source="Begriffsschrift (1879), Preface, trans. S. Bauer-Mengelberg">
In particular, I believe that the replacement of the concepts subject and predicate by argument and function, respectively, will stand the test of time.
</Quote>

### Concepts: functions whose values are truth-values

If $(\ )^2$ applied to $2$ yields the number $4$, what does '$(\ )$ is lighter than carbon dioxide' yield when applied to hydrogen? Not a number. The sentence that results is true, and the answer is that this is its value: the function maps hydrogen to *the True*, and it maps xenon, the Moon and the number 7 to the False. The 1879 book had left the value of such a function unspecified; its author completed the analysis this way in the early 1890s, and the rest of this lesson uses the completed version. A function of this kind gets a name of its own.

<Definition title="concept">
A *concept* is a function of one argument whose value, for every object as argument, is one of the two truth-values, the True or the False. An object *falls under* a concept when the concept maps it to the True. A function of two or more arguments with truth-values as values is a *relation*.
</Definition>

This is the mathematician's definition of a function with the codomain restricted to two values. Four cases fix what it admits.

- **Canonical case.** '$(\ )$ is prime' maps $2, 3, 5, \ldots$ to the True and everything else, $4$ and the Moon included, to the False. $2$ falls under it.
- **A different surface.** '$(\ )$ is a boy' is a concept in exactly the same sense; $b_1$ falls under it. A predicate of ordinary language and a predicate of arithmetic get the same analysis.
- **Degenerate case.** '$(\ )$ is identical with itself' maps every object to the True. It is still a concept: one that nothing fails to fall under.
- **Near miss.** 'the capital of $(\ )$' is a function, and it has a gap, but applied to Germany its value is Berlin, a city, not a truth-value. It violates the one clause about values, so it is not a concept. Write 'Berlin is the capital of $(\ )$' and you have a concept again.

An expression for a concept is *unsaturated*: it has a hole, and it says nothing true or false until the hole is filled. A name such as 'hydrogen' or '$2$' is saturated; it stands for an *object*. The difference is not one of subject matter. It is one of kind, as fixed as the difference between a number and a function on numbers, and an object can no more be filled into another object than $3$ can be applied to $5$.

<Remark title="the price of uniformity">
On this analysis a complete sentence is a name of its truth-value, as $2^2$ is a name of $4$: 'hydrogen is lighter than carbon dioxide' names the True. Then every true sentence denotes the same object, the True, and every false one the False. That is a price because sentences with entirely different contents, 'hydrogen is lighter than carbon dioxide' and '$2$ is prime', end up with one and the same reference, so what a sentence says cannot be read off what it names. Later philosophers dropped the doctrine; <Ref lesson="tractatus-logic-as-tautology">the Tractatus</Ref> argues that a proposition is not a name of anything. Modern logic keeps the function–argument analysis and lets a sentence be true or false instead of naming a value.
</Remark>

## Generality is a concept of concepts

With predicates turned into functions, the open question is where 'every' and 'some' go. The obvious move is to treat 'everything' as one more name: 'everything is mortal' would be the concept '$(\ )$ is mortal' applied to an argument called 'everything'. It fails on the first test. If 'nothing' were a name too, 'nothing is mortal' would say that a certain object, Nothing, falls under '$(\ )$ is mortal', so Nothing would be mortal. And if 'everything' named an object, then 'everything is mortal' and 'everything is not mortal' would be a sentence and its negation, while in fact both are false in a world where some things are mortal and some not. A quantifier phrase does not behave like a name of any object.

### What 'every' applies to

So look at what 'everything is mortal' is about. Its truth depends on one thing only: the concept '$(\ )$ is mortal', and specifically whether that concept maps every object to the True. The sentence says something about the concept, not about any object. The concept-script takes this literally. Already in 1879 its sign of generality attached to a function rather than to a name, and the doctrine of levels below is the worked-out form of that idea. 'Every' is a function whose argument is a concept and whose value is a truth-value.

<Definition title="second-level concept">
A *second-level concept* is a function that takes a concept as its argument and yields a truth-value. A concept *falls within* a second-level concept when it is mapped to the True. The universal quantifier is the second-level concept that maps $F$ to the True exactly when $F$ maps every object to the True; $\forall x\,Fx$ says that $F$ falls within it.
</Definition>

To tell the two kinds apart, the concepts of the previous section, which take objects as arguments, are from here on *first-level* concepts. 'Some' gets the same treatment as 'every'. Take the first cut of the hydrogen sentence again, now with its value written in, and put 'something' where 'hydrogen' was.

Objects and concepts now come in layers. The concept-script needed only one quantifier. 'Something is mortal' is false exactly when everything is not mortal, so $\exists x\,Mx$ is defined as $\neg\forall x\,\neg Mx$. The 1879 system's primitive signs were negation, the conditional, the universal quantifier and a sign for identity of content, the ancestor of $=$; the existential quantifier, 'and' and 'or' are abbreviations. Restricted generality is built from the same pieces: 'every person is mortal' is $\forall x\,(Px \to Mx)$, the universal quantifier applied to the concept 'if $(\ )$ is a person then $(\ )$ is mortal'.

### Building the two readings

Now 'every boy loves some girl' can be assembled, and the assembly shows where the two readings come from. Each quantifier needs a concept as its argument, so each reading is built by forming a concept with one gap and then applying a quantifier to it.

<Example title="the two readings, step by step">
Begin with the two-place relation $L(\xi, \zeta)$, '$\xi$ loves $\zeta$', where the Greek letters mark the gaps.

**Reading (A).** Hold the first place open and close the second with 'some girl'. For any fixed $\xi$, the concept '$\xi$ loves $(\ )$, and $(\ )$ is a girl' is a first-level concept; apply 'some object falls under $(\ )$' to it and you get $\exists y\,(Gy \wedge L\xi y)$. That expression still has the gap $\xi$, so it is itself a first-level concept: '$(\ )$ loves some girl'. Now apply 'every boy' to it: $\forall x\,(Bx \to \exists y\,(Gy \wedge Lxy))$.

**Reading (B).** Do it in the other order. Hold the second place open and close the first with 'every boy': $\forall x\,(Bx \to Lx\zeta)$ is the first-level concept '$(\ )$ is loved by every boy'. Apply 'some girl' to it: $\exists y\,(Gy \wedge \forall x\,(Bx \to Lxy))$.

Same relation, same two quantifiers; the order of application is the whole difference. In (A) the 'some' is applied inside, for each boy separately, which is why the girl may vary with the boy. In (B) it is applied last, to a concept that already says 'every boy', which is why one girl must serve all.
</Example>

What the syllogistic lacked is visible in the middle of that example: a concept such as '$(\ )$ loves some girl' that has a quantifier inside it and a gap still open. In the concept-script a quantifier can bind a place inside a predicate and leave the predicate a predicate. The order in which quantifiers are applied is what is now called their *scope*.

<Solution>
**Reading (D):** one boy loves all the girls. Fix the second place with 'every girl' first: $\forall y\,(Gy \to L\xi y)$ is the concept '$(\ )$ loves every girl'. Apply 'some boy': $\exists x\,(Bx \wedge \forall y\,(Gy \to Lxy))$.

**Reading (E):** every girl is loved by some boy, possibly a different boy for each. Fix the first place with 'some boy' first: $\exists x\,(Bx \wedge Lx\zeta)$ is the concept '$(\ )$ is loved by some boy'. Apply 'every girl': $\forall y\,(Gy \to \exists x\,(Bx \wedge Lxy))$.

(D) implies (E): if one boy $b$ loves every girl, then for each girl $b$ is a boy who loves her. The converse fails in Model 1 of the figure: $g_1$ is loved by $b_1$, $g_2$ by $b_2$, $g_3$ by $b_3$, so (E) is true; but no single boy loves all three girls, so (D) is false. As before, the reading with $\exists$ outermost is the stronger one.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### One rule for both places

The opening's step from (A) to (C) stalled in the syllogistic because 'girl' was sealed inside a term. With the two readings built from a relation, it now follows from rules that do not care where in a sentence a name stands. The basic one is *existential generalisation*: if $\varphi(a)$ is any sentence containing the name $a$, and $\varphi(x)$ results from replacing some occurrences of $a$ by the variable $x$, then

$$
\varphi(a) \to \exists x\,\varphi(x).
$$

With $\varphi(m) = L(j, m)$ it takes 'John loves Mary' to 'John loves something'; with $\varphi(j) = L(j, m)$ it takes the same sentence to 'something loves Mary'. One rule, applied to either argument place. In the concept-script it is not even primitive: it follows from the universal rule $\forall x\,\varphi(x) \to \varphi(a)$ and the definition of $\exists$ as $\neg\forall\neg$, a derivation you can carry out in the exercises.

<Theorem title="the opening's step: (A) implies (C), given a boy">
$\forall x\,\bigl(Bx \to \exists y\,(Gy \wedge Lxy)\bigr) \wedge \exists x\,Bx \;\to\; \exists y\,\bigl(Gy \wedge \exists x\,(Bx \wedge Lxy)\bigr)$. Without the premise $\exists x\,Bx$ the implication is not valid.

<Proof>
Let $b$ be a boy, $Bb$. Instantiating (A) at $b$ gives $Bb \to \exists y\,(Gy \wedge Lby)$, hence $\exists y\,(Gy \wedge Lby)$; let $g$ be a witness, so $Gg$ and $Lbg$. Conjoin $Bb \wedge Lbg$ and apply existential generalisation to the place of $b$, the first argument of $L$: $\exists x\,(Bx \wedge Lxg)$. Conjoin $Gg$ and apply existential generalisation again, now to the place of $g$, the second argument of $L$: $\exists y\,(Gy \wedge \exists x\,(Bx \wedge Lxy))$, which is (C). For the second claim, take a model with girls and no boys: (A) is true because there is no boy for it to fail on, and (C) is false.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof uses the one rule twice, once in each argument place of 'loves', and the second use is the move the syllogistic had no rule for: 'girl' leaves the inside of a predicate and becomes what the sentence quantifies over. The extra premise is the condition the syllogistic carried silently, when it read 'every boy is a girl-lover' as implying that there are boys.

The step from (B) to (A), which in subject–predicate form ran between two sentences with no term in common, is proved by the same rules. It is a different inference from the opening's: its conclusion is (A), a claim about every boy, and it needs no premise that boys exist, because with no boys (A) is true trivially.

<Theorem title="(B) implies (A)">
$\exists y\,\bigl(Gy \wedge \forall x\,(Bx \to Lxy)\bigr) \to \forall x\,\bigl(Bx \to \exists y\,(Gy \wedge Lxy)\bigr)$, and the converse is not valid.

<Proof>
Assume the antecedent and let $g$ be a witness: $Gg$ and $\forall x\,(Bx \to Lxg)$. Take an arbitrary $b$ with $Bb$. Instantiating the universal at $b$ gives $Bb \to Lbg$, hence $Lbg$; with $Gg$ this is $Gg \wedge Lbg$. Existential generalisation on the place of $g$ gives $\exists y\,(Gy \wedge Lby)$. Since $b$ was arbitrary, $\forall x\,(Bx \to \exists y\,(Gy \wedge Lxy))$. For the converse, Model 1 makes the consequent true and the antecedent false, so the converse conditional has a counter-model.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Every step of both proofs is mechanical: instantiate, detach, conjoin, generalise.

## Existence is a property of concepts

The layered picture has a consequence for a word that grammar treats as an ordinary predicate. 'Tame tigers exist' and 'unicorns do not exist' look like 'tame tigers growl' and 'unicorns do not growl'. Take the negative sentence at face value and it goes wrong at once. If 'does not exist' is a predicate true of certain things, then 'unicorns do not exist' says of the unicorns that they fall under it. But there are no unicorns to fall under anything, and if there were, the sentence would be false. One historical escape was to say that unicorns must have some lesser being, that they "subsist", for the sentence to be about them. That buys a true sentence with a population of things that are not there.

### What 'unicorns do not exist' is about

The concept-script gives the sentence a different subject. It is $\neg\exists x\,Ux$: the first-level concept '$(\ )$ is a unicorn' falls within the second-level concept 'no object falls under $(\ )$'. The sentence is about a concept, which is perfectly available to be talked about, and it says that nothing falls under it. No unicorn, existing or subsisting, is needed. 'Tame tigers exist' is $\exists x\,(Tx \wedge Ax)$: the concept 'tame tiger' is instantiated.

### There is no useful first-level 'exists'

You might still try to define existence as a property of objects: say that $a$ exists when $\exists x\,(x = a)$. The formula is well formed, and it shows what such a property would be worth. In first-order logic $\exists x\,(x = a)$ is true for every name $a$, because a name always denotes an object of the domain and that object is identical with itself. A first-level 'exists' is the degenerate concept from the definition above, the one everything falls under. It distinguishes nothing from anything, and so it cannot be what 'unicorns do not exist' denies of anything.

Kant had argued in 1781, in his critique of proofs of God's existence, that being is "not a real predicate": adding 'exists' to a description of a thing adds nothing to what the thing is described as. In the concept-script that is no longer a philosophical thesis to be defended. It is a fact about where existence sits in the notation: on the second level, as a property of concepts, with nothing left on the first level for it to do.

<KeyFact>
'$F$s exist' says that the concept $F$ is instantiated, $\exists x\,Fx$. The same analysis makes a statement of number, 'there are exactly two $F$s', a property of a concept, and it leaves any argument that treats existence as a property a thing can have or lack with a bad premise: either it applies a second-level word to an object, or it uses a first-level 'exists' that every object satisfies and that therefore cannot separate a being that exists from one that does not.
</KeyFact>

The drawing is the part that did not survive. The linear notation you use today, with $\forall$ and $\exists$ written in front of a formula and scope shown by brackets, comes from later logicians; it is easier to set in type and to read from left to right, and it expresses exactly the same contents. Two more parts were dropped. One is the later doctrine that sentences name truth-values (the Remark above); the other, present from 1879, is the view of what logic is for.

### A notation for content, not a calculus

The book's inventor did not think of his system as a set of uninterpreted formulas with rules for shuffling them. Reviewers of the 1879 book had compared it with the algebraic logic of the time, which wrote relations between classes as equations and derived new equations from them by rules modelled on algebra. In a lecture at Jena in 1882, answering that comparison, he put the difference between the two this way:

<Quote source="'On the Aim of the Conceptual Notation' (lecture, Jena, 1882), trans. V. H. Dudman">
I was not trying to present an abstract logic in formulas; I was trying to express contents in an exacter and more perspicuous manner than is possible in words, by using written symbols. I was trying, in fact, to create a "lingua characteristica" in the Leibnizian sense, not a mere "calculus ratiocinator".
</Quote>

A *lingua characteristica*, Leibniz's seventeenth-century project, was a script in which every content is written exactly. A *calculus ratiocinator* is a machine for deriving formulas from formulas. Modern logic went the other way: it treats a formal system as a calculus that may be reinterpreted at will, and it calls an inference valid when it survives every reinterpretation of its non-logical words. The function–argument analysis passed into that tradition intact, and the claim that the script captures content did not.

<Aside>
The disagreement is concrete. Is 'Jo had a dream' a logical consequence of 'Jo had a nightmare'? Read formally, the premise is $Fj$ and the conclusion $Gj$, and the inference fails: interpret $F$ as 'had a dream' and $G$ as 'had a nightmare' and the premise can be true with the conclusion false. Read for content, a nightmare is a bad dream, so the conclusion follows. The modern account counts the inference valid only once the unstated premise $\forall x\,(Fx \to Gx)$ is written in; the content view counts it valid as it stands. The same difference reappears in <Ref lesson="two-dogmas-analyticity">the attack on analyticity</Ref>, as the question whether there is any principled line between truths of logic and truths of meaning.
</Aside>

What the book's preface claimed for itself was modest, and it was right. Ordinary language, it said, is to the concept-script as the eye is to the microscope:

<Quote source="Begriffsschrift (1879), Preface, trans. S. Bauer-Mengelberg">
But, as soon as scientific goals demand great sharpness of resolution, the eye proves to be insufficient. The microscope, on the other hand, is perfectly suited to precisely such goals, but that is just why it is useless for all others.
</Quote>

The tool survived on those terms. Nobody speaks the concept-script, and every quantified statement of modern mathematics is written with its analysis.

<Conclusion>
You can now say what a sentence has to be taken apart into for nested generality to become reasoning: a function applied to arguments, any of whose places may be left open, with 'every' and 'some' as second-level functions applied to the concept that results. The two readings of 'every boy loves some girl' are two orders of applying those functions. The opening's step to 'some girl is loved by a boy' is one rule applied once in each argument place of 'loves', given that there is a boy; (B) implies (A) by the same rules, which ignore the grammatical position a name occupies; and existence turns out to sit on the second level, as the property a concept has of being instantiated.

That last point leaves a question open. 'There are exactly two $F$s' is also a property of a concept, so numbers may belong on the second level too, and if every number statement can be written in this notation without numerals, arithmetic may be nothing but logic. <Ref lesson="frege-logicism">The next lesson</Ref> takes up that claim.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Render 'every natural number has a successor' and 'there is a number that succeeds every number' in quantifier notation, using $Sxy$ for '$y$ is a successor of $x$' and taking the domain to be the natural numbers. Say which implies which, and give a one-line model in which exactly one of them is true.

<Solution>
'Every natural number has a successor': $\forall x\,\exists y\,Sxy$. 'There is a number that succeeds every number': $\exists y\,\forall x\,Sxy$.

As with the boys and girls, the sentence with $\exists$ outermost is the stronger: if one $y_0$ succeeds every $x$, then for each $x$ that same $y_0$ is a successor, so $\exists y\,\forall x\,Sxy \to \forall x\,\exists y\,Sxy$.

The natural numbers with $Sxy$ meaning $y = x + 1$ make the first true and the second false: every $x$ has $x+1$, but no single $y$ equals $x+1$ for every $x$ (it would have to equal both $1$ and $2$). The second sentence fails because it puts the witness outside the scope of 'every'.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "The syllogistic could handle 'every boy loves some girl' perfectly well. Treat 'loves some girl' as the predicate term $P$; the sentence is then 'every boy is $P$', which the syllogistic handles." Show what the move loses, and name the device the syllogistic lacks.

<Solution>
The move renders one reading, (A), and loses the rest.

**It cannot express the difference from (B).** With 'loves some girl' sealed into one term, nothing in 'every boy is $P$' marks that the girl may vary with the boy. (B), 'one girl is loved by every boy', has to be parsed with a different subject, 'some girl is $Q$' with $Q$ = 'loved by every boy'. Both are well-formed syllogistic sentences, but the forms the logic reads are 'every $B$ is $P$' and 'some $G$ is $Q$', and the difference between the readings, which quantifier is applied inside the other, is represented in neither; it is buried in the private structure of $P$ and $Q$.

**It cannot express the inference.** (B) implies (A). In syllogistic form that is an inference from 'some girl is $Q$' to 'every boy is $P$': different subjects, different predicates, no shared term. Every valid syllogistic form needs a middle term shared between premises, and there is none here. The validity lives entirely in the relation between $P$ and $Q$, which the syllogistic cannot see.

**The missing device** is a variable bound inside a predicate: a predicate such as '$(\ )$ loves some girl', $\exists y\,(Gy \wedge L\xi y)$, which contains a quantifier and still has an open place. With it, $P$ and $Q$ are no longer opaque, and the inference is a five-step derivation (the proof that (B) implies (A)).
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The concept-script has only the universal quantifier and defines $\exists x\,\varphi(x)$ as $\neg\forall x\,\neg\varphi(x)$. Using only the universal rule $\forall x\,\psi(x) \to \psi(a)$ (for any formula $\psi$) and propositional logic, derive existential generalisation, $\varphi(a) \to \exists x\,\varphi(x)$.

<Solution>
**Goal.** Unfolding the definition, the goal is $\varphi(a) \to \neg\forall x\,\neg\varphi(x)$.

**Step 1.** Apply the universal rule with $\psi(x) := \neg\varphi(x)$. This gives $\forall x\,\neg\varphi(x) \to \neg\varphi(a)$.

**Step 2.** Contrapose, which propositional logic allows for any conditional $P \to Q$ to give $\neg Q \to \neg P$: $\neg\neg\varphi(a) \to \neg\forall x\,\neg\varphi(x)$.

**Step 3.** By double negation, $\varphi(a) \to \neg\neg\varphi(a)$. Chain it with Step 2 (hypothetical syllogism): $\varphi(a) \to \neg\forall x\,\neg\varphi(x)$, which is $\varphi(a) \to \exists x\,\varphi(x)$ by definition.

Nothing in the derivation depends on where $a$ stands in $\varphi$, so the derived rule holds for every argument place. The transferable move: with $\exists$ defined as $\neg\forall\neg$, every law about $\exists$ is a law about $\forall$ read through contraposition.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take the concept '$(\ )$ is not identical with itself', $x \neq x$. (a) What does 'something falls under it' say, and what is its truth-value? (b) Someone argues: "Nothing is better than eternal happiness. A ham sandwich is better than nothing. So a ham sandwich is better than eternal happiness." Use the analysis of quantifiers to say exactly where the argument goes wrong.

<Solution>
**(a)** 'Something falls under $x \neq x$' is $\exists x\,(x \neq x)$: the concept falls within the second-level concept 'some object falls under $(\ )$'. Every object is identical with itself, so the concept maps every object to the False and the sentence is false. Its negation, $\neg\exists x\,(x \neq x)$, is a truth of logic. The concept is the empty concept, a legitimate concept that nothing falls under. Nothing here is an object called 'nothing'; the word 'nothing' appeared only as part of the second-level concept 'no object falls under $(\ )$'.

**(b)** The two premises use 'nothing' in different ways. Write $Bxy$ for '$x$ is better than $y$' and $e$ for eternal happiness.

- 'Nothing is better than eternal happiness' is a quantified sentence: $\neg\exists x\,Bxe$, equivalently $\forall x\,\neg Bxe$. 'Nothing' is a second-level concept applied to the concept '$(\ )$ is better than eternal happiness'.
- 'A ham sandwich is better than nothing' does not have that form. Read the same way it would be $\neg\exists x\,Bhx$, 'there is nothing the sandwich is better than', which is not what anyone means. What it means is roughly 'having a ham sandwich is better than having nothing at all', a comparison of a situation with an empty one.

The conclusion follows only if 'nothing' is treated as a name $n$ of an object: $Bhn$ and, from the first premise, $Bne$, then transitivity gives $Bhe$. But the first premise contains no such name; it contains a quantifier, and $\neg\exists x\,Bxe$ does not have the form $Bne$. The argument works by moving the quantifier from the second level to the first, the same mistake as reading 'nothing is mortal' as a statement that Nothing is mortal.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Give two different function–argument analyses of the sentence '$2 < 3$', saying in each what the function is and what the argument is, and a third that treats it as a relation. Then consider the theorem 'for every number $n$ there is a prime larger than $n$', $\forall n\,\exists p\,(Pp \wedge n < p)$. Which argument places of $<$ does the theorem generalise over, in which order, and what does the order of generalisation say about how the proof must go?

<Solution>
**Three analyses.** (i) Function '$(\ ) < 3$', argument $2$: the concept 'less than 3', under which $2$ falls. (ii) Function '$2 < (\ )$', argument $3$: the concept 'greater than 2', under which $3$ falls. (iii) Function '$(\ ) < [\ ]$' with two arguments $2$ and $3$: the relation, mapping the pair $(2, 3)$ to the True.

**The theorem.** Start from the relation $\xi < \zeta$ (analysis iii), restricted by $P$ in the second place. The second place is closed first: '$\exists p\,(Pp \wedge \xi < p)$' is the first-level concept 'there is a prime larger than $(\ )$'. The first place is closed last, by $\forall n$. So both places are generalised over, the second by $\exists$ inside, the first by $\forall$ outside.

**What the order says about the proof.** Because $\exists p$ sits inside $\forall n$, the prime may depend on $n$, and the proof must produce it from $n$: given $n$, the standard proof takes a prime factor $p$ of $n! + 1$; dividing $n!+1$ by any number from $2$ to $n$ leaves remainder $1$, so $p > n$. The opposite order, $\exists p\,\forall n\,(Pp \wedge n < p)$, would claim one prime larger than every number, which is false (take $n = p$). The choice of analysis decides which place can be generalised and in what order; the proof is a procedure that turns the outer $n$ into the inner witness.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A student claims: "$f(x) = x^2$ is uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}$, because for every $\varepsilon > 0$ and every $x$ there is a $\delta > 0$ such that $|x - y| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon$." Write out continuity and uniform continuity in quantifier notation, say what the student has actually proved, and show that the claimed conclusion is false.

<Solution>
**The two definitions.** With all variables over $\mathbb{R}$ and $\varepsilon, \delta > 0$:

- Continuity on $\mathbb{R}$: $\forall \varepsilon\,\forall x\,\exists \delta\,\forall y\,\bigl(|x-y| < \delta \to |f(x)-f(y)| < \varepsilon\bigr)$.
- Uniform continuity: $\forall \varepsilon\,\exists \delta\,\forall x\,\forall y\,\bigl(|x-y| < \delta \to |f(x)-f(y)| < \varepsilon\bigr)$.

The only difference is whether $\exists\delta$ stands inside or outside $\forall x$: whether $\delta$ may depend on $x$. The student's sentence has $\exists\delta$ inside $\forall x$, so it states continuity, and that much is true of $x^2$. Passing from it to uniform continuity is the invalid (A)-to-(B) step from the lesson.

**Uniform continuity fails.** Take $\varepsilon = 1$ and suppose some $\delta > 0$ worked for every $x$. Let $y = x + \delta/2$, so $|x - y| < \delta$. Then $|f(y) - f(x)| = |x\delta + \delta^2/4| \ge x\delta$ for $x > 0$, and choosing $x = 1/\delta$ gives $|f(y) - f(x)| \ge 1$, contradicting the requirement $< 1$. So no single $\delta$ serves all $x$.

The error is a scope error of exactly the kind subject–predicate logic could not even represent: two sentences with the same words and the same quantifiers, differing only in the order in which the quantifiers are applied.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Write 'there are exactly two $F$s' using only $\exists$, $\forall$, $\neg$, $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\to$ and $=$, without any numeral. Check your formula against a domain where three objects fall under $F$ and one where only one does. What kind of concept, in the sense of this lesson, does your formula express?

<Solution>
Split 'exactly two' into 'at least two' and 'at most two'. 'At least two': $\exists x\,\exists y\,(x \neq y \wedge Fx \wedge Fy)$. 'At most two': any $F$ is one of those two, $\forall z\,(Fz \to z = x \vee z = y)$. Put together, with the second clause inside the scope of the first:

$$
\exists x\,\exists y\,\bigl(x \neq y \wedge Fx \wedge Fy \wedge \forall z\,(Fz \to z = x \vee z = y)\bigr).
$$

**Checks.** With three $F$s $a, b, c$: any choice of distinct $x, y$ among them leaves the third, and $z$ = the third gives $Fz$ with $z \neq x$ and $z \neq y$, so the formula is false, as it should be. With one $F$, $a$: no two distinct objects both fall under $F$, so the first clause fails and the formula is false. With exactly two, $a$ and $b$, take $x = a, y = b$ and every clause holds.

**What it expresses.** The formula has one gap, the place for $F$, and its value is a truth-value: it is a second-level concept, 'exactly two objects fall under $(\ )$', which belongs on the top tier of the levels figure beside 'some', 'every' and 'no'. Number has been expressed without numerals, purely in logic, as a property of a concept. <Ref lesson="frege-logicism">The next lesson</Ref> asks what the number $2$ itself is, given that every statement of the form 'there are exactly two $F$s' can be written this way, and defines it as what all concepts with exactly two instances have in common.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

'How many things are on this desk?' has no answer until someone says what to count: one book, 150 pages, 38,000 words. So a statement of number is not about the desk; it is about whatever was counted under. Kant had held that $7+5=12$ rests on an intuition of time that no definition could replace. If instead the number words could be defined from logic alone, arithmetic would be as certain, and as free of intuition, as 'no bachelor is married' — and the question is whether the definitions can be written down without smuggling a number into them.

Kant's claim, made in the *Critique of Pure Reason* of 1781, uses two distinctions. A judgement is *analytic* when its predicate is already contained in the concept of its subject, as 'married' is contained in 'husband'; otherwise it is *synthetic*. A judgement is *a priori* when it can be known without appeal to experience, *a posteriori* when it cannot. 'No bachelor is married' is analytic: you need only unpack the word. '$7+5=12$', Kant argued, is a priori but synthetic. The concept 'sum of 7 and 5' does not contain 12; you find 12 only by counting on from 7, one unit at a time, and that successive adding rests on our intuition of time, a form of experience given before any particular experience. If he was right, arithmetic is certain for a different reason than logic is, and no proof from concepts alone could reach it.

The Jena mathematician whose concept-script the <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier">previous lesson</Ref> took apart had a reason to doubt this. Intuition does not scale. Nobody has an intuition of 37,863 fingers, yet $135{,}664 + 37{,}863 = 173{,}527$ is as certain as $7 + 5 = 12$, and it is certain because it can be proved. In a short book of 1884 on the foundations of arithmetic, and then in a two-volume treatise of 1893 and 1903, he set out to show that every truth of arithmetic is provable from laws of logic and definitions. That thesis is *logicism*, and it stands or falls with the definitions: each number word must be defined using only the logical vocabulary of the concept-script, quantifiers, connectives, identity and variables over concepts, with no numeral and no 'and so on' hidden in the definition.

## A statement of number is about a concept

What does the number in a statement of number attach to? Not to the heap on the desk, which was one, 150 and 38,000 at once. The second case is the 1884 book's own, an army: what you can truly call four companies you can, with equal truth, call 500 men. Nothing in the world changes between the two judgements; only the concept under which you count changes.

The decisive case is zero. Say 'Venus has 0 moons' and ask what the sentence is about. Not a moon, and not a heap of moons: there is none. The sentence is still true, and the only thing available for it to be about is the concept 'moon of Venus'.

<Quote source="Foundations of Arithmetic (1884), §46, trans. J. L. Austin">
This suggests as the answer to the first of the questions left open in our last paragraph, that the content of a statement of number is an assertion about a concept. This is perhaps clearest with the number 0. If I say "Venus has 0 moons", there simply does not exist any moon or agglomeration of moons for anything to be asserted of; but what happens is that a property is assigned to the concept "moon of Venus", namely that of including nothing under it.
</Quote>

The <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier" id="generality-is-a-concept-of-concepts">previous lesson</Ref> analysed 'some' and 'every' the same way: as properties of first-level concepts, that is, as second-level concepts, functions whose argument is a concept and whose value is a truth-value. 'Venus has 0 moons' says that the concept 'moon of Venus' falls within the second-level concept 'no object falls under $(\ )$', the same concept as 'unicorns do not exist'. 'The King's carriage is drawn by four horses' says that the concept 'horse that draws the King's carriage' falls within 'exactly four objects fall under $(\ )$'.

## 'Exactly $n$' without the numeral $n$

The obvious next step is to say that 'there are two $F$s' means that the concept $F$ has the property *two*. As an analysis that is right. As a definition it is circular, since 'two' appears on both sides. The question is whether the second-level concept 'exactly two objects fall under $(\ )$' can be written without the numeral.

It can, and you did it in the <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier">last exercise of the previous lesson</Ref>: 'at least two', two distinct objects falling under $F$, and 'at most two', anything falling under $F$ is one of them.

The three formulas are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{exactly } 0: &\quad \neg\exists x\,Fx \\
\text{exactly } 1: &\quad \exists x\,\bigl(Fx \wedge \forall z\,(Fz \to z = x)\bigr) \\
\text{exactly } 2: &\quad \exists x\,\exists y\,\bigl(x \neq y \wedge Fx \wedge Fy \wedge \forall z\,(Fz \to z = x \vee z = y)\bigr)
\end{aligned}
$$

The pattern also has a form that builds each formula from the one before. 'Exactly $n+1$ objects fall under $F$' holds just when some object $a$ falls under $F$ and exactly $n$ objects fall under the concept '$F$ but not $a$', $Fx \wedge x \neq a$. Take a one-member concept, remove its member, and you are left with an empty one; that is 'exactly 1' defined from 'exactly 0'.

### Why this does not yet define the number two

The formulas define, for each numeral, a second-level concept. They do not give arithmetic what it needs, for two reasons.

**There is no number to quantify over.** Arithmetic says 'every number has a successor' and 'for every $n$, $n + 0 = n$'. Those sentences need a variable $n$ that ranges over numbers, which means numbers must be objects, things that can be the values of a variable. 'Exactly two objects fall under $(\ )$' is a concept, and on the previous lesson's levels an object is never a concept; you cannot write $\forall n$ over an infinite list of separately written formulas.

**There is no identity between numbers.** 'The number of Jupiter's moons is four', true of the moons known in 1884, has the form of an identity between two objects, like 'the capital of Germany is Berlin', and arithmetic is full of such identities. The formulas give no object 'the number of $F$s' to stand on either side.

So the definition has to produce an object for each concept: *the number of $F$s*, written $\#F$. For an object, the first thing needed is a criterion of identity: a way of saying, without using any number word, when $\#F$ and $\#G$ are the same object.

## The same number without counting

When do two concepts have the same number? Counting both and comparing the results is the method everyone uses, and it is the one method ruled out here, since counting assigns numerals and the numerals are what is being defined. The 1884 book answers with a waiter. To lay exactly as many knives as plates he need count neither. He lays a knife immediately to the right of every plate and makes sure every knife lies immediately to the right of a plate. The relation 'immediately to the right of' then pairs plates with knives one to one, and that settles that their numbers are equal, whatever the numbers are.

<Figure
  src="./figures/knives-plates.svg"
  alt="Five plates in a row, each with a knife immediately to its right, and blue arrows pairing each plate with its knife; beside the row, the formula #F = #G written once as what the pairing licenses"
  caption="No count is made, yet the pairing settles that the numbers are equal. The relation 'immediately to the right of' does the correlating."
/>

The pairing is a relation, and 'one to one' can be said in pure logic. A relation $R$ correlates the $F$s one to one with the $G$s when every $F$ is paired with some $G$, every $G$ with some $F$, nothing is paired with two things, and no two things are paired with one.

<Definition title="equinumerous concepts">
Concepts $F$ and $G$ are *equinumerous*, $F \approx G$, when there is a relation $R$ such that

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\forall x\,\bigl(Fx \to \exists y\,(Gy \wedge Rxy)\bigr) \;\wedge\; \forall y\,\bigl(Gy \to \exists x\,(Fx \wedge Rxy)\bigr) \\
&\wedge\; \forall x\,\forall y\,\forall z\,\bigl(Rxy \wedge Rxz \to y = z\bigr) \;\wedge\; \forall x\,\forall y\,\forall z\,\bigl(Rxz \wedge Ryz \to x = y\bigr).
\end{aligned}
$$
</Definition>

You know this from Cantor's theory of sets as 'same cardinality'. What is new here is the use it is put to: the right-hand side contains no number word at all, and so it can serve as the criterion of identity for numbers. The 1884 book takes the idea from a remark of David Hume's (1739), "when two numbers are so combined as that the one has always an unit answering to every unit of the other, we pronounce them equal", and the principle now carries his name.

<Remark title="Hume's principle">
For all concepts $F$ and $G$: $\quad \#F = \#G \;\leftrightarrow\; F \approx G.$

This is a principle, not a definition of $\#$: it says when two numbers are the same, but it gives no expression that '$\#F$' could be replaced by.
</Remark>

The definition of $\approx$ begins '$\exists R$': it quantifies over relations, and the definitions below quantify over concepts, '$\exists F$' and '$\forall F$'. A logic with variables over concepts and relations, as well as over objects, is called *second-order*; the first-order logic you work in has variables over objects only. Keep this apart from the previous lesson's 'second-level'. *Level* sorts functions by what they take as arguments: a second-level concept takes a first-level concept. *Order* sorts a language by what its variables range over. The concept-script was second-order from 1879, and logicism needs it to be: without $\exists R$ there is no way to say 'one to one' for arbitrary concepts.

### What Hume's principle cannot decide

Hume's principle says when $\#F$ is the same as $\#G$. It does not say what $\#F$ is. Is the number of moons of Venus identical with England? Nobody believes it, but nothing in Hume's principle rules it out, because the principle only compares an object given as '$\#F$' with an object given as '$\#G$', and England is not given in that form. A definition of number that leaves this open has not yet said which objects the numbers are.

The 1884 book closed the gap by naming an object outright. The *extension* of a concept is the class of the objects that fall under it; the extension of 'planet' is the class of planets. The book defined

$$
\#F \;=\; \text{the extension of the concept 'equinumerous with } F\text{'},
$$

and then proved Hume's principle from this definition, so that the principle became a theorem rather than a starting point: two such classes coincide exactly when $F \approx G$, because $\approx$ is an equivalence relation (one of the closing exercises asks you to check that).

Read with the previous lesson's levels, the definition looks ill-formed. What falls within 'equinumerous with $F$' is concepts, not objects, so it is a second-level concept, and an extension was defined as a class of *objects*. A second-level concept has no extension in that sense.

The way round is to give every concept an extension first and to count that extension as an object, one more thing that can fall under a concept. Then each concept equinumerous with $F$ can be traded for its extension, and the second-level concept for a first-level one: '$(\ )$ is the extension of some concept equinumerous with $F$'. Objects fall under this concept, namely those extensions, so it has an extension in the ordinary sense, and that class is $\#F$.

The construction rests on one assumption, which the book took for granted: every concept has an extension, and that extension is an object. Keep it in view; the last part of this lesson turns on it.

## Zero, the successor and one

With numbers as objects and a criterion for their identity, the individual numbers can be defined. Why not simply posit them: let $0$ be a number, give every number a successor and lay down the Peano axioms? Because that is to assert that certain objects exist, and an assertion of existence is not a definition. A definition, by the concept-script's own standard, must be *eliminable*, so that the defined sign can always be replaced by what defines it, and *conservative*, so that nothing new becomes provable with it. Laying down that $0$ and its successors exist adds a premise. If the premise is synthetic, arithmetic is synthetic, and logicism has assumed what it set out to prove. So each number must be the number of a concept that logic itself supplies.

### Zero and the successor

**Zero.** What concept can logic prove empty? Not 'moon of Venus', which is empty as a matter of astronomy. The concept '$(\ )$ is not identical with itself' is empty on logical grounds alone, since every object is identical with itself:

$$
0 \;=\; \#[x \neq x].
$$

Here $\#[\varphi(x)]$ is the number of the concept '$\varphi(\ )$'. By Hume's principle, every concept nothing falls under has the number $0$: any two empty concepts are correlated by the empty relation, which satisfies all four clauses of the definition of $\approx$ vacuously. Each clause ranges over all objects, but each has an antecedent ($Fx$, $Gy$, $Rxy$ or $Rxz$) that no object satisfies, since nothing falls under an empty concept and no pair stands in the empty relation.

**The successor.** The remove-one-object step from the 'exactly $n+1$' pattern gives the relation between neighbouring numbers. Write $P(m, n)$ for '$m$ immediately precedes $n$':

$$
P(m, n) \;\leftrightarrow\; \exists F\,\exists a\,\bigl(Fa \;\wedge\; n = \#F \;\wedge\; m = \#[Fx \wedge x \neq a]\bigr).
$$

In words: $n$ is the number of some concept, and $m$ is the number of the concept that remains when one object falling under it is taken away.

### One, two and the construction continued

**One.** The number $0$ is now an object, and the concept '$(\ )$ is identical with $0$' has exactly one object under it. So define

$$
1 \;=\; \#[x = 0].
$$

<Example title="0 immediately precedes 1">
Take $F = [x = 0]$ and $a = 0$ in the definition of $P$. Check the three conjuncts.

**$Fa$.** $0 = 0$, so $0$ falls under '$(\ )$ is identical with $0$'.

**$n = \#F$.** $\#[x = 0]$ is $1$ by the definition of $1$.

**$m = \#[Fx \wedge x \neq a]$.** The concept is '$x = 0 \wedge x \neq 0$'. Nothing falls under it, so by Hume's principle its number equals that of the other empty concept $x \neq x$, which is $0$.

All three hold, so $P(0, 1)$. No fact about desks, moons or the world entered: the only object used is $0$, and $0$ was itself defined from a concept that logic proves empty.
</Example>

The construction continues in the same way. With $0$ and $1$ available as objects, the concept '$x = 0 \vee x = 1$' has two objects under it, and $2 = \#[x = 0 \vee x = 1]$; with $0, 1, 2$ in hand, $3$ is the number of '$x = 0 \vee x = 1 \vee x = 2$'. Each number is the number of the concept 'identical with one of the numbers before it', so each step manufactures the object the next step counts.

<Exercise>
Show that $P(1, 2)$. You will first need $0 \neq 1$; prove it from Hume's principle by asking whether any relation can correlate the objects under $x = 0$ with the objects under $x \neq x$.

<Solution>
**First, $0 \neq 1$.** Suppose $0 = 1$, that is, $\#[x \neq x] = \#[x = 0]$. By Hume's principle there is a relation $R$ correlating the objects under $x = 0$ one to one with those under $x \neq x$. The first clause of the definition of $\approx$ applied to the object $0$, which falls under $x = 0$, demands some $y$ with $y \neq y$ and $R0y$. No object is distinct from itself, so there is no such $y$. Contradiction; hence $0 \neq 1$.

**Now $P(1,2)$.** Take $F = [x = 0 \vee x = 1]$ and $a = 1$.

- $Fa$: $1 = 1$, so $1$ falls under $F$.
- $2 = \#F$ by the definition of $2$.
- The remaining concept is '$(x = 0 \vee x = 1) \wedge x \neq 1$'. An object falls under it exactly when it is $0$: the object $0$ does, because $0 = 0$ and $0 \neq 1$ (just proved); and any object under it is $0$ or $1$ and is not $1$, so is $0$. The concept therefore has the same objects under it as $x = 0$, the identity relation correlates the two one to one, and by Hume's principle its number is $\#[x = 0] = 1$.

All three conjuncts hold, so $P(1, 2)$. The step that needed care, $0 \neq 1$, is the one that keeps the concept for $2$ from collapsing into the concept for $1$.
</Solution>

## 'And so on': the ancestral of a relation

The definitions now produce $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$, and any particular number you care to reach. What they do not yet define is *natural number* itself. The natural answer, 'the natural numbers are $0$, $1$, $2$, and so on', uses exactly the phrase that cannot appear in a definition. 'And so on' is where Kant located the intuition of time, in the step-by-step running through a series, and a proof that leans on it has stepped outside logic. The phrase has to be replaced by something written in the concept-script.

### Ancestors without running through the line

Take a family line in which $a$ is the father of $b$, $b$ of $c$ and $c$ of $d$. 'Ancestor' is the relation you get by following 'father of' any number of times, and once more the 'any number of times' is the problem. The 1879 concept-script had already removed it by turning the question around. Call a property *hereditary* in the father-series when every child of something that has it also has it: 'is a descendant of Charlemagne' is hereditary in this sense, 'is left-handed' is not. Now $d$ follows $a$ in the series exactly when $d$ has every hereditary property that all the children of $a$ have. The children of $a$ start the line off, heredity carries any such property down the line, and so whatever has every such property is somewhere down the line; conversely, anything not in the line fails some such property, for instance 'is a descendant of $a$' itself.

<Figure
  src="./figures/ancestral.svg"
  alt="A chain of four objects a, b, c, d joined by blue 'father of' arrows; an amber shaded region labelled F contains b, c and d but not a, annotated: 1. F holds of every child of a; 2. no 'father of' arrow leads out of F; 3. so F holds of d"
  caption="Any region that holds a's children and that no 'father of' arrow can leave must hold d. That is all 'd follows a in the series' means."
/>

<Definition title="the ancestral of a relation">
For a relation $R$, *$b$ follows $a$ in the $R$-series*, $R^*(a, b)$, when

$$
\forall F\,\Bigl[\bigl(\forall y\,(Ray \to Fy) \;\wedge\; \forall u\,\forall v\,(Fu \wedge Ruv \to Fv)\bigr) \;\to\; Fb\Bigr].
$$

The second conjunct says that $F$ is *hereditary* in the $R$-series.
</Definition>

The definition contains no number and no 'and so on'. It quantifies over all concepts $F$, which is why it needs the second-order logic of the previous section, and it is what a mathematician knows as the transitive closure of $R$, written down without iterating anything. The 1884 book insists on the difference: 'if we keep passing from an object to the next we finally reach $b$' describes how you might discover that $b$ follows $a$, and depends on how much time you have; the definition says what it is for $b$ to follow $a$, which is a fact whether or not anyone traces it.

### The natural numbers, and induction as a theorem

Apply the ancestral to $P$, immediate precedence:

$$
\mathrm{Nat}(n) \;\leftrightarrow\; n = 0 \;\vee\; P^*(0, n).
$$

A natural number is $0$ or anything that follows $0$ in the series of immediate precedence. The payoff is that the principle of mathematical induction, the form in which arithmetic says 'and so on', stops being an axiom.

<Theorem title="induction follows from the definition of natural number">
For every concept $F$: if $F0$, and $Fu \wedge P(u, v) \to Fv$ for all $u, v$, then $\mathrm{Nat}(n) \to Fn$ for all $n$.

<Proof>
The plan is to feed $F$ itself into the definition of $P^*$. Assume $F0$ and that $F$ is hereditary in the $P$-series. Let $n$ be a natural number. If $n = 0$, then $Fn$ by the first assumption. Otherwise $P^*(0, n)$, which by definition says: every concept that holds of everything $0$ immediately precedes and is hereditary holds of $n$. Check both conditions for $F$. If $P(0, y)$, then $F0$ and heredity give $Fy$, so $F$ holds of everything $0$ immediately precedes. $F$ is hereditary by assumption. So $Fn$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof uses nothing but the definitions and logic: instantiate a universal quantifier over concepts, detach, conclude. That is what the 1884 book claimed for the definition:

<Quote source="Foundations of Arithmetic (1884), §80, trans. J. L. Austin">
Only by means of this definition of following in a series is it possible to reduce the argument from n to (n + 1), which on the face of it is peculiar to mathematics, to the general laws of logic.
</Quote>

<KeyFact>
Induction, the step from $n$ to $n+1$ that Kant tied to the intuition of time, is a theorem once 'natural number' is defined by the ancestral; but the definition quantifies over all concepts, so it is logic only if second-order logic is.
</KeyFact>

### From the definitions to the Peano axioms

Read with $\mathrm{Nat}$ for 'is a number' and $P$ for 'is the successor of', the Peano axioms become five claims, and each can be traced to the definitions so far.

| Peano axiom | in the defined vocabulary | where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ is a number | $\mathrm{Nat}(0)$ | the first disjunct of $\mathrm{Nat}$ |
| $0$ is no number's successor | $\neg\exists m\,P(m, 0)$ | Hume's principle and the clause $Fa$ in the definition of $P$ (see the exercises) |
| successors are unique, and so are predecessors | $P(m,n) \wedge P(m,n') \to n = n'$, and the converse | Hume's principle: remove one object, then pair up the rest |
| every number has a successor | $\mathrm{Nat}(n) \to \exists m\,P(n, m)$ | the hard one: see below |
| induction | the theorem above | the ancestral |

The fourth row is the hard one because it needs, for each number $n$, a concept with exactly one more object under it than $n$ counts, and the world cannot be asked to supply one. The 1884 book's choice is the concept 'natural number up to and including $n$', which has the numbers $0, \ldots, n$ under it, and so has the number after $n$. Proving that this concept has number $n+1$ rather than $n$ requires showing that no natural number follows itself in the series, which is itself proved by induction. The book only sketched the argument; the treatise of 1893 carried it out.

## What the definitions were for: arithmetic without intuition

Suppose all of this goes through. What has been shown about arithmetic, and why was it worth the better part of twenty years?

The mathematics of the nineteenth century had already been removing intuition from analysis. In 1817 Bernard Bolzano, a Prague priest and mathematician, proved the intermediate value theorem, that a continuous function taking a negative and a positive value takes the value $0$ in between, from a definition of continuity in terms of limits rather than from a picture. A Kantian can draw the graph crossing the axis and call that a demonstration. The trouble is that any graph you draw is smooth once you look closely enough, and continuity does not promise that. In 1872 Karl Weierstrass presented to the Berlin Academy a function that is continuous everywhere and has a tangent at no point: however far you magnify its graph, it never straightens into a line, so nobody can draw it. The theorem is about every continuous function, this one included, while a picture shows at best what holds of the drawable ones; a proof that leans on the picture leaves a gap exactly where the undrawable cases fall, and the proof from the definition has none. The 1884 book applied the same discipline to arithmetic itself.

### Analytic, in a sharpened sense

For that it reworked Kant's distinction. Whether a truth is analytic, it said, is a question about its justification, not about what went through anyone's mind. Follow the proof back to its first premises. If they are only general logical laws and definitions, the truth is analytic; if the proof needs truths of some special science, such as geometry, it is synthetic; if it needs facts about particular objects, it is a posteriori. On this test, if the definitions above are definitions and the laws used are laws of logic, then every truth of arithmetic is analytic and a priori, and Kant was wrong about $7 + 5 = 12$.

The obvious objection is Kant's own: analytic judgements are empty, they only unpack what was put in, and arithmetic plainly tells us something. The 1884 book's reply is that Kant pictured a definition as a list of marks, from which you can take out only what you put in. A fruitful definition, like that of continuity or of the ancestral, draws a boundary that was not there before, and what follows from it cannot be seen in advance.

<Quote source="Foundations of Arithmetic (1884), §88, trans. J. L. Austin">
The conclusions we draw from it extend our knowledge, and ought therefore, on Kant's view, to be regarded as synthetic; and yet they can be proved by purely logical means, and are thus analytic. The truth is that they are contained in the definitions, but as plants are contained in their seeds, not as beams are contained in a house.
</Quote>

The test also explains why the concept-script was built at all. 'Proved from logic and definitions' can be checked only if a proof has no gaps, since a gap is exactly where an appeal to intuition could slip in unnoticed. The concept-script was designed so that every step is one of a small number of rules laid down in advance; the gapless chain of the <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier" id="one-rule-for-both-places">previous lesson's proofs</Ref> was the instrument, and logicism was what it was for.

## The 1902 letter: Basic Law V is inconsistent

Everything above rests on the definition of $\#F$ as an extension, and the 1884 book had assumed that it is known what extensions are. The 1893 treatise could not leave that to assumption: a gapless proof needs every premise written down. So it stated a law of logic for extensions. Write $\mathrm{ext}(F)$ for the extension of $F$.

<Remark title="Basic Law V">
For all concepts $F$ and $G$: $\quad \mathrm{ext}(F) = \mathrm{ext}(G) \;\leftrightarrow\; \forall x\,(Fx \leftrightarrow Gx).$

This is a law, an axiom of the treatise, not a definition: it is not eliminable, and read together with the rest of the system it asserts that every concept has an object as its extension.
</Remark>

It looks as harmless as Hume's principle, and of the same shape: two concepts have the same extension exactly when the same objects fall under them. Membership comes with it: an object $a$ is a member of $\mathrm{ext}(F)$ when $Fa$. The 1884 book's whole construction of the numbers can be run from this law.

### The letter

On 16 June 1902, while the second volume of the treatise was being printed, a letter arrived at Jena from a 30-year-old English logician who had been studying the first volume. He agreed with it, he wrote, in all essentials, except for one point.

<Quote source="B. Russell, letter to Frege, 16 June 1902, trans. B. Woodward">
Let w be the predicate: to be a predicate that cannot be predicated of itself. Can w be predicated of itself? From each answer its opposite follows. Therefore we must conclude that w is not a predicate. Likewise there is no class (as a totality) of those classes which, each taken as a totality, do not belong to themselves.
</Quote>

In the vocabulary of the concept-script the argument takes three lines. Let $W$ be the concept '$(\ )$ is the extension of some concept that it does not fall under', $Wx \leftrightarrow \exists F\,\bigl(x = \mathrm{ext}(F) \wedge \neg Fx\bigr)$, and let $w = \mathrm{ext}(W)$.

1. **If $Ww$:** there is an $F$ with $w = \mathrm{ext}(F)$ and $\neg Fw$. Then $\mathrm{ext}(F) = \mathrm{ext}(W)$, so Basic Law V gives $\forall x\,(Fx \leftrightarrow Wx)$, and $\neg Fw$ becomes $\neg Ww$.
2. **If $\neg Ww$:** then no concept whose extension is $w$ fails to hold of $w$. $W$ is such a concept, so $Ww$.
3. Either way, $Ww \wedge \neg Ww$.

You might expect a contradiction in the foundations of mathematics to need some exotic set. This one needs one concept, defined in the concept-script's own vocabulary, and one use of the law, in its left-to-right direction: different concepts never share an extension unless they hold of the same objects.

That direction is where the law is impossible, and Cantor's theorem says why. A concept is fixed by which objects fall under it, since it is a function from objects to truth-values, so if every way of sorting the objects counts as a concept, the concepts correspond to the subsets of the domain of objects. Basic Law V says that $\mathrm{ext}$ maps concepts into objects with different concepts going to different objects: an injection from the subsets of the domain into the domain. There is none: no set injects its power set into itself. The letter's argument is Cantor's diagonal argument run inside the concept-script.

### The reply and the appendix

The reply went from Jena six days later. In it extensions are called *courses-of-values*, the treatise is called by its German title, *Grundgesetze*, and Basic Law V is 'Rule V'.

<Quote source="G. Frege, letter to Russell, 22 June 1902, trans. B. Woodward">
Your discovery of the contradiction caused me the greatest surprise and, I would almost say, consternation, since it has shaken the basis on which I intended to build arithmetic. It seems, then, that transforming the generalization of an equality into an equality of courses-of-values (§ 9 of my Grundgesetze) is not always permitted, that my Rule V (§ 20, p. 36) is false.
</Quote>

The second volume appeared in 1903 with a hastily written appendix that weakened Basic Law V to exclude the case of an extension falling under its own concept. The repair was ad hoc, it gave no reason why the excluded case should be excluded, and its author soon found it inadequate. He gave up the logicist programme and spent the rest of his working life, until his death in 1925, on clarifying the logic itself.

## What fell and what stood

From the letter you might conclude that the definitions of the numbers were refuted. Look at where the contradiction entered: through Basic Law V, which was used to make $\#F$ an extension. Hume's principle was proved from the extension definition. Every later step, $0$, $P$, $1$, the ancestral, $\mathrm{Nat}$, induction and the rest of the table, used only Hume's principle, the definitions and second-order logic.

<Theorem title="Peano arithmetic from Hume's principle">
In second-order logic, Hume's principle together with the definitions of $0$, $P$ and $\mathrm{Nat}$ implies all of the Peano axioms. Basic Law V is not needed. Hume's principle, unlike Basic Law V, has a model built from the natural numbers, so it is consistent if ordinary arithmetic is.
</Theorem>

The derivations in the 1893 treatise turn out to have been exactly such a proof: after Hume's principle was established, they made no further essential use of Basic Law V. This was noticed only in the 1960s, and worked out in detail in the 1980s and 1990s.

### What logicism now turns on

Is Hume's principle a law of logic? In its favour: it reads like an explanation of what 'the number of' means, as 'two lines have the same direction when they are parallel' explains 'direction'. Against it: it implies that there are infinitely many objects (the numbers $0, 1, 2, \ldots$ must all be distinct), and a law that settles how many things there are does not look like logic, which is supposed to hold whatever the domain. A related doubt attaches to second-order logic itself. The successor step treated the condition '$Fx \wedge x \neq a$' as a concept, and the paradox treated the condition defining $W$ as one; both moves rest on the *comprehension principle*

$$
\exists F\,\forall x\,\bigl(Fx \leftrightarrow \varphi(x)\bigr), \qquad \text{for any condition } \varphi \text{ that does not mention } F,
$$

which says that for every condition you can write there exists a concept holding of exactly the objects that meet it. That is an existence claim of the same kind as Basic Law V, which said that for every concept there exists an object, its extension: each makes logic the supplier of an entity for whatever can be written down. Comprehension by itself is consistent, but an assertion of existence was the very thing Kant called synthetic. Philosophers still dispute both questions; those who answer yes to the first call their position *neo-logicism*.

Two things survived whatever the answer. The ancestral is the standard definition of transitive closure in logic. And the standard for definitions, eliminable and conservative, with no existence claim smuggled in, became the standard by which later attempts to define mathematics in other terms were judged. Basic Law V, which supplied extensions by an axiom asserting that every concept has one, is the standing example of how much a single existence claim can carry.

<Conclusion>
You can now say what it means for arithmetic to be logic, and check each step of the claim. A statement of number assigns a property to a concept; 'exactly $n$' is writable in pure logic; Hume's principle turns one-one correspondence into a criterion of identity for numbers as objects; $0$ is the number of the concept $x \neq x$, and the successor removes one object from a concept; the ancestral replaces 'and so on' and makes induction a theorem. You can also say exactly what the letter of 16 June 1902 destroyed: Basic Law V, and with it the claim that numbers are extensions known to logic, while the derivation of the Peano axioms from Hume's principle stood.

The contradiction left a task: to say what classes are, if they cannot be objects that every concept is entitled to. The response that classes are logical fictions and come in types is taken up in <Ref lesson="logical-constructions-and-acquaintance">logical constructions</Ref>.

A second question stands apart from the letter and comes from Hume's principle itself. The principle makes '$\#F = \#G$' true just when $F \approx G$, and when it is true the two sides stand for one and the same number. Yet finding that two concepts are equinumerous can be news: that the planets and the legs of a spider are equinumerous is learned by counting, not by inspecting the sentence. A true identity '$a = b$' differs in what it tells you from '$a = a$', although both name one object. <Ref lesson="frege-sense-and-reference">The next lesson</Ref> asks what, if not the object named, the two sides of a true and informative identity differ in.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Write 'there are exactly three $F$s' in pure logic. Count its quantifiers and its distinctness clauses, and state how both counts grow with $n$ in the formula for 'exactly $n$'.

<Solution>
At least three distinct $F$s, and nothing else under $F$:

$$
\exists x\,\exists y\,\exists z\,\bigl(x \neq y \wedge x \neq z \wedge y \neq z \wedge Fx \wedge Fy \wedge Fz \wedge \forall w\,(Fw \to w = x \vee w = y \vee w = z)\bigr).
$$

It has four quantifiers (three $\exists$, one $\forall$) and three distinctness clauses. In general 'exactly $n$' has $n$ existential quantifiers plus one universal, so $n + 1$ quantifiers, and one clause $x_i \neq x_j$ for each pair of the $n$ witnesses, so $n(n-1)/2$ distinctness clauses; the final disjunction has $n$ disjuncts. The formulas grow, but every one is finite and contains no numeral, which is the point: each 'exactly $n$' is a second-level concept written in logic alone. The recursive form from the lesson avoids the quadratic growth: 'exactly 3' is $\exists a\,(Fa \wedge \text{exactly 2 objects fall under } [Fx \wedge x \neq a])$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Hume's principle uses $\approx$ as if it were an equivalence relation, and the derivation of Hume's principle from the extension definition needs it to be one. Prove that $\approx$ is reflexive, symmetric and transitive, saying which relation witnesses each.

<Solution>
**Reflexive.** $F \approx F$ is witnessed by identity, $Rxy \leftrightarrow x = y$ (restricted to $F$, or not; both work). Every $F$ is paired with itself, which is an $F$; nothing is paired with two things or two things with one, because identity is a function and one-one.

**Symmetric.** If $R$ witnesses $F \approx G$, the converse relation $R^{-1}$, $R^{-1}yx \leftrightarrow Rxy$, witnesses $G \approx F$: the first two clauses of the definition swap places, and so do the last two.

**Transitive.** Let $R$ witness $F \approx G$ and $S$ witness $G \approx H$. Define the composite $Txz \leftrightarrow \exists y\,(Gy \wedge Rxy \wedge Syz)$. Every $F$, $x$, has an $R$-partner $y$ that is $G$, and $y$ has an $S$-partner $z$ that is $H$, so $Txz$ with $Hz$; symmetrically every $H$ has a $T$-partner that is $F$. If $Txz$ and $Txz'$, the two middle objects are both $R$-partners of $x$, hence equal, and then $z$ and $z'$ are both $S$-partners of that one object, hence equal; the argument for two $x$s with one $z$ runs backwards the same way. So $T$ witnesses $F \approx H$.

In short, a one-one correlation followed by a one-one correlation is one. With it, the concepts equinumerous with $F$ and the concepts equinumerous with $G$ are the same concepts exactly when $F \approx G$, so their extensions, and with them $\#F$ and $\#G$, coincide exactly then, which is Hume's principle.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
(a) Show that no object immediately precedes $0$: $\neg\exists m\,P(m, 0)$. (b) Suppose the clause $Fa$ were dropped from the definition of $P$, leaving $\exists F\,\exists a\,(n = \#F \wedge m = \#[Fx \wedge x \neq a])$. Show that then every number would immediately precede itself. Which clause of the definition is the premise that (a) contradicts?

<Solution>
**(a)** Suppose $P(m, 0)$. Then there are $F$ and $a$ with $Fa$, $0 = \#F$ and $m = \#[Fx \wedge x \neq a]$. From $\#F = 0 = \#[x \neq x]$, Hume's principle gives $F \approx [x \neq x]$: a relation pairing each $F$ with some object not identical with itself. But $a$ is an $F$, and there is no object not identical with itself to pair it with. Contradiction. So nothing immediately precedes $0$.

**(b)** Take any number $n = \#F$, and pick an object $a$ that does *not* fall under $F$ (if $F$ is not everything, there is one). Then $Fx \wedge x \neq a$ holds of exactly the same objects as $Fx$, so its number is $\#F = n$, and the weakened definition gives '$n$ immediately precedes $n$'. Taking $F$ empty gives '$0$ immediately precedes $0$'.

**The clause.** $Fa$ guarantees that removing $a$ actually removes something. It is what makes $P$ a 'one fewer' relation rather than 'at most one fewer', and in (a) it is the premise that collides with the emptiness of every concept whose number is $0$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The 1884 book remarks that if $b$ follows $a$ in a series and $c$ follows $b$, then $c$ follows $a$, and that this can be shown "without even knowing the intervening members". Prove it from the definition of the ancestral: $R^*(a, b) \wedge R^*(b, c) \to R^*(a, c)$.

<Solution>
**Goal.** Take any $F$ that holds of every $R$-successor of $a$ and is hereditary. We must show $Fc$.

**Step 1: $Fb$.** $R^*(a, b)$ says that every such $F$ holds of $b$. Our $F$ is such a concept, so $Fb$.

**Step 2: $F$ holds of every $R$-successor of $b$.** If $Rby$, then $Fb$ (Step 1) and heredity give $Fy$.

**Step 3: $Fc$.** $R^*(b, c)$ says that every hereditary concept holding of all $R$-successors of $b$ holds of $c$. By Step 2 and the heredity of $F$, $F$ is one. So $Fc$.

Since $F$ was arbitrary, $R^*(a, c)$. No member of the series between $a$ and $c$ was named or counted; the proof passes through $b$ only because $b$ is given. In particular, from $P(10, 11)$ and $P(11, 12)$ (each implies its ancestral version directly, since a concept holding of every successor of $10$ holds of $11$) you get $P^*(10, 12)$, although $P(10, 12)$ is false: $P^*$ is 'less than', $P$ only 'immediately before'.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "The 1902 contradiction showed that the definitions of $0$, the successor and natural number were wrong, so the derivation of arithmetic from them had to be abandoned." Name the one premise the contradiction hits, say what survives, and say what the contradiction did refute.

<Solution>
**The premise hit.** The contradiction is derived from Basic Law V alone, in its left-to-right direction (different concepts never share an extension unless they hold of the same objects), together with the concept-script's ordinary logic. No definition of $0$, $P$ or $\mathrm{Nat}$ appears in the three lines.

**What survives.** Basic Law V was used for one purpose: to define $\#F$ as an extension and so prove Hume's principle. The definitions of $0 = \#[x \neq x]$, $P$ and $\mathrm{Nat}$, and the derivations of the Peano axioms, use only Hume's principle and second-order logic. Take Hume's principle as the starting point instead of proving it, and the whole derivation goes through; Hume's principle is consistent.

**What it refuted.** The claim that extensions are logical objects governed by a law of logic, and therefore the claim that the number words had been defined in pure logic. After 1902 the question is no longer whether arithmetic follows (it does, from Hume's principle) but whether its starting point is logic.

The claim fails at the inference from 'the system was inconsistent' to 'each part of it was wrong': the inconsistency is located in one law, and the rest of the derivation is independent of it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Test Basic Law V and Hume's principle on a domain with exactly three objects, letting every way of sorting the three count as a concept. (a) How many concepts are there, and can Basic Law V hold? (b) Hume's principle needs one object $\#F$ for each size a concept can have. How many sizes are there, and can Hume's principle hold? (c) What does (b) imply about any domain on which Hume's principle is true, and why does that bear on whether it is a law of logic?

<Solution>
**(a)** A concept is fixed by which of the three objects fall under it, so there are $2^3 = 8$ concepts. Basic Law V requires $\mathrm{ext}$ to send concepts with different instances to different objects, which needs eight distinct objects. There are three. Basic Law V fails, and by Cantor's theorem the same argument works for every domain, finite or infinite: there are always more ways of sorting the objects than there are objects.

**(b)** A concept on three objects can have $0, 1, 2$ or $3$ instances: four sizes. Hume's principle sends concepts of different sizes to different objects ($\#F = \#G$ only when $F \approx G$), so it needs four distinct objects. There are three. Hume's principle fails too. It is much weaker than Basic Law V, since it needs one object per size, not one per concept, but on a finite domain with $k$ objects there are $k + 1$ sizes.

**(c)** Hume's principle can be true only on an infinite domain: on any finite one there is one size more than there are objects. So it implies that infinitely many objects exist. That is why it can yield the Peano axioms, which require infinitely many numbers, and it is also the main objection to calling it logic: a law of logic is supposed to hold whatever the domain, and this one rules out every finite domain.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
In the <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier" id="what-every-applies-to">levels of the previous lesson</Ref>, objects fall under first-level concepts and first-level concepts fall within second-level ones. Classify each of the following, saying what it takes as argument and what it yields: (a) 'exactly two objects fall under $(\ )$'; (b) '$\#(\ )$', the number of; (c) '$(\ )$ is a natural number'; (d) $\approx$. Which of the four is not a concept at all, and why does logicism need exactly that one?

<Solution>
**(a)** Takes a first-level concept, yields a truth-value: a second-level concept, like 'some object falls under $(\ )$'.

**(b)** Takes a first-level concept, yields an object, the number. It is a second-level function but not a concept, since its value is not a truth-value. It is the near-miss case of the previous lesson ('the capital of $(\ )$' yields Berlin) one level up.

**(c)** Takes an object (a number, or anything else), yields a truth-value: a first-level concept. That is why '$\mathrm{Nat}(n)$' can be quantified over and why induction speaks of 'every $n$'.

**(d)** Takes two first-level concepts, yields a truth-value: a second-level relation.

**The non-concept, (b),** is what logicism needs because arithmetic needs numbers as objects: (a) alone gives a property of concepts for each numeral but nothing to quantify over or to identify. '$\#$' is the bridge from the second level, where the analysis puts statements of number, down to the level of objects, where arithmetic works; and providing that bridge was exactly the job given to extensions, and then to Hume's principle.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Suppose logicism had succeeded without the 1902 contradiction: every truth of arithmetic proved from laws of logic and definitions. Would '$7 + 5 = 12$' then be true in virtue of the meanings of its symbols, or in virtue of facts about concepts and the objects that are their numbers? Argue each side in a paragraph.

<Solution>
**In virtue of meaning.** The proof of $7 + 5 = 12$ would start from definitions, which fix what '$7$', '$+$' and '$12$' mean, and laws of logic, which hold whatever the subject matter. Nothing in the world is consulted. On this reading the sentence is true because of how its signs have been defined, as 'no bachelor is married' is, only with a much longer unpacking. Its certainty is the certainty of having followed a rule of one's own language, and it says nothing about anything.

**In virtue of facts.** The proof would also use the existence of the numbers as objects, extensions or the objects Hume's principle provides, and the truth that $0, 1, 2, \ldots$ are infinitely many distinct objects. On this reading arithmetic is a body of truths about a domain of logical objects, discovered rather than stipulated, and logic itself has a subject matter: concepts, their extensions and their numbers. The 1884 book leaned this way: it treated numbers as self-subsistent objects, and logic as having concepts and objects of its own to be true of.

**Where this leads.** The first answer is the one the logical empiricists of the 1930s took, and they took it without waiting for logicism to succeed: whether or not number can be reduced to logic, they argued, the propositions of mathematics are true by the definitions of their terms, and so analytic. <Ref lesson="a-priori-as-analytic">Necessary truth as analytic truth</Ref> develops that position and the reasons it needed no reduction.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

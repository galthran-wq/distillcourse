'Hesperus is Phosphorus' took astronomers to establish; 'Hesperus is Hesperus' takes nobody. Yet if a name's whole job is to stand for its object, both sentences say the same thing about the same planet. And someone who believes Mark Twain wrote Huckleberry Finn and has never heard of Samuel Clemens cannot be said to believe Clemens wrote it, although Clemens is Twain. A theory on which meaning is standing-for cannot tell these pairs apart; the question is what else a name carries.

'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are the Greek names of the evening star and the morning star: the brilliant point that shows in the west after sunset for some months, and the one that shows in the east before sunrise for some months. Both are the planet Venus. Nobody found that out by studying the two words. It was found by watching the sky: over years, the evening star sinks towards the Sun and vanishes into its glare, and some days later the morning star rises out of the glare on the other side; the two appearances alternate in one fixed cycle.

Standing-for is the theory of meaning behind <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier">the function–argument analysis</Ref>: a name stands for an object, a predicate for a concept (a function from objects to truth-values), and a sentence is built by applying the one to the other. Nothing in that picture distinguishes two names for one object. <Ref lesson="frege-logicism">The previous lesson</Ref> ended on the same gap from the side of arithmetic: Hume's principle makes '$\#F = \#G$' true exactly when the two concepts are equinumerous, both sides then stand for one number, and yet that the planets and the legs of a spider are equinumerous is news, learned by counting.

## Identity: a relation between objects, or between names?

The first puzzle is about one sentence form. Write '$a = b$' for any true identity between two different expressions and '$a = a$' for the same expression repeated. Three cases, all of them true:

- '$253 = 253$' and '$117 + 136 = 253$';
- 'Hesperus is Hesperus' and 'Hesperus is Phosphorus';
- 'Mark Twain is Mark Twain' and 'Mark Twain is Samuel Clemens'.

In each pair the first sentence can be known by looking at it. The second cannot: it takes an addition, years of watching the sky, or a biography. The difference is in the sentences' *cognitive value* (*Erkenntniswert*): what you learn when you come to accept the sentence. '$a = a$' holds a priori (<Ref lesson="frege-logicism">knowable without appeal to experience</Ref>); '$a = b$' often extends knowledge and often cannot be known a priori at all.

### First attempt: a relation between objects

The natural reading of '$=$' is the one you use in mathematics: '$a = b$' is true when $a$ and $b$ are one object, so identity is the relation every object bears to itself and to nothing else. Then say what 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' asserts. It asserts that a certain object, Venus, stands in the relation of identity to Venus. 'Hesperus is Hesperus' asserts that Venus stands in the relation of identity to Venus. If a sentence's content is fixed by the objects it is about and the relation it asserts between them, the two sentences have the same content, and the astronomers' discovery is the triviality that Venus is Venus.

### Second attempt: a relation between names

The concept-script of 1879 had taken the other way out. Its sign for *identity of content*, the ancestor of $=$ (<Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier" id="what-every-applies-to">one of its primitive signs</Ref>), stood between names, and '$a \equiv b$' said that the names '$a$' and '$b$' have the same content. On that reading 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is about two words, and it is no longer the same claim as 'Hesperus is Hesperus', which is about one.

In 1892 the concept-script's author published a 26-page essay that gives this reading up. Which object a sign stands for is a matter of convention: anyone may adopt any sound or mark as a sign for anything. If '$a = b$' said only that two signs had been attached to one object, it would report a fact about our way of designating things, and it would express no knowledge of the things themselves. 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' does express such knowledge; it is astronomy, not lexicography. A sentence about Greek usage would be confirmed by reading Greek texts, and this one was confirmed by observing the planet.

### What is left: the way the object is given

Each attempt puts the content of '$a = b$' in one place, and each place gives the wrong answer: the objects alone make the two sentences equal, the signs alone make the difference conventional. A difference in cognitive value has to come from something that is neither the object nor the sign, and the essay names it:

<Quote source="'Über Sinn und Bedeutung' (1892), p. 26, trans. for this course">
<Original lang="de">
Eine Verschiedenheit kann nur dadurch zu Stande kommen, daß der Unterschied des Zeichens einem Unterschiede in der Art des Gegebenseins des Bezeichneten entspricht.
</Original>

A difference can come about only if the difference between the signs corresponds to a difference in the way in which what they designate is given.
</Quote>

The essay's own illustration is geometric. In a triangle, let $a$, $b$ and $c$ be the three lines that join each corner to the midpoint of the opposite side. The three meet in one point, so 'the intersection of $a$ and $b$' and 'the intersection of $b$ and $c$' name the same point. Each name also says how the point is reached: as the place where these two lines cross. That is why 'the intersection of $a$ and $b$ is the intersection of $b$ and $c$' is a theorem you have to prove, and not a sentence you can check by reading it.

  caption="Two names, two constructions, one point. That the constructions end in the same place is a theorem, which is what makes the identity worth stating."

<KeyFact>
Two different signs are not enough to make '$a = b$' informative. 'The evening star is *der Abendstern*' joins an English sign and a German one that give Venus in the same way, and to anyone who understands both languages it tells no more than '$a = a$'.
</KeyFact>

## Substitution fails under 'believes that'

The second puzzle is about inference rather than knowledge, and it looks unrelated. Mathematics runs on a rule so familiar it is rarely stated: if $S(n)$ is a true sentence containing the name $n$, and $n = m$ is true, then $S(m)$, the sentence with $m$ put for $n$ in one or more places, is true as well. It is the logical form of *Leibniz's law*: what is identical has every property in common. Two instances:

$$
\frac{4 > 3 \qquad 4 = 8/2}{8/2 > 3}
\qquad\qquad
\frac{\text{Mark Twain was an author.} \qquad \text{Mark Twain} = \text{Samuel Clemens.}}{\text{Samuel Clemens was an author.}}
$$

Both are valid: whatever is true of the man under one name is true of him under the other.

### A case where the rule seems to fail

The case from the start needs sharpening. Someone who had never met the name 'Samuel Clemens' could hold no belief put in those words, true or false, so take John, who has heard both names, which is what lets the report with 'Samuel Clemens' be false rather than empty. He learned 'Mark Twain' from the title page of *Huckleberry Finn* and 'Samuel Clemens' from a survey of nineteenth-century Americans that never mentioned the pen name. Run the same rule on a report of what he believes.

<Figure
  src="./figures/substitution.svg"
  alt="Two arguments side by side. Left: Mark Twain was an author; Mark Twain = Samuel Clemens; therefore Samuel Clemens was an author, marked valid. Right: John believes that Mark Twain wrote Huckleberry Finn; Mark Twain = Samuel Clemens; therefore John believes that Samuel Clemens wrote Huckleberry Finn, with 'John believes that' highlighted and the argument marked invalid"
  caption="The same substitution in the same name, one phrase apart. On the left it preserves truth; under 'believes that' it does not."
/>

The premises on the right can be true and the conclusion false. Asked whether Samuel Clemens wrote *Huckleberry Finn*, John would say he has no idea; Clemens, for him, is a name from a list of Americans. So the argument is invalid, and an instance of the rule that holds everywhere in mathematics has a counterexample.

### Why 'belief contexts are exceptions' is not an answer

The first response is to restrict the rule: substitution holds except after 'believes that', 'hopes that', 'knows that' and their kin. That is true, and it is a description of the problem rather than a solution. It does not say what the belief report is about if not the man. On the standing-for theory the report 'John believes that Mark Twain wrote *Huckleberry Finn*' relates John to a man and a book. So does the report with 'Samuel Clemens' in it, and it relates him to the same man and the same book. On that theory the two reports say the same thing, and one cannot be true while the other is false.

That is the first puzzle again. Two sentences that differ only in co-referring names differ in what they say, once in what you learn from them ('$a = a$' against '$a = b$') and once in what John believes. Whatever separates 'Mark Twain' from 'Samuel Clemens' in the first case should separate them in the second.

## Sense: the second thing a name carries

What separates 'Mark Twain' from 'Samuel Clemens', if both stand for one man? The first section has already located such a difference in the way the object is given. The 1892 essay makes the way of being given a second thing every name has, besides the object it stands for:

<Definition title="sense and reference">
The *reference* (*Bedeutung*) of a name is the object it designates. Its *sense* (*Sinn*) is the way that object is given by it, its *mode of presentation*. A name *expresses* its sense and *refers to* its reference; the sense determines which object, if any, is the reference.
</Definition>

'Name' here covers every expression that designates a single object, a complex one included: 'the intersection of $a$ and $b$', '$8/2$' and 'the evening star' count as names as much as 'Venus' does. The word *Bedeutung* has been rendered three ways in English, as 'meaning', 'nominatum' and 'denotation'; all three translate the one word the definition calls reference.

### The puzzle of identity solved

With two values per name, '$a = b$' and '$a = a$' can share one and differ in the other.

<Example title="'Hesperus is Phosphorus' against 'Hesperus is Hesperus'">
'Hesperus' expresses the sense *the bright body seen in the west after sunset*; 'Phosphorus' expresses *the bright body seen in the east before sunrise*. Both senses present Venus, so both names refer to Venus, and both identity sentences are true.

To grasp 'Hesperus is Hesperus' you need one sense, used twice, and grasping it already tells you that the two sides present one object. To grasp 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' you need two different senses, and nothing in either sense tells you that what it presents is what the other presents. That further fact is about the world. It was established by the observations of the cycle described at the start, and it is what the sentence tells you.

The arithmetic case runs the same way. '$4$' and '$8/2$' both refer to the number four. '$8/2$' presents it as the result of dividing eight by two, '$4$' as the numeral says; someone who understands both expressions can still have to calculate to see that they present one number.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/two-routes.svg"
  alt="The name 'Hesperus' in amber with an arrow 'expresses' to a box showing an evening sky, the setting Sun on the horizon and a bright point above it in the west; the name 'Phosphorus' in blue with an arrow to a box showing a morning sky in the east before sunrise; arrows labelled 'presents' from both boxes converge on a single circle labelled 'Venus: one reference'"
  caption="Two senses, one reference. The identity is informative because the two routes are different and their meeting point is a fact about the sky."
/>

The relation between the three levels is regular in one direction and loose in the other. In a well-made notation each sign has one sense and each sense one reference. The converse fails twice. One object has many senses presenting it: Venus is the evening star, the morning star, the second planet from the Sun. And one sense can be expressed by many signs, as a word and its exact translation express one sense in two languages.

A sense also lights up its reference from one side only. Grasping the sense of 'the evening star' is not knowing everything about Venus; complete knowledge of an object would include being able to say, of every given sense, whether it presents that object. Nobody gets there, and it is because senses are partial that identities between them can be discovered.

### A sense is not an idea in someone's head

A mode of presentation sounds psychological: the way a thing appears to me. The essay rules that reading out, and the reason is the use the distinction was built for. The *idea* (*Vorstellung*) a word calls up is a private inner image, made from memories of sensations, coloured by feeling, and different from one person to the next. A painter, a rider and a zoologist each attach their own idea to the name 'Bucephalus', Alexander the Great's horse. If the sense of a word were such an idea, no two people would ever understand a sentence the same way, nobody could contradict anybody, and a theorem proved by one mathematician would not be the theorem another read. The sense of a sign, by contrast, can be the common property of many: it is what passes from speaker to hearer when a sentence is understood.

The essay's analogy is a telescope pointed at the Moon. The Moon is the reference. The real image that the objective lens forms inside the tube is the sense: it shows the Moon from one side, it depends on where the telescope stands, but it is objective, since several observers can look at the same image. The image on each observer's retina is the idea, and each has his own.

<Figure
  src="./figures/telescope.svg"
  alt="The Moon on the left labelled 'reference'; a telescope tube in the middle with its objective lens and, inside it, a small amber circle labelled 'the image inside the telescope: sense', annotated as one-sided, dependent on where the telescope stands, yet the same for every observer; on the right an eye with a blue dot on its retina labelled 'the retinal image: idea, each observer's own'"
  caption="Reference, sense and idea. The sense is partial like the idea and public like the object."
/>

The sense of an everyday proper name is less fixed than that of 'the evening star'. A footnote of the essay concedes that for a name like 'Aristotle', one speaker may take the sense to be *the pupil of Plato and teacher of Alexander the Great* and another *the teacher of Alexander the Great who was born in Stagira*; these fluctuations are tolerable as long as the reference stays the same, and they should not occur in a perfect notation. The footnote gives the sense of a name through a description, but the theory does not require that a speaker carry one in mind. Treating every name as a disguised description is a later and stronger doctrine, <Ref lesson="kripke-against-descriptivism">the description theory of names</Ref>.

### Sense without reference

Because the sense determines the reference rather than the other way round, a sense need not succeed in determining anything. 'The least rapidly convergent series' has a perfectly clear sense, and it can be proved that nothing answers to it: for every convergent series there is one that converges more slowly and still converges (an exercise at the end constructs it). 'Odysseus' has a sense that every reader of the *Odyssey* grasps, and whether it has a reference is at best doubtful.

<Figure
  src="./figures/no-reference.svg"
  alt="Three rows, each running from an expression to an amber sense bubble to a dashed empty circle in the reference column: 'Odysseus' grasped by every reader, reference marked '?'; 'the least rapidly convergent series', fully understood, reference 'none'; 'the present King of France', fully understood, reference 'none'"
  caption="Each expression has a sense that a reader grasps. 'Odysseus' may lack a reference, the series provably lacks one, and France has no king."
/>

The first puzzle needed two values per name because one value made two different sentences equal. This case needs them for the opposite reason: with only the object as a value, an expression with no object would have no value at all, and 'the least rapidly convergent series' would be meaningless. It is not; you understood it well enough to follow a proof that nothing answers to it.

## Sentences: a thought and a truth-value

So far only names have two values. But the puzzles are about sentences: 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' tells you something 'Hesperus is Hesperus' does not, and a sentence is what John believes. If sense and reference are to explain that, the distinction has to reach every part of a sentence and the sentence itself.

### The thought is the sentence's sense

A declarative sentence *expresses a thought*: by 'thought' the essay means not the act of thinking but its objective content, what two people both grasp when they understand the same sentence. Is the thought the sentence's sense or its reference? Take 'the morning star is a body illuminated by the Sun' and put 'the evening star' for 'the morning star', a name with the same reference and a different sense. That cannot change the reference of the sentence, if the reference of a whole is fixed by the references of its parts. It does change the thought:

<Quote source="'Über Sinn und Bedeutung' (1892), p. 32, trans. for this course">
<Original lang="de">
Jemand der nicht wüßte, daß der Abendstern der Morgenstern ist, könnte den einen Gedanken für wahr, den andern für falsch halten.
</Original>

Someone who did not know that the evening star is the morning star could hold the one thought true and the other false.
</Quote>

Since swapping co-referring names changes the thought, the thought is not the sentence's reference. It is its sense.

### The truth-value is the sentence's reference

Then what does a sentence refer to? Start from a sentence whose part may refer to nothing. 'Odysseus was put ashore at Ithaca while sound asleep' has a sense, a thought every reader grasps. Since 'Odysseus' may refer to nothing, and the reference of a whole depends on the references of its parts, the sentence may lack a reference too. Reading the *Odyssey* as a poem, nobody cares whether it does; the thought and the images it calls up are enough. The reference of 'Odysseus' starts to matter only once someone asks whether the sentence is true, and then it matters entirely, because a property can be truly ascribed only to something. So what a sentence's reference adds to its sense is exactly what we look for when we ask about truth.

<Quote source="'Über Sinn und Bedeutung' (1892), p. 33, trans. for this course">
<Original lang="de">
Das Streben nach Wahrheit also ist es, was uns überall vom Sinne zur Bedeutung vorzudringen treibt.
</Original>

It is the striving for truth, then, that everywhere drives us to advance from the sense to the reference.
</Quote>

That suggests the reference of a declarative sentence is its *truth-value*, the True or the False, and the suggestion can be tested. If it is right, the truth-value must stay fixed whenever a part is replaced by an expression with the same reference and a different sense. It does: 'the morning star is a body illuminated by the Sun' and 'the evening star is a body illuminated by the Sun' are both true, and Leibniz's formula, *eadem sunt quae sibi mutuo substitui possunt salva veritate* (the same are those things that can be substituted for each other while preserving truth), makes that preservation the mark of identity itself.

The essay then argues by elimination: what else could the reference be, if it has to belong to every sentence whose parts have references and stay fixed under every such replacement? The question is a challenge, not a proof. Other things survive the swap too, the grammatical form of the sentence for one, but they are features of the sign rather than something the sentence stands for, and the essay finds no candidate of the second kind besides the truth-value. The positive case for the truth-value is the Odysseus argument; the substitution test shows only that the candidate passes.

The function–argument analysis already treated a true sentence as a name of the True, <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier" id="concepts-functions-whose-values-are-truth-values">at a price</Ref>: all true sentences then have one reference, so at the level of reference everything particular about a sentence is erased. Here the doctrine is argued for rather than stipulated, and the other level pays the price: what a sentence says lives in its sense. There are two truth-values and infinitely many thoughts.

### Two values for every part, composed in parallel

The rest of the sentence follows the same pattern. A predicate such as '$(\ )$ loves Mary' refers to a concept, a function from objects to truth-values, exactly as in the function–argument analysis, and it has a sense: a way that concept is given. Write $d[\ ]$ for the reference of an expression and $s[\ ]$ for its sense, with $j$, $m$ and $L$ for 'John', 'Mary' and 'loves'.

<Example title="the two values of 'John loves Mary'">
**Reference.** $d[j]$ is John and $d[m]$ is Mary. $d[L]$ is a function which, applied to Mary, yields the concept '$(\ )$ loves Mary', which is $d[Lm]$. Applied to John, $d[Lm]$ yields $d[jLm]$: the True if John loves Mary, the False if not.

**Sense.** $s[j]$ and $s[m]$ are the ways John and Mary are given by their names. $s[L]$ combines with $s[m]$ to give $s[Lm]$, the sense of 'loves Mary', and that combines with $s[j]$ to give $s[jLm]$, the thought that John loves Mary.

**The link.** Each sense determines the reference beside it: $s[j]$ determines John, and $s[jLm]$, the thought, determines the True or the False, depending on how things stand with John and Mary.
</Example>

  src="./figures/tree.svg"
  alt="Two trees side by side over the words 'John', 'loves', 'Mary'. The amber sense tree has leaves s[j], s[L], s[m], a middle node s[Lm] and a root s[jLm] labelled 'a thought'. The blue reference tree has leaves John, ( ) loves [ ], Mary, a middle node d[Lm] labelled 'concept ( ) loves Mary', and a root d[jLm] labelled 'the True'. A dashed arrow labelled 'determines' runs from the sense root to the reference root"
  caption="One sentence, two compositions: senses combine into a thought, references into a truth-value, and the thought determines the truth-value."
/>

<Remark title="compositionality">
The reference of a complex expression is a function of the references of its parts, and its sense is a function of the senses of its parts. The first half is what licenses substitution of co-referring parts; the second is what lets a reader who has never met a sentence understand it, by understanding its words and their arrangement.
</Remark>

With identities between names already explained, the sentence level adds where cognitive value sits: in the thought. '$4 = 4$' and '$4 = 8/2$' share their truth-value and differ in their thoughts, because $s[8/2] \neq s[4]$. A thought alone is not yet knowledge, though. A *judgement*, in the essay's terms, is the advance from a thought to its truth-value, and the advance from the thought that $4 = 8/2$ to the True takes a division that the advance from the thought that $4 = 4$ does not.

## Indirect reference: what 'believes that' is about

The new theory makes the second puzzle look worse before it makes it better. If a sentence refers to its truth-value and references compose, then in 'John believes that Mark Twain wrote *Huckleberry Finn*' the embedded sentence should be replaceable by any other true sentence. 'John believes that $2 + 2 = 4$' would follow, and so would 'John believes that Venus is a planet', whatever John's views on astronomy. That is absurd; the embedded sentence cannot refer to its truth-value there.

### Words that are about words, and words that are about senses

There is already one context in which words plainly do not refer to their usual objects: quotation. In 'John said "Mark Twain wrote *Huckleberry Finn*"' the quoted words refer to words, the ones John uttered, and putting 'Samuel Clemens' inside the quotation marks would misreport him even though the man is the same. The essay treats reported speech the same way, one level up. In *indirect speech*, 'John said that…' or 'John believes that…', we talk about the sense of someone's words, and the words in the that-clause refer to what is ordinarily their sense.

<Quote source="'Über Sinn und Bedeutung' (1892), p. 28, trans. for this course">
<Original lang="de">
Die ungerade Bedeutung eines Wortes ist also sein gewöhnlicher Sinn.
</Original>

The indirect reference of a word is accordingly its customary sense.
</Quote>

So after 'believes that', 'Mark Twain' refers to the sense of 'Mark Twain', and the whole that-clause refers to a thought. 'Believes' is a relation between a person and a thought; 'John believes that Mark Twain wrote *Huckleberry Finn*' is true when John stands in that relation to the thought that Mark Twain wrote it.

### The puzzle of substitution solved

Run the invalid argument once more. The premise 'Mark Twain = Samuel Clemens' says that two names have the same customary reference. In the conclusion, the two names stand after 'believes that', where each refers to its customary sense. The senses differ: John got one name from a title page and the other from a survey, and nothing in either tells him they present one man. So the conclusion does not replace an expression by one with the same reference in that position, and the rule of substitution, applied correctly, does not license it. Substitution of co-referring expressions holds without exception; what fails is the assumption that a word refers to the same thing in every position.

A mathematician will recognise the move as a type-shift. The function 'John believes that $(\ )$' does not take truth-values as arguments; it takes thoughts, and an expression placed in its argument place is read at the type of its sense. It is the same move as quotation one level down.

<KeyFact>
Making reference depend on position does not stop at one level. In 'Mary believes that John believes that Mark Twain wrote *Huckleberry Finn*' the innermost name is in indirect position twice, so it refers to the sense its customary sense has, and every further 'believes that' calls for one more level: a hierarchy of senses of senses, which the 1892 essay does not explore.
</KeyFact>

## What came of the distinction

Which parts of the theory held? The concept-script had given logic a systematic theory of inference; the 1892 essay added a theory of meaning in which every expression has two values. Its claims could be attacked one at a time, and they fared differently.

### The empty reference, and a rival

A sentence containing 'Odysseus' may lack a truth-value, and the essay calls that a defect of natural language. A notation fit for science must ensure that every name formed by its rules refers to something, and the essay proposes, for divergent series in analysis, that they be stipulated to refer to $0$. Applied to 'the present King of France', the same policy assigns some conventional object by fiat. In 1905 the English logician whose letter ended <Ref lesson="frege-logicism">the logicist programme</Ref> rejected the stipulation as artificial:

<Quote source="B. Russell, 'On Denoting', Mind 14 (1905)">
But this procedure, though it may not lead to actual logical error, is plainly artificial, and does not give an exact analysis of the matter.
</Quote>

The objection by itself leaves senses standing; it attacks only the cure for empty names. What removes senses is his alternative, <Ref lesson="russell-theory-of-descriptions">the theory of descriptions</Ref>. It holds that a phrase like 'the King of France' is not a name at all, so it needs no reference, stipulated or otherwise. It then reads an ordinary proper name such as 'Hesperus' as a description in disguise, roughly 'the star seen at evening', so 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' stops being an identity between two names and becomes a quantified claim about whatever fits each description. That answers both puzzles of this lesson without senses (the last exercise but one shows the quantified form for 'Scott is the author of *Waverley*').

### The sense of a name

The 'Aristotle' footnote let the sense of a proper name be given by a description, and a later generation hardened that into a thesis: a name means some description, or a cluster of them, and refers to whatever satisfies it. Sense, on that thesis, does two jobs at once: it is what a speaker grasps in understanding the name, and it is what decides which object the name refers to. The two can come apart, because a speaker can use a name for the right person while the only description he attaches to it is wrong, or fits someone else. In 1970 the thesis was attacked on exactly that ground (<Ref lesson="kripke-against-descriptivism">the case against description theories of names</Ref>).

### Meaning is not reference

If the rival does without senses and the description thesis falls, does anything of the 1892 theory survive? Go back to what forced it: the pairs of sentences that standing-for cannot tell apart. So every theory of meaning that comes after has to keep the bare distinction: two expressions can have the same reference and differ in meaning. The theory of descriptions keeps it by making 'Scott' and 'the author of *Waverley*' enter the sentence in different ways, though both concern one man; even <Ref lesson="two-dogmas-analyticity">a view that rejects meanings as entities altogether</Ref> keeps it, and argues for it with the evening star and the morning star.

<Remark title="how an analysis can be correct and informative">
An *analysis*, in the sense philosophers use the word, states what a concept consists in. <Ref lesson="frege-logicism" id="the-same-number-without-counting">Hume's principle</Ref> analyses '$F$ and $G$ have the same number' as '$F \approx G$', there is a relation pairing the $F$s one to one with the $G$s. That raises a worry. If the analysis is correct, the two expressions mean the same, and it is trivial; if they do not mean the same, it is incorrect. Read with two values, correctness needs only that the references agree: for every pair of concepts the two expressions yield the same truth-value. The analysis is informative because the senses differ: 'the same number' presents the relation as agreement in the results of counting, '$F \approx G$' as the existence of a pairing, and that the two always agree is something about number you have to be shown, not something you see by understanding both phrases.
</Remark>

<Conclusion>
You can now say what else a name carries besides its object: a sense, the way the object is given, which is objective and determines the reference without being determined by it. With it, 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' and 'Hesperus is Hesperus' share their reference, the True, and differ in the thought they express, which is where what a sentence tells you lives. Every expression has the two values: a predicate refers to a concept, a sentence to a truth-value, and both levels compose. Under 'believes that' a word refers to its customary sense, so the argument from Twain to Clemens fails without any exception to the rule of substitution.

The theory leaves expressions with a sense and no reference, and sentences containing them with no truth-value, and the only cure it offers is a stipulated reference. <Ref lesson="russell-theory-of-descriptions">The next module</Ref> starts from the opposite claim, that 'the present King of France' does not refer even in a perfect notation because it is not a name, and asks what logical form such a phrase has instead.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Give the sense and the reference of each of '$8/2$', '$4$', '$(\ ) > 3$', '$8/2 = 4$' and '$4 = 4$'. Which component makes '$8/2 = 4$' informative to a child who is learning division, and which component do '$8/2 = 4$' and '$4 = 4$' share?

<Solution>
- '$8/2$': reference, the number four; sense, four given as the result of dividing eight by two.
- '$4$': reference, the number four; sense, four given by its numeral.
- '$(\ ) > 3$': reference, the concept that maps every number larger than three to the True and everything else to the False; sense, that concept given as 'greater than three'.
- '$8/2 = 4$': reference, the True; sense, the thought that the result of dividing eight by two is four.
- '$4 = 4$': reference, the True; sense, the thought that four is four.

The two identities share their reference, the True. What the child learns is carried by the thought expressed by '$8/2 = 4$', composed from the sense of '$8/2$', which differs from the sense of '$4$'. Grasping both senses does not reveal that they present one number; the child has to divide, and the division is the step from that thought to its truth-value.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "The sense of a name is just the mental image a speaker associates with it." Give the reason two speakers with different images can grasp the same sense, and say what would go wrong for mathematics if senses were images.

<Solution>
**The claim confuses sense with idea.** An idea is private: it is built from one person's memories and feelings, it varies between persons and over time, and no two people can have the same one, since nobody can compare two people's inner images in one consciousness. The painter, the rider and the zoologist attach different ideas to 'Bucephalus' and still understand one another when one says 'Bucephalus was black'. What they share is the sense: the way the horse is given by the name, the common property of everyone who understands it. In the telescope analogy, they look at one image in the tube with different retinas.

**What would go wrong for mathematics.** If the sense of '$\sqrt 2$ is irrational' were each reader's image, the theorem one mathematician proved would be a thought no one else could have; disagreement and agreement about it would be impossible, since there would be no common thought to accept or reject. A proof would then establish something only about its author's mind. The objectivity of the thought is what lets a proof be checked by someone else.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Prove the claim behind 'the least rapidly convergent series' having no reference. Let $\sum a_n$ be a convergent series of positive terms with tails $r_n = \sum_{k \ge n} a_k$. Show that $\sum a_n/\sqrt{r_n}$ converges, and that it converges more slowly, in the sense that its tails are eventually larger than any constant multiple of $r_n$. Then say what this shows about the sense and the reference of the phrase.

<Solution>
**Convergence.** The tails decrease to $0$ and $a_n = r_n - r_{n+1}$. Factor:

$$
\frac{a_n}{\sqrt{r_n}} = \frac{(\sqrt{r_n} - \sqrt{r_{n+1}})(\sqrt{r_n} + \sqrt{r_{n+1}})}{\sqrt{r_n}} \le 2\,(\sqrt{r_n} - \sqrt{r_{n+1}}),
$$

since $\sqrt{r_{n+1}} \le \sqrt{r_n}$. The right side telescopes: $\sum_{n=1}^{N} 2(\sqrt{r_n} - \sqrt{r_{n+1}}) = 2(\sqrt{r_1} - \sqrt{r_{N+1}}) \le 2\sqrt{r_1}$. The partial sums of the positive series $\sum a_n/\sqrt{r_n}$ are bounded, so it converges.

**Slower.** For $k \ge n$, $r_k \le r_n$, so $a_k/\sqrt{r_k} \ge a_k/\sqrt{r_n}$. The tail of the new series from $n$ on is therefore at least $\sum_{k \ge n} a_k/\sqrt{r_n} = r_n/\sqrt{r_n} = \sqrt{r_n}$. Since $r_n \to 0$, $\sqrt{r_n}/r_n = 1/\sqrt{r_n} \to \infty$: the new tails eventually exceed $C r_n$ for every constant $C$.

**What it shows.** Whatever convergent series is proposed as the least rapidly convergent, the construction yields one that converges more slowly, so the description fits nothing. The phrase still has a sense: you used it to state what was to be proved, and the proof is a proof about what that sense requires. A sense determines a reference only if something answers to it, and here it is provable that nothing does.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
'The present King of France is bald' has a sense. On the theory of this lesson, what is its reference, and what happens at the corresponding node of the composition tree? The 1892 essay adds that whoever asserts 'Kepler died in misery' presupposes, without asserting, that 'Kepler' refers. Use that to say what the negation 'the present King of France is not bald' asserts, and state what a logician loses if a sentence may lack a truth-value. Finally, what did the essay propose for a scientific notation, and why does it remove the gap?

<Solution>
**The tree.** The name has a sense and no reference, so the leaf for 'the present King of France' is empty on the reference side. The concept '$(\ )$ is bald' needs an object as argument to yield a value, and with nothing in its argument place the root on the reference side is empty too: the sentence has no truth-value. On the sense side nothing is missing; it expresses a thought.

**The negation.** If the existence of the king were part of what the sentence asserts, the negation would be 'the present King of France is not bald, or there is no such king', which is true. It is presupposition instead: 'Kepler died in misery' and 'Kepler did not die in misery' both presuppose that 'Kepler' refers, and neither asserts it. So 'the present King of France is not bald' also presupposes that the name refers, and it too lacks a truth-value. Both the sentence and its negation are neither true nor false.

**What is lost.** The law that every sentence is true or false fails, and with it the excluded middle, $p \vee \neg p$, as a law for every sentence. Truth-tables have rows for two values only; a sentence with no value cannot be fed to $\neg$ or $\vee$. Proof by contradiction assumes that refuting $\neg p$ establishes $p$, which presupposes that $p$ has a value.

**The proposal.** A logically perfect notation must ensure that every expression formed by its rules as a name refers to something, by stipulation where necessary, as with divergent series stipulated to refer to $0$. Then every name has a reference, so every sentence has a truth-value, and the gap closes. The price is that the stipulated reference is arbitrary, which is the objection <Ref lesson="russell-theory-of-descriptions">the theory of descriptions</Ref> starts from.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Using the levels of <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier" id="what-every-applies-to">the function–argument analysis</Ref>, explain why the sentence 'the concept *horse* is not a concept' is a problem for the theory of this lesson and that one together. Hint: ask what kind of expression 'the concept *horse*' is, and what kind of thing it must therefore refer to. State the problem in two sentences; do not solve it.

<Solution>
'The concept *horse*' is a complete expression with no gap, a name in the sense of this lesson, so by the theory it refers to an object; but a concept is unsaturated, a function with a gap, and the earlier lesson made object and concept differ in kind, so whatever 'the concept *horse*' refers to is not a concept. The theory therefore cannot say of a concept, using a name, that it is a concept, and since the theory has to talk about concepts to be stated at all, its own statements ('the reference of a predicate is a concept') come out false or have to be read as something other than what they say.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
'Scott is the author of *Waverley*' is true and informative; in 1814 the novel *Waverley* appeared anonymously, and George IV, then Prince Regent, wanted to know whether Scott had written it. (a) Explain the informativeness of 'Scott is the author of *Waverley*' with sense and reference. (b) Explain with indirect reference why 'George IV wondered whether Scott was the author of *Waverley*' is true while 'George IV wondered whether Scott was Scott' is false. (c) The explanation in (a) requires that the phrase 'the author of *Waverley*' has a sense and a reference of its own, just as 'Scott' does. What would it take to say instead that the phrase does not refer to anything, even when someone wrote the book, and how would (a) then have to go?

<Solution>
**(a)** 'Scott' and 'the author of *Waverley*' refer to the same man and present him differently: by his name, and as whoever wrote this novel. The identity is true because the references coincide and informative because nothing in either sense tells you they present one man.

**(b)** After 'wondered whether', each expression refers to its customary sense. The first report relates the king to the thought composed of the sense of 'Scott' and the sense of 'the author of *Waverley*'; the second relates him to the thought that Scott is Scott. These are different thoughts, so the first report can be true and the second false, and replacing 'the author of *Waverley*' by 'Scott' in that position is not substitution of co-referring expressions.

**(c)** The phrase would have to be treated as something other than a name: an expression that contributes to the sentence without picking out an object, as 'every' and 'some' contribute without naming anything. Then 'Scott is the author of *Waverley*' would not be an identity between two names. It would be a quantified claim, roughly that someone wrote *Waverley*, nobody else did, and that person is Scott, and its informativeness would come from what the quantified claim says rather than from a second sense of a name. <Ref lesson="russell-theory-of-descriptions">The theory of descriptions</Ref> works out exactly this analysis, $\exists x\,(Wx \wedge \forall y\,(Wy \to y = x) \wedge x = s)$ with $Wx$ for '$x$ wrote *Waverley*' and $s$ for Scott, and, reading 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' as disguised descriptions of the same kind, uses it to answer both puzzles of this lesson without senses.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The identity of the evening star with the morning star is ancient. Find what the earliest surviving record of it is and roughly when it dates from, describe what observations establish the identity, and use your answer to settle, against the 1879 reading of '$=$', whether 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' is about the planet or about its names.

<Solution>
**The record.** The best-known early source is the Venus tablet of Ammisaduqa, part of a Babylonian omen series: it lists the dates on which Venus first and last became visible in the east and in the west over about twenty-one years. The surviving copies are from the first millennium BCE, and the observations are usually dated to the first half of the second millennium BCE. The tablet treats the western and the eastern appearances as phases of one planet. Greek writers later credited the identification to Pythagoras or Parmenides.

**The observations.** Venus orbits inside the Earth's orbit, so seen from the Earth it never strays more than about $47°$ from the Sun and is visible only in the hours after sunset or before sunrise. Followed over months, the evening star climbs away from the Sun, turns back and sinks into the Sun's glare; days or a few weeks later the morning star appears on the other side of the Sun and climbs away from it; much later it sinks back and the evening star reappears. The whole cycle repeats every 584 days or so. The identity is the hypothesis that turns two separate tables of appearances into one continuous track.

**What the sentence is about.** Every step of that evidence concerns the planet: positions, dates, angular distances from the Sun. None concerns the Greek words. Had '$=$' stood for a relation between names, the claim would have been confirmed by consulting how Greeks used 'Hesperos' and 'Phosphoros', and this one was confirmed by watching the sky. So the sentence is about the object, and its informativeness has to be explained by the different ways the object is given, which is the 1892 conclusion.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

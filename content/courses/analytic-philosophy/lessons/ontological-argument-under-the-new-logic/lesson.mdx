A medieval argument claims to prove that a perfect being exists from the meaning of 'perfect' alone: a being that lacked existence would be less than perfect, so the most perfect being cannot lack it. Something is wrong, since the same form proves a perfect island and a scariest devil; but for eight centuries nobody could say which step fails without disputing a premise. The analysis of 'exists' as a property of concepts rather than of things is a tool built for exactly this; the task is to run it on the argument and see what it shows.

The question is a logician's, not a theologian's. A false conclusion can be reached validly from a false premise, and a true conclusion can be reached by an invalid argument, so nothing below bears on whether God exists. What is at stake is whether this argument shows it, and if not, at which line it breaks.

The tool comes from <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier" id="existence-is-a-property-of-concepts">the analysis of quantifiers</Ref>: '$F$s exist' says that the concept $F$ is instantiated, $\exists x\,Fx$, so existence sits on the second level, as a property of concepts, and the only first-level 'exists' available, $\exists y\,(y = a)$, is true of every object. That lesson ended with the claim that any argument treating existence as a property a thing can have or lack has a malformed premise. A claim like that is cheap until it is run on a real argument, one that went those eight centuries without a located fault and is still defended today.

## The argument, as its two classical authors gave it

The argument has two classical statements, five and a half centuries apart, and they are not the same argument. The diagnosis has to fit both.

### Anselm: a reductio from a description

Anselm was a monk at the abbey of Bec in Normandy, later archbishop of Canterbury. Around 1078 he wrote the *Proslogion*, a short treatise addressed to God, whose second chapter answers the psalm's "fool" who "has said in his heart, there is no God":

<Quote source="Anselm, Proslogion II (c. 1078), trans. S. N. Deane (1903)">
Hence, even the fool is convinced that something exists in the understanding, at least, than which nothing greater can be conceived. For, when he hears of this, he understands it. And whatever is understood, exists in the understanding. And assuredly that, than which nothing greater can be conceived, cannot exist in the understanding alone. For, suppose it exists in the understanding alone: then it can be conceived to exist in reality; which is greater.
</Quote>

Write $G$ for 'that than which nothing greater can be conceived'. The argument is a proof by contradiction in five moves:

1. **Definition.** $G$ is that than which nothing greater can be conceived.
2. **Understood.** The fool understands the phrase, so $G$ exists in the understanding.
3. **Supposition.** $G$ exists in the understanding alone, not in reality.
4. **Premise.** Existing in reality as well as in the understanding is greater than existing in the understanding alone; so something greater than $G$ can be conceived, namely $G$ existing in reality.
5. **Contradiction.** Then $G$ is something than which a greater can be conceived, against 1. So the supposition is false: $G$ exists in reality.

### Descartes: existence in the essence

The second statement is in the fifth of René Descartes's *Meditations on First Philosophy*, published in Paris in 1641. Descartes does not argue by contradiction. He compares God's existence with a property a geometer proves of the triangle:

<Quote source="Descartes, Meditations V §8 (1641), trans. J. Veitch (1901)">
the existence can no more be separated from the essence of God, than the idea of a mountain from that of a valley, or the equality of its three angles to two right angles, from the essence of a [rectilinear] triangle; so that it is not less impossible to conceive a God, that is, a being supremely perfect, to whom existence is awanting, or who is devoid of a certain perfection, than to conceive a mountain without a valley.
</Quote>

In three lines:

1. **Definition.** God is a supremely perfect being: a being with every perfection.
2. **Premise.** Existence is a perfection.
3. **Conclusion.** Existence belongs to God's essence, as the angle sum belongs to the triangle's; so God exists.

### What the two share

The routes differ (one a reductio about greatness, the other a direct inference from an essence), but the skeleton is the same. A concept is fixed by a description. Existence is counted among the things that make a being great or perfect, which is to say among the respects the description is about. And the conclusion is that something answers to the description.

Laid out side by side, that skeleton has four moves. Anselm's supposition (move 3) is folded into his premise, since it only sets up the comparison the premise makes, and his contradiction is stated directly as the conclusion it yields; Descartes's three lines are the same four without the step 'understood'.

## The parodies: the same form proves too much

The first reply came within a few years. Gaunilo, a monk of Marmoutier near Tours, wrote a short defence "in behalf of the fool", and its sixth section runs Anselm's moves on an island:

<Quote source="Gaunilo, In Behalf of the Fool §6, trans. S. N. Deane (1903)">
"You can no longer doubt that this island which is more excellent than all lands exists somewhere, since you have no doubt that it is in your understanding. And since it is more excellent not to be in the understanding alone, but to exist both in the understanding and in reality, for this reason it must exist."
</Quote>

A modern version replaces the island with a devil. The devil is the scariest thing there could be; a devil that exists is scarier than one that does not, since nobody is afraid of a devil that is not there; so the scariest devil exists.

Nobody accepts either conclusion, and that is the evidence. If the form were valid and its premises true by definition, the island and the devil would exist. So the form is invalid, or a premise every version shares is false.

<KeyFact>
A parody shows that an argument proves too much; it does not say where it goes wrong. A diagnosis must name one step that fails in the original and in every parody, for one reason.
</KeyFact>

## Two answers that do not locate the fault

The obvious way to name the step is to deny the premise that existing is greater or more perfect than not existing. That is a counter-assertion, and Anselm has as much right to assert the premise as his critic has to deny it. It does not explain why the premise seemed true, and the devil parody shows that it does seem true: a real devil is scarier than an imaginary one. Disagreeing with a premise is not refuting an argument; refuting it means showing that the premise, properly stated, does not say what the argument needs.

### Kant's hundred thalers

The first attempt at that came in 1781, in Immanuel Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason*, which <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier" id="there-is-no-useful-first-level-exists">the analysis of quantifiers</Ref> already recalled for its slogan that being is "not a real predicate". A *real* predicate, in Kant's sense, is one that adds something to the description of a thing. His test case was money; a thaler was the silver coin of the German states.

<Quote source="Kant, Critique of Pure Reason A599/B627 (1781/1787), trans. J. M. D. Meiklejohn">
<Original lang="de">
Und so enthält das Wirkliche nichts mehr als das bloß Mögliche. Hundert wirkliche Thaler enthalten nicht das Mindeste mehr, als hundert mögliche.
</Original>

Thus the real contains no more than the possible. A hundred real dollars contain no more than a hundred possible dollars.
</Quote>

  caption="Whether the coins exist is not a fact about the concept of them."

The point is about content. If 'a hundred real thalers' described more than 'a hundred possible thalers', then when the coins turned up in your purse, what turned up would not be what you had thought of. So 'exists' adds nothing to a concept, and it cannot be one of the perfections a concept of God contains.

Kant's answer stops short of a diagnosis in one respect: it says what existence is not and gives no account of what it is. Kant calls it "the positing of a thing", which describes an act of judging, not a place in a logical form. That leaves the defender a reply: ordinary existence adds nothing to ordinary concepts, but in exactly one concept, the concept of a supremely perfect being, existence is contained, and Descartes said as much when he wrote that he could "conceive no other being, except God, to whose essence existence [necessarily] pertains". Kant answered that every existential proposition is <Ref lesson="frege-logicism">synthetic</Ref>, true or false according to how the world is rather than by what its terms contain; the defender denies it for one case. The result is again assertion against assertion. What would end it is a notation in which the premise either can be written down and inspected, or demonstrably cannot.

## Running the argument in the concept-script

The concept-script of 1879 is such a notation, and existence already has its place in it, the second-level concept recalled at the start. What remains is to say what a concept "contains", and to try to put existence there.

### Marks and properties of a concept

Take a concept defined from others. With $Ox$ for '$x$ is omnipotent', $Wx$ for '$x$ is omniscient' and $Bx$ for '$x$ is benevolent', the concept 'perfect being' is

$$
D(x) \;:\leftrightarrow\; Ox \wedge Wx \wedge Bx.
$$

$O$, $W$ and $B$ are the *marks* of $D$: the first-level concepts it is built from, each of which every object falling under $D$ must fall under. What the medieval and early modern writers called the essence of a being is, in this vocabulary, the list of marks of its concept. A mark is a property of the objects under a concept; it is not a property of the concept itself. The 1884 book on arithmetic drew the line, and drew the conclusion:

<Quote source="Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik (1884) §53, trans. J. L. Austin">
By properties which are asserted of a concept I naturally do not mean the characteristics which make up the concept. These latter are properties of the things which fall under the concept, not of the concept. […] Because existence is a property of concepts the ontological argument for the existence of God breaks down.
</Quote>

The translation's "characteristics" are marks. Being right-angled is a mark of the concept 'right-angled triangle', a property of each triangle under it. Having no instance is a property of the concept 'right-angled equilateral triangle', and of no triangle, since there is none to have it.

### Writing 'existence is a perfection'

Descartes's premise says that existence is one of the marks of $D$. Try to write that down.

<Example title="three attempts to make existence a mark of 'perfect being'">
**Attempt 1: the second-level concept itself.** Existence is 'some object falls under $(\ )$', and its argument place takes a concept. A mark is a first-level concept, whose argument place takes an object. Putting the quantifier where a mark goes would mean giving $\exists$ the object variable $x$ as its argument, as $O$ has in $Ox$: a concept of concepts applied to an object. The concept-script has no such formula, as arithmetic has no expression applying the number $3$ to the number $5$. The premise, read this way, cannot be written.

**Attempt 2: the existence claim as a conjunct.** Add the sentence 'something is $D$' to the definition:

$$
D^{+}(x) \;:\leftrightarrow\; Ox \wedge Wx \wedge Bx \wedge \exists y\,(Oy \wedge Wy \wedge By).
$$

This is well formed, but the last conjunct has no free $x$: it says nothing about the object $x$, so it is not a mark of anything. Compute what it does to the conclusion. If something is $D^{+}$ then something is $D$; conversely, if some $a$ is $D$, then the conjunct is true (witness $a$), so $a$ is $D^{+}$. Hence

$$
\exists x\, D^{+}(x) \;\leftrightarrow\; \exists x\, D(x).
$$

The concept with the existence clause written into it is instantiated exactly when the concept without it is. This is the hundred thalers as a theorem: adding 'exists' to the content changes nothing about whether the content is realised.

**Attempt 3: the first-level 'exists'.** Use $E(x) :\leftrightarrow \exists y\,(y = x)$, which is a genuine mark, and set $D^{E}(x) :\leftrightarrow Ox \wedge Wx \wedge Bx \wedge E(x)$. Since $E(x)$ is true of every object, $D^{E}(x) \leftrightarrow D(x)$: again nothing has changed. From the definition one can now validly infer $\forall x\,(D^{E}(x) \to E(x))$, 'every perfect being exists', and that sentence is true. But it is a universal sentence, true even if nothing is perfect; the conclusion the argument wanted, $\exists x\, D^{E}(x)$, is a different sentence.
</Example>

There is no fourth attempt, because existence can enter a formula in only three shapes. The quantifier is given an object where its concept should go, which is attempt 1 and not a formula. Or it is given a concept, which closes the formula into a sentence about that concept: attempt 2. Or the result is still open in $x$, and then the only formula that says of $x$ just that it exists is $E(x)$, attempt 3, since any other open formula says something more about $x$.

<Exercise>
Run the three attempts on Gaunilo's island. Take $Ix$ for '$x$ is an island' and $Xx$ for '$x$ is more excellent than every other land', define the concept 'best island', and show for attempts 2 and 3 that adding existence to the definition leaves $\exists x\,(\text{best island})(x)$ exactly where it was.

<Solution>
Define $L(x) :\leftrightarrow Ix \wedge Xx$. Its marks are $I$ and $X$.

**Attempt 2.** $L^{+}(x) :\leftrightarrow Ix \wedge Xx \wedge \exists y\,(Iy \wedge Xy)$. If some $a$ is $L^{+}$, then $Ia \wedge Xa$, so $\exists x\,L(x)$. If some $a$ is $L$, the conjunct $\exists y\,(Iy \wedge Xy)$ is true with witness $a$, so $L^{+}(a)$ and $\exists x\,L^{+}(x)$. Hence $\exists x\,L^{+}(x) \leftrightarrow \exists x\,L(x)$.

**Attempt 3.** $L^{E}(x) :\leftrightarrow Ix \wedge Xx \wedge E(x)$. Since $E(x)$ holds of every object, $L^{E}(x) \leftrightarrow L(x)$ for each $x$, so the two concepts are instantiated together or not at all. What the definition does yield is $\forall x\,(L^{E}(x) \to E(x))$, 'every best island exists', which is true whether or not there is a best island.

The working is the perfect-being working with $I, X$ in place of $O, W, B$; nothing in it used what the marks mean. That is why the island is a parody and not merely an analogy.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### One interpretation refutes every version

The calculation used nothing about omnipotence or islands, which suggests a general statement: no definition of this kind, whatever its marks, proves that something falls under it. Two facts reduce that to a question about logic alone.

First, a definition must be *conservative*, as <Ref lesson="frege-logicism" id="zero-the-successor-and-one">the definitions of arithmetic had to be</Ref>: it introduces a new word and lets nothing new be proved about anything else, so a sentence without the new word that follows from the definition already followed from logic without it. Write the definition as $D(x) \leftrightarrow M_1x \wedge \cdots \wedge M_kx$, with marks $M_1, \ldots, M_k$. It makes $\exists x\,D(x)$ equivalent to $\exists x\,(M_1x \wedge \cdots \wedge M_kx)$, a sentence that does not contain $D$, and attempts 2 and 3 showed that adding either existence conjunct keeps that equivalence. So the definition proves $\exists x\,D(x)$ only if logic alone proves that the marks are jointly instantiated.

Second, logical consequence, in the modern sense the <Ref lesson="frege-function-and-quantifier" id="a-notation-for-content-not-a-calculus">concept-script was absorbed into</Ref>, is truth under every reinterpretation of the non-logical words, and a reinterpretation is free to make a non-logical predicate true of nothing. So logic does not prove that the marks are instantiated, and then neither does the definition. The proof below builds that reinterpretation once, for every choice of marks.

<Theorem title="no definition from non-logical marks proves its concept instantiated">
Let $D$ be defined by $D(x) \leftrightarrow M_1x \wedge \cdots \wedge M_kx$, where $M_1, \ldots, M_k$ are non-logical predicates (predicates other than identity, whose extension an interpretation is free to choose), and optionally with the conjunct $E(x)$ or the conjunct $\exists y\,(M_1y \wedge \cdots \wedge M_ky)$ added. Then $\exists x\,D(x)$ is not a logical consequence of the definition.

<Proof>
It suffices to give one interpretation in which the definition is true and $\exists x\,D(x)$ is false. Take any non-empty domain, say four objects $a, b, c, d$, and interpret every $M_i$ as the empty set. Interpret $D$ as the empty set too.

The definition is true: for each object $o$, $M_1o \wedge \cdots \wedge M_ko$ is false because $M_1o$ is false, and $Do$ is false, so the biconditional holds at $o$. Adding $E(x)$ or the existential conjunct changes nothing, since a conjunction with a false conjunct stays false. And $\exists x\,D(x)$ is false, because $D$ is empty. So the definition does not entail it.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Below, a table: every mark holds of nothing; each definition 'D(x) ↔ its marks' is true; ∃y (y = x) holds of a, b, c, d, so 'every perfect being exists' is true; the conclusion 'some object is a perfect being (or island, or devil)' is false."
  caption="One interpretation makes every definition true and every conclusion false, for the perfect being and both parodies at once."

The step that fails is the move from $\forall x\,(D^{E}(x) \to E(x))$, which the definition yields, to $\exists x\, D(x)$, which it does not: a fact about the marks of a concept taken for a fact about the concept.

<KeyFact>
The marks of a concept can settle that it is empty (contradictory marks such as 'round' and 'square' do) but never, when they are non-logical, that it has an instance. Existence is decided on the second level, and definitions only ever write on the first.
</KeyFact>

The restriction to non-logical marks matters. The same 1884 section warned that it would "be going too far to assert that we can never infer from the component characteristics of a concept to oneness or to existence". The simplest case is the mark $x = x$. Identity is logical vocabulary, true of every object under every interpretation, so the counter-model cannot empty it, and $\exists x\,(x = x)$ is a theorem of first-order logic.

### Anselm's 'greater' fails at the same line

Anselm's version has no list of perfections, but its fourth move needs the same thing. In moves 1 to 5, $G$ was used as a name, as if it already picked out a thing. In the concept-script it has to be read as a concept, $Gx$: '$x$ is something than which nothing greater can be conceived'. 'Exists in the understanding alone' then means that the concept is grasped and nothing falls under it: $\neg\exists x\,Gx$. 'Greater', by contrast, is a relation between objects: one thing is greater than another. Move 4 compares "$G$ in the understanding alone" with "$G$ in reality", and the comparison can be read in two ways.

The first is the one Descartes made explicit: treat 'existing in reality' as a respect in which one object can exceed another, that is, as a first-level mark. The only first-level 'exists' is $E$, and every object has it. So no object lacks it, and there is no pair of objects, one with $E$ and one without, for the premise to rank. Read as a claim about objects, the premise compares nothing with nothing.

The second keeps existence on the level of concepts: move 4 says that the concept $G$ with an instance ranks above the concept $G$ without one. That can be granted. But move 1 ranks objects, and to contradict it, move 5 needs an object greater than an object that is $G$. An instantiated concept is not a being that exceeds anything, so the granted premise supplies no such object, and no contradiction with move 1 is reached. Both readings stop the argument at move 4, the same line where Descartes's version stops.

## Where the diagnosis stops: necessary existence

With the parodies explained and both versions blocked, one would expect the argument to be finished. Anselm's own reply to Gaunilo shows why it was not. He did not defend the island. He said his reasoning fitted exactly one concept, and named what made it unique:

<Quote source="Anselm, Reply to Gaunilo III, trans. S. N. Deane (1903)">
Now I promise confidently that if any man shall devise anything existing either in reality or in concept alone (except that than which a greater be conceived) to which he can adapt the sequence of my reasoning, I will discover that thing, and will give him his lost island, not to be lost again. But it now appears that this being than which a greater is inconceivable cannot be conceived not to be.
</Quote>

The third chapter of the *Proslogion* already had this argument: a being that cannot be conceived not to exist is greater than one that can, and any island can be conceived not to exist. What is claimed to be a perfection here is not existence but *necessary existence*: existing in such a way that not existing was never an option. Descartes, too, wrote that it is "to his essence alone that [necessary and eternal] existence pertains". The diagnosis so far was about existence. Necessary existence has to be written down separately before the level check can be applied to it.

### Necessity written as an operator

The concept-script has no sign for 'necessarily'. For this section, two operators read informally suffice. A *possible world* is a complete way things might have been. $\Box p$, 'necessarily $p$', is true when $p$ is true in every possible world, and $\Diamond p$, 'possibly $p$', when $p$ is true in at least one; so $\Diamond p$ is $\neg\Box\neg p$, as $\exists$ was $\neg\forall\neg$.

With $\Box$ available, a property of an object can be about what holds of it in other worlds, and such a property is not degenerate. '$x$ is prime in every possible world' is true of $7$; '$x$ is reading this sentence in every possible world' is false of you, since you might have been doing something else. These are first-level concepts, defined by applying $\Box$ inside an expression that still has a gap for an object, exactly as '$(\ )$ loves some girl' had a quantifier inside it and a gap still open.

Existence itself stops being degenerate the same way. Each world has its own stock of objects: a world in which your parents never met has no you in it. '$x$ exists at $w$' is $E$ evaluated over $w$'s objects, so '$x$ exists in every possible world' is false of you, who exist but might not have, and true of the number $7$ if numbers are in every world. Inside one domain $E$ is true of everything and distinguishes nothing; across worlds, existence distinguishes.

### The modal version

A version of the argument published in 1974 uses only that much. Let $X(x)$, '$x$ is maximally excellent', mean that $x$ is omnipotent, omniscient and perfectly good, and let $M(x)$, '$x$ is maximally great', mean that $x$ exists and is maximally excellent in every possible world. Then:

1. **Premise.** It is possible that something is maximally great: $\Diamond\,\exists x\, M(x)$.
2. **From the definition of $M$.** If something is maximally great, then in every world something is maximally excellent: $\exists x\, M(x) \to \Box\,\exists y\, X(y)$, and since this follows from a definition it holds in every world.
3. **So** in some world it is necessary that something is maximally excellent: $\Diamond\,\Box\,\exists y\, X(y)$.
4. **Modal principle.** $\Diamond\Box p \to \Box p$: what is possibly necessary is necessary.
5. **Conclusion.** $\Box\,\exists y\, X(y)$, and therefore $\exists y\, X(y)$.

Step 4 is automatic on the reading of $\Box$ given above, where there is one stock of possible worlds: if $\Box p$ holds at some world, $p$ holds at every world, and that is all $\Box p$ says. It has to be stated because some modal logics let what is possible depend on the world you start from (a world can count as possible from here and not from there), and in those the principle can fail. The logic in which every world is possible from every other is called S5; the informal reading above is S5.

Run the level check. $M$ and $X$ are first-level concepts, and the 'exists in every possible world' inside $M$ is not the degenerate $E$: as the operator subsection showed, it is false of things that might not have existed. Every quantifier is applied to a concept; $\Box$ and $\Diamond$ are applied to sentences. No step puts a second-level concept where a mark goes, and the counter-model of the theorem above does not touch it, because premise 1 is not a definition: it is a claim that a certain concept is possibly instantiated, which is a substantive existence claim of its own. The argument is valid in S5, and the level analysis has nothing to object to.

The parody comes back as a premise. 'Possibly, nothing is maximally excellent', $\Diamond\neg\exists y\, X(y)$, is at least as plausible as premise 1, and in S5 it yields the opposite conclusion:

1. Since $\Diamond$ is $\neg\Box\neg$, the new premise is $\neg\Box\,\exists y\,X(y)$.
2. The contrapositive of step 4 then gives $\neg\Diamond\Box\,\exists y\,X(y)$: in no world is it necessary that something is maximally excellent.
3. Step 2 holds in every world, so a world with a maximally great being would be a world where $\Box\,\exists y\,X(y)$ holds. There is none, so $\neg\Diamond\,\exists x\,M(x)$: a maximally great being is impossible.

In S5, in fact, 'possibly a maximally great being exists', 'necessarily one exists' and 'one exists' are equivalent: whatever is maximally great in one world is maximally great in all. The modal argument is valid, and accepting its premise is the same thing as accepting what it was supposed to prove.

### The logician's version and the machine

The last version came from Kurt Gödel, the logician of the incompleteness theorems, in notes dated 1970. It is built on *positive properties*. A positive property is meant to be what the older writers called a perfection: a property it is purely good to have, with no lack or limitation in it. Formally, 'positive' is a property of properties: $P(\varphi)$ says that the first-level property $\varphi$ is positive, so $P$ is a second-level concept, the same level as 'is instantiated'. A *God-like* being is one that has every positive property: $G(x) :\leftrightarrow \forall\varphi\,(P(\varphi) \to \varphi(x))$, where $\forall\varphi$ quantifies over first-level properties as $\forall x$ quantifies over objects. Here $G$ is a new letter, not Anselm's.

Necessary existence needs one more notion, *essence* in a different, technical sense from the list of marks above. An essence of $x$ is a property that captures $x$'s whole nature: a property from which, in every possible world, every property $x$ has follows. Then *necessary existence*, $NE$, is a first-level property: $x$ exists necessarily when each of its essences is instantiated in every possible world. A handful of axioms say which properties are positive, among them that a property and its negation are not both positive, that being God-like is positive, and that necessary existence is positive. The logician Dana Scott circulated a slightly altered version in 1972, differing chiefly in the definition of essence. In it the theorem $\Box\,\exists x\, G(x)$ follows in three steps, the shape of the modal version (the labels are those of the starred table below):

1. Being God-like is positive (A3), and positive properties are possibly instantiated (T1): $\Diamond\,\exists x\, G(x)$.
2. Being God-like is an essence of any God-like being (T2). A God-like being has every positive property, necessary existence among them (A5), so by the definition of necessary existence (D3) its essence $G$ is instantiated in every world: $G(x) \to \Box\,\exists y\, G(y)$, and like step 2 of the modal version this holds in every world.
3. So $\Diamond\Box\,\exists y\, G(y)$, and the S5 principle of step 4 gives $\Box\,\exists y\, G(y)$.

Step 2 is where $P(NE)$ does its work: it puts necessity inside the concept, as the definition of $M$ did in the modal version.

/>

Compare the key premise with Descartes's. 'Existence is a perfection' asked a second-level concept to be a mark. 'Necessary existence is positive', $P(NE)$, applies a second-level concept to a first-level one, the shape of 'the concept unicorn is uninstantiated'. Gödel's argument is not a counter-example to the level diagnosis; it is an argument written by someone who had absorbed it. Its premises are where the fault, if any, has to be.

In 2013 that question was handed to machines. The argument was encoded in classical higher-order logic, the logic of quantifiers over properties, with each sentence given an extra parameter for the world it is evaluated at, so that $\Box$ became a quantifier over worlds. The automated provers [Leo-II](https://github.com/leoprover/Leo-II) and Satallax found the proofs, and the proof assistants [Isabelle/HOL](https://isabelle.in.tum.de/) and Coq checked every step. Three findings matter here:

| what was checked | finding |
| --- | --- |
| Scott's axioms (1972) | consistent: a model exists; the theorem $\Box\,\exists x\,G(x)$ follows, and a modal logic weaker than S5 suffices |
| Gödel's own axioms (1970) | inconsistent: his definition of essence lacks one clause, and without it the axioms prove a contradiction; found by Leo-II in 2013, unnoticed for four decades of commentary |
| the consequence called *modal collapse* | from Scott's axioms, $p \to \Box p$ for every sentence $p$: whatever is true is necessarily true; shown by hand in 1987, machine-verified in 2016 |

The last row is the parody in its modern form. Gaunilo's island showed that the medieval form proves too much; modal collapse shows that the consistent modern axioms prove too much as well, not a best island but the necessity of everything, including that you are reading this sentence. Nothing in either finding is a level confusion. Both are consequences of axioms, found by deriving them, which is the kind of work a proof checker does and the level analysis does not.

## ★ The axioms, and the collapse proved

Scott's version as encoded in 2013: $\varphi, \psi$ range over first-level properties; $\varphi \text{ ess } x$ reads '$\varphi$ is an essence of $x$', a property of $x$ that necessarily entails every property $x$ has.

| | statement | gloss |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A1 | $\forall\varphi\,[P(\neg\varphi) \leftrightarrow \neg P(\varphi)]$ | exactly one of a property and its negation is positive |
| A2 | $\forall\varphi\,\forall\psi\,[(P(\varphi) \wedge \Box\forall x\,(\varphi(x) \to \psi(x))) \to P(\psi)]$ | what a positive property necessarily entails is positive |
| T1 | $\forall\varphi\,[P(\varphi) \to \Diamond\exists x\,\varphi(x)]$ | positive properties are possibly instantiated |
| D1 | $G(x) \leftrightarrow \forall\varphi\,[P(\varphi) \to \varphi(x)]$ | God-like: has every positive property |
| A3 | $P(G)$ | being God-like is positive |
| C | $\Diamond\exists x\,G(x)$ | possibly, something is God-like |
| A4 | $\forall\varphi\,[P(\varphi) \to \Box P(\varphi)]$ | positive properties are necessarily positive |
| D2 | $\varphi \text{ ess } x \leftrightarrow \varphi(x) \wedge \forall\psi\,[\psi(x) \to \Box\forall y\,(\varphi(y) \to \psi(y))]$ | essence |
| T2 | $\forall x\,[G(x) \to G \text{ ess } x]$ | being God-like is the essence of a God-like being |
| D3 | $NE(x) \leftrightarrow \forall\varphi\,[\varphi \text{ ess } x \to \Box\exists y\,\varphi(y)]$ | necessary existence |
| A5 | $P(NE)$ | necessary existence is positive |
| T3 | $\Box\exists x\,G(x)$ | necessarily, something is God-like |

Gödel's 1970 definition of essence omits the first conjunct of D2, $\varphi(x)$. That one clause is the difference between the consistent row of the table above and the inconsistent one; the exercise on Gödel's own definition derives the contradiction.

<Theorem title="modal collapse">
In S5, Scott's axioms imply $p \to \Box p$ for every sentence $p$.

<Proof>
Assume $p$. By T3 and the fact that what is necessary is true, some $g$ is God-like: $G(g)$. Consider the property 'being such that $p$', $\lambda y.\,p$, which an object has exactly when $p$ is true; $g$ has it, since $p$. By T2, $G$ is an essence of $g$, so by D2 every property of $g$ is necessarily entailed by $G$: $\Box\forall y\,(G(y) \to p)$. By T3, $\Box\exists y\,G(y)$. In each world, then, something is God-like, and everything God-like there is such that $p$, so $p$ holds there. Hence $\Box p$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof uses the essence clause in an unexpected direction. An essence of $g$ is required to entail every property $g$ has, and 'being such that $p$' is a property $g$ has as soon as $p$ is true; so the essence of a necessary being drags every truth into necessity. Several later variants of the axioms were designed to avoid the collapse.

<Conclusion>
You can now take an argument that treats existence as a property and locate its fault in the notation: existence is the second-level concept 'is instantiated', a concept's content is its first-level marks, and no definition from non-logical marks entails that the concept has an instance, as one interpretation with all the marks empty shows for the perfect being, the island and the devil together. The reading used the analysis of quantifiers as second-level concepts, the levels of object and concept, and validity as truth under every reinterpretation; it did not need sense and reference. It also showed where the tool runs out. Versions that make necessary existence a positive property respect the levels, and they fail, where they fail, by what their axioms entail: a contradiction in Gödel's set, the necessity of every truth in Scott's, and a premise equivalent to the conclusion in the modal version. Necessity itself was used here informally; its logic, and the question whether possible worlds are anything more than a way of talking, come with <Ref lesson="kripke-rigid-designation">rigid designation</Ref> and <Ref lesson="lewis-possible-worlds">possible worlds taken literally</Ref>.

One version was left unwritten. 'The most perfect being exists' has the grammar of a name followed by a predicate, and the argument treats 'the most perfect being' as standing for something whose properties can be read off. <Ref lesson="russell-theory-of-descriptions">The next module</Ref> asks what 'the so-and-so' contributes to a sentence when there may be nothing it stands for.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Write 'martyrs suffer' and 'martyrs exist' in quantifier notation, with $Mx$ for '$x$ is a martyr' and $Sx$ for '$x$ suffers'. Both English sentences are a plural noun followed by a verb. Say, from the two formulas, which of them attributes a property to martyrs, and what the other is about.

<Solution>
'Martyrs suffer' is $\forall x\,(Mx \to Sx)$: each object under the concept 'martyr' falls under the concept 'suffers'. It attributes a property, suffering, to each martyr; $S$ is a first-level concept applied to objects.

'Martyrs exist' is $\exists x\, Mx$: the concept 'martyr' falls within the second-level concept 'some object falls under $(\ )$'. No first-level concept is applied to martyrs at all; the formula has no predicate in the place where $S$ stood. The sentence is about the concept, and says it is instantiated.

The grammatical match (noun, verb) hides a difference of level. Read 'exist' as a verb like 'suffer' and one is invited to ask what existing is like for a martyr, as one can ask what suffering is like; the formula shows there is no such question. This is the confusion the ontological argument relies on when it lists existence beside omnipotence.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "Kant already refuted the ontological argument with the hundred thalers. The new logic added nothing but notation." State what the thalers establish, what they leave open, and the step the concept-script supplies that the thalers do not.

<Solution>
**What the thalers establish.** A concept's content is the same whether or not it is realised: a hundred real thalers contain no coin more than a hundred possible ones. So existence is not part of what a concept describes.

**What they leave open.** Kant does not say what existence is in positive terms, only that it is "the positing of a thing", an act of judgment. His rejection of the argument rests on the claim that every existential proposition is synthetic, and the defender denies exactly that for one concept: in the concept of a supremely perfect being, they say, existence is contained. Kant's reply is a counter-assertion of his own principle, so the dispute ends where the first denial of the premise ended.

**What the concept-script supplies.** It gives existence a definite place, the second-level concept $\exists x\,\varphi(x)$, and so turns 'existence is not a mark' from a principle into something checkable: the premise either cannot be written (a second-level concept where a first-level one goes), or is written with the degenerate $E(x)$ or a closed conjunct $\exists y\,D(y)$, and then the computation $\exists x\,D^{+}(x) \leftrightarrow \exists x\,D(x)$ shows it makes no difference. The theorem of the lesson adds the proof: one interpretation, with every mark empty, makes the definition true and the conclusion false. "Nothing but notation" is right in one sense: the notation is what made the counter-model statable. That is the step from a thesis about existence to a proof that the argument is invalid.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
'The largest prime does not exist.' (a) Write it in quantifier notation with $Px$ for '$x$ is prime' and $\le$ on the natural numbers. (b) Prove it. (c) A philosopher who holds that every meaningful denial must be about some object says the sentence is about the largest prime, an object that "subsists" without existing. Using your formula, say what the sentence is about instead, and what the subsisting prime would have to be like.

<Solution>
**(a)** 'The largest prime' is a concept, 'prime and at least as large as every prime'. The sentence denies that it is instantiated:

$$
\neg\exists x\,\bigl(Px \wedge \forall y\,(Py \to y \le x)\bigr).
$$

**(b)** Suppose some $p$ were prime with every prime $\le p$. Let $q$ be a prime factor of $p! + 1$. Dividing $p! + 1$ by any number from $2$ to $p$ leaves remainder $1$, so $q > p$; but $q$ is prime, so $q \le p$. Contradiction.

**(c)** The formula is about the concept 'prime and at least as large as every prime', and says it falls within 'no object falls under $(\ )$'. No prime, existing or subsisting, is named or quantified over in a way that requires one to be there. A subsisting largest prime would have to be prime and larger than or equal to $p! + 1$'s prime factor $q$, which is larger than it: its own marks are inconsistent, so it would be an object with contradictory properties.

The case is the boundary of the lesson's KeyFact. Here the marks of the concept do settle a second-level fact, by proof, but the fact is emptiness, never instantiation. Content can rule a concept out; it cannot put an object under it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The lesson's theorem was restricted to non-logical marks. Show that the restriction is needed: (a) in standard first-order logic $\exists x\,(x = x)$ is a theorem, and (b) in <Ref lesson="frege-logicism" id="zero-the-successor-and-one">the logicist construction</Ref> second-order logic and Hume's principle prove that $0$, defined as $\#[x \neq x]$, exists. Then say whether a defender of the ontological argument can use either case, by defining 'perfect' from logical vocabulary alone.

<Solution>
**(a)** First-order logic takes every domain to be non-empty. In any domain pick an object $a$; $a = a$, so $\exists x\,(x = x)$. The counter-model of the theorem cannot be built, because its first step, "interpret the marks as empty", is impossible for the mark $x = x$: it is a logical predicate, interpreted the same way (as the whole domain) in every interpretation.

**(b)** The logicist construction proves $\exists y\,(y = \#[x \neq x])$. The step that delivers an object is not a definition: Hume's principle, $\#F = \#G \leftrightarrow F \approx G$, together with comprehension, which supplies the concept $x \neq x$, implies that the number operator takes a value for every concept, so the existence of $0$ comes from a principle that, as that lesson put it, settles how many things there are. That is why whether Hume's principle is logic remained the open question.

**Can the defender use either?** Only by making 'perfect' a logical concept, and then it is not perfection. Define $D(x) :\leftrightarrow x = x$ and $\exists x\,D(x)$ follows, but $D$ is true of every object, so 'something is perfect' says only that something exists. Define 'perfect' through a number-like principle and existence comes from that principle, which is now a substantive premise the argument has to defend, exactly the position of premise 1 of the modal version and of Gödel's axioms. Either the concept loses its content or the existence claim is moved into a premise. Neither is an argument from the meaning of 'perfect' alone.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A 1905 analysis rewrites 'the most perfect being exists' with the description written out. Let $Px$ mean '$x$ is most perfect'. (a) Write 'the most perfect being exists' as a quantified formula in which no expression stands for the most perfect being, by stating that exactly one object is most perfect. (b) The argument 'the most perfect being has all perfections; existence is a perfection; therefore the most perfect being exists' becomes valid when the description is unpacked. Show which premise it then needs, and that the argument cannot supply it. (c) Take 'most perfect' to mean 'has every positive predicate', and suppose two positive predicates $A$ and $B$ are incompatible: $\neg\exists x\,(Ax \wedge Bx)$. Prove that nothing is most perfect. (d) In your formula from (a), what is the grammatical subject of the English sentence, and is there any part of the formula that stands for it?

<Solution>
**(a)**

$$
\exists x\,\bigl(Px \wedge \forall y\,(Py \to y = x)\bigr).
$$

It says that at least one object is most perfect and at most one is.

**(b)** Unpacked, the premises say: there is exactly one most perfect $x$, and that $x$ has all perfections, existence among them; therefore that $x$ exists. That is valid, but its first premise is $\exists x\,(Px \wedge \forall y\,(Py \to y = x))$, which already says something is most perfect. Without it, what the premises yield is $\forall x\,(Px \to E(x))$: every most perfect being exists, which is true even if there are none. The argument needs as a premise the existence-and-uniqueness claim it was meant to establish. Uniqueness on its own is easy if 'most perfect' means 'more perfect than every other being': two distinct most perfect beings would each be more perfect than the other. Existence is not.

**(c)** Suppose $Pa$. Then $a$ has every positive predicate, in particular $Aa$ and $Ba$, so $\exists x\,(Ax \wedge Bx)$, against the assumption. So $\neg\exists x\,Px$: the concept 'most perfect' is empty, provably, whenever any two positive predicates exclude each other (being infinitely merciful and infinitely just are standard candidates). This is the largest-prime case again: the marks settle emptiness.

**(d)** The grammatical subject is 'the most perfect being'. Nothing in the formula corresponds to it: the phrase has been dissolved into a quantifier, a predicate $P$ and a uniqueness clause, spread across the whole sentence. An expression that contributes to the meaning of sentences in which it occurs but has no meaning on its own, so that no part of the analysed sentence stands for it, will be called an *incomplete symbol* in <Ref lesson="russell-theory-of-descriptions">the theory of descriptions</Ref>, which builds exactly this analysis for every 'the so-and-so'.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Gödel's 1970 notes defined essence this way, writing $\varphi \text{ ess } x$ for '$\varphi$ is an essence of $x$':

$$
\varphi \text{ ess } x \;\leftrightarrow\; \forall\psi\,[\psi(x) \to \Box\forall y\,(\varphi(y) \to \psi(y))],
$$

that is, every property $x$ has is necessarily entailed by $\varphi$. Take with it three more of his principles: the definition of necessary existence, $NE(x) \leftrightarrow \forall\varphi\,[\varphi \text{ ess } x \to \Box\exists y\,\varphi(y)]$ (every essence of $x$ is instantiated in every world); $P(NE)$ (necessary existence is positive); and $\forall\varphi\,[P(\varphi) \to \Diamond\exists x\,\varphi(x)]$ (positive properties are possibly instantiated). Let $\emptyset$ be the empty property, $\emptyset(y) :\leftrightarrow y \neq y$. (a) Show that $\emptyset \text{ ess } x$ for every $x$. (b) Show that $NE(x)$ implies $\Box\,\bot$, where $\bot$ is a contradiction. (c) Use the last two principles to derive a contradiction, assuming that what is necessary is true. (d) Scott's version adds the conjunct $\varphi(x)$ to the definition of essence, so that an essence of $x$ must be a property $x$ has. Explain why the same derivation then fails.

<Solution>
**(a)** Fix $x$ and any property $\psi$ with $\psi(x)$. We need $\Box\forall y\,(\emptyset(y) \to \psi(y))$. In every world, $\emptyset(y)$ is false for every $y$, so $\emptyset(y) \to \psi(y)$ is true for every $y$; hence the universal holds in every world, which is the boxed claim. So $\emptyset$ satisfies Gödel's definition of an essence of $x$, vacuously.

**(b)** Suppose $NE(x)$. By the definition of $NE$, every essence of $x$ is necessarily instantiated; by (a), $\emptyset$ is an essence of $x$; so $\Box\exists y\,\emptyset(y)$, that is, $\Box\exists y\,(y \neq y)$. Since $\exists y\,(y \neq y)$ is false in every world, this is $\Box\,\bot$.

**(c)** By $P(NE)$, $NE$ is positive; positive properties are possibly instantiated, so it is possibly instantiated: $\Diamond\exists x\,NE(x)$. So in some world $w$ some $x$ has $NE$, and by (b), $\Box\,\bot$ is true at $w$. If what is necessary is true, $\bot$ is true at $w$, which is impossible. The axioms are inconsistent.

The contradiction does not need that assumption: it survives in modal logics where what is possible depends on the world you start from. $\Box\,\bot$ is true at a world exactly when no world is possible from it, since $\bot$ is true at none; so no world is possible from $w$. But the first two sentences of this answer derived $\Diamond\Box\,\bot$ from axioms and definitions alone, and axioms hold at every world, so it holds at $w$ as well, and there it requires a world possible from $w$. That is the contradiction, reached without inferring anything from $\Box p$ to $p$.

**(d)** With Scott's definition, an essence of $x$ must be a property $x$ has: $\varphi(x)$ is the added conjunct. $\emptyset(x)$ is false, so $\emptyset$ is not an essence of anything, step (a) fails, and $NE$ no longer demands that the empty property be instantiated. The difference between the consistent and the inconsistent axiom set is one conjunct, which commentators had treated for decades as an oversight not worth recording; the prover's refutation turned on the empty property. The transferable point: a definition with a universally quantified clause and no clause tying it to its subject is satisfied vacuously by degenerate cases, and an axiom set must be checked against those.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Gaunilo's island was written within a few years of the *Proslogion*. Using the parody as your evidence, argue in a paragraph that medieval readers already knew the argument proved too much. Then state what was missing, until the concept-script, for that knowledge to become a diagnosis, and what Anselm's reply to the island shows about where a defender would retreat.

<Solution>
**Possible answer.** Gaunilo's reply keeps every move of Anselm's argument and changes only the concept: the island "more excellent than all lands" is understood by whoever hears the description, exists in the understanding, and would be more excellent if it existed in reality. Gaunilo did not dispute a premise; he presented the form with a different filling and a conclusion nobody accepts, and he said that anyone who accepted the proof should be suspected of "jesting". That is the logical structure of a counter-example to a form, written within a few years of the original, so the knowledge that the form proves too much is as old as the argument.

**What was missing.** A parody shows that a form is invalid only if one can say what the form is, and the form here ran through 'exists', which grammar treats as a predicate like 'is excellent'. Without a notation that puts existence on a different level from the marks of a concept, there was no way to state the shared step, nor to prove that it fails, so every reply was either a parody (showing failure without location) or a denial of a premise (location without proof). The concept-script supplied both: the step is the passage from a fact about marks to the second-level fact $\exists x\,D(x)$, and one interpretation with empty marks shows it invalid for every filling at once.

**Anselm's retreat.** He answered that his reasoning fits exactly one concept, the being that "cannot be conceived not to be": necessary existence, which an island lacks. That is the move from existence to necessary existence, which the level check passes; it is the retreat the modal and Gödelian versions occupy, and where the dispute moved to premises and axioms.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

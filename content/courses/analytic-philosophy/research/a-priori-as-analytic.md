## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** The empiricist's dilemma in Ayer's form: no experience can refute $7 + 5 = 12$ (count five pairs and get nine, and you blame the counting). So either (Mill) mathematics is a very well confirmed empirical generalisation, or it is not about the world at all. Kant's third option: synthetic a priori — informative and necessary — with Euclidean geometry as the showcase; and relativity had just used non-Euclidean geometry for physical space. Sub-problem: can a truth be necessary and cost nothing?
2. **Kant's two distinctions, stated for the reader who has not met them.** Analytic/synthetic (the predicate contained in the subject; 'all bodies are extended' vs 'all bodies are heavy'); a priori/a posteriori (knowable independently of experience). Kant's cell: $7 + 5 = 12$ is a priori and, he says, synthetic — '12' is not contained in '7 + 5'. Why Ayer rejects the containment test: Kant's two criteria (psychological 'thought in the concept' vs logical 'contradiction on denial') are not equivalent, and the first makes analyticity depend on what one happens to have in mind (pp. 71–73).
3. **Mill's horn and why it fails.** If arithmetic were empirical, a counter-instance would be conceivable. Ayer's three cases: the five pairs; the triangle whose angles do not sum to 180° ('we say we measured wrongly, or that it is not Euclidean'); the friend who 'has stopped writing' (excluded middle saved by finding the apparent contradictory is only a contrary). In each, 'the one explanation which would in no circumstances be adopted is that ten is not always the product of two and five'. The because: a principle that is always saved by reinterpretation is not functioning as a hypothesis.
4. **The empiricist's horn: analytic by definition.** 'A proposition is analytic when its validity depends solely on the definitions of the symbols it contains' (p. 73); 'either some ants are parasitic or none are' vs 'there are ants which have established a system of slavery'. Analytic propositions have no factual content but are not senseless: they record usage and unpack what one has committed to (May Queens). The Tractatus's tautologies are the model (`<Ref>`), and logicism is not needed: each logical truth is 'valid in its own right'; Principia 'probably only one among many possible logics' (pp. 75–77). The 1946 refinement: analytic propositions are not *about* language either; they are necessary *given* the conventions.
5. **Geometry.** Axioms are definitions; theorems their consequences; which geometry fits physical space is empirical; 'there is no sense in asking which geometries are false — they are all true' if consistent (pp. 77–79); diagrams are psychological aids. Kant's transcendental aesthetic dismissed, the synthetic a priori cell declared empty. (The relativity half — what Einstein's definitions did — is the application lesson.)
6. **Why tautologies surprise.** $91 \times 79 = 7189$ is a tautology beyond immediate apprehension; calculation is tautological transformation; 'a being whose intellect was infinitely powerful would take no interest in logic and mathematics' (Hahn). Poincaré's claim that induction is synthetic a priori answered: induction is a defining principle of the natural numbers (the Frege lesson's ancestral, `<Ref>`). The because: surprise measures our limits, not the proposition's content.
7. **What refuted or limited it.** (a) Stebbing: the analytic/synthetic distinction is itself neither analytic nor empirical — so by the Circle's lights it is metaphysical; (b) Gödel: Carnap's answer required an infinite tower of languages and non-finite reasoning for 'analytic', stretching 'formal' past what a mathematician will accept as such (Uebel §3.2) — one paragraph for this reader; (c) Quine 1951 (next module): 'true by the definitions of its symbols' presupposes a notion of definition that presupposes synonymy. Survived: 'true by convention' as distinct from 'true by intuition'; the empty cell stayed empty until Kripke filled a different one (necessary a posteriori).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Three senses of 'analytic' are now in play: Kant's (containment), Frege's (provable from logic and definitions), Ayer's (valid by definitions of symbols). Name all three once, in a table, and use Ayer's from then on. Quine's lesson will need the table.
- 'Convention' is dangerous: the 1946 Introduction corrects the 1936 reading that analytic truths are *about* conventions — the lesson states the corrected view.
- Do not re-teach non-Euclidean geometry; one sentence that the parallel postulate's denial yields consistent geometries and that the reader knows which one general relativity uses.
- Ayer's 'twice five is not ten' scenario is a thought experiment about response, not a claim about psychology; keep the register.
- Gödel: state the pressure, do not adjudicate; no incompleteness proof.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Classify under Ayer's definition, stating the definitions that decide it: 'a square has four sides'; 'the square of an even number is even'; 'this square is red'; 'every map can be coloured with four colours'.
2. (refute) "Non-Euclidean geometry refuted Euclid, so geometry is empirical and Mill was right." Break it with the lesson's distinction between a geometry as a system of definitions and its application to physical space; say what *was* refuted (Kant's claim about space, not Euclid's theorems).
3. (derivation) Reconstruct Ayer's argument that Kant's two criteria for analyticity are not equivalent: construct a proposition analytic by the contradiction test whose predicate a competent speaker does not 'think in' the subject, and one the reverse, if possible; state what Ayer concludes.
4. (boundary probe) The four-colour theorem was proved by computer in 1976 with a case analysis no human has checked. On Ayer's account it is a tautology; say what 'surprise' and 'knowledge' come to for it, and whether the infinitely powerful intellect of p. 82 would need the computer. (This is the doorstep of Kripke's prime-number machine; the solution says so.)
5. (claim–evidence) Stebbing's objection: write 'every meaningful proposition is analytic or empirical' and test it by the criterion of the previous lesson; then give Ayer's reply (the principle is a definition) and say whether the same reply rescues the analytic/synthetic distinction.
6. (FORWARD → two-dogmas-analyticity) Ayer's definition rests on 'the definitions of the symbols'. Write the definition of 'bachelor' that makes 'no bachelor is married' analytic, and then ask what makes *that* definition correct rather than another: a fact about English speakers, a stipulation, or a synonymy? The solution says Quine will argue that each answer presupposes the notion being defined, with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped) Poincaré held that mathematical induction is synthetic a priori; Ayer replies that it is a defining principle of the natural numbers. Using the Frege lesson's definition of 'natural number' by the ancestral, show in a few lines that induction follows from the definition, and state what Poincaré would have to deny.

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** 'I name this ship the Queen Elizabeth', bottle smashed — neither true nor false; said by the purser, not false either: the ship simply is not named. Logic from Frege on treated every indicative sentence as a candidate for truth (the assertion sign, the Tractatus's bipolarity) and the Circle sent the rest to emotion (`<Ref>` emotivism). Here is an indicative sentence that does rather than says. Sub-problem: how many such sentences are there, and what happens to 'true' once saying is a kind of doing? Obvious attempt: a short list of exceptions — ceremonial formulas — and the rest are statements. The lesson follows Austin in watching the list grow until the distinction fails.
2. **Performatives and felicity.** 'I do', the ship, the will ('I give and bequeath my watch to my brother'), the bet; not describing, not reporting; assessed as *happy* or *unhappy*. Felicity conditions: there must be a conventional procedure with a certain effect, invoked by the right persons in the right circumstances (else a *misfire* — the purser, the wrong formula; the act is void), and the persons must have the thoughts and feelings the procedure presupposes (else an *abuse* — the insincere promise; the act is done but hollow). The because: an act, unlike a statement, can fail to come off or come off badly, so the dimension of assessment is different.
3. **The collapse.** Austin's own tests: (a) statements misfire too — 'the present King of France is bald' (`<Ref>` descriptions) is void, not false, when there is no king; (b) performatives are assessed against the facts — 'I warn you the bull is about to charge' is mistaken if it is not; 'I advise you to…' can be bad advice; (c) 'I state that…' is a performative formula that produces a statement; (d) no grammatical or lexical criterion separates the two — the first-person present indicative test admits 'I bet' and 'I state' alike, and 'the bull is about to charge' can warn without any formula. So every utterance is both a doing and, where it has content, assessable against the facts. The because: the attempt to isolate a class of *pure* doings fails because saying was never pure either.
4. **The replacement.** Three acts in one utterance: the *locutionary* act (saying something with a certain sense and reference), the *illocutionary* act (what is done *in* saying it: warning, promising, stating, asking — the force), the *perlocutionary* act (what is done *by* saying it: alarming, persuading, deterring — the effect on the hearer). 'I'll be home for dinner' as promise or as prediction: same locution, different force. The illocutionary act is Austin's real subject; the locutionary act is an abstraction from it; the perlocutionary effect is not conventional. The because: once doing and saying are inseparable, what is needed is not two classes of utterance but three dimensions of one.
5. **Truth as a dimension.** 'France is hexagonal' — rough, good enough for a general, not for a geographer, 'neither true nor false'; 'Lord Raglan won the battle of Alma' — true in a schoolbook, exaggerated in a history; 'it takes two to make a truth' (the sentence and the occasion); 'true' is a dimension word like 'free' — a bare minimum covering fair, accurate, rough, exaggerated. The descriptive fallacy: the assumption that every indicative is a description to be assessed as true or false — the 'true/false fetish'. The 1950 'Truth' paper's descriptive and demonstrative conventions in one paragraph (sentence-type to situation-type; statement-token to historic situation). The because: assessment of an utterance is assessment of an act on an occasion, and 'true' is one of the ways an act can be well done.
6. **Why it mattered / what refuted or reshaped it.** Mattered: speech-act theory (Searle's taxonomy; propositional content + illocutionary force as the minimal unit), the philosophy of law (Hart's rules as performatives), linguistics (pragmatics). What must be kept: the force/content split that the *strong* reading ('no force-free locutionary core') would abolish — because 'if the bull is about to charge, run' embeds the content without the warning, the same embedding problem that caught emotivism (`<Ref>`), so logic needs the content separable. What refuted or limited: Grice's and Searle's systematisations are *theories*, which Austin's method distrusted (Williams: the method underestimated theory — the beetle species); 'I know' as a performative (Other Minds 1946) is Austin's one clear error — saying 'I promise' makes it so, saying 'I know' does not (SEP §3.1); the Derrida–Searle quarrel over 'non-serious' acts, named only.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The SEP reads HTDTW as *arguing*, through failed attempts, that no simple distinction exists — not as defending one; the lesson follows that reading and says so (the book's structure is the argument).
- 'Force' was already in Frege (the assertion sign) and the Tractatus dismissed it (4.442); one paragraph of prehistory with `<Ref>`s, no more.
- Do not let the truth section become a theory of truth: the deflationism dispute with Strawson is one clause.
- The strong reading of the 'fetish' passages and the Frege–Geach problem must be stated carefully: the lesson's verdict is that Austin's *analysis* (three acts) is right and his *rhetoric* ('the true/false fetish') overshoots where it denies a separable content.
- HTDTW is not on disk; page pointers via the SEP; References say so.
- Austin's wartime intelligence work and the card game CASE are seductive detail: cut.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) For 'I apologise', 'the meeting is adjourned', 'you're fired', 'it's raining': name the illocutionary act, the felicity conditions (who, what procedure), and whether the utterance can also be assessed against the facts.
2. (refute) "Performatives are the sentences in the first-person present indicative with a performative verb." Break it with Austin's own cases: a performative without the form ('Out!' from the umpire) and the form without a pure performative ('I state that it is raining'); state why no grammatical test survives.
3. (derivation) Reconstruct the collapse as an argument: premise (constatives are assessed only as true/false; performatives only as happy/unhappy), the two cross-cases, conclusion; then write the replacement claim in one sentence and show it entails that 'true' is one dimension among several.
4. (boundary probe) 'I know that the goldfinch is in the garden' — Austin treated 'I know' as giving one's word, like 'I promise'. Show the disanalogy (saying 'I promise' makes it so; saying 'I know' does not make it known — I can be wrong), and say which of the three acts 'I know' performs if it is not a performative. (The solution notes this as the SEP's verdict and the doorstep of gettier-problem.)
5. (claim–evidence) 'France is hexagonal' is 'neither true nor false' for Austin. Construct the two occasions (the general's briefing; the surveyor's report) and say what the *same* locutionary content does on each; then state what a Russellian who keeps 'true or false' for every statement would have to say about the sentence, and what he loses.
6. (FORWARD → strawson-on-referring) 'It takes two to make a truth': the sentence and the occasion. Take 'the King of France is wise' said today and in 1670; say what Austin's demonstrative conventions pick out on each occasion, and what the sentence alone can be assessed as. The solution says the next lesson makes this the objection to Russell, with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped) Searle estimates about a thousand English speech-act verbs. Design the procedure for counting them (the first-person present test with 'hereby'; exclusion of perlocutionary verbs like 'persuade', which name effects), apply it to twenty verbs of your choosing, and state the proportion that pass and why 'persuade' fails.

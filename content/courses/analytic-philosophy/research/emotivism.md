## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** 'Stealing money is wrong' is asserted, denied, argued about; not a tautology; and no observation of the theft reveals a property 'wrongness' beside who took what. Under the three bins it has nowhere to go. Sub-problem: it must either be an empirical statement in disguise, or a tautology in disguise, or something that is not a statement. Obvious attempt 1 (naturalism): it is empirical — 'wrong' means 'generally disapproved' (sociological subjectivism) or 'reduces happiness' (utilitarianism). Why it fails: 'this is generally approved, but is it right?' is not self-contradictory, so the definition is not an analysis (the open question test, one paragraph, with Moore named as its source and the extra lesson's `<Ref>`). Obvious attempt 2 (intuitionism): 'good' names a simple non-natural property known by intuition. Why it fails under the criterion: no observation tests it, and when intuitions conflict nothing decides.
2. **The answer.** Ethical terms are pseudo-concepts: 'you acted wrongly in stealing that money' says no more than 'you stole that money' in a peculiar tone of horror; 'stealing money is wrong' is 'Stealing money!!' with the exclamation marks encoding the kind of disapproval; ethical words express feeling and evoke it, and function as commands of graded force ('it is your duty…', 'you ought…', 'it is good…'). The because: once statements are exhausted by the two bins, what remains for a sentence that is plainly not idle is a *non-statement* function — and expression and command are what the grammar of 'ought' already shows.
3. **Not subjectivism.** 'Tolerance is a virtue' answered by 'you don't approve of it': on subjectivism (the sentence *asserts* my approval) that is a contradiction of it; on emotivism it is not, because expressing boredom in a tone is not saying 'I am bored'. So the theory is 'radically subjectivist' and yet ethical sentences cannot be contradicted — they are neither true nor false.
4. **Disputes.** Moore's argument: if ethical sentences were about feelings we could not dispute about value; Ayer accepts the inference and denies that we ever do — every ethical dispute is about facts (motives, consequences, circumstances) or ends in abuse; the challenge to produce a counter-case (p. 116). Ethics becomes psychology and sociology; Kant's imperative explained by fear of society, hedonism by the observed link of morality and happiness — causes mistaken for definitions.
5. **Religion, the same way.** God's existence is not demonstrable (premises would have to be tautologies), not probable (a transcendent being entails no experience), so 'God exists' is non-significant — and so are its denial and the agnostic's suspension: atheism and agnosticism go too (Price's point). 'Jehovah is angry' meaning 'it is thundering' is significant. Mystical intuition and the yellow patch: 'I am seeing God' claims a transcendent object; 'I am seeing a yellow patch' does not. No conflict between science and religion is possible, only loss of motive.
6. **Why it mattered / what refuted it.** Mattered: for a generation normative ethics was not philosophy; meta-ethics was. Refuted: (a) embedding — 'if stealing is wrong, then getting your brother to steal is wrong; stealing is wrong; so…' is valid; on emotivism the antecedent of the conditional expresses nothing (no one disapproves in an 'if'), so the argument's premise has a content that force does not supply — the same sentence must *say* something as well as *do* something (the force/content split the Austin lesson makes precise); (b) Stevenson's disagreement in attitude answers Ayer's 'no real disputes' by giving disputes a non-factual object; (c) Ayer's own 1946 concessions (ethical statements often classify actions; the theory does not need positivism). Survived: expressivism (Blackburn, Gibbard) — emotivism with a solution to embedding; the demand that any meta-ethics explain the embedding data; and the return of normative theory in 1971, which the closing module glances at.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The lesson is about what the criterion entails for ethics and about the argument structure; it takes no ethical stand and does not caricature religion. Ayer's own register is polemical; quote him, do not match him.
- 'Subjectivism' has two senses in Ayer (orthodox: asserts feelings; radical: expresses them); keep them apart with the 'tolerance' example.
- The open question argument belongs to Moore (1903); the lesson states it in one paragraph as a tool Ayer uses, with the `<Ref>` to the extra lesson for the full treatment and the 'proves too much' objection — the core path must not depend on the extra.
- The embedding objection is stated in the lesson's words, with Geach and Searle in References only (L8a).
- Stevenson 1937/1944 is not on disk; one sentence, flagged.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Rewrite 'you ought to keep your promises' and 'it was cruel of him' in the emotive form, marking what is stated (the facts) and what is expressed.
2. (refute) "Emotivism says that ethical sentences report the speaker's feelings." Break it with the 'tolerance is a virtue' exchange: state what would follow for contradiction if the claim were true, and what Ayer says instead.
3. (derivation — the embedding objection in full) Take 'if lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong' and 'lying is wrong'; show the inference is valid; then try to render each premise emotively and locate the premise that cannot be rendered. State in one sentence what any successor theory must provide.
4. (boundary probe) 'Jehovah is angry' is significant when it means 'it is thundering' and not when Jehovah is transcendent. Construct the borderline: a deity identified with a natural object *and* credited with intentions. Which conjunct makes the sentence lose significance, and does the criterion's weak form really exclude it?
5. (claim–evidence) Ayer challenges the reader to produce an ethical dispute that is not about facts. Produce one (two people agreeing on every fact of a case and disagreeing on the verdict), and then say what Stevenson's 'disagreement in attitude' adds to Ayer's 'mere abuse' as a description of what is happening.
6. (FORWARD → austin-speech-acts) Classify 'I promise to pay you' on the three bins: it is not a tautology, no observation makes it true or false, and it is plainly not an expression of feeling. State what it *does*, and what that implies about a criterion that sorts only statements. The solution names the performative with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped) Ayer explains the categorical imperative by fear of God and society (p. 117). Design the observation that would distinguish 'causes mistaken for definitions' from 'definitions with causes' — what would have to vary across societies for Ayer's explanation to be tested — and say what it shows about the claim that ethics *reduces* to sociology.

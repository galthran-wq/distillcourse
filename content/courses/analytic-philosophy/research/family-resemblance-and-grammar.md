## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** The games: list them, try the candidates (amusement — chess for money; winning — patience; skill — ring-a-ring-a-roses; rules — the child and the wall). Each candidate excludes a game or admits a non-game. Frege (Grundgesetze II §56, via the lesson's first module): a concept without sharp boundaries is no concept, as a region without a boundary is no region. Sub-problem: is 'game' defective, or was the demand wrong? Obvious attempt: there must be a common feature we have not yet found — or (§67's interlocutor) the 'disjunction of all the common properties'. Why the second fails: a disjunction of features is not a feature, and 'you are only playing with words'.
2. **Look.** §66: 'don't think, but look!' — the result is 'a complicated network of similarities overlapping and criss-crossing', like the resemblances among members of a family. §67: the thread — 'the strength of the thread does not reside in the fact that some one fibre runs through its whole length, but in the overlapping of many fibres'. 'Number' as the same kind of concept (cardinals, rationals, reals, complex — each extended by analogy). The because: a concept can be held together by overlap alone, and 'game' is one.
3. **Boundaries and exactness.** §§68–71: we can draw a boundary for a special purpose, but the concept did not have one — and is not therefore unusable: an indistinct photograph is a picture; 'stand roughly there' is an instruction one can follow. §69: 'one pace = 75 cm' is a definition of 'pace' no one needed; 'you still owe me a definition of exactness'. §88: 'dinner at one o'clock exactly' — exactness is relative to a purpose, and 'inexact' is a reproach only against a standard. The because: Frege's demand confused a concept's usefulness with its having a sharp extension.
4. **Where the demand came from: the sublimity of logic.** §§89–97: logic seemed to lie at the bottom of all the sciences, to demand 'the purest crystal'; §97's 'the incomparable essence of language' — the Tractatus's own words turned over; §103 the glasses on our nose we never think of taking off; §107: 'the crystalline purity of logic was not a result of investigation: it was a requirement' — 'we have got on to slippery ice where there is no friction… back to the rough ground!'; §108: 'proposition' and 'language' are families too, so the Tractatus's general form of the proposition was tracing round a frame.
5. **The method, then.** §109: 'we must do away with all explanation, and description alone must take its place'; 'philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language'; §115 'a picture held us captive'; §116 bringing words back from their metaphysical to their everyday use; §122 perspicuous representation ('seeing connections'); §123 'a philosophical problem has the form: I don't know my way about'; §124 philosophy leaves everything as it is — mathematics too; §125 the civil status of a contradiction; §127 reminders; §128 no theses; §130 language-games as objects of comparison, not approximations to an ideal; §133 'the real discovery is the one that makes me capable of stopping'; therapies. §309 the fly-bottle; §371 'essence is expressed by grammar'. The because: if concepts are families and problems are confusions produced by pictures, a philosophy that *explained* would add another picture; what cures is description that lets one see the uses.
6. **Why it mattered / what refuted or limited it.** Mattered: the method of Oxford (next module) and the suspicion of necessary-and-sufficient conditions in every field; prototype theory in cognitive psychology (Rosch — one clause, flagged). Refuted or limited: (a) natural kinds — 'gold' and 'water' *do* have something in common, found by chemistry, so family resemblance is a truth about 'game' and not about every general term (Kripke/Putnam, forward); (b) 'no theses' is itself hard to hold — Williams: the Investigations are 'perfectly straightforward' sentences whose point is the mystery, and 'almost no therefore'; (c) Gettier's analysis of 'know' goes ahead as if §66 had never been written, and the course's closing lesson asks who was right. Survived: the family-resemblance concept, the diagnosis of the crystalline ideal, and the question whether philosophy can be only description.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- 'Family resemblance' is not vagueness: a vague concept has borderline cases; a family-resemblance concept lacks a *common* feature. Keep the two apart; the photograph is about vagueness, the games about commonality.
- Do not turn §§109–133 into a manifesto for quietism; state them, state Williams's complaint, state the open question.
- Frege's boundary demand is from the Grundgesetze; quote via the Frege lesson's apparatus, not as a new source in the body.
- The OCR of this stretch is adequate; verify §§67, 107, 115, 123, 133 wording against print.
- No duck-rabbit here (Part II is not on this module's path).

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Extend the games matrix with three games of your own and one non-game (a tax return) and show that the non-game is excluded without any single column doing the excluding.
2. (refute) "Family resemblance just means the concept is vague." Break it: give a sharp concept that is a family (every 'number' is exactly a number) and a vague concept with a common feature ('tall'), and state the difference in one sentence.
3. (derivation) Reconstruct the argument of §107 that the crystalline purity of logic was 'a requirement, not a result': state what the Tractatus took as data, what it took as demand, and show by the games case that the demand was doing the work.
4. (boundary probe) §124 says philosophy leaves mathematics as it is. Take a contradiction in a formal system (any inconsistent set of axioms): what does 'the civil status of a contradiction' (§125) ask about it that a mathematician's 'the system is useless' does not? Say whether the lesson's method can answer it without a thesis.
5. (claim–evidence) Williams: the sentences of the Investigations are straightforward but have 'almost no therefore'. Take §§65–67 and write out the argument *with* its 'therefore's; then say what, if anything, is lost by doing so — which is the method's own question.
6. (FORWARD → rule-following) If 'game' has no essence, what makes a new activity's being called 'a game' *correct* rather than merely done? Try to state the rule a competent speaker follows when extending the word; the solution says the next lesson shows that no rule contains its own applications, with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped) Prototype theory in psychology (Rosch, 1970s) found that subjects rate 'robin' a better bird than 'penguin' and verify 'a robin is a bird' faster. Say which of §§66–71's claims this supports, which it does not touch (grammar vs psychology — §§89–90), and what experiment would bear on the latter.

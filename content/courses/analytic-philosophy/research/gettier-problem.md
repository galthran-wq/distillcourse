## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** Since the Theaetetus (201) knowledge has been analysed as true belief plus an account; the 1950s versions (a), (b), (c) differ in the third word. A three-page note in 1963 constructs two cases in which all three conditions hold and nobody says the person knows; sixty years of repairs follow. Sub-problem: read the note as a proof — what are its lemmas, what rule does each case use, and where does it stop being logic? Obvious attempt: read it as a report of two odd examples. The lesson reads it instead as a *model-theoretic* refutation: a structure satisfying all the conditions and not the conclusion.
2. **The schema and the lemmas.** (a) $S$ knows $P$ IFF $P$ true, $S$ believes $P$, $S$ justified in believing $P$ — a biconditional, so a proposed analysis in the course's first sense (conditions; the Frege module's 'exactly $n$'; the `<Ref>`). Lemma 1 (fallibilism): justification does not entail truth — one can be justified in believing a falsehood. Lemma 2 (closure): if $S$ is justified in $P$, and $P$ entails $Q$, and $S$ deduces $Q$ and accepts it on that basis, $S$ is justified in $Q$. Both stated in one paragraph on p. 121 'to be kept in mind'. The because: a counterexample to sufficiency needs a justified belief that is true for the wrong reason, and the two lemmas are exactly what lets justification flow from a falsehood to a truth.
3. **Case I with Russell.** Smith has strong evidence for (d) 'Jones will get the job and Jones has ten coins in his pocket' (the president's assurance; he counted). (d) entails (e) 'the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket'; Smith deduces it. Unknown to Smith, *he* gets the job and has ten coins. (e) is true (of Smith), believed, justified (by closure from (d)) — and not known: 'it is equally clear that Smith does not know (e)'. Read (e) with the theory of descriptions (`<Ref>`): $\exists x\,(Jx \wedge \forall y(Jy \to y = x) \wedge Cx)$ — a quantified sentence with no subject, true because Smith satisfies it, while Smith's evidence concerned Jones; the description is *attributive* (whoever gets the job) and Smith took it referentially (Jones). The because: the description's logical form is what lets a sentence be true of one man and justified by evidence about another.
4. **Case II with Ayer.** Smith has strong evidence for (f) 'Jones owns a Ford'; he constructs (g), (h), (i) — '(f) or Brown is in Boston / Barcelona / Brest-Litovsk' — place names chosen at random; by closure he is justified in each. Jones has a rented car; Brown, by sheer coincidence, is in Barcelona. (h) is true, believed, justified — not known. The rule: $P \vdash P \vee Q$ for *any* $Q$; justification transfers to a disjunction at no cost, and truth arrives from the other disjunct. This is the move that let 'the Absolute is lazy' through the verification criterion (`<Ref>` verification-principle §4: $S$ with $S \to O$ entails $O$ — the same cheapness of logical consequence). The because: closure plus a rule that manufactures consequences from any premise lets justification reach a sentence whose truth-maker it never touched.
5. **The conclusion and what it assumes.** (a) is not sufficient; (b) and (c) fall by substitution of their third clause. The whole weight rests on 'it is equally clear that Smith does not know' — the verdict is intuited, in the method's standard way since Moore (`<Ref>` extra, optional): the reader's agreement is the data. The analytic paper's form, on the page: lettered propositions, entailment, a biconditional target, a constructed model — the style Bayer sets beside Lyotard on day two as what the tradition *is*.
6. **What came of it / where the artefact departs.** Fourth conditions: no false lemmas (Clark 1963 — blocks both cases, since each passes through a falsehood; defeated by cases with no false lemma, e.g. the fake-barn county); causal (Goldman 1967); reliabilist; defeasibility — each meets a new case; Williamson 2000: knowledge is unanalysable and the primitive in terms of which belief and evidence are explained — the method's concession that 'know' may have no conditions. The Wittgensteinian objection the paper never meets: 'know' may be a family (`<Ref>`), as the course found 'species' to be (`<Ref>`), and then the fourth-condition industry is the games matrix looking for a full column. Austin: 'enough is enough' — justification is relative to a purpose, not a condition. The data on the data: Weinberg, Nichols and Stich 2001 reported cross-cultural variation in Gettier intuitions; Machery et al. 2017, with larger samples across cultures, found the Gettier intuition robust — the intuition is data after all, and the method survived its own test. Verdict: which course results the reading used (conditions; descriptions; disjunction; closure); where the artefact departs (the four points); and the course's last sentence on the method: a style that can refute an analysis in three pages, and that owes an account — still owed — of why the intuition it rests on should count.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Write schema (a) as a biconditional in logical notation and state what a counterexample to *sufficiency* must satisfy and fail; then state what a counterexample to *necessity* would look like and why Gettier offers none.
2. (derivation — Case II) From (f) and closure derive justification for (h); from 'Brown is in Barcelona' derive the truth of (h); show that (f) is false; conclude that (h) is a justified true belief with its justification and its truth-maker disjoint. Then show that the same construction works for any true proposition $Q$ whatever.
3. (derivation — Case I with Russell) Write (e) in the theory of descriptions, show that Smith's evidence supports the existential and uniqueness conjuncts via Jones and the predicate conjunct via Jones, and that the sentence is true via Smith; say which reading (attributive) makes the case work and whether the referential reading (Jones) would.
4. (boundary probe — no false lemmas) Add the condition 'the justification passes through no false belief' and show it blocks both cases (name the false lemma in each). Then construct, in outline, a case with no false lemma that still fails (the fake-barn county: a true belief formed by a reliable process in an environment where it could easily have been false) and say which lemma of Gettier's it does *not* need.
5. (refute) "Gettier's cases rely on a strange sense of 'justified'; on an ordinary sense Smith was not justified." Break it: state what Lemma 1 (fallibilism) requires of 'justified' and why any sense strong enough to block the cases would make justification entail truth — collapsing the third condition into the first.
6. (claim–evidence — intuition as data) 'It is equally clear that Smith does not know (e).' State what kind of evidence this is, what the 2001 study claimed and the 2017 study found (abstract level), and what the method's answer must be if the intuition had varied — then say which of the course's figures (Moore; Austin; Wittgenstein) would accept intuition as data and which would ask what 'know' is *used* for.
7. (cross-lesson — the same rule twice) Disjunction introduction sank the verification criterion in 1946 and the JTB analysis in 1963. Write the general form — 'a condition $C$ on sentences that is closed under logical consequence admits $P \vee Q$ whenever it admits $P$' — and state what any criterion or analysis must add to resist it (a condition that is not closed under consequence); then say whether 'knowledge' plausibly has such a condition, which is where the course leaves the question.

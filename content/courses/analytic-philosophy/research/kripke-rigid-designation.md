# Research brief: kripke-rigid-designation (Rigid designation and the necessary/a priori split)

status: planned
module: kripke-and-putnam
type: core
goal: Distinguish the a priori, the necessary and the analytic, define rigid designation, and derive from it that a true identity between names is necessary even when it was discovered.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From a-priori-as-analytic: the three senses of 'analytic' and the Circle's identification of necessary with a priori with analytic; from two-dogmas-analyticity: Quine's conflation of the three and his hostility to de re necessity ('undreamed of in my philosophy'); from russell-theory-of-descriptions: descriptions and scope (and the proof-checker lesson's FORWARD exercise on a description under a modal operator); from frege-sense-and-reference: names as carrying senses.
- **Contributes:** The first of the two moves that rebuilt theory after the ordinary-language programme: three notions pulled apart (Goldbach; the prime-number machine); possible worlds as stipulated (the dice); rigid vs nonrigid designators and the modal test; rigidity is not scope; the necessity of identity ($\forall x\forall y\,(x = y \to \Box\, x = y)$); reference-fixing vs meaning-giving (the standard metre; the contingent a priori); the necessary a posteriori previewed.
- **Hands off:** To kripke-against-descriptivism (rigidity kills thesis 6 of the description theory), to necessary-a-posteriori (Hesperus/Phosphorus and kinds), to lewis-possible-worlds (what worlds are if not stipulations), to functionalism-and-its-objections (the modal argument), to meaning-as-use (the standard metre answered).

Spine step: "Necessity and reference come apart from the a priori and from description: names and kind terms designate rigidly, true identities between them are necessary though discovered, reference runs through a causal chain, and meaning is fixed by the world and the community rather than the head."

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** 'Nixon might have lost': true. 'The President in 1970 might not have been President': true on one reading (the man might not have held the office), absurd on another (whoever is President is President). 'Nixon might not have been Nixon': just absurd. Sub-problem: what difference between 'Nixon' and 'the President in 1970' do the readings track? Obvious attempt (Russell, and Quine's suspicion of de re modality): a scope ambiguity — 'the President' can take wide or narrow scope under 'might', and 'Nixon' is a disguised description that happens to take wide scope. Why it fails (Preface pp. 10–14): in a *simple* sentence — 'Nixon might have lost' — there is no operator for the name to take scope over, and the intuition is the same; the difference is in the term, not in the syntax.
2. **Three notions, pulled apart.** Kant and the Circle (and Quine) treated 'necessary', 'a priori' and 'analytic' as one; they are three: necessary (could it have been otherwise? — metaphysical), a priori (can it be known without experience? — epistemic), analytic (true by meaning — semantic). Goldbach's conjecture: if true, necessary (a counterexample would be a finite computation that could not fail), yet known a priori by nobody; 'can be known a priori' is not 'must be' (the prime-number machine: one may learn a priori-knowable truths by machine). The because: a confusion of the three is what made the necessary a posteriori unthinkable — the Circle's cell was emptied by definition, not by argument.
3. **Possible worlds, stipulated.** Two dice: 36 states, 21 qualitative outcomes; 'possible worlds are little more than the miniworlds of school probability blown large'; nobody asks for a criterion of 'trans-state identity' for die A. A world is *stipulated* ('suppose Nixon had lost'), not discovered through a telescope; 'transworld identification' is a pseudo-problem because we start from the object and describe counterfactual situations about *it*. The because: the semantics the reader may know (Kripke frames) needs only this much, and the metaphysical worry came from the telescope picture. (Lewis's disagreement is the closing module.)
4. **Rigid designators.** A term is rigid if it designates the same object in every possible world in which it designates at all; 'Nixon' is rigid, 'the President in 1970' is not (Humphrey holds it in some worlds). Test: 'the President in 1970 might not have been the President in 1970' has a true reading; 'Nixon might not have been Nixon' has none. 'Aristotle was fond of dogs' vs Russell's 'exactly one person was last among the great philosophers of antiquity and was fond of dogs': same truth-value here, different counterfactual conditions — in a world where Aristotle never did philosophy, the first is about Aristotle and the second about whoever did. The because: names track the object across stipulations; descriptions track whatever satisfies them. De jure vs de facto rigidity: 'the smallest prime' is rigid de facto (it is always 2); names are rigid by stipulation.
5. **The necessity of identity.** From $\forall x\,\Box(x = x)$ and Leibniz's law: $\forall x\forall y\,(x = y \to \Box\, x = y)$ — the two-line argument (Preface p. 3): a counterexample would be a pair that are distinct (antecedent false) or an object and itself (consequent true). So if 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' are rigid and 'Hesperus = Phosphorus' is true, it is necessary — though astronomers discovered it. The three theses kept apart (p. 4): the object-level theorem is logic; 'identities between rigid designators are necessary' is logic plus a definition; 'identities between ordinary names are necessary' needs the empirical claim that names are rigid. 'Contingent identity' was a doctrine about non-rigid designators (the inventor of bifocals = the first Postmaster General, contingent because both are descriptions). Preview: the necessary a posteriori (next-but-one lesson); and no universal substitutivity — belief contexts are left open (pp. 20–21).
6. **Reference-fixing vs meaning-giving: the contingent a priori.** 'One metre' is fixed by the standard bar $S$ at $t_0$; 'one metre' then rigidly designates that length; 'S is one metre long at $t_0$' is known a priori by whoever fixed the reference, and is contingent ($S$ might have been longer). Wittgenstein's §50 ('neither one metre long nor not') answered: the bar is one metre long, and the statement is contingent a priori. Frege's 'sense' conflated giving a meaning with fixing a reference (n. 22: in modal semantics, the sense of a rigid term is a constant function). Neptune (n. 33, forward from the Quine module's application) as the astronomical case.
7. **Why it mattered / what refuted or refined it.** Mattered: metaphysics back in business after Quine; the modal semantics given an interpretation its users could state; the three-notion table as the course's own result (outcome co-three-notions). Refined: Dummett's reply that rigidity is a scope convention (answered in the Preface); two-dimensionalism (Chalmers, Jackson), which keeps Kripke's data and re-attaches an a priori 'primary intension' — named only; Barcan Marcus's 1961 'tags' as the precursor (one clause). Kripke's own disclaimer: a picture, not a reductive analysis of modality.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The reader may know Kripke frames; the lesson uses no modal logic beyond $\Box$ and 'in every world', and says once that the semantics is the reader's and the interpretation is the lesson's.
- 'Rigid' is a property of a *term*; 'necessary' of a *truth*; keep the two words from migrating.
- The necessity of identity is a theorem of the object language; its application to *names* is the empirical thesis — the Preface's three theses must be kept apart in the lesson as in the text.
- The lectures were given without notes: Kripke flags compressions; the lesson should not present the 'picture' as a theory.
- The two Kripkes (this book; the 1982 rule-following book) — one clause to prevent confusion with the Wittgenstein module.
- Dummett's and two-dimensionalism's replies are named from the standard account; no text on disk beyond the Preface's response.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Classify as rigid or nonrigid, with the test sentence: 'Nixon'; 'the 37th President'; 'the smallest prime'; '$\pi$'; 'the number of planets'; 'the actual number of planets' (the last with Kripke's 'actual F' stipulation).
2. (derivation) Prove the necessity of identity from $\forall x\,\Box(x = x)$ and Leibniz's law in the reader's own modal logic, and then show that 'the inventor of bifocals = the first Postmaster General' is contingent without contradicting the theorem (the terms are not rigid).
3. (boundary probe — from the Circle) '$7 + 5 = 12$' is necessary, a priori and (on the Circle's view) analytic. Say which of the three 'water is H2O' has and which it lacks, and what Kant's grid of the a-priori-as-analytic lesson does with it — the cell that was declared empty.
4. (refute) "Rigidity is just wide scope: 'Nixon might have lost' means 'of Nixon, it might have been that he lost', and a description can be given wide scope too." Break it with the simple-sentence point (no operator to scope over) and with the 'Aristotle was fond of dogs' counterfactual comparison.
5. (claim–evidence — Goldbach) Suppose a computer finds a counterexample tomorrow. State whether the conjecture was necessary, a priori, or analytic yesterday, and what Addendum (b) says about learning a necessary truth a posteriori; then say what the prime-number machine shows about 'can be known a priori'.
6. (FORWARD → kripke-against-descriptivism) If 'Aristotle' were synonymous with 'the teacher of Alexander', then 'Aristotle taught Alexander' would be necessary. Say in one sentence why it is not, and what this does to the claim that a name *means* a description.

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** The semantics the reader may already use — '$\Box p$' true iff $p$ true at every world accessible from here — was given, by Kripke, an interpretation on which worlds are stipulations and 'in every world' is 'however things might have been'. Quine's question (`<Ref>` holism): a theory is committed to what its variables range over; the semantics quantifies over worlds; what are they? Obvious attempt: nothing — 'world' is a façon de parler, and modal talk is primitive. Why Lewis thinks it fails: a semantics with a domain nobody will state is a semantics whose predictions nobody can check; and if the modal is primitive, nothing has been explained.
2. **Modal realism.** Worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated universes, as real as ours ('actual' is indexical, like 'here'); there is one for every way a world could be; modal truths are truths about them ('Humphrey might have won' — in some world, a Humphrey-counterpart wins). The argument: Quine's own — the hypothesis is serviceable (it analyses modality, counterfactuals, properties, propositions as sets of worlds), and serviceability is the only reason to believe anything exists, so we are committed. The because: Lewis takes the indispensability argument the Quine lesson used for classes and runs it on worlds; Carnap's 'external question, practical' (`<Ref>`) becomes a theoretical one with an answer.
3. **Counterpart theory.** Nothing exists in more than one world; 'Nixon might have lost' is true because in some world a *counterpart* of Nixon — the thing there most similar to him — loses; counterparthood is similarity, not identity, so it can be non-transitive and non-symmetric and one thing may have several counterparts in one world. The formal defect (Kripke n. 13): universal instantiation fails for modal predicates (from 'everything is necessarily self-identical' one cannot infer it of Nixon via a counterpart that is not him). The Humphrey objection: 'Humphrey could not care less whether someone else, no matter how much resembling him, would have been victorious' — the truth-maker for 'he might have won' is about someone else. Lewis's reply (standard, to be verified): Humphrey *himself* has the property of possibly winning, in virtue of his counterpart's winning — the counterpart is the truth-maker, not the subject.
4. **The cheaper answers.** Ersatz worlds: maximal consistent sets of sentences (linguistic ersatzism), or of propositions, or states of affairs — abstract stand-ins; the price: either primitive modality ('consistent' is modal) or a shortage of worlds (alien properties no sentence names) — Lewis's 'incredulous stare' is the price he pays and the one he says the others do not avoid. Kripke's position restated: the dice — a world is a stipulation, and the question 'what are they' is the question what one is describing when one says 'suppose…'.
5. **Why it mattered / what remained.** Mattered: the metaphysics of modality became a field with positions and costs (one paragraph: the course names Plantinga, Stalnaker, Armstrong as the ersatzist and actualist alternatives — names only); possible-worlds semantics for counterfactuals and for properties; Lewis's method — a theory's benefits bought at the price of an ontology — as the Quinean method at full stretch. Remained: the incredulous stare; Putnam's objection that intensions-as-functions leave 'grasping' unexplained and commit the semantics to psychologism (pp. 183–188); and the suspicion the Kripke lesson planted — that a stipulated world was all the semantics ever needed.

(boundary probe) 'I might have been twins' — MIT q10's 'cool thing': show that on strict transworld identity the sentence has no truth-maker (one thing cannot be two), and that counterpart theory handles it (two counterparts in one world); say what this buys and what Humphrey's objection says it costs.

Using Twin Earth, show that two speakers in the same state would then grasp the same intension and so have the same extension — the assumption Putnam rejected — and say which of Lewis's two assumptions (I)/(II) survives in his modified semantics (world, speaker, context).

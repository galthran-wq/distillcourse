# Research brief: meaning-as-use (Meaning as use and language-games)

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** The shopkeeper and 'five red apples': three words, three procedures (a drawer, a colour chart, counting), and only one with an object to stand for. Sub-problem: what is the meaning of 'five'? Obvious attempt (Augustine, Confessions I.8, quoted in §1; and the Tractatus): a word's meaning is the object it stands for; a sentence is a combination of names. Why it fails: 'five' stands for nothing in the shop; 'red' is checked against a sample, not matched to a red object; 'but how does he know where and how he is to look up the word red and what he is to do with the word five?' — 'well, I assume that he acts as I have described. Explanations come to an end somewhere.' The picture describes one job words do and takes it for all.
2. **The builders.** §2: a language with four words — block, pillar, slab, beam — in which A calls and B brings. This *is* the Augustinian language, complete. §§5–6: the words are taught by training, not by explanation; the child does not learn what 'slab' stands for and then use it — the use is what is learned. The because: once a whole language is exhibited in which naming is the entire job, one sees that it is a *limited* language, and that ours has more jobs.
3. **Language-games and forms of life.** §7: 'the whole, consisting of language and the actions into which it is woven'. §8: add numerals, 'this', 'there', colour samples — each a different kind of word with a different use. §23: the open list — giving orders, describing, reporting, speculating, forming and testing a hypothesis, making a joke, translating, asking, thanking, praying — 'countless kinds'. §19: 'to imagine a language means to imagine a form of life'. §§11–12: tools in a box, handles in a locomotive cab — the uniform look of words hides diverse functions. The because: the only thing the uses have in common is that they are woven into activities, so the unit of description is the game, not the name.
4. **PI §43 and what it rules out.** 'For a large class of cases — though not for all — the meaning of a word is its use in the language.' Then §§39–45: the meaning of a name is not its bearer — 'Excalibur has a sharp blade' makes sense when the sword is broken; 'Mr N. N. is dead' does not say the meaning died. The 'this' of §§38–45: the only genuine name on the Tractatus's demand turns out to be no name at all ('language goes on holiday', §38). State what §43 is not: not a theory of meaning (no definition of 'use'), and not 'anything goes' — the use is governed, which the rule-following lesson examines.
5. **The simples undone (§§46–64).** The Theaetetus and the Tractatus demand simples. §47: 'what are the simple constituent parts of a chair?' — the answer depends on what 'composite' means in a game; 'composite' has no absolute sense. §48: the coloured-squares game — nine squares, four colours, a 'sentence' RRBGGGRWW; are the squares simple or the colours? Only relative to the game. §50: the standard metre in Paris is neither one metre long nor not — it is an instrument of representation, not an object described; so 'there are simples' was a demand for instruments mistaken for a discovery about objects. §60: 'my broom is in the corner' versus its 'analysis' into broomstick and brush — the analysed sentence is not more fundamental, only different. The because: 'analysis' presupposed one complete analysis (3.25); there is none, since 'composite' is game-relative.
6. **Why it mattered / what refuted or refined it.** Mattered: the second strand of the tradition, Oxford's method, and the end of the search for an ideal language (§81: logic is not a theory of an ideal language). Refuted or refined: (a) 'use' is not yet a theory — Grice's intentions and Searle's speech acts build one where the Investigations refuse to (forward); (b) the standard metre: Kripke's reply that the bar is one metre long, contingently, and that 'one metre' is fixed by it as a reference-fixer (forward); (c) a kind term's extension is fixed by what its samples *are*, not only by how speakers use them (Putnam) — use with a world attached. Survived: §43 as the first sentence of every pragmatics; the suspicion of the one picture.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) For the words in 'bring me two blue slabs, now', say what the shopkeeper's counterpart does with each (object, sample, count, action), and which word the Augustinian picture fits.
2. (refute) "Meaning as use is a theory: a word's meaning is defined as the set of its uses." Break it with §43's hedge and §23's open list: state why no definition of 'use' is given and what would be lost (the uses are a family, next lesson) if one were.
3. (derivation) Reconstruct the argument of §§46–50 that 'composite' has no absolute sense as a reductio on the Tractatus's 3.25 ('one and only one complete analysis'), using the chessboard and the coloured-squares game; identify the premise about 'simple' that the Tractatus needed and the game that falsifies it.
4. (boundary probe) The standard metre: say why 'this bar is one metre long' has no use in the game that *defines* the metre by the bar, and whether a bar that is used to define a unit and later measured by a better standard (the 1960 krypton line) changes the answer — this is the doorstep of Kripke's reply (the solution says so).
5. (claim–evidence) §2's builders: Wittgenstein says the picture is 'right for a restricted region'. State the two features of the builders' game that make the picture true there, and name one word of §8's expansion ('this' / a numeral / a sample) for which it is false, with the reason.
6. (FORWARD → family-resemblance-and-grammar) List five uses of the word 'game' from §23's spirit (board, card, ball, a child throwing a ball at a wall, ring-a-ring-a-roses) and try to write what they all have in common; the solution shows each candidate failing and says the next lesson names the structure that remains, with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped) Augustine's passage (Confessions I.8) describes how he learned words by watching adults point and name. Design the observation that would distinguish 'learned by ostension' from 'learned by training in a practice' for a word like 'five' — what a child would have to do, or fail to do — and say what §§5–6 predict.

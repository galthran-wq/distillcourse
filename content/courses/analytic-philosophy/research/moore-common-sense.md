## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** 'Here is one hand, and here is another': offered, hands raised, as a proof that external things exist, with the claim that the premises are known better than any philosophical premise that denies them. And 'good' cannot be defined: 'X is pleasant, but is it good?' remains a question. Both refuse to let a theory overrule what everyone can say. Sub-problem: what gives common sense that authority, and whether the open question proves too much. Obvious attempt: Moore is dogmatic — he asserts what the sceptic denies. The lesson shows the argument structure that makes the assertion a *move*.
2. **The rebellion.** Bradley's monism: nothing exists except the whole, relations are internal, so arithmetic is at the mercy of psychology (IEP); Moore (1898–1903) and Russell after him break out — 'escaped from a hot house onto a windswept headland… grass really is green'. The Refutation of Idealism (1903): 'esse est percipi' read as a definition of 'to be' — then it is either a tautology that proves nothing or a substantive claim that needs proof; the method is Moore's — isolate the sentence, ask what it means, and the idealist's thesis dissolves. The because: the idealist's conclusion depended on a sentence doing two jobs at once.
3. **The Defence (1925) and the Proof (1939).** A list of propositions ('there exists at present a living human body which is my body'; 'the earth existed for many years before my body was born') known with certainty; any philosophical argument against them has a premise less certain than they are, so the argument is to be rejected rather than the proposition. The Proof: 'here is one hand, here is another; therefore two external things exist' — the premises known, the inference valid, the conclusion follows; what Moore cannot do is *prove the premises*, and he says so. The structure: a modus-tollens on scepticism (if the sceptic's argument were sound, I would not know this; I do; so it is not sound). The reaction: Wittgenstein's On Certainty — 'I know' has no use for hinge propositions; Moore misdescribed the certainty as knowledge (one paragraph).
4. **The open question argument.** Principia Ethica: 'good' is simple, like 'yellow'; a horse can be defined by its parts, 'yellow' and 'good' cannot; for any natural property $N$ proposed as the definition, 'this is $N$, but is it good?' is open — it is not the trivial question 'this is $N$, but is it $N$?' — so no such definition is an analysis; to offer one is the naturalistic fallacy. Why it mattered: Ayer's emotivism (`<Ref>`) and all of meta-ethics took the result as data.
5. **It proves too much.** 'This is H2O, but is it water?' is also an open question to anyone who does not know the chemistry — yet water *is* H2O, and the identification is correct and informative. So the test does not distinguish 'not an analysis' from 'an analysis the speaker has not yet learned'. The paradox of analysis (Langford 1942): if 'A is C' is a correct analysis, A and C mean the same and it is trivial; if not, it is incorrect — so no analysis is both correct and informative. Frege's way out (`<Ref>` sense-and-reference): same reference, different sense; an analysis is 'informative by being transformative' (Beaney). The because: Moore ran the test on *meanings* and the test only works where meaning is transparent to the speaker — Kripke and Putnam (forward) will show that for kind terms it is not.
6. **Why it matters off the main path.** The Cambridge strand: Stebbing's 1930 textbook, the journal Analysis (1933, Stebbing, Duncan-Jones, Mace, Ryle), the name 'analytic philosophy' first used as an insult (Collingwood 1933); the Defence as the ancestor of Oxford's 'ordinary use' and of Austin's 'words are our tools'; the revisionist point (IEP §5c) that Moore did not think of himself as a linguistic philosopher. What descended from each: the Defence → ordinary-language method and On Certainty; the open question → emotivism, and its 'proves too much' → the necessary a posteriori.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) State Moore's Proof as premises and conclusion and name the one thing Moore admits he cannot do.
2. (refute) "The Proof begs the question: it assumes what the sceptic denies." Break it: state the modus tollens structure, say what Moore claims about the relative certainty of the premises, and identify what the sceptic must then do (produce a premise more certain than 'here is a hand').
3. (derivation) Run the open question test on three proposed definitions of 'good' (pleasure; what is desired; what one would desire on reflection) and then on 'water is H2O'; state the feature of the H2O case that shows the test cannot distinguish 'no analysis' from 'an unknown analysis'.
4. (boundary probe) Suppose someone knows the chemistry and still asks 'this is H2O, but is it water?' State what, on Frege's sense/reference, the question could be asking (a difference of sense with one reference), and whether the open question argument survives once that is admitted.
5. (claim–evidence) On Certainty says 'I know' has no use for 'here is a hand'. Construct the ordinary use of 'I know I have hands' (a context of doubt: after an accident) and the philosophical one; say which of Moore's propositions is a hinge and which the sceptic can actually doubt.
6. (cross-lesson — the course's own) The water/H2O case appears in this lesson as a refutation of Moore and in the Kripke module as a necessary a posteriori truth. Write the one sentence that connects them: what Moore's test assumes about meaning that the necessary a posteriori denies.
7. (research-shaped) Collingwood used 'analytic philosophy' as an insult in 1933; the journal Analysis was founded the same year. From the lesson's dates (Begriffsschrift 1879, Tractatus 1921, Stebbing's textbook 1930), state how long the tradition existed before it had a name, and what that does to the question 'what is analytic philosophy?' — Beaney's own family-resemblance answer, in two sentences.

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** 'Water is H2O': discovered, and yet no possible world has water that is not H2O — a world with a look-alike liquid has no water in it. Both empirical and could-not-be-otherwise: Kant's grid forbids it (the Circle emptied the synthetic-a-priori cell; this is a different cell — necessary a posteriori — that nobody had drawn). Sub-problem: what kind of truth is it, and why does it feel contingent? Obvious attempt: it is not necessary — 'water might have turned out not to be H2O'; the feeling of contingency is evidence. The lesson's whole work is to show that the feeling is real and the contingency is not.
2. **Hesperus and Phosphorus, first.** Two rigid names, one planet; by the necessity of identity (`<Ref>`) the identity is necessary; and it was an astronomical discovery. So necessary a posteriori — the 'might' in 'Hesperus might not have been Phosphorus' is *epistemic*: for all we knew. Compare 'the four-colour theorem might turn out to be false' said before the proof — nobody thinks the theorem's truth-value was contingent. Quine's 'tag the planet twice' and Marcus's reply (one paragraph). The because: an epistemic possibility is a possibility about what our evidence leaves open, not about how the world could have been.
3. **Explaining the illusion.** Why does it *feel* contingent? Because there is a possible world *qualitatively identical to ours in all the evidence* in which two different bodies play the evening-star and morning-star roles; in that world the speakers' 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' name two things — but that is not a world where Hesperus is not Phosphorus; it is a world where *our* names, fixed as they were, were fixed on different objects. Formally: $R_1$ and $R_2$ rigid, reference fixed by non-rigid $D_1$ and $D_2$; '$R_1 = R_2$' necessary, '$D_1 = D_2$' contingent; we confuse the second's contingency with the first's (pp. 140–144). The because: what we imagine when we 'imagine Hesperus not being Phosphorus' is $D_1 \neq D_2$.
4. **Essences of origin.** This table could not have been made of ice (a table made of ice would be another table); Elizabeth II could not have been the Trumans' daughter. Not 'could not have had different properties' — the table might have been painted — but origin is essential. n. 56 sketched for this reader: from the necessity of distinctness (or the necessity of identity with the Brouwersche axiom — symmetric accessibility), plus the premise that making a table from another hunk does not affect whether this one could be made from its hunk. Kripke's own later admission that the footnote is problematic (Preface p. 2). The because: 'could have been otherwise' is about this object, and what makes it this object is where it came from, not what it is like.
5. **Kind terms are rigid.** 'Gold' is not a cluster ('yellow, metallic, valuable'): fool's gold has all the marks and is not gold; if gold turned out blue (an optical illusion in mines; Kant's 'yellow metal' was not even a priori) 'newspapers would announce gold is blue', not 'gold does not exist'; tigers with three legs, or reptilian look-alikes (Ziff); 'cats are animals' and the demon-cats (Putnam, n. 62). Reference of 'gold' is fixed by a sample ('the substance instantiated by almost all of these'), passed along a chain (`<Ref>`), and what it *is* — the element with atomic number 79 — is discovered. Mill was right about names and wrong about kinds; Frege/Russell the reverse; Kripke: kind terms are like names. Water is H2O; light is a stream of photons; heat is molecular motion — theoretical identifications, necessary a posteriori; polywater as the 'what if it had turned out otherwise' case.
6. **Heat: the sensation fixes the reference.** 'Heat' was fixed as 'whatever causes sensation $S$'; what causes $S$ is molecular motion; so heat is molecular motion, necessarily — a world where photons caused $S$ is not a world where heat is photons, but one where something else felt the way heat feels; and 'heat might have been something else' is the loose way of saying 'something else might have caused $S$' (pp. 131–133). This is the model the mind–body argument will test and find wanting (closing module).
7. **Why it mattered / what refuted or limited it.** Mattered: essentialism rehabilitated after Quine's 'undreamed of'; scientific identifications read as discoveries of essence; a new cell in Kant's grid. Limited: (a) Salmon's charge (standard, not on disk) that the essentialism is assumed in the premise that fixes 'water' by *chemical* sameness rather than derived from the semantics — one paragraph; (b) two-dimensionalism (Chalmers, Jackson): 'water' has a primary intension (the watery stuff around here — a priori) and a secondary one (H2O — necessary), so 'necessary a posteriori' becomes two intensions on one sentence — named; (c) the application lesson's finding that the chemistry is messier than 'H2O' (next-but-one). Survived: the epistemic/metaphysical 'might'; rigid kind terms; the Hesperus explanation of the illusion.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Classify each as contingent a priori / necessary a posteriori / neither, with the reason: 'the metre bar is one metre long'; 'Hesperus is Phosphorus'; 'gold has atomic number 79'; 'Goldbach's conjecture'; 'water is wet'.
2. (derivation) Write the Hesperus case in the $R_1$/$R_2$/$D_1$/$D_2$ notation and prove that '$R_1 = R_2$' is necessary if true while '$D_1 = D_2$' may be contingent; then say which sentence a speaker 'imagines false' when imagining Hesperus not being Phosphorus.
3. (boundary probe — unicorns) Addendum (a): no possible world contains unicorns. Say why a world with one-horned horses is not a world with unicorns on the kind-term account (the myth fixes no sample), and contrast with 'tigers' — what fixes the reference there that fails for unicorns.
4. (refute) "'Water is H2O' is analytic now: it is how chemists define 'water'." Break it with the a priori/necessary/analytic table: show that the identity was discovered (not a priori), is necessary (rigid terms), and is not true by meaning (the stereotype 'clear, drinkable' does not mention H2O — Putnam's doorstep); say what 'define' is doing in the claim.
5. (claim–evidence — Salmon's charge) The argument for 'water is necessarily H2O' needs the premise that what counts as 'the same substance' across worlds is chemical composition. State where that premise enters (the sample-fixing step), whether it is semantic or metaphysical, and what the argument proves without it.
6. (FORWARD → putnam-twin-earth) The reference of 'gold' is fixed by a sample and a chain. Suppose two speakers, molecule-for-molecule alike, each fix 'gold' by a sample, and the samples differ in composition. Say whether their words mean the same, and what in their situation decides it. The solution says the next lesson's Twin Earth is this case, with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped — the heat model) Write the heat case as a template (a term fixed by an appearance; the referent found by science; the illusion explained by 'something else could have produced the appearance') and then apply it to 'pain': find the step where the template needs an appearance distinct from the thing, and say whether pain has one — the doorstep of the closing module's modal argument.

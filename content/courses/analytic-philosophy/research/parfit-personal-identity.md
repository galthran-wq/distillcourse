## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** The teletransporter: scanned, destroyed, rebuilt on Mars. Is the person on Mars you? Then the malfunction: two copies. Identity is one–one (`<Ref>` Kripke: $x = y$ and $x = z$ give $y = z$), so they cannot both be you; nothing favours one; so neither is. Yet each stands to you as the single copy did, and the single copy seemed as good as survival. Sub-problem: either 'will I survive?' asks for something we do not care about, or the setup has an error. Obvious attempt: find the error — the copies are not you because the original was destroyed; survival needs the same body. The lesson tests each criterion in turn.
2. **The criteria on offer.** Bodily: Ayer 1936 — one self iff the same body; survives memory loss and character change; makes surviving bodily annihilation 'self-contradictory'. Memory (Locke): the same person iff connected by memory; Butler's objection — memory presupposes identity (only I can remember *my* experiences), so the criterion is circular — answered by 'quasi-memory' (an apparent memory caused in the right way, whoever's it is). §404: 'I' is not a name, and the criteria are many — the grammar leaves the question open. The because of the dispute: each criterion gives verdicts on ordinary cases and diverges on the cases the technology makes possible; the cases are a test of which criterion tracks what we care about.
3. **The spectra and fission.** The Physical Spectrum: replace 1% of your cells, then 2%, … 100% — where does identity stop? Any line is arbitrary; the Psychological Spectrum likewise. Fission: one brain, two hemispheres each sufficient, transplanted into two bodies; both resulting persons are fully psychologically continuous with you. Identity cannot hold to both (one–one), cannot hold to one rather than the other (symmetry), so holds to neither — and yet 'double success' is not death; each outcome contains everything a single success did. The because: the only thing missing in fission is uniqueness, and uniqueness is not something the person cares about for its own sake.
4. **Relation R and the conclusion.** What matters is R: psychological connectedness (direct links — memories, intentions carried out, persisting character) and/or continuity (overlapping chains of connections), with any cause — normal, or a teletransporter, or a hemisphere transplant. Identity is R plus the constraint that R not branch; the constraint is a fact about logic, not about what we value. So: *identity is not what matters*. The teletransporter is 'about as good as ordinary survival'.
5. **Reductionism.** A person's existence consists in the existence of a brain and body and a series of interrelated physical and mental events; there is no 'further fact' — no Cartesian ego, no deep self — beyond these; questions of identity in the spectrum cases are *empty* (there is nothing more to know once the physical and psychological facts are in). The lineage: Hume's bundle; Russell's and Ayer's self as a logical construction (`<Ref>`) — the course's own earlier result, now with the ethics drawn out: the self-interest theory (give special weight to one's own future) is weakened, since the future self is connected by degrees, like a relative; compensation across a life is less different from compensation across persons; the separateness of persons (`<Ref>` Rawls) is a matter of degree. Named, not developed.
6. **Why it mattered / what refuted or reshaped it.** Mattered: the theory of persons as a field reorganised around 'what matters'; reductionism as the default of the next generation. Reshaped: animalism (Olson): we are organisms, the organism does not fission and does not teleport — identity is biological and R is a red herring; four-dimensionalism (Lewis): two persons were present all along, sharing their earlier stages — identity holds to both, because 'you' was two things; the narrative self (Schechtman) — named only. Survived: the fission case as the test every theory must pass; quasi-memory; the question whether identity or R is what the first-person 'I' tracks.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) For the teletransporter, the single hemisphere transplant and fission, give the verdict of the bodily criterion, the memory criterion and Relation R.
2. (derivation — fission) Write the argument: identity is one–one; the two results are symmetric with respect to the original; so identity holds to neither; then show that R holds to both; conclude what the case separates (identity from R) and what it does not touch (whether R is what matters — which needs a further premise about value; state it).
3. (boundary probe — the spectrum) In the Physical Spectrum, say what a believer in a further fact must claim (that there is a sharp line, unknown to us) and what the reductionist claims (the question is empty past the facts); construct the observation that would distinguish them, if any, and state why Parfit says there is none.
4. (refute) "Fission shows only that the thought experiment is impossible: a brain cannot be split with both halves sufficient." Break it: say why the argument needs only possibility, what the actual commissurotomy cases contribute (named, no claims), and what the objector must say about the single transplant case, which is not impossible.
5. (claim–evidence — Butler) Memory presupposes identity. Define quasi-memory (an apparent memory of an experience, caused by that experience in the right way, whoever had it) and show that it breaks the circle; then say what a teletransported person has (quasi-memories) and whether that settles anything about identity.
6. (cross-lesson — Ayer 1936) Ayer makes surviving bodily annihilation 'self-contradictory' and footnotes that a psychological criterion would not. Using Relation R, say which of Ayer's two sentences Parfit keeps and which he reverses, and what Ayer's own construction of the self (an equivalence relation on experiences) has in common with reductionism.
7. (FORWARD → gettier-problem) Every step of this lesson's argument appeals to what 'we would say' about a case. State the two places where an intuition does the work (that the single copy is survival; that fission is not death), and say what would follow if the intuitions varied across readers. The solution says the closing lesson examines intuition as data in the analytic paper, with a `<Ref>`.

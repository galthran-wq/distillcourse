# Research brief: private-language-argument (The private-language argument)

status: planned
module: later-wittgenstein
type: core
goal: Formulate the argument of PI §§243–315 that a language whose words refer to what only the speaker can know is impossible, and distinguish its conclusion from behaviourism.

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** Descartes's starting point: what I know best is my own mind, which no one else can inspect. Russell's acquaintance kept it (`<Ref>`). The diary: a recurring sensation, the sign 'S', no definition possible to others — but surely to myself, by attending. Sub-problem: does 'S' thereby get a meaning? Obvious attempt: yes — I concentrate on the sensation and impress on myself the connection between 'S' and it; that is ostensive definition, which works for 'red' (`<Ref>` meaning-as-use §§28–36). Why the parallel fails is the lesson.
2. **What 'private' means (§§243–255).** Not a code (others could learn it); a language whose words refer to what only the speaker can know — his immediate private sensations. §244: how do words refer to sensations at all? The child cries; adults teach it exclamations and later sentences; 'the verbal expression of pain replaces crying and does not describe it'. §246: 'I know I am in pain' has no use except as a joke or emphasis — 'know' needs the possibility of doubt. §253: 'another person can't have my pains' is a grammatical remark mistaken for a discovery (the criterion of identity for pains is not the person).
3. **The diary (§258).** 'I impress on myself the connection' — 'but this process only means: this process brings it about that I remember the connection right in the future. But in the present case I have no criterion of correctness. One would like to say: whatever is going to seem right to me is right. And that only means that here we can't talk about "right".' The because, from the previous lesson: a rule needs a distinction between following it and thinking one is following it; private ostension supplies no such distinction, since the only check is the very memory being checked. §265: looking up a timetable in memory to confirm a memory — 'as if someone were to buy several copies of the morning paper to assure himself that what it said was true'. §268: the right hand gives the left hand money — the ceremony is there, the transaction is not. §271: a part that can turn with nothing else moving is not part of the mechanism. §270: add a manometer that rises whenever I write 'S' — now 'S' has a use, and it does not matter whether I identified the sensation 'correctly' — the error hypothesis was 'mere show'.
4. **The beetle (§293).** Everyone has a box; 'beetle' is the word for what is in it; no one can look into another's. If the word had a use, the thing in the box 'cancels out, whatever it is' — it could be different for each, or empty. So: if we construe the grammar of sensation words on the model of object and name, the object drops out as irrelevant. The because: a word's meaning is its use in the common language (§43, `<Ref>`), and a use cannot depend on what no use can reach. §304: the sensation is 'not a something, but not a nothing either' — the point is only that a nothing would serve as well as a something about which nothing can be said; what is rejected is 'the grammar which tries to force itself on us here'.
5. **Not behaviourism (§§307–308).** 'Are you not really a behaviourist in disguise?' — 'If I do speak of a fiction, then it is of a grammatical fiction.' The denial is of the picture (inner object, private name), not of the sensation. §580: 'an inner process stands in need of outward criteria' — criteria, not constitution. §281: pain is ascribed to the living and what behaves like it — a grammatical remark about where the word has a use, not a theory that pain *is* behaviour. §412: the 'gulf' between consciousness and brain process is giddiness produced by a sleight of hand. For contrast, Ayer 1936 (p. 140): 'behaves in every way as a conscious being must, by definition, behave… and this is an analytic proposition' — *that* is behaviourism, and the lesson shows the difference: Ayer reduces, the Investigations describe where the word is used.
6. **Why it mattered / what refuted or reshaped it.** Mattered: the Cartesian starting point — private certainty first, the world and others by inference — loses its first step; 'I' is not the name of a person (§404); the inverted spectrum (§272) is not a hypothesis. Oxford's parallel is Ryle's ghost (next module). Reshaped: (a) Ayer 1954: Robinson Crusoe could invent a language alone — the reply distinguishes 'alone' from 'private' (a solitary practice can have criteria; a necessarily inaccessible object cannot); (b) Kripke's community reading makes the argument a corollary of the sceptical solution and 'privately' mean 'in isolation from a community', which Baker–Hacker deny; (c) functionalism (closing module): keep the inner state by giving it a causal job, which is a way of supplying outward criteria that Wittgenstein did not foresee and would call a theory. Survived: the demand for criteria; the diagnosis of 'I know I am in pain'; the beetle as the standing objection to any 'inner object' semantics.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The argument's conclusion is about *language*, not about sensations: the lesson must say twice that no sensation is denied, and the §304 sentence does the work.
- 'Criterion' is the module's term of art (introduced here, applied in the Turing lesson): defeasible, grammatical, public — defined once at §580.
- Do not merge this with the verificationist argument against other minds (Ayer Ch. 7): the Investigations do not say 'unverifiable, therefore meaningless'; they say 'no criterion, therefore no rule'. The exercise on Ayer's swapped spectrum makes the contrast.
- Kripke's and Ayer's objections come from texts not on disk; state them in the lesson's words, flag in References.
- Part II's 'attitude towards a soul' is for the Turing lesson; one clause here at most.
- OCR: check §§258, 265, 293, 304 wording against print.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) State, for each of 'red', 'five', 'S', whether an ostensive definition can be corrected by another speaker, and what the corrector would use.
2. (refute) "Wittgenstein denies that there are sensations." Break it with §304 and §308: quote the sentences, say what *is* denied (a grammar), and construct the one claim a behaviourist makes that the Investigations never make.
3. (derivation — the argument as premises) Reconstruct §258 as: (1) a word has meaning only if there is a distinction between using it correctly and seeming to; (2) private ostension provides only the seeming; (C) 'S' has no meaning. Identify which premise the rule-following lesson established and which is new, and where the manometer (§270) enters.
4. (boundary probe) Ayer's Crusoe: a solitary person names things on an island. Say whether his language is 'private' in §243's sense, what criteria of correctness he has (the island, re-encounters, his own later use), and which of those the diary-keeper lacks. State the difference between 'alone' and 'private' in one sentence.
5. (claim–evidence, cross-lesson) Ayer 1936 handles the swapped spectrum by verificationism ('the difference is systematic, so undetectable, so no hypothesis'); §272 handles it by grammar. Write both arguments in two lines each and find the premise Ayer needs that Wittgenstein does not.
6. (FORWARD → turing-test-as-grammar) §281: 'only of a living human being and what resembles one'. A machine that answers questions in writing: does it resemble a living human in the relevant respect? Say what 'resembles' would have to cover for §281 to apply, and whether a single test could settle it. The solution says the next lesson reads Turing's game against this sentence, with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped) §412: the 'gulf' between consciousness and brain process feels unbridgeable when one clutches one's forehead and says 'THIS is produced by a brain-process!', and not during a cortical-stimulation experiment. Design the observation (what the subject says in each setting) that would show the 'gulf' is a feature of the setting, and say what it establishes and what it does not about the mind–brain relation — the doorstep of the closing module's identity theory.

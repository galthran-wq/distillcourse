## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** Elm and beech: Putnam's concept of each is 'common deciduous tree' ('I blush to confess'), and yet 'elm' in his mouth is true of elms. Whatever fixed that was not in his head. Sub-problem: two assumptions the whole tradition from Frege to Carnap held — (I) knowing a meaning is being in a psychological state; (II) meaning determines extension — and the question which must go. Obvious attempt: keep both; Putnam's 'elm' refers to elms because he *could* find out — his state includes a disposition to defer. Why it fails: the disposition to defer is a disposition to defer to *other people*, which is already the socially fixed extension (step 4).
2. **The argument made exact.** Methodological solipsism: a psychological state in the *narrow* sense presupposes no individual other than the subject (jealousy in the wide sense presupposes the rival; in the narrow sense not). (I) with narrow states, plus (II), gives: the narrow state fixes the extension — two speakers in the same narrow state have the same extension for their word. Now Twin Earth: a planet like ours except that its lakes hold XYZ, indistinguishable from water at normal temperatures; in 1750, before chemistry, Oscar$_1$ and Oscar$_2$ are duplicates in the same narrow state; 'water' is true of H2O for one and XYZ for the other. So (I) and (II) cannot both hold. 'Cut the pie any way you like, meanings just ain't in the head.' The because: extension depends on what the samples *are*, and that is in the lake, not in Oscar.
3. **Two more cases, each closer to home.** Aluminium/molybdenum switched on Twin Earth: pots and pans indistinguishable except to metallurgists — the Earth and Twin Earth speakers differ in extension while most are in the same state. Elm/beech: no science fiction; Putnam himself. The because: the argument does not need Twin Earth; it needs only a speaker whose word has an extension he cannot himself determine.
4. **The division of linguistic labour.** 'Gold' is used by people who cannot test for gold, because the community contains people who can; the community is a factory with jobs (wearing gold rings, selling them, testing them); the Hypothesis of the Universality of the Division of Linguistic Labour; hammer (one user) vs steamship (a crew). So the extension is fixed by the collective — the sociolinguistic state — not by the individual. The because: a word is a tool that can be used by those who cannot calibrate it, as long as someone can.
5. **Indexicality and rigidity: the same point twice.** 'Water' means 'stuff bearing same$_L$ to *this*' — an indexical component; my Doppelgänger's 'I' has a different extension though our concepts are alike; the scope analysis (1')/(2'): the sample is picked in the *actual* world, so 'water' is rigid (Kripke's term, `<Ref>`); water is H2O in every world, 'conceivable but not logically possible' that it is not — the necessary a posteriori from the other side. 'Human intuition has no privileged access to metaphysical necessity.'
6. **What the speaker does have: the stereotype and the vector.** A stereotype: a conventional idea of what an $X$ is like (tigers are striped; gold is yellow — though pure gold is nearly white), which a competent speaker is *obliged* to acquire (the confederate who asks what 'tiger' means; the snowball; driving tests), neither analytic nor a necessary condition nor statistical. The meaning vector: syntactic markers, semantic markers (natural kind; liquid), stereotype, extension; Earth and Twin Earth 'water' differ in the last entry only. Two routes after Twin Earth: give up (II) and keep meaning a concept; or give up (I) and make extension a component of meaning — Putnam takes the second (and Lewis the first — forward). The reply to Quine (`<Ref>`): the stereotype is 'part of the meaning' without being analytic, so the circle is escaped by lowering the demand; Reichenbach on analyticity as unrevisability.
7. **Realism, and why it mattered / what limited it.** Gold and Archimedes: a piece he would have called gold that we can tell is not — the extension of 'gold' has not changed in two thousand years; the operationalist's 'warranted assertibility' rejected; 'X would have behaved differently' under tests Archimedes could not run; binary stars as truths nobody can verify. Mattered: externalism about mind (Burge — 'arthritis in the thigh'), the end of the Cartesian theatre for meaning, the sociolinguistics/psycholinguistics split. Limited: Putnam's own concessions — 'important' in same$_L$ is interest-relative (water as vapour, impure, 'XYZ used as water'), jade is two kinds, kinds need no hidden structure (the application lesson); narrow content and two-dimensionalism as the internalist replies (named); Putnam's later retreat from metaphysical realism (one clause). Survived: Twin Earth as the standard test, the division of labour, the stereotype.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The argument is a reductio on (I)+(II); keep it in that shape, with the narrow-state step explicit — the reader will otherwise take Twin Earth as a mere intuition pump.
- '1750' matters: it removes expert knowledge from the argument; say why.
- Keep Kripke's and Putnam's independence (n. 62) in one clause; do not merge the two lessons.
- The stereotype is not a definition and not a probability; three negations, stated once.
- Burge and two-dimensionalism are not on disk; one sentence each.
- The medieval footnote on 'God' and the conjunction-of-properties tradition is seductive detail: starred or cut.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) For 'elm', 'water', 'gold', 'pencil', state the stereotype a competent speaker must have, who in the community holds the criteria, and whether the term is a natural-kind term.
2. (derivation) Write the Twin Earth argument as a reductio: premises (I), (II), methodological solipsism, the Twin Earth stipulation; derive the contradiction; then show which premise each of the two routes rejects and what each keeps.
3. (boundary probe — 1750 vs 1950) Run Twin Earth in 1950, after chemistry: are Oscar$_1$ and Oscar$_2$ still in the same narrow state? Say what changes, why the 1750 version is the one the argument needs, and what the 1950 version shows instead (the division of labour).
4. (refute) "Twin Earth only shows that 'water' is ambiguous: Earth-water and Twin-water are two words." Break it with the 1750 case (no speaker could draw the distinction) and with the indexical analysis ('stuff like *this*'), and say what 'ambiguous' would require that the case lacks.
5. (claim–evidence — the stereotype vs Quine) Reichenbach's argument (p. 175): if analyticity were unrevisability it would not be closed under conjunction. State the argument, then say how the stereotype is 'part of the meaning' without being analytic, and whether this answers Quine's circle or sidesteps it.
6. (FORWARD → natural-kinds-against-chemistry) Putnam concedes that 'water' as vapour, as impure liquid and as 'XYZ used as water' are related senses, with 'important' in same$_L$ interest-relative (p. 157). Take tap water, heavy water and sea water and say for each whether it bears same$_L$ to the lake sample, and what 'important' has to mean for the answer. The solution says the next lesson takes the examples to chemistry, with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped — Oscar and Buche) Oscar is a German–English bilingual whose 'beech' and 'Buche' have identical meaning vectors; he may not know they are synonyms. Say what this shows about belief (it is under a representation) and construct the sentence Oscar believes under one word and not the other; then say whether Frege's sense could do the job the vector does not.

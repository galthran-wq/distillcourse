## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** After emotivism, a dispute about justice was a dispute of feeling; after Quine, no statement was true by meaning; and political philosophy under ordinary-language method had nothing to say (Williams). In 1971 a six-hundred-page book argued for two specific principles from a decision problem. Sub-problem: how can principles of justice be *derived*, and from what, if not from intuitions that emotivism had reduced to feeling? Obvious attempt: utilitarianism — maximise total (or average) welfare; it is a derivation, it is systematic, and it was the going theory. Why Rawls rejects it: it does not take seriously the distinction between persons — it lets one person's loss be paid for by another's gain, as if society were one person.
2. **The original position.** Justice as fairness: the principles are those that free and equal persons would agree to in a fair initial situation. The situation: parties choose principles for the basic structure of a society behind a *veil of ignorance* — not knowing their class, talents, sex, race, conception of the good, or their society's stage of development; knowing general facts (economics, psychology) and that they want 'primary goods' (liberties, opportunities, income, wealth, self-respect). The because: fairness is built into the choice situation rather than into the principles, so whatever is chosen is fair by construction — a contract without a signing, an explication (`<Ref>` Carnap) of 'fair'.
3. **The two principles.** (1) Each person has an equal right to the most extensive basic liberties compatible with the same for all; (2) social and economic inequalities are to be arranged so that they are (a) to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (the *difference principle*) and (b) attached to positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity. Lexical priority: (1) before (2), (2b) before (2a) — liberty is not traded for wealth. The because: the parties will not risk their liberties for gains, and will accept inequalities only if those inequalities make the worst-off better than equality would.
4. **Why maximin — and the mathematician's objection.** The parties choose by *maximin*: pick the principles whose worst outcome is best. Rawls's three conditions for maximin to be rational: no reliable probabilities over positions (the veil removes them); the chooser cares little for gains above the guaranteed minimum; the alternatives have outcomes one cannot accept (slavery, serfdom). Harsanyi (1975): if the parties are rational and ignorant, the natural assumption is *equiprobability* — each is equally likely to be anyone — and then expected-utility maximisation yields *average utilitarianism*, not the difference principle; maximin as a general rule is 'irrational' (it would refuse a $1 gain against a one-in-a-billion catastrophe). The because of the dispute: maximin and expected utility differ exactly on whether probabilities are available and whether the stakes have a floor; Rawls says the original position is designed so that they are not and it does, Harsanyi says that is to rig the choice. State both; do not decide.
5. **Reflective equilibrium.** How are the principles justified, if not deduced from axioms? By going back and forth between principles and 'considered judgements' (slavery is unjust; religious persecution is unjust), revising either until they cohere — the web of belief in ethics (`<Ref>` holism), borrowed from Goodman's justification of induction (rules and inferences adjusted against each other). The because: there is no foundation (Quine); there is coherence; and the original position is itself justified by the equilibrium it produces.
6. **Why it mattered / what refuted or reshaped it.** Mattered: normative theory returned to philosophy a generation after Ayer; the book set the agenda of political philosophy for fifty years. Reshaped: Nozick 1974 — the entitlement theory ('liberty upsets patterns'; Wilt Chamberlain), justice as historical rather than patterned; Sen — 'equality of what?' (primary goods vs capabilities); the communitarians (Sandel) — the parties behind the veil are 'unencumbered selves' no one is; Rawls's own Political Liberalism (1993) narrowing the claim — named only. Survived: the original position as a device everyone reasons with, the difference principle as the standard egalitarian principle, reflective equilibrium as the method of the field.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) For three distributions of primary goods over three positions, compute the maximin choice and the equiprobable expected-utility choice, and say when they coincide.
2. (derivation — Harsanyi) Assume the parties assign equal probability to every position and maximise expected utility; show that the chosen principle maximises average utility; then state the one assumption Rawls denies (that probabilities are available) and the one he adds (a floor that matters more than the mean).
3. (boundary probe) Let the worst-off position be the same in two societies and the rest differ. The difference principle is indifferent; say what the second principle's (b) clause and the first principle then decide, and whether Rawls's lexical ordering can leave a case undecided.
4. (refute) "The original position is just utilitarianism with a veil: ignorant rational choosers maximise expected welfare." Break it with the three conditions for maximin and with the 'distinction between persons' objection; say what the veil is designed to remove.
5. (claim–evidence — Nozick) Wilt Chamberlain: a just distribution, voluntary payments to watch him play, an unequal result. State what the example shows about *patterned* principles (the difference principle included), and what Rawls can reply (the basic structure, not individual transactions, is the subject of justice).
6. (cross-lesson — from emotivism) Ayer: there can be no ethical science; disputes about value are disputes about fact or end in abuse. State what kind of argument A Theory of Justice is (a derivation from a choice situation plus coherence) and whether it is a counterexample to Ayer's claim or an illustration of it (the choice situation encodes the values).
7. (FORWARD → parfit-personal-identity) The difference principle refuses to let one person's loss be paid for by another's gain — the 'separateness of persons'. Say what that premise assumes about personal identity over a life, and what would happen to it if identity over time were a matter of degree.

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** The pupil at 1000 writes 1004, 1008; 'but I went on in the same way'. Infinitely many functions agree with 2, 4, …, 1000. He is wrong. Sub-problem: what does 'add 2' *contain*, such that 1002 was settled before anyone got there? Obvious attempt 1: the pupil had the wrong formula in mind. Why it fails (§§151–154): understanding a series is not having a formula — 'now I can go on' on 1, 5, 11, 19, 29 may come with the formula $a_n = n^2 + n - 1$, or with the differences 4, 6, 8, or with nothing — and a formula in mind is neither necessary (the pupil who just continues) nor sufficient (the formula itself can be misapplied). The because: 'understand' is grammatically akin to 'can', 'is able to' — mastery of a technique, judged by what follows (§§150, 182).
2. **Obvious attempt 2: the rule determines its steps in advance, as a formula determines a function.** §189: '$y = x^2$' determines $y$ for a given $x$ — but 'determines' here says something about *training*, about a kind of use, not about a mental mechanism that has already run through the steps. §§193–197: the machine as symbol — we say the machine 'already contains' its movements, and we mean a possibility shown by a diagram, not a shadow of the movement; 'the whole use in a flash' is a superlative born of the picture. §218: 'the rails laid to infinity' — the picture of the steps already taken.
3. **The regress.** §198: a rule as a sign-post — it stands there, and how is it to be followed? Any interpretation of it is another sign; 'any interpretation still hangs in the air along with what it interprets'. §85: the sign-post can be read both ways. So if grasping a rule were an interpretation, it would need another, and so on. §201: 'no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made out to accord with the rule… and so would also conflict with it. And so there would be neither accord nor conflict here.' For this reader: the formal statement — any finite initial segment is consistent with uncountably many continuations, and any further *statement* of the rule is itself finite.
4. **The resolution.** §201's second paragraph: 'there is a way of grasping a rule which is not an interpretation, but which is exhibited in what we call obeying the rule and going against it in actual cases'. §202: 'obeying a rule is a practice'; §199: a custom, an institution, a technique; §§206–210: the common behaviour of mankind as the system of reference; 'same' explained by examples and training — understanding does not reach beyond the examples because there is nothing beyond them to reach. §211–219: reasons give out and then I act — 'if I have exhausted the justifications I have reached bedrock, and my spade is turned. Then I am inclined to say: "This is simply what I do"' (§217); 'I obey the rule blindly' (§219). §§241–242: agreement not in opinions but in form of life, and — 'queer as this may sound' — in judgements too: the measuring practice requires that we mostly get the same results. The because: correctness is not a fact *behind* the practice that the practice tracks; it is internal to the practice — the pupil is wrong because that is not how 'add 2' is done among us, and 'how it is done' is a training, a form of life, not an interpretation.
5. **The demo.** Before §201 is stated, the reader is asked to beat the panel: type any next term and watch a rule fit it; try to make the panel fail; it cannot. The prose then says what the reader just saw: the data never settle the rule. Then the lesson says what *does*, and that it is not another datum.
6. **Why it mattered / what came of it.** Mattered: the end of the idea that meaning is a mental item that fixes future use — which the Tractatus, Frege's grasped senses and Russell's acquaintance all needed; and the premise of the private-language argument (next lesson). What came of it: Kripke 1982 — the sceptic asks for a *fact* about me that fixes plus rather than quus ($68 + 57 = 5$ under quus), projective (settling infinitely many cases) and normative (saying what I *should* answer); finding none, offers a sceptical solution: assertibility conditions and community agreement — and reads §201 as a Humean sceptical paradox. The straight readings (Baker and Hacker; McGinn): §201's second paragraph is the *answer*, not a concession; there is no sceptical problem because 'grasping a rule' was never an interpretation. State both at their strongest; note the parallel to Goodman's grue (new riddle of induction) and its difference (Goodman is about evidence, Kripke about constitution). Survived: the regress as a permanent constraint on any theory of meaning; 'practice' as the unit; the question whether 'community' is needed at all — the next lesson's 'privately'.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The math reader will reach for 'underdetermination of a function by finite data' at once; the lesson takes that as a given and then shows that the Investigations' point is *not* epistemic (how do we know which function) but about what determination *is*. §189 is the hinge; spend time there.
- §§156–178 (reading) are cut to one example (the artichoke); the lesson is already long.
- Kripke's book is not on disk; attribute the sceptical reading via SEP §3.5 and keep the two Kripkes distinct for the reader (Naming and Necessity is the other book).
- 'Form of life' and 'agreement' must not become 'majority rules': §241 is about the background that makes disagreement possible, not a vote.
- 'Blindly' (§219) is not 'irrationally'; it means without further interpretation.
- OCR: §233 printed as 235; check numbers.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Give a closed-form rule that yields 2, 4, 6, …, 1000, 1004, 1008, … (the pupil's series) and another that yields 2, 4, …, 1000, 1002, 1004, … and show that both agree on every term up to 1000.
2. (refute) "The pupil is wrong because the teacher had the function $f(n) = 2n$ in mind, and $f(501) = 1002$." Break it with §§151–154 and §189: show that having a formula in mind is neither necessary nor sufficient for understanding, and that '$f(501) = 1002$' is itself a step whose correctness is in question.
3. (derivation — the regress formalised) Let $R_0$ be a rule stated as a finite string; let an 'interpretation' be a function from strings to continuations. Show that any interpretation $I$ of $R_0$ is itself a finite string $R_1$ requiring an interpretation, and that no finite tower settles the continuation; then state in one sentence what §201's second paragraph puts in place of the tower.
4. (boundary probe) §208: the digits of π. 'We cannot write down all the digits' — not a human shortcoming. Say what the rule for computing π *contains* on the practice view, and whether 'the 10$^{100}$-th digit is already determined' is true, false, or a picture — and which answer the mathematician's 'determined' (the Frege lesson's '$y = x^2$') gives.
5. (claim–evidence — Kripke's sceptic) Write the two requirements (projective; normative) on a fact that would fix 'plus' over 'quus', and test three candidates against them: a disposition, a mental image, a formula. State which requirement each fails and what the 'sceptical solution' gives up.
6. (FORWARD → private-language-argument) §202 ends: 'hence it is not possible to obey a rule privately'. Suppose a solitary person keeps a private sequence. Say what, on the practice view, would be the difference between following the rule and thinking one is following it, and whether anything in the person's situation could supply it. The solution says the next lesson's diary case is exactly this, with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped) Goodman's 'grue' (green before 2030, blue after) is consistent with every observed emerald. State what Goodman's riddle is about (which predicates are projectible in induction) and what Kripke's is about (what constitutes my meaning plus), and design the one question that separates them — whether an omniscient observer of all my past behaviour could answer it.

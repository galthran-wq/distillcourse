## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** The visitor who has seen the colleges, libraries and laboratories asks where the University is. The spectator who has seen the batting, bowling and fielding asks who contributes the team spirit. The child who, told the Average Taxpayer pays £X, asks to meet him (the constructions lesson's average woman, `<Ref>`). Each has not made a false factual claim; each has assigned a word to the wrong *category* — treated a collective or dispositional term as naming one more member of the series. Sub-problem: can a mistake of this kind be made about something as familiar as the mind? Obvious attempt: no — the mind's existence is a fact, and three centuries of attack on Descartes's dualism have been attacks on facts (interaction, the pineal gland, parallelism). Why it fails as an approach: every factual attack has left the picture standing, which is what a category mistake does under factual attack.
2. **The official doctrine.** Minds and bodies as two substances, one public and mechanical, one private and non-spatial; every intelligent performance preceded and caused by a private act of the mind — the 'ghost in the machine', a 'para-mechanical' hypothesis. Ryle's claim: this is the University mistake — the mind taken as one more thing of the same type as the body, with 'mental causes' as one more set of causes. The origin: Descartes, impressed by Galileo's mechanics, kept the mental by making it a second mechanism of a different stuff — a category mistake with a date.
3. **Knowing how and knowing that.** Riding a bicycle, playing chess, arguing well: capacities exercised in performance, not propositions consulted. The regress: if every intelligent act required a prior act of considering a proposition (a rule), that prior act would itself have to be done intelligently, requiring a prior act — so no intelligent act could begin. The because: intelligence is a *manner* of doing, not an antecedent episode; 'knowing how' is logically prior to 'knowing that', and the official doctrine had the order reversed.
4. **Dispositions.** 'The glass is brittle' names no event inside the glass; it says that if struck it would shatter. 'Vain', 'believes that…', 'intends to…' are analysed the same way: not reports of inner episodes but statements about what a person would do, say, feel in circumstances — multi-track dispositions, since 'believes the ice is thin' licenses skating carefully, warning others, and the rest. The because: the dispositional reading is what the words *do* in use — we ascribe vanity to someone who has never performed a 'vain act' inwardly, on the strength of what they do; and it dissolves the ghost without denying that people think, feel and intend.
5. **Not behaviourism.** The charge: 'you have reduced the mind to behaviour'. The reply: a disposition is not a behaviour (the glass is brittle while unstruck); a mental predicate names a pattern of possible behaviour and reaction, not any actual item; and sensations, images and silent soliloquy are not denied — only the doctrine that they are the *causes* 'behind' performance. The Cambridge parallel: §580's outward criteria (`<Ref>`); the contrast: Ayer 1936 ('behaves in every way as a conscious being must… analytic') *is* the reduction, and Ryle's 'logical behaviourism' label stuck because the dispositional analysis says nothing about what the disposition's basis is. Why the charge stuck: dispositions need a categorical base — the brittle glass has a molecular structure; the vain person has what?
6. **Why it mattered / what displaced it.** Mattered: the first analytic philosophy of mind; the Oxford method on display — examples, distinctions, dissolution rather than solution (Williams); 'knowing how' as a living field. Displaced: (a) identity theory (Place 1956, Smart 1959) supplies the categorical base — the disposition's ground is a brain state (closing module); (b) functionalism keeps inner states by giving them a causal role — the ghost returns as software (closing module); (c) Williams's critique of the method: it underestimated theory and treated philosophy as separable from subject matter — the beetle species analogy. Survived: the category mistake as a permanent tool; the regress; knowing-how.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Diagnose the category mistake in each: 'I have seen the players; now show me the team'; 'the number 7 is heavier than 6'; 'she has a strong will — where is it kept?'; and give one sentence with the same grammar that makes no mistake.
2. (refute) "Ryle's view is just behaviourism: a vain person is one who performs vain acts." Break it with the unstruck glass: state what a disposition is, what is denied (an inner cause) and what is not (that the person thinks and feels), and give the sentence Ayer 1936 asserts that Ryle does not.
3. (derivation) Write the knowing-how regress as premises and conclusion; identify the premise ('every intelligent act is preceded by an act of considering a proposition') whose denial stops it, and say what 'knowing how' must then be.
4. (boundary probe) Take a disposition with a known base — solubility and molecular structure — and one without — 'generous'. Say what Ryle's analysis gives for each, and what question the first case raises about the second that the analysis leaves open; the solution says the identity theory is the answer offered in 1956, with a `<Ref>` to the closing module.
5. (claim–evidence, cross-lesson) The private-language argument and the category mistake both undo the Cartesian starting point. State the argument each uses (no criterion of correctness; a type error) and construct the case where one applies and the other does not — a sensation word versus a character word.
6. (FORWARD → austin-speech-acts) Ryle's method: find the ordinary use, state the distinctions it embodies, dissolve the problem. Take 'I promise' and apply it: what is the ordinary use, what does the official doctrine (promising = an inner act of resolve + a report of it) get wrong, and what kind of sentence is 'I promise' if not a report? The solution names the performative with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped) Williams's criticism: ordinary-language distinctions are only interesting against a background theory (the beetle species need evolution). Take Ryle's knowing-how/knowing-that and name the background theory — if any — against which it has been found fruitful since (cognitive psychology's procedural/declarative memory distinction; the 'intellectualism' debate of the 2000s), and say whether the distinction survived contact with theory or was absorbed by it.

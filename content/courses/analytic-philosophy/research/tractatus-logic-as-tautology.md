# Research brief: tractatus-logic-as-tautology (Logic as tautology)

status: planned
module: logical-atomism
type: core
goal: Demonstrate that every truth-function is generated by one operation of joint denial and explain why the Tractatus concludes that the propositions of logic say nothing about the world.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From tractatus-picture-theory: propositions picture possible facts, are bipolar, and the previous lesson's boundary exercise found that a tautology pictures nothing. From frege-function-and-quantifier: Frege's and Russell's view that logic is a body of general truths derived from axioms.
- **Contributes:** The core of the Tractatus's logic: truth-tables; tautology and contradiction as limits that say nothing (sinnlos, not unsinnig); entailment as inclusion of truth-grounds, hence no laws of inference; one operation $N$ generating every truth-function and the quantifiers; 'the logical constants do not represent'. This is what the Vienna Circle took as the solution to the empiricist's problem of necessary truth.
- **Hands off:** To tractatus-saying-showing-and-the-ladder (if logic says nothing, philosophy says nothing either), to a-priori-as-analytic (the tautology thesis is Vienna's account of mathematics), to descriptions-in-a-proof-checker (6.1203's decision method vs a proof assistant's rules), and to grice-conversational-implicature (the truth-functional 'or' and 'if' against ordinary use).

Spine step: as for tractatus-picture-theory.

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** 'It is raining or it is not' is true whatever the weather. Frege and Russell treated such truths as the most general truths about everything, derived from axioms as geometry's theorems are. Sub-problem: if a proposition is a picture that can agree or disagree with reality, what does this one picture? Obvious attempt: it pictures the most general fact, 'the world is logical'. Why it fails: a picture must be able to be false (bipolarity, previous lesson), and nothing could make this one false — 'we cannot think anything unlogical' (3.03); so it is not a picture of a general fact or of any fact.
2. **The machinery: truth-possibilities.** $n$ elementary propositions, $2^n$ rows (4.27), a proposition = agreement/disagreement with the rows (4.4); the 4.31 schema and the compressed '(TTFT)(p,q)' (4.442). For this reader: 'the truth table *is* the proposition'. The because: once the elementary propositions are fixed, every proposition is a selection of rows, and the selection is all there is to it.
3. **The limits: tautology and contradiction.** Agreement with all rows (tautology), with none (contradiction). They are not pictures: 'I know nothing about the weather when I know that it is either raining or not raining' (4.461). They are *sinnlos* — without sense — but not *unsinnig* — nonsense — 'as 0 is part of the symbolism of arithmetic' (4.4611); 4.463: a tautology leaves reality the whole of logical space, a contradiction none. 5.142–5.143: the unsubstantial centre and the outer limit. So the propositions of logic are tautologies (6.1), say nothing (6.11), and are recognised from the symbol alone (6.113).
4. **Entailment without laws of inference.** $p$ follows from $q$ iff $q$'s truth-grounds are included in $p$'s (5.11–5.12); 5.123's god who creates a world creates all that follows. Then 5.132: Frege's and Russell's 'laws of inference' are superfluous — the relation is visible in the structure; 6.1264: every logical proposition is a modus ponens in signs. The paradox of inference (Beaney Box 4): if 'from $p$ and $p \to q$ infer $q$' were a premise it would need a further premise — rules are mortar, not bricks. The because: inclusion of rows is a relation between the propositions, not a third proposition.
5. **One operation.** Sheffer: 'not-$p$ and not-$q$' generates all connectives. Wittgenstein's $N(\bar\xi)$: joint denial of *any set* of propositions (5.5, 5.502); $N(p) = \neg p$, $N(p,q) = \neg p \wedge \neg q$, $p \vee q = N(N(p,q))$ (Russell's worked reduction); applied to all values of $fx$: $N(\bar\xi) = \neg\exists x\,fx$ (5.52), so $\forall$ and $\exists$ fall out and generality is separated from truth-functions (5.521). The general form $[\bar p, \bar\xi, N(\bar\xi)]$ (6): start from elementary propositions, apply $N$ repeatedly. The DEMO: the reader builds $\neg$, $\vee$, $\to$ and a tautology from $N$ alone before the prose derives them.
6. **What the one operation shows: the constants do not represent.** 4.0312 'my fundamental thought'; 5.4 no logical objects; 5.44 $\neg\neg p = p$ — if $\neg$ named an object, double negation would mention it twice; 5.512 negation is the common rule of all negating notations, not the sign '$\sim$'. The because: anything generated by $N$ from pictures is a selection of rows, and a selection is not an extra object in the world. 4.0621: '$p$' and '$\neg p$' say the same reality with opposite sense.
7. **Why it mattered / what refuted it.** Mattered: the empiricist's problem of necessary truth is solved at a stroke — logic (and, the Circle added, mathematics) is necessary because it says nothing (next module); 'no surprises in logic' (6.1251), proof a mechanical expedient (6.1262). Refuted or limited: (a) generality as an infinite joint denial needs all the elementary propositions listed — Ramsey's and Russell's worry about infinity (5.535 reinterprets the axiom of infinity as 'infinitely many names'); (b) 6.1203's T/F-line method decides propositional tautologies only; for predicate logic there is no decision procedure (Church 1936), so 'logic takes care of itself' holds exactly where truth tables do — the reader knows Gödel and Church; (c) belief contexts and modality are not truth-functional (5.54's attempt noted). Survived: the tautology account of propositional logic, the truth-table as the meaning of a connective, the proof-theory/model-theory split foreshadowed by 'inclusion of truth-grounds'.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Do not teach truth tables; the reader has them. Teach what the Tractatus *does* with them (the proposition is its row-selection; tautology is the limit; entailment is inclusion).
- 'Sinnlos' vs 'unsinnig' is the lesson's one new distinction; both words appear once with the translation beside them and are then used in English ('senseless' / 'nonsense').
- $N$ over an infinite set is not a formula of any first-order language; say that this is the point Ramsey pressed and where the Tractatus idealises — do not pretend it is Sheffer's theorem.
- The 6.1203 method is propositional; the writer must not suggest it decides predicate logic. Church is named as the reader's known result, not as a source.
- Probability (5.15) and arithmetic (6.02–6.241) are starred; the core path is 4.3–5.52 and 6.1–6.13.
- Frege's assertion sign (4.442) and 'propositions as names' (5.02) belong to a one-paragraph 'against Frege' Remark, not more.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Express $p \leftrightarrow q$ and the Sheffer stroke $p \mid q$ by iterated $N$, and count the applications.
2. (derivation) Prove from the truth-grounds definition that if $p$ entails $q$ and $q$ entails $p$ then $p$ and $q$ select the same rows, and conclude that 'say the same thing' is extensional in the Tractatus; then say what this does to Frege's two senses of one reference (5.53–5.5303).
3. (boundary probe) Take $n = 0$ elementary propositions. How many truth-functions are there, and what are they? Use the answer to explain 4.4611's 'like 0 in arithmetic'.
4. (refute) "The Tractatus shows that all of logic is decidable by truth tables." Break it: state what 6.1203 decides, give the reader's own known result for predicate logic, and say which Tractatus thesis ('no surprises in logic', 6.1251) survives only for the propositional fragment.
5. (claim–evidence) 5.132 says laws of inference are superfluous. Reconstruct the paradox of inference (Box 4) as a regress and state the exact sense in which modus ponens is not a premise; then say what a proof assistant needs that the Tractatus denies (the doorstep of descriptions-in-a-proof-checker).
6. (FORWARD → a-priori-as-analytic) If '$7 + 5 = 12$' is an equation and equations are pseudo-propositions (6.2), what is it that a schoolchild learns when learning it, on this view? Write the Tractatus's answer (substitutability, 6.24) and then the empiricist's: 'true by the definitions of the symbols'. The solution says the next module takes the second answer and calls it 'analytic', with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped) Reproduce Sheffer's 1913 result for two propositions (all 16 functions from one) and then explain in one paragraph what Wittgenstein's $N$ adds that Sheffer's stroke does not (arbitrary and infinite sets of arguments), and why that addition is where Ramsey objected.

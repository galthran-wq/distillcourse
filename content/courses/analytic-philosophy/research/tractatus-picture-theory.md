# Research brief: tractatus-picture-theory (The picture theory of the proposition)

status: planned
module: logical-atomism
type: core
goal: Explain how, in the Tractatus, a proposition represents a possible fact by sharing its logical form, and derive from that account why the form itself cannot be stated.

- **Inherits:** From logical-constructions-and-acquaintance: atomic facts, truth-functional composition, the demand for simples (the FORWARD exercise's failure to exhibit one). From frege-sense-and-reference: sense as what a sentence expresses, and the context principle.

Spine step: "The limit of language drawn from inside: a proposition is a picture sharing form with a fact, logic is tautology and says nothing, so philosophy is an activity and the book's own sentences are a ladder to be thrown away…"

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** A new sentence understood at once, true or false unknown; a list of its words is not a claim. The Paris courtroom (1914): toy cars model the accident; the model claims something because the toys stand in a relation the cars could stand in. Sub-problem: what makes an arrangement of signs an arrangement *about* something? Obvious attempt (Frege/Russell as the Tractatus reads them): the sentence names a truth-value, or expresses a complex of the things named. Why it fails: a list of names names a list; 'Socrates, wisdom' claims nothing (3.142); and if a sentence named a truth-value, a false sentence would name the False and still be a name of something, so the difference between sense and nonsense would not be visible (4.063's black spot). The claim must come from the *arrangement*, not from the names.
2. **The answer: the proposition is a picture.** A picture is a fact (its elements stand in a relation); its elements stand for objects; the relation of the elements represents a relation of the objects. 3.1432: not '$aRb$ says that $a$ stands in $R$ to $b$', but 'that $a$ stands to $b$ in a certain relation says that $aRb$' — the word 'loves' between 'Plato' and 'Socrates' *is* the relation that depicts (Russell's Introduction). 3.1431: imagine the sentence made of tables, chairs, books; their arrangement expresses the sense. The because: only a fact can represent a fact, since only a fact has structure to share.
3. **Sense independent of truth.** The picture represents a *possible* state of affairs; it has sense whether or not the state obtains (2.22–2.225); true if it agrees with reality. Bipolarity: every proposition can be true and can be false; so no picture is true a priori (2.225) — the seam to the next lesson (tautologies are not pictures).
4. **What the picture and the world share: form.** Logical form = the possibility of the structure (2.033); the gramophone record, the score, the sound waves and the symphony share it via a law of projection (4.014); 2.161: there must be something identical in picture and pictured. Then 2.172: a picture cannot depict its own pictorial form; it displays it. The derivation: to depict the form one would need a picture whose structure represents the structure-sharing relation, which would itself need the form — so the form shows itself and cannot be said. This is the lesson's theorem; state it as one.
5. **The world that fits: facts, not things; simples.** 1.1 'the world is the totality of facts'; states of affairs as combinations of objects (2.01), independent of one another (2.061). Simples: 2.0211–2.0212 — if the world had no substance (no simples), whether a proposition had sense would depend on whether another proposition was true; but sense cannot wait on truth (step 3), so analysis must terminate in names of simples (3.23, 3.25: one complete analysis). The because is step 3's independence of sense from truth. Russell: simples 'a logical necessity demanded by theory, like an electron'. No example is given — say so now, since it is the refutation.
6. **Why it mattered / what refuted it.** Mattered: the first fully general truth-conditional account of understanding (4.024: to understand a proposition is to know what is the case if it is true) — the ancestor of every semantics since; and 4.0031: 'all philosophy is a critique of language' with Russell's merit named. Refuted: (a) 6.3751 — two colours at one point are logically incompatible, so elementary propositions are not independent; the 1929 'Some Remarks on Logical Form' tried to fix it and failed, and the author gave the independence up (SEP §5); (b) no elementary proposition was ever exhibited; (c) PI §§46–64: 'composite' has no absolute sense, the standard metre, the broom — the demand for determinacy was a demand (the 1931 dogmatism charge, SEP §3.1). Survived: truth-conditions, compositionality, the shared-form idea in model theory's 'structure'.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The numbering: explain once that decimals comment on the proposition above them; then cite by number freely.
- Sachverhalt/Tatsache: 'state of affairs' (Pears–McGuinness) vs 'atomic fact' (Ogden): pick one, note the other once.
- 'Object' in the Tractatus is a formal concept (next-but-one lesson): do not let the reader picture particles. Russell's electron analogy is about theoretical necessity, not about physics.
- The picture theory is NOT 'sentences resemble things': the shared element is logical multiplicity (4.04), not appearance; the hieroglyph remark (4.016) is about origin, not resemblance.
- Shard 2 of the Tractatus was only partly read by the swarm; the writer reads 1–4.1 in tlp.html directly and checks every quotation against both translations.
- No ladder here, no ethics: those are the third Tractatus lesson.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Write 'the kettle is left of the lamp' as a fact about signs in the manner of 3.1432 (which sign-relation says what), and say what would have to change in the sign-fact for the proposition to say the lamp is left of the kettle.
2. (refute) "The picture theory says a true sentence resembles the fact it describes." Break it: use the score/record example to show that what is shared is logical multiplicity, not appearance, and give a sentence whose 'picture' resembles nothing visual.
3. (derivation) Write out the argument that a picture cannot depict its own pictorial form (2.172) as a short reductio, and identify the step at which it uses 'only a fact can represent a fact'.
4. (boundary probe) A tautology ('it is raining or it is not') pictures which state of affairs? Show that the picture theory's bipolarity (can be true, can be false) fails for it, and state what the next lesson must therefore say about logic. (This is also the FORWARD exercise → tractatus-logic-as-tautology; the solution says so with a `<Ref>`.)
5. (claim–evidence) The argument from substance concludes that there are simples. Reconstruct it as premises and conclusion, locate the premise borrowed from 2.22, and say what the colour-exclusion case (6.3751) does to the *independence* of elementary propositions without touching their existence.
6. (cross-lesson) Compare the Tractatus's names (meaning = object, 3.203) with Frege's names (sense and reference): which of Frege's two puzzles can the Tractatus still solve, and with what (5.53–5.5303 on identity)? State the cost.
7. (research-shaped) The 1929 paper 'Some Remarks on Logical Form' proposed that elementary propositions carry numbers (degrees of a quality) to handle colour exclusion. From the lesson's account of independence, explain why admitting 'this point has colour-degree 3' as elementary breaks 2.061 and what the author concluded from the failure.

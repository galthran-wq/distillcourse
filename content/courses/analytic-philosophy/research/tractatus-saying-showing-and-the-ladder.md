status: planned

goal: Explain why the Tractatus places ethics, the subject and its own sentences outside what can be said, and evaluate the self-refutation objection and the two readings of the ladder.

- **Inherits:** From tractatus-picture-theory: form shows itself and cannot be said (2.172). From tractatus-logic-as-tautology: logic says nothing; the propositions of logic are not pictures.
- **Contributes:** The limit of language drawn from inside: formal concepts ('object', 'number') are variables, not predicates, so 'there are objects' is nonsense; ethics, value, the will, death and the subject lie outside the world; philosophy is an activity, not a doctrine (4.112); the correct method (6.53); the ladder (6.54) and the silence (7); Russell's hierarchy objection; the ineffabilist and resolute readings; the dogmatism charge that the later Wittgenstein brings.

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** A book claiming final solutions ends by calling its own sentences nonsense. Sub-problem: can a limit to what can be said be drawn at all? Obvious attempt: draw it in thought — list the thinkable and the unthinkable. Why it fails (Preface): to draw a limit to thought one would have to think both sides; so the limit is drawn in *language*, and the far side is 'simply nonsense' — not deep, not forbidden, just not a sentence.
2. **The first case already in hand: form.** 2.172 and 4.121: a proposition shows its form and cannot say it; 4.1212. Then generalise: *formal concepts* (4.126–4.1272). 'Object', 'fact', 'number' are expressed by variables; 'there are objects' tries to use a variable as a predicate; it is nonsense in the way '$2+2$ at three o'clock $= 4$' is. For this reader: 'there are $\aleph_0$ objects' is also nonsense, and 'there are infinitely many names' is what the axiom of infinity becomes (5.535). The because: a formal concept's instances are shown by the kind of sign they are (a variable's range), and there is no further proposition that says it. Beaney: the object/concept distinction and the theory of types cannot be stated in their own systems — the Tractatus turns Frege's and Russell's embarrassment into the general rule.
3. **The second case: belief, the soul, the subject.** 5.542: 'A believes $p$' is '"$p$" says $p$' — a correlation of facts, no composite soul (5.5421). 5.6–5.641: 'the limits of my language mean the limits of my world'; the subject is not in the world but its limit, as the eye is not in the visual field (5.633); 'The World as I found it' would not mention the subject; solipsism is correct and unsayable and coincides with pure realism (5.64). Why this is the same move: the subject, like form, is a condition of representation, not a thing represented.
4. **The third case: value.** 6.41: the sense of the world lies outside it; all propositions are of equal value; 6.42 there are no ethical propositions; ethics is transcendental (6.421); 6.4311 death is not an event in life; 6.44 the mystical is *that* the world is; 6.52 when all scientific questions are answered the problems of life are untouched; 6.521 the solution is the vanishing of the problem; 6.522 'there is indeed the inexpressible; this shows itself'. The von Ficker letter: the unwritten part is the important one. The because: a proposition pictures how things stand; value is not how anything stands; so no proposition carries it — and the author took this as protecting ethics, not abolishing it.
5. **Philosophy, then.** 4.003 most philosophical propositions are senseless, not false; 4.111 not a science; 4.112 an activity of clarification, not a doctrine; 4.115 it signifies the unsayable by presenting the sayable clearly; 6.53 the correct method: say only natural science and show the other person his sign has no meaning — 'he would not feel we were teaching him philosophy'. Then 6.54: 'my propositions are elucidatory in this way: he who understands me finally recognizes them as senseless… He must throw away the ladder'; 7.
6. **The objection and the readings.** Russell: the book 'manages to say a good deal about what cannot be said'; escape via a hierarchy of languages, each able to state the structure of the one below; Wittgenstein's implied reply (the hierarchy as a totality would be a fiction) and Russell's 'intellectual discomfort'. Two readings of 6.54: ineffabilist (the sentences convey truths that cannot be said — Anscombe, Hacker) vs resolute (plain nonsense; the book is a therapy against the illusion that it said something — Diamond, Conant). State each at its strongest in one paragraph; do not adjudicate; show that the hierarchy objection bites the first reading only.
7. **What came of it / what refuted it.** Came: Vienna read 6.53 as a programme and the December 1929 conversations gave it a slogan (next module); and the author's own 'philosophy is an activity' survives into the Investigations (PI §§109–133 — SEP §3.7's continuity thesis). Refuted: by 1931 the author called the book dogmatic — it assumed analysis *must* find elementary propositions of a form nobody had seen (SEP §3.1) — and the colour-exclusion crack (previous lessons) was the symptom. What survived: the saying/showing distinction returns, transformed, as 'grammar' (later-wittgenstein module).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The mystical passages invite purple prose; the register stays dry — quote, do not paraphrase into sermon.
- 'Nonsense' (unsinnig) is a technical verdict here; do not let it read as 'silly'. Keep 'senseless' (sinnlos) for tautologies, from the previous lesson.
- Do not settle the resolute/ineffabilist dispute; the lesson's job is to state both and to show what each costs (the ineffabilist owes an account of 'shown truths'; the resolute reading owes an account of why the book was worth writing).
- Solipsism: 5.62–5.64 is the hardest passage in the book for a first reader; one paragraph, the eye figure, and the sentence that it 'coincides with realism'. No more.
- Biography (teacher, gardener, architect) is one clause, because it dates the gap before the return of 1929 that the next lesson in the Wittgenstein module needs.
- Carnap vs Wittgenstein on 'nonsense' (Beaney) is a forward seam, not this lesson's content.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Sort the following into said / senseless / nonsense with the Tractatus number that decides each: 'it is raining'; 'it is raining or not'; 'there are objects'; 'this table is an object'; 'the good is beyond the world'.
2. (refute) "The Tractatus argues that ethics does not matter, since nothing can be said about it." Break it with 6.41–6.421 and the von Ficker letter: state what 'transcendental' claims, and why the author took the unsayability as protection.
3. (derivation) Reconstruct Russell's hierarchy escape as an argument: premises about metalanguages, conclusion that the 'unsayable' is sayable one level up. Then write the one premise the Tractatus denies (that the hierarchy has a totality that is a language) and say which reading of 6.54 the escape refutes if sound.
4. (boundary probe) 5.535 turns the axiom of infinity into 'there are infinitely many names'. Say why 'there are infinitely many objects' is nonsense by 4.1272 and 'there are infinitely many names' is not, and what that does to the question whether arithmetic needs an infinity axiom.
5. (claim–evidence) The 1931 self-charge: the book assumed elementary propositions must exist and must be independent. Cite the two propositions (2.0211, 2.061) that carry the assumption, and say which of the two the colour-exclusion case (6.3751) refutes and which it leaves.
6. (FORWARD → verification-principle) 6.53 says the correct method is to say only the propositions of natural science. Write the sentence the Vienna Circle made of this in December 1929 ('the sense of a proposition is the method of its verification') and show in two lines that it is a *stronger* claim than 6.53: it says what sense is, not only which sentences have it. The solution gives the `<Ref>` and notes the author's later rejection of the Circle's reading.
7. (cross-lesson) Compare 5.542's analysis of 'A believes $p$' with Frege's indirect reference: which of Frege's two puzzles does each handle, and what does each say the belief sentence is *about*?

## APPLICATION spec (L16)

- **Artefact:** A. M. Turing, 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence', Mind 59 (1950): §1 'The Imitation Game' (the original question 'too meaningless to deserve discussion'; the game with interrogator, man, woman, then machine); §2 'Critique of the New Problem'; §6 'Contrary Views', especially (4) 'The Argument from Consciousness' (Jefferson's 'not until a machine can write a sonnet…') and Turing's reply (the solipsist position; 'instead of arguing continually over this point it is usual to have the polite convention that everyone thinks'); the viva voce example. Turing attended Wittgenstein's 1939 Cambridge lectures on the foundations of mathematics (Diamond's edition records the exchanges) — one dated clause.
- **Module results it uses:** meaning as use (§43; 'think' as a family of uses, §§327–339; §360 'we say only of a human being and what is like one that it thinks'); outward criteria (§580); §281; criteria vs symptoms (§354 — introduced here); the private-language result on the 'inner object' (§293: 'but does it *really* think?' asks after the beetle).
- **Where the artefact departs from the idealisation:** (1) Turing's replacement is a single operational *test*, and a test is a verification, not a criterion: it is not defeasible in the way grammatical criteria are (one can pass the game and be 'found out' later; Turing's own §2 treats the question as empirical — 'will there be machines that pass by 2000?'); this is Ayer's 1936 formula ('behaves in every way as a conscious being must… really conscious — analytic'), not §580; (2) 'thinks' is a family of uses, and the game samples one (typed conversation) — Turing knows this and says the restriction is deliberate; the Wittgensteinian objection is not that the sample is small but that no sample is a definition; (3) the reply to the argument from consciousness reaches the private-language point ('the polite convention that everyone thinks') as a convention adopted to avoid solipsism, where §281 says it is a grammatical fact about where the word has a use — the right conclusion for the wrong reason; (4) 'attitude towards a soul' (PPF iv) is not a verdict a test can deliver, and the game asks for a verdict.

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** 1950: 'Can machines think?' declared 'too meaningless to deserve discussion' and replaced by a game. Three readings in circulation: a definition of 'think'; a test for thinking; an evasion. Sub-problem: which reading does the game actually support, and does the question it replaces deserve the verdict? Obvious attempt: Turing is a verificationist — 'meaningless' is Vienna's word — so the game is the method of verification of 'the machine thinks'. The lesson tests this against a second account of 'meaningless' that Turing had heard in 1939.
2. **Read the artefact.** §1: the game (man, woman, interrogator; then the machine in the man's place); 'what will happen when a machine takes the part of A?' replaces 'can machines think?'. §2: the restriction to typed conversation is deliberate ('we do not wish to penalise the machine for its inability to shine in beauty competitions'); the question becomes 'will there be machines that do well?' — an empirical prediction (2000; 10$^9$ bits; 70% after five minutes). §6(4): Jefferson's sonnet and 'the machine must *feel*'; Turing's reply: this is solipsism — by the same standard 'the only way to know that a man thinks is to be that particular man'; 'instead of arguing continually over this point it is usual to have the polite convention that everyone thinks'; the viva voce in which the machine discusses its own sonnet.
3. **The grammatical reading.** §360: 'we say only of a human being and what is like one that it thinks'; 'think' is a tool with a family of uses (§§327–339); §580: an inner process needs outward criteria; so 'the machine thinks' has a use only where the machine's behaviour supplies criteria of the kind the word has among the living — and the game is an attempt to supply exactly such criteria. On this reading Turing's 'meaningless' means: the question, asked apart from any criteria, is the beetle — 'but does it *really* think?' asks about the thing in the box, which divides through (§293). The strength of the reading: it explains why Turing refuses the consciousness objection — not because feeling is irrelevant, but because the demand for private access is a demand nobody can meet for anybody (the 'polite convention').
4. **The verificationist reading.** Ayer 1936, p. 140: 'if I know that an object behaves in every way as a conscious being must, by definition, behave, then I know that it is really conscious. And this is an analytical proposition.' Turing's game is this: behaviour in every way as a thinking being must → thinking, *by definition*. The strength: it fits §2's turn to prediction (an operational definition makes the question empirical) and the single-test form.
5. **What separates them: criteria and symptoms (§354).** A symptom is correlated evidence (the barometer falls); a criterion is what fixes the use of the word in the practice (wet and cold) and is *defeasible* — one can meet it and still be corrected later, because the practice has more in it than any one occasion. A verification is a test whose passing *is* the truth of the sentence. The game is a test: passing it is Turing's proposed truth-condition for 'the machine thinks'. So the game, as stated, sides with 1936 — while §6(4)'s reply ('the polite convention') reaches for the grammatical point: the convention is not polite; it is where the word lives (§281, PPF iv 'an attitude towards a soul, not an opinion').
6. **Verdict.** Which module results the reading used (use; criteria; the beetle; §281); where the artefact departs (a test, not a criterion; one use sampled as a definition; the right conclusion by solipsism-avoidance rather than grammar; a verdict demanded where only an attitude is available). What survives of Turing on the grammatical reading: the refusal to let 'really' name a beetle. What the dispute became: Searle's Chinese room (1980) — a system that passes and 'does not understand' — which is the beetle objection returned as a thought experiment, and which the closing module names.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Classify each of Turing's three sentences — 'too meaningless to deserve discussion', 'will there be machines that do well by 2000?', 'the polite convention that everyone thinks' — as verificationist, grammatical, or neither, with the module sentence that decides it.
2. (refute) "Turing defined 'thinking' as passing the game, so a machine that passes thinks by definition and the question is closed." Break it with §354: show that a definition by a single test makes 'thinks' a symptom-word, and give one ordinary use of 'think' (a chess player thinking before a move; §§327–330) the test does not touch.
3. (derivation) Write Turing's reply to the argument from consciousness as a dilemma (either solipsism or a standard that admits others), then write §281's version (where the word has a use), and show that the two agree on the conclusion and differ on the premise — identify the premise.
4. (boundary probe) Suppose a machine passes the game once and later produces only repetition. On the verificationist reading, did it think during the test? On the criterial reading? State what 'defeasible' adds, and which reading Turing's own §2 ('do well') leans on.
5. (claim–evidence) Ayer p. 140 and Turing §2: compare the two sentences and say whether Turing's restriction to typed conversation is a weakening of Ayer's 'in every way' or a specification of it; then say what a Wittgensteinian would say is wrong with both ('in every way' is a family, not a list).
6. (FORWARD → functionalism-and-its-objections) The Chinese room: a person in a room follows rules that produce fluent Chinese replies without understanding Chinese. Say whether this is the beetle objection (the understanding is the thing in the box) or a new one, and what §293 would say to it. The solution says the closing module places Searle beside functionalism, with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped) Turing predicted that by 2000 an average interrogator would have no more than a 70% chance of a correct identification after five minutes. State what a passing result would show on each of the two readings, and design the second measurement (the same machine over a month of varied use) that the criterial reading requires and the verificationist one does not.

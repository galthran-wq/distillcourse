goal: Formulate Quine's argument that analyticity cannot be defined without presupposing synonymy, necessity or itself, and evaluate the Grice–Strawson and Carnapian replies.

## ARC (L10)

And the replies: Grice–Strawson (a distinction everyone uses is not empty), Carnap (explication need not model usage; Quine's second argument assumes extensionalism), and what survived (degrees; Kripke's re-separation).

Spine step: "The two dogmas fall: analyticity cannot be defined without presupposing itself, and statements face experience only as a body, so no statement is immune to revision and philosophy is continuous with science — a thesis that says something must give and not what."

## Explanation plan

1. **The problem.** 'No bachelor is married' vs 'there are brick houses on Elm Street'. The whole settlement — mathematics certain because analytic, metaphysics meaningless because neither — rests on sorting statements into two bins. Sub-problem: say what puts a statement in the first. First attempt: 'true by meaning' — but what is a meaning? Quine's clearing: meaning is not reference (the Fregean point, kept: 'creature with a heart' and 'creature with a kidney' are coextensive and differ in meaning; 9 and the number of planets); and meanings as *entities* can be dropped — what is needed is only two relations: *synonymy* (sameness of meaning) and *significance* (having one). Analyticity then: the first class, logical truths ('no unmarried man is married', true under every reinterpretation of its non-logical parts — the reader knows this as a validity), is unproblematic; the second class ('no bachelor is married') is in the bin only if it turns into a logical truth by putting synonyms for synonyms. So the whole question is *synonymy*.
2. **Second attempt: definition.** 'Bachelor' is *defined* as 'unmarried man'. Where does the definition come from? The lexicographer reports an observed synonymy — cart before horse; Carnap's explication preserves some contexts and so presupposes synonymy of those; only the explicit stipulation of a new abbreviation creates synonymy by fiat, and that is rare and 'does not explain the rest'. The because: a definition is a record of synonymy or a creation of it; only in the second case does it explain, and in the second case it covers almost nothing.
3. **Third attempt: interchangeability salva veritate.** Two terms are synonymous if either can replace the other anywhere without changing truth-value. Counter-cases: 'bachelor of arts', quotation ('"bachelor" has eight letters') — set them aside as word-fragments. Then the real test: in an *extensional* language (the reader's first-order logic), interchangeability yields only coextension ('creature with a heart' passes). To get synonymy one needs a context that distinguishes coextensive from synonymous terms — 'necessarily'. (4) 'Necessarily all and only bachelors are bachelors' is true; (5) 'Necessarily all and only bachelors are unmarried men' is true iff 'all bachelors are unmarried men' is analytic. So the test works only in a language with an adverb whose sense is 'analytic', and 'the closed curve in space' closes: analytic → synonymy → interchangeability → necessity → analytic. Write it as the circle figure.
4. **Fourth attempt: artificial languages with semantical rules.** Carnap gives $L_0$ a rule listing its analytic sentences. But that explains 'analytic-for-$L_0$', an unbreakable hyphenated word, not 'analytic for $L$' with $L$ variable — 'points in Ohio that are starting points'; meaning postulates are identified 'only by appearing on a page under the heading'; one might as well take a language as an ordered pair (language, its analytic sentences) and 'stop tugging at our bootstraps'. The because: a rule *lists*; the question was what the list is a list *of*.
5. **The conclusion.** Not that analytic statements do not exist, but that no boundary between analytic and synthetic has been drawn, and the belief that one can be is 'an unempirical dogma of empiricists, a metaphysical article of faith'. The linguistic and the factual components of a statement's truth cannot be separated statement by statement — which is the second dogma's doorstep (next lesson).
6. **Why it mattered / what refuted or limited it.** Mattered: the Circle's a-priori-as-analytic thesis loses its criterion; 'analytic' survives only as a matter of degree. Replies: (a) Grice and Strawson 1956: a distinction that competent speakers apply with agreement to new cases cannot be empty; Quine demanded a reductive definition where none was owed — a reply that saves the usage and concedes the theory. (b) Carnap (Uebel): the explication of 'analytic-in-$L$' was never meant to model natural language, so the demand for 'analytic for variable $L$' misfires; and Quine's circle argument assumes extensionalism, which Carnap (with 'necessarily' in Meaning and Necessity) did not. (c) Putnam (forward): what a competent speaker is obliged to know (the stereotype) is neither analytic nor nothing — the circle is escaped by lowering the demand. (d) Kripke (forward): Quine, like the Circle, treats necessary/a priori/analytic as one notion; once pulled apart, 'necessarily' can be explained without analyticity (modal semantics) and the circle's last arc breaks. Survived: the suspicion of 'true by meaning alone'; the conviction that definitions are not foundations.

## EXERCISE PLAN

1. (warm-up) Classify under Quine's two classes, showing the substitution where class 2: 'no circle is square'; 'every vixen is a fox'; 'if $p$ then $p$ or $q$'; 'every even number is divisible by 2'.
2. (derivation) Run the salva veritate test in an extensional language on 'creature with a heart' / 'creature with a kidney' and show it passes; then add 'necessarily' and show it fails; then write out the circle from (4) and (5) as a chain of four 'presupposes' arrows and name the arc Kripke's module will break.
3. (boundary probe) Stipulative definition: suppose every word of a language were introduced by explicit abbreviation from a finite stock of primitives. Would analyticity then be definable? Say what becomes of the primitives' own synonymies and why Quine says this case 'does not explain the rest'.
4. (refute) "Quine proves there are no analytic truths." Break it: quote the sentence stating the actual conclusion ('a boundary has not been drawn'), and state what a *difference of degree* in revisability would look like for '$2 + 2 = 4$' versus 'brick houses on Elm Street'.
5. (claim–evidence — Grice & Strawson) Competent speakers agree on new cases of 'means the same'. Construct two sentences no speaker has considered and predict the agreed verdict; then say what Quine can concede to this (a usage exists) and what he need not (that the usage marks a boundary of the kind empiricism needed).
6. (FORWARD → holism-and-naturalism) Quine says the linguistic and factual components of a statement's truth cannot be separated. Take 'the boiling point of water is 100 °C at sea level' and try to split it into a meaning part and a world part; show two ways the split could go and that nothing in the sentence decides. The solution says the next lesson explains why: confirmation is holistic, with a `<Ref>`.
7. (research-shaped — Putnam's doorstep) Reichenbach's argument (Putnam p. 175): the conjunction of 'normal induction', 'continuity of space' and 'Euclidean space' conflicts with relativity, though each was held a priori. Show that if analyticity were unrevisability, analyticity would fail to be closed under conjunction; state what this does to Quine's equation of 'analytic' with 'held true come what may', and what Putnam proposes instead (the stereotype) — the doorstep of putnam-twin-earth.

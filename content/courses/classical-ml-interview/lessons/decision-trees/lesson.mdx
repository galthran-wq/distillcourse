A decision tree fits by asking one greedy question at a time — which split most reduces impurity — and controls overfitting by pruning the result. This chapter constructs the full CART procedure: the partition-plus-constants model, a root split chosen entirely by hand with Gini impurity, the three impurity measures and why classification error cannot grow a tree, regression trees on residual sums of squares, cost-complexity pruning with $\alpha$ selected by cross-validation, and the trade-off ledger that the three ensemble chapters build on.

## The model: a tree is an axis-aligned partition with constant leaves

A binary decision tree is a flowchart of threshold questions. Each internal node tests one feature against one cutoff: $x_j \le t$ for a numeric feature, $x_j \in S$ for a categorical one. Each answer descends one level; each leaf outputs a constant. Tracing every path at once shows what the flowchart is: a recursive carving of feature space into **axis-aligned rectangles**, one rectangle per leaf.

<Definition title="decision tree (CART)">
A *decision tree* partitions the feature space into disjoint regions $R_1, \dots, R_M$ by recursive binary splits on single features, and predicts a constant per region:

$$
f(x) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} c_m \,\mathbf{1}\{x \in R_m\},
$$

where $c_m$ is the **majority class** of the training points in $R_m$ (classification) or their **mean response** (regression).
</Definition>

<Figure
  src="./figures/duality.svg"
  alt="The same two-split model drawn as a tree on the left and as three nested axis-aligned rectangles in feature space on the right, with colors matching each leaf to its region"
  caption="One model, two drawings: every leaf of the tree is a rectangle of the partition, and prediction is a lookup: find the rectangle, output its constant."
/>

The model class is *non-parametric*: there is no fixed coefficient vector, and the number of regions grows with the data. A leaf's prediction uses only the training points inside its own rectangle, which makes the tree a piecewise-constant estimator with data-chosen pieces.

Fitting the best partition is not an option. Even for binary features, constructing the smallest tree consistent with a training set is NP-complete ([Hyafil & Rivest, 1976](https://doi.org/10.1016/0020-0190(76)90095-8)), and the space of axis-aligned partitions is astronomically large. CART answers with a **greedy heuristic**: choose the single best split now, commit to it, recurse on each child. The rest of the chapter is that heuristic plus its damage control.

<KeyFact>
A tree and its rectangular partition are the same object. Everything CART does (splitting, pruning, predicting) is an operation on axis-aligned rectangles carrying constants.
</KeyFact>

## One split by hand: score every candidate, keep the argmin

The greedy step is the interview set piece, so it is worth executing once with no steps skipped. Ten machines, two features, a binary label:

| machine | vibration | temp (°C) | fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | high | 28 | yes |
| 2 | high | 31 | yes |
| 3 | high | 24 | yes |
| 4 | high | 21 | no |
| 5 | high | 33 | yes |
| 6 | low | 26 | no |
| 7 | low | 19 | no |
| 8 | low | 30 | yes |
| 9 | low | 23 | no |
| 10 | low | 25 | no |

The root node holds 5 failures and 5 survivals. Draw two rows at random with replacement; they carry different labels with probability $2 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.5$. That number, the chance a random pair disagrees, is the node's impurity.

<Definition title="Gini impurity">
For a node whose class proportions are $p_1, \dots, p_K$, the *Gini impurity* is

$$
G = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k (1 - p_k) = 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k^2 ,
$$

the probability that two independent draws from the node disagree. $G = 0$ iff the node is pure; for two classes the maximum is $G = 0.5$ at a 50/50 mix.
</Definition>

A split is scored by the **weighted average** of its children's impurities, weights proportional to child sizes; the best split is the one with the smallest weighted child impurity, equivalently the largest impurity drop.

<Example title="the root split, every candidate scored">
**Candidates.** The binary feature contributes one split, vibration $\in \{$high, low$\}$. For temperature, sort the ten values; only thresholds between adjacent distinct values can produce distinct partitions, giving 9 midpoints: $20, 22, 23.5, 24.5, 25.5, 27, 29, 30.5, 32$.

**Vibration.** High: machines 1–5, with 4 fail / 1 OK, so $G = 1 - (4/5)^2 - (1/5)^2 = 0.320$. Low: machines 6–10, with 1 fail / 4 OK, $G = 0.320$ by symmetry. Weighted: $0.5 \times 0.320 + 0.5 \times 0.320 = 0.320$.

**Temperature, $t = 27$.** Left ($\le 27$): machines 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, with 1 fail / 5 OK, $G = 1 - (1/6)^2 - (5/6)^2 = 10/36 = 0.278$. Right ($> 27$): machines 1, 2, 5, 8, with 4 fail / 0 OK, $G = 0$. Weighted: $\tfrac{6}{10} \times 0.278 + \tfrac{4}{10} \times 0 = 0.167$.

**All nine thresholds**, same arithmetic:

| $t$ | left fail/OK | right fail/OK | weighted Gini |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20 | 0/1 | 5/4 | 0.444 |
| 22 | 0/2 | 5/3 | 0.375 |
| 23.5 | 0/3 | 5/2 | 0.286 |
| 24.5 | 1/3 | 4/2 | 0.417 |
| 25.5 | 1/4 | 4/1 | 0.320 |
| **27** | **1/5** | **4/0** | **0.167** |
| 29 | 2/5 | 3/0 | 0.286 |
| 30.5 | 3/5 | 2/0 | 0.375 |
| 32 | 4/5 | 1/0 | 0.444 |

**Verdict.** Temperature $\le 27$ scores $0.167$ against vibration's $0.320$; the impurity drop is $0.500 - 0.167 = 0.333$ against $0.180$. The root splits on temp $\le 27$.
</Example>

### Recursion and stopping

The right child (temp $> 27$) is pure: it becomes a leaf predicting *fails*. The left child (1 fail / 5 OK, $G = 0.278$) is re-split by the same scan restricted to its six rows: vibration now scores $\tfrac{2}{6} \times 0.5 + \tfrac{4}{6} \times 0 = 0.167$, beating the best temperature threshold in the child ($t = 23.5$, weighted $0.222$), so the second level splits on vibration. Its low branch (machines 6, 7, 9, 10) is a pure OK leaf; the high branch holds machines 3 and 4, separated by one final cut at temp $\le 22.5$. The fully grown tree has 4 leaves and zero training errors, a fact the pruning section returns to.

Growth stops at a node when it is pure, when it holds fewer than a minimum number of points, or when no split reduces impurity by more than a threshold. Per feature, the scan costs one sort plus a linear sweep, $O(n \log n)$, because the class counts on each side update by one point per candidate threshold.

<KeyFact>
CART's entire training loop is: for every feature and every viable threshold, compute the weighted child impurity; keep the argmin; recurse. Executed by hand it is bookkeeping, and interviewers ask for exactly this bookkeeping.
</KeyFact>

## Impurity measures: Gini, entropy, and why error cannot grow a tree

Three impurity functions appear in practice, all functions of the node's class proportions. At the root above, $p = (0.5, 0.5)$: Gini gives $0.5$, entropy gives $1$ bit, misclassification error gives $0.5$.

<Definition title="entropy and information gain">
The *entropy* of a node with class proportions $p_1, \dots, p_K$ is $H = -\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k$, and the *information gain* of a split is the parent's entropy minus the weighted child entropy. The *misclassification error* is $E = 1 - \max_k p_k$, the fraction a majority-vote leaf gets wrong.
</Definition>

<Figure
  src="./figures/impurity-curves.svg"
  alt="Gini, halved entropy, and misclassification error plotted against the class-1 proportion p; Gini and entropy are strictly concave and nearly coincide, while error is two straight segments; a chord on the Gini curve marks the parent-versus-children gap of the worked example"
  caption="Binary impurities vs p. Gini and (scaled) entropy are strictly concave: the chord through any two child distributions lies below the parent's value. Error is piecewise linear, so the chord can lie on the curve."
/>

Running the root scan with entropy instead of Gini picks the same split: temp $\le 27$ has weighted entropy $0.390$ against vibration's $0.722$. That agreement is typical. **Gini and entropy are nearly interchangeable**: both strictly concave, both differentiable, disagreeing only on a small fraction of candidate splits. The honest answer to "which one and why": Gini by default because it skips the logarithm, entropy if you want the information-theoretic reading; the resulting trees rarely differ.

### Error stalls where Gini moves

Misclassification error is the metric that ultimately matters, and it is still the wrong growing criterion. The failure is concrete:

<Example title="purer children, unchanged error">
A node holds 6 positives and 2 negatives; error as a leaf: $2/8$. Split it into children $(3+, 0-)$ and $(3+, 2-)$. Both children still vote positive, so the errors are $0$ and $2$: total $2/8$, unchanged. The Gini score sees the progress: parent $G = 1 - (6/8)^2 - (2/8)^2 = 0.375$; weighted children $\tfrac{3}{8} \times 0 + \tfrac{5}{8} \times 0.48 = 0.300$. Entropy agrees: $0.811 \to 0.607$.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/purity-error.svg"
  alt="A parent node of six positives and two negatives split into a pure child and a five-point child; the tally shows classification error unchanged at two eighths while Gini drops from 0.375 to 0.300"
  caption="A split that manufactures a pure leaf while classification error sits still. Error-driven growing would refuse it; Gini-driven growing takes it and cashes in on the next level."
/>

The pure child is genuine progress (one branch of the tree is finished), yet an error-greedy grower would score the split at zero and could stop. The general fact behind the example:

<Theorem title="strictly concave impurities reward every purification">
Let $i(p)$ be concave on the probability simplex, and let a split send fractions $w_L, w_R$ of a node's points to children with class distributions $p_L, p_R$, so the parent distribution is $p = w_L p_L + w_R p_R$. Then the weighted child impurity never exceeds the parent's:

$$
w_L\, i(p_L) + w_R\, i(p_R) \;\le\; i(p),
$$

with strict inequality whenever $i$ is strictly concave and $p_L \ne p_R$.

<Proof>
The parent's class distribution is the mixture of the children's with weights $w_L + w_R = 1$, so the claim is Jensen's inequality applied to the concave $i$ at the point $p = w_L p_L + w_R p_R$. Strict concavity makes Jensen strict unless the two child distributions coincide. Misclassification error $E(p) = 1 - \max_k p_k$ is concave but only *piecewise linear*: on a linear segment (in the binary case, whenever both children keep the same majority class) the inequality holds with equality, which is exactly the stalled split of the example.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

For **pruning**, the calculus flips: the question is no longer "which split makes progress" but "which subtree is worth its leaves," and there [ESL §9.2](https://hastie.su.domains/ElemStatLearn/) recommends plain misclassification error (or the actual deployment loss), since accuracy is what the pruned tree is judged on.

<Remark title="high-cardinality features cheat the gain">
A categorical feature with many levels (an ID column in the extreme) can shatter a node into near-singleton children, driving impurity to zero with zero generalization value. Information gain therefore systematically favors high-cardinality features; C4.5's *gain ratio* ([Quinlan, 1986](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00116251)) divides the gain by the split's own entropy to tax the fan-out. The same bias resurfaces as inflated variable importance in <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests" />.
</Remark>

<Aside>
Entropy's impurity reading is a KL divergence in disguise. For $K$ classes, $\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, \text{uniform}) = \sum_k p_k \log_2 \frac{p_k}{1/K} = \log_2 K - H(p)$, so minimizing weighted child entropy is the same as pushing the children's class distributions as far from uniform as possible in KL. A pure leaf is the distribution farthest from "no information": the greedy grower is doing information maximization one split at a time, which is the framing in [Weinberger's CS4780 lecture](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/courses/cs4780/2018fa/lectures/lecturenote17.html).
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Grow with Gini or entropy: strict concavity means any purification scores as progress. Misclassification error is piecewise linear, can sit still through a genuinely useful split, and is the right metric only for pruning, where accuracy is the question.
</KeyFact>

## Regression trees: RSS as impurity, leaf means as predictions

For a numeric response the recipe survives with two substitutions: the leaf constant becomes the **mean**, and impurity becomes the **residual sum of squares** around that mean. A concrete scan first:

<Example title="a regression split by hand">
Six employees, $x$ = years of experience, $y$ = salary in \$1000s: $(1, 96), (2, 104), (3, 100), (4, 300), (5, 320), (6, 310)$. The root prediction is the grand mean $\bar y = 205$, with $\mathrm{RSS} = 66{,}382$.

Candidate thresholds sit between adjacent $x$ values. For $t = 3.5$: left mean $= (96 + 104 + 100)/3 = 100$, $\mathrm{RSS}_L = 16 + 16 + 0 = 32$; right mean $= 310$, $\mathrm{RSS}_R = 100 + 100 + 0 = 200$; total $232$. The competitors are not close: $t = 2.5$ gives $32 + 33{,}275 = 33{,}307$, $t = 4.5$ gives $30{,}032 + 50 = 30{,}082$, and the edge splits exceed $52{,}000$. The split at $t = 3.5$ collapses the RSS from $66{,}382$ to $232$ because it separates the two salary regimes; further splits inside each child now shave residuals of tens, not tens of thousands.
</Example>

The general objective, in the notation of [ISLP §8.1](https://www.statlearning.com): at each node choose the feature $j$ and cutpoint $s$ minimizing

$$
\sum_{i:\, x_i \in R_1(j,s)} (y_i - \bar y_{R_1})^2 \;+\; \sum_{i:\, x_i \in R_2(j,s)} (y_i - \bar y_{R_2})^2 ,
$$

where $R_1(j, s) = \{x \mid x_j \le s\}$ and $R_2(j, s) = \{x \mid x_j > s\}$ and each $\bar y$ is the mean response in its region. As in classification, the scan over $s$ is exact and fast: sorted once, the two means and RSS values update in constant time per candidate.

The field's canonical first tree is ISLP's Hitters example: predicting a baseball player's log salary, the root asks Years $< 4.5$; experienced players are then split by Hits $< 117.5$. Back-transformed, the three leaves predict about \$165k for junior players, \$403k for seniors with few hits, and \$845k for seniors with many: a three-rectangle partition of the (Years, Hits) plane that reads as three sentences.

## Grow deep, then prune: cost-complexity and the tree's lasso

The fully grown tree from the hand-built example ended with two singleton leaves whose only job is to classify machines 3 and 4, one training row each. That is memorization: depth is a flexibility dial, and an unrestricted tree drives training error to zero while test error climbs, the variance-dominated regime of <Ref lesson="bias-variance" />. Both failure directions exist: a stump can underfit badly, so trees are not "always overfitting," they are **unstable at high depth and biased at low depth**.

### Why prune instead of stopping early

The cheap control is *early stopping*: cap the depth, require a minimum leaf size, or refuse splits below a minimum impurity drop. The first two are standard and harmless as coarse limits. The third is myopic in a specific way: a split with a small immediate drop can unlock an excellent split one level down, so a grower that stops on low gain never finds the pair (the extreme case is XOR-structured data, taken up in the ledger). CART's answer is to **grow deep first and prune afterwards**, judging each subtree by what it achieved, not by what its first split promised.

<Definition title="cost-complexity pruning">
For a subtree $T$ of the full tree $T_0$, with $|T|$ leaves, the *cost-complexity* criterion is

$$
C_\alpha(T) = \sum_{m=1}^{|T|} \sum_{x_i \in R_m} L\big(y_i, c_m\big) \;+\; \alpha\, |T| ,
$$

training loss plus a per-leaf charge. For each $\alpha \ge 0$, the subtree minimizing $C_\alpha$ is found by *weakest-link pruning*: repeatedly collapse the internal node whose subtree buys the least loss reduction per leaf spent.
</Definition>

The criterion is the tree's version of the penalized objectives of <Ref lesson="ridge-lasso" />: loss plus $\alpha \times$ complexity, with $|T|$ playing the role of $\|\beta\|_1$ and $\alpha$ walking the model from memorization ($\alpha = 0$ keeps $T_0$) to a bare root ($\alpha$ large). And as with the lasso path, the whole family comes cheap:

<Example title="weakest-link pruning of the machines tree">
The grown tree has 4 leaves and training error $0$; internal nodes are the root, the vibration node (6 rows, 1 fail / 5 OK), and the temp $\le 22.5$ node (2 rows). Collapsing a node to a leaf costs errors and saves leaves; the *link strength* is $g = \frac{\Delta\text{error rate}}{\Delta\text{leaves}}$:

- temp $\le 22.5$ node: collapse costs $1/10$, saves $1$ leaf $\Rightarrow g = 0.10$;
- vibration node (3-leaf subtree): collapse costs $1/10$, saves $2$ leaves $\Rightarrow g = 0.05$;
- root (whole tree): collapse costs $5/10$, saves $3$ leaves $\Rightarrow g = 0.167$.

The weakest link is the vibration node: at $\alpha = 0.05$ its whole subtree collapses to an OK leaf, giving a 2-leaf tree (temp $\le 27 \to$ OK, else fail) with error $1/10$. Recomputing on the survivor, the root's link is $g = (0.5 - 0.1)/1 = 0.4$. The full solution path is therefore: 4 leaves for $\alpha < 0.05$, 2 leaves for $0.05 \le \alpha < 0.4$, the root for $\alpha \ge 0.4$. The 2-leaf tree is the one worth keeping: the two singleton leaves existed to memorize machines 3 and 4.
</Example>

<Remark title="the sequence is nested, not exhaustive">
Weakest-link pruning does not consider every subtree of $T_0$; it produces one **nested sequence** $T_0 \supset T_1 \supset \cdots \supset \{\text{root}\}$, and a theorem of [Breiman et al. (1984)](https://doi.org/10.1201/9781315139470) guarantees the $C_\alpha$-optimal subtree for every $\alpha$ lies on that path. Candidates think every subtree is in play; only the path is, which is what makes the search tractable.
</Remark>

Selecting $\alpha$ is a one-dimensional tuning problem, handled exactly like $\lambda$ in penalized regression, by the machinery of <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap" />. ISLP's Algorithm 8.1, in full:

1. Grow $T_0$ on the training data, stopping only at a minimum leaf size.
2. Weakest-link prune to obtain the nested sequence of subtrees indexed by $\alpha$.
3. For each of $K$ folds: grow and prune on the other $K - 1$ folds, and record each $\alpha$'s held-out error.
4. Pick the $\alpha$ minimizing the CV error (or the simplest tree within one standard error of the minimum); return that member of the sequence from step 2.

On the Hitters data this procedure cuts a 12-leaf grown tree down to the 3-leaf tree shown above: the CV curve bottoms out almost immediately, and the one-SE rule lands on the smallest tree near the bottom.

<KeyFact>
Cost-complexity pruning is the tree's lasso: training loss plus $\alpha|T|$, one nested solution path traced by weakest-link collapses, one scalar $\alpha$ chosen by cross-validation.
</KeyFact>

## The ledger: what one tree buys and what it costs

The next three chapters each exist to fix one line of this table, so it is the map of the whole module.

| trees buy | trees cost |
| --- | --- |
| Readable models: a small tree is an executable flowchart | High variance: small data changes rebuild the tree |
| Mixed numeric/categorical features, no dummy coding required | Axis-aligned bias: diagonal boundaries become staircases |
| Invariance to monotone feature transforms; no scaling ever | Greedy myopia: gain-blind at split time (XOR) |
| Native missing-value handling (surrogates, default directions) | Piecewise-constant fits: no smooth trends, no extrapolation |
| Automatic interaction discovery and feature selection | Accuracy: a single pruned tree rarely wins benchmarks |

### Instability: the argmin flips

Every split is a discrete argmin over candidates, and near-ties are everywhere. Delete one row, machine 8, from the hand-built table and the root scan reverses: vibration now scores $0.178$ against temperature's $0.185$, so the root feature itself changes, and everything below it is rebuilt. Two trees, one row apart, similar accuracy, disjoint structure: that is **variance in model space**, and it is the raw material <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests" /> averages away.

### Axis-aligned bias: diagonals become staircases

Rectangles are the model class, so any boundary that is not axis-parallel is approximated by stairs. A true boundary $x_2 = x_1$ costs a tower of splits to imitate, and the staircase's corners are regions of guaranteed error; a linear model draws the same boundary with one coefficient pair. The mismatch runs both ways: when the truth is a checkerboard of interactions, the tree wins and the linear model needs hand-built cross terms.

<Figure
  src="./figures/staircase.svg"
  alt="A diagonal true class boundary approximated by an axis-aligned staircase of tree splits, with each step corresponding to one split"
  caption="Axis-aligned bias: a diagonal boundary is representable only as a staircase, one split per stair. Ensembles of trees average many staircases into a nearly smooth boundary."
/>

Greedy myopia has the same flavor. On XOR-structured data (positives in two opposite quadrants), every single split leaves both children at 50/50, so every candidate's gain is zero and the greedy score is blind, even though a depth-2 tree separates the classes perfectly. Implementations that split anyway on an arbitrary tie-break recover; the point is that the greedy criterion, not the model class, is what fails.

Two practicalities complete the ledger. A categorical feature with $q$ levels admits $2^{q-1} - 1$ subset splits: exponential, though for binary classification and regression, ordering the levels by their mean response finds the optimum among only $q - 1$ ordered splits (ESL §9.2.4). Missing values never stop a tree: CART routes them by *surrogate splits* (backup features that mimic the primary split), and gradient-boosted variants learn a per-node *default direction*, the mechanism <Ref lesson="xgboost" /> makes central.

<KeyFact>
Everything the ensemble chapters fix is a line in this ledger: bagging attacks the variance, random forests the correlation among bagged trees, boosting the bias. A single pruned tree is an interview staple and a strong baseline, and it is almost never the final model.
</KeyFact>

A node holds 9 positives and 3 negatives. A candidate split produces children $(5+, 0-)$ and $(4+, 3-)$. Show that misclassification error refuses this split while Gini accepts it, computing all quantities.

<Solution>
Parent as a leaf predicts positive: error $3/12 = 0.25$. After the split both children still vote positive, with errors $0$ and $3$: total $3/12$, unchanged: an error-greedy grower scores the split at exactly zero.

Gini: parent $G = 1 - (9/12)^2 - (3/12)^2 = 1 - 0.5625 - 0.0625 = 0.375$. Left child pure, $G = 0$. Right child: $G = 1 - (4/7)^2 - (3/7)^2 = 24/49 = 0.490$. Weighted: $\tfrac{5}{12} \times 0 + \tfrac{7}{12} \times 0.490 = 0.286$, a drop of $0.089$: Gini takes the split. The split is genuinely useful: five points are finished in a pure leaf, and the remaining impurity is concentrated where later splits can attack it. Error misses this because both children kept the parent's majority, which is precisely the piecewise-linear blind spot.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Categorical and missing data: (a) count the distinct binary splits of a categorical feature with $q = 4$ levels and with $q = 20$ levels; (b) state the trick that avoids the exponential search for binary classification; (c) describe two mechanisms by which trees handle missing feature values at test time.

<Solution>
(a) A binary split assigns each level to one of two sides, up to swapping sides and excluding the empty split: $2^{q-1} - 1$. For $q = 4$: $2^3 - 1 = 7$. For $q = 20$: $2^{19} - 1 = 524{,}287$: infeasible to enumerate inside every node.

(b) For binary classification (and for regression), sort the levels by their proportion of positives (mean response) and consider only the $q - 1$ splits of that ordering; the optimal subset split under Gini/entropy (RSS) is among them (ESL §9.2.4). The categorical search becomes the same sorted scan as a numeric feature.

(c) CART's *surrogate splits*: at each node, backup splits on other features are ranked by how well they mimic the primary split's left/right assignment on the training data; a point missing the primary feature is routed by the best surrogate it does have. Gradient-boosted trees instead learn a *default direction* per node: during training, points with missing values are tried down each branch and the direction that reduces the loss more is stored; this is the sparsity-aware rule at the core of <Ref lesson="xgboost" />. Either way, no imputation step is required, unlike for linear models or k-NN.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Instability, verified. Delete machine 8 (low vibration, 30 °C, fails) from the chapter's table and rescore the two leading root candidates, vibration and temp $\le 27$. Show the winning root split changes, then explain in the terms of <Ref lesson="bias-variance" /> why deep trees are high-variance and what averaging over bootstrap resamples would do to that term.

<Solution>
Nine rows remain: 4 fail / 5 OK, root $G = 1 - (4/9)^2 - (5/9)^2 = 40/81 = 0.494$.

Vibration: high unchanged, 4 fail / 1 OK, $G = 0.32$; low is now 0 fail / 4 OK, $G = 0$. Weighted: $\tfrac{5}{9} \times 0.32 = 0.178$. Temp $\le 27$: left unchanged, 1 fail / 5 OK, $G = 0.278$; right $\{28, 31, 33\}$, pure. Verdict flips: $0.178 < 0.185$, the root now splits on vibration, and the entire subtree below is rebuilt.

Bias–variance reading: a deep tree can realize almost any labeling of the training set, so its bias is near zero and its error is dominated by variance: the fitted model is a jumpy function of the training draw, exactly like the degree-9 sheaf of the bias–variance chapter. The mechanism is discrete: each split is an argmin over candidates, near-ties flip under one-point perturbations, and the hierarchy propagates a flipped root into a completely different partition. Averaging $B$ trees fitted on bootstrap resamples leaves the (low) bias essentially unchanged and divides the independent part of the variance, which is why bagging targets trees specifically; the correlated remainder is the next chapter's subject.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Does standardizing features change a CART tree? Prove your answer for any strictly increasing transform of a single feature, and name a model from an earlier chapter where the answer is the opposite.

<Solution>
No. Let $g$ be strictly increasing and replace feature $j$ by $g(x_j)$. Splits use only threshold comparisons: for any threshold $t$ on the original scale, the split $x_j \le t$ induces the same left/right partition of the training points as $g(x_j) \le g(t)$ on the transformed scale, and conversely every transformed-scale partition arises this way, because $g$ preserves order and the candidate set (one partition per gap between adjacent sorted values) depends only on the ordering. Identical candidate partitions have identical class counts, hence identical impurities: the grown tree is the same tree with relabeled thresholds, and predictions are unchanged. Standardization is affine with positive slope, a special case; the same argument covers $\log$, rank, and any monotone rescaling.

The opposite: k-NN (<Ref lesson="knn-curse" />) computes distances, so rescaling one feature reweights its contribution to every distance and can change every neighborhood; feature scaling is mandatory there and irrelevant here. (SVMs and penalized regressions are also scale-sensitive, through the inner products and the penalty respectively.)
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
When would a decision tree beat logistic regression, and when the reverse? Give at least three concrete conditions each way.

<Solution>
Tree wins: (1) strong feature interactions: a tree's nested splits model "risk is high only if A and B" natively, while logistic regression needs hand-built cross terms; (2) nonlinear, threshold-like effects (dose above a cutoff, age bands): piecewise-constant fits match them with one split; (3) mixed numeric/categorical features with missing values: no dummy coding, no scaling, surrogate/default routing; (4) the audience requires a readable decision rule.

Logistic regression wins: (1) the true boundary is close to linear in the features: the tree must build a staircase (axis-aligned bias) while one coefficient vector nails it; (2) small samples with many weak additive effects: a deep tree's variance overwhelms the signal a stable linear fit captures; (3) calibrated probabilities and inference are required: coefficients, odds ratios, and confidence statements (<Ref lesson="logistic-regression" />), where a small tree outputs coarse leaf frequencies; (4) extrapolation beyond the training range, where any tree predicts the constant of its edge leaf.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

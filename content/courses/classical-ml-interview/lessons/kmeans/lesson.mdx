k-means alternates two moves — assign each point to its nearest center, recompute each center as the mean of its points — and each move can only decrease the same objective, so the algorithm converges. This chapter runs that loop to convergence by hand on eight points, proves the convergence claim as a two-lemma coordinate-descent argument, and then covers the three questions interviewers attach to it: how to seed (restarts, k-means++), how to choose $k$ (the elbow and its limits), and where the method fails (non-convex shapes, unequal clusters, unscaled features).

## The objective: one number scores a partition

Eight unlabeled points in the plane: $A=(1,1)$, $B=(2,1)$, $C=(1,2)$, $D=(2,2)$, $E=(5,4)$, $F=(6,4)$, $G=(5,5)$, $H=(6,5)$. The visually obvious partition into $k=2$ groups is $\{A,B,C,D\}$ and $\{E,F,G,H\}$. Scoring it: place a center at each group's mean, $\mu_1 = (1.5, 1.5)$ and $\mu_2 = (5.5, 4.5)$, and sum every point's squared distance to its own center. Each of the eight points sits at squared distance $0.5^2 + 0.5^2 = 0.5$ from its mean, so the total is $8 \times 0.5 = 4.0$. Any worse grouping produces a larger total; the total is the number k-means minimizes.

<Definition title="distortion">
Given points $x_1, \dots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$, centers $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and one-hot assignment variables $r_{nj} \in \{0,1\}$ with $\sum_j r_{nj} = 1$, the **distortion** is

$$
J(r, \mu) \;=\; \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{k} r_{nj}\, \lVert x_n - \mu_j \rVert^2 ,
$$

the total squared distance from each point to the center of its cluster.
</Definition>

The same quantity appears as *inertia* in scikit-learn and as the *within-cluster variation* in [ISLP §12.4.1](https://www.statlearning.com); the 0/1 assignment notation is Bishop's ([PRML §9.1](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/pattern-recognition-machine-learning/), there written $r_{nk}$; this course indexes clusters by $j$ since $k$ counts them), and it is worth adopting now: <Ref lesson="gmm-em" /> will soften exactly these hard 0/1 assignments into posterior probabilities.

Minimizing $J$ jointly over assignments and centers is a combinatorial problem: the number of partitions of $n$ points into $k$ groups grows exponentially, and exact minimization is NP-hard even for $k = 2$ ([Aloise et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-009-0103-x)). What runs in practice is [Lloyd's algorithm](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.1982.1056489) (proposed 1957, published 1982): a local descent that fixes one block of variables and exactly minimizes over the other, alternating until nothing changes.

<KeyFact>
k-means is not an algorithm that finds the best clustering; it is a local descent on the distortion $J(r, \mu)$, exact minimization of which is NP-hard even for $k = 2$. Everything that follows (convergence, local optima, seeding) is a statement about that descent.
</KeyFact>

## Lloyd's algorithm: a full run by hand

The two moves must be executable from a standing start: given centers, produce assignments; given assignments, produce centers. The **assignment step** sends each point to its nearest center. The **update step** replaces each center by the mean of the points assigned to it. The whole algorithm is these two moves repeated, and interviewers ask for it executed on paper.

### Iteration 1: assign, then update

<Example title="Lloyd's run, iteration 1">
Data: the eight points above. Seeds: $\mu_1 = (1,1)$, $\mu_2 = (2,1)$ (two data points, deliberately both in the left blob's row).

**Assignment.** Squared distances to each center:

| $G\,(5,5)$ | $32$ | $25$ | 2 |
| $H\,(6,5)$ | $41$ | $32$ | 2 |

Cluster 1 is $\{A, C\}$, cluster 2 is $\{B, D, E, F, G, H\}$. Summing the chosen column entries: $J = 0 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 18 + 25 + 25 + 32 = 102$.

**Update.** Each center moves to the mean of its points:

$$
\mu_1 = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[(1,1) + (1,2)\bigr] = (1, 1.5), \qquad

Recomputing $J$ with the same assignment and the new centers: $0.25 + 0.25$ for cluster 1, and $11.69 + 7.69 + 0.69 + 3.03 + 2.69 + 5.03 = 30.83$ for cluster 2, so $J = 31.33$. The update alone cut $J$ from $102$ to $31.33$.
</Example>

**Update.** $\mu_1 = \tfrac{1}{4}[(1,1)+(2,1)+(1,2)+(2,2)] = (1.5, 1.5)$ and $\mu_2 = (5.5, 4.5)$: the blob means. Every point now sits at squared distance $0.5$ from its center, so $J = 4.0$.

**Iteration 3.** Every point is already nearest its own center; the assignment step changes nothing, so the means change nothing either. The algorithm stops with $J = 4.0$, the partition scored at the top of the chapter.
</Example>

The trace $102 \to 31.33 \to 14.44 \to 4.0 \to 4.0$ fell at every half-step. [Bishop §9.1](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/pattern-recognition-machine-learning/) runs the same loop on the Old Faithful data (Figs. 9.1–9.2) with the same signature: a monotone staircase of $J$ values, halting after a handful of iterations.

### The algorithm in general

1. Choose $k$ initial centers $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_k$ (seeding is treated below).
2. **Assignment:** for each $n$, set $r_{nj} = 1$ for $j = \arg\min_{j'} \lVert x_n - \mu_{j'} \rVert^2$ and $0$ otherwise.
3. **Update:** for each $j$, set $\mu_j = \dfrac{\sum_n r_{nj}\, x_n}{\sum_n r_{nj}}$, the mean of cluster $j$.
4. Repeat 2–3 until no assignment changes.

<Remark title="ties and empty clusters">
Two edge cases need fixed conventions. **Ties:** a point equidistant to two centers must be assigned by a deterministic rule (lowest center index, or keep its previous cluster); an inconsistent tie rule is the one way the partition can oscillate without $J$ changing. **Empty clusters:** an update step can leave a center with no points (its $r$-column all zero); the standard repair is to re-seed that center at the point farthest from its current center, which strictly decreases $J$. Interviewers occasionally probe exactly this implementation detail.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
One Lloyd iteration is two exact minimizations: the assignment step is the best possible $r$ for the current $\mu$, and the update step is the best possible $\mu$ for the current $r$. The run above never chose approximately.
</KeyFact>

### The two lemmas

<Theorem title="the assignment step minimizes J over r">
Fix the centers $\mu$. Among all assignments $r$, the nearest-center rule $r_{nj} = 1 \iff j = \arg\min_{j'} \lVert x_n - \mu_{j'} \rVert^2$ minimizes $J(r, \mu)$.

<Proof>
$J$ is a sum of $N$ independent terms, one per point: point $n$ contributes $\lVert x_n - \mu_j \rVert^2$ for whichever single $j$ it is assigned to. Each term is minimized separately by picking the smallest available value, which is the nearest center; no assignment can beat a pointwise minimum.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The update step's lemma is the reason the mean, rather than any other summary, is the right center. A concrete check first: in iteration 1, cluster 2 held $\{B, D, E, F, G, H\}$ with its center still at the seed $(2,1)$, contributing $0 + 1 + 18 + 25 + 25 + 32 = 101$ to $J$; moving the center to the cluster mean $(4.33, 3.5)$ dropped that contribution to $30.83$. The lemma says no other point in the plane does better.

<Theorem title="the mean minimizes total squared distance">
Fix a cluster with points $x_{(1)}, \dots, x_{(m)}$. The function $F(\mu) = \sum_{i=1}^m \lVert x_{(i)} - \mu \rVert^2$ is minimized uniquely at the mean $\bar{x} = \tfrac{1}{m} \sum_i x_{(i)}$.

<Proof>
Differentiate with respect to $\mu$:

$$
\nabla_\mu F = \sum_{i=1}^m 2\,(\mu - x_{(i)}) = 2\Bigl(m\mu - \sum_i x_{(i)}\Bigr).
$$

Setting the gradient to zero gives $\mu = \tfrac{1}{m}\sum_i x_{(i)} = \bar{x}$. The Hessian is $2m I \succ 0$, so $F$ is strictly convex and $\bar{x}$ is the unique global minimizer. Applied cluster by cluster, the update step therefore minimizes $J(r, \mu)$ over all centers $\mu$ with $r$ fixed.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### Termination

<Theorem title="Lloyd's algorithm terminates in finitely many iterations">
With a deterministic tie rule, Lloyd's algorithm reaches an iteration in which no assignment changes, after finitely many iterations.

<Proof>
By the two lemmas, neither half-step can increase $J$, so the sequence of $J$ values is non-increasing and bounded below by $0$. After each update step the state is determined entirely by the partition (centers are its cluster means), and there are at most $k^N$ partitions of $N$ points into $k$ labeled groups: finitely many. If an assignment step changes the partition under a deterministic tie rule, some point strictly reduced its own term, so $J$ strictly decreases; a strictly decreasing sequence can never revisit a partition. A finite set of partitions with no revisits forces the assignment step to eventually change nothing, at which point the means are already the cluster means and the algorithm halts.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The guarantee is exactly termination at a fixed point of the two moves, nothing more. The fixed point reached depends on the seeds, and it can be far from the global minimizer of $J$: Exercise 2 verifies a four-point dataset on which Lloyd's algorithm converges, from bad seeds, to a partition whose distortion is $16$ times the optimum. Worst-case iteration counts are also theoretically bad — [Vattani (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00454-011-9340-1) constructs plane instances needing exponentially many iterations — but in practice the loop stabilizes in tens of iterations, and implementations additionally cap `max_iter`.

<KeyFact>
k-means always converges: both half-steps minimize the same $J$ in their own block of variables, $J \ge 0$, and there are finitely many partitions. It converges to a local optimum only — no seeding-free guarantee connects the result to the global minimum of $J$.
</KeyFact>

## Initialization: restarts and k-means++

The flaw just established — the fixed point depends on the seeds — has two standard fixes, and interviewers expect both.

### Random restarts

Run Lloyd's algorithm $R$ times from independent random seeds and keep the run with the lowest final $J$. This is valid because every run minimizes the same objective on the same data: final distortions are directly comparable. (Comparisons of $J$ across different $k$ or differently scaled features are meaningless; only same-$k$, same-features runs compete.) [ISLP §12.4.1](https://www.statlearning.com) recommends 20–50 restarts as routine practice (R's `nstart`; scikit-learn's `n_init`).

### k-means++ seeding

Restarts treat bad seeds as noise to average over; *k-means++* ([Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007](https://theory.stanford.edu/~sergei/papers/kMeansPP-soda.pdf)) prevents them. Let $D(x)$ denote the distance from point $x$ to the nearest already-chosen center:

1. Choose the first center uniformly at random from the data.
2. Choose each next center from the data with probability proportional to $D(x)^2$.
3. After $k$ centers are chosen, run Lloyd's algorithm as usual.

Squaring the distances pushes new seeds hard toward regions no existing center covers, so the seeds spread across the data instead of piling into one dense blob.

<Example title="D²-sampling on the eight points">
Suppose step 1 draws $B = (2,1)$. The squared distances to $B$ are $D^2 = 1, 2, 1$ for $A, C, D$ and $18, 25, 25, 32$ for $E, F, G, H$, summing to $104$. The probability that the second seed lands in the right blob is

$$
\frac{18 + 25 + 25 + 32}{104} = \frac{100}{104} \approx 0.96,
$$

against $4/7 \approx 0.57$ for a uniform draw from the remaining points. One squaring turns a coin flip into near-certainty that the two seeds straddle the two blobs.
</Example>

The construction also carries a guarantee: in expectation the k-means++ initialization alone (before any Lloyd iteration) achieves $\mathbb{E}[J] \le 8(\ln k + 2)\, J_{\text{opt}}$, i.e. it is $O(\log k)$-competitive with the optimal clustering. In practice the two fixes are combined: k-means++ seeding plus a handful of restarts is the scikit-learn default.

<KeyFact>
Seeding is the only randomness in k-means, and it decides which local optimum the descent lands in. The interview answer has two parts: restarts (keep the lowest final $J$) and k-means++ ($D^2$-sampled seeds, $O(\log k)$-competitive in expectation).
</KeyFact>

## Choosing k: the objective is no guide

$J$ cannot select $k$. Let $J^*(k)$ be the best distortion achievable with $k$ centers. Adding a center never hurts: keep the old $k$ centers and place center $k+1$ on any data point not already a center — that point's distance drops to zero and no point's minimum distance rises, so $J^*(k+1) \le J^*(k)$, strictly in non-degenerate cases (Exercise 5 makes this an argument to reproduce). At $k = n$ every point is its own center and $J^*(n) = 0$. Minimizing $J$ over $k$ therefore always answers $k = n$, which is not a clustering.

<Figure
  src="./figures/elbow.svg"
  alt="Optimal distortion versus k from 1 to 8 on three overlapping blobs, falling 314, 88, 34, 27, 21, 16, 14, 12, with amber circles marking defensible elbows at both k equal 2 and k equal 3 and mean silhouette values 0.62, 0.63, 0.55 for k equal 2, 3, 4"
  caption="J*(k) on three overlapping blobs (80 restarts per k). The curve bends at both k = 2 and k = 3; the elbow heuristic does not decide."
/>

The *elbow method* inspects this curve and picks the $k$ after which the decrease flattens: the bend where added centers stop paying for themselves. It is a heuristic, not a method — the figure's curve, computed on data actually generated from three blobs, bends defensibly at both $k = 2$ and $k = 3$, because two of the blobs overlap. Real elbow plots are frequently this ambiguous.

Two better answers exist. The *silhouette score* compares each point's mean distance to its own cluster against its distance to the nearest other cluster, giving a number that peaks rather than decreases in $k$; it is defined and computed in <Ref lesson="hierarchical-dbscan" />. On the figure's data it scores $0.62$ at $k=2$ and $0.63$ at $k=3$: honest about the ambiguity rather than hiding it. The second answer is the one interviewers rate highest: $k$ is usually a requirement, not an estimate — the number of customer segments the campaign can act on, the number of colors in the compressed image, the number of prototypes the downstream classifier wants. When the task dictates $k$, no curve inspection is needed.

<KeyFact>
$J^*(k)$ is non-increasing in $k$ and hits $0$ at $k = n$, so minimizing distortion over $k$ is vacuous. The elbow is a heuristic reading of the $J^*(k)$ curve; silhouette gives a peaked criterion; a task-imposed $k$ beats both.
</KeyFact>

## Failure modes: k-means draws convex cells

Every failure mode of k-means follows from one geometric fact. The assignment step partitions space by nearest center: the region claimed by center $j$ is $\{x : \lVert x - \mu_j \rVert \le \lVert x - \mu_{j'} \rVert \ \forall j'\}$, a *Voronoi cell*. Each boundary, the perpendicular bisector of a pair of centers, is a hyperplane, so every cell is an intersection of half-spaces: convex, with linear boundaries. A cluster that is not convex (a ring, a crescent, two moons) cannot be one k-means cluster at any $k$, no matter the seeding or the number of restarts.

### The gallery, panel by panel

**Non-convex shapes.** On two concentric rings, the best $k=2$ distortion is achieved by cutting the data with a chord: both resulting cells are convex, the rings are not. Density-based and graph-based methods (DBSCAN, spectral clustering) recover the rings; <Ref lesson="hierarchical-dbscan" /> develops the first.

**Unequal sizes and spreads.** The *mouse data* (one large blob, two small ones) defeats k-means even though all three clusters are convex: squared distance charges the big cluster's outlying points heavily, so the ear centers drift inward and annex the head's shoulders. k-means implicitly assumes clusters of similar spherical extent; when sizes or covariances differ, the Gaussian mixture model of <Ref lesson="gmm-em" />, which learns per-cluster shapes and weights, is the standard upgrade, and this dataset reappears there as the motivating example.

**Unscaled features.** Distortion is built on Euclidean distance, so the feature with the largest numeric range owns the objective — the same metric-domination arithmetic quantified in <Ref lesson="knn-curse" /> for k-NN. In the third panel, $x_1$ spans $1000$ units and $x_2$ spans $10$; the true horizontal bands differ only in $x_2$, and k-means slices the data vertically instead. Standardizing features before clustering is mandatory, and Exercise 6 runs the numbers.

### Off the gallery: outliers and robust variants

**Outliers.** The update step is a mean, and means chase outliers: one far point drags its center off the cluster, distorting the cell boundaries for every other point. The robust variants swap the center statistic: *k-medoids* restricts centers to data points under arbitrary dissimilarities ([ESL §14.3.10](https://hastie.su.domains/ElemStatLearn/)), and *k-medians* pairs the $L_1$ distance with per-coordinate medians (Exercise 3 derives why the median is the $L_1$ analogue of the mean).

### Cost and stopping

One iteration costs $O(nkd)$: each of $n$ points against $k$ centers in $d$ dimensions, plus a linear-time mean update. With tens of iterations this is among the cheapest clusterings available, which is why k-means is the default first tool even when its assumptions are suspect. At web scale, [mini-batch k-means](https://doi.org/10.1145/1772690.1772862) (Sculley, 2010) updates centers from small random batches with a decaying step size, trading a slightly worse $J$ for orders-of-magnitude less compute.

<Remark title="k-means is not k-NN">
The name-confusion question is a standard interview opener: k-NN is a supervised prediction rule whose $k$ counts neighbors consulted per query; k-means is an unsupervised partitioning algorithm whose $k$ counts clusters. The full four-row comparison is in <Ref lesson="knn-curse" />; the one shared ingredient is a distance metric, which is why both inherit the scaling requirement.
</Remark>

<Aside>
k-means as compression: vector quantization. Bishop §9.1.1 compresses an image by clustering its pixels' RGB vectors with k-means and storing, per pixel, only the index of its center plus a codebook of the $k$ center colors. A $240 \times 180$ image holds $43{,}200$ pixels at 24 bits each, $1{,}036{,}800$ bits total; with $k = 2$ colors it needs $43{,}200 \times 1 + 2 \times 24 = 43{,}248$ bits (4.2%), with $k = 3$, $86{,}472$ bits (8.3%), and with $k = 10$, $173{,}040$ bits (16.7%). The distortion $J$ is then literally the reconstruction error of the compressed image, which is where the term originated in signal processing.
</Aside>

<Exercise>
Interview set piece: "Prove that k-means converges. Does it find the optimal clustering?" Give the full argument, then settle the second question with the dataset $x_1=(0,0)$, $x_2=(0,1)$, $x_3=(4,0)$, $x_4=(4,1)$ and $k=2$: show the horizontal split $\{x_1,x_3\}, \{x_2,x_4\}$ is a fixed point of Lloyd's algorithm and compare its distortion to the optimum.

<Solution>
**Convergence.** Both half-steps weakly decrease the same objective $J(r,\mu) = \sum_n \sum_j r_{nj}\lVert x_n - \mu_j\rVert^2$: the assignment step minimizes $J$ over $r$ with $\mu$ fixed ($J$ separates over points, and each point takes its pointwise-minimal term), and the update step minimizes $J$ over $\mu$ with $r$ fixed (setting $\nabla_{\mu_j} \sum_n r_{nj} \lVert x_n - \mu_j \rVert^2 = 2\sum_n r_{nj}(\mu_j - x_n) = 0$ gives the cluster mean, and the Hessian $2m_j I$ is positive definite). So $J$ is non-increasing and bounded below by $0$. The state after an update is determined by the partition, of which there are at most $k^N$; with a deterministic tie rule any partition change strictly decreases $J$, so no partition repeats, and the algorithm must reach a pass with no changes: termination in finitely many iterations.

**Optimality: no.** Horizontal split: centers are the row means $(2,0)$ and $(2,1)$; every point sits at squared distance $4$ from its own center, so $J = 16$. Fixed-point check for $x_1=(0,0)$: distance$^2$ to $(2,0)$ is $4$, to $(2,1)$ is $5$, so it stays; by the symmetry of the configuration all four points stay, and the means then do not move. The vertical split $\{x_1,x_2\},\{x_3,x_4\}$ has centers $(0,0.5)$ and $(4,0.5)$ and $J = 4 \times 0.25 = 1$. Lloyd's algorithm seeded with $(2,0)$ and $(2,1)$ therefore converges immediately to a fixed point whose distortion is $16$ times the optimum. Convergence is to a local optimum determined by the seeds, which is why restarts and k-means++ exist.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Show that the minimizer of $\sum_{i=1}^m \lVert x_i - \mu \rVert^2$ is the mean. Then show that in one dimension the minimizer of the $L_1$ objective $\sum_{i=1}^m |x_i - m|$ is the median, and verify both claims on the cluster $\{0, 1, 2, 3, 100\}$: compute each objective at the mean and at the median. Name the clustering variant that this produces and the situation where it is preferred.

<Solution>
**Mean.** $\nabla_\mu \sum_i \lVert x_i - \mu \rVert^2 = 2(m\mu - \sum_i x_i) = 0$ gives $\mu = \bar{x}$; the Hessian $2mI$ is positive definite, so the mean is the unique minimizer.

**Median.** $g(m) = \sum_i |x_i - m|$ is piecewise linear with derivative (wherever defined) $\#\{i : x_i < m\} - \#\{i : x_i > m\}$. The derivative is negative while more points lie above $m$ than below and positive in the reverse case, so $g$ is minimized where the counts balance: any median (for odd $m$, the middle order statistic).

**Numbers.** Mean of $\{0,1,2,3,100\}$ is $21.2$; median is $2$. Squared objective: at the mean, $21.2^2 + 20.2^2 + 19.2^2 + 18.2^2 + 78.8^2 = 449.44 + 408.04 + 368.64 + 331.24 + 6209.44 = 7766.8$ (the minimum); at the median, $4+1+0+1+9604 = 9610$. $L_1$ objective: at the median, $2+1+0+1+98 = 102$ (the minimum); at the mean, $21.2+20.2+19.2+18.2+78.8 = 157.6$. The single outlier $100$ drags the mean $19$ units off the bulk of the cluster but moves the median not at all.

Pairing the $L_1$ distance with per-coordinate medians gives **k-medians**, preferred when outliers would otherwise capture the centers. The split is the same one <Ref lesson="ols" /> established for regression losses: squared error is minimized by means and is outlier-sensitive, absolute error by medians and is robust.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Six points on a line: $\{0, 1, 2, 10, 11, 12\}$, $k = 2$. The first k-means++ seed is drawn uniformly and lands on $x = 1$. Compute the $D^2$ sampling distribution for the second seed and the probability that it falls in $\{10, 11, 12\}$. Compare with the probability under uniform sampling from the remaining five points, and state the expected-distortion guarantee that k-means++ carries.

<Solution>
Distances squared to the chosen center $x=1$: $D^2(0) = 1$, $D^2(2) = 1$, $D^2(10) = 81$, $D^2(11) = 100$, $D^2(12) = 121$ (and $D^2(1) = 0$, so the chosen point cannot be redrawn). The normalizer is $1+1+81+100+121 = 304$, so the sampling distribution is $\bigl(\tfrac{1}{304}, \tfrac{1}{304}, \tfrac{81}{304}, \tfrac{100}{304}, \tfrac{121}{304}\bigr)$ on $(0, 2, 10, 11, 12)$, and

$$
P(\text{second seed} \in \{10,11,12\}) = \frac{81+100+121}{304} = \frac{302}{304} \approx 0.993,
$$

against $3/5 = 0.6$ under a uniform draw. With one seed per blob, Lloyd's algorithm converges directly to the two-blob optimum. The guarantee (Arthur & Vassilvitskii, 2007): the k-means++ initialization alone satisfies $\mathbb{E}[J] \le 8(\ln k + 2)\,J_{\text{opt}}$ — $O(\log k)$-competitive before a single Lloyd iteration runs.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Interview question: "Why not choose $k$ by minimizing the k-means objective over $k$?" Prove that $J^*(k)$, the optimal distortion with $k$ centers, is non-increasing in $k$; compute $J^*(n)$; and state what is used instead, with the weakness of each option.

<Solution>
**Monotonicity.** Take an optimal solution with $k$ centers, achieving $J^*(k)$. Add a $(k{+}1)$-th center on any data point $x_m$ that is not already a center. Every point's distance to its nearest center either stays the same or falls (there is one more candidate center), and $x_m$'s own contribution falls to $0$. This feasible $(k{+}1)$-center solution has distortion $\le J^*(k)$, so the optimum satisfies $J^*(k{+}1) \le J^*(k)$. With $k = n$, one center per point gives every point distance zero: $J^*(n) = 0$. Hence $\arg\min_k J^*(k) = n$ always — the criterion degenerates into "every point is its own cluster."

**What is used instead.** (1) The elbow heuristic: pick the $k$ where the $J^*(k)$ curve's decrease flattens; weakness: real curves frequently show multiple or no clean bends, and reading one is subjective. (2) The silhouette score: peaks at a preferred $k$ rather than decreasing; weakness: it presumes compact, separated clusters, and computing it is $O(n^2)$ in distances. (3) Task-driven choice: the application fixes $k$ (segments, colors, prototypes); this is the strongest answer when available, because it replaces an unidentifiable statistical question with a requirement.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Spaced review. A customer dataset has features (age in years, income in dollars). A k-means run has current centers $\mu_1 = (30,\ 60000)$ and $\mu_2 = (55,\ 60500)$. (a) Assign the customer $x = (31,\ 61000)$ using raw Euclidean distance. (b) Reassign after dividing each feature by its standard deviation, $\mathrm{sd}_{\text{age}} = 10$ years and $\mathrm{sd}_{\text{income}} = 30000$ dollars. (c) Explain the reversal in one sentence, using the argument this course first made for k-NN.

<Solution>
**(a) Raw.** $d^2(x, \mu_1) = (31-30)^2 + (61000-60000)^2 = 1 + 10^6 = 1{,}000{,}001$; $d^2(x, \mu_2) = (31-55)^2 + (61000-60500)^2 = 576 + 250{,}000 = 250{,}576$. The point is assigned to $\mu_2$, the cluster of 55-year-olds, despite being one year of age from $\mu_1$.

**(b) Standardized.** Distances only involve coordinate differences, so dividing by the standard deviations suffices: age differences become $0.1$ and $2.4$; income differences become $1000/30000 = 0.033$ and $500/30000 = 0.017$. Then $d^2(x,\mu_1) = 0.01 + 0.0011 = 0.011$ and $d^2(x,\mu_2) = 5.76 + 0.0003 = 5.76$: the point is assigned to $\mu_1$.

**(c)** Euclidean distance sums squared coordinate differences with equal weight, so the feature with the largest numeric range (income, range $\sim$thousands) silently owns the metric and the age feature never enters the comparison — the metric-domination argument of <Ref lesson="knn-curse" />, which applies verbatim to k-means because the assignment step is a nearest-center rule under the same metric. Standardize before clustering.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Interview question: "Name three datasets on which k-means fails, explain each failure in terms of the algorithm's geometry, and say what you would use instead."

<Solution>
Any three of the following, with the geometric reason stated:

1. **Concentric rings / two moons.** k-means cells are Voronoi cells — convex, with linear (hyperplane) boundaries — so a non-convex cluster cannot be one k-means cluster at any $k$. Use DBSCAN (density-based, arbitrary shapes) or spectral clustering (graph connectivity).
2. **Mouse data: one large blob, two small ones.** All clusters are convex, but squared distance charges the big cluster's periphery heavily, so the small clusters' centers drift inward and annex it; k-means implicitly assumes similar spherical spreads. Use a Gaussian mixture fitted by EM, which learns per-component covariances and weights.
3. **Unscaled features of very different ranges.** The largest-range feature owns the Euclidean metric, so the partition ignores the informative feature. Standardize first (or use a learned metric); this is a preprocessing failure, not a model choice.
4. **Data with heavy outliers.** The update step is a mean, and one far point drags its center off the cluster. Use k-medoids or k-medians, whose center statistics are robust.
5. **Categorical data.** Euclidean distance and means are undefined on categories; one-hot embedding distorts distances. Use k-modes (matching dissimilarity, mode centers) or model-based clustering.

The unifying answer interviewers listen for: k-means is least-squares clustering — Euclidean metric, mean centers, convex Voronoi cells — and each failure is one of those three commitments meeting data that violates it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

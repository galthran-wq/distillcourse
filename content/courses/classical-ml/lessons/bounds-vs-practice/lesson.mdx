The margin theory makes measurable claims: run AdaBoost for a thousand rounds, plot the margin distribution at rounds 5, 100, and 1000, and the curves must march right while test error falls — this lesson runs the experiment and prices every bound in the module.

The module's ladder is complete on paper: finite-class counting, VC dimension, <Ref lesson="rademacher-stability">Rademacher complexity and stability</Ref>, and <Ref lesson="margin-bounds">the margin bounds that paid the course's two debts</Ref>. What stands open is whether the explanations survive contact with data at the course's own scale, and the field settled how to ask: the experiment in [Schapire, Freund, Bartlett, and Lee (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1024691352), the paper whose theorems the previous lesson proved. This lesson reruns that experiment with the course's own algorithms on the course's own corpus, evaluates each of the module's bounds to an actual number on the fitted model, and closes the module with an honest ranking of what the theory delivers.

The artifact is Figure 1 of the 1998 paper, the figure that turned "AdaBoost keeps improving after zero training error" from an anomaly into a theory. The experiment ran C4.5, Quinlan's standard decision-tree learner of the 1990s, on the *letter* benchmark: 16,000 training examples of handwritten capital letters rendered from 20 fonts, 26 classes, 4,000 held out. A single C4.5 tree scores 13.8% test error. Boosted for 1,000 rounds, the combined vote reaches training error zero **at round 5** and its test error then falls from 8.4% to 3.1% over the remaining 995 rounds. The bottom row is the paper's explanation: the cumulative distribution of training margins at rounds 5, 100, and 1000. At round 5 about 8% of training examples have margin below $0.5$; by round 100 every one is above it. The curves march right while the error falls, and the bagging column beside them is the control: 1,000 bagged trees reach 6.6% test error and their margin distribution barely moves.

### The replication contract: shapes, not values

The reproduction discipline is the module's standard, stated before running anything. Our base learners are not C4.5 and our corpus is not letter, so the **values will not replicate** and are not supposed to; the claims under test are the shapes. Four of them: training error exhausted early; test error not degrading over hundreds of rounds after; the low end of the margin distribution moving right while both happen; and no comparable motion under bagging.

## The protocol: two lanes, every dial stated

The contract above names the four shapes under test; the run that tests them is fixed next, dial by dial, so that the check can be repeated. Corpus: the 4,601-message spam corpus on <Ref lesson="spam-filter">ESL's fixed 3,065/1,536 split</Ref>, the course's benchmark since module 4, carrying the floor <Ref lesson="spam-ensembles">module 7 measured</Ref> (one pair of identical feature vectors with opposite labels in the training split, so no function of the features trains below $1/3{,}065$). Algorithm: AdaBoost exactly as <Ref lesson="adaboost">module 7 defined it</Ref>, $T = 1{,}000$ rounds, nothing tuned, error curves and margins read off the staged sums $F_t = \sum_{s\le t}\alpha_s h_s$, the same machinery <Ref lesson="boosting-lab">the boosting lab</Ref> built: one pass, no refitting per $T$. Margins: the normalized margin $y_i F_T(x_i)/\sum_{t \le T}\alpha_t \in [-1,1]$, <Ref lesson="margin-bounds">the margin lesson's normalization</Ref> exactly (the paper's definition is the multiclass generalization; on two classes they coincide).

### Two lanes: depth-4 trees and stumps

The paper ran its experiment twice, with C4.5 and with decision stumps, and the reproduction mirrors that: two lanes. The **depth-4 lane** boosts depth-4 weighted-gini trees, the stand-in for C4.5 and the learner behind <Ref lesson="adaboost">the AdaBoost lesson's mystery figure</Ref>. The **stump lane** boosts depth-1 stumps, the course's canonical weak learner and the one whose capacity every bound in this module has already priced. Trees are our own implementation; a different grower's tie-breaking moves these numbers by tenths of a point (the ensembles lesson's depth-4 run of the same recipe finished a few tenths lower), which is part of why shapes, not values, are the claims. All certificates below use $\delta = 0.05$.

### The error row: exhausted at round 82, flat to 1,000

The error row replicates the paradox. Training error reaches the corpus floor at **round 82** and never moves again; the 918 rounds that follow multiply the ensemble's parameter count twelvefold, and the test error stays inside a 4.1–4.9% band. No degradation reproduces everywhere; continued improvement is a property letter has and this corpus, at this base learner, does not.

### The margin row: two motions, one of them in the theory

The margin row splits into two motions, and only one was in the original's headline. The low tail marches right, exactly as in 1998: the minimum margin climbs $-0.78 \to -0.006 \to -0.001$, and the fraction below $0.05$ falls sixty-fold. (The stubborn $-0.001$ is the contradictory pair: its two rows share one score, so one of the two margins is negative at every $T$; this is the corpus floor, visible in margin currency.) The bulk, meanwhile, drifts *left*: the median margin falls $0.72 \to 0.27 \to 0.18$. From the letter figure one would expect the whole distribution to march; ours moves only its tail, and the paper itself documents the mechanism: boosting is "willing to suffer significant reductions in the margins of those examples that already have large margins" to lift the small ones, the same median-for-minimum trade <Ref lesson="adaboost">the AdaBoost lesson's forward exercise</Ref> measured on the $\chi^2$ problem. The theory is indifferent to the difference: every bound in the module reads the CDF at small $\rho$, and the tail is what the push theorem moves.

### The stump lane: the paper's second experiment

The stump lane is the paper's other experiment, reproduced. Training error never exhausts (3.9% at $T = 1{,}000$ and still creeping), the test error falls for the full thousand rounds, and the margin distribution *compresses*: the median slides from $0.46$ to $0.09$ as late rounds arrive with errors $\varepsilon_t \approx 0.49$ and vanishing vote weights. SFBL's own stump runs show precisely this: stumps are too weak for large margins, consistent with the push theorem's form, and generalization survives because the base class is so small.

<KeyFact>
What it would take for letter's whole-distribution march to reproduce, the push theorem states itself: its per-round factor $2\sqrt{\varepsilon_t^{1-\rho}(1-\varepsilon_t)^{1+\rho}}$ shrinks at every level $\rho < 1$ only if the weighted errors $\varepsilon_t$ stay small round after reweighted round — a base learner strong enough to keep winning as the distribution hardens, which C4.5's full trees were and neither of our lanes is. And this corpus refuses outright: the contradictory pair keeps one training margin negative at every $T$, so no base class marches this CDF wholly past zero. Letter's march is a property of a strong base learner on a clean corpus, not a law of boosting.
</KeyFact>

## Three certificates, one machine: the bounds priced

The reproduction settled what the ensembles do; whether the module's ladder explains it is settled next, by pricing. Each of the module's bounds is now priced to a risk ceiling on the stump-lane ensemble, the lane whose capacity inputs are already on the module's books, at $T = 5$, $100$, and $1{,}000$, each certificate set against the measured test error. Three routes: the <Ref lesson="learning-theory">finite-class bound</Ref> on a discretized vote; the <Ref lesson="vc-dimension">VC bound</Ref> through the ensemble's dimension; the <Ref lesson="margin-bounds">ensemble margin bound</Ref>, whose capacity term is the stump class's Rademacher complexity priced by Massart and Sauer. (The module's fourth route, <Ref lesson="rademacher-stability">uniform stability</Ref>, declines the case honestly: its theorem prices algorithms with a regularization knob $\lambda$, and AdaBoost has none.)

<Example title="pricing all three at T = 100">
**Finite class.** Discretize each stump to 8-bit thresholds: $|\mathcal{H}_{\text{stump}}| = 57 \times 256 \times 2$, $\ln|\mathcal{H}_{\text{stump}}| = 10.3$. A vote of 100 stumps with 8-bit weights has $\ln|\mathcal{H}| = 100 \times (10.3 + 8\ln 2) = 1{,}583$, so the uniform tolerance is $\varepsilon = \sqrt{(1583 + \ln 40)/(2 \cdot 3065)} = 0.51$. Certificate: risk $\le 0.060 + 0.51 = 0.57$.

**VC.** By <Ref lesson="vc-dimension">SSBD's ensemble lemma</Ref>, $d(100) = 100 \cdot 11 \cdot (3\log_2 1100 + 2) \approx 3.6 \times 10^4$, more than eleven times $n$: the growth function at $n = 3{,}065$ is $2^n$, so Massart's lemma prices the capacity term at exactly $\sqrt{2\ln 2} \approx 1.18$ — the term's ceiling, and its value whenever $d \ge n$, however large $d$ grows. Certificate: risk $\le 0.060 + 1.18 + 0.022 = 1.26$, the last term the $\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/2n}$ surcharge at the declared $\delta = 0.05$. Nothing — and with the capacity term pinned at $1.18$, nothing on this route can come in below training error plus $1.20$ at any $T$.

**Margin.** Capacity $\sqrt{2 \cdot 10 \cdot \ln(e \cdot 3065/10)/3065} = 0.209$ for the stump class ($d_B \le 10$), so the bound reads $\widehat{\mathrm{CDF}}_{100}(\rho) + (2/\rho)(0.209) + 0.022$. The measured CDF gives $0.74$ at $\rho = 0.3$, total $2.16$; optimizing $\rho$ (legitimate at a $\sqrt{\log\log}$ surcharge below $0.02$ here) reaches $1.45$ and no lower. Certificate: risk $\le 1.45$. Also nothing, but for a measurable reason: our stump margins are small.
</Example>

### Reading the table: three different ways to fail

The table is the module's verdict, and it must be read for discrimination, not despair. No bound certifies the deployed model: the sole sub-1 entry covers the toy-sized $T = 5$ vote, and the best ceiling at $T = 1{,}000$ — the VC column's $1.24$ — overshoots the measured $0.058$ by a factor of 21. The paper's own §2.2 said this in 1998: evaluated at $|\mathcal{H}| = 10^6$, its bounds "start to be meaningful only when the size of the training set is in the tens of thousands," and its Figure 3 plots them above $1$ at $n = 10{,}000$ for every level $\theta \le 1/8$. But the three columns fail differently, and the differences are the explanations' fingerprints. The finite-class column grows like $\sqrt{T}$: it prices the *description* of the vote, so every round costs capacity. That is the wrong currency, per the mystery. The VC column is dead at every $T$: already at $T = 5$, where $d(5) \approx 1.1 \times 10^3$ still sits below $n$, Sauer's exponent $d\ln(en/d) \approx 2{,}190$ exceeds $n \ln 2 \approx 2{,}124$, so the capacity term is pinned at its $2^n$ ceiling of $1.18$ from the start; from round 13, $d(T)$ outruns $n$ outright. The margin column alone is $T$-stable and moves only through a measurable property of the fit; priced on <Ref lesson="margin-bounds">the ring machine, where the fitted margins are large</Ref>, this same route produced the module's one working certificate, $0.35$ against a measured $0.00$. Vacuity here is a statement about our margins and our $n$, not about the route.

<KeyFact>
The table prices certificates, not explanations: a ceiling above 1 falsifies the promise of a guarantee at this $n$, not the margin account itself. Explanations are tested by prediction, and the margin account makes a sharp one: remove the persistent edge and the no-degradation regime should collapse. The noise experiment below runs exactly that test.
</KeyFact>

## The noise flip: the theory predicts its own failure

Nine hundred flat rounds past exhaustion invite a general rule — train AdaBoost as long as you like — and the margin account refuses the generalization, because its load-bearing hypothesis is the persistent edge: <Ref lesson="margin-bounds">the push theorem</Ref> moves the tail only while $\varepsilon_t \le \frac12 - \gamma$, and <Ref lesson="adaboost">the exponential loss</Ref> chases mislabeled points, so flipped labels should kill the edges, stall the march, and convert late rounds from margin-building to noise-memorizing, restoring the classical upturn the clean run never showed. The check: flip 321 of the 3,065 training labels (10.5%, seed fixed), rerun the depth-4 lane, score against the untouched test set.

### The upturn on schedule

Every predicted symptom appears, with numbers. The test error bottoms at 7.5% around round 235 and then climbs about two points over the following several hundred rounds, while the clean lane sits flat; stopping early is suddenly worth something, exactly as the U-curve says once its hypothesis holds. The edges die on schedule: by the late rounds $\varepsilon_t \approx 0.495$, an edge of half a point, against double digits in the early rounds.

### Where the capacity went

At round 5 the flipped points sit where truth put them, at median margin $-0.40$: the early ensemble votes against the corrupted labels. A thousand rounds later 88% of them have *positive* margin (the booster has memorized the noise) and the clean points' median has been taxed from $0.61$ down to $0.07$ to fund it. Read against the bound, both lanes now tell one story: on clean data the loss term at small $\rho$ falls with $T$ and rounds are free; on noisy data the tail is "improved" only by fitting the flipped labels, the clean mass slides left, and the certificate degrades like the test error. The theory did not merely survive this check; it specified it. The published record agrees: Dietterich's noise experiments and the Breiman comparisons <Ref lesson="adaboost">quoted in module 7</Ref> found the same inversion at 5% noise.

## Bagging beside boosting: margins do not travel between classes

The 1998 figure's control column completes the reproduction, and it contains a trap the module's theorems already name. Bagging 300 full-grown trees on the same split (the <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests">variance tool</Ref>, resampling and averaging, nothing reweighted) finishes at 5.2% test error, and its training-margin distribution does what the paper's did: nothing. The median vote margin is $1.0$ at $B = 5$ and $0.99$ at $B = 300$; four fifths of the training points sit above $0.9$ at every committee size. Averaging converges to its expectation, which for deep trees that memorize their bootstraps is near-unanimity on the training set, and no mechanism ever pushes it anywhere. Boosting's distribution, beside it, is still being reshaped at round 1,000. That is the mechanism contrast in one picture: a variance tool settles; a margin tool optimizes.

### The trap: margins do not compare across base classes

The trap is in the comparison: bagging's margin CDF *dominates* boosting's over nearly the whole axis, yet bagging generalizes worse (5.2% against 4.7%). If margins alone certified, the plot would rank the two backwards. They do not, and the module's own theorem says why: the ensemble bound charges $\widehat{R}_{S,\rho}$ *plus* the base class's capacity, and bagging's base class is full-grown trees. Hundreds of leaves are a capacity a depth-4 tree never approaches, so bagging's large margins are priced against a class that devalues them. SFBL flag exactly this ("one must also always consider the complexity of the base classifiers"), and <Ref lesson="margin-bounds">the delta-function trap</Ref> is the limiting case: a base class rich enough makes any margins, on any labels, worth nothing. A margin CDF is evidence only at fixed base class; across classes it is not even comparable.

## What an engineer keeps

The module closes by ranking its own products, cashed against everything measured above.

### The ranking: measurement, explanation, certificate

**Measurement first.** At practical sample sizes, held-out error and <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap">the resampling machinery of module 5</Ref> remain the only instruments that say what a model's risk *is*. Nothing in this module unthroned them, and the pricing table shows the gap: a factor of 25 between the margin route's ceiling and the measured error.

**Explanation second**, and it does real work. The margin-plus-capacity ledger predicted which lane would tolerate a thousand rounds and which would turn up at 235, and identified the exact hypothesis (the edge) whose death flips the regimes. It is also cheap to monitor: one pass over the staged sums the lab already computes buys the whole tail CDF. Two live gauges warn of trouble: $\varepsilon_t$ creeping to $\tfrac12$ (edges dying), and formerly very-negative margins climbing toward zero (labels being memorized).

**Certificates last.** Real ones exist where margins are large relative to $\sqrt{n}$ (the ring machine's $0.35$) and do not exist for this corpus at this $n$. A promised ceiling above 1 is a fact about the promise, not the model.

### What stands established

The 1998 experiment replicates on the course's own tools in the claims the theorems need (early exhaustion without degradation, the tail's march, bagging's immobility) and departs where letter was special. The module's bounds, priced on one fitted machine, separate cleanly: a counting bound that degrades with $T$, a counting bound dead on arrival, and a $T$-stable margin certificate, loose here and meaningful only where large margins and large $n$ coincide. And the theory's noise prediction was delivered by experiment, upturn and all. The module's question of why any of it works has its honest answer: constraints and margins explain generalization and predict its failure; they do not yet price it at the scale engineers work. <Ref lesson="bayesian-linear-regression">The next module</Ref> changes what a guarantee is: instead of bounding one number, keep a distribution over predictions and let the model price its own uncertainty. Where even margins fall silent, in models that interpolate, <Ref lesson="double-descent">the course's final act</Ref> begins.

<Exercise>
Mixed review with <Ref lesson="learning-theory">module 9's opening lesson</Ref>, as a derivation the body skipped: the table's finite-class column was computed at three values of $T$, and the claims that it "grows like $\sqrt{T}$" and crossed 1 near $T \approx 350$ were asserted, not derived. (a) Write the finite-class certificate for the 8-bit vote as a function of $T$ ($\ln|\mathcal{H}_{\text{stump}}| = 10.28$, 8-bit weights, $\delta = 0.05$, $n = 3{,}065$), show that its tolerance grows like $\sqrt{T}$ from a few rounds on, and derive the crossing round $T^{*}$ at which the ceiling passes 1, taking training error $\approx 5\%$ near the crossing. (b) The formula's $T$-free ingredient, the bare 8-bit stump class, has tolerance $\varepsilon = 0.048$ — the only comfortably sub-1 number anywhere in this lesson. State why quoting it for the deployed 1,000-stump vote repeats the 1,000-coins error, and what happens to it at 16-bit thresholds.

<Solution>
(a) One stump contributes $\ln|\mathcal{H}_{\text{stump}}| = 10.28$ and its 8-bit weight another $8\ln 2 = 5.55$, so $\ln|\mathcal{H}_T| = 15.83\,T$ and

$$\varepsilon(T) = \sqrt{\frac{15.83\,T + \ln 40}{2 \cdot 3{,}065}} \approx 0.051\sqrt{T},$$

the $\ln 40 = 3.7$ buried by $15.83\,T$ after a couple of rounds. Check against the table: $0.051\sqrt{100} = 0.51$, $0.051\sqrt{1000} = 1.61$. The ceiling passes 1 when $\varepsilon(T^{*}) = 1 - 0.05 = 0.95$, i.e. $T^{*} = (0.95^2 \cdot 6{,}130 - \ln 40)/15.83 \approx 349$ — the "crossed 1 near $T \approx 350$" of the pricing exercise, derived. The growth is structural, not a defect of the encoding: this route prices the *description* of the vote, each round appends one stump and one weight, so $\ln|\mathcal{H}|$ is linear in $T$ under any fixed per-round format — a cleverer format moves the constant $0.051$, never the $\sqrt{T}$.

(b) $\varepsilon = \sqrt{(10.28 + \ln 40)/(2 \cdot 3065)} = 0.048$: every discretized stump's training error is within **4.8 points** of its risk, a genuinely meaningful, uniform, distribution-free certificate. But it covers the wrong object: the deployed model is a weighted vote of 1,000 stumps, and a certificate is a statement about a class — quoting a member class's certificate for a composite model repeats the 1,000-coins error, and part (a) is the price of honesty: a factor of $\sqrt{T}$. At 16 bits, $|\mathcal{H}| = 57 \times 65{,}536 \times 2$ gives $\ln|\mathcal{H}| = 15.8$ and $\varepsilon = 0.056$: the certificate loosens because the *storage format* changed, the discretization trick's known dishonesty from the opening lesson. The class that matters is what the fitting procedure can reach, not what a float can spell.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Forward computation. The margin bound's capacity term for the stump class is $(2/\rho)\sqrt{2 \cdot 10 \ln(e n/10)/n}$. (a) Evaluate it at $\rho = 0.5$ for $n = 300$, $n = 3{,}065$, and $n = 30{,}650$. (b) The letter run achieved margin $\ge 0.5$ on *every* training point by round 100, at $n = 16{,}000$; evaluate the full bound (zero loss term, $\delta = 0.05$) for a stump-capacity class in that regime. (c) Now the wall: module 11 studies models that interpolate, meaning zero training error on data whose labels are partly noise. State what the noise experiment above implies about their margins, and what kind of guarantee could survive there.

<Solution>
(a) $4\sqrt{20\ln(e n/10)/n}$: $n = 300 \Rightarrow 2.17$; $n = 3{,}065 \Rightarrow 0.84$; $n = 30{,}650 \Rightarrow 0.31$. The term crosses from absurd to potentially meaningful over two decades of $n$: the bound enters its operating range as $\sqrt{n}$ grows, precisely SFBL's "tens of thousands."

(b) $0 + 4\sqrt{20\ln(e \cdot 16000/10)/16000} + \sqrt{\ln 20/32000} \approx 0 + 0.41 + 0.01 \approx 0.42$: with letter's margins at letter's $n$, the certificate is real, loose against the measured 3.1% but a genuine sub-half guarantee. The theory's meaningful range is where large margins and large $n$ coincide.

(c) On noisy labels the experiment showed margins at noise points go positive only by memorization, taxing clean margins to near zero. An interpolating model on noisy data cannot have large margins on the noise, so every bound in this module goes vacuous on it, and the classical prediction is the upturn we produced on demand. Yet module 11's models interpolate noise and generalize anyway; margins cannot save them, and something else must. Two roads leave this point: guarantees that abandon risk ceilings for calibrated *distributions* over predictions (<Ref lesson="bayesian-linear-regression">the next module's posterior</Ref> and its conformal endgame), and the direct assault on interpolation in <Ref lesson="double-descent">the epilogue's opening lesson</Ref>, where the U-curve itself is the casualty.

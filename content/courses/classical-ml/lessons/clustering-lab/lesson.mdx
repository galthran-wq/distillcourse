Two algorithms, one convergence argument: you build k-means and EM side by
side, compress a real photograph with your own codebook, and watch the
log-likelihood your code prints increase at every EM step because the module
proved it must.

Both algorithms were earned earlier in this module: Lloyd's alternation, its
block-descent convergence argument and the vector-quantization arithmetic in
<Ref lesson="kmeans" />, the responsibilities, the M-step formulas and the
ELBO monotonicity proof in <Ref lesson="gmm-em" />. What the lab contributes
is those two proofs as executable assertions — a distortion history your
notebook checks is non-increasing, a log-likelihood staircase it checks
climbs at every one of 50 iterations on real data — plus the numerical craft
the derivations skip: log-sum-exp responsibilities that survive a point 500
standard deviations out (the same pattern the logistic-regression lab's
`logaddexp` loss used), a covariance floor that closes off the mixture
likelihood's singular paths, and an empty-cluster repair Lloyd's formula
silently assumes away.

The lab runs as a Jupyter notebook — open it in Colab with the button above,
or in any Jupyter you like. Pair the notebook with the code from this page;
each checkpoint submits your function's outputs for verification and the
checklist above tracks what stands. Seven of the ten checkpoints are
required; k-means++ seeding, the open center-recovery task and the written
answer are optional — partial completion is a normal way to finish a lab.

Expect about four hours of real work for the required set; the optional
seeding and center-recovery tasks add roughly another hour. The two deepest
required checkpoints are the
E-step, whose responsibilities must be computed entirely in log space (the
checkpoint inputs contain an outlier that turns any exponentiate-first
implementation into NaN), and the full EM fitter, verified value-for-value
over 50 iterations from a deliberately crossed initialization. The
compression section runs on a real photograph (astronaut Eileen Collins,
NASA, public domain) with the bit ledger computed honestly, and the EM
section fits the canonical two-regime dataset: 272 eruptions of the Old
Faithful geyser (Azzalini & Bowman 1990, the version distributed with R). If
a check goes red, the verdict often names the exact bug — read it before
rereading your code.

This lab closes the unsupervised module. Established now, as your own
verified code: the module's two engines — restarts selected by distortion,
responsibilities as soft cluster evidence — the same machinery
<Ref lesson="tumor-microarray" /> drove against real expression data. Every
verdict the module reached, there and here, was an empirical estimate from
one finite sample: a distortion, a log-likelihood, a bootstrap stability
rate. <Ref lesson="learning-theory" /> opens module 9 with the question that
observation leaves standing — when a quantity computed on a finite sample
can be trusted about data never seen — and answers it with concentration
inequalities and capacity measures, the course's turn from methods to
guarantees.

A decision tree fits by asking one greedy question at a time — which split most reduces impurity — and controls overfitting by pruning the result.

This is the second half of the module's move: giving up the global formula. <Ref lesson="kernel-smoothing">The smoothing lesson</Ref> dropped the formula but kept a fixed notion of "near": a window of one shape, which its closing exercise showed going empty or global as dimension grows, and which has no meaning at all for a categorical feature. This lesson lets the data place the neighborhood boundaries instead. Recursive axis-aligned splits build a partition that is coarse where the response is flat and fine where it varies; a greedy impurity criterion chooses each split; cost-complexity pruning cuts the grown tree back to the size the evidence supports. The method's costs are then stated with evidence: an axis-aligned bias, and an instability that <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests">module 7</Ref> exists to average away.

The route: the partition and the tree that encodes it, one split priced fully by hand, the impurity criterion and why it must be concave, the same step under a variance impurity for regression, weakest-link pruning, and the buy/cost accounting.

## From fixed windows to fitted partitions

<Ref lesson="kernel-smoothing">The previous lesson's</Ref> closing exercise left a fixed-shape window in $p = 8$ dimensions choosing between holding no points and holding most of the cube; the repair is to stop fixing the neighborhood's shape before seeing the data. The smallest predictor built this way uses one feature and one boundary: split the line at $x < 4.5$, average the training responses on each side, and predict that average everywhere on its side — say $2$ to the left of the boundary and $8$ to the right. Two regions, two fitted constants, and the boundary at $4.5$ is itself an output of the fit. With more boundaries and more features the regions become $M$ disjoint rectangles $R_1, \dots, R_M$, each carrying its own constant:

$$
f(x) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} c_m \,\mathbf{1}\{x \in R_m\}.
$$

The rectangles take over the role of the kernel's window, with sizes and shapes chosen by the fit rather than by a bandwidth: a region can span the full range of a coordinate the response ignores and a sliver of one it depends on. Read this way a tree is an **adaptive nearest-neighbor method**: each leaf is a neighborhood, like <Ref lesson="knn-curse">$k$-NN's</Ref> ball of $k$ points, except that the data decide where the neighborhood ends, coordinate by coordinate.

### Recursive binary splits: the partitions a tree can write down

Arbitrary partitions are off the table for two reasons: there are too many to search, and a region assembled from unions of boxes cannot be written down compactly. CART (classification and regression trees), the methodology this lesson teaches, restricts itself to partitions reachable by *recursive binary splitting*: split the whole space on one coordinate at one threshold, then split one of the two halves the same way, and so on. Every region produced this way is a rectangle, and the whole partition is encoded by a binary tree whose internal nodes hold questions of the form $X_j < s$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/triptych.svg"
  alt="Three panels: a recursive binary partition of a two-feature space into five rectangles, the binary tree of four questions that encodes it, and the piecewise-constant prediction surface over the partition"
  caption="One model, three pictures: the recursive partition, the tree of questions that encodes it, and the piecewise-constant surface the pair defines."
/>

<Definition title="decision tree (CART)">
A **decision tree** is the predictor $f(x) = \sum_m c_m \mathbf{1}\{x \in R_m\}$ whose regions are generated by recursive binary splits $X_j < s$ and whose *leaf constants* $c_m$ are fitted per region: the mean of the training responses in $R_m$ for regression, the majority class for classification.
</Definition>

The two leaf rules are not conventions; each is the minimizer of the training loss inside its region — the mean minimizes the residual sum of squares, the majority class minimizes the count of errors. Both derivations are two lines and the lesson uses them silently from here on; the leaf-rule exercise at the end has you produce them.

Two design choices deserve their one sentence each. Splits are binary rather than multiway because a multiway split fragments the data in one step, starving every node below it, and any multiway split is reachable by a chain of binary ones. Splits are single-coordinate rather than oblique ($\sum_j a_j X_j < s$) because the coordinate question is searchable, readable, and invariant to how each feature is scaled; the price of that choice is the bias examined at the end of the lesson.

## The greedy step: every candidate split, priced

Encoding is settled; the open question is which split to make. Finding the binary partition that minimizes training loss over all trees is not an option: the problem is NP-complete ([Hyafil & Rivest, 1976](https://doi.org/10.1016/0020-0190(76)90095-8)), so no algorithm is known that beats brute force in general. CART is greedy instead: at each node it makes the single best split available and never reconsiders. The grown tree is therefore not "the best tree", only the best reachable by locally optimal moves.

### The price of a split: impurity decrease

A greedy step needs a price. For a classification node $m$ holding $N_m$ training points, write $\hat p_{mk}$ for the fraction of its points in class $k$. The price this section uses is the **Gini impurity**

$$
G_m = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \hat p_{mk}(1 - \hat p_{mk}),
$$

which is $0$ exactly when the node is pure (one class only) and largest when the classes are evenly mixed; for two classes it reduces to $2p(1-p)$ with $p$ the class-1 fraction. A candidate split sends each point left or right; its value is the **impurity decrease**

$$
\Delta = G_{\text{parent}} \;-\; \frac{N_L}{N}\, G_L \;-\; \frac{N_R}{N}\, G_R ,
$$

the parent's impurity minus the child impurities weighted by the fraction of points each child receives. The candidates themselves are finite: for a numeric feature, only the order of the points matters, so it suffices to test one threshold between each pair of adjacent observed values — at most $N-1$ per feature.

## The growing criterion must be concave

The sweep above priced splits with Gini impurity, and the choice was not free. Three impurity measures are standard for a classification node; for two classes, with $p$ the class-1 fraction:

$$
\underbrace{\min(p,\, 1-p)}_{\text{misclassification error}}
\qquad
\underbrace{2p(1-p)}_{\text{Gini}}
\qquad
\underbrace{-p \log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)}_{\text{entropy}} .
$$

Misclassification error is the training error rate of the node's majority vote, the same quantity <Ref lesson="classification-metrics">the metrics lesson</Ref> put in the top-left of the confusion matrix. *Entropy* needs one sentence of definition: it is the average number of bits required to name the class of a point drawn from the node, zero for a pure node and maximal ($\log_2 K$) for an even mixture; the impurity decrease under entropy is called **information gain**. All three measures vanish exactly on pure nodes and peak at even mixtures, and the natural expectation is that the choice among them barely matters.

  src="./figures/impurity-curves.svg"
  alt="Three impurity curves against the class-1 fraction p: the misclassification tent min(p, 1-p), the Gini parabola 2p(1-p), and the entropy curve scaled by one half, all zero at the endpoints and maximal at one half"
  caption="The three impurity measures for a two-class node (entropy scaled by ½ for comparison). The tent is piecewise linear; the other two are strictly concave."
/>

### Misclassification error cannot grow a tree

For growing the tree, the expectation is wrong: misclassification error fails, and the failure is structural, not numerical.

<Example title="two splits misclassification cannot tell apart (Bishop, Ex. 14.11)">
A node holds 400 points of each class. Split A produces children $(300, 100)$ and $(100, 300)$; split B produces $(200, 400)$ and $(200, 0)$, where $(n_0, n_1)$ counts the classes.

**Misclassification.** The parent's majority vote errs on $400/800 = 0.5$ of its points. After A, each child errs on $100/400$: weighted error $0.25$. After B, the first child errs on $200/600$ and the second on none: $\tfrac{6}{8}\cdot\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{2}{8}\cdot 0 = 0.25$. The two splits are exactly tied.

**Gini.** Parent: $2(0.5)(0.5) = 0.5$. After A each child has $p = \tfrac14$ or $\tfrac34$, $G = 0.375$, weighted $0.375$: decrease $0.125$. After B: first child $p = \tfrac23$, $G = \tfrac49$, weighted $\tfrac{6}{8}\cdot\tfrac49 = \tfrac13$; second child $0$: decrease $0.167$.

**Entropy.** Parent: $1$ bit. After A: $H(\tfrac14) = 0.811$ bits, weighted $0.811$: gain $0.189$. After B: $\tfrac{6}{8} H(\tfrac13) = 0.75 \times 0.918 = 0.689$: gain $0.311$.

Split B manufactures a pure node holding a quarter of the data, an unambiguously better structure for further splitting, and both concave measures prefer it decisively. Misclassification error cannot see the difference.
</Example>

### Concavity is the mechanism

Worse than ties is blindness. Take a node with counts $(80, 20)$ and split it into $(40, 0)$ and $(40, 20)$: the split makes a pure node of size 40, yet the misclassification decrease is $0.2 - \bigl(0.4 \cdot 0 + 0.6 \cdot \tfrac13\bigr) = 0$, because both children still vote for class 0 and the vote's error count does not move. Gini scores the same split at $\Delta = 0.053$. A grower that stops when no split improves its criterion would refuse this split outright.

<Figure
  src="./figures/impurity-chord.svg"
  alt="The Gini parabola and misclassification tent with the (80,20) to (40,0)+(40,20) split drawn as a chord: the chord under the parabola leaves a positive gap at the parent, while the chord on the tent lies on the curve"
  caption="A split's gain is curve minus chord at the parent's p. Strict concavity makes the gap positive; on the tent's linear branch the chord lies on the curve and the gain is zero."
/>

The figure is the general argument. A split with child proportions $p_L, p_R$ and weights $w_L, w_R$ has parent proportion $p = w_L p_L + w_R p_R$, so its gain under impurity $C$ is

$$
\Delta = C(p) - \bigl[w_L\, C(p_L) + w_R\, C(p_R)\bigr]:
$$

the height of the curve above the chord joining $(p_L, C(p_L))$ to $(p_R, C(p_R))$, evaluated at $p$. If $C$ is *strictly concave*, as Gini and entropy are, the chord lies strictly below the curve, so **every split that separates the classes at all** ($p_L \neq p_R$) **has strictly positive gain**. Misclassification error is piecewise linear: whenever both children fall on the same linear branch, which is exactly when they share a majority class, chord and curve coincide and the gain is zero.

<KeyFact>
Grow with Gini or entropy; prune and report with misclassification error. Growing needs a strictly concave criterion so that progress toward purity is always visible; final accuracy is measured in errors, so the pruning criterion is the error rate the tree will actually be judged by.
</KeyFact>

### Gini as variance reduction

Gini impurity has a second reading that ties this section to the course's spine. Code class $k$ membership as a 0/1 variable; its variance within the node is $\hat p_{mk}(1 - \hat p_{mk})$, the retrieval-gate quantity, and summing over classes gives $G_m$ exactly. Choosing the split of maximum Gini decrease is choosing the split that most reduces within-node variance — the variance term of <Ref lesson="bias-variance">the decomposition</Ref>, attacked greedily one region at a time.

One line of genealogy, since the names recur in every library's documentation: ID3 and its successors C4.5 and C5.0 (Quinlan) grow with information gain and historically favored categorical features and multiway splits; CART (Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone, 1984) grows binary trees with Gini and is the algorithm behind this course and behind scikit-learn's implementation. The differences are engineering; the greedy-sweep skeleton is identical.

## Regression trees: the same step under a variance impurity

The variance reading makes the regression case a substitution rather than a new algorithm. For a regression node, define its impurity as the mean squared deviation from the node mean, $Q_m = \tfrac{1}{N_m}\sum_{x_i \in R_m} (y_i - \bar y_m)^2$, and the greedy step is unchanged: for each feature $j$ and threshold $s$, solve

$$
\min_{j,\,s} \;\Bigl[\, \min_{c_1} \sum_{x_i \in R_L(j,s)} (y_i - c_1)^2 \;+\; \min_{c_2} \sum_{x_i \in R_R(j,s)} (y_i - c_2)^2 \Bigr],
$$

where the inner minimizations are solved by the child means. The sweep enumerates the same $N-1$ thresholds per feature; only the price changed, from class mixing to residual variance.

The bias is the price of flat leaves, and flat leaves are what survive in high dimension and mixed feature types, where no smoother can operate at all.

## Grow deep, then prune: cost-complexity

The greedy sweep says which split to make, not when to stop making them. The tempting rule — stop when the best available $\Delta$ falls below a threshold — fails, and the failure has a two-line witness.

### Zero gain now, perfect fit one level down

Four points, two classes: $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ in class 0, $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ in class 1. Every single split on either coordinate produces two children each holding one point of each class: $p_L = p_R = \tfrac12 = p$, so every criterion of the previous section prices every split at exactly $\Delta = 0$. A threshold rule stops at the root and predicts a coin toss. Yet the depth-2 tree that splits on $x_1$ and then on $x_2$ in both children classifies all four points perfectly. The first split buys nothing on its own and everything one level later; a greedy stopping rule cannot see one level later.

### Grow deep, prune by rent per leaf

CART's resolution: **grow deep, then prune**. Grow $T_0$ until leaves are small (a minimum of about 5 points is conventional), accepting zero-gain splits along the way, then delete whatever the evidence does not support. Grown fully, the tree reproduces <Ref lesson="statistical-learning">the memorizer's</Ref> behavior: near-zero training error, every noise point wrapped in its own rectangle.

<Definition title="cost-complexity criterion">
For a subtree $T \subseteq T_0$ (obtained by collapsing internal nodes of $T_0$) with $|T|$ leaves, node error $Q_m(T)$ (misclassification rate, or mean squared error for regression) and node sizes $N_m$:

$$
C_\alpha(T) \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{|T|} N_m\, Q_m(T) \;+\; \alpha\, |T| .
$$

For each $\alpha \geq 0$ there is a unique smallest subtree $T_\alpha$ minimizing $C_\alpha$.
</Definition>

The structure is <Ref lesson="ridge-lasso">the penalized-fit template</Ref>: training loss plus a price per unit of complexity, with $\alpha$ playing $\lambda$'s role and the leaf count playing the norm's. At $\alpha = 0$ the full tree wins; as $\alpha$ grows, subtrees whose fit improvement no longer covers their per-leaf rent collapse into single leaves. And as with the lasso, the whole path comes cheaply: **weakest-link pruning** repeatedly collapses the internal node whose removal raises $\sum_m N_m Q_m$ least per leaf removed, producing a nested sequence of subtrees down to the root, and a theorem of Breiman et al. guarantees this sequence contains $T_\alpha$ for every $\alpha$. One backward pass prices every model size the tree can take.

### Cross-validation picks the stopping point

Choosing $\alpha$ is a one-dimensional model-selection problem, handed to <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap">$k$-fold cross-validation</Ref>: for each fold, grow and prune on the training portion, score every $\alpha$ on the held-out portion, and keep the $\alpha$ minimizing the averaged error (the one-standard-error rule applies verbatim). On the Hitters data, a tree grown on a 132-player training half reaches 12 leaves; six-fold cross-validation puts the error minimum at three leaves, and the three-leaf tree is exactly the salary tree of the previous section.

## What the partition buys

Four assets, each traceable to the split's form $X_j < s$.

**Mixed features.** A categorical feature enters natively: a split on it is a two-set partition of its levels, no dummy encoding required. The starred section below shows that for two-class and regression targets, the best of the $2^{q-1}-1$ partitions of $q$ levels is found by one sort.

**No scaling, ever.** A threshold sweep depends only on the *order* of a feature's values, and the chosen split adapts to any order-preserving change of units. Precisely: if $x_j$ is replaced by $g(x_j)$ with $g$ strictly increasing, every candidate partition of the data is unchanged, so the sweep picks the same partition at the transformed threshold, and the fitted tree makes identical predictions. Logs, standardization, rank transforms: all invisible. This proves the claim <Ref lesson="feature-engineering">the feature-engineering lesson</Ref> tabled, that trees are the model family scaling cannot touch; the invariance exercise at the end has you verify it by refitting.

<Figure
  src="./figures/monotone-invariance.svg"
  alt="Two number lines with the same ten points, one on a raw x1 scale and one on a log10 x1 scale, connectors pairing each point with itself, and the threshold x1 < 3.5 mapped to log10 x1 < 0.544; three points fall left of the threshold on both scales"
  caption="The no-op made visible: a strictly increasing transform slides every candidate threshold along with the points, so each candidate keeps exactly its membership and the sweep returns the same partition."
/>

**Missing values, without imputation.** CART handles a missing $x_j$ at prediction time by *surrogate splits*: when the primary split is chosen, the algorithm also ranks backup splits on other features by how well they mimic the primary's left/right assignment of the training points, and a point missing the primary feature is routed by the best surrogate it does have. Correlated features make good surrogates, so the information loss is smallest exactly when a proxy exists. For categorical features there is a blunter option, a dedicated "missing" level, which additionally lets missingness itself predict. (<Ref lesson="xgboost">XGBoost</Ref> replaces surrogates with a learned per-node default direction; the idea is the same, cheapened.)

**A readable decision path.** Every prediction is a conjunction of explicit conditions, the path from root to leaf. The Hitters tree's claim about junior players was read off the tree, not extracted by a separate interpretability tool.

The four assets compound: the tables production models are actually fit on — hospital records, loan applications — are mixed-typed, unscaled and full of holes, and the tree is the one method in this course that consumes such a table as-is and returns a decision its owner can re-trace. That combination, not accuracy, is what keeps an unstable, axis-biased model in production; the next section prices those two adjectives.

## What it pays: bias off the axes, variance everywhere

The first cost is inherited from the split's form. A tree can only draw axis-aligned boundaries, so a class boundary at $45^\circ$ to the axes must be approximated by a staircase, spending many leaves, each fitted on few points, to represent what one oblique cut states exactly. The comparison cuts both ways and is worth internalizing as a pair of pictures: when the truth is linear, the linear model of module 4 wins and the tree wastes its budget on stairs; when the truth is blocky, the tree wins and the linear model cannot bend.

### Instability: the root contest is winner-take-all

The second cost is the one this module's closing lesson measures and module 7 is built on. The greedy sweep is a sequence of winner-take-all contests, and the root's contest decides the context for everything below: a different root split changes which sub-tables every later sweep sees. When the top candidates are nearly tied, as they were on the ten-point table ($0.320$ against $0.309$), a small perturbation of the data flips the winner and the whole tree redraws.

The ten-point table makes the flip concrete. Bootstrap-resample it, drawing 10 rows with replacement, and refit. A resample that draws $i_5$ twice and misses $i_7$ makes $x_2 < 4.5$ a perfect single split: the fitted tree is one question. A resample that draws $i_2$ and $i_6$ twice while missing $i_5$ and $i_{10}$ hands the root to the other feature entirely, $x_1 < 6.5$ followed by $x_1 < 8.5$. Three draws from the same process, three different trees, three different stories about which feature matters.

This is high variance in the exact sense of <Ref lesson="bias-variance">the decomposition</Ref>: the fitted function swings with the sample. Removing a *single* training point from a depth-2 tree on the iris flower measurements reorganizes the decision surface; the hierarchical structure is the amplifier, since an error at the root propagates to every descendant. Smoothers average and are stable; trees partition and are not. The instability does not much harm the tree's own test error, but it destroys the interpretation: a tree that redraws under resampling is a story about this sample, not about the population.

<KeyFact>
A single tree's weakness is variance, not bias: grown deep it can fit anything, and refit on a neighboring sample it becomes a different tree. That specific failure shape is what makes trees the ideal raw material for <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests">bagging and random forests</Ref>, which average many refits exactly like the three in the figure.
</KeyFact>

What stands established: a recursive binary partition encoded as a tree, grown by a concave-impurity sweep that is priced by hand above, pruned by cost-complexity with $\alpha$ cross-validated, monotone-invariant and native to mixed and missing features, axis-aligned-biased and unstable. <Ref lesson="spam-tree">The next lesson</Ref> runs this machinery on the 4,601-message spam corpus: a pruned 23-leaf tree at 8.3% test error (ESL's published run: 17 leaves at 9.3%), readable split by split, and measurably worse than the additive model — all three facts doing work.

## ★ Categorical splits: one sort replaces $2^{q-1}-1$ subsets

A categorical feature with $q$ unordered levels admits $2^{q-1} - 1$ distinct two-set partitions, an unsearchable number by $q = 30$. For the targets this course cares about, the search collapses.

<Theorem title="ordered-splits suffice (Fisher 1958; Breiman et al. 1984)">
For a regression target under squared error, order the $q$ levels by their mean response $\bar y_g$; for a two-class target under Gini or entropy, order them by their class-1 proportion. Then some optimal partition among all $2^{q-1}-1$ is a split of this ordering, so only $q - 1$ candidates need pricing.

<Proof>
Sketch, for the regression case. Fix any partition into groups $L$ and $R$ with fitted constants $c_L < c_R$ (the child means). The split's total RSS is $\sum_g \text{SS}_g + \sum_g N_g (\bar y_g - c_{s(g)})^2$, where $\text{SS}_g$ is level $g$'s internal scatter, which no assignment can change, and $s(g)$ names the side holding level $g$. If some level with $\bar y_g > \tfrac{c_L + c_R}{2}$ sits in $L$, moving it to $R$ strictly decreases the second sum, and re-optimizing the two means decreases it further; symmetrically for the other side. An optimal partition therefore has every level's mean on its own side of the midpoint $\tfrac{c_L+c_R}{2}$, which makes it a threshold split of the mean ordering. The two-class impurity case runs the same exchange with proportions in place of means.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The trap hiding in the same arithmetic: because a $q$-level feature offers exponentially many partitions, it offers exponentially many chances to fit noise, and the sweep takes whichever scores best, signal or not. A high-cardinality identifier (ZIP code, user ID) tends to win splits on training data and generalize as badly as <Ref lesson="feature-engineering">target encoding without folds</Ref>, and for the same reason. Multi-class targets get no exact shortcut; implementations fall back on heuristics.

## ★ A generalization bound priced in bits

The pruning criterion charged $\alpha$ per leaf on empirical grounds. Learning theory states the honest version of the same charge, and trees are the course's first sight of it.

A tree with $k$ leaves can shatter $k$ points (each point steered to its own leaf, labeled freely), so unrestricted trees have infinite capacity and no distribution-free guarantee. Restrict instead by description length. Over binary features, $X = \{0,1\}^d$, encode a tree node by node: each of the $n$ nodes is one of $d$ possible questions or one of two leaf labels, so a block of $\log_2(d+3)$ bits per node suffices, and a tree costs about $(n+1)\log_2(d+3)$ bits in total. Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David (Eq. 18.1) then give: with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over $m$ training points, **simultaneously for every** tree $h$ with $n$ nodes,

$$
L_{\mathcal D}(h) \;\leq\; L_S(h) \;+\; \sqrt{\frac{(n+1)\log_2(d+3) + \log(2/\delta)}{2m}} .
$$

True risk is bounded by training error plus a complexity term that grows with the bits needed to write the tree down. Capacity is paid for in description length; a small tree that fits well is guaranteed to generalize, and a huge one earns no guarantee no matter how clean its training error. The right-hand side is exactly what pruning heuristically minimizes, with $\alpha|T|$ standing in for the bit cost, and minimizing it exactly is the NP-complete problem that made growing greedy in the first place. Module 9 develops the machinery behind bounds of this shape, uniform convergence over a hypothesis class, starting in <Ref lesson="learning-theory">the learning-theory lesson</Ref>.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A node holds class counts $(30, 10)$. Compute its Gini impurity, its entropy in bits, and its misclassification error. Then price the split into children $(20, 0)$ and $(10, 10)$ under all three criteria and explain the pattern in the three answers.

<Solution>
Class-1 fraction $p = 10/40 = 0.25$. Gini: $2(0.25)(0.75) = 0.375$. Entropy: $-0.25\log_2 0.25 - 0.75 \log_2 0.75 = 0.5 + 0.311 = 0.811$ bits. Misclassification: $\min(0.25, 0.75) = 0.25$.

The split sends half the points to each child. Child $(20,0)$ is pure: all three impurities are $0$. Child $(10,10)$ is an even mixture: Gini $0.5$, entropy $1$, misclassification $0.5$.

Gini decrease: $0.375 - 0.5(0) - 0.5(0.5) = 0.125$. Information gain: $0.811 - 0.5(1) = 0.311$. Misclassification decrease: $0.25 - 0.5(0) - 0.5(0.5) = 0$.

The pattern is the chord construction: the children's proportions are $0$ and $0.5$, and the parent's $0.25$ sits midway. Gini and entropy are strictly concave, so the chord from $(0,0)$ to $(0.5, C(0.5))$ passes strictly below the curve at $p = 0.25$ and both gains are positive. Misclassification is linear on $[0, 0.5]$, the chord lies on the curve, and the gain is exactly zero even though the split manufactured a pure node of 20 points.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The lesson used both leaf rules without proof. (a) Show that the constant $c$ minimizing $\sum_{i \in R} (y_i - c)^2$ is the mean $\bar y_R$. (b) Show that the class $k$ minimizing the number of errors $\sum_{i \in R} \mathbf 1\{y_i \neq k\}$ is the majority class. (c) State what each result implies about the height of a regression leaf and the label of a classification leaf when the node contains a single point.

<Solution>
(a) Expand around the mean: $\sum_i (y_i - c)^2 = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y_R)^2 + N_R(\bar y_R - c)^2$, since the cross term $2(\bar y_R - c)\sum_i (y_i - \bar y_R)$ vanishes by definition of the mean. The first term does not involve $c$; the second is minimized uniquely at $c = \bar y_R$.

(b) $\sum_i \mathbf 1\{y_i \neq k\} = N_R - n_k$ where $n_k$ counts the points of class $k$ in the node; minimizing it is maximizing $n_k$, achieved by any modal (majority) class, uniquely when the mode is unique.

(c) With one point, the mean is that point's response and the majority is that point's label: a fully grown tree with singleton leaves reproduces its training data exactly, which is the memorizing predictor of <Ref lesson="statistical-learning">the first lesson</Ref> and the reason growing must be followed by pruning.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two boundary probes. (a) A node is pure. Show that all three impurity measures are $0$, that every candidate split has $\Delta = 0$ under every measure, and name what CART therefore does with the node. (b) The XOR configuration: four points $(0,0), (1,1)$ in class 0 and $(0,1), (1,0)$ in class 1. Verify that every single split has zero gain under misclassification, Gini, and entropy, and that a depth-2 tree is exact. State precisely which hypothesis of the concavity argument fails, and what the pair of examples implies for stopping rules.

<Solution>
(a) A pure node has $p = 0$ or $p = 1$; all three formulas give $0$ there. Any split of a pure set makes two pure children with the same $p$, so parent and weighted-child impurities are all $0$ and $\Delta = 0$. CART declares the node a leaf: no split can ever help, and this stopping condition is safe, unlike a gain threshold.

(b) Each of the four possible splits ($x_1$ or $x_2$, threshold between $0$ and $1$) sends one point of each class to each side: $p_L = p_R = \tfrac12$, equal to the parent's $p = \tfrac12$. Every impurity of every node involved equals its maximum value and $\Delta = 0$ for all three criteria. Splitting on $x_1$ and then on $x_2$ inside both children yields four singleton leaves, zero errors.

The concavity argument guarantees $\Delta > 0$ only when $p_L \neq p_R$: a split must change the class proportions to register. XOR's splits change nothing about the proportions while changing everything about the geometry. Together the two cases bracket stopping rules: "stop when pure" is exactly right, "stop when no split gains" is wrong, because zero gain now (XOR) can hide full separation one level down. Hence grow deep and prune, never stop early on gain.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute or confirm: "Switching from Gini to entropy materially changes the fitted tree." Then state the genuinely load-bearing distinction among the three criteria.

<Solution>
Parent entropy: $H(0.4) = 0.971$ bits. Candidates (weighted child entropy, then gain): $x_2 < 1.5$: right child $H(\tfrac47) = 0.985$, weighted $0.690$, gain $0.281$. $x_2 < 3$: right child $H(0.8) = 0.722$, weighted $0.361$, gain $\mathbf{0.610}$. $x_2 < 4.5$: left child $H(\tfrac17) = 0.592$, weighted $0.414$, gain $0.557$. $x_2 < 5.5$: left child $H(\tfrac13) = 0.918$, weighted $0.9 \times 0.918 = 0.826$, gain $0.145$. $x_1 < 4.5$: right child $H(\tfrac13) = 0.918$, weighted $0.551$, gain $0.420$.

Same winner ($x_2 < 3$), same runner-up ($x_2 < 4.5$), same ordering of every candidate computed. The claim is refuted for this table, and the table is typical: both criteria are strictly concave and nearly proportional over most of $[0,1]$, so they disagree only in near-ties, mostly in multi-class problems where entropy weighs rare classes slightly differently; an empirical study by Raileanu and Stoffel (2004) put the disagreement rate at a few percent of splits. The load-bearing line is not between Gini and entropy but between the concave pair and misclassification error, which the Bishop example separates: only the concave criteria can see a purity-manufacturing split behind an unchanged vote.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Monotone invariance, verified by doing, then broken by a neighbor. (a) Replace $x_1$ by $100 x_1$ in the ten-point table (a change of units) and refit the tree. Show the sweep selects the same partitions and the tree's predictions are unchanged. (b) Now classify the query point $(6, 4.6)$ with 1-nearest-neighbor from <Ref lesson="knn-curse">module 1</Ref> under both scalings, and compare. (c) State the general conclusion for preprocessing pipelines.

<Solution>
(a) Multiplying $x_1$ by $100$ multiplies every candidate threshold by $100$ and permutes nothing: each candidate still partitions the rows identically, so every $\Delta$ in the sweep is byte-for-byte the same and the winner is still $x_2 < 3$ (untouched), then $x_1 < 350$ in place of $x_1 < 3.5$, assigning exactly the same rows. Same leaves, same predictions.

(b) Raw units: the nearest training point to $(6, 4.6)$ is $i_5 = (5,5)$ at distance $\sqrt{1 + 0.16} \approx 1.08$ (runner-up $i_7$ at $1.17$), so 1-NN predicts class 1. After scaling, the query is $(600, 4.6)$ and distances are dominated by $x_1$: the nearest point is $i_6 = (600, 2)$ at distance $2.6$, while $i_5$ and $i_7$ sit $100$ away. Prediction flips to class 0. The tree's answer is identical under both scalings (query has $x_2 = 4.6 \geq 3$ and $x_1$ on the right of the second split: class 1).

(c) Distance-based methods make scaling a modeling decision that changes predictions; trees make it a no-op. A pipeline that standardizes features for a $k$-NN or penalized model, per <Ref lesson="feature-engineering">the feature-engineering lesson</Ref>, can and should skip that step for trees; nothing about the tree changes either way.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Instability, quantified. Generate $n = 500$ points with $x_1, x_2 \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ independently and $y = \mathbf 1\{x_1 + x_2 > 1\}$, each label flipped with probability $0.15$ (fix a seed). Grow a full tree (no pruning) on each of 20 bootstrap resamples and record which feature the root split uses. Report the frequency table and, before running, predict its shape from the symmetry of the problem. State what would count as evidence of instability.

<Solution>
Prediction first: the generating process is exchangeable in $x_1$ and $x_2$, so neither feature is truly better at the root; the best single threshold on either feature yields about the same impurity decrease, and the root contest is a near-tie decided by sampling noise. Expected outcome: the root feature flips between resamples, with a frequency table in the vicinity of $10{:}10$ and any split from about $6{:}14$ to $14{:}6$ unsurprising.

Running it (any CART implementation; scikit-learn's `DecisionTreeClassifier` with default settings) typically produces a table such as $x_1{:}\,11$, $x_2{:}\,9$, with the root threshold itself varying by several hundredths across resamples. Evidence of instability is exactly this: the single most consequential decision in the model, the root split that every other split conditions on, is not a stable property of the data-generating process but of the particular 500-point draw. A frequency table like $19{:}1$ would instead indicate a stable root and refute the instability claim for this problem. The deeper structure is worse: since every sub-table below the root differs when the root differs, the 20 trees disagree not just at the root but nearly everywhere.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Continue the previous exercise. Draw a fresh test set of $2{,}000$ points from the same process. Compare (a) the test error of one full tree grown on the original 500 points against (b) the test error of the *average* of the 20 bootstrap trees, predicting the majority vote of their predictions at each test point. <Solution>
Typical numbers: the single full tree lands near $0.23$–$0.25$ test error, the 20-tree vote near $0.18$–$0.20$; both sit above the Bayes floor of $0.15$, which no predictor can beat since 15% of test labels are flipped coins. The gap is the instability of the previous exercise, cashed out. Each full tree is a low-bias, high-variance estimator: it can represent the diagonal boundary's staircase, but which staircase it fits swings with the resample. The trees' errors are partly independent, so voting cancels a fraction of the variance term of <Ref lesson="bias-variance">the decomposition</Ref> while leaving bias untouched. This procedure, bootstrap resamples plus aggregation, is *bagging*, and it opens <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests">module 7</Ref>: the next question, answered there, is why the variance does not cancel completely and what the residual correlation between trees costs.
</Solution>

Uncertainty becomes code twice over: your own GP delivers error bars that
believe the kernel, your own conformal wrapper delivers coverage that believes
nothing, and the lab ends by measuring both on held-out data.

Everything the lab builds was derived in this module. The squared-exponential
kernel and the predictive equations come from <Ref lesson="gaussian-processes" />,
along with Algorithm 2.1 — the Cholesky routine you implement, factorize once
and reuse for the mean, every predictive variance, and the log marginal
likelihood. The evidence itself, and its use as a hyperparameter selector
without a validation set, is <Ref lesson="bayesian-linear-regression" />'s
result kernelized. The calibration quantile and
the coverage theorem it satisfies are <Ref lesson="conformal-prediction" />'s,
off-by-one included — the lab asks you to reconstruct the index, not to copy
it. The record the lab points all of it at, and the eleven-hyperparameter
composite kernel that ships complete for the forecasting experiment, are
<Ref lesson="mauna-loa" />'s.

What the lab contributes is the module's central comparison, executed rather
than asserted. The same GP and the same conformal wrapper are measured twice on
the Mauna Loa CO₂ record: once on a split that holds months out at random,
where exchangeability holds by construction, and once on a forecast, where it
does not. The ordering of the two bands reverses between the experiments, and
the written checkpoint asks you to say why. Along the way your own grid search
reproduces the two-optima phenomenon on real data — at 201 training months the
evidence prefers a smooth model that files the annual cycle under measurement
noise; seventy-three months later it prefers the model that treats the cycle as
signal.

The lab runs as a Jupyter notebook — open it in Colab with the button above, or
in any Jupyter you like. Pair the notebook with the code from this page; each
checkpoint submits your function's outputs for verification and the checklist
below tracks what stands. The seven exact checkpoints are the required set —
the kernel, the prior sampler, the posterior, the evidence, the grid search,
the conformal quantile and the coverage report; the written answer and the
final open task are optional, and partial completion is a normal way to finish
a lab. Expect four to five hours of real work: the Cholesky form of the
predictive equations is derived rather than printed, the conformal quantile's
index is the checkpoint's whole content, and the closing task is open — screen
150 held-out months of the record for corrupted readings with a band you
calibrate yourself. If a check goes red, the verdict often names the exact bug
— read it before rereading your code.

k-means alternates two moves — assign each point to its nearest center, recompute each center as the mean of its points — and each move can only decrease the same objective, so the algorithm converges.

Seven modules of this course fitted models to labeled data, and every verdict rested on the same instrument: risk against a target, estimated honestly on labels the fit never touched. This module drops the labels, and the instrument goes with them: there is no target to be wrong about, so an objective must be built from something else, and the two candidates are geometry (this lesson) and likelihood (<Ref lesson="gmm-em">the Gaussian mixture lesson</Ref>). k-means is the geometric answer, and it comes with the module's first theorem: a two-line proof that its iteration cannot fail to converge, and an equally short demonstration that converging is not the same as being right.

## Learning without labels: what the objective can no longer be

Every objective so far has compared a prediction with a recorded truth. <Ref lesson="statistical-learning">The statistical learning lesson</Ref> defined risk as expected loss against $y$ on a fresh draw, and everything since (the Bayes floor, cross-validated error, every benchmark ladder) was a version of that comparison. A clustering task supplies inputs $x_1, \dots, x_N$ and no $y$: there is no floor to approach, no held-out error against truth, and no way to score a proposed grouping by asking how often it is "wrong".

The loss is deeper than a missing metric. Grouping "similar points together, dissimilar points apart" is two demands, and they can contradict each other: similarity is not transitive (a chain of pairwise-similar points can connect two very dissimilar ends), while sharing a cluster is. The same point set can support two incompatible groupings, each defensible: split by horizontal bands, or by left and right halves. No computation on the unlabeled data can arbitrate, because the "right" answer depends on what the clusters are for. Validation in this module is therefore intrinsically weaker than in the supervised modules: internal scores exist and are useful, but nothing plays the role held-out risk played. <Ref lesson="hierarchical-dbscan">The next lesson</Ref> sharpens this into a theorem of Kleinberg's: no clustering function satisfies three natural axioms at once.

### The objective k-means declares

What remains possible is to *declare* an objective and optimize it. k-means declares squared Euclidean distance to a set of $K$ representative points, and a concrete evaluation shows the bookkeeping. Six points in the plane, $a=(1,1)$, $b=(1,2)$, $c=(2,1)$, $d=(6,5)$, $e=(7,5)$, $f=(6,6)$, with $K=2$ centers placed at $\mu_1 = (1,1)$ and $\mu_2 = (2,1)$. Assign each point to its nearer center: $\{a, b\}$ go with $\mu_1$, $\{c, d, e, f\}$ with $\mu_2$, and the total squared distance from each point to its assigned center is $0 + 1 + 0 + 32 + 41 + 41 = 115$. That number is the objective, and the definition only adds notation for who is assigned where.

<Definition title="distortion">
Given data $x_1, \dots, x_N \in \mathbb{R}^d$, centers $\mu_1, \dots, \mu_K \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and binary indicators $r_{nk} \in \{0, 1\}$ with $\sum_k r_{nk} = 1$ (point $n$ belongs to exactly one cluster), the **distortion** is
$$
J(r, \mu) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{k=1}^{K} r_{nk}\, \lVert x_n - \mu_k \rVert^2 ,
$$
the total squared distance from each point to the center of its cluster. The k-means problem is to minimize $J$ jointly over the assignments $r$ and the centers $\mu$.
</Definition>

In the six-point computation, a different assignment can only match or raise $115$; different centers can lower it, and $115$ is nowhere near the best this data allows.

Minimizing $J$ exactly is a combinatorial search: the number of ways to partition $N$ points into $K$ non-empty groups is a Stirling number that reaches $S(10,4) = 34{,}105$ for ten points and roughly $10^{10}$ by $N = 19$ (ESL Eq. 14.30). Enumeration is out at any realistic size, and no efficient exact algorithm is known. What the structure of $J$ offers instead is two coordinate directions in which the minimization is easy.

## Lloyd's algorithm: each half-step is an exact minimization

$J$ depends on two blocks of variables, the assignments $r$ and the centers $\mu$, and fixing either block makes minimizing over the other trivial. Alternating the two exact block-minimizations is *Lloyd's algorithm*, formulated by Stuart Lloyd at Bell Labs in 1957 for quantizing telephone signals (published only in 1982); the name *k-means* is MacQueen's (1967). Initialize the centers somehow, then repeat:

1. **Assignment step.** With $\mu$ fixed, set $r_{nk} = 1$ for $k = \arg\min_j \lVert x_n - \mu_j \rVert^2$: each point joins its nearest center.
2. **Update step.** With $r$ fixed, set each $\mu_k$ to the mean of the points assigned to it.

Neither step is a heuristic, and the convergence proof rests on establishing exactly that.

<Theorem title="each step minimizes J in its block (Bishop 9.1)">
Step 1 minimizes $J(r, \mu)$ over all valid assignments $r$ with $\mu$ fixed. Step 2 minimizes $J(r, \mu)$ over all centers $\mu$ with $r$ fixed.

<Proof>
**The assignment block.** $J$ is a sum over $n$, and the constraint (one $k$ per point) couples nothing across points, so each term $\sum_k r_{nk} \lVert x_n - \mu_k \rVert^2$ is minimized separately: point $n$ contributes $\lVert x_n - \mu_k \rVert^2$ for whichever single $k$ it selects, and the smallest available value is the distance to the nearest center. Choosing anything else raises that term and touches no other.

**The center block.** With $r$ fixed, $J = \sum_k \sum_{n: r_{nk}=1} \lVert x_n - \mu_k \rVert^2$ splits into one quadratic per center, and each is the residual-sum-of-squares problem whose minimizer is the mean: setting the gradient $2 \sum_n r_{nk} (\mu_k - x_n)$ to zero gives $\mu_k = \sum_n r_{nk} x_n / \sum_n r_{nk}$. This is the same one-line lemma that made leaf means optimal in <Ref lesson="decision-trees">the decision trees lesson</Ref>, reused verbatim.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Convergence is now an accounting fact.

<Theorem title="Lloyd's algorithm converges in finitely many iterations">
Break assignment ties toward the point's current cluster. Then $J$ never increases, and the algorithm reaches an iteration that changes nothing, a partition that is a fixed point of both steps, after finitely many iterations.

<Proof>
Each assignment step and each update step minimizes $J$ over its own block while the other is held fixed, and the current configuration is one of the candidates in that minimization, so neither step can raise $J$: the sequence of $J$ values is non-increasing, and it is bounded below by $0$.

Monotonicity alone does not force termination in finitely many steps; the finiteness of the state space does. After an update step the centers are determined by the assignment (they are its cluster means), so the algorithm's trajectory is a sequence of assignments, and there are at most $K^N$ of them. If an assignment step changes any point's cluster, the tie-breaking rule means the change was a strict improvement, so $J$ strictly decreases and no earlier assignment (each with a $J$ at least as large) can ever recur. A strictly decreasing sequence over a finite set halts: some assignment step changes no point, the following update recomputes the same means, and the configuration repeats forever. Without the tie-break, a point equidistant between two centers could in principle oscillate between equal-$J$ states; the rule costs nothing and closes the gap.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<KeyFact>

<Remark title="k-means is not k-NN">
The two share a letter and a distance computation and nothing else. $k$-NN (<Ref lesson="knn-curse">the nearest-neighbor lesson</Ref>) is a supervised predictor: $k$ counts the labeled neighbors that vote on a query, and there is nothing to optimize. k-means is unsupervised: $K$ counts the clusters being fitted, the "means" are fitted parameters, and the algorithm is an optimizer. A useful tell in either direction: if there is a training label anywhere, it is not k-means; if something iterates to convergence, it is not k-NN.
</Remark>

### The implementation wrinkle: a cluster can empty

Nothing in the assignment step guarantees every center attracts at least one point; a center seeded far from the data, or crowded out as others move, can end an assignment step with no members, and its mean is then undefined. The two standard repairs are to drop the cluster and continue with $K-1$ centers, or to reseed it at the point currently farthest from its own center. Both preserve the convergence argument: an empty center contributes nothing to $J$, so dropping it changes nothing, and reseeding at a data point can only give the next assignment step a better option than it had.

## Convergence is not optimality

The theorem guarantees a fixed point, not the minimum: which fixed point Lloyd's algorithm reaches depends entirely on the seeds. ## Restarts, and seeding by construction

The standard defense costs only compute: run Lloyd's algorithm from many random seedings and keep the run with the lowest final $J$. The selection is legitimate because $J$ is computable from the data alone: no labels are needed to compare two runs, which is exactly the comparison held-out risk performed in the supervised modules. On the easy geometry above, random data points as seeds reach the global optimum in about $87\%$ of runs, so a handful of restarts all but guarantees it; harder geometries shift the odds toward the bad fixed points, and restarts stop being cheap insurance.

Seeding can also be engineered rather than repeated. The failure mode of uniform seeding is two seeds in one cluster; the fix is to make the next seed avoid the territory existing seeds already cover. *k-means++* (Arthur and Vassilvitskii, 2007) draws seeds sequentially: the first uniformly at random, and each next seed equal to data point $x$ with probability proportional to $D(x)^2$, the squared distance from $x$ to its nearest already-chosen seed.

The rule spreads seeds not by forbidding anything but by making a wasted seed improbable.

The construction carries a guarantee that restarts lack. Arthur and Vassilvitskii prove that the seeding alone, before Lloyd's algorithm runs a single step, achieves $\mathbb{E}[J] \le 8(\ln K + 2)\, J_{\text{opt}}$, an expected distortion within a factor $O(\log K)$ of the global optimum, for every data set. Lloyd iterations started from those seeds only lower $J$ further, so the bound survives. The guarantee is in expectation, the factor grows (slowly) with $K$, and it bounds distortion, not agreement with any intended grouping. When an exact optimum is out of reach, a randomized construction with a provable approximation ratio is the honest substitute.

<KeyFact>
k-means' guarantees stack as: any seeding converges (finitely many strict improvements); k-means++ seeding converges to an expected distortion within $8(\ln K + 2)$ of optimal. No efficient algorithm guarantees the optimum itself, and none is likely: minimizing $J$ exactly is NP-hard even for $K = 2$ (Aloise et al., 2009).
</KeyFact>

## Vector quantization: the objective's operational meaning

Distortion was declared, not derived, and the declaration has a defense: $J$ is exactly the cost of using the centers as a compressed code for the data. Store, for each of $N$ points, only the index of its nearest center, plus the $K$ centers themselves; reconstruct each point as its center. The reconstruction error is $\sum_n \lVert x_n - \mu_{c(n)} \rVert^2 = J$, so minimizing distortion is minimizing the damage of this compression scheme at a fixed budget of $K$ code vectors. In signal processing the scheme is called *vector quantization*, the centers are the *codebook*, and Lloyd's 1957 telephone-signal setting was exactly this.

A $240 \times 180$ color image has $N = 43{,}200$ pixels, each a point in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (red, green, blue at 8 bits apiece): stored raw, $24N = 1{,}036{,}800$ bits. Run k-means on the pixels with $K = 10$ and store indices instead: each pixel needs $\lceil \log_2 10 \rceil = 4$ bits to name its center, plus $24$ bits for each of the ten codebook entries, totalling $24 \cdot 10 + 43{,}200 \cdot 4 = 173{,}040$ bits, $16.7\%$ of the original (Bishop §9.1.1). The ceiling means $K = 10$ pays the same index cost as $K = 16$, so on this ledger $K$ might as well be a power of two:

## Choosing K: the one thing the objective cannot do

Every run so far took $K$ as given, and the objective itself cannot supply it. The training distortion is trivially monotone: an optimal set of $K{+}1$ centers can always place $K$ centers where the optimal $K$-run put them and spend the extra one anywhere a point stands off-center, so $J^*_{K+1} \le J^*_K$, all the way down to $J^*_N = 0$ with every point its own center, a perfect score for a clustering that says nothing.

The supervised reflex is to evaluate on held-out data and let the U-curve pick the complexity, as <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap">the resampling lesson</Ref> did for every hyperparameter in the course. It fails here, and the failure is structural (Murphy §21.3.7): a set of centers is a degenerate density model, $K$ spikes with no notion of how much space a cluster claims. More spikes cover the input space more finely, so a fresh point's distance to its nearest center also shrinks as $K$ grows, and held-out distortion decreases monotonically too: no minimum, no elbow guaranteed, nothing to select. Overfitting in the supervised sense never registers, because no term in $J$ ever pays a price for complexity.

### What practice uses instead

The tools that survive are honest heuristics and one principled exit. The *elbow method* plots $J^*_K$ against $K$ and picks the bend where the curve's decrease flattens; since a convex decreasing curve always bends somewhere, the method is a judgment call dressed as a procedure, and on real data the bend is frequently ambiguous or plural. The *silhouette score*, which compares each point's within-cluster distances against its distances to the nearest other cluster, actually peaks rather than decreasing, and <Ref lesson="hierarchical-dbscan">the next lesson</Ref> computes it in full. The *gap statistic* (Tibshirani, Walther and Hastie, 2001) calibrates the $J^*_K$ curve against the same curve on reference data with no clusters, which turns the bend into a testable excess. And the principled exit is to stop pretending $K$ spikes are a model: give each cluster a shape and a weight, and marginal likelihood or BIC can select $K$ the way this course selects everything else. That is <Ref lesson="gmm-em">the Gaussian mixture lesson's</Ref> opening argument.

## Failure geometry: what squared Euclidean distance assumes

The objective scores every direction and every cluster with the same ruler: $\lVert x_n - \mu_k \rVert^2$ is isotropic (a unit of error north costs the same as a unit east) and shared (every cluster pays into the same sum, so a cluster with more points casts more votes). Both properties are assumptions about what clusters look like, and data that violates them defeats the *objective*, not just the optimizer: the failures below are global optima.

The bands are long, so their far ends are expensive under an isotropic ruler, and the objective prefers two compact halves to two correct stripes. ### Scaling changes the answer, in both directions

The ruler is also unit-dependent, and this cuts opposite ways. <Ref lesson="knn-curse">The nearest-neighbor lesson</Ref> showed a distance owned by whichever feature has the biggest numbers (income in dollars burying age in years), and standardizing to unit variance was mandatory hygiene there. For clustering the same operation can destroy the answer (ESL §14.3.3, the idea of its Fig. 14.5): below, feature 2 separates two real groups cleanly, while feature 1 records an irrelevant nuisance (say, which of two instruments measured the sample). On raw features k-means recovers the real groups exactly; standardizing shrinks the informative direction's real separation and leaves the nuisance direction's spurious one, wide gap against tight subgroups, and the best k-means solution flips to splitting along the nuisance axis, at chance agreement with the truth.

<Figure
  src="./figures/standardize-flip.svg"
  alt="The same data clustered twice, real group shown by marker shape and k-means assignment by color: on raw features the two colors match the two real groups exactly; after standardizing both features the colors split along the nuisance batch axis, cutting across both real groups"
  caption="Standardization is a modeling decision: here it hands the nuisance direction the same vote as the real one, and the partition flips."

There is no neutral choice: raw units weight features by the accident of their scales, standardized units weight them by the assumption that each deserves an equal vote, and <Ref lesson="feature-engineering">the feature engineering lesson's</Ref> verdict, that scaling is a modeling decision encoding what "similar" means, binds harder here than anywhere in the supervised half, because no held-out target will catch a wrong choice.

What stands established: clustering has no risk to minimize, so k-means declares one, distortion, and Lloyd's algorithm descends it by exact block-minimization, converging in finitely many steps to a fixed point that need not be, and often is not, the minimum; k-means++ seeding bounds the expected gap by $8(\ln K + 2)$; the objective's operational meaning is vector quantization, its blind spots are $K$, anisotropy, unequal density, and units. <Ref lesson="hierarchical-dbscan">The next lesson</Ref> takes up the shapes k-means cannot see, replacing distance-to-center with linkage and density; <Ref lesson="gmm-em">the lesson after it</Ref> softens the hard assignment $r_{nk} \in \{0,1\}$ into a probability, and inherits this lesson's convergence proof with the finite state space knocked out.

(c) Conclude termination, stating exactly where finiteness enters.

<Solution>
**(a)** $J = \sum_n \bigl( \sum_k r_{nk} \lVert x_n - \mu_k \rVert^2 \bigr)$ and the constraint $\sum_k r_{nk} = 1$, $r_{nk} \in \{0,1\}$ binds each $n$ separately, so the minimization decomposes over points. Point $n$'s term equals $\lVert x_n - \mu_k \rVert^2$ for its chosen $k$; the choice minimizing a finite set of numbers is the smallest, the nearest center. Summing pointwise minima minimizes the sum.

**(b)** Expand around the mean. With $S_k = \{n : r_{nk} = 1\}$ and $\bar{x}_k = \tfrac{1}{N_k}\sum_{n \in S_k} x_n$,
$$
\sum_{n \in S_k} \lVert x_n - \mu_k \rVert^2
= \sum_{n \in S_k} \lVert (x_n - \bar{x}_k) + (\bar{x}_k - \mu_k) \rVert^2
= \sum_{n \in S_k} \lVert x_n - \bar{x}_k \rVert^2 + N_k \lVert \bar{x}_k - \mu_k \rVert^2 ,
$$
because the cross term $2(\bar{x}_k - \mu_k)^\top \sum_{n \in S_k} (x_n - \bar{x}_k)$ vanishes: deviations from a mean sum to zero. The first term does not involve $\mu_k$ and the second is minimized, uniquely, at $\mu_k = \bar{x}_k$. This is <Ref lesson="decision-trees">the decision trees lesson's</Ref> leaf-mean lemma, and the same add-and-subtract-the-mean move that opened <Ref lesson="bias-variance">the bias–variance decomposition</Ref>.

**(c)** By (a) and (b), neither step increases $J$, and a step that changes the assignment strictly decreases it under the incumbent tie-break. The trajectory is determined by the assignment sequence (centers are its means), assignments number at most $K^N$ (this is the only place finiteness enters), and a strictly decreasing $J$ forbids revisiting any of them. A process that must keep visiting new states of a finite set halts.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Boundary probe, on the six points of the hand iteration above. (a) Compute the converged $J$ at $K = 6$ and at $K = 1$ (give the $K=1$ value exactly), and state what each end of the range says about using $J^*_K$ to choose $K$. (b) Now run $K = 2$ on the 1-D data $\{0, 1, 10\}$ from seeds $\mu_1 = 4$, $\mu_2 = 20$: show that a cluster empties, apply each of the two standard repairs, and say which part of the convergence proof each repair has to re-establish.

<Solution>
**(a)** At $K = 6$ each point is its own center and $J^*_6 = 0$: a perfect score carrying no information, the degenerate end of the monotone $J^*_K$ curve. At $K = 1$ the single optimal center is the grand mean $\bar{x} = (\tfrac{23}{6}, \tfrac{20}{6})$ and $J^*_1 = \sum_n \lVert x_n \rVert^2 - 6\lVert \bar{x} \rVert^2 = 219 - \tfrac{929}{6} = \tfrac{385}{6} \approx 64.2$, the data's total scatter. The whole curve from $64.2$ down to $0$ is achievable, is decreasing at every step, and contains no signal that $2$ is the natural value: the objective ranks every $K$ against $K{-}1$ the same way, in favor.

**(b)** Assignment from $(4, 20)$: distances of $0, 1, 10$ to $\mu_1$ are $16, 9, 36$; to $\mu_2$ are $400, 361, 100$. All three points join $\mu_1$; cluster 2 is empty and its mean is $\tfrac{0}{0}$. *Repair 1, drop:* continue with $K = 1$; the empty center contributed no term to $J$, so $J$ is unchanged and the monotone-descent argument continues verbatim; only the state space shrinks. *Repair 2, reseed at the farthest point:* the costliest point is $10$ (contribution $36$ to its center at $4$); set $\mu_2 = 10$. Moving a center no point is assigned to leaves $J$ unchanged, and the next assignment step now has a strictly better option for point $10$, so descent resumes. What each repair must re-establish is strictness: both do, because neither ever increases $J$ and any subsequent assignment change is still a strict improvement over a configuration whose $J$ was not raised in between.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "k-means finds the $K$-partition minimizing $J$; that is what the convergence theorem says." Use the five 1-D points $\{0, 1, 8, 9, 20\}$ with $K = 2$: run Lloyd's algorithm from seeds $(9, 20)$ and from seeds $(0, 12)$, name the step of the claim that fails, and state what is actually guaranteed: by the theorem, and by k-means++ seeding.

<Solution>
**Seeds $(9, 20)$.** Assignment: $0, 1, 8, 9$ join the seed at $9$; $20$ stands alone. Means: $4.5$ and $20$. Re-assignment changes nothing ($9$ is $4.5$ from its center, $11$ from the other), so this is the fixed point $\{0,1,8,9\}, \{20\}$ with $J = 20.25 + 12.25 + 12.25 + 20.25 + 0 = 65$.

**Seeds $(0, 12)$.** Assignment: $0, 1$ join $0$; $8, 9, 20$ join $12$. Means: $0.5$ and $\tfrac{37}{3} \approx 12.33$. Re-assignment changes nothing ($8$ is $7.5$ from the left center, $4.33$ from the right), so this is the fixed point $\{0,1\}, \{8,9,20\}$ with $J = 0.5 + \tfrac{169 + 100 + 529}{9} \approx 89.2$.

Both runs converged; they disagree. A minimizing partition can be taken to assign every point to a nearest center (reassigning a point to a strictly nearer center would lower $J$), so on the line some optimum splits at the midpoint of its two centers: only the four contiguous splits are candidates. The runs above found two of them; the remaining two, $\{0\},\{1,8,9,20\}$ at $J = 185$ and $\{0,1,8\},\{9,20\}$ at $J = 98.5$, score worse, so $65$ is the global optimum and the second run converged to a non-minimum. The claim's failing step is the substitution of "reaches a fixed point of both block-minimizations" for "reaches the joint minimum": the theorem proves monotone descent to the former only. What k-means++ adds is a guarantee about the *seeding*, in expectation over its randomness: $\mathbb{E}[J] \le 8(\ln K + 2) J_{\text{opt}}$ before any Lloyd step, preserved by descent afterwards; an approximation ratio, not optimality, and not a per-run promise.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Reproduce ESL's compression figures for the Fisher photograph from the bit ledger: $1024 \times 1024$ pixels at 8 bits, cut into $2 \times 2$ blocks and coded with $K = 200$ block patterns. Compute bits per pixel and the compression ratio, ignoring the codebook, and check them against the published $1.91$ bits/pixel and $0.239$. Then compute the codebook's actual share (each codeword is 4 components at 8 bits) and say whether ignoring it was fair. Finally, compute the same two numbers for $K = 4$ and check against $0.50$ bits/pixel and $0.0625$.

<Solution>
Each $2 \times 2$ block covers 4 pixels and stores one index of $\log_2 200 \approx 7.644$ bits, so the rate is $7.644 / 4 = 1.911$ bits per pixel, matching the published $1.91$. Against 8 raw bits per pixel the ratio is $1.911 / 8 = 0.2389 \approx 0.239$. ✓

The codebook holds $200$ codewords of $4 \times 8 = 32$ bits: $6{,}400$ bits total, against $512^2 \times 7.644 \approx 2.0 \times 10^6$ index bits: a $0.3\%$ overhead, fairly ignored. (Contrast the tiny-image ledger in the chapter, where $N$ is small enough that the $24K$ codebook term is worth printing.)

For $K = 4$: $\log_2 4 = 2$ bits per block, $2/4 = 0.50$ bits per pixel, ratio $0.50 / 8 = 0.0625$. ✓ The two operating points bracket the trade: $0.239$ of the storage at barely visible loss, $0.0625$ at heavy loss, and the choice between them is a rate–distortion judgment the objective $J$ prices but cannot make.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Review, from the supervised half. <Ref lesson="knn-curse">The nearest-neighbor lesson</Ref> scored customers described by (age in years, income in dollars): query $q = (30, 60000)$, candidates $A = (31, 61000)$ and $B = (55, 60500)$. Over the customer base, age has standard deviation $12$ years and income $8000$ dollars. (a) Rank $A$ and $B$ by raw squared Euclidean distance to $q$, then by standardized distance, and state which feature owns each ranking. (b) That lesson called standardizing before any distance computation mandatory hygiene, while this lesson's standardization figure shows the same operation destroying a correct clustering. Identify what the supervised setting has that licenses the word "mandatory", and why its absence here downgrades the same operation to a judgment call.

<Solution>
**(a)** Raw: $d^2(q, A) = 1^2 + 1000^2 \approx 10^6$ and $d^2(q, B) = 25^2 + 500^2 \approx 2.5 \times 10^5$, so $B$ ranks nearer, and income owns the ranking: a 500-dollar gap outweighs 25 years. Standardized (each coordinate difference divided by its feature's deviation): $d^2(q, A) = (1/12)^2 + (1000/8000)^2 \approx 0.022$ and $d^2(q, B) = (25/12)^2 + (500/8000)^2 \approx 4.34$, so $A$ ranks nearer by a factor of about two hundred in squared distance, and age owns the ranking.

**(b)** The supervised setting has a label, so a metric choice is answerable to held-out error: if standardizing worsened $k$-NN's predictions, <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap">cross-validated</Ref> risk would report it, and "mandatory" was shorthand for "raw units fail that audit, reliably". Clustering has no label and no held-out risk (this lesson's opening section), so whether the informative direction or the nuisance direction deserves the vote cannot be read off the data: standardizing encodes the assumption that every feature deserves an equal vote, raw units encode the accident of measurement scales, and no instrument catches a wrong choice. The same operation is hygiene where an audit exists and a modeling decision where none does.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Research-shaped. Generate three clusters in the plane: $200$ points from a unit-variance Gaussian at the origin, and $20$ points each from tight Gaussians (spread $0.5$) at $(6, 1.25)$ and $(6, -1.25)$. Run $K = 3$ k-means from $20$ random-data-point seedings and record, for each run, the final $J$ and whether the dense cluster was split across two centers. Report the split fraction. <Solution>

**A reference run** (the chapter's own, seeds drawn as random data points): $19$ of $20$ restarts split the dense cluster; final $J$ ranged from $344.5$ to $419.3$. Your counts will differ with the draw, but the fraction should be large.

Splitting $200$ tight points saves more squared distance than keeping $40$ far-flung ones apart costs. <Exercise>
Forward computation. A converged $K = 2$ run on 1-D-in-the-plane data has centers $\mu_1 = (1, 3)$ and $\mu_2 = (3, 3)$. (a) For the point $x = (1.98, 3)$, compute both squared distances and the assignment $r$. (b) The two distances differ by about $4\%$, yet $r$ commits fully. Compute the quantity $\sigma_1 = e^{-d_1^2/2} / (e^{-d_1^2/2} + e^{-d_2^2/2})$ for this point and for a point at $(2, 3)$, and describe what it does that $r$ cannot.

<Solution>
**(a)** $d_1^2 = (0.98)^2 = 0.9604$ and $d_2^2 = (1.02)^2 = 1.0404$. The nearest center is $\mu_1$, so $r_{n1} = 1$, $r_{n2} = 0$: a full commitment decided by a margin of $0.08$.

**(b)** For $x = (1.98, 3)$: $\sigma_1 = 1 / (1 + e^{(d_1^2 - d_2^2)/2}) = 1/(1 + e^{-0.04}) \approx 0.510$: the point is treated as $51\%$ cluster 1, $49\%$ cluster 2, recording exactly the ambiguity the hard assignment erased. For $x = (2, 3)$, on the perpendicular bisector of the two centers: $d_1^2 = d_2^2 = 1$, so $\sigma_1 = 0.5$ exactly — a point the hard assignment must still hand wholly to one side by tie-break is claimed by neither. The quantity is a *responsibility*, the posterior probability that a Gaussian centered at $\mu_1$ (unit variance, equal weights) generated $x$, and replacing $r_{nk}$ with it is precisely the move from k-means to the Gaussian mixture model, where <Ref lesson="gmm-em">the EM lesson</Ref> re-derives both of this lesson's steps with responsibilities in place of indicators.
</Solution>

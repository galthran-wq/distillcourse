The direction that maximizes projected variance is the top eigenvector of the covariance matrix; everything else in PCA — SVD, scree plots, reconstruction — follows from that one Lagrange computation.

This module has so far organized unlabeled data by *membership*: <Ref lesson="kmeans">k-means</Ref> assigned each point to a center, and <Ref lesson="gmm-em">the mixture lesson</Ref> softened the assignment into a posterior. Principal component analysis organizes by *direction* instead, asking which few axes of the feature space carry the data's variation. The answer is owed twice over: <Ref lesson="ridge-lasso">the ridge lesson</Ref> named the right singular vectors $v_j$ as principal-component directions without constructing them, and <Ref lesson="zipcode-digits">the ZIP-code lesson</Ref> used a two-component projection as a black box to draw 360 rotations of a '6' as a circle. This lesson pays both debts.

The route runs from the projected-variance objective on a six-point cloud, through the Lagrange derivation and the equivalent reconstruction-error formulation, to the SVD that computes both, components read on real digits and faces, the choice of dimension, and the three boundaries: units, labels, and linearity.

## The objective: variance along a direction

A direction summary needs two ingredients defined before any optimization: what "along a direction" means, and what "variation" means. Both are fixed by a small computation. Six raw observations of two features:

$$
(5,4),\quad (4,3),\quad (2,1),\quad (1,0),\quad (4,1),\quad (2,3).
$$

The mean is $\bar x = \tfrac16(18, 12) = (3, 2)$. Subtracting it from every row gives the *centered* data

$$
(2,2),\quad (1,1),\quad (-1,-1),\quad (-2,-2),\quad (1,-1),\quad (-1,1),
$$

a cloud around the origin, stretched along the diagonal. Everything below operates on this centered cloud: PCA describes variation *about the mean*, so the mean is removed first.

<Definition title="sample covariance matrix">
For centered data $x_1, \dots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$ stacked as the rows of $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, the **sample covariance matrix** is

$$
\hat\Sigma \;=\; \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i x_i^\top \;=\; \frac{1}{n} X^\top X ,
$$

a symmetric positive semi-definite $d \times d$ matrix. Entry $\hat\Sigma_{jk}$ is the sample covariance of features $j$ and $k$; the diagonal holds the feature variances.
</Definition>

For the six points: $\sum_i x_{i1}^2 = 4{+}1{+}1{+}4{+}1{+}1 = 12$, likewise $\sum_i x_{i2}^2 = 12$, and $\sum_i x_{i1}x_{i2} = 4{+}1{+}1{+}4{-}1{-}1 = 8$, so

$$
\hat\Sigma = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 12 & 8 \\ 8 & 12 \end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix} 2 & \tfrac43 \\ \tfrac43 & 2 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

Each feature has variance $2$; the covariance $\tfrac43$ records the diagonal stretch. (Statistics texts divide by $n-1$; the choice rescales every eigenvalue by one common factor and moves no direction, but it must be made consistently.)

Now project. A direction is a unit vector $u \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and projecting $x_i$ onto it gives the scalar $u^\top x_i$. Along the $x_1$-axis, $u = (1,0)$, the six projections are $2, 1, -1, -2, 1, -1$: variance $\tfrac{12}{6} = 2$. Along the diagonal, $u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, they are $2\sqrt2, \sqrt2, -\sqrt2, -2\sqrt2, 0, 0$: variance $\tfrac{8+2+2+8}{6} = \tfrac{10}{3} \approx 3.33$. The diagonal keeps more of the cloud's spread, and the general formula behind both numbers is a quadratic form: for centered data the projections have mean zero, so

$$
\operatorname{Var}(u^\top x) \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(u^\top x_i\bigr)^2
\;=\; u^\top \Bigl( \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i x_i^\top \Bigr) u
\;=\; u^\top \hat\Sigma\, u .
$$

<KeyFact>
Center **always**. On raw data, $\tfrac1n \sum_i x_i x_i^\top = \hat\Sigma + \bar x \bar x^\top$: a rank-one term of size $\lVert \bar x \rVert^2$ is added to the covariance, and whenever the mean offset outweighs the spread (that is, $\lVert \bar x \rVert^2 \gg \lambda_1$, the typical case for positive-valued features), the reported top direction points at the mean, not along the variation. Skipping this one line is the most common PCA implementation bug; the degenerate-cases exercise runs the numbers on this very dataset and watches the answer bend toward $\bar x$.
</KeyFact>

## The derivation: the best direction is the top eigenvector

The problem is now exact: maximize $u^\top \hat\Sigma u$ over unit vectors. The constraint $u^\top u = 1$ is not decoration: scaling $u$ by $c$ scales the objective by $c^2$, so without it the supremum is infinite and the problem says nothing.

**Lagrangian.** One constraint, one multiplier:

$$
\mathcal{L}(u, \lambda) \;=\; u^\top \hat\Sigma\, u \;-\; \lambda\,\bigl(u^\top u - 1\bigr).
$$

**Stationarity.** Using $\nabla_u (u^\top A u) = 2Au$ for symmetric $A$:

$$
\nabla_u \mathcal{L} = 2\hat\Sigma u - 2\lambda u = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\hat\Sigma u = \lambda u .
$$

Every candidate maximizer is an **eigenvector** of $\hat\Sigma$, with its multiplier equal to the corresponding eigenvalue.

**Which eigenvector.** Left-multiply the eigenvector equation by $u^\top$ and use $u^\top u = 1$:

$$
u^\top \hat\Sigma u = \lambda\, u^\top u = \lambda .
$$

The objective's value *at* an eigenvector *is* its eigenvalue. Maximizing over the finitely many candidate directions means picking the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$, and the maximum projected variance achieved is $\lambda_1$ itself.

### The remaining components: one new multiplier, then induction

The second component maximizes the same objective among unit vectors orthogonal to $v_1$, and the constrained problem collapses back onto the same equation. The Lagrangian gains one term, $-\mu\, u^\top v_1$, and stationarity reads $2\hat\Sigma u - 2\lambda u - \mu v_1 = 0$. Left-multiply by $v_1^\top$: the first term is $2 v_1^\top \hat\Sigma u = 2 (\hat\Sigma v_1)^\top u = 2\lambda_1 v_1^\top u = 0$ by the orthogonality constraint, the second vanishes the same way, and what remains is $\mu = 0$. The eigenvector equation returns, now restricted to the complement of $v_1$, where the best available eigenvalue is $\lambda_2$. The deflation exercise at the end carries the induction to all $d$ components.

<Theorem title="variance-maximizing directions">
Let $\hat\Sigma$ have eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_d$. Then $\max_{\lVert u \rVert = 1} u^\top \hat\Sigma u = \lambda_1$, attained at $v_1$; for each $k$, the maximum of $u^\top \hat\Sigma u$ over unit vectors orthogonal to $v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}$ is $\lambda_k$, attained at $v_k$; and the $q$-dimensional subspace maximizing total projected variance is $\operatorname{span}(v_1, \dots, v_q)$, capturing $\lambda_1 + \dots + \lambda_q$.
</Theorem>

<Definition title="principal components, scores, loadings">
The **principal components** (or *principal directions*) of centered data are the orthonormal eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_d$ of $\hat\Sigma$, ordered by eigenvalue. The **scores** of point $x_i$ are its coordinates $z_{ij} = v_j^\top x_i$ in that basis; the entries of $v_j$, read as weights on the original features, are its **loadings**. The variance of the $j$-th score across the data is exactly $\lambda_j$.
</Definition>

<Remark title="signs and ties are arbitrary">
If $v$ is a unit eigenvector, so is $-v$: every principal direction is defined only up to sign, so two correct implementations can report PC1 pointing opposite ways, and a flipped score plot is not a bug. When eigenvalues tie ($\lambda_j = \lambda_{j+1}$), any orthonormal basis of the shared eigenspace is equally valid, and which basis a solver reports is an artifact of its arithmetic. An isotropic cloud, $\hat\Sigma = \sigma^2 I$, is the extreme case: every direction is an eigenvector and no axis is preferred at all. This is the degenerate case that the theorem's strict ordering $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2$ quietly excludes and the degenerate-cases exercise probes.
</Remark>

## The second derivation: the same subspace minimizes reconstruction error

Maximum variance is one of two classical definitions of PCA, and the historically first is the other way around: [Pearson's 1901 formulation](https://doi.org/10.1080/14786440109462720) asks for the line, and in general the $q$-dimensional affine subspace, with the smallest total squared distance to the data; Hotelling's variance formulation arrived in 1933. The two are equivalent, and the equivalence is one Pythagoras identity per data point.

Take the dataset's own points and the direction $u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$. The point $(1,1)$ projects to itself: score $\sqrt2$, kept $(u^\top x)^2 = 2$, residual $0$. The point $(1,-1)$ projects to the origin: score $0$, kept $0$, and the whole squared length $\lVert x \rVert^2 = 2$ is residual. In both cases kept plus lost equals $\lVert x \rVert^2$, and that is not an accident of the example.

<Theorem title="max variance = min reconstruction error">
For centered data and any unit vector $u$,

$$
\underbrace{\frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \lVert x_i \rVert^2}_{\text{total, fixed}}
\;=\; \underbrace{u^\top \hat\Sigma\, u}_{\text{kept variance}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl\lVert x_i - (u^\top x_i)\,u \bigr\rVert^2}_{\text{mean reconstruction error}} ,
$$

so the unit direction maximizing projected variance is the direction minimizing mean squared reconstruction error, and likewise for $q$-dimensional subspaces.

<Proof>
Decompose $x_i = (u^\top x_i)u + r_i$ with $r_i = x_i - (u^\top x_i)u$. Then $u^\top r_i = u^\top x_i - (u^\top x_i)(u^\top u) = 0$: the residual is orthogonal to the projection, so $\lVert x_i \rVert^2 = (u^\top x_i)^2 + \lVert r_i \rVert^2$. Averaging over $i$ turns the first term into $u^\top \hat\Sigma u$ and gives the display. The left side does not depend on $u$, so over unit vectors the two right-hand terms are maximized and minimized by the same $u$. For a subspace with orthonormal basis $u_1, \dots, u_q$, project onto each basis vector in turn: the kept term becomes $\sum_{j \le q} u_j^\top \hat\Sigma u_j$ and the identical argument applies.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### The exact price of truncation

Averaging the identity at the optimal basis and summing over discarded directions gives the exact price of truncation:

$$
\text{mean reconstruction error at dimension } q \;=\; \sum_{j = q+1}^{d} \lambda_j .
$$

Dropping component $j$ costs exactly $\lambda_j$ in mean squared error: on the running example, the best line loses $\lambda_2 = \tfrac23$ of the total $\operatorname{tr}\hat\Sigma = 4$. This formula is the engine of the "choosing $q$" section below, in both its honest and its dishonest uses.

The subspace version of the optimality claim holds for arbitrary point sets, with no distributional assumption at all.

<Theorem title="optimality of the top-q eigenspace (Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David, Thm. 23.2)">
For any $x_1, \dots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$, the rank-$q$ orthogonal projection minimizing $\sum_i \lVert x_i - U U^\top x_i \rVert^2$ over matrices $U \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times q}$ with orthonormal columns is given by the top $q$ eigenvectors of $A = \sum_i x_i x_i^\top$.

<Proof>
Expanding as in the previous proof, minimizing the reconstruction error is maximizing $\operatorname{trace}(U^\top A U)$. Write $A = V D V^\top$ with $D_{11} \ge \dots \ge D_{dd} \ge 0$, and set $B = V^\top U$, so $U = VB$ and

$$
\operatorname{trace}(U^\top A U) = \operatorname{trace}(B^\top D B) = \sum_{j=1}^{d} D_{jj} \, \beta_j ,
\qquad \beta_j := \sum_{i=1}^{q} B_{ji}^2 .
$$

The columns of $B$ are orthonormal ($B^\top B = U^\top V V^\top U = I$), so $\sum_j \beta_j = q$; and each $\beta_j \le 1$, because the rows of $B$ extend to rows of a $d \times d$ orthogonal matrix, whose rows have unit norm. Maximizing $\sum_j D_{jj}\beta_j$ over weights $\beta \in [0,1]^d$ summing to $q$ puts full weight on the $q$ largest $D_{jj}$, and choosing $U$'s columns as the corresponding eigenvectors attains that value. ∎
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The two formulations are interchangeable from here on: "directions of maximum variance" and "best low-rank approximation" name one subspace. The reconstruction reading is the more general, surviving into settings where "variance" stops being the natural word (images, kernelized versions); the variance reading is the one the eigenvalue arithmetic serves directly.

## The SVD: what code computes

Everything so far eigendecomposes $\hat\Sigma$; correct code never forms $\hat\Sigma$ at all. The reason is the factorization <Ref lesson="ridge-lasso">the ridge lesson</Ref> already leaned on: any centered $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ factors as $X = U D V^\top$ with orthonormal-column $U$ and $V$ and singular values $d_1 \ge \dots \ge 0$ on the diagonal of $D$. Substituting,

$$
\hat\Sigma = \frac1n X^\top X = \frac1n V D U^\top U D V^\top = V\, \frac{D^2}{n}\, V^\top ,
$$

an eigendecomposition read off by inspection: the right singular vectors $v_j$ **are** the principal directions, with eigenvalues

$$
\lambda_j = \frac{d_j^2}{n},
$$

and the score matrix is $Z = XV = UD$: column $j$ holds all $n$ points' coordinates along $v_j$. Truncating the SVD after $q$ terms is simultaneously the top-$q$ PCA and, by the Eckart–Young theorem, the best rank-$q$ approximation to $X$ itself in squared error.

  caption="One factorization answers everything: directions (V), variances (D²/n), scores (UD), and the best rank-q approximation (the shaded blocks). The covariance matrix itself is never materialized."

**Why never form $X^\top X$.** The condition number squares: $\kappa(X^\top X) = \kappa(X)^2$. A direction whose singular value sits five decimal digits below $d_1$ is still perfectly representable in the SVD of $X$, but in $X^\top X$ its eigenvalue sits ten digits down, at the edge of double-precision noise, and these are precisely the small-variance directions whose eigenvalues the trailing end of a scree plot reports. <Ref lesson="regularization-lab">The regularization lab</Ref> issued the same warning for the normal equations, solving least squares by `lstsq` on $X$ rather than by inverting $X^\top X$; it is one fact about one matrix product, met twice.

**Cost.** A full SVD costs $O(nd^2)$ for $n \ge d$; when only the top $q$ components are wanted, truncated and randomized solvers deliver them in roughly $O(ndq)$, the difference between feasible and not for image-sized $d$.

<KeyFact>
The SVD does not center for you. `svd` applied to raw $X$ is a flawless factorization of the wrong matrix, and every identity of this section ($v_j$ as the principal directions, $\lambda_j = d_j^2/n$, $Z = UD$) presumes $X$ was centered before the factorization. Nothing errors when it was not: the "center always" bug of the first section returns silently, one abstraction level down.
</KeyFact>

### The Gram trick: eigenproblems in $n \times n$ when $d \gg n$

A face image at $50 \times 50$ pixels is a point in $d = 2{,}500$ dimensions; a training set may hold a few hundred faces. Eigendecomposing the $2{,}500 \times 2{,}500$ covariance is wasteful, and the SVD's shape already says why: with $n \ll d$, $X$ has at most $n$ nonzero singular values, so at most $n$ principal directions carry any variance at all. The computation can live entirely in $n \times n$. Let $B = XX^\top$ be the **Gram matrix** of inner products, and let $B\tilde u = \lambda \tilde u$. Multiplying by $X^\top$:

$$
X^\top X\, (X^\top \tilde u) = \lambda\, (X^\top \tilde u) :
$$

$X^\top \tilde u$ is an eigenvector of $X^\top X$ with the *same* eigenvalue. For a unit eigenvector $\tilde u$, its norm is $\lVert X^\top \tilde u \rVert^2 = \tilde u^\top B \tilde u = \lambda$, so the normalized principal direction is $v = X^\top \tilde u / \sqrt{\lambda}$. The bill drops from $O(d^3)$ to $O(n^3)$ plus the $O(n^2 d)$ of forming $B$: for $300$ faces, a $300 \times 300$ eigenproblem instead of a $2{,}500 \times 2{,}500$ one.

Two remarks the trick carries with it: it is what made eigenfaces computable on 1990s hardware, and since it touches the data only through inner products $\langle x_i, x_j \rangle$, replacing those with a kernel gives kernel PCA, the same move that <Ref lesson="svm-dual-kernels">the SVM dual</Ref> made on its own inner products.

### Ridge and PCA: one spectrum, two treatments

The ridge lesson's central display can now be read from the other side. Both methods operate on the same directions $v_j$ with the same variances $d_j^2/n$; they differ only in what they do per direction. Ridge **discounts** continuously, multiplying coordinate $j$ by $d_j^2/(d_j^2 + \lambda)$; PCA **truncates**, keeping directions $1$ through $q$ at full weight and deleting the rest, whether as a preprocessing step or as *principal component regression*, the derived-direction estimator whose prostate column <Ref lesson="prostate-study">the case study</Ref> carried as context.

Soft against hard shrinkage on one spectrum: the exact analogue, in the design's rotated basis, of ridge-versus-subset in the coordinate basis. The mixed-review exercise puts the two treatments side by side numerically.

## Components on real images: directions of variation you can look at

Nothing about the derivation says what a principal direction *means*, and on tabular data the loadings need careful reading. Image data is the exception: there, $x$ is a picture, so every direction $v_j$ is a picture, and $\bar x + z v_j$ can be watched as $z$ slides. This section reads components on the two canonical image families.

## Choosing q: what the spectrum can and cannot say

Every use of PCA ends at the same dial: how many components. The raw material for the choice is the spectrum itself.

<Definition title="explained variance">
The **explained-variance ratio** of component $j$ is $\lambda_j / \sum_{k=1}^{d} \lambda_k$, the share of total variance carried along $v_j$; the cumulative sum over $j \le q$ is the fraction retained by a $q$-dimensional projection, and one minus it is the reconstruction-error share $\sum_{j>q} \lambda_j / \sum_k \lambda_k$.
</Definition>

The standard diagnostic is the *scree plot*, eigenvalues against component index. Where the data has $q^\ast$ strong directions plus noise, the plot falls fast to $q^\ast$ and flattens after, and the bend (the "elbow") marks the boundary. Reading the elbow by eye is folklore; [Zhu and Ghodsi's profile likelihood](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.csda.2005.09.010) automates it by fitting, for each candidate $q$, a two-group Gaussian model to the eigenvalues (one mean for $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_q$, another for the rest) and picking the $q$ with the highest likelihood. Both are heuristics about the spectrum's shape; neither promises the discarded tail is noise.

### The negative result: held-out reconstruction cannot choose

What is *not* a heuristic is the negative result. The natural instinct from <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap">module 5</Ref>, holding data out and picking the $q$ that minimizes error on it, provably cannot work here.

<Theorem title="held-out reconstruction error cannot select q">
Fix a training set, and let $V_q$ be the span of its top $q$ principal directions. For every point $x$ (held-out or not), $\lVert x - P_{V_{q+1}} x \rVert \le \lVert x - P_{V_q} x \rVert$, so held-out mean reconstruction error is non-increasing in $q$ and attains its minimum at $q = d$.

<Proof>
PCA's components are nested by construction: $V_q \subset V_{q+1}$. Projection onto a larger subspace never lengthens the residual, since $v_{q+1} \perp V_q$ gives $P_{V_{q+1}}x = P_{V_q}x + (v_{q+1}^\top x)\,v_{q+1}$ and hence $\lVert x - P_{V_{q+1}}x \rVert^2 = \lVert x - P_{V_q}x \rVert^2 - (v_{q+1}^\top x)^2$. The inequality holds pointwise, hence in expectation over any held-out set. ∎
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The contrast with supervised validation is exact, and it is this module's recurring lesson met a third time. A supervised model with more capacity fits the *training* labels better but is scored against *independent* held-out labels, so overfitting registers as a rising test curve. A reconstruction score has no independent target: the quantity being evaluated, how well a subspace approximates points, is monotone in the subspace by geometry.

<Ref lesson="kmeans">k-means</Ref> failed the same way (held-out distortion falls at every $K$), and <Ref lesson="gmm-em">the mixture lesson</Ref> repaired it by becoming a density, where normalization makes probability a conserved budget and held-out log-likelihood genuinely peaks. PCA as presented here is not a density, so it has no such budget; the starred section names the probabilistic variant that restores one.

<KeyFact>
Resampling does not transfer to this problem: held-out reconstruction error decreases monotonically in $q$ (a theorem, not an empirical tendency), so it can never choose the dimension. What can: the spectrum's shape (elbow, profile likelihood) when the question is about the data, and downstream validation error when a supervised task exists — then $q$ is a pipeline hyperparameter of <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap">module 5's</Ref> machinery like any other.
</KeyFact>

## ★ Probabilistic PCA

The repair the previous section points at exists and has a closed form. *Probabilistic PCA* (Tipping and Bishop, 1999) posits a latent linear Gaussian model, $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_q)$ and $x = W z + \mu + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I_d)$: a $q$-dimensional Gaussian factor observed through noise. Its maximum-likelihood solution is PCA's subspace: $\hat W$ spans the top $q$ principal directions, scaled by $\sqrt{\lambda_j - \hat\sigma^2}$, with $\hat\sigma^2$ the mean of the discarded eigenvalues; as $\sigma^2 \to 0$ the model collapses onto the projection of this lesson.

Because it is a normalized density, held-out log-likelihood peaks at a finite $q$, EM can fit it with missing entries, and mixtures of PCAs make sense. The classical algorithm is the zero-noise limit of a generative model, exactly as k-means was the zero-variance limit of the Gaussian mixture.

## The boundaries: what variance does not buy

The derivation optimizes one quantity, the projected variance of centered data, and each of its known failure modes is a way that quantity can diverge from what a user wants. Three matter in practice.

### Units: the objective is denominated in the features' scales

$u^\top \hat\Sigma u$ has units, so changing one feature's unit changes the answer. Re-express a kilogram column in grams and its variance multiplies by $10^6$; the top eigenvector swings to that coordinate axis, and PC1 reports "the feature measured in small units".

The fix is to **standardize** each feature to unit variance first (equivalently, to eigendecompose the *correlation* matrix), and the decision is a modeling claim, not hygiene. Standardize when units are incommensurable, dollars beside years beside counts; do not when the features share one meaningful scale and their variance differences are signal, as with pixel intensities, where inflating the quietest border pixels to unit variance amplifies noise. Covariance PCA and correlation PCA answer different questions, and reporting one while intending the other is a silent error of the same family as <Ref lesson="kmeans">k-means'</Ref> unit-dependent distances.

### Labels: variance is not relevance

PCA never sees labels, so nothing aligns its ranking with a classification task's needs, and the misalignment can be total, with the discriminative direction ranked *last*.

<Example title="the informative direction is the last component">
Two balanced classes in $\mathbb{R}^2$: means $\mu_0 = (0,-1)$ and $\mu_1 = (0,+1)$, shared within-class covariance $\Sigma_W = \operatorname{diag}(9, \tfrac14)$, so both clouds are elongated along $x_1$ and separated along $x_2$. For a balanced mixture the pooled covariance is within plus between: the between part is $\operatorname{diag}(0,1)$, so $\hat\Sigma \approx \operatorname{diag}(9, 1.25)$.

PCA ranks $e_1$ first with $\lambda_1 = 9$ ($88\%$ of the variance) and $e_2$ last. Project onto PC1: both classes become $\mathcal{N}(0, 9)$, identical distributions, so no threshold beats chance and the error is $50\%$. Project onto the *last* component $e_2$: the classes become $\mathcal{N}(\mp 1, \tfrac14)$, and the midpoint threshold errs with probability $\Phi(-1/0.5) = \Phi(-2) \approx 2.3\%$. The direction carrying $88\%$ of the variance carries none of the class signal; the direction carrying the signal is the one a variance rule discards first. Fisher's criterion from <Ref lesson="lda-gda">the discriminant lesson</Ref>, which maximizes between-class over within-class scatter, recovers it: $w \propto \Sigma_W^{-1}(\mu_1 - \mu_0) = (0, 8)^\top \propto e_2$.
</Example>

  src="./figures/pca-vs-lda.svg"
  alt="Two elongated class clouds, blue and green, stacked vertically, with a horizontal amber PC1 arrow along the shared elongation and a vertical Fisher-direction arrow; below, the 1D projection onto PC1 shows the two class histograms fully overlapping while the projection onto the Fisher direction separates them"
  caption="The counterexample drawn: PC1 follows the elongation both classes share; the class signal lives on the last component, which is Fisher's direction."

<KeyFact>
Variance is a property of the inputs; relevance is a relation to a target. The two can be orthogonal (literally, as above), so no variance threshold — $90\%$, $95\%$, any\% — guards predictive information. When a downstream task exists, its validated error is the only criterion with standing; the refutation exercise breaks the folklore $95\%$ rule against these numbers.
</KeyFact>

### Linearity: one subspace for everything

PCA fits a single linear subspace. Data on a curved manifold, like <Ref lesson="zipcode-digits">the rotated-'6' circle</Ref> or the two rings that defeated the Gaussian mixture in <Ref lesson="gmm-em">the EM lesson's</Ref> final exercise, has no faithful low-dimensional *linear* summary: the circle's two scores trace the loop but a one-component projection folds it onto a chord.

The module's remaining lessons are the two escapes. <Ref lesson="spectral-clustering">Spectral clustering</Ref> keeps the eigenproblem but moves it onto a similarity graph, where "directions" become indicator-like eigenvectors of a Laplacian and the rings separate; <Ref lesson="tsne">the neighbor-embedding lesson</Ref> abandons linearity altogether and preserves neighborhoods instead of variance. The final exercise walks to that doorstep on the digits.

What stands established: the top-$q$ eigenvectors of the covariance matrix solve both the maximum-variance and the minimum-reconstruction problems, with the discarded eigenvalues as the exact price; the SVD computes them without forming $X^\top X$, in $n \times n$ when $d \gg n$; on real images the leading components are legible directions of variation; the spectrum's shape can suggest $q$ but held-out reconstruction provably cannot; and the method's guarantees end where units, labels, or curvature enter.

<Ref lesson="spectral-clustering">The next lesson</Ref> takes the eigenproblem machinery onto graphs: build a similarity graph, take its Laplacian, and the eigenvectors attached to the *smallest* eigenvalues cut clusters no centroid or subspace can see.

$$

Share: $4/5 = 80\%$. <Exercise>
The lesson proved the second component solves its constrained problem by showing the orthogonality multiplier vanishes. Complete the induction: show that maximizing $u^\top \hat\Sigma u$ over unit vectors orthogonal to $v_1, \dots, v_{k-1}$ is solved by $v_k$ with value $\lambda_k$. Then give a second, multiplier-free proof of the same statement by expanding $u$ in the eigenbasis. Write the constraint set before opening.

<Solution>
**Induction with multipliers.** Constraints $u^\top u = 1$ and $u^\top v_j = 0$ for $j < k$; Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = u^\top \hat\Sigma u - \lambda(u^\top u - 1) - \sum_{j<k} \mu_j\, u^\top v_j$. Stationarity: $2\hat\Sigma u - 2\lambda u - \sum_{j<k} \mu_j v_j = 0$. Left-multiply by $v_\ell^\top$ ($\ell < k$): $v_\ell^\top \hat\Sigma u = (\hat\Sigma v_\ell)^\top u = \lambda_\ell v_\ell^\top u = 0$ and $v_\ell^\top u = 0$ by the constraints, and $v_\ell^\top v_j = \delta_{\ell j}$, so the equation reads $\mu_\ell = 0$: every orthogonality multiplier vanishes. What remains is $\hat\Sigma u = \lambda u$: the candidates are eigenvectors lying in $\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{k-1})^\perp = \operatorname{span}(v_k, \dots, v_d)$, where the objective at an eigenvector is its eigenvalue and the largest available is $\lambda_k$, attained at $v_k$.

**Eigenbasis expansion.** Write $u = \sum_j c_j v_j$. The constraints say $c_1 = \dots = c_{k-1} = 0$ and $\sum_j c_j^2 = 1$; the objective is $u^\top \hat\Sigma u = \sum_{j \ge k} \lambda_j c_j^2$, a weighted average of $\lambda_k, \dots, \lambda_d$ with weights $c_j^2$ summing to one. An average never exceeds its largest entry, so the value is at most $\lambda_k$, with equality at $c_k = \pm 1$, i.e. $u = \pm v_k$. The transferable move: in the eigenbasis, every quadratic-form question about $\hat\Sigma$ becomes a question about weighted averages of eigenvalues, the same expansion that proved the trace bound in the subspace theorem.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

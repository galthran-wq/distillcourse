The estimators this module derived become verified code: you solve the
least-squares problem the normal equations characterize, shrink along
singular directions, soft-threshold your way down the lasso path, and
reproduce the published prostate results within tolerance. The dataset is
the module's running example — Stamey et al.'s 97 prostatectomy patients
(1989), with the study's fixed 67/30 split and the 30 held-out `lpsa`
values kept on the course server.

Everything the lab uses was earned earlier in the module: the
standardization convention and both penalties from
<Ref lesson="ridge-lasso" />, the coefficient table and the 0.521 held-out
benchmark from <Ref lesson="prostate-study" />. What it contributes is the
module's claim made executable — least squares projects, penalties shrink
— as a toolkit of your own solvers that reproduces the published table
under an honest protocol and then beats the study's baseline on data it
never saw.

The lab runs as a Jupyter notebook — open it in Colab with the button
below, or in any Jupyter you like. Pair the notebook with the code from
this page; each checkpoint submits your function's outputs for
verification and the checklist below tracks what stands. The six exact
checkpoints are the required set; the written answer and the final open
task are optional — partial completion is a normal way to finish a lab.
Expect about four hours of real work: the ridge shrinkage factor and the
coordinate-descent update are derived, not printed, and the closing task
is open — tune λ on fixed cross-validation folds and beat least squares'
held-out test error. If a check goes red, the verdict often names the
exact bug — read it before rereading your code.

The open task hands you its tuning machinery ready-made: fixed folds, the
one-standard-error rule. The validation lab of module 5 wraps this same
toolkit in what this lab borrows on faith — where the folds come from,
what the 1-SE bar means, and how tuning on the test set corrupts all of
it.

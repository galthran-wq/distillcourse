## Attribution: each gap read off a formula

A leaderboard is not the payoff; the module's claim is that each gap in it was predicted before the race was run. Three mechanisms were derived; each now gets its audit.

### The plateau is ρσ², and the forest's win is decorrelation

The <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests">correlated-average formula</Ref> says a $B$-average's variance is $\rho\sigma^2 + (1-\rho)\sigma^2/B$: the second term dies at rate $1/B$, the first never moves, so the curve must fall steeply over the first few dozen trees and then sit on a floor no additional tree can lower. That shape is what the race shows, empirically: bagging's curve moves by single messages from 50 trees to 1,000, so the removable term is spent early and the remaining 950 trees dispute a plateau only $\rho$ can move. The formula supplies the shape and the direction of every lever; where exactly the motion stops is a fact of this corpus, and the warm-up exercise works the approach rate at the properly measured sampling correlation, which <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests">the bagging lesson</Ref> put at about 0.05 for bootstrapped trees — far below any number measurable from a single fitted forest.

It also predicts where the forest's advantage must come from, and the prediction is checkable because it is lopsided. Feature subsampling weakens each tree (the best of 7 candidate splits loses to the best of 57) while decorrelating the ensemble, so if the formula is the right account, the forest should have worse individual trees and a better plateau, with the pair correlation moving the same direction as the plateau.

<Figure
  src="./figures/rho-audit.svg"
  alt="A two-column audit table under the formula rho sigma squared plus one minus rho sigma squared over B: bagging has single-tree test error 10.0 percent, pair agreement of 0.77 as a proxy for rho, ensemble plateau 5.3 percent; the m equals 7 forest has a worse single-tree error of 11.2 percent, lower agreement 0.70, and a lower plateau of 4.9 percent"
  caption="The audit: the forest's trees are individually worse and its ensemble is better, so the 0.4-point gain can only have come through ρ."

Measured on our 1,000-tree runs: the forest's trees are individually worse (11.2% mean single-tree test error against bagging's 10.0%) and its pair correlation is lower (0.77 to 0.70 — the same agreement proxy the <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests">bagging lesson</Ref> used, with the same caveat: every easy message both trees get right inflates it, so it overstates the sampling $\rho$ by an order of magnitude and is quoted here only for its direction). Strength down, correlation down, plateau down 5.3% to 4.9%: the gain arrived through the only channel the formula leaves open. That is <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests">Breiman's strength–correlation trade</Ref> audited on real mail rather than asserted on a simulation.

### What boosting fixes that no average can

The formula's other prediction is negative: an average of identically distributed trees keeps a single tree's expectation, so wherever the typical tree is wrong, the vote is wrong at any $B$. Bias is invisible to averaging, and <Ref lesson="gradient-boosting">stagewise fitting</Ref> exists to attack it. The corpus lets both claims be tested point by point: take bagging's 82 errors and sort them by how many of the 1,000 bagged trees err on each message.

The bins separate the decomposition on real data. On the 32 messages where the bagged trees lean wrong without agreeing (50–70% of trees err), disagreement means fluctuation, fluctuation is variance, and the decorrelated forest repairs just under half of them while the boosters repair three-fifths to two-thirds. On the 33 messages where 70–90% of trees agree on the error, the typical tree is wrong: that is bias, the forest fixes 2 of 33, and boosting fixes a fifth to a third, because rounds that <Ref lesson="adaboost">upweight what the current fit gets wrong</Ref> are aimed at exactly this bin. And on the 17 messages the trees err on near-unanimously, nothing helps: every method leaves at least 16 of 17 wrong. In total, of bagging's 82 errors the forest repairs 16, each booster 29, and 43 are repaired by no method in the race.

<KeyFact>
The unfixable 43 are a measurement of the corpus, not of the methods: messages whose feature vectors genuinely look like the other class, the empirical remnant of the Bayes floor from <Ref lesson="statistical-learning">module 1</Ref>. The overlap is literal: the 3,065-row training split holds exactly one feature vector that appears with both labels — two rows identical in all 57 features, one marked spam and one not (identical rows, not necessarily identical messages: the features are frequencies). No fit that is a function of the features can clear that floor, and the race's last half-point is being fought inside it.
</KeyFact>

## Out-of-bag error against the held-out set

The module's remaining claim about the averaging methods is that they carry their own validation: the <Ref lesson="bagging-random-forests">out-of-bag estimate</Ref> grades each training point with the roughly third of the trees that never saw it, and is advertised as cross-validation for free. The benchmark has a held-out set, so for once the free estimate can be graded against a real one.

The claim survives with its fine print visible. Both OOB curves start high, since at $B = 25$ each point's committee averages nine trees and a nine-tree forest is genuinely worse; past $B \approx 200$ the forest's OOB error runs at 5.0–5.1% against a test error of 4.9%, agreement well within one standard error of either estimate (the OOB side is measured on 3,065 points, so its own SE is about 0.4). The practical readings from the bagging lesson stand unchanged on the benchmark: the OOB curve says 200 trees suffice about as well as the test curve does, and it said so without spending a single held-out message.

### Significance: McNemar on the discordant messages

Error-rate differences on a shared test set deserve a sharper instrument than two SEs added in quadrature, because the two classifiers are graded on the same messages. *McNemar's test* uses only the disagreements: if two classifiers were equally good, the messages that exactly one gets wrong should split evenly between them, so the split is a coin-flip count. ESL applies it to this race (its Exercise 10.6) and reports that bagging is significantly worse than both rivals, the forest not significantly worse than boosting.

<Example title="McNemar on our bagging-versus-forest run">
On the 1,536 test messages, bagging alone errs on $n_{01} = 16$, the forest alone on $n_{10} = 9$; the other 1,511 messages are concordant and carry no information about the difference. Under the null the 25 discordant messages split $\mathrm{Bin}(25, \tfrac12)$. The continuity-corrected statistic is

$$
\chi^2 = \frac{(|n_{01} - n_{10}| - 1)^2}{n_{01} + n_{10}} = \frac{(7-1)^2}{25} = 1.44,
$$

well short of the 3.84 that the $\chi^2_1$ distribution demands at the 5% level; the exact binomial two-sided $p$ is $0.23$. The 0.5-point gap in the headline error rates rests on a 16-to-9 coin-flip count.
</Example>

Our run therefore sharpens the moral rather than the ranking. Forest versus boosting: 21 discordant messages each way, agreement so exact the test returns $p = 1$. Bagging versus forest: the computation above, the direction the book found but short of its significance, because our bagging finished a step better and the discordant pool is 25 messages. A half-point gap at $n = 1{,}536$ lives at the edge of what a paired test can certify, and whether it crosses the line depends on the draw. The reproducible facts are the ordering and the mechanism audits above; the pairwise verdicts are properties of one run.

### What did not change, and what a deployment decides on

Two of the module's standing audits survive into the winner. First, leakage: the boosted model's importance list leads with `ch$`, `ch!`, and `remove`, and still carries `hp` at fourth and `george` in its top ten: the recipient's employer and first name, the features <Ref lesson="spam-filter">module 4's audit</Ref> flagged as portable to no other mailbox. Every model in the race fit them; every error rate on this page is optimistic about deployment on anyone else's mail; a 4.5% with `george` in the model is not a 4.5% claim about spam.

Second, <Ref lesson="classification-metrics">the metric</Ref>: the ladder ranks by 0–1 error, and the corpus's own documentation prices a false positive at ten times a false negative. Re-thresholding the boosted model at the implied $p > 10/11$ trades a tranche of false positives for extra false negatives at a tenth of the price, and the saving is not marginal: that one threshold move is worth more expected cost than the entire bagging-to-boosting gap (the cost-machinery exercise below runs the arithmetic). A deployment would also weigh what no ladder shows: the forest's trees train independently in parallel and ship an OOB estimate for free, the booster needs its $\nu$–$M$ schedule tuned and monitored, and all four models inherit the drift problem of a 1999 vocabulary. On the ladder's own metric the race has a winner; on a deployment's, the module's honest summary is that averaging, decorrelation, and stagewise bias attack each bought exactly what their formulas said, and the last half-point between them is smaller than the decisions the formulas do not cover.

What stands established: the published race reproduces on our fits in ordering and shape (5.3 / 4.9 / 4.9, XGBoost 4.5, against the book's 5.4 / 4.88 / 4.5); bagging's plateau is the $\rho\sigma^2$ floor and the forest's 0.4-point win rides on measured decorrelation with weaker trees; boosting's further gain is bias repair, visible as reach into the bins where bagged trees agree on their errors; OOB tracks held-out error to within a fraction of a point; and of the ladder's fine structure, only the tree-to-ensemble chasm and the mechanisms themselves are settled at this test-set size. <Ref lesson="boosting-lab">The lab that follows</Ref> rebuilds the winning machine from raw numpy, and the exercise below that boosts past a zero-ish training error at benchmark scale hands its unexplained plot to <Ref lesson="margin-bounds">module 9</Ref>.

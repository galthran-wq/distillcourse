Screen 5,000 noise features against the labels, keep the best 100, then cross-validate: the protocol reports 3% error on a problem whose true error rate is 50% — and versions of this blunder have appeared in top-journal papers.

The evaluation module has built its discipline in three layers: <Ref lesson="classification-metrics">the metrics lesson</Ref> fixed what each number answers, <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap">the resampling lesson</Ref> built the protocols that estimate those numbers without optimism, and <Ref lesson="imbalance-leakage">the leakage lesson</Ref> with <Ref lesson="feature-engineering">the features lesson</Ref> established the pipeline law: every data-dependent step is part of the model and runs inside the fold.

What stands open is scale: the law has so far been violated only in miniature, in closing exercises that read 20% and 24% on coin-flip labels. This lesson is the module's application — the full-size demonstration the ESL authors published after watching the blunder reach print repeatedly, reproduced with our own code and stated seeds.

The route: the construction that makes 50% the only honest answer; the reproduction; the mechanism, dissected until the bogus number is predictable to two decimals; the fix and its one legitimate exception; the audit that converts the pure case into a test for the disguised ones.

## Pure noise referees the protocol

The experiment's design solves a measurement problem: to weigh a protocol, the true error must be known exactly, and no real dataset supplies that.

<Task>
Closed book, from the module so far. (1) From <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap">the resampling lesson</Ref>: a cross-validated score estimates the error of the whole procedure, so every fitted step must run inside the folds. (2) From <Ref lesson="feature-engineering">the features lesson</Ref>: selection is fitting; a correlation filter reads the labels, and its output (which columns exist) is a function of them. (3) From <Ref lesson="imbalance-leakage">the leakage lesson</Ref>: a transform fitted on all rows is the fourth leakage carrier, and its audit question is what the step was computed from.
</Task>

The construction, from [ESL §7.10.2](https://hastie.su.domains/ElemStatLearn/): $N = 50$ samples in two classes of 25, and $p = 5{,}000$ features, each an independent standard Gaussian drawn without reference to the labels. Independence makes the conditional class probability $\tfrac12$ at every point of feature space, so the Bayes error (the floor <Ref lesson="statistical-learning">the opening lesson</Ref> proved no classifier beats) is 50%, and every classifier's true error is exactly 50%. The dataset is a referee: any protocol reporting materially less than 50% is measuring its own leak, in percentage points, with the sign known in advance.

The recipe under test is ESL's "typical strategy" for genomic-scale problems, three steps long: (1) screen the predictors, keeping a subset with strong univariate correlation with the class labels; (2) build a classifier on the survivors; (3) cross-validate to tune and to estimate the error. Each step is standard. With a top-100 correlation screen and a 1-nearest-neighbor classifier, the recipe's 5-fold CV reported an **average error of 3%** over 50 simulations of the pure-noise problem.

## The reproduction: 5% claimed, 50% true

A reproduction is worth exactly as much as its protocol statement. Ours, for each seed 0–9: draw $X \in \mathbb{R}^{50 \times 5000}$ standard normal from `numpy.default_rng(seed)`, fix labels at $+1$ for the first 25 rows and $-1$ for the rest; screen by keeping the 100 features with the largest correlation with the label vector; assign rows to five folds of 10 by a random permutation; classify with 1-NN under Euclidean distance on the kept columns (<Ref lesson="knn-curse">the nearest-neighbor lesson's</Ref> memorizer, chosen deliberately: no model converts contamination into agreement faster).

The wrong way screens once, on all 50 rows, before the folds exist; the right way re-screens inside each fold, on its 40 training rows. Both share the seed and the fold assignment.

The results: wrong-way CV errors of 6, 0, 10, 4, 8, 6, 4, 4, 6, 4 percent across the ten seeds, **mean 5.2% ± 0.9**, against right-way errors averaging **50.4% ± 3.1**, scattered around the truth exactly as an honest estimate from 50 samples should be.

## The mechanism: the survivors remember every fold

A 45-point error deserves a mechanism precise enough to predict it, and the screening step supplies one in two moves: an order-statistics move that manufactures strong-looking features, and an exchangeability move that explains why holding out a fold removes none of their false strength.

### Where the survivors' strength comes from

Start with one feature. Its sample correlation $r$ with 50 balanced labels is, under independence, approximately Gaussian with mean 0 and standard deviation $1/\sqrt{N} \approx 0.14$. One such feature is unremarkable; the screen ranks 5,000 of them and keeps the extreme tail.

<Example title="predicting the screening histogram">
Keeping $k = 100$ of $p = 5{,}000$ keeps the fraction $\alpha = 0.02$, so the survival threshold is the upper-$\alpha$ Gaussian quantile scaled by the sd: $\Phi^{-1}(0.98) \cdot 0.141 = 2.05 \times 0.141 = 0.29$. The survivors' mean is the conditional mean beyond that quantile, $\frac{\varphi(2.05)}{0.02} \times 0.141 = 2.42 \times 0.141 = 0.34$, and the largest of the 5,000 sits near $\sqrt{2\ln p} \times 0.141 \approx 0.58$. Measured at seed 0: threshold 0.286, survivor mean 0.339, maximum 0.536. The first two predictions land within 0.005; the crude extreme-value bound overshoots the maximum as it should.

Each survivor, inspected alone, would pass a naive significance test: $r = 0.34$ at $N = 50$ gives $t = r\sqrt{N-2}/\sqrt{1-r^2} \approx 2.5$, nominal $p \approx 0.02$. The strength is real arithmetic and false evidence: the expected best of 5,000 tries, not a property of any feature.
</Example>

</Solution>

### Why holding out a fold removes nothing

The screen used all 50 labels; cross-validation then holds 10 of them out and treats them as fresh. They are not, and the failure is quantitative. Write the screening score of feature $j$ as the label-weighted average $s_j = \tfrac{1}{50}\sum_i x_{ij}y_i$ (with standardized features this is the correlation, up to vanishing centering terms). The sum splits over the five folds:

$$
s_j \;=\; \frac{1}{5}\sum_{k=1}^{5} s_j^{(k)}, \qquad s_j^{(k)} = \frac{1}{10}\sum_{i \in F_k} x_{ij}\,y_i ,
$$

and the five fold scores $s_j^{(k)}$ are exchangeable given their average. Exchangeability forces $\mathbb{E}\bigl[s_j^{(k)} \mid s_j\bigr] = s_j$ for every fold $k$, including the fold about to be held out. A survivor selected for $s_j \approx 0.34$ is therefore *expected* to score about 0.34 on the validation fold too: the split attenuates the leak by exactly nothing. Under the right way the selection reads only the other 40 rows, $s_j^{(k)}$ is independent of it, and the held-out score has mean 0.

The consequence, stated in the resampling lesson's terms: honest CV grades a fitted rule on data that is, conditional on the rule, a fresh draw from the population. Here the rule includes the kept feature set, and conditional on that set the validation fold's labels are tilted toward agreement with the kept features, agreement a 1-NN classifier converts straight into matching neighbors. The fold is disjoint in rows and contaminated in distribution.

<KeyFact>
After full-data screening, each held-out fold's labels correlate about 0.3 with every kept feature, on a problem whose population correlations are all zero — and no fold geometry touches this. The exchangeability identity holds for folds of any size, so 10-fold, leave-one-out, stratification, and repeated CV all inherit the same tilt; the only variable with any purchase is whether the fold's labels entered the selection.
</KeyFact>

### The protocol is innocent

The blunder invites a wrong general lesson, and ESL records it verbatim: a scientist at a proteomics lab meeting argued that cross-validation must fail in high dimensions *even done right* — a predictor fitted to the whole training set splits the data well, the argument runs, so it will also split any four-fifths of it well, and its CV error will come out small.

The argument is false, and the step that fails is identifiable: it grades a *fixed* predictor, but honest cross-validation refits the entire procedure per fold, search included. The scientist's argument is an accurate description of the wrong way (fit once, then rotate folds around the frozen result), mistaken for a description of cross-validation.

ESL's companion simulation (§7.10.3) settles it: $N = 20$ samples, $p = 500$ noise features, the classifier a best single-split stump. The best stump chosen on each fold's 16 training samples carries no information about the held-out 4, its split point re-estimated on 16 samples misclassifies half of them, and honest 5-fold CV averages 50%, with the wide per-dataset variability an $N = 20$ estimate must carry.

## The fix is the position of the step

The mechanism clears the individual parts: screening is a legitimate technique, 1-NN a legitimate classifier, 5-fold CV a legitimate protocol. ESL's corrected recipe changes only the order: divide the samples into folds *first*; then, for each fold, screen on the other four folds, build the classifier on the same four, and score the held-out one. Selection moved inside the fold is what the reproduction ran as "the right way," and it reads 50%.

### Selection is part of the model

The general rule is the module's pipeline law with its scope stated fully: **every data-dependent choice is part of the model** — not only fitted transforms like scalers and encoders, but searches: which features exist, which hyperparameter won, which model family was kept. Cross-validation is honest about exactly the procedure that runs inside its folds, so the procedure inside the folds must be the whole procedure.

This is the shape <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap">the resampling lesson</Ref> named nested cross-validation (the outer loop measures, every selection lives in the inner one), met here at the feature-selection stage. When a knob like the screen size $k$ remains to be tuned, honesty demands the figure's inset: an inner CV on each round's 40 rows picks $k$, and the outer fold grades a choice it never took part in. The final exercise measures the optimism of skipping that level.

### The leak scales with the pool

How much the step's position matters is set by the pool it selects from. Rerunning both protocols at three pool sizes turns the worked example's arithmetic into a dose curve: the wrong way reads **4.3%** at $(p, k) = (5{,}000, 100)$, **34.7%** at $(500, 100)$, and **46.3%** at $(50, 10)$, while the right way stays within a few points of 50% at every setting. A shallower tail makes weaker survivors, and fewer survivors accumulate less false agreement in the 1-NN distance.

### The one exception: steps that read no labels

ESL attaches one qualification, and it is the discriminating boundary of the whole subject: *unsupervised* screening may run before the split. Keeping the 1,000 highest-variance of the 5,000 features on all 50 samples is acceptable, because a step that never reads the labels cannot hand its survivors any label agreement for the folds to mistake for skill.

The audit question is what a step *reads*, not what it computes: label-reading selection outside the folds rewrote the reported error by 45 points, while row-reading standardization outside the folds moved it by half a percent in <Ref lesson="feature-engineering">the features lesson's</Ref> closing measurement.

<Ref lesson="learning-theory">Module 9</Ref> will restate this section as a theorem: concentration bounds hold for a hypothesis fixed before the sample is drawn, and anything chosen by looking at the sample needs a uniform guarantee over everything that could have been chosen; the wrong way is what spending a fixed-hypothesis guarantee on a data-chosen hypothesis looks like at $N = 50$.

<KeyFact>
The exception has a boundary of its own: a variance screen on all 50 samples still *reads the validation rows*, and the leakage lesson's row-reading audit flags it. It is tolerated because CV's verdict is label agreement, which a step that never saw a label cannot tilt — a concession measured in tenths of a percent, withdrawn the moment the step's inputs include $y$, as a supervised imputer's do.
</KeyFact>

## The audit: five disguises of one leak

The pure case is naked: nobody screens 5,000 features on all their data without noticing that a step ran. This is where the artifact departs from practice: real pipelines commit the same leak through steps with innocent names, and the audit consists of mapping each onto the pure case.

<Figure
  src="./figures/disguise-map.svg"
  alt="A generic pipeline of raw table, fitted step, fold split, and fit-plus-score, with an amber arrow showing the scored rows feeding the fitted step; below, five occupants of the fitted-step slot: a scaler's parameters, an imputer's fill values, a feature screen's kept set, hyperparameters tuned against the test set across papers, and a benchmark's surviving entries, each labeled with what it reads"
  caption="One slot, five occupants. The audit question is constant: what did this step read, and was it recomputed inside every fold?"
/>

**A scaler's $\mu$ and $\sigma$, fit on all rows.** Same shape, mildest dose: the step reads test rows but no labels, and <Ref lesson="feature-engineering">the features lesson</Ref> measured the damage at half a percent of MSE against the screen's 30 points of error rate. It stays inside the fold because the discipline is cheap and the exceptions (near-duplicate rows, tiny $N$) are not announced in advance.

**An imputer's fill values, learned from all rows.** Identical analysis, until the imputation model regresses each feature on the others and the response sneaks into the imputer's inputs, at which point the step reads labels and the dose jumps categories.

**A feature screen at genomic scale.** The pure case in its native habitat, and the reason the ESL authors were reading such papers at all: tens of thousands of expression measurements, dozens of tissue samples, and a screening step that touches all of them before any validation begins.

**Hyperparameters tuned against the test set, paper after paper.** No single author leaks: each evaluates a frozen model once. The *field* runs the wrong way, with the community as the outer loop: the benchmark's fixed test set plays the role of the 50 labels, and the literature's accepted numbers are the maximum of many measurements on it. The KDDCUP99 fragility numbers from <Ref lesson="imbalance-leakage">the leakage lesson</Ref> (a published 6.2% becoming 18% when the training rows were reshuffled) show what such a maximum is made of.

**A benchmark's surviving entries.** The same failure from the leaderboard's side: selection among thousands of submissions, graded on one test set, is a screen with the test labels in the loop; what survives is partly the submission that fit the test set's noise best.

### The published record

The damage this lesson reproduces is not hypothetical, and one sentence from the source states its extent: "we have seen this blunder committed many times in published papers in top rank journals" (ESL §7.10.2). The detailed audit the authors point to is [Ambroise and McLachlan (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.102102699), which re-analyzed the flagship cancer-microarray datasets of the era (62 colon tissue samples, 72 leukemia samples) and showed that published gene-signature classifiers whose selection had run outside the resampling carried near-zero reported error rates that rose to roughly 15% and 5% under external validation. The pattern outlived microarrays: the reproducibility survey <Ref lesson="imbalance-leakage">the leakage lesson</Ref> cited traces leakage-driven failures through hundreds of papers across 17 fields, with selection outside the evaluation loop among the recurring types.

What stands established: the module's pipeline law now has a measured worst case, a reported 3–5% on a true 50%, with a mechanism that predicts both the survivors' false strength (order statistics of $p$ correlations) and its survival of the split (fold exchangeability), a fix that is a change of position rather than of technique, and an audit reducible to one question asked of every step. <Ref lesson="validation-lab">The module's lab</Ref> builds the harness that makes the right way the path of least resistance: its checkpoints include both pipelines from this lesson, and watching its report change from 3% to 50% is the harness's own acceptance test. <Ref lesson="learning-theory">Module 9</Ref> then proves the principle the fix instantiates: guarantees require the hypothesis fixed before the data.

(a) Honest protocol: $S = S(X, y_{-k})$ does not read $y_{F_k}$, so conditional on $(X, S)$ and the fitted classifier, the validation labels keep their unconditional law — under the null, fair coins independent of $X_S$'s validation rows. (b) Wrong way: $S$ reads all of $y$. Conditioning on the realized selection, Bayes' rule gives

$$
\Pr\bigl(y_{F_k} \mid X, S = s\bigr) \;=\; \frac{\Pr\bigl(S = s \mid X, y_{F_k}, y_{-k}\bigr)\,\Pr(y_{F_k})}{\Pr(S = s \mid X, y_{-k})},

and the likelihood factor is not constant in $y_{F_k}$: the screen keeps feature $j$ when its total score $\tfrac15\sum_m s_j^{(m)}$ is extreme, and that total contains the fold-$k$ term $s_j^{(k)}(y_{F_k})$. (c) The classifier was trained, on the other folds, to predict in exactly the kept features' direction, since the training folds are tilted the same way by the same conditioning. Predictor and validation labels therefore agree more often than chance, and the fold error's conditional expectation drops below $\tfrac12$: bias strictly downward, by an amount growing with the tilt. The sketch is the general template: any evaluation is biased exactly when the conditional law of the evaluation labels, given everything fitted, differs from the law of future data — the formalization of "the fold is no longer exchangeable with the future."

What removes it is an outer loop that never took part in the choice: nested cross-validation, exactly as <Ref lesson="cross-validation-bootstrap">the resampling lesson</Ref> prescribed for tuned models. Building that fully nested harness, and verifying it reports 50% on this problem while the shortcuts report 46% and 3%, is <Ref lesson="validation-lab">the lab that closes this module</Ref>.

A complex exponential packages sinusoidal rotation into one mode whose magnitude and angle can be propagated algebraically.

<Ref lesson="perching-glider-system-identification">Longitudinal System Identification from Skywalker X8 UAV Flight Tests</Ref> closed the time-domain module with the fitted homogeneous roots $0.8710731\pm j0.2640202$. Their radius said decay, but their angle was left as a handoff: we now need a representation that carries decay and rotation without expanding a second-order recurrence sample by sample.

Start with the root in the upper half-plane. Rectangular-to-polar conversion gives

$$
\rho=\sqrt{0.8710731^2+0.2640202^2}=0.9102060,
\qquad
\omega_0=\operatorname{atan2}(0.2640202,0.8710731)=0.2942962\ \text{rad/sample}.
$$

One multiplication by that root shrinks a complex value by $0.9102060$ and rotates it counterclockwise by $0.2942962$ rad. Repeating the multiplication produces

| $n$ | $\operatorname{Re}\{r^n\}$ | $\operatorname{Im}\{r^n\}$ | $\lvert r^n\rvert$ | angle before wrapping |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 1.0000000 | 0.0000000 | 1.0000000 | 0.0000000 |
| 1 | 0.8710731 | 0.2640202 | 0.9102060 | 0.2942962 |
| 2 | 0.6890617 | 0.4599618 | 0.8284750 | 0.5885925 |
| 3 | 0.4787839 | 0.5825865 | 0.7540829 | 0.8828887 |

The table is already a damped rotation. The rest of the lesson names its coordinates, establishes which parameter choices describe the same discrete sequence, and shows why a real sinusoid needs both directions of rotation.

## One multiplication advances a phasor

### A four-sample orbit before the general form

Set decay aside for a moment and predict the next value after $1,j,-1$. Multiplication by $j=e^{j\pi/2}$ makes one quarter-turn per sample, so

$$
e^{j\pi n/2}=1,\ j,\ -1,\ -j,\ 1,\ldots
\qquad (n=0,1,2,3,4,\ldots).
$$

The real projection is $1,0,-1,0,\ldots$; the imaginary projection is $0,1,0,-1,\ldots$. Either projection vanishes at alternate indices, while the complex magnitude remains one at every index.

<Figure src="./figures/phasor-projections.svg" alt="A unit phasor advances counterclockwise through four quarter-turn positions while separate cosine and sine stem plots show the synchronized samples 1, 0, -1, 0 and 0, 1, 0, -1." caption="The phasor never vanishes: a zero in one projection is carried by its quadrature partner." />

<Definition title="complex exponential">
A discrete-time **complex exponential** or rotating phasor is

$$
z[n]=A e^{j(\omega n+\phi)},\qquad n\in\mathbb Z,
$$

with $A\ge 0$ its magnitude, $\phi$ its angle at $n=0$, and $\omega$ its angle increment in radians/sample. The canonical unit example is $e^{j\pi n/2}$. A damped rotation $A\rho^n e^{j(\omega n+\phi)}$ is a related mode, not a constant-magnitude phasor. At the boundary $A=0$, every sample is zero and phase is undefined. A real cosine alone is a projection of this object, not a second name for the full complex sequence.
</Definition>

Magnitude, phase, and frequency do different jobs. $A$ sets the circle radius, $\phi$ rotates the entire orbit before sampling begins, and $\omega$ advances the angle per integer step. With engineering $j$, positive $\omega$ means counterclockwise rotation. A negative amplitude is redundant because $-A e^{j\theta}=A e^{j(\theta+\pi)}$, so the $A\ge0$ convention keeps magnitude distinct from phase. Throughout this lesson, displayed phase uses the canonical interval $(-\pi,\pi]$; $-\pi$ is displayed as $+\pi$.

<KeyFact>
For $A>0$, the update is $z[n+1]=e^{j\omega}z[n]$: magnitude stays $A$, phase advances by $\omega$ radians/sample, and the initial phase is understood modulo $2\pi$.
</KeyFact>

## Euler's identity exposes the two quadrature coordinates

### Euler turns one rotation into two real streams

The rotating update is compact because Euler's identity converts polar motion into rectangular components:

$$
e^{j\theta}=\cos\theta+j\sin\theta.
$$

Therefore

$$
z[n]
=A\cos(\omega n+\phi)
+jA\sin(\omega n+\phi).
$$

The real, or *in-phase*, coordinate and imaginary, or *quadrature*, coordinate are separated by one quarter-cycle. “Quadrature” here means a $90^\circ$ coordinate relationship, not that either coordinate is intrinsically imaginary hardware. Two real number streams can store or carry the pair.

<Figure name="PhasorCoordinateLedger" alt="The complex number one plus j is decomposed into an amber horizontal coordinate I equals one, a blue vertical coordinate Q equals one, magnitude square root of two, and phase pi over four, beside matching rectangular and polar formulas." caption="Rectangular coordinates are convenient for addition; magnitude and angle are convenient for repeated rotation." />

### Rectangular and polar forms fail differently at zero

For example, $z=1+j$ has rectangular coordinates $(1,1)$ and polar coordinates

$$
|z|=\sqrt2,
\qquad
\arg z=\operatorname{atan2}(1,1)=\frac{\pi}{4}.
$$

Use `atan2(imaginary, real)`, not the one-argument arctangent: $(-1,-1)$ has the same ratio but lies at $-3\pi/4$ under the principal convention. Software may instead report $5\pi/4$; those angles differ by $2\pi$ and identify the same point. At $z=0$, `atan2(0,0)` may return a placeholder value, but the mathematical phase is undefined.

<Aside title="From hertz to radians/sample">
Dimensional angular frequency is $\Omega=2\pi f$ radians/second. Sampling every $T_s$ seconds produces the discrete angle increment

$$
\omega=\Omega T_s=2\pi\frac{f}{f_s}\ \text{rad/sample}.
$$

Thus $1$ kHz sampled at $8$ kHz gives $\omega=\pi/4$ rad/sample, or $1/8$ cycle/sample. A bare $\omega$ does not retain the sample rate: converting it back to hertz requires the declared $f_s$.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Rotation redistributes value between the two coordinates while preserving $\sqrt{I^2+Q^2}$. Therefore any amplitude detector that keeps only $I$ or only $Q$ must have a phase-dependent blind spot.
</KeyFact>

## A real sinusoid is a conjugate pair

### Conjugation cancels the imaginary coordinate

The two coordinates explain a single complex phasor; a real-valued sequence imposes another constraint. Conjugating Euler's identity gives $e^{-j\theta}=\cos\theta-j\sin\theta$. Adding the two directions cancels their imaginary coordinates:

$$
\cos\theta=\frac12e^{j\theta}+\frac12e^{-j\theta}.
$$

Consequently

$$
A\cos(\omega n+\phi)
=\frac{A}{2}e^{j\phi}e^{j\omega n}
+\frac{A}{2}e^{-j\phi}e^{-j\omega n}.
$$

The positive- and negative-frequency coefficients have equal magnitude and conjugate phase. One rotating component alone is complex; the conjugate partner is what makes every sum sample real.

<FigureSequence name="conjugate-pair-assembly" alt="Three frames reveal a positive-frequency half-amplitude phasor, its equal conjugate negative-frequency partner, and their real sum after opposite imaginary coordinates cancel." caption="A real cosine is assembled by two equal conjugate phasors rotating in opposite directions." />

### The engineering phasor is twice one conjugate coefficient

This representation has a normalization choice worth naming. The **engineering phasor** is $P=Ae^{j\phi}$, so $x[n]=\operatorname{Re}\{Pe^{j\omega n}\}$. In the two-sided expansion, the positive-frequency coefficient is $C_+=P/2$ and the negative-frequency coefficient is $C_-=P^*/2=C_+^*$. The phasor $P$ and coefficient $C_+$ are not the same amplitude convention; confusing them creates the common factor-of-two error in spectral software.

There is also a directional ambiguity in the real projection:

$$
\cos(-\omega n+\phi)=\cos(\omega n-\phi).
$$

The full complex sequence distinguishes clockwise from counterclockwise motion; a real cosine by itself does not. That boundary is why real-signal frequency representations later appear in conjugate pairs.

<KeyFact>
At $\omega=0$ and $\omega=\pi$ modulo $2\pi$, positive and negative frequency are the same discrete sequence. The two-member conjugate pair then collapses to one self-conjugate component, and a real signal requires its coefficient to be real.
</KeyFact>

## Integer sampling wraps the frequency axis

The phase parameter was already periodic modulo $2\pi$. Integer sample indices make the frequency parameter periodic too. For any integer $k$,

$$
e^{j(\omega+2\pi k)n}
=e^{j\omega n}e^{j2\pi kn}
=e^{j\omega n}.
$$

Thus $\omega$, $\omega+2\pi$, and $\omega-4\pi$ generate exactly the same complex sequence. The canonical interval $(-\pi,\pi]$ chooses one representative; it does not make the other labels physically different sequences.

This is a sequence-equivalence result, sometimes called a discrete-frequency alias relation. It does **not** yet claim that an analog waveform can be reconstructed from samples. The later <Ref lesson="sampling-aliasing-and-reconstruction">Sampling, Aliasing, and Reconstruction</Ref> lesson will add continuous-time spectra, sample-rate constraints, and the irreversible overlap boundary.

### Frequency periodicity is not time periodicity

Every complex exponential is periodic as a function of its frequency label, but not every one is periodic in sample index. A positive integer $N$ is a period only if

$$
e^{j\omega(n+N)}=e^{j\omega n}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\omega N=2\pi m
$$

for some integer $m$. Therefore $e^{j\omega n}$ is time-periodic exactly when $\omega/(2\pi)$ is rational. If $\omega/(2\pi)=p/N$ in lowest terms, its fundamental period is $N$. The sequence $e^{j\pi n/2}$ has period four; $e^{j\sqrt2 n}$ has no integer period.

DC and Nyquist are special for a real projection. At $\omega=0$, $A\cos(\omega n+\phi)=A\cos\phi$ is constant. At $\omega=\pi$, it is $A\cos\phi(-1)^n$. The sampled sine coordinate vanishes in both cases, so a real-only record cannot separately identify $A$ and $\phi$. A full nonzero complex phasor retains both coordinates. Zero magnitude is stricter: phase is undefined even for the complex representation.

Modulo $2\pi$, DC and Nyquist are also their own negative-frequency partners. The usual pair of two distinct counter-rotating components therefore collapses to one self-conjugate boundary component, whose real-signal coefficient must itself be real.

<KeyFact>
Frequency error is circular: estimates just below $-\pi$ and at $+\pi$ are near neighbors, not nearly $2\pi$ apart. Compare their wrapped difference before judging an estimator or controller.
</KeyFact>

## Quadrature measurement prevents a phase null

### Orthogonal references recover signed coordinates

Away from those self-conjugate boundaries, both quadrature coordinates can be measured independently. Make the measurement contract exact: the lab uses the noise-free record

$$
x[n]=\cos(0.3\pi n+\phi),\qquad n=0,\ldots,19.
$$

Thus $A=1$, $\omega_0=0.3\pi$ rad/sample $=0.15$ cycle/sample, and the $N=20$ samples contain exactly three cycles. On this non-DC, non-Nyquist interval, cosine and sine are orthogonal, each squared reference sums to $N/2$, and the negative-exponent analysis convention gives

$$
I=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\cos(\omega_0n)=\cos\phi,
\qquad
Q=-\frac{2}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\sin(\omega_0n)=\sin\phi.
$$

A sine-only detector uses $\hat A_{\rm sine}=|Q|$. It happens to report one at $\phi=90^\circ$, but it reports zero at $0^\circ$ and $\pm180^\circ$ even though the true amplitude remains one. The I/Q estimate instead uses $P=I+jQ$, $\hat A_{IQ}=\operatorname{hypot}(I,Q)$, and $\hat\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(Q,I)$ mapped to $(-180^\circ,180^\circ]$.

The benchmark fixes $A=1$, so phase is always scoreable for the I/Q pair. In general, phase must not be scored when recovered magnitude is zero. Circular error wraps $\hat\phi-\phi$ into $(-180^\circ,180^\circ]$ before taking its absolute value; ordinary subtraction would report a false error near $360^\circ$ at the branch cut. A recursive phase accumulator likewise should wrap deliberately rather than grow until finite precision erodes its increment.

<KeyFact>
The exact I/Q magnitude is guaranteed by this record's orthogonal integer-cycle interval, not by the two formulas alone. For an off-frequency tone or arbitrary finite window, the reference columns can mix and raw $\operatorname{hypot}(I,Q)$ need not equal amplitude.
</KeyFact>

## Decay changes the radius, not the rotation rule

### Conjugate roots turn one complex mode into a real transient

Return now to the X8 root $r=\rho e^{j\omega_0}$. Integer powers separate its two effects:

$$
r^n=(\rho e^{j\omega_0})^n=\rho^n e^{j\omega_0n}.
$$

The factor $\rho^n$ is a radial envelope; $e^{j\omega_0n}$ is the rotation. A conjugate root supplies the opposite rotation. Combining the two with conjugate coefficients produces a real homogeneous mode

$$
q_h[n]=A\rho^n\cos(\omega_0n+\phi).
$$

For the fitted X8 pair, $\rho=0.9102060$ and $\omega_0=0.2942962$ rad/sample. At the nominal $40$ samples/s, that angle is $0.0468387$ cycle/sample or $1.87355$ Hz. These numbers describe the fitted recurrence's homogeneous mode, not a claim that the aircraft contains an isolated physical oscillator at exactly $1.87355$ Hz.

This bridge needs no transform variable. It only rewrites the recurrence roots already computed in <Ref lesson="difference-equations-and-stability">Difference Equations and Stability</Ref>. Pole-zero geometry, regions of convergence, and the z-transform remain for their own lesson.

We have now established how amplitude sets radius, phase sets the initial angle, and discrete frequency sets the wrapped per-sample rotation, including the zero-magnitude, DC, Nyquist, and modulo-$2\pi$ boundaries. **DTFT and Frequency Response** next asks how an LTI system assigns one exact complex multiplier to each such mode and generalizes those multipliers across the frequency axis.

<Solution>
The engineering phasor is $P=2e^{j\pi/4}$, so its rotating sequence is

z[n]=2e^{j(\pi n/3+\pi/4)},\qquad x[n]=\operatorname{Re}\{z[n]\}.

In the two-sided representation, the positive-frequency coefficient is $C_+=e^{j\pi/4}=P/2$ and $C_-=C_+^*$. Thus

The samples, rounded to six decimals, are

Because adding six to $n$ adds $2\pi$ to the angle, $x[6]=2\cos(2\pi+\pi/4)=\sqrt2=x[0]$. The amplitude is $2$, phase is $\pi/4$ rad modulo $2\pi$, and discrete frequency is $\pi/3$ rad/sample, or $1/6$ cycle/sample.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Probe four boundaries for $x[n]=A\cos(\omega n+\phi)$ and its associated complex phasor: $A=0$, $\omega=0$, $\omega=\pi$, and replacing $\phi$ by $\phi+2\pi$. State exactly which parameters are not identifiable from the real samples and which conclusion changes if both complex coordinates are observed.

<Solution>
At $A=0$, both the real sequence and complex phasor are identically zero. Phase and frequency cannot be inferred; phase is mathematically undefined.

At $\omega=0$, the real sequence is the constant $A\cos\phi$, so real samples do not separate $A$ from $\phi$. At $\omega=\pi$, it is $A\cos\phi(-1)^n$ because $\sin(\pi n)=0$; again the real samples identify only the product $A\cos\phi$. With both complex coordinates and $A>0$, $Ae^{j\phi}$ at DC or $Ae^{j\phi}(-1)^n$ at Nyquist retains magnitude and phase modulo $2\pi$.

Finally, $\phi$ and $\phi+2\pi$ always describe the same samples, real or complex. A software branch chooses a representative angle, not a new signal.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim “changing phase changes a tone's energy over an integer number of periods.” State the interval condition under which the refutation works and identify the DC, Nyquist, and truncated-record exceptions.

<Solution>
An eternal nonzero sinusoid is a power signal with infinite total energy, so the claim must mean energy over a declared finite interval. For $x[n]=A\cos(2\pi pn/N+\phi)$ over $n=0,\ldots,N-1$,

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]^2
=\frac{A^2N}{2}
+\frac{A^2}{2}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\cos(4\pi pn/N+2\phi).
$$

If the interval contains a whole period and $2p\not\equiv0\pmod N$, the second sum is zero, leaving $A^2N/2$, independent of phase. The same holds over any integer number of such complete periods.

At DC or Nyquist, $2p\equiv0\pmod N$ and the real samples reduce to $A\cos\phi$ times a constant or alternating sign, so their energy can depend on phase. A truncated interval that does not complete the cancellation can also have phase-dependent measured energy. The claim is therefore false only with its nondegenerate-frequency and compatible-interval conditions stated.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Starting from Euler's identity, derive the conjugate-pair representation of

$$
x[n]=a\cos(\omega n)+b\sin(\omega n).
$$

Name the engineering phasor $P$ and recover the equivalent $A\cos(\omega n+\phi)$ parameters. Then, for an orthogonal integer-cycle interval at a non-DC, non-Nyquist frequency, relate $P$ to the normalized analysis projections $I=(2/N)\sum x[n]\cos(\omega n)$ and $Q=-(2/N)\sum x[n]\sin(\omega n)$. Include the zero-amplitude boundary.

<Solution>
Using

$$
\cos\theta=\frac{e^{j\theta}+e^{-j\theta}}2,
\qquad
\sin\theta=\frac{e^{j\theta}-e^{-j\theta}}{2j},
$$

gives

$$
x[n]
=\frac{a-jb}{2}e^{j\omega n}
+\frac{a+jb}{2}e^{-j\omega n}.
$$

The coefficients are conjugates because $a$ and $b$ are real. The engineering phasor is $P=a-jb$; the two-sided coefficients are $P/2$ and $P^*/2$. Expanding $A\cos(\omega n+\phi)$ gives $a=A\cos\phi$ and $b=-A\sin\phi$. Therefore

$$
A=\sqrt{a^2+b^2},
\qquad
\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(-b,a)\pmod{2\pi}.
$$

On the declared orthogonal interval, the normalized projections give $I=a$ and $Q=-b$, so $P=I+jQ$, $A=\operatorname{hypot}(I,Q)$, and $\phi=\operatorname{atan2}(Q,I)$. If both coordinates are zero, then $A=0$ and no phase is defined. Without the interval, normalization, frequency-boundary, and sign conventions, those projection equalities need not hold.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Combine $2\cos(\pi n/4)$ and $2\sin(\pi n/4)$ using rectangular I/Q components. Convert the result to one polar phasor, use it to predict $x[37]$, and verify that value against direct trigonometric evaluation to within $10^{-12}$. Explain why the two representations suit different steps.

For $\theta=\pi n/4$,

Rectangular coordinates make addition immediate. The coefficient has magnitude $2\sqrt2$ and phase $-\pi/4$, so

x[n]=2\sqrt2\cos\left(\frac{\pi n}{4}-\frac{\pi}{4}\right).

At $n=37$, the phasor angle is $9\pi$, hence $x[37]=-2\sqrt2$. Directly, $37\pi/4=9\pi+\pi/4$, so

2\cos(37\pi/4)+2\sin(37\pi/4)=-2\sqrt2.

The exact forms agree, so their numerical difference is zero apart from floating-point roundoff and is below $10^{-12}$. Rectangular I/Q is best for component addition; polar form exposes one amplitude and supports a long-index rotation without separately carrying two sums.

Retrieve that representation and specialize it to a causal impulse response with $h[0]=h_0$, $h[1]=h_1$, $h[2]=h_2$, and zero elsewhere. Then pass the eternal input $x[n]=e^{j\omega n}$, defined for every integer $n$, through the resulting three-tap FIR and factor the output into the same exponential times one complex number. <Solution>
The earlier convolution result is

$$
y[n]=\sum_m x[m]h[n-m].

Only $h[0]$, $h[1]$, and $h[2]$ are nonzero, so changing variables to the tap index gives

$$

Substituting the eternal complex exponential gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
y[n]
&=h_0e^{j\omega n}+h_1e^{j\omega(n-1)}+h_2e^{j\omega(n-2)}\\
&=e^{j\omega n}\left(h_0+h_1e^{-j\omega}+h_2e^{-j2\omega}\right).
\end{aligned}

Define

$$
H(e^{j\omega})=h_0+h_1e^{-j\omega}+h_2e^{-j2\omega}.

The output retains the same discrete frequency. $|H(e^{j\omega})|$ scales its amplitude and $\arg H(e^{j\omega})$ shifts its phase.

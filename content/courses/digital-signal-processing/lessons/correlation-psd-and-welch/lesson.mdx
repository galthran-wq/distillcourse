Random power is an ensemble property, so one finite periodogram must be treated as an estimator rather than a deterministic transform result.

## One record is a realization, not an ensemble

The unresolved variability first requires a change in what the sample array represents. A *random process* $x[n]$ is a probability model for a family of random variables indexed by integer sample number. A *realization* is one complete sequence drawn from that model. The 50 arrays in the preceding experiment were 50 realizations from one declared process; none of the individual arrays was the process itself.

### Stationarity is a model condition

At a fixed index $n$, the population mean is $\mu_x[n]=\mathbb{E}\{x[n]\}$. At indices separated by lag $\ell$, their second-order relation is $\mathbb{E}\{x[n+\ell]x^*[n]\}$. Here $^*$ denotes complex conjugation and $\mathbb{E}\{\cdot\}$ averages across the population of possible realizations, not across the samples stored in one file.

<Definition title="wide-sense stationary process">
A process is **wide-sense stationary**, abbreviated WSS, when its mean is constant,

$$
\mathbb{E}\{x[n]\}=\mu_x,
$$

and its second-order product depends on the lag but not on the absolute time,

$$
r_{xx}[\ell]=\mathbb{E}\{x[n+\ell]x^*[n]\}.
$$
</Definition>

The definition is deliberately weaker than requiring every joint probability distribution to remain unchanged under a shift. It is sufficient for the second-order power quantities used here. Core formulas assume zero mean or a known mean has been removed. Otherwise a nonzero mean contributes a spectral line at zero frequency. Removing a sample mean is not algebraically neutral: it introduces additional finite-record bias and must appear in a measurement ledger.

Two boundary examples prevent stationarity from becoming a synonym for “noisy-looking.” Let $x[n]=A$ for every $n$, where $A$ is one zero-mean random variable with finite variance. The process is WSS because $r_{xx}[\ell]=\mathbb{E}\{|A|^2\}$ for every lag, although any realization is a constant line. A deterministic impulse at one fixed index is not WSS: its squared value depends on absolute time. A flat transform magnitude does not convert that impulse into white noise.

</Task>

### A time average needs an additional premise

A finite record can form sample averages, but WSS alone does not make them equal to population expectations. For the constant random process, an arbitrarily long realization reveals only one value of $A$; it does not reveal the ensemble distribution of $A$. Ergodicity or an appropriate mixing condition is the extra modeling bridge that permits long time averages to approach ensemble moments. The estimators below use that bridge as an assumption, not as a consequence of a smooth spectrum.

<KeyFact>
Stationarity says that the target moments do not depend on absolute time. It does not certify that one record contains enough independent evidence to estimate those moments accurately.
</KeyFact>

The target quantity must therefore be defined at the process level before a finite transform can be evaluated as evidence.

## Autocorrelation and PSD describe the same stationary power

Once the process target is fixed, lag products provide a route from stationary power to frequency. Begin with zero-mean, unit-variance white noise. Its samples are uncorrelated at nonzero lag, so

$$
r_{xx}[\ell]=\delta[\ell].
$$

The frequency representation is consequently flat. By contrast, the constant random process has the same correlation value at every lag and concentrates its power at zero frequency. The distinction comes from correlation structure, not from whether one realization appears irregular.

### The population transform pair fixes scaling and units

<Definition title="power spectral density">
For a WSS process with an admissible autocorrelation sequence, its two-sided **power spectral density** is

$$
S_{xx}(e^{j\omega})
=\sum_{\ell=-\infty}^{\infty}r_{xx}[\ell]e^{-j\omega\ell},
\qquad -\pi\le\omega<\pi,
$$

with inverse

$$
r_{xx}[\ell]
=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
S_{xx}(e^{j\omega})e^{j\omega\ell}\,d\omega.
$$

Its values are average power per normalized angular-frequency measure, and
$r_{xx}[0]=(1/2\pi)\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}S_{xx}(e^{j\omega})\,d\omega$.
</Definition>

Under this convention, unit-variance white noise has $S_{xx}(e^{j\omega})=1$. The conjugate symmetry $r_{xx}[-\ell]=r_{xx}^*[\ell]$ makes the PSD real and nonnegative. In normalized frequency $f=\omega/(2\pi)$ cycles/sample, the same pair uses $e^{-j2\pi f\ell}$ and integrates over $-1/2\le f<1/2$ without the factor $1/(2\pi)$. Every numerical result below uses that cycles/sample convention.

### A finite lag average remains an estimate

For a record $x[0],\ldots,x[N-1]$, adopt the biased finite estimate

$$
\widehat r_b[\ell]
=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1-\ell}x[n+\ell]x^*[n],
\qquad 0\le\ell<N,
$$

mirror it by $\widehat r_b[-\ell]=\widehat r_b^*[\ell]$, and set it to zero outside the available lags. “Biased” names the fixed denominator $N$: at large lags fewer products survive, yet the divisor does not shrink. Dividing by $N-\ell$ gives an unbiased alternative for suitable WSS data, but its high-lag variance grows and its transformed estimate need not remain nonnegative.

For a rectangular record, the DTFT of $\widehat r_b[\ell]$ is exactly the periodogram

$$
\widehat I_N(f)=\frac{1}{N}
\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi fn}\right|^2.
$$

This is an equality between two finite-record estimators. It is not an equality between one realization and the ensemble PSD.

<Example title="four alternating samples">
For $x=[1,-1,1,-1]$, the lag-zero product sum is $4$, so $\widehat r_b[0]=1$. Three negative products survive at lag one, giving $\widehat r_b[1]=-3/4$. The remaining positive-lag values are $1/2$ and $-1/4$. The alternating pattern should therefore concentrate the finite estimate near $f=1/2$, or $\omega=\pi$.
</Example>

<Exercise>

<Solution>

$$
\widehat r_b[0]=1,
\quad \widehat r_b[1]=-\frac34,
\quad \widehat r_b[2]=\frac12,

$$

The record is real, so the negative lags mirror these values. Therefore

$$
\widehat S(0)
=1+2\left(-\frac34+\frac12-\frac14\right)=0,
$$

and

$$
\widehat S(\pi)
=1+2\left[\left(-\frac34\right)(-1)
+\frac12+\left(-\frac14\right)(-1)\right]=4.
$$

The finite estimate places the alternating record's power at $\omega=\pi$. It does not prove that a population process generating the record has that PSD.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Autocorrelation has now supplied a computable spectral estimate while preserving the population-versus-sample distinction. The remaining question is whether more samples make that single estimate reliable.

## A longer raw periodogram does not become stable

The exact correlogram identity does not give the raw periodogram small error. Under finite observation, the expected periodogram is the true PSD smoothed by a window-power kernel. A longer rectangular record narrows that kernel, so bias can decrease at a continuous interior frequency. Its pointwise variance follows a different limit.

### Bias and variance answer different questions

For an estimator $\widehat S(f)$ of $S(f)$,

$$
\operatorname{bias}(f)=\mathbb{E}\{\widehat S(f)\}-S(f),
$$

$$
\operatorname{var}(f)
=\mathbb{E}\!\left\{
\left|\widehat S(f)-\mathbb{E}\{\widehat S(f)\}\right|^2
\right\},
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{MSE}(f)=\operatorname{var}(f)+|\operatorname{bias}(f)|^2.
$$

A *consistent estimator* has error that vanishes as the available data grow. Decreasing bias alone is insufficient because variance may remain nonzero.

{/* fig PeriodogramVarianceEnvelope: responsive two-panel fixed-scale seeded white-noise periodograms for short and long records; many thin realizations, one amber realization, blue ensemble target, fixed-frequency mean/SD/CV slices; KaTeX footer shows bias down while variance stays order one */}
<Figure name="PeriodogramVarianceEnvelope" alt="Short and long white-noise periodograms have different frequency detail but retain a similarly broad distribution of power at one fixed frequency." caption="A narrower expectation kernel does not make a raw white-noise periodogram ordinate concentrate around its mean." />

For circular complex Gaussian white noise, a normalized periodogram ordinate is exponentially distributed. Its mean is the PSD value $S$, its variance is $S^2$, and its coefficient of variation is therefore one. The estimator can become nearly unbiased while the relative scatter stays of order one.

For $T$ seeded trials with powers $P_t$, the empirical coefficient of variation in this lesson is

$$
\overline P=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}P_t,
\qquad
s^2=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(P_t-\overline P)^2,
\qquad
\operatorname{CV}=\frac{s}{\overline P}.
$$

The $T-1$ denominator makes every reported empirical CV a sample statistic rather than a population identity.

The frozen study makes this distinction executable. It generates 96 circular complex unit-variance white-noise records with seed `0x5eedc0de`, evaluates the rectangular periodogram at $f=0.25$, and uses prefix lengths $N=256,1024,4096,16384$. The measured CVs are respectively $0.843520$, $0.812286$, $0.869418$, and $1.061033$. The last record is 64 times longer than the first, yet its pointwise CV has not collapsed.

<KeyFact>
Asymptotic unbiasedness is not consistency. For a raw Gaussian-white-noise periodogram ordinate, the standard deviation remains approximately as large as the mean even when a longer record narrows spectral leakage.
</KeyFact>

This failure differs from the interpolation limit in the preceding lesson. Padding changes neither the underlying random evidence nor the deterministic window kernel. Increasing $N$ narrows the deterministic kernel but still leaves one unaveraged random squared magnitude. A stable PSD estimate needs an operation that reduces variance.

## Welch averaging buys stability with shorter, correlated frames

Variance falls when several spectral estimates contribute to one reported value. Welch's method creates those estimates by dividing one stationary record into segments, applying an <Ref lesson="windows-leakage-and-resolution">analysis window whose main-lobe width and noise normalization are declared</Ref>, forming a modified periodogram for each segment, and averaging the results.

### The estimator ledger fixes every scale

Let the complete record contain $N_{\text{total}}$ samples. Use segment length $M$, hop $H$, FFT length $L\ge M$, and starts $mH$ for

$$
K=1+\left\lfloor\frac{N_{\text{total}}-M}{H}\right\rfloor
$$

complete segments. With periodic window $w[n]$ and window energy

$$
U=\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}|w[n]|^2,
$$

the frame transform and its two-sided cycles/sample periodogram are

$$
X_m[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{M-1}w[n]x[n+mH]e^{-j2\pi kn/L},
\qquad
P_m[k]=\frac{|X_m[k]|^2}{U}.
$$

The Welch estimate is the arithmetic average

$$
\widehat S_W[k]=\frac1K\sum_{m=0}^{K-1}P_m[k].
$$

The divisor is $U$, not the coherent sum $\sum_nw[n]$ used to calibrate deterministic tone amplitude. For density per hertz, divide each squared transform by $f_sU$. For a real signal's one-sided per-hertz PSD, double only positive interior bins and leave DC and even-$L$ Nyquist undoubled. No one-sided folding applies to complex or I/Q data. A PSD in decibels uses $10\log_{10}(S/S_{\mathrm{ref}})$ because PSD is a power quantity.

{/* fig WelchAveragingPipeline: left-to-right record, length-M periodic-window frames with hop H, normalized squared transforms, arithmetic average, final PSD; second row contrasts disjoint frames with 50-percent overlap and labels reused samples as correlated evidence; numbered badges bind pipeline to formula */}
<Figure name="WelchAveragingPipeline" alt="Welch estimation windows several segments, squares and normalizes each transform, and averages them; overlapping segments visibly reuse samples and therefore are correlated." caption="Welch averaging reduces variance by combining segment estimates, while segment length fixes the resolution kernel." />

If $K=16$ segment periodograms were independent exponential ordinates with the same mean, their average would have CV approximately $1/\sqrt{16}=0.25$. This is the variance reduction that one raw transform lacks. It is purchased with segment length $M$: the segment window, not $N_{\text{total}}$, now determines main-lobe width. Increasing $L$ can densify the displayed curve but cannot reverse that resolution cost.

### Overlap adds frames faster than evidence

Overlap means adjacent segments reuse samples. Their normalized window inner product at hop $H$ is

$$
\rho_h=
\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{M-1-hH}w[n+hH]w^*[n]}{U},
\qquad 0\le hH<M,
$$

and $\rho_h=0$ when the shifted windows do not overlap. The conjugate is immaterial for the study's real Hann window but keeps the inner product valid for a complex analysis window.

For the study's circular Gaussian one-bin powers, an independent-equivalent count is

$$
K_{\mathrm{eff}}
=\frac{K^2}{K+2\sum_{h\ge1}(K-h)|\rho_h|^2},
\qquad
\operatorname{CV}_{\mathrm{pred}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{K_{\mathrm{eff}}}}.
$$

This correction is exact only under the declared model. It is a useful diagnostic, not a universal formula for every signal or frequency. Empirical trial variability remains the acceptance measurement.

For $N_{\text{total}}=16384$, periodic Hann, and $M=512$, the frozen dependence audit is:

| overlap | hop $H$ | nominal $K$ | $K_{\mathrm{eff}}$ | measured CV |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0% | 512 | 32 | 32.000000 | 0.178210 |
| 50% | 256 | 63 | 59.734114 | 0.140159 |
| 75% | 128 | 125 | 65.213007 | 0.126204 |

At 75-percent overlap the nominal count is almost four times the nonoverlap count, but the effective count only slightly exceeds twice the independent count. Most of the additional frames recycle evidence already present.

<KeyFact>
Report overlap with its hop, nominal frame count, and a dependence audit. “$K$ frames” does not mean “$K$ independent averages” when frames share samples.
</KeyFact>

Welch estimation therefore trades three quantities together: segment width governs spectral resolution, averaging governs variability, and overlap governs how efficiently one record supplies correlated estimates.

## A PSD estimate passes only a joint resolution-and-variability test

The tradeoff becomes actionable only after the spectral claim supplies acceptance thresholds. Its resolution branch evaluates the exact frame-start mean for two analytic tones of amplitude $0.5$ at $0.18$ and $0.22$ cycles/sample plus a unit noise floor; their separation is $0.04$ cycles/sample. Separately, its variability branch generates $T=96$ pure circular complex unit-variance Gaussian-noise records with seed `0x5eedc0de` and measures sample CV at $f=0.3125$. Both branches use $N_{\text{total}}=16384$ and a periodic Hann window, but only the variability branch is a Monte Carlo trial set.

### Smoothness and sharpness can fail separately

A tone-separation verdict requires two distinct local maxima, each within $0.01$ cycles/sample of truth, with an intervening valley at least $1$ dB below the lower peak. ENBW and null-to-null width are diagnostic scales, not substitutes for this peak-and-valley test. The rule is declared only for this equal-tone experiment. CV must be strictly below $0.30$, and rounded display values never decide either verdict.

An auditable stationary PSD report records the process model and mean treatment; record length; segment length; window coefficients and endpoint convention; hop and overlap; FFT length; $U$ normalization; one- or two-sided units; trial seed and count; the frequency where variability is measured; the sample-CV formula; and the resolution rule. The estimator audit is now complete.

Complex exponentials are LTI eigenfunctions, so their gains across frequency form a complete steady-state description.

For a stable LTI system with absolutely summable impulse response, those gains exist at every point on the unit circle. <Ref lesson="complex-sinusoids-and-phasors">Complex Sinusoids and Phasors</Ref> ended by separating amplitude, initial phase, and wrapped rotation frequency, then derived one three-tap complex multiplier. <Ref lesson="impulse-response-and-convolution">Impulse Response and Convolution</Ref> established that the same LTI system acts on arbitrary inputs by $y=x*h$. This lesson joins those results: a finite or absolutely summable sequence becomes a continuous frequency function, and convolution becomes multiplication by the transform of $h[n]$.

The first two calculations stay finite. We will transform the earlier record $[1,2,-1]$, then evaluate a three-tap averager at frequencies that pass, shift, and cancel an eternal mode. Those computations determine the definitions that follow.

## A finite sequence already defines a continuous frequency function

The previous convolution lesson used this zero-extended three-sample sequence:

$$
x[0]=1,\qquad x[1]=2,\qquad x[2]=-1.
$$

### Three samples determine every frequency

Weight sample $n$ by a clockwise unit phasor $e^{-j\omega n}$ and add. Because only three samples are nonzero, the result is the finite expression

$$
X(e^{j\omega})=1+2e^{-j\omega}-e^{-j2\omega}.
$$

It can be evaluated at any real $\omega$, not only at a grid selected by software. Three exact checkpoints are

| $\omega$ (rad/sample) | $X(e^{j\omega})$ | magnitude | principal phase |
|---:|:---:|---:|---:|
| $0$ | $2$ | $2$ | $0$ |
| $\pi/2$ | $2-j2$ | $2\sqrt2$ | $-\pi/4$ |
| $\pi$ | $-2$ | $2$ | $\pi$ |

The finite sum varies continuously between those checkpoints and repeats after $2\pi$ because $e^{-j(\omega+2\pi)n}=e^{-j\omega n}$ for integer $n$.

### Absolute summability makes continuity ordinary

<Definition title="discrete-time Fourier transform">
For an absolutely summable sequence, $\sum_n|x[n]|<\infty$, the **discrete-time Fourier transform** and its inverse are

$$
X(e^{j\omega})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]e^{-j\omega n},
\qquad
x[n]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\omega})e^{j\omega n}\,d\omega.
$$

The analysis sign is negative, the synthesis sign is positive, and the inverse carries $1/(2\pi)$. Under this sufficient convergence condition, $X(e^{j\omega})$ is finite, continuous, and $2\pi$-periodic.
</Definition>

The inverse formula says that $X(e^{j\omega})d\omega/(2\pi)$ supplies the infinitesimal coefficient of the mode $e^{j\omega n}$. A unit impulse is the boundary identity $\delta[n]\leftrightarrow1$; a delay gives $\delta[n-n_0]\leftrightarrow e^{-j\omega n_0}$. A finite sequence is automatically absolutely summable, so no convergence qualification was hidden in the opening calculation.

<Aside title="Ordinary DTFTs do not cover every useful mode">
Absolute summability is sufficient, not necessary. A square-summable sequence $x\in\ell^2$ has a DTFT interpreted as an $L^2$ frequency function, with convergence and inversion understood in the mean-square sense; it need not have a continuous pointwise spectrum. An eternal complex exponential $e^{j\omega_0n}$ lies outside both $\ell^1$ and $\ell^2$, and its defining DTFT series does not converge as an ordinary function. It can instead be represented by a $2\pi$-periodic train of frequency impulses, while its LTI eigenfunction property can be used directly without assigning it an ordinary DTFT.

These cases protect the continuity claim: an ordinary DTFT of an absolutely summable sequence is continuous in $\omega$, an $\ell^2$ DTFT is an equivalence class under mean-square interpretation, and a generalized line spectrum is a distribution rather than a discontinuous ordinary curve.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
If code zero-pads this three-sample record to a length $N\ge3$ and evaluates it at $\omega_k=2\pi k/N$, the resulting unscaled $X[k]$ values are samples of the same DTFT. Increasing $N$ densifies the grid; it does not replace the continuous function or add nonzero samples to $x[n]$.
</KeyFact>

## Three taps assign one complex gain to each rotation

The finite record supplied a signal transform. A second finite calculation supplies the system transform before the general eigenfunction statement.

### The averager closes a phasor triangle

Let the causal three-tap averager have

$$
h[n]=\frac13\bigl(\delta[n]+\delta[n-1]+\delta[n-2]\bigr),
$$

so $y[n]=(x[n]+x[n-1]+x[n-2])/3$. Apply the eternal input $x[n]=e^{j2\pi n/3}$. Factoring out its current sample gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
y[n]
&=\frac13e^{j2\pi n/3}
\left(1+e^{-j2\pi/3}+e^{-j4\pi/3}\right)\\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Three equally spaced unit phasors close to a triangle, so the average cancels this frequency exactly. At $\omega=\pi/3$, the same finite calculation gives

$$
\frac13\left(1+e^{-j\pi/3}+e^{-j2\pi/3}\right)
=\frac23e^{-j\pi/3}.
$$

That mode leaves with two-thirds of its input amplitude and a phase shift of $-\pi/3$.

### The impulse-response transform is the eigenvalue

<Definition title="frequency response">
For an LTI system whose impulse response is absolutely summable, its **frequency response** is the DTFT of that impulse response:

$$
H(e^{j\omega})=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}h[m]e^{-j\omega m}.
$$

The eternal input $e^{j\omega n}$ produces the exact output

$$
y[n]=H(e^{j\omega})e^{j\omega n}.
$$

Thus $H(e^{j\omega})$ is the complex eigenvalue at discrete frequency $\omega$.
</Definition>

For the averager,

$$
H(e^{j\omega})
=\frac{1+e^{-j\omega}+e^{-j2\omega}}{3}
=e^{-j\omega}\frac{1+2\cos\omega}{3}.
$$

The factorization separates a one-sample linear phase from a real amplitude factor. Its exact values are $H(e^{j0})=1$, $H(e^{j2\pi/3})=0$, and $H(e^{j\pi})=1/3$. The phase of $H(e^{j\omega})$ is undefined at the exact null because the zero complex number has no angle.

On a connected band where $H(e^{j\omega})\ne0$, choose a continuous phase branch $\theta_H(\omega)$. For $\omega\ne0$ on that branch, the **phase delay** is

$$
\tau_p(\omega)=-\frac{\theta_H(\omega)}{\omega}
\quad\text{samples}.
$$

It is undefined at a response zero, and its value at $\omega=0$ requires a separately justified limit rather than direct substitution.

The eternal-input boundary matters. If $u[n]e^{j\omega n}$ starts at $n=0$ from zero state, the first two averager outputs contain only partial tap overlap. The eigenfunction formula applies after all three taps overlap and before an input-off gate; those edge samples are transients, not failures of the frequency response. For a stable IIR system, the same distinction holds, but its startup transient generally decays asymptotically rather than ending after a fixed number of taps.

<KeyFact>
At a response null, the only meaningful steady-state claim is zero output magnitude for that mode. Phase shift, phase delay, and group delay at the exact zero are undefined, even if plotting software displays a number there.
</KeyFact>

## The DTFT turns linear convolution into multiplication

The eigenfunction calculation handled one eternal rotation. Fourier synthesis superposes all rotations, while the convolution theorem states the same result directly for complete sequences.

### Absolute convergence licenses the product

<Theorem title="convolution theorem">
If $x[n]$ and $h[n]$ are absolutely summable and $y=x*h$, then

$$
Y(e^{j\omega})=X(e^{j\omega})H(e^{j\omega}).
$$

The goal is to derive this pointwise product from linear convolution. Because $x,h\in\ell^1$, the double series of absolute values is finite. A direct proof substitutes the convolution sum into the DTFT, exchanges the two sums, and uses $r=n-m$ to separate the factors.

<Proof>
Start from the earlier convolution representation and transform the output:

$$
Y(e^{j\omega})
=\sum_n\sum_m x[m]h[n-m]e^{-j\omega n}.
$$

Absolute summability licenses exchanging the sums. With $r=n-m$, so $n=r+m$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
Y(e^{j\omega})
&=\sum_m x[m]e^{-j\omega m}
  \sum_r h[r]e^{-j\omega r}\\
&=X(e^{j\omega})H(e^{j\omega}).
\end{aligned}
$$
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Task>
Identify the one step that could fail without the $\ell^1$ hypotheses. It is the exchange of the $n$- and $m$-sums; the change of variable and factorization that follow are algebraic once absolute convergence licenses that rearrangement.
</Task>

<Figure name="ConvolutionMultiplicationBridge" alt="Aligned time- and frequency-domain rows show linear convolution y[n] as a sum over m and its DTFT Y(e to the j omega) as the pointwise product X(e to the j omega)H(e to the j omega), with the course transform signs and inverse scale." caption="The DTFT diagonalizes linear LTI convolution: a sum across time indices becomes independent multiplication at each continuous frequency." />

This identity is pointwise in the continuous variable $\omega$. It is not yet an FFT algorithm and does not authorize circular convolution on an unpadded finite buffer. The later DFT lesson will state when sampled transform multiplication reproduces linear rather than wrapped convolution.

### A delay is a unit-magnitude phase ramp

The same calculation yields the time-shift property. If $x_d[n]=x[n-n_0]$, changing the summation index gives

$$
X_d(e^{j\omega})=e^{-j\omega n_0}X(e^{j\omega}).
$$

The multiplier has unit magnitude, so an integer delay preserves the magnitude spectrum and adds the phase ramp $-\omega n_0$. At frequencies where $X=0$, the shifted spectrum is still zero and its phase remains undefined.

<KeyFact>
Transform multiplication describes the zero-state LTI response only where the transforms and convolution are defined. It does not erase startup state, finite gates, or a failure of the underlying infinite sums to converge.
</KeyFact>

## Magnitude and phase describe different output changes

Pointwise multiplication now separates into two ledgers. Write, wherever $H(e^{j\omega})\ne0$,

### Real coefficients preserve conjugate pairs

$$
H(e^{j\omega})=|H(e^{j\omega})|e^{j\theta_H(\omega)}.
$$

Magnitude multiplies a component's amplitude; phase adds to its angle. If $h[n]$ is real, then

$$
H(e^{-j\omega})=H^*(e^{j\omega}),
$$

so magnitude is even and phase is odd modulo wrapping wherever it is defined. The conjugate input pair for a real cosine therefore remains a conjugate output pair:

$$
A\cos(\omega_0n+\phi)
\longmapsto
A|H(e^{j\omega_0})|
\cos\!\left(\omega_0n+\phi+\theta_H(\omega_0)\right).
$$

This is an eternal or settled sinusoidal response. A finite record also has onset and offset transients because the gate contributes its own spectral structure and changes which taps overlap.

</Task>

### Equal magnitudes do not preserve alignment

Equal magnitude responses therefore do not determine equal time waveforms. The identity response $H_1(e^{j\omega})=1$ and the three-sample-delay response $H_2(e^{j\omega})=e^{-j3\omega}$ both have unit magnitude at every frequency, yet their outputs are aligned three samples apart. More general phase variation changes the relative alignment of different frequency regions and can reshape a multi-component waveform even when its magnitude spectrum is unchanged.

<KeyFact>
A stable allpass response preserves the total energy of every finite-energy input but need not preserve its indexed sample values. Only the special allpass factor $e^{-j\omega n_0}$ makes every output an exact $n_0$-sample shift; a nonlinear phase response cannot generally be removed by one time alignment.
</KeyFact>

## Group delay converts local phase slope into envelope delay

The time-shift property made a linear phase ramp into an exact whole-signal delay. A narrowband signal needs only a local version of that ramp around its center frequency.

### A local phase line delays a narrowband envelope

Choose a continuous, unwrapped phase $\theta_H(\omega)$ on a band where $H(e^{j\omega})\ne0$. The **group delay** is

$$
\tau_g(\omega)=-\frac{d\theta_H(\omega)}{d\omega}
\quad\text{samples}.
$$

For a pure $n_0$-sample delay, $\theta_H(\omega)=-\omega n_0$ and $\tau_g(\omega)=n_0$. Group delay is not generally the phase delay $-\theta_H(\omega)/\omega$; it is the negative local slope of unwrapped phase.

Suppose a packet is concentrated near $\omega_c$ and the response varies smoothly across its narrow occupied band. The first-order approximation

$$
\theta_H(\omega)
\approx
\theta_H(\omega_c)
-(\omega-\omega_c)\tau_g(\omega_c)
$$

shows that the carrier receives the phase $\theta_H(\omega_c)$ while the packet envelope is delayed approximately $\tau_g(\omega_c)$ samples. If magnitude or group delay changes substantially across the packet bandwidth, one gain and one delay no longer describe the output; the envelope can distort.

### The averager has one-sample delay away from its nulls

For the three-tap averager,

$$
H(e^{j\omega})=e^{-j\omega}\frac{1+2\cos\omega}{3}.
$$

On any open band that does not cross a null or sign change of the real factor, the continuous phase has slope $-1$, so $\tau_g(\omega)=1$ sample. At $\omega=2\pi/3$, $H(e^{j2\pi/3})=0$ and no group delay exists. Differentiating a wrapped principal-phase plot at that jump would manufacture a numerical spike rather than a physical envelope delay.

<Figure name="GroupDelayPackets" alt="Two narrowband packets enter aligned at frequencies omega one and omega two where the local magnitude is one; output envelopes are displaced by five and twenty samples according to separate unwrapped phase slopes, while a response null outside the occupied bands is marked undefined." caption="Equal local gain does not imply equal timing: on a smooth nonzero band, unwrapped phase slope sets a narrowband envelope's delay." />

<KeyFact>
Interpret a measured group-delay curve only after overlaying the input's occupied band. Excursions outside that support cannot delay that packet, while response zeros inside the support must be masked rather than joined by an unwrapped phase curve.
</KeyFact>

The lesson has now established the DTFT pair, frequency response as an LTI eigenvalue, convolution multiplication, real-signal conjugacy, phase-only time shifts, and the narrowband meaning of group delay. **Sampling, Aliasing, and Reconstruction** next maps a continuous-time spectrum onto this periodic discrete-frequency axis and tests when shifted copies remain separable.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
For $h[n]=(1/3)[1,1,1]$ on indices $0,1,2$, compute $H(e^{j\omega})$ at $\omega=0$, $2\pi/3$, and $\pi$. Report magnitude and principal phase where defined, verify the exact null at $2\pi/3$, and check $H(e^{j(\omega+2\pi)})=H(e^{j\omega})$ at one of the nonzero points.

<Solution>
The response is

$$
H(e^{j\omega})=\frac{1+e^{-j\omega}+e^{-j2\omega}}3.
$$

At DC, $H(e^{j0})=1$, so magnitude is $1$ and phase is $0$. At $\omega=2\pi/3$,

$$
1+e^{-j2\pi/3}+e^{-j4\pi/3}=0,
$$

so magnitude is zero and phase is undefined. At $\omega=\pi$,

$$
H(e^{j\pi})=\frac{1-1+1}{3}=\frac13,
$$

so magnitude is $1/3$ and phase is $0$. Replacing $\pi$ by $3\pi$ leaves both exponentials unchanged at integer tap indices, hence $H(e^{j3\pi})=1/3=H(e^{j\pi})$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
An LTI system has $H(e^{j\omega_0})=0$. An engineer reports “gain $0$, phase shift $0$, and group delay $12$ samples at $\omega_0$.” Decide which parts of that report are meaningful for an eternal input $Ae^{j(\omega_0n+\phi)}$, and state what can still be said about the output.

<Solution>
The gain statement is meaningful: $|H(e^{j\omega_0})|=0$, so the steady-state output component is identically zero. Its output amplitude is zero for every $A$ and $\phi$.

The phase-shift statement is not meaningful because $\angle0$ is undefined. The group-delay statement at the exact null is also undefined because no continuous phase exists there to differentiate. A nearby nonzero band may have a defined phase slope, but its limiting value cannot be assigned to the null without an explicit limiting argument. The complete exact-frequency claim is simply that the mode is removed in steady state.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim “two LTI systems with equal magnitude responses produce identical waveforms.” Use $h_1[n]=\delta[n]$ and $h_2[n]=\delta[n-3]$, compute both frequency responses, and apply them to the finite input $x=[1,-1,2]$ beginning at $n=0$.

<Solution>
The responses are

$$
H_1(e^{j\omega})=1,
\qquad
H_2(e^{j\omega})=e^{-j3\omega}.
$$

Both have magnitude one at every frequency, but their phases are $0$ and $-3\omega$ modulo $2\pi$. Therefore

$$
y_1=[1,-1,2]
$$

on indices $0,1,2$, while

$$
y_2=[1,-1,2]
$$

occupies indices $3,4,5$ and is zero before index $3$. The sample values have the same shape and magnitude spectrum but are not the same indexed waveform. Equal magnitude cannot determine delay; more general phase differences can also change relative component alignment and waveform shape.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the conjugate symmetry of the DTFT for a real sequence $x[n]$. From it, identify the parity of the real part, imaginary part, magnitude, and phase, and state the boundary at a transform zero.

<Solution>
For real $x[n]$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
X^*(e^{j\omega})
&=\left(\sum_n x[n]e^{-j\omega n}\right)^*\\
&=\sum_n x[n]e^{j\omega n}\\
&=X(e^{-j\omega}).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus the real part is even in $\omega$, the imaginary part is odd, and the magnitude is even. A continuous unwrapped phase can be chosen odd on paired nonzero bands; principal phase obeys the same relation modulo $2\pi$ and branch wrapping. At a transform zero, phase has no value and no oddness claim applies to that point. At the self-conjugate frequencies $0$ and $\pi$ modulo $2\pi$, a nonzero transform value must be real.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
<Ref lesson="impulse-response-and-convolution">Impulse Response and Convolution</Ref> established zero-extended linear convolution. For the three-tap averager, first use frequency response to predict the settled output to $\cos(\pi n/3)$. Then gate exactly five cycles to indices $0\le n\le29$, compute $y[0]$ through $y[4]$ by time-domain convolution, and identify the first index range on which the settled formula agrees exactly.

<Solution>
At $\omega=\pi/3$,

$$
H(e^{j\pi/3})=\frac23e^{-j\pi/3}.
$$

Therefore the eternal or fully overlapped sinusoidal output is

$$
y_{\rm ss}[n]=\frac23\cos\left(\frac{\pi n}{3}-\frac{\pi}{3}\right).
$$

For the gated input, fix $x[n]=\cos(\pi n/3)$ for $0\le n\le29$ and $x[n]=0$ otherwise. With $y[n]=(x[n]+x[n-1]+x[n-2])/3$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
y[0]&=\frac13,\\
y[1]&=\frac12,\\
y[2]&=\frac13,\\
y[3]&=-\frac13,\\
y[4]&=-\frac23.
\end{aligned}
$$

The steady formula gives $1/3,2/3,1/3,-1/3,-2/3$ at those indices. The accidental agreement at $n=0$ does not erase the incomplete overlap at the opening gate; $n=1$ exposes it. All three taps see the ungated sinusoid for $2\le n\le29$, so that is the first complete interval of exact agreement. Input-off transients follow with $y[30]=0$ and $y[31]=1/6$ before $y[32]=0$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A continuous-time signal has spectrum $X_c(j\Omega)=0$ outside $|\Omega|\le\Omega_B$. Model ideal sampling by

$$
p(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT_s),
\qquad
x_s(t)=x_c(t)p(t),
$$

with $\Omega_s=2\pi/T_s$. Derive the sampled spectrum scale and sketch the three copies centered at $-\Omega_s$, $0$, and $+\Omega_s$, labeling each support interval. This is the single forward handoff.

<Solution>
The impulse train has continuous-time Fourier transform

$$
P(j\Omega)=\frac{2\pi}{T_s}
\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(\Omega-k\Omega_s).
$$

Multiplication in time becomes convolution in angular frequency with factor $1/(2\pi)$, so

$$
\begin{aligned}
X_s(j\Omega)
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,X_c(j\Omega)*P(j\Omega)\\
&=\frac1{T_s}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}
X_c\!\left(j(\Omega-k\Omega_s)\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

The three requested copies have supports

$$
[-\Omega_s-\Omega_B,-\Omega_s+\Omega_B],
\quad
[-\Omega_B,\Omega_B],
\quad
[\Omega_s-\Omega_B,\Omega_s+\Omega_B],
$$

and each is scaled by $1/T_s$. Whether adjacent intervals remain separate or overlap determines whether the original spectrum can be isolated. <Ref lesson="sampling-aliasing-and-reconstruction">Sampling, Aliasing, and Reconstruction</Ref> names that overlap boundary, treats equality carefully, and turns it into an acquisition requirement.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

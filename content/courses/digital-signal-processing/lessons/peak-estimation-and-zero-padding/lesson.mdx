A sampled main lobe can locate one isolated tone between bins, but denser FFT samples do not add observation time or separate unresolved components.

<Ref lesson="windows-leakage-and-resolution">Windows, Leakage, and Resolution</Ref> established that $N$ acquired, windowed samples fix a continuous main-lobe shape and its coherent amplitude calibration. <Ref lesson="fft-factorization-and-layout">FFT Factorization and Machine Layout</Ref> established that an FFT changes the evaluation schedule without changing the declared DFT coefficients. This lesson uses those facts to separate two claims that similar-looking spectra often blur: localization of one isolated peak and physical resolution of multiple components.

The route is the exact zero-padding identity, a local frequency-and-amplitude fit, the fit's window-dependent bias, a phase-controlled failure of the two-bump test, and an acquisition decision that keeps localization distinct from resolution.

## A denser grid samples the same finite-record spectrum

### Padding changes coordinates without changing observation

The first distinction is visible in one fixed record. Let the sample rate be $f_s=1024$ samples/s and acquire $N=64$ samples of the analytic complex tone

$$
x[n]=e^{j2\pi(10.31/64)n},\qquad 0\le n<64.
$$

Its physical frequency is $10.31(f_s/64)=164.96$ Hz. The signal is explicitly complex, so it contains only the displayed positive-frequency exponential; no negative-frequency image from a real cosine enters the comparison. Apply the periodic Hann window from the prerequisite, $w[n]=0.5-0.5\cos(2\pi n/N)$. The 64 acquired samples span $N/f_s=62.5$ ms and produce a Hann null-to-null main-lobe width of $4f_s/N=64$ Hz.

With $L=64$, the raw transform evaluates the lobe every $f_s/L=16$ Hz. Appending $960$ zeros and using $L=1024$ evaluates it every $1$ Hz. The second plot has sixteen times as many coordinates per original-record bin, and its largest sampled value lies much nearer the continuous maximum. It still represents the same 62.5 ms observation and the same 64 Hz main lobe.

</Task>

<FigureSequence name="padding-versus-observation" alt="Three fixed-axis frames show one 64-sample Hann-windowed analytic tone on 64-point and 1024-point grids, followed by a 256-sample observation whose main lobe is four times narrower." caption="Padding makes the same finite-record curve easier to sample; acquiring four times as many informative samples makes the physical lobe four times narrower." />

The identity behind the first two frames contains no approximation. Define the continuous finite-record DTFT

$$
X_w(e^{j\omega})=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]w[n]e^{-j\omega n}.
$$

After appending zeros to length $L\ge N$, the unscaled $L$-point transform is

$$
\begin{aligned}
X_L[k]
&=\sum_{n=0}^{L-1}x_L[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/L}\\
&=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]w[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/L}\\
&=X_w(e^{j2\pi k/L}),\qquad 0\le k<L.
\end{aligned}
$$

The zero-valued terms vanish in the second line. Increasing $L$ changes only the evaluation frequencies

$$
\omega_k=\frac{2\pi k}{L}\ \text{radians/sample},
\qquad
f_k=\frac{k f_s}{L}\ \text{Hz}.
$$

One *original-record bin* is $f_s/N$ Hz. One padded-grid step is $f_s/L=(N/L)(f_s/N)$ Hz, or $N/L$ original-bin units. This vocabulary prevents a padded bin from silently replacing the physical unit set by the acquired record.

<Theorem title="zero padding preserves the finite-record DTFT">
Fix an integer $N\ge1$, a sample rate $f_s>0$, acquired samples $x[n]$ on $0\le n<N$, and a length-$N$ window $w[n]$. For an integer $L\ge N$, define $x_L[n]=x[n]w[n]$ for $0\le n<N$ and $x_L[n]=0$ for $N\le n<L$. Then every length-$L$ DFT coefficient equals the finite-record DTFT sample

$$
X_L[k]=X_w(e^{j2\pi k/L}),\qquad 0\le k<L.
$$

<Proof>
The three-line identity above substitutes the zero extension and removes its zero-valued terms. The remaining sum is exactly the definition of $X_w(e^{j\omega})$ evaluated at $\omega=2\pi k/L$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

**Corollary for the fixed acquisition and window.** With the same $N$, $f_s$, $x[n]$, and $w[n]$, padding leaves the acquired duration $N/f_s$ unchanged. It also leaves the window transform

$$
W(e^{j\omega})=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w[n]e^{-j\omega n}
$$

unchanged, so its main-lobe width in radians/sample and, at the fixed $f_s$, in hertz cannot depend on $L$. Padding reduces coordinate-sampling error by selecting a denser finite subset of the same $X_w(e^{j\omega})$.

### Longer acquisition changes the window kernel

Now acquire $N=256$ informative samples at the same $f_s$, physical tone frequency, and periodic-Hann convention, while keeping $L=1024$. Duration becomes $250$ ms, the display spacing remains $1$ Hz, and the Hann main lobe narrows from $64$ Hz to $16$ Hz. The apparent smoothness of the plot has been held fixed while the evidence changes.

<Definition title="spectral resolution">
*Spectral resolution* is the ability of a finite observation and window to support distinct frequency claims, not merely the spacing of plotted FFT samples.
</Definition>

The canonical resolution change is the longer $N=256$ observation. A structurally different valid change selects a narrower-main-lobe window while accepting its other leakage and calibration costs. The degenerate $N=1$ record is valid data but carries no useful separation evidence. A longer $L$ with the same $N$ is the near-miss: it supplies more coordinates while violating the requirement that resolving evidence change. A proposed resolution improvement is decided by whether the acquired aperture or the signal model changed, not by whether the plotted line became smoother.

## Three local samples estimate one isolated peak

### A concave local fit returns location and height

The unchanged-DTFT result gives a legitimate use for a larger $L$: it places samples close enough to one isolated maximum that a local curve can estimate the maximum between coordinates. The method estimates both location and height, but each result inherits the single-peak model.

Let $k^*$ be the largest sampled point of an interior local maximum. First divide raw magnitudes by the coherent response $C_w=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w[n]$, then express the three neighboring analytic-tone amplitudes in decibels:

$$
\alpha=20\log_{10}\frac{|X_L[k^*-1]|}{C_w},\quad
\beta=20\log_{10}\frac{|X_L[k^*]|}{C_w},\quad
\gamma=20\log_{10}\frac{|X_L[k^*+1]|}{C_w}.
$$

*Parabolic peak interpolation*, also called quadratic peak interpolation, fits $y(r)=a(r-p)^2+b$ through these values at local coordinates $r=-1,0,1$. The dimensionless offset $p$ is measured in padded-grid bins from $k^*$, not in original bins or hertz. Solving the three equations gives

$$
p=\frac12\frac{\alpha-\gamma}{\alpha-2\beta+\gamma},
\qquad
\widehat b=\beta-\frac14(\alpha-\gamma)p.
$$

The frequency and analytic-tone amplitude-magnitude estimates are

$$
\widehat f=(k^*+p)\frac{f_s}{L},
\qquad
\widehat A=10^{\widehat b/20}.
$$

<Figure name="PeakParabolaFit" alt="Three adjacent log-magnitude samples define a concave fitted parabola whose vertex lies p padded bins from the largest sample and supplies an interpolated peak height." caption="The fit is a local model of one isolated peak; its vertex is not valid merely because three numbers are available." />

<Example title="frequency and amplitude from one concave triplet">
Suppose a calibrated analytic-tone spectrum has $\alpha=-6$ dB, $\beta=0$ dB, and $\gamma=-2$ dB at $k^*-1,k^*,k^*+1$. Let $k^*=40$, $L=1024$, and $f_s=4096$ samples/s, so one padded-grid step is $4$ Hz. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
p
&=\frac12\frac{-6-(-2)}{-6-2(0)+(-2)}
=\frac12\frac{-4}{-8}
=\frac14,\\
\widehat f
&=(40+0.25)(4\ \text{Hz})
=161\ \text{Hz},\\
\widehat b
&=0-\frac14[-6-(-2)]\left(\frac14\right)
=0.25\ \text{dB},\\
\widehat A
&=10^{0.25/20}
\approx1.029.
\end{aligned}
$$

The positive correction from $0$ to $0.25$ dB is the fitted height above the largest sampled coordinate. It is not another coherent-gain correction: $C_w$ was already applied before $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ were formed.
</Example>

### Scale, isolation, and curvature decide admissibility

For a real cosine at an interior positive-frequency bin whose positive- and negative-frequency window lobes satisfy the same isolation check, each conjugate spectral line carries half the cosine amplitude. Replace the three normalized magnitudes by $2|X_L[k]|/C_w$ before taking decibels. Omit that factor of two at DC and, for even $L$, Nyquist. This translation is separate from interpolation and cannot be inferred from the fitted height.

Define $D=\alpha-2\beta+\gamma=2a=y''(r)$, the fitted parabola's signed curvature. It must be negative for a concave peak and nonzero for a finite vertex. The selected point must also be an interior local maximum, and a correctly centered three-sample fit normally satisfies $|p|\le1/2$. An endpoint, non-maximum, zero-curvature triplet, or offset outside that branch is rejected rather than forced into a frequency report.

## Padding reduces grid error until the peak model becomes the limit

### Window shape sets deterministic interpolation bias

The local calculation was exact for its fitted parabola, but a practical window lobe is generally not an exact parabola in decibels. The resulting systematic difference between an estimate and the true tone parameter is *bias* from model mismatch, distinct from random scatter across noisy records.

For the frozen $N=64$ analytic tone at original-bin coordinate $q=10.31$, a coarse $L=N$ grid and the three-point dB fit give different errors for different periodic windows. In original-bin units, the rectangular estimate errs by about $-0.1670$, the Hann estimate by $+0.01591$, and the periodic Blackman estimate by $+0.006584$. With $L=4N$, all three absolute errors fall below $0.002$ original bin in the deterministic study. The new samples shorten the interval over which the parabola approximates the unchanged lobe.

<Figure src="./figures/interpolation-error-by-padding.svg" alt="Peak-interpolation error decreases with padding factor for rectangular, periodic Hann, and periodic Blackman windows, while the three curves retain different window-dependent values." caption="Denser sampling shrinks the local approximation interval; it does not make distinct window lobes share one interpolation error law." />

Two symmetry cases have zero frequency error under the declared estimator. A bin-centered tone has equal side samples and $p=0$. A tone exactly halfway between grid coordinates gives tied central choices; consistent indexing produces $p=+1/2$ from the lower center or $p=-1/2$ from the upper center, which identifies the same frequency. These zeros do not make the estimator generally unbiased between them.

An ideal Gaussian window transform is exceptional because its log magnitude is exactly quadratic before practical truncation. Rectangular, Hann, and Blackman lobes are not exact dB parabolas. Their local Taylor approximations improve as the evaluation samples approach the maximum, which is why noiseless isolated-tone error can approach zero as $L/N$ grows. A nonzero asymptotic floor would require another declared effect such as a neighboring tone, noise, quantization, or a different peak model; padding alone does not create such a floor.

### A denser grid cannot repair a violated peak model

The improvement stops addressing the measurement problem when the single-peak assumption fails. A nearby component contributes a complex window response at the same three coordinates. Low signal-to-noise ratio can also make the selected largest point follow a noise fluctuation rather than the tone lobe. In both cases, a very large $L$ supplies a finely sampled version of the contaminated peak. It does not restore the isolated-lobe model.

Zero amplitude is a sharper boundary than low amplitude. All log magnitudes are undefined, phase is undefined, and frequency cannot be inferred from an absent line. Three coincident complex tones form one phasor sum at one frequency; they can reinforce, partially cancel, or cancel exactly. No peak interpolator can recover the number of coincident components from that sum.

## Relative phase can manufacture two misleading maxima

### Fixed frequencies can produce a phase-controlled peak trap

The bias boundary becomes decisive when two window lobes overlap. Their complex values add before magnitude is taken, so relative phase can change the number and location of visible maxima without changing either physical tone frequency.

Use the study's explicit analytic complex pair at $f_s=1024$ samples/s:

$$
x_\phi[n]
=e^{j2\pi(10/64)n}
+e^{j(2\pi(10.5/64)n+\phi)}.
$$

The true frequencies are $160$ Hz and $168$ Hz. This study freezes $N_0=64$ as its reference aperture, so one reference-bin unit is $f_s/N_0=16$ Hz and the two tones are separated by $0.5$ reference bin. Under a rectangular $N=64$ record, the in-phase case $\phi=0$ has one qualifying maximum at $q=10.25$. Changing only the relative phase to $\phi=\pi/2$ creates two deep-looking bumps at approximately $q=9.5828$ and $q=10.9172$. Assigning them in order to the two true tones gives errors of about $-0.4172$ and $+0.4172$ in the fixed $N_0$ reference-bin unit. The display looks more separated while both location estimates move away from truth.

## Acquisition buys resolution while interpolation buys localization

### A localization claim may spend computation

The preceding results make the engineering choice depend on the claim. One isolated off-bin tone in a fixed record is a localization problem. Two components merged by that record's window are a resolution problem. The first can use denser evaluation and a local estimator; the second needs more informative observation, a changed window with audited tradeoffs, or a stronger explicitly tested signal model.

{/* fig LocalizationResolutionLedger: responsive two-row decision ledger; columns claim, held evidence, action, changed quantity, failure check; row 1 isolated localization uses larger L and interpolation while N and lobe stay fixed; row 2 component separation uses longer N or changed window/model and audits phase-stable truth locations; amber action and blue evidence */}
<Figure name="LocalizationResolutionLedger" alt="A decision ledger sends one isolated fixed-record peak to denser FFT sampling and interpolation, but sends unresolved components to longer acquisition or a changed window or model." caption="The permitted action follows the claim: improve coordinates for localization, improve evidence for resolution." />

For the $N=64$ Hann record, increasing $L$ from $64$ to $1024$ changes the grid from $16$ Hz to $1$ Hz and reduces the largest-bin coordinate bound from one-half to one-thirty-second of an original bin. The record remains $62.5$ ms and the main lobe remains $64$ Hz wide. This is a correct and efficient use of a longer FFT when the tone is isolated and the interpolation checks pass.

### A resolution claim must change evidence

For two tones inside that same lobe, the larger $L$ gives more samples of their coherent sum. Acquiring $N=256$ samples changes duration to $250$ ms and narrows the same-family Hann main lobe to $16$ Hz. A different window could also change main-lobe width, but the preceding lesson requires its sidelobe, coherent-gain, scalloping, and noise-bandwidth costs to be re-audited. A multi-component parametric model can make a stronger claim only by adding assumptions; it is not a consequence of zero padding.

An auditable peak report therefore records $f_s$, informative length $N$, FFT length $L$, window and endpoint convention, forward scale, analytic-versus-real signal convention, coherent response, peak-selection rule, local fit, and rejection checks. A resolution report additionally records the component geometry, a perturbation set such as relative phase, a location or separation tolerance, and the acquisition or model change that supplied new evidence.

The single deterministic move is now established: dense sampling plus a checked local model estimates frequency and amplitude magnitude for one isolated windowed sinusoid, while record duration and window shape bound component separation. Correlation, PSD, and Welch Estimation next treats a different failure: repeated random records do not yield one stable squared-magnitude spectrum merely because each record receives a dense FFT grid.

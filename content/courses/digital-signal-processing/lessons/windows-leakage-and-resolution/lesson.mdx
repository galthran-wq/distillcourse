## Finite observation creates leakage before an FFT is chosen

### An off-grid record cannot join its periodic copy

The DFT grid states where a finite-record transform is sampled; the shape being sampled is already fixed by the observation. Take the complex sinusoid

$$
x[n]=e^{j2\pi(1.5/8)n},\qquad 0\le n<8.
$$

Its phase advances by one and a half cycles across an eight-sample aperture. Repeating the stored block, as the DFT coordinate model does, places the last part of one copy beside the first part of the next. The two copies do not join as one continuous sinusoid because $1.5$ is not an integer bin coordinate. That boundary mismatch is present before an FFT algorithm or display length is selected.

### Every finite weight becomes a spectral kernel

The rectangular observation is itself a weighting: it keeps each stored sample with weight one and assigns zero outside the record. Replacing it with a taper changes the weighting rather than removing finite observation.

<Definition title="analysis window">
An *analysis window* is a finite weighting $w[n]$ applied to a record before spectral analysis. For a declared $N$-sample record,

$$
x_w[n]=x[n]w[n],\qquad 0\le n<N,
$$

and $x_w[n]=0$ outside that support unless another extension is stated.
</Definition>

The rectangular window $w[n]=1$ on $0\le n<N$ is the canonical case. The periodic Hann window introduced below is structurally different because its weights taper toward the support boundary. The degenerate $N=1$ window still meets the definition but cannot distinguish frequency. An infinite exponential weighting is a near-miss: it may define a transform, but it violates the finite-weighting clause. A proposed example is decided by whether its nonzero support and index convention are declared.

For one unit complex tone at frequency $\omega_0$, multiplication gives

$$
x_w[n]=w[n]e^{j\omega_0n},
\qquad
X_w(e^{j\omega})=W(e^{j(\omega-\omega_0)}).
$$

Every measured line therefore has the shape of the shifted window transform. For a general signal, the same result is periodic convolution:

<Theorem title="finite windowing convolves spectra">
Let $x[n]$ have DTFT $X(e^{j\omega})$ and let the finite window $w[n]$ have

$$
W(e^{j\omega})=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w[n]e^{-j\omega n}.
$$

Then $x_w[n]=x[n]w[n]$ has DTFT

$$
X_w(e^{j\omega})=
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}
X(e^{j\theta})W(e^{j(\omega-\theta)})\,d\theta.
$$

<Proof>
The goal is to expose the contribution of every source frequency to one measured frequency. Substitute the inverse DTFT of $x[n]$ into the finite sum for $X_w$ and interchange the finite sum with the integral:

$$
\begin{aligned}
X_w(e^{j\omega})
&=\sum_n w[n]e^{-j\omega n}
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})e^{j\theta n}\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})
\sum_n w[n]e^{-j(\omega-\theta)n}\,d\theta\\
&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}X(e^{j\theta})
W(e^{j(\omega-\theta)})\,d\theta.
\end{aligned}
$$

The argument uses the DTFT multiplication theorem, not sampling. It would fail only if the transforms required a broader generalized interpretation than the declared finite-window case.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Definition title="spectral leakage">
*Spectral leakage* is spreading caused when finite observation convolves a signal spectrum with the window transform. It is distinct from aliasing, which makes different continuous frequencies produce the same samples.
</Definition>

An integer-bin complex sinusoid under a rectangular $N$-point DFT is a boundary case: the DFT samples the shifted Dirichlet kernel at its zeros, so only one coefficient is nonzero. The continuous finite-record DTFT still has sidelobes between those samples. Zero padding exposes them; it does not create them.

<KeyFact>
Changing the sampling front end can prevent aliasing, while changing $w[n]$ can redistribute finite-record leakage; the two failures have different causes and cannot be repaired interchangeably.
</KeyFact>

## Endpoint behavior exchanges a narrow main lobe for lower sidelobes

The convolution model says every tone inherits $W(e^{j\omega})$, so window selection becomes selection of a spectral kernel. The comparison requires one endpoint convention before it requires window names.

This lesson uses *periodic* or DFT-even coefficients on the half-open set $n=0,\ldots,N-1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
w_R[n]&=1,\\
w_H[n]&=0.5-0.5\cos(2\pi n/N),\\
w_M[n]&=0.54-0.46\cos(2\pi n/N),\\
w_B[n]&=0.35875-0.48829\cos(2\pi n/N)
 +0.14128\cos(4\pi n/N)-0.01168\cos(6\pi n/N).
\end{aligned}
$$

The last formula names one particular four-term minimum Blackman–Harris window; the family name alone does not fix coefficients. A symmetric library window normally replaces $N$ in the cosine denominators by $N-1$. Its endpoint samples and finite-$N$ metrics differ, so results from the two conventions cannot be mixed.

### Two lobe measurements answer different questions

Normalize every spectral curve by $|W(1)|=\sum_n w[n]$ and measure frequency in *current-record bin units*, where one bin is $2\pi/N$ radians/sample. The **null-to-null main-lobe width** is the distance between the first exact nulls around zero. The **peak sidelobe level** is the largest magnitude outside that interval, expressed as $20\log_{10}(|W|/|W(1)|)$.

Freeze the finite reference length at $N_{\mathrm{ref}}=128$. Under the periodic definitions, dense direct-DTFT evaluation gives:

| window | null-to-null width (bins) | peak sidelobe at $N_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (dB) | far falloff | coherent gain | scalloping loss at $N_{\mathrm{ref}}$ (dB) | ENBW (bins) |
|:--|--:|--:|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| rectangular | 2 | $-13.26$ | about 6 dB/octave | 1 | 3.92 | 1 |
| Hann | 4 | $-31.47$ | about 18 dB/octave | 0.5 | 1.42 | 1.5 |
| Hamming | 4 | about $-42.62$ | about 6 dB/octave | 0.54 | 1.75 | 1.363 |
| four-term Blackman–Harris | 8 | about $-92.0$ | not used as its ranking metric here | 0.35875 | 0.826 | 2.004 |

The displayed sidelobe and scalloping values are finite-$N_{\mathrm{ref}}$ reference values for these coefficient formulas, not universal properties of names exposed by a library. Main-lobe width, coherent gain, and ENBW take the shown values for these periodic cosine sums at the nondegenerate lengths used here. A 3 dB or 6 dB bandwidth is another legitimate measure, but it must not be called the null-to-null width. Harris's coherent two-line resolution argument uses the 6 dB width because two equal responses add at their crossover.

### Smoothness controls the far field, not every sidelobe

The continuous Hann formula reaches zero with zero first derivative at the support boundary, so the first unmatched derivative is of higher order and its distant sidelobes fall at roughly 18 dB per octave. Hamming is a different window, not a spelling variant: its endpoint level is $0.08$, so truncation to zero leaves a value discontinuity and the distant envelope eventually falls at roughly 6 dB per octave. Hamming's adjusted coefficients nevertheless cancel its first sidelobes more strongly than Hann's, which is why its peak sidelobe is lower even though its far decay is slower.

This distinction separates **peak sidelobe** from **asymptotic falloff**. The former can control a nearby strong interferer; the latter controls sufficiently distant leakage. Endpoint smoothness predicts the tail order, but it does not by itself rank the first sidelobe or the main-lobe width.

The rectangular window retains the narrowest main lobe in the table. From that fact alone one might expect it to resolve every pair best. A strong unequal neighbor breaks the expectation: the rectangle's high sidelobes can set a leakage floor far above the weak line.

<KeyFact>
No scalar window ranking survives both tasks: main-lobe width limits separation of close comparable lines, while sidelobe response limits visibility of a weak line near a strong one.
</KeyFact>

## Coherent gain and scalloping calibrate deterministic tones

Lobe plots normalized to $W(1)$ hide the absolute attenuation applied to a tone. A bin-centered line can have no sampled leakage and still report the wrong amplitude if the weights are not calibrated.

<Example title="a four-sample periodic Hann amplitude audit">
For $N=4$, the periodic Hann window is

$$
w=[0,0.5,1,0.5].
$$

Its sum is $2$. Apply it to the unit complex sinusoid $x[n]=e^{j2\pi n/4}$ and evaluate the matching raw DFT coordinate:

$$
X_w[1]=\sum_{n=0}^{3}w[n]e^{j2\pi n/4}e^{-j2\pi n/4}
=\sum_{n=0}^{3}w[n]=2.
$$

Dividing by the record length alone reports $|X_w[1]|/N=0.5$, a $-6.02$ dB amplitude bias. Dividing by the coherent response $C_w=\sum_nw[n]=2$ recovers the unit complex amplitude exactly.

For a real cosine at an interior bin, the positive-frequency coefficient receives half the real amplitude. Its one-sided peak-amplitude estimator is therefore

$$
\widehat A=\frac{2|X_w[k_0]|}{\sum_nw[n]},
$$

with the factor of two omitted at DC and even-$N$ Nyquist.
</Example>

The example separates two related conventions. The **normalized coherent gain** is

$$
G_c=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w[n],
$$

while the unnormalized coherent response is $C_w=NG_c=\sum_nw[n]$. Harris calls the sum the coherent signal gain and tabulates its value normalized by $N$. This lesson always writes $G_c$ or $C_w$ so the two cannot be confused.

The strategy is to compute the coherent response before treating the DFT value as an amplitude. Here

$$

At the matching coordinate, the sinusoid and analysis kernel cancel, so $V_v[1]=C_v=2$. Dividing only by $N$ gives $2/4=0.5$, again a $-6.02$ dB bias. The complex-tone estimator is $|V_v[1]|/C_v=2/2=1$.

For a real interior-bin cosine, the conjugate frequency pair splits the amplitude. The one-sided estimator is

$$
\widehat A=\frac{2|V_v[1]|}{C_v}.
$$

The transferable step is to determine whether the coefficient represents one complex exponential or one member of a real conjugate pair before applying the coherent correction.
</Solution>

### A half-bin displacement exposes scalloping

Calibration by $C_w$ is exact only at the kernel peak. A tone halfway between two DFT coordinates is sampled at offsets $\pm\pi/N$ from its true peak. Define the positive **scalloping loss**

$$
L_s=-20\log_{10}
\left(
\frac{|W(e^{j\pi/N})|}{|W(1)|}
\right)\ \text{dB}.
$$

The minus sign makes attenuation a positive loss. At the table's $N_{\mathrm{ref}}=128$, the rectangular value is about $3.92$ dB, Hann about $1.424$ dB, and four-term Blackman–Harris about $0.826$ dB. Finite length matters: the four-sample Hann above has exact ratio $(2+\sqrt2)/4$ and loss $1.375$ dB, not the $N=128$ reference value. This metric concerns the sampled location of one isolated tone. It is not the sidelobe level and does not decide whether a neighbor is masked.

## Equivalent noise bandwidth uses squared weights

### White noise adds through squared weights

A deterministic tone adds through $\sum_nw[n]$, whereas independent zero-mean noise samples add in power through $\sum_nw^2[n]$. Dividing every spectrum by coherent gain therefore does not make the noise floors of different windows equal.

Let $q[n]$ be white noise with variance $\sigma^2$. At any raw DFT coordinate,

$$
Q_w[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}q[n]w[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}.
$$

The cross terms vanish in expectation because distinct white-noise samples are uncorrelated. Hence

$$
E\{|Q_w[k]|^2\}=\sigma^2\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w^2[n].
$$

After tone calibration by $C_w=\sum_nw[n]$, the expected coefficient power is

$$
\sigma^2\frac{\sum_nw^2[n]}{(\sum_nw[n])^2}
=\frac{\sigma^2}{N}B_e,
$$

where the dimensionless bandwidth is measured in original DFT bins.

### ENBW turns the squared sum into a calibrated bandwidth

<Definition title="equivalent noise bandwidth">
For a nonzero finite window, its equivalent noise bandwidth is

$$
B_e=N\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w^2[n]}
{\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}w[n]\right)^2}
\quad\text{bins}.
$$

At sample rate $f_s$, the same bandwidth is $B_e f_s/N$ hertz.
</Definition>

For the four-sample periodic Hann, $\sum w=2$ and $\sum w^2=0.25+1+0.25=1.5$. Therefore

$$
B_e=4\frac{1.5}{2^2}=1.5\ \text{bins}.
$$

The rectangle is the canonical one-bin case because both sums equal $N$. A scaled rectangle $w[n]=a\ne0$ is structurally different in amplitude but has the same $B_e=1$ because the scale cancels. The one-sample case also has $B_e=1$, although it offers no useful frequency discrimination. A zero-sum window is a near-miss for this definition because its coherent response is zero and the denominator cannot calibrate a DC-centered tone. A candidate window is admissible here only when its coherent sum is nonzero.

The ENBW result assumes white noise and a two-sided coefficient-power convention. Colored noise is weighted by the detailed shape $|W|^2$, not summarized exactly by one bandwidth. A one-sided PSD in power per hertz also needs the course's endpoint and factor-of-two rules; $B_e$ alone is not a complete PSD normalization.

<KeyFact>
For tone-calibrated white noise, a Hann coefficient collects $1.5$ times the power of a one-bin rectangular reference; that cost is independent of the Hann window's $0.5$ coherent gain.
</KeyFact>

## A weak neighbor turns the metrics into a decision

The five metrics answer separate questions, but a window is selected only against a signal claim. The controlled study uses complex tones so negative-frequency images do not introduce another source of leakage:

$$
x_\phi[n]=e^{j2\pi(20.5/128)n}
+0.01e^{j(2\pi(26/128)n+\phi)}.
$$

The strong line has amplitude one at original-bin coordinate $q=20.5$. The weak line is $20\log_{10}(0.01)=-40$ dB at $q=26$, exactly $5.5$ original bins away. The reference aperture is $N_0=128$ samples. When the acquired length changes among $64$, $128$, and $256$, these physical normalized frequencies stay fixed; the word *original* always means the $1/128$ cycles/sample coordinate system.

### Local leakage is not the headline sidelobe number

For a chosen $N$ and window, evaluate exact DTFT sums on the fixed neighborhood $25.5\le q\le26.5$ at increments of $1/64$ original bin. Normalize by $\sum_nw[n]$. Let $L$ be the maximum strong-only magnitude on this grid and $P_\phi$ the maximum full two-tone magnitude there. The phase margin is

$$
M_\phi=20\log_{10}\frac{P_\phi}{L}.
$$

The audit uses the 16 declared phases $\phi_m=2\pi m/16$, not runtime randomness. A choice passes only if

$$
\min_{0\le m<16}M_{\phi_m}\ge3.000\ \text{dB}
$$

using unrounded values. The result is an all-tested-phase statement for a noiseless analytic two-tone study, not a probability of detection.

For $L=1024$, the spacing is $64/1024=1/16$ original bin. The largest-bin estimator's maximum coordinate error falls to $1/32$ original bin. The tone coordinate $10.3$ lies between $10.25$ and $10.3125$, so the nearest grid point is $10.3125$ and the error is $0.0125$ original bin.

The acquired samples, $N=64$ aperture, periodic Hann coefficients, continuous $X_w(e^{j\omega})$, four-bin null-to-null main-lobe width, about $-31.47$ dB peak sidelobe, $G_c=0.5$, about $1.42$ dB scalloping loss, and $1.5$-bin ENBW all remain unchanged. Padding has only sampled the same curve more densely. A coordinate closer to the maximum improves interpolation of one isolated peak; it neither narrows the curve nor separates components already merged under the 64-sample window. <Ref lesson="peak-estimation-and-zero-padding">Peak Estimation and Zero-Padding Limits</Ref> formalizes that distinction and develops estimators that use the sampled main-lobe shape.

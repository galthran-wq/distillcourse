A model has scored the day's 8,300 payments and the investigators can open 100 of them. Accuracy says nothing useful when 99% of payments are genuine, and the usual summary of a ranking, the area under the ROC curve, averages over thresholds the team will never use. What number should you compute from the ranked list so that a better number means more fraud caught within the hundred?

The day in that question is a real one from the transaction simulator of
<Ref lesson="canonical-datasets" id="the-transaction-simulator-a-world-whose-rules-are-known">the
canonical datasets</Ref>, the generator whose fraud scenarios are known.
A depth-2 decision tree, the one that lesson read against its answer key,
is trained on the week of 25 to 31 July 2018 and scores the week of 8 to
14 August, days 129 to 135 of the simulation. That test week holds 58,264
payments, about 8,300 a day, and 385 frauds on cards the bank had not
already caught: about 55 a day, two thirds of one percent.

<Ref lesson="economics-of-detection" id="why-hit-rate-is-not-a-target">The
economics of detection</Ref> already showed what the team experiences:
it works the top $k$ of a ranking, and the share of those $k$ that turn
out to be fraud, the hit rate, is the number that matters when $k$ is
fixed. What it did not settle is how to score a model before it is
deployed, from a labelled test week, in a way that predicts that hit rate.
The tools a course in classical machine learning hands you for this are
threshold metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1) and the ROC curve with
its area. Each of them answers some question about the scored list, and
none of them asks the team's: how much fraud lands among the hundred cards
opened each day.

## A threshold metric judges a cut nobody chose

The first instinct is to turn the scores into decisions and count
mistakes: alert on every payment whose score is at or above a threshold
$\tau$, typically $0.5$, and report the error rate or the accuracy. The
smallest list on which this can be checked by hand has ten payments.

### The ten-payment toy

Ten payments, two of them fraud, scored by some model. Sorted by score,
the first fraud sits at rank 1 with score 0.90, three genuine payments
follow at 0.45, 0.40, then the second fraud at 0.35, and six genuine
payments trail with 0.20, 0.20, 0.20, 0.10, 0.10 and 0.00. Each threshold
cuts this list in one place.

At $\tau = 1$ nothing is alerted and both frauds are missed: two errors in
ten, an error rate of 0.2. At $\tau = 0.5$ one payment is alerted, the
first fraud, and the second is missed: error 0.1. At $\tau = 0.3$ four
payments are alerted, both frauds and two genuine payments: two errors,
error 0.2 again. The rule that does nothing ties with the rule that
catches every fraud, and both lose to the rule that catches half. That is
the failure <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="accuracy-prices-every-mistake-the-same">accuracy pricing every
mistake the same</Ref> already predicted.

The toy has four genuine payments per fraud. A simulator day has about
8,250 genuine payments for 55 frauds, 150 per fraud, and there the
do-nothing rule makes 55 errors in 8,305 payments: accuracy
$8{,}250/8{,}305 \approx 0.993$. A detector that alerts on a hundred
payments and catches 29 of the 55 frauds, about what the tree used below
achieves, makes 71 false alerts and 26 misses, 97 errors: accuracy
$0.988$. By accuracy, catching half the day's fraud is worse than
catching none.

### Every threshold metric picks its own winner

The fix that comes next is a better threshold metric: one that does not
let the genuine payments drown the frauds. The table below lists, for each
threshold that changes the alerts, the recall (share of frauds alerted),
the precision (share of alerts that are fraud), and two metrics built to
resist imbalance, F1 (the harmonic mean of precision and recall) and the
balanced error rate (the average of the miss rate on frauds and the
false-alarm rate on genuine payments).

| Threshold $\tau$ | Alerts | Recall | Precision | F1 | Balanced error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.90 | 1 | 0.5 | 1.00 | 0.67 | 0.25 |
| 0.45 | 2 | 0.5 | 0.50 | 0.50 | 0.31 |
| 0.40 | 3 | 0.5 | 0.33 | 0.40 | 0.38 |
| 0.35 | 4 | 1.0 | 0.50 | 0.67 | 0.13 |
| 0.20 | 7 | 1.0 | 0.29 | 0.44 | 0.31 |
| 0.10 | 9 | 1.0 | 0.22 | 0.36 | 0.44 |
| 0.00 | 10 | 1.0 | 0.20 | 0.33 | 0.50 |

Read down any column and a different threshold wins. Precision picks
$\tau = 0.9$. The balanced error picks $\tau = 0.35$. F1 ties the two.
None of these metrics is wrong: each scores one cut of the list, and
which cut is best depends on what the cut is for. A threshold metric is
meaningful once the objective has fixed the threshold ("catch as much
fraud as possible while keeping precision above 60%"), and only then.

The fraud team's objective does fix a cut, but not by score. It fixes it
by count: the hundred most suspicious cards, whatever their scores happen
to be. Tomorrow's top hundred may start at 0.8 or at 0.3, depending on the
day. So the model has to be judged as a ranking, by how much fraud it
puts near the top, and the threshold drops out.

## ROC-AUC: a ranking metric averaged over cuts the team never makes

The standard way to judge a ranking without choosing a threshold is the
ROC curve, which you know from classical machine learning: sweep $\tau$
from the top of the list to the bottom and plot, at each cut, the recall
$\mathrm{TPR}$ against the false-positive rate $\mathrm{FPR}$, the share
of genuine payments alerted. Its area, ROC-AUC, is the probability that a
randomly chosen fraud is scored above a randomly chosen genuine payment.
It uses every cut at once. That looks like exactly what is needed.

### Reading AUC off the toy

On the toy, the pair reading is the quickest. There are $2 \times 8 = 16$
(fraud, genuine) pairs. The fraud at rank 1 is above all 8 genuine
payments. The fraud at rank 4 is above the 6 genuine payments at ranks 5
to 10. So 14 of 16 pairs are in the right order and $\mathrm{AUC} =
14/16 = 0.875$.

The same number comes from the curve. Walking down the list, the cuts
give the points $(\mathrm{FPR}, \mathrm{TPR})$ = $(0, 0)$, $(0, 0.5)$,
$(0.125, 0.5)$, $(0.25, 0.5)$, $(0.25, 1)$ and then along the top to
$(1, 1)$. The area under that staircase is $0.25 \times 0.5$ for the
first quarter of the axis plus $0.75 \times 1$ for the rest: $0.125 +
0.75 = 0.875$.

Now move the first fraud from rank 1 to rank 2 and the second from rank 4
to rank 3. The fraud at rank 2 is above 7 genuine payments, the fraud at
rank 3 above 7: $14/16$ again. Equal areas can hold different tops of the
list, as <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection" id="exercises">the three
rankings of ROC area 0.625</Ref> already showed: the area averages over
every cut, and a team works at one.

### How much of the curve a hundred alerts can reach

The test week makes the point quantitative. A day has about 8,250
genuine payments and the team opens 100. Even if every one of the hundred
were genuine, the false-positive rate could not exceed
$100 / 8{,}250 \approx 0.012$. Any point of the ROC curve to the right of
$\mathrm{FPR} = 0.012$ describes a cut that would send more than a
hundred alerts a day, so the team cannot operate there. On the simulator
that is 98.8% of the horizontal axis.

The simulator is generous. A real issuer sees hundreds of thousands to
millions of payments a day: on <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="a-hundred-cases-a-day-the-models-output-is-a-ranking">the Belgian
stream</Ref>, about 400,000 payments and a team of about a hundred cards,
the reachable strip is under 0.1% of the axis wide. ROC-AUC integrates
over the whole axis with equal weight, so 99.9% of what it measures is
behaviour the team will never see.

The two curves in the figure are drawn for illustration, but the
situation is common: curves that cross, one detector strong at the top of
its list, the other better in the middle. On the simulator's test week,
the depth-2 tree has AUC 0.763 and a tree grown to full depth on the same
training week has AUC 0.788. By AUC the deeper tree is better; whether
it is better in the strip a hundred alerts reach, the area cannot say.

The full-depth tree's ROC curve also shows why such curves look strange.
Its leaves are pure on the training data, so it outputs only two scores,
0 and 1. All the payments at one score are tied, so no threshold can
separate them: there is one cut between the two scores, the curve is a single corner
joined to $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$ by straight lines, and most of its area
comes from the straight segment on the right that no team can reach. The
depth-2 tree has four leaves, so four scores and a curve of four corners.
A curve with few corners is not a plotting error; it is a model with few
distinct scores.

### Precision sees the base rate; the ROC axes do not

Why does the ROC curve hide the reachable strip so well? Both of its axes
are rates within one class: recall is a share of the frauds, the
false-positive rate a share of the genuine payments. Neither depends on
how many of each there are, which is the property <Ref
lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="the-same-detector-on-different-streams">the economics of
detection</Ref> stated as "error rates travel between streams". At 55
frauds against 8,250 genuine payments, a false-positive rate of 0.012 is
99 false alerts, almost twice as many as there are frauds to find. On the
ROC axis, 0.012 is indistinguishable from zero.

Precision is the quantity that does see the class sizes. <Ref
lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="bayes-rule-and-why-the-error-rates-cannot-rescue-precision">The
economics of detection</Ref> wrote it through Bayes' rule, from the two
error rates and the base rate; counted on one day it is

$$
\text{precision} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}} ,
$$

with $\mathrm{TP}$ at most 55 on a simulator day and $\mathrm{FP} =
\mathrm{FPR} \times 8{,}250$. A cut that sends $a$ alerts in total
therefore has precision at most $55/a$. Inside the budget, $a \le 100$,
that bound leaves precision free to take any value from 1 down: a ranking
good at the top is near 1 for its first alerts and still around a quarter
at its hundredth. Past the budget the bound falls fast. Beyond 825 alerts,
a false-positive rate of about 0.1, precision is below $55/825 \approx
0.07$, and at the bottom of the list it reaches the fraud share $m/n
\approx 0.0066$, with $m$ frauds among $n$ payments.

### Where the hundred alerts land on the PR plot

Plot precision against recall at every cut: the *precision-recall curve*,
or PR curve. Its horizontal axis is recall, which the budget does not
shrink: a hundred alerts can reach any recall the ranking reaches within
its top hundred, all the way to 1 on a day with 55 frauds. The budget
shows up in the vertical direction instead. A cut of at most a hundred
alerts has $\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP} \le 100$ and $\mathrm{TP} = 55
\times \text{recall}$, so its precision is at least $0.55 \times
\text{recall}$: every cut the team can make lies on or above the line
from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 0.55)$. Detector A's hundredth alert, for instance,
sits near recall 0.4 and precision 0.22, and its shorter cuts climb toward
precision 1 at the left. Every cut beyond the hundred, the 98.8% of the
ROC axis the team cannot use, sits below that line, and the nine tenths
of the ROC axis beyond $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.1$ are squeezed into the band of
precision under 0.07, just above the floor $m/n$. The PR plot gives the
reachable cuts most of its height: it is the zoom of the third frame,
done by arithmetic.

### Dominance survives the change of axes

The zoom does not change which curve is on top. To say that precisely,
"on or above" needs a meaning at a recall where a curve has several
points. Take rankings without tied scores. Recall moves only when a fraud
is reached, so the recalls a ranking reaches are $1/m, 2/m, \dots, 1$. At
recall $j/m$ it has a run of cuts, from the one that has just reached the
$j$-th fraud down to the one just before the next fraud, each with one
more false alert and a lower precision than the one before. On the toy,
recall 0.5 is held by the cuts after ranks 1, 2 and 3, at precision 1,
1/2 and 1/3. The first of
the run is the best point: it has the fewest false alerts, written
$\mathrm{FP}(j)$, the number of genuine payments ranked above the $j$-th
fraud. On the PR plot it is the top of the stack at recall $j/m$; on the
ROC plot it is the corner where the staircase steps up to $j/m$, at
$\mathrm{FPR} = \mathrm{FP}(j)/(n-m)$.

- A's PR curve is *on or above* B's when, at every recall $j/m$, A's best
  precision is at least B's.
- A's ROC curve is on or above B's when, at every false-positive rate,
  A's recall is at least B's. For two staircases that is the same as A
  stepping up to each recall $j/m$ no later than B:
  $\mathrm{FP}_A(j) \le \mathrm{FP}_B(j)$ for every $j$.

<Theorem title="dominance is the same in ROC and PR space">
Fix a test set with $m > 0$ frauds and $n - m > 0$ genuine payments. For
two rankings A and B of it without tied scores, A's ROC curve lies on or
above B's everywhere if and only if A's PR curve lies on or above B's
everywhere.

<Proof>
At recall $j/m$ the best point has $\mathrm{TP} = j$ and
$\mathrm{FP}(j)$ false alerts, so its precision is $j/(j +
\mathrm{FP}(j))$, which strictly decreases as $\mathrm{FP}(j)$ grows.
Hence A's best precision at $j/m$ is at least B's exactly when
$\mathrm{FP}_A(j) \le \mathrm{FP}_B(j)$. Requiring this for every $j$
gives PR dominance on one side and, by the second definition, ROC
dominance on the other.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

So ROC and PR can only disagree about curves that cross, which is the
situation of the figure and of the two trees. When they cross, the two
summaries weight the crossing differently: ROC-AUC weights every
false-positive rate the same, while anything computed from the PR curve
is dominated by the reachable corner, because that is where precision
varies.

## Average precision: the precision at each fraud, averaged

The PR curve is the right picture; the next problem is to summarise it in
one number. The obvious summary is its area, computed the way the ROC
area is, with straight lines between points. The toy shows why that does
not work.

### Walking down the toy's list

Cut the toy after each rank and record recall and precision. After rank 1:
one fraud of two found, one alert, so recall 0.5 and precision 1. After
rank 2 the alert is genuine: recall stays 0.5, precision drops to 1/2.
After rank 3, another genuine: recall 0.5, precision 1/3. After rank 4
the second fraud arrives: recall 1, precision 2/4 = 0.5. Below that only
genuine payments remain, and precision sinks to 2/7, 2/9 and finally
2/10, the fraud share of the whole list.

Two things in that walk matter. First, precision went down and then up
again, 0.5 → 0.33 → 0.5, while recall moved from 0.5 to 1. A PR curve is
not monotone, unlike a ROC curve. Second, each fraud moves recall by the
same step, one over the number of frauds, and every rank between two
frauds adds a point stacked under the last one at the same recall, the
runs of cuts the dominance comparison had to choose among.

A straight line drawn between PR points describes no cut that exists: no
threshold produces the precision halfway along the segment from (0.5,
1/3) to (1, 0.5). And because several points share a recall, the area
depends on which of them the line starts from. On the toy, with precision
1 up to recall 0.5 in both cases, joining (0.5, 1) to (1, 0.5) gives an
area of 0.875; joining (0.5, 1/3) to (1, 0.5) gives 0.708. Neither is a property of the ranking.

### What the team sees at each fraud

A summary that needs no interpolation comes from the walk itself. Each
time the team, working down the list, reaches a fraud, it has a precision
so far: the share of what it has opened that was fraud. Average that
precision over the frauds.

<Example title="average precision of the toy">
The frauds sit at ranks 1 and 4.

**First fraud, rank 1.** One alert opened, one fraud: precision 1.

**Second fraud, rank 4.** Four alerts opened, two frauds: precision 2/4.

**Average.** $(1 + 0.5)/2 = 0.75$.

The same number as a sum over the cuts, weighting each cut's precision
$P_n$ by the recall it added, $R_n - R_{n-1}$: the cuts after ranks 1
and 4 each add recall 0.5, every other cut adds nothing, so the sum is
$0.5 \times 1 + 0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.75$.
</Example>

<Definition title="average precision">
Rank a list of $n$ items containing $m$ frauds, and let $r_1 < r_2 <
\dots < r_m$ be the ranks of the frauds. The *average precision* of the
ranking is
$$
\mathrm{AP} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m} \frac{j}{r_j}
= \sum_{n'} (R_{n'} - R_{n'-1})\,P_{n'} ,
$$
where $j/r_j$ is the precision when the $j$-th fraud is reached, and the
second form runs over the cuts of the list, with $P_{n'}$ and $R_{n'}$ the
precision and recall at cut $n'$.
</Definition>

The two forms are equal because $R_{n'} - R_{n'-1}$ is $1/m$ at the cut
that reaches a fraud and $0$ at every other cut. The second form is the
step-wise area under the PR curve: each step is a rectangle, never a
trapezoid. Libraries that report "area under the PR curve" by the
trapezoid rule compute something else, and on short lists the difference
is large.

The definition assumes every fraud has a rank of its own, $r_1 < r_2 <
\dots$. A model that gives several payments the same score breaks that:
nothing orders the payments inside the tied block, so the frauds in it
have no ranks to put into $j/r_j$. The threshold table already showed
what the block is. The toy's three genuine payments at 0.20 enter
together, which is why the alerts jump from 4 to 7: no threshold opens one
of them without the other two. The same holds for frauds in a block, and
it fixes the convention.

<KeyFact>
**Ties.** A block of tied scores is one cut. Every fraud in the block is
reached at that cut's precision, (frauds down to the end of the block) /
(payments down to the end of the block), and adds $1/m$ times that
precision to the AP. The ROC curve treats the block the same way: one
cut, joined to the previous one by a straight segment, which counts each
tied (fraud, genuine) pair as half a correct pair.
</KeyFact>

A model that gives all ten toy payments one score has a single block,
reached at precision $2/10$; both frauds are reached there, so its AP is
$\tfrac12(0.2 + 0.2) = 0.2$, the fraud share of the list.

The boundaries of AP are read straight off the first form. AP is 1
exactly when $r_j = j$ for every fraud, that is when all frauds come
before all genuine payments. It falls as frauds slide down the list, and a
fraud found late contributes its small precision $j/r_j$. On a simulator
day of 55 frauds, a fraud reached at rank 5,000 is reached at a precision
of at most $55/5{,}000 = 0.011$, and adds a fifty-fifth of that to the
AP.

### Why the first target has only two answers

With frauds at ranks $r_1 < r_2$ among ten payments, the fraud at $r_1$
is above $9 - r_1$ genuine payments and the one at $r_2$ above $10 -
r_2$, so $\mathrm{AUC} = (19 - r_1 - r_2)/16$, and $\mathrm{AP} =
\tfrac12(1/r_1 + 2/r_2)$.

- **AP below 0.5** needs $1/r_1 + 2/r_2 < 1$. A fraud at rank 1 makes the
  first term 1 on its own, so it is ruled out: the top payment must be
  genuine.
- **AUC at least 0.75** needs 12 correct pairs of 16, so $r_1 + r_2 \le
  7$: both frauds high.

Only two orders satisfy both: frauds at 2 and 5 (AP 0.45) and at 3 and 4
(AP 0.42), each with AUC 0.75. Both put two frauds within the top five, a
list a team of five would be glad of, and both score lower AP than the
start, whose second fraud is at the very bottom. AP is ruled by where the
first frauds land, AUC by how many pairs are in order anywhere. For the
second target, frauds at ranks 4 and 7 sit above 5 and 3 genuine
payments, 8 of 16 pairs, the start's AUC, with nothing above the line at
3: the precision of the top 3 sees only the top.

### What a ranking with no information scores

To read an AP you need its floor. A model that gives every payment the
same score is one tied block: by the tie convention every fraud is reached
at the precision of the whole list, $m/n$, so its AP is exactly the fraud
share $m/n$, as the constant toy model's 0.2 showed. A model that orders the list
at random does slightly better on average, by a term of order
$(\ln n)/n$ that comes from the lucky case of a fraud landing at the very
top (the exercise on the floor of average precision derives it); on a test week of 58,264 payments that
term is 0.0002 against a fraud share of 0.0066. In practice, an
uninformative ranking scores the base rate $\pi$.

That floor has two consequences. On the simulator's test week the depth-2
tree's AP of 0.496 is 75 times the floor of about 0.007; the full-depth
tree's 0.309 is 47 times it. AP reverses the AUC order of the two trees,
0.496 against 0.309 where AUC had 0.763 against 0.788. The full-depth
tree has only two scores, so it is two tied blocks. Every fraud it does
not score 1 sits in the block at 0 with nearly every genuine payment, and
the block ends at the bottom of the list, so by the tie convention that
fraud is reached at the precision of the whole list: exactly the base
rate. The depth-2 tree's four scores split the list into four blocks and
reach more of its frauds in the blocks near the top.

The second consequence is that AP, like precision, belongs to the stream
it was measured on. The same comparison was run on real Belgian
e-commerce payments, about one fraud in 400, with five standard models,
each trained on one week of that stream and scored on a later week:
logistic regression, the depth-2 and the full-depth decision tree, a
random forest, and boosted trees (XGBoost). Their APs run from 0.003 to
0.035 against a floor of 0.002, and their PR curves hug the bottom of the
plot.

An AP of 0.035 is seventeen times what random ordering achieves on that
stream; on the simulator's test week it would be five times the floor,
where the two trees reach 47 and 75 times. Two APs can be compared only
on the same test set.

<KeyFact>
AP has a floor equal to the base rate, not 0.5 as AUC does. Read an AP as a
multiple of the fraud share of its test set: 0.035 at one fraud in 400 is
a strong model, 0.035 at one in 150 a weak one.
</KeyFact>

## Precision at k: the metric cut where the team cuts

AP still averages over every fraud in the list, including the ones at
rank 5,000. The team stops at rank 100. A model can raise its AP by
moving frauds from rank 3,000 to rank 1,000, which changes nothing the
team will ever open. The metric that looks only where the
team looks is the hit rate of <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="why-hit-rate-is-not-a-target">the economics of detection</Ref>, the
share of fraud in the top $k$, now computed on a labelled test set.

### One day of the simulator

The budget renews every morning, so the list is cut per day. Pooling the
week into one list of 58,264 and cutting it at the week's budget, seven
days times a hundred, 700 payments, would let a day with many
high-scoring frauds fill most of those 700 and starve a quiet day, which
is not what a team working a hundred a day experiences.

The team ranks cards, not payments, but the labelled test week is a table
of payments, one label per row, and AP and AUC count payments too. Cut on
payments, the daily metric differs from them in one respect only, where
it stops counting, so whatever it shows is the effect of the cut. Moving
from payments to cards is a separate change with separate effects.

<Definition title="precision at k">
For day $t$, rank the day's payments by score and let $A_t$ be the top
$k$. The *precision at $k$* of day $t$ is
$$
P@k(t) = \frac{\#\{\text{frauds in } A_t\}}{k} ,
$$
and over a test period of days $\mathcal{D}$ the reported value is the
mean of the daily values, $P@k = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{t \in
\mathcal{D}} P@k(t)$.
</Definition>

On day 129 the depth-2 tree's top hundred payments hold 26 frauds:
$P@100(129) = 0.26$. Over the week the daily values are 0.26, 0.35, 0.26,
0.33, 0.30, 0.35 and 0.19, with mean 0.291. That number reads directly
in the team's units: about 29 frauds found per hundred payments opened.
A random hundred would hold less than one, since the fraud share is
0.0066.

### Reading a day against its ceiling

Day 129 has only 55 frauds. Even a perfect ranking puts at most 55 of them
into the top hundred, so $P@100(129)$ cannot exceed 0.55. In general, with
$\gamma_t$ frauds on day $t$,

$$
P@k(t) \le \min\Big(1, \frac{\gamma_t}{k}\Big) .
$$

<Figure
  src="./figures/topk-week.svg"
  alt="Seven pairs of bars for days 129 to 135: an outlined bar of each day's frauds (55, 60, 56, 56, 59, 58, 41) and inside it an amber bar of the frauds among the top 100 payments (26, 35, 26, 33, 30, 35, 19); P@100 printed under each day (0.26 to 0.19); a dashed line at 100 marks the budget"
  caption="Every day the outline is below the budget line, so P@100 can never reach 1; the tree finds about half of what is there."
/>

Read against that ceiling, the tree's 0.26 on day 129 is 26 of 55
reachable frauds, close to half; its 0.19 on day 135 is 19 of 41, also
close to half. The low last day is a quiet day, not a worse model. A
mean $P@k$ therefore mixes the model's quality with the calendar's supply
of fraud. That is harmless when two models are compared on the same days,
since the ceiling is the same for both, and misleading when a number from
one month is compared with a number from another.

<KeyFact>
The ceiling splits a mean $P@k$'s distance from 1 into two parts. On the
test week the mean ceiling is $385/700 = 0.55$, so of the depth-2 tree's
gap from 0.29 to 1, about 0.26 is the model's (0.29 up to 0.55) and 0.45
is the calendar's (0.55 up to 1), which no model can close. On a stream
with more than $k$ frauds every day the ceiling is 1 and the whole gap is
the model's.
</KeyFact>

<Aside>
**Fixing recall instead of $k$.** A related metric integrates the PR curve
only up to a fixed recall, usually 0.2: the area of the precision over
the first fifth of the frauds, whose maximum is 0.2. It keeps the
focus on the top of the list without choosing $k$, which helps when the
budget is unknown or differs between teams. It answers a different
question from $P@k$: $P@k$ fixes the number of alerts and asks how many
are fraud; the partial area fixes the share of fraud to be found and asks
at what precision it was found. On a day with many frauds the first fifth
may lie far beyond rank 100, and on a quiet day well inside it, so the
two can disagree.
</Aside>

## Card precision at k: the team calls cards

$P@k$ still counts the wrong things, because its unit is the payment. <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="a-hundred-cases-a-day-the-models-output-is-a-ranking">The layered
system</Ref> established that an investigator calls a cardholder and
settles all the card's recent payments in that one call, and that on
real card data a compromised card produces about three frauds a day. Three high-scoring
frauds on one card are one call and one detection, and in a top-hundred
list of payments they occupy three slots.

### Collapsing a day to cards

The ranking itself needs no repair, because the team already ranks cards:
<Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="ranking-then-cutting-at-the-capacity">the layered system</Ref> sorts
the day's cards by their highest score and hands the top $k$ to the
investigators. What the metric still lacks is a label per card, since the
test week labels payments. The card's label that day is the highest label
among its payments: the card counts as compromised on day $t$ if any of
its payments that day is fraud.

<FigureSequence

<Task>
In the first frame, count the calls the team makes if it works the top
three payments, and how many of its three calls it has left. Then in the
second frame, check which payment of card C sets the card's score.
</Task>

<Definition title="card precision at k">
For day $t$, give each card active that day the maximum score and the
maximum label of its payments that day, rank the cards by score, and let
$C_t$ be the top $k$ cards. The *card precision at $k$* of day $t$ is
$$
CP@k(t) = \frac{\#\{\text{compromised cards in } C_t\}}{k} ,
$$
averaged over the days of the test period like $P@k$.
</Definition>

The payment view in the figure reports $P@3 = 1$ and describes one call
that finds one card. The card view reports $CP@3 = 2/3$: three calls,
two compromised cards found. The second number is what the team gets.
The ceiling carries over with $\gamma_t$ now counting compromised cards:
$CP@k(t) \le \min(1, \gamma_t/k)$.

On the simulator's day 129 the depth-2 tree's top hundred cards hold 24
of the day's 50 compromised cards: $CP@100(129) = 0.24$. Over the week
the mean is 0.286, close to the $P@100$ of 0.291, because a simulated
compromised card rarely makes more than one fraud a day. Both of <Ref
lesson="canonical-datasets" id="three-scripted-frauds">the simulator's</Ref>
main scenarios give that. Scenario 2, compromised terminals, makes most of
the frauds (9,099 of 14,681): every payment at a compromised terminal is
fraud, whoever's card pays there. A customer spreads about two payments a
day over some 75 nearby terminals, so a card that pays at a compromised
terminal on a given day almost never pays there twice that day. Such a
card was not stolen; "compromised" in the card metric means only that one
of its payments that day was fraud, and it has another fraud only on a
later day it returns to that terminal within the terminal's 28 days.
Scenario 3, leaked credentials, turns a third of a leaked card's payments
into fraud for 14 days, which at about two payments a day is less than one
fraud a day on average.

counts card P's second fraud as a new detection; the card metric falls

### On real card data

On the Belgian stream the card view is not a nicety. About 300 cards are
compromised every day and the team calls a hundred, so even a perfect
ranking reaches at most a third of them. A random hundred cards hold about
a quarter of one compromised card, $CP@100 \approx 0.0025$; the logistic
regression of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="ranking-then-cutting-at-the-capacity">the layered system</Ref>,
whose top hundred holds about 15 compromised cards, has $CP@100 \approx
0.15$, and a depth-2 tree about 0.10.

<KeyFact>
$P@k$ and $CP@k$ part where cards repeat near the top. If no card has two
payments among the day's top $k$, the top $k$ cards are the cards of the
top $k$ payments, and $CP@k$ can exceed $P@k$ only through a card whose
fraud scored below one of its genuine payments. Every repeat in the top
$k$ spends a payment slot on a card already counted: on the simulator,
with about one fraud per compromised card a day, the two differ by
0.005; on real data, with about three, one card can fill three of the
hundred payment slots.
</KeyFact>

## A detected card is a blocked card

The week's $CP@100$ of 0.286 was computed day by day, each day scored as
if the previous days had not happened. They did happen. A card the team
found on day 129 is blocked on day 129, so its payments on day 130 never
take place, and they are certainly not a new detection. In the simulator
the same card is compromised on many days. A leaked card stays
compromised for fourteen days, and a card that pays at a compromised
terminal is compromised again on every later day it returns there within
the terminal's 28 days. Each time the tree ranks such a card in the top
hundred, the day-by-day metric credits it with a new detection of a card
the team blocked at the first call, up to fourteen for one leaked card.

### Removing cards the team has already found

The repair follows the team: carry the set of cards detected so far
through the test week, and before scoring day $t$ remove every payment of
a card detected on an earlier day. The day's ranking is then built only
from cards the team could still call. The ceiling shrinks with it, since
$\gamma_t$ now counts only compromised cards not yet found.

<Figure
  src="./figures/card-removal.svg"
  alt="For each test day 129 to 135, a blue bar of compromised cards still to find when each day is scored alone (50, 54, 51, 54, 55, 54, 38) beside an amber bar after removing cards detected on earlier days (50, 50, 49, 47, 46, 44, 31); under them the daily CP@100 without removal in blue (0.24, 0.35, 0.26, 0.32, 0.30, 0.34, 0.19) and with removal in amber (0.24, 0.32, 0.27, 0.25, 0.23, 0.26, 0.12); headers give the means 0.286 and 0.241"
  caption="Removing the cards found on earlier days shrinks the pool of compromised cards and the daily card precision; day 129 is unchanged because nothing has been found yet."

Day 129 is unchanged: nothing has been found yet. From day 130 on, the
pool of compromised cards is smaller by the cards the tree caught earlier
that are still compromised, and the daily card precision falls with it:
0.32 instead of 0.35 on day 130, 0.12 instead of 0.19 on day 135. The
mean falls from 0.286 to 0.241. About 4.5 of the 28.6 cards a day that
the day-by-day metric credited to the tree were cards the team had
already blocked. Written without qualification, $CP@k$ means the version
with removal.

The test days had already been through one such cleaning: the week's
385 frauds exclude the payments of cards the bank
knew to be compromised from the training period; the raw week held 74 to
91 frauds a day. Which cards count as known at a given test day depends on
when their labels arrived, which is a question about the label delay
<Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">the next lesson</Ref> answers.

<Example title="removal on two days">
A two-day test with $k = 2$. Day 1: cards U (0.9, compromised), V (0.8,
genuine), W (0.4, compromised). Day 2: U (0.95, compromised, still
active), W (0.85, compromised), X (0.5, genuine).

**Without removal.** Day 1: top two U, V, one compromised: $CP@2 = 0.5$.
Day 2: top two U, W, both compromised: $CP@2 = 1$. Mean 0.75.

**With removal.** Day 1 as before; U is detected and blocked. Day 2:
remove U; the top two of W, X hold one compromised card: $CP@2 = 0.5$.
Mean 0.5.

The day-2 "detection" of U was a card blocked the day before. Removing it
also opens a slot, which here goes to a genuine card.
</Example>

### Normalising by the day's ceiling

The ceiling problem of $P@k$ is sharper after removal, because the pool
shrinks as the week goes on: by day 135 only 31 compromised cards remain
and $CP@100(135)$ cannot exceed 0.31. For backtesting, where days from
different periods are compared, the daily value can be divided by its
ceiling:

$$
NCP@k(t) = \frac{CP@k(t)}{\min(1, \gamma_t/k)} ,
$$

the *normalised card precision*, the share of the reachable maximum that
the model achieved. If a day has 50 compromised cards and the hundred calls
find 40, then $CP@100 = 0.4$ and $NCP@100 = 0.4/0.5 = 0.8$: four fifths
of everything findable was found. On day 135 the tree's 0.12 becomes
$0.12/0.31 \approx 0.39$.

Two limits come with it. $\gamma_t$ is the number of compromised cards on
day $t$, which is known only once that day's labels have arrived, so
$NCP@k$ cannot be computed on the live system; it is a backtesting number,
and the running dashboard shows $CP@k$. And on a day with no compromised
card left the ceiling is zero and $NCP@k$ is undefined.

## Three numbers for three questions

Three metrics have survived: ROC-AUC, average precision and card
precision at $k$. The tempting conclusion is that $CP@k$ replaces the
other two. The same five models as on the Belgian stream, this time
trained on the simulator's week of 25 to 31 July and scored on its test
week, show why that is premature.

<Figure
  src="./figures/three-rankings.svg"
  alt="Three columns ranking the same five models on the simulator's test week. ### What each disagreement means

**The two trees.** AUC prefers the full-depth tree by 0.025; AP prefers
the depth-2 tree by 0.187; $CP@100$ separates them by 0.002, a fifth of a
card a day, which is a tie. The AP gap comes from the full-depth
tree's two scores: every fraud outside its block of 1s is tied at 0 with
nearly all the genuine payments and is reached at the base rate. Its AUC is propped up by the straight
segment of its ROC curve through the unreachable region, and the budget of
a hundred cards sees neither advantage.

**The top three.** AUC puts logistic regression first, AP the random
forest, $CP@100$ logistic regression again. Their $CP@100$ values differ by
at most 0.018, under two cards a day, on a week of seven days with about
45 findable cards each. A gap that size is within what a different week
could reverse, which <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">the next
lesson</Ref> measures when it repeats the evaluation over several weeks.

Each metric answers its own question:

- **ROC-AUC**: how well does the model separate fraud from genuine over
  the whole list? It ignores the base rate and the budget, which makes it
  comparable across streams and with the published literature, and blind
  to the strip the team works in.
- **Average precision**: how much fraud sits at the top of the list,
  without choosing $k$? It weights the reachable corner heavily, floors at
  the base rate, and is the summary to use when the budget may change.
- **$CP@k$**: of the $k$ cards the team calls tomorrow, how many are
  compromised and not already blocked? It is the team's own number, in
  its own unit, and it is noisy, capped by the day's supply of fraud, and
  valid only for the $k$ it was computed at.

<KeyFact>
Report all three, because they disagree for reasons. A model can win AUC
on the part of the curve no team reaches. A model with few distinct
scores loses AP, because the frauds in its big tied blocks are reached at
close to the base rate. And two models can tie on $CP@k$ within a card a
day while AUC and AP separate them. When they disagree,
$CP@k$ at the team's $k$ decides the deployment, and AP breaks a tie when
the budget is uncertain.
</KeyFact>

You can now take a scored test week and compute what the team would get
from it: rank each day's cards by their highest score, cut at $k$, remove
the cards found on earlier days, and average the daily card precision,
reading each day against its ceiling $\gamma_t/k$. You can compute
average precision as the mean precision at each fraud and read it against
the base rate, and you can explain why the ROC curve, whose axes ignore
the class sizes, spends 99% of its horizontal axis on cuts a hundred alerts a
day cannot reach. Each simulator number came from a test week that starts
seven days after the training week ends, with cards already known to be
compromised removed. Why that gap is needed, which cards count as known
on a given test day, and how to cut a history into training and test
sets so that a validation number predicts the next week are the problems
of <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">the next lesson</Ref>.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A day has 20 payments, 4 of them fraud, at ranks 1, 3, 8 and 15 of the
model's ranking. Compute its average precision, its precision at 5, and
its ROC-AUC as the share of correctly ordered (fraud, genuine) pairs.

<Solution>
**AP.** Precision at each fraud: rank 1, $1/1$; rank 3, $2/3$; rank 8,
$3/8$; rank 15, $4/15$. $\mathrm{AP} = (1 + 0.667 + 0.375 + 0.267)/4
\approx 0.577$.

**P@5.** The top five hold the frauds at ranks 1 and 3: $2/5 = 0.4$.

**AUC.** There are $4 \times 16 = 64$ pairs. A fraud is above every
genuine payment ranked below it. Rank 1: 16 genuine below. Rank 3: ranks
4 to 20 hold 17 payments, two of them frauds (8 and 15), so 15. Rank 8:
ranks 9 to 20 hold 12, one a fraud, so 11. Rank 15: ranks 16 to 20, 5.
Total $16 + 15 + 11 + 5 = 47$, $\mathrm{AUC} = 47/64 \approx 0.734$.

The fraud at rank 15 adds 5 correctly ordered pairs to the AUC but only
$4/15$ to the AP sum, which is then divided by 4: AP discounts late frauds
and AUC does not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push the ceiling to its edges. (a) Show that $CP@k(t) \le \min(1,
\gamma_t/k)$ for any ranking, and give a ranking that attains it. (b)
What does $CP@100$ report on a day with $\gamma_t = 0$, and what does
$NCP@100$ report? How should a seven-day mean treat such a day? (c) Now
let $k$ grow to the number of active cards. What does $CP@k$ become, and
what does it then measure about the model?

<Solution>
**(a)** The top $k$ cards contain at most $k$ cards, and at most
$\gamma_t$ of all cards are compromised, so the count of compromised cards
in the top $k$ is at most $\min(k, \gamma_t)$; dividing by $k$ gives
$\min(1, \gamma_t/k)$. A ranking that puts every compromised card above
every genuine one attains it.

**(b)** With no compromised card, the top $k$ hold none and $CP@100 = 0$
whatever the model does. The ceiling is $\min(1, 0) = 0$, so $NCP@100 =
0/0$, undefined. Including the day in a $CP@k$ mean pulls every model
down by the same amount and changes no comparison made on the same days,
but it lowers the level; including it in an $NCP@k$ mean is impossible.
The day carries no information about the model and should be dropped
from both means and reported as a count of fraud-free days.

**(c)** With $k$ equal to the number of active cards, every card is in
the top $k$, so $CP@k = \gamma_t / k$, the share of compromised cards,
for every model. The metric has stopped measuring the ranking at all:
the budget decides which part of the ranking is scored, and a budget that
covers everything scores nothing. The same happens to precision at the
bottom of the PR curve, which ends at the fraud share for every model.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Equal ROC areas with different tops were met on ten payments in <Ref
lesson="economics-of-detection" id="exercises">the three rankings of
area 0.625</Ref>. Take it to the scale of a budget. On a test set of
1,000 payments with 100 frauds, construct two rankings with the same AUC
whose top 100 hold 60 frauds and 0 frauds. Then compute the AP of each,
and say in which range of false-positive rates their ROC curves differ.

<Solution>
**Ranking Y (0 frauds in the top 100).** Put 100 genuine payments at
ranks 1 to 100, the 100 frauds at ranks 101 to 200, the remaining 800
genuine below. Each fraud is above 800 genuine payments:
$\mathrm{AUC} = 100 \times 800 / (100 \times 900) = 8/9 \approx 0.889$.

**Ranking X (60 frauds in the top 100).** It needs the same total of
80,000 correctly ordered pairs. Put 60 frauds at ranks 1 to 60; each is
above all 900 genuine payments, 54,000 pairs. The other 40 frauds must
supply 26,000 pairs, 650 each, so each must have 650 genuine payments
below it. Place 250 genuine payments at ranks 61 to 310, the 40 frauds at
311 to 350, and the last 650 genuine at 351 to 1,000. Total 80,000,
$\mathrm{AUC} = 8/9$. Top 100: ranks 1 to 60 fraud, 61 to 100 genuine,
$P@100 = 0.6$.

**AP.** In Y the $j$-th fraud sits at rank $100 + j$, so $\mathrm{AP}_Y =
\frac{1}{100}\sum_{j=1}^{100} \frac{j}{100+j} \approx 0.31$. In X the
first 60 frauds are reached at precision 1, and the $j$-th of the last 40
at rank $250 + j$: $\mathrm{AP}_X = \frac{1}{100}\big(60 +
\sum_{j=61}^{100} \frac{j}{250+j}\big) \approx 0.70$. AP separates what
AUC does not, by more than a factor of two.

**Where the curves differ.** The top 100 of Y are all genuine, so Y's cut
at 100 sits at $\mathrm{FPR} = 100/900 \approx 0.11$ with recall 0, while
X's sits at $\mathrm{FPR} = 40/900 \approx 0.044$ with recall 0.6. X is
far above Y at small false-positive rates; Y catches up and passes X
between $\mathrm{FPR} \approx 0.11$ and $0.28$, where Y has found all its
frauds and X still has 40 to place. Beyond 0.28 both are at recall 1.
The areas balance inside the first 28% of the axis. A budget of 100
reaches at most $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.11$, where X leads; Y's lead lies
entirely beyond it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the floor of average precision. A list of $n$ payments with $m$
frauds is put in uniformly random order. (a) Show that, conditional on a
given fraud landing at rank $r$, the expected precision there is
$\big[1 + (m-1)(r-1)/(n-1)\big]/r$. (b) Deduce that the expected AP is
$$
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{AP}] = \frac{H_n}{n} + \frac{m-1}{n-1}\Big(1 - \frac{H_n}{n}\Big),
\qquad H_n = \sum_{r=1}^n \frac{1}{r},
$$
and check it on the ten-payment toy. (c) Show that AP $= 1$ if and only if
all frauds precede all genuine payments.

<Solution>
**(a)** Fix one fraud at rank $r$. The $r-1$ items above it are a
uniformly random subset of the other $n-1$ items, of which $m-1$ are
frauds, so the expected number of frauds above it is
$(r-1)(m-1)/(n-1)$. The precision at rank $r$ counts those plus the fraud
itself, over $r$ items: $\big[1 + (m-1)(r-1)/(n-1)\big]/r$.

**(b)** AP is the average over frauds of the precision at each fraud's
rank, so its expectation is the expected precision at the rank of one
fraud. That rank is uniform on $1, \dots, n$, so
$$
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{AP}] = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\Big[\frac{1}{r} + \frac{m-1}{n-1}\cdot\frac{r-1}{r}\Big]
= \frac{H_n}{n} + \frac{m-1}{n-1}\Big(1 - \frac{H_n}{n}\Big),
$$
using $\sum_r (r-1)/r = n - H_n$. For $n$ large, $H_n \approx \ln n$, and
the expectation is the fraud share $(m-1)/(n-1) \approx m/n$ plus a term
of order $(\ln n)/n$.

**Toy check.** $n = 10$, $m = 2$, $H_{10} \approx 2.929$:
$0.2929 + \tfrac{1}{9}(1 - 0.2929) \approx 0.371$. Enumerating all 45
placements of the two frauds and averaging $(1/r_1 + 2/r_2)/2$ gives the
same 0.371. On a list of ten the random ranker is well above the fraud
share 0.2, because a fraud at rank 1 contributes a full 1; on the test
week ($n = 58{,}264$, $m = 385$) the formula gives 0.0068 against a fraud
share of 0.0066.

**(c)** Each term $j/r_j \le 1$ because the $j$-th fraud cannot sit
above rank $j$. The average is 1 exactly when every term is 1, that is
$r_j = j$ for all $j$: the frauds occupy ranks 1 to $m$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague says that dividing by the day's ceiling cannot change which
of two models is better on the same days, since the ceiling does not
depend on the model. Test the claim with removal on days 129 and 130 of
the depth-2 tree's week. With removal the tree had $\gamma_t$ = 50, 50
and $CP@100$ = 0.24, 0.32; without removal day 130 had $\gamma_{130} =
54$, so 4 of the cards the tree found on day 129 were still compromised
on day 130. A second model B also finds 24 compromised cards on day 129,
but none of those 4, and finds 34 on day 130. Compare the two models by
mean $CP@100$ and by mean $NCP@100$ over the two days, decide which
comparison the team should believe, and state the condition under which
the colleague would be right.

<Solution>
**Day 129.** Nothing has been removed yet, so both models face
$\gamma_{129} = 50$: $CP = 0.24$, $NCP = 24/50 = 0.48$ for both.

**Day 130.** The tree removed the 4 still-compromised cards it found, so
its pool is 50 and its $NCP = 32/50 = 0.64$. B found none of them, so
they are still in B's pool: $\gamma_{130} = 54$ and $NCP = 34/54 \approx
0.63$.

**Means.** By $CP$: tree $0.28$, B $0.29$. By $NCP$: tree $0.56$, B
$0.555$. The two metrics rank the models in opposite orders.

**Which to believe.** $CP$ counts cards actually found: B found 58
distinct compromised cards over the two days, the tree 56. The tree's
$NCP$ edge comes from a smaller denominator on day 130, and that
denominator is smaller because of what the tree itself did on day 129.
The colleague's claim holds without removal, where $\gamma_t$ is the
day's count of compromised cards and the same for every model. With
removal it fails from the second day on. A normalisation is neutral
between models only if its denominator does not depend on what the
models did.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Price the difference between 0.286 and 0.241 with the value formula of
<Ref lesson="economics-of-detection" id="the-value-of-the-next-call">the
economics of detection</Ref>, $U = V \cdot (\text{compromised cards
called}) - C_a k$. Take $k = 100$ calls a day, $C_a = \text{€}5$ per call,
and $V = \text{€}360$ saved per compromised card called (three further
frauds of €120 each that the call prevents). (a) A business case for the
depth-2 tree quotes the weekly value implied by 0.286. Is that the value
it may claim, and by how much does it overstate or understate a week?
(b) Below what card precision does working a hundred calls lose money?

<Solution>
**(a)** No: the claimable figure is the one from 0.241. The extra 4.5
cards a day in 0.286 are cards the team blocked on an earlier day; a
second call to a blocked card prevents nothing, so $V$ is not saved
again, while the call still costs $C_a$, which both figures already
charge for all hundred calls. The overstatement is therefore the value
of those 4.5 cards alone: $4.5 \times 7 \times \text{€}360 =
\text{€}11{,}340$ a week. Against the honest value, $(24.1 \times 360 -
500) \times 7 = \text{€}57{,}232$, that is about a fifth.

**(b)** $U > 0$ requires $100 \cdot CP \cdot V > 100\,C_a$, so
$CP > C_a/V = 5/360 \approx 0.014$, about one and a half compromised
cards per hundred calls. This is the stopping rule of the economics
lesson read as an average: the hundred calls pay as a whole once their
mean card precision exceeds $C_a/V$, even if the last calls in the list
do not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The simulator's leaked cards stay compromised for 14 days, and suppose a
fraud's label reaches the bank 7 days after the payment. Training covered
days 115 to 121; the test week is days 129 to 135. Five compromised cards
have their first fraud on days a: 116, b: 120, c: 124, d: 126 and e: 130,
and each stays compromised for 14 days from then. Before scoring a test
day, cards "already known" are removed. Compare two rules: (1) remove
cards with a fraud during the training days; (2) remove cards whose first
fraud label had reached the bank by the morning of the test day. For days
129 and 135, list the compromised cards each rule leaves in the test.

<Solution>
**Which cards are compromised.** Card a is compromised on days 116 to
129, b on 120 to 133, c on 124 to 137, d on 126 to 139, e on 130 to 143.
Day 129: a, b, c, d. Day 135: c, d, e.

**Rule (1).** Cards with a fraud during days 115 to 121: a and b. Day
129 keeps c and d. Day 135 keeps c, d and e.

**Rule (2).** A card is known on day $t$ if its first fraud was on or
before day $t - 7$. Day 129: first frauds on or before day 122, so a and
b are removed, c and d kept, the same as rule (1). Day 135: first frauds
on or before 128, so a, b, c and d are known; only e is kept.

On day 135 the fixed rule still scores the model on c and d, two cards
whose labels the bank received on days 131 and 133, during the test week
itself. The right removal date is not a fixed date at the end of
training but "the test day minus the label delay", and it moves with
each test day. That is the construction <Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">the lesson on delayed labels and
prequential validation</Ref> builds, together with the gap between
training and test that the delay forces.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

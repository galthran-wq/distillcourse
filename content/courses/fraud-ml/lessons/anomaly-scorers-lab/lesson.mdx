Two scorers that use no labels — random isolation trees and a reconstruction network — built from the formulas, checked against the paper's constants and a finite-difference referee, and ranked on the real card set by average precision.

Every label-free number in this module so far came out of a library call.
A library call hides the lines those numbers depend on: which sample size
the isolation score is normalised by, what a tree charges a point it
stopped splitting, which rows the network learnt to reproduce, and in
which coordinates a new payment is placed. Each is one line of code, and
each can be wrong without any error being raised.

The definitions all come from earlier lessons. The yardstick
$c(n) = 2(H_n - 1)$, the score $s = 2^{-E[h]/c(n)}$, the tree grown to a
height limit of $\lceil \log_2 \psi \rceil$ and the $c(\text{Size})$ added
at a node the limit truncated come from <Ref lesson="isolation-forest" />.
The autoencoder whose reconstruction error is the score, and the reason to
train it on genuine payments only, come from
<Ref lesson="one-class-and-reconstruction" />. The time split of the ULB
file, its inputs (`V1` to `V28` and $\log(1 + \text{Amount})$) and the
finding that the distance from the mean and both forests rank the frauds
at a ROC-AUC of 0.96, level with the logistic regression, with the
autoencoder below that (0.86 trained on all rows, 0.92 on genuine ones),
while every label-free score trails the supervised ones far on average
precision come from
<Ref lesson="ulb-unsupervised-scores" />. Average precision itself is the
step sum built in <Ref lesson="cost-aware-evaluation-lab" />, and it ships
with this lab complete.

You write the scorers in numpy, one checkpoint each: the yardstick and the
score; one isolation tree; the path length through it; the forest, which
calls all three; the forward pass and loss of a tied-weight autoencoder;
its gradient, derived by you and refereed in the notebook by finite
differences; and the training loop with the scorer around it, fitted on
genuine rows and placing every payment in the training rows' coordinates.
Along the way the forest reproduces the original paper's first experiment,
a planted outlier isolated in 2.7 cuts on average where the central point
of the cloud needs 13, and shows masking on a clump of 40 anomalies. Then both
scorers are built on the first 16 hours of the ULB file's first day and
judged on its last 8, beside the distance from the mean (no model) and a
logistic regression trained on labels (the ceiling).

The lab runs as a Jupyter notebook — open it in Colab with the button
below, or in any Jupyter; it needs no GPU and fetches about 9 MB of data.
Pair the notebook with the code from this page; each checkpoint submits
your function's outputs for verification, and the checklist above tracks
what stands. The seven exact checkpoints are the required set. The written
answer (why ROC-AUC rates the label-free scores level with the regression
while average precision does not) and the open task are optional. The open
task takes the 1,000 payments of day 2 farthest from day 1's mean, 91 of
them fraud, and asks for a label-free order of them that beats a ROC-AUC
bar of 0.76 over the shortlist: among payments that are all unusual,
ROC-AUC asks what average precision asks of the whole day, which of the
unusual ones are fraud. Expect about five hours. When a check goes red,
the verdict often names the bug — the data set's size where the sample
size belongs, a sub-sample drawn with replacement, the decoder's half of a
tied weight's gradient left out, a network fitted to frauds — so read it
before rereading your code.

The ULB file has no cards, merchants or accounts, so every score here
judges one payment by its own columns.
<Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph" /> starts on what the file removed: the
links between cards, accounts and merchants, where organised fraud shows as
structure.

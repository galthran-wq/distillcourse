A cardholder usually pays at 22:00, 23:00, 02:00 and 03:00. The average of those clock times is 12:30, an hour at which they never pay, so a feature built on 'distance from the usual time' with ordinary arithmetic would flag their most ordinary purchases. How should a 'usual time', a 'usual peer' and a 'usual self' be computed so that a departure from each is a feature a detector can use?

<Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates">Entity history aggregates</Ref>
placed a card's past beside its payment as counts and sums over windows:
the number of payments in the last day, the mean amount over the last 30
days. Three things a cardholder's habit holds are not counts. One is
*when* the card pays, a clock time. Another is whether the card is
behaving like cards of its kind. The third is whether it has just
changed, measured against its own recent payments rather than a long
average.

Each of the three needs its own arithmetic. The constraint is the one the
windows obeyed: a feature holds only what is known when the payment is
scored, which is the payment's own fields and the card's payments before
it, and it is cheap enough to compute for every payment.

## Clock time lives on a circle

The hour of a payment is a number from 0 to 24, and the first attempt
treats it like any other number.

### Why the average hour fails

The opening's card pays at hours 22, 23, 2 and 3. Their arithmetic mean
is $(22 + 23 + 2 + 3)/4 = 12.5$, half past noon. Their deviations from it
are $\pm 9.5$ and $\pm 10.5$ hours, so the standard deviation is about 10
hours. A rule "flag payments more than two standard deviations from the
mean" can flag nothing on a 24-hour clock, and a feature "hours from the
usual time" gives the card's own 23:00 payment a distance of 10.5 hours
and a 13:00 payment, at an hour the card never uses, a distance of half
an hour.

The fault is where the numbers start. Hour 23 and hour 0 are one hour
apart on the clock and 23 apart as numbers, because the number line cuts
the clock at midnight. Moving the cut does not help. Cut at noon instead,
and the night card is fixed, but a card that pays at 11:00 and 13:00 now
averages to midnight. Every cut point splits some card's habit, so a usual
time has to be computed without cutting the circle at all.

### Averaging directions instead of numbers

A point on a circle is a direction, and directions can be averaged
without a cut. Treat each payment time as an arrow of length one from the
centre of the clock face, add the arrows, and read where the sum points.
Arrows that point near each other add up to a long sum; arrows that point
in opposite directions cancel.

<Definition title="circular mean and mean resultant length">
An hour $h$, with $0 \le h < 24$, corresponds to the angle
$\theta = 2\pi h / 24$ (15° per hour), measured from midnight in the
direction the clock runs, and to the point $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$,
whose first coordinate points to midnight and second to 06:00. For times
$h_1, \dots, h_n$ with angles $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n$,
$$
\bar C = \frac1n \sum_{j=1}^{n} \cos\theta_j, \qquad
\bar S = \frac1n \sum_{j=1}^{n} \sin\theta_j, \qquad
\bar R = \sqrt{\bar C^{2} + \bar S^{2}} .
$$
The *circular mean* $\hat\mu$ is the direction of the point
$(\bar C, \bar S)$: the angle with $\cos\hat\mu = \bar C / \bar R$ and
$\sin\hat\mu = \bar S / \bar R$, converted back to hours. $\bar R$, the
*mean resultant length*, lies between 0 and 1: it is 1 when all times
coincide and 0 when the arrows cancel, in which case $\hat\mu$ is
undefined.
</Definition>

<Example title="the four late-night times">
| time | $\theta$ | $\cos\theta$ | $\sin\theta$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 22:00 | 330° | 0.866 | −0.500 |
| 23:00 | 345° | 0.966 | −0.259 |
| 02:00 | 30° | 0.866 | 0.500 |
| 03:00 | 45° | 0.707 | 0.707 |
| sum | | 3.405 | 0.448 |

Dividing by 4 gives $\bar C = 0.851$ and $\bar S = 0.112$, so
$\bar R = \sqrt{0.851^2 + 0.112^2} = 0.86$. The point lies almost
straight toward midnight, tilted slightly toward 06:00: its angle is
$\arctan(0.112 / 0.851) = 7.5°$, and at 15° per hour that is half an
hour, $\hat\mu = $ 00:30. The circular mean sits in the middle of the
card's habit, and $\bar R = 0.86$ says the four arrows point nearly the
same way.
</Example>

<Exercise>

<Solution>

$\bar S = 0$. Then $\bar R = 0.805$ and the point $(0.805, 0)$ lies
exactly toward midnight: $\hat\mu = $ 00:00, which the symmetry of the
three times about midnight already says.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

When a card's times are bunched far from midnight, the two means nearly
agree: 13:00, 14:00 and 16:00 average to 14:20 by arithmetic and 14:19 on
the circle. Arithmetic fails only for habits the cut splits, but a
feature computed for every card has to be right for all of them.

## A usual range: the von Mises arc

A mean says where a card's habit is centred. A feature has to say whether
this payment's hour belongs to the habit, which takes a range, and a range
with a stated probability takes a distribution. The normal distribution
lives on a line: fitted to clock times it puts probability on hour 25 and
hour −3, and it inherits the cut.

### A distribution on the circle

<Definition title="von Mises distribution">
The *von Mises distribution* with direction $\mu$ and concentration
$\kappa \ge 0$ has density on the angle $\theta$
$$
f(\theta) = \frac{e^{\kappa \cos(\theta - \mu)}}{2\pi\, I_0(\kappa)} ,
$$
where $I_0(\kappa)$ is the constant that makes the total probability 1
(a modified Bessel function, which numerical libraries compute).
</Definition>

The density is largest at $\theta = \mu$ and falls off symmetrically on
both sides, and $\cos(\theta - \mu)$ takes the same value at $\theta$ and
at $\theta + 2\pi$, so the distribution does not care where the clock is
cut. The concentration $\kappa$ sets the spread. At $\kappa = 0$ every
hour is equally likely. For large $\kappa$ the distribution is close to a
normal distribution with standard deviation $1/\sqrt{\kappa}$ radians,
that is $3.82/\sqrt{\kappa}$ hours: it is the normal distribution of the
circle.

**Fitting it to a card's past.** Given the card's earlier times, the
direction is estimated by the circular mean $\hat\mu$. The concentration
is the $\hat\kappa$ for which a von Mises distribution would produce, in
the long run, a mean resultant length equal to the observed $\bar R$:
the solution of $A(\hat\kappa) = \bar R$, where
$A(\kappa) = I_1(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa)$, a ratio of two Bessel functions, is an
increasing function that a library inverts numerically. Both are the
maximum-likelihood estimates. A tight cluster, $\bar R$ near 1, gives a large $\hat\kappa$.

### The arc and the flag

<Definition title="periodic time feature">
For a payment, fit $\hat\mu$ and $\hat\kappa$ to the times of the card's
earlier payments in a history window. The *arc at level $\alpha$* is the
range $[\hat\mu - c,\ \hat\mu + c]$ around the circular mean, with the
half-width $c$ chosen so that the fitted von Mises distribution puts
probability $\alpha$ inside it:
$$
\int_{\hat\mu - c}^{\hat\mu + c} f(\theta)\, d\theta = \alpha ,
\qquad f \text{ the density with } \mu = \hat\mu,\ \kappa = \hat\kappa .
$$
The *periodic time feature* is 1 if the payment's
hour lies inside the arc and 0 otherwise.
</Definition>

The integral that fixes $c$ has no closed form, so $c$ is found
numerically: a statistics library's von Mises interval function, given
$\hat\kappa$ and $\alpha$, returns it in radians, and $\times 24/2\pi$
turns it into hours. The hand check is the normal approximation above: a
central 90% of a normal distribution lies within 1.645 standard
deviations, so
$$
c \approx 1.645 \times \frac{3.82}{\sqrt{\hat\kappa}} \text{ hours} ,
$$
a little too narrow when $\hat\kappa$ is small, where the von Mises
density is flatter than the normal one.

For the opening's card the whole path runs:
$\bar R = 0.86$; solving $A(\hat\kappa) = 0.86$ gives $\hat\kappa = 3.9$;
the numerical half-width is $c = 3.45$ hours, 3 h 27 min (hand check
$1.645 \times 3.82/\sqrt{3.9} = 3.2$ hours); and the arc is 00:30 minus and
plus 3 h 27 min, from 21:03 to 03:57. A payment at 01:00 is inside, value
1; a payment at 06:00 is outside, value 0. The tighter the card's times,
the narrower the arc, and the closer the hand check comes:

### What α means

$\alpha$ is the share of the fitted distribution inside the arc, not a
significance level, and that fixes how often the flag fires on the
cardholder's own payments. The flag is an input for the model to weigh
beside the amount and the windows, not an alarm.

<KeyFact>
If a card's habit really is the fitted distribution, a 90% arc flags one
genuine payment in ten on that card, however regular the card; fitted to
few payments, it flags more.
</KeyFact>

## How much history the arc needs

The seven-payment card was scored from its own first day and a half. Run
the same construction from its start and the flag misfires. Payment 3, at
22:30, scored from 18:20 and 20:35: $\bar R = 0.96$, $\hat\kappa = 11.9$,
arc 17:36 to 21:19, flag 0. Payment 4, at 00:50, scored from the first
three: arc 17:39 to 23:18, flag 0. Two of the card's first four payments,
all in its ordinary evenings, are called unusual.

### Few points look concentrated

$\bar R$ computed from a few times is large even when there is no habit.
For times spread uniformly over the clock, the root-mean-square value of
$\bar R$ is exactly $1/\sqrt{n}$: about 0.5 for four payments, 0.27 for
fourteen. Four payments of a card that pays at any hour therefore give
$\hat\kappa \approx 1.2$ and a 90% arc about 16 hours wide, a habit that
does not exist. A card that does have a habit gets an arc that is too
narrow for the same reason, and more than one of its genuine payments in
ten falls outside it.

### A window shorter than a day puts the current hour at its edge

If the history window is shorter than 24 hours, every earlier payment in
it happened in the hours just before the current one, so on the clock
the current hour lies at the leading edge of the cluster. Payment 3
against 18:20 and 20:35 is this case: whatever the card's habit, each
new evening payment comes after the ones the arc was fitted to.

**The rule.** Fit the arc on at least the previous seven days, and only
once that window holds at least seven payments. The week fixes the
leading-edge problem and covers weekdays and the weekend. The count is a
judgment, not a theorem: from seven payments, times with no habit give
$\bar R$ around $1/\sqrt7 = 0.38$ and an arc about 18 hours wide around
a habit that does not exist; and on simulated cards whose times are drawn
from a fixed von Mises habit (not the course's transaction simulator,
which gives every customer the same times), a 90% arc fitted on seven
payments leaves about one genuine payment in five outside, twice the
nominal rate. Fewer payments push both errors further. Below the
minimum, leave the feature missing rather than 0, so that "no history
yet" is not read as "unusual hour", and keep the payment count beside the
flag so the model can weigh a young arc. By this rule none of the
seven-payment card's flags would be emitted yet: its example showed the
arithmetic, not a deployable value.

The same construction with $\theta = 2\pi d/7$ for day $d$ of the week
gives a day-of-week arc, for cards used only on weekend nights or only in
working hours.

Whether the arc pays is a measurement. <Ref lesson="example-dependent-cost"

comparison</Ref> fitted eight detectors (a tree, logistic regression and a
random forest, each also with the per-payment cost rule, and the two
cost-sensitive models) on a European card processor's data, and scored
each by its savings against flagging nothing. <Ref
lesson="transaction-aggregates"

gave every detector in-arc flags of this lesson's kind, once on the raw
fields alone and once on top of both aggregate sets (the plain windows and
the windows conditioned on the payment's type, country or merchant).

Averaged over the eight detectors, the periodic set moved the savings on
the raw fields from about 15% to 22%, and on top of both aggregate sets
from about 47% to 54%: **about seven points in both settings**. The
aggregates did not absorb the gain. Had the in-arc flag been a disguised
form of what the windows hold, how often and how much the card pays, the
stacked gain would have shrunk toward nothing once the windows were in
the model. It did not, because the windows hold counts and sums of
amounts and never the payment's hour against the card's own hours. In
relative terms the flag matters more where the model knows less: seven
points are about half again the raw fields' 15%, and about a seventh of
the stacked 47%.

The average hides one exception in each setting. On the raw fields the
random forest fell from 21% to 18% and the plain tree held at 19%;
stacked, the plain logistic regression fell from 15% to 12%. Every other
detector rose in both. The same caveat as before applies: one split
without a delay block, so the levels are optimistic, a fall of three
points on one detector is within what one split can produce, and the
direction is what transfers.

On <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets" id="the-generating-rules">the
transaction simulator</Ref> the flag has nothing to find. The generator
draws every payment's time from one distribution around noon, the same for
all customers, and its leaked cards keep the owner's own payment times. A
card-specific hour does not exist there, so neither does an unusual one: a
feature finds only the fraud that changes what it measures.

## The usual self: has the card just changed?

A windowed ratio, the amount divided by the card's 30-day mean, compares
one payment with the card's past; it was the strongest single feature for
the simulator's leaked cards in <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates"
id="exercises">the previous lesson's exercises</Ref>. "Has this card changed?" is a question
about several payments. A single €300 purchase on a card that usually
pays €40 is a jump; a card that has paid twice its usual amount at every
payment for several days has a new level, and one ratio at a time cannot
tell the two apart. A difference in euros also has no scale: a rise of
€40 is large for a card whose payments vary by €8 and small for one whose
payments vary by €80.

### An old part, a new part, and a t-statistic

<Definition title="break-point analysis">
Take the card's last 24 payments, the current one included, with values
$x$ (here the amounts). The current amount is a field of the payment
being scored, known at that moment, so it may enter the new part; no later
payment may. The first 20 form the *old part*, the card's
recent habit; the last 4 form the *new part*. With means
$\bar x_{\text{old}}, \bar x_{\text{new}}$ and standard deviations
$s_{\text{old}}, s_{\text{new}}$, the break-point statistic is the
two-sample $t$-statistic
$$
t_{\text{bp}} = \frac{\bar x_{\text{new}} - \bar x_{\text{old}}}{s_p \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{20} + \tfrac{1}{4}}},
\qquad
s_p^2 = \frac{19\, s_{\text{old}}^2 + 3\, s_{\text{new}}^2}{22} .
$$
Accounts are ranked by $t_{\text{bp}}$; a large positive value says the
level has risen.
</Definition>

$t_{\text{bp}}$ is the usual $t$-statistic of two independent samples:
the difference of the means in units of its own standard error. Each
choice in the construction answers a part of the question.

- **Counted in payments, not days**, so the window fits each card's
  rate: 24 payments are about 12 days for a card that pays twice a day
  and three months for one that pays twice a week.
- **Twenty old payments** are enough to estimate a mean and a spread and
  recent enough to be the current habit.
- **Four new payments**, not one, so that the question is the level of a
  run of payments and the new part has a spread of its own.
- **Ranked, not tested.** Amounts are far from normal and consecutive
  windows overlap, so the $t$-distribution's p-values mean little; the
  statistic is used as a score, and the investigators work down the list
  either way.

<Example title="a card whose level moves">
A card's 20 old payments are €32, 45, 38, 51, 29, 40, 36, 47, 55, 33, 41,
28, 44, 39, 50, 35, 42, 31, 46 and 38: $\bar x_{\text{old}} = 40.00$,
$s_{\text{old}} = 7.56$. Its four newest are €80, 85, 75 and 90:
$\bar x_{\text{new}} = 82.50$, $s_{\text{new}} = 6.45$.

**Pooled spread.**
$s_p^2 = (19 \times 7.56^2 + 3 \times 6.45^2)/22 = (1{,}086 + 125)/22 = 55.0$,
so $s_p = 7.42$.

**Statistic.** $\sqrt{1/20 + 1/4} = 0.548$, and
$t_{\text{bp}} = (82.50 - 40.00)/(7.42 \times 0.548) = 42.5/4.06 = 10.5$.

No single new payment is out of the ordinary for a card payment; what
moved is the level of all four together, about six old standard
deviations above the old mean.
</Example>

<Exercise>
The same card's four newest payments are instead €38, 44, 300 and 41.
Compute $t_{\text{bp}}$ and the ratio of the €300 payment to the old mean.
Which of the two numbers reacts to this pattern, and why does the other

<Solution>
**New part.** $\bar x_{\text{new}} = 423/4 = 105.75$, a larger mean than
in the example. Its standard deviation is $s_{\text{new}} = 129.5$,
dominated by the one €300 payment.

**Statistic.**
$s_p^2 = (1{,}086 + 3 \times 129.5^2)/22 = (1{,}086 + 50{,}326)/22 = 2{,}337$,
$s_p = 48.3$, and
$t_{\text{bp}} = (105.75 - 40.00)/(48.3 \times 0.548) = 65.75/26.5 = 2.5$.

**Ratio.** $300/40 = 7.5$.

**Why.** The single large payment raises the new part's mean, but it
raises its spread far more, and the spread is in the denominator:
$t_{\text{bp}}$ drops from 10.5 to 2.5 although the new mean is higher.
The break-point statistic measures whether a run of payments has moved
together; one outlier among ordinary payments is what the ratio of one
payment to the card's mean measures. A card needs both: the ratio for a
jump, the break point for a new level.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

The same dilution happens whenever a fraud inflates some of a card's
payments and leaves the rest alone, which is how the simulator's leaked
cards behave.

## The usual peer: when every card changes at once

A comparison of the card with its own past looks like the safest
reference there is: nothing enters it but the card. Christmas breaks it.
In the weeks before 25 December nearly every card spends more than it did
in October, so a card's December against its own autumn is unusual for
almost all cards at once. A break-point statistic, or a *z-score* (the
week's spend minus the card's autumn mean, divided by its autumn standard
deviation), flags the whole population.

  caption="A self-comparison flags a season every card shares; the same week judged against similar cards in the same week is ordinary."
/>

The card in the figure spent about €315 a week through the autumn, with a
standard deviation of about €37, and €620 in Christmas week: $z \approx 8$
against its own past. Its peers spent €600 that week on average. The
question that separates the season from the fraud is not "has this card
changed?" but "has it changed more than cards like it, over the same
weeks?"

### Choosing the peers

A card's *peer group* is the $k$ accounts that behaved most like it over
a reference period before the period being scored. Peers come from
business knowledge (the same kind of card, the same customer segment:
students, small businesses) or from a distance between profiles, such as
weekly spend, number of payments and mean amount, standardised and
compared by Euclidean distance.

- **Chosen in the past and then held fixed.** Peers chosen on the
  current week's behaviour would let a card whose spending a thief has
  inflated pick big spenders as its peers and look ordinary.
- **$k$ neither small nor large.** With three peers the spread is
  estimated from three numbers, and one peer's holiday or one peer's own
  fraud moves the reference. With thousands, the peers are no longer
  similar to the card and the comparison becomes a threshold for the
  whole population, the global rule a peer group exists to replace.

### The peer t-score

<Definition title="peer-group analysis">
Let $y_n$ be an account's statistic in period $n$ (its spend this week so
far, the current payment included, say), and $x_{1,n}, \dots, x_{k,n}$ the same statistic for its $k$
peers in the same period, with mean $\bar x_{k,n}$ and standard deviation
$s_{k,n}$. The peer score is
$$
t_{\text{peer}} = \frac{y_n - \bar x_{k,n}}{s_{k,n}} ,
$$
and accounts are ranked by it.
</Definition>

For the card in the figure, $t_{\text{peer}} = (620 - 600)/80 = 0.25$:
ordinary. A card of the same group that spent €900 that week scores
$(900 - 600)/80 = 3.75$; both cards would have been flagged against their
own pasts. The peers' statistic is computed from their payments before
the scored payment's timestamp, like every other feature.

<KeyFact>
The peer score does not replace the self-comparison. Its denominator is
the spread between cards, which is wider than one card's spread over
time: a card that spends €250 ± 20 a week in October, in a peer group at
€320 ± 80, and then spends €400 scores $t_{\text{peer}} = 1.0$ against its
peers and $z = 7.5$ against itself. Peers cancel a shared season; only the
card's own past sees a change smaller than the peers' spread.
</KeyFact>

## The time since the previous payment

The windows count payments, and counts lose their order: five payments in
one hour and five spread over a day both give a 1-day count of 5. The
order is where a burst shows. A card in a fraudster's hands is used
quickly and repeatedly, as <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="card-fraud-a-label-that-does-arrive">the typology</Ref> described,
because the card is worth something only until the owner notices.

The finest-grained measure of that is the gap $\Delta_i = t_i - t_{i-1}$
between a payment's timestamp and that of the card's previous payment,
which is
<Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates"
id="summarising-a-cards-past-recency-frequency-monetary">recency</Ref>,
the R of the RFM aggregates. What it shows is clearest on the cloned
telephone of <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="a-telephone-account-call-by-call">the typology</Ref>: a subscriber
whose calls start in New York in the daytime, until short night calls
from Boston appear on the account.

<Figure
  src="./figures/gaps.svg"
  alt="Two rows of dots on a logarithmic time axis from under a minute to over 100 hours. Genuine calls, blue: gaps of 1 h 22 min, 5 h 19 min, 9 h 33 min, 24 h 5 min, 2 d 19 h and 4 d 5 h, with a vertical blue line at the shortest, 1 h 22 min, labelled shortest genuine gap. Fraudulent calls, amber: 53 s, 3 min 33 s, 4 min 25 s, 9 h 17 min and 34 h 37 min"
  caption="The time since the previous call for each call of the cloned telephone: three of the five fraudulent calls came within five minutes of the one before; no genuine call came within an hour."
/>

The shortest gap before any of the subscriber's genuine calls is 1 hour
22 minutes; three of the five fraudulent calls came 53 seconds, 3½ and
4½ minutes after the previous call. A gap is short or long only against
the account's own rhythm, though: a card that pays ten times a day has
gaps of an hour or two, a card that pays twice a week has gaps of days.
The construction of the previous sections applies again. Take the
logarithm of the gaps, since they range from seconds to weeks and what
matters is their ratio, and express the current one in units of the
card's own spread: $(\ln \Delta_i - m_\Delta)/s_\Delta$, with $m_\Delta$
and $s_\Delta$ the mean and standard deviation of the log-gaps over the card's previous 20 payments.

On the simulator the gap finds nothing. The generator's leaked cards and
compromised terminals change which payments are fraud, not when payments
happen: in one run, the median gap before a leaked-card fraud was about
5½ hours, like a genuine payment's 6. Bursts are exactly what the script
leaves out. A run of short gaps is also a sequence, and a model that
reads the sequence of a card's payments directly can learn such runs
itself, which is the subject of <Ref
lesson="sequence-models-for-transactions">sequence models for
transactions</Ref>.

## One pattern under four features: the card is the context

Why is 01:45 an ordinary hour for a card whose arc spans the night and an
unusual one for a card that pays in daylight, and why is €620 in
Christmas week unusual against the card's autumn and ordinary against its
peers? And what do these features give a model that the windowed
aggregates did not? In each pair the value is the same; what differs is
the reference it is judged against. Each of the four features takes a
value of the current payment (an hour, an amount, a level of spending, a
gap) and scores it against a reference built from a context: the card's
own hours, the card's own previous 20 payments, the card's peers in the
same week, the card's own rhythm.

Run the cloned telephone through all four.

- **Hour.** The subscriber's six genuine calls before the fraud, between
  09:42 and 16:28, give $\hat\mu = $ 13:41 and a 90% arc from 09:14 to
  18:09. The calls at 01:45 and 22:00 are outside; the genuine call at
  11:23 is inside.
- **Gap.** Against the record's six genuine gaps (mean log-gap 2.71, about
  15 hours, standard deviation 1.62), the three short fraudulent gaps
  score $-4.3$, $-3.4$ and $-3.3$; the shortest genuine gap scores $-1.5$.
- **Old against new.** The comparison of the break point, run on call
  durations: half-minute calls after a habit of calls lasting minutes.
- **Peers.** Chosen from the past, other daytime callers in New York, the
  Boston night calls stand out. Chosen by the place of the current call,
  Boston residents, they look ordinary. The context has to come from the
  account's past, never from the event being judged.

This is also what separates these features from the aggregates. An
aggregate states a reference, a 30-day mean, and leaves the comparison to
the model. These features are the comparison, in the card's own units of
spread: an in-arc flag, a $t$-statistic, a standardised log-gap. A tree
can learn "amount well above the mean" from the amount and the mean
given enough depth; it cannot rebuild an arc from aggregates that never
held the sums of cosines and sines.

<KeyFact>
A value that is unusual only relative to a context is a *contextual
anomaly*; the four features are its scores built by hand, with the card
as the context.
</KeyFact>

<Conclusion>
You can now compute a usual hour on the circle, fit a von Mises arc to a
card's past week and emit the in-arc flag, knowing that a 90% arc flags
one genuine payment in ten and more when fitted to few payments; test
whether a card's last payments have moved its level with a break-point
$t$-statistic; compare a card with its peers in the same period so that a
shared season cancels; and standardise the gap since the previous payment
against the card's own rhythm. You can explain why each is a score of a
value against the cardholder as the context, and why the periodic flag
adds about as much with the aggregates as without them: it holds what
they do not.

Every feature so far needs no label. The terminal's recent fraud rate
does, and labels arrive a delay late; <Ref
lesson="point-in-time-features">point-in-time features</Ref> builds it, and
the rule that every feature in a training row holds only what was known
at that row's moment. <Ref lesson="anomaly-detection-framing">Anomaly
types</Ref> takes up the contextual anomaly as one kind among several.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
A card pays on Saturday, Sunday, Sunday, Monday and Monday. Number the
days Monday = 0 to Sunday = 6 and use the angle $\theta = 2\pi d/7$.
Compute the arithmetic mean of the day numbers, the circular mean (as a
day and a time of day) and $\bar R$.

<Solution>
**Arithmetic.** $(5 + 6 + 6 + 0 + 0)/5 = 3.4$: Thursday, a day the card
never uses.

**Angles.** One day is $360°/7 = 51.43°$: Saturday 257.1°, Sunday 308.6°,
Monday 0°.

**Sums.** Cosines: −0.222, 0.623, 0.623, 1, 1, so
$\bar C = 3.024/5 = 0.605$. Sines: −0.975, −0.782, −0.782, 0, 0, so
$\bar S = -2.539/5 = -0.508$.

**Direction and length.** $\bar R = \sqrt{0.605^2 + 0.508^2} = 0.79$.
The angle is $360° - \arctan(0.508/0.605) = 360° - 40.0° = 320.0°$, which
is $320.0/51.43 = 6.22$ days: Sunday plus 0.22 of a day, about 05:20 on
Sunday. The weekend card's centre falls in its weekend, between the
Saturday and Monday payments, where the arithmetic put it on Thursday.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
(a) A card's history holds two payments, at 06:00 and 18:00. Compute
$\bar R$ and say what the circular mean, $\hat\kappa$ and the 90% arc
become. (b) Compute the circular mean of 06:00 and 17:50, and of 06:00
and 18:10. (c) What should the periodic feature be for this card's next
payment, and why is 0 the wrong value?

<Solution>
**(a)** The angles are 90° and 270°, the points $(0, 1)$ and $(0, -1)$.
They cancel: $\bar R = 0$, so the direction is undefined. $A(\kappa) = 0$
gives $\hat\kappa = 0$, the uniform distribution, under which every arc
21.6 hours long holds 90%: there is no unique arc, and inside or outside
depends on where software happens to put it.

**(b)** 06:00 and 17:50: angles 90° and 267.5°. $\bar C = (0 - 0.044)/2 =
-0.022$, $\bar S = (1 - 0.999)/2 \approx 0.0005$: the point lies toward
noon, $\hat\mu \approx $ 11:55. 06:00 and 18:10: $\bar C = +0.022$, so
$\hat\mu \approx $ 00:05. Moving one payment by twenty minutes moves the
"usual time" by twelve hours, with $\bar R = 0.02$ in both cases.

**(c)** The feature should be missing, with the history size, or $\bar R$,
available beside it so that a model can tell how much the arc is worth.
Setting it to 0 asserts "this hour is unusual for the card" on the basis
of an arc that does not exist; for every new card, and every card that
has no daily habit, the model would learn that "unusual hour" means
"short history". A tree handles a missing value as its own branch; the
values 0 and 1 are kept for arcs fitted on at least seven payments over
at least a week.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
"A card that pays at the same time every day has a huge concentration, so
its arc is narrow and its flag is the most reliable: a payment off the
card's routine is almost certainly not the cardholder." Refute the claim.
Use these measurements: for a card whose payment times scatter around
08:00 with a standard deviation of 10 minutes, a 90% arc fitted on 7
payments has a median half-width of 14 minutes and leaves 20% of the
card's next genuine payments outside; fitted on 14 payments, 15%; on 28,
12%; on 200, 10%. For a card with a 60-minute standard deviation the
shares are 21%, 14%, 11% and 10%.

<Solution>
**What the arc measures.** $\alpha$ fixes the share of the fitted
distribution inside the arc. A tighter habit gives a narrower arc, not a
smaller share of genuine payments outside it: if the fit were exact,
10% of the card's own payments would fall outside a 90% arc whether the
habit spans ten minutes or six hours. The 200-payment rows show exactly
that, 10% for both cards. A narrow arc means "unusual by minutes", not
"unlikely to be the cardholder".

**What few payments add.** With 7 payments both cards leave about 20% of
their genuine payments outside, double the nominal rate, because
$\bar R$ from few points overstates the concentration and the arc comes
out too narrow (median half-width 14 minutes against the true 16). The
rate depends on the number of payments, not on the regularity: the claim
picks the wrong variable.

**The degenerate case.** If the timestamps are recorded to the minute and
a card's seven payments all read 08:00, $\bar R = 1$ and $\hat\kappa$ is
infinite: the arc has zero width and every later payment, a minute off,
is flagged. A pipeline needs a floor, a cap on $\hat\kappa$ or a minimum
half-width such as 30 minutes, because no real habit is that exact and
the recorded resolution is not the habit.

**The step that fails.** "Narrow arc, so reliable flag" equates the
arc's width with its error rate. The error rate is $1 - \alpha$ plus the
small-sample excess; the width only says what counts as "off routine".
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the fit the lesson stated. For angles $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n$
drawn from a von Mises distribution, (a) show that the maximum-likelihood
estimate of $\mu$ is the circular mean, and that it is a maximum; (b)
show that the estimate of $\kappa$ solves $A(\kappa) = \bar R$, using
$I_0'(\kappa) = I_1(\kappa)$; (c) show that for times bunched within a
few hours of each other the circular mean is close to the arithmetic mean
of the hours, computed with the cut placed far from the bunch, and check
it on 13:00, 14:00 and 16:00.

<Solution>
**Log-likelihood.**
$\ell(\mu, \kappa) = \kappa \sum_j \cos(\theta_j - \mu) - n \ln\bigl(2\pi I_0(\kappa)\bigr)$.

**(a)** $\partial\ell/\partial\mu = \kappa \sum_j \sin(\theta_j - \mu)$.
Expanding $\sin(\theta_j - \mu) = \sin\theta_j \cos\mu - \cos\theta_j \sin\mu$
and setting the derivative to zero gives $n\bar S \cos\mu = n\bar C \sin\mu$,
so $\tan\mu = \bar S/\bar C$. Two angles satisfy it, the direction of
$(\bar C, \bar S)$ and the opposite one. The second derivative is
$-\kappa \sum_j \cos(\theta_j - \mu) = -\kappa n (\bar C\cos\mu + \bar S\sin\mu)$.
At the direction of $(\bar C, \bar S)$, $\bar C\cos\mu + \bar S\sin\mu = \bar R > 0$,
so the second derivative is $-\kappa n \bar R < 0$: a maximum, the circular
mean. The opposite direction gives $+\kappa n \bar R$, the minimum.

**(b)** $\partial\ell/\partial\kappa = \sum_j \cos(\theta_j - \hat\mu) - n I_0'(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa)$.
The sum is $n(\bar C\cos\hat\mu + \bar S\sin\hat\mu) = n\bar R$, and
$I_0' = I_1$, so the condition is $I_1(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa) = \bar R$, that
is $A(\kappa) = \bar R$.

**(c)** Write $\theta_j = \mu + \varepsilon_j$ with every $|\varepsilon_j|$
small (a few hours are a few tenths of a radian). Then
$\sin(\theta_j - \mu) = \varepsilon_j - \varepsilon_j^3/6 + \dots$, and the
condition of (a), $\sum_j \sin(\theta_j - \mu) = 0$, becomes
$\sum_j (\theta_j - \mu) = 0$ up to third-order terms: $\mu$ is the
arithmetic mean of the angles, provided they are written without a cut
between them. For 13:00, 14:00 and 16:00 (195°, 210°, 240°): $\bar C =
-2.332/3$, $\bar S = -1.625/3$, direction $180° + 34.9° = 214.9°$, which is
14.32 hours, 14:19; the arithmetic mean is 14:20. The minute of
difference is the cubic term. The circular mean is the arithmetic mean
corrected for the cut, and the correction matters only for habits that
straddle it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
This exercise uses a different seeded run of the same generator, with no
removal of known cards: the cards whose fraud was already labelled before
the test week stay in it. Take the test week (days 129 to 135) and keep
the genuine payments and the leaked-card frauds: 68,860 payments, 176 of
them fraud, a base rate of 0.0026. The previous lesson's run, with known
cards removed, had a base rate of 0.0014 and gave the ratio below an
average precision of 0.61. Since a random ranking's average precision is
the base rate itself, the two runs' values do not compare one to one. For
each payment compute (i) the break-point statistic on the card's last 24
amounts, current payment included, and (ii) the ratio of the amount to
the card's mean amount over the strictly earlier 30 days. (a) Before
computing, predict which ranks the leaked-card frauds better. (b) The run
gives average precision 0.047 for the break point and 0.71 for the ratio;
scored per card and day instead of per payment, 0.06 and 0.82. Explain the gap from what a leaked card does: for 14 days,
a third of its payments are multiplied by five. (c) For which kind of
change would you expect the ranking of the two to reverse?

<Solution>
**(a)** The ratio. The fraud is a per-payment event, one payment in three
inflated, and the ratio scores each payment against a reference the
fraud barely touches; the break point scores runs of payments.

**(b)** Four effects, all visible in the definitions.

- **Dilution.** The new part holds four payments, on average $4/3$ of
  them inflated. When the current payment is a fraud it is averaged with
  three others, mostly genuine.
- **Spread.** An inflated payment among ordinary ones raises the new
  part's standard deviation far more than its mean, as in the in-body
  exercise with the €300 payment: the denominator grows with the very
  change being tested.
- **Contamination.** For a card that pays two or three times a day, the
  20 old payments reach back about a week, so after a few days of the
  14-day leak the old part holds inflated payments too and its mean rises.
  The 30-day mean is diluted far less.
- **Unit.** $t_{\text{bp}}$ describes the card's recent level, and the
  card's genuine payments during the leak get the same high values as its
  frauds; per payment, they compete with the frauds for the top of the
  ranking. Scoring per card and day removes this effect, and the gap
  stays (0.06 against 0.82) because the first three remain.

With a base rate of 0.0026, 0.047 is an informative ranking, about
eighteen times the base rate, but an order of magnitude below the ratio.

**(c)** A change that moves every payment of the card together: a
fraudster who has replaced the owner and spends at a different level on
every payment, or an account whose whole inflow changes. Then the new
part's spread stays small, the old part is clean until the change, and
$t_{\text{bp}}$ separates a level shift from one large purchase, which
the ratio cannot. The transferable point: choose the statistic by the
shape of the change it must catch.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A pipeline builds the periodic feature with a group-by over each card's
payments within three and a half days on either side of the payment, "so
that each row gets a full week of history". Take the seven-payment card,
with its seventh payment at 00:00 on 3 January, and score payment 4 (2
January 00:50). (a) Compute the flag from the payments before it. (b) The
pipeline's window holds payments 1 to 3 and 5 to 7; it gives
$\hat\mu = $ 21:26 and an arc from 17:49 to 01:02. What flag does the

<Solution>
**(a)** From payments 1 to 3 (18:20, 20:35, 22:30), angles 275°,
308.75° and 337.5°.

- **Sums.** Cosines 0.087, 0.626, 0.924, so $\bar C = 1.637/3 = 0.546$;
  sines −0.996, −0.780, −0.383, so $\bar S = -2.159/3 = -0.720$.
- **Direction and length.** $\bar R = \sqrt{0.546^2 + 0.720^2} = 0.903$;
  the angle is $360° - \arctan(0.720/0.546) = 360° - 52.8° = 307.2°$,
  20.48 hours: $\hat\mu = $ 20:29.
- **Concentration.** Solving $A(\hat\kappa) = 0.903$ numerically gives
  $\hat\kappa = 5.5$ (between the table's rows for 0.86 and 0.95).
- **Half-width.** The von Mises interval at $\hat\kappa = 5.5$ and
  $\alpha = 0.9$ gives $c = 2.83$ hours, 2 h 50 min; hand check

- **Arc and flag.** $20.478 \mp 2.829$ hours, from 17.649 to 23.307
  hours: 17:39 to 23:18. 00:50
  is outside: flag 0. (Three payments are below the history rule's

**(b)** 00:50 lies inside 17:49 to 01:02: flag 1. The later payments at

row says "usual hour", while the live system, which sees only payments 1

at each row's own timestamp.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Build a detector from peer groups alone, with no labels. On a run of the
simulator, give each customer a profile from days 99 to 128 (mean amount
per payment and payments per day, each divided by its standard deviation
over customers) and take as peers its 20 nearest customers. For each day
of the test week, compute every active card's spend that day and its
$t_{\text{peer}}$ against its peers' spend the same day, and alert the 100
cards with the highest score. (a) Predict which of the three scripted
frauds the alerts can find. (b) The run gives a share of compromised
cards among the 100 alerts of 0.06 to 0.15, mean 0.11, against about 0.02
compromised among active cards; with 5 peers the mean is 0.08, with 100
it is 0.12, with 1,000 it is 0.14. Interpret the precision and the effect
of $k$. (c) Compare with a supervised detector on this stream.

<Solution>
**(a)** Spend is an amount, so the alerts can see fraud that changes
amounts: the leaked cards, whose payments are multiplied by five, and

payments of ordinary size, and a card's daily spend does not move.
Indeed, of the roughly 11 compromised cards a day among the 100 alerts,
about 8 are leaked cards and about 1 an amount-rule fraud.

**(b)** A share of 0.11 is about five times the 0.02 a random draw of

**(c)** The depth-2 tree trained on the fifteen standard inputs reaches <Ref

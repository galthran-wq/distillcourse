The field runs on five public datasets, and each omits a different part of the loop: two days of PCA components without time, a simulator with three scripted fraud scenarios, a mobile-money log whose fraud was added after the paper, a Bitcoin graph with no feature names, and a laundering world with complete labels that no real bank has.

The module so far has built the system a detector sits in and the arithmetic of that system. <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">The first lesson</Ref> fixed the loop: five layers, a budget of about a hundred alerts a day, and labels that return by two routes after a verification latency $\delta$. <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology">The typology lesson</Ref> gave each crime its unit, label source and positive rate, and <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">the economics lesson</Ref> turned the positive rate and the budget into precision and money. What stands open is where any of this can be measured, since real transaction logs are private. This lesson reads the five public datasets against the loop: for each, what a row is, how its label was made, what its time axis allows, and which of the loop's difficulties it removes, so that every later claim in the course is tested on a dataset that can test it.

## The reading frame: what a dataset keeps of the loop

The loop of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">the first lesson</Ref> makes a detector's problem different from a benchmark's in a fixed number of ways, and a dataset either keeps each of them or removes it.

<Task>
Answer from memory before reading on. (1) What is the verification latency $\delta$, and what is a transaction's label before $\delta$ has passed? (2) Which two label streams reach the model, and which of them does the model itself choose? (3) From <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">the economics lesson</Ref>: at a positive rate $\pi$ well below 1%, what sets the precision of a detector with a given false-positive rate? Write the three answers, then open the fold.
</Task>

<Aside>
**Answers.** (1) The wait between a transaction and the moment its label is treated as final, about a week for cards; before it, an undisputed transaction is read as genuine. (2) Feedbacks, the investigators' verdicts on the top $k$ alerts, chosen by the model; and delayed samples, everything else, labelled by disputes after $\delta$. (3) The ratio of prevalence to false-positive rate: precision is about $\pi\,\mathrm{TPR}/\mathrm{FPR}$ when $\pi$ is small.
</Aside>

### Six things a dataset can keep

Each later module depends on one property of real data. A dataset is useful for that module only if the property survived into the release.

1. **Day budget.** A time axis with days, and enough transactions per day, so that the top $k$ of each day can be ranked and scored. Every metric of <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">the next module</Ref> needs it.
2. **Label delay.** Either label timestamps or a span long enough to impose a delay block of $\delta$ days between training and test, as <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">the temporal-validation lesson</Ref> does.
3. **Entity history.** Card, customer, terminal or account identifiers that recur over weeks, so that a transaction can be compared with its entity's own past. Module 3 is built on this.
4. **Graph.** Edges between entities or transactions, for module 6.
5. **Drift.** Fraud patterns that change over the span, for module 7.
6. **Adversary.** Fraud that responds to the detector, for module 8.

Two descriptive questions come before the six: what one row is, and how many rows are positive. The positive rate places a dataset in the funnel of <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">the economics lesson</Ref>, and the unit of a row decides whether the typology's decision unit (a transaction, a card, an account, a pattern) can be reconstructed at all.

### The question the label column hides

The label column looks the same in every dataset: a 0 or 1 per row. What differs is who wrote it and whether every positive was written.

<Definition title="label completeness">
Let $P$ be the set of rows that are truly positive and $L$ the set of rows labelled positive. The *label completeness* of a dataset is
$$
c = \frac{|L \cap P|}{|P|},
$$
the share of true positives that carry a positive label.
</Definition>

The canonical case is card fraud after the delay: the cardholder disputes almost every unauthorised payment within weeks, so $c$ is close to 1, short only of frauds no one disputes. A structurally different case is a simulator, where the generator writes the label at the moment it creates the fraud, so $c = 1$ by construction. The boundary case is real laundering data: $c$ is small and, more importantly, unknown, because nobody counts the laundering that was never reported.

The near-miss is a dataset in which most rows have no label at all. Elliptic, below, labels 23% of its rows and leaves 77% unlabelled. Its completeness is not 23%: the definition's denominator is the set of true positives, not the set of rows, and the unlabelled rows contain an unknown number of positives and negatives. A labelled share measures annotation effort; completeness measures how many of the crimes were caught, and only a generator knows the second number.

<KeyFact>
Completeness can be computed only where the truth is known independently of the label, which in financial crime means only in synthetic data; for any real dataset it is an estimate or an assumption.
</KeyFact>

## ULB 2013: real card labels, two days, no entities

The ULB credit-card set is the only public table of real card payments, and the reading frame shows why it serves one purpose well and the rest not at all.

### What it contains

The set was released in 2015 with a paper on probability calibration under undersampling by the Machine Learning Group of the Université Libre de Bruxelles (ULB) and its industrial partner, the payment processor Worldline. It holds a subset of online card transactions made over two days in September 2013: **284,807 transactions, of which 492 are fraud, a positive rate of 0.172%**. The team put it on [Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/mlg-ulb/creditcardfraud) in 2016, and as late as the handbook's writing it was still the only public set of real card transactions.

Each row has 30 attributes. Twenty-eight are principal components of the original fields, which were transformed for confidentiality; the other two are `Amount` and `Time`, the seconds elapsed since the first transaction of the set. There is no card number, no customer, no merchant, no terminal and no date.

The labels come from the processor's own fraud process. In the Worldline data this subset is drawn from, a label is set by human investigators after consultation with the cardholder, and is 1 only when the transaction was issued by a third party rather than the rightful holder. These are the feedbacks and delayed samples of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">the first lesson</Ref>, collapsed into one column with no record of when each label arrived.

### What the two days remove

Two days decide most of the reading.

<Figure
  src="./figures/ulb-window.svg"
  alt="A day axis from 0 to 32: the ULB set is an amber bar from day 0 to day 2; a 7-day feature window, a 30-day feature window and a train-delay-test split of 7+7+7 days all extend into the shaded region beyond day 2 where no data exists"
  caption="Every feature window and every delayed split the course uses is longer than the set."
/>

The day budget survives, barely: `Time` can be cut at 86,400 seconds into two days of about 140,000 transactions and 250 frauds each, and the top $k$ of each day can be scored. That gives two numbers, with no spread across days to estimate. Label delay does not survive: a training week, a delay week and a test week need 21 days, and the set has 2. Entity history does not survive twice over: there are no identifiers to group by, and no 7-day or 30-day past to aggregate if there were. There is no graph, no drift that two days could show, and no adversary.

The PCA has a second consequence. A principal component is a fixed linear combination of the original fields, fitted on these two days. A feature engineer cannot tell which component carries the amount in a foreign currency or the merchant category, so no domain feature can be built on top of them. Only `Amount` and `Time` keep a meaning.

### What it is good for

What remains is a fixed feature matrix with real labels at a real positive rate. That is the setting of a comparison between scorers on identical inputs: it serves <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">the imbalance lesson</Ref>, which needs a real positive rate to test the undersampling correction (the release paper's smallest negative-keep rate is $\beta \approx 0.00173$, the rate that balances 492 frauds against 284,315 genuine transactions), and <Ref lesson="ulb-unsupervised-scores">the unsupervised-scores application</Ref>, which needs real fraud in a fixed space.

<KeyFact>
The ULB set tests scorers, not systems: any claim that depends on a card's history, on a label's arrival time or on more than two days of ranking cannot be checked on it.
</KeyFact>

## The handbook simulator: the loop without an adversary

Everything the ULB set removes, the simulator restores, at the price of realism. It is the generator published in the ULB–Worldline handbook *Reproducible Machine Learning for Credit Card Fraud Detection* (2022), and it is this course's workbench: the evaluation, feature, scorecard and capstone labs, <Ref lesson="arms-race-replay">the arms-race replay</Ref> and <Ref lesson="tra-exemption-engine">the TRA exemption engine</Ref> all run on its output.

### Five steps of the generator

The generator is about a hundred lines of numpy and pandas with fixed seeds, and it builds a transaction table with six fields: transaction ID, date-time, customer ID, terminal ID, amount and fraud label.

1. **Customers.** Each customer gets a location on a $100 \times 100$ grid, a mean amount $\mu \sim U(5, 100)$ with standard deviation $\mu/2$, and a mean number of transactions per day $\lambda \sim U(0, 4)$.
2. **Terminals.** Each terminal gets a location on the same grid.
3. **Association.** A customer can use only terminals within radius $r = 5$ of its location.
4. **Transactions.** For each customer and day, the number of transactions is Poisson($\lambda$); each one gets a time drawn from a normal distribution centred on noon with a standard deviation of 20,000 seconds (kept only if it falls within the day), an amount from $N(\mu, \mu/2)$ (redrawn from $U(0, 2\mu)$ if negative), and a terminal drawn uniformly from the customer's list.
5. **Fraud.** Three scenarios label transactions as fraud.

The reference run has 5,000 customers and 10,000 terminals over 183 days from 2018-04-01, and produces **1,754,155 transactions, of which 14,681 are fraud: 0.84%**. The run is reproducible to the unit: re-running the published code unchanged gives the same totals and the same per-scenario counts.

<Aside>
**A documented parameter that does not match its output.** The handbook says $r = 5$ gives around 100 terminals per customer. A radius-5 disc covers 0.785% of the grid, which holds about 78 of the 10,000 terminals, and the run's average is 75, lower at the grid's edges.
</Aside>

### Three scenarios, three unit tests for a detector

The three fraud scenarios differ in what a detector must know to catch them, and each one tests a different part of a detector.

- **Scenario 1: any amount above 220 is fraud.** The handbook states that it is not inspired by real fraud; it is an obvious pattern that any baseline detector should find, a check that the pipeline works. It does: the handbook's depth-2 decision tree learns the split at an amount of 218.67, the training sample's estimate of 220.
- **Scenario 2: compromised terminals.** Every day two terminals are drawn at random, and every transaction on them in the next 28 days is fraud. A detector needs a feature that counts recent fraud at the terminal, and since each terminal goes stale after 28 days, the feature must forget. This is the scenario <Ref lesson="point-in-time-features">the point-in-time lesson</Ref> uses for its terminal risk, computed only from labels known at scoring time.
- **Scenario 3: leaked credentials.** Every day three customers are drawn, and for the next 14 days a third of their transactions have their amounts multiplied by 5 and are marked fraud. The customer keeps transacting, as in a card-not-present compromise. A detector needs the customer's spending habits, the windowed aggregates of <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates">the aggregates lesson</Ref>.

The scenarios are not cleanly separated by amount. After the multiplication, 2,281 of the 4,631 scenario-3 frauds in the reference run exceed 220, so a threshold rule built for scenario 1 also catches about half of scenario 3, those of customers whose usual amounts are high. Frame 4 shows a customer from the other half.

<KeyFact>
Every fraud in the simulator follows a rule written before the run: the drift is two scripted windows of 28 and 14 days, and no fraudster ever reacts to a detector, so no adversarial claim of module 8 can be tested on this generator without changing it.
</KeyFact>

### The steady state: about 85 frauds a day

The scenario parameters predict the daily fraud count before the run is inspected, and the prediction is the check that the generator was read correctly.

<Example title="expected frauds per day from the scenario rules">
The run has 1,754,155 transactions over 183 days, about 9,585 a day. Spread over 5,000 customers that is $1.917$ transactions per customer per day; spread over 10,000 terminals, $0.959$ per terminal per day.

**Scenario 2.** Two terminals are drawn each day and each stays compromised for 28 days, so once 28 days have passed, $2 \times 28 = 56$ terminals are compromised at any time. They carry $56 \times 0.959 \approx 53.7$ transactions a day, all fraud.

**Scenario 3.** Three customers are drawn each day for 14 days, so $3 \times 14 = 42$ are compromised at any time after day 14. A third of their transactions are fraud: $42 \times 1.917 / 3 \approx 26.8$ a day.

**Scenario 1.** Amounts above 220 come from customers with a high mean amount, and the run counts 978 of them, $978/183 \approx 5.3$ a day.

**Total.** $53.7 + 26.8 + 5.3 \approx 85.8$ frauds a day. The run's mean after day 28 is 84.8, of which scenario 2 contributes 53.4 and scenario 3 contributes 26.3. The handbook reports "around 85".
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/daily-counts.svg"
  alt="Line chart over days 0 to 182: transactions divided by 50 flat near 190; fraudulent transactions rising from 3 on day 0 to about 85 by day 28 and fluctuating around 85 after; compromised cards tracking slightly below the frauds; days 0 to 27 shaded as ramp-up"
  caption="The first 28 days are a windowing artefact of the generator: scenario 2 needs 28 days and scenario 3 needs 14 to fill up."
/>

The same arithmetic explains the ramp-up. On day 0 only one draw of each scenario is active, and the run has 3 frauds; the scenario-2 count grows by about two compromised terminals a day until day 27. Over the first 30 days the run averages 57 frauds a day against 85 afterwards. A validation design that puts its test week in the first month measures the detector at two-thirds of the steady-state prevalence. The first closing exercise runs the same prediction for the transaction count and for day 7.

### The counts are increments, not memberships

The handbook prints three per-scenario counts, 978, 9,099 and 4,604, and adds that their sum does not equal the 14,681 frauds because a transaction may be marked by two or more scenarios. The arithmetic says otherwise: $978 + 9{,}099 + 4{,}604 = 14{,}681$ exactly. The code computes each count as the number of frauds the scenario *added* to those already marked, so the three numbers are increments, and increments always sum to the total.

The overlap the sentence describes is real, and a second column shows it. Each transaction also stores the scenario that marked it last, and the counts of that column are 973, 9,077 and 4,631, which also sum to 14,681. The difference between the two triples is the overlap: five scenario-1 frauds were later re-marked by another scenario, and 27 scenario-3 frauds were already fraud when scenario 3 chose them. Tracking each scenario's set separately in a re-run gives the full picture: 4 transactions are in scenarios 1 and 2, 1 in scenarios 1 and 3, 26 in scenarios 2 and 3, none in all three, so 31 frauds carry two scenarios. The scenario-bookkeeping exercise at the end derives the bounds from the two printed triples alone.

The point for a reader of any dataset is the bookkeeping, not the 31. A count column states what was counted only through the code that computed it, and a scenario label stored as "last writer wins" is not a membership. Any per-scenario recall a lab reports is computed on the stored column, which assigns every overlapping fraud to one scenario.

## PaySim: a replay of aggregates, with fraud added after the paper

PaySim is the standard synthetic set for mobile-money fraud, and the reading frame finds that its genuine half and its fraud half have different origins.

### What the generator replays

PaySim was published in 2016 by Lopez-Rojas, Elmir and Axelsson as an agent-based simulator of a mobile-money service. Its input is a month of logs from a real service run in an African country, provided by the telecoms company Ericsson. The simulator covers five transaction types: CASH-IN (paying cash in at a merchant), CASH-OUT (withdrawing it), DEBIT (moving money to a bank account), PAYMENT (paying a merchant) and TRANSFER (sending money to another user). One simulation step is one hour, so a month is 744 steps.

What it takes from the real log is a table of aggregates, not behaviour. The paper gives an example of one entry of its Aggregated Transaction File:

<Quote source="Lopez-Rojas, Elmir & Axelsson 2016, Sec. 4.3">
At Day 1 and hour 15, simulate 8703 transactions of the type PAYMENT with the average transaction size of 180000 and the standard deviation of 15000.
</Quote>

Each step, client agents are created with the probabilities of each transaction type for that hour, and they transact with each other until the step's quota is met. The real log fixes the count, mean and standard deviation per type and hour; it does not fix any customer's sequence of transactions, which is drawn from those distributions.

### How the paper validated it

The authors ran the simulator several times with different seeds and selected the run with the lowest sum of squared errors against the original aggregates, named PS41840, about 23 million rows. The fidelity evidence is a set of overlays of the original and the synthetic count, sum, mean and standard deviation per step for each type.

<Figure
  src="./figures/paysim-fidelity.png"
  alt="Four groups of four stacked line plots, titled CASH_IN, TRANSFER, CASH_OUT and PAYMENT. Each group shows COUNT, SUM, AVG and STD per step from 0 to about 740. A solid red line (the original log) and a dashed blue line (run 41840) follow the same daily peaks, which are tall up to about step 400 and low afterwards. The blue line falls short of the red in TRANSFER SUM and AVG and in CASH_IN COUNT and SUM, and runs above it in PAYMENT STD."
  caption="Per-step count, sum, mean and standard deviation for the four main transaction types: the original log (red, solid) against the selected run PS41840 (blue, dashed). The simulator reproduces the hourly rhythm and the busier first ~400 hours it was fed; where the levels disagree, as in TRANSFER sums and means, the run still came out as the best of several seeds."

The overlays show the per-step aggregates of the synthetic run tracking the real ones, including higher activity in the first 14 days, which the authors attribute to income arriving at the start of the month. They are the only fidelity evidence, they measure only the quantities the generator was fed, and the run shown is the best of several seeds by the same error. A good fit of aggregates that were inputs is a check that the replay works, not evidence that individual accounts behave like real ones.

### Where the fraud label comes from

The 2016 paper contains no fraud. Its introduction says so:

<Quote source="Lopez-Rojas, Elmir & Axelsson 2016, Sec. 1">
The injection of malicious fraud behaviour and the application of different fraud detection methods are outside the scope of this paper and are the topics for further work with the PaySim simulator.
</Quote>

The fraud entered later, in the version published on [Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ealaxi/paysim1): 6,362,620 rows and 11 attributes, scaled down to a quarter of the original. Its description defines `isFraud` as the transactions "made by the fraudulent agents inside the simulation", agents that take control of customer accounts, transfer the funds to another account and then cash out. A second flag, `isFlaggedFraud`, marks attempts to transfer more than 200,000 in one transaction.

The Kaggle file shows what that design implies, and each fact can be counted in it. Of its 6,362,620 rows, 8,213 are fraud, a positive rate of 0.129%; every fraud is a TRANSFER or a CASH_OUT; and the number of frauds per step is approximately flat over the 744 steps, while genuine transactions follow the daily cycle of the real log.

These are generator facts. A model trained on the Kaggle file learns that fraud happens only in two types and at every hour equally, because the fraud agent was written that way; nothing in the real Ericsson log was consulted for either fact. The hour-of-day pattern of genuine traffic is real at the aggregate level, so a time-of-day feature separates the classes on PaySim for a reason that holds in no real system: the fraud agent ignores the clock.

What PaySim keeps is a month of hourly steps, origin and destination account identifiers, and a transfer-then-cash-out shape that touches two accounts. Its features therefore fall into two families, and <Ref lesson="paysim-feature-matrix">the PaySim application of module 3</Ref> sorts its feature matrix by them.

<KeyFact>
A PaySim feature that separates the classes reads either the fraud agent's rule (the TRANSFER and CASH_OUT gate, the two-account sequence, the emptied origin balance) or the gap between the agent and the replay (hour of day). The first family encodes a real account-takeover shape in scripted form; the second is valid only to the extent that real fraud shares the agent's indifference to the clock, which the Kaggle file cannot show.
</KeyFact>

## Elliptic: a real graph with heuristic labels and frozen snapshots

Elliptic is real data of a kind no bank can release, because the Bitcoin ledger is public; the reading frame finds the graph intact and the time axis and labels transformed.

### Nodes, edges, features

Elliptic, a company that sells cryptocurrency-intelligence services, released the set in 2019 with a paper by Weber and colleagues. A node is a Bitcoin transaction and a directed edge is a flow of bitcoin from one transaction to the next: **203,769 nodes and 234,355 edges**, a small sample of a ledger that had about 438 million nodes and 1.1 billion edges at the time.

Each node has 166 features. The first 94 are local: the time step, the numbers of inputs and outputs, the fee, the output volume and aggregates over the addresses involved. The other 72 aggregate the same information over the transactions one hop backward and forward (maximum, minimum, standard deviation, correlation). The released feature table does not name them, and the construction was never fully documented; a 2026 audit had to reverse-engineer their meaning.

### Labels by entity attribution

A transaction is labelled illicit if the entity that initiated it, the one controlling the keys of its input addresses, belongs to an illicit category (scams, malware, terrorist organisations, ransomware, Ponzi schemes), and licit if it belongs to a licit one (exchanges, wallet providers, miners, licit services). The attribution is a heuristic reasoning process of the company's analysts; transactions through mixers, whose inputs are controlled by several entities, are ignored for simplicity. The result: **2% of nodes illicit (4,545), 21% licit (42,019), 77% unlabelled**.

This label clock differs from both of the card case's. It is neither an investigator's call on an alert nor a victim's dispute; it is an analytics firm's attribution of addresses to entities, made at some unrecorded time after the transaction. Its completeness is unknown in the sense of the definition above: the unlabelled 77% are not negatives.

### Forty-nine snapshots

Each node carries one of 49 time steps, spaced about two weeks apart. Each step is a single connected component of transactions that appeared on the ledger within three hours of each other, and **no edge connects two steps**. The set is therefore 49 disconnected snapshots of 1,000 to 8,000 nodes, not a stream.

<Figure src="./figures/elliptic-class-balance.png" float="right" width="m" alt="Two panels sharing a time-step axis from 1 to 49. Top: a blue line of the ratio of illicit to licit nodes, near zero for the first six steps, then swinging sharply between near zero and peaks of about 0.47 at step 9, 0.56 at step 13, 0.41 at step 20 and 0.43 at step 28, lower and flatter through the late 30s, near zero from step 43 to 47 and climbing to about 0.14 by step 49. Top: illicit nodes per licit node at each time step. Bottom: nodes per step, split into unknown (blue), licit (orange) and illicit (green); the illicit share is a sliver in every snapshot." />

The illicit-to-licit ratio swings from near zero to above 0.4 between steps and falls close to zero for steps 43 to 47, after a dark market, an online marketplace for illegal goods, shut down during the span of the data. The paper's benchmark trains on steps 1 to 34 and tests on steps 35 to 49, and every model it tests fails after the shutdown; <Ref lesson="elliptic-illicit-nodes">the Elliptic application of module 6</Ref> reads that collapse.

Against the frame: the graph survives, and so does drift, though as an exogenous event rather than an adaptation to a detector. The day budget does not, because there are no days, only snapshots two weeks apart. Entity history does not, because the nodes are transactions, entities are hidden by design, and nothing links a snapshot to the next. Label delay does not exist as a variable.

## AMLworld: complete labels that no bank has

The typology lesson used AMLworld to show what real laundering labels miss; here the same generator is read as a dataset, and its strength is the property that makes it unlike any real data.

### A world, not a log

AMLworld was published in 2023 by Altman and colleagues at IBM Research. It does not replay real transactions; it simulates a virtual economy of individuals, companies and banks, in which people are paid salaries, companies buy supplies and own shares of each other, and every entity holds accounts at one or more banks. Criminal entities obtain funds from nine sources, among them smuggling, extortion and drugs, and launder them through the whole cycle of placement, layering and integration recalled in <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology">the typology lesson</Ref>: through eight named layering patterns (fan-out, fan-in, cycles and their combinations) and through ordinary-looking payments such as payroll. Against the reading frame, the world is what supplies two columns: every account persists over the whole span, so entity history survives, and every payment is an edge between two accounts, so the graph survives with it.

The paper's Table 1 places it on a scale ladder of synthetic laundering data.

| Generator | Transactions | Laundering rate |
|---|---|---|
| MLDP | 2,340 | 3 in 5 |
| AMLsim | 1.32 million | 1 in 762 |
| AMLworld | 180 million | 1 in 807 and 1 in 1,750 |

Six sets are released, in two families with higher (HI) and lower (LI) laundering rates and three sizes: 10 days and 5 to 7 million transactions, 16 days and about 31 million, and 97 days and 176 to 180 million. Their laundering rates run from 1 in 807 to 1 in 1,948. The largest low-rate set, LI-Large, has 176 million transactions over a little over three months and 1 laundering transaction in 1,750.

The ladder's bearing is the funnel of <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">the economics lesson</Ref>. A generator at 3 in 5 poses no base-rate problem at all. LI-Large's 1 in 1,750, about 0.06%, lies below the ULB set's 0.17%, and its 176 million rows still hold about 100,000 laundering transactions, about a thousand a day, so a day budget can be ranked and scored on every one of its 97 days.

### Transitive taint: the label follows the money

The generator knows the source of every unit of money, so it labels by following it. In the paper's example, A pays B \$100 of illicit funds, B pays \$50 of it to C, and C pays \$25 of that to D: all three payments are laundering, including after mixing with legitimate funds.

<Figure
  src="./figures/taint.svg"
  alt="Two panels joined by an arrow: on the left, accounts A, B, C, D with transfers of 100, 50 and 25 dollars all marked laundering in amber; on the right, the same chain in a bank's own table with only the 100-dollar transfer marked and the 50 and 25 dollar transfers dashed and unlabelled"
  caption="The same chain labelled by the generator and by a bank that filed one report: completeness 1 against 1 in 3."
/>

The paper states the consequence directly: such complete labelling "is nearly impossible with real-world data". The completeness $c$ of AMLworld is 1 by construction; the completeness of a bank's report-based labels is far below 1 and unknown. For evaluation this is the set's value: recall can be measured, which no real AML dataset permits.

### Realism checked against one real count

Realism is the open question for any generator, and the paper's main check is a single comparison. A study of a major Italian bank by Starnini and colleagues found, in 180 million transactions over six months, 855 laundering motifs of the gather-scatter and scatter-gather kinds, about 1 laundering transaction in 21,000. LI-Large has 8,212 transactions in those two patterns, 1 in 21,440.

The match is close, and it calibrates the generator against one pattern rate at one bank. That bank's count is itself a lower bound, since the study looked for only these patterns and had no guarantee of finding every instance. Most of AMLworld's laundering lies outside the named patterns, as <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology">the typology lesson's</Ref> last exercise computed (about four laundering transactions in five), and that part has no real count to be checked against.

### Data released, generator withheld

The authors released the data and not the code, and argued for the choice in the paper's ethics section. Card fraud, they note, has a close ground truth through customers' reports of purchases they did not make, while laundering in real data has none. Their argument against publishing the generator is that launderers holding the source code could tune their behaviour against whatever detection algorithms they could obtain, while a static dataset is much harder to use that way.

The argument is the adversary column of the reading frame, stated by the dataset's authors: the release is static so that it cannot be played against. The same choice means no one can rerun the world with a fraudster who adapts. <Ref lesson="label-maturation">The label-maturation lesson</Ref> uses AMLworld's complete labels to measure how far a report-based label set falls short of them.

<KeyFact>
AMLworld is the only public financial-crime set on which a detector's recall is measurable; a model trained on its complete labels learns a target that no bank's training data contains.
</KeyFact>

## The private replays: what the course quotes but cannot share

The five public sets leave a gap that only real processor data fills, and the course fills it by quotation.

The handbook's authors ran their methods on Worldline's own data: e-commerce card transactions in Belgium in 2018, about 400,000 a day, about 1,000 of them fraud (0.25%), from slightly over 300 compromised cards a day. The data cannot be shared for confidentiality reasons, and the same holds for the Worldline replays in the research papers the course reads: the drift strategies of module 7, the model comparisons of module 4 and the feedback-and-delay experiments of module 8. What they add is exactly what the public sets lack together: real volume, a real positive rate, real card identifiers over months, and real label delay. The course reports their results as published, static numbers; a reader cannot recompute them.

### Reading the columns

Each column names the lessons that need it, and the table says where those lessons can run.

- **Day budget**: the simulator and AMLworld fully, PaySim after grouping hours into days, ULB for two days. The metrics of module 2 run on the simulator.
- **Label delay**: no public set records when a label arrived. The simulator lets a delay be imposed in the split, because its span is long and its labels are known; this is how <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">the temporal-validation lesson</Ref> builds its delay block. The real distribution of dispute times exists only in the private replays.
- **Entity history**: the simulator (customers and terminals over six months) and AMLworld (accounts); PaySim's identifiers belong to agents drawn from aggregate distributions, so their histories measure the generator.
- **Graph**: Elliptic and AMLworld; the simulator's customer–terminal graph is the shape the scenario-2 fraud spreads over.
- **Drift**: Elliptic's dark-market shutdown is the only real drift in the table; the simulator's is scripted.
- **Adversary**: empty for all five. Module 8's claims are tested by modifying the simulator, which is what <Ref lesson="arms-race-replay">the arms-race replay</Ref> does.

### Where each set sits in the funnel

The positive-rate column connects the table to <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">the economics lesson</Ref>: the base rate, not the detector, sets most of a detector's precision.

<Example title="the 99/99 detector on two datasets">
Precision with true-positive rate $\mathrm{TPR}$, false-positive rate $\mathrm{FPR}$ and positive rate $\pi$ is
$$
\text{precision} = \frac{\pi\,\mathrm{TPR}}{\pi\,\mathrm{TPR} + (1-\pi)\,\mathrm{FPR}}.
$$
Take $\mathrm{TPR} = 0.99$ and $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.01$.

**ULB**, $\pi = 0.00172$: the numerator is $0.00172 \times 0.99 = 0.00170$; the false-positive term is $0.99828 \times 0.01 = 0.00998$. Precision is $0.00170/(0.00170 + 0.00998) = 0.146$: about 15 frauds per 100 alerts.

**Simulator**, $\pi = 0.00837$: the numerator is $0.00829$; the false-positive term is $0.99163 \times 0.01 = 0.00992$. Precision is $0.00829/(0.00829 + 0.00992) = 0.455$: about 46 frauds per 100 alerts.

The same detector is three times as precise on the simulator because the simulator has five times as much fraud. Precision figures from different datasets are not comparable until their positive rates are.
</Example>

<Exercise>
Compute the precision of the same 99/99 detector on Elliptic twice: with $\pi$ the illicit share of all 203,769 nodes, and with $\pi$ the illicit share of the 46,564 labelled nodes, which is the population a classifier trained and tested on labelled nodes sees. State which of the two a published Elliptic precision measures, and what the gap implies for comparing it with a card-fraud precision.

<Solution>
**All nodes.** $\pi = 4{,}545/203{,}769 = 0.0223$. Numerator $0.0223 \times 0.99 = 0.0221$; false-positive term $0.9777 \times 0.01 = 0.00978$. Precision $0.0221/(0.0221 + 0.00978) = 0.693$.

**Labelled nodes.** $\pi = 4{,}545/46{,}564 = 0.0976$. Numerator $0.0966$; false-positive term $0.9024 \times 0.01 = 0.00902$. Precision $0.0966/(0.0966 + 0.00902) = 0.915$.

**Which one is published.** Classifiers on Elliptic are trained and scored on labelled nodes only, since the unlabelled ones have no class to score against; a published precision is the second kind, at a positive rate near 10%. That is about 57 times the ULB rate and 170 times AMLworld's LI-Large rate. A precision of 0.9 on Elliptic and 0.15 on card data can come from the same detector quality; the comparison is meaningless until both are converted to the same base rate. The transferable move: before comparing a metric across datasets, recompute $\pi$ for the population the metric was actually computed on.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
From the simulator's parameters, compute the expected number of transactions per day in the reference run: 5,000 customers, $\lambda \sim U(0, 4)$, and a transaction time drawn from $N(43{,}200,\ 20{,}000^2)$ seconds that is discarded if it falls outside $(0, 86{,}400)$. Compare with the run's 9,585 a day and say where the difference can come from. Then predict the number of scenario-2 and scenario-3 frauds on day 7, using the run's 0.959 transactions per terminal per day and 1.917 per customer per day; the run has 17 and 15.

<Solution>
**Transactions.** The expected number of attempted transactions is $5{,}000 \times E[\lambda] = 5{,}000 \times 2 = 10{,}000$ a day. A time is kept if $|t - 43{,}200| < 43{,}200$, that is $|z| < 43{,}200/20{,}000 = 2.16$, which has probability about $0.969$. Expected kept transactions: $10{,}000 \times 0.969 \approx 9{,}690$. The run has 9,585, about 1% fewer. Every customer in the run has at least 22 terminals in reach, so no transactions are lost for lack of a terminal; the gap is the sample: the 5,000 drawn values of $\lambda$ average $9{,}585/(0.969 \times 5{,}000) \approx 1.98$ rather than 2, a deviation of about one standard error ($4/\sqrt{12}/\sqrt{5{,}000} \approx 0.016$).

**Scenario 2 on day 7.** Terminal pairs drawn on days 0 through 7 are all still within their 28 days, so $8 \times 2 = 16$ terminals are compromised, carrying $16 \times 0.959 \approx 15$ transactions. The run has 17.

**Scenario 3 on day 7.** Customer triples drawn on days 0 through 7 are within their 14 days: $8 \times 3 = 24$ customers, a third of whose $24 \times 1.917 = 46$ transactions are fraud, about 15. The run has 15.

Both predictions are the steady-state formulas of the worked example with the number of active draws, $d + 1$ on day $d$, in place of the window length; the ramp lasts until $d + 1$ reaches 28.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The ULB set's `Time` column runs from 0 to 172,792 seconds. (a) A feature engineer computes, for each transaction, the number of transactions in the previous 7 days "for the same card", grouping by a combination of `Amount` and a few PCA components as a proxy identifier. State the two reasons the feature cannot mean what its name says. (b) Show that a prequential fold with a training week, a 7-day delay block and a test week cannot be built. (c) Name one claim of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">the first lesson</Ref> about labels that this set cannot test, and say why.

<Solution>
**(a)** First, there is no card identifier: a principal component is a mixture of the original fields, and no combination of components and amount identifies a card; transactions grouped this way are similar transactions, not one card's history. Second, there is no 7-day past: the set covers $172{,}792/86{,}400 = 2.0$ days, so for every transaction the window reaches before the start of the data, and the "7-day" count is a count over at most two days, shorter for transactions early in the set. The feature is mislabelled twice.

**(b)** The fold needs $7 + 7 + 7 = 21$ consecutive days. The set has 2. Even a fold scaled down to one day each (train, delay, test) needs 3 days.

**(c)** The claim that labels arrive by two streams of different speed, feedbacks on the alert day and delayed samples after $\delta$. The set has one label column with no arrival time, so it cannot show which frauds would have been known at the end of day 1 and which only a week later; every label is effectively final at once, $\delta = 0$. Any experiment on delay run on ULB measures a world with instant labels. The general move: before using a dataset to test a claim, check that the variable the claim is about exists in the data.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "PaySim's `isFraud` label marks behaviour the simulator modelled from the real fraud in the Ericsson mobile-money log." Use the 2016 paper's scope statement and the counts in the Kaggle release, and name the generator fact the claim fails on.

<Solution>
**The paper.** The 2016 paper states that "the injection of malicious fraud behaviour ... [is] outside the scope of this paper" and lists fraudulent agents as future work. What it modelled from the real log is the aggregated count, mean and standard deviation per transaction type and hour, together with balance and repetition distributions. No fraud from the log enters the published generator, so the published paper cannot be cited for the fraud label.

**The Kaggle release.** The label belongs to a later version, described as the transactions "made by the fraudulent agents inside the simulation": agents that take over accounts, transfer the funds and cash out. Counted in the file, every fraud is a TRANSFER or a CASH_OUT, and fraud is spread approximately uniformly over the 744 hourly steps, while genuine transactions follow the daily cycle of the real log.

**The failing fact.** Uniformity over steps. Fraud modelled from a real log would inherit some time structure from it (fraudsters are people with working hours, and card fraud has its own time-of-day profile), as the genuine traffic inherits the log's daily cycle. A flat profile is what an agent scheduled independently of the clock produces. The label marks a rule written by the simulator's authors; the two-type restriction is the same rule seen from another side. What a classifier learns from `isFraud` is, in part, the difference between the replayed genuine aggregates and the scripted fraud agent.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The simulator prints per-scenario counts of 978, 9,099 and 4,604 frauds, computed as the number of transactions each scenario newly marked. Its stored column `TX_FRAUD_SCENARIO` keeps the last scenario that marked each transaction, with counts 973, 9,077 and 4,631. Scenarios run in the order 1, 2, 3. (a) From the two triples alone, derive how many scenario-1 frauds were re-marked by a later scenario and how many scenario-3 frauds were already fraud when scenario 3 chose them. (b) Writing $a$ for scenario-1 frauds re-marked by scenario 2, $b$ for scenario-2-labelled frauds re-marked by scenario 3, and $c$ for scenario-1-labelled frauds re-marked directly by scenario 3, derive the bounds on the number of frauds that carry two or more scenarios. (c) Describe how to get the exact number from a run of the generator.

<Solution>
**(a)** The stored scenario-1 count can only fall after scenario 1 runs: $978 - 973 = 5$ scenario-1 frauds were re-marked. The stored scenario-3 count includes every transaction scenario 3 chose, while the increment counts only those not already fraud: $4{,}631 - 4{,}604 = 27$ were already fraud.

**(b)** The three equations are: scenario 1 loses $a + c = 5$; scenario 3 takes over $b + c = 27$; scenario 2's stored count is its increment plus what it took from scenario 1 minus what scenario 3 took from it, $9{,}099 + a - b = 9{,}077$, so $a - b = -22$, which is the first two equations combined and adds nothing. With $a, c \ge 0$ and $a + c = 5$, $a$ ranges from 0 to 5 and $b = 22 + a$ from 22 to 27.

The transactions counted in $a$, $b$, $c$ are those marked by two or more scenarios, except that a transaction marked by all three is counted once in $a$ and once in $b$. The number of distinct multiply-marked frauds is therefore between $b + c = 27$ (every scenario-1-and-2 transaction also re-marked by scenario 3) and $a + b + c = 27 + a \le 32$ (no transaction in all three).

**(c)** Re-run the generator, keeping three boolean columns, one per scenario, set to true whenever that scenario selects the transaction, instead of a single overwritten column. In the reference run this gives 4 transactions in scenarios 1 and 2, 1 in 1 and 3, 26 in 2 and 3 and none in all three: $a = 4$, $b = 26$, $c = 1$, and 31 multiply-marked frauds, inside the bounds. The 978 + 9,099 + 4,604 = 14,681 identity holds for any overlap, because increments partition the set of frauds by the first scenario that marked each one.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The Elliptic benchmark trains on time steps 1 to 34 and tests on steps 35 to 49. (a) Explain why a random 70:30 split of labelled nodes would overstate a model's quality, using two facts about how the graph and the features are built. (b) The top panel of the paper's Figure 1, the per-step ratio of illicit to licit nodes, rises above 0.4 at several steps before step 43, falls to near zero from step 43 to 47 and stays below its pre-shutdown peaks through step 49. State what the temporal test set contains that no random split's test set isolates, and what a model's test score therefore measures.

<Solution>
**(a)** First, each time step is one connected component of transactions within three hours, and edges are flows between transactions, often of the same entity; a random split puts a node in the test set and its direct neighbours in the training set. Second, 72 of the 166 features are aggregates over one-hop neighbours, so a test node's features are partly statistics of training nodes. With neighbours sharing labels more often than chance (an illicit entity's transactions pass funds to each other), the model can score a test node by recognising the neighbourhood it saw in training. The random split's test score includes this lookup; a deployed screener scoring tomorrow's transactions has no labelled neighbours from the same three hours.

**(b)** The temporal test set contains the period after the dark-market shutdown, steps 43 to 49: the illicit-to-licit ratio drops by an order of magnitude for steps 43 to 47, from around 0.1 in the steps just before to about 0.01, and recovers only to about 0.14 by step 49, below the peaks of the training period. A random split mixes those steps into training, so the test set holds no period the model has not partly seen. The temporal score therefore measures generalisation to a later regime, including an exogenous shock; the paper shows every model failing after step 43. This is the course's first instance of drift that the training data could not anticipate, and the reason <Ref lesson="elliptic-illicit-nodes">the Elliptic application</Ref> reports scores per time step rather than one average.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For each of the five public sets, decide whether a "precision of the top 100 per day" can be computed, and if so, what the largest value it can reach on a typical day is. Use: ULB, 492 frauds over 2 days; the simulator, about 85 frauds a day after day 28 and 3 on day 0; PaySim (Kaggle), about 8,200 frauds over 744 hourly steps; Elliptic, 49 snapshots two weeks apart with 77% of nodes unlabelled; AMLworld LI-Large, 100,604 laundering transactions over 97 days. Then state what the simulator's ceiling implies for reading a daily precision of 0.8.

<Solution>
**ULB.** A day exists (`Time` cut at 86,400 s), with about 246 frauds a day, so the top 100 can all be fraud: ceiling 1. Only two values exist, and a per-card version cannot be computed, because there is no card identifier.

**Simulator.** Computable for all 183 days. After the ramp, about 85 frauds a day means at most 85 of the top 100 can be fraud: ceiling about 0.85, varying day to day with the count (the run ranges from 59 to 110 frauds a day from day 28 on). On day 0, with 3 frauds, the ceiling is 0.03.

**PaySim.** Computable after grouping 24 hourly steps into a day: about $8{,}200/31 \approx 265$ frauds a day, ceiling 1.

**Elliptic.** No day axis: snapshots are two weeks apart, each a few thousand nodes. A top 100 per snapshot can be formed, but 77% of the nodes have no label, so the precision of a top 100 that contains unlabelled nodes is undefined, unless they are dropped, which changes the population being ranked.

**AMLworld LI-Large.** About $100{,}604/97 \approx 1{,}037$ laundering transactions a day among about 1.8 million: computable, ceiling 1.

**Reading 0.8 on the simulator.** On a day with 85 frauds, a precision of 0.80 at $k = 100$ means 80 of the 85 frauds were in the top 100: 94% of the reachable maximum. On a day with 110 frauds the same 0.80 is 80% of the maximum. The raw number confounds the detector with the day's fraud count. <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">The next lesson</Ref> defines precision at $k$ per day with exactly this ceiling, the number of frauds that remain to be found on the day, and its card-level version, which needs the identifiers the ULB set lacks.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design the smallest public dataset that would keep all six properties of the reading frame. List its fields, its time span and its label fields. Then, for each field, name the real constraint that blocks its release, drawing on what the five datasets' authors did: the ULB set's PCA, Elliptic's unnamed features and entity attribution, PaySim's aggregate replay, and the AMLworld authors' decision to withhold the generator.

<Solution>
**A possible design.**

- *Rows*: authorised card transactions of one processor, at least 120 days, so that 30-day windows, a delay block and several test weeks fit after a warm-up.
- *Fields*: timestamp; card ID and merchant (or terminal) ID, pseudonymised but stable over the whole span; amount; channel and merchant category in the clear.
- *Labels*: the fraud label plus two timestamps per positive, the investigator feedback date (if alerted) and the dispute date, and a flag for whether the transaction was alerted by the production model on its day.
- *Graph*: the card–merchant edges follow from the IDs; device or IP links would add a second graph.
- *Drift and adversary*: a span covering at least one documented change in fraud patterns, and the production model's daily alert lists, so the reaction of fraud to the detector can be studied.

**The blocks.**

- *Clear fields and stable IDs*: privacy law and confidentiality. The ULB release replaced every field but two with principal components and dropped all identifiers; stable pseudonymous IDs over months allow re-identification from spending patterns, and <Ref lesson="gdpr-financial-crime-data">the GDPR lesson</Ref> reads why such data remains personal data.
- *Feature semantics*: commercial interest. Elliptic released 166 features without names; the construction is the company's product.
- *Label dates and alert lists*: the alert list reveals the production detector's behaviour, which is what the AMLworld authors refused to give launderers. Their argument applies more strongly to real alert decisions than to a generator.
- *Long span with real behaviour*: if the data are simulated instead, realism reduces to whatever was fed in. PaySim shows the result: real aggregates, behaviour drawn from distributions, fraud written by the authors.

Every design that keeps all six properties is either a real processor's log, which cannot be published, or a simulator, whose fraud and adversary are written by its authors. This is why the course runs on a simulator and quotes the private replays.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

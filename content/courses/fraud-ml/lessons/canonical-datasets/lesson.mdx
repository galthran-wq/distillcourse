No bank publishes its transactions, so the published results of this field come from a handful of public datasets: two days of anonymised card payments, a simulator, a replayed mobile-money log, a Bitcoin transaction graph and a synthetic laundering world. Before any number from them is trusted, what does each one actually contain, where did its labels come from, and which part of the detection loop does it leave out?

<Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">The economics of detection</Ref>
ended on a list of quantities every calculation had to assume: the base
rate, the amounts, when the labels arrive. Looking for real values, a
newcomer loads a public file with a column called `Class` or `isFraud`.
The obvious move is to treat the file as "the data" and that
column as "the truth": fit a model, report its score, and expect the
score to say something about a bank.

That move skips a question. Every one of these files was made by a
process: an anonymisation, a script, a calibrated generator, a company's
guess at who owns which Bitcoin account. The process decides what a row is,
what the label means, and which parts of the real problem never made it
into the file. A score on the file measures the model against that
process, so it cannot be read until the process is known.

## What to ask of a public dataset

Which features of a file decide whether a score on it says anything about
a bank? Start with why public files are so limited.

### Why the public data is so thin

A bank's payment log is personal data (each row says who paid whom,
where and when) and a competitive asset (it shows the bank's customers
and fraud losses). A bank can release it only after removing what
identifies people, or not at all.

What identifies a person is the entity: the card number, the account,
the customer. And the entity is exactly what a fraud detector needs most,
because the signal for card fraud and account takeover lives in an
entity's own history. This leaves two routes to a public dataset, each
with a price:

- **Anonymise real data.** The rows and the labels are real, but the
  entity and anything that could re-identify it are stripped out.
- **Synthesise.** A program generates transactions, so nothing is personal
  and the entity can stay. But the labels mean whatever the program says
  they mean.

Synthetic data splits once more. A *simulator* invents a world from
parameters someone chose: how many customers, how often they pay, what
fraud looks like. A *replay* calibrates its generator to statistics of a
real log, so that the synthetic data reproduces the real one's
aggregates.

### The reading grid

Each part of the real problem met so far becomes a question to ask of a
file.

Three come from the coordinates of <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="four-questions-the-card-loop-answered-without-asking">the
typology</Ref> (unit, label source, label delay, signal structure):

1. **Entity.** Is there an identifier (card, customer, account, terminal)
   that groups rows into histories?
2. **Time.** How long does the file span? Long enough to contain a label
   delay $\delta$ of a week or more, a weekly cycle, a change in the fraud?
3. **Label source.** Who or what wrote the label column?

The typology also showed that the label source decides how much of the
crime gets labelled at all:

4. **Completeness.** Is every criminal row labelled?

The last two come from the loop of <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="the-fast-labels-are-a-sample-the-model-chose">the layered detection
system</Ref>, where the fastest labels are the investigators' verdicts on
the cards yesterday's model alerted on, so the model chooses which labels
it gets back; and from fraud being <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="rarity-is-one-difficulty-out-of-five">time-evolving</Ref>: when a
method is blocked, fraudsters change it, as structuring split deposits to
stay below a reporting threshold.

5. **Feedback.** Do the labels depend on which rows an earlier model
   alerted on?
6. **Adversary.** Does the fraud change in response to detection?

The fourth question separates the crimes of the typology more sharply
than any base rate does, so it gets a precise name.

<Definition title="label completeness">
A dataset's labels are *complete* when every criminal unit in it carries a
positive label, so that every unlabelled unit is genuinely clean. Its
*label completeness* is the share of the criminal units that are
labelled.
</Definition>

Card fraud comes close to complete, because <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology" id="where-the-card-label-comes-from">the
cardholder who lost money disputes the payment</Ref>; the gaps are cases
nobody looks at, such as a counterfeit copy. Laundering sits at the other
end: <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="where-the-laundering-label-comes-from">its only label is a bank's own
suspicious transaction report</Ref>, and most laundering is never
reported. A generated dataset can sit at exactly 1, because the generator
wrote both the crime and the label. A dataset in which every row carries
a label, but where "genuine" means "not reported within $\delta$", is
fully *labelled* and still incomplete, since some of its genuine rows are
unreported fraud.

<KeyFact>
No public dataset has both a real entity history and real labels:
anonymisation keeps the labels, synthesis keeps the entity.
</KeyFact>

## The ULB card set: two days with the card removed

Take the first route. What is left of a card log once the card has been
removed, and what can a model still learn from it?

Only one public file has real card data. In 2015 the Machine Learning
Group of the Université Libre de Bruxelles (ULB) and the payment
processor it worked with released it through the data platform
[Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/mlg-ulb/creditcardfraud); most
fraud tutorials start from it.

### What the file contains

It holds the online card payments of European cardholders over two days
of September 2013: 284,807 payments, of which 492 are fraud. The base rate
is 0.172%, one payment in 580, between the Belgian stream's one in 400
and the one in a thousand of the economics lesson.

Each row has 31 columns. `Time` is the number of seconds since the first
payment in the file, `Amount` is the payment's amount, and `Class` is 1
for fraud. The other 28 columns, `V1` to `V28`, are principal components:
the processor ran PCA on its original features and released only the
projections, so that the originals, which are not disclosed, cannot be
recovered.

<Figure
  src="./figures/ulb-anonymisation.svg"
  alt="Left, a column of boxes for the fields a card processor holds: card identifier, time, amount, three boxes of merchant and derived features, fraud verdict. Time, amount and the verdict map by arrows to the released columns Time, Amount and Class; the feature boxes feed an amber PCA box that outputs V1 to V28; the card identifier ends in a cross marked dropped"
  caption="Anonymisation keeps time, amount and the label, compresses the rest into 28 components, and drops the card."
/>

### What the anonymisation removed

A classifier fitted on `V1`–`V28` works and tests something, but the file
can no longer show three things.

**No entity.** There is no card column, so neither the investigators'
unit of work nor the strongest card-fraud features, which compare a
payment with the same card's recent past, exist here: no card history, no
ranking of cards, no count of compromised cards in a top 100.

**No feature meaning.** A principal component mixes every original
feature, so no one can say which component carries the merchant or the
country: no new feature can be built from domain knowledge, and no score
explained in words.

**Two days.** The label delay of the card loop is a week to a month; two
days cannot hold even one delay window, a weekly cycle or a change in the
fraud. Any split, at random or day 1 against day 2, keeps training and
test inside the same 48 hours.

The labels, from the processor's calls to cardholders and from their
disputes, are as good as card labels get: near-complete. But the file
records no label dates. What a model can still
learn here is to score one payment at a time from anonymous columns at a
real card base rate.

<Exercise>
For each feature below, say whether it can be computed from the ULB file,
and if not, which removal prevents it: (a) the payment's amount divided by
the same card's average amount over the previous seven days; (b) the hour
of the day; (c) whether the payment is the largest in the file so far;
(d) the number of other payments made at the same merchant in the
previous hour. Decide each before opening.

<Solution>
**(a)** No. It needs the card identifier to group payments into a card's
history, and seven days of history; the file has neither.

**(b)** Only up to an unknown offset. `Time` counts seconds from the
first payment in the file, not from midnight, so `Time` modulo 86,400
gives a time of day shifted by the unknown clock time of that first
payment. The daily rhythm is visible; the actual hours are not.

**(c)** Yes. It needs only `Amount` and the order given by `Time`, both
released.

**(d)** No. The merchant, if it was among the original features, is
mixed into the principal components and cannot be recovered as an
identifier to group by.

The pattern: anything defined on a single row survives anonymisation;
anything defined on a group of rows sharing an entity does not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## The transaction simulator: a world whose rules are known

The opposite route is a generator, whose entities can stay because
nobody real stands behind them. In 2021 the same ULB group
published an open-source transaction simulator together with a reference
dataset generated by it.

A result on simulated data might seem to say nothing. But because the
rules are known, the simulator is a test with an answer key: when a
detector finds a pattern, you can check whether it is the pattern that
was put there. And it has what real public data lacks: customers,
terminals and half a year.

### The generating rules

The world is a square grid, 100 units on a side. On it sit 5,000
customers and 10,000 terminals (a terminal is the merchant's point of
sale), each at a uniformly random location. A customer pays only at
terminals within a radius of 5 of their home, which leaves about 75
terminals each. Each customer gets three numbers, drawn once:

- a mean number of payments per day, drawn uniformly between 0 and 4; the
  actual count each day is Poisson with that mean;
- a mean amount, drawn uniformly between 5 and 100; each amount is normal
  with that mean and a standard deviation of half of it;
- payment times are normal around noon.

<Figure
  src="./figures/simulator-world.svg"
  alt="A 20 by 20 corner of the grid scattered with grey terminal dots at a density of one per unit square; a blue square marks a customer at the centre of an amber circle of radius 5, inside which about 75 terminals are amber; a panel lists the customer's parameters"
  caption="A customer and the terminals it may use: every payment it makes goes to one of the amber dots."
/>

Run for 183 days from 1 April 2018, these rules produce 1,754,155
payments, about 9,600 a day. None is fraud yet.

### Three scripted frauds

Fraud is added afterwards by three scenarios, each a pattern a detector
must learn in a different place:

1. **Large amounts.** Every payment over 220 is fraud. No real fraud
   works this way; the scenario is a sanity check, since a single split
   on the amount finds it.
2. **Compromised terminals.** Each day two terminals are drawn at random,
   and every payment at them over the next 28 days is fraud, as when a
   terminal is used to phish the customers who pay there. The amounts
   look normal, so the signal is in the terminal's recent history of
   fraud.
3. **Leaked card credentials.** Each day three customers are drawn, and
   over the next 14 days a third of their payments are multiplied by 5 and
   marked fraud, as when stolen card details are used remotely while the
   owner keeps paying. The signal is in the customer's own spending
   history.

These are the three places a signal can live in <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="four-questions-the-card-loop-answered-without-asking">the
typology</Ref>: the single row, a terminal's history, a customer's
history. Run in that order, scenario 1 marks 978 payments, scenario 2
adds 9,099 and scenario 3 adds 4,604, for 14,681 frauds: a base rate of
0.84%, about 85 frauds a day.

Next to each payment's fraud label the generator writes a second column,
the *scenario* that made it fraud. A payment touched by more than one
scenario (a large payment at a compromised terminal, say) is credited to
the last, whereas the printed counts are the payments each scenario newly
made fraud, so the column credits 973 payments to
scenario 1, 9,077 to scenario 2 and 4,631 to scenario 3, still 14,681 in
total. This column is what makes the simulator an answer key.

<Example title="reading a detector against the answer key">
A decision tree of depth 2 (at most two questions in a row: three splits,
four leaves) is trained on one week of the simulated data, 25 to 31 July
2018, with the amount among its inputs. One of its splits sends payments with amount above **218.67** to
a leaf that is nearly all fraud: of the training payments that land
there, only two are genuine.

**What it found.** Scenario 1, whose rule is amount above 220. Scenario 1
turned every larger payment into fraud, so no genuine payment exceeds
219.98, and the two in the leaf lie between 218.67 and 220. The tree
picks the threshold that best separates fraud from genuine in the
training week; had the range held only those two genuine payments, a
split just above them would have done better, so it must also hold some
frauds from scenarios 2 or 3, worth letting two genuine payments in.

**What the key also shows.** Scenario 3 multiplies amounts by 5, so
2,281 of the 4,631 payments the column credits to scenario 3 also exceed
220. A detector credited with "catching large-amount fraud" catches half
of the leaked-card fraud by the same split.

</Example>

### What the calendar shows, and what it leaves out

Plot the frauds per day and the first month looks different from the rest.

<Figure
  src="./figures/simulator-daily.svg"
  alt="Over days 0 to 182, an amber line of fraudulent payments per day rising from near zero to about 85 by day 28 and then fluctuating between 60 and 110, and a blue line of customers with at least one fraud that day, rising to and fluctuating around 75; the first 28 days are shaded and marked ramp-up"
  caption="Scenarios 2 and 3 keep a terminal compromised for 28 days and a card for 14, so the fraud rate climbs for four weeks before it settles."
/>

Each day adds two compromised terminals and three customers. A customer
stays compromised for 14 days, so from day 14 on the customers leaving
their window balance the ones joining, and the count holds at
$3 \times 14 = 42$. A terminal stays compromised for 28 days, so the
terminal count grows until day 28 and then holds at $2 \times 28 = 56$.
The first month averages 57 frauds a day, the rest 85.

The blue line, customers with at least one fraud that day, settles near
75, well above the 42 leaked cards, because
most of scenario 2's fifty-odd daily frauds fall on different customers
who happened to pay at a compromised terminal. Fraud spread over
customers this way is invisible in any one customer's history, which is
why scenario 2 can only be found from the terminal's.

A study should start training after the ramp-up or treat the first month
as a different regime; a model trained on April and tested on September
sees a base-rate jump that is an artefact of the simulation's start, not
of fraud.

Against the reading grid, the simulator supplies the entity (customer and
terminal) and the time. It leaves out four things:

- **Labels are complete and instant.** A delay exists only if the analyst
  imposes one, by hiding the labels of the last $\delta$ days when
  training.
- **No feedback.** No model chooses which labels come back.
- **No adversary.** The scenarios run the same script whatever a detector
  does.
- **No channel.** There is no card-present or card-not-present field, no
  merchant category, no country; the only features are the customer, the
  terminal, the time and the amount.

## PaySim: a replayed mobile-money log

The third dataset leaves cards for *mobile money*: an account held on a
mobile phone, into which a customer pays cash at a merchant and from
which they pay, send money to another user or withdraw cash again. Its
label column, `isFraud`, would seem to mark a real service's fraud,
imitated; what the generator was built to copy says otherwise.

### What was replayed

PaySim was built in 2016 from one month of logs of a mobile-money
service in an African country. The log itself stayed private. What the
generator took from it was an *aggregate file*: for each hour of the
month and each transaction type, how many transactions there were, and
the mean and standard deviation of their amounts. A line has the form:
day 1, hour 15, 8,703 transactions of type PAYMENT, mean 180,000, standard
deviation 15,000. Those numbers illustrate the format only; the real file
was never published, and the currency is not disclosed.

The five types are CASH-IN (cash paid in at a merchant), CASH-OUT (cash
withdrawn at a merchant), DEBIT (money sent to a bank account), PAYMENT
(paying a merchant) and TRANSFER (sending money to another user).
Generated *agents*, small programs that each play one client, draw every
hour a type, an amount and a counterparty so that the totals per hour and
type match the log.

<Figure
  src="./figures/paysim-replay.svg"
  alt="A table row from the aggregate file (day 1, hour 15, PAYMENT, count 8,703, mean 180,000, sd 15,000) in blue feeds a box of agents, which emits a synthetic row whose step, type and amount cells are blue and whose originator, destination and balance cells are amber; a legend says blue is matched to the log's hourly aggregates and amber is invented and never checked"
  caption="What a replay copies and what it makes up: the hourly totals per type come from the real log; who pays whom does not."
/>

One run, about 24 million transactions, was kept because its aggregates
had the smallest sum of squared errors against the log's, and its
fidelity was shown by overlaying, per hour and type, the synthetic
count, sum, mean and spread on the original's.

<Figure
  src="./figures/paysim-fidelity-overlay.png"
  alt="Eight small line charts in four pairs, one pair per transaction type: CASH-IN, TRANSFER, CASH-OUT and PAYMENT. />

The overlays track closely, but every check is on aggregates: no
synthetic account or network of who pays whom was compared with a real
one, so the agents' individual behaviour, the very thing an
entity-history feature would read, was never validated.

### Where the fraud label came from

The 2016 generator injects no fraud at all: fraudulent agents are listed
as future work. The file people download, from
[Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ealaxi/paysim1), is a later run
scaled to a quarter of the original size: 6,362,620 transactions over 744
hourly steps, with fraudulent agents added. Their behaviour is a script:
take control of a customer's account, transfer its balance to another
account, then cash it out.

The script explains the patterns people "discover" in the file: fraud
occurs only in TRANSFER and CASH-OUT rows, its two steps.
It makes up 0.129% of all rows, about 8,200, spread evenly over the 744
hours, while genuine traffic has the daily rhythm the replay copied from
the log.

Each row also records the sender's and the recipient's balance before
and after the transaction. Inside the simulation the service runs its
own fraud control, and a transaction it detects as fraud is cancelled:
the row stays in the file, but the balances after it record a reversal
rather than a transfer. Only fraud rows are ever cancelled, so the way the
balances relate to the amount differs systematically between fraud and
genuine rows: it is the label written a second time, in another column.
A real detector scores a payment before anyone has decided to cancel it,
so the release says these four columns must not be used for fraud
detection.

<KeyFact>
In PaySim the type mix and the hourly rhythm are copied from a real log;
the accounts' behaviour and the fraud are not. A detector trained on its
`isFraud` column learns a two-step script and the bookkeeping of
cancelled transactions, not mobile-money fraud.
</KeyFact>

## Elliptic: a graph whose labels come from names

The last two datasets concern laundering, where no victim complains and
the evidence lies in connections rather than rows. The first is real.

### What it contains

[Bitcoin](https://bitcoin.org) is a currency whose every transaction is
written to a public ledger. A Bitcoin transaction takes coins from one or
more *addresses* (the identifiers that hold coins) and sends them to
others; owners are not recorded, only addresses. The Elliptic data set,
released in 2019 by Elliptic, a company that sells analysis of such
ledgers, turns part of that ledger into a graph: each node is a
transaction, and an edge runs from one transaction to the next when coins
flow between them.

It has 203,769 transactions and 234,355 edges, a small slice of a ledger
that holds over 400 million transactions. Each node carries 166 features.
The first 94 describe the transaction itself (time step, inputs and
outputs, fee, volume); the other 72 summarise the same quantities over
its neighbours one step away in the graph. The feature names are
withheld.

The nodes fall into 49 *time steps* about two weeks apart, and each step
is a single connected component of transactions that reached the ledger
within three hours of one another. No edge joins two steps, so a model
cannot follow money from one step to the next, and graph features see
only the neighbourhood inside a snapshot.

<Figure
  src="./figures/elliptic-snapshots.svg"
  alt="A row of 49 small boxes, the first 34 blue and marked train, the last 15 amber and marked test, with step 43 marked by a dashed line as the closure of a dark market; below, an inset graph of eleven transaction nodes joined by arrows, one amber (illicit), three blue (licit), the rest hollow (unknown), with a legend giving 2%, 21% and 77%"
  caption="Elliptic is 49 separate snapshots, not one growing graph, and most of its nodes carry no label at all."
/>

### Where the labels came from

Since the ledger records no owners, Elliptic labels a transaction by the
entity that controls its input addresses. It is
*illicit* if that entity belongs to an illicit category (scams, malware,
ransomware, Ponzi schemes, terrorist organisations, dark markets) and
*licit* if it belongs to a licit one (exchanges, wallet providers, miners,
other legal services). Which entity controls which addresses is the
company's own attribution, built from heuristics. The main one: spending
coins needs the key of the address that holds them, so addresses spent
together as the inputs of one transaction usually belong to one owner.

That rule fails on purpose for a *mixer* transaction, which pools coins
from several owners and pays them out again so that no output can be
traced back to whoever paid in: the <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="placement-layering-integration">layering</Ref> step of laundering in
a single transaction. With several controllers there is no single entity
whose category could become the label, so it stays unattributed.

Of the 203,769 nodes, 4,545 (2%) are illicit, 42,019 (21%) licit, and the
remaining 77% are *unknown*. Unknown is not licit: the owner was never
identified, or, as in a mixer, there is no single owner. A technique
built to hide an owner therefore moves a transaction into the unknown
share, not the illicit one. The dataset also has two base
rates: 2% over all nodes, and one in ten over the labelled ones, the only
ones a classifier can be trained and scored on.

  alt="Two panels over time steps 1 to 49. Bottom: stacked bars of nodes per step, between about 1,000 and 8,000, mostly blue unknown with an orange licit band and a thin green illicit sliver on top"
  caption="The 49 steps of the Elliptic set. Top: illicit nodes per licit node in each step, swinging from near zero to above 0.5 between neighbouring steps. Bottom: nodes per step, unknown in blue, licit in orange, illicit in green; every step holds between about 1,000 and 8,000 transactions."
  float="right"
  width="m"
/>

The standard split trains on steps 1 to 34 and tests on 35 to 49,
forward in time, which the ULB set cannot do. And the data holds the one
real change of regime among the five datasets: at step 43 a large dark
market closed, and every model tested at the release fails to recognise
illicit transactions after it, even one retrained at every step with
labels assumed available immediately.

Against the grid, Elliptic has real transactions and real time, in
snapshots. It hides the entity (no address or owner identifier is
released), its labels are a company's attributions, and they carry no
date, so there is no delay and no feedback.

## AMLworld: complete labels no bank has

Elliptic's labels cover less than a quarter of its nodes, and real
laundering labels are worse still, since <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="where-the-laundering-label-comes-from">most laundering is never
reported</Ref>. Only a generator that knows where every unit of money
came from can label all of it. AMLworld, released in 2023, is such a
generator's output.

### What the world contains

AMLworld simulates individuals, companies and banks: people are paid
salaries, companies buy supplies and pay staff, and each may hold
accounts at several banks. A few agents are criminals, with income from
one of nine activities such as smuggling or extortion. They launder it through the three stages of <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="placement-layering-integration">placement, layering and
integration</Ref>, partly through eight standard shapes of transfers
between accounts (one account fanning out to many, many fanning in to
one, cycles, and combinations of these) and partly by spending it in ways
that look ordinary, such as a criminal boss paying employees.

Six datasets were published: a higher-illicit and a lower-illicit
version, each in small, medium and large, from 5 million transactions
over 10 days to 180 million over 97. Laundering runs from one transaction
in 807 to one in 1,942. As a check of realism, the shapes scatter-gather
(money split across accounts and gathered again into one) and
gather-scatter (the reverse) make up one transaction in 21,440 in the
large lower-illicit set, against about one in 21,000 in six months of
real transactions at a large Italian bank.

### Labels that follow the money

The label is *transitive*: the generator tracks illicit funds through
every transaction, including when they are mixed with legitimate money in
one account, and tags every transfer that carries them. <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology" id="exercises">The typology's exercise
on transitive taint</Ref> showed what this does to a model moved into a
bank: transfers several hops downstream carry labels in the simulation
and none in the bank.

What AMLworld adds is the size of that downstream mass. In the large
lower-illicit set, 100,604 of about 176 million transactions are
laundering, one in 1,750. Only 19,461 of them, about one in five, belong
to one of the eight shapes; the other 81,143 are integration disguised as
payroll, supplies and other ordinary payments. A detector that looks for
the shapes misses four laundering transactions in five by construction,
and only a dataset with complete labels can show that miss. The
generator also records each laundering transaction's shape, an answer
key like the simulator's scenario column.

The generator itself was withheld, so that launderers cannot produce
variants and test them against detection methods; the laundering in the
data is static, the adversary kept out along with the code.

## Five datasets against one loop

Is there a part of the loop that every dataset leaves out, so that no
choice of dataset can supply it?

<Figure
  src="./figures/scorecard.svg"
  alt="A table with five columns (ULB card set, simulator, PaySim on Kaggle, Elliptic, AMLworld) and rows positive rate, entity to follow, time span, label source, labels complete, label delay, alert feedback loop, adaptive adversary; amber dots mark present, hollow dots absent, half dots partial; the last three rows are hollow in every column except one half dot for Elliptic's adversary, and are shaded"
  caption="Each dataset supplies some parts of the detection loop. None supplies a label delay or a feedback loop."
/>

### What every column is missing

None of the five records when a label became known, so none contains
the delay; and in none do the labels depend on which rows an earlier
model alerted on, so none contains the feedback loop of <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="the-fast-labels-are-a-sample-the-model-chose">the layered
system</Ref>. Both have to be imposed by the person running the
experiment. Only one dataset contains a real change of regime, and it is
an event (a market closing), not an adversary watching the detector.

### Which question each one can answer

A study question is about one part of the loop: an entity's history, a
span of time, the labels, or the adversary. Take a dataset whose
scorecard column shows that part present; if none does, as for the delay
and the feedback, build the part on top of a dataset by hand. Then name
the parts the chosen column lacks, because the answer silently assumes
them. Whether an hour-of-day feature helps is a
question about time, and PaySim's hourly rhythm is copied from a real
log; but its fraud script has no rhythm at all, so the answer would
measure the contrast between a real rhythm and none, not between genuine
and fraudulent habits. Whether a model holds up at a laundering rate of
one in 1,750, scored against every laundering transaction, goes to
AMLworld and inherits laundering that never reacts to the model.

<KeyFact>
Complete labels (simulator, PaySim, AMLworld) come only from a generator,
which can also record which pattern produced each crime, an answer key no
real log has; the price is a crime that follows a fixed script and never
reacts to a detector. The only real change in the criminal population
sits in Elliptic, whose labels are partial, so no public dataset lets
complete labels and a changing crime be studied together.
</KeyFact>

<Conclusion>
You can now take any result reported on a public financial-crime dataset
and say what it can mean: what a row is and whether rows can be grouped
into histories, who wrote the label and how completely, and which part
of the detection loop the number cannot speak to.

The first thing read off each dataset was its base rate: 0.17%, 0.84%,
0.13%, one in ten of the labelled, one in a thousand or two. <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics">The next lesson</Ref> takes up what to
compute from a detector's ranked output at such rates, when a team can
open only a hundred alerts a day and accuracy and the ROC curve no longer
say which model catches more fraud.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Run the 99%/99% detector of <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="what-share-of-the-alerts-are-fraud-the-base-rate-decides">the
economics lesson</Ref> (it flags 99% of positives and 1% of negatives) on
three base rates: AMLworld's large lower-illicit set (one in 1,750),
Elliptic over all nodes (4,545 of 203,769) and Elliptic over the labelled
nodes only (4,545 of 46,564). Give the precision in each case, then say
which Elliptic figure describes what a model scored on the dataset
experiences and why neither describes a real Bitcoin screening system.

<Solution>
Precision is $\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi / (\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi + \mathrm{FPR}(1-\pi))$
with $\mathrm{TPR} = 0.99$, $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.01$.

**AMLworld, $\pi = 1/1{,}750 \approx 0.000571$.**
$0.000566 / (0.000566 + 0.009994) \approx 0.054$: about 5 alerts in 100
are laundering.

**Elliptic, all nodes, $\pi = 4{,}545/203{,}769 \approx 0.0223$.**
$0.0221 / (0.0221 + 0.0098) \approx 0.69$.

**Elliptic, labelled nodes, $\pi = 4{,}545/46{,}564 \approx 0.0976$.**
$0.0966 / (0.0966 + 0.0090) \approx 0.91$.

**Which applies.** A model trained and scored on Elliptic is scored only
on labelled nodes, since the unknown ones have no truth to compare with,
so its reported precision lives at the one-in-ten rate: 91% for this
detector, 17 times AMLworld's.

**Why neither describes a real system.** The labelled nodes are those a
company could attribute, overrepresenting known exchanges and known
illicit services; an unknown launderer would sit in the 77% left
unattributed. The all-nodes figure treats every unknown node as licit,
which the labels do not say. A real screening system scores every
transaction, at a base rate nobody knows.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "The ULB set has real labels produced by investigators, so a
model that reaches an average precision of 0.8 on a random split of it
would reach about 0.8 in production." Name the parts of the production
setting the file does not contain and the step of the claim that fails
first.

<Solution>
**The premise is true; the step to production fails first at the
split.** A random split of two days trains and tests on the same two days, with fraud of the same cards on
both sides. In production a model is trained on labels at least a delay
$\delta$ old, a week or more, and tested on later days, where the fraud
has moved on. Two days cannot contain a single delay window, so no split
of this file can imitate that.

**Missing parts.** No card identifier, so no history features and no
card-level ranking at a budget of about a hundred cards a day. No label
dates, so no delay. No feedback: in production the fast labels exist only
for the cards the previous model alerted on. No drift over weeks.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
In the simulator, change two settings. (a) Scenario 2 compromises its
terminals for 1 day instead of 28. (b) Scenario 3 compromises its
customers for all of the remaining days instead of 14. For each, say
which kind of feature could still detect the scenario, and what happens
to the first-month ramp-up.

<Solution>
**(a) One-day terminals.** A terminal receives about one payment a day
(1.75 million payments over 10,000 terminals and 183 days), so a
compromise now produces about one fraud and is over the same day. A
terminal-history feature sees the fraud only after its label exists, by
which time the terminal is clean again; the amount and the terminal are
normal for the customer, so neither the row nor the customer's history
shows anything. Scenario 2 becomes label noise no feature can learn, and its ramp-up
shrinks to one day.

**(b) Permanent leaked cards.** After day $d$ there are $3(d+1)$
compromised customers, about 550 by the end, one in nine. A history
feature such as "this amount divided by the customer's 30-day average"
works at first, but
after a month the average itself contains the inflated payments, so the
contrast shrinks: the fraud becomes the customer's normal. And the
ramp-up never ends: scenario 3's fraud count grows linearly over all 183
days, so every test period has a higher base rate than the training
period before it.

The scenario windows of 28 and 14 days are what make the simulated stream
reach a steady state and what make history features informative; both
are choices of the script.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Running the simulator's three scenarios in order prints 978, 9,099 and
4,604 frauds, and the total number of fraud payments is 14,681, exactly
their sum. Someone concludes that no payment was hit by two scenarios.
The scenario column, which records the last scenario that touched each
fraud, counts 973, 9,077 and 4,631. (a) Explain why the printed counts
always sum to the total, whatever the overlaps. (b) From the two sets of
counts, derive how many payments were relabelled, and what can and cannot
be said about which scenario overlapped which.

<Solution>
**(a)** Each printed count is the number of fraud payments after the
scenario ran minus the number before. Scenario 2's 9,099 is therefore the
number of payments it newly made fraud, not the number of payments at
compromised terminals; a scenario-1 fraud at a compromised terminal is
touched by scenario 2 and not counted again. Increments of a running
total always sum to the final total, so the equality holds for any
overlap and refutes nothing.

**(b)** Count the changes of the scenario column. Let $a$ be the
payments whose column passed from 1 to 2, $b$ those that passed from 1
directly to 3, and $c$ those that passed from 2 to 3, whether or not they
had been scenario 1 before. Each passage takes one payment from the old
scenario's count and gives it to the new one's:
$$
973 = 978 - a - b,\qquad 9{,}077 = 9{,}099 + a - c,\qquad 4{,}631 = 4{,}604 + b + c .
$$
The first gives $a + b = 5$, the third $b + c = 27$; the second, $c - a =
22$, follows from the other two, so there are only two independent
equations for three unknowns.

**How many relabellings.** Each passage is one relabelling, so there were
$a + b + c = 27 + a$ of them, between 27 and 32 since $a$ lies between 0
and 5. In distinct payments the bounds are the same: a payment enters scenario 3
only once, so the $b + c = 27$ passages into it are 27 different
payments, and the $a$ passages from 1 to 2 add at most 5 more (fewer if
some went on to scenario 3).

**What cannot be said.** Exactly 5 scenario-1 frauds left scenario 1, but
how they split between scenarios 2 and 3 ($a$ against $b$) cannot be
recovered from the counts alone; it needs the data.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The 2016 PaySim run has 24,638,052 rows: 4,496,947 CASH-IN, 9,014,407
CASH-OUT, 2,030,969 TRANSFER, 8,955,794 PAYMENT, 139,935 DEBIT. The
Kaggle file is a quarter-scale run with 6,362,620 rows, of which
2,237,500 are CASH-OUT, and 0.129% are fraud, all in TRANSFER and
CASH-OUT. (a) Using the 2016 type shares, estimate the number of
TRANSFER rows in the Kaggle file. (b) Estimate the fraud rate within
TRANSFER and CASH-OUT together. (c) If each fraud consists of one
TRANSFER followed by one CASH-OUT, estimate the fraud rate within each
type separately. (d) State what you would compute on the file to check
each estimate, and what would count as agreement.

<Solution>
**(a)** TRANSFER share in 2016: $2{,}030{,}969 / 24{,}638{,}052 \approx
8.24\%$. Applied to the Kaggle file: $0.0824 \times 6{,}362{,}620 \approx
524{,}000$ TRANSFER rows. The same method predicts $2{,}329{,}000$
CASH-OUT rows against the 2,237,500 observed, so the type mix holds to
within about 4%.

**(b)** Fraud rows: $0.00129 \times 6{,}362{,}620 \approx 8{,}200$.
Candidate rows: $524{,}000 + 2{,}237{,}500 \approx 2{,}760{,}000$. Fraud
rate within the two types: $8{,}200 / 2{,}760{,}000 \approx 0.30\%$, one in
about 340, more than twice the overall rate because the other three types
carry no fraud.

**(c)** Half the fraud rows in each type, about 4,100: TRANSFER
$4{,}100 / 524{,}000 \approx 0.78\%$, CASH-OUT $4{,}100 / 2{,}237{,}500
\approx 0.18\%$. Relative to its type, a fraud TRANSFER is four times as
common as a fraud CASH-OUT.

**(d)** `value_counts` of `type` for (a), within a few percent of 524,000;
a cross-tabulation of `type` against `isFraud` for (b) and (c). Agreement
with (c) is a near-even split of fraud rows between the two types; a
clearly uneven one would mean some frauds stop after the TRANSFER. Either
way, the estimate describes the fraud script, not mobile-money fraud.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Four study questions. For each, choose the dataset (or datasets) of this

model detect laundering better than a gradient-boosted tree when the

abrupt change in the criminal population keep working after it? (d) Does

<Solution>
**(a)** The simulator, the only dataset with a terminal identifier and

**(b)** AMLworld, with several banks and complete labels. It cannot say

**(c)** Elliptic, whose dark-market closure at step 43 is the only real

<Exercise>
In the simulator, take a test week of days 129 to 135 and suppose the
labels of every payment up to day 128 are known when the test begins.
(a) Using only the scenario rules, estimate the share of the test week's
scenario-2 frauds that occur at terminals whose compromise began before
day 129. (b) Estimate the same share for scenario 3, for customers whose
compromise began before day 129. (c) A re-run of the generator gives 330
of 384 for (a) and 110 of 152 for (b). Explain what a model can exploit
in such a test week that has nothing to do with recognising new fraud.

<Solution>
**(a)** On test day $d$, the compromised terminals are the two drawn on
each of the days $d - 27$ to $d$: 28 draws. Those drawn on day 128 or
earlier number $28 - (d - 128)$. Over $d = 129, \dots, 135$ the share runs
$27/28, 26/28, \dots, 21/28$, averaging $24/28 \approx 86\%$, which is
the share of frauds if every terminal has equal traffic. The re-run gives
$330/384 = 86\%$.

**(b)** The same count with a 14-day window: on day $d$, the share of
active draws made by day 128 is $(14 - (d - 128))/14$, running
$13/14$ down to $7/14$, average $10/14 \approx 71\%$. The re-run gives
$110/152 = 72\%$.

**(c)** Most test-week fraud continues compromises already visible, with
labels, before the week started. A model can score well by
flagging the next payments of terminals and customers that had fraud in
the last weeks, without recognising any new compromise, although a bank
would already have blocked many of those cards. <Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">Temporal validation with delayed
labels</Ref> builds the test week the honest way: it leaves a delay
block between training and test, and removes from the test days the
payments of cards known to be compromised by then.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

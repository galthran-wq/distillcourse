No bank publishes its transactions, so the published results of this field come from a handful of public datasets: two days of anonymised card payments, a simulator, a replayed mobile-money log, a Bitcoin transaction graph and a synthetic laundering world. Before any number from them is trusted, what does each one actually contain, where did its labels come from, and which part of the detection loop does it leave out?

<Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">The economics of detection</Ref>
ended on a list of quantities every calculation had to assume: the base
rate, the amounts, when the labels arrive. A newcomer looking for real
values reaches for a public file, loads it, and finds a column called
`Class` or `isFraud`. The obvious move is to treat that file as "the data"
and that column as "the truth": fit a model, report its score, and expect
the score to say something about a bank.

That move skips a question. Every one of these files was made by a
process: an anonymisation, a script, a calibrated generator, a company's
guess at who owns which Bitcoin account. The process decides what a row is,
what the label means, and which parts of the real problem never made it
into the file. A model's score on the file measures the model against
that process, so a score cannot be read until the process is known.

## What to ask of a public dataset

Before opening any of the five files, it helps to know what to look for.
Which features of a file decide whether a score on it says anything about
a bank? The answer depends on two things: why public files are so
limited in the first place, and which parts of the real problem a file
can leave out.

### Why the public data is so thin

A bank's payment log is personal data: each row says which person paid
whom, where and when. It is also a competitive asset, since it shows a
bank's customers and its fraud losses. So a bank can release its data
only after removing what identifies people, or not at all.

What identifies a person is the entity: the card number, the account,
the customer. And the entity is exactly what a fraud detector needs most,
because the signal for card fraud and account takeover lives in an
entity's own history. This leaves two routes to a public dataset, each
with a price:

- **Anonymise real data.** The rows are real, and so are the labels, but
  the entity and anything that could re-identify it are stripped out.
- **Synthesise.** A program generates transactions, so nothing is personal
  and the entity can stay. But the labels mean whatever the program says
  they mean.

Among synthetic data there is a further split that matters for trusting
the label. A *simulator* invents a world from parameters someone chose:
how many customers, how often they pay, what fraud looks like. A *replay*
calibrates its generator to statistics of a real log, so that the
synthetic data reproduces the real one's aggregates. Of the five datasets
below, one is anonymised, one is a simulator, one a replay, one an
anonymised graph with company-made labels, and one a simulator of a whole
economy: people, companies and banks holding accounts, not only the
payments between them.

### The reading grid

Which parts can a file leave out? Any part of the real problem met so
far, so each part becomes a question to ask of a file.

From the coordinates of <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="four-questions-the-card-loop-answered-without-asking">the
typology</Ref> (unit, label source, label delay, signal structure) come
three questions. The signal for card fraud and account takeover lives in
an entity's history, so the first question is whether the file keeps
that entity. The label source becomes the question of who wrote the
label column. The label delay becomes a question about the calendar: a
file can only contain a delay if it spans more time than the delay does.

1. **Entity.** Is there an identifier (card, customer, account, terminal)
   that groups rows into histories?
2. **Time.** How long does the file span? Long enough to contain a label
   delay $\delta$ of a week or more, a weekly cycle, a change in the fraud?
3. **Label source.** Who or what wrote the label column?

The typology also showed that the label source decides how much of the
crime gets labelled at all, so a fourth question follows:

4. **Completeness.** Is every criminal row labelled?

The last two questions come from how the loop of <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="the-fast-labels-are-a-sample-the-model-chose">the layered detection
system</Ref> gets its labels and from how fraud behaves over time. In
that loop the fastest labels are the investigators' verdicts on the
cards yesterday's model alerted on, so the model chooses which labels it
gets back. And fraud is <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="rarity-is-one-difficulty-out-of-five">time-evolving</Ref>: when a
method is blocked, fraudsters change it, as structuring showed when it
split deposits to stay below a reporting threshold.

5. **Feedback.** Do the labels depend on which rows an earlier model
   alerted on?
6. **Adversary.** Does the fraud change in response to detection?

The fourth question needs a precise name, because it separates the crimes
of the typology more sharply than any base rate does.

<Definition title="label completeness">
A dataset's labels are *complete* when every criminal unit in it carries a
positive label, so that every unlabelled unit is genuinely clean. Its
*label completeness* is the share of the criminal units that are
labelled.
</Definition>

Card fraud comes close to complete, because <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology" id="where-the-card-label-comes-from">the
cardholder who lost money disputes the payment</Ref>; the gaps are cases
nobody looks at, such as a counterfeit copy. Laundering sits at the other
end: <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="where-the-laundering-label-comes-from">its only label is a bank's own
suspicious transaction report</Ref>, and most laundering is never
reported. A generated dataset can sit at
exactly 1, because the generator wrote both the crime and the label. A
near-miss shows what the definition does not cover: a dataset in which
every row carries a label, but where "genuine" means "not reported within
$\delta$", is fully *labelled* and still incomplete, since some of its
genuine rows are unreported fraud.

<KeyFact>
Anonymisation keeps the labels real and removes the entity; synthesis
keeps the entity and makes the labels whatever the generator says. No
public dataset has both a real entity history and real labels.
</KeyFact>

## The ULB card set: two days with the card removed

Take the first route: a real card log, anonymised for release. The
entity has to go, because it identifies the cardholder. What is left of a
card log once the card has been removed, and what can a model still learn
from it?

Only one public file lets you find out with real card data. In 2015 the Machine
Learning Group of the Université Libre de Bruxelles (ULB) and the payment
processor it worked with released a set of real card transactions,
distributed through the data platform
[Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/mlg-ulb/creditcardfraud). It is
the file most fraud tutorials start from.

### What the file contains

It holds the online card payments of European cardholders over two days
of September 2013: 284,807 payments, of which 492 are fraud. The base rate
is 0.172%, one payment in 580, between the Belgian stream's one in 400
and the one in a thousand of the economics lesson.

Each row has 31 columns. `Time` is the number of seconds since the first
payment in the file, `Amount` is the payment's amount, and `Class` is 1
for fraud. The other 28 columns, `V1` to `V28`, are principal components:
the processor ran PCA on its original features and released only the
projections, so that the originals cannot be recovered. What those
originals were is not disclosed.

<Figure
  src="./figures/ulb-anonymisation.svg"
  alt="Left, a column of boxes for the fields a card processor holds: card identifier, time, amount, three boxes of merchant and derived features, fraud verdict. Time, amount and the verdict map by arrows to the released columns Time, Amount and Class; the feature boxes feed an amber PCA box that outputs V1 to V28; the card identifier ends in a cross marked dropped"
  caption="Anonymisation keeps time, amount and the label, compresses the rest into 28 components, and drops the card."
/>

### What the anonymisation removed

The obvious way to use the file is to treat `V1`–`V28` as features like any
other and fit a classifier. That works, and it tests something, but look
at what the file can no longer show.

**No entity.** There is no card column. The investigators of the layered
system work cards, and the strongest card-fraud features compare a payment
with the same card's recent past. On this file neither can be computed:
no card history, no ranking of cards, no count of how many compromised
cards a top 100 holds.

**No feature meaning.** A principal component mixes every original
feature. A model can still learn from it, but no one can say which
component carries the merchant or the country, so a new feature cannot be
built from domain knowledge and an explanation of a score cannot be given
in words.

**Two days.** The label delay of the card loop is a week to a month; two
days cannot hold even one delay window. There is no weekly cycle, no
change in the fraud over time, no retraining. The file can be split, at
random or day 1 against day 2, but every split keeps training and test
inside the same 48 hours, so no split can hold a delay window between
them or a weekly cycle.

The labels themselves are as good as card labels get. The processor
labels a payment from its investigators' calls to cardholders and from
cardholders' disputes, which is why they are near-complete. But the file
records no date at which each label became known, so nothing about the
delay survives. What a model can still learn here is to score one
payment at a time from anonymous columns at a real card base rate; the
card, the calendar and the label clock are gone.

<Exercise>
For each feature below, say whether it can be computed from the ULB file,
and if not, which removal prevents it: (a) the payment's amount divided by
the same card's average amount over the previous seven days; (b) the hour
of the day; (c) whether the payment is the largest in the file so far;
(d) the number of other payments made at the same merchant in the
previous hour. Decide each before opening.

<Solution>
**(a)** No. It needs the card identifier to group payments into a card's
history, and seven days of history; the file has neither.

**(b)** Only up to an unknown offset. `Time` counts seconds from the
first payment in the file, not from midnight, so `Time` modulo 86,400
gives a time of day shifted by the unknown clock time of that first
payment. The daily rhythm is visible; the actual hours are not.

**(c)** Yes. It needs only `Amount` and the order given by `Time`, both
released.

**(d)** No. The merchant, if it was among the original features, is
mixed into the principal components and cannot be recovered as an
identifier to group by.

The pattern: anything defined on a single row survives anonymisation;
anything defined on a group of rows sharing an entity does not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## The transaction simulator: a world whose rules are known

The ULB set removed the entity to protect it. The opposite route is a
generator that has entities from the start, because nobody real stands
behind them. In 2021 the same ULB group published an open-source
transaction simulator together with a reference dataset generated by it.

The first objection is that simulated data is fake and a result on it
says nothing. That misses what the simulator is for. Because its rules
are known, it is a test with an answer key: when a detector finds a
pattern, you can check whether it found the pattern that was put there.
And it has what real public data lacks: customers, terminals and half a
year of time.

### The generating rules

The world is a square grid, 100 units on a side. On it sit 5,000
customers and 10,000 terminals (a terminal is the merchant's point of
sale), each at a uniformly random location. A customer pays only at
terminals within a radius of 5 of their home, which leaves about 75
terminals each. Each customer gets three numbers, drawn once:

- a mean number of payments per day, drawn uniformly between 0 and 4; the
  actual count each day is Poisson with that mean;
- a mean amount, drawn uniformly between 5 and 100; each amount is normal
  with that mean and a standard deviation of half of it;
- payment times are normal around noon.

<Figure
  src="./figures/simulator-world.svg"
  alt="A 20 by 20 corner of the grid scattered with grey terminal dots at a density of one per unit square; a blue square marks a customer at the centre of an amber circle of radius 5, inside which about 75 terminals are amber; a panel lists the customer's parameters"
  caption="A customer and the terminals it may use: every payment it makes goes to one of the amber dots."
/>

Run for 183 days from 1 April 2018, these rules produce 1,754,155
payments, about 9,600 a day. None is fraud yet.

### Three scripted frauds

Fraud is added afterwards by three scenarios, each standing for a
pattern a detector must learn in a different place:

1. **Large amounts.** Every payment over 220 is fraud. No real fraud
   works this way; the scenario is a sanity check that any detector should
   pass, since a single split on the amount finds it.
2. **Compromised terminals.** Each day two terminals are drawn at random,
   and every payment at them over the next 28 days is fraud. It stands
   for a terminal used criminally, for instance by phishing customers who
   pay there. The amounts look normal, so the signal is in the terminal's
   recent history of fraud.
3. **Leaked card credentials.** Each day three customers are drawn, and
   over the next 14 days a third of their payments are multiplied by 5 and
   marked fraud. It stands for card details stolen and used remotely while
   the owner keeps paying. The signal is in the customer's own spending
   history.

The three scenarios map onto the three places a signal can live in <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="four-questions-the-card-loop-answered-without-asking">the
typology</Ref>: the single row, a terminal's history, a customer's
history. Run in that order, scenario 1 marks 978 payments, scenario 2
adds 9,099 and scenario 3 adds 4,604, for 14,681 frauds: a base rate of
0.84%, about 85 frauds a day.

Next to each payment's fraud label the generator writes a second column,
the *scenario*: which of the three scenarios made the payment fraud. A
payment can be touched by more than one scenario (a large payment at a
compromised terminal, say), and the column then records the last one.
That is why the column's counts differ from the printed ones. The printed
978, 9,099 and 4,604 count the payments each scenario newly made fraud;
the column credits 973 payments to scenario 1, 9,077 to scenario 2 and
4,631 to scenario 3, because a payment an earlier scenario had already
made fraud and a later one touched again moves to the later one. The
total is 14,681 either way. This column is what makes the simulator an
answer key.

<Example title="reading a detector against the answer key">
A decision tree of depth 2 is trained on one week of the simulated data
(25 to 31 July 2018), with the amount among its inputs. Such a tree asks
at most two questions in a row, so it has at most three splits and four
leaves. One of its splits sends payments with amount above **218.67** to
a leaf that is nearly all fraud: of the training payments that land
there, only two are genuine.

**What it found.** Scenario 1: the true rule is amount above 220. No
genuine payment anywhere in the data exceeds 219.98, because scenario 1
turned every larger one into fraud, so the two genuine payments in the
leaf are training-week payments with amounts between 218.67 and 220. The
tree does not know the rule; it picks the threshold that best separates
fraud from genuine in the training week. Had that range held only those
two genuine payments, a split just above them would have separated
better, so the range must also hold some frauds, from scenarios 2 or 3.
Catching them was worth letting in two genuine payments, and the split
landed a little below 220.

**What the key also shows.** Scenario 3 multiplies amounts by 5, so
2,281 of the 4,631 payments the scenario column credits to scenario 3
also exceed 220. A detector credited with
"catching large-amount fraud" catches half of the leaked-card fraud by
the same split.

**Why this needs generated labels.** On real data there is no rule to
compare the split with. Here the scenario column says which pattern each
caught fraud came from.
</Example>

### What the calendar shows, and what it leaves out

Plot the frauds per day and the first month looks different from the rest.

<Figure
  src="./figures/simulator-daily.svg"
  alt="Over days 0 to 182, an amber line of fraudulent payments per day rising from near zero to about 85 by day 28 and then fluctuating between 60 and 110, and a blue line of customers with at least one fraud that day, rising to and fluctuating around 75; the first 28 days are shaded and marked ramp-up"
  caption="Scenarios 2 and 3 keep a terminal compromised for 28 days and a card for 14, so the fraud rate climbs for four weeks before it settles."
/>

On day 0 only the terminals and customers drawn that day are
compromised. Each later day adds two terminals and three customers. A
customer stays compromised for 14 days, so from day 14 on the customers
leaving their window balance the ones joining, and the count holds at
$3 \times 14 = 42$. A terminal stays compromised for 28 days, so the
terminal count keeps growing until day 28 and then holds at
$2 \times 28 = 56$. The first month averages 57 frauds a day, the rest 85.

The blue line counts something else: customers with at least one fraud
that day, from any scenario. It settles near 75, well above the 42
leaked cards, because most of scenario 2's fifty-odd daily frauds fall on
different customers who happened to pay at a compromised terminal. Fraud
spread over customers this way is invisible in any one customer's
history, which is why scenario 2 can only be found from the terminal's.

Any study on this data has to start its training after the ramp-up or treat the
first month as a different regime; a model trained on April and tested on
September sees a base rate jump that is an artefact of the start of the
simulation, not of fraud.

Against the reading grid, the simulator supplies the entity (customer and
terminal) and the time (half a year). Four things it leaves out:

- **Labels are complete and instant.** Every fraud is labelled at the
  moment it is generated. A delay exists only if the analyst imposes one,
  by hiding the labels of the last $\delta$ days when training.
- **No feedback.** No model chooses which labels come back; every label
  is in the file.
- **No adversary.** The scenarios run the same script whatever a detector
  does.
- **No channel.** There is no card-present or card-not-present field, no
  merchant category, no country; the only features are the customer, the
  terminal, the time and the amount.

## PaySim: a replayed mobile-money log

The ULB set and the simulator both describe card payments. The third
dataset describes *mobile money*: an account held on a mobile phone, into
which a customer pays cash at a merchant and from which they pay, send
money to another user or withdraw cash again. Its label column is called
`isFraud`, and the question is what that column marks.

The obvious reading is that it marks the fraud of a real mobile-money
service, imitated. To check, look at what the generator was built to
copy.

### What was replayed

PaySim was built in 2016 from one month of logs of a mobile-money
service in an African country. The log itself stayed private. What the
generator took from it was an *aggregate file*: for each hour of the
month and each transaction type, how many transactions there were, and
the mean and standard deviation of their amounts. A line has the form:
day 1, hour 15, 8,703 transactions of type PAYMENT, mean 180,000, standard
deviation 15,000. Those numbers are an illustration of the format, not a
line of the real file, which was never published; the currency is not
disclosed either.

The five types are CASH-IN (cash paid in at a merchant), CASH-OUT (cash
withdrawn at a merchant), DEBIT (money sent to a bank account), PAYMENT
(paying a merchant) and TRANSFER (sending money to another user).
Generated *agents*, small programs that each play one client, draw for
every hour a transaction type, an amount and a counterparty from those
distributions, so that the totals per hour and type match the log.

<Figure
  src="./figures/paysim-replay.svg"
  alt="A table row from the aggregate file (day 1, hour 15, PAYMENT, count 8,703, mean 180,000, sd 15,000) in blue feeds a box of agents, which emits a synthetic row whose step, type and amount cells are blue and whose originator, destination and balance cells are amber; a legend says blue is matched to the log's hourly aggregates and amber is invented and never checked"
  caption="What a replay copies and what it makes up: the hourly totals per type come from the real log; who pays whom does not."
/>

One run, about 24 million transactions, was kept. It was chosen because
its aggregates had the smallest sum of squared errors against the log's, and
its fidelity was shown by overlaying, per hour and type, the count, sum,
mean and spread of the synthetic data on the original's.

<Figure
  src="./figures/paysim-fidelity-overlay.png"
  alt="Eight small line charts in four pairs, one pair per transaction type: CASH-IN, TRANSFER, CASH-OUT and PAYMENT. In each pair the top chart is the number of transactions per step (COUNT) and the bottom one is their total amount (SUM), plotted against the hourly step of the month on the horizontal axis. A continuous red line for the original log and a dashed blue line for synthetic run 41840 rise and fall together in a daily rhythm.   caption="How the fidelity of the kept run was checked. For each hour and transaction type, the real log's count and total amount (red) are drawn against the simulation's (blue, dashed). The two curves rise and fall together. Every point is a total over a whole hour; at no point is one account compared with another."
/>

The overlays track closely, and they show where the evidence stops. Every
check is on aggregates. Nothing compared a synthetic account's sequence of
transactions with a real account's, or the network of who pays whom with
the real network. The agents' individual behaviour, the very thing an
entity-history feature would read, was never validated.

### Where the fraud label came from

The 2016 generator injects no fraud at all: fraudulent agents are listed
as future work. The file people download, from
[Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/ealaxi/paysim1), is a later run
scaled to a quarter of the original size: 6,362,620 transactions over 744
hourly steps, with fraudulent agents added. Their behaviour is a script:
take control of a customer's account, transfer its balance to another
account, then cash it out.

That script explains the patterns people "discover" in the file. Fraud
occurs only in TRANSFER and CASH-OUT rows, because those are the two steps
of the script. It makes up 0.129% of all rows, about 8,200. It is spread
evenly over the 744 hours, while genuine traffic has a daily rhythm,
because the replay copied the log's rhythm for genuine transactions and
the fraud script has none.

The balance columns need a warning of their own, which the release
itself gives. Each row records the sender's and the recipient's balance
before and after the transaction. Inside the simulation the mobile-money
service runs its own fraud control, and a transaction it detects as
fraud is cancelled: the row stays in the file, but the money does not
move as it would for a completed transaction, so the balances after it
record a reversal rather than a transfer. Only fraud rows are ever
cancelled, so the way the balances relate to the amount differs
systematically between fraud and genuine rows. A model given the
balance columns can learn that difference, and the difference exists
only because the label was already decided: it is the label written a
second time, in another column. A real detector scores a payment before
anyone has decided to cancel it, so the release says these four columns
must not be used for fraud detection.

<KeyFact>
In PaySim the type mix and the hourly rhythm are copied from a real log;
the accounts' behaviour and the fraud are not. A detector trained on its
`isFraud` column learns a two-step script and the bookkeeping of
cancelled transactions, not mobile-money fraud.
</KeyFact>

## Elliptic: a graph whose labels come from names

The first three datasets concern card and account fraud, where a victim
eventually complains. The last two concern laundering, where nobody does,
and where the evidence lies in connections rather than rows. The first of
them is real.

### What it contains

[Bitcoin](https://bitcoin.org) is a currency whose every transaction is
written to a public ledger. A Bitcoin transaction takes coins from one or
more *addresses* (the identifiers that hold coins) and sends them to
others; owners are not recorded, only addresses. The Elliptic data set,
released in 2019 by Elliptic, a company that sells analysis of such
ledgers, turns part of that ledger into a graph: each node is a
transaction, and an edge runs from one transaction to the next when coins
flow between them.

It has 203,769 transactions and 234,355 edges, a small slice of a ledger
that holds over 400 million transactions. Each node carries 166 features.
The first 94 describe the transaction itself (its time step, the number
of inputs and outputs, the fee, the volume, averages over the addresses
involved); the other 72 summarise the same quantities over the
transactions one step before and after it in the graph. The feature names
are not released, for intellectual-property reasons.

The time structure is unusual. The nodes fall into 49 *time steps* about
two weeks apart, and each step is a single connected component of
transactions that reached the ledger within three hours of one another.
No edge joins two steps.

<Figure
  src="./figures/elliptic-snapshots.svg"
  alt="A row of 49 small boxes, the first 34 blue and marked train, the last 15 amber and marked test, with step 43 marked by a dashed line as the closure of a dark market; below, an inset graph of eleven transaction nodes joined by arrows, one amber (illicit), three blue (licit), the rest hollow (unknown), with a legend giving 2%, 21% and 77%"
  caption="Elliptic is 49 separate snapshots, not one growing graph, and most of its nodes carry no label at all."
/>

So the graph is 49 disconnected snapshots, each a three-hour window two
weeks from the next. A model cannot follow money from one step to the
next, and features built from the graph see only the neighbourhood inside
a snapshot.

### Where the labels came from

The ledger records addresses and never owners, so a label such as
"illicit" cannot be read off it: someone has to decide who controls a
transaction's coins and what kind of entity that is. Elliptic labels a
transaction by the entity that controls its input addresses. It is
*illicit* if that entity belongs to an illicit category (scams, malware,
ransomware, Ponzi schemes, terrorist organisations, dark markets) and
*licit* if it belongs to a licit one (exchanges, wallet providers, miners,
other legal services). Which entity controls which addresses is the company's own attribution,
built from heuristics. The main one: spending coins needs the key of the
address that holds them, so addresses spent together as the inputs of
one transaction usually belong to one owner.

That rule fails for one kind of transaction on purpose. A *mixer*
transaction pools coins from several owners as its inputs and pays them
out again, so that no output can be traced back to whoever paid in. It
performs the <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="placement-layering-integration">layering</Ref> step of laundering,
hiding where money came from, inside a single transaction. Its inputs
have several controllers, so there is no single entity whose category
could become the label, and the transaction stays unattributed.

Of the 203,769 nodes, 4,545 (2%) are illicit, 42,019 (21%) licit, and the
remaining 77% are *unknown*. Unknown is not licit: it means the company
could not attribute the transaction either way, because its owner was
never identified or because, as in a mixer, there is no single owner to
identify. A technique built to hide an owner therefore moves a
transaction into the unknown share, not into the illicit one. The split
also gives the dataset two base rates. Over all nodes it is 2%; over the labelled ones, the only
ones a classifier can be trained and scored on, it is one in ten.

  src="./figures/elliptic-steps-balance.png"

Bottom: stacked bars of nodes per step, between about 1,000 and 8,000, mostly blue unknown with an orange licit band and a thin green illicit sliver on top"
  caption="The 49 steps of the Elliptic set. Top: illicit nodes per licit node in each step, swinging from near zero to above 0.5 between neighbouring steps. Bottom: nodes per step, unknown in blue, licit in orange, illicit in green; every step holds between about 1,000 and 8,000 transactions."
  float="right"
  width="m"
/>

The standard use of the data trains on steps 1 to 34 and tests on 35 to
49, so it tests generalisation forward in time, which the ULB set cannot.
And it contains the one real change of regime among the five datasets: at
step 43 a large dark market closed, and all the models tested on the
data at its release fail to recognise illicit transactions after it, including a model
retrained at every step with the labels assumed to be available
immediately.

Against the grid, Elliptic has real transactions and real time, in
snapshots. It hides the entity: nodes are transactions, and no address or
owner identifier is released. Its label source is attribution by a
company, not investigation or a victim's complaint. Labels carry no
date, so there is no delay and no feedback.

## AMLworld: complete labels no bank has

Elliptic's labels cover less than a quarter of its nodes. <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="where-the-laundering-label-comes-from">The typology</Ref> explained
why real laundering labels are worse still: the only label is a bank's
own report, most laundering is never reported, and the report arrives
years later. Only a generator that knows where every unit of money came
from can label all of it. AMLworld, released in 2023, is such a
generator's output.

### What the world contains

AMLworld simulates individuals, companies and banks. People are paid
salaries and pensions, companies buy supplies and pay staff, and each
person or company may hold accounts at several banks and own shares of
other companies. A few agents are criminals, with income from one of nine
activities (smuggling, drugs, extortion, embezzlement and others). They
launder it through the three stages of <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="placement-layering-integration">placement, layering and
integration</Ref>, partly through eight standard shapes of transfers
between accounts (one account fanning out to many, many fanning in to
one, cycles, and combinations of these) and partly by spending it in ways
that look ordinary, such as a criminal boss paying employees.

Six datasets were published: a higher-illicit and a lower-illicit
version, each in small, medium and large, from 5 million transactions
over 10 days to 180 million over 97. Laundering runs from one transaction
in 807 to one in 1,942. As a check of realism, in the large lower-illicit
set the two shapes known as scatter-gather (money split across several
accounts and gathered again into one) and gather-scatter (the reverse)
make up one
transaction in 21,440; six months of real transactions at a large Italian
bank contained about one such laundering transaction in 21,000.

### Labels that follow the money

The label is *transitive*: the generator tracks illicit funds through
every transaction, including when they are mixed with legitimate money in
one account, and tags every transfer that carries them. <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology" id="exercises">The typology's exercise
on transitive taint</Ref> already worked out what this does to a model
moved into a bank: the transfers several hops downstream carry labels in
the simulation and none in the bank, so a score measured on one is not a
score measured on the other.

What AMLworld adds is the size of that downstream mass. In the large
lower-illicit set, 100,604 of about 176 million transactions are
laundering, one in 1,750. Only 19,461 of
them, about one in five, belong to one of the eight shapes; the other
81,143 are integration disguised as payroll, supplies and other ordinary
payments. A detector that looks for the shapes misses four laundering
transactions in five by construction, and only a dataset with complete
labels can show that miss. The generator records which shape each
laundering transaction belongs to, as the simulator records its
scenario, so this column too is an answer key: a detector's catches can
be sorted into the shapes it found and the ordinary-looking payments it
did not.

The release also leaves one thing out on purpose. Only the data was
published, not the generator, so that launderers cannot produce variants
of the data and test them against detection methods. The dataset is
static, and so is the laundering in it: the adversary was kept out of the
data along with the code.

## Five datasets against one loop

Each dataset, read alone, answered the grid's six questions in its own
way. Is there a part of the loop that every one of them leaves out, so
that no choice of dataset can supply it? Setting the five answers side
by side shows it.

### What every column is missing

Read the shaded rows first. None of the five records when a label became
known, so none contains the delay; and in none do the labels depend on
which rows an earlier model alerted on, so none contains the feedback
loop of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="the-fast-labels-are-a-sample-the-model-chose">the layered
system</Ref>. Both have to be imposed by the person running the
experiment. Only one dataset contains a real change of regime, and it is
an event (a market closing), not an adversary watching the detector.

Then read the columns. Each dataset hides a different part:

- the **ULB set** hides the entity and the calendar, and keeps real labels;
- the **simulator** keeps entity and calendar and hides the adversary,
  with labels that are complete because a script wrote them;
- **PaySim** keeps a real hourly rhythm and hides where its fraud came
  from, which turns out to be a two-step script;
- **Elliptic** keeps real transactions in time and hides the entity and
  most labels, the rest coming from attribution;
- **AMLworld** keeps the whole money trail and hides the incompleteness
  that defines real laundering labels.

### Which question each one can answer

A study question is about one part of the loop, so the dataset that can
answer it is one whose column has that part. The rule has three steps.
First, name the part the question is about: an entity's history, a span
of time, the labels, or the adversary. Second, take a dataset whose
scorecard column shows that part present; if none does, as for the delay
and the feedback, the part has to be built on top of a dataset by hand.
Third, name the parts the chosen column lacks, because the answer
silently assumes them. Whether an hour-of-day feature helps is a
question about time, and PaySim's hourly rhythm is copied from a real
log; but its fraud script has no rhythm at all, so the answer would
measure the contrast between a real rhythm and none, not between genuine
and fraudulent habits. Whether a model holds up at a laundering rate of
one in 1,750 (the large lower-illicit set's 100,604 laundering
transactions among about 176 million), scored against every laundering
transaction, goes to AMLworld and inherits laundering that never reacts
to the model.

<KeyFact>
Every file with complete labels (the simulator, PaySim, AMLworld) got
them from a generator, and a generator can also record which pattern
produced each crime: the simulator's scenarios, AMLworld's shapes, an
answer key no real log has. The price is the same in all three: the
crime is a fixed script that never reacts to a detector. The only real
change in the criminal population sits in Elliptic, whose labels are
partial, so no public dataset lets complete labels and a changing crime
be studied together.
</KeyFact>

You can now take any result reported on a public financial-crime dataset
and say what it can mean: what a row is and whether rows can be grouped
into histories, who wrote the label and how completely, and which part
of the detection loop the number cannot speak to. The first thing read
off each dataset was its base rate: 0.17%, 0.84%, 0.13%, one in ten of
the labelled, one in a thousand or two. <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics">The next lesson</Ref> takes up what to
compute from a detector's ranked output at such rates, when a team can
open only a hundred alerts a day and accuracy and the ROC curve no longer
say which model catches more fraud.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Run the 99%/99% detector of <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="what-share-of-the-alerts-are-fraud-the-base-rate-decides">the
economics lesson</Ref> (it flags 99% of positives and 1% of negatives) on
three base rates: AMLworld's large lower-illicit set (one in 1,750),
Elliptic over all nodes (4,545 of 203,769) and Elliptic over the labelled
nodes only (4,545 of 46,564). Give the precision in each case, then say
which Elliptic figure describes what a model scored on the dataset
experiences and why neither describes a real Bitcoin screening system.

<Solution>
Precision is $\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi / (\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi + \mathrm{FPR}(1-\pi))$
with $\mathrm{TPR} = 0.99$, $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.01$.

**AMLworld, $\pi = 1/1{,}750 \approx 0.000571$.**
$0.000566 / (0.000566 + 0.009994) \approx 0.054$: about 5 alerts in 100
are laundering.

**Elliptic, all nodes, $\pi = 4{,}545/203{,}769 \approx 0.0223$.**
$0.0221 / (0.0221 + 0.0098) \approx 0.69$.

**Elliptic, labelled nodes, $\pi = 4{,}545/46{,}564 \approx 0.0976$.**
$0.0966 / (0.0966 + 0.0090) \approx 0.91$.

**Which applies.** A model trained and scored on Elliptic is scored only
on labelled nodes, since the unknown ones have no truth to compare with,
so its reported precision lives at the one-in-ten rate: 91% for this
detector. That is the most favourable of the three numbers, by a factor
of 17 over AMLworld's.

**Why neither describes a real system.** The labelled nodes are those a
company could attribute, which overrepresents transactions of known
exchanges and known illicit services; the 77% that could not be
attributed are where an unknown launderer would be. The all-nodes figure
treats every unknown node as licit, which the labels do not say. A real
screening system scores every transaction, at a base rate nobody knows.
The transferable move: before quoting a precision, say over which rows
the base rate was counted.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "The ULB set has real labels produced by investigators, so a
model that reaches an average precision of 0.8 on a random split of it
would reach about 0.8 in production." Name the parts of the production
setting the file does not contain and the step of the claim that fails
first.

<Solution>
**The labels are real; the setting is not.** The claim's premise is
true: the labels come from investigators and disputes and are
near-complete. What fails is the step from "real labels" to "the same
score in production".

**The first failing step is the split.** A random split of two days
trains and tests on the same two days, with fraud of the same cards on
both sides. In production a model is trained on labels at least a delay
$\delta$ old, a week or more, and tested on later days, where the fraud
has moved on. Two days cannot contain a single delay window, so no split
of this file can imitate that.

**Missing parts.** No card identifier, so no history features and no
card-level ranking: production scores are computed with features this
file cannot have, and judged per card at a budget of about a hundred
cards a day. No label dates, so no delay. No feedback: in production the
fast labels exist only for the cards the previous model alerted on. No
drift over weeks.

The score is a statement about separating fraud from genuine payments
within two days, from anonymous per-row features. It is not an estimate
of a deployed system's precision.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
In the simulator, change two settings. (a) Scenario 2 compromises its
terminals for 1 day instead of 28. (b) Scenario 3 compromises its
customers for all of the remaining days instead of 14. For each, say
which kind of feature could still detect the scenario, and what happens
to the first-month ramp-up.

<Solution>
**(a) One-day terminals.** A terminal receives about one payment a day
(1.75 million payments over 10,000 terminals and 183 days), so a
compromise now produces about one fraud and is over the same day. A
feature counting the terminal's recent frauds sees the fraud only after
its label exists, by which time the terminal is clean again; with any
label delay it never sees it. The fraud payment has a normal amount at a
terminal the customer normally uses, so neither the row nor the
customer's history shows anything. Scenario 2 becomes label noise that no
feature can learn. Its ramp-up shrinks to one day.

**(b) Permanent leaked cards.** Three customers join every day and none
leaves, so after day $d$ there are $3(d+1)$ compromised customers, about
550 by the end, one customer in nine. A third of their payments are five
times their usual size, indefinitely. A history feature such as "this
amount divided by the customer's 30-day average" works at first, but
after a month the average itself contains the inflated payments, so the
contrast shrinks: the fraud becomes the customer's normal. And the ramp-up
never ends: scenario 3's fraud count grows linearly over all 183 days, so
there is no stationary period at all, and every test period has a higher
base rate than the training period before it.

The hypothesis these probes expose: the scenario windows of 28 and 14
days are what make the simulated stream reach a steady state, and what
make history features informative. Both are choices of the script.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Running the simulator's three scenarios in order prints 978, 9,099 and
4,604 frauds, and the total number of fraud payments is 14,681, exactly
their sum. Someone concludes that no payment was hit by two scenarios.
The scenario column, which records the last scenario that touched each
fraud, counts 973, 9,077 and 4,631. (a) Explain why the printed counts always sum to
the total, whatever the overlaps. (b) From the two sets of counts, derive
how many payments were relabelled, and what can and cannot be said about
which scenario overlapped which.

<Solution>
**(a)** Each printed count is computed as the number of fraud payments
after the scenario ran minus the number before. Scenario 2's 9,099 is
therefore the number of payments it newly made fraud, not the number of
payments at compromised terminals; a scenario-1 fraud at a compromised
terminal is touched by scenario 2 and not counted again. Increments of a
running total always sum to the final total, so the equality holds for
any overlap and refutes nothing.

**(b)** Count the changes of the scenario column. Let $a$ be the
payments whose column passed from 1 to 2, $b$ those that passed from 1
directly to 3, and $c$ those that passed from 2 to 3, whether or not they
had been scenario 1 before. Each passage takes one payment from the old
scenario's count and gives it to the new one's:
$$
973 = 978 - a - b,\qquad 9{,}077 = 9{,}099 + a - c,\qquad 4{,}631 = 4{,}604 + b + c .
$$
The first gives $a + b = 5$, the third $b + c = 27$; the second, $c - a =
22$, follows from the other two, so there are only two independent
equations for three unknowns.

**How many relabellings.** Each passage is one relabelling, so there were
$a + b + c = 27 + a$ of them, and since $a$ lies between 0 and 5, between
27 and 32. A payment touched by all three scenarios passes 1 to 2 and
then 2 to 3, so it counts once in $a$ and once in $c$, and each equation
still counts it exactly where its column lost or gained it. In distinct
payments the bounds are the same: the $b + c = 27$ passages into
scenario 3 are 27 different payments, since a payment enters scenario 3
only once, and the $a$ passages from 1 to 2 add at most 5 more, fewer if
some of those payments went on to scenario 3.

**What cannot be said.** Exactly 5 scenario-1 frauds left scenario 1, but
how they split between scenarios 2 and 3 ($a$ against $b$) cannot be
recovered from the counts alone; it needs the data.

The transferable move: when a count is an increment of a running total,
the overlap is in the difference between "new" counts and "final" counts,
never in their sum.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The 2016 PaySim run has 24,638,052 rows: 4,496,947 CASH-IN, 9,014,407
CASH-OUT, 2,030,969 TRANSFER, 8,955,794 PAYMENT, 139,935 DEBIT. The
Kaggle file is a quarter-scale run with 6,362,620 rows, of which
2,237,500 are CASH-OUT, and 0.129% are fraud, all in TRANSFER and
CASH-OUT. (a) Using the 2016 type shares, estimate the number of
TRANSFER rows in the Kaggle file. (b) Estimate the fraud rate within
TRANSFER and CASH-OUT together. (c) If each fraud consists of one
TRANSFER followed by one CASH-OUT, estimate the fraud rate within each
type separately. (d) State what you would compute on the file to check
each estimate, and what would count as agreement.

<Solution>
**(a)** TRANSFER share in 2016: $2{,}030{,}969 / 24{,}638{,}052 \approx
8.24\%$. Applied to the Kaggle file: $0.0824 \times 6{,}362{,}620 \approx
524{,}000$ TRANSFER rows. As a check on the method, the 2016 CASH-OUT
share, $36.6\%$, predicts $2{,}329{,}000$ CASH-OUT rows against the
2,237,500 observed (35.2%), a 4% overestimate: the quarter-scale run keeps
the type mix to within a few percent.

**(b)** Fraud rows: $0.00129 \times 6{,}362{,}620 \approx 8{,}200$.
Candidate rows: $524{,}000 + 2{,}237{,}500 \approx 2{,}760{,}000$. Fraud
rate within the two types: $8{,}200 / 2{,}760{,}000 \approx 0.30\%$, one in
about 340, more than twice the overall rate because the other three types
carry no fraud.

**(c)** Half the fraud rows in each type, about 4,100: TRANSFER
$4{,}100 / 524{,}000 \approx 0.78\%$, CASH-OUT $4{,}100 / 2{,}237{,}500
\approx 0.18\%$. A fraud TRANSFER is four times as common, relative to
its type, as a fraud CASH-OUT.

**(d)** `value_counts` of `type` for (a), within a few percent of 524,000;
a cross-tabulation of `type` against `isFraud` for (b) and (c). Agreement
with (c) means fraud rows split nearly evenly between the two types; a
clearly uneven split would mean some frauds stop after the TRANSFER (for
instance because the cash-out was blocked), which the script's
description does not say. Either way, what is being estimated is a
property of the fraud script, not of mobile-money fraud.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Four study questions. For each, choose the dataset (or datasets) of this
chapter that can answer it, and name the part of the question the chosen
dataset cannot speak to. (a) Does a feature counting a terminal's frauds
over the last seven days improve a card-fraud detector? (b) Does a graph
model detect laundering better than a gradient-boosted tree when the
laundering crosses several banks? (c) Does a model trained before an
abrupt change in the criminal population keep working after it? (d) Does
the <Ref lesson="undersampling-calibration">probability correction for
undersampling</Ref> restore calibration on real card data?

<Solution>
**(a)** The simulator: it is the only dataset with a terminal identifier
and fraud planted at terminals (scenario 2). It cannot say whether real
compromised terminals behave like a 28-day script, and its labels are
instant, so the delay that a real terminal-fraud feature must respect has
to be imposed by hand.

**(b)** AMLworld: it has several banks, accounts and complete labels for
cross-bank patterns. It cannot say how the comparison looks when scored
against a bank's incomplete reports, and its laundering does not adapt.

**(c)** Elliptic: the dark-market closure at step 43 is the only real
change of regime in the five datasets. It is one event, the labels are
attributions rather than investigations, and there is no entity to
follow across it.

**(d)** The ULB set: the only real card labels, at a real card base rate
of 0.172%. It cannot say whether the correction holds over time, since it
spans two days and a single base rate.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
In the simulator, take a test week of days 129 to 135 and suppose the
labels of every payment up to day 128 are known when the test begins.
(a) Using only the scenario rules, estimate the share of the test week's
scenario-2 frauds that occur at terminals whose compromise began before
day 129. (b) Estimate the same share for scenario 3, for customers whose
compromise began before day 129. (c) A re-run of the generator gives 330
of 384 for (a) and 110 of 152 for (b). Explain what a model can exploit
in such a test week that has nothing to do with recognising new fraud.

<Solution>
**(a)** On test day $d$, the compromised terminals are the two drawn on
each of the days $d - 27$ to $d$: 28 draws. Those drawn on day 128 or
earlier number $28 - (d - 128)$. Over $d = 129, \dots, 135$ the share runs
$27/28, 26/28, \dots, 21/28$, averaging $24/28 \approx 86\%$. With equal
traffic at every terminal, about 86% of the week's scenario-2 frauds are
at terminals compromised before the week began. The re-run gives
$330/384 = 86\%$.

**(b)** The same count with a 14-day window: on day $d$, the share of
active draws made by day 128 is $(14 - (d - 128))/14$, running
$13/14$ down to $7/14$, average $10/14 \approx 71\%$. The re-run gives
$110/152 = 72\%$.

**(c)** Most test-week fraud continues compromises that were already
visible, with labels, before the week started. A model can score well by
remembering which terminals and customers had fraud in the last weeks
and flagging their next payments, without recognising any new
compromise. A bank would already have blocked many of those cards, so
those frauds would never reach the model. <Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">Temporal validation with delayed
labels</Ref> builds the test week the honest way: it leaves a delay
block between training and test, and removes from the test days the
payments of cards known to be compromised by then.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

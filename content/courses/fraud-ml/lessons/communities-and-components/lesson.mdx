A payment network has about 34,000 users and a few hundred known fraudsters. Investigators cannot look at every user, and a fraud ring is a group, not a node. How do you cut the graph into groups so that the few groups containing a known fraudster are where to look, and how do you then draw rings whose membership an auditor can check line by line?

<Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality">The previous chapter</Ref>
ended with a table of columns, one row per node: degree split by label,
triangles, walk counts, distance to fraud, betweenness. Every column
describes one node, and none of them sees that D, E and F of its toy
form a ring. The investigators' questions are about groups: which
accounts act together, and who else belongs with a fraudster already
caught.

Two different jobs hide in those questions. The first is to narrow the
search: find the few groups where a known fraudster sits, so that a team
which can work about <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="ranking-then-cutting-at-the-capacity">a hundred cases a day</Ref> looks there
first. A wrong group costs some wasted calls. The second is to assign
rings: produce a list "these seven users are one ring" that a bank will
act on, defend in a dispute, and reproduce next month. A wrong group
there freezes an innocent customer's account, and the bank must be able
to say why each name is on the list.

The obvious first move is to take each flagged user and list their
neighbours. That gives a set of suspects per fraudster, one hop deep,
overlapping, and with no boundary: a ring of seven whose members are
three hops apart is cut into pieces, and a fraudster who paid a large
shop pulls in the shop's thousand customers. What is missing is a
definition of "a group" that a computer can apply to the whole graph,
and each of the two jobs needs its own.

## Cutting a graph: what makes a split good

The simplest definition of a group says what a group is not: it is not
connected much to the rest. Split the nodes into two sides and count what
runs between them.

### The cheapest cut isolates one account

Take seven accounts and a month of transfers between them; the number
on an edge counts transfers between the two accounts, and is the edge's
weight. A, B and C transfer 20 times among themselves, D, E and F 27
times. G deals only with F, three times. Between the two sides run C–D
(3 transfers) and B–E (1).

<Definition title="cut">
For a split of the nodes into two sides $S$ and $T$, the *cut*
$\operatorname{Cut}(S, T)$ is the total weight of the edges with one end
in $S$ and the other in $T$. The *MinCut* split is the one with the
smallest cut.
</Definition>

The split everyone sees, $\{A, B, C\}$ against $\{D, E, F, G\}$, cuts
$3 + 1 = 4$ transfers. MinCut prefers something else: cutting G off
alone crosses only its 3 transfers with F. The cheapest way to separate
a graph is to detach its quietest node, and a group of one account is no
group.

### Pricing the cut by what it leaves on each side

The repair is to make a lopsided split pay. Two ways to do it divide by
the size of each side, measured either in nodes or in the weight inside
it. Write $\operatorname{in}(S)$ for the total weight of the edges with
both ends in $S$.

$$
\operatorname{RatioCut}(S, T) = \operatorname{Cut}(S, T)\left(\frac{1}{|S|} + \frac{1}{|T|}\right),
\qquad
\operatorname{MinMaxCut}(S, T) = \operatorname{Cut}(S, T)\left(\frac{1}{\operatorname{in}(S)} + \frac{1}{\operatorname{in}(T)}\right).
$$

Detaching G costs a RatioCut of $3 \times (1/1 + 1/6) = 3.5$, while
the two trading groups cost $4 \times (1/3 + 1/4) = 7/3$. With
MinMaxCut, G alone has nothing inside it, $\operatorname{in}(\{G\}) = 0$,
and the term $1/0$ makes the split infinitely expensive; the two groups
cost $4 \times (1/20 + 1/30) = 1/3$. Checked against every one of the 63
ways to split seven nodes into two sides, both criteria pick
$\{A, B, C\}$ against $\{D, E, F, G\}$.

then each half in two, and so on, needs a rule for when to stop. What is
missing is one number that judges a split into any number of groups.

## Modularity: the share inside, against a random baseline

"Most of the weight lies inside the groups" sounds like the right test,
and it fails at once: put every node in one group and all of the weight
is inside. The share inside has to be compared with the share that
would fall inside the same groups if the edges had nothing to do with
them. The <Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="the-two-ratios">dyadicity of the previous chapter</Ref> made the
same move for labels, comparing fraud–fraud edges with what chance gives.

### Counting edge ends

Every edge has two ends. The toy's total weight is $M = 54$, so it has
108 weighted edge ends. The *strength* $s_v$ of a node is the total
weight at it, its weighted degree: A has $8 + 6 = 14$, G has 3, and the
seven strengths add up to 108. For a group $C$:

- $e_{CC}$ is the share of edge ends that belong to edges inside $C$:
  $2\operatorname{in}(C)/2M$. For $\{A, B, C\}$, $40/108$.
- $a_C$ is the share of edge ends that sit at nodes of $C$, its
  strength over $2M$. For $\{A, B, C\}$, $(14 + 15 + 15)/108 = 44/108$.

If each edge end were attached to a node at random, with probability
proportional to the node's strength, an edge would have both ends in $C$
with probability $a_C \times a_C$. So $a_C^2$ is the share of weight that
would fall inside $C$ by chance, given only how active its members are.

<Definition title="modularity">
For a partition of a graph with total edge weight $M$ into groups
$C_1, \dots, C_k$, the *modularity* is

$$
Q = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \left( e_{ii} - a_i^2 \right),
$$

where $e_{ii}$ is the share of edge ends inside $C_i$ and $a_i$ the share
of edge ends at nodes of $C_i$. A group of a partition with high $Q$ is
called a *community*, and finding such a partition is *community
detection*.
</Definition>

<Example title="modularity of the two trading groups">
$\{A, B, C\}$: $e = 40/108 = 0.370$, $a = 44/108 = 0.407$, term
$0.370 - 0.166 = 0.204$.

$\{D, E, F, G\}$: inside weight $27 + 3 = 30$, so $e = 60/108 = 0.556$;
strength $21 + 19 + 21 + 3 = 64$, so $a = 0.593$, term
$0.556 - 0.351 = 0.204$.

$Q = 0.204 + 0.204 = 0.41$. Every one of the 877 partitions of seven
nodes scores lower.
</Example>

<Solution>

while the chance baseline falls only by a little, because G is a quiet
node. Unlike MinCut, modularity charges for cutting off a node that
belongs.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Two boundary cases show what the baseline does. One group holding every
node has $e = 1$ and $a = 1$, so $Q = 0$: nothing is gained over chance.
Every node alone has $e_{ii} = 0$ for every group, so $Q$ is negative
($-0.16$ on the toy). A partition earns a positive $Q$ only by putting
more weight inside its groups than the members' activity alone would.

## Louvain: climbing modularity one move at a time

Modularity says which of two partitions is better. Finding the best one
is another matter: seven nodes already have 877 partitions, and the
count grows faster than exponentially.

An older method attacks from the top. The betweenness of an edge,
defined like the <Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="betweenness-the-node-every-route-passes-through">betweenness of a
node</Ref>, counts the shortest paths running through it, and the few
edges between communities carry most of them. The *Girvan–Newman*
algorithm removes the edge with the highest betweenness, recomputes, and
repeats, and modularity picks the best of the splits it passes through.
It finds good
splits, but every round recomputes shortest paths from every node: on a
graph of a million edges that is a million rounds of a million searches.

### The gain of one move

The method used on large graphs, *Louvain*, climbs from the bottom
instead. It needs only one number: how much $Q$ changes when two groups
are merged, a single node joining a group being the smallest case.

<Proposition title="the gain of a merge">
In a graph of total weight $M$, let $X$ and $Y$ be two groups of a
partition with strengths $s_X$ and $s_Y$, joined by edges of total
weight $w_{XY}$. Merging them changes the modularity by

$$
\Delta Q = \frac{w_{XY}}{M} - \frac{s_X\, s_Y}{2M^2}.
$$

<Proof>
Only the terms of $X$ and $Y$ change. The inside share of the merged
group gains the ends of the edges between $X$ and $Y$: $2w_{XY}/2M =
w_{XY}/M$. Its strength share is $a_X + a_Y$, and
$(a_X + a_Y)^2 - a_X^2 - a_Y^2 = 2a_X a_Y = 2 s_X s_Y/(2M)^2 =
s_X s_Y/(2M^2)$, which is subtracted.
</Proof>
</Proposition>

The first term is the weight between the two; the second is the weight
chance would put between two groups that active. A merge pays when the
groups trade more with each other than their activity alone explains.

A node that already sits in a community has to leave it before it can
join another, and leaving is the merge run backwards: it changes $Q$ by
minus the gain of merging the node with the rest of its community. The
gain of a move is therefore the gain of joining the new community minus
the gain of staying with the old one.

<Example title="where C goes">
Suppose A and B have already joined, and C, alone, weighs its options;
$M = 54$, $s_C = 15$.

**Into $\{A, B\}$:** $w = 6 + 6 = 12$, $s_{\{A,B\}} = 14 + 15 = 29$:
$\Delta Q = 12/54 - 29 \times 15/(2 \times 54^2) = 0.222 - 0.075 =
+0.148$.

**Into $\{D\}$:** $w = 3$, $s_D = 21$: $\Delta Q = 3/54 - 21 \times
15/5{,}832 = 0.056 - 0.054 = +0.002$.

Both moves raise $Q$; C takes the larger and joins A and B.
</Example>

### Two phases, repeated

Louvain starts with every node as its own community and runs two phases.

**Phase 1, local moves.** Visit the nodes one at a time. Each node
computes, for each neighbouring community, the gain of joining it minus
the gain of staying, moves to the best one if that difference is
positive, and otherwise stays. Repeat the
passes until a full pass moves nobody.

**Phase 2, aggregation.** Replace each community by one node. Its
internal weight becomes a self-loop, and the weights between communities
are summed into single edges. Then run phase 1 on this smaller graph.

Each repetition of the pair is a *level*, and the levels form a
hierarchy: small tight communities at level 1, merged into larger ones
at level 2, and so on until no move raises $Q$.

Each move is a sum over one node's edges, so a pass costs about one
sweep of the edge list, and the graph shrinks at every level. That is
why Louvain handles graphs of millions of nodes in minutes. The price is
that it climbs: it stops where no single move helps, which need not be
the highest $Q$ there is, and where it stops can depend on the order in
which nodes are visited. On the toy every one of the 5,040 visiting
orders ends in the same two groups; on a graph with many near-ties two
runs can put a borderline account in different communities, and the
numbers that name the communities differ from run to run even when the
membership does not.

## Narrowing the search on a peer-to-peer graph

The <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="what-a-fraudster-has-to-reuse">peer-to-peer payment graph of the
first graph chapter</Ref> has users who send each other money, the cards
they register, the devices they log in from and the network addresses
they connect from: about 33,700 users in a graph of roughly 790,000 nodes,
the rest being cards, devices and addresses. About 0.7% of the users, 241 of them,
are flagged as fraudsters, each after at least one card chargeback (a
payment reversed because the cardholder disputed it).

### The run

Louvain is run on users, cards and devices, with transfers, card
registrations and device logins as edges. Network addresses are left
out: a few of them are shared by so many users that they would tie
unrelated people together. The result is about 11,500 communities, with
modularity about 0.98. About 200 of them, 1.7%, contain at least one
flagged user.

A note: 98% of the communities are set aside, not 98% of the users"
  caption="One run turns the whole graph into a short list of communities, ranked and handed to the investigators."

The investigators now have a list instead of a graph. They rank the 200
by the number of flagged members, which favours large rings, and by the
share of members flagged, which favours tight ones. Inside a top
community, the users not yet flagged are the targets: each is an
accomplice, a mule, a victim, or a bystander, and finding out which is
the call.

### What the two numbers say

The 98% is a share of communities, not of users. Communities differ in
size, and the 200 that hold a flagged user may well be among the larger
ones, so the share of users set aside is smaller by an amount the run
does not report. It is still the right kind of cut for this job: a
fraudster whose community holds no other flagged user is not missed,
because their community is on the list; what drops away are the
thousands of communities nobody has any reason to look at.

The modularity of 0.98 is the other number to read carefully. A
community's $e_{ii} - a_i^2$ approaches $e_{ii}$ when the community is a
small slice of the graph, so a graph that falls apart into thousands of
small pieces with few edges between them scores close to 1 under almost
any partition that follows the pieces (<Ref
lesson="communities-and-components" id="exercises">an exercise</Ref>
derives the bound). A payment graph in which most users register their
own cards, use their own devices and pay a handful of people is nearly a
set of islands, and a $Q$ of 0.98 is what such a graph gives under the
partition along its islands, rings or no rings.

<KeyFact>
Modularity contains no label. A $Q$ near 1 says the graph splits cleanly
into dense pieces; it says nothing about whether any piece is a fraud
ring, and on a fragmented graph it is high for almost any sensible split.
</KeyFact>

## Why a community is not a ring

For narrowing a search Louvain is cheap and good enough. For the second
job, a list of ring members the bank will act on, three of its
properties disqualify it, and none of them is a bug in the
implementation.

### Small cells disappear in large graphs

From the gain of a merge, two communities joined by weight $w_{XY}$
are worth merging exactly when

$$
w_{XY} > \frac{s_X\, s_Y}{2M}.
$$

The right side shrinks as the whole graph grows. Take a cell of three
accounts that transfer among themselves (three edges, so with one edge
out its strength is $s_X = 7$) and one edge to a neighbouring community
with 20 internal edges ($s_Y = 41$). In a graph of 100 edges the
threshold is $7 \times 41/200 = 1.4$: one edge is below it, and the cell
stays a community of its own. Embed the same cell and the same
neighbour in a graph of a million edges and the threshold is
$287/2{,}000{,}000$: the single edge clears it, and maximising $Q$ merges
the cell into its neighbour.

  src="./figures/resolution-limit.svg"
  alt="Two panels showing the same triangle of three amber accounts joined by one amber edge to a neighbouring group with 20 edges. Right, whole graph of 1,000,000 edges: one box around both, merge gain 10⁻⁶ − 7 × 41/(2 × 10¹²) greater than 0, swallowed"
  caption="Nothing about the cell changed; only the graph around it grew. The baseline term shrinks with the square of the graph's size."
/>

This is the *resolution limit* of modularity: two groups joined by a
single edge are merged whenever the product of their strengths is below
$2M$, so two groups of equal strength are merged whenever each is below
$\sqrt{2M}$, about 1,400 in a graph of a million edges. It is a property of
$Q$, so every algorithm that maximises $Q$ inherits it. A three-person
cell in a million-edge graph is precisely the group the investigators
want, and it is exactly what the community will not show as a group:
it shows up inside a larger community, mixed with whoever it trades with.

### A run is not a record

A ring list is evidence. It is filed with a case, challenged by a
customer's lawyer, compared with the list from the month before, and fed
to systems that key their records on the ring's identifier. All of
that needs a membership and a name that stay put, and Louvain's
<Ref lesson="communities-and-components" id="two-phases-repeated">move
with the visiting order</Ref>. A user who is in community 179,061 today
and in another community after a rerun with the same data has no stable
answer to "which ring are they in?"

### Density is not a reason

Ask why two users share a community and the only true answer is that
moving either of them out would lower $Q$. That sentence cannot be
checked by an auditor, and it groups by density, not by any pattern of
fraud: a fraudster and the honest buyers they cheated trade with each
other and land in one community together. What the second job needs is
a group defined by a rule a person can state, applied the same way every
time.

## Connected components: groups defined by a path

The strictest such rule ignores density altogether: two users belong
together if any chain of edges leads from one to the other.

<Definition title="connected component">
Two nodes are *connected* if a path joins them. A *connected component*
is a maximal set of nodes any two of which are connected. In a directed
graph, components that ignore edge direction are called *weakly
connected components*.
</Definition>

"Connected" is an equivalence relation: every node is connected to
itself, the relation is symmetric, and two paths that meet chain into
one. Its classes are the components, so the partition into components
is unique. There is no objective to maximise, no tie to break and
nothing for a visiting order to change, and the reason two users share a
component is a list of edges anyone can follow.

Run on the raw graph, the rule is useless. An identifier shared by
hundreds of strangers, like the <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="entity-resolution-shared-identifiers-become-edges">café's network
address</Ref> that gives one address to every customer, puts all of
them into one component, together with everyone each of them is
connected to; one popular payee does the same with everyone who paid
them. Components only mean something when the edges do.

### Union-find

The components can be computed without ever following a path, by
reading the edges once in any order and keeping, for every node, a
pointer to another node of its group.

- **make-set**: every node starts as its own group, pointing at itself;
  a node that points at itself is a *root*.
- **find(x)**: follow the pointers from x until a root; the root names
  x's group.
- **union(x, y)**: find both roots; if they differ, point one at the
  other.

```python
parent = {}

def find(x):
    parent.setdefault(x, x)
    while parent[x] != x:
        parent[x] = parent[parent[x]]  # point at the grandparent: later finds are shorter
        x = parent[x]
    return x

def union(x, y):
    rx, ry = find(x), find(y)
    if rx != ry:
        parent[max(rx, ry)] = min(rx, ry)  # the smaller id stays the root

for u, v in edges:
    union(u, v)
```

Because the larger root is always pointed at the smaller, the root of
every group is the smallest id in it. Each component is then named by
its smallest member, whatever order the edges arrived in: a stable
identifier, which is what the case files need.

<Proposition title="the order of the edges does not matter">
After union-find has read every edge of a graph, in any order,
$\operatorname{find}(u) = \operatorname{find}(v)$ exactly when $u$ and
$v$ lie in the same connected component.

<Proof>
The invariant: after any prefix of the edges, two nodes share a root
exactly when a path made of the edges read so far joins them. It holds
at the start, when nothing is joined and every node is its own root.
Reading edge $u$–$v$ either finds one root on both sides, and then a
path already joined them and the new edge joins nothing new; or it
merges the two groups, and every node of one is now joined to every
node of the other through $u$–$v$, while no other pair gains a path.
After the last edge, "joined by a path of edges read so far" is "in the
same component", which does not mention the order.
</Proof>
</Proposition>

on the order, the components do not.
</Solution>

### Cost and change

Each union is two finds, and with the pointer shortening in `find`
a find costs a small constant on average. Production code also attaches
the smaller group under the larger, which keeps the pointer chains short
but no longer leaves the smallest id at the root; it names each
component by its smallest member in one more pass after the run, so the
names stay stable. Union-find therefore runs in
close to one pass over the edge list; on the peer-to-peer users below it
takes milliseconds.

Edges also arrive over time. A new edge is one more union, so components
can be updated without starting again, keeping yesterday's roots as the
starting point. A removed edge is different: a card unregistered or a
rule tightened can split a component in two, and pointers only ever
merge, so union-find cannot undo a union. When edges are removed the
components are recomputed from scratch, which at this cost is cheap. A
weight threshold, keeping only edges above some weight before the run,
is the same kind of choice as which edges to draw at all.

## Drawing the edges so that the components are rings

Components on a graph mean "joined by any chain of these edges". So the
edges have to carry the hypothesis: an edge between two users should
exist only where the data says they are probably one person or one
operation. That is <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="entity-resolution-shared-identifiers-become-edges">entity
resolution</Ref>, the addition of an edge between two users who satisfy
a rule built from shared identifiers, and the component algorithm is
then run on those edges alone.

### Rule 1: a transfer between users who share a card

The first rule comes straight from what the flagged users have in
common, a chargeback: draw an edge between two users if one sent money
to the other and both have registered the same card. That is the middle
step of the <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="entity-resolution-shared-identifiers-become-edges">chargeback
scheme</Ref> the first graph chapter described: a
stolen card funds account 1, the money moves to account 2 before the
dispute arrives, and the reversal hits an empty account. On the
peer-to-peer graph the rule draws 6,240 edges.

### The high-degree identifier

The second rule wants users who share identifiers more broadly: cards,
devices, network addresses. <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="entity-resolution-shared-identifiers-become-edges">Sharing several
identifiers is harder to explain away than sharing one</Ref>, and a
café's network address, shared by every customer, joins strangers. For
components such an identifier does more than add noise. Kept, it joins
every pair of its users, and since one edge between two components
merges them whole, a single busy identifier merges every ring any of
its users belongs to, together with everyone those rings touch. It need not be innocent to do this, only shared for reasons that
have nothing to do with one operation; a library computer qualifies, and
so does a card number stolen and sold to many buyers at once.

What separates those identifiers from a ring's shared card is how many
users they serve. So count, for every identifier, the users attached to
it (its *degree* in the user–identifier graph, the degree of the
previous chapter taken at an identifier node), and ignore identifiers
above a cap when drawing edges.

The cap can fail in both directions. Set it too high and the café and
the library merge rings with their customers: every customer of the
café or the library lands in a ring's component and is labelled. Set
it too low and the identifiers that
join a real ring disappear too: a card used by three members of a ring
is a degree-3 identifier, and a cap of 2 discards it.

### Rule 2: several shared identifiers, one of them rare

The second rule combines both conditions: draw an edge between two users
if they share at least one card or device used by at most ten accounts
in all, and share more than two identifiers in total, counting cards,
devices and network addresses. The rare card or device is what ties
them; the extra identifiers make a coincidence unlikely. A café address
can still count toward the total, but it can never create an edge on
its own. On the peer-to-peer graph the rule draws 5,316 edges.

The same reasoning works far from payments. An insurer whose claimant A
shares a home address, a car, a bank account and a phone number with
claimant B treats the two as one person: four identifiers at once, each
used by few people, are hard to share innocently.

### Components on the resolution edges alone

Union-find runs on the 33,732 users with only the 11,556 rule edges;
transfers, card registrations and device logins are left out. Two users
share a component only if a chain of rule edges joins them, so a
component is a set of users that the two rules, applied transitively,
say are one operation.

<Figure
  src="./figures/component-sizes.svg"
  alt="Bar chart on a log scale of the number of components by size: 24,283 of size 1 in blue; 3,165 of size 2, 481 of size 3, 151 of size 4, 61 of size 5 and 62 of size 6 to 175 in amber; the largest has 175 users. Note: 452 users share a component with a flagged user, 241 flagged (118 of them alone in their component) and 211 not yet flagged"
  caption="Most users are joined to nobody by the rules. The groups that remain are mostly pairs and triples, and one has 175 members."
/>

There are 28,203 components. Most, 24,283, are single users the rules
joined to no one; 3,165 are pairs, 481 triples, and 62 have six or more
members, the largest 175. The labelling step marks every user who shares
a component with a flagged user as a fraud risk. That gives 452 users:
the 241 flagged ones, of whom 118 sit alone in their component, and 211
users nobody had flagged.

The 118 matter as much as the 211. Almost half the known fraudsters
share no rule edge with anyone: their fraud, as far as these two rules
can see, was not organised, or was organised through identifiers the
rules do not use. Components find the rings the rules describe and
nothing else.

### What an auditor reads

Every member of a component is there because of a chain of rule edges,
and each edge is a sentence: "U17 sent money to U42, and both registered
card 4471" or "U305 and U2210 share device 8812, used by three accounts
in all, and also card 6090 and a network address". The explanation of a
ring is the list of edges along a *spanning tree* of the component, a
set of its edges that joins every member with no cycle; the unions that
were not redundant during the run are one such set. One line per edge, every line
checkable against the raw records. Rerun next month on the same data and
the same rules give the same components under the same names.

<KeyFact>
Union-find is the simplest algorithm in this chapter; what makes
a component a ring is the edge rules and the identifier cap, which are
the analyst's hypothesis written as data. Change a rule and the rings
change; keep the rules and they never do.
</KeyFact>

## Checking the rings without labels

The 211 cannot be checked against labels: if the bank knew which users
were fraudsters, it would not need the rings. What can be checked is a
consequence of the hypothesis. If the rules found accounts used to move
money ahead of chargebacks, those accounts should carry a large share of
the money, far more than their numbers.

### The volume test

The 211 are 0.63% of the users. Transfers that involve one of them as
sender or receiver add up to 12.7% of all the money transferred on the
service. They also carry 65% of the money that flagged accounts sent to
or received from unflagged ones: most of the known fraudsters' money
moved through the users the rings added.

How large is 12.7%? A set of users chosen at random touches a transfer
if either of its two ends is in the set, so a random 0.63% of the users
touches about $1 - (1 - 0.0063)^2 \approx 1.3\%$ of transfers, and about
that share of the money if amounts do not depend on who sends them. The
211 touch about ten times that.

### What the test does not show

A random set is a weak baseline. Users who transfer a lot also register
more cards, use more devices and connect from more addresses, so they
share more identifiers and are more likely to be joined by rule 2 to
someone, flagged or not. Part of the ten-fold excess could come from
the rules favouring active users rather than from fraud. The sharper
comparison draws random sets of 211 users matched on activity, and
asks how often a matched set touches 12.7% (<Ref
lesson="communities-and-components" id="exercises">an exercise</Ref>
designs it).

Even a clean volume test proves no individual guilty. A FraudRisk label
says that a chain of rule edges joins this user to a user with a
chargeback; it is a reason to call, not a verdict, and the chain
itself rests on settings, the cap of ten and the demand of more than two
shared identifiers, that were chosen rather than measured.

<KeyFact>
Without labels, a ring is validated by a quantity its hypothesis
predicts, here money, and against a baseline that shares the rings'
selection bias, not against a random draw. The result orders the calls;
it decides nothing about any one user.
</KeyFact>

## The method: explore, hypothesise, encode, resolve, label, validate

Where did rule 1 come from? Looking inside the top Louvain communities
shows flagged users sending money to unflagged users who share their
cards. The two algorithms are therefore steps of one procedure, with the
analyst's rules between them: the density-based one finds what to look
at, the observation becomes a hypothesis, the hypothesis becomes edges,
and the deterministic one turns the edges into rings.

<Figure
  src="./figures/method-loop.svg"
  alt="Six boxes in a loop. Explore: Louvain, about 200 communities with a flagged user. Hypothesise: shared card plus transfer equals one controller. Encode, amber: entity-resolution edges, 6,240 plus 5,316. Resolve, amber: union-find components, 28,203. Label: components with a flag, 211 new FraudRisk users. Validate: share of P2P amount, 12.7% for 0.6% of users. A dashed blue arrow from validate back to hypothesise: tune the cap, add rules"
  caption="The density-based algorithm explores; the deterministic one assigns; the analyst's rules sit between them."
/>

The loop closes from validation back to the rules, and it is where the
cap of ten and the demand of more than two shared identifiers come from:
they are settings of the loop, not facts about fraud. A bank adds rules
(same device and same address; transfers in quick succession), weights
edges by the number of shared identifiers and thresholds them, and moves
the cap, judging each change by what it labels and what investigators
confirm. Every version produces a different but reproducible set of
rings, and the rules that produced it are written down with it.

<Conclusion>
You can now cut a graph two ways and say which job each cut does. A
plain cut prefers to detach the quietest node; RatioCut and MinMaxCut
pay for lopsided splits; modularity $Q = \sum_i (e_{ii} - a_i^2)$
compares the share of weight inside each group with what the members'
activity alone would put there, and scores the toy's two trading groups
0.41. You can run Louvain's moves by hand with the merge gain
$\Delta Q = w_{XY}/M - s_X s_Y/(2M^2)$, and say why its output narrows a search (one
run sets aside 98% of the peer-to-peer graph's communities) but cannot
assign a ring: the resolution limit hides small cells in large graphs,
reruns can move members, and "modularity grouped them" is not an
explanation. You can compute connected components with union-find, show
that the result does not depend on the order of the edges, and design
the entity-resolution edges, with the identifier degree cap, on which
the components are the rings; and you can check those rings without
labels, against a baseline that shares their bias.

A component labels every member the same, whether they sent money to the
fraudster yesterday or are four rule edges away, and it says nothing
about users joined to fraud by ordinary transfers rather than by rules.
The next problem is a score that lets suspicion flow from known fraud
along every edge, weaker with each step and with the age of each
payment: <Ref lesson="guilt-by-association">guilt by association</Ref>.
</Conclusion>

A graph has eight accounts and twelve unweighted edges: 1–2, 1–3, 1–4,
2–3, 2–4, 3–4 (the first four all joined), 4–5, 3–6, 5–6, 5–7, 6–7 and
7–8. For the split $S = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$, $T = \{5, 6, 7, 8\}$, compute the
cut, RatioCut, MinMaxCut and the modularity of the partition $\{S, T\}$.
Which split does MinCut choose, and what is its RatioCut?

<Solution>
**Cut.** The edges 4–5 and 3–6 cross: $\operatorname{Cut} = 2$.

**RatioCut.** $2 \times (1/4 + 1/4) = 1$.

**MinMaxCut.** $\operatorname{in}(S) = 6$ (the four accounts all
joined), $\operatorname{in}(T) = 4$ (5–6, 5–7, 6–7, 7–8):
$2 \times (1/6 + 1/4) = 5/6$.

**Modularity.** $M = 12$, 24 edge ends. Degrees: 1, 2: 3 each; 3, 4: 4
each; 5, 6, 7: 3 each; 8: 1. Strength of $S$: $3 + 3 + 4 + 4 = 14$; of
$T$: $3 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 10$.
$Q = (12/24 - (14/24)^2) + (8/24 - (10/24)^2) = (0.500 - 0.340) + (0.333
- 0.174) = 0.160 + 0.160 = 0.32$.

**MinCut.** Account 8 has a single edge, so detaching it cuts 1, less
than the 2 of the balanced split. Its RatioCut is $1 \times (1/1 + 1/7)
= 8/7 \approx 1.14$, above the balanced split's 1: once the sizes are
priced, the balanced split wins, as on the toy of the chapter.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

On the nine users of the identifier-cap figure, an edge joins two users
who share any identifier of degree at most the cap; U2 and U7 are
flagged. (a) List the components and the users labelled as fraud risks
for caps of 1, 2, 10, 60 and 400. (b) Why does neither extreme find a
ring? (c) The real rule 2 also demands more than two shared identifiers.
Which of the toy's ring edges would survive that demand, and what does
it cost?

<Solution>
**(a)** Cap 1: every identifier has degree at least 2, so no edges; nine
components of one; labelled: nobody besides U2 and U7. Cap 2: k1 and d3
(degree 3) drop; d1 joins U1–U2, d2 joins U2–U3, k2 joins U6–U7;

U3, U6. Cap 10: k1 and d3 return; $\{U1, U2, U3\}$ and $\{U6, U7, U8\}$;
labelled U1, U3, U6, U8. Cap 60: the library PC d4 joins U3 to U4, U5,

labelled 61 unflagged (U1, U3, U4, U5, U9 and the 56 others) plus U6 and

holding both rings, U4, U5, U9 and the 449 users not drawn, all

**(b)** A cap of 1 draws no edge at all, since an identifier used by one
user is shared with no one: every user is alone and the method labels
only the already flagged. An unbounded cap lets the busiest identifiers
draw edges between strangers, and a single one of them merges every
component it touches, so the labels follow café visits, not fraud.
Rings exist only in the band between, here caps 3 to 59, and the band is
found by looking at what each cap labels.

**(c)** Count shared identifiers per ring pair: U1–U2 share k1 and d1

the 241 did on the peer-to-peer graph. The demand of a third shared
identifier buys confidence that an edge is no coincidence and pays for
it in recall: a ring whose members share exactly two things, which a
careful ring can arrange, is invisible to it. Rule 1 (transfer plus
shared card) is what catches pairs that share fewer identifiers but move
money between them.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Show that for any partition into $k$ groups, $Q \leq 1 - 1/k$, with
equality exactly when no edge runs between groups and every group has
the same strength. Then compute $Q$ for a graph made of 11,500 separate
pieces of equal strength, partitioned into those pieces.

<Solution>
$Q = \sum_i e_{ii} - \sum_i a_i^2$. The first sum is the share of edge
ends inside groups, at most 1, with equality when no edge runs between
groups. The $a_i$ are shares of all edge ends, so $\sum_i a_i = 1$. By
the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $(\sum_i a_i)^2 \leq k \sum_i a_i^2$, so
$\sum_i a_i^2 \geq 1/k$, with equality when all $a_i = 1/k$. Together,
$Q \leq 1 - 1/k$, with equality when both conditions hold.

For 11,500 equal pieces with no edges between them,
$Q = 1 - 1/11{,}500 = 0.99991$. Unequal pieces lower the bound only to
$1 - \sum a_i^2$, still near 1 as long as no piece holds a large share of
the edges. A graph that is already in pieces scores near 1 under the
partition that follows them, whatever is in the pieces.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "Louvain reached a modularity of 0.98 on the peer-to-peer graph,
so the communities containing flagged users are fraud rings with 98%
confidence." Name each step of the claim that fails.

<Solution>
**$Q$ is not a probability.** It is a difference of two shares of edge
ends, inside minus chance, summed over communities; nothing in it is a
probability of anything about a user.

**$Q$ contains no label.** The flagged users never enter its
computation. The same partition would score 0.98 if every flag were
moved to a random user.

**A high $Q$ reports fragmentation.** By the previous exercise, a graph
in many small pieces scores near $1 - 1/k$ for the partition along its
pieces; most users here register their own cards and use their own
devices, so the graph is nearly in pieces and 0.98 says mostly that.

**A community is not a ring.** A community holding a flagged user also
holds whoever that user trades with, victims included; a small ring
touching a larger group is merged into it by the resolution limit; and
membership can shift between runs.

What $Q$ does say: the partition puts far more weight inside groups than
the members' activity alone would, so the communities are a meaningful
way to shorten the list of places to look.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The 211 new fraud-risk users are 0.63% of 33,732 users and touch 12.7%
of the transferred money. (a) Derive the share of transfers that a
random set holding a fraction $f$ of the users touches, as sender or
receiver, and evaluate it. (b) Explain why the random set is a biased
baseline here, in which direction, and design a test that removes the
bias, stating what result would count as support for the rings. (c) Part
of the 12.7% is money that moved inside a component: between two of the
211, or between one of them and a flagged member of the same component.
Should those transfers count as evidence for the rings?

<Solution>
**(a)** A transfer is missed only if both ends are outside the set:
probability about $(1 - f)^2$ for a random set. Touched: $1 - (1 - f)^2
= 2f - f^2$. At $f = 0.0063$: $0.0126 - 0.00004 \approx 1.3\%$, by money
too if amounts do not depend on the users. The 211 touch about ten times
that.

**(b)** Rule 2 needs shared identifiers, and users who transfer more
also register more cards and devices and connect from more addresses,
so they are more likely to receive a rule edge and land in a component
with someone. The 211 are therefore selected partly for activity, and
activity alone raises the money they touch: the random baseline is too
low, and the ten-fold ratio overstates the evidence. A matched test:
bin all users by number of transfers and number of identifiers; draw
211 users at random with the same bin counts as the 211, many thousands
of times; compute the share of money each draw touches. The rings are
supported if 12.7% lies far in the upper tail of those draws (say,
above all of 10,000), and the size of the excess over the matched median
is the effect attributable to the rules rather than to activity.

**(c)** Not as they stand. Rule 1 draws an edge only where one user
sent money to another, so every rule-1 edge is a transfer inside a
component, and a user who entered the 211 through one is guaranteed to
touch that money: the test would count, as confirmation, the very
transfers that built the rings. A matched random set has no such
guaranteed transfers, so the matched test does not remove this.
Recompute the share with transfers inside a component left out, for the
211 and for every random draw alike. If the 211 still touch far more
money than the matched sets, the excess is evidence the rules did not
manufacture; if it collapses, the 12.7% was mostly the transfers rule 1
was built from.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A component of the rule graph has five users with edges U1–U2, U1–U3,
U2–U3, U3–U4 and U4–U5; U2 is flagged. (a) The labelling step marks
U1, U3, U4 and U5 alike. Using the adjacency matrix $A$ and the vector
$\mathbf{f}$ with a 1 at U2 (<Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="counting-walks-with-matrix-powers">walk counts from the previous
chapter</Ref>), compute the number of walks of length 1, 2, 3 and 4
from each user to U2. (b) Which user looks most suspicious by each
length, and what goes wrong as the length grows? (c) Propose one number
per user that uses walks of every length but trusts short walks more.

<Solution>
**(a)** $A\mathbf{f}$ counts neighbours of U2: U1 1, U3 1, U4 0, U5 0.
Then each step sums the previous vector over neighbours:

| length | U1 | U2 | U3 | U4 | U5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| 2 | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 | 0 |
| 3 | 3 | 2 | 4 | 1 | 1 |
| 4 | 6 | 7 | 6 | 5 | 1 |

(The U2 column counts walks from U2 back to itself.)

**(b)** At length 1, U1 and U3 tie and U4, U5 score 0. At length 3, U3
(4) leads U1 (3), not because it is closer but because it has more
neighbours to walk through. At length 4, U4 (5) has almost caught up
with U1 and U3 (6), although it never touched U2. Longer walks reach
everyone and the counts grow with degree, so a single length either
ignores distant members or ranks by how busy the neighbourhood is.

**(c)** Weight each length by a factor that shrinks geometrically,
$\sum_{k \geq 1} \beta^k (A^k \mathbf{f})_v$ for some $0 < \beta < 1$:
with $\beta = 0.3$, U1's first four terms are $0.3 + 0.09 + 0.081 +
0.049 = 0.52$, and summed to the end the scores are U1 0.62, U3 0.67,
U4 0.22 and U5 0.07. The sum converges only if $\beta$ is below
$1/\lambda_{\max}$, one over the largest eigenvalue of $A$; here
$\lambda_{\max} \approx 2.2$, so any $\beta$ below about 0.45 will do.
The scale still grows with degrees: U3 outranks U1, though both are one
edge from U2, only because U3 has three neighbours to U1's two. Normalising each step
so that the score is a probability, and restarting the walk at the known
fraud, gives the random walk with restart of <Ref
lesson="guilt-by-association">guilt by association</Ref>, which does
this for a million nodes at once.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The team can make 100 calls a day. The model's ranking of cards gives
the card at rank $r$ a probability $p_r = 0.3\,e^{-r/60}$ of being
compromised, a call costs $C_a = \text{€}10$ and a compromised card
found saves $V = \text{€}500$, as in <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="the-value-of-the-next-call">the staffing example</Ref>. The ring
labelling hands over 211 fraud-risk users. (a) If a fraud-risk user
turns out to be a fraudster with probability $q$, and finding one saves
the same $V$, from which rank should the model's calls be given up for
fraud-risk users? Evaluate for $q = 0.1$. (b) The volume test said the
211 touch ten times their share of the money. Does that give $q$? How
would you estimate it at a cost the team can bear?

<Solution>
**(a)** A call is worth its expected saving minus its cost: $V p - C_a$.
The cost is the same for both lists, so a fraud-risk call should replace
a model call wherever $q > p_r$. With $q = 0.1$: $0.3\,e^{-r/60} < 0.1$
when $e^{-r/60} < 1/3$, i.e. $r > 60 \ln 3 \approx 65.9$. Ranks 66 to
100, 35 calls a day, go to fraud-risk users; the 211 are worked in about
six days. Each such call is worth $500 \times 0.1 - 10 = \text{€}40$
against $500\,p_r - 10$ for the model call it replaces, which is just
under €40 at rank 66 and about €18 at rank 100: the swap gains about
€400 a day.

**(b)** No. Money touched is not the probability that a given member is
a fraudster: a few large accounts can carry the share while most members
are bystanders, and part of the excess is the activity bias of the rules.
$q$ is estimated by calling: investigate a random sample of, say, 20 of
the 211 first (two days of the swapped slots), count the confirmed
fraudsters, and keep swapping only if the observed rate stays above the
$p_r$ of the calls it displaces. With 20 calls the estimate is rough
($q = 0.1$ means two hits, and anywhere from zero to five is plausible),
so the comparison is revisited as more of the 211 are worked, and the
calls are best spent on the fraud-risk users of the components with the
most flagged members first.
</Solution>

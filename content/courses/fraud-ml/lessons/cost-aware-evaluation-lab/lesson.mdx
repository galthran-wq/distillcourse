The harness you build here is the referee for every later lab: forward-in-time folds with a delay block, precision at the investigator's budget, amount-weighted savings, and probabilities corrected for the undersampling you trained on.

Module 2 established each of those pieces as a result. <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">The metrics lesson</Ref> defined average precision as a step sum and card precision at $k$ as a daily count of compromised cards, with caught cards leaving the pool; <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">the validation lesson</Ref> built the training–delay–test geometry, known-card removal and the shift of $\delta + \Delta_{\text{valid}}$ that makes a validation fold admissible; <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">the imbalance lesson</Ref> derived the β correction and its matched threshold; <Ref lesson="example-dependent-cost">the cost lesson</Ref> priced decisions in money and derived the per-transaction minimum-risk rule; and <Ref lesson="ulb-handbook-benchmark">the benchmark lesson</Ref> read the handbook's numbers that all of them are checked against. What stands open is whether those definitions survive contact with code. This lab writes them as one module of seven functions and runs it on a 91-day, 890,000-transaction run of the handbook simulator.

The run reproduces the module's numbers in its own draw. Known-card removal takes the test week from 653 frauds to 443; a depth-2 tree scores a mean CP@100 of 0.29 against the handbook's 0.24; an uncorrected undersampled logistic regression fed to the minimum-risk rule raises 5,567 alerts and loses money, and the same scores after the β correction save 70% of the fraud. The depth sweep on four prequential folds shows the selection result: AP ranks the fully grown tree last, at little more than half the best value, while ROC-AUC climbs back up to it and leaves it within one fold-standard-deviation of the best depth.

The test week's labels did not exist when the model was trained, so a validation that mimics the test must shift its own training window back by the label delay plus the test width, and must drop the cards the bank already knew were compromised.

<Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">The first lesson</Ref> put the detector inside a loop whose labels return after a verification latency $\delta$, and <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">the metrics lesson</Ref> replaced ROC-AUC by average precision and card precision at $k$, computed per day with known and detected cards removed. What stands open is how to estimate those numbers before the week they describe has happened, and how to choose a model with the estimate. This lesson builds the validation geometry the rest of the course uses: a training window, a delay block the width of the latency, a test window, and the test days cleaned of cards the bank already knew. It shows on the handbook's simulator that random folds and training scores cannot stand in for that geometry, that a forward hold-out shifted back by $\delta + \Delta_{\text{valid}}$ can, that prequential folds also measure the test's week-to-week spread where repeated hold-out does not, and that the metric used for selection decides which tree depth wins.

## The deployment geometry: training, delay, test

The validation has to imitate something, and the thing it imitates is the moment a model goes live. That moment fixes which labels exist and which transactions the model will be asked to score.

(2) From <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">the metrics lesson</Ref>: which two kinds of card are removed before CP@k is computed on a test day? (2) Cards already known to be compromised before the test period, and cards detected on an earlier test day, which the bank has blocked. (3) Folds that train on an expanding past and validate on the block that follows it, so that no training row comes after a validation row.
</Aside>

### Three blocks and the clock between them

Discretise the transaction stream into blocks of equal length, here weeks. A model deployed at time $t_0$ will score the transactions of the next block, $[t_0, t_0 + \Delta_{\text{test}})$. To train it, the bank has every transaction before $t_0$, but labels only for those before $t_0 - \delta$: the frauds of the last $\delta$ days have mostly not been disputed yet, and their silence would be read as genuine.

<Definition title="delay period">
For a model deployed at $t_0$ under verification latency $\delta$, the *delay period* is the window $[t_0 - \delta, t_0)$. Its transactions are excluded from training because their labels are incomplete at $t_0$. The training window is the $\Delta_{\text{train}}$ days that precede it, $[t_0 - \delta - \Delta_{\text{train}},\ t_0 - \delta)$, and the test window is $[t_0, t_0 + \Delta_{\text{test}})$.
</Definition>

The delay period is not a buffer chosen for safety. It is the set of transactions whose labels the deployed model cannot have: training on them with their final labels uses information from the future, and training on them with the labels available at $t_0$ treats every fraud not yet disputed as genuine. Its width is the latency itself, $\Delta_{\text{delay}} = \delta$, and this lesson writes $\delta$ for both.

Blocks older than the training window are dropped as well. Spending habits and fraud scenarios change, so a block from three months back describes a regime the test week no longer follows; <Ref lesson="concept-drift">the drift lesson</Ref> measures how much training on all of history costs against a recent window.

<KeyFact>
Every validation scheme in this lesson is judged by one criterion: whether its estimate, made with the labels available at $t_0$, predicts what this geometry measures on the test block.
</KeyFact>

## Random folds train on the future

The validation habit of classical machine learning is $K$-fold cross-validation with rows assigned to folds at random. On a transaction stream it estimates a different quantity from the one the deployment produces, and the gap has three separate causes.

### How much of the training set lies after a validation row

Take a window of length $T$ with transactions spread uniformly in time, split at random into $K$ folds. Hold out one fold. A validation transaction at relative time $u \in [0,1]$ faces a training set of which a fraction $1 - u$ lies after it. Averaged over the validation transactions,
$$
\int_0^1 (1 - u)\, du = \tfrac12 .
$$
Half the training data postdates the transaction being predicted, whatever $K$ is. The forward split of the previous section has zero.

<Figure
  src="./figures/random-folds.svg"
  alt="Two panels of 21 days by four transactions each. Left, random 4-fold: about a quarter of the cells, scattered over all three weeks, are amber validation cells, the rest blue training cells, so blue cells lie after amber ones throughout. Right, forward split: week 1 all blue training, week 2 grey and unused as the delay, week 3 all amber validation"
  caption="A random fold scatters the validation rows through the window; the forward split puts all of them after the training rows and a delay block after those."
/>

<Example title="labels the deployed model could not have had">
On the same window, count the training rows whose labels a model deployed at the validation row's time $u$ could not have had: every row after $u - d$, where $d = \delta/T$ is the latency as a fraction of the window. A row at $u \le d$ has no usable training row at all; a row at $u > d$ has a fraction $\min(1,\, 1 - u + d)$ unusable. Averaged over $u$:
$$
\int_0^d 1\, du + \int_d^1 (1 - u + d)\, du = d + \frac{(1-d)(1+d)}{2} = d + \frac{1 - d^2}{2}.
$$
For three weeks of data and $\delta = 7$ days, $d = 1/3$, and the fraction is $1/3 + 4/9 = 7/9 \approx 0.78$. Under random folds, more than three quarters of the labels a validation row is predicted from were not available when that row was scored.
</Example>

<Exercise>
A window of four weeks is split into random folds, and the verification latency is two weeks. Compute the average fraction of training rows that postdate a validation row, and the average fraction whose labels were not available at the validation row's time. Write $d$ first.

<Solution>
**Postdating rows.** The fraction after $u$ is $1-u$, with average $\tfrac12$ independently of the latency and of $K$.

**Unavailable labels.** $d = 2/4 = 1/2$. The average is $d + (1 - d^2)/2 = 1/2 + (3/4)/2 = 0.875$: seven training labels in eight could not have been used. A longer latency raises the fraction without changing the window or the folds, which is why the random-fold estimate is more optimistic on streams with slower disputes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### Three carriers of the leak

**Labels from the future.** The previous computation: a model validated under random folds is fitted on labels, including fraud labels, that the deployed model will never see in time.

**Sibling frauds.** A compromised card or terminal produces fraud over days or weeks. In the simulator's scenarios, a compromised terminal turns fraudulent for 28 days and a compromised customer for 14. Under random folds, a validation fraud on such a terminal almost always has siblings from the same episode in the training folds, before and after it, and a model that recognises the terminal's other frauds scores it high. Dal Pozzolo and co-authors remove the card identifier from their feature vectors for the same reason: a classifier fed `CARD_ID` learns to detect repeated frauds of one card on different days, which a deployed system never sees, because the card is blocked after its first detected fraud. The unit that is independent, if anything is, is the card, not the transaction.

**Recency.** The rows nearest a validation transaction in time are the most similar to it, and random folds put many of them in training. Jurgovsky and co-authors start their test period more than a week after the training period for this reason: classification is more accurate on transactions that closely follow the training period, and that accuracy is not available to a production system whose labels are a week late.

<KeyFact>
A random split is not a noisier version of the forward one: it answers a different question, how well the model interpolates inside a period whose labels are complete, and every one of its three biases makes that answer higher.
</KeyFact>

All three push the random-fold estimate above what the deployment will measure, so they do not cancel. The correct replacement is a forward split with the deployment's own delay.

## Known-card removal: the test days the bank would actually score

The forward split removes the future from the training set. The test set also needs cleaning, because some of its frauds are not detection problems at the time they happen.

### The rule, day by day

A card whose fraud has been reported is blocked. Its later transactions are either refused or found without a model, so a test-day fraud on a card the bank already knew about would credit the detector with work the bank had done before. Which cards the bank knew about depends on the date and on $\delta$.

<Definition title="known-card removal">
For a test day $T$ under latency $\delta$, the *known compromised cards* are the cards with at least one fraud on a day $\le T - \delta - 1$, whose labels have arrived by the start of day $T$. *Known-card removal* drops every transaction of those cards from day $T$ before the day is scored.
</Definition>

The known set grows by one day of frauds per test day. In the handbook's `get_train_test_set`, it starts as the cards with a fraud in the training block, and for each test day $T$ it adds the cards with a fraud on day $T - \delta - 1$. On the first test day that day is the last training day; on the second it is the first day of the delay block.

<Example title="one test day under δ = 3">
Test day $T = 10$, latency $\delta = 3$. Frauds on days $\le 10 - 3 - 1 = 6$ have labels by the end of day 9, so their cards are known at the start of day 10. Eight cards transact on day 10:

- C1 had a fraud on day 5 and C4 on day 6: both known, both removed, along with their day-10 frauds.
- C2 had a fraud on day 8, inside the window of days 7 to 9 whose labels are still in transit: not known, so its day-10 fraud stays.
- C6's first fraud is on day 10 itself: stays.
- C3, C5, C7 and C8 have no fraud: stay.

The raw log has four frauds on day 10; the detector can be credited with at most two, C2 and C6.
</Example>

### What it removes on the simulator

On the handbook's test week the raw counts of 74 to 91 frauds a day fall to 41 to 60 once known cards are removed: between a third and a half of each day's frauds belong to cards whose earlier fraud was already reported. Those frauds are the easiest in the stream for a model trained on the reported ones, and including them would raise every metric while measuring nothing the bank needs.

Known-card removal and the removal of detected cards in <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">the metrics lesson</Ref> are different operations. The first depends only on the data and on $\delta$, and is applied when the test set is built. The second depends on the model, because it removes the cards that model's own alerts caught on earlier test days, and is applied inside the CP@k computation. The handbook's 0.241 for the depth-2 tree applies both.

## Training performance selects the deepest tree

With the geometry fixed, the first candidate estimate of test performance is the cheapest: the model's score on its own training week. The handbook sweeps a decision tree's maximum depth over $2, 3, \dots, 10, 20, 50$ on the split of the first section.

Training ROC-AUC and AP reach 1.000 at depth 50: a tree grown until its leaves are pure separates the training week perfectly. On the test week all three metrics peak at depth 5 or 6, at ROC-AUC 0.817, AP 0.588 and CP@100 0.280, and AP falls to 0.309 at depth 50. The test ROC-AUC does something the other two metrics do not: it falls to 0.739 at depth 20 and rises to 0.788 at depth 50, while CP@100 stays at 0.243. On the test week, ROC-AUC prefers depth 50 to depth 20 by 0.049 while CP@100 is identical for both: any selection procedure that maximises ROC-AUC inherits a pull towards the fully grown tree, whatever validation set it uses.
</KeyFact>

## Hold-out validation: the test geometry shifted back by δ + Δ_valid

The test block cannot be used for selection: at $t_0$ its transactions have not happened. The estimate has to come from the labelled past, by running the deployment geometry on dates where everything it needs is already known.

### The smallest legal shift

A validation geometry is the test geometry with the test block renamed the validation block, of length $\Delta_{\text{valid}}$, and the whole arrangement moved back in time. Two constraints fix how far: every label it uses, in its training block and in its validation block, must be available at $t_0$; and its training and validation blocks must be separated by the same delay $\delta$ as in deployment, so that it measures a model of the same staleness.

<Theorem title="the validation shift">
Let the test geometry have training window $[t_0 - \delta - \Delta_{\text{train}},\, t_0 - \delta)$, delay $[t_0 - \delta, t_0)$ and test window $[t_0, t_0 + \Delta_{\text{test}})$. A copy of the geometry with validation block of length $\Delta_{\text{valid}}$, shifted back by $s$ days, uses only labels available at $t_0$ if and only if $s \ge \delta + \Delta_{\text{valid}}$. At the smallest shift, the validation block is $[t_0 - \delta - \Delta_{\text{valid}},\, t_0 - \delta)$: it ends where the deployment's delay period begins.

<Proof>
Shifted by $s$, the copy's validation block starts where the test block started, $s$ days earlier: it is $[t_0 - s,\ t_0 - s + \Delta_{\text{valid}})$. Its training window ends $\delta$ days before its validation block starts, so the validation block holds the copy's latest transactions. Labels are available at $t_0$ exactly for transactions before $t_0 - \delta$. The copy is therefore admissible if and only if its validation block ends by then: $t_0 - s + \Delta_{\text{valid}} \le t_0 - \delta$, which is $s \ge \delta + \Delta_{\text{valid}}$. Substituting $s = \delta + \Delta_{\text{valid}}$ gives the stated block.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

With $\delta = \Delta_{\text{valid}} = 7$ days the shift is 14 days, two blocks. The handbook's code sets the training start for validation to `start_date_training - (delta_delay + delta_valid)`. The validation block is then 07-25 to 07-31, the test geometry's own training week: the newest block whose labels are all in at $t_0$.

  src="./figures/holdout.svg"
  alt="Two rows of week blocks from 06-20 to 08-08. The test geometry row has training at 07-25, delay at 08-01 and test at 08-08, with t0 marked at the test block. The validation geometry row, shifted left by two blocks, has training at 07-11, delay at 07-18 and validation at 07-25. A dashed vertical line at 08-01 is labelled t0 minus delta: every label used by validation lies left of this line"
  caption="The validation geometry is the test geometry moved back by δ + Δvalid: its validation week is the newest fully labelled week, the deployment's own training week."
/>

Validation repeats the test-day construction too. Each validation day $V$ has its known cards, those with a fraud on a day $\le V - \delta - 1$, removed, and those labels are available at $t_0$ because $V < t_0 - \delta$. A shift of only $\Delta_{\text{valid}}$, without $\delta$, would put the validation block inside the deployment's delay period, whose labels are still in transit.

### What one validation week predicts

For the depth-2 tree, the hold-out validation week gives ROC-AUC 0.775, AP 0.524 and CP@100 0.243, against 0.763, 0.496 and 0.241 on the test week. One week of labelled past predicts the next unseen week to within 0.03 on every metric.

Over the depth sweep the agreement is weaker where it matters. Validation AP is flat between 0.52 and 0.54 from depth 2 to 8 and falls after, which selects depth 3 against the test's 6; validation ROC-AUC is highest at depth 50 (0.820), and validation CP@100 is highest at depths 20 and 50 (0.259), where the test's CP@100 is 0.243. A single validation week is one draw of a quantity that varies from week to week, and a selection that compares draws differing by 0.01 is selecting on that variation. The next two schemes repeat the validation to measure it.

## Repeated hold-out and prequential: what each one varies

A validation estimate needs an error bar before it can select between candidates whose estimates differ by less than a week-to-week fluctuation. The two repeated schemes in the handbook produce one each, by resampling different things.

### Repeated hold-out resamples rows of one week

*Repeated hold-out* keeps the hold-out geometry and draws, for each of $n$ repetitions, a random 70% of the training week's transactions. Each repetition trains a model on its subsample and scores the same validation week. For the depth-2 tree, with four repetitions:

| | ROC-AUC | AP | CP@100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| mean | 0.776 | 0.519 | 0.245 |
| std over 4 repetitions | 0.0012 | 0.0065 | 0.0018 |

The spread measures how much the model changes when its training rows are resampled. It says nothing about how much the validation week differs from another week, because every repetition is scored on the same validation week. Training on 70% of the data also makes each repetition slightly weaker than the model that will be deployed.

### Prequential folds move the whole geometry back

<Definition title="prequential validation">
Let $s_0$ be the validation training start given by the validation shift, $s_0 = s_{\text{test}} - (\delta + \Delta_{\text{valid}})$. *Prequential validation* with $n$ folds builds, for $i = 1, \dots, n$, the geometry with training window $[s_0 - (i-1)\Delta_{\text{valid}},\ s_0 - (i-1)\Delta_{\text{valid}} + \Delta_{\text{train}})$, a delay of $\delta$, and a validation block of $\Delta_{\text{valid}}$ after it. Each fold trains on a full-size window; the estimate is the mean over folds, and its spread is their standard deviation.
</Definition>

Fold 1 is the hold-out. Fold $i$ is the same geometry $i - 1$ blocks further into the past, and every fold has the deployment's training size and delay. When $\Delta_{\text{valid}} = \delta$, as here, the validation block of fold $i+1$ sits exactly where fold $i$ had its delay block.

<KeyFact>
Repeated hold-out reports the uncertainty of retraining on one week; the uncertainty of a test estimate is dominated by which week it is, and only a scheme that varies the week, as prequential folds do, measures it.
</KeyFact>

The price is computation: prequential validation fits $n$ models per candidate where hold-out fits one, so the 11-depth sweep with four folds fits 44 trees instead of 11. The folds are also not independent draws: with a training window longer than one block, consecutive folds share most of their training data.

## ★ Stream evaluation proper: interleaved test-then-train

The handbook's prequential folds are a model-selection device: a fixed number of batch models, each trained once on a window and scored on a block. The name comes from a different procedure in the data-stream literature, which this section describes so that the two are not confused.

### Interleaved test-then-train

Gama and co-authors' survey of concept drift rules out cross-validation on evolving streams, because it mixes the temporal order of the data, and names two stream procedures. *Holdout* evaluation tests the current model on a held-out set from the current concept, an unbiased estimate when the concept active at time $t$ is known. *Prequential* evaluation, or interleaved test-then-train, uses every arriving instance first to test the model and then to update it, so that the model is always tested on an instance it has not seen. Its error after $n$ instances is the accumulated loss
$$
S_n = \sum_{t=1}^{n} f(\hat y_t, y_t),
$$
reported as the running mean $S_n/n$. No data are held out, and the error is a curve over time rather than one number.

### Forgetting, and labels that arrive late

The running mean weights the first instance as much as the last, so it reacts slowly to a change in performance. Two forgetting mechanisms fix this: a sliding window of the $w$ most recent losses, and a fading factor $\alpha < 1$ with $S_\alpha(n) = f(\hat y_n, y_n) + \alpha S_\alpha(n-1)$ and $N_\alpha(n) = 1 + \alpha N_\alpha(n-1)$, reported as $S_\alpha(n)/N_\alpha(n)$. Gama, Sebastião and Rodrigues show that both approximate the holdout estimate on the current concept.

With delayed labels, "test then train" becomes test at $t$, train at $t + \delta$: the update for an instance waits for its label. The handbook's folds are the batch, block-wise version of the same discipline: each block is predicted by a model that has seen only blocks whose labels were available a delay earlier.

## Model selection: the metric picks the depth

Validation exists to choose. The handbook's selection rule is the standard one: train each candidate $h_k$, compute its validation performance $A_{\text{valid}}(h_k)$, and deploy
$$
k^* = \arg\max_{k}\ A_{\text{valid}}(h_k).
$$
The choice of $A$ is part of the rule, and on the depth sweep it changes the answer.

### The selection triplet

For each metric the handbook reports the depth that maximises prequential validation performance, that depth's validation and test scores, the depth that maximises the four-week test performance, and its test score:

| | ROC-AUC | AP | CP@100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| depth selected on validation | 50 | 3 | 5 |

Selected by CP@100, the depth is the test optimum. Selected by AP, it is one step shallower, at a cost of 0.022 in test AP and 0.003 in test CP@100. Selected by ROC-AUC, it is depth 50, whose ranking is two blocks: its four-week test AP is 0.334 against 0.601 at depth 4, and its test CP@100 is 0.258 against 0.284 at depth 5. On a budget of 100 cards a day, that is about 2.6 fewer compromised cards found every day, 9% of what the CP@100-selected tree finds, chosen by a procedure that did everything else correctly.

The error is not one of validation. The prequential ROC-AUC curve follows the shape of the test ROC-AUC curve, including its rise at depth 50, which has the cause found in the depth sweep: a fully grown tree's two-block ranking. The validation estimated ROC-AUC faithfully; ROC-AUC rewarded the wrong tree.

<KeyFact>
The metric is necessary for a good selection but not sufficient: on the single hold-out week even CP@100 selects depth 20 or 50, 3.7 cards a day below the test optimum, and only the four prequential folds make the same metric pick the test-optimal depth 5.
</KeyFact>

### The pipeline in scikit-learn

None of scikit-learn's splitters (`KFold`, `StratifiedKFold`, `TimeSeriesSplit` and the rest) produces a delay block or known-card removal, and none of its scorers knows about days or cards. Both enter through hooks the library already has: `cv` accepts an explicit list of (train indices, test indices) pairs, and `make_scorer` accepts extra arguments. The handbook's construction:

```python
def prequential_split(df, start_train, n_folds=4,
                      d_train=7, d_delay=7, d_assess=7):
    folds = []
    for i in range(n_folds):
        start = start_train - timedelta(days=i * d_assess)
        train, assess = get_train_test_set(df, start, d_train, d_delay, d_assess)
        folds.append((list(train.index), list(assess.index)))
    return folds

def cp_at_k(y_true, y_score, top_k, transactions_df):
    frame = transactions_df.iloc[y_true.index.values].copy()  # day and card back
    frame["predictions"] = y_score
    return card_precision_top_k(frame, top_k)[2]              # mean CP@k
```

The split calls the same `get_train_test_set` that builds the test, so every fold carries the delay and the known-card removal. The scorer receives only labels and scores; it recovers each row's day and card by looking the fold's indices up in the full frame, which works because scikit-learn passes `y_true` as a slice of the `y` given to `fit` with its index intact. The index must be positional for `iloc` to find the rows. The grid search is then `GridSearchCV(pipe, param_grid, scoring={"roc_auc": ..., "average_precision": ..., "cp@100": make_scorer(cp_at_k, response_method="predict_proba", top_k=100, transactions_df=df)}, cv=folds, refit=False)`. `refit=False` is required: refitting fits the best candidate on every row passed to `fit`, which here includes the delay and test periods. Versions before 1.4 spell the response method `needs_proba=True`, which 1.6 removed.

<Ref lesson="cost-aware-evaluation-lab">The evaluation lab</Ref> builds this harness from scratch and checks it against the handbook's numbers.

The lesson has established the deployment geometry: a training window, a delay block whose width is the verification latency, a test window, and test days from which the cards known $\delta + 1$ days earlier have been removed. It has shown that random folds put half the training set after the row being predicted and most of its labels out of reach, that a validation copy of the geometry is admissible exactly when it is shifted back by at least $\delta + \Delta_{\text{valid}}$, that prequential folds reproduce both the test's mean and its week-to-week spread where repeated hold-out reports an eighth of the CP@100 spread, and that the selected depth is 50, 3 or 5 depending on whether ROC-AUC, AP or CP@100 is maximised. <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">The next lesson</Ref> runs the imbalance toolkit through this harness: class weights, SMOTE and undersampling raise ROC-AUC and lower AP, and an undersampled model's probabilities are wrong by a factor that has a closed form.

<Exercise>
Derive how often a random split leaks a sibling fraud, and show that the forward split with known-card removal never does. (a) A compromised card has $m$ frauds, assigned independently to $K$ random folds. For one of its frauds in the validation fold, compute the probability that at least one of its other frauds is in the training folds, and evaluate it for $m = 3$, $K = 4$ and for $m = 10$, $K = 5$. (b) Under the test geometry of this lesson with known-card removal, prove that no scored test fraud has a fraud of the same card in the training window. (c) Name the transactions of such a card that the forward split does still put in training, and say why they are legitimate.

<Solution>
**(a)** Each of the other $m - 1$ frauds lands in the validation fold with probability $1/K$, independently, so all of them miss the training folds with probability $(1/K)^{m-1}$ and
$$
P(\text{a sibling in training}) = 1 - K^{-(m-1)}.
$$
For $m = 3$, $K = 4$: $1 - 1/16 = 0.9375$. For $m = 10$, $K = 5$: $1 - 5^{-9} \approx 1$.

**(b)** The training window ends at $t_0 - \delta$, so every training fraud is on a day $\le t_0 - \delta - 1$. Every test day $T$ satisfies $T \ge t_0$, hence $T - \delta - 1 \ge t_0 - \delta - 1$, so every training fraud lies on a day $\le T - \delta - 1$ and its card is known on day $T$. Known cards are removed from day $T$. A test fraud that survives removal therefore belongs to a card with no fraud in the training window.

**(c)** The card's genuine transactions before its compromise, and its frauds inside the delay period, which are not in training at all. The genuine history is what the bank actually has when the card is attacked, and features built on it (<Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates">the aggregates lesson</Ref>) are the legitimate use of a card's past.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Set $\delta = 0$ and shrink the blocks to one day: train on all days before $t$, test on day $t$, and move forward one day at a time. (a) Show that this is Gama's interleaved test-then-train at the granularity of a day. (b) State what changes in the delay period, in known-card removal and in the validation shift. (c) Compared with the handbook's four prequential folds, name what this evaluation gains and what it loses. (d) Explain why its numbers would overstate a card system's performance.

<Solution>
**(a)** Each day is first predicted by a model that has not seen it, then added to the training data for the next day. That is test-then-train with the instance replaced by a day, and with a landmark window, since all past days are kept.

**(b)** The delay period $[t - \delta, t)$ is empty. Known cards on day $t$ are those with a fraud on any day $\le t - 1$. The validation shift becomes $\delta + \Delta_{\text{valid}} = 1$ day: the previous day is the validation block.

**(c)** It gains a test on every day of the stream, a performance curve over time that shows when the model degrades, and maximal use of the data. It loses the same-size training sets of the handbook's folds: with a landmark window the training set grows every day, so day 90's model is fitted on far more data than day 10's and their scores are not comparable estimates of one deployment. With a sliding window of fixed width the sizes are restored.

**(d)** At $\delta = 0$ every fraud is known the day after it happens, so the training data include yesterday's frauds and known-card removal blocks cards a day after their first fraud. A card system's disputes take days to weeks; under its real latency those frauds are unlabelled when the model is retrained and the cards are still active when they are scored. Both effects make the $\delta = 0$ evaluation easier than deployment.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "Repeated hold-out gives a confidence interval, so its mean ± 2 standard deviations is the honest uncertainty of the test estimate." Use the depth-2 tree's numbers from this lesson, compute the interval for CP@100 and for AP, check whether the test week's values (CP@100 0.241, AP 0.496) and the four-week test mean lie in it, and name what the procedure resampled against what varies between the validation and the test.

<Solution>
**The intervals.** CP@100: $0.245 \pm 2 \times 0.0018 = [0.241, 0.248]$. AP: $0.519 \pm 2 \times 0.0065 = [0.506, 0.532]$.

**Against the test.** The test week's CP@100 of 0.241 sits on the lower edge; its AP of 0.496 lies outside, below. The four-week test mean, CP@100 0.265 and AP 0.542, lies outside both intervals, above. The test weeks themselves vary with standard deviation 0.020 in CP@100 and 0.031 in AP, eleven and five times the repeated hold-out's.

**The failing step.** The interval quantifies the variation of the estimate under the resampling performed: here, 70% subsamples of one training week, all scored on one validation week. The test estimate differs from the validation estimate mainly because the test is a different week, with different cards, compromised terminals and daily fraud counts. That source of variation is held fixed by repeated hold-out, so its interval omits it. The prequential spread, CP@100 0.014 and AP 0.022, varies the week and is of the same order as the test's.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>

(a) Find the depth each metric selects on validation and the test CP@100 of each selection. (b) Compute the regret in CP@100 against the test optimum, in cards a day at $k = 100$. Design an experiment that decides whether depth 5 is better than depth 4 on this stream, and state what result would count as a decision.

<Solution>
**(a)** ROC-AUC: maximum 0.820 at depth 50; test CP@100 0.243. AP: maximum 0.538 at depth 3; test CP@100 0.267. CP@100: maximum 0.259, tied at depths 20 and 50; test CP@100 0.243 either way.

**(c)** The difference between depths, not either value, is what must be estimated. <Exercise>
Train on the handbook's training week (67,240 transactions, 598 frauds) after undersampling: keep every fraud and keep each genuine transaction with probability $\beta = 598/66{,}642$, so the training sample is about half fraud. Assume the classifier learns the posterior of the sample exactly. (a) Using Bayes' rule and the fact that undersampling does not change $p(x \mid y)$, write the sample posterior $p_s(x)$ in terms of the full-data posterior $p(x) = P(\text{fraud} \mid x)$ and $\beta$. (b) Evaluate $p_s$ for a transaction with $p = 0.0066$, the test week's fraud rate, and for one with $p = 0.5$. (c) Score the test week, whose fraud rate is 0.66%, and state roughly what the mean score will be compared with 0.0066, and what happens to AP and CP@100. (d) State which property of the map in (a) decides (c).

<Solution>
**(a)** Let $s = 1$ mean "kept". $P(s = 1 \mid \text{fraud}) = 1$ and $P(s = 1 \mid \text{genuine}) = \beta$, independently of $x$. Then
$$
p_s(x) = P(\text{fraud} \mid x, s = 1) = \frac{p(x)}{p(x) + \beta\,(1 - p(x))}.
$$

**(b)** $\beta = 0.00897$. For $p = 0.0066$: $p_s = 0.0066/(0.0066 + 0.00897 \times 0.9934) = 0.0066/0.01551 = 0.43$. For $p = 0.5$: $p_s = 0.5/(0.5 + 0.0045) = 0.991$.

**(c)** A transaction at the base rate is scored 0.43, sixty-five times its probability, and every transaction with $p \ge \beta \approx 0.009$ is scored at least $1/(2 - \beta) \approx 0.5$. The mean score on the test week is therefore orders of magnitude above 0.0066, although nothing about the test week changed. AP and CP@100 depend only on the ranking, and the map from $p$ to $p_s$ is strictly increasing, so a classifier that learned $p_s$ exactly ranks the test week as the one that learned $p$ does: the two metrics are unchanged by the distortion itself. In practice the undersampled model is fitted on 1,196 rows instead of 67,240, and that, not the distortion, is what changes its ranking.

**(d)** Monotonicity: the scores are wrong as probabilities and right as a ranking. Inverting the map turns $p_s$ back into a calibrated probability without moving a single rank; <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">the next lesson</Ref> derives that inverse, $p = \beta p_s/(\beta p_s - p_s + 1)$, and measures what rebalancing does to AP and CP@k under the prequential folds of this lesson.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

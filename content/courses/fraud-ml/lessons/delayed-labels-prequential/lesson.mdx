You want to know how well the model will do on next week's payments. But a payment's label arrives a week or more after the payment, so the latest labelled data ends a week before today, and some of the cards in next week's data are ones the bank already blocked. How do you cut the history into training and test sets so that the test number predicts what next week will show?

Take a concrete today. In <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="the-transaction-simulator-a-world-whose-rules-are-known">the
transaction simulator</Ref>, where day 0 is 1 April 2018,
today is day 129, 8 August. The bank wants to put a decision tree into
service this morning and tell the investigators what to expect: how many
of the hundred cards they call each day will turn out to be compromised.
<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="three-numbers-for-three-questions">The
metrics under an alert budget</Ref> gave the number to predict, card
precision at 100 ($CP@100$), and computed it for a depth-2 tree: 0.241.
That number came from a particular cut of the history, a training week of
days 115 to 121, a test week of days 129 to 135, and a gap of seven days
between them. Nothing so far has said why the cut looks like that.

<Remark title="a backtest">
The simulator's file does not stop at day 129: it holds the weeks after
it too, labels included. So you can stand at day 129, make the cut using
only what the bank held that morning, and then check the model against
what days 129 onward actually brought. That check is a *backtest*, and
every test number in this lesson is one: a measurement on weeks that,
from where the bank stands, have not happened yet.
</Remark>

The method a course in classical machine learning gives for this is to
shuffle the labelled rows, hold out a fifth of them, or run $k$-fold
cross-validation, and average. It rests on one assumption: the rows are
exchangeable, drawn independently from one distribution, so that any
subset is as good a test as any other. Each of the three facts in the
opening breaks that assumption in its own way. Payments arrive in time
order, and neighbouring payments share a card, a terminal and a date.
Labels have arrival dates of their own. And the bank acts on what it
learns, so some payments in the data would never have been scored.

## A shuffled split tests a model that cannot exist

The test number has a job: to predict what the deployed model will
achieve. So the first step is to write down exactly what the deployed
model does, and then see where the shuffle departs from it.

### What deployment does

On the night of day 128 the model is trained on the labelled payments the
bank holds. From day 129 on it scores every payment as it arrives, and
each morning the investigators call the hundred cards with the highest
scores. The model never sees a payment from after the one it is scoring,
and never sees a label the bank has not yet received. An honest test has
to put the model in the same position.

### What the shuffle does instead

In the backtest, shuffle the 21 days from 115 to 135 and draw a fifth of the payments as
the test set. Consider one leaked card from the simulator's third
scenario, whose details are used remotely for 14 days. Its owner makes
about two payments a day and a third of them are fraud, so the card
carries about nine frauds spread over two weeks. Each of them lands in the
test set with probability 0.2. When one of them does, each of its eight
siblings is in the training set with probability 0.8: on average six or
seven of them, and about half of those from days after the test fraud.
The chance that none of the eight is in training is $0.2^8$, about one in
400,000.

  alt="Two panels over days 115 to 135. Top, shuffled split: a leaked card's nine frauds and a compromised terminal's thirteen frauds as dots, most blue (training) and a few amber (test) scattered among them; a blue bracket over the card's training frauds on days 126 to 133 reads trained on days after the test fraud of day 124. Bottom, split in time: the same dots inside three shaded bands, blue training for days 115 to 121, a dashed grey delay band for days 122 to 128 where the dots are hollow, and amber test for days 129 to 135"
  caption="Shuffled, every test fraud has labelled siblings in training, many of them later. Split in time, training ends before the test begins."
/>

A compromised terminal is worse. The simulator's terminals receive just
under one payment a day, 0.96 on average, and every payment at a
compromised terminal is fraud for 28 days, so each such terminal carries
about $0.96 \times 28 \approx 27$ frauds, and a shuffled test
fraud from it sits among two dozen labelled frauds at the same terminal on
the days around it. The question the shuffled test asks is therefore "can
the model recognise a payment from a compromise it has already seen
labelled, including its future?" The deployed model has to answer a
harder question: payments from compromises that started yesterday, or
start tomorrow, with no labelled example yet.

Time also changes the stream itself. Fraud is time-evolving, the trait of
<Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="rarity-is-one-difficulty-out-of-five">the typology</Ref> by which
fraudsters change what they do once a pattern gets caught, so next week
is not distributed like the average of the last three. A shuffle averages
over exactly the differences the test is supposed to expose.

<KeyFact>
A test number predicts deployment only if the split reproduces
deployment's information: training on payments and labels that existed
when the model was trained, testing on payments that come after them. On
a stream that means cutting in time; no shuffle of the rows does it.
</KeyFact>

## Where the training set must end: the delay block

Cutting in time answers which side of the line a payment falls on. It
does not say where the line goes. The obvious answer is to train on
everything up to the night before the test, days up to 128, and test on
days 129 to 135. The labels say otherwise.

### The label clock

<Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="what-the-model-gets-back-two-kinds-of-label-on-two-clocks">The layered
system</Ref> defined the verification latency $\delta$: a day's payments
count as fully labelled $\delta$ days later, so on day $t$ the bank
receives the labels of the payments of day $t - \delta$. With
$\delta = 7$, the labels of day 121 arrive on day 128, in time for the
model trained that night. The labels of day 122 arrive on day 129, a day
too late. A model scoring day $d$ can therefore use labels only up to day
$d - \delta - 1$, which for the first test day is day 121.

<Figure
  src="./figures/label-clock.svg"
  alt="A row of day cells from 113 to 137. Days 115 to 121 are filled blue under a bracket labelled training block; days 122 to 128 are filled light grey under a bracket labelled delay block; days 129 to 135 are amber outlines under a bracket labelled test block; days 113 and 114 are faint and labelled dropped. A dashed vertical line before day 129 reads start of day 129: the model goes live. A legend reads payment and label known, payment known but label not yet, payments to be scored"
  caption="What the bank holds on the morning of day 129. The last seven days of payments exist, but their labels do not yet."
/>

The payments of days 122 to 128 are in the database: the bank processed
them, and they count toward the history of every card and terminal. Only
their labels are missing.

### Why not train up to day 128 anyway

The simulator labels every payment the moment it is generated, as <Ref
lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="what-the-calendar-shows-and-what-it-leaves-out">the canonical
datasets</Ref> noted, so the file does hold labels for days 122 to 128.
Training on them would give the test model information the deployed
model never had. It is also the most useful information there is,
because those frauds belong to compromises that are still running in the
test week. On day 129 the simulator has 56 compromised terminals, two
drawn on each of the last 28 days. The 14 drawn on days 122 to 128, a
quarter of them, have their only labelled frauds in those seven days. Of
the 42 leaked cards active on day 129, the 21 drawn on days 122 to 128,
half of them, are in the same position. A model trained through day 128
has labelled frauds from 21 leaked cards and 14 terminals that the
deployed model has never seen labelled. The effect is not peculiar to the simulator: on real card data a
classifier is noticeably more accurate on payments that closely follow
its training period, so a test placed right after training overstates
what a system without those labels achieves.

So the training set ends $\delta$ days before the test begins, and the
days in between are used for nothing that needs a label.

<Definition title="train, delay and test blocks">
Cut a stream of daily payments into consecutive blocks: a *training
block* of $\delta_{train}$ days, a *delay block* of $\delta_{delay}$ days
and a *test block* of $\delta_{test}$ days. The model is fitted on the
labelled payments of the training block and scored on the test block. The
delay block is as wide as the verification latency, $\delta_{delay} =
\delta$; its payments may enter the history features of later payments,
but none of its labels is used for fitting, for scoring or for
evaluation.
</Definition>

On the simulator all three widths are seven days. The training block,
days 115 to 121, holds 67,240 payments, 598 of them fraud.

### What the delay block is and is not

- **The delay block is not a validation set.** It is the part of the
  history whose labels do not exist yet on deployment day; using its
  labels for anything, tuning included, brings back the leak it removes.
- **Its payments are not discarded.** A card's spending over the last
  seven days, computed for a test payment on day 130, includes that
  card's payments of days 123 to 129, and that is legitimate: the bank
  saw those payments. A feature built from labels, such as a terminal's
  recent fraud rate, is a different matter, and <Ref
  lesson="point-in-time-features">point-in-time features</Ref> shifts it
  by the same delay.
- **One week is a simplification.** Real labels trickle in: an
  investigator's call labels a card the same day, a dispute arrives days
  or weeks later, and most of the feedback is in after about a week. The
  width of the delay block is a modelling parameter. Set it shorter than
  the bank's real latency and the test is optimistic; longer, and the
  model is trained on staler data than it needs to be.

Blocks older than the training block are dropped here. One week of
training is a choice made to keep the example small, and how much history
to keep on a stream that moves is a separate question, taken up with <Ref
lesson="drift-strategies-on-worldline">drift strategies</Ref>.

## Which test payments would really be scored: removing known cards

The test block now starts at day 129. On the raw file it holds between 74
and 91 frauds a day. Many of them are payments on cards whose fraud the
bank had already labelled. <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="investigators-fast-labels-on-the-alerted-cards">The layered
system</Ref> blocks a card as soon as it is found compromised, so in the
bank those payments never take place. The simulator does not block
anything: it keeps generating payments, and fraud, on a leaked card for
all fourteen of its days. Scoring them credits the model with frauds the
bank never sees, and they are the easiest ones, because the card's own
recent payments already look like fraud.

<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="a-detected-card-is-a-blocked-card">Card precision at k</Ref> already
removed the cards the model itself detects during the test week. The
cards the bank knew about from labels are the other half of the same
correction, and they need a date.

### Which cards count as known on a given day

A card is known on test day $d$ if a fraud of it was labelled by the
morning of day $d$, when that day's scoring starts. By the label clock
the labels of day $d - \delta$ arrive during day $d$, too late for that
morning, so a known card is one with a fraud on some day up to
$d - \delta - 1$. That date moves with the test day. On day 129 the
known cards are those with a fraud by day 121; by day 135 the labels of
days 122 to 127 have arrived as well, and the known set has grown.

<Figure
  src="./figures/known-card-removal.svg"
  alt="Two panels, scoring day 129 and scoring day 135. Each shows five cards a to e as horizontal bars of 14 compromised days starting on days 116, 120, 124, 126 and 130, a blue shaded region of known labels up to day 121 in the first panel and up to day 127 in the second, and an amber vertical line at the scoring day. In the first panel cards a and b are faded and listed as removed, c and d are scored; in the second, c and d are faded and removed, e is scored"
  caption="The removal date is the test day minus the delay minus one, so the set of known cards grows through the week."
/>

The five cards in the figure are the ones of <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="exercises">the exercise on the removal
date</Ref> that closed the metrics lesson. On day 129, cards a and b
started before day 122 and are known; c and d are compromised and still
unknown, so they are scored. On day 135, c and d have been known since
their first labels arrived, and only e is left to find. That exercise
counted a card as known from a first fraud on or before day $d - 7$,
which lets a label that lands during day $d$ count that same morning;
the rule here is one day stricter, and the five cards fall the same way
under both. The bug that exercise exposed is to fix the date at the end of training: that rule
keeps c and d in the test on day 135, two cards whose labels the bank
received in the middle of the test week.

<Definition title="known-card removal">
Before evaluating a model on test day $d$, remove every payment of day $d$
made by a card that has a fraud on some day up to $d - \delta - 1$.
</Definition>

On the simulator's test week the removal takes the daily frauds from 74–91
down to 41–60: 385 frauds among 58,264 payments, 0.66%, against 0.89%
in the training block. The lower base rate of the test week is the
removal at work, not a quieter stream. Seven days of 74 to 91 frauds come
to roughly 580, so the removal takes out about a third of the raw test
fraud: that is the share sitting on cards the bank already knew. It does
not take out every continuing compromise. <Ref
lesson="canonical-datasets" id="exercises">The canonical datasets</Ref>
estimated that most of a test week's fraud continues compromises begun
before the week, terminal compromises included, and the bank blocks
cards, not terminals: a fraud at a compromised terminal leaves the test
only if the card that paid there has a labelled fraud of its own. The
leaked card of the first figure is one of the removed ones. Its frauds of days 120 and 121
were labelled by the morning of day 129, so its frauds in the test week
leave the test.

<KeyFact>
A test day loses two kinds of card: the ones the bank knew from labels by
day $d - \delta - 1$, and the ones the model itself detected on earlier
test days. Both removals copy something the bank does, and a test that
skips either counts frauds no investigator would ever be shown.
</KeyFact>

### Why the card's identity stays out of the features

The same reasoning rules out a tempting feature. Give a model the card
number as an input, and on historical data it learns that certain card
numbers are fraudulent: a card with frauds on several days of the
training data teaches the model its own identity. In a backtest the model
then flags that card's later frauds and is credited for them. In the
bank, the card was blocked at its first detection and those later frauds
do not exist. The card number is still needed to compute the card's
history features, so it stays in the data and leaves the feature vector.
Dropping it and removing known cards guard against one leak from two
sides: the first stops the model from learning which cards were already
caught, the second stops the test from rewarding it.

## Choosing a depth without touching the test week: hold-out validation

The split now gives an honest test number for a model whose settings are
fixed. A decision tree has a setting that matters a great deal, its
maximum depth, and the bank has to choose it before day 129. The
obvious places to look both fail.

### Neither the training week nor the test week can choose

Train trees of depth 2, 3, … , 10, 20 and 50 on days 115 to 121 and
score them on their own training week. Every metric rises with depth, and
the tree of depth 50, which grows until its leaves are pure, reaches
ROC-AUC 1.000 and average precision 1.000: it fits its training week
perfectly. Scored on the test week, average precision rises from 0.496 at
depth 2 to a peak of 0.588 at depth 6, then falls to 0.309 at depth 50.
The training curve keeps climbing exactly where the test curve turns
down, which is overfitting as classical machine learning describes it.

  caption="Training performance only rises. The validation week tracks the test week, but its pick depends on the metric: AP chooses 3, AUC and CP@100 choose 50."

<Task>
In each panel, find the amber dot (the test week's best depth) and the
dashed blue line (the depth the validation week would choose). In which
panel are they close, and in which panel does the test curve rise at the
very depth the validation week picks?
</Task>

The test week cannot choose either. On day 129 it has not happened yet.
And even in a backtest, where its labels exist, picking the best of eleven
depths on the test week and then reporting that week's number reports the
largest of eleven noisy estimates, optimistic by construction. The test
week has to stay untouched until the choice is made.

### A validation week that stands where the test week stands

What the bank does have on day 129 is labelled history up to day 121.
Inside it, the deployment geometry can be rebuilt one step earlier: a
*validation block*, the latest week with complete labels, days 115 to
121, playing the part of the test week; a training block that ends
$\delta$ days before it, days 101 to 107; and a delay block in between,
days 108 to 114. The whole arrangement is the deployment one shifted back
by $\delta_{delay} + \delta_{valid} = 14$ days. This is *hold-out
validation*: the validation week is held out of training, and its
estimate stands in for the test week's.

<Task>
In the second frame, check that every label the validation row uses is
known on the morning of day 129. Which block of the deployment row does
the validation week coincide with?
</Task>

The validation week gets the same treatment as the test week, including
the removal: on validation day $v$ the known cards are those with a fraud
by day $v - \delta - 1$, the cards the bank knew then, not the larger set
it knows today. Each detail is copied because each difference in design
would be a systematic reason for the two numbers to part: a validation
week placed straight after its training would be optimistic, as the delay
block showed, and one cleaned with today's known cards would be harder
than the test week ever is. With the geometry copied, no difference in
design is left. One difference remains that no design can remove: the
two rows look at different weeks, with different compromises and a
different amount of fraud. How far that week-to-week variation moves a
score is measured in the last section. For the depth-2 tree:

| | ROC-AUC | Average precision | $CP@100$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Validation week (days 115–121) | 0.775 | 0.524 | 0.243 |
| Test week (days 129–135) | 0.763 | 0.496 | 0.241 |

The validation row only chooses the depth. The tree that goes into
service is not the one fitted on days 101 to 107: once the depth is
fixed, a tree of that depth is retrained on the deployment's training
block, days 115 to 121, because the deployed model should learn from the
freshest labelled week the bank holds. That refitted tree is the one
scored on the test week, so the test row above is the depth-2 tree of
<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="three-numbers-for-three-questions">the
metrics lesson</Ref>, and every test number below belongs to a refit.

Repeat this for every depth and choose by each metric. Average precision
picks depth 3 (validation AP 0.538), whose refitted tree scores test AP
0.562, a little
under the test-week best of 0.588. ROC-AUC picks depth 50 (0.820) and so
does $CP@100$ (0.259, tied with depth 20); on the test week the depth-50
tree has the worst average precision of all eleven and a $CP@100$ of
0.243 against a best of 0.280.

These are two different failures. Across depths, the validation $CP@100$
values span 0.243 to 0.259, a spread of 1.6 cards a day, and <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="what-each-disagreement-means">the
metrics lesson</Ref> already found that one week of seven days cannot
separate models closer than about two cards a day: the $CP@100$ pick is
noise.

The AUC pick has a part that repeats and a part that does not. On both
weeks AUC jumps between depth 20 and depth 50 (0.775 to 0.820 on
validation, 0.739 to 0.788 on test) at the very depth where average
precision hits its lowest; that jump is systematic. Where it lands is
not. On the validation week the full tree's 0.820 clears the best of the
moderate trees, 0.804 at depth 7; on the test week its 0.788 falls short
of depth 6's 0.817. Two questions follow: why the full tree earns a high
AUC with the worst AP, and why its rank against the moderate trees
changes from one week to the next.

## What AUC sees in the fully grown tree

The tree of depth 50 outputs only two scores, 0 and 1, because its leaves
are pure on the training data; <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="how-much-of-the-curve-a-hundred-alerts-can-reach">the metrics
lesson</Ref> drew its ROC curve as a single corner. With two scores, both
metrics reduce to formulas in two numbers.

### Two scores, one corner

Let $T$ be the recall of the block scored 1 and $F$ its false-positive
rate. By <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-the-team-sees-at-each-fraud">the tie convention</Ref>, the ROC
curve runs from $(0, 0)$ to the corner $(F, T)$ and on to $(1, 1)$ in
straight lines. Its area is a triangle plus a trapezoid:

$$
\mathrm{AUC} = \frac{F\,T}{2} + \frac{(1 - F)(T + 1)}{2}
= \frac{1 + T - F}{2} .
$$

Average precision, by the same convention, reaches every fraud in the
top block at that block's precision $P_1$, and every other fraud at the
precision of the whole list, the base rate $\pi$:

$$
\mathrm{AP} = T\,P_1 + (1 - T)\,\pi .
$$

<Example title="what the full-depth tree does on the test week">
Its test-week numbers are AUC 0.788 and AP 0.309, with $\pi = 385 /
58{,}264 = 0.0066$.

**Recall.** $F$ is small, so as a first pass $\mathrm{AUC} \approx (1 +
T)/2$, giving $T \approx 2 \times 0.788 - 1 = 0.576$.

**Precision of the top block.** $0.309 = 0.576\,P_1 + 0.424 \times
0.0066$, so $P_1 \approx (0.309 - 0.0028)/0.576 = 0.53$.

**False alerts.** The top block holds $0.576 \times 385 \approx 222$
frauds; at precision 0.53 it holds about $222 \times 0.47/0.53 \approx
195$ genuine payments, so $F \approx 195/57{,}879 = 0.0034$. Putting $F$
back into the AUC formula moves $T$ to $0.576 + 0.0034 \approx 0.579$,
which changes nothing below.

**Reading.** The tree flags about 420 payments in the week, 60 a day,
half of them fraud, and finds 58% of the fraud. AUC scores that as a high
corner at the far left of the plot: $\tfrac12(1 + 0.58 - 0.003)$. AP sees
the other 42% of the frauds tied at score 0 with nearly 58,000 genuine
payments, contributing $0.42 \times 0.0066 \approx 0.003$, and is left
with $0.58 \times 0.53$.
</Example>

AUC of a two-score model, $\tfrac12(1 + T - F)$, is the average of the
recall on frauds and the true-negative rate on genuine payments. It pays
for any gain in $T$ at a small $F$ and is indifferent to the frauds left
in the bottom block: they are all tied there, so there is nothing to
order. AP is capped near $T\,P_1$, because the frauds the top block
misses are reached at the base rate. A depth-6 tree, with up to 64 leaves
and as many scores, puts the frauds it does not score highest into
intermediate leaves reached at precisions far above the base rate, which
is why its test AP is 0.588 where the full-depth tree's is 0.309. That
answers the first question. A top block that finds 58% of the fraud at a
false-positive rate of 0.003 is worth an AUC of 0.79 whatever happens to
the other 42%; AP is charged for them.

<Exercise>
On the validation week the depth-50 tree scores AUC 0.820 and AP 0.324.
Take the validation week's fraud share as 0.0066 and its size as about
58,000 payments. Find $T$ and $P_1$ as in the example, and say what
raised the validation AUC above the test week's 0.788 while AP stayed
near 0.32.

<Solution>
**Recall.** First pass: $T \approx 2 \times 0.820 - 1 = 0.640$.

**Precision of the top block.** $0.324 = 0.640\,P_1 + 0.360 \times
0.0066$, so $P_1 \approx (0.324 - 0.0024)/0.640 \approx 0.50$.

**Correction.** With about $0.0066 \times 58{,}000 \approx 383$ frauds,
the top block holds $0.64 \times 383 \approx 245$ frauds and, at
precision 0.50, about 245 genuine payments: $F \approx 245/57{,}600
\approx 0.0043$, and $T \approx 0.644$.

**Reading.** On the validation week the tree's top block finds 64% of the
fraud instead of 58%, at about the same precision, one half. AUC rises
by half the gain in recall, $0.06/2 = 0.03$, from 0.79 to 0.82. AP stays
near $T \times P_1 \approx 0.32$, because the extra recall arrives at the
same precision and the missed frauds still sit at the base rate. A
selection by AUC sees the 0.03 and nothing else.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### Why the ranking changes from week to week

The exercise answers the second question. The full tree's AUC is almost
a function of one number, its top-block recall $T$, and $T$ belongs to
the week: it is the share of that week's fraud that resembles the
training frauds closely enough to land in a pure fraud leaf. It was 0.64
on the validation week and 0.58 on the test week. Half that difference,
0.03, is what moved the full tree from above the moderate trees to below
them. Nothing about the bottom block entered either verdict.

So the AUC pick fails twice over. It is **blind**: it chose the full tree
for its top block and never saw the 42% of the fraud left tied with
nearly all the genuine payments, which is where AP and $CP@100$ lose. And
it is **unreliable**: its margin on the validation week, 0.820 against
0.804, is half the 0.032 by which the full tree's own AUC moved between
the two weeks. Judged by AUC itself, the pick is wrong on the week it was
made for: 0.788 against 0.817 at depth 6.

<KeyFact>
A metric that cannot see part of the list ranks models only on the part
it sees. When two candidates differ mainly in the part it cannot see, as
the full tree and the depth-6 tree differ in the frauds below the top
block, its ranking follows how that week's visible part happened to
fall.
</KeyFact>

## How far one validation week can be trusted: repeated hold-out and prequential folds

On the hold-out week, depth 3 scores AP 0.538 and depth 4 scores 0.535.
Whether that difference means anything depends on how much a validation
estimate moves for reasons unrelated to depth, and one validation week
gives one number with no spread. The bank needs an interval.

### Repeated hold-out: a spread from one week

The obvious way to get one is to repeat the hold-out on resampled
training data: draw four random samples of 70% of the training week,
train a tree on each, score each on the same validation week, and report
the mean and the standard deviation of the four scores. This is
*repeated hold-out validation*. For the depth-2 tree it gives AP $0.519
\pm 0.0065$ and $CP@100$ $0.245 \pm 0.0018$, the second number being the
standard deviation over the four folds.

<Task>
In the first frame, count the different validation weeks the four folds
are scored on; then do the same in the second. Which of the two frames
resembles the bottom half of the third, where the real result is measured
week after week?
</Task>

That interval answers a narrower question than the bank's. It measures
how much the model changes when one training week is resampled, scored
on one validation week. Next week's uncertainty comes mostly from
elsewhere: next week holds different compromises, a different number of
frauds (the remaining frauds per day already ranged from 41 to 60 within
one test week), a different mix of the three scenarios. The four
repeated folds share all of that, so their spread contains none of it.
They also train on 70% of the data, which costs a little accuracy:
validation AP 0.519 against 0.524 for the full week.

### Prequential folds: a spread across weeks

The variation that matters is across weeks, so the folds should differ
by week. Keep the full deployment geometry and shift it back one block
per fold: fold 1 validates on days 115 to 121 as before, fold 2 trains on
days 94 to 100 and validates on 108 to 114, and so on to fold 4, which
validates on days 94 to 100.

<Definition title="prequential validation">
Given a test block that starts on day $d_0$, a delay $\delta$, and block
widths $\delta_{train}$ and $\delta_{valid} = \delta_{test}$, fold
$j = 1, \dots, J$ validates on the block of $\delta_{valid}$ days ending
on day $d_0 - \delta - 1 - (j-1)\delta_{valid}$. If that block's first
day is $s$, the fold trains on the $\delta_{train}$ days ending on day
$s - \delta - 1$, so that $\delta$ full days lie between the two blocks,
and it applies known-card removal with each validation day's own date. The
estimate is the mean over the $J$ folds, and its uncertainty is their
standard deviation.
</Definition>

The name comes from "predictive sequential": every fold predicts a stretch
of the stream from what came before it. The same word is used in <Ref
lesson="stream-learning-and-adaptation">stream learning</Ref> for a
finer scheme that tests on each payment and then trains on it; both
test only on data that comes after the training, and that lesson
separates the two.

For the depth-2 tree, prequential validation gives AP $0.550 \pm 0.022$
and $CP@100$ $0.256 \pm 0.014$. The standard deviations are 3.4 times
and 8 times those of repeated hold-out. To see which spread is right,
run the same geometry forward: retrain the depth-2 tree each week and
test it on the four real weeks from day 129 to day 156. Its AP over those
four weeks is $0.542 \pm 0.031$ and its $CP@100$ $0.265 \pm 0.020$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/spread.svg"
  alt="Two bar charts of standard deviations for the depth-2 tree. Average precision: repeated hold-out 0.0065 (grey), prequential 0.022 (green), four test weeks 0.031 (amber). CP@100: repeated hold-out 0.0018, prequential 0.014, four test weeks 0.020"
  caption="The real result moves from week to week by about 0.03 in AP and 0.02 in CP@100. Prequential folds see most of that movement; repeated hold-out sees a fraction."
/>

The first test week's AP of 0.496 is itself one draw from that spread,
about 1.5 standard deviations below the four-week mean, which is why
a single hold-out estimate of 0.524 and a single test number of 0.496
could disagree by 0.03 without anything being wrong.

<KeyFact>
Two candidates whose prequential means differ by less than about one
standard deviation of the folds are not separated by them. And since the
prequential spread still runs below the forward one, 0.022 against 0.031
in AP, that standard deviation is a floor on the real uncertainty, not a
margin of safety.
</KeyFact>

### What prequential selection picks

Run the prequential folds for every depth and pick the best mean by each
metric, then compare with the depth that did best over the four forward
test weeks.

| Metric | Prequential pick | Four-week test optimum | Test value at the pick | Test value at the optimum |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ROC-AUC | depth 50 (0.823) | depth 4 | 0.797 | 0.813 |
| Average precision | depth 3 (0.573) | depth 4 | 0.579 | 0.601 |
| $CP@100$ | depth 5 (0.268) | depth 5 | 0.284 | 0.284 |

Averaging over four weeks fixed the $CP@100$ pick: the noise that sent
the single hold-out week to depth 50 averages out, and the folds now pick
the depth the test weeks prefer. Average precision lands one depth away
from the optimum, 0.022 below it. Its folds put depth 3 at 0.573 and
depth 4 at 0.554, a difference smaller than either depth's fold spread
(0.027 and 0.038), so they could not have told the two apart. Its verdict
on the full tree is another matter: 0.342 against 0.573 is about nine
standard deviations, and the forward weeks agree (0.334).

ROC-AUC still picks depth 50, for the reasons of the previous section
rather than because it found a better tree. Its folds give the full tree
$0.823 \pm 0.013$ against $0.804 \pm 0.017$ at depth 5, a margin of about
one standard deviation: not separated. The margin rests on the full
tree's top-block recall, about $2 \times 0.823 - 1 \approx 0.65$ over
the four validation weeks against $2 \times 0.797 - 1 \approx 0.59$ over
the four forward weeks, and averaging four weeks cannot remove a
difference between the stretch before day 129 and the stretch after it.
Judged by AUC itself on the forward weeks, the pick loses, 0.797 against
0.813 at depth 4. On the other two metrics it loses badly: four-week test
AP 0.334 against 0.601, and $CP@100$ 0.258 against 0.284, about 2.6 fewer
compromised cards found every day.

<KeyFact>
The validation design and the selection metric are separate decisions.
Prequential folds make the estimate honest and its spread realistic; they
do not stop AUC from choosing the tree whose top block it likes. Select
by the metric the deployment is judged on, on folds that copy the
deployment.
</KeyFact>

The price is computation: four trees per candidate depth instead of one,
44 trees for the eleven depths. On a real stream with a longer training
block that factor matters, and <Ref lesson="gbm-model-selection">model
selection for boosted trees</Ref> weighs it against what the folds buy.

<Aside>
**In scikit-learn.** None of the library's built-in splitters builds these
folds. `KFold` and `ShuffleSplit` shuffle or cut without regard to time;
`TimeSeriesSplit` cuts in order, but by row count, its `gap` counts rows
rather than days, and it knows nothing about cards. The library accepts
any explicit list of `(train_indices, validation_indices)` pairs as its
`cv` argument, so the folds are built by hand, with the delay and the
removal applied, and passed to `GridSearchCV`. Two settings follow from
the geometry. `refit=False`, because the model that goes into service is
trained afresh on the latest training block with the chosen depth, not
on the union of the folds' training weeks. And card precision needs a
custom scorer that receives each payment's card and day, which the
standard scorer signature does not carry.
</Aside>

You can now cut a payment history the way deployment sees it: a training
block, a delay block as wide as the label latency, a test block, with
the cards the bank already knew removed from each test day by that day's
own date. You can place a validation week where the test week stands,
repeat it across weeks to get a spread that matches what next week will
show, and explain why a depth chosen by AUC loses on the metrics the
investigators live by. Every model so far was trained on the stream as
it is. <Ref lesson="undersampling-calibration">The next lesson</Ref>
trains on a stream from which most genuine payments have been thrown
away, and asks what that does to the probabilities the model outputs.

<Exercise>
A bank's history is cut into weekly blocks $B_1, \dots, B_{14}$, and the
model goes into service at the start of $B_{15}$. Labels arrive one week
late, and every block width is one week. Write down the training, delay
and test blocks of the deployment, then the training, delay and
validation blocks of four prequential folds. Which blocks are used by no
fold, and why is $B_{14}$ among them?

<Solution>
**Deployment.** The test block is $B_{15}$. Its delay block is $B_{14}$,
and its training block is $B_{13}$.

**Folds.** The most recent block with complete labels at the start of
$B_{15}$ is $B_{13}$, so fold 1 validates on $B_{13}$, with delay
$B_{12}$ and training $B_{11}$. Each further fold shifts back one block:

| Fold | Training | Delay | Validation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $B_{11}$ | $B_{12}$ | $B_{13}$ |
| 2 | $B_{10}$ | $B_{11}$ | $B_{12}$ |
| 3 | $B_{9}$ | $B_{10}$ | $B_{11}$ |
| 4 | $B_{8}$ | $B_{9}$ | $B_{10}$ |

**Unused.** $B_1$ to $B_7$ (older than any fold needs) and $B_{14}$.
$B_{14}$'s labels have not arrived at the start of $B_{15}$: it is the
deployment's delay block, so no validation can use its labels. A block
can be training in one fold, delay in another and validation in a third,
as $B_{11}$ is; each fold is a separate model, and within each fold the
geometry is the deployment's.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push the delay to its two edges on the simulator, keeping 7-day training
and test blocks and a test week of days 129 to 135. (a) Take
$\delta = 0$: labels arrive at the end of the payment's own day. Where
do the blocks sit, which cards does the removal rule take out on day
$d$, and which of the simulator's three scenarios mostly survive in the
test? (b) Take $\delta = 30$. Where do the blocks sit now, what does the
removal rule take out, and what happens to the number of frauds left to
find and to the ceiling of $CP@100$? (c) Is a $CP@100$ measured at
$\delta = 30$ comparable with one measured at $\delta = 7$?

<Solution>
**(a)** With $\delta = 0$ the delay block vanishes: training on days 122
to 128, testing on 129 to 135. On day $d$ the removal takes out every
card with a fraud on a day up to $d - 1$. A leaked card is then removed
from the day after its first fraud, so of scenario 3 only the first-day
frauds of the three cards drawn each day survive, about two frauds a day.
A card that pays at a compromised terminal is almost always a card with
no earlier fraud, since such a card was not stolen and is hit only by
paying there, so most of scenario 2's fifty-odd frauds a day survive, as
do the large-amount frauds of scenario 1 on cards with no earlier fraud.
The test becomes mostly a test of recognising terminal fraud and the
first payment of a new compromise.

**(b)** With $\delta = 30$ the training block ends 30 days before the
test: training on days 92 to 98, delay on days 99 to 128. On day $d$ the
known cards are those with a fraud by day $d - 31$. A leaked card is
compromised for 14 days, so a card known by then stopped producing fraud
more than two weeks earlier; a terminal-hit card is removed only if it
had an earlier, unrelated hit a month before. The removal takes out
almost nothing, the daily frauds stay close to the raw 74 to 91, the
compromised cards per day rise with them, and so does the ceiling
$\min(1, \gamma_t/100)$.

**(c)** No. At $\delta = 30$ the test has more fraud to find, and easier
fraud (continuing compromises the bank has not yet blocked), while the
model is trained on data a month old. A higher $CP@100$ there does not
mean a better model; it means the bank learns too late to block anything
itself. Card precisions are comparable only at the same $\delta$, which
should be the bank's real latency.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague proposes repeated hold-out with ten random 70% subsamples
instead of four prequential folds: "Ten folds give a standard deviation
of the mean about a fifth of the four prequential folds', so the estimate
is tighter and better." Using the depth-2 numbers of this lesson, refute
the claim and name the step that fails.

<Solution>
**What ten subsamples tighten.** The ten models are trained on resamples
of the same training week and scored on the same validation week.
Their spread, about 0.0065 in AP for any number of folds, measures how
much the model moves when the training rows are resampled, and the
standard deviation of their mean shrinks as $0.0065/\sqrt{10} \approx
0.002$, against $0.022/\sqrt{4} = 0.011$ for the four prequential folds:
the colleague's arithmetic is right. That is a precise estimate of one
quantity: this model family's score on days 115 to 121.

**What the bank needs.** The quantity to predict is next week's score,
and next week is a different week. Over four real test weeks the
depth-2 tree's AP moved with a standard deviation of 0.031, about five
times the repeated hold-out spread and fifteen times the standard
deviation of a ten-fold mean. An interval of $\pm 0.004$ around 0.519
would have excluded the first test week's 0.496 and the four-week mean
of 0.542 alike.

**The failing step.** "Tighter interval, so better estimate" assumes the
folds vary in the same way the target varies. They share the validation
week, so they cannot vary the way weeks do; more of them only shrinks
the interval around the wrong quantity. Prequential folds vary the week,
and their spread of 0.022 is the right order. They also train on full
weeks, where the subsamples throw away 30% of each.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the validation geometry instead of taking it from the figure.
Deployment tests on days $d_0, \dots, d_0 + \delta_{test} - 1$ with a
delay $\delta$. (a) Show that the latest validation block of width
$\delta_{valid}$ whose labels are all known on the morning of day $d_0$
starts on day $d_0 - \delta - \delta_{valid}$, so that the whole
geometry is shifted back by $\delta_{delay} + \delta_{valid}$. (b) Does
the shift change if the training block is two weeks wide instead of one?
(c) On validation day 117 of the simulator, which cards should be
removed: those with a fraud by day 109, or those with a fraud by day
121? What goes wrong with the other choice?

<Solution>
**(a)** On the morning of day $d_0$ the labels are known for days up to
$d_0 - \delta - 1$. The validation block must lie inside that range, and
the most recent choice ends on its last day, $d_0 - \delta - 1$. With
width $\delta_{valid}$ it starts on $d_0 - \delta - \delta_{valid}$. The
validation block is to its own model what the test block is to the
deployed one, so its delay block and training block sit at the same
offsets before it. Every block therefore moves back by $d_0 - (d_0 -
\delta - \delta_{valid}) = \delta + \delta_{valid}$, which is
$\delta_{delay} + \delta_{valid}$; on the simulator, $7 + 7 = 14$ days.

**(b)** No. The shift is fixed by where the validation block must end,
which depends only on $\delta$ and $\delta_{valid}$. A two-week training
block starts a week earlier in both rows; the offset between the rows
is unchanged.

**(c)** Those with a fraud by day $117 - 7 - 1 = 109$. That was the bank's
knowledge on day 117, and the validation week has to copy the test
week, where the rule uses each day's own date. Removing every card known
by day 121 would remove, from day 117, cards the bank had not yet
identified and was still being defrauded on: the frauds the model could
legitimately have found. The validation week would become harder than
the test week in a way the test week never is, and its estimate would be
pessimistic, mostly so on its first days.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Price the AUC pick and design the experiment that explains it. (a) Over
the four forward test weeks, the depth chosen by prequential AUC (50) has
AP 0.334 and $CP@100$ 0.258; the depth chosen by prequential AP (3) has AP
0.579 and $CP@100$ 0.281. How many compromised cards a week does the AUC
pick lose against the AP pick at 100 calls a day? With the value of
$V = \text{€}360$ per compromised card called used in the metrics
lesson's exercise on pricing card precision, what is that per week? (b)
This lesson attributes AUC's preference to the depth-50 tree's pure
leaves. Design an experiment on the same folds that would confirm or
refute that explanation, and state what result counts as each.

<Solution>
**(a)** The difference in $CP@100$ is $0.281 - 0.258 = 0.023$, that is
2.3 compromised cards per hundred calls, per day: about $2.3 \times 7
\approx 16$ cards a week. At €360 a card, about €5,800 a week, with the
calls costing the same in both cases since the team makes a hundred
either way.

**(b)** The explanation predicts that the depth-50 tree wins AUC because
it has only two scores, so that AUC becomes $\tfrac12(1 + T - F)$ and
rewards its high-recall top block. A control that keeps the depth but
removes the purity: rerun the prequential sweep over depths with
`min_samples_leaf` set to 1 (pure leaves allowed), 20, 100 and 500. With
a floor on leaf size the deep trees can no longer isolate single frauds;
their leaves are mixed, they output many distinct scores, and the
two-score formula no longer applies.

- **Confirms:** with `min_samples_leaf` of 100 or more, the AUC curve
  over depths flattens or peaks at moderate depth, and AUC's pick moves
  to within one prequential standard deviation of AP's pick, while with
  `min_samples_leaf = 1` it stays at 50.
- **Refutes:** AUC still picks the deepest tree with mixed leaves, by
  more than a standard deviation. Then the preference comes from how the
  deep trees order the bulk of the list, not from the two-score corner,
  and the explanation has to be revised.

Report the AP and $CP@100$ of each pick on the forward test weeks
alongside, since the practical question is whether the cap also closes
the gap measured in (a).
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Carry the geometry over to laundering, using <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="where-the-laundering-label-comes-from">the typology's account of
its label</Ref>. The label is a suspicious transaction report, filed
months to years after the transfers, and most laundering is never
reported. (a) How wide is the delay block, and what does it do to the
training data a validation fold can use? (b) What is a "known account"
for the removal rule, and when does it count as known? (c) The test
block's negatives are accounts with no report. What does that do to any
precision measured on it, whatever the geometry? (d) For which crime in
the typology is the geometry still workable, and why?

<Solution>
**(a)** The delay must cover the time to a report, months to years, so
the delay block is that wide and the training block ends that long
before the test. The model is trained on activity a year or more old,
against a crime whose able practitioners drop a method once it is
detected. Few folds fit in any history a bank keeps, each separated by
the same long delay, so the prequential spread rests on a handful of
weeks or months.

**(b)** An account is known once a report was filed on it and the bank
acted on it, typically by closing or restricting it. It counts as known
from the day that report entered the bank's records, which is the
day-$d$-minus-delay rule with the report's own date in place of a
fixed latency. Removing it copies the bank: it no longer processes
transfers on an exited account.

**(c)** An unreported account is not a clean account. The test's
"genuine" class contains an unknown amount of laundering, so a flag on
such an account counts as a false positive when it may be a correct
detection. Any precision is measured against what was reported, not
against what happened, and the geometry cannot repair that: it fixes
when labels are available, not whether they are complete.

**(d)** Money mules. Their report arrives within months, because rapid
in-and-out flows and daily sums trip transaction monitoring, so a delay
block of a few months is enough and a year of history holds several
folds. The geometry is workable where the label clock is short enough
for the stream not to have moved on by the time the labels arrive.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A published study has five weeks of card payments, numbered days 0 to 34,
with investigators' labels arriving within two weeks of each payment. It
uses weeks 1 and 2 only as a pool for computing each payment's history
features, trains on weeks 3 and 4, and tests on week 5. (a) Name what is
wrong with this split as an estimate of deployment, and show it with the
label clock on the first test day. (b) Rebuild a defensible split from
the same five weeks with a 14-day delay, and say what it costs. (c) What
else does the description leave unsaid that this lesson would require?

<Solution>
**(a)** There is no delay block. On day 28, the first test day, labels
are known only for days up to $28 - 14 - 1 = 13$: weeks 1 and 2.
Weeks 3 and 4 were trained on with labels that would arrive during, or
after, the test week. The test number is optimistic in the way this
lesson measured on the simulator: the model has labelled examples of
compromises that continue into the test week.

**(b)** With $\delta = 14$, the training labels must come from days 0 to
13. Weeks 3 and 4 become the delay block, and week 5 stays the test. The
training rows then have to come from weeks 1 and 2, which were the
feature pool. One option is to train on week 2 only, with its history
features computed from week 1, and to compute the test payments'
features over weeks 3 and 4, whose payments the bank did see. The
training set shrinks from two weeks to one, and the model's training
data are three weeks older than the test. No room is left for a
validation fold, so the depth or any other setting must be fixed in
advance or tuned on other data.

**(c)** Known-card removal: the test week should drop, day by day, the
cards whose fraud was labelled by day $d - 15$, and the cards the model
itself detected on earlier test days. Without it, week 5 contains
payments on cards the bank had blocked.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take a leaf of a tree trained on the training week. It receives 2,000
training payments, 20 of them fraud, so the tree says "fraud with
probability 0.01" for any payment that lands there. Now retrain on the
same week with every fraud kept and only one genuine payment in ten,
and suppose the leaf keeps the same region of feature space. (a) What
fraction of fraud does the leaf now hold, in expectation? (b) On the
validation week, the payments landing in the leaf are fraud about 1% of
the time. Compare. (c) Write the leaf's new value for a true fraud share
$p$ and a keep rate $\beta$ for genuine payments, and evaluate it for
$p = 0.05$ and $p = 0.5$ at $\beta = 0.1$. (d) Does this change which
payments the tree ranks highest?

<Solution>
**(a)** All 20 frauds stay; of the 1,980 genuine payments, about 198
stay. The leaf now holds $20/(20 + 198) \approx 0.092$ fraud.

**(b)** The retrained tree says 0.092 for payments that are fraud 1% of
the time on the validation week: more than nine times too high.

**(c)** Out of $n$ payments, $pn$ frauds stay and $\beta(1-p)n$ genuine
ones do, so the leaf reports
$$
p_s = \frac{p}{p + \beta(1 - p)} .
$$
For $p = 0.05$: $0.05/(0.05 + 0.095) \approx 0.34$. For $p = 0.5$:
$0.5/(0.5 + 0.05) \approx 0.91$. The distortion is largest in relative
terms for the rare-fraud leaves and squeezes everything toward 1.

**(d)** For a fixed set of leaves, no. $p_s$ increases with $p$, so the
leaves keep their order, and with it the ranking and card precision.
What changes is the probability attached to each payment. Whether that
probability can be turned back into the real one is the question of <Ref
lesson="undersampling-calibration">the undersampled posterior and its
correction</Ref>, which derives this formula and its inverse.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

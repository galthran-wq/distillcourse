The simplest way to score how unusual a payment is: measure how far it sits from its nearest neighbours in feature space. A point at the edge of a sparse but legitimate group then scores higher than a true outlier beside a dense group. How do you score distance relative to the local crowd, and what do the statistical versions of the same idea assume?

"Feature space" is the table the course has been building. Each payment is
a row of numbers, the amount and the card's and the terminal's windows of
<Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates">the aggregates</Ref>, so each payment
is a point, and two payments that look alike are two points close
together. The nearest-neighbour family of <Ref
lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="every-detector-family-assumes-one-thing-about-normal-data">the
framing lesson's table</Ref> assumes that normal payments sit in dense
neighbourhoods and anomalies far from their neighbours, so a payment far
from every other payment is suspect.

The last exercise of that lesson already broke the plain version. A dense
cloud of 500 points, a sparse cloud of 50, and one planted point five
standard deviations off the dense cloud: scored by the distance to its 5th
nearest neighbour, the planted point ranked 51st of 551, below every point
of the sparse cloud. In payments, the dense cloud is a commuter's morning
coffee, the same shop and nearly the same amount every weekday; the sparse
cloud is a traveller's purchases, spread over cities and amounts. A stolen
card used once beside the commuter's habit sits closer to its neighbours
than the traveller's ordinary purchase in Lisbon sits to hers, and the
global distance ranks the traveller first.

## Distance to the k-th neighbour: a first score

Before any neighbour can be found, the distance between two payments has to
mean something, and a raw feature table gives it no meaning.

### The columns have to share a scale

Take two payments described by amount and by recency, the days since the
card's previous payment: one at €1,000 after 5 days, one at €1,500 after
10 days. The Euclidean distance is
$\sqrt{500^2 + 5^2} \approx 500.02$, the Manhattan distance (the sum of the
absolute differences) is $500 + 5 = 505$. Either way the recency, which
doubled, contributes almost nothing, because euros come in hundreds and
days in units. Every neighbour search on these columns is a search on
amount alone.

The fix is to standardise each column before measuring: subtract its mean
and divide by its standard deviation, both taken from the reference period
and reused unchanged on new payments, the rule <Ref
lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="standardise-on-the-training-block-compress-a-long-tail-first">the
network lesson</Ref> applied to its inputs. If amounts spread with a
standard deviation of €400 and recencies with 4 days, the two differences
become 1.25 and 1.25, and the doubling of the recency counts as much as the
€500.

<Aside title="Binary red flags need a different similarity">
Some feature tables hold yes/no indicators: "new device", "billing and
shipping address differ", "card added this week". Suppose two claims
answer five such flags as (yes, no, yes, yes, no) and (yes, yes, no, no,
no). The simple matching coefficient counts agreements of either kind: 2
of 5. The Jaccard index leaves out the positions where both say no: 1
agreement out of the 4 flags raised by at least one, so 1/4. With 100
flags of which about 5 are raised on a typical case, nearly every pair
agrees on 90-odd "no"s, every matching coefficient lands near 0.9, and no
neighbourhood structure survives; the Jaccard index measures only the
flags that fired, which is why it is the usual choice for red-flag data.
</Aside>

### One neighbour hides too much

The obvious score is the distance to the single nearest neighbour. It
fails on an ordinary event. A fraudulent payment that is declined is often
retried a minute later: same card, same terminal, same amount, so the two
rows are nearly identical and each one's nearest neighbour is the other,
at distance close to zero. Two payments from the same scheme hide each
other the same way.

The repair is to look further down the list of neighbours. Score each
payment by its distance to its *k-th nearest neighbour*: a retry pair has
one close neighbour each, so with $k = 2$ each member's score is the
distance to the nearest payment outside the pair.

The figure scores three points of a planted dataset: 1,000 normal points

<KeyFact>
A member of a group of $g$ anomalies has $g - 1$ close neighbours, so a
k-th neighbour score sees the group only when $k \ge g$.
</KeyFact>

### Counting within a radius: the same score read as a density

The score has a dual. Instead of fixing $k$ and measuring the radius that
holds $k$ neighbours, fix a radius $r$ and count the neighbours inside it:
few neighbours within $r$ means unusual. Both are estimates of the same
quantity. A ball of radius $r$ around $x$ that holds $n$ other payments
says the data around $x$ has about $n$ payments per volume of that ball;
in two dimensions the density is about $n / (\pi r^2)$.

<Definition title="k-th neighbour distance score">
Let $d$ be the distance on standardised columns and $N$ the number of
payments in the reference set. The *k-distance* $r_k(x)$ of a payment $x$ is the
distance from $x$ to its $k$-th nearest neighbour in the reference set,
and $\mathcal{N}_k(x)$ is the set of those $k$ nearest neighbours. The
k-th neighbour distance score of $x$ is $r_k(x)$; the radius-count score
fixes $r$ and uses $1/n(x, r)$, with $n(x, r)$ the number of reference
payments within distance $r$ of $x$.
</Definition>

Since a ball of radius $r_k(x)$ in $p$ columns has volume proportional to
$r_k(x)^p$ and holds $k$ points, $k / r_k(x)^p$ is a density estimate, and
the score $r_k(x)$ ranks payments from the lowest estimated density to the
highest. The neighbour family is therefore a density estimate in disguise.
That reading predicts the failure of the opening: a fraud beside a dense
group sits where the density is still higher than anywhere in a sparse
group of honest payments.

## What the score costs: every payment against every other

With no labels and no period believed clean, the
<Ref lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="where-normal-comes-from-three-label-regimes">unsupervised regime</Ref>,
there is no separate reference to search: the anomalies have to be found
inside the data itself, so every payment of the set is scored against all
the others. That is $N(N-1)/2$ distances, and quadrupling the data
multiplies the work by sixteen. Scoring one new payment against a
reference that is already fixed is only a scan: $N$ distances, then the
$k$ smallest.

Measured on the ULB card set, <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="the-ulb-card-set-two-days-with-the-card-removed">two days of European
card payments</Ref> whose columns V1 to V28 are principal components of
undisclosed features, a brute-force search for the neighbours of 32,000 of
its rows among themselves takes about 3.7 seconds on one processor core.
Scaled by the square of the row count:

- one day of 400,000 payments scored against itself: about 10 minutes;
- a 30-day reference of 12 million payments scored against itself: about
  six days of one core;
- for comparison, each new payment scored against that reference once it
  is built: about 0.04 seconds, five core-hours a day for 400,000
  payments.

### Only the top of the ranking is needed

Investigators read about a hundred alerts a day, so the score is used only
for its top few hundred payments. That opens a shortcut. Process the
payments in random order and keep the 500 highest scores found so far; let
$c$ be the smallest of them. For the next payment, scan the reference in
random order, keeping the $k$ closest points found so far. The distance to
the $k$-th of those can only fall as the scan continues, so once it drops
below $c$ the payment can no longer enter the top 500 and the scan stops.
A normal payment in a dense region finds $k$ close neighbours among the
first few hundred random draws and is abandoned there, so on randomly
ordered data the average cost per payment stays close to constant and the
whole pass close to linear. The price is that only the top 500 get exact
scores.

### Fewer comparisons: indexes and samples

Two other speed-ups trade something different:

- **Index trees** (k-d trees, R-trees) cut the space into boxes and skip
  boxes too far away to hold a neighbour. In a handful of columns they
  skip most of the data; as the columns grow toward the dozens a card
  feature table holds, nearly every box is close to every query and the
  search falls back toward brute force.
- **Sub-sampling** compares each payment with a random sample of $M$
  reference payments instead of all $N$, cost $O(MN)$. Scoring all
  284,807 rows of the ULB card set against 20,000 of them takes about
  21 seconds on the same core, against an estimated five minutes for the
  full pairwise pass. The k-distances grow, since neighbours are sparser in a
  sample, and a sample too small loses the small normal groups.

## Two densities, one scale: the failure the local outlier factor repairs

Return to the opening's failure with a dataset small enough to compute by
hand: ten payments in two standardised columns, $k = 2$.

### Comparing a point's density with its neighbours'

A distance is unusual or not only relative to the spacing of the region it
is in. So compare each point's density with the densities of its own
neighbours: a point whose neighbours are much more tightly packed among
themselves than it is with them is an outlier of that neighbourhood,
whatever the absolute scale. In a uniform region the ratio is about 1,
dense or sparse.

<Definition title="local outlier factor">
For payments $x$ and $o$, the *reachability distance* is
$$
\mathrm{reach}_k(x, o) = \max\bigl(r_k(o),\ d(x, o)\bigr).
$$
The *local reachability density* of $x$ is the inverse of its mean
reachability distance to its neighbours,
$$
\mathrm{lrd}_k(x) = \Bigl(\frac{1}{k} \sum_{o \in \mathcal{N}_k(x)} \mathrm{reach}_k(x, o)\Bigr)^{-1},
$$
and the *local outlier factor* of $x$ is the mean ratio of its neighbours'
densities to its own:
$$
\mathrm{LOF}_k(x) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{o \in \mathcal{N}_k(x)} \frac{\mathrm{lrd}_k(o)}{\mathrm{lrd}_k(x)}.
$$
</Definition>

The definition makes LOF quadratic even when the reference is clean. A new
payment's reachability distances need $r_k(o)$ for each of its neighbours
$o$, and its LOF needs their densities $\mathrm{lrd}_k(o)$, which need the
k-distances of their own neighbours. Those are known only once every
reference payment has been scored against the rest of the reference, and
again each time the reference is rebuilt.

The obvious version of this comparison is the ratio $r_k(x) / r_k(o)$,
averaged over the neighbours $o$. The definition changes it in three ways.
**The mean** over all $k$ neighbours replaces the single $k$-th distance,
so one odd neighbour moves the density less. **The floor** $r_k(o)$ in the
reachability distance handles the retry problem inside a cluster: a point
sitting almost on top of a neighbour would otherwise get a near-zero
distance and an enormous density, and every point within $o$'s own
k-distance now counts as equally close to $o$. Points deep inside a
cluster then get the same density as their neighbours and an LOF of
exactly or nearly 1. **The ratio** cancels the scale of the region: halve
every distance in the sparse group and its LOF values do not change.

<Example title="LOF of v and of a sparse point, k = 2">
**The knot.** D2's two nearest neighbours are D1 and D4, both at 0.2, and
each has $r_2 = 0.2$. So $\mathrm{reach}_2(\text{D2}, \cdot) = \max(0.2,
0.2) = 0.2$ for both, and $\mathrm{lrd}_2(\text{D2}) = 1/0.2 = 5$. By
symmetry every knot point has density 5. The outlier $v$ is not among any
knot point's two nearest neighbours, so these densities do not involve it.

**The outlier v.** Its neighbours D2 and D4 lie at 0.806, above their
k-distance 0.2, so $\mathrm{reach}_2(v, \cdot) = 0.806$ and
$\mathrm{lrd}_2(v) = 1/0.806 = 1.24$. Then
$$
\mathrm{LOF}_2(v) = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\frac{5}{1.24} + \frac{5}{1.24}\Bigr) = 4.03 .
$$

**A sparse point.** S1's neighbours S2 and S3 lie at 2, with $r_2 = 2$:
reachability 2, density 0.5. Its neighbours have density 0.5 as well, so
$\mathrm{LOF}_2(\text{S1}) = 1$.

The ranking by LOF: $v$ (4.03), then $u$, then all eight honest points at
1.00.
</Example>

### Back to the planted point

On the framing lesson's 551 points, the planted point at $(1, 0)$ that the
5th-neighbour distance ranked 51st has $\mathrm{LOF}_5 = 4.8$ and ranks
first; the sparse cloud's median LOF is 1.1. Nothing changed but the
reference against which "far" is judged.

<KeyFact>
The local outlier factor is about 1 inside a group of normal points only
if the group holds more than $k$ of them. In a group of $k$ or fewer, each
member has to take neighbours from outside the group, and honest payments
score as outliers.
</KeyFact>

## A crowd of anomalies is its own neighbourhood

LOF repairs the varying-density failure. It inherits the other failure of
the family, and makes it worse. A card-testing ring that runs 40 €1
payments through one web shop in a night produces 40 nearly identical
rows: each test has 39 close neighbours, all of them tests.
<Ref lesson="anomaly-detection-framing" id="three-planar-experiments">Panel
(c) of the planar experiments</Ref> was this geometry, and the group-size
rule of the first section says when a k-th neighbour score is blind to
it: whenever $k < 40$. LOF is blinder still. Even when a clump is loose
enough that each member's k-th neighbour is far away in absolute terms,
so that the plain distance flags it, the members are spaced just as
loosely among themselves, and the density ratio stays near 1.

### On real card data, fraud comes in clumps

The ULB card set of the cost section shows how common this is. Take a
random 50,000 of its rows, 78 of them fraud, score each row against the
other rows of the sample using V1 to V28 as they are, and measure the
ranking by <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-the-team-sees-at-each-fraud">average precision</Ref> against the
fraud labels.

The first section said to standardise every column before measuring any
distance. Here the V columns are used as they are, and the reason is
where they came from:

1. **A rotation keeps distances.** The processor's PCA turned its own
   rows onto 28 perpendicular directions, the way turning a map moves
   every town but keeps the distance between any two. Distances between
   rows in V1 to V28 are distances between the processor's rows, as far
   as the 28 released directions carry them.
2. **So the V's already share one unit.** The euros-against-days problem
   of the first section cannot arise: every V column is measured in the
   unit of the rotated rows.
3. **Their spreads are not units.** The variances fall from 3.8 for V1 to
   0.11 for V28 because the data spreads widely along the first direction
   and barely along the last. Dividing each column by its standard
   deviation would multiply a difference along V28 by about 3 and one
   along V1 by about 0.5, so the near-constant directions would count six
   times as much, relative to the main one, as they do between the
   original rows. That is a reweighting of the data, not a change of unit.

A fraud's 10 nearest rows are on average 59% fraud, against 0.04% for a
genuine row's: the frauds sit in clumps. With $k = 10$ the LOF ranking is
no better than chance (average precision 0.003, base rate 0.0016), because
each fraud's neighbourhood is other frauds. The plain k-th neighbour
distance does better, 0.16, because the clumps lie in regions where the
data is sparse in absolute terms. At $k = 200$ the neighbourhoods reach
past the clumps into genuine rows and LOF climbs to 0.44, ahead of every
distance score. Three other random samples give the same picture (LOF at
$k = 10$: 0.002 to 0.003; at $k = 200$: 0.48 to 0.57).

### Two ways out, each with a price

**Raise k** above the size of any anomalous group. The price is
locality: a neighbourhood of 200 points is not local to a normal group of
fifty, and, as the LOF section showed, an honest group of $k$ or fewer is
judged against points that are not its own.

**Score new payments against a reference believed normal.** The
semi-supervised version of the same score searches each new payment's
neighbours among last month's payments only. Tonight's 40 tests are then
not in the reference and cannot be one another's neighbours; each finds
only the shop's genuine history, at a distance. This is why the framing
lesson's panel (c) fell to the semi-supervised versions of the neighbour
and cluster scores.

<KeyFact>
Scored against the data's own rows, a local score needs $k$ at least the
size of the largest group of anomalies and smaller than the smallest
group of honest payments. Nothing guarantees that such a $k$ exists; a
reference the anomalies are not in removes the first bound.
</KeyFact>

<Aside title="Three variants of the local score">
**Connectivity-based outlier factor** grows the neighbourhood one point at
a time, always adding the point closest to the set built so far, instead
of taking a ball. Normal data shaped like a line, a card's amounts drifting
up month by month, then counts as one connected neighbourhood, where a
ball would mix in empty space. **Outlier detection using in-degree number**
counts how many of a point's $k$ nearest neighbours also list it among
their own $k$ nearest; a point nobody chooses as a neighbour is an outlier.
**Local correlation integral** compares each point's density with the
spread of its neighbours' densities at several radii, so it can flag a
small group of points as a whole, the micro-cluster that defeats a fixed
$k$.
</Aside>

## Clustering: comparing with a few centroids instead of every neighbour

A neighbour score compares each payment with its own neighbourhood, which
is why it costs $O(N)$ per payment. A clustering summarises a set of
payments in advance: run k-means, from the classical-ml course, and keep
$K$ centroids. (The $k$ of this lesson counts neighbours; the number of
clusters is written $K$.) Scoring a payment then costs $K$ distances, a
few dozen instead of millions, and even fitting the clustering costs only
$NK$ distances per pass of k-means, not $N^2/2$.

### Which payments the centroids come from

The neighbour score had two references: a period believed normal, or the
data being scored itself. A clustering has the same two. It can be fitted
to last month's payments and each of tonight's payments scored against
those centroids, or, in the unsupervised regime, fitted to tonight's
unlabelled payments and each of them scored against the clusters they
form among themselves. What turns a clustering into an anomaly score
depends on what is assumed about normal data, and which of the two sets
it is fitted to decides whether a group of card tests can hide.

<Figure
  src="./figures/clusters.svg"
  alt="Three panels. (1) in no cluster, normal points belong to one: two clouds of black points and an amber square away from both, labelled in no cluster; note: needs a method that may leave a point unassigned. (2) far from its centroid, normal points sit near one: one cloud with a blue cross labelled centroid and an amber square connected to it by a dashed line, labelled far from it; note: fails once anomalies form a cluster with its own centroid.   caption="Three assumptions that turn a clustering into an anomaly score."

1. **Normal payments belong to a cluster, anomalies to none.** This needs
   an algorithm allowed to leave points unassigned, such as DBSCAN, which
   grows clusters through dense regions of the data it is run on and
   labels what it cannot reach as noise. K-means assigns every point
   somewhere, so an anomaly lands in the nearest cluster and looks like a
   member. Run on tonight's payments, DBSCAN fails on the card tests too:
   40 nearly identical rows are a dense region like any other.
2. **Normal payments lie near their centroid.** The score is the distance
   to the nearest centroid. Fitted to last month, the centroids hold none
   of tonight's tests, and each test lies far from all of them: this is
   the reference believed normal of the neighbour score, and it works.
   Fitted to tonight's unlabelled payments, the 40 tests are numerous and
   tight enough to pull a centroid of their own, and each then sits at
   distance almost zero from it.
3. **Normal payments form large, dense clusters; anomalies small or sparse
   ones.** This version needs the anomalies inside the data it clusters,
   since a group can form a small cluster only where it is part of the
   data: it is fitted to the set being scored, tonight's payments or the
   month that includes them. A payment is judged by the size of the
   cluster it lands in, set against the sizes of the other clusters, which
   play the part of normal. The score combines that size with the
   payment's distance from its centroid (the cluster-based local outlier
   factor is one such combination), so 40 tests that form a cluster of
   their own beside clusters of thousands are flagged for its size.

### Does the small-cluster score find organised fraud?

<Ref lesson="paysim-feature-matrix" id="exercises">The PaySim
exercises</Ref> ran k-means with $K = 8$ on the 532,909 transfers,
fraud included and labels unused, the third version's setting, on
coordinates that include balances known only after the transfer. The two
smallest clusters are close in size:

| cluster | rows | of them fraud |
|---|---|---|
| smallest | 3,891 | 1 |
| second smallest | 4,159 | 4,014 |
| third smallest | 23,016 | 60 |
| the other five | 59,287 to 137,266 | 22 in all |

Ranked by the size of its cluster, smallest first, a transfer in the fraud
cluster comes second. The first 3,891 alerts hold one fraud; the next
4,159 are 96.5% fraud, and the 8,050 rows of the two smallest clusters
hold 4,015 of the 4,097 fraud transfers, 98%. The fraud was found because
it was organised: one scheme repeated thousands of times makes a cluster,
the cluster is small because fraud is still a minority, and the very
repetition that blinded the neighbour scores is what the small-cluster
score detects. The price is the one LOF paid for honest groups of $k$ or
fewer. "Small" is only relative to the other clusters, so an honest
pattern rarer than the fraud ranks above it, and the score ranks whole
clusters: all 3,891 honest rows reach the investigators before the first
fraud transfer.

<KeyFact>
A score that flags small clusters turns the repetition of organised fraud
into its signal, as long as the scheme stays a minority of the data. An
honest pattern rarer than the scheme ranks above it.
</KeyFact>

## When "far" has units: z-scores, fences and Grubbs' test

A neighbour score answers "how far, compared with the neighbours" and
nothing more. An LOF of 4 is large on the toy; nothing says whether it is
large enough to call, or how many honest payments score that high by
chance. A model of how normal values are distributed answers both: "far"
becomes "improbable under the model", with a probability attached.

### One column: three standard deviations or the box-plot fence

The oldest rule fits a Gaussian to a column, mean $\mu$ and standard
deviation $\sigma$ estimated from the data, and flags any value more than
$3\sigma$ from $\mu$, the rule $|z| > 3$ for the z-score
$z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$. The interval $\mu \pm 3\sigma$ holds 99.73% of a
Gaussian.

The box-plot rule looks different. With quartiles $Q_1$ and $Q_3$ and
interquartile range $\mathrm{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$, it flags anything below
$Q_1 - 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}$ or above $Q_3 + 1.5\,\mathrm{IQR}$. It uses no
mean and no standard deviation, so it is not pulled by the very values it
should flag.

<Figure
  src="./figures/fences.svg"

On Gaussian data the two coincide. The quartiles of a Gaussian lie at
$\mu \pm 0.674\sigma$, so $\mathrm{IQR} = 1.349\sigma$ and the upper fence
is $0.674\sigma + 1.5 \times 1.349\sigma = 2.70\sigma$: the region between
the fences holds 99.30% of the data. Both rules say "outside the
region that holds about 99.5% of a fitted Gaussian".

That makes them detectors of extreme values, which is narrower than
anomalies. An *extreme value* sits in the tail of the same distribution
as the normal data; an anomaly may come from a different distribution
altogether and sit anywhere, including in the middle. On raw amounts,
which are skewed, the lower fence lies below zero, so a €1 card test can
never fall outside it.

### Grubbs' test: how far is far for N values

A fixed $|z| > 3$ ignores how many values were looked at. Among 100,000
genuine Gaussian values about 270 lie beyond $3\sigma$ by chance, so the
rule's false alarms grow with the sample. Grubbs' test fixes the error rate
for the whole sample instead. It looks at the single most extreme value,
$G = \max_i |x_i - \bar x| / s$ with $\bar x$ and $s$ the sample mean and
standard deviation, and calls it an outlier at level $\alpha$ if
$$
G > \frac{N - 1}{\sqrt{N}} \sqrt{\frac{t^2}{N - 2 + t^2}}, \qquad t = t_{\alpha/(2N),\, N-2},
$$
the upper $\alpha/(2N)$ critical value of Student's t-distribution with
$N - 2$ degrees of freedom. The $\alpha/(2N)$ is the price of looking at
$N$ values in both directions and keeping only the largest: each of the
$2N$ chances to be extreme is tested at a level $2N$ times stricter. The
bar therefore rises with $N$: at $\alpha = 0.05$ the critical $G$ is 2.73
for 21 values, 3.38 for 100, 4.04 for 1,000 and 5.03 for 100,000. If a
value is flagged, it is removed and the test runs again on the rest.

<Example title="Grubbs' test on one card's amounts">
Take the 20 earlier amounts of <Ref lesson="behavioural-profiles"
id="an-old-part-a-new-part-and-a-t-statistic">the break-point
card</Ref>, mean €40.00, standard deviation €7.56, and add a new payment
of €80.

**Statistic.** With 21 values, $\bar x = 41.90$ and $s = 11.42$; the most
extreme value is €80, and $G = (80 - 41.90)/11.42 = 3.33$.

**Critical value.** $t_{0.05/42,\, 19} = 3.50$, so the bar is
$\tfrac{20}{\sqrt{21}} \sqrt{3.50^2 / (19 + 3.50^2)} = 4.364 \times 0.626 = 2.73$.
Since $3.33 > 2.73$, the €80 is an outlier at the 5% level.

**Iteration.** Removing it leaves the 20 old amounts, whose most extreme
value, €55, gives $G = 1.98$ against a bar of 2.71: nothing more to flag.
</Example>

The test has a blind spot that matters for fraud. Add three large
payments, €80, €82 and €85, instead of one: they inflate $s$ to €16.20,
the largest gives $G = 2.44$ against a bar of 2.78, and nothing is
flagged. A run of large amounts masks itself, exactly as a clump of card
tests masks itself from a neighbour score. A run is a change in the card's
level, the question the break-point statistic asks; Grubbs' test asks
whether one value does not belong.

## Many columns at once: the Mahalanobis distance

The rules so far score one column. A payment has many, and the obvious
extension scores them one at a time and adds the results: a z-score per
column, or, where a column is far from Gaussian, a *frequency profile*,
which keeps a histogram of each column over the reference period and
scores a new value by how rare its bin is.

### A large payment on a small card

Take two standardised columns: $x_1$, the log of the amount, and $x_2$,
the log of the card's 30-day mean amount. Across payments they are
strongly correlated, since cards that usually spend much make large
payments; suppose the correlation is 0.8. Compare two payments:

- **A** $= (2.5, 2.5)$: a large payment on a card that always spends
  large;
- **B** $= (1.5, -1.5)$: a moderately large payment on a card that usually
  spends little.

Column by column, B is ordinary: each of its values falls in a
well-filled bin, with about 13% of payments further out on each axis.
Every per-column score calls B ordinary, and so does any sum of them,
including the standardised Euclidean distance from the centre, the square
root of the summed squared z-scores: 3.54 for A, 2.12 for B, so A looks
more unusual. The cloud says the opposite. Points like A lie along the
cloud's long axis, where payments are plentiful; nothing lies near B,
whose two values pull against the correlation. What is rare is the pair,
a common amount on a card where it is not common.

<Definition title="Mahalanobis distance">
For a payment $x$ with $p$ columns, sample mean $\bar x$ and sample
covariance matrix $S$, the squared Mahalanobis distance is
$$
D^2(x) = (x - \bar x)^\top S^{-1} (x - \bar x).
$$
</Definition>

For one column, $S = s^2$ and $D = |x - \bar x|/s$, the z-score. For
uncorrelated columns $S$ is diagonal and $D^2$ is the sum of the squared
z-scores, the standardised Euclidean distance: standardising the columns
is the Mahalanobis distance with the correlations ignored.

For the two columns with correlation $\rho$, inverting $S$ gives
$D^2 = (x_1^2 - 2\rho x_1 x_2 + x_2^2)/(1 - \rho^2)$. For A:
$(6.25 - 10 + 6.25)/0.36 = 6.94$, so $D = 2.64$. For B:
$(2.25 + 3.6 + 2.25)/0.36 = 22.5$, so $D = 4.74$. The order is reversed.

<Figure
  src="./figures/mahalanobis.svg"
  alt="Scatter of 400 grey points in a tilted elliptical cloud on axes x1 log amount and x2 log of the card's 30-day mean, correlation 0.8. A dashed blue circle of Euclidean radius 3.41 and an amber ellipse of Mahalanobis distance 3.41 are centred at the origin. Point A at (2.5, 2.5), blue, inside the ellipse and outside the circle, labelled Euclid 3.54, Mahalanobis 2.64. Point B at (1.5, minus 1.5), amber, inside the circle and outside the ellipse, labelled Euclid 2.12, Mahalanobis 4.74."
  caption="The circle calls A unusual and B ordinary; the ellipse, drawn from the covariance, says the reverse."
/>

The distance comes with a scale. If the payments are Gaussian, $D^2$
follows a chi-square distribution with $p$ degrees of freedom, so a
threshold has a meaning: in two columns $P(D^2 > c) = e^{-c/2}$, and the
region holding 99.7% of payments is $D^2 \le -2 \ln 0.003 = 11.62$, that
is $D \le 3.41$, the ellipse in the figure. B lies outside it.

<Exercise>
A third payment C sits at $(0.5, -0.5)$ in the same two columns. Compute
its Euclidean and Mahalanobis distances and say whether the 99.7%
threshold flags it.

<Solution>
**Euclidean.** $\sqrt{0.25 + 0.25} = 0.71$.

**Mahalanobis.** $D^2 = (0.25 + 2 \times 0.8 \times 0.25 + 0.25)/0.36 =
(0.25 + 0.4 + 0.25)/0.36 = 2.5$, so $D = 1.58$. Below 3.41: not flagged.
C has B's direction at a third of its size, and the squared distance
scales with the square, $22.5/9 = 2.5$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### What the Gaussian costs on real data

The probability attached to $D$ is only as good as the Gaussian. On the
ULB card set the columns V1 to V28 are principal components, so they are
uncorrelated and $D^2$ is simply $\sum_j V_j^2 / \mathrm{var}(V_j)$. Over
all 284,807 rows, the chi-square threshold that should flag 0.3% of a
Gaussian sample (52.9 for 28 columns) flags 15,078 rows, 5.3%, eighteen
times the promise. The columns have heavy tails, as amounts do.

The ranking survives where the probability does not: those 15,078 rows
hold 431 of the 492 frauds, a fraud share of 2.9% against 0.17% in the
whole file. A Mahalanobis score is usable as a ranking on non-Gaussian
data; its threshold is not a confidence level there.

The mean and covariance are themselves estimated from data that contains
the anomalies, and a few far-out rows can move them enough to hide
themselves.

## Where the rare combinations live: principal coordinates

The Mahalanobis distance caught B from the covariance alone; nobody told
it which pair of columns to compare. Which direction did it find, and
what does a score see that keeps only some of the directions?

Principal component analysis, from the classical-ml course, rotates the
columns into uncorrelated directions $y_1, \dots, y_p$, ordered by their variances
$\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_p$, the eigenvalues of $S$.

<Theorem title="Mahalanobis distance in principal coordinates">
Let $S = V \Lambda V^\top$ with $V$ orthogonal and
$\Lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_p)$, all $\lambda_i > 0$,
and let $y = V^\top (x - \bar x)$. Then
$$
D^2(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{p} \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}.
$$
<Proof>
Since $V$ is orthogonal, $S^{-1} = V \Lambda^{-1} V^\top$. Substituting,
$D^2 = (x - \bar x)^\top V \Lambda^{-1} V^\top (x - \bar x) = y^\top
\Lambda^{-1} y = \sum_i y_i^2/\lambda_i$, because $\Lambda^{-1}$ is
diagonal with entries $1/\lambda_i$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Each principal coordinate is measured in units of its own standard
deviation and the squares are added: the Mahalanobis distance is a
standardised Euclidean distance in the rotated frame.

For the two columns with correlation 0.8, the directions are
$y_1 = (x_1 + x_2)/\sqrt 2$ with variance $\lambda_1 = 1.8$ and
$y_2 = (x_1 - x_2)/\sqrt 2$ with $\lambda_2 = 0.2$. Payment A lies on the
first axis, $y_1 = 3.54$, contributing $3.54^2/1.8 = 6.9$. Payment B lies
on the second, $y_2 = 2.12$, contributing $2.12^2/0.2 = 22.5$: a modest
displacement along a direction in which payments barely vary. That second
direction, $x_1 - x_2$, is the log amount minus the log of the card's mean,
the log of the ratio column that <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates"
id="an-amount-needs-a-reference-point">the aggregates</Ref> built by hand,
up to scale. From the data alone, the covariance found the direction
that column was designed to measure.

<Figure
  src="./figures/principal.svg"
  alt="The same cloud rotated into principal coordinates, drawn at equal scale: a wide flat band along the horizontal axis y1 equals (x1 plus x2) over root 2, variance lambda 1 equals 1.8, and narrow along the vertical axis y2 equals (x1 minus x2) over root 2, variance lambda 2 equals 0.2. Point A, blue, on the horizontal axis at y1 = 3.54, at the edge of the band, labelled y1 squared over lambda 1 equals 6.9.   caption="In principal coordinates A is at the end of the long axis; B is far out along the short one."
/>

The sum splits at any $q < p$ into two scores. The first $q$ terms,
$\sum_{i \le q} y_i^2/\lambda_i$, are the Mahalanobis distance inside the
subspace of the $q$ leading components: how far a payment lies along the
directions in which normal payments vary most, its *major-component
score*. The remaining terms, $\sum_{i > q} y_i^2/\lambda_i$, measure how
far it lies off that subspace, each departure in units of the small
variance of its direction: its *minor-component score*. The Mahalanobis
distance is the sum of the two,
$$
D^2(x) = \sum_{i \le q} \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i} + \sum_{i > q} \frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i}.
$$
With $q = 1$ in the two-column example, A scores $6.9 + 0$ and B scores
$0 + 22.5$. The minor-component score is the subspace family of <Ref
lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="every-detector-family-assumes-one-thing-about-normal-data">the
framing lesson's table</Ref>: normal data lies near the subspace of the
leading components, an anomaly leaves it, and the score fails on an
anomaly that stays in the subspace and is only extreme along it, a
payment like A pushed further out along the long axis.
The major-component score has the opposite blind spot and gives B zero.
Under a Gaussian the two halves follow chi-square distributions with $q$
and $p - q$ degrees of freedom, so each has its own threshold. In
practice the components are estimated with *robust PCA*, a method that
resists anomalies, since a few far-out rows can tilt the leading
directions toward themselves as they move the covariance.

<KeyFact>
Rare combinations of common values live in the low-variance directions,
so a principal-component detector sees them only through its minor half.
A score built from the leading components alone is blind to payment B,
however robustly the components are estimated.
</KeyFact>

## Which score for which data

<Ref lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="every-detector-family-assumes-one-thing-about-normal-data">The framing
lesson's table</Ref> gave one assumption per family. This one splits the
families into the scores of this chapter and adds what each costs per new
payment.

| score | normal data is assumed to | it misses | cost per new payment |
|---|---|---|---|
| k-th neighbour distance | have one density everywhere | an outlier beside a dense group; a group of more than $k$ anomalies | a scan of the reference |
| local outlier factor | be locally uniform, in groups larger than $k$ | anomalous groups larger than $k$; honest groups of $k$ or fewer | a scan, plus every reference point's own density |
| distance to a centroid | form clusters with anomalies outside | anomalies that form their own cluster | $K$ distances |
| small-cluster score | form large clusters, anomalies small ones | an honest pattern rarer than the fraud | $K$ distances |
| z-score, fence, Grubbs' test | follow one Gaussian per column | rare combinations; runs that inflate $s$; low-end anomalies | one subtraction per column |
| Mahalanobis distance | follow one Gaussian in all columns | normal data with several modes; its probability, once tails are heavy | $p^2$ operations |
| major-component score (first $q$ terms) | vary mostly along a few leading directions | rare combinations, which lie off those directions | $pq$ operations |
| minor-component score (the other $p - q$) | lie near the subspace of the leading directions | anomalies inside the subspace, extreme only along it | $p(p - q)$ operations |

The <Ref lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="three-planar-experiments">three planar experiments</Ref> read as they
did there. The new row, LOF, sides with the neighbour distance: it
succeeds on the ring of clouds and fails on the dense anomalous cloud of
panel (c), more surely, since even a loose anomalous cloud has density
ratios near 1.

<Conclusion>
You can now score a payment by its distance to its k-th neighbour or by
the count within a radius, read either as a density estimate, and say why
$k$ must reach the size of any group of anomalies you want to find. You can compute
the local outlier factor by hand, explain that it repairs the
varying-density failure by comparing a point's density with its
neighbours', and explain why it fails when $k$ is smaller than the clumps
of fraud.
You can read the z-score, the box-plot fence, Grubbs' test and the
Mahalanobis distance as the same idea under a Gaussian, compute each, and
show that the Mahalanobis distance is a sum over principal components
that splits into a major half along the leading directions and a minor
half off them, the half that holds the rare combinations of common
values.

Two limits stay open. Every neighbour score costs a pass over a reference
set that grows with the square of its size, and every one of them is
blinded by a group of more than $k$ anomalies. <Ref
lesson="isolation-forest">Isolation Forest</Ref> scores a payment by how
few random cuts separate it from the rest, a computation linear in the
data, and turns sub-sampling from a shortcut into the cure for the
masking.
</Conclusion>

top 100. (c) Explain both results.

<Solution>
**(a)** With all rows: average precision 0.16, 18 frauds in the top 100.
With the genuine rows' mean and covariance: average precision 0.52, 92
frauds in the top 100. The base rate is 0.0017. A reasonable prediction is
"similar", since the 492 frauds are 0.17% of the rows; the measured gap is
threefold.

**(b)** First two components: average precision 0.03, 6 frauds in the
top 100. V3 to V28: average precision 0.18, 22 frauds in the top 100,
slightly better than the full sum.

**(c)** **The covariance.** The V columns are principal components of
all rows, frauds included, so over all rows they are exactly uncorrelated
and the all-rows covariance is diagonal: its score is a sum of squared
z-scores. Among the genuine rows alone the same columns are correlated
(up to 0.17). The frauds take values up to 38 genuine standard deviations
from zero in some columns, and their cross-products are large enough to
cancel those correlations in the all-rows estimate. Fraud rows lie against
the genuine correlations, which is the rare-combination case, and the
diagonal covariance cannot see it. A check confirms where the gain comes
from: the genuine rows' variances with the correlations dropped give an
average precision of 0.17, no better than all rows. The anomalies masked
themselves through the estimate. Estimating the covariance on rows
believed normal, the semi-supervised regime, removes the masking; on a
live stream the "genuine" rows are those not reported within the label
delay, with the contamination that brings. **The subspace.** The first two
components give the major-component score with $q = 2$: it measures how
far a row lies along the two directions in which the data varies most.
The other 26 terms are the minor-component score, the distance off that
plane, and they hold nearly all the signal: fraud rows depart from the
data in its low-variance directions, the rare-combination case, so the
major half is close to blind and the minor half alone does as well as
the whole distance. **Setup**: Python
with numpy and scikit-learn's `average_precision_score`, the file as
published on Kaggle or OpenML (dataset 1597).
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Draw random samples of 2,000, 8,000 and 32,000 rows of the ULB set's V1 to
V28 and time a brute-force search for every row's 10 nearest neighbours
among the sample's own rows, on one processor core. (a) Fit the growth
rate and extrapolate to one day of 400,000 payments and to a 30-day
reference. (b) Plant 40 points in the 32,000-row sample: copies of a single
point placed at twice the mean of the fraud rows, each perturbed by
Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.05 per column. Score all rows by
the distance to the 10th neighbour, then to the 50th. Where do the 40
rank? (c) Score the same 32,040 rows against a random reference of only
2,000 of them, with $k = 10$. Where do the 40 rank now, and why?

<Solution>
**(a)** On one core of the machine used for this course: 0.04 s, 0.24 s
and 3.7 s. Each fourfold increase multiplies the time by about 6, then 16;
the larger step is the quadratic regime, once fixed overheads stop
dominating. Extrapolating from 32,000 by the square: 400,000 rows,
$(400{,}000/32{,}000)^2 \times 3.7 \approx 580$ s, about 10 minutes; 12
million rows, about $5 \times 10^5$ s, six days. Your times will differ;
the ratios should not.

**(b)** With $k = 10$ the best-placed of the 40 planted points ranks
30,945th of 32,040 and their median 31,323rd: among the most normal 4% of
rows. Each has 39 neighbours inside its own group, about 0.35 away. With
$k = 50$ the 50th neighbour lies outside the group: the best-placed ranks
108th, the median 128th. A single copy of the planted point, alone, ranks 63rd: the group
hides itself, and $k$ above the group size is the only fix the full
reference offers.

**(c)** Against a 2,000-row reference, all 40 rank in the top 100 (best
around 30th). The reference holds each sampled row with probability
$2{,}000/32{,}040 \approx 1/16$, so on average 2.5 of the 40 planted
points, fewer than $k = 10$: the group can no longer fill its own
neighbourhoods. Sub-sampling cut the cost sixteenfold and cleared the
masking at the same time. A method built on tiny random sub-samples, and
on cuts instead of distances, is <Ref lesson="isolation-forest">Isolation
Forest</Ref>, which shows that sub-sampling to a few hundred points
improves detection for exactly this reason.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A card's last 24 payments are the 20 amounts of the break-point card
(32, 45, 38, 51, 29, 40, 36, 47, 55, 33, 41, 28, 44, 39, 50, 35, 42, 31,
46, 38) followed by 80, 82, 85 and 78. (a) Compute the break-point
statistic of <Ref lesson="behavioural-profiles"
id="an-old-part-a-new-part-and-a-t-statistic">the behavioural
profiles</Ref>, old part 20, new part 4. (b) Run Grubbs' test at
$\alpha = 0.05$ on the 24 amounts. (c) Explain why the two disagree, and
which question each answers.

<Solution>
**(a)** Old part: mean 40.00, standard deviation 7.56. New part 80, 82,
85, 78: mean 81.25, standard deviation 2.99. Pooled variance
$(19 \times 7.56^2 + 3 \times 2.99^2)/22 = (1{,}086 + 26.8)/22 = 50.6$, so
$s_p = 7.11$. Then
$t_{bp} = (81.25 - 40.00)/(7.11 \sqrt{1/20 + 1/4}) = 41.25/3.89 = 10.6$:
a strong break.

**(b)** All 24 amounts: mean 46.88, standard deviation 17.18. The most
extreme value is 85, $G = (85 - 46.88)/17.18 = 2.22$. With $N = 24$,
$t_{0.05/48,\, 22} = 3.49$ and the bar is
$\tfrac{23}{\sqrt{24}} \sqrt{3.49^2/(22 + 3.49^2)} = 4.695 \times 0.597 = 2.80$.
Since $2.22 < 2.80$, nothing is flagged.

**(c)** Grubbs' test asks whether a single value does not belong to one
Gaussian sample. Four large amounts inflate the sample's standard
deviation from 7.6 to 17.2, so none of them stands out: the run masks
itself. The break-point statistic asks whether the recent part of the
sequence has a different mean from the earlier part, a question about the
order of the values that Grubbs' test ignores. A card that starts
spending at a new level, as a stolen card does, is a change in level; an
isolated typo or a one-off purchase is a single outlying value. The first
is the break point's case, the second Grubbs'.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For each situation, choose a score from this lesson and say which property
of the data decides it. (a) A merchant's payments over a month, two
columns (log amount, hour), with a lunchtime crowd of small payments and a
thin spread of large evening orders; you want the single odd payment in
either group. (b) Thirty standardised columns for every card payment of
the last month, strongly correlated, and the payments to flag are those
whose columns disagree with each other. (c) Tonight's payments at one web
shop, scored as they arrive, with the risk of a burst of identical small
payments. (d) The daily totals of one ATM over a year, to flag the single
strangest day.

<Solution>
**(a)** Two groups of very different density: the local outlier factor,
with $k$ below the size of the evening group. A global k-th neighbour
distance would rank every large evening order above an odd lunchtime
payment.

**(b)** The anomalies are rare combinations, departures from the
correlations: the Mahalanobis distance with all components, its
covariance estimated on a period believed clean. A per-column profile or a
major-component score would discard those directions; a minor-component
score would keep them.

**(c)** A burst of identical payments is a group that can mask itself:
any score whose reference is tonight's data needs $k$ above the burst
size. Score each arriving payment against the shop's previous month
instead, so that tonight's payments are never one another's neighbours.

**(d)** One column, one sample, a question about a single extreme value:
Grubbs' test, on the log of the totals if they are skewed. If the strange
days come as a run, test for a change in level instead.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

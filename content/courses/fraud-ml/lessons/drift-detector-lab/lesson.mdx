The last three lessons quoted a detector's delay, the 3.25 times one rang for three switches, and a model's Kappa at its own flagging rate. Each number rests on a line that can be wrong without raising an error: whether the running mean takes the current item before the deviation does, what a count starts from after a reset, whether a model reads the label before it predicts. A wrong line still prints a plausible number, so the output alone cannot say which of them to trust before a detector goes on a live model.

So you write the tools and check them: a bit-stream generator with planted abrupt
and gradual changes, four change detectors compared by false alarms and detection
delay over random runs, a prequential scorer with a sliding window, and a streaming
naive Bayes that resets on alarm, run on a drifting synthetic stream and then on the
simulator.

Every definition comes from an earlier lesson. The run-length measures, the
cumulative sums, Page–Hinkley, DDM, the two-window test and ADWIN's cut come from
<Ref lesson="change-detectors" />. Prequential evaluation, the sliding and fading
scores, Kappa and reset on alarm come from
<Ref lesson="stream-learning-and-adaptation" />. The sigmoid transition is
from <Ref lesson="concept-drift" />, the strategies that retrain on a schedule
from <Ref lesson="drift-strategies-on-worldline" />, the simulator from
<Ref lesson="canonical-datasets" />. Naive Bayes is taken as known.

You write the tools in numpy, one checkpoint each, and each is first checked
locally against a value you can follow by hand or against a published stream:
the seeded generator; Page–Hinkley, one- and two-sided, by analogy with a CUSUM
class that ships complete; DDM with its warning and drift bands; the reference
window against the sliding window; ADWIN's cut, and ADWIN as a detector; the
scorecard, which runs a detector over many seeded streams and reports its MTFA,
MTD and missed detections; the landmark, sliding and fading scores with sliding
Kappa; a Gaussian naive Bayes learned one item at a time, and the loop that
predicts, feeds the detector, resets and only then learns. Then your detectors
are compared on one plot of delay against false alarms, and the learner runs on
a SEA stream and on 60 days of payments in which the fraud changes at day 25
and again at day 45.

The lab runs as a Jupyter notebook — open it in Colab with the button above, or
in any Jupyter. It needs no GPU and downloads a data file of about 4.5 MB. The
comparisons run for a minute or two, in plain Python on purpose: every detector
has to be fed one item at a time. Pair the notebook with the code from this
page; each checkpoint submits your function's outputs for verification, and the
checklist above tracks what stands. The nine exact checkpoints are the required
set. The written answer (which detector to put on a deployed fraud model whose
labels come a week late) and the open task are optional. The open task scores
the unlabelled half of days 40–59 with a learner of your design that may read a
label only seven days after its payment, to a ROC-AUC above 0.83. Trees trained
once on days 0–24 reach 0.66, and the same trees retrained daily on all the labels
old enough reach 0.815. The grade is a ROC-AUC because a ranking asks for no
flagging rate. On the labelled half of the same days the notebook draws two curves
from your scores: ROC-AUC in bins of about three days, which shows where a lagging
model loses its grade, and the sliding Kappa you built, which is the operating view. Expect about five hours. When a check goes red, the verdict often
names the bug — the mean updated after the deviation, $t$ counted from the stream's
start, a reference window that slides, the arithmetic mean in the cut, a late alarm
counted as false, the landmark returned for the window, the model learning before it
predicts — so read it before rereading your code.

The first alarms on the payment stream ring when no fraud has changed: DDM's on days 2 and 6, because its
warm-up ended on the stream's calmest items and a reset model then met its first
weekend, and the others' on day 7, when columns computed from labels seven days old
stop being zero. A detector on an error stream reports that something moved, and
only that.

The hand-built aggregates this lab's stream carries took a person to design, for one
task on one issuer's data. <Ref lesson="limits-of-hand-built-features" /> starts on
whether a model can learn that summary from the events themselves.

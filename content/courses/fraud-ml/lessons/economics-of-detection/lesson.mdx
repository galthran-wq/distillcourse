A detector catches 99 of every 100 frauds and wrongly flags 1 of every 100 genuine payments. On a stream where one payment in a thousand is fraud, what share of its alerts are actually fraud, what does each alert and each miss cost, and how many alerts a day should the bank pay people to look at?

<Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology">The typology of financial crime</Ref>
placed each crime by who labels it and how late. For the crimes that do
get labels, card fraud first, a detector now has to be judged, and the
first instinct from a course in classical machine learning is to read its
two error rates. Catching 99% of fraud and clearing 99% of genuine
payments sounds like a detector that is right 99 times in 100, so its
alerts should be fraud about 99 times in 100.

That instinct fails on all three questions of the opening. The share of
alerts that are fraud turns out to be about one in eleven. The error rates
say nothing about money, and the two kinds of error cost different amounts
to different people. And the number of alerts worth working is a staffing
decision that neither error rate answers. Each question needs one more
quantity the error rates leave out: the share of fraud in the stream, the
price of each outcome, and the size of the team.

## What share of the alerts are fraud: the base rate decides

Start with the first question, because the other two are priced in
alerts. The detector's two numbers are rates *within* a class: of the
frauds, 99% are flagged; of the genuine payments, 1% are flagged. The
question asks about a different group, the flagged payments, which mixes
the two classes. How they mix depends on how large each class is.

### Counting a thousand payments

The share of fraud in the stream is the *base rate*, written $\pi$: the
prevalence of the positive class, here $\pi = 0.001$.

<Example title="the 99%/99% detector at one fraud in a thousand">
Take 1,000 payments. With $\pi = 0.001$, one of them is fraud and 999 are
genuine.

**The fraud.** The detector flags it with probability 0.99, so on average
$0.99$ true alerts per thousand payments.

**The genuine payments.** It flags each with probability 0.01, so
$0.01 \times 999 \approx 10$ false alerts per thousand.

**The alerts.** About $0.99 + 9.99 \approx 11$ alerts per thousand
payments, of which about 1 is fraud. The share of alerts that are fraud is

$$
\frac{0.99}{0.99 + 9.99} \approx 0.09 .
$$

Out of every 100 alerts, about 9 are fraud and 91 are genuine customers.
</Example>

### Bayes' rule, and why the error rates cannot rescue precision

The example computed the *precision* of the detector, the share of its
alerts that are fraud, which you know from classical machine learning as
$\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})$. Written with the recall
$\mathrm{TPR}$ (the share of frauds flagged) and the false-positive rate
$\mathrm{FPR}$ (the share of genuine payments flagged), the same count is
Bayes' rule:

$$
\text{precision} = P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{alert})
= \frac{\mathrm{TPR}\cdot \pi}{\mathrm{TPR}\cdot \pi + \mathrm{FPR}\cdot(1-\pi)} .
$$

With $\mathrm{TPR} = 0.99$, $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.01$, $\pi = 0.001$ it gives
$0.00099 / (0.00099 + 0.00999) \approx 0.09$, the example's 9%.

The intuition that 99% and 99% make 99% treats the two error rates as if
they were the whole story. That intuition is common enough to have a name,
*base-rate neglect*: judging $P(\text{fraud} \mid \text{alert})$ from how
well the test separates the classes while ignoring how many members each
class has. It fails because the true alerts are drawn from the tiny class
and the false alerts from the huge one, so a small rate applied to 999
outnumbers a large rate applied to 1.

The cleanest way to see what a detector can and cannot do is to rewrite
Bayes' rule in odds:

$$
\underbrace{\frac{P(\text{fraud}\mid\text{alert})}{P(\text{genuine}\mid\text{alert})}}_{\text{odds after the alert}}
= \underbrace{\frac{\mathrm{TPR}}{\mathrm{FPR}}}_{\text{set by the detector}}
\times \underbrace{\frac{\pi}{1-\pi}}_{\text{set by the stream}} .
$$

The detector's only contribution is the ratio $\mathrm{TPR}/\mathrm{FPR}$,
here $0.99/0.01 = 99$. The stream starts at odds of 1 to 999. An alert
multiplies them by 99, to about 1 to 10: one fraud for every ten genuine
customers in the alert queue, the eleven squares of the figure.

<Remark title="what it takes to reach even odds">
For an alert to be fraud more often than not, $\mathrm{TPR}/\mathrm{FPR}$
must exceed $(1-\pi)/\pi \approx 999$. With recall near 1 that means a
false-positive rate below about 0.1%: one genuine payment in a thousand.
At one fraud in ten thousand it means one in ten thousand. The rarer the
crime, the more of the burden falls on the false-positive rate.
</Remark>

### The same detector on different streams

Hold the detector fixed and change only the stream. How does its
precision move as fraud gets rarer, from one payment in ten to one in a
hundred thousand?

At one in a hundred the alerts are a coin flip. At the 0.25% of the
Belgian e-commerce stream from <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"

At one fraud in a thousand, a detector that is 99% right on each class
produces about 9 frauds per 100 alerts. Error rates travel between streams
and precision does not. At a fixed base rate only the ratio
$\mathrm{TPR}/\mathrm{FPR}$ moves precision, and with recall already near 1
that means the false-positive rate.
</KeyFact>

## Accuracy prices every mistake the same

The detector of the first section misses one fraud in a hundred and
flags one genuine payment in a hundred. Whether that is good depends on
what each mistake costs, and the measure most courses start from,
accuracy, assumes that both cost the same.

On a stream with $\pi = 0.001$, a "detector" that never raises an alert is
wrong only on the frauds: its error rate is $0.001$, its accuracy
$0.999$. It catches nothing. The 99%/99% detector has accuracy $0.99$, so
by accuracy the do-nothing rule wins. The comparison is absurd for a
plain reason: accuracy counts a missed fraud and a wrong alert as one
error each, while a missed fraud costs the bank the stolen money and a
wrong alert costs an investigator a phone call.

So each of the four outcomes of a decision gets its own price.

<Definition title="cost matrix">
For a decision to alert or not on a payment that is fraud or genuine, the
*cost matrix* assigns a cost to each outcome: $c_{TP}$ (fraud, alerted),
$c_{FN}$ (fraud, not alerted), $c_{FP}$ (genuine, alerted) and $c_{TN}$
(genuine, not alerted). The cost of a set of decisions is the weighted
loss
$$
\text{cost} = \mathrm{TP}\cdot c_{TP} + \mathrm{FN}\cdot c_{FN} + \mathrm{FP}\cdot c_{FP} + \mathrm{TN}\cdot c_{TN} .
$$
</Definition>

Accuracy is the special case $c_{FN} = c_{FP} = 1$ and
$c_{TP} = c_{TN} = 0$. For card fraud both halves of that special case are
wrong: an alert costs money even when it is right, and a miss costs far
more than a false alert.

### What an alert costs: the call, and sometimes the customer

An alert flags a payment, and an investigator phones the holder of the
card that made it. That call costs the same whether the card turns out
compromised or not, so both alert cells carry the same price, the *alert
cost* $C_a$: $c_{TP} = c_{FP} = C_a$. The cost of a call is small next to
the loss from a fraud, a few euros of an investigator's time.

The unit needs a word of care. The detector scores payments, but the call
is about a card, and <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="a-hundred-cases-a-day-the-models-output-is-a-ranking">the layered
system</Ref> showed that one call settles all of a card's recent payments,
about three frauds on a compromised card. Until the budget section, every
flagged payment is priced as if it were its own call. That simplification
keeps the confusion matrix, the costs and the precision on one unit, the
payment; the budget section moves to cards and says how the two prices
relate.

That price holds only while the alert is a question. If the system acts on
its own, by blocking the card, a false positive costs the customer a card
that stops working, and the bank a customer who may move their spending
elsewhere. Scale makes this decisive. On a base of a million accounts, a
daily false-alarm rate of 1% shuts down 10,000 genuine customers a day.
That is why, as in <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="why-a-block-has-to-be-nearly-always-right">the argument for blocking
rules</Ref>, automatic action is kept for the rare patterns that are
almost always fraud, and everything else becomes a call.

### What a miss costs: the amount, or more

A missed fraud is where the conventions split. The common choice sets
$c_{FN}$ to the payment's amount, because the bank reimburses the
cardholder for an unauthorised payment. That makes each miss cost what it
actually cost, and makes a missed €2,000 fraud a hundred times worse than
a missed €20 one.

The objection comes from how fraudsters use a card. A stolen card is
usually tested first with a small payment, and if that goes through it is
used for large ones. A missed €1 probe costs far more than €1: it costs
the spending that follows. Charging every missed fraud the same fixed
price $c$ gives small and large frauds equal weight, which the probe
pattern argues for, at the price of no longer measuring the money lost.
The card's spending limit is a third candidate, as the most a missed
fraud can lead to.

No convention is agreed. The cost matrix is a modelling decision, made by
the bank, and every metric computed from it inherits that decision. Whatever
the convention, every outcome costs the bank something except one: leaving
a genuine payment alone, $c_{TN} = 0$.
<Ref lesson="example-dependent-cost">The lesson on example-dependent
cost</Ref> takes the amount convention and derives the decision rule it
implies, payment by payment.

<KeyFact>
The two errors are paid by different people in different currencies: a
missed fraud by the bank in reimbursed money (and by the next payments on
the card), a false alert by an investigator in minutes, and a wrong block
by a customer who cannot pay.
</KeyFact>

## What a detector is worth: the cost it removes

A cost matrix turns a confusion matrix into a sum of money. On its own
that sum means nothing: a detector that costs the bank €300,000 a month is
good if the alternative costs €1,000,000 and bad if the alternative costs
€200,000. A detector is a purchase, and it is judged against the
alternative the bank would otherwise run.

### The baseline: doing nothing

The alternative that every detector can be compared with is to accept
every payment and alert on none. Its cost is the blue column of the
figure above: $c_{FN}$ on every fraud, nothing else. With the amount
convention that is the total amount of fraud in the stream.

That baseline is also a ceiling. If 0.1% of 100 million payments are
fraud at £10 each, doing nothing loses £1 million, and no detector on
that stream can save more than £1 million, since a perfect one would
only remove the fraud loss and would still pay for its alerts. The
*value* of a detector is the cost of doing nothing minus its own cost:

$$
\text{value} = \text{cost}(\text{do nothing}) - \text{cost}(\text{detector}).
$$

Put the cost matrix into both terms, with $c_{TP} = c_{FP} = C_a$ and
$c_{TN} = 0$. Doing nothing misses every fraud, the $\mathrm{TP} +
\mathrm{FN}$ frauds the detector splits into caught and missed. The
detector pays a call on each of its alerts and the miss cost on the frauds
it lets through:

$$
\text{cost}(\text{do nothing}) = (\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN})\,c_{FN},
\qquad
\text{cost}(\text{detector}) = (\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP})\,C_a + \mathrm{FN}\,c_{FN}.
$$

Subtracting, the $\mathrm{FN}\,c_{FN}$ of the missed frauds appears in
both and cancels:

$$
\text{value} = \mathrm{TP}\,c_{FN} - (\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP})\,C_a
= \mathrm{TP}\cdot(c_{FN} - C_a) - \mathrm{FP}\cdot C_a :
$$

each caught fraud saves its miss cost less the call, and each false alert
costs a call. Nothing in this expression has to be positive.

<Example title="a 99%/99% detector worth less than nothing">
A bank sees 1,000,000 card payments a day at $\pi = 0.001$: 1,000 frauds
and 999,000 genuine payments. It runs the 99%/99% detector and,
unrealistically, works every alert, each priced as one call. A call costs $C_a = \text{€}10$; a missed
fraud costs its amount, on average $A$.

**Counts.** $\mathrm{TP} = 990$, $\mathrm{FN} = 10$,
$\mathrm{FP} = 9{,}990$. Alerts: $10{,}980$.

**Detector's cost.** Calls: $10{,}980 \times \text{€}10 = \text{€}109{,}800$. Misses:
$10 A$.

**Doing nothing.** $1{,}000 A$.

**At $A = \text{€}100$.** Doing nothing costs €100,000. The detector costs
$\text{€}109{,}800 + \text{€}1{,}000 = \text{€}110{,}800$. Its value is $-\text{€}10{,}800$ a day.

**At $A = \text{€}200$.** Doing nothing costs €200,000; the detector costs
€111,800; its value is $+\text{€}88{,}200$ a day.
</Example>

Where does the sign change? Setting the value to zero gives
$990 \cdot (A - 10) = 9{,}990 \times 10$, so $A \approx \text{€}111$. That number
has a direct reading. Precision is 9%, so each caught fraud comes with
about 11 alerts, and catching it costs about $11 \times \text{€}10 = \text{€}111$ in
calls. In general, the cost of one caught fraud is the alert cost divided
by the precision, and the detector pays only if a caught fraud is worth
more than that:

$$
\text{value} > 0 \iff c_{FN} > \frac{C_a}{\text{precision}} .
$$

<KeyFact>
Each caught fraud costs $C_a/\text{precision}$ in alerts: about eleven
calls at 9% precision. A detector with excellent error rates can lose
money when the frauds it catches are small and the stream is clean.
</KeyFact>

$C_a$ would make the detector break even? (b) Alternatively keep
$C_a = \text{€}10$: what false-positive rate would make it break even, with the
recall fixed at 99%? Write the value expression with the unknown in it

<Solution>
**(a)** Value $= 990\,(100 - C_a) - 9{,}990\,C_a = 99{,}000 - 10{,}980\,C_a$.
It is zero at $C_a = 99{,}000/10{,}980 \approx \text{€}9.02$. A call that costs a
euro less turns the loss into a break-even.

**(b)** With $C_a = \text{€}10$, value $= 990 \times 90 - \mathrm{FP}\times 10$,
zero at $\mathrm{FP} = 8{,}910$. Out of 999,000 genuine payments that is
$\mathrm{FPR} = 8{,}910 / 999{,}000 \approx 0.89\%$. Precision there is
$990/(990 + 8{,}910) = 10\%$, and $C_a/\text{precision} = \text{€}100 = A$, as
the break-even condition says.

The transferable move: with the stream and the recall fixed, the alert
cost and the false-positive rate both enter the value through the alert
bill $(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP})\,C_a$, and break-even fixes that bill at
$\mathrm{TP}\cdot c_{FN} = 990 \times \text{€}100 = \text{€}99{,}000$
however it is reached: $10{,}980 \times \text{€}9.02$ in (a),
$9{,}900 \times \text{€}10$ in (b). Because $\mathrm{FP}$ is ten times
$\mathrm{TP}$, almost all of that bill is false alerts
($\mathrm{FP}\cdot C_a \approx \text{€}90{,}000$ in (a), €89,100 in (b)),
so a cheaper call and a cleaner detector buy nearly the same thing.
</Solution>

## How many alerts to pay for: the budget that maximises value

The example worked all 10,980 alerts. No bank does: the team calls about
a hundred cards a day. <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="ranking-then-cutting-at-the-capacity">The layered system</Ref>
already drew the consequence: the model's output is used as a ranking,
and the team works its top $k$. What that lesson took as given, the
size of $k$, is the third question of the opening. Headcount sets $k$,
and someone decides the headcount.

### The value of the next call

The unit is now the card, because the team calls cards. Rank the day's
cards by the model's score. Write $p_r$ for the probability that the card
at rank $r$ is compromised; the ranking puts the likeliest first, so $p_r$
falls with $r$. A call still costs $C_a$, but what it saves is no longer
one payment's $c_{FN}$: it is the sum of $c_{FN}$ over the frauds the card
would otherwise carry until its holder noticed. Call that sum $V$. Under
the amount convention, $V$ is the money those remaining frauds would
take, several times the average fraud $A$ when a card carries about three.

The value of working the top $k$ cards is then the value formula of the
previous section with cards for payments, taken in expectation. Among the
top $k$, the expected number of compromised cards (the true positives) is
$\sum_{r \le k} p_r$ and the expected number of genuine ones is
$k - \sum_{r \le k} p_r$, so against doing nothing

$$
U(k) = \Big(\sum_{r=1}^{k} p_r\Big)(V - C_a) - \Big(k - \sum_{r=1}^{k} p_r\Big)C_a
= V\sum_{r=1}^{k} p_r \;-\; C_a\,k .
$$

Adding the $k$-th call changes the value by $V p_k - C_a$. Because $p_k$
falls, the early calls add a lot, later calls add less, and from some rank
on each call costs more than it is expected to save. The best budget
is the last rank at which the next call still pays:

$$
\text{work card } k \text{ while } p_k > \frac{C_a}{V} .
$$

<Example title="staffing for one ranking">
Suppose the card at rank $r$ is compromised with probability
$p_r = 0.3\,e^{-r/60}$. Summed over the top 100 cards that is about 15
compromised cards, the figure a logistic regression achieved on the
Belgian stream. Take $V = \text{€}500$ and $C_a = \text{€}10$.

**Stopping rank.** Work card $k$ while $0.3\,e^{-k/60} > 10/500 = 0.02$,
that is while $e^{-k/60} > 1/15$, so up to $k = 60 \ln 15 \approx 162$.

**Value there.** $\sum_{r \le k} p_r \approx 18\,(1 - e^{-k/60})$, so
$U(162) \approx 500 \times 16.8 - 10 \times 162 \approx \text{€}6{,}775$ a day.
At the current $k = 100$: $500 \times 14.6 - 1{,}000 \approx \text{€}6{,}300$.
At $k = 500$: $500 \times 18 - 5{,}000 \approx \text{€}4{,}000$.

**Hit rate there.** At $k = 162$ the team finds about 16.8 compromised
cards in 162 calls: about 10% of calls find fraud, and the last call has
a 2% chance.
</Example>

### Why hit rate is not a target

Banks track the share of investigated cases that turn out to be fraud,
and it is tempting to manage the team by it.

<Definition title="hit rate">
The *hit rate* at budget $k$ is the share of the $k$ investigated cases
that are fraud: $h(k) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{r \le k} y_r$, with $y_r = 1$ if
the case at rank $r$ is fraud. It is the precision of the top $k$.
</Definition>

The figure shows why raising it is the wrong goal. Along a fixed ranking
the hit rate falls with every call added, because each added card is less
likely than the one before. The highest hit rate is reached by working
one card, the most obvious one. A 100% hit rate does not mean the team is
efficient; it means the team stopped while the next cards in line were
still worth calling. In the example the value-maximising budget runs at
10% and leaves nine calls in ten finding nothing, and cutting to the 20%
hit rate of $k \approx 50$ throws away about €2,200 a day.

The hit rate does carry information when the budget is held fixed:
between two models working the same $k$, the one with the higher hit rate
finds more fraud. It stops carrying information when the budget is what
is being chosen.

<KeyFact>
The best budget stops where the last call's chance of finding fraud equals
$C_a/V$, here 2%, so a well-staffed team spends most of its calls on
genuine customers. A team proud of its hit rate is usually understaffed.
</KeyFact>

<Aside>
**Utilities with four signs.** The example priced only two outcomes, a
call and a caught card. A fuller accounting gives each of the four
outcomes a value in money. Investigating a fraud earns the amount
recovered plus any penalty or fine, less legal and investigation costs.
Investigating an innocent case costs the investigation but may earn a
deterrent effect: fraudsters who see that inspections happen commit less.
Leaving an innocent case alone is worth zero. Leaving a fraud alone is
strongly negative, and may carry a non-economic value set by management,
such as the damage to trust if the bank is known as an easy target.
Total utility at each budget is the number of each outcome times its
value, and the staffing rule is the same: grow the budget while the
marginal case adds utility. The same calculation sets the share of
customers to target in a marketing campaign, with expected response in
place of fraud probability.
</Aside>

## ★ Which alerts first: expected amount, not probability

The staffing example gave every compromised card the same worth $V$. Under
the amount convention two cases at the top of the queue can differ by a
factor of a thousand in what they put at stake, and ranking by
probability alone ignores that.

Compare two cases. One has a 90% probability of fraud on a €10 payment;
the other a 25% probability on a €10,000 payment. Ranked by probability,
the first goes ahead. In money, the first case puts
$0.9 \times \text{€}10 = \text{€}9$ at stake, the second $0.25 \times \text{€}10{,}000 =
\text{€}2{,}500$. An investigation that costs more than €9 is not worth starting
on the first case at all.

### Ranking by money at stake

The quantity that orders the queue is the *expected fraud amount*,
$P(\text{fraud}) \times \text{amount}$: the money the case is expected to
put at risk. Banding both axes turns the rule into a table.

### Where the line falls, and what it assumes

Where the line falls is set by the stopping rule of the budget section,
with the case's amount in place of $V$: a case is worth a call while its
expected amount exceeds $C_a$. With an unlimited team and a €10 call the
line would sit at €10. A real team runs out first: sorted by expected
amount, a day's queue reaches the end of the team's capacity at some case,
and the line sits at that case's expected amount. The grid is drawn for a
day on which the last case the team can afford puts €200 at stake. With a
larger team the line moves left and down toward €10, with a smaller one
up and right, and its stepped shape stays. Two cautions come
with it. The probabilities must be calibrated, since a score that reads
0.6 for cases that are fraud 10% of the time places every case in the
wrong column; <Ref lesson="undersampling-calibration">the lesson on
undersampling and calibration</Ref> shows how a common training trick
distorts exactly this. And the per-payment version of the staircase is
the decision rule of <Ref lesson="example-dependent-cost">the lesson on
example-dependent cost</Ref>, while <Ref lesson="score-to-action">the
lesson on score-to-action policies</Ref> builds the full set of zones a
bank acts on.

## What waiting costs: value falls as evidence grows

Every calculation so far scored a case once. A stolen card is not a single
payment: it produces a sequence of them, and the bank can choose when to
act. Waiting makes the decision easier and the saving smaller.

### One card, five chances to act

<Example title="one stolen card">
A card is stolen. The thief tests it with a €1 payment, then makes five
payments of €200 over two days, after which the holder notices and
complains. Suppose the model's probability that the card is compromised,
computed from its payments so far, grows as below. Calling after a given
payment saves the €200 payments still to come if the card is compromised,
so the expected saving of a call is that probability times the amount
still to be stolen.

| Bank acts after | Still to be stolen | P(fraud) so far | Expected saving |
|---|---|---|---|
| the €1 probe | €1,000 | 0.05 | €50 |
| 1st €200 | €800 | 0.40 | €320 |
| 2nd €200 | €600 | 0.70 | €420 |
| 3rd €200 | €400 | 0.85 | €340 |
| 4th €200 | €200 | 0.95 | €190 |
| 5th €200 | €0 | 0.99 | €0 |
| the complaint | €0 | 1.00 | €0 |
</Example>

### Two curves that cross

Acting at the probe saves the most but almost never
fires, because a €1 payment looks like any other. Waiting for certainty
means waiting for the complaint, when nothing is left to save: a fraud
that reveals itself is detected for free and worth nothing to detect. The
expected saving peaks in between, and where depends on how fast the
evidence accumulates against how fast the money leaves.

### Time to detection and graded responses

Two consequences follow for how a detector is judged and run. First, a
count of frauds caught hides when they were caught. A detector that flags
the card after the fifth payment and one that flags it after the second
have the same recall on that card and very different value, so the time
to detection, in minutes or in payments after the first fraud, belongs in
the evaluation beside the hit rate and the cost. Second, the budget can
be spent in grades. Cards with very high scores get an immediate,
expensive response: a call now, or a block. Cards with high but not
extreme scores go on a watch list, where the next payment on the card
triggers a call at a lower score than a fresh card would need, so that
their evidence can mature without a call spent on each.

<KeyFact>
The money still at stake is largest when the evidence is weakest, and the
evidence is strongest when nothing is left, so the expected saving of a
catch peaks in between: on the stolen card, €420 after the second large
payment against €50 at the probe and nothing at the complaint. A metric
that counts
only whether a fraud was caught, and not after how many payments, cannot
see the difference between a detector that saves the card and one that
confirms the loss.
</KeyFact>

You can now answer the three questions of the opening for any detector.
The share of alerts that are fraud follows from the error rates and the
base rate by Bayes' rule, and at one fraud in a thousand a 99%/99%
detector delivers about 9 in 100. Each outcome gets a price in a cost
matrix whose miss cell is a modelling decision, and a detector's value is
the cost it removes from doing nothing, which can be negative and turns
positive only when a caught fraud is worth more than $C_a/\text{precision}$.
The number of alerts to work is the budget at which the next call's
expected saving equals its cost, which leaves a low hit rate by design;
and the timing of a catch is part of its value. Every number in these calculations had to be
assumed: the base rate, the amounts, when the labels arrive. <Ref
lesson="canonical-datasets">The next lesson</Ref> asks what the public
datasets of this field actually contain, how their labels were made, and
which of these quantities each one hides.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Run the 99%/99% detector over two other streams: a card stream with one
fraud in 200 payments, and a laundering stream with one suspicious
transfer in 10,000. Give the precision of each, and the odds of fraud
after an alert.

<Solution>
**One in 200.** $\pi = 0.005$. Precision
$= 0.99 \times 0.005 / (0.99 \times 0.005 + 0.01 \times 0.995)
= 0.00495 / 0.01490 \approx 0.33$. In odds: $1{:}199$ times 99 is about
$1{:}2$, one fraud per two genuine alerts.

**One in 10,000.** $\pi = 0.0001$. Precision
$= 0.000099 / (0.000099 + 0.009999) \approx 0.0098$, about 1%. In odds:
$1{:}9{,}999$ times 99 is about $1{:}101$.

The same detector goes from a third of its alerts being right to one in a
hundred, a factor of 34, as the base rate falls by a factor of 50. Once
the posterior odds are small, precision is nearly proportional to the
base rate.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push the false-positive rate to its edges. (a) A detector has
specificity 100%, so $\mathrm{FPR} = 0$, and recall 50%. What is its
precision at $\pi = 0.001$ and at $\pi = 0.000001$? (b) Now take
$\mathrm{FPR} = 0.01\%$ and recall 99%. At what base rate is the
precision exactly 50%? (c) For each case, name the step of the funnel
argument that changes.

<Solution>
**(a)** With $\mathrm{FPR} = 0$ there are no false alerts: precision
$= \mathrm{TPR}\,\pi / (\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi + 0) = 1$ at any base rate and
any nonzero recall. Recall decides how many frauds are flagged, not what
share of the flags are fraud.

**(b)** Precision is 50% when true and false alerts are equal:
$0.99\,\pi = 0.0001\,(1 - \pi)$, so $\pi = 0.0001/0.9901 \approx 0.000101$,
about one fraud in 9,900. In odds: the detector multiplies the odds by
$0.99/0.0001 = 9{,}900$, so it reaches even odds from a starting point of
$1{:}9{,}900$.

**(c)** The funnel's whole effect rests on false alerts being drawn from
the huge class: a small rate times a large count. In (a) the rate is zero,
so the size of the genuine class no longer matters and the base rate drops
out. In (b) the rate is small enough to balance the count at one in about
ten thousand; for any rarer crime the false alerts win again. No real
detector reaches (a), which is why rare crimes push all the work onto the
false-positive rate.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "Our new model raised the hit rate from 40% to 80%, so it
is better than the old one." Construct one ranking for which both numbers
come out of the same model, and name the step at which the claim fails.
Then, with $V = \text{€}500$ and $C_a = \text{€}10$, say which of the two operating
points is worth more.

<Solution>
**One ranking, two numbers.** Suppose a model's top 20 cards hold 16
compromised cards and its top 100 hold 40. At $k = 20$ its hit rate is
$16/20 = 80\%$; at $k = 100$ it is $40/100 = 40\%$. If the old model was
measured at $k = 100$ and the new one at $k = 20$, the "improvement" is
produced by the budget, not by the model.

**The failing step.** The claim compares hit rates without fixing $k$.
Along any ranking the hit rate falls as $k$ grows, because each added card
is less likely than the last, so a smaller budget always shows a higher
hit rate. The comparison is valid only at the same $k$.

**Which point is worth more.** At $k = 20$: $16 \times 500 - 20 \times 10
= €7{,}800$. At $k = 100$: $40 \times 500 - 100 \times 10 = €19{,}000$. The
40% operating point is worth more than twice the 80% one, because the
80 extra calls find 24 more compromised cards at €500 each for €800 of
calls.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the stopping rule for a general cost structure. The cases are
worked in rank order, the case at rank $r$ is fraud with probability
$p_r$ (falling in $r$), and the four outcomes have utilities $u_{TP}$
(investigate a fraud), $u_{FP}$ (investigate an innocent case), $u_{FN}$
(skip a fraud) and $u_{TN}$ (skip an innocent case). Show that the total
expected utility of working the top $k$ is maximised by working case $k$
exactly while
$$
p_k\,(u_{TP} - u_{FN}) + (1 - p_k)\,(u_{FP} - u_{TN}) > 0 ,
$$
and check that the lesson's rule $p_k > C_a/V$ is a special case.

<Solution>
**Total utility.** With $n$ cases, working the top $k$ gives expected
utility
$$
T(k) = \sum_{r \le k} \big[p_r u_{TP} + (1-p_r) u_{FP}\big]
+ \sum_{r > k} \big[p_r u_{FN} + (1-p_r) u_{TN}\big].
$$

**One more case.** Moving case $k$ from the skipped set to the worked set
changes the total by
$$
T(k) - T(k-1) = p_k (u_{TP} - u_{FN}) + (1-p_k)(u_{FP} - u_{TN}) =: g(p_k).
$$

**Monotonicity.** $g$ is linear in $p$ with slope
$(u_{TP} - u_{FN}) - (u_{FP} - u_{TN})$. Catching a fraud gains more over
skipping it than investigating an innocent case gains over skipping it
(the first difference is large and positive, the second usually negative),
so the slope is positive. Since $p_k$ falls with $k$, so does $g(p_k)$:
the increments are positive up to some rank and negative after it. $T$
therefore rises while $g(p_k) > 0$ and falls afterwards, and the maximum
is at the last $k$ with $g(p_k) > 0$.

**Special case.** In the lesson, utilities are measured against doing
nothing: $u_{FN} = u_{TN} = 0$, $u_{TP} = V - C_a$, $u_{FP} = -C_a$. Then
$g(p) = p(V - C_a) - (1-p)C_a = pV - C_a$, and $g(p_k) > 0$ is
$p_k > C_a/V$.

The threshold in general is
$p^* = (u_{TN} - u_{FP}) / \big[(u_{TP} - u_{FN}) + (u_{TN} - u_{FP})\big]$:
a deterrent value of inspecting innocent cases (raising $u_{FP}$) or a
penalty attached to unpunished fraud (lowering $u_{FN}$) both lower $p^*$
and enlarge the best budget.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Suppose an insurer compares two detectors on a test set of claims holding 67
fraudulent claims. A boosted-tree model flags 90 claims, 58 of them
fraudulent; a nearest-neighbour model flags 147, 59 of them fraudulent.
Measured by ROC area the tree wins (0.86 against 0.75), by recall the
neighbour model wins narrowly (88% against 87%). The average fraudulent
claim is \$60,302, and investigating a flagged claim costs \$4,214 of an
investigator's month. (a) Using a cost matrix with $c_{TP} = c_{FP} =$
the investigation cost, $c_{FN} =$ the average claim and $c_{TN} = 0$,
compute each model's cost and the percentage by which one is cheaper. (b)
Recompute with an investigation cost of \$400 and say which model wins
and why. (c) State one way this cost model is too simple.

<Solution>
**(a) Confusion counts.** Tree: $\mathrm{TP} = 58$, $\mathrm{FN} = 67 - 58 = 9$,
$\mathrm{FP} = 90 - 58 = 32$. Neighbours: $\mathrm{TP} = 59$,
$\mathrm{FN} = 8$, $\mathrm{FP} = 147 - 59 = 88$.

Tree, in dollars: misses $9 \times 60{,}302 = 542{,}718$; investigations
$90 \times 4{,}214 = 379{,}260$; total \$921,978.

Neighbours, in dollars: misses $8 \times 60{,}302 = 482{,}416$;
investigations $147 \times 4{,}214 = 619{,}458$; total \$1,101,874.

The tree is cheaper by $1 - 921{,}978/1{,}101{,}874 \approx 16.3\%$. The
neighbour model's one extra caught claim saves \$60,302; its 57 extra
investigations cost \$240,198.

**(b) At \$400.** Tree: $542{,}718 + 90 \times 400 = 578{,}718$ dollars.
Neighbours: $482{,}416 + 147 \times 400 = 541{,}216$ dollars. The neighbour
model now wins by about \$37,500. When investigations are cheap, the 57
extra flags cost only \$22,800 and the extra caught claim is worth more
than that. The ROC area did not change, but the ranking of the two models
reversed: which model is better depends on the cost ratio
$c_{FN}/C_a$, not on a threshold-free summary.

**(c)** Possible answers: every fraudulent claim is charged the average
amount, while the misses of the two models may be smaller or larger
claims than average; an investigated fraud is assumed to recover the full
claim; investigation cost is a month's salary regardless of the case;
there is no value for deterrence; and both models are compared at their
default thresholds, while each could be cut at the budget that maximises
its own value.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Go back to the five crimes of <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="the-typology-five-crimes-four-coordinates">the typology</Ref>: card
fraud, account takeover, laundering, mules and structuring, sanctions
evasion. For each, say whether the convention "$c_{FN}$ = the amount" is
even definable, and if not, what a missed case costs the bank instead.

<Solution>
**Card fraud.** Definable: the bank reimburses the unauthorised payment,
so the amount is a real loss. It understates the cost of a missed probe,
whose real cost is the spending that follows.

**Account takeover.** Definable: the amounts drained from the account,
which the bank usually reimburses. The unit is the account's stream, so
the miss cost is the sum over the payments made after the takeover, not
one payment's amount.

**Laundering.** Not definable as an amount lost. No customer is robbed,
and the bank's money is not taken. A missed case costs regulatory
exposure: fines and remediation if a supervisor finds the bank failed to
report, plus a social cost the bank is required to weigh. The label is
an STR, years late or never, so even the count of misses is unknown.

**Mules and structuring.** The amounts passed through the account are
measurable but are not the bank's loss; the cost is again regulatory, and
where the mule account receives a fraud victim's money the bank may face a
reimbursement claim.

**Sanctions evasion.** Not a cost trade-off at all: a missed designated
counterparty is a breach of a legal duty, with penalties set by law
regardless of the amount. There is no budget at which missing one is
acceptable, which is why screening is list matching rather than a ranking
cut.

Read down the list and the cost matrix gets less meaningful as the
label disappears: the crimes with a complaining victim have a price for a
miss, and the others have a penalty that a bank cannot write into one
cell.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Ten payments are scored, two of them fraud. Three models rank them, and
the positions of the two frauds in each ranking (1 = most suspicious) are:
model A at 1 and 8; model B at 4 and 5; model C at 3 and 6. (a) Compute
the area under the ROC curve of each, as the share of (fraud, genuine)
pairs in which the fraud is ranked above the genuine payment. (b) Compute
the share of fraud among the top 1 and among the top 3 for each. (c) A
team can work one alert a day; another can work three. Which model should
each choose, and what does (a) tell them?

<Solution>
**(a)** There are $2 \times 8 = 16$ pairs. A fraud at rank $r$ is above
every genuine payment ranked below it.

- A: the fraud at 1 is above all 8 genuine payments; the fraud at 8 is
  above the 2 genuine payments at 9 and 10. $10/16 = 0.625$.
- B: the fraud at 4 is above ranks 5–10 minus the other fraud, 5 genuine;
  the fraud at 5 is above 5 genuine. $10/16 = 0.625$.
- C: the fraud at 3 is above ranks 4–10 minus one fraud, 6 genuine; the
  fraud at 6 is above 4. $10/16 = 0.625$.

**(b)** Top 1: A $= 1$, B $= 0$, C $= 0$. Top 3: A $= 1/3$, B $= 0$,
C $= 1/3$.

**(c)** The one-alert team should take A, the only model whose first
alert is fraud. The three-alert team is indifferent between A and C and
should never take B, which finds nothing in either budget. The ROC area
is the same for all three, so it cannot make either choice: it averages
over every possible cut, while each team works at one cut. This is the
gap that <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">the lesson on alert-budget
metrics</Ref> closes by measuring precision at the budget, the hit rate
of this lesson at the team's own $k$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

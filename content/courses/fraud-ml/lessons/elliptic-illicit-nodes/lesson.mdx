On 203,769 Bitcoin transactions, 2% labelled illicit, a random forest on the given features beats a two-layer graph network, every model's overall F1 sits between 0.92 and 0.99, and at the 43rd fortnight all of them fall to zero. What in the dataset, the features and the time split produces each of those three facts?

The numbers come from <Ref lesson="graph-neural-networks"
id="the-elliptic-comparison">the Elliptic comparison</Ref> of the
previous chapter: logistic regression, a feed-forward network and a
random forest, each on the 94 local columns and on all 166, against a
GCN, a Skip-GCN and EvolveGCN, all trained on time steps 1 to 34 and
scored on steps 35 to 49. The forest on all 166 columns reached an
illicit-class F1 of 0.788, the plain GCN 0.628. The same comparison
printed a second, "micro-averaged" F1 for every model, and a per-step
chart in which every line drops at step 43.

Each fact invites a reading that is wrong. The first says graph networks
do not work on transaction graphs. The second says the task is nearly
solved. The third says the models were stale and retraining would have
fixed them. None of the three survives a look at what the benchmark
contains: which nodes are scored, what the 166 columns already hold,
what each F1 averages over, and what happened to the illicit
transactions at step 43.

## What is scored: 16,670 labelled nodes in fifteen snapshots

Every number in the comparison is computed on a test set, and the test
set is not the 203,769 nodes. <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="elliptic-a-graph-whose-labels-come-from-names">The Elliptic
graph</Ref> is 49 snapshots about two weeks apart, each one connected
component of transactions that reached the ledger within three hours,
and 77% of its nodes carry no label.

### The test set: labelled nodes only

Only labelled nodes can be scored, so the test set is the labelled
nodes of steps 35 to 49: 16,670 transactions, 1,083 of them illicit
(6.5%). The training set, the labelled nodes of steps 1 to 34, holds
3,462 illicit among 29,894 (11.6%). The unknown nodes stay in each
step's graph, where a graph network aggregates over them, but they are
never scored, <Ref lesson="graph-neural-networks"
id="which-nodes-carry-a-loss">because unknown is not licit</Ref>.

### No edge crosses the split

The split by time follows the rule of <Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="a-shuffled-split-tests-a-model-that-cannot-exist">the validation
chapter</Ref>: a test predicts deployment only if the model is trained
on what existed before the test period. On a graph a random split
breaks a second thing. Draw 30% of every step's labelled nodes at
random as the test set, and about 70% of a test node's labelled
neighbours are training nodes, whose labels the model was fitted to. Illicit transactions sit next to illicit ones
often enough for that to matter: in the training steps, a third of the
illicit nodes have an illicit neighbour. A column that counts illicit
neighbours then reads part of the answer off the training labels next
door, and a graph network trained on the same step has fitted its
weights to those neighbours, three hours of the ledger away from the
node it is tested on.

<Figure
  src="./figures/split.svg"
  alt="Left: four small graphs labelled steps 33, 34, 35 and 36; the first two have blue training nodes, the last two amber test nodes, and a vertical line between steps 34 and 35 is marked: no edge crosses the cut. Right: one graph whose nodes are a random mix of blue training and amber test nodes; the edges that join a test node to a training node are drawn thick in amber"
  caption="Because no edge joins two steps, the split by time leaves no training node beside a test node; a random split inside each step puts training labels next to test nodes."
/>

The clean split also decides which graph networks can be tested at all.
The test steps are graphs the model never saw in training, so a model
that learns one row per node has no row for a test node; only a layer
whose weights apply to any graph, <Ref lesson="graph-neural-networks"
id="a-model-that-transfers-to-graphs-it-has-not-seen">an inductive
one</Ref>, can score them.

### The time step is a column

The first of the 94 local columns is the time step itself. A model is
free to learn "later steps are more illicit", and in training that is
what the data says: steps 1 to 6 hold 1% illicit among their labelled
nodes, steps 7 to 34 about 16%. In the test steps the share falls to
6.5%. A model that uses the step therefore carries a trend from
training into a test period where the trend has reversed.

<Example title="what the step column does to a logistic regression">
Rerunning the comparison on the public files (the release distributes
the 166 columns, the labels and the edge list), fit a logistic
regression on the 94 local columns, standardised on the training
steps, and cut at 0.5. Its coefficient on the standardised step is
0.58; the step's standard deviation over the training nodes is 10.3
steps, so the logit rises by $0.58 / 10.3 = 0.057$ per step.

A node at step 42 then gets $8 \times 0.057 = 0.45$ more on the logit
than an identical node at step 34, and a node at step 49 gets
$15 \times 0.057 = 0.85$ more: its odds of being called illicit are
multiplied by $e^{0.85} = 2.3$. Nothing in the test steps supports
that; their illicit share is below the training share.

The model flags 4,382 test nodes and finds 79% of the illicit ones,
with precision 0.20: illicit F1 0.31. Dropped from the inputs, the step
leaves a model that flags 2,016 nodes, finds 71% of the illicit ones at
precision 0.38: illicit F1 0.50. One column, used the way a linear
model uses it, costs 0.18 of F1.
</Example>

The published logistic regression on the same 94 columns, step
included, scored 0.457, far above the rerun's 0.31, and no default
setting brings the rerun there: on the columns as released, without
standardising, with either of the two optimisers scikit-learn has used
by default, it scores 0.32 to 0.36 with the step and 0.48 to 0.51
without. The published run differs in something it does not report. The
0.18 therefore compares two fits of the rerun that differ in one column
and nothing else; it does not explain the published row.

A tree cannot carry the trend forward: every threshold it can place on the
step lies between two training values, so every test step lands on the
same side as step 34 (<Ref lesson="elliptic-illicit-nodes"
id="exercises">the exercise on a tree and the step column</Ref> works
this out). The rerun checks this on the published forest configuration:
50 trees, 50 columns tried at each split, trained on steps 1 to 34, cut
at 0.5, fitted with three random seeds. On all 166 columns it scores
illicit F1 0.81 to 0.82, a spread of under 0.02 from seed to seed, and
dropping the step moves each seed's score by at most 0.01. The column is
still a hazard for any model that extends a fitted trend beyond the last
training step, and the safe default is to drop it.

## The forest's win: the graph was already in the columns

The previous chapter left a question open: what in the 166 columns lets
the forest win? <Ref lesson="graph-neural-networks"
id="reading-the-table">Its reading of the table</Ref> gave the general
reason, that a forest cuts the features it was given with thresholds
while a GCN ends in a logistic regression on features it learned. That
leaves two things to find out: what the forest was given, and where in
its predictions the lead lies.

### The 72 aggregates are a fixed layer

For each local quantity of a transaction (number of inputs, fee,
volume and so on), the aggregated columns hold the maximum, the minimum
and the standard deviation of that quantity over the transactions one
hop away, backward and forward, together with correlation coefficients
computed over the same neighbours. A correlation pairs two quantities,
and which pairs were used is not stated: the release does not describe
its columns exactly. They are <Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="recursive-aggregates-over-the-neighbours">the recursive aggregates</Ref>
of the neighbourhood chapter.

<Figure
  src="./figures/aggregates-layer.svg"
  alt="A node v with three neighbours carrying the values 0.2, 0.9 and 0.4 of one feature. Lines fan from v to four boxes: max 0.9, min 0.2, std 0.29, corr with a second feature. An arrow leads to a blue box: appended to v's row as fixed columns, no weight is learned. A list beside it: gather, v's neighbours; aggregate, max, min, std, corr, chosen in advance; update, append to v's own 94. A note: a GCN layer gathers the same neighbours but aggregates with the normalised sum and learns W"
  caption="The aggregated columns run the gather, aggregate and update steps of a graph layer once, with aggregators chosen by hand and nothing learned."
/>

Set beside <Ref lesson="graph-neural-networks"
id="one-layer-of-message-passing">a message-passing layer</Ref>, that
construction is one: it gathers each node's neighbours, aggregates with
four functions, and updates the node's row by appending the results.
Its maximum and minimum are aggregators that
<Ref lesson="graph-neural-networks"
id="functions-of-a-set-sum-mean-and-maximum">a sum or mean over
neighbours</Ref> can only approximate through a learned transform.

So every model on all 166 columns reads the graph, and the comparison
is not the graph against no graph. It is a hand-built layer read by
thresholds against a learned layer stacked on it (a GCN's first layer averages the aggregates over the neighbours again,
reaching two hops) and read by a logistic output. A threshold on "the
largest fee among the neighbours" is the use a hand-built maximum was
made for.

### The lead is precision

The forest's F1 of 0.788 against the Skip-GCN's 0.705 does not say whether the forest finds
more illicit transactions or raises fewer false alerts. The published
table gives each model's illicit precision and recall, and with the
1,083 illicit test nodes the two give back the counts: recall says how
many illicit nodes were found, precision how many nodes were flagged to
find them.

<Example title="the forest's counts from its precision and recall">
The forest on all 166 columns has illicit recall 0.670, so it finds
$0.670 \times 1{,}083 = 726$ illicit nodes and misses 357. Its precision
is 0.956, so it flags $726 / 0.956 = 759$ nodes, of which
$759 - 726 = 33$ are licit: 33 false alerts.

As a check, illicit F1 is
$2 \cdot 726 / (2 \cdot 726 + 33 + 357) = 1{,}452 / 1{,}842 = 0.788$,
the printed value.
</Example>

The same arithmetic fills the rest of the table: the graph networks'
rows, and the feed-forward network's, which reads the same 166 columns
through layers of weights on each node's own row, with no graph layer.

| model (all 166 columns) | precision | recall | illicit found | flagged | false alerts |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| random forest | 0.956 | 0.670 | 726 | 759 | 33 |
| Skip-GCN | 0.812 | 0.623 | 675 | 831 | 156 |
| GCN | 0.812 | 0.512 | 554 | 682 | 128 |
| EvolveGCN | 0.850 | 0.624 | 676 | 795 | 119 |
| feed-forward network | 0.694 | 0.617 | 668 | 963 | 295 |

The forest and the Skip-GCN find nearly the same number of illicit
transactions, 726 against 675. The forest raises 33 false alerts to
get them; the Skip-GCN raises 156, almost five times as many reviews
spent on licit transactions for 51 fewer finds. The plain GCN loses on
recall too, which is <Ref lesson="graph-neural-networks"
id="reading-the-table">the dilution the skip term repairs</Ref>.

Where does the forest's precision come from? The same forest on the 94
local columns alone had precision 0.803 and recall 0.611. Adding the 72
aggregates raised its recall by six points and its precision by fifteen,
to 0.956: the forest's thresholds on those columns isolate regions that
hold almost nothing licit.

Is the graph networks' lower precision the price of their learned graph
layer? The feed-forward network answers that. It has no graph layer, it
ends in the same logistic output, and it finds 668 illicit transactions,
about as many as the Skip-GCN, with 295 false alerts. The learned graph
layer helps a network, from 295 false alerts to 156, but every network
in the table stops between 0.69 and 0.85 precision on these columns,
with a graph layer or without one, while the forest's thresholds reach
0.956. The gap lies between the readers of the columns, thresholds
against weighted sums, which is what <Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="a-tie-on-the-simulator-a-loss-below-the-top-on-real-data">the
comparison of networks and trees on card payments</Ref> found: on
columns that are already engineered summaries, a network ties a tree
ensemble at best.

### How sure the numbers are

Every published number is a single run, so a gap between two of them
may be a gap between two seeds. The one that carries the argument above
is the forest's gain from the aggregates, <Ref
lesson="graph-neural-networks" id="reading-the-table">0.694 to
0.788</Ref>, the largest any model drew from them. The rerun's forest,
fitted with three seeds, measures it again. On all 166 columns the three
seeds score 0.822, 0.811 and 0.809; on the 94 local columns, 0.779,
0.758 and 0.766. Paired by seed, the aggregates add 0.043, 0.053 and
0.043, at least twice the seed-to-seed spread, which is 0.013 on all 166
columns and 0.021 on the local ones. The direction reproduces. The size
does not: the published forest on local columns scored 0.694, below
every run of the rerun, so the published gain of 0.094 is nearly twice
the largest the rerun finds.

The same spread settles the smaller gaps between published models.
EvolveGCN's 0.720 against the Skip-GCN's 0.705 is a difference of
0.015, which one forest moves between two seeds; the table cannot rank
those two.

## The overall F1: a score the licit majority sets

The second column of the published table, an F1 "micro-averaged" over
both classes, is printed between 0.920 and 0.986 for the same models whose
illicit-class F1 runs from 0.457 to 0.796. Two scores of one prediction
cannot both describe its quality, and the difference lies in what each
one counts.

### Two averages of one confusion matrix

The illicit-class F1 is computed from three counts of the illicit
class: illicit nodes found (true positives), licit nodes flagged (false
positives) and illicit nodes missed (false negatives). The
micro-averaged F1 pools those counts over both classes, and for a
classifier that gives each node one label it equals accuracy, as <Ref
lesson="graph-neural-networks" id="exercises">the last exercise of the
previous chapter</Ref> showed.

The forest's counts from <Ref lesson="elliptic-illicit-nodes"
id="the-lead-is-precision">its precision and recall</Ref> give both. Of
the 15,587 licit test nodes it flags 33 and leaves $15{,}587 - 33 =
15{,}554$ alone, so it is right on $726 + 15{,}554$ nodes:

$$
\text{accuracy} = \frac{726 + 15{,}554}{16{,}670} = 0.977,
$$

the printed micro-averaged F1, against an illicit F1 of 0.788 from the
same four counts. The 15,554 licit nodes correctly left alone fill the
micro numerator and appear nowhere in the illicit F1.

The same recount checks every row of the micro column, and the top of
its printed range fails it. The 0.986 belongs to the feed-forward
network on the local columns plus the GCN's hidden vectors, whose
precision 0.682 and recall 0.578 give 626 illicit found, 457 missed and
$626 / 0.682 - 626 = 292$ licit flagged: $749$ mistakes, an accuracy of
$1 - 749 / 16{,}670 = 0.955$. No one prediction has all three printed
values. The forests' and graph networks' rows pass within rounding and
run from 0.961 to 0.978; the other logistic-regression and feed-forward
rows print micro values a few thousandths to two hundredths above their
recount, as <Ref lesson="elliptic-illicit-nodes" id="exercises">the
first exercise</Ref> finds on one of them.

### Doing nothing scores 0.935

The predictor that calls every test node licit is right on the 15,587
licit nodes and wrong on the 1,083 illicit ones: accuracy, hence
micro-F1, $15{,}587 / 16{,}670 = 0.935$. That is the floor against which
the micro column must be read.

Two of the published models sit below the floor: logistic regression at
0.920 on the local columns and 0.931 on all 166. They flag so many
licit transactions that they make more mistakes than flagging nothing.

  src="./figures/micro-vs-illicit.svg"

Above the floor, the micro column measures how a model trades its few
illicit hits against its licit mistakes, both counted on a scale set by
15,587 licit nodes. This is <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="accuracy-prices-every-mistake-the-same">accuracy's flaw</Ref> in
graph form: every mistake costs the same, and the licit class is
fourteen times larger, so any detector that flags little scores close
to 1. An investigation team needs something else again. It works down a
ranked list until its daily budget runs out, so what it needs is <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="precision-at-k-the-metric-cut-where-the-team-cuts">the precision of
that list at its budget</Ref>, computed step by step, or average
precision; both F1s judge <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="a-threshold-metric-judges-a-cut-nobody-chose">one cut nobody
chose</Ref>, the default 0.5. The illicit-class F1 is the closer of the
two to the team's measure, because it ignores the licit nodes nobody
flagged.

## Step 43: the fall no model survives

Where does the fall at step 43 come from? The opening offered one
reading: the models went stale, and retraining would have fixed them. A
fall in illicit F1 can come from three places, each with its own
repair. It can lie in the model, in one architecture or in weights
fitted to old data, and then another model or a retrained one repairs
it. It can lie in the threshold, if the scores still rank the new
illicit transactions high but no longer above 0.5, and then a lower cut
repairs it. Or it can lie in the data, if the illicit transactions
themselves changed, and then no model fitted to the old labels repairs
it. The evidence answers in that order.

### The model: every architecture falls, retrained or not

Scored step by step, the rerun's forest on all 166 columns (its first
seed) shows what its pooled 0.82 is made of: illicit F1 between 0.78
and 0.96 on every step up to 42, then 0, 0.06, 0, 0.29, 0, 0 and 0.03.
Pooled over steps 35 to 42 it is 0.91; over steps 43 to 49, 0.03. The
pooled figure looks like a good model because 914 of the 1,083 illicit
nodes come before the break.

<Figure
  src="./figures/per-step.svg"

Amber, illicit F1, lies between 0.78 and 0.96 up to step 42, then falls to 0 at step 43 and stays between 0 and 0.29 to step 49. A dashed line before step 43 is marked: dark market closes. Labels: steps 35 to 42 pooled 0.91; steps 43 to 49 pooled 0.03"

The micro-averaged F1 does not register the break. On step 43 the
forest finds none of 24 illicit nodes, flags 2 of the 1,346 licit ones,
and scores $1{,}344 / 1{,}370 = 0.981$, because the 1,344 licit nodes
it still calls licit fill the count. A monitoring dashboard that tracked
the overall F1 would have shown nothing at the moment the detector
stopped working.

Is the fall this forest's? The per-step chart published with the
benchmark scores seven models: three forests and four graph networks.
The forests are the one on all features, one fed the GCN's hidden
vectors as extra columns, and one retrained before every test step on
all labels up to the step before, as if investigators labelled each
fortnight instantly and completely. The fourth graph network is
Skip-EvolveGCN, EvolveGCN with the skip term added.

Every one of the seven scores near zero from step 43 on, apart from
isolated steps where a handful of illicit nodes were found. The rerun
agrees for the retrained forest: 0, 0.07, 0, 0.5 (on a step with two
illicit nodes), 0, 0.11 and 0.16 on steps 43 to 49. Forests and graph
networks fall together, so the fall is not an architecture's; the
forest retrained on every label falls with them, and so does EvolveGCN,
whose weights change from step to step, so it is not stale weights
either.

### The threshold: the ranking collapses too

A failure at one cut could be a calibration problem: the scores may
still rank illicit above licit, only below 0.5. They do not. On step 43
the forest's scores give an average precision of 0.029, against the
0.018 that <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-a-ranking-with-no-information-scores">a ranking with no
information</Ref> scores at that step's base rate of 24 in 1,370; its
fifty highest-scored transactions hold no illicit one. Before the break
the same fifty held between 29 and 50 per step. The scores carry
almost nothing about the new illicit transactions, so no choice of cut
or budget would recover them.

### The data: fewer illicit transactions, and the rest look licit

Two things changed at step 43, and the data shows both. The first is
the count: step 42 holds 239 illicit transactions, step 43 holds 24,
and the illicit share among labelled nodes falls from 11% to under 2%.
A dark market, a site selling illegal goods for Bitcoin, closed
between the two snapshots, and the illicit count fell with it.

The second is what the remaining illicit transactions look like. Take
each test transaction, find the five labelled training transactions
nearest to it on the 93 local columns other than the step, each column
standardised over the training nodes so that no single unit dominates
the distance, and count how many of the five are illicit.

<Figure
  src="./figures/neighbours.svg"

Before the closure, an illicit test transaction has on average 78%
illicit among its nearest training transactions: it resembles what
training called illicit. After it, 8%, against 5% to 6% for licit test
transactions. The illicit transactions of steps 43 to 49 sit in the
part of the feature space that training filled with licit ones. A
model that learned where illicit transactions lie cannot find
transactions that now lie elsewhere, whatever its architecture.

### Why retraining on every label cannot catch up

The change in the data also explains why the retrained forest failed.
Each new step adds about two dozen illicit transactions of the new kind
to the 4,000 and more older ones, and a forest's vote in a region of
the feature space follows the majority of the training transactions
there, which stay licit. And the labels of a step arrive only after the
step has been scored; in a bank they would arrive <Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential" id="the-label-clock">a delay later
still</Ref>, so the forest retrained on instant, complete labels is the
best case. EvolveGCN fails
for the same reason: its weights for a step are computed from the
weights of earlier steps, which were fitted to illicit transactions of
the old kind.

<KeyFact>
The break also bounds every pooled number of the comparison. 169 of the
1,083 illicit test transactions lie beyond it, so a model that misses
them recalls at most $914/1{,}083 = 0.84$, not 1: the published
recalls, none above 0.68, are read against that ceiling, and
the pooled table measures steps 35 to 42 with a fixed penalty for the
seven steps after.
</KeyFact>

A change in how often the illicit class occurs and a change in which
transactions are illicit are different changes with different remedies.
<Ref lesson="concept-drift">The drift chapter</Ref> separates them and
measures them without waiting for labels.

## What the benchmark cannot tell a bank

What can a bank take from these numbers for its own detector? Elliptic
differs from a bank's setting in four ways, and each limits what its
numbers say there.

### Labels, unknowns and snapshots

The labels are <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="where-the-labels-came-from">a company's attributions, and 77% of the
nodes carry none</Ref>. What matters here is that the labelled part is
not a sample of the graph: a model scored only on the attributed nodes
is scored on the transactions someone could attribute, which need not
resemble the illicit ones nobody has. And each step is an isolated
three-hour snapshot, while a bank's graph accumulates, so a neighbourhood
there includes last month, <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="choosing-the-decay-from-a-horizon">weighted by age</Ref>.

### The module's tools on this graph

The split by time also rules out <Ref lesson="guilt-by-association"
id="restarting-at-the-known-fraud-the-exposure-score">the exposure
score</Ref> and every column that counts labelled neighbours. The
exposure score restarts its walk at known illicit nodes, and a
labelled-neighbour count needs known labels next door; a test step
shares no edge with training and has no label when it is scored, so
neither has a node to start from. On Elliptic as split, only the label-free graph columns
apply: the aggregates, <Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="reading-the-table">the structural columns</Ref> such as degree,
triangles and betweenness, and a graph network's hidden vectors. A bank's graph has no
such wall: yesterday's confirmed frauds sit in today's graph, and the
exposure score works there.

<Conclusion>
You can now take the three facts of the Elliptic benchmark apart. The
forest beats the GCN because the 72 aggregate columns are already one
round of message passing with hand-chosen aggregators, and a forest's
thresholds use them with a precision no network in the table reaches,
with a learned graph layer or without one: 33 false alerts against 156
for the Skip-GCN and 295 for the feed-forward network, at similar
numbers of illicit transactions found. The overall F1 sits between 0.92 and 0.99 because
it is accuracy on a test set whose licit nodes alone score 0.935 (its
printed top, 0.986, recounts to 0.955); it
did not move when the detector failed. Every model falls at step 43
because the illicit count fell from 239 to 24 a step when a dark market
closed, and the transactions that remained lie among the licit ones;
the pooled F1 hides that, the ranking collapses with it, and retraining
on every label does not repair it.

<Ref lesson="graph-propagation-lab">The lab</Ref> builds the module's
tools in code (the sparse graph, components, the restart walk, egonet
columns and a two-layer GCN) and scores them on an Elliptic subset,
split by time as here. The collapse at step 43 stays
open: it is a change in the stream itself, and the next module asks
what can change in a transaction stream, how to tell a change in how
often fraud occurs from a change in what fraud looks like, and how to
see either before the labels arrive.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Logistic regression on the local columns has a printed illicit
precision of 0.348, recall of 0.668 and micro-averaged F1 of 0.920, on
the 1,083 illicit and 15,587 licit test nodes. Check the row. What do you
find, and which conclusion of the chapter survives it?

<Solution>
Found $0.668 \times 1{,}083 = 723$, missed 360. Flagged
$723 / 0.348 = 2{,}078$, so 1,355 licit nodes flagged. Wrong
predictions $1{,}355 + 360 = 1{,}715$, accuracy
$1 - 1{,}715 / 16{,}670 = 0.897$, not the printed 0.920. The printed
precision, recall and micro-F1 of this row cannot all describe one
prediction on this test set; the printed value may come from a
different run or a different set of nodes. Either number lies below
the 0.935 of calling everything licit, so the conclusion that this
model makes more mistakes than doing nothing survives both. The
transferable move: recompute a table's counts before reading it, and
keep only the conclusions that every consistent version of it supports.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Scores per step meet small numbers after step 43. (a) Step 46 has 2
illicit and 710 licit labelled nodes; the rerun's forest flags 5, one
of them illicit. Compute its illicit precision, recall and F1, and its
micro-averaged F1. (b) What is the illicit F1 of any predictor on a
step with no illicit node, if it flags nothing? If it flags one node?
(c) The rerun's per-step illicit F1 values average to 0.51 over the
fifteen steps, while the pooled F1 is 0.82. Which steps does each
number weight, and which would you report?

<Solution>
**(a)** Precision $1/5 = 0.2$, recall $1/2 = 0.5$, F1
$= 2 \cdot 0.2 \cdot 0.5 / 0.7 = 0.286$. Wrong predictions: 4 licit
flagged and 1 illicit missed, so micro-F1 $= 1 - 5/712 = 0.993$. One
node more or less found moves the F1 between 0 and 0.5: on this step
the illicit F1 is mostly noise.

**(b)** With no illicit node, recall is $0/0$; with nothing flagged,
precision is $0/0$ too, and F1 is undefined. Libraries return 0 by
convention, which reads as a total failure on a step where there was
nothing to find. If the predictor flags one node, precision is $0/1 = 0$
and F1 is 0 (or undefined, depending on how $0/0$ recall is treated).
Steps with no illicit node have to be dropped from a per-step average,
for the reason <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="normalising-by-the-days-ceiling">the normalised card precision</Ref>
is undefined on a day with no compromised card: there is nothing to
find, so no score describes finding it.

**(c)** The pooled F1 counts illicit nodes, so it weights steps by
their illicit counts: 914 of 1,083 come before step 43, and it reads
0.82. The average over steps weights each step equally, so the seven
steps after the break, with 169 illicit nodes between them, count as
much as the eight before, and it reads 0.51. Neither alone describes
the detector; report both periods separately (0.91 before, 0.03 after)
with the counts, which is what the per-step figure does.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "The random forest beats every graph network on Elliptic, so
the graph carries no useful signal there." Name the step of the
argument that fails.

<Solution>
**The step that fails** is equating "a graph network" with "the
graph". The forest on all 166 columns reads 72 columns built from the
graph, one round of aggregation over each node's neighbours.

Evidence that the graph carries signal: adding those 72 columns raised
illicit F1 for logistic regression (0.457 to 0.481), the feed-forward
network (0.649 to 0.653) and the forest (0.694 to 0.788), and in the
rerun the forest's gain (0.043 to 0.053, paired by seed) is at least
twice its seed spread. The
GCN's hidden vectors, computed from the graph, raised logistic
regression on the local columns from 0.457 to 0.543. What the
comparison shows is narrower: on this data, a hand-built graph layer
read by a forest beat a learned graph layer read by a logistic output.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design and run the check that the aggregates help the forest. Use the
public files: labelled nodes, steps 1–34 for training and 35–49 for
testing, a forest of 50 trees with 50 columns tried at each split, cut
at 0.5. (a) What do you train, how many times, and what do you report?
(b) What result counts as reproducing "aggregates help the forest", and
what does not have to match?

<Solution>
**(a)** Two feature sets, the 94 local columns and all 166, each
trained with at least three random seeds; for each run report illicit
precision, recall and F1 on the pooled test set, and the F1 on steps
35–42 and 43–49 separately. Report the spread across seeds.

**(b)** Reproduction means the same order with a gap larger than the
seed spread. A rerun gives 0.822, 0.811 and 0.809 on all 166 columns
and 0.779, 0.758 and 0.766 on the local ones: paired by seed, gaps of
0.043, 0.053 and 0.043 against a seed-to-seed spread of 0.013 and 0.021,
so the direction reproduces. The published gap was 0.094 (0.694 to
0.788), and the published forest on local columns is below every rerun;
the size of the gap does not reproduce, and need not, since it was one
run with no spread. Before 43 and after it, the gap should
be checked separately: an improvement confined to steps 35–42 says
nothing about the period the detector fails in.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A decision tree is trained on steps 1 to 34 with the step among its
columns. (a) Show that it scores a test node from step 49 exactly as it
would score the same node at step 34. (b) Why then is the step column
still a risk for the tree, though a smaller one than for the logistic
regression? (c) Design the check that decides whether to keep the
column.

<Solution>
**(a)** A tree chooses each threshold on a column between two values
seen in training, conventionally their midpoint; the largest step value
in training is 34, so every threshold on the step is at most 33.5. A
node at step 49 and the same node at step 34 both satisfy
"step $> t$" for every such $t$, so they follow the same path to the
same leaf and get the same score. The tree treats every test step as
the last training step.

**(b)** It can still learn a rule specific to the last training steps
("step above 31.5 and fee above some value: illicit") from patterns
that held only then, and apply it to all fifteen test steps. Step 34
holds 7% illicit among labelled nodes; if the late training steps had
an unusual illicit share or an unusual kind of illicit transaction, the
tree projects it forward unchanged. The logistic regression does worse:
it extends a slope, so the error grows with the distance from step 34.

**(c)** Train with and without the column, several seeds each, and
compare F1 on the test steps per period. In the rerun the forest moves
by less than its seed spread; the logistic regression gains 0.18 when
the column is dropped. Keep a time column only if the gain survives the
seeds and has an explanation that holds beyond the training period.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Measure the change at step 43 without a model of illicitness. Take the
illicit nodes of steps 40–42 and of steps 43–45, label them "before"
and "after", and train a classifier to tell the two groups apart on the
93 local columns other than the step, scoring it by AUC under 5-fold
cross-validation. (a) A rerun gives an AUC of 0.986. Does that show
that the illicit transactions changed at step 43? (b) The same check
gives 0.967 on the licit nodes of the same steps, and 0.979 on the
illicit nodes of steps 37–39 against 40–42. What do the three numbers
together say? (c) Which measurement in the chapter does separate the
illicit transactions after step 43 from those before, and what does it
measure that the AUC does not?

<Solution>
**(a)** Not by itself. A high AUC says the two groups are separable,
but any two periods may be separable for reasons that have nothing to
do with illicitness.

**(b)** They are: licit transactions of the same steps separate almost
as well (0.967), and so do illicit transactions of two earlier periods
with no event between them (0.979). Several local columns change with
time for everyone; one of them correlates 0.58 with the step. Telling
periods apart measures a change in the inputs, which happens all the
time on this ledger, and an AUC near 1 is the normal state, not a
signal. A distance between periods needs a baseline from periods known
to be ordinary.

**(c)** The nearest-neighbour count: 78% illicit among the five nearest
training transactions before the closure, 8% after, the same as for
licit transactions. It asks where the new illicit transactions sit
relative to the training labels, which is the change that moves a
classifier's decision; the AUC asks only whether the inputs moved.
Telling those two kinds of change apart, a change in the inputs and a
change in which inputs are illicit, is where <Ref lesson="concept-drift">the
drift chapter</Ref> begins.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A ring-based alert would group connected illicit transactions. (a) Run
<Ref lesson="communities-and-components"
id="connected-components-groups-defined-by-a-path">union-find</Ref> on
all of step 43's edges, ignoring direction. What does it return, and why
was that certain before running it? (b) Restrict the graph to step 43's
24 illicit nodes and the edges among them: a rerun finds 15 components,
the largest of 4 nodes. On step 42 the 239 illicit nodes form 193
components, the largest of 12. About a quarter of the illicit test
nodes have an illicit neighbour, before the break and after it. What
would a component-based alert have found at step 43, and what can it
not do on this split?

<Solution>
**(a)** One component of all 5,063 nodes. Each Elliptic step was built
as a single connected component of transactions, so union-find on its
raw edges must return the whole step: on a raw graph, components are
useless, as the communities chapter found for a café's IP address
linking everyone.

**(b)** On the illicit-only graph the components are small: of
step 43's 24 illicit nodes, at most 4 are connected, and 15 groups
cover them, so most stand alone. A component-based alert would have
grouped a handful of pairs and quadruples, each needing at least one
known illicit member to be flagged. On this split no test node has a
known label when its step is scored, so there is no member to start
from: the alert would have to start from the model's own flags, and the
model flagged no illicit node at step 43. Components describe
organisation once some members are known; they do not find a new kind
of illicit transaction that the classifier misses. A quarter of illicit
nodes with an illicit neighbour is evidence of clustering, but too
little for neighbour labels to carry the detection alone.
</Solution>

A missed fraud costs the bank the amount that was stolen, which runs from a few euros to thousands, while every alert costs the same few euros of an investigator's time whether it was fraud or not. A threshold of 0.5 on the fraud probability treats a EUR 10 payment and a EUR 10,000 payment alike. What decision rule accounts for the amount, and how do you measure a detector in money against the bank that does nothing?

Every comparison in <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">the
rebalancing lesson</Ref> counted frauds, each worth one. The
bank's books count euros. A rule for turning a probability into a
decision in euros already exists: <Ref lesson="undersampling-calibration"
id="a-threshold-priced-in-euros">undersampling and calibration</Ref>
compared the alert cost $C_a$ with the expected loss $p\,c_{FN}$ and
flagged a payment when $p \ge C_a/c_{FN}$. That rule needs one number for
the price of a miss. <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="what-a-miss-costs-the-amount-or-more">The economics of detection</Ref>
left that price as the bank's modelling decision, with the payment's
amount as the common convention. The amount is not one number: it changes
with every payment.

## One price for every miss buys the wrong alerts

The quickest way to keep the threshold rule is to pick one price. A
convention used in published evaluations of card detectors charges a miss
a hundred alerts: with a call at $C_a = €5$, every missed fraud costs
$c = €500$, and the rule flags every payment with $p \ge 5/500 = 0.01$.
Throughout this lesson $p$ is the calibrated probability, the $p'$ of the
calibration lesson.

### Two payments the constant price gets wrong

Take two payments.

- **A**: €10, $p = 0.05$. The rule flags it, since $0.05 \ge 0.01$. Left
  alone, it is expected to lose $0.05 \times €10 = €0.50$. The call costs
  €5: the bank pays ten times what the payment puts at risk.
- **B**: €3,000, $p = 0.008$. The rule leaves it, since $0.008 < 0.01$.
  Left alone, it is expected to lose $0.008 \times €3{,}000 = €24$, almost
  five calls' worth.

The constant prices A's miss at €500, fifty times the most A can lose, and
B's at €500, a sixth of what B can lose. Moving the constant only trades
one error for the other.

<Task>
Find the range of constant prices $c$ for which the rule leaves A alone,
and the range for which it flags B. The rule leaves A when
$5/c > 0.05$, that is $c < €100$; it flags B when $5/c \le 0.008$, that is
$c \ge €625$. No single price does both.
</Task>

### The miss cost is the payment's own amount

<Definition title="example-dependent cost matrix">
For payment $i$ with amount $\mathrm{Amt}_i$, the fraud cost matrix is
$$
c_{TP,i} = c_{FP,i} = C_a, \qquad c_{FN,i} = \mathrm{Amt}_i, \qquad c_{TN,i} = 0 .
$$
It is *example-dependent*: the four costs belong to the payment, not to
the problem, and can differ from row to row.
</Definition>

In a table of payments the costs become columns, and the miss column is a
copy of the amount.

<Figure
  src="./figures/cost-columns.svg"
  alt="A table of five payments with columns payment, amount, features, fraud label, c_TP, c_FP, c_FN and c_TN. The c_TP and c_FP columns hold C_a in every row, c_TN holds 0, and the c_FN column, shaded amber, repeats each row's amount: 12, 640, 3, 2,150 and 48 euros, joined to the amount column by an amber bracket"
  caption="The costs are columns of the data. Only the miss column changes from row to row, and it copies the amount."
/>

Each cell has a reason. A missed fraud costs its amount because the bank
reimburses the cardholder. A false alert costs a call. A caught fraud also
costs a call, $c_{TP,i} = C_a$, and nothing else: the matrix assumes that
catching the fraud saves its whole amount. A frequent bug is
$c_{TP,i} = 0$, copied from matrices in which correct decisions are
free; it prices the call on every caught fraud at nothing, which
flatters a detector's cost and shifts its decision rule.

The amount is the reimbursed payment, not the whole loss: a stolen card
is often tested with a small payment before the large ones, and a missed
probe costs the spending that follows (<Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="what-a-miss-costs-the-amount-or-more">the economics of
detection</Ref>). The framework does not care which per-payment number
goes in the miss column. A bank that prefers the card's remaining credit,
the most a missed fraud could lead to, writes that number there instead,
and a €1 probe on a card with €5,000 of credit then weighs like a large
fraud. Everything below works for any such column; the examples use the
amount.

## What a day of decisions costs, against the bank that does nothing

To say that a rule buys the wrong alerts, its decisions have to be priced.
Write $y_i = 1$ if payment $i$ is fraud and $d_i = 1$ if the rule flags
it.

### Summing the four cells, row by row

A row contributes one of four costs: $C_a$ if it is flagged, fraud or
not; $\mathrm{Amt}_i$ if it is a fraud left alone; nothing if it is a
genuine payment left alone. Summed over the $N$ payments of a day,
$$
\mathrm{Cost}(d) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Big[\, y_i\,(1 - d_i)\,\mathrm{Amt}_i \;+\; d_i\,C_a \Big].
$$
This is the weighted loss of <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="accuracy-prices-every-mistake-the-same">the economics of
detection</Ref> with the miss cost read off each row.

### The baseline: the cheaper of two rules that need no model

A cost of a few thousand euros a day says nothing alone. Two rules need no model at
all. Flagging nothing costs the day's fraud amount,
$\sum_i y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i$. Flagging everything costs $N C_a$. A
detector is worth running only if its cost is below both, so the
baseline is
$$
\mathrm{Cost}_{\text{base}} = \min\Big(\sum_{i} y_i\,\mathrm{Amt}_i,\; N\,C_a\Big).
$$

On a card stream the minimum is flagging nothing. Flagging everything is
cheaper only when $C_a$ is below the fraud loss per payment, the base rate
times the average fraud amount. At one fraud in 400 payments and frauds
averaging €100 (round numbers for illustration), that is €0.25 a payment,
and no call is that cheap. So the baseline is the do-nothing bank of the
economics lesson.

<Definition title="savings">
The *savings* of a set of decisions $d$ is
$$
S(d) = \frac{\mathrm{Cost}_{\text{base}} - \mathrm{Cost}(d)}{\mathrm{Cost}_{\text{base}}} .
$$
With the do-nothing baseline it is the economics lesson's value of the
detector, now as a share of the day's fraud amount: the share of the
fraud loss removed, net of the calls spent removing it.
</Definition>

The share makes days and test sets with different fraud totals
comparable. It has a ceiling below 1: a perfect detector flags exactly
the $n_f$ frauds and still pays for their calls, so
$S \le 1 - n_f C_a / \sum_i y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i$. It has no floor: a
detector whose calls cost more than the fraud they stop has negative
savings, as the economics lesson found for its value.

probability predicts.

## The decision that minimises expected cost

The example priced the rules after the labels were known. At decision
time the bank has only each payment's probability $p_i$, and it wants the
decisions with the lowest expected cost. Each row's cost depends only on
its own decision, so the total is minimised row by row.

### Two expected costs, four cells

The calibration lesson compared $C_a$ with $p\,c_{FN}$. That comparison
assumed two cells without saying so; written with all four, the expected
cost of each action on a payment with probability $p$ is
$$
\mathbb{E}[\text{cost} \mid \text{flag}] = p\,c_{TP} + (1-p)\,c_{FP},
\qquad
\mathbb{E}[\text{cost} \mid \text{leave}] = p\,c_{FN} + (1-p)\,c_{TN}.
$$
Choosing the cheaper action under the probability is the *Bayes
minimum-risk* decision. Substitute the example-dependent matrix:
$$
p_i\,C_a + (1-p_i)\,C_a \;\le\; p_i\,\mathrm{Amt}_i + 0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
C_a \;\le\; \mathrm{Amt}_i \cdot p_i .
$$
The left side lost its $p$: the call is charged on a caught fraud too,
so it costs $C_a$ whatever the payment turns out to be. The right side
is the payment's expected fraud amount.

<Theorem title="the per-payment Bayes minimum-risk rule">
Let $p_i = P(\text{fraud} \mid x_i)$ be calibrated probabilities and let
the costs be example-dependent with $c_{TP,i} = c_{FP,i} = C_a$,
$c_{FN,i} = \mathrm{Amt}_i$, $c_{TN,i} = 0$. Among all decision vectors
$d \in \{0,1\}^N$, the expected cost $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Cost}(d)]$ is
minimised by
$$
d_i = 1 \iff C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}_i \cdot p_i ,
$$
that is, by flagging payment $i$ exactly when $p_i \ge p_i^* = C_a/\mathrm{Amt}_i$.

<Proof>
By linearity of expectation, with $\mathbb{E}[y_i] = p_i$,
$$
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Cost}(d)] = \sum_{i=1}^N \Big[\, p_i(1-d_i)\,\mathrm{Amt}_i + d_i\,C_a \Big]
= \sum_{i=1}^N p_i\,\mathrm{Amt}_i \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^N d_i\,\big(C_a - p_i\,\mathrm{Amt}_i\big).
$$
The first sum does not depend on $d$. The second is a sum of terms each
involving one $d_i$ only, so it is minimised by minimising each term:
set $d_i = 1$ when $C_a - p_i\mathrm{Amt}_i \le 0$ and $d_i = 0$
otherwise. (At equality both choices cost the same; the rule flags.)
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof needs the $p_i$ to be the real probabilities: it replaces each
$y_i$ by its expectation. A score that is only an order does not qualify.

### Reading the threshold

The threshold $p^* = C_a/\mathrm{Amt}$ falls as the amount rises. With
$C_a = €5$: a €10 payment needs $p \ge 0.5$; a €10,000 payment needs only
$p \ge 0.0005$.

  caption="The per-payment threshold is a line across the plane; a fixed threshold is a horizontal line that agrees with it at one amount only."

For a €10,000 payment, 0.5 is a thousand times too strict. At the other
edge, a payment worth less than the call has $p^* > 1$ and is
never flagged: even a certain fraud of €4 costs less to reimburse than to
catch.

With one price $c$ for every miss, the same comparison gives the
horizontal line $p \ge C_a/c$ of the calibration lesson.

<KeyFact>
Every fixed threshold $\tau$ on the probability is the cost-minimising
rule for one amount only, $C_a/\tau$: 0.5 assumes every payment is worth
€10 at a €5 call, and $\tau = 0.01$ assumes €500.
</KeyFact>

## The rule reads probabilities as numbers

The per-payment rule multiplies a probability by euros, so it reads the
probability's value, not its rank. <Ref lesson="undersampling-calibration"
id="an-amount-weighted-queue-reverses">Undersampling and calibration</Ref>
showed what that means for a model fitted on undersampled data and never
corrected: its outputs keep the right order, but their products with
amounts come out wrong, enough to turn a queue upside down. A queue only
orders the calls. The per-payment rule decides how many to make, so the
same error now sets the size of the bill.

### The same day, scored by an undersampled model

Suppose the model was trained after keeping every fraud and one genuine
payment in fifty, $\beta = 0.02$, so that it reports
$p_s = p/(p + \beta(1-p))$ instead of $p$. On the day:

| group | amount | $p$ | $p_s$ | $\mathrm{Amt} \times p_s$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| G1 | €15 | 0.001 | 0.048 | €0.72 |
| G2 | €80 | 0.002 | 0.091 | €7.29 |
| G3 | €300 | 0.005 | 0.20 | €60 |
| G4 | €20 | 0.05 | 0.72 | €14.50 |
| G5 | €1,200 | 0.03 | 0.61 | €729 |
| G6 | €6 | 0.6 | 0.99 | €5.92 |
| G7 | €400 | 0.4 | 0.97 | €388 |
| G8 | €250 | 0.8 | 0.995 | €249 |

The order of the groups is the same under $p$ and $p_s$, so the ranking
metrics score the two models alike. The rule does not: on $p_s$ every
group except G1 clears €5. It makes 5,900 calls,
€29,500, misses G1's €60, and costs €29,560, almost twice the fraud it was
meant to stop. Savings: $-96\%$.

The repair is the correction from that lesson: map $p_s$ back to
$p' = \beta p_s/(\beta p_s - p_s + 1)$ before applying the rule, with β
read off the code that built the training set. The undersampling cut is
one source of miscalibration among several; a tree's leaf frequencies or
a boosted ensemble's scores need the same check, a reliability diagram on
an unresampled week, and a calibrator fitted there if they fail it, with
the methods the classical-ml course gave for it.

<KeyFact>
A fixed threshold survives an uncorrected model at the price of retuning
one number: the correction is increasing, so every cut on $p_s$ is some
cut on $p'$. The per-payment rule has no such number to retune, since each
amount would need its own matched cut, and computing those is applying
the correction. On the day, skipping it takes savings from 64% to −96%.
</KeyFact>

## Costs in the fit, or only in the decision

The rule fixed the decision, but the model behind it was still fitted by
log-loss, which gives every fraud the same weight. A €6 fraud of G6 and a
€1,200 fraud of G5 pull on the fit equally, although missing the second
costs two hundred times more; where the features cannot separate both
groups well, the fit favours G6, which holds twelve times as many frauds.
Can the training see the amounts?

### The cost is a weighted error

Split the day's cost by class. A fraud costs $C_a$ if flagged and
$\mathrm{Amt}_i = C_a + (\mathrm{Amt}_i - C_a)$ if missed; a genuine
payment costs $C_a$ if flagged. So
$$
\mathrm{Cost}(d) = \underbrace{\sum_{y_i = 1} C_a}_{\text{fixed}}
\;+\; \sum_{y_i = 1} (1 - d_i)\,\big(\mathrm{Amt}_i - C_a\big)
\;+\; \sum_{y_i = 0} d_i\,C_a .
$$
The first sum does not depend on the decisions. What is left is a count
of errors with a weight on each: a missed fraud weighs
$\mathrm{Amt}_i - C_a$, a false alarm weighs $C_a$. Minimising the cost
is minimising this weighted error, so any learner that accepts per-row
sample weights can be fitted to it: weight each fraud by
$\mathrm{Amt}_i - C_a$, €1 for G6's €6 frauds and €1,195 for G5's €1,200
ones, and each genuine payment by $C_a$. A fraud smaller than the call
gets a negative weight: missing it is cheaper than catching it.

### Why the weighted model's own 0.5 cut is the cost decision

**What the weighted fit estimates.** A row of weight $w$ counts in the
loss as $w$ copies of itself, as <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="why-every-remedy-moves-the-boundary-the-same-way">the imbalance
lesson</Ref> found for weights and copies. The amount is among the
features, so payments with the same features $x$ share an amount; among
them a share $p$ are frauds, each now counted $\mathrm{Amt} - C_a$ times,
and $1-p$ genuine, each counted $C_a$ times. A model fitted by log-loss
estimates the fraud share of the data it is fitted to, here the weighted
share $q$, whose odds are
$$
\frac{q}{1-q} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{Amt} - C_a}{C_a} .
$$

**What a cut at its own default means.** $q \ge 0.5$ is odds $\ge 1$, that is
$p\,(\mathrm{Amt} - C_a) \ge (1-p)\,C_a$, which simplifies to
$p\,\mathrm{Amt} \ge C_a$: the per-payment rule. The weights move the
amount from the decision into the model, as far as the fitted model
recovers those weighted odds.

### Two learners built around the cost

Weighting leaves the learner as it was; the alternative rewrites what
the learner minimises.

- **Cost-sensitive logistic regression** replaces log-loss by the
  expected cost, reading the model's output $h_\theta(x_i)$ as a soft
  decision:
  $J(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \big[\, y_i\big(h_\theta(x_i)\,C_a + (1 - h_\theta(x_i))\,\mathrm{Amt}_i\big) + (1 - y_i)\,h_\theta(x_i)\,C_a \big]$.
  This is a sum of sigmoids in θ and is not convex, so it is minimised
  by a general-purpose search rather than the usual solver.
- **A cost-sensitive tree** labels a leaf "flag" when its calls cost
  less than its frauds, $C_a \times (\text{rows in the leaf}) \le
  \sum (\text{fraud amounts in the leaf})$, and chooses each split by how
  much it lowers that cost. A leaf of 40 payments holding frauds of €900
  and €30 is flagged ($€200 \le €930$); an impurity-trained tree sees 2
  frauds in 40 and calls it genuine.

### Why resampling cannot carry the amount

<Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="why-every-remedy-moves-the-boundary-the-same-way">The imbalance
lesson</Ref> showed that class weights, copies and deletions are one move:
each sets one ratio between the weight of a fraud and the weight of a
genuine row. For costs set per class, resampling and cost-sensitive
learning are therefore the same thing, and from there one would expect a
rebalanced training set to serve for amounts too.

It does not, and the trouble is all on the fraud side. Under this
lesson's matrix every genuine payment weighs the same $C_a$, so deleting
genuine rows at random is harmless to the cost: it is the class ratio
once more. The frauds are where the weights differ, by a factor of
hundreds, €1 for a €6 fraud against €1,195 for a €1,200 one. A class
factor, whether applied as a weight, as copies of frauds or as deletions
of genuine rows, gives both frauds the same weight, and it matches the
cost only on a stream where every fraud had the same amount. Synthetic
frauds do worse: each sits between two real ones with an interpolated
amount nobody lost, and the extra weight they add follows where frauds
are dense in feature space, not what they cost. Genuine rows would need
weights of their own only if a false alarm cost more on some payments
than on others, as when an automatic block risks the customer (<Ref
lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="what-an-alert-costs-the-call-and-sometimes-the-customer">the
economics of detection</Ref>). Sampling can carry the amount only if each
fraud is copied or kept in proportion to its own weight, which is
weighting by another route.

<KeyFact>
Decide with costs always; fit with costs where the learner accepts them;
never use class rebalancing to express them. Put the amounts in the
weights or in the threshold, not in both: a cost-weighted model's score
is no longer a probability, and the per-payment rule on top of it counts
the amount twice.
</KeyFact>

### What a published comparison on processor data shows

One comparison measures all of these routes on real card data. A large
European card processor's 120 million transactions from January 2012 to
June 2013 held 40,000 labelled frauds, 0.025%. The study worked on a
subset of 236,735 transactions selected where fraud concentrates, at
1.50% fraud and €895,154 of fraud losses, split 50/25/25 into training,
validation and test sets; the test quarter holds 71,226 transactions,
1.45% fraud and €262,167 of fraud, the baseline its savings are measured
against. Eight detectors were fitted on the same four feature sets, each
named by the kinds of feature it combines: raw transaction fields (raw),
aggregates of the card's recent payments (agg1, and agg2 for aggregates
restricted to the same merchant type or country) and periodic,
time-of-day features (per). The detectors were a decision tree, logistic
regression and a random forest, each also with the
per-payment rule applied to calibrated probabilities, and the
cost-sensitive logistic regression and tree.

Against the test baseline, logistic regression saves about €31,000 cut at
its default and about €118,000 with the rule: the same fitted model,
nearly four times the money, from the decision alone. Fitting with costs
adds a few more points at the top, about €186,000 for the cost-sensitive
tree against €168,000 for the random forest with the rule. In earlier
studies on the same data, neither undersampling nor oversampling
improved savings.

Two departures from what this course demands keep these numbers
indicative. The partition is a single split with no delay block of the
kind <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">the delayed-labels
lesson</Ref> requires, so the levels carry the optimism of a test that
does not reproduce deployment; and the subset was chosen for its fraud
density, which moves the baseline. The
ordering, decision first and fit second, is the part that transfers.

## Savings beside average precision and card precision

<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="three-numbers-for-three-questions">The
alert-budget metrics</Ref> ended with three numbers: ROC-AUC, average
precision and card precision at $k$. Savings answers a question none of
them asks. The three depend only on the order of the payments; savings
depends on the amounts, on the decision rule, on the calibration and on
the chosen $C_a$. A model can rank more frauds near the top and still
save less, if the frauds it promotes are small. It also changes when the bank revises its call cost, which no
ranking metric does.

### Why savings does not choose the model

Savings is the number the bank cares about, so the obvious move is to
select models by it on the prequential folds. Two properties of savings
argue against that.

**It rests on a few frauds.** On the day, G5's three €1,200 frauds are
worth 24 points of savings, and G6's 36 frauds of €6 are worth 1.4. A
model that catches or misses three payments moves by a quarter of the
scale, and a fold that happens to hold one more large fraud, or one fewer,
moves every model's savings with it. Compared across folds, savings is
dominated by which large frauds each fold drew, and the difference
between two models drowns in that variation sooner than a count of
caught frauds does.

**It is not the model's alone.** Savings changes with the $C_a$ the bank
set, with whether β was undone, and with the decision rule: on the day
the same probabilities give 64.4% at a €5 call and 34.5% at €40. Two
models compared by savings are compared under one setting of all three,
and a different setting can reorder them.

<KeyFact>
Choose models by card precision on prequential folds: it measures the
ranking alone, which is what training controls, and each caught card
counts once, so a single large fraud cannot swing it. The choice is blind
to amounts; they enter through the decision rule and, where the learner
allows, the weights. Report savings under the per-payment rule, on
corrected probabilities and at the bank's current $C_a$, beside it for
the chosen model.
</KeyFact>

<Conclusion>
You can now price a day of decisions payment by payment, with a call cost
on every alert and the amount on every missed fraud; measure a detector
by its savings against the cheaper of flagging nothing and flagging
everything, which on card streams is doing nothing; derive from the two
expected costs the rule that flags a payment exactly when its expected
fraud amount covers the call, $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}\cdot p$; and explain
why that rule needs corrected probabilities, why costs belong in the
decision always and in the fit where the learner allows, and why class
rebalancing cannot carry them.

The rule says which payments are worth a call, not how many calls the
team can make: on a busy day it may flag more than the budget, on a quiet
one fewer. Combining it with a fixed budget is the problem of <Ref
lesson="score-to-action">score-to-action policies</Ref>. The next
lesson, <Ref lesson="ulb-handbook-benchmark">the benchmark of five
baseline models</Ref>, puts five standard models through the module's metrics
on simulated and real card data.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
With $C_a = €5$, compute the per-payment threshold $p^*$ for payments of
€2, €50, €500 and €5,000. Which of them can never be flagged, and what is
the smallest probability at which each of the others is?

<Solution>
$p^* = C_a/\mathrm{Amt}$:

- €2: $5/2 = 2.5$. Above 1, so never flagged: a certain fraud of €2
  costs less to reimburse than the €5 call.
- €50: $5/50 = 0.1$.
- €500: $5/500 = 0.01$.
- €5,000: $5/5{,}000 = 0.001$.

Each tenfold rise in the amount lowers the threshold tenfold, and every
payment of €5 or less is out of reach of the rule whatever its
probability.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push the costs to their edges. (a) On a stream where every payment is
smaller than $C_a$, what does the per-payment rule do, what is the best
savings any detector can reach, and what weight does the weighted-error
form give a fraud? (b) Now let $C_a \to 0$. What does the rule flag, and
what happens to the savings? (c) For each case, name the part of the
savings definition that decides the outcome.

<Solution>
**(a)** Every $p^* = C_a/\mathrm{Amt}_i > 1$, so the rule flags nothing
and its cost is the fraud amount. The baseline is
$\min(\sum y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i, N C_a)$; since every fraud is worth less
than $C_a$, $\sum y_i\mathrm{Amt}_i < n_f C_a \le N C_a$, so the baseline
is doing nothing and the rule's savings are exactly 0. Any detector that
flags anything pays $C_a$ per flag and recovers at most
$\mathrm{Amt}_i < C_a$, so its savings are negative: 0 is the best
possible. In the weighted-error form a missed fraud weighs
$\mathrm{Amt}_i - C_a < 0$, so missing it lowers the cost. Such a row
should be fitted as if it were genuine, with weight
$C_a - \mathrm{Amt}_i$ on flagging it; that is what the cost says,
however odd it looks to train on a fraud labelled genuine.

**(b)** With $C_a = 0$, $p^* = 0$: the rule flags every payment with
$p > 0$. But flagging everything now costs $N \times 0 = 0$, so the
baseline is 0 and the savings are $0/0$, undefined. For small positive
$C_a$ the baseline is $N C_a$, flagging everything, and a detector beats
it only if its calls plus its missed fraud cost less than $N C_a$: one
missed €100 fraud already exceeds $N C_a$ once $C_a < €100/N$.

**(c)** In (a), the ceiling: the calls on caught frauds exceed what
catching recovers, so even a perfect detector lands below zero. In (b),
the minimum in the baseline: when calls are free, flagging everything is
a free perfect detector, and savings measure the distance to it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break the claim: "A model with higher average precision always has higher
savings." Take the ten highest-scored payments of a day of 2,000; the
other 1,990 are genuine and no model flags them. Among the ten are one
fraud of €2,000 and three of €20. The team calls the top 3 of each
model's ranking, at $C_a = €5$. Construct two rankings of the ten with
the same labels, the first with the higher average precision and the
second with the higher savings, and compute both numbers for each.

<Solution>
**Rankings.** Model A puts the three €20 frauds at ranks 1, 2, 3 and the
€2,000 fraud at rank 10. Model B puts the €2,000 fraud at rank 1 and the
€20 frauds at 4, 5, 6.

**Average precision** (mean precision at each fraud, as in the
alert-budget lesson): A: $\frac14(1 + 1 + 1 + \frac{4}{10}) = 0.85$.
B: $\frac14(1 + \frac24 + \frac35 + \frac46) \approx 0.69$.

**Savings.** The fraud amount is €2,060; flagging all 2,000 payments
would cost €10,000, so the baseline is €2,060. A calls its top 3: €15 in
calls, the €2,000 fraud missed, cost €2,015, savings
$45/2{,}060 \approx 2.2\%$. B calls the €2,000 fraud and two genuine
payments: €15 in calls, €60 missed, cost €75, savings
$1{,}985/2{,}060 \approx 96.4\%$.

**The failing step.** Average precision counts each fraud as one and
rewards A for three cheap ones at the top; savings weighs each fraud by
its amount. The claim assumes the two metrics read the same thing, which
holds only when every fraud has the same amount.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the Bayes minimum-risk threshold for an arbitrary cost matrix.
(a) From the two expected costs, show that a payment is flagged exactly
when
$$
p \ge \tau = \frac{c_{FP} - c_{TN}}{(c_{FP} - c_{TN}) + (c_{FN} - c_{TP})},
$$
and state the conditions on the costs that make this the right direction.
(b) Recover the per-payment threshold. (c) Recover the common form
$\tau = 1/(1 + c_{FN}/c_{FP})$ for free correct decisions, and the
threshold $\tau = \pi$ obtained by setting $c_{FP} = \pi$ and
$c_{FN} = 1 - \pi$, with π the base rate. (d) Apply the zero-diagonal
form to the fraud matrix by setting $c_{TP} = 0$ while keeping
$c_{FP} = C_a$ and $c_{FN} = \mathrm{Amt}$: what threshold results for a
€10 and a €4 payment at $C_a = €5$, and what does it do to the day's
savings of 64.4%?

<Solution>
**(a)** Flag when $p\,c_{TP} + (1-p)\,c_{FP} \le p\,c_{FN} + (1-p)\,c_{TN}$.
Collect the $p$ terms: $c_{FP} - c_{TN} \le p\,[(c_{FN} - c_{TP}) + (c_{FP} - c_{TN})]$.
If $c_{FN} > c_{TP}$ (a fraud costs more missed than caught) and
$c_{FP} > c_{TN}$ (a genuine payment costs more flagged than left), the
bracket is positive and dividing gives $p \ge \tau$, with
$0 < \tau < 1$. If either condition fails, one action is never worse
than the other and there is no threshold to set.

**(b)** $c_{FP} = c_{TP} = C_a$, $c_{FN} = \mathrm{Amt}$, $c_{TN} = 0$:
$\tau = C_a / (C_a + \mathrm{Amt} - C_a) = C_a/\mathrm{Amt}$.

**(c)** With $c_{TP} = c_{TN} = 0$: $\tau = c_{FP}/(c_{FP} + c_{FN}) =
1/(1 + c_{FN}/c_{FP})$. With $c_{FP} = \pi$, $c_{FN} = 1 - \pi$:
$\tau = \pi/(\pi + 1 - \pi) = \pi$, the cut at the base rate that, as
the calibration lesson showed, a 0.5 cut on a balanced training set
amounts to.

**(d)** $\tau = C_a/(C_a + \mathrm{Amt})$. For €10: $5/15 = 0.33$
instead of 0.5. For €4: $5/9 = 0.56$, so a €4 payment at $p = 0.9$ is
flagged, a €5 call to recover an expected €3.60. On the day, G6 (€6) gets
$\tau = 5/11 = 0.45 \le 0.6$ and is flagged: 60 calls, €300, to recover
€216, the loss the per-payment rule had avoided. In the evaluation, the
same bug prices the calls on the 23 frauds the rule catches (G5, G7, G8)
at nothing, which raises the reported savings by $23 \times 5/15{,}056
\approx 0.8$ points. The decision error is small for large amounts,
where $C_a + \mathrm{Amt} \approx \mathrm{Amt}$, and large where amounts
are close to the call.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A team fits a boosted tree with sample weights, the payment's amount on
every fraud and $C_a$ on every genuine payment, and then flags a payment
when $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt} \times \text{score}$. Diagnose the pipeline.
Suppose the weighted model recovers the weighted odds of the section on
the weighted model's 0.5 cut exactly, with the correct fraud weight
$\mathrm{Amt} - C_a$. What does the team's rule do to a €1,000 payment
with $p = 0.001$, and what should the team have done?

<Solution>
**The team's rule.** For the €1,000 payment the expected fraud amount is
€1, below the €5 call, so it should be left. The weighted score has odds
$\frac{0.001}{0.999} \times \frac{995}{5} \approx 0.199$, so
$q \approx 0.166$, and $\mathrm{Amt} \times q \approx €166 \ge €5$: it is
flagged. The amount entered twice, once in the weights and once in the
rule, and large payments flood the queue.

**The diagnosis.** Two errors: the score of a cost-weighted model is not
a probability, and the per-payment rule needs one; and the amount is
counted twice. A minor third: the fraud weight should be
$\mathrm{Amt} - C_a$, not $\mathrm{Amt}$. **Fix**: either keep the
weights and cut the score at 0.5, or drop the weights, calibrate the
score and apply the rule.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The test week of <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="which-test-payments-would-really-be-scored-removing-known-cards">the
delayed-labels lesson</Ref> removes, on each test day, the payments of
cards with a fraud labelled by the morning of that day, because the bank
has blocked them. On one test day the raw fraud amount is €15,000, of
which €5,000 sits on such known cards. On the remaining payments a
detector's calls and missed frauds cost €4,000. (a) Compute its savings
correctly. (b) Compute them with the baseline taken over all the day's
frauds, as a pipeline that removes known cards from scoring but not from
the baseline would. (c) Why is the second number wrong, and in which
direction does the error run on a stream where about a third of raw test
fraud sits on known cards?

<Solution>
**(a)** The scored payments carry €10,000 of fraud; flagging them all
would cost far more on any card stream, so the baseline is €10,000.
Savings $(10{,}000 - 4{,}000)/10{,}000 = 60\%$.

**(b)** $(15{,}000 - 4{,}000)/15{,}000 \approx 73.3\%$.

**(c)** The €5,000 on known cards is not lost to the detector's failure
or saved by its success: the bank blocked those cards before the day
began, and the detector never scored their payments. Putting their fraud
in the baseline and not in the detector's cost credits the detector with
stopping it. The error always inflates savings, and with about a third of
raw test fraud on known cards it adds the whole removed share to the
numerator. The baseline must be computed on exactly the payments the
detector is charged for.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Use the published test set: 71,226 transactions, 1.45% fraud, €262,167
of fraud. (a) How many frauds, and what is the average fraud amount? (b)
Below which call cost $C_a$ would flagging every transaction be cheaper
than flagging none, so that the baseline changes? (c) At $C_a = €5$, what
is the highest savings any detector could reach? (d) Convert savings of
71% and 45% into euros saved. (e) On the full stream the fraud rate was
0.025%. What does that do to the answer to (b), and what does it tell you
about reading savings measured on a subset chosen for its fraud density?

<Solution>
**(a)** $0.0145 \times 71{,}226 \approx 1{,}033$ frauds, averaging
$262{,}167/1{,}033 \approx €254$.

**(b)** Flagging everything costs $71{,}226\,C_a$; it beats flagging
nothing when $71{,}226\,C_a < 262{,}167$, that is $C_a < €3.68$.

**(c)** $1 - 1{,}033 \times 5/262{,}167 \approx 1 - 0.020 = 98.0\%$.

**(d)** $0.71 \times 262{,}167 \approx €186{,}000$ saved, leaving a cost
of about €76,000; $0.45 \times 262{,}167 \approx €118{,}000$.

**(e)** At 0.025% fraud and a similar average amount, the fraud loss per
transaction is about $0.00025 \times €254 \approx €0.06$, so flagging
everything would beat doing nothing only for a call under six cents:
doing nothing is the baseline for any real call. On the dense subset the
threshold of (b) is €3.68, close enough to plausible call costs that the
choice of $C_a$ decides which trivial rule the detector is compared
against. A subset selected for fraud also holds far fewer genuine
payments per fraud, so a detector likely flags fewer genuine payments per
fraud caught than it would on the stream. Savings from such a subset
rank the detectors but overstate the share they would save on the full
stream.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Return to the day in eight groups, under the per-payment rule with
$C_a = €5$. The team can make only 100 calls. (a) Which 100 payments
should it call, and why that order? (b) What are the expected savings?
(c) Now suppose the team could make 200 calls. Should it fill them, and
with what? (d) What do (a) and (c) say about the relation between the
rule and the budget?

<Solution>
**(a)** The rule flags 140 payments: G8 (10), G7 (30) and G5 (100). Each
call on a payment is expected to save $\mathrm{Amt} \times p - C_a$:
€195 in G8, €155 in G7, €31 in G5. Call in that order: all of G8 and G7,
then 60 of G5's 100.

**(b)** Calls: €500. Missed: the €4,656 the rule already leaves, plus the
40 uncalled G5 payments, expected $40 \times 0.03 \times €1{,}200 =
€1{,}440$. Expected cost €6,596; savings
$(15{,}056 - 6{,}596)/15{,}056 \approx 56.2\%$, against 64.4% unconstrained.

**(c)** No. After the 140 flagged payments, the next best is G6 with an
expected saving of $€3.60 - €5 < 0$ per call; every further call loses
money. The 60 spare calls should go unused, or to another queue.

**(d)** The budget binds when the rule flags more than $k$; then the
effective threshold on the expected amount rises above $C_a$ until only
$k$ payments clear it. When the rule flags fewer than $k$, the rule binds
and the budget is idle. Neither rule nor budget alone decides the day,
which is the problem <Ref lesson="score-to-action">score-to-action
policies</Ref> take up, together with the actions other than a call.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

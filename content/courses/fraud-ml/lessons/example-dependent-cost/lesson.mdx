A missed fraud costs the transaction amount and a flagged transaction costs a fixed administrative sum, so the optimal decision depends on the amount: flag iff $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}\cdot P(\text{fraud}\mid x)$. The rule is a curve in the plane of amount and probability, not a threshold on the score, and it is only as good as the probability it is fed.

<Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">The economics lesson</Ref> priced an alert at $C_a$ and a missed fraud at $c_{FN}$, showed that a queue pays only if its precision clears $C_a/c_{FN}$, and noted that the amount is the one fraud cost a transaction table can supply. <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">The imbalance lesson</Ref> derived the correction $p'$ that turns an undersampled model's scores back into probabilities, and showed that for ranking metrics the correction changes nothing. What stands open is the decision itself once each transaction carries its own stake: which rule is optimal, how a detector is scored against the money it saves, and whether the costs belong in the decision or in the training. This lesson derives the Bayes-minimum-risk rule from the example-dependent cost matrix, defines savings against the do-nothing baseline and shows they can be negative, shows why the rule needs calibrated probabilities where a ranking metric does not, and separates learning with costs from deciding with costs on the published card-fraud results.

## The amount enters the cost matrix

The cost matrix of <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">the economics lesson</Ref> had one number per outcome. The fraud costs that a bank actually pays vary from transaction to transaction, and this section writes a matrix that lets them.

### One price per fraud, or the fraud's own amount

Hand and co-authors, in their [performance criteria for card fraud tools](https://doi.org/10.1057/palgrave.jors.2602418), charge an alert the administrative cost $C_a$ of analysing the transaction and contacting the cardholder, on a true and a false alert alike, and charge a missed fraud a constant one hundred times that: $C_{FN} = 100\,C_a$. Losses on real frauds range from a few euros to thousands. At $C_a = €25$ the constant matrix prices a missed €8 fraud and a missed €2,000 fraud at the same €2,500, overstating the first by a factor of 300 and understating the second.

Correa Bahnsen and co-authors replace the constant by the transaction's own amount. In their notation a transaction $i$ has true label $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ (1 for fraud) and receives the decision $c_i \in \{0, 1\}$ (1 for flag), and each of its four outcomes has its own cost.

<Definition title="example-dependent cost matrix">
An *example-dependent cost matrix* assigns to each transaction $i$ four costs $C_{TP_i}, C_{FP_i}, C_{FN_i}, C_{TN_i}$, the costs of flagging a fraud, flagging a genuine transaction, passing a fraud and passing a genuine transaction. The card-fraud matrix is
$$
C_{TP_i} = C_{FP_i} = C_a, \qquad C_{FN_i} = \mathrm{Amt}_i, \qquad C_{TN_i} = 0,
$$
with $C_a$ a fixed administrative cost per alert and $\mathrm{Amt}_i$ the transaction amount.
</Definition>

<Figure
  src="./figures/matrix.svg"
  alt="Two 2-by-2 cost matrices side by side, rows flag and pass, columns fraud and genuine. Left, Hand et al.: flag cells C_a, pass-fraud cell 100 C_a in blue, pass-genuine 0; a missed 2,000-euro fraud and a missed 8-euro fraud both cost 2,500 euros. Right, example-dependent: the same except the pass-fraud cell is Amt_i in amber; the two missed frauds cost 2,000 and 8 euros. An arrow joins the panels."
  caption="Only the false-negative cell changes; with it, the price of a miss spans three orders of magnitude."
/>

The economics lesson wrote $c_{FN}$ for one number; from here on $C_{FN_i}$ carries the subscript of the transaction it belongs to. Four cases fix what the definition covers:

- **Canonical.** $C_a = €25$, $C_{FN_i} = \mathrm{Amt}_i$: the matrix the rest of this lesson computes with.
- **A different structure.** The cost of a missed fraud can be any per-row quantity. Correa Bahnsen and co-authors propose the available credit on the card, so that a small fraud on a card with a large open limit is priced at what the fraudster can still take. A problem set of the IIT Hyderabad fraud-analytics course gives each row a false-negative cost computed from a risk parameter, with $C_{TP} = C_{FP} = 6$ and $C_{TN} = 0$.
- **Degenerate.** Every amount equal: $\mathrm{Amt}_i = A$ for all $i$. The matrix is the constant matrix of the economics lesson, and Hand's matrix is the case $A = 100\,C_a$.
- **Near miss.** $C_{TP_i} = 0$, $C_{FN_i} = \mathrm{Amt}_i$. It violates the clause that a true alert pays for its investigation. The difference is small for large amounts and large for small ones, and an exercise at the end computes how large.

### The cost of a set of decisions

One transaction first. Transaction 2 of the running example below is a fraud of €2,000. Flagged, it costs the €25 call; passed, it costs €2,000. A genuine €900 transaction costs €25 flagged and nothing passed. Summing the cost of the realised outcome over a set $S$ of $N$ transactions gives the cost of a classifier $f$ that makes the decisions $c_i$:
$$
\mathrm{Cost}(f(S)) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Bigl[ y_i\bigl(c_i C_{TP_i} + (1 - c_i) C_{FN_i}\bigr) + (1 - y_i)\bigl(c_i C_{FP_i} + (1 - c_i) C_{TN_i}\bigr) \Bigr].
$$
Substituting the card matrix, the flag term is $C_a$ whatever the label, and only the passed frauds remain:
$$
\mathrm{Cost}(f(S)) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Bigl[ y_i (1 - c_i)\,\mathrm{Amt}_i + c_i\,C_a \Bigr].
$$
The cost is the calls made plus the frauds passed. It needs labels: it is the evaluation of decisions already taken, after the investigations and disputes have reported.

## The Bayes-minimum-risk rule: compare two expected costs per transaction

The cost above is computed after the labels arrive. At scoring time the label is unknown and only a probability $p_i = P(\text{fraud} \mid x_i)$ is available, so each decision is chosen by comparing two expected costs.

### Flag when the expected loss exceeds the call

Take the €2,000 transaction with $p = 0.05$. Flagging costs €25 for certain. Passing costs €2,000 with probability 0.05 and nothing otherwise, an expected €100. Flagging is cheaper by €75. Take a €15 transaction with $p = 0.6$: flagging costs €25, passing an expected $0.6 \times €15 = €9$. Passing is cheaper by €16, although the second transaction is twelve times as likely to be fraud.

<Theorem title="Bayes-minimum-risk decision">
Let transaction $i$ have fraud probability $p_i$ and costs with $C_{FN_i} > C_{TP_i}$ and $C_{FP_i} > C_{TN_i}$. Flagging has no higher expected cost than passing if and only if
$$
p_i \ge \tau_i = \frac{C_{FP_i} - C_{TN_i}}{(C_{FP_i} - C_{TN_i}) + (C_{FN_i} - C_{TP_i})}.
$$
For the card matrix this is
$$
C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}_i \cdot p_i, \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad p_i \ge \frac{C_a}{\mathrm{Amt}_i}.
$$

<Proof>
The expected cost of flagging is $R(\text{flag}) = p_i C_{TP_i} + (1 - p_i) C_{FP_i}$ and of passing $R(\text{pass}) = p_i C_{FN_i} + (1 - p_i) C_{TN_i}$. Flagging is no worse iff $R(\text{flag}) \le R(\text{pass})$, that is $(1 - p_i)(C_{FP_i} - C_{TN_i}) \le p_i (C_{FN_i} - C_{TP_i})$. Collecting the terms in $p_i$ gives $p_i\bigl[(C_{FP_i} - C_{TN_i}) + (C_{FN_i} - C_{TP_i})\bigr] \ge C_{FP_i} - C_{TN_i}$, and the bracket is positive by hypothesis, which gives $\tau_i$. For the card matrix $R(\text{flag}) = p_i C_a + (1 - p_i) C_a = C_a$ and $R(\text{pass}) = p_i \mathrm{Amt}_i$, so the condition is $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}_i\, p_i$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The card form is the paper's Eq. 15, $C_a P(f \mid x_i) + C_a P(l \mid x_i) \le \mathrm{Amt}_i P(f \mid x_i)$, with $f$ and $l$ for fraud and legitimate. The left side is the expected cost of an alert, which is $C_a$ whatever the label; the right side is the expected loss of letting the transaction through. The hypothesis $C_{FN_i} > C_{TP_i}$ fails for every transaction with $\mathrm{Amt}_i \le C_a$: such a transaction is never worth flagging, because even a certain fraud loses less than the call.

### Optimal transaction by transaction

The rule compares two numbers for one transaction. Its optimality for the whole stream follows because the expected total cost is a sum of per-transaction terms.

<Theorem title="the rule minimises expected total cost">
Among all decision rules $c_i = c(x_i, \mathrm{Amt}_i)$, the Bayes-minimum-risk rule applied with the true $p_i$ minimises the expected cost $\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Cost}(f(S)) \mid x_1, \dots, x_N]$.

<Proof>
Conditional on the features, $\mathbb{E}[y_i] = p_i$, so the expected cost is $\sum_i \bigl[c_i C_a + (1 - c_i)\,p_i \mathrm{Amt}_i\bigr]$. Each term depends on $c_i$ alone, so the sum is minimised by minimising each term, which is choosing $c_i = 1$ exactly when $C_a \le p_i \mathrm{Amt}_i$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Two hypotheses carry the theorem. The decisions are unconstrained: nothing limits how many transactions may be flagged, which the investigator budget of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">the first lesson</Ref> contradicts. And the $p_i$ are the true probabilities: a model's score enters the rule as if it were one.

### Constant costs are the special case

With every amount equal to $A$, the threshold $C_a/A$ is one number for all transactions, and the rule is the threshold of the classical cost-sensitive literature. With correct decisions free, $C_{TP} = C_{TN} = 0$, the general theorem gives
$$
p > \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}} = \frac{1}{1 + C_{FN}/C_{FP}},
$$
the cut-off that Baesens and co-authors derive from the expected misclassification cost and that depends only on the cost ratio. Hand's matrix gives $p \ge C_a/(100\,C_a) = 0.01$ under the card convention and $p > 1/101$ with a free true alert. The threshold $\tau = \pi^+$ of <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">the imbalance lesson</Ref>, where the error costs were set to the class priors, is a third instance.

<KeyFact>
Under example-dependent costs the decision boundary is the hyperbola $p = C_a/\mathrm{Amt}$, so no threshold on the score alone reproduces it: two transactions with the same score receive opposite decisions when their amounts straddle $C_a/p$.
</KeyFact>

## Savings: the cost of a detector against the cost of doing nothing

The example compared each rule with doing nothing by hand. The savings measure makes that comparison the definition, and its baseline has to be stated with care, because "doing nothing" is not always the cheapest trivial policy.

### The baseline is the cheaper of two trivial policies

Two classifiers need no model: $f_0$ passes everything and $f_1$ flags everything. Their costs are $\mathrm{Cost}(f_0(S)) = \sum_i y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i$, every fraud paid, and $\mathrm{Cost}(f_1(S)) = N\,C_a$, every transaction called. Correa Bahnsen and co-authors set the cost of using no algorithm to the smaller:
$$
\mathrm{Cost}_l(S) = \min\{\mathrm{Cost}(f_0(S)),\ \mathrm{Cost}(f_1(S))\}.
$$
Passing everything is the cheaper of the two exactly when $N C_a \ge \sum_i y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i$, that is when the alert cost exceeds the fraud loss per transaction. On the running day that loss is $€2{,}533/1{,}000 = €2.53$. On the subset of a European processor's card data that the paper evaluates on, 236,735 transactions with 1.50% fraud and €895,154 of fraud losses, it is €3.78. Any plausible call cost is above it, so on card data $\mathrm{Cost}_l$ is the total fraud amount.

### From cost to savings

<Definition title="savings">
The *savings* of a classifier $f$ on a set $S$ are
$$
\mathrm{Savings}(f(S)) = \frac{\mathrm{Cost}_l(S) - \mathrm{Cost}(f(S))}{\mathrm{Cost}_l(S)}.
$$
</Definition>

When $\mathrm{Cost}_l(S) = \sum_i y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i$, substituting the card cost gives a closed form. The numerator is $\sum_i y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i - \sum_i \bigl[y_i(1 - c_i)\mathrm{Amt}_i + c_i C_a\bigr] = \sum_i \bigl[y_i c_i \mathrm{Amt}_i - c_i C_a\bigr]$, so
$$
\mathrm{Savings}(f(S)) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl[y_i c_i \mathrm{Amt}_i - c_i C_a\bigr]}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i}:
$$
the amounts of the frauds caught, minus the calls spent catching them and everything else flagged, as a share of all fraud. On the running day the threshold rule catches $15 + 40 + 120 + 8 = €183$ with €125 of calls, savings $58/2{,}533 = 2.3\%$; the minimum-risk rule catches $2{,}000 + 40 + 120 + 350 = €2{,}510$ with €125 of calls, savings $2{,}385/2{,}533 = 94.2\%$.

### The perfect classifier is not the ceiling

From a classification view one would expect the classifier that flags exactly the frauds to have the highest possible savings. It does not. Flagging all six frauds costs $6 \times €25 = €150$ and passes nothing: savings $94.1\%$, a tenth of a point below the minimum-risk rule, which was wrong on three of the ten rows. The best possible decisions flag the frauds worth more than a call, 2, 3, 5 and 7, and pass the €15 and €8 ones: cost $€100 + €23 = €123$, savings $95.1\%$. The ceiling on savings is $1 - \sum_i y_i \min(\mathrm{Amt}_i, C_a)/\sum_i y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i$, and the misclassification view of the problem cannot see it, because it counts a passed €8 fraud as an error.

### Savings can be negative

Savings are at most the ceiling and have no lower bound. Flagging all 1,000 transactions of the day costs €25,000, savings $(2{,}533 - 25{,}000)/2{,}533 = -887\%$. The threshold rule's savings are $(183 - 5 C_a)/2{,}533$, which is negative once $C_a > €36.60$.

The minimum-risk curve also goes below zero. Above $C_a = €100$ the only transaction whose expected loss exceeds the call is the genuine €900 one at $p = 0.3$, and the €2,000 fraud at $p = 0.05$ no longer clears the bar. The rule flags one transaction, it is genuine, and the realised savings are about $-4\%$. The theorem of the previous section is about expected cost, and its expected cost at $C_a = €120$ is still below doing nothing. Its realised cost on one day is a single draw, and a small day can land on the wrong side. Savings measured over the 71,226 transactions of the paper's test set average over many such draws; savings measured on ten do not.

### Whitrow's normalised cost

The total cost has no scale of its own. Whitrow and co-authors normalise it by the cost of misclassifying every example, flagging every genuine transaction and passing every fraud:
$$
\mathrm{Cost}_n(f(S)) = \frac{\sum_i \bigl[y_i(1 - c_i)\mathrm{Amt}_i + c_i C_a\bigr]}{|S_0|\,C_a + \sum_i y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i},
$$
with $S_0$ the genuine transactions. On the running day the denominator is $994 \times €25 + €2{,}533 = €27{,}383$, and the minimum-risk rule scores $148/27{,}383 = 0.005$, the threshold rule $0.090$, doing nothing $0.093$. The normalisation is against a policy nobody runs, so the number for doing nothing has to be computed before any other can be read. Correa Bahnsen and co-authors choose savings because, in their words, "companies do not use predictive models": the operational comparison is against running none, and savings place that comparison at zero by construction.

<KeyFact>
The savings ceiling $1 - \sum_i y_i \min(\mathrm{Amt}_i, C_a)/\sum_i y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i$ moves with the alert cost: on the running day it is 95.1% at $C_a = €25$ and 91.6% at €50. Two savings figures are comparable only at the same $C_a$ on the same set of transactions.
</KeyFact>

## The rule needs probabilities, not scores

The minimum-risk rule multiplies $p_i$ by an amount, which is where it parts company with every metric of <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">the metrics lesson</Ref>. Average precision and card precision at $k$ read only the order of the scores; this rule reads their values.

### A monotone distortion that the ranking metrics forgive

The imbalance lesson showed that undersampling with keep rate $\beta$ replaces the true posterior $p$ by $p_s = p/(p + \beta(1 - p))$, that the map is strictly increasing, and that every ranking metric is therefore unchanged. Applied to the minimum-risk rule, the uncorrected score flags transaction $i$ when $\mathrm{Amt}_i\, p_{s,i} \ge C_a$. At $\beta = 0.1$ the genuine €1,500 transaction of the running day, true $p = 0.01$, is scored $p_s = 0.092$, so its apparent expected loss is €138 instead of €15, and it is flagged.

Under constant costs the damage is repairable by moving one threshold: <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">the imbalance lesson</Ref> showed that thresholding $p_s$ at the full-data cost threshold is the correct rule for a false-positive cost $\beta$ times too small. Under example-dependent costs no single repair of that kind exists.

<Theorem title="no alert cost absorbs the bias">
Let $o(u) = u/(1 - u)$ denote the odds. For $\mathrm{Amt} > C_a$, the uncorrected rule $\mathrm{Amt}\cdot p_s \ge C_a$ flags a transaction iff
$$
o(p) \ge \frac{\beta\,C_a}{\mathrm{Amt} - C_a},
$$
while the correct rule flags it iff $o(p) \ge C_a/(\mathrm{Amt} - C_a)$. For $0 < \beta < 1$ there is no alert cost $C'_a$ for which the correct rule with $C'_a$ coincides with the uncorrected rule at every amount.

<Proof>
The correct rule $p \ge C_a/\mathrm{Amt}$ is, in odds, $o(p) \ge o(C_a/\mathrm{Amt}) = C_a/(\mathrm{Amt} - C_a)$. Undersampling gives $o(p_s) = o(p)/\beta$, so $p_s \ge C_a/\mathrm{Amt}$ iff $o(p)/\beta \ge C_a/(\mathrm{Amt} - C_a)$, which is the displayed condition. The correct rule with $C'_a$ has the boundary $C'_a/(\mathrm{Amt} - C'_a)$. Equating it with $\beta C_a/(\mathrm{Amt} - C_a)$ for every $\mathrm{Amt}$ gives $C'_a(\mathrm{Amt} - C_a) = \beta C_a(\mathrm{Amt} - C'_a)$; the coefficients of $\mathrm{Amt}$ force $C'_a = \beta C_a$, and the constant terms then require $\beta C_a^2 = \beta^2 C_a^2$, so $\beta = 1$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

For amounts far above $C_a$ the uncorrected rule behaves as if every call cost $\beta C_a$; near $\mathrm{Amt} = C_a$ the two rules meet. The distortion is therefore largest on the expensive transactions, where an alert is most often raised.

  caption="Uncorrected scores lower the bar by a factor near β at large amounts and not at all near €25: the error is not a change of cost."

The correction $p' = \beta p_s/(\beta p_s - p_s + 1)$ of the imbalance lesson removes the distortion exactly when the sampler ignored $x$. The last exercise runs the running day through both versions.

### What the measured results show

Correa Bahnsen and co-authors apply the minimum-risk rule to three conventional classifiers, a decision tree, a logistic regression and a random forest, and compare each with the same classifier thresholded in the usual way. The model behind their rule comes from a companion paper titled "Improving credit card fraud detection with calibrated probabilities". Read from the bars of their Fig. 4, on the raw transaction features the rule lifts logistic regression from about 3% to about 13% savings and lowers the tree from about 19% to 16% and the forest from about 21% to 13%. With the full feature set of their Fig. 6 it lifts all three: the tree from about 52% to 60%, the forest from 57% to 64%, the logistic regression from 12% to 45%. A rule that multiplies a probability by an amount can only be as good as the probability: where the scores are poor estimates of $p$, the minimum-risk decisions inherit the error, and where they are good, the gain over a fixed threshold is large.

<KeyFact>
Ranking metrics are invariant to any monotone distortion of the scores; savings under the minimum-risk rule are not, so the model that wins on average precision can lose on savings, and every score that feeds the rule must be a calibrated probability.
</KeyFact>

## Learning with costs versus deciding with costs

The minimum-risk rule puts the costs into the decision and leaves the model untouched. The costs can also enter the training, and the two choices are distinct: one changes what is learned, the other only what is done with it.

### Three places a cost can enter

1. **The decision.** Any model's calibrated probability, fed to $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}\cdot p$. This is the BMR variant of each model in the paper.
2. **The objective.** The training loss is replaced by the cost itself, so that the fitted model trades errors at their prices. Cost-sensitive logistic regression and the cost-sensitive decision tree below are of this kind.
3. **The data.** Rows are reweighted or resampled before a standard model is fitted. Class weights and the samplers of <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">the imbalance lesson</Ref> work here.

### Cost-sensitive logistic regression

Correa Bahnsen and co-authors replace the log loss of a logistic regression $h_\theta(x) = 1/(1 + e^{-\theta^\top x})$ by the cost of Eq. 1 with $h_\theta$ in place of the hard decision:
$$
J^c(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Bigl[ y_i\bigl(h_\theta(x_i) C_{TP_i} + (1 - h_\theta(x_i)) C_{FN_i}\bigr) + (1 - y_i)\bigl(h_\theta(x_i) C_{FP_i} + (1 - h_\theta(x_i)) C_{TN_i}\bigr) \Bigr].
$$
The objective is not convex in $\theta$, and the paper minimises it with a binary genetic algorithm. The starred section below shows what its minimiser is: a decision surface, not a probability.

### Cost-sensitive trees split on cost

A classification tree chooses each split by the decrease of an impurity such as Gini, which counts misclassified rows. The cost-sensitive tree replaces the impurity of a node by the cost of the node's cheaper constant decision, $\min\bigl(\sum_{i \in \text{node}} y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i,\ n_{\text{node}} C_a\bigr)$, the do-nothing baseline computed on the node, and chooses the split with the largest decrease of that cost, which is the largest increase of savings.

### Sampling and the measured ranking

The data route is the cheapest to implement, and the two main sources disagree on it. Baesens and co-authors cite a comparison of sampling and cost-sensitive learning on imbalanced benchmarks that found the two equivalent, and recommend sampling in a fraud setting as the simpler choice; the costs they consider are, in their words, "typically also determined on an aggregated basis, rather than on an observation-by-observation basis". Correa Bahnsen and co-authors, with per-transaction costs, report that neither under- nor oversampling improved savings, because sampling modifies the distribution of the example-dependent costs. The mechanism is visible in the class weights themselves: a class-level reweighting multiplies every fraud by the same factor, so a €8 fraud gains exactly as much weight as a €2,000 one, and the amount never enters the objective.

On the paper's test set, 71,226 transactions whose frauds total €262,167, the two cost-sensitive learners rank first with every feature set. With all features the cost-sensitive tree saves about 71% and cost-sensitive logistic regression about 67%, against 60 to 64% for the tree and the forest with the minimum-risk rule and 45% for logistic regression with it. Fitting with the costs adds eleven points over deciding with them for the tree (71% against 60%) and twenty-two for logistic regression (67% against 45%).

## ★ What the cost-sensitive objectives learn

The core path used cost-sensitive logistic regression and the cost-sensitive tree as black boxes with published scores. This section looks inside the logistic objective and at the meta-algorithm that makes any classifier cost-sensitive.

### The objective is minimised by the oracle decision

With the card matrix, the per-row term of $J^c$ is
$$
y_i\bigl(h_i C_a + (1 - h_i)\mathrm{Amt}_i\bigr) + (1 - y_i)\, h_i C_a = y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i + h_i\bigl(C_a - y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i\bigr),
$$
with $h_i = h_\theta(x_i)$. It is linear in $h_i$ with slope $C_a - y_i \mathrm{Amt}_i$. For a fraud worth more than $C_a$ the slope is negative and the term falls as $h_i \to 1$; for a genuine row, and for a fraud worth less than $C_a$, it is positive and the term falls as $h_i \to 0$. The objective rewards an output that is the oracle decision of the savings ceiling (flag exactly the frauds worth more than a call), pushed to 0 or 1; it contains no term that would make $h_\theta$ a probability. The model's output is therefore read as a decision at $h \ge 0.5$, never fed to the minimum-risk rule. A sum of sigmoids of linear functions has many local minima, which is why the paper uses a genetic search rather than gradient descent.

The convex alternative keeps the log loss and weights each row by the regret of the wrong decision: a fraud by $\mathrm{Amt}_i - C_a$, a genuine row by $C_a$. The weighted posterior then flags at 0.5 exactly when $(\mathrm{Amt}_i - C_a)\,p_i \ge C_a(1 - p_i)$, which is $\mathrm{Amt}_i\, p_i \ge C_a$: the minimum-risk rule, learned into the model. The research exercise below compares the two routes on synthetic data.

### MetaCost

Domingos' MetaCost turns any classifier into a cost-sensitive one without touching its learning algorithm. It estimates each training row's class probabilities with an ensemble trained on resamples of the data, relabels each row with the class of minimal expected cost under those probabilities, and trains the final classifier on the relabelled data. With the card matrix the relabelling step is the minimum-risk rule applied to the training set: a €15 fraud at estimated $p = 0.6$ is relabelled genuine, a €2,000 genuine transaction at $p = 0.05$ is relabelled fraud. The final classifier learns the decision, as the cost-sensitive logistic regression does, and its outputs are not probabilities either.

With all features, the six tree, forest and cost-sensitive models span about twenty points of savings, from 52% to 71%; moving from raw features to the full set moves each of them by thirty to fifty-five points. Averaged over the eight models, adding aggregates of each card's recent transactions (counts and sums over the previous hours, by card and by transaction type) raises savings by 201% over raw features alone; adding aggregates grouped by country and merchant as well raises it by 252%; adding features of the customer's usual time of day raises it by 287%. The minimum-risk rule and the cost-sensitive objectives decide how well a probability is spent; the features decide how good the probability is.

Two departures from the idealisation qualify every number. The evaluation subset was chosen as the segment where most fraud occurs, with a fraud rate of 1.50% against 0.025% in the full 120 million transactions. And the 50/25/25 partition into training, validation and test sets is not described as chronological, which is the validation <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">the validation lesson</Ref> showed overstates what a deployed model achieves.

### The missed fraud is not only its amount

The amount is the cost the transaction table can supply; it is not the whole cost, and there is no agreement on the right way to measure the cost of fraud.

- **Probing.** Fraudsters usually test a card with a small amount and replicate with larger ones if it succeeds. A passed €8 test costs the frauds it licenses, and the minimum-risk rule, by construction, never flags a transaction worth less than $C_a$. Pricing the miss at the card's available credit, the variant Correa Bahnsen and co-authors propose, restores the weight of a small fraud on a card with a large open limit.
- **Reaction time.** Once a fraud succeeds the fraudster tries to spend what is left on the card, so the loss grows with the time to detection, which <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">the economics lesson</Ref> treated as a cost that depends on delay.
- **Who pays.** Under the EU's second Payment Services Directive the payer's bank refunds an unauthorised transaction by the end of the following business day (Art. 73), and the payer can be made to bear at most €50 of losses from a lost or stolen instrument (Art. 74). The amount in $C_{FN_i}$ is therefore largely the bank's loss; <Ref lesson="psd2-sca-tra">the TRA lesson</Ref> reads the rest of that liability regime.

### The rule has no budget

The optimality theorem assumed that any number of transactions could be flagged. With investigators who open about a hundred alerts a day, the rule's alert set is a wish list: the decision becomes which $k$ transactions to work, and ranking by the expected net value $\mathrm{Amt}_i p_i - C_a$, the quantity the rule compares with zero, is the prioritisation Baesens and co-authors give for selecting cases to investigate. <Ref lesson="score-to-action">The score-to-action lesson</Ref> builds the queue and the decision table under that constraint, and <Ref lesson="adversarial-classification">the adversarial-classification lesson</Ref> gives the fraudster a cost matrix of their own, under which the amount is a choice made against the rule.

This lesson has established the example-dependent cost matrix with $C_{FN_i} = \mathrm{Amt}_i$ and $C_{TP_i} = C_{FP_i} = C_a$, the Bayes-minimum-risk rule $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}_i\, p_i$ as the per-transaction minimiser of expected cost with the constant-cost threshold $1/(1 + C_{FN}/C_{FP})$ as its special case, savings against the cheaper trivial policy with a ceiling below one and no floor, the dependence of the rule on calibrated probabilities that no change of $C_a$ can replace, and the published ranking in which cost-sensitive learners lead and features matter more than either. <Ref lesson="ulb-handbook-benchmark">The next lesson</Ref> returns to the ranking metrics and runs the handbook's five baseline models on simulated and real Worldline transactions; <Ref lesson="cost-aware-evaluation-lab">the evaluation lab</Ref> then implements savings and the minimum-risk decision as checkpoints of the harness every later lab is refereed by.

Against a card that made a few purchases the day before, a count one higher and a total €1 higher is an ordinary day; the 24-hour window dilutes the probe into the cardholder's own history, and whether $p_G$ clears 0.052 depends on a signal that window barely carries. A one-hour window reads one transaction worth €1 where the card usually shows none, followed by a €480 spend: recent-low then recent-high, the probe-then-spend pattern, visible at spend 1 rather than from spend 2 on. Neither window helps the test itself, which is worth less than a call. <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates">The transaction-aggregates lesson</Ref> builds these counts and sums over windows of several lengths, from rows known at the time of each transaction; the +201% that Correa Bahnsen and co-authors measured for aggregates is this effect averaged over a test set.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

A model was trained on an undersample with $\beta = 0.1$ and outputs, for the ten transactions of the running day, the uncorrected scores $p_s$ = 0.938, 0.345, 0.983, 0.811, 0.959, 0.924, 0.577, 0.714, 0.989, 0.092 (rows 1 to 10). (a) Apply the minimum-risk rule at $C_a = €25$ to $p_s$ directly and list the flagged rows. (b) Correct the scores with the map of <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">the imbalance lesson</Ref> and apply the rule again. (c) Count the decisions that change, compute the savings of both, and say why the average precision of $p_s$ and $p'$ on this day is identical.

<Solution>
**(a)** $\mathrm{Amt}\cdot p_s$: 14.1, 690, 39.3, 730, 115, 23.1, 202, 42.8, 7.9, 138. Flagged: 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10. Row 6, at 23.1, is just below €25.

**(b)** $p' = 0.1\,p_s/(1 - 0.9\,p_s)$. Row 8: $0.0714/(1 - 0.6426) = 0.200$; row 10: $0.0092/(1 - 0.0828) = 0.010$; the other rows return to 0.60, 0.05, 0.85, 0.30, 0.70, 0.55, 0.12, 0.90 to rounding. The rule flags 2, 3, 4, 5, 7.

**(c)** Two decisions change: rows 8 (€60) and 10 (€1,500), both genuine, flagged only on the uncorrected scores, where their expected losses look like €43 and €138 instead of €12 and €15. Uncorrected: 7 calls (€175) plus passed frauds €23, cost €198, savings $(2{,}533 - 198)/2{,}533 = 92.2\%$. Corrected: €148, savings $94.2\%$. Average precision depends only on the order of the scores, and the correction is strictly increasing, so it orders the ten rows identically; the €50 difference is invisible to every ranking metric and visible only to a rule that multiplies the score by an amount.

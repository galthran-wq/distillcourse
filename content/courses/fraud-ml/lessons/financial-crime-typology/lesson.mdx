Stolen card numbers used online, a customer's own account emptied by someone who took it over, cash from drug sales turned into apartment purchases, a student paid to let money pass through their account, a sanctioned company paying through a cousin's firm: all of these land on a bank as 'financial crime'. Which of them can a classifier learn from examples, which show up only as a change in one person's habits, which are visible only in the connections between accounts, and which arrive with no reliable labels at all?

The <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">layered detection system</Ref> was
built for one of these five: card payments, and every part of it rested on
a fact about card fraud. The model scored payments because the bank decides
payment by payment. Labels came back, from the investigators' same-day calls
on the hundred highest-ranked cards and from cardholders' disputes weeks
later, because a cardholder who loses money complains. The features came
from the card's own past because a stolen card behaves differently from its
owner.

The obvious way to handle the other four crimes is to keep the loop and
change the label column: train the same kind of classifier on accounts
marked "laundering" or "mule" instead of payments marked "fraud". That
works only if those facts carry over, and for most of the opening's crimes
they do not.

## Rarity is one difficulty out of five

Fraud is rare: about one payment in four hundred in the <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system">e-commerce card stream</Ref> the layered
system scored. Rarity has a known remedy, the class-imbalance playbook of
resampling, class weights and threshold choice. If rarity were the whole
difficulty, the five crimes would be one problem at five base rates.

### Five traits, five difficulties

A working definition used across the field lists five traits: fraud is an
uncommon, well-considered, imperceptibly concealed, time-evolving and often
carefully organised crime.

- **Uncommon.** Few cases are fraud, and only some of those are ever known
  to be fraud, so there are few labelled positives to learn from.
- **Well considered.** Fraud is planned, and the plan is aimed at the checks
  the fraudster knows about. Any check that becomes known gets avoided.
- **Concealed.** Fraudsters try to look like everyone else, so fraudulent
  and genuine records overlap: an online purchase of eighty euros is an
  online purchase of eighty euros, whoever makes it.
- **Time-evolving.** When a method is blocked, fraudsters change it. A model
  trained on last month's fraud describes a crime that has moved on.
- **Organised.** Fraudsters work with allies, and some crimes, laundering
  above all, need structures of many accounts. The evidence then sits in
  the links between records, not in any one record.

  alt="Five boxes down the left, uncommon, well considered, concealed, time-evolving and organised, each with an arrow to the difficulty it creates; a bracket marks only the first row as covered by the imbalance playbook and the other four as not addressed"
  caption="Rarity is the only trait the imbalance playbook treats. The other four need different tools."

### Why the playbook covers only the first row

Resampling and class weights change how much each labelled example counts.
None of the other four difficulties is about how much an example counts: a
reweighted model still misses the case steered around its checks, still
cannot split rows that look alike, still learns last month's fraud, and
still finds nothing in a row that is not itself the crime. And reweighting
needs labels, which some of the opening's crimes barely have.

The five crimes do not share the traits in equal measure. A stolen card
number used online is hidden inside a payment that looks like any other,
so card fraud is above all uncommon and concealed. Laundering needs many
accounts acting together, so it is above all organised. When the dominant
traits differ, so does what a detector needs, which is why one loop cannot
fit all five.

<KeyFact>
Before tuning class weights for a crime, ask which trait dominates it. If
the answer is not rarity, the fix lies in the features, the labels or the
unit being scored, and no choice of weights reaches it.
</KeyFact>

## Four questions the card loop answered without asking

Each of the card loop's four choices was forced by card fraud, and each,
asked of another crime, can come out differently.

1. **What is scored?** For cards, the payment, worked per card. Elsewhere it
   may be an account, a set of linked transfers, or a company.
2. **Who produces the label?** For cards, the investigator's call and the
   cardholder's dispute. A crime with no victim who notices has no dispute.
3. **How late does the label arrive?** For cards, the same day for the
   alerted cards and after the verification latency $\delta$, a week to a
   month, for the rest. Elsewhere it can be years, or never.
4. **Where is the signal?** For cards, in the payment itself (an amount, a
   merchant) and in the card's own history. Elsewhere it may lie in the
   connections between entities (money moving through a chain of accounts).

### The four coordinates

<Definition title="the coordinates of a detection problem">
For a given crime, the *unit* is the object a detector scores and an
investigator decides on; the *label source* is the person or process that
declares a unit criminal; the *label delay* is the time from the crime to
that declaration; and the *signal structure* is where the evidence
distinguishing criminal from genuine units lives: in a single record, in one
entity's history, or in the connections between entities.
</Definition>

## Card fraud: a label that does arrive

For stolen card numbers used online, the natural unit seems to be the
stolen card. But the bank decides per payment: each payment is accepted or
denied, and each is later labelled. So the unit is the payment, grouped
into cards for the investigators' calls, as in the <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system">layered system</Ref>.

### Card present, card not present

How a card is compromised decides what the fraudster has to copy. In a
*card-present* payment the physical card is used, at a shop terminal or a
cash machine, and fraud needs the card itself or a physical copy of it. In a *card-not-present* (CNP) payment, made on a website, by
phone or by mail, no card touches anything. The fraudster needs only the
details printed on it: number, expiry date, security code, and sometimes
the billing address. No chip and no PIN are involved.

The split moves two of the coordinates. The first is the signal in the
single row. A chip is hard to copy and the PIN stops a thief who has only
the card, so a card-present payment that passed both checks already shows
that the payer holds the real card and knows its PIN. A card-not-present
row says only that someone typed the right digits, and more of the weight
falls on the card's history. The second is the organised trait. Card
numbers are stolen in bulk, from a merchant's database or by a fake message
asking the cardholder for them, and sold on to people who did not steal
them. Remote card fraud is therefore often one operation spread over
thousands of cards, and what links them, such as the merchant they were all
stolen from, appears in no single card's history.

<Aside>
**Card-present subtypes and their labels.** A *lost or stolen* card, the
commonest, is reported within days, because the owner knows it is gone. A
*counterfeit* card carries details copied ("skimmed") from a real card
without the owner noticing, so nothing is reported until a strange payment
shows up on a statement, and the copy can be used for a long time before
that. A *card not received*, a new card intercepted in the post, is soon
reported missing if the customer ordered it; if the fraudster ordered it in
the customer's name, nobody is waiting for it and nobody reports it.
</Aside>

The missing check shows in the losses, and it makes the channel itself one
of the strongest fields of the single row. Worldwide in 2018,
card-not-present payments carried 54% of the money lost to card fraud
while making up under 15% of the money spent by card.

In Europe the shift is further along. For cards issued in the Single Euro
Payments Area (SEPA, the EU countries and a few neighbours that share one
set of rules for euro payments), card fraud cost €1.8 billion in 2018, and
card-not-present payments carried 79% of that value. All card fraud
together stayed near 0.04% of the value paid by card: four euros in every
ten thousand. That card-wide rate hides the split: a detector that watches
only remote payments faces a base rate several times higher.

<Example title="how much riskier is a remote euro?">
Use the worldwide 2018 shares: card-not-present payments carry 54% of fraud
losses on 15% of card spending (an upper bound on the spending share, so the
result is a lower bound on the ratio).

**Fraud per euro spent remotely.** Losses share over spending share:
$0.54 / 0.15 = 3.6$, in units of the overall fraud rate per euro.

**Fraud per euro spent with the card present.** The rest of the losses over
the rest of the spending: $0.46 / 0.85 \approx 0.54$.

**Ratio.** $3.6 / 0.54 \approx 6.7$. A euro paid remotely is at least six to
seven times as likely to be fraudulent as a euro paid at a terminal. The
overall rate cancels out, so the ratio holds whatever the absolute fraud
level is.
</Example>

### Where the card label comes from

Both label sources of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="what-the-model-gets-back-two-kinds-of-label-on-two-clocks">the layered
system</Ref>, the investigator's call and the cardholder's dispute, rest on
a victim who notices. And the labels are nearly complete: the cardholder has lost money and has a reason to read the
statement. The gaps are the cases above where nobody is looking, such as a
counterfeit copy or a card ordered in the customer's name. That is why
card fraud is the crime on which supervised classifiers work best, and why
most published results in the field are about it.

The signal sits partly in the payment itself, which is concealed but not
perfectly: goods that resell easily, such as electronics and jewellery, or a
new card used at once in many places. The rest sits in the card's history,
since a thief spends as much as possible as fast as possible, which no
owner does.

## Account takeover: nothing is wrong with the payment

The second crime is a customer's own account emptied by someone who took it
over, usually by obtaining the customer's online-banking login or card
details. The obvious approach treats it like card fraud: score each payment
and label the bad ones. But the fraudster now has the customer's own access
and does what customers do. Each transfer goes to a real account, for an
ordinary amount, through the customer's usual channel.

### A telephone account, call by call

Mobile telephony, where this was studied first, gives the clearest
picture: the call records of one subscriber over eleven days. The last
column marks the calls later found to be fraudulent, made

the genuine night calls of Boston residents; only the comparison with the
subscriber's own past separates them.

### Superimposed and subscription fraud

The cloned phone added its calls to a real subscriber's stream. That is one
of two shapes fraud can take on an account.

<Definition title="superimposed and subscription fraud">
*Superimposed fraud* is fraudulent activity added to the stream of a
legitimate account: the account is genuine, and fraudulent events are mixed
in with the owner's own. *Subscription fraud* is an account opened for the
purpose of fraud, usually with a false identity: every event on it is
fraudulent.
</Definition>

  src="./figures/superimposition.svg"

The two shapes need different units. In subscription fraud the account is
the unit, labelled as a whole, typically when the bills go unpaid. In
superimposed fraud the unit is an event inside an account, labelled when
the owner notices "phantom" events on a bill, which can take a long time.
Card fraud has both shapes. A stolen card number used online is
superimposed on the cardholder's own spending. *Application fraud*, a card
or loan obtained with a false or stolen identity and spent quickly, is the
subscription shape.

### What this does to the detector

Account takeover is superimposed fraud in its purest form: even the
per-row signal card fraud still has (an odd merchant, an odd country) can
be gone. The
unit is the account's stream of events, the signal is the departure from the
account's own past, and the label comes from the customer's complaint,
typically weeks later. A feature like "amount" means nothing on its own; a
feature like "amount compared with this account's usual transfers" carries
the signal, and a detector without such features cannot see account
takeover at all. Building them from windows over the entity's history is
the subject of <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates">windowed transaction
aggregates</Ref> and <Ref lesson="behavioural-profiles">behavioural
profiles</Ref>.

## Laundering: a crime with no one to complain

Card fraud and account takeover both take money from a customer who
notices. The third crime of the opening, cash from drug sales turned into
apartment purchases, takes money from no one. The obvious detector looks for
the dirty money directly, for instance by flagging large cash deposits.
Laundering is designed to defeat exactly that.

### Placement, layering, integration

*Money laundering* is the process of obscuring the source of funds that are
the profits of crime, so that they can be used as if they were legitimate.
It runs in three stages.

1. **Placement**: the cash enters the financial system, for instance
   deposited as the takings of a cash-heavy shop the launderer controls, or
   turned into a bank cheque.
2. **Layering**: the money is moved through many transactions across
   accounts with different owners at different banks, so that tracing it
   back requires following every hop. The accounts often belong to *shell
   companies*, firms that exist on paper to hold accounts, or to nominees,
   people who hold an account in their own name on someone else's behalf.
3. **Integration**: the money is merged with legitimately earned funds and
   spent, on property, a business, an apartment.

  name="laundering"

Frame 2: transfers from the shop account to shell company X at bank B and a nominee at bank C, then on to shell company X at bank D. Frame 3: the bank D account buys an apartment. Frame 4: the same diagram with four nested outlines: one transfer marked transaction, the nominee's box marked account, shell company X's two accounts marked business, the whole scheme marked ring"

<Task>
Step through the four frames. In frame 2, pick any single transfer and ask
what a bank seeing only that transfer would learn. Then, in frame 4, find
the smallest outline that contains something suspicious.
</Task>

### Why a single transaction tells nothing

Taken one at a time, the transfers of frame 2 are a business paying a
supplier and a person paying a firm, each for an ordinary amount. What is
odd shows only when several are put together: the
shop of frame 1 deposits more cash than its trade could take in, and money
entering company X at bank B leaves at once for company X's own account at
bank D. Laundering is visible in a pattern of transactions, which can be
examined at four levels: the single transaction, the account, the business (one owner
may hold several accounts, like shell company X), and the ring of
businesses acting together.

A single bank also sees only its own part. In the frames, banks A to D each
hold one or two boxes: layering is built to cross bank boundaries, so no
bank's data contains the whole scheme.

<Aside>
**Placement through trade.** Cash does not have to be deposited to be
placed. One way is to pay hugely inflated prices for imported goods, so
that a payment for goods carries the dirty money abroad. Statistical checks
of national trade records turned up cases such as a drug, erythromycin,
imported at \$1,694 a gram while the same country exported it at
\$0.08 a gram. The anomaly is visible only when a single invoice price is set
against the prices of the same goods in other invoices: the unit is the
trade flow, not the payment.
</Aside>

### Where the laundering label comes from

There is no dispute. The only label a bank produces is its own suspicion: a
*suspicious transaction report* (STR), which a bank must file with its
country's financial intelligence unit, the public agency that collects such
reports and passes cases to investigators. In the United States the same
document is called a suspicious activity report (SAR). An STR is a statement
of suspicion, not a verdict. Whether the money really was laundered is
settled, if ever, by an investigation and a court, and that can come years
after the transfers.

Three consequences follow for a classifier.

- **Most laundering is never labelled.** Most of it is never detected, so
  the negatives in a bank's data contain an unknown number of laundering
  accounts. The convention of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
  id="silence-read-as-genuine-is-a-decision-not-a-fact">reading silence as
  genuine</Ref>, which labels a payment genuine once its verification
  latency passes without a complaint, is defensible for cards and close to
  meaningless here.
- **The labelled positives are the failures.** Each method that gets
  detected stops working: the able launderers drop it and move to another,
  the clumsy ones keep using it until they are reported. A model trained on
  the caught cases learns how the clumsy launder.
- **The labels are as late as the investigation.** The verification latency
  is measured in years, longer than the life of most patterns.

<KeyFact>
What makes laundering a different learning problem from card fraud is label
completeness, more than base rate: the cardholder who loses money reports
card fraud, and laundering takes money from no one who would report it.
</KeyFact>

Only a simulated world, in which the generator knows the origin of every
euro, can label every laundered transfer. What such a world can and cannot
teach a real bank's model is taken up with <Ref
lesson="canonical-datasets">the canonical datasets</Ref>.

## Structuring and mules: the crime answers the rule

The fourth crime, a student paid to let money pass through their account,
is laundering's answer to the obvious detector for laundering, a reporting
threshold. In 1970 the United States required banks to report every cash
transaction over \$10,000 to the authorities (the Bank Secrecy Act), so that
large cash movements would show up in the reports.

### Splitting below the line

Launderers divided large sums into many amounts of just under \$10,000 and
deposited them at different banks. This is *structuring*, also called
smurfing: splitting a sum so that each piece falls below a reporting
threshold. Structuring itself was later made a crime in the United States,
but the move keeps recurring: the well-considered and time-evolving traits
at their starkest. Meanwhile the threshold reports piled up:
over 10 million of them in 1994 alone, with about 0.5% of them flagged by
the banks as suspicious. A report triggered by an amount carries almost no
information about whether the money is dirty.

  caption="Structuring: the same money, every piece under the threshold, and no report anywhere."

### Mules

Splitting needs accounts to split into. A *money mule* is a person who lets
their account be used to receive and pass on money, for a fee, sometimes
without understanding what it is. Students and recent arrivals are commonly
recruited, because their accounts are real, have a genuine history, and
attract no attention.

So the evidence moves again. The mule's account has a pattern of its own: money arrives and leaves within
hours, often to a destination the mule has never paid before. And the mules
are connected to one another through the source and the destination. A
detector therefore has to aggregate: sum the amounts entering an account
over a short window such as a day, and link transfers that share a sender,
a receiver or a timing.

EU law writes the same idea into its own threshold. Every transfer carries
the names and account numbers of payer and payee; above €1,000 the banks
must also check those details against reliable documents, and the payer's
bank must be able to supply the payer's address or date and place of
birth. A launderer could dodge that by sending €900 ten times, so the rule
applies to transfers above €1,000 whether they are

<Quote source="Regulation (EU) 2023/1113, Art. 5(2)(a)">
carried out in a single transaction or in several transactions which appear
to be linked
</Quote>

What counts as linked, and what the receiving bank must check before it
pays out, is the subject of <Ref lesson="transfer-of-funds-crypto">the rules
on information that travels with a transfer</Ref>.

<KeyFact>
A published threshold is a decision boundary the adversary can read: the
rule did not stop the money, it changed its shape from one row the rule
could see into many rows it could not. Every fixed rule on a single amount
invites the same reply, which is why the unit for mules and structuring is
the set of linked transfers, not the transfer.
</KeyFact>

### Where the mule label comes from

The labels, as for all laundering, are STRs, but they come sooner. A mule
account cannot spread itself over years and banks the way a layering chain
does: money arrives and leaves within hours, and the day's sum is large for
an account whose history is a student loan and a supermarket. Those are
exactly the window sums a bank's monitoring computes, so mule accounts tend
to be reported within months. What the report names, though, is the mule, who may
not have known they were committing a crime. The people who recruited the
mules are found as late as any launderer, or never.

## Sanctions evasion: a list, and the way around it

The last crime of the opening is a sanctioned company paying through a
cousin's firm. The tempting approach is the card one: collect past cases,
train a classifier, set a threshold. But the cousin's firm exists to get
around something else first: a payment that names the sanctioned company
itself.

### A direct payment: a duty fixed by a list

*Sanctions*, or restrictive measures in EU language, are legal orders that
freeze the funds of designated persons and companies and forbid making
funds available to them. The designations are published as lists. A bank
must not execute a transfer to or from a listed party, whatever any model
estimates, and EU law requires every payment service provider to have
internal controls to apply the measures, including screening against Union
and national lists of designated persons.

For a payment whose payer or payee is on the list, every coordinate
changes. The unit is the counterparty, the payer or payee of each transfer. The label source is the
list, and the label exists before the payment does. There is no threshold
to tune against a cost of false alarms, because missing a listed party is a
breach of the law, not an expensive error. Nor can the bank rank payments
and work the top hundred, as the card investigators did: every payment to a
listed party must be stopped.

What remains hard is deciding whether the payee is the listed party. The
payee field may spell a listed name differently, transliterate it from
another alphabet, or abbreviate it, so the check is a fuzzy comparison of
names, dates of birth, addresses and registration numbers. <Ref
lesson="transfer-of-funds-crypto">The information that travels with each
transfer</Ref> is the input to that comparison, and a learned model can help
score how likely two spellings are the same party. Whether a match is acted
on is decided by the law, not the model.

### The cousin's firm: evasion through an unlisted party

The list is published, and a listed company can read it as well as the
bank can. This is the well-considered trait again, the same reply
structuring gave the reporting threshold: the company stops appearing as
payer and pays through a firm that is not on the list, here a relative's
company. Screening that firm returns no hit, and correctly, because the firm
is not designated.

The evasion is visible only as a link between the firm and the listed
company: a shared owner, director, address or account, or money that reaches
the firm from the listed company shortly before the firm pays it on. So the
unit is a link in the graph of ownership and payments, not a counterparty.
No list labels it. The only label is a bank's own suspicion report, filed if
someone finds the link, and the delay is as long as for laundering, or the
label never comes.

<KeyFact>
A bank can screen every payment against the list without a single miss and
still detect no evasion at all. Catching it needs the graph methods of
laundering, and inherits laundering's missing labels.
</KeyFact>

| Laundering | account, business or ring | STR | years, or never | the pattern across accounts | organised, concealed |
| Mules and structuring | linked transfers | STR | months for the mule; years, or never, for the organisers | window sums and links | well considered, time-evolving, organised |
| Sanctions: payment to a listed party | counterparty | the list | known before the payment | name matching against the list | none: a legal duty, not a learning problem |
| Sanctions evasion through a related firm | link in the ownership or payment graph | STR | years, or never | shared owners, addresses, accounts; payments passed on | well considered, concealed, organised |

Read down the table and the label gets later and less complete as the
victim disappears, while the evidence moves outward, from the row, to one
entity's history, to the links between entities. The listed-party row
stands apart, but it is exactly the case the evader avoids. The label and
the signal move together for a reason: the crimes with no complainant are
the organised ones, which use many accounts and firms precisely so that no
single account, and no single bank, sees enough to object.

<KeyFact>
A result on one crime says little about another. A card model's precision
is measured against labels most of which arrive within weeks; the same
number for laundering or evasion is measured against reports filed years
later on the cases someone happened to catch, so it describes the labels as
much as the model.
</KeyFact>

<Conclusion>
A financial crime you have not met before can now be placed: name the unit
a detector would score, who would ever declare it criminal and how late,
and whether one record, one entity's history or the connections between
entities carry the evidence. That tells you whether the card loop applies,
needs history features, needs a graph, or is not a learning problem at all.

What the typology leaves open is the price of the decisions inside it. A
detector catches some crimes and raises alarms on some genuine customers,
and the investigators can work only a hundred alerts a day. <Ref
lesson="economics-of-detection">The next lesson</Ref> computes what share
of a detector's alerts are real at a given base rate, what each alert and
each miss costs, and how many alerts are worth paying people to work.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A loan is taken out online with a stolen identity: the fraudster supplies a
real person's name, date of birth and address, receives the money, and makes
no repayment. Fill in the four coordinates (unit, label source, label delay,
signal structure), say whether it is superimposed or subscription fraud, and
name the dominant trait.

<Solution>
**Unit.** The application, and the loan account it opens. Every event on
the account is fraudulent, so the account as a whole is the unit.

**Label source.** Usually the missed repayments, then collection attempts
that reach the real person, who says they never applied; sometimes the real
person notices first, from a credit report or a letter.

**Delay.** At least until the first repayment falls due, typically a month,
and often several months before the bank concludes it is fraud rather than
ordinary default.

**Signal.** Mostly in the single record at application time (the details
on the form, checks against credit-bureau data) and in the account's first
weeks: a first-time borrower who draws the full amount at once and stops
responding.

**Shape and trait.** Subscription fraud: the account was opened to defraud.
The dominant trait is concealment, since the application is built to look
genuine; and the label suffers from the uncommon trait's second half: some
of these cases stay in the data as bad debt, never known to be fraud.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "Laundering detection is card-fraud detection with a lower base
rate." Name which coordinates differ, and the step of a card-style pipeline
that fails first.

<Solution>
All four coordinates differ, while the base rate only shifts the
threshold. **Unit:** a payment against a pattern; a per-payment classifier
scores each transfer of a layering chain as an ordinary business payment.
**Label source:** the cardholder who complains, so most card fraud ends up
labelled, against a bank's own STR, which misses most laundering and skews
towards the launderers who were caught. **Delay:** days to weeks against
years. **Signal:** the row plus the card's history against connections
across accounts, often across banks, so one bank's data may not contain the
evidence at all.

**The first failing step** is the training set: the card pipeline assumes
labelled payments whose negatives are mostly genuine. For laundering the
positives are reports on accounts, not payments, and the negatives contain
undetected laundering, so the model is trained on the wrong unit with
unreliable negatives before the base rate even enters.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A simulated laundering world labels taint transitively: if A pays B \$100 of
illicit funds, B pays \$50 of those funds to C and C pays \$25 of the \$50 to D,
then all three transfers are labelled laundering, including when illicit and
legitimate funds are mixed in an account. A classifier trained there reaches
high precision on the simulator's own test set. (a) What does it learn that
no bank's data could teach? (b) It is deployed in a bank whose only labels
are its filed STRs. What happens to the precision measured in the bank, and
does the measured number mean the model got worse?

<Solution>
**(a)** The full extent of each scheme: downstream transfers, three or four
hops from the source, that look entirely ordinary and that no investigator
would ever report. And complete negatives, since every unlabelled transfer
in the simulator is clean. A bank's labels cover only what it suspected,
and its negatives hide undetected laundering.

**(b)** The deep downstream transfers the model was trained to find were
never reported in the bank, so each such alert counts as a false positive
and measured precision drops sharply. That need not mean a worse model:
some of those "false positives" are laundering nobody reported. The
measured precision is a lower bound on the true one, and the bank cannot
tell how loose without investigating the unlabelled alerts. When
the label source changes from complete to incomplete, the metric stops
measuring the model and starts measuring the labels.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take the telephone subscriber's call records from the account-takeover
section. (a) Write a rule that flags all five fraudulent calls and none of
the seven genuine ones for this subscriber. (b) Apply the same rule to all
subscribers of the network and say what happens. (c) Rewrite the rule so it
can be applied to every subscriber, and name what each subscriber's version
needs that the single-call rule did not.

<Solution>
**(a)** Every fraudulent call starts in Boston; no genuine call does. "Flag
calls whose origin is Boston" separates the twelve calls perfectly. So does
"flag calls under 40 seconds between 21:00 and 03:00": the two genuine
short calls (53 s and 24 s) are in the afternoon and late morning.

**(b)** Across the network, the first rule flags every call made from
Boston, millions of genuine ones; the second flags every short night call.
Neither feature is abnormal in itself, only for this subscriber.

**(c)** The per-subscriber version compares each call with the subscriber's
own history: flag a call whose origin region has not appeared in the
subscriber's last, say, 90 days of calls, or whose hour and duration both lie
outside the subscriber's usual range. Each subscriber's version needs what
the single-call rule did not: a profile, a stored summary of their past
calls (regions used, usual hours, typical duration), updated as calls
arrive.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Recall the verification latency $\delta$ of <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system">the layered detection system</Ref>: after
$\delta$ days, every undisputed payment of a day is labelled genuine. (a)
For each of the five crimes, with sanctions split into a direct payment to
a listed party and evasion through a related firm, say what event plays the
role of the dispute, and whether a fixed $\delta$ of a week to a month
would capture most of the crime's labels. (b) For which crime is "silence
after $\delta$ days means genuine" most wrong, and what does a model
trained under that convention learn?

<Solution>
**(a)** *Card fraud:* the cardholder's dispute (plus the investigator's
call); a week to a month captures most labels, which is why the convention
was adopted for cards. *Account takeover:* the customer's complaint; often
within weeks, but superimposed fraud can stay unnoticed longer, so a short
$\delta$ misses some. *Laundering:* an STR; a month captures almost
nothing, since discovery takes years or never comes. *Mules and
structuring:* an STR on the mule account, within months, so a week captures
few and a month some; the organisers are labelled years later or never.
*Payment to a listed party:* nothing to wait for; the list precedes the
payment, so $\delta$ has no role. *Evasion through a related firm:* an STR
if a bank finds the link; as for laundering, $\delta$ captures almost
nothing.

**(b)** Laundering, and with it evasion through a related firm. Under the
convention, every laundering account not reported within $\delta$ days,
which is most of them, enters training as genuine. The model learns that the patterns of successful launderers are
normal and treats as positive only the patterns of the launderers who were
reported quickly: it learns the boundary between quickly reported and
everything else, not between laundering and clean money.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A structuring scheme moves money from one source account S through four
mule accounts M1 to M4 to one destination account D. On a single day S sends
\$4,900 to each mule, and each mule forwards \$4,700 to D within three hours,
keeping \$200. The day's ledger at the bank holding all six accounts also
contains 10,000 ordinary transfers, many of them between \$1,000 and \$9,000.
(a) Write the eight scheme transfers as rows of a table (sender, receiver,
amount, time). (b) Draw them as nodes and arrows. (c) Could a classifier
that scores one row at a time, with only that row's fields, flag any of the
eight rows? What would make them stand out in the drawing?

<Solution>
**(a)** For instance:

| Sender | Receiver | Amount | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | M1 | 4,900 | 09:10 |
| S | M2 | 4,900 | 09:25 |
| S | M3 | 4,900 | 09:40 |
| S | M4 | 4,900 | 10:05 |
| M1 | D | 4,700 | 11:30 |
| M2 | D | 4,700 | 12:00 |
| M3 | D | 4,700 | 12:15 |
| M4 | D | 4,700 | 12:50 |

**(b)** S on the left, M1 to M4 in a column in the middle, D on the right;
four arrows fan out from S, four fan in to D: a diamond with four middle
paths.

**(c)** No. Every row is an amount between \$1,000 and \$9,000, between real
accounts, at office hours, indistinguishable from thousands of ordinary
transfers; nothing is abnormal until the eight rows are seen together,
and a row classifier never sees more than one. In the drawing the scheme
is obvious: a fan-out followed within hours by a fan-in through the same
intermediaries, each passing on nearly what it received, a property of the
graph, not of any row. Turning a transaction log into
such a graph and naming its laundering shapes is the work of <Ref
lesson="fraud-as-a-graph">transactions as a graph</Ref>, where this diamond
is the scatter-gather pattern.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Stolen card numbers used online, a customer's own account emptied by someone who took it over, cash from drug sales turned into apartment purchases, a student paid to let money pass through their account, a sanctioned company paying through a cousin's firm: all of these land on a bank as 'financial crime'. Which of them can a classifier learn from examples, which show up only as a change in one person's habits, which are visible only in the connections between accounts, and which arrive with no reliable labels at all?

The <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">layered detection system</Ref> was
built for one of these five: card payments. A model scored each accepted
payment, the investigators called the holders of the hundred highest-ranked
cards, and labels came back from those calls the same day and from
cardholders' disputes days or weeks later. Every part of that loop rested
on a fact about card fraud. The model scored payments because the bank
decides payment by payment. The labels arrived because a cardholder who
loses money complains. The model's features came from the card's own past
because a stolen card behaves differently from its owner.

The obvious way to handle the other four crimes is to keep the loop and
change the label column: train the same kind of classifier on accounts
marked "laundering" or "mule" instead of payments marked "fraud". That
works only if the four facts carry over, and for most of the opening's
crimes they do not. Nobody complains about laundered money, so there is no
dispute to produce a label. A mule's single transfer looks like any other
transfer, so scoring it row by row finds nothing. To see which loop each
crime needs, start with what all five share, then take them one at a time.

## Rarity is one difficulty out of five

The first thing a reader who has finished a course on classical machine
learning notices about fraud is that it is rare: about one payment in four
hundred in the <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">e-commerce card
stream</Ref> the layered system scored. Rarity has a known
remedy, the class-imbalance playbook of resampling, class weights and
threshold choice. If rarity were the whole difficulty, every crime in the
opening would be the same problem with a different base rate.

### Five traits, five difficulties

A working definition of fraud used across the field lists five traits:
fraud is an uncommon, well-considered, imperceptibly concealed,
time-evolving and often carefully organised crime. Each trait hands the
detector a different difficulty.

- **Uncommon.** Few cases are fraud, and of those, only some are ever known
  to be fraud. There are few labelled positives to learn from.
- **Well considered.** Fraud is planned, not impulsive, and the plan is
  aimed at the checks the fraudster knows about. A detector faces an
  opponent who picks the cases its checks miss, so any check that becomes
  known is a check that gets avoided.
- **Concealed.** Fraudsters try to look like everyone else, so fraudulent
  and genuine records overlap: an online purchase of eighty euros is an
  online purchase of eighty euros, whoever makes it.
- **Time-evolving.** When a method is blocked, fraudsters change it. A model
  trained on last month's fraud describes a crime that has moved on.
- **Organised.** Fraudsters work with allies, and some crimes, laundering
  above all, need structures of many accounts. The evidence then sits in
  the links between records, not in any one record.

  alt="Five boxes down the left, uncommon, well considered, concealed, time-evolving and organised, each with an arrow to the difficulty it creates; a bracket marks only the first row as covered by the imbalance playbook and the other four as not addressed"
  caption="Rarity is the only trait the imbalance playbook treats. The other four need different tools."

### Why the playbook covers only the first row

Resampling and class weights change how much each labelled example counts.
They do nothing when the fraudster studies the checks and steers around
them (well considered), when the positives look like the negatives
(concealed), when the positives of last month are not the positives of this
month (time-evolving), or when no single row is a positive at all because
the crime is a pattern across many rows (organised). And they need labels
to reweight, which some of the opening's crimes barely have.

The five crimes do not share the traits in equal measure. A stolen card
number used online is hidden inside a payment that looks like any other,
so card fraud is above all uncommon and concealed. Laundering needs many
accounts acting together, as the trait list already said, so it is above
all organised. When the traits that dominate differ, so does what a detector
needs. That is why one loop cannot fit all five, and why each crime has to
be asked the questions the card loop answered without noticing.

<KeyFact>
Before tuning class weights for a crime, ask which trait dominates it. If
the answer is not rarity, the fix lies in the features, the labels or the
unit being scored, and no choice of weights reaches it.
</KeyFact>

## Four questions the card loop answered without asking

The card loop made four choices, each forced by card fraud. Written out,
they become the four questions to ask of any crime before building a
detector for it.

1. **What is scored?** The card model scored payments, and investigators
   worked cards. For another crime the object that carries the suspicion
   may be an account, a set of linked transfers, or a company.
2. **Who produces the label?** For cards, the investigator's call and the
   cardholder's dispute. A crime with no victim who notices has no dispute.
3. **How late does the label arrive?** For cards, the same day for the
   alerted cards and after the verification latency $\delta$, a week to a
   month, for the rest. For other crimes it can be years, or never.
4. **Where is the signal?** In the payment itself (an amount, a merchant), in
   the entity's own history (unusual for this card), or in the connections
   between entities (money moving through a chain of accounts).

### The four coordinates

<Definition title="the coordinates of a detection problem">
For a given crime, the *unit* is the object a detector scores and an
investigator decides on; the *label source* is the person or process that
declares a unit criminal; the *label delay* is the time from the crime to
that declaration; and the *signal structure* is where the evidence
distinguishing criminal from genuine units lives: in a single record, in one
entity's history, or in the connections between entities.
</Definition>

The <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">card loop</Ref> is one point in these coordinates:
unit, the payment (worked per card); label source, investigators and
cardholders; delay, a day to a few weeks; signal, the payment plus the
card's history. The other four crimes sit at other points.

## Card fraud: a label that does arrive

Take the first crime of the opening, stolen card numbers used online. The
natural unit seems to be the stolen card. But the bank's decision is made
per payment: each payment is accepted or denied, and each is later labelled.
So the unit is the payment, grouped into cards for the investigators'
calls, exactly as in the <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system">layered
system</Ref>.

### Card present, card not present

How a card is compromised decides what the fraudster has to copy. In a
*card-present* payment the physical card is used, at a shop terminal or a
cash machine. Fraud there needs the card itself: a lost or stolen card, a
counterfeit made by copying the magnetic stripe, or a new card intercepted
in the post. In a *card-not-present* (CNP) payment, made on a website, by
phone or by mail, no card touches anything. The fraudster needs only the
details printed on it: number, expiry date, security code, and sometimes
the billing address. A card-not-present fraud is the card number used
remotely, with no chip and no PIN involved.

The split moves two of the coordinates. The first is the signal in the
single row. A chip card at a terminal is hard to copy, and the PIN check
stops a thief who has only the card, so a card-present payment that passed
both checks already carries evidence that the payer holds the real card
and knows its PIN. A card-not-present payment carries no such check: the
row says only that someone typed the right digits, and more of the weight
falls on the card's history. The second is the organised trait. A card
number is a string of digits that can be stolen in bulk from a merchant's
database or by a fake message asking the cardholder for it, and stolen
numbers are usually sold on to people who did not steal them. Remote card
fraud is therefore often one operation spread over thousands of cards, and
what links those cards, such as the merchant they were all stolen from,
appears in no single card's history.

<Aside>
**Card-present subtypes and their labels.** A *lost or stolen* card is the
commonest card-present fraud. The thief spends as much as possible before
the owner has it blocked, and the owner, who knows the card is gone, reports
it within days. A *counterfeit* card carries details copied ("skimmed") from
a real card without the owner noticing. The owner does not know to report
anything until a strange payment shows up on a statement, so the label is
late, and the copy can be used for a long time before it comes. A *card not
received* is a new card intercepted in the post. If the customer ordered
it, they soon report it missing; if the fraudster ordered it in the
customer's name, nobody is waiting for it and nobody reports it.
</Aside>

The missing check shows in the losses, and it makes the channel itself one
of the strongest fields of the single row. Worldwide in 2018,
card-not-present payments carried 54% of the money lost to card fraud
while making up under 15% of the money spent by card.

In Europe the shift is further along. For cards issued in the Single Euro
Payments Area (SEPA, the EU countries and a few
neighbours that share one set of rules for euro payments), card
fraud cost €1.8 billion in 2018, and card-not-present payments carried 79%
of that value. All card fraud together stayed near 0.04% of the value paid
by card: four euros in every ten thousand. That card-wide rate hides the
split. A detector that watches only remote payments faces a base rate
several times higher, and the example below computes how many times.

<Example title="how much riskier is a remote euro?">
Use the worldwide 2018 shares: card-not-present payments carry 54% of fraud
losses on 15% of card spending (an upper bound on the spending share, so the
result is a lower bound on the ratio).

**Fraud per euro spent remotely.** Losses share over spending share:
$0.54 / 0.15 = 3.6$, in units of the overall fraud rate per euro.

**Fraud per euro spent with the card present.** The rest of the losses over
the rest of the spending: $0.46 / 0.85 \approx 0.54$.

**Ratio.** $3.6 / 0.54 \approx 6.7$. A euro paid remotely is at least six to
seven times as likely to be a fraudulent euro as a euro paid with the card
at a terminal. The overall rate cancels out, so the ratio holds whatever the
absolute fraud level is.
</Example>

### Where the card label comes from

Card fraud has a victim who notices. Both label sources of <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="what-the-model-gets-back-two-kinds-of-label-on-two-clocks">the layered
system</Ref>, the investigator's same-day call and the cardholder's dispute
days or weeks later, rest on that victim. What matters here is how complete
the result is: most card fraud does end up labelled, because the cardholder
has lost money and has a reason to read the statement. The gaps are the
cases above where nobody is looking, such as a counterfeit copy or a card
ordered in the customer's name. That is why card fraud is the crime on which
supervised classifiers work best, and why most published results in the
field are about it.

The signal sits partly in the payment itself, which is concealed but not
perfectly: goods that resell easily, such as electronics and jewellery, or a
new card used at once in many places. The rest sits in the card's history,
since a thief spends as much as possible as fast as possible, which no
owner does.

<KeyFact>
Card fraud has a victim who loses money and complains, so the cardholder's
dispute labels most of it within weeks. Account takeover has such a victim
too; laundering, mules and sanctions evasion do not. Within card fraud, the
remote channel carries over half the losses on under a sixth of the
spending, because its single row lacks the chip and PIN evidence.
</KeyFact>

## Account takeover: nothing is wrong with the payment

The second crime is a customer's own account emptied by someone who took it
over, usually by obtaining the customer's online-banking login or card
details. The obvious approach treats it like card fraud: score each payment
and label the bad ones. The difficulty is that the fraudster now has the
customer's own access and does what customers do. Each transfer goes to a
real account, for an ordinary amount, through the customer's usual channel.
Nothing in the payment, read alone, is abnormal.

### A telephone account, call by call

The clearest picture of this comes from mobile telephony, where it was
studied first. Below are the call records of one subscriber over eleven
days. The last column marks the calls later found to be fraudulent (made

No single fraudulent call is suspicious. Millions of people make

that this particular subscriber, whose calls start in New York City and its suburbs
(Boston is about 300 km to the north-east), during the day, and often last
minutes, suddenly has short night calls from Boston. A classifier trained
on single calls from all subscribers cannot separate these rows from the
genuine night calls of Boston residents, because the rows are identical.
Only the comparison with the subscriber's own past separates them.

### Superimposed and subscription fraud

The cloned phone added its calls to a real subscriber's stream. That is one
of two shapes fraud can take on an account.

<Definition title="superimposed and subscription fraud">
*Superimposed fraud* is fraudulent activity added to the stream of a
legitimate account: the account is genuine, and fraudulent events are mixed
in with the owner's own. *Subscription fraud* is an account opened for the
purpose of fraud, usually with a false identity: every event on it is
fraudulent.
</Definition>

<Figure
  src="./figures/superimposition.svg"
  alt="Two timelines. Top: account A, every event amber, labelled subscription fraud. Bottom: account B over eleven days, blue daytime New York calls with five amber Boston night calls added on 1/10 and 1/11, labelled superimposed fraud"
  caption="The same five amber calls would be unremarkable on most accounts; on account B they break the owner's pattern."

The two shapes need different units. In subscription fraud the account is
the unit, and it is labelled as a whole, typically when the bills go unpaid.
In superimposed fraud the unit is an event inside an account, and it is
labelled when the owner notices "phantom" events on a bill, which can take a
long time. Card fraud has both shapes. A stolen card number used online is
superimposed on the cardholder's own spending. *Application fraud*, a card
or loan obtained with a false or stolen identity and spent quickly, is the
subscription shape: the whole account was fraudulent from the start.

### What this does to the detector

Account takeover is superimposed fraud in its purest form. The fraudster
holds the customer's full access, so the per-row signal that card fraud
still has (an odd merchant, an odd country) can disappear entirely. The
unit is the account's stream of events, the signal is the departure from the
account's own past, and the label comes from the customer's complaint,
typically weeks later. A feature like "amount" means nothing on its own; a
feature like "amount compared with this account's usual transfers" carries
the signal. Building such features from windows over the entity's history is
the subject of <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates">windowed transaction
aggregates</Ref> and <Ref lesson="behavioural-profiles">behavioural
profiles</Ref>.

<KeyFact>
In superimposed fraud the same payment is normal for one account and
alarming for another, so the label belongs to a payment but the evidence
belongs to the account's history. A detector without features computed from
that history cannot see account takeover at all.
</KeyFact>

## Laundering: a crime with no one to complain

Card fraud and account takeover both take money from a customer who
notices. The third crime of the opening, cash from drug sales turned into
apartment purchases, takes money from no one. The obvious detector looks for
the dirty money directly, for instance by flagging large cash deposits.
Laundering is designed to defeat exactly that, and the design is easiest to
see stage by stage.

### Placement, layering, integration

*Money laundering* is the process of obscuring the source of funds that are
the profits of crime, so that they can be used as if they were legitimate.
It runs in three stages.

1. **Placement**: the cash enters the financial system, for instance
   deposited as the takings of a cash-heavy shop the launderer controls, or
   turned into a bank cheque.
2. **Layering**: the money is moved through many transactions across
   accounts with different owners at different banks, so that tracing it
   back requires following every hop. The accounts often belong to *shell
   companies*, firms that exist on paper to hold accounts, or to nominees,
   people who hold an account in their own name on someone else's behalf.
3. **Integration**: the money is merged with legitimately earned funds and
   spent, on property, a business, an apartment.

<FigureSequence
  name="laundering"
  alt="Four frames of one scheme. Frame 1: cash from drug sales enters a shop account at bank A. Frame 2: transfers from the shop account to shell company X at bank B and a nominee at bank C, then on to shell company X at bank D. Frame 3: the bank D account buys an apartment. Frame 4: the same diagram with four nested outlines: one transfer marked transaction, the nominee's box marked account, shell company X's two accounts marked business, the whole scheme marked ring"
  caption="Each frame adds one stage; the last frame marks the four levels at which a detector can look."
/>

<Task>
Step through the four frames. In frame 2, pick any single transfer and ask
what a bank seeing only that transfer would learn: one company paying
another, an ordinary amount, between ordinary accounts. Then, in frame 4,
find the smallest outline that contains something suspicious.
</Task>

### Why a single transaction tells nothing

Take the transfers of frame 2 one at a time. The shop's payment to shell
company X is a business paying a supplier. The nominee's payment on to
company X at bank D is a person paying a firm. Each has an ordinary amount
between ordinary accounts. What is odd is visible only when several are put
together: the shop of frame 1 deposits more cash than its trade could take
in, and money entering company X at bank B leaves at once for company X's
own account at bank D. Laundering is rarely visible in an individual transaction; it is
visible in a pattern of transactions, and the pattern can be examined at
four levels: the single
transaction, the account, the business (one owner may hold several accounts,
like shell company X above), and the ring of businesses acting together.

A single bank also sees only its own part of the pattern. In the frames,
banks A, B, C and D each hold one or two boxes. The layering stage is built
to cross bank boundaries, so no bank's data contains the whole scheme.

<Aside>
**Placement through trade.** Cash does not have to be deposited to be
placed. One way is to pay hugely inflated prices for imported goods, so
that a payment for goods carries the dirty money abroad. Statistical checks
of national trade records turned up cases such as a drug, erythromycin,
imported at $1,694 a gram while the same country exported it at
$0.08 a gram. The anomaly is visible only when a single invoice price is set
against the prices of the same goods in other invoices: the unit is the
trade flow, not the payment.
</Aside>

### Where the laundering label comes from

There is no dispute. The only label a bank produces is its own suspicion: a
*suspicious transaction report* (STR), which a bank must file with its
country's financial intelligence unit, the public agency that collects such
reports and passes cases to investigators. In the United States the same
document is called a suspicious activity report (SAR). An STR is a statement
of suspicion, not a verdict. Whether the money really was laundered is
settled, if ever, by an investigation and a court, and that can come years
after the transfers.

Three consequences follow for anyone hoping to train a classifier.

- **Most laundering is never labelled.** Most of it is never detected, so
  the negatives in a bank's data contain an unknown number of laundering
  accounts. The convention of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
  id="silence-read-as-genuine-is-a-decision-not-a-fact">reading silence as
  genuine</Ref>, which labels a payment genuine once its verification
  latency passes without a complaint, is defensible for cards and close to
  meaningless here.
- **The labelled positives are the failures.** Each method that gets
  detected stops working, and the able launderers drop it and move to
  another; the clumsy ones keep using it until they are reported. The caught
  cases are therefore mostly the launderers who did not adapt, and a model
  trained on them learns how the clumsy launder.
- **The labels are as late as the investigation.** The verification latency
  is measured in years, longer than the life of most patterns.

<KeyFact>
Card fraud has a near-complete label because the cardholder reports it;
laundering has none, because nobody loses money they will report. This
difference in label completeness, more than any difference in base rate,
is what makes laundering a different learning problem.
</KeyFact>

Only a simulated world, in which the generator knows the origin of every
euro, can label every laundered transfer. What such a world can and cannot
teach a real bank's model is taken up with <Ref
lesson="canonical-datasets">the canonical datasets</Ref>.

## Structuring and mules: the crime answers the rule

The fourth crime, a student paid to let money pass through their account,
is laundering's answer to a detector. The obvious detector for laundering
was a reporting threshold. In 1970 the United States required banks to
report every cash transaction over $10,000 to the authorities (the Bank
Secrecy Act). Large cash movements, the reasoning went, would show up in the
reports.

### Splitting below the line

The answer came quickly. Launderers divided large sums into many amounts of
just under $10,000 and deposited them at different banks. This is
*structuring*, also called smurfing: splitting a sum so that each piece
falls below a reporting threshold. Structuring itself was later made a crime
in the United States, but the move it represents keeps recurring. Meanwhile
the threshold reports piled up: over 10 million of them in 1994 alone,
with about 0.5% of them flagged by the banks as
suspicious. A report triggered by an amount carries almost no information
about whether the money is dirty.

<Figure
  src="./figures/structuring.svg"
  alt="Left: one bar of $25,000 crossing a dashed $10,000 threshold line, marked reported. Right: five bars of $4,900 in five mule accounts at different banks, all under the line, with arrows converging on one destination receiving $24,500, no report filed"
  caption="Structuring: the same money, every piece under the threshold, and no report anywhere."
/>

### Mules

Splitting needs accounts to split into. A *money mule* is a person who lets
their account be used to receive and pass on money, for a fee, sometimes
without understanding what it is. Students and recent arrivals are commonly
recruited, because their accounts are real, have a genuine history, and
attract no attention. A mule account looks like a customer, because it is
one.

So the evidence moves again. Each deposit is below every threshold. The
mule's account has a pattern of its own: money arrives and leaves within
hours, often to a destination the mule has never paid before. And the mules
are connected to one another through the source and the destination. A
detector therefore has to aggregate: sum the amounts entering an account
over a short window such as a day, and link transfers that share a sender,
a receiver or a timing.

EU law writes the same idea into its own threshold. Every transfer carries
the names and account numbers of payer and payee; above €1,000 the banks
must also check those details against reliable documents, and the payer's
bank must be able to supply the payer's address or date and place of
birth. A launderer could dodge that by sending €900 ten times, so the rule
applies to transfers above €1,000 whether they are

<Quote source="Regulation (EU) 2023/1113, Art. 5(2)(a)">
carried out in a single transaction or in several transactions which appear
to be linked
</Quote>

What counts as linked, and what the receiving bank must check before it
pays out, is the subject of <Ref lesson="transfer-of-funds-crypto">the rules
on information that travels with a transfer</Ref>.

<KeyFact>
A published threshold is a decision boundary the adversary can read, and
structuring is the adversary moving just below it. Every fixed rule on a
single amount invites the same reply, which is why the unit for mules and
structuring is the set of linked transfers, not the transfer.
</KeyFact>

Here the well-considered and time-evolving traits show in their starkest
form. The rule did not stop the money; it changed the shape of the money,
from one row the rule could see into many rows it could not.

### Where the mule label comes from

The labels, as for all laundering, are STRs, but they come sooner. A layering
chain can be spread over years and many banks, each hop an ordinary business
payment. A mule account cannot hide that way: money arrives and leaves within
hours, and the day's sum is large for an account whose history is a student
loan and a supermarket. Those are exactly the window sums a bank's monitoring
computes, so mule accounts tend to be reported within months. What the report
names, though, is the mule, who may not have known they were committing a
crime. The people who recruited the mules sit one level up, and they are
found as late as any launderer, or never.

## Sanctions evasion: a list, and the way around it

The last crime of the opening is a sanctioned company paying through a
cousin's firm. The tempting approach is the one that served for cards:
collect past cases, train a classifier, set a threshold. To see why that
misses, first look at what the cousin's firm is there to get around: a
payment that names the sanctioned company itself.

### A direct payment: a duty fixed by a list

*Sanctions*, or restrictive measures in EU language, are legal orders that
freeze the funds of designated persons and companies and forbid making
funds available to them. The designations are published as lists. A bank
must not execute a transfer to or from a listed party, whatever any model
estimates, and EU law requires every payment service provider to have
internal controls to apply the measures, including screening against Union
and national lists of designated persons.

For a payment whose payer or payee is on the list, that changes every
coordinate. The question is not "does this look criminal?" but "is a party
to this payment someone the law forbids us to pay?". The unit is the
counterparty, the payer or payee of each transfer. The label source is the
list, and the label exists before the payment does: there is nothing to
learn about who is designated. There is no threshold to tune against a cost
of false alarms, because missing a listed party is a breach of the law, not
an expensive error. Nor can the bank rank payments and work the top
hundred, as the card investigators did: every payment to a listed party must
be stopped, not the most suspicious hundred of them.

What remains hard is deciding whether the payee is the listed party. The
payee field may spell a listed name differently, transliterate it from
another alphabet, or abbreviate it, so the check is a fuzzy comparison of
names, dates of birth, addresses and registration numbers. <Ref
lesson="transfer-of-funds-crypto">The information that travels with each
transfer</Ref> is the input to that comparison, and a learned model can help
score how likely two spellings are the same party. It does not decide
whether a match is acted on; the law does.

### The cousin's firm: evasion through an unlisted party

The list is published, and a listed company that wants to keep moving money
can read it as well as the bank can. This is the well-considered trait
again, the same reply structuring gave the reporting threshold: the company
stops appearing as payer and pays through a firm that is not on the list,
here a relative's company. Screening that firm returns no hit, and returns
it correctly, because the firm is not designated.

The evasion is visible only as a link between the firm and the listed
company: a shared owner, director, address or account, or money that reaches
the firm from the listed company shortly before the firm pays it on. So the
unit is a link in the graph of ownership and payments, not a counterparty.
No list labels it. The only label is a bank's own suspicion report, filed if
someone finds the link, and the delay is as long as for laundering, or the
label never comes. In all four coordinates the opening's crime sits with
laundering, not with the list.

<KeyFact>
A bank can screen every payment against the list without a single miss and
still detect no evasion at all, because the related firm passes the screen
by not being listed. Catching it needs the graph methods of laundering, and
inherits laundering's missing labels.
</KeyFact>

## The typology: five crimes, four coordinates

The four questions now have an answer for each crime of the opening, with
sanctions split into the direct payment the list catches and the evasion
the opening describes. Placed side by side, they show how far apart the
problems are.

  alt="A grid with six rows (card fraud, account takeover, laundering, mules and structuring, a payment to a listed party, and sanctions evasion through a related firm) and columns for the unit scored, the label source, the label delay drawn as a bar on a log time axis from a day to ten years, and where the signal lives; card fraud's bar spans a day to a month, account takeover's a week to three months, mules' a month to three years, laundering's one to ten years with a dashed extension marked or never, the listed-party row has a green dot marked known before the payment, and the related-firm row has the same bar as laundering, one to ten years with a dashed extension marked or never"
  caption="The crimes in the four coordinates. Only a direct payment to a listed party has its label before the payment; the evasion built around the list waits for a suspicion report like laundering."

| Crime | Unit | Label source | Delay | Signal | Dominant traits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Card fraud | payment, worked per card | dispute, investigator call | days to weeks | the row and the card's history | uncommon, concealed |
| Account takeover | the account's stream | customer complaint | weeks | the account's own history | concealed |
| Laundering | account, business or ring | STR | years, or never | the pattern across accounts | organised, concealed |
| Mules and structuring | linked transfers | STR | months for the mule; years, or never, for the organisers | window sums and links | well considered, time-evolving, organised |
| Sanctions: payment to a listed party | counterparty | the list | known before the payment | name matching against the list | none: a legal duty, not a learning problem |
| Sanctions evasion through a related firm | link in the ownership or payment graph | STR | years, or never | shared owners, addresses, accounts; payments passed on | well considered, concealed, organised |

Read down the label columns and the label gets later and less complete as
the victim disappears. The two crimes that take money from a customer are
labelled by that customer within weeks. Laundering, mules and sanctions
evasion take money from no one, so they wait for a bank's own STR: months
for a mule whose account trips the window sums, years or never for the rest.
One row sits apart. A payment that names a listed party has its label
written by the law, in the list, before the payment exists; but that row is
exactly the case the evader avoids, and the evasion drops back among the
crimes nobody reports. Read down the signal column and the evidence moves
outward, from the row, to one entity's history, to the links between
entities. The two columns move together for a reason. The crimes with no
complainant are the organised ones, which use many accounts and firms
precisely so that no single account, and no single bank, sees enough to
object.

<KeyFact>
A result on one crime says little about another. A card model's precision
is measured against labels most of which arrive within weeks; the same
number for laundering or evasion is measured against reports filed years
later on the cases someone happened to catch, so it describes the labels as
much as the model.
</KeyFact>

You can now take a financial crime you have not met before and place it:
name the unit a detector would score, say who would ever declare it
criminal and how late, and say whether one record, one entity's history or
the connections between entities would carry the evidence. From that you
can say whether the card loop applies, needs history features, needs a
graph, or is not a learning problem at all. What the typology leaves open is
the price of the decisions inside it. For the crimes that do get labels, a
detector catches some of them and raises alarms on some genuine customers,
and the investigators can work only a hundred alerts a day. <Ref
lesson="economics-of-detection">The next lesson</Ref> computes what share
of a detector's alerts are real at a given base rate, what each alert and
each miss costs, and how many alerts are worth paying people to work.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A loan is taken out online with a stolen identity: the fraudster supplies a
real person's name, date of birth and address, receives the money, and makes
no repayment. Fill in the four coordinates (unit, label source, label delay,
signal structure), say whether it is superimposed or subscription fraud, and
name the dominant trait.

<Solution>
**Unit.** The application, and the loan account it opens. Every event on
the account is fraudulent, so the account as a whole is the unit, not any
single transaction on it.

**Label source.** Usually the missed repayments, followed by collection
attempts that reach the real person, who says they never applied. Sometimes
the real person notices first, from a credit report or a letter.

**Delay.** At least until the first repayment falls due, typically a month,
and often several months before the bank concludes it is fraud rather than
ordinary default. Some of these cases are never relabelled and stay in the
data as bad debt.

**Signal.** Mostly in the single record at application time (the details
on the form, checks against credit-bureau data) and in the account's first
weeks: a first-time borrower who draws the full amount at once and stops
responding.

**Shape and trait.** Subscription fraud: the account was opened to defraud.
The dominant trait is concealment, since the application is built to look
like a genuine one, and the label is contaminated by the uncommon trait's
second half: some application fraud is filed as default and never known to
be fraud.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "Laundering detection is card-fraud detection with a lower base
rate." Name which coordinates differ, and the step of a card-style pipeline
that fails first.

<Solution>
**Base rate is not the main difference.** Both are rare. If rarity were the
only difference, the imbalance playbook would carry a card model over to
laundering with a different threshold.

**The unit differs.** Card fraud is decided per payment. Laundering is a
pattern: a deposit just under the threshold is innocent, many of them are
not. A per-payment classifier scores each transfer of a layering chain as an
ordinary business payment.

**The label source and completeness differ.** Card labels come from
cardholders who lose money and complain; most card fraud ends up labelled.
Laundering has no complainant; its only label is a bank's own STR, most
laundering is never detected, and the labelled cases skew towards the
launderers who were caught.

**The delay differs.** Days to weeks against years.

**The signal structure differs.** Card fraud is visible in the row plus the
card's history. Laundering is visible in connections across accounts, often
across banks, so one bank's data may not contain the evidence at all.

**The first failing step** is the training set: the card pipeline assumes
labelled payments whose negatives are mostly genuine. For laundering the
positives are reports on accounts, not payments, and the negatives contain
undetected laundering, so the model is trained on the wrong unit with
unreliable negatives before the base rate even enters.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A simulated laundering world labels taint transitively: if A pays B $100 of
illicit funds, B pays $50 of those funds to C and C pays $25 of the $50 to D,
then all three transfers are labelled laundering, including when illicit and
legitimate funds are mixed in an account. A classifier trained there reaches
high precision on the simulator's own test set. (a) What does it learn that
no bank's data could teach? (b) It is deployed in a bank whose only labels
are its filed STRs. What happens to the precision measured in the bank, and
does the measured number mean the model got worse?

<Solution>
**(a)** It learns the full extent of each scheme: the downstream transfers,
three or four hops from the source, that look entirely ordinary and that no
investigator would ever report. It also learns from complete negatives,
since every unlabelled transfer in the simulator is genuinely clean. No bank
has either: its labels cover only what it suspected, and its negatives hide
undetected laundering.

**(b)** Measured against STRs, much of what the model flags carries no
label: the deep downstream transfers it was trained to find were never
reported in the bank. Each such alert counts as a false positive, so
measured precision drops sharply. This is not necessarily a worse model.
The bank's labels are incomplete, so some of those "false positives" are
laundering that nobody reported. The measured precision is a lower bound on
the true precision, and the bank cannot tell how loose the bound is without
investigating the unlabelled alerts. The boundary this probes: when the
label source changes from complete to incomplete, the metric stops
measuring the model and starts measuring the labels.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take the telephone subscriber's call records from the account-takeover
section. (a) Write a rule that flags all five fraudulent calls and none of
the seven genuine ones for this subscriber. (b) Apply the same rule to all
subscribers of the network and say what happens. (c) Rewrite the rule so it
can be applied to every subscriber, and name what each subscriber's version
needs that the single-call rule did not.

<Solution>
**(a)** Every fraudulent call starts in Boston; no genuine call does. "Flag
calls whose origin is Boston" separates the twelve calls perfectly. So does
"flag calls under 40 seconds between 21:00 and 03:00": the two genuine
short calls (53 s and 24 s) are in the afternoon and late morning.

**(b)** Across all subscribers, the first rule flags every call made from
Boston, which includes every genuine Boston resident and visitor: millions of
false alarms. The second flags every short evening or night call on the
network. Neither rule's features are abnormal in themselves; they were
abnormal only for this subscriber.

**(c)** The per-subscriber version compares each call with the subscriber's
own history: flag a call whose origin region has not appeared in the
subscriber's last, say, 90 days of calls, or whose hour and duration both lie
outside the subscriber's usual range. Each subscriber's version needs a
profile: a stored summary of their past calls (regions used, usual hours,
typical duration), updated as calls arrive. The single-call rule needed
only the call. Under superimposed fraud the entity's history is the
context, and a rule can be shared across subscribers only once it is
written relative to that context.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Recall the verification latency $\delta$ of <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system">the layered detection system</Ref>: after
$\delta$ days, every undisputed payment of a day is labelled genuine. (a)
For each of the five crimes, with sanctions split into a direct payment to
a listed party and evasion through a related firm, say what event plays the
role of the dispute,
and whether a fixed $\delta$ of a week to a month would capture most of the
crime's labels. (b) For which crime is "silence after $\delta$ days means
genuine" most wrong, and what does a model trained under that convention
learn?

<Solution>
**(a)** *Card fraud:* the cardholder's dispute (plus the investigator's
call); a week to a month captures most labels, which is why the convention
was adopted for cards. *Account takeover:* the customer's complaint; often
within weeks, but superimposed fraud can stay unnoticed longer, so a short
$\delta$ misses some. *Laundering:* an STR, and later an investigation; a
fixed $\delta$ of a month captures almost nothing, since discovery takes
years and most laundering is never discovered. *Mules and structuring:* an
STR, often triggered by the mule account's window sums, within months; a
$\delta$ of a week captures few, a month some, and the organisers behind
the mules are labelled years later or never. *Sanctions, payment to a listed
party:* there is no dispute and nothing to wait for; the list is known
before the payment, so $\delta$ has no role. *Sanctions evasion through a
related firm:* the firm is not listed, so the only label is an STR filed if
a bank finds the link to the listed company; as for laundering, a fixed
$\delta$ captures almost nothing.

**(b)** Laundering, and with it evasion through a related firm. Under the convention, every laundering account not
reported within $\delta$ days enters training as a genuine account, and
those are most of the laundering accounts. The model learns that the
patterns of successful launderers are normal, and treats as positive only
the patterns of the launderers who were reported quickly. It learns the
boundary between quickly reported and everything else, not between
laundering and clean money.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A structuring scheme moves money from one source account S through four
mule accounts M1 to M4 to one destination account D. On a single day S sends
$4,900 to each mule, and each mule forwards $4,700 to D within three hours,
keeping $200. The day's ledger at the bank holding all six accounts also
contains 10,000 ordinary transfers, many of them between $1,000 and $9,000.
(a) Write the eight scheme transfers as rows of a table (sender, receiver,
amount, time). (b) Draw them as nodes and arrows. (c) Could a classifier
that scores one row at a time, with only that row's fields, flag any of the
eight rows? What would make them stand out in the drawing?

<Solution>
**(a)** For instance:

| Sender | Receiver | Amount | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | M1 | 4,900 | 09:10 |
| S | M2 | 4,900 | 09:25 |
| S | M3 | 4,900 | 09:40 |
| S | M4 | 4,900 | 10:05 |
| M1 | D | 4,700 | 11:30 |
| M2 | D | 4,700 | 12:00 |
| M3 | D | 4,700 | 12:15 |
| M4 | D | 4,700 | 12:50 |

**(b)** Six nodes: S on the left, M1 to M4 in a column in the middle, D on
the right. Four arrows fan out from S to the mules, four arrows fan in from
the mules to D: a diamond with four middle paths.

**(c)** No. Every row is an amount between $1,000 and $9,000, below every
threshold, between real accounts, at office hours, indistinguishable from
the thousands of ordinary transfers of the same size. A row classifier
would have to look at all eight rows together before anything abnormal
appears, and it never looks at more than one. In the drawing the scheme is
obvious: one node scattering to four others that each gather into the same
node within hours, with each middle account passing on nearly what it
received. That shape, a fan-out followed by a fan-in through the same
intermediaries, is a property of the graph, not of any row. Turning a
transaction log into such a graph and naming its laundering shapes is the
work of <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph">transactions as a graph</Ref>, where
this diamond is the scatter-gather pattern.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

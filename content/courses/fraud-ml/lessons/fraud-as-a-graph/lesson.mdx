Eight rows of a transfer table look ordinary one at a time; drawn as accounts and arrows, they show money leaving A, passing through C and D and returning to A. Fraud is organised, and a per-row feature cannot see the organisation. How do you turn a transaction log into a graph, what should be a node and an edge, what do shared cards, devices and addresses add, and which shapes in the graph are the laundering patterns investigators look for?

Every score in the course so far judged one row. The features were the
row's own fields plus <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates">windows over one
entity's past</Ref>: how often this card paid in the last day, how much this
terminal took in the last week. The anomaly scores of the last module went
further in one direction only, asking how far a payment sits from the
others, and the ULB file they ran on had <Ref lesson="ulb-unsupervised-scores">no
cards, accounts or merchants at all</Ref>.

Here are the eight rows. Read row 4 alone: account C pays account D €1,300
on 20 May, which looks like a business paying a supplier. A history feature for C
would say it received three transfers in May and sent one, an unremarkable
month for a small firm. What makes rows 1, 4 and 6 suspicious is that the
money C sent on came from A and ended up back at A five weeks later, minus a
cut at each hop. That fact belongs to no single account's history: it is a
property of three accounts together.

| # | date | from | to | amount (€) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 May | A | C | 1,400 |
| 2 | 9 May | B | C | 700 |
| 3 | 12 May | E | C | 950 |
| 4 | 20 May | C | D | 1,300 |
| 5 | 27 May | E | D | 2,300 |
| 6 | 4 Jun | D | A | 1,200 |
| 7 | 11 Jun | B | E | 650 |
| 8 | 19 Jun | E | D | 2,500 |

The obvious repair is more history features: count, for each account, the
transfers in and out over a month. Those counts see that C collects from
three senders. They cannot see where the money goes after it leaves C,
because following it means joining the table to itself once per hop, and the
number of hops is not known in advance. The <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology" id="why-a-single-transaction-tells-nothing">typology</Ref>
already placed laundering at the level of the pattern, not the transaction.
What is missing is an object in which a pattern can be looked for directly.

## Eight rows, one cycle: the transfer log as a directed multigraph

The rows already say who paid whom. The question is what to draw so that
"who paid whom, and then whom did they pay" becomes something you can read
off rather than compute with joins.

### Each row is an edge

Make every account a point, and every row an arrow from the payer to the
payee. Nothing is thrown away: the arrow carries the row's date and amount,
and the table can be rebuilt from the drawing.

Frame 3: rows 1, 4 and 6 and the arrows A to C, C to D and D to A highlighted in amber, with the note 2 May to 4 June, 1,400 out, 1,200 back"

<Task>
Step through the three frames. In frame 2, before looking at frame 3, find
a path of arrows that starts and ends at the same account and whose dates
increase along the way. Then check whether B → C → D → A is also such a
path, and why it is not a cycle.
</Task>

<Definition title="transaction graph">
A *graph* $G = (V, E)$ is a set $V$ of nodes and a set $E$ of edges, each
edge joining two nodes. In the *transaction graph* of a transfer log the
nodes are accounts and each transfer is a *directed* edge from the payer's
account to the payee's, carrying the transfer's timestamp and amount. Two
accounts that transfer money more than once are joined by several parallel
edges, so the transaction graph is a *directed multigraph*.
</Definition>

Rows 5 and 8 show why parallel edges stay separate: E paid D twice, on 27
May and 19 June, for different amounts. Merging them into one arrow labelled
€4,800 would keep the volume and lose the order, and the order is what the
next step needs. A laundering label belongs to a transfer, so in this
graph it sits on an edge; the card fraud label below does too.

### Money that returns: a cycle in time order

A *cycle* is a path of edges that starts and ends at the same node and
visits no other node twice. Rows 1, 4 and 6 form one, and two more things
make it look like money going round rather than three unrelated payments:
the dates increase along it (2 May, 20 May, 4 June), so each hop could have
paid out what the previous one paid in; and the amounts shrink by about the
size of a fee (1,400, 1,300, 1,200). A loop whose dates ran backwards could
not be the same money travelling.

B → C → D → A, the path the Task asked about, is a path in time order too,
but it ends at A, not at B, so it carries B's money away and never back.
Whether that is suspicious depends on what B is; the drawing only makes the
question askable.

<KeyFact>
A self-join has to be told how many hops to follow before it runs, so "does
money leaving A come back to A?" becomes one query per guessed cycle length.
A walk along the arrows needs no such number: it stops when it is back at A
or out of arrows, however many hops that takes.
</KeyFact>

## Card payments: a graph with two kinds of node

A card payment also joins two parties, a cardholder and a merchant, so the
same move applies. The difference is that the two ends are never the same
kind of thing: a cardholder does not pay another cardholder by card, and a
merchant does not pay a merchant.

### Cardholders, merchants, and no edge between two of a kind

You have already built one feature that reads across this link. The <Ref
lesson="point-in-time-features" id="a-feature-built-from-labels-what-the-bank-knew-that-morning">delayed
terminal risk</Ref> scores a payment by the share of the terminal's other
payments that turned out to be fraud: it looks from the payment to its
merchant, and from the merchant to the labels on that merchant's other
payments. One kind of fraud escapes that look. In the card-not-present fraud
of the <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="card-present-card-not-present">typology</Ref>, card numbers are stolen
in bulk from one merchant's database and then spent at other merchants, so
the frauds land scattered over many shops and no terminal's fraud rate
rises. What they share lies behind them, and the graph reaches it in three
steps: from the fraudulent payments to their cards, from those cards to the
merchant they all paid in the weeks before, and from that merchant to the
other cards it served, which are now at risk although none has been misused
yet. The step through the card is the one a terminal's own statistics never
take.

<Definition title="bipartite graph">
A graph is *bipartite* if its nodes split into two sets $V_1$ and $V_2$
such that every edge joins a node of $V_1$ to a node of $V_2$:
$E \subseteq V_1 \times V_2$. In the card graph $V_1$ holds the
cardholders, $V_2$ the merchants, and each payment is an edge between its
cardholder and its merchant.
</Definition>

"Bipartite" does not mean that the graph falls into two halves. A card
graph is usually one connected graph; what makes it bipartite is only that
no edge joins two cardholders or two merchants.
Two cardholders are related only through a merchant they both used, two
steps away. The edges are undirected: money always flows from cardholder to
merchant, so a direction would carry no information.

The toy below has three cardholders $c_1, c_2, c_3$, three merchants
$m_1, m_2, m_3$ and five payments $p_1, \dots, p_5$, of which $p_1$, by
$c_1$ at $m_1$, was later confirmed as fraud. The number on each edge is a
weight that the next section derives from the payment's age; for now, read
it as "how recent".

Right: the same payments drawn as five boxes p1 to p5 between the cardholders and merchants, each box joined to one cardholder and one merchant, p1 in amber"

### Matrix or list: what the graph costs to store

To compute on a graph you write it down in one of two ways. The *weight
matrix* has a row and a column for every node, and the cell $(i, j)$ holds
the weight of the edge between $i$ and $j$, or 0 if there is none; with
weights all equal to 1 it is the *adjacency matrix*. The *weight list* has
one line per edge: from, to, weight.

<Figure
  src="./figures/adjacency.svg"
  alt="Left: a 6 by 6 weight matrix for nodes c1 to c3 and m1 to m3, with the five weights 0.97, 0.59, 0.91, 0.17 and 0.70 each appearing twice and the other 26 cells zero. Right: a weight list of five lines, from, to and weight"
  caption="The toy as a weight matrix and as a weight list. The matrix is symmetric because the edges are undirected, and its two cardholder–cardholder and merchant–merchant blocks are zero because the graph is bipartite."
/>

Even at six nodes most of the matrix is zero, and the zeros grow with the
square of the node count. Take a bank with a million cardholders, a hundred
thousand merchants and a million payments a day. The full matrix has about
$1.2 \times 10^{12}$ cells; a month of payments is about thirty million
lines of list. So the list, or a sparse-matrix format that stores only the
non-zero cells and is a list in disguise, is what runs. The matrix is what
the mathematics is written in, because the operations of the coming lessons,
such as "give each node the weighted sum of a value held by its
neighbours", are products of the matrix with a vector, and a sparse product
touches only the stored cells.

For a bipartite graph the matrix has a fixed shape. Order the nodes with
all cardholders first; write $W$ for the $n_c \times n_m$ block of
cardholder–merchant weights, with $n_c$ cardholders and $n_m$ merchants.
Then the full matrix is

$$
Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & W \\ W^\top & 0 \end{pmatrix},
$$

and $W$ alone, the upper-right $3 \times 3$ block of the toy's matrix,
holds all the information.

## Time on the edges: weights that fade

A payment made three years ago at a merchant says almost nothing about a
cardholder today; one made this morning at a merchant that was used by a
stolen card an hour later says a lot. In a graph where every payment ever
made is an edge of weight 1, the two count the same, and a merchant
compromised in 2019 stays linked to every card it ever served.

### Choosing the decay from a horizon

The fix is to let an edge's weight fall with its age. With $h$ the time
elapsed since the payment, measured at the moment the graph is used, set

$$
w = e^{-\gamma h},
$$

with $\gamma > 0$ the *decay constant*. A fresh payment has weight 1, and
every further $\ln 2 / \gamma$ hours halves it. The constant is a design
choice with units: $\gamma$ is "per hour" when $h$ is in hours, and the
same number per minute or per day describes a different graph. The useful
way to choose it is from a horizon $H$, the age after which a payment
should hardly count. Asking for weight $\varepsilon$ at age $H$ gives
$e^{-\gamma H} = \varepsilon$, so

$$
\gamma = \frac{\ln(1/\varepsilon)}{H}.
$$

<Example title="the week graph">
Take $H = 168$ hours (a week) and let "hardly count" mean $\varepsilon = 0.05$,
one twentieth. Then $\gamma = \ln 20 / 168 = 3.00/168 \approx 0.0178$ per
hour, and the weight halves every $\ln 2 / 0.0178 \approx 39$ hours.

Score the toy at the moment it is drawn. Payment $p_1$ is 2 hours old, so
$w = e^{-0.0178 \times 2} = e^{-0.036} \approx 0.97$. The others: $p_2$ at
30 hours, $e^{-0.53} \approx 0.59$; $p_3$ at 5 hours, $0.91$; $p_4$ at
100 hours, $e^{-1.78} \approx 0.17$; $p_5$ at 20 hours, $0.70$. These are
the weights on the edges of the toy.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/decay.svg"
  alt="Weight against payment age on a log axis from 1 hour to about 3 months, three falling curves: blue for the day horizon, amber for the week horizon, dark for the month horizon. Each curve has a dot at weight 0.5 and a dot at weight 0.05, the latter at 1 day, 1 week and 1 month respectively; the amber half-weight dot is labelled 39 h"
  caption="Three horizons, three decay constants. Each curve reaches 0.05 at its horizon and half weight at 23% of it."
/>

No single horizon is right, because frauds move at different speeds: card
testing runs over minutes, a compromised terminal over weeks. So the graph
is built several times over the same payments, once per horizon, typically
a day, a week and a month, and each version yields its own features. The
cost of the decay is that a payment's weight depends on when you look, so
the graph is rebuilt on a schedule, and payments whose weight has fallen
below a floor are dropped, which also keeps the list from growing forever.

the stolen card did. $c_3$ hangs on by a weak edge to $m_3$, and $m_3$ no
longer connects it to $c_2$, because $p_4$ is four days old. The week graph
of the example keeps all five edges; the horizon decides which cardholders

</Solution>

### Several payments between the same pair

A cardholder who pays at the same café every morning has many payments
with one merchant. The transfer graph kept such repeats as parallel edges
because their order mattered; here two things change. The weight matrix
has one cell per cardholder–merchant pair, so the pair's payments have to
be combined into one number to be stored in it at all. And the decay has
already put each payment's recency into its weight, so combining them loses
less than merging the transfers would have. The bipartite graph therefore
keeps one edge per pair and adds the weights: $W_{ij} = \sum_k
e^{-\gamma h_k}$ over the pair's payments $k$. A recent payment adds nearly
1 and one older than the horizon nearly nothing. That sum is a recency-weighted count, the frequency of the <Ref
lesson="transaction-aggregates" id="summarising-a-cards-past-recency-frequency-monetary">recency,
frequency, monetary summaries</Ref> attached to a pair instead of to one
entity. The rule of every history feature holds here too: the graph used
to score a payment at time $t$ contains only payments made before $t$.

## Where the label sits: promoting payments to nodes

The fraud label belongs to a payment, which is an edge. For one hop that
is no obstacle: the delayed terminal risk stands at a merchant and counts
the labelled edges that touch it. The trouble starts when suspicion should
travel further, from $p_1$ to $m_1$, from $m_1$ to $c_2$, who paid there
too, and from $c_2$ to the other merchants $c_2$ paid.

Spreading suspicion one step is a product of the matrix with a vector. Let
$x$ hold one number per node, $x_j$ meaning how suspect node $j$ is. Then

$$
(Qx)_i = \sum_j Q_{ij} \, x_j
$$

gives node $i$ the suspicion of its neighbours, each weighted by the edge
that joins them. Run it on the toy with $x = 1$ at $m_1$ and 0 elsewhere.
Only the column of $m_1$ contributes, so $c_1$ receives 0.97 and $c_2$
receives 0.91, the weights of $p_1$ and $p_3$; $c_3$, which never paid at
$m_1$, and the three merchants, which have no edges among themselves,
receive 0. A second product carries the suspicion on to $m_2$ and $m_3$,
and the walks of <Ref lesson="guilt-by-association">a later lesson</Ref>
are exactly such repeated products. But the step had to start from a
vector indexed by cardholders and merchants. "Payment $p_1$ was fraud" has
no entry in it, so the label cannot be where the spreading starts.

The obvious move, the one the step above made, puts the label on an
endpoint. Marking $c_1$ fraudulent
because $p_1$ was gets the victim wrong: the card was stolen, and its other
payment $p_2$ was the owner's. Marking $m_1$ accuses what may be a perfectly
honest shop where a stolen number was used once. Each endpoint is only
partly implicated, and pushing the label onto either one says something the
bank does not know.

### The tripartite graph

The repair is to make the payment itself a node. Replace each edge
$c$–$m$ by a new node $p$ and two edges, $c$–$p$ and $p$–$m$, both with the
old edge's weight. The right half of the toy figure is the result.

<Definition title="tripartite transformation">
Given a bipartite card graph with cardholders $V_1$, merchants $V_2$ and
payments as edges, the *tripartite graph* $G = (V_1, V_2, V_3, E)$ has one
node in $V_3$ per payment and edges
$E \subseteq (V_1 \times V_3) \cup (V_2 \times V_3)$, such that every
payment node has exactly one cardholder neighbour and exactly one merchant
neighbour. Each new edge carries the weight of the payment it came from.
</Definition>

Write $n_p$ for the number of payments, and $M$ for the
$(n_c + n_m) \times n_p$ matrix whose row for an entity holds, in the
column of each of its payments, that payment's weight. No edge joins two
entities and none joins two payments, so with entities first and payments
last the full matrix is

$$
Q_{\text{tri}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & M \\ M^\top & 0 \end{pmatrix},
$$

of size $n_c + n_m + n_p$. It has the block shape of the bipartite matrix,
because the tripartite graph is itself bipartite: entities on one side,
payments on the other.

<Example title="the toy's tripartite matrix">
Order the rows $c_1, c_2, c_3, m_1, m_2, m_3$ and the columns
$p_1, \dots, p_5$. Payment $p_1$ joins $c_1$ and $m_1$ with weight 0.97, so
column $p_1$ of $M$ has 0.97 in rows $c_1$ and $m_1$ and zeros elsewhere.
Column by column:

| | $p_1$ | $p_2$ | $p_3$ | $p_4$ | $p_5$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $c_1$ | 0.97 | 0.59 | | | |
| $c_2$ | | | 0.91 | 0.17 | |
| $c_3$ | | | | | 0.70 |
| $m_1$ | 0.97 | | 0.91 | | |
| $m_2$ | | 0.59 | | | |
| $m_3$ | | | | 0.17 | 0.70 |

Every column has exactly two entries, one in a cardholder row and one in a
merchant row, which is the definition's condition read off the matrix.
$Q_{\text{tri}}$ is $11 \times 11$, with $M$ in its upper-right block and
$M^\top$ in its lower-left.
</Example>

The top-left 6 by 6 block and the bottom-right 5 by 5 block are grey and empty; the top-right block M and the bottom-left block M transposed are blue and hold the ten weights; the row and column of p1 and its two entries 0.97 are amber"
  caption="The toy's tripartite matrix. Only the two blue blocks hold weights, and the fraud label now has a row and a column of its own: p1."

The label now sits on node $p_1$, and any method that starts from labelled
nodes can start there: one step of $Q_{\text{tri}}$ from $x = 1$ at $p_1$
puts 0.97 on $c_1$ and on $m_1$, both endpoints partly implicated, and
nothing elsewhere. Two prices are paid. Every path between a cardholder
and a merchant is twice as long, since it now passes through a payment
node. And the graph has a node per payment, so it is as large as the log
inside the horizon; the decay of the previous section, which lets old
payments drop out, is what keeps it bounded.

<KeyFact>
The tripartite form changes no relation between cardholders and merchants;
it gives the label its own entry in the vectors a node-based method
multiplies. Whatever carries the label, a payment, a transfer, a claim, has
to be a node before suspicion can flow from it more than one hop.
</KeyFact>

## One person, many accounts: identifiers as nodes

The card graph links people through the merchants they pay. A second kind
of link matters more for organised fraud. A fraudster who opens ten
accounts, or buys a hundred stolen card numbers, spreads one operation over
many nodes, and each of them, young and quiet, looks ordinary. Treating the
accounts as separate nodes reproduces exactly the view the fraudster wants
the bank to have.

### What a fraudster has to reuse

The names on the accounts do not help: they are false or borrowed. What
joins the accounts is whatever is expensive to replace: the phone or laptop
they log in from, the card numbers they fund the accounts with, the network
address, the postal address the cards are sent to, the phone number used
for verification. Each of these is an *identifier*, and the move is to make
identifiers nodes too.

A peer-to-peer payment service, where users send money to each other, shows
the shape. Its anonymised data has four kinds of node, User, Card, Device
and IP, and four kinds of edge: P2P (a transfer from one user to another),
HAS_CC (a user registered a card), USED (a user logged in from a device) and
HAS_IP (a user connected from an address). About 33,700 of its roughly
790,000 nodes are users; more than nine nodes in ten are identifiers. A graph with
several kinds of node and edge is *heterogeneous*; the bipartite and
tripartite card graphs are its simplest cases.

<Figure
  src="./figures/identifiers.svg"
  alt="Left: a schema with a User node linked by HAS_CC to Card, USED to Device and HAS_IP to IP, and by a P2P arrow to a second User. Right: users U17 and U42, each with its own device, both linked in amber to card 4471, and a dashed amber edge between U17 and U42 labelled added edge: same card"
  caption="Left, the four kinds of node in a peer-to-peer payment graph. Right, two accounts that share a card become one connected object."

The same move works far from cards. When a fraudster takes over a phone
account, the account starts calling numbers its owner never called: the
fraudster's own family and friends, the same numbers the fraudster's
previous account called. The contact list is an identifier, and the graph
of calls links the new account to the old one.

<Aside>
**Companies that go bankrupt on purpose.** In social-security fraud an
employer avoids paying contributions by running a company into bankruptcy,
which discharges its debts, and moving its staff, machinery, address and
accountant to a newly founded company a few weeks later. The companies
change; the resources do not. A bipartite graph of companies and the
resources they use links the new company to the bankrupt one through every
shared resource, and a web of companies linked in this way, each passing
its resources on, is called a spider construction.
</Aside>

### Entity resolution: shared identifiers become edges

Once identifiers are nodes, two users who share one are two steps apart:
user, card, user. Why add a user–user edge when that path already says
they share a card? First, a rule can combine conditions no two-step path
states: "share a card *and* transferred money to each other" joins a fact
about a card node with a fact about a P2P edge. Second, the operations of
later lessons, connected components and counts of a user's neighbours, run
on user–user edges; run through the identifier nodes, they would count
cards and devices as members of a ring.

<Definition title="entity resolution (in this course)">
*Entity resolution* is the addition of an edge between two entities, users
or accounts, whenever they satisfy a rule built from shared identifiers,
such as "share a card" or "share a card and transferred money to each
other". Each rule encodes the hypothesis that the two entities are
controlled by the same person or group.
</Definition>

The second rule above targets a known scheme. A stolen card is registered on account 1 and used
to fund it; the money is sent on to account 2; the card's real owner sees
the charge and disputes it, and the bank reverses it against account 1,
which by then is empty, because the money left for account 2 before the
dispute arrived. If accounts 1 and 2 also share that card, one person can
reach both, and the transfer between them is that scheme's middle step.
Sharing one identifier can be innocent: a family
shares a laptop, a couple share a card. Sharing several identifiers at once
is harder to explain away.

One kind of identifier breaks the idea. A café's Wi-Fi gives the same
network address to hundreds of customers, and a library computer is used by
thousands; a rule "same IP" draws an edge between every pair of them and
merges strangers into one object. How to keep such identifiers from
swallowing the graph is the subject of <Ref
lesson="communities-and-components">the lesson on communities and
components</Ref>; for now it is the reason a graph is not built by adding
every identifier the log contains.

### Which graph to build

The same data can be drawn in three ways, and each loses something.

| Graph | Nodes | Captures | Loses |
|---|---|---|---|
| users only | users; P2P edges | who paid whom | shared cards, devices, addresses |
| users and identifiers | users, identifiers | shared infrastructure | the transfers |
| heterogeneous | all of them | both | nothing, at the price of several node and edge types |

The choice follows the question. For "which accounts belong to one
operator" the identifier graph is the evidence; for "where did the money
go" only the transfer edges answer; most investigations need both, and drop
the identifier types, such as IP addresses, that link too many people to
mean anything.

## The shapes of laundering: eight motifs

With accounts as nodes and transfers as edges, the laundering stages of
<Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology" id="placement-layering-integration">the
typology</Ref> leave traces with a geometry: placement collects money,
layering moves it through intermediaries, integration spends it. The obvious
detector looks for large transfers, and structuring, splitting a sum below a
reporting line, was designed to beat it. What survives the splitting is the
shape: <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology" id="exercises">the
structuring diamond</Ref>, one source paying four mules who each pass the
money to one destination, has small rows and an unmistakable drawing.

### Eight definitions

A *motif* is a small subgraph pattern of money movement. The eight below
are the shapes AMLworld plants, the <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="what-the-world-contains">simulated laundering world</Ref> with complete
labels, each defined on the directed multigraph with $k \geq 2$:

<Definition title="the eight laundering motifs">
- **Fan-out**: an account $v$ pays at least $k$ distinct accounts.
- **Fan-in**: an account $v$ is paid by at least $k$ distinct accounts.
- **Gather-scatter**: a fan-in and a fan-out at the same account $v$.
- **Scatter-gather**: a fan-out from $v$ and a fan-in to $u$ whose
  intermediate accounts are the same set: $v$ pays each intermediate, and
  each intermediate pays $u$.
- **Simple cycle**: a path that starts and ends at the same account and
  visits no other account twice.
- **Random**: a chain through accounts the same party controls, like a
  cycle except that the money does not return to its source.
- **Bipartite**: a set of input accounts pays a set of output accounts.
- **Stack**: a bipartite motif with a further bipartite layer behind it,
  so the outputs of the first pay the outputs of the second.
</Definition>

  src="./figures/motifs.svg"

Read the eight rows against these definitions. C is a fan-in with $k = 3$
(A, B and E pay it). E is a fan-out with $k = 2$: it pays C and D, and the
second payment to D adds an edge but not a distinct payee. B is also a
fan-out with $k = 2$ (it pays C and E). D is a fan-in with $k = 2$: C pays
it in row 4 and E in rows 5 and 8, and again the repeat adds no distinct
payer. Rows 1, 4 and 6 form a simple cycle of length three. And B's fan-out
and D's fan-in share their intermediates: B pays C (row 2, 9 May) and C pays
D (row 4, 20 May); B pays E (row 7, 11 June) and E pays D (row 8, 19 June).
That is a scatter-gather from B to D through C and E, each leg in time
order. Four of the five accounts are a fan-in or a fan-out, and two of the
eight shapes are present, in eight ordinary-looking rows.

### A shape is evidence only with a clock and an amount

With $k = 2$, almost every account in a bank is a fan-in or a fan-out. An
employer paying forty salaries is a fan-out; a shop paid by hundreds of
customers is a fan-in; a household paid by two employers that pays rent,
groceries and a phone bill is a gather-scatter. The shapes are the vocabulary, not the
verdict. What separates the laundering version is what happens around the
shape:

- **Time.** Money passing through is passed on fast. A detector counts a
  shape only if its edges fall within a window, for instance six hours for
  a scatter-gather and a day for the others, and only in time order along
  the path.
- **Amounts.** An intermediary that forwards nearly all it received, minus
  a cut, is a conduit; one that receives and spends on unrelated things at
  unrelated times is a customer.
- **The accounts.** Intermediaries that are young, have no other activity,
  or are linked to each other through shared identifiers point to one
  controller behind the shape.

Run the scatter-gather of the eight rows through these tests. Its legs
run from 9 May to 19 June, six weeks, so a six-hour window discards it at
once. The amounts agree: B sent 700 and 650, while C passed on 1,300 of the
3,050 it had received from three payers, and E paid D 2,500 eight days
after receiving 650 from B. Neither forward looks like B's money passing
through. As far as these rows show, the diamond is a shape and nothing
more.

The window cuts both ways, though. The cycle in the same rows took five
weeks to close; a one-day window never sees it either, and a five-week
window also sees every customer who paid a firm and got a refund. Shape
detectors are therefore built as features: for each transfer, how many cycles of up to,
say, ten accounts it closes within the window, whether it is part of a
scatter-gather, how many distinct accounts its payer paid in the last day.
They are computed by processing the transfers in time order, so that a
transfer's features read only earlier transfers, and handed to a classifier
next to the row's own fields.

<Exercise>
On the 25th, firm F pays salaries to 40 accounts it has paid every month
for a year. On the same day account X, opened three weeks ago, receives
€2,000 to €3,000 from each of six accounts it has never dealt with, all
within two hours, and within the next hour sends three transfers, totalling
the six receipts minus 2%, to three accounts abroad. (a) Name the motif at
F and the motif at X. (b) List the facts beyond the shapes that make X's
pattern suspicious and F's ordinary, and say for each which feature of a
detector would record it.

<Solution>
**(a)** F is a fan-out with $k = 40$. X is a gather-scatter: a fan-in with
$k = 6$ and a fan-out with $k = 3$ at the same account.

**(b)** *Time*: X's money passes through in about three hours; F's
salaries are paid from a balance built up over the month. A feature: total
received in the last six hours against total sent in the same window.
*Amounts*: X forwards 98% of what it received; F's outflow is unrelated to
any single inflow. A feature: the ratio of outflow to inflow within the
window. *History*: F pays the same 40 accounts every month, while X is
three weeks old and every counterparty is new to it. Features: account age,
and the share of counterparties never seen before (a <Ref
lesson="transaction-aggregates">windowed aggregate</Ref> conditioned on the
counterparty). The shape alone ranks F above X, since 40 payees beat 3; the
clock, the amounts and the history reverse the order.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### What the shapes miss

Organised laundering produces these shapes, and the shapes still miss most
of it. In AMLworld, whose labels are complete, only about one laundering
transaction in five sits in one of the eight shapes; the rest is integration disguised as payroll, supplier
payments and other ordinary spending, as <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="labels-that-follow-the-money">the canonical datasets</Ref> showed. A
perfect shape detector therefore has recall of about one in five on that
world, before any false alarm is counted, and the remaining four in five
have to be found by what the accounts and their neighbours look like.

## What a fraudster cannot fake, and what a graph cannot see

A fraudster can dress each row up: a plausible amount, a usual hour, a
device that looks like a customer's. If every row can be disguised, why
should a drawing of the same rows catch anything the rows miss? The graph
can only win if something about fraud lives between the rows, where the
disguise does not reach, and that is true for some fraud and not for all.

### Two ways fraud is social

The first way is opportunistic spread, by word of mouth: someone who
commits a fraud tells acquaintances how, and they try it. The second is
organisation: a ring needs mules, shell companies, stolen cards and
devices, and every one of those creates links among its members. Both show
up as fraud sitting close to fraud in the graph.

### A position in the graph is hard to forge

The graph has a second advantage. A fraudster controls what their own
accounts look like: the device fingerprint, the login hour, the stated age,
the amount of each transfer. They cannot see the whole graph of the bank,
let alone of the payment network, and so cannot shape their position in it
to look like everyone else's. Laundering imitates legitimate transactions
as far as it can, and the better the imitation the longer it survives; the
imitation is easy for a row and hard for a neighbourhood.

Two queries on the peer-to-peer data show what reading a position looks
like. The first follows paths of five transfers that start and end at known
fraudulent users; the users in the middle are mostly unflagged, and they
become the new suspects, since money passed through them on its way between
two fraudsters. The second asks which flagged users are linked to the same
card or device; each shared identifier is a candidate for one person behind
several accounts. Neither query needs a model; both are walks in the graph.
Neither is yet proof that fraud clusters, either: how often flagged users
share an identifier means something only against how often unflagged users
do.

<KeyFact>
The graph helps exactly where fraud is organised. A lone opportunist with
one stolen card leaves a single edge and no structure, and the card's own
history remains the place to find it; a ring leaves links that no member
can see or remove.
</KeyFact>

<Conclusion>
You can now turn a transaction log into a graph and say why each choice is
made. Transfers become a directed multigraph of accounts, with parallel
edges kept apart because order matters. Card payments become a bipartite
graph of cardholders and merchants, stored as a weight list, with weights
$e^{-\gamma h}$ whose constant is set from a horizon. When the label sits
on the payment, the payment is promoted to a node, giving the tripartite
matrix $Q_{\text{tri}}$. Identifiers become nodes, and shared identifiers
become entity-resolution edges, with the warning that some identifiers link
everyone. And the eight laundering motifs can be named and read as features
that need a time window and amounts to mean anything, and that miss most
laundering by construction.

What is left open is whether fraud actually sits next to fraud in a given
graph, and what to compute about a node's neighbourhood once it does. <Ref
lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality">The next lesson</Ref> tests the first
with a count of edges between fraudulent and legitimate nodes, and turns the
second into numbers an ordinary classifier can use.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
A card log within the week horizon ($\gamma = 0.0178$ per hour) has four
payments: $c_1$ at $m_1$, 12 hours old; $c_1$ at $m_2$, 48 hours old; $c_2$
at $m_2$, 6 hours old; $c_2$ at $m_2$ again, 72 hours old. Write (a) the
weight list of the bipartite graph, summing payments of the same pair, (b)
the $2 \times 2$ block $W$, and (c) the matrix $M$ of the tripartite graph.
Why does $M$ need no summing?

<Solution>
Weights: $e^{-0.0178 \times 12} = e^{-0.214} \approx 0.81$;
$e^{-0.854} \approx 0.43$; $e^{-0.107} \approx 0.90$;
$e^{-1.28} \approx 0.28$.

**(a)** $(c_1, m_1, 0.81)$, $(c_1, m_2, 0.43)$,
$(c_2, m_2, 0.90 + 0.28 = 1.18)$.

**(b)** Rows $c_1, c_2$, columns $m_1, m_2$:

$$
W = \begin{pmatrix} 0.81 & 0.43 \\ 0 & 1.18 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**(c)** Rows $c_1, c_2, m_1, m_2$; columns $p_1, \dots, p_4$ in the order
given:

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix}
0.81 & 0.43 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0.90 & 0.28 \\
0.81 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0.43 & 0.90 & 0.28
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Each payment has its own column, so two payments by the same pair are two
nodes and nothing has to be merged; each column still has exactly two
non-zero entries. The bipartite graph has one cell per pair, so the pair's
payments must be combined there, and summing the decayed weights is the
choice that keeps a recency-weighted count. The tripartite form keeps each
payment's own weight, at the price of a larger matrix.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Let $Q_{\text{tri}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & M \\ M^\top & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ as
in the chapter. (a) Compute $Q_{\text{tri}}^2$ in block form, and show that
its entity block $MM^\top$ has, for a cardholder $i$ and a merchant $j$,
the entry $\sum w_p^2$ over the payments $p$ between them, and zero for two
different cardholders. (b) Show that odd powers of $Q_{\text{tri}}$ have
zero diagonal blocks and even powers zero off-diagonal blocks, and say what
that means for a walk that moves along one edge per step.

<Solution>
**(a)** Block multiplication gives

$$
Q_{\text{tri}}^2 = \begin{pmatrix} MM^\top & 0 \\ 0 & M^\top M \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The entry of $MM^\top$ for entities $a$ and $b$ is
$\sum_p M_{ap} M_{bp}$, a sum over payments that touch both. A payment
touches exactly one cardholder and one merchant. For a cardholder $i$ and a
merchant $j$ the sum runs over the payments between them, each contributing
$w_p \cdot w_p = w_p^2$. For two different cardholders no payment touches
both, so the entry is 0; the same holds for two merchants. Two steps on the
tripartite graph are therefore one step of the bipartite graph, with
squared weights, plus the diagonal terms $\sum_p M_{ap}^2$, which are walks
that go out to a payment and come back.

**(b)** Induction: $Q_{\text{tri}}$ has the off-diagonal form, the square has
the diagonal form, and multiplying a diagonal-form matrix by
$Q_{\text{tri}}$ gives the off-diagonal form again, and vice versa. Entry
$(a, b)$ of $Q^k$ sums the weighted walks of $k$ steps from $a$ to $b$, so
after an odd number of steps a walk that started at an entity is always on
a payment, and after an even number always on an entity. The walk
alternates between the two sides, and every cardholder–merchant step of the
bipartite graph takes two steps here. <Ref lesson="guilt-by-association">The
random walk with restart</Ref> runs on exactly this matrix, starting from
the fraudulent payment nodes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
(a) What graph does $w = e^{-\gamma h}$ produce as $\gamma \to 0$, and as
$\gamma \to \infty$? Why can neither carry a fraud signal through time?
(b) A configuration file sets $\gamma = 0.004$ with $h$ in hours, and
documents it as the graph that "forgets after a week". Compute the weight of
a week-old payment, the half-life, and the age at which the weight reaches
0.05. Which horizon does this setting actually implement?

<Solution>
**(a)** As $\gamma \to 0$ every weight tends to 1: the graph of all
payments ever made, unweighted. A merchant compromised years ago stays as
strongly linked as one compromised this morning, so old structure drowns
the new. As $\gamma \to \infty$ every weight with $h > 0$ tends to 0: the
graph is empty except for the payment being made now, so there is no
neighbourhood to read. Both extremes destroy the comparison of recent with
old that the decay exists to make; any useful $\gamma$ sits between them,
set by how long a fraud pattern stays active.

**(b)** Week-old: $e^{-0.004 \times 168} = e^{-0.672} \approx 0.51$, half
the weight of a fresh payment, not "forgotten". Half-life
$\ln 2/0.004 \approx 173$ hours, about 7.2 days. Weight 0.05 at
$\ln 20/0.004 \approx 749$ hours, about 31 days. The setting is the month
graph of the chapter ($\gamma \approx 0.0042$ per hour), off by the units or
by the horizon. The check costs one line, and a graph whose horizon is four
times longer than documented gives features that react four times more
slowly than everyone believes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "Money returning to its source along a cycle is a strong indicator
of laundering, so the bank should alert on every transfer that closes a
cycle of up to ten accounts within a month." Give at least two kinds of
legitimate cycle, then estimate the precision of the rule for a stream in
which one transfer in 1,750 is laundering, under the generous assumption
that a laundering transfer is twenty times as likely as an ordinary one to
close such a cycle. Name the step in the claim that fails.

<Solution>
**Legitimate cycles.** A customer pays a firm and receives a refund; a
person moves savings from an account at one bank to another and back; firms
in one group pay each other for services and settle the balance; a loan is
paid out and repaid. With a month's window and ten accounts allowed, a
large bank holds a very large number of such cycles.

**Precision.** Use the <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="bayes-rule-and-why-the-error-rates-cannot-rescue-precision">odds form of
Bayes' rule</Ref>: posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds. The
prior odds are $1 : 1{,}749$, the likelihood ratio is 20, so the posterior
odds are $20 : 1{,}749 \approx 1 : 87$, a precision of about 1.1%. About 99
alerts in 100 go to cycles of ordinary customers.

**The failing step.** "Strong indicator" was read as "most cycles are
laundering", which is the statement $P(\text{laundering} \mid \text{cycle})$
large. What is true at best is that $P(\text{cycle} \mid \text{laundering})$
is several times $P(\text{cycle} \mid \text{genuine})$, and at a base rate
of one in 1,750 that ratio cannot carry the precision. The rule also fails
the other way: most laundering sits in no shape at all, so even its recall
is bounded. A cycle is a feature to combine with time, amounts and the
accounts' history, not an alert.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

In the Kaggle PaySim file, one fraud is two consecutive rows in the same

found that a fraud transfer's receiver keeps a balance of 0 and that the
cash-out's sender is never the transfer's receiver; that almost every sender
appears in exactly one row. (a) Draw the four accounts and two transfers as
a graph. Which of the eight motifs does the fraud form? (b) Design a check,
runnable on the whole file, that measures how often a fraud transfer's
receiver later sends money. State the result you expect and what would
count as agreement. (c) What does the answer mean for using PaySim to test
a motif detector or a graph feature?

<Solution>
**(a)** Two disjoint edges: C2122951913 → C878866926 and

**(b)** Take the set of receivers of rows with `type == "TRANSFER"` and

Report the share of fraud transfers with at least one such row, and the

<Exercise>
Twelve users are linked by entity-resolution edges. Four of them,
$F_1$ to $F_4$, are known fraudsters; eight, $L_1$ to $L_8$, are not. The
fourteen edges are $F_1F_2$, $F_1F_3$, $F_2F_3$, $F_3F_4$, $F_2F_4$,

that each endpoint of an edge is a fraudster with probability $f = 4/12$
and not with probability $l = 8/12$, independently. How many of the 14
edges would you expect to join the two kinds? (c) What does the comparison
suggest, and what would you need before trusting it?

<Solution>
**(a)** Two: $F_4L_1$ and $F_1L_5$. Five edges join two fraudsters and
seven join two non-fraudsters.

**(b)** An edge joins the two kinds if one end is a fraudster and the other
is not, in either order: probability $2lf = 2 \times \tfrac{2}{3} \times

\approx 6.2$.

**(c)** Two observed against about six expected: fraudsters are linked to
each other far more than chance would give, so their labels cluster and a
user's neighbours carry information about the user. Before trusting it you
need to know whether two against six could arise by chance in a graph this
small, which is a significance test, and whether the clustering holds in a
real graph rather than one drawn to show it. This comparison is the
homophily test that <Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality">the next
lesson</Ref> defines and makes into a test, before using a node's
neighbourhood as a feature.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A fraud feature for payment $i$, made at time $t_i$, is read from the
tripartite week graph: for instance, the summed weight of the fraudulent
payment nodes two steps from the cardholder. Using <Ref
lesson="point-in-time-features" id="two-ways-a-training-row-can-know-too-much">point-in-time
correctness</Ref>, specify the graph from which the training row of $i$ may
read: which payment nodes it contains, the age $h$ each weight uses, and
which payment nodes may carry a fraud label. Then name the two ways a
graph rebuilt once from the final training table leaks.

<Solution>
**Payment nodes.** Only payments made strictly before $t_i$, with their
cardholder and merchant nodes; payment $i$ itself may appear as the node
being scored, but none after it.

**Weights.** Each weight is $e^{-\gamma (t_i - t_p)}$, with $t_p$ the
time of payment $p$: its age at $t_i$, not at the date the table was
built. Ages measured at the build date make every payment look older than
it was when $i$ was scored, and give values the serving path, which
computes them at $t_i$, never produces.

**Labels.** A payment node may carry a fraud label only if that label had
arrived by $t_i$: in the course's convention, payments at least $\delta$
old, whose labels have arrived, plus any feedback label whose arrival time
is recorded and precedes $t_i$. A fraud confirmed after $t_i$ is unlabelled
in the graph of row $i$.

**The two leaks.** A graph built once from the final table contains future
payments, the first leak of the point-in-time lesson, now as edges reaching
forward from the cardholder; and it labels payments with fraud verdicts
that had not arrived at $t_i$, the second leak, which inflates exactly the
features that count fraudulent neighbours. The second is the graph version
of the naive terminal fraud rate on final labels.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

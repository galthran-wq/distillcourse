A payment network sees about four hundred thousand card payments a day and must answer each one within a second or two. Around a thousand of those payments are fraud, the team that can phone a cardholder and check can handle about a hundred cases a day, and whether a payment really was fraud is only known days or weeks later, if anyone ever says. Where in that flow can a learned model sit, what can it be asked to do, and what does it get back?

The obvious answer comes straight from a first course in machine learning.
Collect payments labelled fraud or genuine, train a classifier that returns a
probability of fraud, put it in front of every payment, and refuse the payment
when the probability passes one half. Each of the three constraints in the
opening breaks one piece of that picture.

- **The deadline.** Whatever refuses a payment must decide within the
  second or two the till waits, and turns a customer away each time it is
  wrong, so it must almost never be wrong; a classifier's "fraud" is rarely
  that sure.
- **The team.** A classifier that wrongly says "fraud" on just one genuine
  payment in a hundred says it about 4,000 times a day. A hundred cases is
  all anyone can check.
- **The labels.** The labelled payments the classifier is trained on are
  produced by those same hundred cases and by customers who complain, late
  or never. Nobody hands the bank a labelled copy of the day.

## The deadline splits the work in two

When a card is used at a till or on a website, the terminal sends a request to
the bank that issued the card (the card's *issuer*) and waits for one word back: accept or deny. End to end the
answer must arrive within one or two seconds, and most of that goes on the
round trip from the terminal through the card network to the issuer and
back. The issuer's own checks get the few milliseconds left over.

### What can be checked before the answer

Some questions need only the request and a lookup of values the bank already
keeps for the card's account: is the PIN right (for a chip card), how many wrong attempts have there been, is
the card active or blocked, is there enough balance, is the spending limit
reached. These are the *terminal checks*. A request that fails one is denied.
They are not fraud detection as such, but they stop a stolen card that has
already been reported.

On top of them the bank runs rules its fraud staff have written over the same
information. One example:

> IF internet transaction AND unsecured website THEN deny the transaction.

This is a *blocking rule*: an if-then statement evaluated on the request alone,
without the card's history, that refuses the payment when it fires. Many run
side by side; a payment that fires any of them is refused, while the card
stays active.

### Why a block has to be nearly always right

A refused genuine payment is a lost sale for the merchant and an embarrassed
customer at the till, who may take their next purchase to a different card.
Of the 400,000 daily payments, about 399,000 are genuine. A rule that
wrongly fires on 1% of genuine payments refuses about 4,000 genuine customers a
day: four for every fraud in the whole stream, even if it caught every fraud.

Blocking rules are therefore few and narrow, each written by a person who can
say why it is safe.

<KeyFact>
What may refuse a payment must finish before the answer and keep its false
alarms to a handful a day out of 400,000 payments. The split is between
fast-and-precise and everything else, not between rules and models.
</KeyFact>

### Where a learned model fits: after the answer

A learned model is good at a different job: noticing that a payment which
looks ordinary on its own is unusual *for this card*. Eighty euros at an
online electronics shop is ordinary. It is not ordinary for a card that has
spent twenty euros a week on groceries for two years and was used on another
continent forty minutes ago. To see that, the model needs features computed
over the card's history: counts and sums of its payments over the last hour,
day, week and month, its average weekly spend, the location of its last
purchase; beside them, the same kind of feature for the merchant, such as
how often it has been hit by fraud lately. A working model reads dozens of
them. Building them for each payment is called *feature augmentation*.

### Could the model refuse payments?

Test the model against the two demands, precision first, because precision
decides. A model's score adds up many weak hints: among the hundred cards it
is most suspicious of on a typical day, about 85 turn out to be genuine, as
the next section measures. Letting those scores refuse payments would turn
away genuine customers on most of the cards it acts on, every day. Raising the
threshold until its refusals are almost always fraud leaves only a thin
sliver of the scores above it, so as a whole the model cannot do a blocking
rule's job.

Time alone does not rule the model out. Many history features can be kept
ready before a request arrives (a stored count of the card's payments today,
plus one, is today's count including this payment; a weekly average can be
updated overnight), and before the answer the deadline allows exactly that:
values ready at request time and an evaluation that finishes in milliseconds.
But since the model may not refuse anyway, nothing is gained by squeezing it
into that slot. After the answer every feature can be recomputed with the new
payment included, and the model can be as large as it needs to be.

So the model scores a payment that has already been accepted, enriched with
its history features, within a second or so after the answer. It cannot
refuse that payment. It can
block the *card* for future payments, and a second's delay costs almost
nothing there, because a stolen card is used in a burst over minutes and
hours. And it can ask a person to look: that request is an *alert*, which
refuses nothing and puts the card in front of an investigator.

## A hundred cases a day: the model's output is a ranking

One piece of the obvious picture still stands: the threshold at one half that
turns the probability into "fraud" or "not fraud". The investigation team
breaks it. An investigator phones the cardholder and goes through all of the
card's recent payments in one call, so the unit of their work is the card, and
the team's capacity is about a hundred cards a day.

<Example title="one day of a real card stream">
A Belgian e-commerce card stream in 2018 carried about 400,000 payments a day.
About 1,000 of them were fraudulent, 0.25% of the stream, spread over slightly
more than 300 cards, so a compromised card carried about three fraudulent
payments a day.

The figures do not say how many distinct cards paid; take about 120,000, a
little over three payments per card. Then the 300
compromised cards are one card in 400, the same quarter of a percent that
fraud is of payments.

**A perfect model.** A model that knew exactly which cards were compromised
could still send only 100 of the 300 to the investigators: about 200,
two-thirds of the day's compromised cards, are never looked at that day.

**A thresholded model.** A model that flags every payment whose probability
passes one half, wrong on only 1% of genuine payments, flags about $0.01 \times 399{,}000 \approx 4{,}000$ genuine
payments, plus whatever frauds it catches. That is about forty times what the
team can work: the yes/no output describes 4,000 decisions while the bank
makes at most 100.
</Example>

Frauds outnumber compromised cards about three to one on every day of the three weeks, because
a stolen card is used several times before anyone stops it. So one call to a
cardholder settles about three fraudulent payments at once, and the card,
not the payment, is the natural unit to rank and to count.

### Ranking, then cutting at the capacity

The useful question for the model is therefore not "is this payment fraud?"
but "which hundred cards should the team call today?". So the day's cards are
sorted by their highest score and the top $k$ go to the investigators, with
$k \approx 100$. Write $T_t$ for the payments of day $t$.
The day's *alerts* $A_t$ are the payments of $T_t$ on the $k$
highest-ranked cards.

$k$ is fixed by people (how many investigators, how many calls each can make
in a day), not by the model: there will be about a hundred alerts tomorrow
whatever the model's quality. What a better model buys is a more precise top
hundred: more real fraud among the cards the team was going to call anyway.

How much better than chance does a real model do? On the same Belgian
stream, a logistic regression at default settings, nothing tuned, was
trained on one week of the bank's labelled payments and then scored a
later week, leaving out cards the bank already knew were compromised. On
each day of that later week, about 15 of its 100 highest-scored cards
turned out to be compromised. A hundred
cards picked at random from the example's 120,000 would hold on average
$100 \times 300 / 120{,}000 = 0.25$, a quarter of a compromised card, so
fifteen in a hundred is about sixty times what chance finds. The assumed card
count barely matters: a random hundred would hold even one compromised card
only if the 400,000 payments came from fewer than 30,000 cards, more than
thirteen payments per card per day. Precision also matters for a reason beyond the count:
investigators who spend most of their calls on legitimate customers start to
distrust the alerts.

<Exercise>
A larger issuer sees 2,000,000 card payments a day, 0.1% of them fraudulent,
with about four fraudulent payments per compromised card. Its team of 25
investigators can each work 12 cards a day. Compute: the number of compromised cards a day; the largest share of them a

<Solution>
Fraudulent payments: $0.001 \times 2{,}000{,}000 = 2{,}000$. At about four per

Capacity: $k = 25 \times 12 = 300$ cards a day. A perfect ranking sends 300 of
the 500 compromised cards, so at most $300/500 = 60\%$ are reached and 40% are
never looked at that day, even with every call finding fraud.

Genuine payments: $2{,}000{,}000 - 2{,}000 = 1{,}998{,}000$. A 1% false-flag
rate flags about $19{,}980 \approx 20{,}000$ of them, about $20{,}000 / 300
\approx 67$ times what the team can work.

The team is three times the example's, yet the false flags, which scale with
the genuine payments while the team does not, still overwhelm it by more than
an order of magnitude.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Rules beside the model: two tools with different upkeep

Once a model ranks the cards, it is tempting to retire the rules people
wrote. The rules that run after the answer are of a second kind.

A *scoring rule* reads the same history features as the model, but instead of
refusing a payment it gives it a score, and its alerts join the model's in the
investigators' queue. An example:

> IF previous transaction in a different continent AND less than one hour from
> the previous transaction THEN fraud score = 0.95.

### What a rule can do that the model cannot

**Speed of change.** When investigators notice a new pattern on Monday, a rule
can encode it on Monday. The model learns a pattern only after enough labelled
examples of it have reached its training set, which takes days at least.

**Readability.** A rule can be read and argued over: the investigator knows
exactly why a card was flagged, and the rule can be deleted the day it starts
raising false alarms. The model cannot be opened and edited; the only way to
change what it does is to retrain it on new labels.

### What the model can do that a rule cannot

A rule catches only patterns someone has already discovered, and only patterns
simple enough to state in a few features. Different experts write different
rules, and hundreds of them become hard to keep consistent. The
model combines dozens of features at once, nonlinearly, and finds patterns no
one has stated. The two tools fail differently, so a system uses both.

| | When it runs | What it sees | What it can do | Who changes it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Terminal checks | before the answer, milliseconds | the request, the account's status | deny | the bank's IT |
| Blocking rules | before the answer, milliseconds | the request alone | deny | investigators, any day |
| Scoring rules | after the answer, under a second | request plus history features | score, alert | investigators, any day |
| Model | after the answer, under a second | request plus history features | score, alert, block the card | retraining on labels |
| Investigators | the same day | every recent payment of a card | label, block the card | hiring |

<KeyFact>
The five rows are one bank's arrangement: another bank may merge the two kinds of rule or split the model into several. What the
three constraints fix is the order: fast and precise before the answer, a
slower ranking after it, people at the end, and their labels flowing back
into the model.
</KeyFact>

## What the model gets back: two kinds of label on two clocks

The model, unlike every rule in the table, is built from labelled payments. A
classroom dataset arrives with a label on every row; the bank's training data
cannot, because nobody checks 400,000 payments a day. Only two groups of people ever say whether a payment was
fraud.

### Investigators: fast labels on the alerted cards

During the call on an alerted card, each of its recent payments is marked
fraud or genuine and returned to the system the same day; a card found
compromised is blocked straight away. These labels are called *feedbacks*. Write $F_t$ for the
recent payments of the cards alerted on day $t$, with their labels. One call
can produce many feedback labels, most of them on genuine payments of a card
that was alerted for one bad payment, or of a card that turned out not to be
compromised at all.

### Customers: slow labels, and silence

Every other payment gets a label only if the cardholder notices it on a
statement and disputes it. That takes days or weeks, depending on how often
the customer reads statements, plus the bank's own handling of the claim. In
the EU, the payment services directive
([PSD2](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2015/2366/oj), Article 71) gives a
customer up to thirteen months after the debit to claim an unauthorised
payment.

Most payments are never disputed, and no one confirms them either. The
system cannot wait thirteen months, so it waits a fixed number of days and
then labels every undisputed payment genuine.

<Definition title="verification latency">
The *verification latency* $\delta$ is the number of days after which a day's
payments are treated as fully labelled: disputed payments as fraud, all others
as genuine. At day $t$ the system receives the *delayed samples*
$D_{t-\delta}$: every payment of day $t - \delta$, each with a label.
</Definition>

Banks set $\delta$ between about a week and a month; the examples here use
$\delta = 7$. The feedbacks of day $t - \delta$ are part of $D_{t-\delta}$: the
delayed samples confirm them and add everything else.

### Silence read as genuine is a decision, not a fact

A fraud that the cardholder never notices (a small amount, an unread statement,
a card they rarely use) enters the training set labelled genuine. A dispute
that arrives after $\delta$ days means a fraud was already used in training as
a genuine payment. How many such rows the training data hold is unknown,
because nobody measures what nobody reported. Every negative label in the
training set is therefore "not reported within $\delta$ days", not "verified
genuine".

<Example title="what the system knows at the end of day t">
Take $\delta = 7$. At the end of day $t$, the training data hold:

- complete labels for day $t - 7$ and every earlier day;
- for days $t-6$ through $t$, only the feedbacks $F_{t-6}, \dots, F_t$, plus
  whatever early disputes have arrived.

On those seven days about $7 \times 1{,}000 = 7{,}000$ fraudulent payments were
made. The system has labels for the ones on cards the investigators called:
at fifteen fraud cards found per day and about three frauds each, roughly 45
a day, about 300 over the week. The other 6,700 or so are, for now, unlabelled
payments indistinguishable from genuine ones.
</Example>

## The fast labels are a sample the model chose

The feedbacks are the freshest labels the bank has, and fraud changes from week
to week, so the natural move is to train mostly on them.

The investigators called the top hundred cards of yesterday's model, so every
fraud in $F_t$ is a fraud that model already scored high. A kind of fraud the model scores low
lands below the cut, never reaches an investigator, and never appears in the
feedbacks. It reaches the training set only through the slow channel, a week
or more later, and only if the customer disputes it.

The class mix is distorted as well: about 15 of the 100 alerted cards are
compromised, against a quarter of one in a random hundred. A model trained on
feedbacks as if they were a sample of the stream learns that compromised cards are about sixty times as common as
they are, and learns the boundary of its own past choices rather than
the boundary of fraud.

### Why the investigators cannot just sample at random

The obvious repair is to spend some calls on randomly chosen cards, so that
the fast labels become a fair sample. Every such call finds a compromised card
about one time in four hundred, against about fifteen in a hundred for the
model's top cards, and those calls are the bank's only way to stop fraud this
week. So the fast labels stay selected by the model, and the system has
to learn from them as they are.

## How often the model changes: a nightly retrain beside hand-edited rules

A model trained once encodes the stream of the weeks it was trained on, and
both sides of that stream move: fraudsters change methods when they are
caught, and customers change habits with holidays, new shops and new devices.

Feedbacks trickle in all day as investigators finish calls, but a retrain
needs many labels at once and takes time to run. So the model is usually
retrained once a day, at the end of the day, on everything available then:
the day's feedbacks $F_t$, the newly matured delayed samples $D_{t-\delta}$,
and the older data. Tomorrow's scores come from a model that has seen
today's feedbacks and complete labels only up to a week ago. Rules run on a
different clock: investigators add a blocking or scoring rule the day they
spot a pattern, and remove one that raises too many false alarms.

<Remark title="the fraud cycle">
As a process rather than a set of layers, the system is a cycle of four
activities: *detection* (scoring every payment), *investigation* (a person
examines the flagged cards), *confirmation* (the true label is settled, by
the call or by a dispute) and *prevention* (blocking the card, writing a new
rule). The arrow from confirmation back to detection is the feedback loop of
the previous section.
How often it should turn depends on how fast the fraud changes, how good the
current model is, how many confirmed cases arrive per day and how much a
retrain costs.
</Remark>

<Conclusion>
You can now derive each part of the system from a constraint: the deadline
and the cost of refusing a genuine customer put fast, very precise checks
before the answer and the model after it, blocking cards and raising alerts;
a team of about a hundred calls a day makes the model's output a ranking cut
at $k$; labels arrive on two clocks, the fast ones chosen by the model
itself, and feed a nightly retrain beside hand-edited rules.

All of this treated "fraud" as one thing on one kind of payment.

financial crimes have a label at all, who produces it and how late it
arrives, and whether the signal sits in one payment, in one customer's
history or in the connections between accounts.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
A day carries 1,000 fraudulent payments on 300 compromised cards, as in the
example, but the frauds are not spread evenly: 50 cards carry ten frauds each
and the other 250 carry two each. The team calls 100 cards.

(a) A ranking that finds only compromised cards, but cannot tell how many
frauds each one carries, calls 100 of them at random. How many fraudulent
payments do the calls settle on average? What are the most and the fewest a
ranking could settle while calling only compromised cards?

(b) Ranking cards by their single highest payment score, as in the lesson,
prefers neither kind of card. Propose a card score that prefers the heavily
used compromised cards, and say what it risks on genuine cards.

(c) Does the share of compromised cards the team can reach depend on the
spread?

<Solution>
**(a)** A compromised card carries on average $1{,}000/300 \approx 3.3$
frauds, so 100 cards picked at random among them settle about 333. The most:
all 50 heavy cards ($50 \times 10 = 500$) plus 50 light ones
($50 \times 2 = 100$), 600 frauds, 60% of the day's fraud. The fewest: 100
light cards, 200 frauds, 20%. With the same 100 calls, all of them on
compromised cards, the fraud settled varies threefold.

**(b)** Score a card by the number of its payments above some score level, or
by the sum of its payment scores over the day. A card with ten suspicious
payments then outranks a card with one. The risk is that both scores grow
with activity: a genuine card that pays thirty times a day collects many
small scores, and a summed score can lift it above a compromised card with
two clearly fraudulent payments. Counting only payments above a high level
limits that, at the price of missing cards whose frauds each look only
mildly odd. The count also ignores that a card with two frauds may be at the
start of its burst, and calling it now blocks the frauds it has not yet made.

**(c)** No. Whatever the spread, 100 calls reach at most $100/300$, a third,
of the compromised cards. The spread changes how many fraudulent payments
those calls settle, so rankings judged in cards and in payments can disagree
about which is better.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push the team's capacity to its two extremes. (a) Set $k = 0$: nobody is ever
called. (b) Set $k$ to the number of cards that pay in a day, so that every
card is called. For each case say what the model's ranking is used for,
which kinds of label exist and how fast, and whether the selection problem of
the feedbacks survives. Then name the constraint from the lesson that is
untouched in both cases.

<Solution>
**(a) $k = 0$.** There are no alerts, so the ranking has no use in the
investigation step; the score can only drive automatic card blocks. There are
no feedbacks: every label comes through disputes and silence, $\delta$ days
late. The selection problem disappears, because nothing the model does
chooses which payments get labelled, but so does every fast label: the model
always trains on data at least $\delta$ days old.

**(b) Every card called.** The ranking no longer decides who is called; at
most it decides the order of the day's calls. Every card's recent payments
are labelled within the day, so the feedbacks cover the whole stream: labels
are fast and unselected, and the delayed channel adds only the frauds that
cardholders themselves failed to recognise on the phone.

**Untouched in both.** The authorisation deadline. Whatever the team's size,
whatever refuses a payment must finish in milliseconds and be wrong on almost
no genuine payments, so the model still scores after the answer. The
selection problem and the label delay come from $k$ being small relative to
the stream; the deadline does not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "The model scores every payment in under a second, so it can
replace the blocking rules." Use the numbers of the lesson, and name the step
in the system at which the claim fails.

<Solution>
**Timing is not where it fails.** As deployed, the model scores a payment
already accepted, so "under a second" is beside the point. But that placement
is a choice: fed only features ready when the request arrives, a model can be
evaluated within the milliseconds before the answer. Speed neither supports
the claim nor refutes it.

**Precision.** Move the model before the answer and let it refuse. Its most confident hundred cards a day hold about 15 compromised
cards, so blocking their payments would refuse genuine customers on about 85
cards for every 15 frauds stopped. Lowering the bar further makes it worse: a
1% false-alarm rate on 399,000 genuine payments is about 4,000 refused
customers a day.

**The failing step** is the assumption that a fast score is a blocking
decision. A block must be both before the answer and nearly always right;
speed can be engineered, the precision is not there. A model can take over
blocking only in the narrow form of the aside on models inside the deadline:
features ready in advance and a threshold high enough to meet the blocking
bar.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design the measurement that would tell a bank whether its convention
"undisputed after $\delta$ days means genuine" is safe. State which quantity
you would estimate, on which sample of payments, over which horizon, and what
result would count as "safe".

<Solution>
Two kinds of fraud are labelled genuine by the convention, and they need
different measurements: frauds disputed after $\delta$ days, and frauds never
disputed at all.

**Late disputes.** Take a cohort: every payment of a set of past days, at
least thirteen months old, since that is the dispute window under PSD2. For
each, compare the label it had at $\delta$ days with the label it had at
thirteen months. The quantity is the share of frauds known at thirteen months
that were still labelled genuine at $\delta$ days, overall and split by
amount and by the model's score band. Plot it against $\delta$ (7, 14, 30, 60
days): the curve shows how many frauds each choice of $\delta$ mislabels.

**Unnoticed fraud.** No dispute data can find it. The only estimate is an
audit: investigators call the holders of a random sample of
cards whose payments are all labelled genuine, and go through the payments
with them. Because the expected rate is small, the sample must be large: to
see even ten such frauds at a true rate of one in ten thousand payments needs
on the order of $10^5$ audited payments, so the audit is run occasionally,
stratified towards high-score cards, not daily.

**What counts as safe.** The mislabelled share of the training negatives,
late plus unnoticed, must be small compared with the fraud rate itself: if
the labelled fraud is 0.25% of payments and the negatives hide another
0.05%, one fraud in six is labelled the wrong way, which is not safe. Where
the mislabelled frauds sit matters too: if they concentrate in one score band or one kind of fraud, the model is taught
precisely to ignore that kind, even if the overall share is small.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

only feedbacks. (b) With $\delta = 7$ and 1,000 frauds a day, the
logistic-regression ranking (about 15 compromised cards in each day's 100
calls, three frauds each) leaves about 6,700 of the last week's 7,000 frauds
unlabelled at the end of day $t$. Redo the estimate for a perfect ranking that
fills all 100 calls with compromised cards, and say what the comparison tells
you.

<Solution>
**(a)** At day $s$ the system receives $D_{s-\delta}$, the complete labels of
day $s - \delta$. By the end of day $t$ it has received $D_{s-\delta}$ for
every $s \le t$, so complete labels for every day up to $t - \delta$. Days
$t - \delta + 1, \dots, t$ have not yet matured: that is $\delta$ days known
only through their feedbacks $F$ (and any early disputes).

**(b)** A perfect ranking calls 100 compromised cards at about three frauds
each: about 300 labelled frauds a day, 2,100 over the seven feedback-only
days, leaving about 4,900 of the 7,000, 70%, unseen, against about 95% for
the model's ranking. Even a perfect model sees less than a third of the
newest fraud before $\delta$ days have passed. The bound comes from $k$ and $\delta$, not from the model,
which is why a newly appearing kind of fraud reaches the training data mostly
through the delayed samples, a week late.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Simulate the loop. Each day, draw 10,000 rows with two standard-normal
features and a 1% fraud rate; make half of the frauds shifted by $+2.5$ on
the first feature and the other half by $+2.5$ on the second. Start from a
seed training set of one fully labelled day of 1,000 rows and fit a logistic
regression. Then for ten days: score the day's rows, label only the top 100,
add those 100 labelled rows to the training set, and refit. Plot the fraud
share of the training set against the day, next to the stream's 1%, and
record how many frauds of each kind reach the top 100 each day. What do you
see, and what does it mean for training on the fast labels?

<Solution>
A minimal implementation, with numpy only:

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def day(n=10_000, rate=0.01):
    y = (rng.random(n) < rate).astype(int)
    X = rng.normal(size=(n, 2))
    first = rng.random(n) < 0.5
    X[(y == 1) & first, 0] += 2.5
    X[(y == 1) & ~first, 1] += 2.5
    return X, y, first

def fit(X, y, iters=500, lr=0.1):
    Xb = np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), X]
    w = np.zeros(Xb.shape[1])
    for _ in range(iters):
        p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-Xb @ w))
        w -= lr * Xb.T @ (p - y) / len(y)
    return w

TX, TY, _ = day(1_000)
w = fit(TX, TY)
for d in range(1, 11):
    X, y, first = day()
    top = np.argsort(-(np.c_[np.ones(len(X)), X] @ w))[:100]
    TX, TY = np.r_[TX, X[top]], np.r_[TY, y[top]]
    w = fit(TX, TY)
    print(d, TY.mean().round(3), y.mean().round(3),
          (y[top] & first[top]).sum(), (y[top] & ~first[top]).sum())
```

**What you see.** Each day's top 100 holds about 20 to 35 frauds. The training set's fraud share
climbs from about 1% to about 3% after the first day, about 9 to 10% after
five and about 13 to 14% after ten, while the stream stays at 1%. In many
runs the split between the two kinds of fraud also drifts: one kind takes
most of the top 100, and the other almost stops reaching the labels, even
though both are equally common in the stream.

**What it means.** The training set is a record of what the model chose to
show, and it moves further from the stream every day the loop turns. A model trained on it overestimates the
fraud rate and keeps learning the kind of fraud it already finds; <Ref
lesson="alert-feedback-selection-bias">the lesson on feedback selection
bias</Ref> shows why reweighting cannot undo it and trains on the feedbacks
and the delayed samples separately instead.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

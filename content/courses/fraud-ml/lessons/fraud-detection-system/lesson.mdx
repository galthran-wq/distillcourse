A fraud score is produced in under a second by the fourth of five layers, is acted on by investigators who can open about a hundred alerts a day, and is corrected by labels that return days to weeks later for the alerted transactions only.

The classical-ml course treated a classifier as a function fitted on a labelled table and judged on a held-out sample of the same table. In a deployed fraud system none of those three things is given: the table is assembled by the system itself, the judge is a team of people with a fixed day's capacity, and the labels for most rows do not exist yet when the model is retrained. This lesson is the first move of the course: it fixes the system diagram that every later lesson annotates, with the model as one layer among five, alerts as a ranking at a budget, and labels as two streams of different speed and composition.

## Five layers on three clocks

The five layers sit in a fixed order around one moment, the authorization of a payment, and that moment decides how much time each layer has.

### The order of the layers

In the system run by Worldline, a European payment processor, a card payment attempt meets the layers in this order:

1. **Terminal.** Conventional checks on every request: PIN (where the card has a chip), number of attempts, whether the card is active or blocked, available balance, spending limit. The terminal queries the issuing bank's server and must answer in a few milliseconds.
2. **Transaction-blocking rules.** Hand-written *if-then* rules on the fields of the request itself. A request that fires any rule is denied; the card stays active.
3. **Scoring rules.** Hand-written *if-then* rules that assign a score to an authorized transaction, using its history as well as its fields.
4. **Data-driven model.** A classifier that estimates the probability that the authorized transaction is a fraud, and outputs it as the score.
5. **Investigators.** People who review the highest-scored cases, call cardholders, block cards and record a verdict.

Between layers 2 and 3 the transaction is authorized, and something else happens there that the later layers depend on: the transaction is enriched with *aggregated features*, summaries of the card's recent activity such as average spend over the past week, number of transactions today, or the location of the previous purchase. The raw request plus these aggregates form the feature vector $x$ that layers 3 and 4 read.

### Authorization divides the clocks

The layers fall into three timing classes. Terminal and blocking rules run in **real time**: within milliseconds and before authorization, because their output is the authorization decision. Scoring rules and the model run in **near real time**, after authorization, on the enriched feature vector; their purpose in this arrangement is to block the card and prevent the fraudster's next transactions. Investigators work **offline**, on a scale of hours for a case and days to weeks for the labels the system eventually collects.

The time budget of the automatic layers is fixed by the payment, not by the model. Worldline Belgium's online check is subject to what [the APATE paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dss.2015.04.013) calls the six-seconds rule: acceptance checks and the on-line detection step together must decide within six seconds. Baesens and co-authors give eight seconds as the typical decision time for a card and draw the consequence for features: a trend variable that takes too long to compute in real time cannot be used, however predictive it would be.

<Remark title="two placements of the model">
The handbook's system scores authorized transactions, so the model blocks the card for the future and lets the current payment through. APATE describes Worldline's on-line sanity check as running inside the authorization window, where a high probability aborts the transaction itself. Both arrangements exist. This course uses the handbook's, in which the model's output is a case for investigators rather than a decline; <Ref lesson="real-time-scoring-architecture">the capstone module's architecture lesson</Ref> returns to the in-authorization variant and what its latency budget costs.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
A layer's clock fixes which information it may use: anything that needs the card's history or a model evaluation after aggregation is unavailable to the layers that decide authorization in milliseconds.
</KeyFact>

## What each layer may be: rules are edited, the model is retrained

The clocks say when each layer runs; the second property that separates the layers is who can change them and how. The two rule layers are expert-driven, written and removed by investigators. The model is data-driven, and the only way to change it is through labelled data.

### Blocking rules: fast, precise, blind to history

A blocking rule sees only what the request carries. The handbook's illustrative example is *"IF internet transaction AND unsecured website THEN deny the transaction"*: both conditions are fields of the request. Two requirements follow from its position before authorization. It must be **quick to compute**, since it shares the millisecond budget with the terminal. It must be **very precise**, raising very few false alarms, because a false alarm here is a declined purchase: a lost sale for the merchant and an insult to a genuine cardholder at the checkout.

### Scoring rules: known patterns at arbitrary scores

A scoring rule reads the enriched feature vector and attaches a number. The handbook's example uses two aggregates:

| | Blocking rule | Scoring rule |
|---|---|---|
| Example | IF internet transaction AND unsecured website THEN deny | IF previous transaction in a different continent AND less than one hour since it THEN fraud score = 0.95 |
| Reads | fields of the request | feature vector, including the card's history |
| Output | deny or pass | a score; high scores become alerts |
| Runs | before authorization, milliseconds | after authorization, near real time |
| Written by | investigators | investigators |

The scoring rule cannot be moved into the blocking layer: "previous transaction" and "time since it" are aggregates that do not exist until the request has been joined to the card's history. Its score of 0.95 is also not an estimated probability. Investigators set rule scores by judgment, so two experts write different rules with different scores for the same pattern. Scoring rules detect only fraud strategies investigators have already discovered, and only patterns that involve a few features; they are hard to keep complete and current.

### The model: a probability nobody can hand-edit

The data-driven model (the handbook's *DDM*) estimates $P(\text{fraud} \mid x)$ from a set of labelled transactions and returns it as the score. It can combine many components of $x$ nonlinearly, so it can flag patterns no investigator wrote down. The price is that investigators can neither read it as rules nor modify it. When a new attack appears, an investigator can add a scoring rule the same afternoon; the model changes only when it is retrained on labelled examples of the attack. Retraining needs many labels, so although verdicts arrive throughout the day, the model is typically retrained once, at the end of the day.

<KeyFact>
A new attack is answered by a scoring rule within a day and by the model only once the attack's frauds are labelled: through investigator calls on at most about a hundred alerted cards a day, and for every other card only after the days-to-weeks wait for disputes.
</KeyFact>

## Investigators turn the score into a ranking cut at k

The model's score has no effect until a person acts on it, and the number of people is the binding constraint of the whole system.

### A hundred cards against four hundred thousand transactions

The handbook's real-world data, Belgian e-commerce transactions from 2018, carry about **400,000 transactions a day**, of which about 1,000 are fraudulent (one in four hundred), spread over a little more than 300 compromised cards. Investigators review cases by calling cardholders, a slow process, and can check on the order of **a hundred cards a day**, the budget the handbook and Dal Pozzolo et al. use throughout. The ratio is one investigated card per four thousand transactions.

A fixed probability threshold does not respect that ratio. Provost, commenting on [Bolton and Hand's review](https://doi.org/10.1214/ss/1042727940), puts it in one line: on a customer base of a million accounts, a daily false-alarm rate of even 1% yields 10,000 false alarms a day. Whatever threshold the model uses, the investigators can open a hundred cases; the rest are never seen. Investigators also stop trusting the queue when it is mostly false alarms, which is why the stated objective of the model is **precise alerts**, not a correct decision on every row.

### The alert set is a top-k

Dal Pozzolo et al. formalize the alerts as a ranking rather than a threshold. Write $T_t$ for the transactions authorized on day $t$ and $K_{t-1}$ for the classifier trained on everything labelled up to the end of day $t-1$.

<Definition title="alert set">
Let $r(x) \in \{1, \dots, |T_t|\}$ be the rank of transaction $x \in T_t$ by the posterior $P_{K_{t-1}}(+ \mid x)$, highest first, and let $k > 0$ be the number of alerts investigators can check in a day. The **alert set** of day $t$ is
$$
A_t = \{\, x \in T_t : r(x) \le k \,\}.
$$
</Definition>

Four cases fix what the definition covers.

- **The canonical case.** $k = 100$ on a day of 400,000 transactions: the hundred highest-scored transactions are alerted, whatever their scores are.
- **A different unit.** Investigators check cards, and one compromised card produces about three frauds a day. Ranking cards by their highest transaction score and cutting at $k = 100$ cards gives the card-level alert set the course uses from <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">the metrics lesson</Ref> on.
- **The degenerate case.** $k \ge |T_t|$: every transaction is alerted, the ranking carries no information, and every label is known the same day. No real budget reaches this case, which is what makes the rest of the lesson necessary.
- **The near miss.** "Alert every transaction with score above 0.5" violates exactly one clause, the fixed cardinality $|A_t| = k$. On a quiet day it alerts ten transactions and wastes ninety investigator slots; on a day with an attack it alerts five thousand, and which hundred get opened is then decided by someone other than the model.

The threshold has not disappeared; it has become an output. Each day it falls wherever the hundredth-ranked score lands, and it moves with the day's score distribution while $k$ stays fixed.

  alt="Left: a ten-by-ten grid of the top 100 ranked cards with 30 cells filled amber as compromised; an amber vertical line marked k = 100; right: a large box of the remaining cards with scattered amber dots for about 270 compromised cards not inspected today"
  caption="An illustrative day: 30 of the 100 alerted cards are compromised; the other 270 compromised cards are below the cut and get no label today."

<Aside>
**Graded investigation.** Bolton and Hand recommend a graded system rather than a single queue: accounts with very high suspicion scores get immediate, intensive and expensive investigation, accounts with large but less dramatic scores get closer but cheap observation. Provost draws the same line from the other side: automatic intervention only on very strong evidence, mixed human-computer handling for everything else. The handbook notes a modern instance: transactions with very high risk scores can bypass investigators and go straight to the cardholder as an SMS request for confirmation, which widens the feedback bandwidth at some risk to the customer's trust. Each tier is its own budget $k$, and the course's single $k$ is the investigator tier.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
With $k$ fixed, the model's job is to order the day, and the quantity it is judged on is the fraction of fraud among its top $k$; the 399,900 transactions below the cut contribute to no decision anyone makes that day.
</KeyFact>

## Labels arrive by two routes, and silence counts as genuine

The alert set decides more than whom the investigators call: it decides which transactions get a label quickly. The model is retrained from labels, and those labels come by two routes that differ in speed, coverage and fraud content.

### Feedbacks: fast, few, and chosen by the model

An investigator who opens an alert calls the cardholder, assigns "genuine" or "fraudulent", blocks the card if it is compromised, and checks all its recent transactions while on the call. The labels produced this way are the *feedbacks*, and the mechanism is the *alert–feedback interaction*.

<Definition title="feedbacks">
Let $\mathrm{cards}(A_t)$ be the set of cards with at least one transaction in $A_t$. The **feedbacks** of day $t$ are the labelled pairs
$$
F_t = \{\, (x, y) : x \text{ is a recent transaction of a card in } \mathrm{cards}(A_t) \,\}.
$$
</Definition>

Feedbacks are the most recent labelled data the system has, and they are few: a hundred cards' worth of transactions out of four hundred thousand. They are also fraud-rich, because the model put them at the top of its ranking. A fraud on an alerted card enters $F_t$ even if the model scored it low, since investigators label the whole card; a fraud on a card nobody alerted does not enter it at all.

### Delayed samples and verification latency

Every other transaction stays unlabelled until something outside the system acts. A cardholder reads a statement and disputes a payment; the dispute goes through administrative processing; the label arrives. Cardholders check statements on their own schedules, so this takes days to weeks. A transaction nobody disputes is, after enough time, taken to be genuine.

<Definition title="verification latency and delayed samples">
The **verification latency** $\delta$ is the number of days after which a transaction's label is treated as final. Taking $\delta$ constant, at the end of day $t$ the labels of every transaction authorized on day $t - \delta$ become available: disputed ones as fraud, investigator-labelled ones as recorded, and all others as genuine. These are the **delayed samples**
$$
D_{t-\delta} = \{\, (x, y) : x \in T_{t-\delta} \,\}.
$$
</Definition>

Since the alerted transactions of day $t-\delta$ are among that day's transactions, $F_{t-\delta} \subset D_{t-\delta}$. The values of $\delta$ in the sources run from about a week (APATE: off-line checks and customer notifications "can take up to one week"; Dal Pozzolo et al. draw their timeline with $\delta = 7$) to about a month. The course works with **$\delta \approx 7$ days** unless a lesson says otherwise.

  caption="At the end of day t: whole labelled days end at t − 7; the seven most recent days are represented only by their small, fraud-rich feedback slices."

The two streams describe different populations. Delayed samples are complete days with the natural fraud rate of about 0.25%, a week or more old. Feedbacks are current but small, with a fraud rate set by the model's precision, and drawn from the transactions the previous model ranked highest. Dal Pozzolo et al. argue from exactly this difference that a model should be trained on the two separately; <Ref lesson="alert-feedback-selection-bias">the selection-bias lesson</Ref> takes that argument up.

<Example title="alerts, feedbacks and the frauds left waiting">

|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A | 0.91 | fraud |
| 2 | B | 0.84 | genuine |

**Cards.** $\mathrm{cards}(A_t) = \{A, B\}$. Card A is compromised; card B is a genuine cardholder whose purchase looked unusual.

### What the formal model simplifies

<Remark title="simplifications in the end-of-day model">
The top-$k$ definition ranks the whole day at once, and the delayed-sample definition delivers a whole day's labels at exactly $\delta$. Neither is literally true. Real alerts are raised online, while transactions are being processed, without waiting to rank the whole of $T_t$; disputes trickle in, some before $\delta$ days and some after. Dal Pozzolo et al. state both simplifications and keep the model because it retains the three features that matter for learning: a limited number of alerts, labels chosen by the alerts, and a delay before everything else is labelled. <Ref lesson="label-maturation">The label-maturation lesson</Ref> replaces the constant $\delta$ by the distribution of dispute arrival times.
</Remark>

## The loop makes this a different learning problem

The pieces close into a loop: the model ranks today's transactions, its top $k$ become today's fastest labels, those labels retrain tonight's model, which ranks tomorrow's transactions. How often the model is retrained is set by four things: how fast fraud behaviour changes, how good the current model is, how many confirmed cases exist and how fast new ones arrive, and the effort retraining costs.

### The benchmark assumption fails

The usual benchmark treatment of fraud detection assumes something else. Dal Pozzolo et al. open their paper by stating it: most learning algorithms proposed for fraud detection rely on assumptions that hardly hold in a deployed system, namely that the label of every transaction is available immediately and that performance is measured over all transactions. That assumption is false in the system above, and the reason is mechanical: a label for a non-alerted transaction requires a cardholder to read a statement and a dispute to be processed, and nothing the bank does shortens that beyond the investigators' $k$ calls a day.

The replacement model is the one this lesson built: today's labels are $F_t$ only, complete labels reach back to day $t - \delta$, and the judge is the top $k$. The discriminating case is the toy day above: a benchmark that scores the model on all ten transactions with their true classes measures something that no one in the deployed system will learn for a week, and then only partly.

### Three consequences

Three consequences follow, and each has its own lesson.

- **The objective is precision at the budget.** A ranking cut at $k$ is judged by its top, and accuracy or the full ROC curve averages over operating points the budget never reaches. <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">The economics lesson</Ref> computes what the base rate does to an alert queue; <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">the metrics lesson</Ref> builds the metrics that see only the reachable region.
- **The test is next week.** The model is trained on labels up to day $t - \delta$ plus feedbacks, and is used on transactions whose labels do not yet exist. A validation that ignores $\delta$ uses information the deployed model never has; <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential">the temporal-validation lesson</Ref> builds the delay block into the split.
- **The training set is the model's own choice.** Feedbacks are selected by the model they then retrain, and undisputed frauds enter as genuine. <Ref lesson="alert-feedback-selection-bias">The selection-bias lesson</Ref> shows why the standard correction for a biased sample fails here.

A fourth pressure comes from outside the loop: fraudsters learn which patterns get their cards blocked and change them, so the patterns the model learned from last week's labels are partly obsolete by the time it uses them. <Ref lesson="adversarial-classification">The adversary module</Ref> treats that.

<KeyFact>
Every later difficulty in this course is a property of this loop: the base rate and the budget set the metric, $\delta$ sets the validation split, and the alert set selects the training data.
</KeyFact>

## Placing a detection problem on the diagram

The diagram is a reading instrument: for any detection problem it asks four questions, and the answers determine which of the later lessons' difficulties apply and how strongly. The four questions are: which layer sees the event first and on what clock; what the human budget is; where the label comes from; and how late it arrives.

For card payments, the case this lesson has worked through, the answers are these.

| | Card payment (this lesson) |
|---|---|
| First layer and clock | terminal and blocking rules, milliseconds, before authorization; model after authorization, under a second |
| Human budget | about 100 cards a day against about 400,000 transactions |
| Label source | investigator call for alerted cards (feedbacks); cardholder dispute for the rest; silence read as genuine |
| Delay | same day for feedbacks; $\delta$ of a week to a month for everything else |

The same four rows, filled in for an anti-money-laundering monitor and for account takeover at login, are an exercise below; the values change, and so do the layers that matter.

The lesson has established the five-layer system and its three clocks, the division between rules that investigators edit and a model that only labels can change, the alert set as a top-$k$ ranking at a budget of about a hundred cards a day, and the two label streams, fast selected feedbacks and slow complete delayed samples, separated by a verification latency in which silence counts as genuine. <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology">The next lesson</Ref> fills the table's columns with the major types of financial crime, each of which changes the unit of detection, the label source and the delay.

<Exercise>
Set the verification latency to zero, $\delta = 0$. State what changes in the loop: the relation between $F_t$ and $D_{t-\delta}$, the composition of the training data, and the meaning of "silence is genuine". Then explain why a deployed card system cannot operate at $\delta = 0$, naming the step that sets the delay.

<Solution>
**What changes.** At $\delta = 0$ the delayed samples of day $t$ are available at the end of day $t$: $D_t$ labels every transaction of the day, and $F_t \subset D_t$ adds nothing that $D_t$ does not already contain. The training data become complete days with the natural fraud rate, so the selection effect of the alert set disappears: the model no longer chooses which transactions it learns from. Validation needs no gap between training and test, since nothing is unknown at training time.

**What breaks.** "Silence is genuine" is an inference from elapsed time: a fraud nobody disputed within $\delta$ days is presumed not to exist. At $\delta = 0$ no time has elapsed, so silence carries no information, and every unalerted fraud would be labelled genuine immediately. The rule only works with $\delta$ large enough that most frauds have been disputed.

**Why it is impossible.** A label for a non-alerted transaction comes from the cardholder reading a statement and disputing a payment, followed by administrative processing. That step is outside the bank's control and runs on the cardholder's schedule. The only labels the bank can produce on its own schedule are investigator calls, and those are capped at $k$ a day; reaching $\delta = 0$ by investigation would require $k = |T_t|$, four thousand times the handbook's budget. The degenerate case of the alert-set definition and the degenerate case of the latency coincide.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "A blocking rule with perfect precision should be moved into the model layer, because the model is the more accurate component and should own every fraud decision." Name the property of the model layer that makes the move a loss, and give two further reasons.

<Solution>
**The property that fails: the model's clock.** In the handbook's system the model runs after authorization on the enriched feature vector. A blocking rule decides before authorization, in milliseconds, from the request fields. Moving the rule into the model means the transaction is authorized first and scored afterwards: the fraud the rule used to stop now goes through, and the best the model can do is put the card in the alert queue to stop later transactions.

**Second: the model cannot be edited.** A rule can be written and deployed by an investigator the day an attack is identified, and removed when it starts misfiring. The model learns a pattern only from labelled examples of it, which arrive as feedbacks for alerted cards and otherwise after $\delta$ days. Encoding a known pattern in the model means waiting for labels of the very fraud the rule already recognises.

**Third: the output types differ.** A blocking rule's output is a decision; the model's output is a score whose effect is a place in a ranking cut at $k$. A perfectly precise pattern placed in the model competes for the same hundred investigator slots as every uncertain case, and consumes them, where the rule would have disposed of it automatically at no investigator cost.

The claim fails on its premise too: accuracy is not the relevant comparison between layers, because each layer answers a different question on a different clock.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Assume investigators label alerted cards correctly, that only true frauds are ever disputed, and that each fraud on a non-alerted card is disputed within $\delta$ days independently with probability $\rho$. Show that the transactions of day $t$ whose training label is wrong at day $t + \delta$ are exactly the undisputed frauds on non-alerted cards, that every such error runs in one direction, and bound the expected number of wrong labels. Evaluate the bound for the handbook's day with $\rho = 0.9$.

<Solution>
**Which labels are wrong.** A transaction $x$ of day $t$ receives one of three labels by day $t + \delta$:

1. its card is in $\mathrm{cards}(A_t)$: the investigator's label, correct by assumption;
2. its card is not alerted and it is disputed: labelled fraud, correct because only frauds are disputed;
3. its card is not alerted and it is not disputed: labelled genuine.

Case 3 is correct for genuine transactions and wrong for frauds. So the wrong labels are exactly $W_t = \{x \in T_t : y = +,\ \mathrm{card}(x) \notin \mathrm{cards}(A_t),\ x \text{ undisputed}\}$, and every error turns a fraud into a genuine: the label noise is one-sided.

**Expected size.** Let $P_t$ be the frauds of day $t$ and $P_t^{\bar A}$ those on non-alerted cards. Each is undisputed with probability $1 - \rho$, so
$$
\mathbb{E}\,|W_t| = (1 - \rho)\,|P_t^{\bar A}| \le (1 - \rho)\,|P_t|.
$$

**Handbook day.** With $|P_t| \approx 1{,}000$ the bound is $0.1 \times 1{,}000 = 100$ frauds a day entering training as genuine. Using the first exercise's perfect-precision alert set, $|P_t^{\bar A}| \approx 670$ and the expected count is about 67. The better the alert set, the fewer frauds are exposed to the dispute lottery, so the model's precision reduces its own label noise; the noise that remains sits on exactly the frauds the model failed to rank, so the errors in the training labels are concentrated where the model is already weakest.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

```text
for each day t:
    # near real time, during the day
    for x in T_t:
        s[x] = K_prev.predict_proba(x)

    # end of day: investigators
    A_t   = top_k(T_t, by=s, k=k)                 # rank and cut
    C_t   = cards(A_t)
    F[t]  = {(x, investigator_label(x)) : card(x) in C_t}   # <- depends on K_prev

    # labels that mature today
    for x in T_{t-delta}:
        y[x] = fraud     if x in F[t-delta] and labelled fraud
             = fraud     if disputed(x) by day t
             = genuine   otherwise              # silence read as genuine
    D[t-delta] = {(x, y[x]) : x in T_{t-delta}}

    # retrain
    train  = F[t] ∪ ... ∪ F[t-Q+1]  ∪  D[t-delta] ∪ ... ∪ D[t-delta-M+1]
    K_prev = fit(train)
```

The marked line is where the loop closes: $F_t$ is a function of $K_{t-1}$, and $K_t$ is fitted on $F_t$. Two design decisions are visible in the code and absent from a benchmark: the gap between the newest delayed day ($t - \delta$) and today, which feedbacks alone cover, and the choice to pool $F$ and $D$ in one training set. Dal Pozzolo et al. (Sec. V) instead fit separate classifiers on feedbacks and on delayed samples and average their posteriors. The capstone lab of the course runs this cycle on the handbook's simulator.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A day has 8,300 transactions, 60 of them frauds, and investigators check $k = 100$. Compute the best possible precision and recall in the alert set. Then compute the range of false-positive rates that a 100-transaction alert set can produce, and state what fraction of the ROC curve's horizontal axis lies outside that range.

<Solution>
**Best precision and recall.** At most 60 of the 100 alerts can be frauds, so precision is at most $60/100 = 0.60$, reached when every fraud is in the top 100. That same ranking gives recall $60/60 = 1$. Precision cannot reach 1 on this day whatever the model, because there are fewer frauds than slots.

**Reachable false-positive rates.** There are $8{,}300 - 60 = 8{,}240$ genuine transactions. An alert set of 100 contains between 40 false positives (all 60 frauds inside) and 100 (no fraud inside), so
$$
\frac{40}{8{,}240} \approx 0.005 \;\le\; \mathrm{FPR} \;\le\; \frac{100}{8{,}240} \approx 0.012.
$$

**The unreachable part.** Every ROC point with $\mathrm{FPR} > 0.012$ needs more than 100 alerts, so about 99% of the FPR axis describes operating points the investigators can never use. ROC-AUC integrates over all of it. This is the argument with which <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics">the metrics lesson</Ref> opens, and the reason it replaces ROC-AUC by average precision and card precision at $k$, which only look at the reachable top of the ranking.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Fill the four rows of the closing table for two further detection problems, and state for each which layer and which clock differ from the card case.

(a) **Anti-money-laundering transaction monitoring** at a bank: outgoing transfers are screened against sanctions lists before execution; a monitoring system then scores executed transfers and account activity in daily batches; analysts review alerts and decide whether to file a suspicious-activity report with the national financial intelligence unit (FIU), the authority that receives such reports.

(b) **Account takeover at login**: a fraudster logs in to a customer's online banking with stolen credentials and then moves money out.

<Solution>
**(a) AML monitoring.**

- *First layer and clock:* sanctions screening before execution plays the blocking-rule role, in real time. The monitoring model runs in daily batches, after the money has moved, so its clock is a day rather than a second.
- *Budget:* the analysts' daily capacity for alerts, the same top-$k$ structure.
- *Label source:* the analyst's decision to file a report. There is no cardholder to dispute anything: the customer is the suspect, not the victim, so the dispute route of the card case does not exist. Confirmation that a report concerned actual laundering would have to come from the FIU or a later prosecution and rarely reaches the bank.
- *Delay:* the analyst's label arrives in days; the true label arrives months later, or never. Silence cannot be read as innocence, because nobody is positioned to complain.

What differs: layer 4 moves from near real time to batch, and the label stream loses its delayed route, so nearly all labels are the analysts' and therefore selected by the alerts. The <Ref lesson="eu-aml-single-rulebook">EU AML lesson</Ref> reads the legal obligations behind this loop.

**(b) Account takeover.**

- *First layer and clock:* authentication at login (password, device recognition, a second factor) takes the terminal's role, in real time, before the session is granted; scoring the session must also happen in real time, because the money leaves within minutes of the login.
- *Budget:* investigators or automated step-up challenges for suspicious sessions; a step-up challenge is the graded-investigation tier sent to the customer.
- *Label source:* the customer reporting that they did not log in, or an investigator call on an alerted session.
- *Delay:* hours to days, typically shorter than a card dispute, since the customer notices a drained account sooner than a line on a statement.

What differs: the decisive model moves before the harmful action, into the real-time bracket, and the unit of detection is the session rather than the transaction. <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology">The typology lesson</Ref> places account takeover among the fraud types and reads its traits.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

A boosted tree ensemble with the right depth, learning rate and number of trees beats a single tree, but the right values depend on the data and the metric, and every candidate costs minutes to hours of compute. How do you choose the settings for next week's data when the only labels you have are a week old, and how much does the choice buy over a well-tuned single tree?

You already have a procedure for one setting. <Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential" id="what-prequential-selection-picks">The
delayed-labels lesson</Ref> chose a decision tree's depth: eleven candidate
depths, each validated on four prequential folds that copy the deployment's
training, delay and validation blocks, the depth with the best mean card
precision at 100 kept, and a tree of that depth refitted on the latest
labelled week. A tree
fits in under a second, so the eleven candidates cost nothing worth counting.

The ensembles that topped <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="six-ensembles-two-streams">the imbalance lesson's tables</Ref> were each
reported "at the setting its prequential folds picked", with no word on what
that search tried or what it cost. A random forest has two settings that
matter and XGBoost three. A few values of each makes a dozen to twenty
candidates, each fitted on four folds, each fit up to forty times slower than
a single tree's. Three things make the choice hard. The folds validate on
weeks that are already a delay old, so their best candidate need not be next
week's. Candidates often differ by less than the folds' own spread, so the
search may be choosing among ties. And at the end stands the question <Ref
lesson="scorecards-woe">the scorecard lesson</Ref> left open: whether the
ensemble finds enough extra fraud to justify giving up a model a reviewer can
read.

## What a search buys: default, best-estimated and optimal settings

A search is a procedure, and a procedure is judged like a model: by what it
delivers on weeks it has not seen. That needs a way to score the search
itself, separately from the model it returns.

### Three test numbers for one model class

Every experiment below has the same shape. A model class (decision tree,
logistic regression, random forest, XGBoost) comes with a list of candidate
settings. Each candidate is validated on the four prequential folds, and the
one with the best mean is the winner: the setting a bank would deploy on the
morning of day 129.

The check comes from the four forward test weeks of the delayed-labels
lesson, starting on day 129, which exist only because the simulator's file
runs past that day. For this backtest every candidate, not only the winner,
is fitted a second time on the four training blocks that end a delay before
each test week, and each fit is scored on its own week. The winner's mean
over the four weeks says what the search delivered; the other candidates'
means say what it could have delivered had it chosen differently.

<Definition title="default, best-estimated and optimal settings">
Let $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_M$ be the candidate settings of a model class
and $A$ a metric. Write $V(\theta_m)$ for candidate $m$'s mean over the
validation folds and $T(\theta_m)$ for its mean over the test weeks. Three
numbers describe the class:

- the **default** score $T(\theta_0)$, where $\theta_0$ is the library's
  setting, used without any search;
- the **best-estimated** score $T(\hat\theta)$, where
  $\hat\theta = \arg\max_m V(\theta_m)$ is the candidate the folds pick;
- the **optimal** score $T(\theta^\star) = \max_m T(\theta_m)$, the best test
  score any candidate reached, known only in hindsight.

The *selection gain* is $T(\hat\theta) - T(\theta_0)$. The *cost of
selecting* is $T(\theta^\star) - T(\hat\theta) \ge 0$.
</Definition>

The names are easy to swap. "Best-estimated" is the candidate anyone could
have chosen on the morning of day 129. "Optimal" is not a choice at all: it is
found by looking at weeks that had not happened, and it flatters the grid,
since the largest of $M$ noisy test numbers is optimistic by construction, as
<Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="neither-the-training-week-nor-the-test-week-can-choose">the delayed-labels
lesson</Ref> warned for picking a depth on the test week. A better search can
only close the gap between the two; no search can beat the optimal row of its
own grid.

can separate the new candidates from the old pick.

## Two ensembles, more settings: what the search finds on the simulator

The tree's search paid because its default is the worst candidate in its own
grid. Whether a search pays for the ensembles depends on where their defaults
sit, and they have more settings to search. All runs below use the same
fifteen inputs as the tree (<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="five-models-scored-on-a-test-week">the five baseline models'
inputs</Ref>), the same folds and the same four test weeks.

### Random forest: depth stops mattering once trees are averaged

A random forest averages many trees, each grown on a bootstrap sample of the
training rows and choosing each split among a random subset of the inputs
(<Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="balanced-ensembles-the-gain-is-not-the-balance">recalled in the imbalance
lesson</Ref>). Its two main settings are the maximum depth of each tree and the
number of trees. The grid tried depths 5, 10, 20 and 50 with 25, 50 and 100
trees: twelve candidates. The library default is 100 trees grown until pure.

<Figure
  src="./figures/rf-depth.svg"
  alt="Line chart of test average precision against maximum depth 5, 10, 20 and 50 on the simulator. A black line for a single decision tree falls from 0.600 at depth 5 to 0.505, 0.439 and 0.334 at depth 50. An amber line for a forest of 100 trees rises from 0.627 at depth 5 to 0.665 and 0.678 at depth 20, then 0.672 at depth 50"
  caption="Depth that ruins a single tree costs a forest almost nothing: averaging removes most of what each deep tree memorised."
/>

A single tree loses almost half its average precision between depth 5 and
depth 50. A forest of 100 trees at depth 50 loses 0.006 from its best. A
fully grown tree fits its own training rows exactly, including the payments
that are fraud or genuine for reasons the inputs do not capture, and those
idiosyncrasies are different in each bootstrap sample. Averaging a hundred
such trees keeps what most of them agree on and cancels what each one
memorised alone. The forest at depth 5 is the weakest of the four: averaging
cannot add detail that every one of its trees is too coarse to hold.

The number of trees behaves differently. At depth 20, going from 25 to 50 to
100 trees raises test average precision from 0.660 to 0.673 to 0.678, while
the time to fit one forest on one fold grows from 3.2 to 6.0 to 11.5 seconds:
each doubling costs twice as much and buys less than the one before. The
twelve candidates took 587 seconds on one processor core, validation and
backtest fits together.

The folds pick 100 trees at depth 20 by all three metrics. On the test weeks
that scores ROC-AUC 0.870, average precision 0.678 and $CP@100$ 0.299; the
default scores 0.870, 0.672 and 0.299. The search gained 0.006 of average
precision and nothing else, and the optimal candidates (depth 10 for ROC-AUC
and card precision) sit 0.004 to 0.005 above it, a third of the test weeks'
standard deviation of about 0.013. The library default was already on the
plateau.

### XGBoost: three settings, and the one that only shrinks

XGBoost fits trees in sequence, each one to the errors the sum of the previous
trees still makes; <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="boosting-without-rebalancing-near-best-not-the-pick">the imbalance
lesson</Ref> read those errors as each payment's label minus its predicted
probability. Its *learning rate* η multiplies every new tree's output before
it is added to the sum. It is not a step size on weights, as in gradient
descent for a network: it shrinks each correction, so that a smaller η needs
more trees to travel the same distance, and the number of trees times η is a
rough measure of how far the model has moved from the base rate.

The grid crossed depths 3, 6 and 9, 25, 50 and 100 trees, and η of 0.1 and
0.3: eighteen candidates, written trees/rate/depth below. The library default
is 100/0.3/6. The search and its backtest took 1,251 seconds on one core.

| XGBoost on the simulator | ROC-AUC | average precision | $CP@100$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| default 100/0.3/6 | 0.859 | 0.659 | 0.285 |
| folds' pick | 100/0.3/3 | 50/0.3/3 | 25/0.3/3 |
| best-estimated | 0.869 | 0.687 | 0.303 |
| optimal (and where) | 0.872 (50/0.3/3) | 0.694 (100/0.1/3) | 0.304 (100/0.1/3) |

The search gained 0.010 of ROC-AUC, 0.028 of average precision and 0.018 of
card precision, almost two compromised cards a day, and its cost is 0.001 to
0.007.

Every pick has depth 3, shallower than the default's 6, and the grid shows
when depth hurts. At η = 0.3, depth-6 ensembles lose test average precision
as trees are added, 0.682 at 25 trees, 0.674 at 50, 0.659 at 100, and depth 9
behaves the same way; depth-3 ensembles stay between 0.679 and 0.687. The
first rounds fit the patterns the inputs can describe. Once those are fitted,
each further round fits what is left: training payments whose label the
inputs do not explain. A depth-6 tree has up to 64 leaves and can cut out a
small group of such payments and correct it; a depth-3 tree has eight, too
few to isolate groups that small, so its extra rounds do little harm. The
default, deep trees and many large steps at once, is the combination that
memorises. The picks disagree on the number of trees, 100, 50 and 25, and the
card-precision pick is the smallest ensemble in the grid.

**Within a class**, the gain is large only where the default is a memoriser:

## Paying less for the search

The ensembles' searches gained little, yet they took ten to twenty minutes
each on three months of simulated payments, and a real stream has many times more. A
search worth running has to cost less.

### What a grid costs

The bill of a search is the sum, over candidates, of the time to fit one
model, times the number of folds. XGBoost's eighteen candidates take from
1.8 seconds per fit (25 trees, η = 0.3, depth 3) to 18.6 seconds (100 trees,
η = 0.1, depth 9), about 140 seconds for one fit of each; four folds make 560
seconds. That is the validation pass, the only one a bank deploying the
procedure runs. The backtest of the first section adds a second pass of the
same size: every candidate fitted again on the four training blocks that end
a delay before each test week, another 560 seconds or so. The two passes and
the scoring of every fit on its week make the 1,251 seconds. The second pass
is how this lesson checks the search, not part of the search.

The grid's size is the product of its levels per setting. Adding a fourth
setting with four values, say the fraud weight `scale_pos_weight` that the
imbalance lesson tuned, multiplies the bill by four. Every new setting
multiplies; that is why grids stop at two or three settings with two or three
values each.

### What the folds can tell apart

Before cutting the bill, look at what the eighteen fits bought: how far apart
the folds put the candidates they had to choose between.

On card precision the eighteen validation means span 0.278 to 0.289, a range
of 0.011, while each candidate's fold standard deviation is about 0.015. By
<Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="prequential-folds-a-spread-across-weeks">the delayed-labels rule</Ref>,
candidates closer than about one fold standard deviation are not separated: on
this metric the search is choosing among eighteen ties, and on the test weeks
their card precision spans 0.285 to 0.304, with no way to have known which
would land where.

On average precision the folds do separate something. Fourteen candidates sit
within one standard deviation of the best. The four outside are the three
with 25 trees at η = 0.1, whose trees times η, 2.5, is the smallest in the
grid, and 50 trees at η = 0.1 with depth 3, where the product is 5 and each
tree, with eight leaves, corrects the least; with depth 6 or 9 the same 50
trees are inside the band. They have not moved far enough from the base
rate. The search's real work was to stay out of that corner, not to find a
peak inside the band.

### Random search on the same grid

The obvious saving is to fit fewer candidates. *Random search* draws $n$
combinations of settings at random and validates only those; scikit-learn's
`RandomizedSearchCV` accepts the same prequential splits as the grid search.
Drawing 10 of the 18 XGBoost candidates gave the same picks for average
precision and card precision, 50/0.3/3 and 25/0.3/3, and for ROC-AUC picked
50/0.3/3, which scores 0.872 on the test weeks, the grid's optimum. It took
690 seconds against 1,251.

Read the two numbers before crediting the method. The time ratio,
$690/1{,}251 = 0.55$, is the candidate ratio, $10/18 = 0.56$: the whole saving
is that fewer candidates were fitted. And the scores could hardly have come
out differently. With only four of the eighteen candidates outside the
average-precision band, any ten of them contain at least six inside it, and
on card precision every draw is inside. On this grid, random search saved
time because the folds could not tell the candidates apart anyway.

### Why random search beats a grid when settings are ranges

The case for random search lies elsewhere. Above, it drew from the grid's own
points, so it tried the same three depths as the grid. When a setting is a
range instead of a list (η anywhere between 0.01 and 0.3, depth from 2 to 10,
trees from 25 to 500), the two designs spend their fits differently.

A grid of $n$ points over $k$ settings tries $n^{1/k}$ distinct values of
each: 27 points over three settings give three values of each. $n$ random
draws from continuous ranges try $n$ distinct values of each setting. When
one setting matters and the others barely do, as the average-precision band
showed (what mattered there was how far the model had moved, trees times η,
far more than depth), the grid repeats each value of the important setting
$n^{1-1/k}$ times with different values of the unimportant ones, and learns
nothing new from the repeats.

<Figure
  src="./figures/grid-vs-random.svg"
  alt="Two square panels of nine trials each over an important parameter on the horizontal axis and an unimportant one on the vertical axis. Above each square an amber curve shows the score against the important parameter, with a narrow peak at about 0.62 of its range; a flat blue line beside each square marks the unimportant parameter. Left, grid: a 3 by 3 lattice whose ticks on the important axis fall at three values, none on the peak. Right, random: nine scattered points whose ticks fall at nine different values, one of them on the peak"
  caption="Nine grid trials try the important parameter three times over; nine random trials try it nine times."
/>

How many draws are enough follows from what a search has to achieve: land
inside the band of candidates the folds cannot separate from the best. Suppose
that band covers a fraction $q$ of the space being searched. Each independent
draw misses it with probability $1-q$, so

$$
P(\text{at least one of } n \text{ draws lands in the band}) = 1 - (1 - q)^n .
$$

For a band covering 5% of the space, $n = 59$ draws give $1 - 0.95^{59}
\approx 0.95$, whatever the number of settings. A grid lands in the band only
if its levels straddle the band in every setting at once, and the narrower
the band along the important setting, the more levels that takes.

<Exercise>
(a) How many random draws give a 95% chance of landing in a band that covers
10% of the space? (b) How many give a 99% chance for a band of 5%? (c) The
XGBoost grid's average-precision band held 14 of 18 candidates. If the
continuous space behaved the same way, about what $q$ would that be, and how
many draws would a 95% guarantee take?

<Solution>
Solve $1 - (1-q)^n \ge P$ for $n$: $n \ge \ln(1-P)/\ln(1-q)$.

**(a)** $\ln 0.05 / \ln 0.9 = -2.996 / -0.105 = 28.4$, so 29 draws.

**(b)** $\ln 0.01 / \ln 0.95 = -4.605 / -0.0513 = 89.8$, so 90 draws.

**(c)** $q \approx 14/18 = 0.78$. Then $n \ge \ln 0.05/\ln 0.22 = -2.996 /
-1.514 = 1.98$: two draws. A band that wide is the flat surface of the
previous subsection: almost any setting outside the under-trained corner is as
good as the folds can tell, and the search barely needs to search.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyFact>
A search only has to land inside the band of candidates its folds cannot
separate from the best. $n$ random draws reach a band covering a fraction $q$
of the space with probability $1-(1-q)^n$, independent of how many settings
there are; a grid reaches it only if it has a level inside the band along
every setting.
</KeyFact>

## On real card data: the tuned tree against the ensembles

The simulator's frauds are nearly separable (<Ref
lesson="ulb-handbook-benchmark"
id="the-simulator-half-the-fraud-in-two-leaves">two leaves of a shallow tree
hold half of them</Ref>), and on it the ensembles' lead
over the tree was clearest in average precision. The Belgian e-commerce
stream, real payments with real fraud, is where the trade between compute and
card precision has to be settled.

### The four searches on the real stream

The same four grids ran on that stream with the same geometry: four
prequential folds of one training week, one delay week and one validation
week, and four forward test weeks, as in <Ref
lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited" id="the-real-stream">the imbalance
lesson's real-stream rows</Ref>, whose unrebalanced tree, forest and XGBoost
are the best-estimated models of these searches.

| real stream | folds' pick (AUC / AP / CP) | ROC-AUC | average precision | $CP@100$ | optimal $CP@100$ | grid time |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| decision tree | depth 7 / 6 / 6 | 0.869 ± 0.03 | 0.068 ± 0.01 | 0.232 ± 0.04 | 0.232 | 1,617 s |
| logistic regression | regularisation 0.1 for all three | 0.816 ± 0.02 | 0.054 ± 0.01 | 0.211 ± 0.03 | 0.212 | 695 s |
| random forest | 25/10 / 100/10 / 100/10 | 0.912 ± 0.02 | 0.080 ± 0.03 | 0.227 ± 0.05 | 0.227 | 3,616 s |
| XGBoost | 100/0.1/6 for all three | 0.928 ± 0.01 | 0.095 ± 0.03 | 0.235 ± 0.07 | 0.249 (50/0.1/6) | 8,789 s |

The folds pick depths 6 and 7 for the tree, and the best test depths are 6
for average precision and card precision and 8 for ROC-AUC, against 4 and 5
on the simulator: on real payments a tree can take more splits before it
starts to memorise. The
costs of selecting are again small. The one visible gap is XGBoost's card
precision: 50 trees would have scored 0.249 instead of 0.235.

### Where the classes differ, and where they do not

By ROC-AUC, XGBoost leads the tree by 0.06, beyond both standard deviations.
By average precision it leads 0.095 to 0.068, 40% more, a gap of about one
weekly standard deviation of XGBoost's own score. By card precision at 100
the tree finds 23.2 compromised cards a day and XGBoost 23.5: a difference
of 0.3 cards a day, against weekly standard deviations of 0.04 and 0.07, four
to seven cards a day. The forest, at 0.227, is in the same tie.

The two verdicts are compatible because the metrics read different parts of
the ranking. A day on this stream has about 200 to 350 compromised cards not
yet found (<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="on-real-card-data">the
alert-budget lesson's real-data numbers</Ref>), and card precision at 100
depends only on which cards fill each day's top hundred places. ROC-AUC counts
every fraud–genuine pair, and the strip of false-positive rates a hundred
alerts can reach holds at most 0.001 of its area (<Ref
lesson="ulb-handbook-benchmark"
id="xgboost-a-lead-where-the-team-never-works">located in the benchmark
lesson</Ref>), so XGBoost's 0.06 lead lies almost entirely below the hundredth
card. Average precision counts every fraud at the precision where it is
reached, so it too can rise through frauds ranked far below the hundred. At
the very top, the cards a depth-6 tree already isolates, compromised
terminals and sudden bursts of payments on one card like those of <Ref
lesson="ulb-handbook-benchmark" id="a-compromised-terminal-and-a-burst">the
benchmark's real depth-2 tree</Ref>, are the cards any of these models put
first. That last step is an explanation consistent with the numbers; nobody
outside the processor can check it on the confidential data.

### The fifty trees the folds did not find

The 50-tree XGBoost scores 0.249 on card precision, 1.4 cards a day above the
100-tree model the folds chose. It looks like a cost of selecting, but the
two test numbers carry standard deviations of 0.04 and 0.07, and 0.249 is the
best of eighteen noisy test numbers. The gap is inside the noise on the test
weeks themselves.

The folds' own class comparison makes the same point from the other side. On
validation, XGBoost's card precision was 0.261 against the tree's 0.214, 4.7
cards a day; on the test weeks the two tie. Choosing between classes is a
selection like choosing a depth, and it obeys the same rule: a gap under one
fold standard deviation separates nothing, and the real stream's folds have
standard deviations of 0.02 to 0.04 in card precision.

The grid times are wall-clock seconds on a 40-core server, where the
forest's and XGBoost's fits ran in parallel across the cores and the tree's
ran on one. In wall-clock time XGBoost's search took $8{,}789/1{,}617 = 5.4$
times the tree's. Its processor time is that ratio times the number of cores
its fits kept busy on average, which the record does not give: 5.4 times the
tree's if the parallel run bought nothing, up to $5.4 \times 40 \approx 220$
times if all forty cores were busy throughout.

The simulator gives the ratio on equal hardware, because there every fit
ran on a single core. One fit of each of the tree's eleven candidate depths took 7.9 seconds
in all, 0.4 to 1.4 seconds per depth; one fit of each of XGBoost's eighteen
candidates took 140 seconds. On one core each, XGBoost's search costs about
18 times the tree's, an order of magnitude. The real stream's own ratio was
not measured; its range, 5.4 to 220, contains the simulator's 18.

The search is also not a one-off. The bank retrains on a nightly cadence (<Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system" id="how-often-the-model-changes-a-nightly-retrain-beside-hand-edited-rules">the system
lesson</Ref>), refitting the chosen setting every time and re-running the
search whenever the stream has moved, so the gap in compute is paid again and
again.

<KeyFact>
On real card data, a tuned decision tree of depth 6 finds as many
compromised cards in the daily top hundred as tuned XGBoost, 0.232 against
0.235 inside weekly spreads of 0.04 to 0.07, for a fraction of the compute:
a fifth of XGBoost's wall-clock time with one core against forty, and an
eighteenth of its processor time when both run on one core, as measured on
the simulator. XGBoost's lead is in ROC-AUC and average precision, which read
the ranking below the hundredth card.
</KeyFact>

The logistic-regression rows answer the question <Ref lesson="scorecards-woe"
id="the-card-and-the-regression-on-one-test-week">the scorecard lesson</Ref>
left open: does a boosted tree find enough extra fraud to justify giving up a
card a reviewer can read? The card tied the plain regression on card
precision at 100, so the regression's rows stand in for it. On the simulator
tuned XGBoost finds 0.303 against the regression's 0.297, 0.6 cards a day; on
the real stream 0.235 against 0.211, 2.4 cards a day, inside weekly standard
deviations of 0.03 and 0.07, three and seven cards. At a budget of a hundred
cards, neither stream shows the ensemble buying a measurable number of
cards. What it does buy on the real stream is the ranking below the top
hundred: ROC-AUC 0.928 against 0.816, average precision 0.095 against
0.054.

Which to ship is the question the three metrics were built to separate
(<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="three-numbers-for-three-questions">three numbers for three
questions</Ref>). If the team
works a fixed hundred cards a day, the tree does that job at a fraction of the
cost, and its at most 64 leaves can be read rule by rule, though not added up line by
line like the scorecard's points. If the bank needs the ranking below the top
hundred, because the team is growing, or because a queue ordered by expected
loss (<Ref lesson="example-dependent-cost">example-dependent cost</Ref>) has
to rank payments by probability times amount across the whole range, the
ensemble's lead in average precision is what it pays for.

## ★ Boosting on labels that may be wrong

Every comparison above trusted the labels. On a card stream a fraud nobody
reported within the delay is labelled genuine, a choice <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="silence-read-as-genuine-is-a-decision-not-a-fact">the system lesson</Ref>
made explicit, so some training rows say "genuine" about payments that were
fraud.

The two ensembles treat such a row differently. Boosting fits each new tree to
the current errors, label minus predicted probability. A mislabelled fraud that the model scores high keeps a large error round
after round, and each round pushes its score down, and with it the scores of
the real frauds that resemble it.
A forest's trees never look at each other's errors: the mislabelled row pulls
on each tree that draws it, and the average dilutes the pull. The number of
trees and the learning rate set how far boosting goes in chasing rows it
cannot fit, which is one more reason to choose them on folds instead of
taking the defaults. No experiment here measures the effect; it is a reason
to watch for it, not a result.

<Conclusion>
You can now run a model selection for a class with several settings and
account for it in three test numbers: what the default scores, what the
folds' pick scores, and what the best candidate would have scored in
hindsight; you can read the gain and the cost of selecting against the folds'
spread, price a search as fits times folds, cut it with random search and
size the number of draws from the width of the band the folds cannot
separate. On the simulator, tuning matters most for the single tree, little
for the ensembles, and the metric decides which class wins. On real card
data a tuned depth-6 tree ties tuned XGBoost on card precision at 100 for a
fraction of the compute, a fifth of the wall-clock time on one core against
forty and an eighteenth of the single-core time the simulator measured, while
XGBoost leads in ROC-AUC and average precision. Even logistic regression, the
scorecard's model, trails XGBoost at the hundred-card budget by less than the
weekly noise.

The tuned XGBoost on the simulator, 0.869, 0.687 and 0.303, is the bar the
next family has to clear. <Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors">Feed-forward
networks on transaction features</Ref> asks what it takes to train a network
on the same fifteen inputs to that level, and what it gives that trees do not.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Fix XGBoost's depth at 3 and its learning rate at 0.3 and vary only the
number of trees, whose library default is 100. On the simulator:

| trees | validation AUC | test AUC | validation AP | test AP | validation $CP@100$ | test $CP@100$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | 0.877 | 0.871 | 0.698 | 0.679 | 0.289 | 0.303 |
| 50 | 0.883 | 0.872 | 0.708 | 0.687 | 0.288 | 0.302 |
| 100 | 0.887 | 0.869 | 0.706 | 0.685 | 0.286 | 0.299 |

For each metric, write the default, best-estimated and optimal scores, the
gain and the cost, and judge them against fold and test standard deviations
of about 0.01 to 0.02.

<Solution>
**ROC-AUC.** The folds pick 100 trees (0.887), the default. Default 0.869,
best-estimated 0.869, optimal 0.872 at 50 trees. Gain 0, cost 0.003.

**Average precision.** The folds pick 50 trees (0.708). Default 0.685,
best-estimated 0.687, optimal 0.687 at 50 trees. Gain 0.002, cost 0.

**$CP@100$.** The folds pick 25 trees (0.289). Default 0.299, best-estimated
0.303, optimal 0.303 at 25 trees. Gain 0.004, cost 0.

The three metrics pick three different numbers of trees, and none of it
matters: every gain and cost is a fraction of one standard deviation. On
average precision and card precision the three validation means lie closer
together than one fold spread; on ROC-AUC the folds rank 100 trees above 25
by 0.010, about one spread, and the test weeks put them in the opposite
order. The transferable move: before reading a pick as a finding, check
whether the candidates it was picked from are separated at all; here the
search confirmed that the number of trees, in this range, is not worth
searching.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take XGBoost with a fixed number of trees $M$ and push the learning rate to
its edges. (a) As $\eta \to 0$, show that the ranking the model produces
approaches that of a single tree of the same depth, and say what happens to
the probabilities. (b) At $\eta = 1$ with 100 trees, what does each round do
to the training frauds, and why does that hurt the test weeks? (c) Which of
the two would you expect at the 25-tree, η = 0.1 corner that the folds ruled
out on average precision?

<Solution>
**(a)** The model starts from the base rate $F_0$ and adds $\eta h_m(x)$ at
each round, where $h_m$ is fitted to the errors of the current sum. When η is
tiny, each addition barely changes the sum, so the errors the second tree sees
are almost those the first saw, and $h_2 \approx h_1$; by induction every tree
is close to $h_1$, and the model is close to $F_0 + M\eta\, h_1(x)$. Adding a
constant and multiplying by a positive number keeps the order, so the ranking
is $h_1$'s: one tree of the chosen depth, fitted to label minus base rate. The
probabilities stay close to the base rate for every payment, since
$M\eta h_1$ is small, which ranking metrics cannot see and any rule reading
the probability's value would get badly wrong.

**(b)** At $\eta = 1$ each tree is added in full, so each round corrects the
current errors of the training rows in its leaves completely, as far as its
depth allows. After a few rounds the frauds the inputs can describe are fitted;
the remaining rounds keep correcting what is left, the training rows whose
label the inputs cannot explain, including frauds labelled genuine. The model
memorises those rows, and memorised regions rarely hold fraud again on later
weeks: the overfitting of the fully grown single tree, reached by a different
route.

**(c)** The underfitting side, the direction of case (a). Trees times η,
$25 \times 0.1 = 2.5$, is the smallest in the grid, so this model has moved
the least from the base rate: its later trees have had the fewest rounds to
correct what the earlier ones missed, and its average precision falls short
of the rest of the grid's, which is where the folds found it. A useful boosted model needs
trees times η large enough to leave case (a) and η small enough to stay out of
case (b); the grid's η of 0.1 and 0.3 with 50 to 100 trees lies between the
two edges.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague writes: "On the real stream the folds chose 100 trees for
XGBoost on all three metrics, but 50 trees score 0.249 on card precision
against 0.235. The search made a mistake that costs 1.4 compromised cards a
day, and a finer grid over the number of trees would have found it." Refute
both sentences, naming the step that fails in each.

<Solution>
**First sentence.** Calling the gap a cost treats both test numbers as exact.
They are means over four test weeks with standard deviations of 0.07 (100
trees) and 0.04 (50 trees), and 0.249 is the largest of eighteen such numbers,
optimistic for the same reason a depth picked on the test week is. A gap of
0.014 is a fifth of the larger standard deviation; the two settings are not
separated even on the weeks they are scored on. The failing step is reading
optimal minus best-estimated as a loss when it is below the spread.

**Second sentence.** A finer grid adds candidates. Each one adds a noisy test
number, so the optimal row, the maximum of more noisy numbers, can only rise,
whether or not any new setting is better. The best-estimated row moves only if
the folds can separate the new candidate from the old pick, and on card
precision the folds could not separate any of the simulator's eighteen. The
failing step is assuming the search was limited by its candidates; it was
limited by its folds. Four validation weeks with standard deviations of 0.02
to 0.04 cannot locate a difference of 0.014 however fine the grid.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
(a) A grid has $m$ equally spaced levels per setting over $k$ settings, each
scaled to $[0,1]$. Only the first setting matters, and the band of
acceptable candidates is an interval of width $q$ along it, anywhere in the
range. Show that the grid is guaranteed to hit the band only if $m \ge 1/q$,
so that it needs $(1/q)^k$ points. (b) Compare, for $q = 0.05$ and $k = 3$,
with the number of random draws that give a 95% chance. (c) How many grid
points would give the important setting 27 distinct values with three
settings, and how many random draws?

<Solution>
**(a)** Levels spaced $1/m$ apart leave gaps of width $1/m$ between
neighbours; an interval of width $q$ placed in a gap misses every level when
$q < 1/m$. A level is guaranteed inside the interval wherever it lies only if
$1/m \le q$, i.e. $m \ge 1/q$. The grid takes $m$ levels in every setting,
because it does not know which one matters, so it has $m^k \ge (1/q)^k$
points.

**(b)** $(1/0.05)^3 = 20^3 = 8{,}000$ grid points for a guarantee, against
59 random draws for a 95% chance. The grid's cost grows with $k$; the random
search's does not.

**(c)** 27 levels per setting over three settings: $27^3 = 19{,}683$ points.
27 random draws give 27 distinct values of every setting.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design the backtest that would decide whether the tuned tree and tuned XGBoost
really differ on card precision at 100 on the real stream. Assume the
difference between the two models' weekly card precisions, computed week by
week on the same test week, has a standard deviation of 0.02. (a) Why compare
paired weekly differences rather than the two four-week means? (b) How many
test weeks would show a true difference of 0.003 at two standard errors?
(c) And a difference of 0.02? (d) What makes the answer to (b) impractical
beyond the arithmetic?

<Solution>
**(a)** Most of each model's week-to-week movement comes from the week: how
many compromised cards it held and how easy they were. Both models face the
same week, so that movement cancels in their difference, and the difference's
spread (assumed 0.02) is smaller than either model's own (0.04 and 0.07).

**(b)** With $n$ weeks the mean difference has standard error $0.02/\sqrt n$.
Two standard errors equal 0.003 when $\sqrt n = 2 \times 0.02 / 0.003 =
13.3$, so $n \approx 178$ weeks, more than three years.

**(c)** $\sqrt n = 2 \times 0.02/0.02 = 2$, so $n = 4$ weeks.

**(d)** Over three years the stream drifts and the fraud adapts, so the weeks
are not draws from one distribution, and both models would be retrained many
times along the way: the question "which model is better" stops having a
single answer before the experiment ends. The practical conclusion is that
the two are tied for any decision the bank can make, and the choice rests on
cost, readability and what the ranking below the top hundred is used for.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The real-stream XGBoost grid of eighteen candidates took 8,789 seconds, both
passes. The imbalance lesson found that a mild fraud weight,
`scale_pos_weight` $= 5$, gave card precision 0.249 against 0.235 without it.
(a) Add the weight with values 1, 5, 10 and 100 to the grid: how many
candidates, and roughly how long for both passes? (b) Why must the weight be
searched on the folds instead of set to $1/\mathrm{IR}$, about 400 on this
stream? (c) What does a random search of 18 draws over the enlarged grid cost,
and what is the chance that it contains at least one of the four candidates
with depth 6, η = 0.1 and 50 or 100 trees? (d) If the search instead
undersampled genuine payments, what two steps would it need that the weight
does not?

<Solution>
**(a)** $18 \times 4 = 72$ candidates; at the same cost per candidate, about
$4 \times 8{,}789 \approx 35{,}000$ seconds, close to ten hours, half of it
for the pass a bank would actually run.

**(b)** The imbalance lesson showed that the best weight changes with the
metric, the model and the stream, and that the heuristic $1/\mathrm{IR}$
pushes genuine payments over the line once boosting has fitted the frauds; on
this stream the folds picked 5, and 10 to 100 did no better. A weight is a
setting like depth: a grid that includes 1, prequential folds, and a pick by
card precision.

**(c)** 18 draws cost about what the original grid did, 8,789 seconds. The
four target candidates are $4$ of $72$. The probability that 18 draws without
replacement miss all four is $\binom{68}{18}/\binom{72}{18} =
\frac{54 \cdot 53 \cdot 52 \cdot 51}{72 \cdot 71 \cdot 70 \cdot 69} \approx
0.31$, so the chance of including at least one is about 0.69. Whether that
matters depends on the band: if, as on the simulator, the folds cannot
separate most candidates on card precision, missing these four costs nothing
measurable.

**(d)** The sampler has to run inside each fold, on the training block only,
so that validation weeks keep the stream's own base rate; and any decision
that reads the probability's value has to correct the outputs with
$\beta = \mathrm{IR}/r$, the keep rate of <Ref
lesson="undersampling-calibration">undersampling and calibration</Ref>. A
weight changes the loss and needs neither the resampling step nor, for the
ranking, a correction.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The pipeline behind every search in this lesson standardises each input (it
subtracts the training mean and divides by the standard deviation) before the
model sees it. (a) Show that for the decision tree, the forest and XGBoost
this changes nothing in the fitted model, and that replacing the amount by
$\log(1 + \text{amount})$ changes nothing either. (b) Why can the same step
change logistic regression's fit at a given regularisation strength? (c) What
would you expect for a model whose first step is a weighted sum of all inputs,
fitted by gradient steps?

<Solution>
**(a)** A tree splits an input at a threshold: payments with a value at or
below it go one way, the rest the other. A strictly increasing transform of an
input keeps the order of the values, so every split available before the
transform is available after it, with the threshold transformed, and it sends
exactly the same payments each way. The impurity of every candidate split is
therefore unchanged, the same splits are chosen, the leaves hold the same rows
and give the same scores. Standardising and $\log(1+x)$ are both strictly
increasing, so the tree, every tree of the forest and every boosting round are
unchanged.

**(b)** Logistic regression's penalty adds the squared coefficients to the
loss. The coefficient that an input needs depends on its scale: amounts in
euros need a small coefficient, a 0/1 flag a large one, so without
standardising the penalty falls unevenly on the inputs. Standardising puts
them on one scale, which changes what the penalty does and so the fitted
coefficients and the order they give.

**(c)** A weighted sum is not invariant to scale: an input in the thousands
dominates the sum and its gradient, and a heavy-tailed one like the amount
puts a few huge values into every step. Such a model needs the inputs scaled,
and the heavy tails compressed, before it can be trained at all, while the
trees above were indifferent. That is the first of the steps that <Ref
lesson="neural-tabular-detectors">feed-forward networks on transaction
features</Ref> takes, and the reason a network is harder to bring to the
XGBoost bar on the same inputs.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

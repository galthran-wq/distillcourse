Hand-built neighbourhood features summarise a node's neighbours with fixed statistics — counts, means, maxima — chosen in advance. Could a network learn which summary of the neighbourhood matters, the way a convolution learns which summary of nearby pixels matters, and what does one layer of such a network compute?

The fixed summaries have a known flaw. <Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality" id="recursive-aggregates-over-the-neighbours">The recursive aggregates</Ref>
of the neighbourhood chapter take the maximum, minimum or mean of a
column over a node's neighbours, and the maximum grows with the number
of neighbours even when nobody is unusual: 0.85 standard deviations
expected over three honest neighbours, 2.51 over a hundred. A tree that
splits on "maximum neighbour amount" is partly splitting on degree, and
the mean, which does not drift, hides the one unusual neighbour the
maximum was there to find. <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="elliptic-a-graph-whose-labels-come-from-names">The Elliptic
graph</Ref>, the Bitcoin transaction graph whose 166 columns are 94 that
describe the transaction itself and 72 that summarise the same
quantities over its neighbours, ships those 72 already built, each
chosen by someone who guessed it might matter.

The exposure score of <Ref lesson="guilt-by-association">the previous
chapter</Ref> learns nothing either. At every step of the walk a node
keeps a share $1 - \alpha$ of its restart value and takes a share
$\alpha$ from its neighbours, each neighbour handing on its score in
parts the graph's edge weights set. Both the shares and the parts are
fixed before any label is seen, and no label can change them. On <Ref
lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality" id="when-fraud-avoids-fraud">an
auction graph</Ref>, where fraudsters trade with honest-looking
accomplice accounts and seldom with each other, the walk therefore
pushes suspicion onto the accomplices.

Images had the same problem: which summary of a pixel's surroundings
tells a cat from a dog? A *convolution* answers it by learning the
summary. A 3×3 filter is nine weights, one for each position relative
to the pixel (above-left, above, above-right, and so on). The filter
multiplies the nine pixel values around a pixel by the nine weights and
adds them up, and the same nine weights are applied at every pixel of
the image. Training sets the nine weights, so the summary computed is
whichever one the labels reward. The one-dimensional version, one set
of weights slid over neighbouring payments, is the convolutional row of
<Ref lesson="worldline-model-comparison"
id="three-rows-that-read-the-cards-past">the eight-family
comparison</Ref>. The question is what this construction becomes on a
payment graph, where a node has three neighbours or three thousand, in
no order.

## A convolution needs positions; a graph has none

The filter's nine weights are indexed by position: the weight for "the
pixel above" exists because every pixel has a pixel above it. Before
borrowing the idea, check what a graph offers in place of positions.

### An image is a graph whose every node has the same eight neighbours

Draw each pixel of a 5×5 image as a node and join it to the pixels
around it. Every pixel not on the border has exactly eight neighbours,
and they come in a fixed arrangement, so "the neighbour above" is
well defined. Number the pixels row by row and the adjacency matrix is
banded: pixel 13, the centre, is joined to 7, 8, 9, 12, 14, 17, 18 and
19, so its row has ones in three short runs near the diagonal.

A payment graph has none of that regularity. A cardholder has paid at
four merchants, a merchant has been paid by 3,000 cardholders, and
neither list has an order: no merchant is "the one above". A weight per
neighbour position has nothing to attach to.

### Why the adjacency matrix cannot be the input

The obvious way round is to feed the whole adjacency matrix to a
network, the way an image's pixel array is fed to one. Two things break.

**The size.** The adjacency matrix of $n$ nodes has $n^2$ cells. <Ref
lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="matrix-or-list-what-the-graph-costs-to-store">The first graph
chapter</Ref> priced a month of card payments at about $10^{12}$ cells,
almost all zero, against about 30 million lines of an edge list.

**The order.** The matrix depends on how the nodes were numbered. Take
the four-node graph of <Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="counting-walks-with-matrix-powers">the walk-count section</Ref>,
edges 1–2, 1–4, 2–4 and 3–4. Its four nodes can be numbered in
$4! = 24$ ways; because swapping nodes 1 and 2 leaves the graph
unchanged, those 24 numberings give 12 different matrices, all of the
same graph. A network that reads a matrix cell by cell has no reason to
give the 12 the same output. It would have to learn, from data, that
they are one graph, and for $n$ nodes there can be up to $n!$ of them.

  src="./figures/orderings.svg"

  caption="One graph, three of its twelve adjacency matrices. A model must give all of them the same answer."

Whatever a graph layer computes for node $v$ must therefore come out the
same however the nodes are numbered, and must accept any number of
neighbours.

### Functions of a set: sum, mean and maximum

Both conditions say the layer may treat a node's neighbours only as a
set: a collection with no order and any size.

<Definition title="permutation-invariant aggregation">
An *aggregation* maps a finite set of vectors $\{h_u : u \in N(v)\}$,
of any size and with the same vector allowed more than once, to one vector of the same length, and is *permutation
invariant* if its value does not depend on the order in which the
vectors are listed. The sum $\sum_u h_u$, the mean
$\frac{1}{|N(v)|}\sum_u h_u$ and the coordinate-wise maximum are the
three standard choices.
</Definition>

Each of the three, applied to the raw values, forgets something. Give
every neighbour one number, its fraud probability from a local model,
and compare neighbourhoods:

| neighbourhood | sum | mean | max | what it is |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\{0.9\}$ | 0.9 | 0.9 | 0.9 | one suspect neighbour |
| $\{0.9, 0.9, 0.9\}$ | 2.7 | 0.9 | 0.9 | three suspect neighbours |
| $\{0.9, 0.1\}$ | 1.0 | 0.5 | 0.9 | one suspect among two |
| $\{0.5, 0.5\}$ | 1.0 | 0.5 | 0.5 | two half-suspect |

The mean and the maximum cannot tell one suspect neighbour from three:
they do not see how many. The sum and the mean cannot tell
$\{0.9, 0.1\}$ from $\{0.5, 0.5\}$: they do not see whether the
suspicion sits in one neighbour. The maximum sees that, and loses the
count. The sum keeps the count, which is why it grows with degree like
the walk counts did; the mean is size-free, which is why it hides a
single outlier. Applied to raw values, none of the three keeps
everything.

<Exercise>
(a) Find a pair of neighbourhoods that the sum and the maximum both
tell apart but the mean does not. (b) A neighbourhood of 3,000 honest
cardholders with probabilities around 0.01 and one of 30 cardholders
with probabilities around 0.5: which aggregation scores the first
higher, and why is that the wrong order for an investigator?

<Solution>
**(a)** Take $\{0.4\}$ and $\{0.8, 0.0\}$: sums 0.4 and 0.8, maxima 0.4
and 0.8, means both 0.4. The mean loses the count and the spread at
once.

**(b)** The sum: about $3{,}000 \times 0.01 = 30$ against
$30 \times 0.5 = 15$. A busy merchant's honest crowd outweighs a small
group of suspects, the degree effect again. The mean ranks the second
first (0.5 against 0.01), and so does the maximum.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### A learned transform before the aggregation

The table aggregates the raw scores. A layer need not: it can first
pass each neighbour's value through a function, the same function for
every neighbour so that the order still does not matter, and aggregate
the results. If that function is learned, which of the table's blind
spots survive it?

Take $f(h) = \max(0, h - 0.7)/0.2$, one ReLU unit. It gives a neighbour
at 0.9 the value 1 and a neighbour at 0.5 or below the value 0, so the
sum of $f$ over the neighbours counts the suspect ones: 1 for
$\{0.9, 0.1\}$, 0 for $\{0.5, 0.5\}$, 3 for $\{0.9, 0.9, 0.9\}$. The
sum of the transformed values now sees where the suspicion sits, which
the sum of raw values could not. With several transforms side by side
the sum can tell any two neighbourhoods apart: the sums of
$1, h, h^2, \dots, h^m$ fix the number of neighbours and then their
values, for every neighbourhood of at most $m$.

The mean and the maximum keep one blind spot whatever the transform.
The mean of $f$ over $\{a\}$ is $f(a)$, and over $\{a, a\}$ it is
$(f(a) + f(a))/2 = f(a)$: it sees the proportions of a neighbourhood,
never how many neighbours carry them, so $\{0.9, 0.1\}$ and
$\{0.9, 0.9, 0.1, 0.1\}$ are the same to it. A second copy of a value
never changes a maximum either: with several transforms the maximum can
report which values occur among the neighbours, never how often. On raw
values it is blinder still and sees only the largest.

Where the learned weights sit therefore matters. Weights applied to
each neighbour before a sum let the labels choose what the sum counts;
weights applied only after the aggregation work on what it has already
merged and cannot separate it again.

<KeyFact>
A graph layer may combine a node's neighbours only through an
order-free aggregation. A learned transform applied to each neighbour
before a sum recovers what the raw sum loses; no transform makes the
mean see how many neighbours there are ($\{a\}$ and $\{a, a\}$ have the
same mean), or the maximum see how often a value occurs.
</KeyFact>

## Message passing: gather, aggregate, update

With an order-free aggregation in hand, the convolution can be rebuilt
for graphs. The baseline comes first, because the question is what the
graph adds.

### The network that ignores the graph

The simplest graph network applies one feed-forward network to every
node's own feature vector, the same weights for every node, and uses
the edges for nothing. For classifying nodes it is exactly the network
of <Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="one-output-unit-read-as-pfraud">the network chapter</Ref>, with a
transaction's or an account's features as inputs. Any graph network
has to beat it to justify the edges.

### One layer of message passing

Write $h_v$ for node $v$'s current vector, at first its feature vector.
A *message-passing* layer computes a new vector for every node in three
steps:

1. **Gather** the current vectors of $v$'s neighbours, $\{h_u : u \in N(v)\}$.
2. **Aggregate** them with an order-free function, such as the sum or
   the mean.
3. **Update**: compute $v$'s new vector from its own vector and the
   aggregate, through a learned transformation (a weight matrix and a
   nonlinearity).

The convolution's three steps are the same: gather the pixels around
the centre, combine them, pass the result on. What changes is the
middle step. The filter combines its neighbours with one weight per
position; the graph layer, having no positions, uses one aggregation for
all neighbours, and puts the learned weights before or after it, on the
vectors instead of on the positions.

### Each layer reaches one hop further

After one layer, $v$'s vector depends on its own features and its
neighbours'. A second layer aggregates the new vectors, each of which
already holds its own neighbours, so $v$ now depends on every node
within two steps. The set of nodes whose features can influence $v$'s
vector is called its *receptive field*, the name the convolution uses
for the pixels that can affect one output. With $L$ layers it is the
ball of radius $L$ around $v$.

The recursive aggregates reached one hop further per round in the same
way. The difference is in what is applied at each round: there, a fixed
maximum or mean chosen by a person; here, a transformation whose
weights the labels set.

## One graph convolutional layer, in matrix form

The three steps are a recipe. Written for every node at once, they
become a matrix product, which is also how they are computed.

### Sum aggregation is a sparse matrix product

Stack the node vectors as the rows of a matrix $H$, one row per node,
one column per feature; at the input, $H = X$, the feature matrix.
(This $H$ is unrelated to the heterophilicity of the neighbourhood
chapter.) Row $v$ of $AH$ is

$$
(AH)_v = \sum_u A_{vu}\, h_u = \sum_{u \in N(v)} h_u ,
$$

because $A_{vu}$ is 1 exactly for $v$'s neighbours. So $AH$ is sum
aggregation for every node at once, and like <Ref
lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="counting-walks-with-matrix-powers">the walk counts</Ref> it costs
one pass over the edge list. Two such products, $A(AH)$, sum over walks
of length 2, which is the two-hop receptive field in matrix form.

Plain $AH$ has two defects. It leaves out $v$'s own vector, since
$A_{vv} = 0$; adding the identity, $\tilde{A} = A + I$, puts it back,
and each node now counts itself among its neighbours. And it is a sum,
so a merchant with 3,000 neighbours gets a row about a thousand times
the size of a cardholder's with three. That is the scale problem of
<Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="scaling-the-inputs-trees-compare-networks-multiply">the network
chapter</Ref> moved into the graph: one learning rate cannot suit
inputs whose sizes differ a thousandfold.

### Dividing by degree, at both ends of the edge

The obvious repair divides each row by its size. Let $\tilde{d}_v$ be
$v$'s degree plus one (the self-loop) and $\tilde{D}$ the diagonal
matrix of the $\tilde{d}_v$; this $\tilde{D}$ is the degree matrix,
unrelated to dyadicity and to the toy's node D. Then
$\tilde{D}^{-1}\tilde{A}H$ replaces the sum by the mean over $v$ and its
neighbours, and every node receives a vector of ordinary size. It is the
row division of <Ref lesson="guilt-by-association"
id="why-g-outranks-i">the relational neighbour rule</Ref>, which
averaged the labels of a node's neighbours.

The mean repairs the receiving end of the edge and ignores the sending
end: a neighbour counts the same whatever its own degree. A cardholder
who paid at one small shop and at one merchant of 3,000 customers gives
the two the same share of its attention. But the busy merchant is the
neighbour of everyone, so its vector says little about any one of them;
on the identifier graph of <Ref lesson="communities-and-components"
id="the-high-degree-identifier">the communities chapter</Ref>, the café
IP shared by 400 users was exactly such a neighbour. A neighbour should
count for less the more neighbours it has, which is the column division
of the walk in the previous chapter: each node handed on its value in shares
divided by its own strength.

### Five ways to weight an edge, tried on one hub

Which of the two divisions to use, or how to combine them, can be
settled on a single star: a merchant joined to 3,000 cardholders who
have no other neighbour, each cardholder carrying the same value $x$.
With self-loops the merchant has $\tilde{d} = 3{,}001$ and each
cardholder $\tilde{d} = 2$. Two numbers decide: what the merchant
receives from its cardholders, which should not be a thousand times what
a small merchant receives, and the weight at which a cardholder hears
the merchant, which should be small.

| weight on the edge from sender $u$ to receiver $v$ | merchant receives | cardholder hears merchant at |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (the sum) | $3{,}000x$ | 1 |
| $1/\tilde{d}_v$, receiver's degree (the mean) | $\approx x$ | $1/2$ |
| $1/\tilde{d}_u$, sender's degree (the walk's division) | $1{,}500x$ | $1/3{,}001$ |
| $1/(\tilde{d}_v\tilde{d}_u)$, both degrees | $\approx x/2$ | $1/6{,}002$ |
| $1/\sqrt{\tilde{d}_v\tilde{d}_u}$, square root of both | $\approx 39x$ | $0.013$ |

Each one-sided division repairs one end and breaks the other. The mean
keeps the merchant's total ordinary but lets the merchant set half of
every cardholder's vector. Dividing by the sender's degree quiets the
merchant at its cardholders, but the merchant's own total is back at
$1{,}500x$, the scale problem again. Dividing fully by both degrees
quiets the merchant at both ends and loses the count: a merchant with
three such cardholders receives $3x/(2 \cdot 4) \approx 0.38x$, one with
3,000 receives $0.50x$, so, like the mean in the aggregation table, it
can hardly tell three neighbours from three thousand.

The square root lands between keeping the count and keeping the scale.
The merchant's total grows with the square root of its degree: three
cardholders give $3x/\sqrt{8} \approx 1.06x$, 3,000 give
$3{,}000x/\sqrt{6{,}002} \approx 38.7x$, so a thousandfold difference in
degree arrives as about a 36-fold difference in size, the count still visible
on a compressed scale. A cardholder hears the merchant at
$1/\sqrt{2 \cdot 3{,}001} \approx 0.013$, nearly as little as under the
sender's division. Nothing forces exactly the square root; it is the
compromise the standard layer makes.

<Definition title="graph convolutional layer">
With $\tilde{A} = A + I$, $\tilde{d}_v = \sum_u \tilde{A}_{vu}$ and
$\tilde{D} = \operatorname{diag}(\tilde{d}_v)$, the *normalised
adjacency* is

$$
\hat{A} = \tilde{D}^{-1/2}\,\tilde{A}\,\tilde{D}^{-1/2},
\qquad \hat{A}_{vu} = \frac{\tilde{A}_{vu}}{\sqrt{\tilde{d}_v\,\tilde{d}_u}} ,
$$

and a *graph convolutional (GCN) layer* maps the node matrix $H$ to

$$
H' = \sigma\big(\hat{A}\, H\, W\big),
$$

with $W$ a learned weight matrix (input width × output width) and
$\sigma$ a nonlinearity, usually the ReLU, $\max(0, \cdot)$.
</Definition>

### What the square root does to repeated layers

A network stacks layers, so the matrix is applied again and again, and
how its powers behave matters as much as one product. The square-root
weight is the same in both directions of an edge,
$\hat{A}_{vu} = \hat{A}_{uv}$: $\hat{A}$ is symmetric, and a symmetric
matrix has only real eigenvalues. That is what turns a bound on the
size of an eigenvalue into an interval: a real number of size at most 1
lies in $[-1, 1]$, a complex one need not lie on the real line at all.

<Proposition title="the normalised adjacency has no eigenvalue above 1">
Every eigenvalue of $\hat{A}$ lies in $[-1, 1]$, and the vector with
entries $\sqrt{\tilde{d}_v}$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue 1.

<Proof>
$\hat{A}$ is symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real, and it remains to
bound their size. Write $P = \tilde{D}^{-1}\tilde{A}$, the mean over
closed neighbourhoods; its rows are non-negative and sum to 1. Then
$\hat{A} = \tilde{D}^{1/2} P \tilde{D}^{-1/2}$, so $\hat{A}$ and $P$
have the same eigenvalues. If $Px = \lambda x$ and $x_i$ is the entry
of largest absolute value, $|\lambda|\,|x_i| = |\sum_j P_{ij} x_j| \le
\sum_j P_{ij} |x_i| = |x_i|$, so $|\lambda| \le 1$. Finally
$\hat{A}\sqrt{\tilde{d}} = \tilde{D}^{-1/2}\tilde{A}\mathbf{1} =
\tilde{D}^{-1/2}\tilde{d} = \sqrt{\tilde{d}}$, since the rows of
$\tilde{A}$ sum to the $\tilde{d}_v$.
</Proof>
</Proposition>

With no eigenvalue above 1 in size, powers of $\hat{A}$ do not blow up,
as powers of $A$ do on a graph with hubs. With an eigenvalue of exactly
1 they do not die out either, as powers of the full division
$\tilde{D}^{-1}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-1}$ do: once every node has a
neighbour, every $\tilde{d}_u \ge 2$, each row of that matrix sums to at
most $\frac{1}{\tilde{d}_v} \cdot \tilde{d}_v \cdot \frac{1}{2} =
\frac{1}{2}$, and each layer at least halves what passes through it.

### One layer on four accounts

<Example title="a GCN layer on the four-node graph">
Read the four-node graph as accounts joined by transfers. Each account
has two standardised features: $x_1$, the share of incoming money it
forwards within a day, and $x_2$, its age. Account 3 forwards nearly
everything and is new; account 4, the hub, is its only counterpart.

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix} -0.5 & 1.0 \\ -0.5 & 0.5 \\ 2.0 & -1.5 \\ 0.5 & -0.5 \end{pmatrix},
\qquad
W = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

The first column of $W$ computes $x_1 - x_2$, "forwards fast and is
young"; the second passes the age on. These are weights training might
set; here they are given.

**Degrees with self-loops:** $\tilde{d} = (3, 3, 2, 4)$.

**Normalised adjacency:** $\hat{A}_{vv} = 1/\tilde{d}_v$ and, for each
edge, $1/\sqrt{\tilde{d}_v \tilde{d}_u}$:

$$
\hat{A} = \begin{pmatrix}
0.333 & 0.333 & 0 & 0.289 \\
0.333 & 0.333 & 0 & 0.289 \\
0 & 0 & 0.500 & 0.354 \\
0.289 & 0.289 & 0.354 & 0.250
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

**Each account's own message**, $XW$, one row per account:
$(-1.5, 1.0)$, $(-1.0, 0.5)$, $(3.5, -1.5)$, $(1.0, -0.5)$.

**Aggregate**, row by row of $\hat{A}(XW)$. Account 3 takes half of
its own message and 0.354 of the hub's:
$0.5 \times 3.5 + 0.354 \times 1.0 = 2.104$ on the first channel and
$0.5 \times (-1.5) + 0.354 \times (-0.5) = -0.927$ on the second. The
hub takes 0.354 of account 3's message, 0.289 of each of accounts 1
and 2, and a quarter of its own:
$0.289\,(-1.5 - 1.0) + 0.354 \times 3.5 + 0.25 \times 1.0 = 0.766$, and
$-0.222$ on the second channel.

**Update** with the ReLU: account 3 becomes $(2.10, 0)$, the hub
$(0.77, 0)$. The hub's own first channel was 1.0; in its new 0.77,
its own message contributes 0.25, account 3's 1.24 and the two old
accounts' $-0.72$.
</Example>

<Exercise>
Compute the outputs of accounts 1 and 2 from the same $\hat{A}$, $X$
and $W$. Why are they equal, although the two accounts differ in age?

<Solution>
Account 1: $0.333\,(-1.5) + 0.333\,(-1.0) + 0.289 \times 1.0 = -0.545$
on the first channel, ReLU 0; $0.333 \times 1.0 + 0.333 \times 0.5 +
0.289 \times (-0.5) = 0.356$ on the second. Account 2's row of
$\hat{A}$ is the same: $0.333$ for account 1, $0.333$ for itself,
$0.289$ for the hub. So both get $(0, 0.356)$.

They are equal because the two accounts have the same closed
neighbourhood, $\{1, 2, 4\}$, with the same degrees, and each gives its
own message exactly the weight it gives the other's. The layer has
averaged the two accounts into one; account 1's extra age (1.0 against
0.5) is gone. A node's own features enter only through the self-loop,
at weight $1/\tilde{d}_v$, which is what the skip connection below is
for.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### What is learned, and what is fixed

A two-layer network for node classification stacks two such layers
and ends in a softmax over the classes:

$$
\text{output} = \operatorname{softmax}\Big(\hat{A}\;\operatorname{ReLU}\big(\hat{A} X W^{(0)}\big)\; W^{(1)}\Big).
$$

The learned objects are $W^{(0)}$, of size (features × hidden width),
and $W^{(1)}$, of size (hidden width × classes). Nothing else is
learned. $\hat{A}$ is the graph, fixed before training; it decides how
neighbours are weighted, and $W^{(0)}$ and $W^{(1)}$ decide what is
passed: which combination of features each neighbour contributes, and
how the combined result is read. That is the answer to the opening's
question: the layer learns which features of the neighbourhood matter,
not how they are summarised.

The parameter count does not depend on the graph. With 166 features, a
hidden width of 100 and two classes, $W^{(0)}$ and $W^{(1)}$ hold
$166 \times 100 + 100 \times 2 = 16{,}800$ numbers, the same for a
graph of a thousand nodes or a million.

<KeyFact>
In a GCN layer the graph fixes how neighbours are combined and
training fixes only what is combined; one layer is a degree-weighted
blend of linear functions of the neighbours' features, so a maximum, or
a count of suspicious neighbours, can be approximated only through the
ReLU between layers.
</KeyFact>

## Logistic regression as one setting: the skip connection

The exercise on accounts 1 and 2 showed a cost of the layer. A node's
own vector enters only through the self-loop, at weight
$1/\tilde{d}_v$: a quarter for the hub, $1/3{,}001$ for a merchant with
3,000 neighbours. Where a node's own features are what separates fraud,
the layer dilutes them among its neighbours', and a GCN can then do
worse than the network that ignores the graph, worse even than a
logistic regression on the node's own features.

The repair is to give the node's own features a route to the output that
does not pass through the aggregation. The *Skip-GCN* adds a term that
reads $X$ directly:

$$
\text{output} = \operatorname{softmax}\Big(\hat{A}\;\operatorname{ReLU}\big(\hat{A} X W^{(0)}\big)\; W^{(1)} \;+\; X \tilde{W}\Big),
$$

with $\tilde{W}$ a learned matrix of size (features × classes). Set
$W^{(0)} = W^{(1)} = 0$. The first term vanishes and the output is
$\operatorname{softmax}(X\tilde{W})$. With two classes the softmax of
two scores $z_0, z_1$ is the sigmoid of $z_1 - z_0$, the identity used
for <Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="-imbalance-levers-inside-the-loss">two-output wrappers in the
network chapter</Ref>, so this is the ordinary logistic regression on
the node's own features, and every such logistic regression is one
setting of the Skip-GCN.

<Figure
  src="./figures/skip-gcn.svg"
  alt="Two panels of the same wiring. Left, Skip-GCN: X enters a box labelled A hat X W0 then ReLU, giving H1; H1 enters a box labelled A hat H1 W1; a bypass arrow carries X to a box labelled X W tilde; the two outputs meet at a plus sign and pass to softmax. Right, the same diagram with the two graph boxes greyed and crossed with W0 = W1 = 0, leaving only X to X W tilde to softmax, labelled logistic regression on the node's own features"
  caption="The bypass makes logistic regression on the node's own features one setting of the network; the graph term is added on top of it."
/>

The bypass is one matrix from features to class scores, a linear map,
so what the Skip-GCN contains is the logistic regression, not the
baseline network with its hidden layer: no choice of $\tilde{W}$
reproduces a hidden layer's ReLU units. A bypass through a hidden layer
of its own would contain that network too; the Skip-GCN measured below
uses the linear one.

Containing a model is a statement about what the network can represent,
not about what training finds. Training does not start at the logistic
regression: $W^{(0)}$, $W^{(1)}$ and $\tilde{W}$ start at small random
values and are fitted together, and on few labelled nodes they can
settle on a setting that scores worse than a logistic regression fitted
alone; and a setting that fits the training weeks better can still lose
on a test week. What the skip
removes is the forced dilution: the node's own features no longer have
to pass through its neighbourhood to reach the output.

The skip connection also completes <Ref lesson="guilt-by-association"
id="exercises">the previous chapter's second-to-last exercise</Ref>:
one step of the walk, $\xi \leftarrow Q\xi\,\alpha + e\,(1-\alpha)$,
with $Q$ the walk's column-normalised weight matrix and $e$ the restart
vector, is a graph term plus a term in each node's own input, each
multiplied by a weight. That is the shape of the Skip-GCN's score, a graph term plus
$X\tilde{W}$, with the weights frozen at
$\alpha$ and $1 - \alpha$. The Skip-GCN learns them, and learned
weights can be negative. On an inverse-homophilic graph, where <Ref
lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality" id="when-fraud-avoids-fraud">the
neighbourhood chapter</Ref> found fraudsters linked to accomplices
rather than to each other, the network can learn that a suspicious
neighbourhood lowers the score; the walk cannot.

## How deep: two layers, and why more layers blur

Two layers see two hops: on the cardholder–merchant graph, a
cardholder's merchants and those merchants' other cardholders, the
reach at which a card that shares a compromised terminal with stolen
cards shows up. A third and fourth layer would reach
further. From the receptive field one would expect each added layer to
help, since it adds information; on graphs the opposite tends to happen.

### Repeated averaging forgets the node

Strip the layers of their weights and nonlinearities and $L$ layers
compute $\hat{A}^L X$. By the Proposition, $\hat{A}$ has eigenvalue 1
with eigenvector $\sqrt{\tilde{d}}$, and on a connected graph with
self-loops every other eigenvalue is strictly smaller than 1 in size
(stated here without proof). Because $\hat{A}$ is symmetric, each
column of $X$ splits into components along perpendicular eigenvectors,
and each multiplication scales each component by its eigenvalue: every
component except the one along $\sqrt{\tilde{d}}$ shrinks, so

$$
\hat{A}^L X \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{\sqrt{\tilde{d}}\;\sqrt{\tilde{d}}^{\top} X}{\sum_u \tilde{d}_u}
\qquad (L \to \infty):
$$

row $v$ of the limit is $\sqrt{\tilde{d}_v}$ times one vector shared by
every node. Each node's features have been averaged with everyone
else's until only its degree is left. On the four accounts the second
eigenvalue is 0.56, so the differences shrink by about half per layer.

<Figure
  src="./figures/smoothing.svg"
  alt="Line chart of the first channel of A hat to the power L times X, divided by the square root of d tilde, for the four accounts, against the number of layers L from 0 to 16. Account 3, amber, starts at 1.41 and falls to 0.83, 0.55, 0.39, 0.29; account 4 starts at 0.25; accounts 1 and 2 start at minus 0.29 and coincide from L = 1. All four lines meet at 0.175 by about L = 12. A dashed line marks L = 2"
  caption="Repeated averaging on the four accounts: after a dozen layers every account carries the same value per unit of square-root degree, and account 3's signal is gone."
/>

Real layers have weights and ReLUs between the products, so the limit
is a tendency rather than an outcome: the weights can partly undo the
averaging, but every added layer hands them inputs averaged once more.
The usual practice is to start at two layers and add one only if
validation folds say so.

### Reaching further without more layers

Two layers cannot see a laundering cycle of six hops. Two additions
reach further without stacking layers. A *global node*, joined to every
node, gives each node a two-step route to every other: whatever the
global node gathers in one layer, every node receives in the next. And
*attention* replaces the fixed weight $1/\sqrt{\tilde{d}_v\tilde{d}_u}$
by a learned one: a score computed from the pair of vectors
$(h_v, h_u)$, normalised over $v$'s neighbours by a softmax. The weights
then depend on the neighbours' features, so the layer can attend to the
one suspicious neighbour among three thousand, and because the score
is computed per pair, the result is still order-free.

## Training on a graph: labels, masks and sampled neighbourhoods

A GCN is trained like the network of the earlier module, by gradient
steps on cross-entropy, with three differences that come from the
graph.

### Which nodes carry a loss

Every node takes part in the forward pass, as a neighbour if not as a
target, but only labelled nodes carry a loss. On Elliptic that is 23%
of the nodes (2% illicit, 21% licit); the 77% unknown are aggregated
over and never scored, because unknown is not licit. The illicit class
is rare among the labelled, so the loss is weighted, <Ref
lesson="neural-tabular-detectors" id="-imbalance-levers-inside-the-loss">a
class weight inside the cross-entropy</Ref>; the Elliptic GCN below
used weights 0.3 for licit and 0.7 for illicit, a ratio of about 2.3,
mild by the standard of <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="the-ratio-is-a-hyperparameter-chosen-by-the-metric-that-decides">the
imbalance chapter</Ref>.

The graph a training row sees must obey <Ref
lesson="point-in-time-features"
id="two-ways-a-training-row-can-know-too-much">the point-in-time
rule</Ref> like any feature: edges that existed and features as they
were at the node's moment. A neighbour's features are inputs, so a
neighbour's later behaviour must not leak in.

### A model that transfers to graphs it has not seen

Elliptic's 49 time steps are separate graphs: no edge joins two steps.
A model trained on steps 1 to 34 must score the nodes of steps 35 to 49,
which it has never seen in any graph. A GCN can, because its parameters
are the weight matrices, which apply to any node of any graph; nothing
in it belongs to a particular node. Such a model is called *inductive*.
A model that learns something per node, such as an embedding row for
every node like the terminal table of <Ref
lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="an-embedding-is-a-linear-layer-on-the-one-hot-input">the network
chapter</Ref>, is *transductive*: it scores only nodes it was trained
with, and a new node gets the untrained "unknown" row.

### Mini-batches of sampled neighbourhoods

An Elliptic step holds 1,000 to 8,000 nodes, and the whole step fits in
one batch. A month of a card issuer's graph does not, and a mini-batch
of nodes is not a mini-batch of independent rows: to compute two layers
for a node, the batch must hold its two-hop neighbourhood. A cardholder
who paid at a merchant with 50,000 customers has a two-hop neighbourhood
of at least 50,000 cardholders, and a batch of such nodes can be most of
the graph.

The standard repair samples. Pick a batch of *core* nodes; for each,
keep at most a fixed number of its neighbours, and for each of those a
fixed number of theirs; run the layers on this sampled sub-graph. The
outer nodes have incomplete neighbourhoods, so their outputs are wrong,
and the loss is computed only on the core nodes, whose two-hop
receptive field the sample contains (in sampled form). The sample also
caps the cost of a hub: a merchant's 50,000 customers enter a batch as
at most the fixed number drawn.

<Exercise>
A two-layer GCN is trained with at most 10 sampled neighbours per hop
and batches of 512 core nodes. (a) At most how many nodes does one
batch hold? (b) How many would a single core node at the 50,000-customer
merchant need without sampling, if it is a cardholder who paid there,
on the cardholder–merchant graph? (c) Why is the loss not computed on the sampled first-hop nodes
too?

<Solution>
**(a)** Each core node brings at most $1 + 10 + 10 \times 10 = 111$
nodes, so a batch holds at most $512 \times 111 = 56{,}832$, fewer where
neighbourhoods overlap.

**(b)** One hop reaches the cardholder's merchants, two hops their
customers: at least the merchant's 50,000 cardholders, for one core
node.

**(c)** A first-hop node's second layer needs its own neighbours'
first-layer vectors, which need their neighbours: two hops beyond it,
three beyond the core. The sample stops two hops from the core, so the
first-hop node's output is computed from a truncated neighbourhood, and
a loss on it would train the network on outputs it would never produce
at scoring time.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## When a graph network beats hand-built neighbour features

Everything above says what a GCN can compute. Whether it should replace
the hand-built aggregates is a question for measurement, and two public
datasets have been used to answer it.

### The Elliptic comparison

The setup, on <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="elliptic-a-graph-whose-labels-come-from-names">the Elliptic
graph</Ref>: train on time steps 1 to 34, test on steps 35 to 49, so
every model is scored forward in time on graphs it has not seen; only
labelled nodes are trained on and scored. Logistic regression (library
defaults), a random forest (50 trees) and a feed-forward network (one
hidden layer of 50 units, Adam at 0.001, 200 epochs) are each trained
twice: on the 94 local features alone, and on all 166, the local ones
plus the 72 hand-built one-hop aggregates. The GCN has two layers of
hidden width 100, trained for 1,000 epochs with Adam at 0.001 and the
0.3/0.7 weighted cross-entropy; the Skip-GCN adds the bypass.
*EvolveGCN* is a GCN whose weights change with time: it keeps one
weight matrix per time step and lets a second network compute each
step's weights from the previous step's. The score is the F1 of the illicit class: precision and recall of the
illicit predictions at each model's default decision, combined by their
harmonic mean. Like every threshold metric it judges <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="a-threshold-metric-judges-a-cut-nobody-chose">one cut nobody
chose</Ref>; no spread over repeated runs is reported.

| illicit F1, test steps 35–49 | local features (94) | local + aggregates (166) |
|---|---|---|
| logistic regression | 0.457 | 0.481 |
| feed-forward network | 0.649 | 0.653 |
| random forest | 0.694 | **0.788** |
| GCN | | 0.628 |
| Skip-GCN | | 0.705 |
| EvolveGCN | | 0.720 |

### Reading the table

From "the GCN sees the graph and the others do not" one would expect
the graph network on top. The table shows the opposite, in three steps.

**The plain GCN loses to the network without the graph** (0.628
against 0.649 and 0.653). This is the dilution of the exercise on
accounts 1 and 2. Its precision equals the Skip-GCN's (0.812 for both);
its recall is lower (0.512 against 0.623), which is what passing every
node's features through its neighbourhood would do if it blurred illicit
transactions into their licit neighbours.

**The skip term repairs it** (0.705), lifting the GCN above both
feed-forward networks and above the forest on local features (0.694).
That the bypass helps this much says the transaction's own features
carried most of the signal.

<Figure
  src="./figures/elliptic-f1.svg"
  alt="Horizontal bars of illicit-class F1 on the Elliptic test steps 35 to 49, axis from 0 to 1. Grey bars, local features only: logistic regression 0.457, network 0.649, random forest 0.694. Blue bars, local plus the 72 hand-built aggregates: logistic regression 0.481, network 0.653, random forest 0.788. Amber bars, graph networks: GCN 0.628, Skip-GCN 0.705, EvolveGCN 0.720. A note: single reported results, no spread over runs"
  caption="The forest on hand-built aggregates leads; the plain GCN falls below the network that ignores the graph, and the skip term lifts it above every other model except that forest and EvolveGCN."
/>

**The forest on hand-built aggregates wins** (0.788). It has one hop of
the graph through the 72 columns, and it cuts strong, unevenly scaled
inputs with thresholds, which the <Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="a-tie-on-the-simulator-a-loss-below-the-top-on-real-data">network
chapter</Ref> found trees do better than networks on engineered tables.
A GCN ends in a softmax over a weighted sum of its last hidden vectors,
a logistic regression on features it learned; a forest ends in
thresholds on the features it was given, and here those features were
already the neighbourhood summaries. The aggregates helped every model,
so the graph carries signal; on this data the hand-built route used it
better.

Two further results bear on how to use a GCN rather than whether. The
hidden vectors of the trained GCN, its *node embeddings*, concatenated
to the features as extra columns, raised logistic regression from 0.481
to 0.533 and the forest from 0.788 to 0.796: the GCN as a feature
builder for another model. And EvolveGCN, whose weights follow the time
steps, reached 0.720 against the Skip-GCN's 0.705. With no
spread reported, gains of a point or two are not established;
<Ref lesson="elliptic-illicit-nodes">the Elliptic chapter</Ref> reads
these numbers against the dataset's time structure.

### A synthetic world with complete labels

The second measurement runs on <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="amlworld-complete-labels-no-bank-has">AMLworld</Ref>, the generated
banking world whose laundering labels are complete. There the label
sits on transactions, which are edges, so the graph networks classify
edges from the vectors of the two accounts they join. Data were split
60/20/20 in time; the score is again the F1 of the laundering class.
On one side, boosted trees from two libraries (LightGBM and XGBoost) on transaction features
plus graph features computed beforehand by a preprocessor: account
statistics and counts of cycles and other <Ref
lesson="fraud-as-a-graph" id="eight-definitions">laundering
shapes</Ref>. On the other, graph networks with no hand-built graph
features, two layers of width 64, trained on sampled neighbourhoods of
up to 100 accounts at each hop. The strongest, PNA, aggregates with
several functions at once (sum, mean, maximum, spread), scaled by
degree. A sum after a learned transform could in principle see
everything, but only if training finds the transform; PNA hands the
next layer the counts, the proportions and the extremes side by side
instead.

| laundering F1 (%) | HI-Small | LI-Small | HI-Medium | LI-Medium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PNA, no hand features | 56.8 | 16.5 | 59.7 | 27.7 |
| LightGBM + graph features | 62.9 | 20.8 | 59.5 | 20.9 |
| XGBoost + graph features | 63.2 | 27.3 | 65.7 | 28.2 |

(HI and LI are the higher- and lower-illicit-ratio worlds; Small and
Medium their sizes.) Without any of the cycle counts and statistics the
trees were handed, the graph network is level with LightGBM on
HI-Medium and ahead of it on LI-Medium, behind both on the two Small
worlds, and behind XGBoost on all four. It paid in compute: the
graph-network experiments, tuning included, took about 1,000 GPU hours,
and the two largest worlds got no graph-network result at all because
training them needed more.

<KeyFact>
The two measurements agree. Where good neighbourhood features have been
built, the best tree ensemble on them is the bar a graph network has
not cleared. A graph network earns its place where those features do
not exist yet: on a new graph, a new kind of edge, or a typology whose
shape nobody has written down, it learns a usable summary from the
labels without someone guessing the columns first, and its embeddings
can be handed to the trees as extra columns.
</KeyFact>

<Conclusion>
You can now say what one layer of a graph network computes:
$H' = \sigma(\hat{A}HW)$, a sum over each node and its neighbours with
every edge weighted by $1/\sqrt{\tilde{d}_v\tilde{d}_u}$, followed by a
learned linear map and a nonlinearity. You can derive why it must
aggregate over sets (no positions, a dozen matrices for one four-node
graph), why it divides at both ends of an edge and by the square root
(sums grow with degree, hubs are uninformative neighbours, and full
division loses the count), why the skip term makes logistic
regression one of its settings, why stacking more layers blurs
rather than helps (repeated averaging leaves only degree), and how it is trained when the
graph does not fit in a batch. On Elliptic the forest on hand-built
aggregates (0.788) beat the Skip-GCN (0.705); the graph network's case
is where nobody has built the aggregates.

The Elliptic table leaves three questions open: what in the 166
features lets the forest win, what the illicit-class F1 hides that a
score over both classes does not, and why every model fails after the
43rd time step, when a dark market closed. <Ref
lesson="elliptic-illicit-nodes">Illicit-node classification on the
Elliptic graph</Ref> takes the dataset, the split and the metric apart
to answer them.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Push the normalisation to its edges. (a) A node has no neighbours.
What is its row of $\hat{A}$, and what does a GCN layer compute for it?
What would the formula do without the self-loop? (b) Two merchants,
each of whose cardholders paid nowhere else: a busy one with 3,000
cardholders, of whom 30 carry a suspicion score of 1 and the rest 0,
and a small one with 3 cardholders, all three scored 1. Both merchants
carry 0 themselves. Compute what each merchant receives under the sum,
the mean over the closed neighbourhood and $\hat{A}$, and say which
merchant each ranks as more exposed.

<Solution>
**(a)** $\tilde{d}_v = 1$, so its row of $\hat{A}$ has a single 1 on
the diagonal: the layer computes $\sigma(x_v W)$, a feed-forward layer
on its own features. Without the self-loop its degree is 0, $A_{v\cdot}$
is all zeros and $D^{-1/2}$ divides by zero; even with the division
defined as 0, its row would be zero and its own features would never
reach the output. The self-loop guarantees $\tilde{d}_v \ge 1$ and that
every node hears itself.

**(b)** With self-loops the busy merchant has $\tilde{d} = 3{,}001$,
the small one $\tilde{d} = 4$, every cardholder $\tilde{d} = 2$.

| | busy merchant | small merchant |
|---|---|---|
| sum | 30 | 3 |
| mean | $30/3{,}001 \approx 0.010$ | $3/4 = 0.75$ |
| $\hat{A}$ | $30/\sqrt{2 \cdot 3{,}001} \approx 0.39$ | $3/\sqrt{2 \cdot 4} \approx 1.06$ |

The sum ranks the busy merchant first: thirty suspects outnumber three,
although they are 1% of its customers against 100%. The mean ranks the
small merchant first and leaves the busy merchant's thirty suspects
almost invisible at 0.010. $\hat{A}$ ranks the small
merchant first too, but the busy merchant's thirty still register at
about a third of the small merchant's value: the count survives on a
compressed scale. The move that transfers:
check every normalisation at a leaf and at a hub before trusting it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "A four-layer GCN sees four hops and the two-layer model only
two, so with the same training data the four-layer model can only do
better." Use the four accounts, and name the step of the argument that
fails.

<Solution>
**The step that fails** is "more information in the receptive field
means more information in the output". The layer does not keep its
inputs side by side; it averages them. Without weights and ReLUs, four
layers compute $\hat{A}^4 X$, and the averaging shrinks every component
of $X$ except the one along $\sqrt{\tilde{d}}$ by the corresponding
eigenvalue, once per layer.

On the four accounts the first channel per unit of
$\sqrt{\tilde{d}_v}$ is $(-0.29, -0.29, 1.41, 0.25)$ at the input,
$(0.02, 0.02, 0.55, 0.22)$ after two layers and
$(0.13, 0.13, 0.29, 0.19)$ after four: account 3's lead over accounts 1
and 2 falls from 1.70 to 0.53 to 0.17, and the limit, reached by about
twelve layers, is 0.175 for all four. What the extra layers add is
mostly the same global average for everyone.

**Why "can only do better" is false even with learned weights:** the
four-layer model has more parameters fitted to the same labels, its
own-node signal passes through four rounds of mixing, and the
optimiser must undo the smoothing with its weights. A model that
contains the two-layer model as a special case would need layers 3 and
4 to act as the identity, and a GCN layer cannot: $\hat{A}$ is applied
whatever $W$ is. That is one reason skip connections are added in
deeper graph networks.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
(a) Show that a two-layer GCN with the identity in place of the ReLU
computes $\operatorname{softmax}(\hat{A}^2 X W^{(0)} W^{(1)})$, and that
this is a logistic regression on the two-hop-smoothed features
$\hat{A}^2 X$. How many free parameters does it really have, with 166
features, hidden width 100 and two classes? (b) Compute $\hat{A}^2 X$
for account 3 from $\hat{A}$ and $X$ of the worked example. (c) What
does the ReLU between the layers add that the linear model cannot
represent?

<Solution>
**(a)** With $\sigma$ the identity, the first layer gives
$\hat{A}XW^{(0)}$ and the second $\hat{A}(\hat{A}XW^{(0)})W^{(1)} =
\hat{A}^2 X (W^{(0)}W^{(1)})$, by associativity. Write
$V = W^{(0)}W^{(1)}$, a (166 × 2) matrix: the model is
$\operatorname{softmax}(\tilde{X}V)$ with $\tilde{X} = \hat{A}^2X$, a
logistic regression on fixed, precomputed features. It stores
$16{,}800$ numbers, but every $V$ it can reach is a 166 × 2 matrix,
and every 166 × 2 matrix is reachable (take $W^{(0)}$ with $V$ in its
first two columns and $W^{(1)}$ picking them out), so it has
$166 \times 2 = 332$ effective parameters. With two classes only the
difference of the two columns matters, 166 numbers.

**(b)** Row 3 of $\hat{A}^2$ is $(0.102, 0.102, 0.375, 0.265)$, so
the first feature is
$0.102(-0.5) + 0.102(-0.5) + 0.375(2.0) + 0.265(0.5) = 0.781$ and the
second $0.102(1.0) + 0.102(0.5) + 0.375(-1.5) + 0.265(-0.5) = -0.542$.

**(c)** With the ReLU, the second layer aggregates

</Solution>

<Exercise>
The bank's graph is the auction graph of <Ref
lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality" id="exercises">the neighbourhood
chapter's exercise</Ref>: two fraudsters, accomplices that trade with
them, honest users; dyadicity $D = 0$ and heterophilicity
$H = 1.06$ with $n_1 = 2$ fraud nodes. The label marks only the
fraudsters. A Skip-GCN is trained whose inputs include a local model's
score, high for the fraudsters. (a) Use the homophily gate to decide
which way the aggregated neighbour score points. (b) What sign should
training give the weight that reads the aggregated score, and could
the exposure walk represent the same thing? (c) What changes if the
label also marks the accomplices?

<Solution>
**(a)** The gate compares $D(n_1 - 1)$, which tracks how many fraud
neighbours a fraud node has, with $H n_1$, which tracks how many a
legitimate node has. Here $D(n_1 - 1) = 0$ and $H n_1 = 2.12$: fraud
nodes have no fraudulent neighbour, while legitimate nodes, the
accomplices, do (the chapter's count gave 0 against 0.83 on average).
A high aggregated neighbour score points at the accomplices, away from
the fraudsters.

**(b)** Negative, with the node's own score carrying a positive weight
through the bypass: among nodes with similar own scores, a suspicious
neighbourhood marks an accomplice, which the label calls legitimate.
The exposure walk cannot: its weights are $\alpha > 0$ and the
entries of $Q$, all non-negative, so suspicion only ever flows outward
with a plus sign, and it ranks the accomplices high.

**(c)** With accomplices labelled as fraud, a fraud node's neighbours
are now partly fraud too, the graph is no longer anti-dyadic for the
label, and the weight on the aggregated score turns positive. The sign
a GCN learns is a property of the label as much as of the graph; the
gate must be run on the label the model is trained on.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

**(b)** Reproduction means the same order (local + embeddings above
local alone, and above local + aggregates, which was 0.481) with a gap
of the reported size, roughly 0.08 to 0.09 in F1. Before believing it:
repeat the GCN training with several seeds, since the embeddings depend
on the GCN's random start, and report the spread of the logistic
regression's F1 across seeds; and report the F1 per test step, since
fifteen steps of very different illicit shares, and the collapse after
step 43, can carry a pooled gap. A gap smaller than the seed spread is
not a finding.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A test set of labelled nodes holds one illicit node in ten. A
classifier calls every node licit. (a) Compute its accuracy and the F1
of the illicit class. (b) *Micro-averaged* F1 pools the true positives,
false positives and false negatives of both classes before computing
precision and recall. Show that for a single-label classifier it equals
accuracy, and compute it here. (c) Every model in the Elliptic
comparison had a micro-averaged F1 between 0.92 and 0.99. What does
(b) say about reading those numbers?

<Solution>
**(a)** Accuracy 0.9. It predicts no illicit node, so illicit recall is
0 and illicit precision is undefined (0 of 0), conventionally 0: F1 is
0.

**(b)** Each node gets one predicted class. A correct prediction is a
true positive for its class; a wrong one is a false positive for the
predicted class and a false negative for the true class. Pooled over
both classes, TP = number correct, FP = FN = number wrong, so micro
precision = micro recall = correct / all = accuracy, and so is their
harmonic mean. Here it is 0.9.

**(c)** A micro-averaged F1 of 0.92 sits just above what calling
everything licit scores on a test set with a tenth illicit; the range
0.92–0.99 is mostly the licit majority. The illicit-class F1 (0.46 to
0.80 for the same models) is the number that separates them. This is
the accuracy trap of a rare class in graph form, and <Ref
lesson="elliptic-illicit-nodes">the Elliptic chapter</Ref> takes it
apart on the actual test steps.
</Solution>

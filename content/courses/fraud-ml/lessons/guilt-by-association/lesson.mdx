A cardholder has never been flagged, but three of the shops they paid at were used by cards later confirmed stolen. How much suspicion should flow from the confirmed frauds across the graph to this cardholder, how far, and how fast should it fade with time — computed for a million nodes before the next payment arrives?

<Ref lesson="communities-and-components">The previous chapter</Ref> ended
on what a connected component cannot do: it labels every member alike,
and it says nothing about users joined to fraud by ordinary payments
rather than by ring rules. The cardholder above is such a user. They
share no card, no device and no address with anyone flagged. All they
share is three shops.

In the card graph that link is a path. The fraud label sits on a payment
node, as in <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph" id="the-tripartite-graph">the
tripartite form</Ref>, so the route from a stolen card's payment to our
cardholder runs payment → shop → the cardholder's own payment →
cardholder: three edges. The one-hop column "fraudulent neighbours" of
<Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="degree-split-by-the-neighbours-labels">the neighbourhood
chapter</Ref> scores this cardholder zero, because its only neighbours
are its own payments and none of them is fraud. Walk counts $A^k\mathbf{f}$
reach three edges and further, but they grow with the number of
neighbours along the way: in <Ref lesson="communities-and-components"
id="exercises">the last exercise on walk counts</Ref>, U3 outranked U1
only because it had more neighbours to walk through.

So the opening asks for a score with five properties. It should take
paths of every length into account and trust short ones more. It should
not reward a node merely for being busy, whether the busy node lies on
the path or at its end. Old payments and old labels
should count less than fresh ones. It must be computable for every node
of a graph of a million cardholders in one night. And it must give a
value for a payment that arrives after that night's computation, within
the authorisation deadline.

## One hop with weights: the relational neighbour classifier

Start with the shortest path. The obvious rule looks one hop out and
asks what share of a node's links lead to fraud. You computed it on the
20-node toy in <Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="exercises">an exercise of the neighbourhood chapter</Ref>, where G
scored 3 of 4 and A 1 of 6. With weights on the edges it reads as
follows.

<Definition title="relational neighbour classifier">
For a node $v$ with neighbours $N(v)$, edge weights $w_{vu}$ and known
labels $y_u$, the *relational neighbour* estimate is

$$
P(\text{fraud} \mid v) = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{u \in N(v),\; y_u = \text{fraud}} w_{vu},
$$

with $Z$ the total weight of $v$'s labelled neighbours. When every node
not known as fraud counts as legitimate, $Z$ is the strength
$s_v = \sum_{u} w_{vu}$, the weighted degree of <Ref
lesson="communities-and-components" id="counting-edge-ends">the
modularity section</Ref>.
</Definition>

With unit weights the rule is a fraction. A node with two fraudulent and
three legitimate neighbours scores $(1 + 1)/(1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1) = 2/5$.
With the decayed weights of <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="choosing-the-decay-from-a-horizon">the first graph chapter</Ref>, a
link made this morning counts nearly 1 and a link from three weeks ago
nearly nothing, so the same five neighbours can score very differently.

<Exercise>
The same node's two fraudulent links have weights 0.9 and 0.1; its three
legitimate links have weights 0.2, 0.2 and 0.1. Compute
$P(\text{fraud} \mid v)$ and say what the weights changed.

<Solution>
Fraud weight $0.9 + 0.1 = 1.0$; total $1.0 + 0.5 = 1.5$;
$P = 1.0/1.5 = 0.67$, against $0.40$ with unit weights. The legitimate
links are old, so they count little, and the recent fraudulent link
dominates. The decay turns "two of five" into "most of what this node did
lately".
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### Neighbours that carry probabilities instead of labels

The labelled rule ignores every neighbour with no label, and in a fraud
graph that is almost every neighbour. The repair is to let each neighbour
contribute its own estimated probability, which can come from the
supervised model of the earlier modules scoring the neighbour on its own
columns:

$$
P(\text{fraud} \mid v) = \frac{1}{Z} \sum_{u \in N(v)} w_{vu}\, P(\text{fraud} \mid u), \qquad Z = s_v .
$$

This is the *probabilistic relational neighbour* rule. A node whose five
neighbours carry probabilities 0.25, 0.20, 0.80, 0.10 and 0.90 at unit
weights scores $2.25/5 = 0.45$.

Both rules push suspicion from fraud onto whatever fraud links to. That
is only right where a fraudulent neighbour is evidence of fraud, which
<Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="which-way-a-fraudulent-neighbour-points">the homophily gate</Ref>
checks: fraud nodes must have more fraudulent neighbours on average than
legitimate nodes, roughly dyadicity above heterophilicity. On an auction
graph, where fraudsters trade with accomplices, both rules raise the
accomplices and the honest buyers, and no setting of the rule can turn
that around.

### Repeating the rule: collective inference

The probabilistic rule needs the neighbours' probabilities, and those
depend on the neighbours' own neighbours. So the rule is repeated: score
every unknown node from its neighbours' current scores, then do it
again. The exercise on the toy ran two rounds; H, zero after the first,
scored 0.25 after the second, because suspicion had arrived from two
steps away. Updating linked unknown nodes from one another until their
estimates settle is called *collective inference*.

Run to the end, plain repetition fails. Every round replaces a node's
score by an average of its neighbours', the known fraud nodes stay
fixed at 1, and nothing else is fixed. Averages of numbers that are at
most 1 and pulled toward 1 at the fraud nodes creep up everywhere: on a
connected graph every unknown node converges to 1, which says that
everyone near anyone is a fraudster. What the repetition lacks is a
discount for distance, a rule by which suspicion weakens with every hop.

## Following a walk: PageRank and why it ranks the busy

The discount by distance already appeared once: in the walk-count
exercise of the previous chapter, a path of length $k$ was weighted by
$\beta^k$. Busy nodes broke it in two places. On the way, every node
multiplies the walks through it by its number of neighbours, so the sum
converges only for $\beta$ below $1/\lambda_{\max}$, and $\lambda_{\max}$
is set by the busiest nodes. A hub joined to 400 users who touch nothing
else has $\lambda_{\max} = 20$ (put 20 on the hub and 1 on each user: one
product with $A$ gives the hub 400 and each user 20), so $\beta$ must fall
below 0.05, and the discount meant to choose the reach is chosen by one
hub. At the end of the path, U3 outranked U1 because it had more
neighbours. A walker that divides what it carries among the neighbours
of the node it leaves removes the first defect.

### A walker who follows edges and sometimes restarts

Put a walker on the graph. Standing at node $j$, it moves to neighbour
$i$ with probability proportional to the edge's weight:

$$
Q_{ij} = \frac{w_{ij}}{s_j}, \qquad \text{that is} \qquad Q = W S^{-1},
$$

with $W$ the weight matrix of entries $w_{ij}$ and $S$ the diagonal
matrix of the strengths. Every column of $Q$ sums to 1, since it divides the weights at $j$ by
their total; $Q$ is the *column-normalised* weight matrix, and the column
index is where the walker comes from. A walker at G, with four neighbours
of weight 1, goes to A, D, F or I with probability $1/4$ each. If $x$
holds the probability of standing at each node, one step later it is
$Qx$: the step divides each node's probability among its neighbours, so
a busy node hands on small shares.

Then add a restart. At every step the walker follows an edge with
probability $\alpha$; with probability $1 - \alpha$ it jumps to a node
drawn from a fixed *restart vector* $e$, whose entries are non-negative
and sum to 1. The share of time the walker spends at each node settles
to a vector $\xi$ that one more step leaves unchanged:

$$
\xi = \alpha Q \xi + (1 - \alpha)\, e .
$$

<Definition title="random walk with restart; PageRank">
For a column-normalised weight matrix $Q$, a follow probability
$0 < \alpha < 1$ and a restart vector $e$, the *random walk with
restart* score is the solution $\xi$ of
$\xi = \alpha Q \xi + (1 - \alpha) e$. With $e$ uniform, $e_v = 1/n$ for
all $n$ nodes, $\xi$ is the *PageRank* of the graph, first used to rank
web pages linked by hyperlinks. The conventional follow probability is
$\alpha = 0.85$.
</Definition>

Substituting the equation into itself shows what the score adds up:

$$
\xi = (1-\alpha)\, e + \alpha Q \big((1-\alpha) e + \alpha Q \xi\big) = \dots = (1 - \alpha) \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \alpha^k Q^k e .
$$

$Q^k e$ is where a walker started from $e$ stands after $k$ steps, and
$\alpha^k$ is the chance that it made $k$ steps without restarting. The
score of a node is therefore a sum over paths of every length from the
restart nodes, with weight $\alpha^k$ for length $k$, and every step of
a path divided by the strength of the node it leaves. Because the
columns of $Q$ sum to 1, every $Q^k e$ is again a probability vector: a
busy node on the way splits what it receives instead of multiplying it.
The sum therefore converges for every $\alpha < 1$ on every graph, the
scores add up to 1 however large the hubs, and $\alpha$ can be chosen
for reach alone.

### What the division leaves: the endpoint's own strength

Take the five users of the walk-count exercise, edges U1–U2, U1–U3,
U2–U3, U3–U4 and U4–U5, restart at the flagged U2, $\alpha = 0.85$. The
walk settles at U2 0.322, U3 0.282, U1 0.217, U4 0.125 and U5 0.053,
summing to 1. U3 still outranks U1, by more than the walk counts had it
(0.282 against 0.217, where the $\beta$-sum gave 0.67 against 0.62).

The reason is the endpoint's own doors. U2 has two neighbours and hands
U1 and U3 equal halves of whatever it holds; what separates them is
what arrives through their other edges. U1 has one more door, from U3.
U3 has two, from U1 and from U4, and a node with more edges is entered
more often. Dividing each score by the node's strength undoes that:
U1 $0.217/2 = 0.108$, U3 $0.282/3 = 0.094$, and the order reverses. Per
link, U1 is the more exposed: both its neighbours are U2 and a
neighbour of U2, while one of U3's three, U4, leads away from U2.

Dividing by strength is not only a repair for these two nodes: the
divided score obeys an averaging rule of its own. Write each score as strength times a per-link
value, $\xi_v = s_v p_v$, that is $\xi = S p$ and $p = S^{-1}\xi$. Put
this into the restart equation with $Q = W S^{-1}$:
$S p = \alpha W S^{-1} S p + (1 - \alpha) e = \alpha W p + (1 - \alpha) e$,
and divide by $S$:

$$
p = \alpha S^{-1} W p + (1 - \alpha) S^{-1} e, \qquad
p_v = \alpha \cdot \frac{1}{s_v} \sum_u w_{vu}\, p_u + (1 - \alpha)\frac{e_v}{s_v} .
$$

The first term is a weighted average of the neighbours' $p$, and an
average does not grow with the number of neighbours. On the five users,
U1 is not a restart node, so $p_{U1} = 0.85 \times (0.161 + 0.094)/2 =
0.108$, from U2's $0.322/2$ and U3's $0.282/3$. So the walk removes the
growth through busy intermediate nodes, and what it returns is exactly
this average multiplied by the strength of the node it lands on;
$p = S^{-1}\xi$ is the per-link reading to use when the endpoint's
busyness must be removed.

### Plain PageRank ranks by degree

With a uniform restart the walker starts anywhere, and the labels never
enter. What is left to rank by is the shape of the graph. On an
undirected graph the shape enters through the effect the five users
showed: a node with more edge weight is entered more often. With no
labels beside it, that effect is the whole score: activity.

<Proposition title="the walk without restart lands on the strengths">
On an undirected weighted graph with total edge weight $M$, the vector
$\pi_v = s_v / 2M$ satisfies $Q\pi = \pi$: it is the stationary
distribution of the walk without restart ($\alpha = 1$), and on a
connected graph it is the only one.

<Proof>
$(Q\pi)_i = \sum_j \frac{w_{ij}}{s_j}\cdot\frac{s_j}{2M} =
\frac{1}{2M}\sum_j w_{ij} = \frac{s_i}{2M} = \pi_i$, using
$w_{ij} = w_{ji}$. The strengths sum to $2M$, so $\pi$ is a probability
vector. That it is the only one is taken here without proof: on a
connected graph the walker can get from any node to any other, and that
is what leaves the walk a single stationary vector.
</Proof>
</Proposition>

At $\alpha = 0.85$ the uniform restart pulls the scores a little toward
equal, but not far. On the 20-node toy, plain PageRank puts A first, the
node of degree 6, and its correlation with degree is 0.97. Node Q comes
second with degree 3, because two leaves send it everything they
receive. The four fraud nodes D, E, F and I are scattered through the
ranking according to their degrees.

<KeyFact>
Plain PageRank measures activity, not suspicion: without restart it is
exactly each node's share of the edge weight, so on a card graph the
busiest merchant ranks first whatever its labels.
</KeyFact>

## Restarting at the known fraud: the exposure score

The restart vector is the one place where the labels can enter. Put all
of it on the known fraud: with $\mathbf{f}$ the fraud indicator vector
and $|\mathbf{f}|$ the number of fraud nodes, $e = \mathbf{f}/|\mathbf{f}|$.
The walker now starts only at fraud and returns there whenever it
restarts.

<Definition title="exposure score">
The *exposure score* of a node is its random-walk-with-restart score
when the restart vector is spread over the known fraud nodes:
$\xi = \alpha Q \xi + (1 - \alpha)\,\mathbf{f}/|\mathbf{f}|$. It is the
long-run share of time a walker that restarts at fraud spends at the
node.
</Definition>

On the toy, with the restart on D, E, F and I, the ranking becomes D
(0.169), F (0.145), E (0.114), G (0.109), I (0.094), then A (0.088). The
three nodes of the fraud triangle lead. Then comes G, which is not known
as fraud, ahead of I, which is.

### Why G outranks I

G has four neighbours, three of them restart nodes. Every step from D, F
or I hands G a quarter, a third and a quarter of their current
probability, and those three are where the walker returns most often. I
is a restart node, but none of its four neighbours is: the probability
it receives at each restart leaves at rate $\alpha$ toward A, G, K and
N, and comes back mainly through G. Exposure measures the fraud a node
sits among, and G sits among more of it than I does; whether G is the
next fraudster or the next victim, as <Ref
lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality" id="reading-the-table">the feature
table</Ref> already warned, the score cannot say.

Inside the triangle the order D, F, E is the order of the degrees, 4, 3
and 2.

<Remark title="spreading versus averaging">
The relational neighbour rule is the row-normalised step, $p = S^{-1} W
p$: each node averages its neighbours. The walk is the column-normalised
step, $\xi = W S^{-1} \xi$: each node spreads what it holds among its
neighbours. [The per-link
form](#what-the-division-leaves-the-endpoints-own-strength) joins them
through $\xi = S p$, which is why D, F and E, each beside the other two
frauds, follow their degrees. Divided by degree, the triangle reads E
(0.057), F (0.048), D (0.042): per link, E is the most exposed.
</Remark>

### Which way to walk on a transfer graph

On the card graph an edge has no direction, because money always flows
from cardholder to merchant. On a graph of transfers between accounts it
has one, and the walk can follow it either way. Restart at a confirmed
mule and walk along the edges, and the score lands on whom the mule
paid. Walk against them, along incoming transfers, and it lands on who
fed the mule, which is where the organisers and the next mules usually
are. The second direction is the one laundering investigations want;
in matrix terms it is the same equation with the weight matrix
transposed.

## Computing it for a million nodes: power iteration

The definition says what $\xi$ is. Computing it for every node of a card
graph, every night, is a separate problem.

### Not by inverting a matrix

The equation is linear, $(I - \alpha Q)\xi = (1 - \alpha) e$, so
$\xi = (1 - \alpha)(I - \alpha Q)^{-1} e$. For a million nodes,
$I - \alpha Q$ is a $10^6 \times 10^6$ matrix. $Q$ itself is
<Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="matrix-or-list-what-the-graph-costs-to-store">sparse</Ref>, one
entry per edge end, but the inverse of a sparse matrix of a connected
graph is dense: $10^{12}$ numbers, eight terabytes in double precision,
before any arithmetic.

### One sparse product per step

Instead, start from the restart vector and apply the equation's right
side again and again:

$$
\xi^{(0)} = e, \qquad \xi^{(k+1)} = \alpha Q \xi^{(k)} + (1 - \alpha)\, e .
$$

This is *power iteration*. Each step is one product of the sparse $Q$
with a vector, a single pass over the edge list.

<Example title="the first step on the toy">
Start with $\xi^{(0)} = 0.25$ at each of D, E, F, I and 0 elsewhere.

**G** (not a restart node) receives from its fraud neighbours D
(degree 4), F (degree 3) and I (degree 4):
$0.85\,(0.25/4 + 0.25/3 + 0.25/4) = 0.85 \times 0.208 = 0.177$.

**D** receives from E (degree 2) and F (degree 3), and gets its restart
share: $0.85\,(0.25/2 + 0.25/3) + 0.15 \times 0.25 = 0.177 + 0.0375 =
0.215$.

After one step G already stands at 0.177, third behind D and F.
</Example>

<Exercise>

<Solution>

**A**: one neighbour holds anything, I (degree 4):
$0.85 \times 0.25/4 = 0.053$.

I falls because everything it held moved to its four neighbours, none of
which sent anything back yet. It recovers over the next steps as

</Solution>
</Exercise>

Why does it converge, and how fast? The difference from the fixed point
obeys $\xi^{(k+1)} - \xi = \alpha Q(\xi^{(k)} - \xi)$, and a
column-normalised $Q$ never increases the sum of absolute values of a
vector. Each step therefore shrinks the distance to $\xi$ by at least
the factor $\alpha$, whatever the graph, and the fixed point is unique.
On the toy the change per step falls from 0.78 at the first step to
0.0016 at the twentieth and $2 \times 10^{-6}$ at the fiftieth.

```python
import numpy as np
import scipy.sparse as sp

def exposure(W, seeds, alpha=0.85, tol=1e-9, max_steps=200):
    s = np.asarray(W.sum(axis=0)).ravel()
    Q = W @ sp.diags(1.0 / np.where(s > 0, s, 1))  # an isolated node keeps a zero column
    e = seeds / seeds.sum()
    xi = e.copy()
    for _ in range(max_steps):
        new = alpha * (Q @ xi) + (1 - alpha) * e
        if np.abs(new - xi).sum() < tol:
            return new
        xi = new
    return xi
```

On the toy this stops at the 85th step, the first whose change falls
below the tolerance. A month of card payments is tens of millions of
edges; at a pass over the edge list per step and about ninety steps,
the computation fits in a night with room for several graphs.

<KeyFact>
The exposure score costs one pass over the edge list per step, and every
step cuts the remaining error by the factor $\alpha$; $\alpha$ sets both
how far suspicion travels and how many steps the computation needs.
</KeyFact>

## Labels on payments, weights that fade: the card graph

The toy put labels on nodes and gave every edge weight 1. A card graph
has its labels on payments and its edges age. Both were prepared in the
first graph chapter: the payment became a node of <Ref
lesson="fraud-as-a-graph" id="the-tripartite-graph">the tripartite
matrix</Ref> $Q_{\text{tri}}$, and every edge carries the weight
$e^{-\gamma h}$ of <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="choosing-the-decay-from-a-horizon">its age</Ref>. Two changes turn
the walk into a card-fraud score.

**The walk runs on the tripartite graph.** Normalise the columns of
$Q_{\text{tri}}$ and use it as $Q$. A payment node has exactly one
cardholder and one merchant neighbour, so the walker alternates: entity,
payment, entity, payment. A cardholder's exposure is what reaches it
through its own payments from the merchants where fraud happened.

**The restart sits on confirmed fraud payments, weighted by age.** Entry
$i$ of the restart vector is $e^{-\gamma h_i}$ if payment $i$ is a
confirmed fraud, with $h_i$ the time since it was confirmed, and 0 for
every other node; the vector is then normalised to sum to 1. A fraud
confirmed this morning restarts the walk more often than one confirmed
three weeks ago.

<Example title="the card toy, week graph">
Take the five payments of the first graph chapter with their week
weights: $p_1$ ($c_1$–$m_1$, 0.97, fraud), $p_2$ ($c_1$–$m_2$, 0.59),
$p_3$ ($c_2$–$m_1$, 0.91), $p_4$ ($c_2$–$m_3$, 0.17), $p_5$
($c_3$–$m_3$, 0.70). One fraud, so the restart vector is 1 at $p_1$.

**Step 1.** $p_1$ has two edges of equal weight, so it hands
$0.85 \times 1/2 = 0.425$ to each of $c_1$ and $m_1$, and keeps the
restart share 0.15.

**Step 2.** $m_1$ has strength $0.97 + 0.91 = 1.88$, so it passes
$0.91/1.88$ of what it holds to $p_3$, the payment of $c_2$:
$0.85 \times 0.425 \times 0.484 = 0.175$. Suspicion has reached the
payment of a cardholder who never touched the stolen card.

**Converged.** $p_1$ 0.324, $m_1$ 0.186, $c_1$ 0.175, $p_3$ 0.114,
$p_2$ 0.088, $c_2$ 0.052, $m_2$ 0.037, and the far corner $p_4$,
$m_3$, $p_5$, $c_3$ between 0.003 and 0.008.
</Example>

### Three horizons, nine columns, computed at midnight

Frauds move at different speeds, which is why the first graph chapter
built the graph three times, with horizons of a day, a week and a month.
The walk runs once on each, and each run scores every cardholder,
merchant and payment. That gives nine columns per payment: the exposure
of its cardholder, of its merchant, and of the payment itself, at three
horizons.

The scores are recomputed every night at midnight and read during the
next day. Like every label-derived feature they obey <Ref
lesson="point-in-time-features"
id="two-ways-a-training-row-can-know-too-much">the point-in-time
rule</Ref>: tonight's restart vector may hold only the frauds whose
confirmation has arrived by tonight, and a training row reads the scores
of the night before it, never a later night's.

One property of the numbers matters when they become columns. The
exposure scores of a graph sum to 1, so as the graph grows each score
shrinks, and a value means different things on nights with different
numbers of nodes. Multiplying by the night's node count, so that 1 means
average exposure, keeps a column comparable from one night to the next.
The score is a ranking of nodes on one night, not a probability of
fraud.

## A payment the graph has not seen: the local update

At 10:14 cardholder $c_3$ pays at merchant $m_1$ for the first time. The
pair has no payment in last night's graph, so the payment has no
exposure, and rerunning the walk on a graph of a million nodes inside
the <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="the-deadline-splits-the-work-in-two">authorisation deadline</Ref> is
out of the question.

What the walk would do with the new payment can be read off its
columns. Add a payment node $p_6$ with edges of weight 1 to $c_3$ and
$m_1$, the weight of a payment made now. The strength of $c_3$ rises
from $s_{c_3}$ to $s_{c_3} + 1$, so a walker at $c_3$ now moves to $p_6$
with probability $1/(s_{c_3} + 1)$; likewise at $m_1$. One step of the
walk from last night's scores therefore brings $p_6$

$$
\xi_{p_6} \approx \alpha \left( \frac{\xi_{c_3}}{s_{c_3} + 1} + \frac{\xi_{m_1}}{s_{m_1} + 1} \right),
$$

with no restart term, since $p_6$ is not a confirmed fraud. The new
payment absorbs a share of each endpoint's exposure, and the share is
larger for an endpoint with few other payments.

<Example title="the 10:14 payment">
From the converged card toy: $\xi_{c_3} = 0.0028$, $s_{c_3} = 0.70$;
$\xi_{m_1} = 0.186$, $s_{m_1} = 1.88$.

$$
\xi_{p_6} \approx 0.85 \left( \frac{0.0028}{1.70} + \frac{0.186}{2.88} \right) = 0.85\,(0.0017 + 0.065) = 0.85 \times 0.066 = 0.056 .
$$

Rerunning the whole walk with $p_6$ in the graph gives 0.075. The local
value runs low because it uses last night's endpoint scores: in the
rerun the new edge lets mass from $m_1$ reach $c_3$, whose exposure
rises from 0.0028 to 0.042. Both values place the payment in the same
neighbourhood, below $p_2$ (0.088) and far above the corner's 0.003 to
0.008, almost all of it from the merchant.
</Example>

Two cases complete the rule. If the pair has paid each other before, the
payment takes the score of their most recent payment: that payment node
sits between the same two endpoints, so last night's walk has already
scored a node in the place the new one would take. If the cardholder or the merchant is new to the
graph, its exposure is 0: nothing has reached it yet.

## What the exposure adds to a detector

The exposure score is never used alone. Its nine values become columns
beside the history features of <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates"
id="summarising-a-cards-past-recency-frequency-monetary">the feature
module</Ref>, and the question is what they add there.

### Five weeks of a card issuer

The measurement comes from about 3.3 million card payments of a large
Belgian issuer over five consecutive weeks, about 48,000 of them fraud
(about 1 in 70), each labelled by investigators within two weeks.
Payments refused for ordinary reasons (wrong PIN, input errors) and the
few above €5,000 were removed. The first two weeks served only to build
history and exposure columns for the next two, which were the training
set (2.2 million payments); the fifth week, about 500,000 payments, was
the test set, scored out of time. The model was a random forest of 500
trees, each tree grown on all the training frauds and twice as many
genuine payments drawn at random. The columns: 9 exposure scores, 60
recency, frequency and monetary aggregates, and a few customer and
location fields. The score reported is ROC-AUC on the test week.

<Figure
  src="./figures/ablation.svg"
  alt="Horizontal bars of test-week ROC-AUC on an axis from 0.90 to 1.00: network exposure only (9 columns) 0.920 in amber; recency, frequency, monetary plus customer data 0.953 in blue; the same plus country, currency and averages 0.955 in blue; all columns 0.986 in grey. A note: the axis starts at 0.90; correlation between the network columns and the rest at most 0.1"
  caption="Nine exposure columns alone rank worse than the history columns, and lift them by three points of AUC when added: they carry what the history does not."

The nine exposure columns alone reach 0.920; the history and customer
columns 0.953; all together 0.986. No exposure column correlates with
any other column by more than 0.1. The exposure is weaker than the
history on its own and almost unrelated to it, which is why adding it
helps: it measures where a payment sits, the history measures what the
card did.

Read with <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="how-much-of-the-curve-a-hundred-alerts-can-reach">this course's
metrics</Ref>, the numbers say less than they seem to. ROC-AUC averages
over false-positive rates a team of a hundred calls a day never reaches.
At the experiment's other reported point, 1% false positives, the forest
finds 87% of the frauds, but 1% of a week of 500,000 payments is about
700 genuine alerts a day. The test is out of time with no delay block,
the split <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential" id="exercises">the
delay exercise</Ref> took apart: weeks 3 and 4 were trained on with
labels that would only arrive during or after the test week. And the
setup does not say whether each night's restart vector held only labels
confirmed by then. The result shows that the graph carries signal
the history does not; it does not say how many more compromised cards
the team would find in its hundred.

### What the signs say

A logistic regression fitted on the same columns gives the direction of
each. The month-horizon exposures of the cardholder and of the payment
come out positive: more exposure, more fraud. The month-horizon exposure
of the merchant comes out negative. The Remark on spreading suggests one
reading, offered as a reading and not shown: a month of walk mass
collects at merchants in proportion to their strength, so a merchant's
long-horizon exposure measures in part how busy and established it is,
and the fraud signal it carries is already in the payment's own score.

### Anticipating and forgiving

A propagation of the same kind, built for social-security fraud, runs on
a bipartite graph of companies and their resources: employees,
suppliers, accountants, machinery. Companies in a fraudulent set-up go
bankrupt to escape their contributions and pass their resources on to
new companies; the confirmed fraudulent companies are the restart nodes,
and edge weights fall with the age of each company–resource link.

<Figure src="./figures/gotcha-exposure-anticipating-forgiving.png" alt="Scatter of resources' exposure scores (0 to 1) against the number of fraudulent companies they were linked to (0 to 8), on social-security fraud data. Scores rise with the count along a fitted line (R² = 0.87). At zero links an ellipse marks resources scoring up to about 0.25 above the trend, labelled anticipating effect; at one link an ellipse marks low scores below the line, labelled forgiving effect. A horizontal line near 0.1 separates high-risk from low-risk." caption="Exposure against direct fraudulent links: it rises with them, with outliers above the trend (anticipating) and below it (forgiving)." float="right" width="m" />

Plotting each resource's exposure against the number of fraudulent
companies it was linked to shows the two effects that make the walk more
than a count. Some resources score high although they were never linked
to a fraudulent company: fraud is close in the graph and approaching,
the *anticipating* effect. Some resources that were linked to fraud
score low, because the link is old and the decay has worn it down, the
*forgiving* effect. A count of fraudulent neighbours shows neither. The
0-to-1 axis is not the scale of the scores defined here: that study
rescaled its scores to 0-1, and its risk line is a cut chosen on that
scale, not a probability of fraud.

That system was evaluated the way this course evaluates: trained at one
date, scored at the next, and judged by how many of the 100 top-ranked
companies, the number the inspectors could visit, turned out to be
fraudulent. The model was a random forest on the companies' own columns
plus the network columns, compared with a baseline on the companies'
own columns alone, and a listed company counted as a fraud whether the
fraud surfaced within six months, within a year or later. Over three
years the baseline found 24, 19 and 13 frauds in its top 100; the model
with network columns 44, 37 and 44. The study does not report how many
companies were scored each year or what share of them were fraudulent,
so there is no random top 100 to set these against: the numbers say
only that the network columns nearly doubled what the same 100 visits
found in two years and more than tripled it in the third.

<KeyFact>
Exposure enters a detector as columns beside the history features, and
it pays because it is almost uncorrelated with them; its own value is a
ranking of one night's graph, never a probability of fraud.
</KeyFact>

## ★ Other collective inference procedures

The random walk with restart is one member of a family of procedures
that score linked unknown nodes jointly. Four others are common, and
each starts from a local model that scores every node on its own
columns.

**Iterative classification** assigns each unknown node the most
probable label from the local model, then repeatedly refits a
<Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="link-columns-for-a-regression">relational logistic regression</Ref>
on link columns (count, binary, mode of the neighbours' current labels)
and reassigns hard labels until they stop changing.

**Relaxation labelling** does the same with probabilities instead of
hard labels: each round recomputes every unknown node's probability from
its neighbours' current probabilities, the repeated probabilistic
relational neighbour rule of this chapter.

**Gibbs sampling** draws a label for every unknown node from its current
probability, recomputes the probabilities from the drawn labels, draws
again, and so on. The first rounds, the *burn-in*, are discarded; the
final estimate is the share of later rounds in which a node was drawn as
fraud.

**Loopy belief propagation** passes messages along edges: each node
tells each neighbour how likely each class is for it, given everything
else it has heard, and a node's belief is the normalised product of its
incoming messages.

Exact joint inference over a graph's labels is NP-hard; these procedures
are approximations that cost a pass over the edges per round. None of
them is guaranteed to converge, and the order in which nodes are updated
can change the result. The random walk with restart is the exception on
that count, because each step contracts by $\alpha$.

<Conclusion>
You can now answer the opening's three questions with one equation. How
much suspicion flows to a node is its share of a walk that restarts at
the confirmed frauds, $\xi = \alpha Q \xi + (1 - \alpha) e$, a sum over
paths of every length weighted by $\alpha^k$ and divided at every step
by the strength of the node it leaves, so a busy node on the way cannot
multiply it. The share still grows with the node's own strength (U3 0.282
above U1 0.217); $p = S^{-1}\xi$, suspicion per unit of strength, removes
that (U1 0.108 above U3 0.094). How far is set by $\alpha$. How
fast it fades is set by the decayed edge weights and the decayed restart
weights of each horizon. You can say why the uniform restart of plain
PageRank only measures activity, compute the walk by power iteration at
a pass over the edges per step, run it on the tripartite card graph to
get nine columns a night, and score a payment the night's graph never
saw by the local update. On the toy, G, never flagged, outranks I, a
known fraud; on real card data the nine columns are weak alone and
useful beside the history.

Every step of this walk applies a fixed rule: average the neighbours
with weights set by the graph, add the node's own restart term, repeat.
Nothing in it is learned from the labels, so it cannot learn that on an
auction graph the neighbours of fraud are accomplices. The next problem
is a step whose weights are learned: <Ref
lesson="graph-neural-networks">graph convolutional networks</Ref>.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Push the follow probability to its limits on the toy with the restart
at D, E, F, I. (a) What does $\xi$ become as $\alpha \to 0$, and as
$\alpha \to 1$? Why is neither a fraud score? (b) At $\alpha = 0.5$ the
order of G and I is reversed. Explain the direction of the change.
(c) A new horizon graph has no confirmed fraud yet. What does the

<Solution>
**(a)** As $\alpha \to 0$ the walker always restarts: $\xi = e$, 0.25 at
each known fraud and 0 everywhere else. The score is the label
restated, and G scores 0. As $\alpha \to 1$ the walker never restarts,
and by the Proposition its time-share tends to $s_v/2M$, whatever the
restart vector: the labels drop out and the score is degree again (at
$\alpha = 0.999$ the toy's top node is A). The restart is what carries
the labels, and the follow step is what carries the graph; each limit
throws one of them away. The iteration also slows as $\alpha \to 1$,
since the error shrinks only by $\alpha$ per step.

**(b)** With $\alpha = 0.5$ a walker restarts every other step on
average, so probability stays close to the restart nodes, and the score
of I, a restart node, is propped up by its own restart share. G
receives only what travels one step, which halves. Larger $\alpha$ lets
the walk travel, so a node surrounded by fraud gains on a fraud node
surrounded by none. $\alpha$ is therefore a choice about reach, to be
selected like any other setting on validation folds.

**(c)** The restart vector is $\mathbf{0}/0$, undefined. Run with
$e = 0$, the iteration gives $\xi = 0$ for every node. A zero would tell
a model "no exposure", which is false: there is no information. The
column should be missing for that night, and the model must handle a
missing value, as with the <Ref lesson="behavioural-profiles"
id="how-much-history-the-arc-needs">periodic flag without enough
history</Ref>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "A merchant with a high exposure score is a fraudulent
merchant." Name each step that fails, using the card toy, where $m_1$
has the highest merchant exposure (0.186).

<Solution>
**The score measures position, not conduct.** $m_1$ is exposed because a
stolen card paid there once. A merchant where stolen numbers are used is
usually a victim of the scheme or simply a popular place to spend them;
a terminal where numbers were skimmed is compromised, not complicit.
Nothing in the walk distinguishes these.

**The score grows with strength.** By the per-link form, $\xi_v = s_v p_v$: the
walk's mass at a node is an averaged suspicion times its strength. A
large merchant collects mass from every fraud payment made there, and
in a month a large merchant sees some. Divided by strength, $m_1$'s
$0.186/1.88 = 0.099$ is below $c_1$'s $0.175/1.56 = 0.112$.

**The fitted sign says the opposite.** On the issuer's data the
month-horizon merchant exposure enters a logistic regression with a
negative coefficient: with the other columns held fixed, payments at
highly exposed merchants were less likely fraud.

**What the score is evidence of:** fraud has passed through this
merchant recently, so its next payments and the cards that pay there
deserve a higher rank. It is a column for scoring payments and cards,
and a reason to look at the merchant, not a verdict on it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
(a) From $\xi^{(0)} = e$, show that
$\xi^{(k)} = (1 - \alpha)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \alpha^j Q^j e + \alpha^k Q^k e$,
and that $\lVert \xi^{(k)} - \xi \rVert_1 \le 2\alpha^k$, where
$\lVert x \rVert_1 = \sum_v |x_v|$. (b) How many steps guarantee an
error below $10^{-6}$ at $\alpha = 0.85$? (c) The code stops when the
change in one step falls below a tolerance. Show that
$\lVert \xi^{(k)} - \xi \rVert_1 \le \frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}
\lVert \xi^{(k)} - \xi^{(k-1)} \rVert_1$, so that the stopping rule
controls the error.

<Solution>
**(a)** Induction. For $k = 0$ the sum is empty and the formula gives
$e$. If it holds for $k$, then
$\xi^{(k+1)} = \alpha Q \xi^{(k)} + (1-\alpha) e =
(1-\alpha)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\alpha^{j+1}Q^{j+1}e + \alpha^{k+1}Q^{k+1}e +
(1-\alpha)e$, and the first and last terms make the sum up to $j = k$.

For the bound, subtract the fixed-point equation from the step:
$\xi^{(k+1)} - \xi = \alpha Q(\xi^{(k)} - \xi)$. For any vector $x$,
$\lVert Qx \rVert_1 = \sum_i |\sum_j Q_{ij}x_j| \le \sum_j |x_j|
\sum_i Q_{ij} = \lVert x \rVert_1$, because the columns of $Q$ sum to 1.
So the error shrinks by at least $\alpha$ per step:
$\lVert \xi^{(k)} - \xi\rVert_1 \le \alpha^k \lVert e - \xi \rVert_1$.
Both $e$ and $\xi$ are probability vectors, so their distance is at most
2.

**(b)** $2 \times 0.85^k < 10^{-6}$ needs
$k > \ln(2 \times 10^6)/\ln(1/0.85) = 14.5/0.163 = 89.3$: 90 steps,
whatever the size of the graph. On a graph of 30 million edges that is
90 passes over the edge list.

**(c)** By the triangle inequality and the contraction,
$\lVert \xi^{(k)} - \xi \rVert_1 = \lVert \alpha Q(\xi^{(k-1)} - \xi)
\rVert_1 \le \alpha\lVert \xi^{(k-1)} - \xi^{(k)} \rVert_1 +
\alpha\lVert \xi^{(k)} - \xi \rVert_1$, so
$(1-\alpha)\lVert \xi^{(k)} - \xi\rVert_1 \le \alpha \lVert \xi^{(k)} -
\xi^{(k-1)}\rVert_1$. At $\alpha = 0.85$ the error is at most 5.7 times
the last change: stopping at a change of $10^{-9}$ leaves an error below
$6 \times 10^{-9}$. The move that transfers: a contraction lets the
observable step size certify the unobservable error.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="elliptic-a-graph-whose-labels-come-from-names">Elliptic graph</Ref>,
a colleague proposes to restart a walk at the illicit transactions of
time steps 1 to 34, walk against the coin flow, and score the
transactions of step 35, measuring average precision against step 35's
labels with in-degree as the baseline. (a) What in the structure of the
data set makes this design impossible as stated? (b) Design an
experiment the data can support that asks whether the walk adds signal
beyond degree, and say what it does and does not measure. (c) State the

<Solution>
**(a)** Each time step is a separate connected component, and no edge

**(b)** Work inside each test step. For each step $t$ from 35 to 49,

**(c)** The walk's AP clearly above both the base rate of the scored

<Exercise>
Write one power-iteration step $\xi \leftarrow \alpha Q \xi + (1 -

and $H'$ are matrices with one row per node, $X$ holds each node's own

Take $H = \xi$ and $X = e$ as single columns, $\hat{A} = Q$,

$X W_2$ is the node's own input, carried past the aggregation.

With many columns, learned weights and a nonlinearity, the same shape is
a layer of a graph network with a skip connection: each node's new
representation mixes a normalised sum of its neighbours'
representations with its own features, in proportions fitted to the
labels instead of fixed at 0.85 and 0.15. Three things change. The
weights can be negative, so the layer can learn that on an inverse
homophilic graph fraud's neighbours are less suspect, which the walk
cannot. The inputs can be every column of the node, not only a restart
indicator. And two layers see two hops, where power iteration runs to
convergence over ninety. That layer is the subject of <Ref
lesson="graph-neural-networks">graph convolutional networks</Ref>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A bank confirms frauds with a delay of $\delta = 7$ days, and builds
each night's restart vector, as the point-in-time rule requires, only
from confirmations that have arrived. Edges older than their horizon
are dropped from each graph (day, week, month). Merchant $m$ is
compromised on day 0, with fraud payments on days 0, 1 and 2, each
confirmed 7 days after it happened; cardholder $c$ paid at $m$ on day 1
and nowhere near any other fraud. (a) On which night does $c$ first
have nonzero exposure in each of the three graphs? (b) Which horizon
does the delay hollow out, and in general? (c) Where do short-horizon
graphs get their seeds in practice, and what does that imply about
them?

<Solution>
**(a)** The day-0 fraud is confirmed on day 7, so no restart vector
holds any of $m$'s frauds before night 7. On night 7 the fraud payment
of day 0 is 7 days old.

*Day graph:* only edges from the last day survive, and on night 7 both
the fraud payment (day 0) and $c$'s payment (day 1) are long gone. The
fraud payments of days 1 and 2 are confirmed on nights 8 and 9, when
they are 7 days old. $c$ never gets day-graph exposure from $m$.

*Week graph:* on night 7 the day-0 fraud payment sits at the edge of
the horizon and $c$'s day-1 payment is 6 days old; on night 8 the day-1
fraud payment is seeded and $c$'s payment is 7 days old. $c$ is exposed
on nights 7 and 8 at most, through edges near the horizon's floor, and
then drops out.

*Month graph:* from night 7, and for the next three weeks or so, while
$c$'s payment and the fraud payments are younger than a month.

**(b)** Any horizon no longer than $\delta$ is emptied: its seeds are
older than the graph remembers. With $\delta = 7$ the day graph has no
seeds from slow confirmations at all, and the week graph keeps them for
a day or two. In general a graph whose horizon $H$ is at most $\delta$
can be seeded only by confirmations faster than $H$.

**(c)** From the fast labels: the cards the investigators called after
an alert, confirmed the same day (<Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="investigators-fast-labels-on-the-alerted-cards">the fast
feedback</Ref>). Those are the cards yesterday's model chose to alert,
so the short-horizon exposure spreads suspicion from the model's own
picks, and fraud the model missed seeds nothing until its slow
confirmation arrives, by which time the short graphs have forgotten it.
That loop, in which the model selects the labels that feed its own
features, is <Ref lesson="alert-feedback-selection-bias">selection bias
from alert feedback</Ref>.
</Solution>

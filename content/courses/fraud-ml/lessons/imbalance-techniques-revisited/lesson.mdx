The standard toolkit for rare classes — class weights, oversampling, synthetic minority points, undersampling, rebalanced ensembles — was mostly judged by ROC-AUC and balanced accuracy. Under precision at the top hundred and average precision, on a forward-in-time split, which of those techniques still help a fraud detector, and why would a technique raise one metric while lowering another?

Every one of these remedies is a line of
code in a standard library, and a fraud team reaches for them by reflex:
the training set holds one fraud per hundred payments, the model seems to
ignore the frauds, so the classes get rebalanced. The published evidence
says this works. It was measured, though, with two numbers that fail a fraud queue:
balanced accuracy, which judges one cut nobody
chose, and ROC-AUC, most of whose area lies where a hundred alerts a day
never reach (<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="how-much-of-the-curve-a-hundred-alerts-can-reach">the alert-budget
metrics</Ref>). The last exercise of <Ref
lesson="undersampling-calibration" id="exercises">undersampling and
calibration</Ref> already showed the symptom on one model: undersampling
lifted balanced accuracy from 0.56 to 0.79 and left ROC-AUC and average
precision exactly where they were.

One number describes how rare the frauds are in a training set, and every
remedy is tuned by it.

<Definition title="imbalance ratio">
The imbalance ratio of a set of labelled payments is
$$
\mathrm{IR} = \frac{\text{number of frauds}}{\text{number of genuine payments}} .
$$
A rebalancing technique is set by the ratio $r$ it should leave in the
training set: $r = 1$ is "balanced", one fraud per genuine payment.
</Definition>

The convention runs frauds over genuine, so IR is small. On the stretch
of <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="what-the-calendar-shows-and-what-it-leaves-out">the transaction
simulator</Ref> that holds every prequential fold and test week below, it
is about 0.009; on the Belgian e-commerce stream, at 0.25% fraud, it is
about 0.0025. Undersampling to a
target $r$ keeps every fraud and a share $\beta = \mathrm{IR}/r$ of the
genuine payments, which is the keep rate β of <Ref
lesson="undersampling-calibration" id="reading-β-off-a-training-set">the
previous lesson</Ref>: balancing the simulator's training set keeps about
one genuine payment in 110.

## What every remedy does to the decision boundary

Asked what a rebalancing technique does, most people answer that it makes
the classes equal. That describes the training set. The detector is
judged on the stream, where nothing was rebalanced, so the question is
what changes in the fitted model: which payments it now puts above a
threshold, and in what order.

### The remedies, side by side on one toy

A small task with two features makes the change visible. Draw 5,000
points, 5% of them in the minority class, from two clouds that overlap
(scikit-learn's `make_classification` with `class_sep=0.5`); call the
minority points frauds. Fit a decision tree of depth 5 on four fifths of
them and look at the other 1,000, of which 54 are frauds. Then refit the
same tree in two rebalanced ways:

- **Class weight.** Each training row's contribution to the tree's
  impurity is multiplied by a weight for its class. Write ω for the ratio
  of the fraud weight to the genuine weight: a fraud counts as ω genuine
  payments. The usual default, called "balanced", is $\omega =
  1/\mathrm{IR}$, here $0.95/0.05 = 19$, so that the frauds' total weight
  equals the genuine payments'.
- **Random undersampling.** Keep every fraud and draw genuine rows at
  random until there are as many as frauds: 216 of each, from 3,784
  genuine training rows.

### Why every remedy moves the boundary the same way

The tree chooses its splits and leaf values to make the training labels
as pure as possible, and in the overlap it must choose: call a leaf fraud
and pay for its genuine rows, or call it genuine and pay for its frauds.
Each remedy changes that price in the same direction.

- A **class weight** ω makes each fraud in a leaf count ω times.
- **Random oversampling** copies frauds until the target ratio is
  reached, which in expectation is the same as weighting each fraud by
  the number of its copies.
- **Undersampling** keeps a share β of the genuine rows, which in
  expectation weights each genuine row by β, the same ratio
  $\omega = 1/\beta$ obtained by deletion instead of multiplication.

With frauds dearer relative to genuine rows, a leaf that held one fraud
for every five genuine payments, a sure "genuine" before, now holds 19
weighted frauds against five at $\omega = 19$ and is labelled fraud. The boundary moves
until the weighted counts balance, and that happens deep inside the
genuine cloud.

<KeyFact>
Weights, copies and deletions are one move: they make a missed fraud
dearer than a false alarm in the training loss, and a learner pays for
that by labelling more of the overlap as fraud. The question for a fraud
team is never whether the boundary moved, but whether the order of the
payments at the top improved.
</KeyFact>

### Synthetic frauds: SMOTE

Copying a fraud twenty times adds no new information: the tree sees the
same point with a heavier weight. The *synthetic minority oversampling
technique*, SMOTE, tries to do better by inventing new frauds between the
existing ones.

<Example title="one synthetic fraud">
A fraud has age 30 and income 1,000; one of its five nearest neighbours
among the frauds, drawn at random, has age 62 and income 3,200. Draw
$u$ uniformly from $[0, 1]$; say $u = 0.75$. The synthetic fraud is
$$
x_{\text{new}} = x + u\,(x_{\text{nb}} - x):
\quad 30 + 0.75 \times (62 - 30) = 54,
\quad 1{,}000 + 0.75 \times (3{,}200 - 1{,}000) = 2{,}650 .
$$
The number of synthetic frauds is set by the target ratio. In the toy's
training set, 216 frauds and 3,784 genuine rows, a target $r = 0.5$ needs
$3{,}784 \times 0.5 = 1{,}892$ frauds, so SMOTE invents
$1{,}892 - 216 = 1{,}676$ of them, almost eight per real fraud.
</Example>

The rule never looks at the genuine payments. When two frauds sit on
either side of a stretch of genuine payments, the segment between them
runs through it, and the synthetic frauds placed there teach the model
that this stretch is fraud. In an overlapping task such as card fraud,
many segments do.

Compute the synthetic fraud for $u = 0.25$. (b) SMOTE to $r = 0.5$ is
followed by random undersampling to $r = 1$. How many genuine rows does
the toy's training set keep, and what share β of its genuine rows is
that?

<Solution>
**(a)** Age $30 + 0.25 \times 6 = 31.5$; income $1{,}000 + 0.25 \times
700 = 1{,}175$. The new point is (31.5, 1,175), a quarter of the way to
the neighbour.

**(b)** After SMOTE there are 1,892 frauds. Undersampling to one genuine
row per fraud keeps 1,892 of the 3,784 genuine rows, so $\beta = 0.5$:
the combined pipeline trains on 1,892 frauds, 216 real and 1,676
invented, against half of the genuine payments.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Which metrics see the move

The three frames give three verdicts. Balanced accuracy climbs from 0.82
to 0.91 to 0.94, a large and steady gain. Average precision falls from
0.49 to 0.44 to 0.42. ROC-AUC stays at 0.90 and then jumps to 0.94. The
remedies are judged better, worse or unchanged depending on which number
is read.

### Balanced accuracy rewards any cut that moves toward the base rate

*Balanced accuracy* is the mean of the recall on frauds and the recall
on genuine payments, at a fixed cut, usually 0.5:
$\mathrm{BA} = \tfrac12\left(\mathrm{TP}/n_1 + \mathrm{TN}/n_0\right)$,
with $n_1$ frauds and $n_0$ genuine payments. Add one payment to the
flagged set. If it is fraud with probability $q$, the expected change is
$$
\tfrac12\left(\frac{q}{n_1} - \frac{1-q}{n_0}\right),
\qquad\text{positive exactly when}\qquad
\frac{q}{1-q} > \frac{n_1}{n_0} = \mathrm{IR} .
$$
Balanced accuracy therefore keeps rising as long as the flagged payments
are more likely to be fraud than the base rate; its best cut flags every
payment above the base rate. On the simulator that means flagging any
payment more suspicious than about one in a hundred, thousands a day
against a budget of a hundred cards. That is also where the remedies put
the 0.5 cut. A cut at 0.5 on a
balanced training set is a cut at the base rate on the stream, as <Ref
lesson="undersampling-calibration" id="a-threshold-priced-in-euros">the
previous lesson</Ref> showed for undersampling, and weights and copies
shift the cut the same way. Balanced accuracy is the metric a balanced
training set is built to win.

The cleanest case is a logistic regression on the toy, averaged over five
folds. With $\omega = 19$, balanced accuracy goes from 0.641 to 0.899.
ROC-AUC is 0.937 both times, and average precision moves from 0.535 to
0.536. For a logistic regression whose log-odds really are linear in the
features, as they nearly are on the toy, weighting the classes only adds $\ln\omega$ to the intercept,
as the lesson on imbalance and leakage in the classical-ml course proved
for any well-specified weighted fit, so every payment's
score moves by the same amount on the log-odds scale and the order does not change. The weights
moved the cut, and only a metric that reads the cut noticed.

### The ranking metrics: one number unmoved, one falling

A tree is different. Its weighted splits are not the unweighted splits
shifted: the weights change which split looks purest, so the tree
partitions the space differently and the order of the payments changes.
Over the five folds of the toy, every remedy behaves the same way on one
metric and not on the other.

| depth-5 tree, five-fold mean | ROC-AUC | average precision | balanced accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| no rebalancing | 0.906 | 0.528 | 0.786 |
| class weight $\omega = 19$ | 0.887 | 0.471 | 0.898 |
| random oversampling to $r = 1$ | 0.880 | 0.456 | 0.888 |
| SMOTE to $r = 1$ | 0.913 | 0.499 | 0.910 |
| random undersampling to $r = 1$ | 0.913 | 0.408 | 0.896 |
| SMOTE to $r = 0.5$, then undersampling to $r = 1$ | 0.915 | 0.494 | 0.912 |

Balanced accuracy rises for every remedy. Average precision falls for
every remedy. ROC-AUC goes either way: down by 0.02 to 0.03 for weights
and copies, up by less than 0.01 for SMOTE and undersampling, all inside
the spread of the folds, whose standard deviations run from 0.02 to 0.04.

<KeyFact>
The three verdicts disagree because the metrics read different parts of
the ranking: balanced accuracy one cut, ROC-AUC every threshold with equal
weight, average precision mostly the top, since it averages the precision
at which each fraud is reached. Only the last reads the part a budgeted
queue works, and on the toy it says that no remedy helped.
</KeyFact>

## Why a remedy can raise ROC-AUC and lower average precision

ROC-AUC and average precision have split before. In <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-a-ranking-with-no-information-scores">the alert-budget
metrics</Ref> the full-depth tree beat the depth-2 tree on ROC-AUC, 0.788
against 0.763, and lost on average precision, 0.309 against 0.496. Its
area came from a straight ROC segment through false-positive rates the
budget never reaches, and its precision was dragged down by a tied block:
every fraud it scored 0 sat with nearly all the genuine payments and was
reached at the base rate (<Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="what-auc-sees-in-the-fully-grown-tree">the delayed-labels
lesson</Ref> took that two-score model apart). Undersampling produces the
same split from one tree trained two ways: on the fold of the
step-through, ROC-AUC rises from 0.90 to 0.94 while average precision
falls from 0.49 to 0.42. Since a ranking that dominates another wins both
metrics (<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="dominance-survives-the-change-of-axes">dominance survives the change
of axes</Ref>), the two curves must cross. The question is what the
remedy did to the order of the payments on either side of the crossing.

### Where the undersampled tree wins ROC area

The toy has no days and no investigators, so the dashed line marks a
notional budget: 50 alerts on a fold of 1,000 payments, about 5% of it,
which reaches a false-positive rate of about $50/946 \approx 0.05$. That
is generous. On the simulator a hundred alerts a day reach a
false-positive rate of 0.012 at most, as <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="how-much-of-the-curve-a-hundred-alerts-can-reach">the alert-budget
lesson</Ref> computed.

Read the left panel from the right. Without rebalancing, a handful of
frauds sit inside the genuine cloud, in leaves the tree calls nearly
certainly genuine; they are ranked below almost every genuine payment,
and the blue curve creeps from a true-positive rate of about 0.89 to
0.94 across almost the whole false-positive axis. The undersampled tree's
large fraud region takes in the lower tail of the fraud band, so those
frauds now rank above about nine genuine payments in ten.

ROC-AUC is the share of fraud–genuine pairs ranked in the right order, so
moving one fraud from below the whole cloud to above 90% of it adds about
$0.9/54 \approx 0.017$ to the area. Three such frauds add about 0.05,
more than the whole jump of 0.04; the difference is the area the
undersampled tree loses to the left of the crossing, where its curve runs
below the blue one. Every one of those gains lies at false-positive rates
of 0.05 and beyond, at or past the notional budget line and four times
past the simulator's reach: area no investigator ever works.

### Where it loses the top

The right panel shows the price, and it is the tie mechanism that sank
the full-depth tree, moved to the other end of the list. The undersampled
tree's top is a flat stretch of precision 0.39 that runs to recall 0.37:
a single block of 51 test payments tied at score exactly 1, of which 20
are fraud. The unrebalanced tree's top six payments hold four frauds, and
its precision stays above 0.5 to recall 0.75.

The score is exactly 1 because of what undersampling deleted. The tree
was fitted to 216 genuine rows, one in 17.5 of those it was given, and in
the lower right of the plot, where genuine payments are thin, the draw
kept none. A leaf with frauds and no genuine rows says 1. On the test
fold the same region holds genuine payments at the stream's rate, 31 of
them, and they all tie with the 20 frauds at the top. By <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="what-the-team-sees-at-each-fraud">the
tie convention</Ref>, every fraud in a tied block is reached at the
block's end precision. The full-depth tree's frauds tied at 0 were all
reached at the base rate; these twenty, tied at 1, are all reached at
0.39, though the plain tree finds its first four at precision 0.67 and
above. The same happens on every fold of the toy: the undersampled tree's
top block holds between 51 and 72 test payments, with precision between
0.32 and 0.56, while the unrebalanced tree's top block holds two to seven.

<KeyFact>
A remedy can buy ROC area by lifting frauds from the bottom of the
ranking into the middle, at false-positive rates the budget never
reaches, while blurring the top into large mixed blocks. ROC-AUC counts
both moves alike; average precision and precision at k see only the
second.
</KeyFact>

## On card data, under forward-in-time folds

The toy is a picture, not evidence: its folds are shuffled, its classes
are two Gaussian-like clouds, and its budget is notional. The test that
matters runs the remedies on payment streams, scored by average precision
and card precision at 100 on <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="prequential-folds-a-spread-across-weeks">prequential folds</Ref>,
which choose each remedy's setting, and on four forward test weeks, which
score the choice.

### The simulated stream

Start with the depth-5 tree on the simulated transactions, the depth the
prequential folds of <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="what-prequential-selection-picks">the delayed-labels lesson</Ref>
picked for card precision. Without rebalancing, over the four test weeks,
it scores ROC-AUC 0.810, average precision 0.600 and $CP@100$ 0.284.
Now apply SMOTE inside the training block at five target ratios.

| SMOTE target ratio $r$ | ROC-AUC | average precision | $CP@100$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| none (IR ≈ 0.009) | 0.810 | **0.600** | 0.284 |
| 0.01 | 0.805 | 0.587 | 0.282 |
| 0.05 | 0.824 | 0.573 | 0.285 |
| 0.1 | 0.827 | 0.544 | 0.290 |
| 0.5 | 0.843 | 0.558 | 0.289 |
| 1 | **0.845** | 0.546 | 0.295 |

ROC-AUC climbs almost steadily with the ratio, from 0.805 to 0.845.
Average precision is below the unrebalanced tree at every ratio. Card
precision drifts from 0.282 to 0.295, about one card a day, while the
weekly standard deviations run from 0.007 to 0.016. Two settings closer
than about one fold standard deviation are not separated, so on card
precision SMOTE did nothing measurable.

The other remedies repeat the pattern.

- **Class weights**, ω from 1 to 100: the tree's average precision is
  best at $\omega = 1$, 0.600, against 0.537 at $\omega = 100$. A logistic
  regression loses more, 0.623 at $\omega = 1$ against 0.571 at
  $\omega = 100$, while its ROC-AUC rises from 0.868 to 0.871. On these
  features its log-odds are not linear, so the weights changed the
  slopes, not only the intercept, and the order moved.
- **Random undersampling**, $r$ from 0.01 to 1: ROC-AUC is best at full
  balance, 0.83; average precision at the mildest ratio, 0.594, still
  below no rebalancing.
- **SMOTE to 0.1, then undersampling**: as the undersampling target goes
  from 0.1 to 0.5 to 1, ROC-AUC goes from 0.827 to 0.848 to 0.847, and
  average precision from 0.544 to 0.471 to 0.385.

### The real stream

On the Belgian e-commerce payments the prequential folds pick a tree of
depth 6, and without rebalancing it scores ROC-AUC about 0.87, average
precision 0.068 and $CP@100$ 0.232: 23 compromised cards found a day among
the top 100. These sit well above the numbers <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-a-ranking-with-no-information-scores">the alert-budget
lesson</Ref> gave for this stream, APs of 0.003 to 0.035 and $CP@100$
about 0.10 for the depth-2 tree and 0.15 for logistic regression, and the
setup accounts for the gap. Those five models ran with untuned default
settings, trained on one week and scored on one. Here every model's
hyperparameters are chosen on prequential folds and its scores averaged
over four forward test weeks.

Every remedy raises the tree's ROC-AUC, to between 0.88 and 0.89 at its
best ratio. To compare the precision metrics across the two streams on
equal terms, take for each remedy its best test-week value over the same
grid of settings, separately for each metric. That flatters the remedies:
no selection procedure can do better than the best cell.

| depth-5 / depth-6 tree, best setting per metric | simulator AP | simulator $CP@100$ | real AP | real $CP@100$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| no rebalancing | **0.600** | 0.284 | **0.068** | **0.232** |
| class weight | **0.600** ($\omega = 1$) | 0.289 ($\omega = 100$) | **0.068** ($\omega = 1$) | **0.232** ($\omega = 1$) |
| random undersampling | 0.594 ($r = 0.01$) | 0.292 ($r = 0.5$) | 0.066 ($r = 0.01$) | 0.201 ($r = 0.01$) |
| SMOTE | 0.587 ($r = 0.01$) | 0.295 ($r = 1$) | 0.056 ($r = 0.01$) | 0.168 ($r = 0.01$) |
| SMOTE then undersampling | 0.544 ($r = 0.1$) | **0.300** ($r = 0.5$) | 0.046 ($r = 0.5$) | 0.119 ($r = 0.1$) |

Read across a row. On the simulator the resamplers' best average
precision is 1% to 9% below the plain tree's, and their best card
precision is up to 0.016 above it, 1.6 cards a day: under the two cards a
day below which, as <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-each-disagreement-means">the alert-budget lesson</Ref> found, a
different week could reverse a gap, and inside the weekly spreads
of about 0.01 to 0.02. On the real stream the
same remedies lose 3% to 32% of average precision, and on card precision
they lose 3 to 11 cards a day, beyond the two-card line, the two larger
losses beyond the weekly spread of about 0.04. For class weights the best
precision setting on real data is "no weights", and for every resampler
it is the mildest ratio tried. The combined pipeline at its best finds 12
compromised cards a day where the plain tree finds 23.

The real stream punishes the same remedies harder, and the likely reason
is overlap. Every mechanism of the last two sections works through it:
synthetic frauds land among genuine payments, deleted genuine rows leave
regions the tree calls certain fraud, and the more genuine payments sit
among the frauds, the more of them those mistakes reach. There is a sign
that the real classes are more mixed. An AP is read as a multiple of its
stream's fraud share, the score of a ranking with no information (<Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-a-ranking-with-no-information-scores">the floor of average
precision</Ref>): the plain tree's 0.068 is about $0.068/0.0025 \approx
27$ times the real stream's share, its 0.600 about $0.600/0.009 \approx
67$ times the simulator's. It is only a sign, since weaker features or
noisier labels would lower that multiple just as well.

<KeyFact>
On both streams, for every remedy, the setting that maximises average
precision is no rebalancing or the mildest rebalancing tried, and the
setting that maximises ROC-AUC is near full balance. Card precision at 100
separates the streams: on the simulator no remedy moves it by more than
the noise, on real data every resampler costs between 3 and 11 cards a
day.
</KeyFact>

## Balanced ensembles: the gain is diversity, not balance

One family of remedies does better than the rest on card data, and its
name credits the rebalancing. *Bagging* fits many trees, each on a
bootstrap sample of the training rows (drawn with replacement, about
63% of distinct rows each), and averages their scores. *Balanced
bagging* undersamples each bootstrap sample to a target ratio before its
tree is fitted, so every tree sees all, or most, of the frauds and a
different small subset of the genuine payments. A *balanced random
forest* does the same inside a random forest, whose trees also draw a
random subset of features at each split.

This is the repair for the waste <Ref lesson="undersampling-calibration"
id="what-undersampling-still-costs-precision-at-the-top">the previous
lesson</Ref> priced. Balancing a card chunk at 0.4% fraud deletes about
99.6% of its genuine payments, and the model fitted to what is left is
noisy, most of all at the top of the scale. Fitting several models, each
on a different undersample, and averaging them recovers part of the
deleted information: on card streams retrained daily, an ensemble of
five undersampled models per day beats plain undersampling on average
precision.

| balanced bagging | 0.879 | 0.680 | 0.305 | 0.919 | 0.086 | 0.248 |
| random forest | 0.870 | 0.678 | 0.299 | 0.912 | 0.080 | 0.227 |
| balanced random forest | 0.872 | 0.677 | 0.302 | 0.925 | 0.093 | 0.254 |

### Why the balance is not what helps

The natural reading is that rebalancing, useless for a single tree,
works once the trees are averaged. The test is to vary the ratio and
watch the gain. Balanced bagging was run at five target ratios on both
streams.

  alt="Six small line charts in two rows, simulated stream above and real card data below, columns ROC-AUC, average precision and CP@100, each plotted against the imbalance ratio each tree is trained at on a log axis from 0.01 or 0.02 to 1. On the real stream balanced bagging's card precision is 0.248 at the
mildest ratio, 0.01, and it falls steadily as the trees are trained
closer to balance, to 0.189 at $r = 1$, below plain bagging. If balance
were the active ingredient the gain would grow toward $r = 1$; it
shrinks, and turns into a loss. Average precision tells the same story
on both streams.

What the mildest ratio does do is subsample. At $r = 0.01$ on a stream
with $\mathrm{IR} \approx 0.0025$, each tree keeps $\beta = 0.0025/0.01$,
a quarter of the genuine rows, a different quarter for every tree. A
hundred trees fitted on a hundred different subsets disagree more than
a hundred trees fitted on overlapping bootstrap samples, and averaging
trees that disagree cancels more of their individual overfitting. That
is the gain: diversity among the trees, supplied cheaply by
undersampling, and partly cancelled by the balance that comes with it.
The exercise on diversity and rebalancing designs the experiment that
separates the two.

### Boosting without rebalancing: near-best, not the pick

XGBoost with no rebalancing at all has the best or near-best average
precision on both streams, 0.687 on the simulator and 0.095 on the real
one against 0.097 for its weighted version, and the best ROC-AUC on the
real stream. Its only rebalancing knob is a weight on each fraud's loss,
`scale_pos_weight`, which plays the role of ω. The heuristic $\omega =
1/\mathrm{IR}$ would set it at about 110 on the simulator and 400 on the
real stream; the prequential folds pick 1 on the simulator and 5 on the
real stream, and weights of 10 to 100 do no better than 5.

On the metric that decides, plain XGBoost is not the pick. On the real
stream its $CP@100$ is 0.235, against 0.249 with $\omega = 5$, 0.248 for
balanced bagging and 0.254 for balanced random forest: 1.3 to 1.9 fewer
compromised cards a day. Every one of those gaps is under the two-card
line and under a third of the weekly standard deviations of 0.03 to
0.07, so none is separated. The card-precision pick on real data is a
mild fraud weight or a balanced ensemble, and the margin by which it
beats unweighted boosting is noise-sized.

Why boosting gains so little from rebalancing, and why the weight that
helps at all is 5 rather than 400, likely lies in how it fits. Each round fits a new tree to
the current model's errors, the gradients of the log-loss, which for a
payment are its label minus its predicted probability. On a rare-class
stream the largest errors belong mostly to frauds the model still scores
low, so each round concentrates on them: an implicit weighting of the
minority, applied where the model is wrong and fading as those frauds
get fitted. A fixed class weight keeps pushing after the frauds are
fitted, and what it then pushes is genuine payments over the line.

<Aside>
Undersampling still has a use the precision tables do not show:
training time. On the real stream, balanced random forest trained in
about 115 to 140 seconds per setting and plain XGBoost in about 480 to
640, and undersampling cut the ensembles' training time by up to a
fifth. Where the retraining window is tight, a mild ratio that costs
nothing measurable on the precision metrics can be worth it for speed
alone.
</Aside>

## The ratio is a hyperparameter, chosen by the metric that decides

A common recipe for choosing the class mix starts from the original
distribution, shifts it five points at a time toward balance, 95/5 to
90/10 to 85/15, records ROC-AUC on a validation set at each step and
stops when it stagnates; 80/20 is a frequent industry choice. Every step
of the recipe is sound except its metric: stopping on ROC-AUC walks the
mix toward the balance that metric rewards.

<KeyFact>
There is no right ratio to look up: its best value changes with the
metric, the model and the stream. Treat the target ratio or the class
weight exactly as <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="what-prequential-selection-picks">the delayed-labels lesson</Ref>
treated tree depth: a grid of candidates that includes "no
rebalancing", prequential folds, and a pick by card precision at the
team's k, with average precision to break ties.
</KeyFact>

### Where the sampler runs

Choosing the ratio on validation folds needs the sampler to stay out of
them, the rule the classical-ml course established for cross-validation:
resample training rows only, and score validation rows at the stream's own
base rate. On prequential folds the sampler is therefore fitted on each
fold's training block, after the split in time, and the validation and
test weeks are scored exactly as they arrive.

<Figure
  src="./figures/pipeline.svg"
  alt="One prequential fold drawn as a row of three blocks: a training block, a dashed delay block and a blue validation block. The training block flows down into an amber sampler, then a model, which scores the untouched validation payments for AUC, AP and CP@100"
  caption="The sampler is a step of the model, refitted on each fold's training block; the validation block keeps the stream's own payments and base rate."
/>

imbalanced-learn's `Pipeline` makes this automatic: it applies a sampler
during `fit` and skips it during `predict`, so a grid search over the
pipeline, run on the prequential splits, resamples only training blocks.

If the chosen setting does undersample, the model's outputs carry the
cut's distortion: with $\beta = \mathrm{IR}/r$, the correction of <Ref
lesson="undersampling-calibration">undersampling and calibration</Ref>
returns them to the stream's scale for any decision that reads the
probability's value.

<Conclusion>
You can now read a rebalancing result: name the boundary move every
remedy makes, explain why balanced accuracy rewards it whatever happened
to the ranking, find in the ROC and precision–recall curves the part of
the ranking a remedy improved and the part it damaged, and check whether
a reported gain in ROC-AUC lies beyond the budget. On card data under
forward folds the honest metrics favour little or no rebalancing, the
target ratio is a hyperparameter chosen by card precision on prequential
folds with the sampler inside each fold, and balanced ensembles gain
through the diversity of their trees. A boosted tree without
rebalancing reaches the best or near-best average precision on both
streams; on real card data card precision at 100 prefers a mild fraud
weight or a balanced ensemble, by a margin inside the noise.

Every comparison here counted frauds, each worth one. A missed fraud
costs the bank its amount, and resampling multiplies every fraud's weight
by the same factor whether it stole ten euros or ten thousand; <Ref
lesson="example-dependent-cost">example-dependent cost-sensitive
learning</Ref> builds the decision rule and the measure that count the
money.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
The simulator's days 71 to 166 hold 919,767 payments, 8,195 of them
fraud. (a)
Compute its imbalance ratio and the "balanced" class weight ω. (b) A
training week of 67,240 payments has 598 frauds. Undersampling it to
$r = 0.1$: what keep rate β, and how many rows remain? (c) A model fitted
on that set outputs 0.5 for a payment. What is the payment's probability
of fraud on the stream?

<Solution>
**(a)** $\mathrm{IR} = 8{,}195/(919{,}767 - 8{,}195) = 8{,}195/911{,}572
\approx 0.00899$; $\omega = 1/\mathrm{IR} \approx 111$.

**(b)** The week's ratio is $598/66{,}642 \approx 0.00897$, so $\beta =
\mathrm{IR}/r \approx 0.0897$. The set keeps 598 frauds and $598/0.1 =
5{,}980$ genuine payments, 6,578 rows in all, about a tenth of the week.

**(c)** Multiply the odds by β: odds 1 become 0.0897, so $p' =
0.0897/1.0897 \approx 0.082$. A model trained at $r = 0.1$ calls 0.5 a
payment that is fraud about one time in twelve.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague writes: "SMOTE raised the tree's ROC-AUC on the simulator
from 0.81 to 0.845, so it will catch more fraud in the top hundred."
Refute it with the numbers of the lesson, and name the step that fails.

<Solution>
**The numbers.** At $r = 1$, where ROC-AUC is 0.845, average precision is
0.546 against 0.600 without SMOTE, and $CP@100$ is 0.295 against 0.284, a
difference of about one card a day inside a weekly spread of about 0.01
to 0.016. Over the four test weeks the top of the ranking got worse by
average precision and did not measurably improve by card precision.

**The failing step.** "Higher AUC, so more fraud in the top hundred"
assumes the AUC gain happened at the top. A simulator day has about 8,250
genuine payments, so a hundred alerts reach a false-positive rate of at
most $100/8{,}250 \approx 0.012$. ROC-AUC averages the true-positive rate
over all false-positive rates from 0 to 1, and a gain anywhere on that
axis counts. SMOTE's synthetic frauds widen the fraud region, lifting
frauds from the bottom of the ranking into the middle, where the gain in
area lies beyond 0.012. The step from "AUC rose" to "the top improved"
holds only when one ROC curve dominates the other, and here they cross.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push the overlap to zero. The two classes are separated by a line: every
fraud lies on one side, every genuine payment on the other. (a) Suppose
the learner, with or without a remedy, separates them, scoring every
fraud above 0.5 and every genuine payment below. Show that ROC-AUC,
average precision and balanced accuracy are all 1, whatever the remedy.
(b) Why can SMOTE not break the separation here, though it can on card
data? (c) Which steps of the lesson's explanation of the AUC-up, AP-down
pattern needed overlap?

<Solution>
**(a)** Every fraud scores above every genuine payment, so every
fraud–genuine pair is in order and ROC-AUC is 1. The frauds take the
first $m$ ranks, the precision at the $j$-th fraud is $j/j = 1$, and
average precision is 1. At the 0.5 cut both recalls are 1, so balanced
accuracy is 1. No remedy can improve on any of them, and by hypothesis
none damages them.

**(b)** The fraud side of a line is a half-plane, and a half-plane
contains the whole segment between any two of its points. Every
synthetic fraud therefore lands on the fraud side, never among genuine
payments. On card data the frauds do not occupy a region of their own,
and segments between them cross genuine territory.

**(c)** All of them. The boundary moved because leaves holding both
classes had to be labelled one way or the other and the weights changed
which; with no mixed leaves there is nothing to relabel. The undersampled
tree's top block mixed frauds with genuine payments because deleting
genuine rows emptied regions where the stream still has genuine payments;
between separated classes such regions lie on the fraud side, where the
test set has none either. And the remedies lifted frauds out of the
genuine cloud, which needs frauds inside it. The whole pattern is a
property of overlapping classes, which card fraud is.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Recall from the lesson on imbalance and leakage in the classical-ml
course that a well-specified fit to the log-loss with each fraud weighted
ω times targets odds ω times the true odds, so a logistic regression whose
log-odds really are linear only gains $\ln\omega$ on its intercept, and
its ranking cannot change. Weighting nonetheless moved the average
precision of the toy's depth-5 tree, 0.528 to 0.471, and of the logistic
regression on the simulator's features, 0.623 to 0.571. (a) Why does the
argument not protect the tree, although for a fixed set of splits a
weighted leaf value is an increasing function of the unweighted one? (b)
Why does it not protect the simulator's logistic regression?

<Solution>
**(a)** For fixed splits a leaf's weighted fraud share is
$w_1 n_1/(w_1 n_1 + w_0 n_0)$, which rises with the unweighted share
$n_1/(n_1+n_0)$, so the leaves would keep their order. But the argument
needs a model free to output any value at each $x$, and a tree's freedom
is chosen by the splits. The weights change the impurity of every
candidate split, so the tree picks different splits and the partition
itself changes; payments that shared a leaf are separated, payments that
were apart now share one, and the order moves.

**(b)** On the simulator's features the logistic regression is
misspecified: no line follows the true log-odds, so the fitted line is a
compromise among errors made at different points. The weighted loss
prices those errors differently, a fraud's error ω times a genuine
payment's, so the best compromise under weights has different slopes,
not only a different intercept. A change of slope reorders payments, and
average precision, read from the order, moves with it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design the experiment that separates "diversity" from "rebalancing" as
the source of balanced bagging's gain. State the arms, what each holds
fixed, and what result would favour each explanation. Then check your
design against this result on the toy, five-fold means, depth-5 trees,
100 per ensemble:

| ensemble | ROC-AUC | AP | balanced accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| bagging, full bootstrap | 0.943 | 0.597 | 0.748 |
| bagging, each tree on a random draw of 432 rows | 0.938 | 0.592 | 0.713 |
| balanced bagging, $r = 1$ (each tree on 432 rows) | 0.947 | 0.586 | 0.920 |

<Solution>
**The confound.** Balanced bagging changes two things at once: each tree
sees fewer rows, and a different mix of them. A gain could come from
either.

**The arms.** (1) Plain bagging. (2) Size control: bagging in which each
tree sees a random subsample of the same size as a balanced bag, drawn
without regard to class, so the class mix stays at the stream's IR. (3)
Balanced bagging at ratio $r$. Repeat (2) and (3) across several $r$,
matching sizes each time, and score all arms on the same prequential
folds with ROC-AUC, average precision and card precision at 100.

**Reading it.** If (2) matches (3) on the ranking metrics, the gain is
the subsampling, diversity; if (3) beats (2) at equal size, the change of
mix contributes. If (3)'s advantage over (2) grows as $r$ goes to 1,
balance helps; if it shrinks or reverses, balance hurts. One caution: on
a rare-class stream a 10% random subsample holds 10% of the frauds,
which also changes what each tree can learn, so a third control keeps
all frauds and subsamples only genuine rows at the stream's own ratio,
which is undersampling with $r = \mathrm{IR}$, no rebalancing at all.

**The toy.** The second row is arm (2), the third arm (3). They agree on ROC-AUC (0.938 against 0.947) and average precision (0.592
against 0.586) within the folds' spread; only balanced accuracy separates
them, 0.713 against 0.920, which is the boundary move and nothing else.
On the toy the rebalancing adds nothing to the ranking. On the real
stream the ratio sweep points the same way: the gain is largest at the
mildest ratio and turns into a loss at balance.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A balanced-bagging ensemble averages trees each trained on an
undersample with $\beta = 0.05$. For one payment, two trees output
$p_s = 0.9$ and $0.1$. (a) Correct each output with $p' =
\beta p_s/(\beta p_s - p_s + 1)$ and average, and separately correct the
average of the two outputs. (b) Which should the team report, and why
do the two disagree? (c) Is the order of the payments affected by the
choice?

<Solution>
**(a)** Correcting each: $0.9$ has odds 9, times 0.05 gives 0.45, $p' =
0.45/1.45 \approx 0.310$; $0.1$ has odds 0.111, times 0.05 gives 0.00556,
$p' \approx 0.0055$. Their average is about 0.158. Correcting the
average: the outputs average 0.5, odds 1, times 0.05 gives 0.05, $p'
\approx 0.048$. The two answers differ by a factor of three.

**(b)** Correct the average once. Every tree estimates the same
undersampled probability $p_s$, since every tree was cut with the same β,
and averaging is how the ensemble reduces their noise; the correction
then maps the averaged estimate back to the stream. Correcting each tree
first and averaging applies the correction $g$ to noisy estimates, and
$g$ is convex: its slope rises from β at the bottom to $1/\beta$ at the
top, as the slope of the correction in <Ref
lesson="undersampling-calibration"
id="what-undersampling-still-costs-precision-at-the-top">the previous
lesson</Ref> showed. By Jensen's inequality the average of $g$ over
noisy estimates exceeds $g$ of their average, so per-tree correction
inflates the probability, most of all where the trees disagree near the
top, the payments that get called first.

**(c)** Correcting the average is one increasing map applied to the
ensemble's score, so it keeps the order. Averaging per-tree corrections
is a different score, which weights confident trees more heavily, and it
can reorder payments whose trees disagree differently. Ranking metrics
can therefore differ between the two, and only the first is the ensemble
whose ranking was validated.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two detectors raise ten alerts each in a day. Detector A, without
rebalancing, catches four frauds, of €900, €650, €400 and €350.
Detector B, trained with SMOTE, catches five, of €450, €60, €45, €30
and €25. An alert costs €5 of investigator time, and a caught fraud
saves its amount. (Made-up figures.) (a) Which detector has the higher
precision at 10? (b) How many euros does each save, net of the alerts?
(c) What would have to change in the way the models were trained, and in
the way they are scored, for the comparison to see the money?

<Solution>
**(a)** B: 5 of 10 against A's 4 of 10.

**(b)** A saves $900 + 650 + 400 + 350 = €2{,}300$ and pays $10 \times 5 =
€50$ in alerts: €2,250. B saves $450 + 60 + 45 + 30 + 25 = €610$ and pays
€50: €560. A saves four times as much with one fraud fewer.

**(c)** Both the training loss and the metric count each fraud as one.
SMOTE then multiplies the count of every fraud alike, so nothing steers
the model toward the large ones, and its extra catches can be the small
frauds of a dense region. To see the money, the score must be measured
in euros against the bank that does nothing, the value of <Ref
lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="what-a-detector-is-worth-the-cost-it-removes">the economics of
detection</Ref> with each miss priced at its own amount, and the
decision rule must compare each payment's expected loss, probability
times amount, with the alert cost. That rule, and the savings measure
built on it, are the subject of <Ref lesson="example-dependent-cost">the
next lesson</Ref>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

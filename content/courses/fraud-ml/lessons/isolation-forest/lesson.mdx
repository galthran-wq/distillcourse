Scoring every point by its distance to every other costs time quadratic in the data, which a stream of hundreds of thousands of payments a day cannot pay. Anomalies are few and different: a random axis-aligned cut separates them from the rest in fewer tries than a normal point. Can 'how many random cuts does it take' be turned into a score, and why does a tiny random sub-sample of the data make it better?

<Ref lesson="density-and-distance-scores">The neighbour scores</Ref> ended
against two walls. With no labels and no period believed clean, every
payment is scored against every other: <Ref
lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
id="what-the-score-costs-every-payment-against-every-other">one day of
400,000 payments against itself takes about ten minutes of one processor
core, a 30-day set about six days</Ref>. And <Ref
lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
id="a-crowd-of-anomalies-is-its-own-neighbourhood">a group of more than
$k$ anomalies is its own neighbourhood</Ref>: forty card tests at one web
shop have each other as nearest neighbours and score as normal. The last
exercise of that lesson planted such a group of 40 rows among 32,000 rows
of the ULB card set; the 10th-neighbour distance put them at a median rank
of 31,323, among the most normal rows in the file. Scored against a random
2,000 of those rows instead, all 40 reached the top 100: the smaller
reference held about 2.5 of them, fewer than $k$, so the group could no
longer fill its own neighbourhoods. A small sample lowered the first wall
and removed the second. It still leaves every payment measured against
2,000 others in all 28 columns, and the whole result resting on one draw
of the reference.

Both walls come from the same question, "how far is this payment from the
others?", which needs the others. A different question needs much less:
how easy is this payment to cut off from the rest? A point far to one side
of a cloud can be fenced off with a line or two. A point in the middle of
the crowd needs many lines before nothing else is left on its side.
Answering it requires comparing one value with one threshold at a time,
and no distance at all.

## Cutting a point off from the rest: one random tree

To turn "easy to cut off" into a number, the cuts have to be made by a rule
that knows nothing about which point is unusual. The simplest such rule is
a random one.

### Random cuts, counted

Take 135 payments described by two standardised columns: 134 drawn from a
Gaussian cloud, and one, called $o$, placed at $(3.2, 2.6)$, off to the
upper right. Call $m$ the point nearest the centre. Draw a box around all
135 points and repeat one step:

1. pick one of the two columns at random;
2. pick a position uniformly between that column's smallest and largest
   value among the points still in the box, and cut there;
3. keep the side that holds the point you are following, and set the other
   side aside.

The step stops when the point is alone in its box. *Isolation* means
exactly that: separating one point from all the others.

Step through frames 2 to 5 and note how many points each cut removes from

</Task>

The first two cuts barely help: they remove 9 points and then 1. A cut
position drawn uniformly over a column's range lands in the sparse tails
as often as their width allows, and the tails are as wide as the crowd.
The third cut removes 77 of the remaining 125, and the fourth falls in the empty
strip between $o$ and the crowd below it. The point $m$, in the same tree,
needs 12 cuts: every cut that reaches its neighbourhood leaves dozens of
neighbours on its side.

Two properties of $o$ shorten its path. It is **different**: on the
horizontal column it lies 0.74 beyond the largest value of any other
point, so every cut on that column that lands in this gap isolates it.
And it is **few**: nothing lies near it, so a cut that separates it from
the crowd leaves it alone instead of in a group.

### The cuts form a tree

Follow every point instead of one and the cuts form a binary tree: the
root is the first cut, its two children are the cuts made on each side,
and each branch ends where a single point is left.

<Definition title="isolation tree, path length">
An *isolation tree* on a set of $n$ points is built recursively. A node
holding more than one point stores a test "column $q$ < value $p$", with
$q$ drawn at random among the columns and $p$ drawn uniformly between the
smallest and largest value of $q$ among the node's points; the points
with $q < p$ go to the left child, the others to the right. A node holding
one point, or points that are all identical, is a leaf. The *path length*
$h(x)$ of a point $x$ is the number of edges from the root to the leaf
that holds it: the number of cuts it took to isolate $x$.
</Definition>

With $n$ distinct points the tree has $n$ leaves and $n - 1$ internal
nodes. It is not a decision tree: no label enters, no split is chosen to
separate anything, and the tree is not fitted to the data in any sense
beyond keeping each cut inside the range of the points it divides.

<KeyFact>
Because each test reads one column against a threshold drawn between that
column's own minimum and maximum, shifting a column or multiplying it by a
positive factor changes no path's distribution: the forest needs none of
<Ref lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
id="the-columns-have-to-share-a-scale">the standardisation the neighbour
scores did</Ref>. A nonlinear change does
matter. Taking the logarithm of the amount moves where uniform cuts fall,
and so changes the paths.
</KeyFact>

## One tree is a lottery: averaging over a forest

The tree in the figure gave $o$ a path of 4 and $m$ a path of 12. Another
random tree gives other numbers. Over 1,000 independent trees on the same
135 points, $o$'s path ranged from 1 to 10 cuts and $m$'s from 4 to 22,
with standard deviations of 1.7 and 2.9. These two points are rarely
confused, $m$'s path was as short as $o$'s in fewer than 1% of trees, but
the ranking at the top is crowded: the next two easiest points of the
cloud need on average 4.9 cuts, about one more than $o$ and less than one
tree's spread.

The repair is to average. Build $t$ independent trees and score each point
by its mean path length $E[h(x)]$ over them.

The standard deviation of a mean falls with the square root of the number
of trees: for $o$, 1.7 for one tree, 0.53 for ten, 0.17 for a hundred. A
hundred trees is the usual default, and the figure shows why little is
gained beyond it.

## How long is a typical path?

A mean path of 12 cuts says little on its own. Among 135 points it is
long; among 100,000 points, where the crowd is far deeper, it is very short.
Comparing paths across data sets of different sizes, or forests built on
samples of different sizes, needs a yardstick: the path length a typical
point would get among $n$ points.

### A cut at a random gap

Start with points spread evenly along one column. A cut drawn uniformly
over the range lands in each of the $n - 1$ gaps between neighbouring
points with the same probability, so it leaves $j$ points on the left with
probability $1/(n-1)$, for $j = 1, \dots, n - 1$. A typical point is a
point picked at random; it is on the left with probability $j/n$. Write
$c(n)$ for its expected path length.

Small cases come straight from the rule. One point needs no cut:
$c(1) = 0$. Two points need exactly one: $c(2) = 1$. Three points: the cut
leaves one point on the left or two, each with probability 1/2. A random
point is the lone one with probability 1/3 and finishes after that cut;
otherwise it is in a pair and needs one more. So
$c(3) = 1 + \tfrac13 \cdot 0 + \tfrac23 \cdot 1 = \tfrac53$.

<Theorem title="expected path of a typical point">
If every cut falls in each gap with equal probability, the expected path
length of a point picked at random from $n$ points is
$$
c(n) = 2\,(H_n - 1) = 2H_{n-1} - \frac{2(n-1)}{n},
$$
where $H_n = 1 + \tfrac12 + \dots + \tfrac1n$ is the $n$-th harmonic number.
<Proof>
**Recursion.** The first cut costs one step. With probability $1/(n-1)$ it
leaves $j$ points on the left and $n - j$ on the right, and a random point
then continues in a set of $j$ points with probability $j/n$ and of $n - j$
with probability $(n-j)/n$. So
$$
c(n) = 1 + \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\Bigl(\frac{j}{n}\,c(j) + \frac{n-j}{n}\,c(n-j)\Bigr)
= 1 + \frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1} j\,c(j),
$$
since the two halves of the sum run over the same terms.

**Difference of two consecutive cases.** Write $S_n = \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} j\,c(j)$,
so $n(n-1)\bigl(c(n) - 1\bigr) = 2S_n$, and the same for $n + 1$:
$(n+1)n\bigl(c(n+1) - 1\bigr) = 2S_n + 2n\,c(n)$. Subtracting the first from
the second and dividing by $n(n+1)$ gives $c(n+1) = c(n) + 2/(n+1)$.

**Sum.** From $c(1) = 0$, $c(n) = 2\bigl(\tfrac12 + \tfrac13 + \dots + \tfrac1n\bigr) = 2(H_n - 1)$.
Since $H_n = H_{n-1} + 1/n$, this equals $2H_{n-1} - 2(n-1)/n$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The formula reproduces the small cases: $c(2) = 2 \cdot \tfrac12 = 1$ and
$c(3) = 2(\tfrac12 + \tfrac13) = \tfrac53$. For larger $n$ the harmonic
number is close to $\ln n + 0.5772$, so $c(n)$ grows like $2\ln n$:
$c(135) = 8.97$, $c(256) = 10.25$, $c(4{,}096) = 15.79$. The same number is
the average cost of a search for an absent key in a binary search tree
built from $n - 1$ keys inserted in random order, which is the form in
which software libraries usually quote it.

### What the yardstick assumes

The theorem assumes each gap is equally likely to receive the cut, which
holds for evenly spread points. Real cuts are uniform in value, not in
gap. On the 135-point cloud the mean path over all points, measured with
100 trees, is 10.44, not 8.97: a Gaussian bunches its points in the middle,
so cuts land in the wide, sparse tails more often than their share of
points and peel off a few points at a time. On 135 points drawn uniformly
over a square the same measurement gives 8.83. The yardstick is the path
of a typical point on evenly spread data, used as a fixed reference, not a
prediction of the mean.

### Why not divide by n, or by log n?

Path lengths run from 1 up to $n - 1$ (a tree that peels off one point per
cut), so dividing by $n - 1$ bounds the score. It also drives every typical
point toward zero as $n$ grows: $8.97/134 = 0.067$ at 135 points, 0.0002 at
100,000. Dividing by $\log_2 n$ tracks the growth, but not exactly: the
typical point's ratio $c(n)/\log_2 n$ is 1.19 at 16 points, 1.28 at 256 and
1.32 at 4,096, so the same ratio means different things at different
sizes. Dividing by $c(n)$ puts the typical point at exactly 1 for every
$n$.

## The anomaly score: 0.5 for a typical path

A path ratio $E[h(x)]/c(n)$ runs from 0 upward, with 1 for a typical point.
Raising 2 to minus that ratio turns it into a number between 0 and 1 that
falls as the path grows.

<Definition title="isolation anomaly score">
For a forest whose trees are each built on $n$ points, the anomaly score
of a point $x$ with mean path length $E[h(x)]$ is
$$
s(x, n) = 2^{-E[h(x)]/c(n)}.
$$
</Definition>

Three values fix how to read it. A typical path, $E[h(x)] = c(n)$, gives
$s = 2^{-1} = 0.5$ for every $n$. A path near zero gives $s$ near 1. The
longest paths give $s$ near 0. The $n$ in the score is the number of
points each tree was built on, not the size of the data set being scored;
using the data set's size is a common bug, and it shifts every score.

<Example title="the scores of o and m">
The trees were built on all $n = 135$ points, so
$c(135) = 2(H_{135} - 1) = 2(5.486 - 1) = 8.97$.

**Point o.** $E[h(o)] = 3.75$, the ratio is $3.75/8.97 = 0.418$, and
$s = 2^{-0.418} = 0.75$.

**Point m.** $E[h(m)] = 12.08$, the ratio is $12.08/8.97 = 1.347$, and
$s = 2^{-1.347} = 0.39$.
</Example>

<Exercise>
The mean path over all 135 points of the cloud is 10.44. Compute the score
of a point with that path, and explain from the previous section why the
average point of this cloud scores below 0.5.

<Solution>
$s = 2^{-10.44/8.97} = 2^{-1.164} = 0.45$. The yardstick $c(135) = 8.97$ is
the expected path when every gap is equally likely to receive a cut. In a
Gaussian cloud the cuts, uniform in value, land in the sparse tails more
often than the tails' share of points, so most cuts peel off only a few
points and the average path, 10.44, is longer than 8.97. A longer path
gives a lower score: on bunched data the typical point sits a little below
0.5. The reading "0.5 means typical" is exact only for evenly spread data.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyFact>
Within one forest the score ranks points exactly as the mean path length
does, so it adds nothing to the ranking an investigator works. It matters
when scores from forests built on different sample sizes are compared, and
its reading, near 1 for an anomaly and about 0.5 for a typical point, is
approximate on bunched data.
</KeyFact>

## Fewer points per tree: swamping and masking

The trees so far were built on all 135 points, which held one anomaly.
The planted group of the opening is the harder case: forty anomalies that
are each other's company. Does a crowd lengthen a path the way it shortened
a neighbour distance, and does a smaller sample per tree clear it the way
the 2,000-row reference did?

### Two ways a crowd hides an anomaly

A crowd acts on a path in two ways:

- **Masking**: the anomalies are numerous and close together. A member of
  the group is not alone after the cuts that separate the group from the
  crowd; the cuts must then work through its companions one by one, and
  its path grows toward a normal one. Forty card tests at one shop mask
  each other this way.
- **Swamping**: normal points sit pressed against the anomalies. First,
  they lengthen an anomaly's path: a cut that would have fenced it off
  leaves those neighbours on its side, and further cuts must strip them
  away one by one. Second, those neighbours are themselves out in the
  sparse fringe of the cloud, where the anomaly is, so the same few cuts
  that fence off the fringe fence them off too: their paths are as short
  as the anomaly's or shorter, and they rank beside it or above it. Normal
  points ranked as anomalies are the false alarms swamping produces.

The figure shows both effects on a generated data set built to have them,
from the Mulcross generator: 4,096 points, a large Gaussian cloud of
normal points and two dense clusters of anomalies, 10% of the data, placed
at the cloud's edge with normal points pressed against them. Each cluster
masks its own members, and the normal points around it swamp it. A forest
built on all 4,096 points ranks the anomalies with a ROC-AUC of 0.67.
Built on random samples of 128 points each, it reaches 0.91. In a sample
of 128 each cluster shrinks to a handful of points and the normal points
that surrounded it are mostly gone: the crowd that hid the anomalies is no
longer in the data the tree sees.

### How small: about one member of the crowd per sample

How much smaller should the sample be? Return to the planted group of the
previous lesson's last exercise. Take 32,000 random rows of the ULB card
set, columns V1 to V28, and add a tight group of $g$ rows: copies of one
point placed at twice the mean of the fraud rows, each moved by Gaussian
noise of standard deviation 0.05 per column. Build forests of 100 trees,
each tree on a random sample of $\psi$ rows (the *sub-sample size*), score
every row, and measure the ranking by the <Ref

precision</Ref> of finding the planted rows.

average precision 0.16 for 40 rows, 0.30 for 200, 0.43 for 1,000. Each
curve peaks at a smaller sample: around $\psi = 1{,}024$ for 40 rows

every case the peak is where a sample holds about one or two planted rows
on average, $\psi \approx N/g$ with $N = 32{,}000$: a single planted row
in a sample sits alone in a sparse region and is cut off early, which is
exactly what the forest rewards. Below the peak the samples are too small
to describe the ordinary rows, and the planted rows compete with every
sparse honest row.

At $\psi = 256$ all 40 planted rows of the smallest group land in the top
100 of 32,040, at a median rank of 53; with every row only 21% of them do,
at a median rank of 119. The 10th-neighbour distance of the opening
recovered the same way, and the two recoveries have one cause. A group
stops hiding once the set each payment is compared against holds fewer of
its members than the score needs to treat them as company: fewer than
$k = 10$ for the neighbour distance, which the 2,000-row reference met,
and about one for a tree, since every member past the first adds cuts of
its own. The tree asks for the smaller sample.

<KeyFact>
Isolation needs the anomalies to be few in each tree's sample, not few in
the data. A group of $g$ anomalies among $N$ rows stops masking itself once
the sample size $\psi$ is near $N/g$.
</KeyFact>

Two things separate the forest from a neighbour score on a sample. Each
tree draws its own sample, without replacement, so a member of the crowd
that shares one tree's sample with two others is alone in another's, and
the mean path combines a hundred draws where the neighbour score rested on
one reference. And the sample is the whole model: a tree on 256 rows is a
few hundred stored tests, and a payment is scored by walking each tree
from the root, one comparison per cut, about ten for a typical path on 256
rows ($c(256) = 10.25$), instead of being measured against 2,000 rows in 28
columns.

### When the frauds come in small clumps, a small sample buys nothing

The same rule says when sub-sampling has nothing to clear. On the whole
ULB file, 284,807 rows and 492 real frauds, the frauds sit in small
clumps: <Ref lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
id="on-real-card-data-fraud-comes-in-clumps">a fraud's ten nearest rows are
59% fraud</Ref>: a fraud and some six fraudulent neighbours, a clump of
something like seven, not a crowd of hundreds. For $g \approx 7$, $N/g$ is about 40,000 rows. A
sample of 256 then holds a member of a given clump in fewer than one tree
in a hundred ($256 \times 7/284{,}807 = 0.006$): there is no crowd in it to
thin, and the smaller sample only describes the genuine rows more coarsely.

## A partial tree is enough: the height limit

Growing every tree until each point is alone spends most of its cuts deep
in the crowd, on paths that are long whatever happens. The ranking an
investigator reads is the top of the list, the short paths, and a tree can
stop where paths stop being short.

### Stopping at depth ⌈log₂ ψ⌉, and accounting for it

The usual limit is $l = \lceil \log_2 \psi \rceil$: depth 8 for
$\psi = 256$, below the typical path $c(256) = 10.25$. Every path shorter
than 8 is computed exactly. A node at depth $l$ is not split further,
whatever it holds, and becomes a leaf that records how many sample points
reached it, its *Size*.

A point that ends in such a leaf has been cut $l$ times and still shares
its box with Size − 1 sample points. Reporting $h = l$ would treat it as
isolated. The repair adds the cuts the unbuilt subtree would have needed
on average: a point reaching a leaf at depth $e$ that holds Size points
gets $h(x) = e + c(\text{Size})$.

<Exercise>
A forest uses $\psi = 256$ and $l = 8$. In one of its trees, payment A
reaches a leaf at depth 8 holding 5 sample points, payment B a leaf at
depth 3 holding one, payment C a leaf at depth 8 holding one. Compute the
three path lengths and, as if this tree were the whole forest, the three
scores. What would the ranking be without the adjustment?

<Solution>
**Paths.** $c(5) = 2(H_5 - 1) = 2(2.2833 - 1) = 2.57$, so
$h(\mathrm{A}) = 8 + 2.57 = 10.57$; $h(\mathrm{B}) = 3$;
$h(\mathrm{C}) = 8 + c(1) = 8$.

**Scores**, with $c(256) = 10.25$: A, $2^{-10.57/10.25} = 2^{-1.031} = 0.49$;
B, $2^{-3/10.25} = 2^{-0.293} = 0.82$; C, $2^{-8/10.25} = 2^{-0.781} = 0.58$.

**Without the adjustment** A and C both get $h = 8$ and tie, although C was
alone at depth 8 and A still had four sample points with it. The
adjustment separates them and puts A near the typical score of 0.5, where
a point still in a crowd belongs. B's path is untouched: the limit never
changes a path shorter than $l$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### What a forest costs

A tree on $\psi$ points has at most $2\psi - 1$ nodes, 511 for
$\psi = 256$, and the height limit caps it at $2^{l+1} - 1$, also 511.
Building $t$ trees costs about $t\psi\log\psi$ steps and does not grow with
the size of the data. Scoring one payment walks each tree from the root to
a leaf, at most $l$ comparisons per tree, so scoring $N$ payments costs
about $N t \log\psi$: linear in $N$.

Measured on the whole ULB file on one processor core, with $t = 100$ and
$\psi = 256$: building the forest takes 0.2 seconds and scoring all 284,807
rows 0.9 seconds. The previous lesson estimated five minutes for the
pairwise neighbour pass on the same file and measured 21 seconds against a
20,000-row sample. A 30-day stream of 12 million payments would take about
40 seconds to score, and one new payment costs 100 walks of at most 8
comparisons.

<KeyFact>
A larger sample is cheap where it is used. Scoring grows with
$\log_2 \psi$: $\psi = 4{,}096$, the best mean on the ULB file, means 12
comparisons per tree instead of 8. Building grows with
$\psi\log\psi$, about 24 times the work for sixteen times the sample, and
is paid once per forest, not once per payment.
</KeyFact>

## Where isolation fails: columns that carry nothing

Each cut picks its column at random, which assumes every column might
separate an anomaly. A column that carries no information about any
payment still receives its share of the cuts.

Take a random 50,000 rows of the ULB file, 78 of them fraud, and add 500
columns of uniform random noise to its 28 V columns. The table gives the
mean over several forests of 100 trees with $\psi = 256$, and the range in
brackets. A cut now lands on a
noise column 500 times in 528, and a noise cut splits frauds and genuine
rows alike, so every path is mostly noise.

| columns given to the forest | ROC-AUC | average precision |
|---|---|---|
| V1 to V28 | 0.95 | 0.20 (0.16 to 0.26) |
| V1 to V28 and 500 noise columns | 0.76 (0.71 to 0.79) | 0.015 (0.005 to 0.027) |
| the same 528, each tree keeping the 28 of highest kurtosis in its sample | 0.95 | 0.16 (0.13 to 0.19) |

The repair chooses the columns per tree before cutting. *Kurtosis*
measures how heavy a column's tails are relative to its spread; a few
extreme values raise it sharply, and uniform noise has thinner tails than
a Gaussian. Ranking the columns by kurtosis on each tree's own sample and
keeping the top 28 picks exactly the 28 V columns in every one of the 100
trees, and the ranking comes back: average precision 0.16 against 0.20,
inside the spread between forests, against a base rate of 0.0016. A
distance-based score suffers the same way, since 500 noise columns add to
every distance, and needs the same kind of selection.

The forest's assumption, in the form of <Ref
lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="every-detector-family-assumes-one-thing-about-normal-data">the framing
lesson's table</Ref>, is that anomalies are few in each sample and differ
from normal payments in the value of at least one of the columns the cuts
use. Noise columns break the second half; a group as large as the sample
can thin it breaks the first.

## What an isolation score finds in payments

The ULB file has labels and anonymous columns. The simulator shows which
kinds of fraud an isolation score finds and why.

### The setup

The stream is <Ref lesson="cost-aware-evaluation-lab">the evaluation
lab's</Ref> simulator run, with its deployment geometry: the forest, 100
trees with $\psi = 256$, is built on the 68,898 payments of training days
42 to 48 without their labels, and scores test days 56 to 62 after the
known-card removal of <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="which-test-payments-would-really-be-scored-removing-known-cards">the
delayed-labels lesson</Ref>: 60,212 payments, 440 frauds, a base rate of
0.73%, about 8,600 payments a day. Two input sets are compared:

- **12 label-free inputs**: the amount, the weekend and night flags, the
  card's count and mean amount over 1, 7 and 30 days, and the terminal's
  counts over the same windows;
- **the 15 inputs**: the same 12 plus the terminal's three <Ref
  lesson="point-in-time-features"
  id="the-delayed-terminal-risk-a-window-that-ends-δ-days-ago">delayed fraud
  rates</Ref>, which are computed from labels.

For comparison, a logistic regression and a random forest of 100 trees,
at library defaults, are trained with labels on the 15 inputs of the same
training week and scored on the same test week. The isolation-forest rows
are means over three forests. The last column is <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="card-precision-at-k-the-team-calls-cards">card precision at 100</Ref>:
of the 100 cards the team calls each day, the share that were compromised,
averaged over the week.

| detector | inputs | ROC-AUC | average precision | CP@100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| isolation forest | 12 label-free | 0.60 | 0.03 | 0.05 |
| isolation forest | 15 | 0.84 | 0.26 | 0.27 |
| logistic regression, labels | 15 | 0.89 | 0.63 | 0.33 |
| random forest, labels | 15 | 0.89 | 0.69 | 0.35 |

### Which frauds reach the top

<Figure
  src="./figures/sim-scenarios.svg"
  alt="Grouped bar chart of the share of each scenario's test frauds that reach the daily top 100 payments. Amount over 220, 26 test frauds: forest on 12 label-free inputs 0.42, forest on 15 inputs 0.15, logistic regression 0.92. Compromised terminal, 295 test frauds: 0.01, 0.64, 0.66. Leaked card with five-fold amounts, 119 test frauds: 0.32, 0.23, 0.65."
  caption="Without label-derived inputs the forest finds part of the amount and leaked-card frauds and none of the compromised terminals; the terminal fraud rates bring those in."
/>

On the label-free inputs the forest finds part of what departs from
normal. Of the frauds above €220, 42% reach a day's top 100 payments; of
the leaked-card payments with five-fold amounts, 32%. The
compromised-terminal frauds, two-thirds of the test week, reach it 1% of
the time: <Ref lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="context-turned-into-a-column">nothing in the label-free data departs
from normal for them</Ref>, since the generator leaves the terminal's
traffic and the cards that pay there unchanged.

The top of the list fills with honest payments that are different along
one column. 73% of the honest payments in the daily top 100 are night
payments, against 17% of the stream: a 0/1 column whose ones are a
minority hands every one of them a short path, because a single cut on that
column separates the minority from the rest. Different is not the same as
fraudulent.

With the terminal fraud rates added, the forest finds 64% of the terminal
frauds, as many as the logistic regression. A terminal rate of 0.5 is
extreme on its column, and the forest isolates it like any extreme value.
But that column was built from labels, so the forest is no longer
detecting "unlike normal"; it is reading a supervised signal that the
feature carries.

<KeyFact>
The row that measures the forest as a detector for a stream without labels
is the label-free one: average precision 0.03. The 0.26 of the 15-input
row exists only where labels already arrive, and there a model trained on
those labels reaches 0.63.
</KeyFact>

### A decent ranking, a poor top

Even with the terminal rates, the forest's ROC-AUC, 0.84, is close to the
supervised models' 0.89, while its average precision is less than half of
theirs. <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="how-much-of-the-curve-a-hundred-alerts-can-reach">ROC-AUC weighs every
false-positive rate equally</Ref>, most of them far beyond what a hundred
alerts a day can reach; average precision reads the top, where the forest
mixes frauds with honest night payments and big spenders. A score can
order the bulk of the stream well, which AUC rewards, and still fill the
hundred places the team reads with payments that are merely unusual, and
those hundred places are all the team ever sees.

<Conclusion>
You can now build an isolation tree by hand, read a payment's path length
as the number of random cuts that isolate it, and average it over a
forest. You can derive the yardstick $c(n) = 2(H_n - 1)$ from the
random-gap recursion, turn a mean path into the score
$s = 2^{-E[h]/c(n)}$, and say why 0.5 marks a typical point only roughly.
You can explain swamping and masking, choose the sample size near $N/g$
for a crowd of $g$ anomalies among $N$ payments, and say why a small
sample buys nothing when the frauds come in small clumps. You can account for a truncated leaf with
$c(\text{Size})$, give the forest's linear cost, and recognise its two
failures: columns that carry nothing, and inputs that smuggle labels in.

The forest scores a payment by how easily it is cut off. <Ref
lesson="one-class-and-reconstruction">The next lesson</Ref> scores it by
whether it falls outside a boundary fitted around normal payments, or by
how badly a network trained to reproduce normal payments reproduces it,
and asks whether adding such a score to a supervised model helps.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Compute $c(n) = 2H_{n-1} - 2(n-1)/n$ for $n = 16$, 256 and 4,096, using
$H_i \approx \ln i + 0.5772$, and the score of a point whose mean path is
5 in each case. Explain in one sentence why the same path length gives
three different scores.

<Solution>
**c(n).** $c(16) = 2(\ln 15 + 0.5772) - 2 \cdot 15/16 = 2(3.2853) - 1.875 = 4.70$.
$c(256) = 2(\ln 255 + 0.5772) - 2 \cdot 255/256 = 2(6.1185) - 1.992 = 10.25$.
$c(4{,}096) = 2(\ln 4{,}095 + 0.5772) - 2 \cdot 4{,}095/4{,}096 = 2(8.8947) - 2.000 = 15.79$.

**Scores for a path of 5.** $2^{-5/4.70} = 2^{-1.064} = 0.48$;
$2^{-5/10.25} = 2^{-0.488} = 0.71$; $2^{-5/15.79} = 2^{-0.317} = 0.80$.

**Why.** Five cuts are about the typical path among 16 points, so the
score is near 0.5; among 4,096 points the typical path is about 16 cuts,
and a point isolated in 5 is far quicker than its crowd. The exact
harmonic numbers give $c(16) = 4.76$ instead of 4.70; the approximation is
close from about ten points up.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push the sub-sample size to its smallest values. (a) With $\psi = 2$: what
is the height limit, what tree can be built, and what score does any
payment get? (b) With $\psi = 3$: what paths are possible for a payment
passed through one tree, and what scores do they give? (c) What do the two
cases say about the bottom end of the planted-group figure?

<Solution>
**(a)** $l = \lceil \log_2 2 \rceil = 1$, and $c(2) = 1$. The tree is one
cut between the two sampled points, with two leaves of one point each.
Every payment, whatever its values, lands in one of the two leaves after
one cut: $h = 1$ in every tree, $E[h] = 1$, and
$s = 2^{-1/1} = 0.5$ for every payment. The forest ranks nothing.

**(b)** $l = \lceil \log_2 3 \rceil = 2$ and $c(3) = 5/3$. The first cut
separates one sampled point from the other two. A payment that falls on
the single point's side has $h = 1$; one that falls on the pair's side is
cut again and has $h = 2$. The scores are $2^{-1/(5/3)} = 2^{-0.6} = 0.66$
and $2^{-2/(5/3)} = 2^{-1.2} = 0.44$. Over many trees a payment's score
reflects how often it falls on the side of a lone sampled point: some
information, but each tree knows only which side of one random cut a
payment is on.

**(c)** A tree on a handful of points cannot describe where ordinary
payments are, so a planted row and an ordinary row in a sparse region
look alike. That is why the curves whose $N/g$ lies well above 16 fall
again at the smallest $\psi$: the 40-row group ($N/g = 800$) drops from 0.36
at $\psi = 256$ to 0.13 at 16, the 200-row group ($N/g = 160$) from 0.79 at
64 to 0.54. The 1,000-row group ($N/g = 32$) reads 0.94 at both 16 and 64:
the smallest tested size is already near its best, so the fall would come
below the tested range. The sample must be small enough to thin the
crowd and large enough to describe the normal data; for a crowd of $g$
among $N$, $\psi \approx N/g$ balances the two.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
"A payment whose every column lies well inside the normal range cannot get
a high isolation score, since every cut on every column has normal
payments on both sides of it." Refute the claim with payment B of <Ref
lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
id="many-columns-at-once-the-mahalanobis-distance">the Mahalanobis
section</Ref>: two standardised columns with correlation 0.8, B at
$(1.5, -1.5)$, each value with about 13% of payments further out on its
axis. Explain which cuts isolate it, and name the step of the claim that
fails.

<Solution>
**The cuts.** Consider a cut on the second column just above B's value
$-1.5$. On B's side lie only the payments with a second value below $-1.5$,
about 5% of them, and because of the correlation nearly all of these also
have a low first value, mostly below 0. A cut on the first column anywhere
between those payments and B's 1.5 then leaves B alone. Two cuts suffice,
each of a kind that random cuts produce often, so B's mean path is short.

**Measured.** A forest of 1,000 trees on 254 points drawn with correlation
0.8 plus A $= (2.5, 2.5)$ and B gives B a mean path of 6.6 cuts against a
median of 12.5, a score of 0.64, rank 5 of 256. A, at the end of the
cloud's long axis, scores 0.61, rank 8.

**The failing step.** "Normal payments on both sides of every cut" is
true of the first cut, not of the later ones. After one cut the box holds
a subset of the payments, and the second cut divides only those. The
claim treats each column on the whole data; the tree cuts each column
inside a box already narrowed by the other column, which is how a
combination of common values becomes a sparse corner.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A point $x$ lies beyond all other points on column $q$: their values span
a range $R_q$, and $x$ exceeds their largest by a margin $m_q$. (a) Show
that a cut on column $q$, drawn among the points of the box including $x$,
isolates $x$ immediately with probability $m_q/(R_q + m_q)$. (b) With $d$
columns chosen with equal probability, write the probability that the
first cut isolates $x$, counting only columns on which $x$ is outside the
others' range. (c) Apply it to $o$ of the lesson's cloud: on the first
column the other 134 points span $-2.83$ to $2.46$ and $o$ is at 3.2; on
the second, $o$ is inside the range. (d) Now let $x$ be one of a tight
group of $g$ points beyond the range. What does the first cut in the gap
achieve, and roughly how many more cuts does a member of the group need?

<Solution>
**(a)** With $x$ included, the box spans $[\min, \max + m_q]$ on column
$q$, a length of $R_q + m_q$. The cut position is uniform on it. $x$ is
alone on its side exactly when the cut lands strictly between the others'
maximum and $x$, an interval of length $m_q$: probability
$m_q/(R_q + m_q)$.

**(b)** $P = \dfrac{1}{d}\sum_{q} \dfrac{m_q}{R_q + m_q}$, the sum running
over the columns on which $x$ lies outside the others' range; a column on
which $x$ lies inside cannot isolate it with one cut while others remain
on both sides.

**(c)** $R = 2.46 - (-2.83) = 5.29$ and $m = 3.2 - 2.46 = 0.74$; only the
first column counts, so $P = \tfrac12 \times 0.74/6.03 = 0.061$. A single
cut rarely isolates $o$. What shortens its path is that every cut that
keeps $o$ shrinks the box while $o$'s margin stays: after the tree's third
cut the 47 other points in the box spanned 0.28 to 2.46 on the first
column, and a cut on that column isolated $o$ with probability
$0.74/(3.2 - 0.28) = 0.25$.

**(d)** A cut in the gap separates the whole group from the rest, not
$x$. The group is then a box of $g$ nearly identical points, and isolating
one of them takes about $c(g)$ more cuts: $c(40) = 2(H_{40} - 1) = 6.56$,
$c(200) = 9.76$. A member of a group of 40 has the path of a lone outlier
plus six or seven cuts, which is masking measured in cuts. In a sample of
$\psi$ rows the group appears with about $\psi g/N$ members, and once that
is near 1 the extra $c(\cdot)$ vanishes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the evaluation lab's simulator run, with the lesson's geometry
(forest built on days 42 to 48 without labels, scored on days 56 to 62
after known-card removal), sweep the sub-sample size $\psi$ over 16, 64,
256, 1,024, 4,096 and all 68,898 training payments, for both input sets.
Before running, predict from the $N/g$ rule whether the curve should rise
or fall, and say what result would show masking on this stream. Then
report the average precision at each $\psi$, averaged over three seeds.

<Solution>
**Prediction.** Masking needs a crowd: many anomalies close together in
the columns the forest reads. The simulator's frauds are scattered: a
compromised terminal's frauds are ordinary payments by different cards,
a leaked card's inflated payments are spread over two weeks, and the
amount-rule frauds are a few a day. With no large group, $N/g$ is large,
so the average precision should rise with $\psi$ and level off. Masking
would show as a peak at a small $\psi$ followed by a fall as $\psi$ grows.

**Measured** (seeds 0 to 2): with the 12 label-free inputs, 0.011, 0.017,
0.034, 0.074, 0.088 and 0.081; with the 15 inputs, 0.036, 0.14, 0.26, 0.31,
0.31 and 0.32. Both rise and level off from about 1,024: no masking. A
card-testing night, by contrast, would put dozens of near-identical rows
at one merchant into the scored data, and the same sweep on that night
would peak where a sample holds one or two of them.

**Setup.** scikit-learn's `IsolationForest(n_estimators=100,
max_samples=psi)`, fitted on the training rows and scored with

</Solution>

<Exercise>
On the same geometry, standardise the 15 inputs with the training week's
means and standard deviations, fit a principal component analysis with 2
components on the training week, and score each test payment by its
squared reconstruction error: the squared distance between the payment
and its projection back from the 2 components. Report the ROC-AUC and
average precision, and compare them with the isolation forest's 0.84 and

**Measured.** ROC-AUC 0.86, average precision 0.27: level with the forest

the label-free forest reached in the previous exercise's sweep.

**Which score.** The reconstruction error is the squared distance off the

**The connection.** A projection onto a few directions and back is the
simplest model trained to reproduce normal payments. <Ref

The label-free forest of the scenario figure has a CP@100 of 0.05: about

The prediction is too high. Using <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"

<Solution>
**(a)** $0.42 \times 26 + 0.01 \times 295 + 0.32 \times 119 \approx 11 + 3 +

**(b)** Two steps take cards out, both through the leaked cards. The 119

<Exercise>
For each situation choose among the k-th neighbour distance, the local
outlier factor, the Mahalanobis distance and an isolation forest, and name
the property of the data that decides. (a) Tonight's 400,000 payments must
be scored before morning with no clean reference period, and card-testing
bursts of a few dozen identical payments are expected. (b) A merchant's
month of payments in two columns, a dense lunchtime crowd of small
amounts and a thin spread of large evening orders; you want the odd
payment in either group. (c) Thirty strongly correlated columns, where the
payments to flag are those whose columns disagree with each other, and a
clean month exists to estimate from. (d) The same thirty columns plus
three hundred columns of device and browser attributes, most of them
irrelevant.

<Solution>
**(a)** Isolation forest, or a neighbour score built for the bursts. The
deciding properties are the bursts and the missing clean reference: the
scores are fitted to the data that holds the bursts, so a burst is its own
neighbourhood for any neighbour score with $k$ below its size. Time does not
decide: the pairwise pass on 400,000 payments takes about ten minutes, well
before morning. Two neighbour scores also work: $k$ at least the largest
expected burst, or scoring against a small random reference that holds
fewer than $k$ members of a burst, as the opening's 2,000 rows did. The
forest gets the same effect with $\psi$ near $N/g$, about
400,000/40 = 10,000 for bursts of 40, and draws a fresh sample per tree
instead of resting on one reference.

**(b)** Local outlier factor, with $k$ below the size of the evening group.
The two groups have very different densities; a global distance ranks
every evening order above an odd lunchtime payment, and a forest's random
cuts also isolate the sparse evening orders early, for the same reason.

**(c)** Mahalanobis distance with all components, estimated on the clean
month. The anomalies are departures from the correlations, the
rare-combination case, and the covariance finds those directions exactly;
a forest finds them only through sequences of axis cuts.

**(d)** Isolation forest with per-tree column selection, or a reduction to
the relevant columns before any score. Irrelevant columns dilute every
distance and receive most random cuts; kurtosis selection on each tree's
sample keeps the columns whose tails hold the anomalies.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Before a node's neighbours are used to judge it, one fact has to be checked: do fraudulent nodes actually sit next to other fraudulent nodes in this graph, or next to honest ones? If they do cluster, what numbers about a node's neighbourhood — how many neighbours, how many of them fraudulent, how tightly knit, how central in the whole network — can be handed to an ordinary classifier?

<Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph">The previous chapter</Ref> turned a
transaction log into a graph: accounts or users as nodes, transfers and
shared identifiers as edges, labels on the nodes that carry them. It ended
on a claim it could not yet back: fraud is social, by word of mouth or by
organisation, so <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph" id="two-ways-fraud-is-social">fraud
should sit close to fraud</Ref>. The classifiers of the supervised module,
from <Ref lesson="scorecards-woe">the logistic scorecard</Ref> on,
take a table, one row per node, so the graph has to become columns.

The first column anyone writes is "how many of this user's neighbours are
known fraudsters". It is cheap, one pass over the edge list, and it is the
whole idea of guilt by association in one number. It is also only as good
as the claim behind it. If fraudsters link to fraudsters, a high count marks
a suspect. If they mostly link to honest users, the count is high for the
honest users around a fraudster and zero for the fraudster, and the column
points the wrong way. The claim has to be tested first, on the labels the
bank already holds, before any such column is built.

## Does fraud sit next to fraud? A test against chance

Take the twelve users of the <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="exercises">previous chapter's last-but-one exercise</Ref>: four known
fraudsters $F_1$ to $F_4$, eight users $L_1$ to $L_8$ with no fraud on
record, and fourteen entity-resolution edges between them. Two edges join
a fraudster to a non-fraudster; the other twelve join two of a kind.

### Counting cross-labelled edges

Two out of fourteen sounds like few, but few compared with what? Call an
edge *cross-labelled* if its two ends carry different labels. The same <Ref
lesson="fraud-as-a-graph" id="exercises">exercise</Ref> worked out the
comparison: with $f$ the fraction of fraudulent nodes and $l = 1 - f$
the rest, labels that had nothing to do with the edges would make each
edge cross-labelled with probability $2lf$, one end fraudulent and the
other not, in either order. Here $2lf = 4/9 \approx 0.44$, about 6.2 of
the fourteen edges. Two are.

<Figure
  src="./figures/homophily-test.svg"
  alt="Twelve users: four amber fraudsters F1 to F4 joined to each other by five edges, a square of four honest users L1 to L4 and a chain of four honest users L5 to L8. Two edges, F4 to L1 and F1 to L5, are thick amber and labelled cross. A side panel reads: observed 2 of 14; expected 14 times 2 times 8/12 times 4/12, about 6.2; one-tailed test z about minus 2.27, p about 0.012"
  caption="The twelve-user graph. Labels independent of edges would give about six cross-labelled edges; the graph has two."
/>

Nodes keeping company with their own kind more than independent labels
would give is called *homophily*. The cross-edge count tests one
consequence of it: if fraud keeps company with fraud, fewer edges than
chance join a fraudster to a non-fraudster.

<Definition title="cross-edge test">
Take two labels, fraudulent and legitimate, with fractions $f$ and
$l = 1-f$ of the nodes, and $M$ edges. Let $\pi$ be the probability that an
edge is cross-labelled, estimated by the observed fraction $\hat r$ of
cross-labelled edges. The *cross-edge test* is the one-tailed test of a
proportion of

$$
H_0 : \pi \geq 2lf \quad\text{against}\quad H_1 : \pi < 2lf.
$$

Rejecting $H_0$ says that fraudulent and legitimate nodes are joined less
often than independent labels would give.
</Definition>

<Example title="the test on twelve users">
Treat the $M = 14$ edges as fourteen draws, each cross-labelled with
probability $\pi_0 = 2lf = 4/9$ under $H_0$. The observed fraction is
$\hat r = 2/14 = 0.143$. With the normal approximation to the binomial, the
standard error under $H_0$ is

$$
\sqrt{\pi_0(1-\pi_0)/M} = \sqrt{0.444 \times 0.556 / 14} = 0.133,
$$

so $z = (0.143 - 0.444)/0.133 = -2.27$, and the one-tailed p-value is
$\Phi(-2.27) \approx 0.012$. That is the normal approximation's value for
the probability of two or fewer cross-labelled edges out of fourteen, if
the labels were independent of the edges; at the 5% level $H_0$ is
rejected: on the twelve users, fraudsters are joined to non-fraudsters less
often than chance.
</Example>

Fourteen draws is small for a normal approximation. The exact binomial
probability of two or fewer is 0.019, about one in fifty, and the verdict
at the 5% level is the same.

### What the test assumes

**Edges are not independent draws.** A user with forty edges contributes
forty draws that all share one label. The test then counts that user's
label forty times, and a few high-degree users can produce a significant
result on their own. The check that makes no such assumption shuffles the
labels over the nodes many times, keeps the edges fixed, and asks how often
a shuffle gives as few cross-labelled edges as the real labels did
(the <Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality" id="exercises">exercise on
the Elliptic aggregates</Ref> designs one).

**"Legitimate" means "not known as fraud".** On a fraud graph the second
label is the absence of a label, and an undetected fraudster counts as
honest. That is <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="silence-read-as-genuine-is-a-decision-not-a-fact">silence read as
genuine</Ref> again, and it pushes the test the conservative way: a hidden
fraudster linked to a known one turns a same-label edge into a cross edge.

The test also needs edges between nodes that carry labels. In the bipartite
card graph no edge joins two cardholders, so it is run on the users joined
by entity resolution, on the accounts joined by transfers, or on a
projection that links two cardholders through a shared merchant.

## Two separate questions: dyadicity and heterophilicity

The cross-edge count looks at one kind of edge, fraud to non-fraud, and
asks whether there are too few. Fraud keeping company with fraud has a
second side the count never looks at: whether there are too many
fraud-to-fraud edges. Fraudsters can seek each other out and still trade
with honest users at close to the usual rate, and then the count sees
nothing unusual.

### A second toy, and a test that says no

A second toy, used from here on, has twenty nodes and twenty-five edges;
four nodes, D, E, F and I, are known fraudulent. D, E and F form a closed triangle; I sits among honest
nodes.

<Figure
  src="./figures/toy20.svg"
  alt="A graph of twenty nodes A to T and 25 edges. Nodes D, E, F and I are amber. D, E and F form a triangle on the left, joined to G; A in the centre has six neighbours B, G, H, I, O and T; O leads to P and Q, Q to R and S; I leads to K and N; N, L and M form a triangle at the bottom; H joins A and K; J hangs off K; C hangs off B"
  caption="The 20-node toy: four fraudulent nodes, three of them in one triangle."
/>

Run the cross-edge test. Seven of the 25 edges are cross-labelled
(B–D, D–G, F–G, G–I, A–I, I–K, I–N); with
$f = 0.2$, $2lf = 0.32$ and eight are expected. The standard error is
$\sqrt{0.32 \times 0.68/25} = 0.093$, so $z = (0.28 - 0.32)/0.093 = -0.43$,
a p-value of 0.33, and $H_0$ stands. Yet three of the four
fraudsters are joined to each other in a triangle. The test missed it
because it never counts fraud–fraud edges: three of them is far more than
chance gives, but they are three of 25 and move $\hat r$ only a little.

### The two ratios

The repair splits the edges by kind and compares each kind with its own
expectation. Write $n_1$ for the number of fraudulent nodes, N for all
nodes, and $m_{11}$, $m_{10}$, $m_{00}$ for the numbers of fraud–fraud,
fraud–legitimate and legitimate–legitimate edges, with
$M = m_{11} + m_{10} + m_{00}$.

If edges were placed between nodes regardless of their labels, each of the
$N(N-1)/2$ possible pairs would be an edge with the same probability, the
*connectance*

$$
p = \frac{2M}{N(N-1)},
$$

the share of possible edges that exist. There are $n_1(n_1-1)/2$ pairs of
fraudulent nodes and $n_1(N - n_1)$ mixed pairs, so the expected counts are

$$
E[m_{11}] = \frac{n_1(n_1 - 1)}{2}\,p, \qquad E[m_{10}] = n_1(N - n_1)\,p.
$$

<Definition title="dyadicity and heterophilicity">
The *dyadicity* of a labelled graph is $D = m_{11}/E[m_{11}]$ and its
*heterophilicity* is $H = m_{10}/E[m_{10}]$. The graph is *dyadic* if
$D > 1$ (fraud–fraud edges more frequent than chance gives) and
*anti-dyadic* if $D < 1$; it is *heterophobic* if $H < 1$
(fraud–legitimate edges less frequent than chance) and *heterophilic* if
$H > 1$. A graph that is dyadic and heterophobic is *homophilic*; one that
is anti-dyadic and heterophilic shows *inverse homophily*.
</Definition>

<Example title="both toys">
Twelve users: $N = 12$, $M = 14$, $n_1 = 4$, so $p = 28/132 = 0.212$.
$E[m_{11}] = 6 \times 0.212 = 1.27$ against $m_{11} = 5$, so $D = 3.9$;
$E[m_{10}] = 32 \times 0.212 = 6.79$ against $m_{10} = 2$, so $H = 0.29$.
Dyadic and heterophobic: homophilic.

Twenty nodes: $N = 20$, $M = 25$, $n_1 = 4$, so $p = 50/380 = 0.132$.
$E[m_{11}] = 6 \times 0.132 = 0.79$ against $m_{11} = 3$, so $D = 3.8$;
$E[m_{10}] = 64 \times 0.132 = 8.42$ against $m_{10} = 7$, so $H = 0.83$.
Also dyadic and heterophobic, so also homophilic, with $H$ close to 1.
</Example>

The cross-edge test sees only the second ratio. Divide each toy's observed
cross fraction by the $2lf$ the test compares it with: $0.143/0.444 = 0.32$
for the twelve users, against $H = 0.29$, and $0.28/0.32 = 0.88$ for the
twenty nodes, against $H = 0.83$. The two differ only by a factor
$N/(N-1)$ (an <Ref lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality"
id="exercises">exercise</Ref> derives it), so the test's $H_0$ is in effect
$H \geq 1$, and rejecting it says the graph is heterophobic.

### Which way a fraudulent neighbour points

The column the chapter started from, a node's number of fraudulent
neighbours, is evidence of fraud if fraudulent nodes score higher on it
than legitimate ones. The tempting reading is that $D$ alone decides this,
since $D$ counts fraudsters linking to fraudsters. But the legitimate
nodes score on the same column, and their fraudulent neighbours come from
the fraud–legitimate edges, which $H$ counts. Both ratios enter.

Count the scores on the twenty-node toy. Each fraud–fraud edge gives a
fraudulent neighbour to both of its ends, so the four fraudulent nodes
hold $2m_{11} = 6$ fraudulent neighbours between them, 1.5 each. Each
fraud–legitimate edge gives one to its legitimate end, so the sixteen
legitimate nodes hold $m_{10} = 7$, 0.44 each. The column points at the
fraudsters, by more than three to one.

In general the two averages are $2m_{11}/n_1$ and $m_{10}/(N - n_1)$.
Write the counts through the ratios, $m_{11} = D\,n_1(n_1-1)p/2$ and
$m_{10} = H\,n_1(N - n_1)p$, and the averages become

$$
\frac{2m_{11}}{n_1} = D\,(n_1 - 1)\,p, \qquad \frac{m_{10}}{N - n_1} = H\,n_1\,p.
$$

The connectance $p$ is common to both, so fraudulent nodes have more
fraudulent neighbours on average exactly when

$$
D\,(n_1 - 1) > H\,n_1, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \frac{D}{H} > \frac{n_1}{n_1 - 1}.
$$

On the toy, $3.8 \times 3 = 11.4$ against $0.83 \times 4 = 3.3$; times
$p = 0.132$ these are the 1.5 and 0.44 counted above. The factor
$n_1/(n_1 - 1)$ is $4/3$ with four fraudsters and close to 1 with
hundreds, so on a real graph the condition is in effect $D > H$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/dh-plane.svg"
  alt="A plane with dyadicity D from 0 to 4.5 on the horizontal axis and heterophilicity H from 0 to 2 on the vertical, dashed lines at D = 1 and H = 1 and a dotted diagonal D = H.   caption="Both toys lie in the homophilic quadrant. Right of the diagonal D = H, fraudulent nodes have more fraudulent neighbours than legitimate ones, up to the factor n₁/(n₁ − 1)."
/>

Homophily, $D > 1 > H$,
puts $D$ above $H$, and the condition holds unless both ratios sit so close
to 1 that $D/H$ falls short of $n_1/(n_1 - 1)$, a margin that vanishes as
$n_1$ grows. Inverse homophily, $D < 1 < H$, rules it out: then
$D(n_1 - 1) < n_1 - 1 < n_1 < H n_1$. The other two quadrants depend on
which ratio is larger. Take twenty nodes, four of them fraudulent, and 40
edges, only one of them fraud–fraud and one fraud–legitimate. Then
$p = 80/380 = 0.21$, $D = 1/1.26 = 0.79$ and $H = 1/13.5 = 0.07$: the
graph is anti-dyadic, yet the fraudulent nodes average 0.5 fraudulent
neighbours against 0.06 for the legitimate ones, because $H$ is smaller
still: the fraudsters almost never touch a legitimate node.

### When fraud avoids fraud

From the previous chapter one would expect every kind of fraud to cluster.
Online auction fraud does the opposite. A seller who means to take payment
and ship nothing first needs a good reputation, and reputation comes from
positive feedback after trades. So fraudsters open extra accounts, the
*accomplices*, which trade with honest users and look honest. The
fraudster then trades cheaply with its accomplices to collect feedback,
and, once the score is high enough, cheats honest buyers. Fraudsters link
mostly to accomplices and seldom to each other; accomplices link to both
sides. Labelled as fraudster against everyone else, the graph has $D < 1$
and $H > 1$: anti-dyadic and heterophilic, inverse homophily.

In accounting, by contrast, an account with suspicious entries tends to
transact with other suspicious accounts, and the graph is homophilic. By
the comparison above, the same column, "fraudulent neighbours", is a
positive signal for the accounting graph and a misleading one for the
auction graph, where it is highest for the accomplices and zero for the fraudsters themselves (the <Ref
lesson="neighbourhood-and-centrality" id="exercises">auction exercise</Ref>
works the case).

A classifier fitted on the column could learn a negative weight on its
own, given enough labelled fraudsters. The propagation methods of <Ref
lesson="guilt-by-association">a later chapter</Ref> cannot: they push
suspicion from known fraud to its neighbours by construction, and on an
inverse-homophilic graph they push it onto the accomplices and honest
buyers rather than onto the next fraudster.

<KeyFact>
A count of fraudulent neighbours is higher, on average, for fraudulent
nodes than for legitimate ones exactly when $D(n_1 - 1) > H\,n_1$, which
on a real graph is in effect $D > H$. Homophily all but guarantees it;
inverse homophily rules it out. A fitted classifier can still learn a
negative weight on the column, but propagation cannot, and on an
inverse-homophilic graph it pushes suspicion onto the accomplices.
</KeyFact>

## One hop: what a node's neighbourhood says

Where the test finds clustering, the cheapest columns describe a node's
immediate surroundings. Each is computable from the edge list in one or two
passes, and each answers a different question about the node's one-hop
world.

### Degree, split by the neighbours' labels

The *degree* of a node is its number of neighbours; in a directed graph,
the *in-degree* counts edges arriving and the *out-degree* edges leaving.
Split by the neighbours' labels it becomes two columns, the fraudulent
degree and the legitimate degree. Node G of the toy has degree 4, of
which three neighbours, D, F and I, are fraudulent: no node of the
toy, fraudulent or not, has more fraudulent neighbours.

Degree alone measures activity, and it falls off steeply in real networks:
most nodes have a handful of neighbours and a few have thousands. A
merchant with a thousand customers is not suspicious for having them; what
the split adds is the composition of those neighbours.

### The egonet: triangles and density

A count of neighbours says nothing about whether the neighbours know each
other. Five accounts that all transact with one another are a tight group;
five accounts that each deal only with the central one are a hub with
spokes. Both give the centre degree 4.

<Definition title="egonet">
The *egonet* of a node, the *ego*, is the subgraph made of the ego, its
neighbours (the *alters*) and every edge among those nodes, including the
edges between two alters.
</Definition>

The edges between alters are what the neighbour set lacks. Each one closes
a *triangle* with the ego: three nodes all joined to each other. A
triangle is *fraudulent* if both alters in it are fraudulent, *legitimate*
if neither is, and *semi-fraudulent* if one is. Finally, the *density* of
the egonet compares its edges with the most it could have: with N nodes
and M edges,

$$
\text{density} = \frac{M}{N(N-1)/2} = \frac{2M}{N(N-1)}.
$$

It is the connectance of the previous section, measured inside one egonet.

possible edges among five nodes, how many are present?
</Task>

<Example title="the egonet of G">
**Degree.** G has neighbours A, D, F, I: degree 4, fraudulent

**Egonet.** Among the alters, A–I and D–F are edges; A–D,

**Triangles.** Each alter edge closes one triangle: G–D–F, whose

</Example>

Density read alone misleads at small degree. An egonet with one neighbour
has two nodes and one edge, density $2/(2 \times 1) = 1$, as dense as a
triangle; on the toy every leaf (C, J, P, R, S, T) scores 1.
Density is informative only beside the degree that sets how much room
there was.

### Link columns for a regression

The quickest way for this information to reach a model is as ordinary
columns beside the node's own fields, its *intrinsic* features (an
account's age, a company's sector). Three forms keep different amounts of
the neighbours' labels. The *count*, the number of neighbours with each
label, is the degree split above and keeps everything, but it grows with
the degree. The *binary* indicator, whether any neighbour is fraudulent,
keeps only whether the fraudulent count is above zero, so a node with one
fraudulent neighbour among a thousand scores the same as a node whose one
neighbour is fraudulent. The *mode*, the most frequent label among the
neighbours, keeps only the majority: where fraud is rare it reads
"fraudulent" only for a node most of whose neighbours are known fraud,
like E, F and G on the toy. A logistic
regression on intrinsic plus link columns is called a *relational logistic
regression*, and it is <Ref lesson="scorecards-woe">the logistic
regression of the supervised module</Ref> with a few more inputs.

The three forms are functions of the same counts, so they are strongly
correlated, and a selection step keeps one or two. They also read labels,
so they obey the rule of every label-derived feature: the label on a
neighbour counts only if it <Ref lesson="point-in-time-features"
id="a-feature-built-from-labels-what-the-bank-knew-that-morning">had arrived
when the row's prediction was made</Ref>.

## Beyond one hop: walks, distances and position

Every column so far stops at the neighbours. H, J and R have no
fraudulent neighbour, but H is two steps from I along two different
routes, J two steps from it through K, and R four steps from the nearest
fraud. A model that sees only one hop scores the three alike.

### Counting walks with matrix powers

A *walk* of length $k$ is a sequence of $k$ edges, each starting where the
previous one ended; unlike a path, it may revisit a node. The adjacency
matrix $A$ of the previous chapter (<Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="matrix-or-list-what-the-graph-costs-to-store">rows and columns indexed
by nodes, 1 for an edge</Ref>) already counts walks of length 1: $A_{ij}$
is the number of one-edge walks from $i$ to $j$. Its powers count longer
ones.

Take four nodes with edges 1–2, 1–4, 2–4 and 3–4. The entry $(A^2)_{44}$ is
$\sum_m A_{4m}A_{m4}$, and the term for $m$ is 1 exactly when 4–$m$ and
$m$–4 are both edges, that is, when $m$ is a neighbour of 4. Node 4 has
three neighbours, so $(A^2)_{44} = 3$: the walks 4–1–4, 4–2–4 and 4–3–4.

A has rows 0 1 0 1, 1 0 0 1, 0 0 0 1, 1 1 1 0. A squared has rows 2 1 1 1, 1 2 1 1, 1 1 1 0, 1 1 0 3, with the bottom-right 3 highlighted in amber.   caption="Each entry of A² sums over the possible middle node. The highlighted 3 is the three ways out of node 4 and back."
/>

<Proposition title="powers count walks">
For the adjacency matrix $A$ of a graph and every $k \geq 1$, the entry
$(A^k)_{ij}$ is the number of walks of length $k$ from node $i$ to node $j$.
<Proof>
By induction on $k$. For $k = 1$ it is the definition of $A$. Suppose it
holds for $k$. Every walk of length $k+1$ from $i$ to $j$ is a walk of
length $k$ from $i$ to some node $m$, followed by one edge from $m$ to $j$.
For a fixed $m$ there are $(A^k)_{im}$ such walks if $m$–$j$ is an edge and
none otherwise, which is $(A^k)_{im} A_{mj}$ in both cases. Summing over
$m$ gives $\sum_m (A^k)_{im}A_{mj} = (A^{k+1})_{ij}$.
</Proof>
</Proposition>

Applied to labels, the proposition turns "how much fraud is within $k$
steps" into a matrix–vector product. Let $\mathbf{f}$ be the vector with 1
for each fraudulent node and 0 elsewhere. Then $(A\mathbf{f})_v$ is $v$'s
fraudulent degree, and $(A^2\mathbf{f})_v$ counts the two-step walks from
$v$ that end on a fraudulent node. For G it is 5: G–D–E,
G–D–F, G–F–D, G–F–E and G–A–I. Computed as
$A(A\mathbf{f})$, two sparse products, it costs two passes over the edge
list, never the dense matrix $A^2$.

Two cautions come with the count. Walks revisit, so the count grows with
the degrees along the way and is not a count of distinct routes. And a
fraudulent node's own label returns to it: $(A^2)_{vv}$ is $v$'s degree,
so for a fraudulent $v$ the count includes one walk out and back per
neighbour. On a training row that term is the row's own label inside its
feature. The column used below subtracts it, keeping only walks that end
on another fraudulent node.

### Distance to the nearest fraud, and closeness

A walk count mixes how far the fraud is with how many routes lead there.
Distance separates the first. The *geodesic distance* $d(u, v)$ between
two nodes is the number of edges on a shortest path between them. On an
unweighted graph a breadth-first search finds all distances from one node
in one pass over the edges; started from all fraudulent nodes at once, the
same search gives every node its distance to the nearest fraud. On the toy
that is 1 for A, B, G, K and N, 2 for H and J, and 4 for R
and S. On a weighted graph, Dijkstra's algorithm plays the same role, but
it needs lengths, where a large number means far; the recency weights of
the previous chapter mean close, so they are turned into lengths first, for
instance as $1/w$.

The same distances, taken to every node instead of to the fraud, measure
how central a node is in the whole graph. The *farness* of $v$ is its mean
distance to the other $N - 1$ nodes, and its *closeness* is the inverse:

$$
\operatorname{closeness}(v) = \left( \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{u \neq v} d(v, u) \right)^{-1}.
$$

Node A reaches six nodes in one step, seven in two and the remaining six
in three: farness $(6 + 14 + 18)/19 = 2.0$, closeness 0.50, the highest of
the toy. Between two components
the distance is infinite and the closeness would be 0; the convention used
here, and by most software, computes it within the node's own component.

### Betweenness: the node every route passes through

Closeness rewards a node for being near everything. A different position
matters for laundering: the account through which money must pass between
two groups that have no other link. Its degree may be small and its
closeness unremarkable, but cutting it disconnects the groups.

<Definition title="betweenness">
Let $g_{jk}$ be the number of shortest paths between nodes $j$ and $k$, and
$g_{jk}(v)$ the number of those that pass through $v$. The *betweenness*
of $v$ is

$$
\operatorname{betweenness}(v) = \sum_{j < k,\; j, k \neq v} \frac{g_{jk}(v)}{g_{jk}},
$$

the sum, over all pairs of other nodes, of the share of their shortest
paths that run through $v$.
</Definition>

<Figure
  src="./figures/bridge.svg"
  alt="Three groups of four nodes, each group fully connected, and a single amber node X joined by one amber edge to one member of each group. X is labelled degree 3, betweenness 48: every shortest path between two groups passes through it. A member inside one group, shaded blue, is labelled degree 3, betweenness 0"
  caption="Two nodes with degree 3. Only X lies on the routes between groups, and betweenness is what separates them."
/>

In the figure, the 48 is $3 \times 4 \times 4$: each of the three pairs of
groups has $4 \times 4$ pairs of members, and each of their shortest paths
goes through $X$. The blue node is on no shortest path between two other
nodes, because its group-mates reach each other directly.

<Example title="betweenness of Q and O on the toy">
R and S are joined only to Q, so every path that leaves either of
them starts with an edge into Q. Pair R with each of the 17 nodes
outside Q, R and S, and S likewise: $2 \times 17 = 34$ pairs whose
shortest paths all pass through Q. The pair R–S adds one more, since
its only path is R–Q–S. Betweenness 35, with a degree of 3.

O joins P, Q, R and S to the other fifteen nodes. Each of the
$4 \times 15 = 60$ pairs across O passes through it, and so do the three
pairs P–Q, P–R, P–S on its own side: betweenness 63, against a
degree of 3.
</Example>

<Exercise>
Compute the betweenness of N, whose neighbours are I, L and M.

<Solution>
L and M are joined to each other and to N, and to nothing else. Every
shortest path from L or M to the 17 nodes outside L, M and N passes
through N: $2 \times 17 = 34$ pairs. The pair L–M is joined directly,
so N gains nothing from it, and no path between two outside nodes enters
N, since it would have to leave by the same edge. Betweenness 34, the
toy table's value.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Fractions appear when a pair has several shortest paths. Take the pair A
and D: two routes of length 2 join them, A–B–D and A–G–D, so B and G
each lie on one of the two and each collect $1/2$ for the pair. B's whole
betweenness adds up from three kinds of pair:

- C is joined only to B, so every route from C to the other 18 nodes
  passes through B: 18 pairs at a full count each, 18.
- D reaches A, and the nodes that D can reach only through A (H, O,
  P, Q, R, S, T), by two equally short routes, one through B and one
  through G: 8 pairs at $1/2$, 4.
- E reaches the same eight nodes by three shortest routes, E–D–B–A,
  E–D–G–A and E–F–G–A, only the first through B: 8 pairs at $1/3$,
  2.67.

The total is $18 + 4 + 2.67 = 24.67$. The same split, applied to the
other routes of those pairs, gives the remaining fractional values in the
table below: G 35.3, D 13.8, F 6.2.

A mule account in a laundering chain receives from one group and pays out
to another, and is a candidate for this profile: small degree, large
betweenness. It is also the most expensive column here. Betweenness needs
the shortest paths from every node, one search per node, so on a graph of
a million nodes it is a million searches; in practice it is estimated from
searches started at a random sample of nodes, or computed only inside
small subgraphs. One more centrality, PageRank, ranks nodes by where a
random walk spends its time; it waits for <Ref
lesson="guilt-by-association">the chapter on random walks</Ref>, where it
is aimed at the known fraud.

## Columns nobody lists by hand

Each column so far was chosen by someone who thought it might matter. The
list is finite and the graph is not, so two moves generate columns
mechanically: aggregate what the neighbours have, and compare each egonet
with the egonets of everyone else.

### Recursive aggregates over the neighbours

Take any column a node already has, its degree, its amount sent last week,
its fraudulent degree, and add the mean, minimum and maximum of that column
over the node's neighbours. Node A has no unusual fraudulent degree
itself (1), but the maximum fraudulent degree among its neighbours is 3,
reached at G: one aggregate carried the information two steps. Repeating
the step on the new columns reaches three steps, and so on; each round is
one pass over the edges. Columns that come out nearly identical to an
existing one are dropped as they appear, or the table grows without
adding anything.

The <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="elliptic-a-graph-whose-labels-come-from-names">Elliptic Bitcoin
graph</Ref> was published with exactly this construction: next to each
transaction's 94 local features come 72 aggregated ones, the maximum,
minimum, standard deviation and correlation coefficients of the
neighbouring transactions' local features, one hop back and forward.

The aggregates have one weakness, and it is built into the maximum. A
maximum over three neighbours and a maximum over three hundred are
different quantities even when no neighbour is unusual.

<Figure
  src="./figures/aggregate-max.svg"
  alt="Expected value of an aggregate over k neighbours whose feature is standard normal, against k from 1 to 3,000 on a log scale. The blue line for the expected mean is flat at 0. The amber curve for the expected maximum rises from 0 at k = 1 through 0.85 at k = 3 and 2.51 at k = 100 to about 3.5 at k = 3,000. Note: every neighbour drawn from the same ordinary distribution, nobody unusual"
  caption="With no unusual neighbour at all, the maximum climbs with the number of neighbours. A tree split on it splits partly on degree."
/>

If every neighbour's feature is a standard normal draw, the expected
maximum is 0.85 standard deviations for three neighbours and 2.51 for a
hundred. A node with a hundred honest neighbours then shows a "maximum
neighbour amount" that a node with three would reach only with a strongly
unusual neighbour, and a classifier that splits on the column is partly
splitting on the degree. The mean does not drift this way, but it hides
the one unusual neighbour the maximum was there to find. Summaries of a
set whose size varies from node to node lose information either way, and
that loss is what <Ref lesson="graph-neural-networks">a layer that learns
its own aggregation</Ref> is built to reduce.

### Egonets against the law the others follow

The second move needs no labels. Compute two egonet columns for every
node, the number of neighbours $k$ and the number of edges in the egonet
$M$, and plot one against the other. Since the egonet's edges are the
$k$ spokes plus one edge per triangle, $M$ lies between $k$ (a *star*: no
neighbour knows another) and $k(k+1)/2$ (a *clique*: every neighbour knows
every other).

<Figure
  src="./figures/egonet-law.svg"
  alt="Log–log scatter of edges in the egonet against number of neighbours, with blue dots for about 170 nodes following a fitted blue line M proportional to k to the 1.25, between a lower dashed bound star M = k and an upper dashed bound clique M = k(k+1)/2. Two amber outliers: a near-clique with 12 neighbours and 58 edges among them, close to the upper bound, and a near-star with 150 neighbours and 3 edges among them, on the lower bound"
  caption="An illustrative synthetic graph of about 170 nodes, its law fitted to its own dots. Most egonets lie near one power law between the star and clique bounds; the nodes far from the fitted line are the ones to look at."
/>

On real graphs most nodes fall near a straight line on log–log axes, a
power law $M \propto k^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha$ between 1 and 2; the graph
in the figure is synthetic, built to follow such a law, and the line
through it, with $\alpha = 1.25$, is fitted to its own dots. On any graph,
the law is fitted to that graph's nodes, and the distance of a node's
point from the fitted line is a score. Far below it
sits a near-star: 150 counterparties who never deal with each other, the
shape of an account that collects from or pays out to many strangers, a
fan-in or fan-out of the <Ref lesson="fraud-as-a-graph"
id="eight-definitions">laundering motifs</Ref>. Far above sits a
near-clique: twelve accounts that all deal with one another, a closed
group. The score is "unlike the others", the <Ref
lesson="anomaly-detection-framing">anomaly score of the previous
module</Ref>, computed on graph columns instead of transaction fields, and
it carries the same caution: it ranks unusual shapes, not fraud.

### Reading the table

**The label-reading columns describe exposure.** D, E and F have two
fraudulent neighbours each, from their shared triangle. But the row with
the most fraudulent neighbours, three, and the most two-step walks to
fraud, five, is G, which is not known as fraud. And I, which is, has
no fraudulent neighbour at all. A tree split on "fraudulent neighbours
$\geq 2$" catches D, E, F and G, and misses I.

**The structural columns describe position.** I's row is distinguished
by its betweenness (65, second only to A) and its closeness (0.45), and
those columns do not read labels. They also rank A and O above I,
so they are not a fraud signal by themselves either.

With four positive rows, a random forest learns nothing reliable from this
table; the toy exists to make each column computable by hand. On a real graph with thousands of labelled
fraudsters it can learn which combinations of exposure and position go
with fraud in that graph, at a cost that rises from one pass over the
edges (degree, link counts, walk counts, aggregates) to one search per
node (closeness, betweenness). What it cannot learn is anything beyond the
reach of its columns: the one-hop columns see one hop, the aggregates as
many hops as rounds were run, and none of them sees the ring that D, E
and F form as a group.

<KeyFact>
A node next to fraud looks like fraud in every label-reading column. G is
either the next fraudster or the next victim, and no column of the table
can tell which; the investigator who calls G can.
</KeyFact>

<Conclusion>
You can now check whether fraud clusters in a labelled graph: the
cross-edge test sees only the heterophilicity $H$, the dyadicity $D$ adds
the fraud–fraud side, and a fraudulent neighbour is evidence of fraud when
$D(n_1 - 1) > H n_1$, in effect when $D > H$, which is why the column works
on an accounting graph and misleads on an auction graph. Where it works,
you can turn any node's surroundings into columns, from its degree split
by label, egonet triangles and density, through walk counts, distance to
fraud, closeness and betweenness, to recursive aggregates and an
egonet-law score, and say where each misleads: density at small degree,
the walk count through a node's own label, the maximum through the size of
the neighbourhood.

Every one of these columns describes one node. A fraud ring is a group,
and the next problem is to cut a graph of tens of thousands of users
into groups so that the few containing a known fraudster are where the
investigators look: <Ref lesson="communities-and-components">communities
and connected components</Ref>.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
(a) Show that the number of triangles through a node $v$ is
$(A^3)_{vv}/2$, and check it for G on the toy, where $(A^3)_{GG} = 4$.
(b) The cross-edge test expects a fraction $2lf$ of cross-labelled edges;
$H$ expects $E[m_{10}]/M$. Show that
$E[m_{10}]/M = 2 n_1 (N - n_1)/(N(N-1))$, compute both for the twelve
users, and explain where the difference comes from.

<Solution>
**(a)** By the proposition, $(A^3)_{vv}$ counts walks of length 3 from $v$
back to $v$: $v$–$a$–$b$–$v$ with $a$, $b$ neighbours of $v$ joined to each
other. $a \neq v$ and $b \neq v$ because there are no self-loops, and
$a \neq b$ for the same reason, so every such walk goes round a triangle.
Each triangle $\{v, a, b\}$ gives exactly two walks, one in each
direction: $v$–$a$–$b$–$v$ and $v$–$b$–$a$–$v$. Hence triangles $=
(A^3)_{vv}/2$. For G: $4/2 = 2$, the triangles G–D–F and
G–A–I.

**(b)** $E[m_{10}] = n_1(N - n_1)p$ with $p = 2M/(N(N-1))$, so
$E[m_{10}]/M = 2n_1(N - n_1)/(N(N-1))$. Twelve users: $2 \times 4 \times 8
/(12 \times 11) = 0.485$, an expectation of $6.79$ edges, against
$2lf = 2 \times \tfrac{8}{12} \times \tfrac{4}{12} = 0.444$ and $6.22$
edges. The ratio is $N/(N-1)$. The $2lf$ model draws each end's label
independently, as if with replacement, so it allows both ends of an edge
to be the same fraudulent node; the pair count draws two distinct nodes,
and once one end is a fraudster, a fraudster is slightly less likely at
the other end. The relative difference is $N/(N-1) - 1 = 1/(N-1)$: here
$1/11$, about 9%, which is $0.04$ per edge and $0.57$ of an edge over all
fourteen. On a graph of ten thousand nodes it is a hundredth of a percent.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

mule accounts, ten are not known as fraud, and neither is $h$. (a) Compute
$m_{11}$, $m_{10}$, $D$, $H$ and the cross-edge test. (b) Compute the

<Solution>
**(a)** $N = 16$, $M = 15$, $n_1 = 5$. No two fraudsters are joined:
$m_{11} = 0$. The five fraudster–hub edges are cross-labelled: $m_{10} =

\times \tfrac{5}{16} \times \tfrac{11}{16} = 0.430$, standard error
$\sqrt{0.430 \times 0.570/15} = 0.128$, $z = -0.75$, p-value 0.23: $H_0$

**(b)** $h$ has fraudulent degree 5 and betweenness
$\binom{15}{2} = 105$, since every pair of leaves is joined only through
it. Each mule has degree 1, fraudulent degree 0 and betweenness 0.

**(c)** Both columns point at $h$ and neither at the mules. $D = 0$: no
mule is joined to another, so a mule's neighbours carry no fraud, and
$D(n_1 - 1) = 0$ against $H n_1 = 3.6$; no neighbour-label column can
single out the next mule. $H = 0.73$ makes the
graph heterophobic, but too weakly for fifteen edges to show; the graph is
anti-dyadic, so not homophilic, and a propagation from the five known
mules reaches the ten other payees only through $h$, all ten alike. What
the columns do find is $h$: an account that has paid five confirmed mules
among fifteen payees is either the controller who recruits and pays them
or an honest payer the mules also deal with, such as a casual-work
employer. Like G on the toy, the columns cannot say which. The homophily
check asks whether a node's label predicts its neighbours' labels; a
column can still say something about a node with a different role.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
On the Elliptic graph, the published aggregates include the maximum of
each local feature over a transaction's neighbours. Design a check, runnable
on the released files, of whether the maximum aggregate depends on the
number of neighbours for reasons that have nothing to do with fraud. State

<Solution>
**Design.** Take the licit-labelled transactions only, so that fraud
cannot drive the result. For one local feature, standardise it over all
transactions, and for each licit transaction compute the maximum of the
standardised feature over its neighbours (recomputed from the edge list,
which also checks the published column). Group transactions by neighbour
count, 1, 2–3, 4–10, 11–30, 31–100, above 100, and report the median
maximum per group.

**Expected.** If neighbours' values behaved like independent draws, the
medians would rise like the expected maximum of the figure: about 0 for
one neighbour, 0.85 for three, 1.5 for ten, 2.5 for a hundred. Real
features are skewed and correlated between neighbours, so the levels will
differ; agreement means a median that rises steadily with the neighbour
count among licit transactions. A flat median would mean the
size effect is negligible for that feature.

**Correction.** Replace the maximum by its position against what $k$
ordinary neighbours give: subtract the median maximum of licit nodes with
the same neighbour count, or use the share of neighbours above a fixed
high quantile, which does not grow with $k$. Keep the neighbour count as
its own column.

**Shuffle test for homophily.** Keep the edges fixed, permute the labels
over the nodes (the same number of fraudsters, placed at random) a few
thousand times, and count cross-labelled edges each time. The p-value is
the share of permutations with as few cross edges as observed. High-degree
nodes are then handled correctly, because each permutation moves a node's
label together with all of its edges.
</Solution>

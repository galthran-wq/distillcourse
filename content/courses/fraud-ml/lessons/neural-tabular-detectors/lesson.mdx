On a table of engineered features, boosted trees usually win and are cheaper to train. Yet banks train neural networks on the same tables. What has to be done to make a feed-forward network competitive on transaction features — scaling, optimiser, stopping, regularisation, categorical inputs — and what does the network buy that the trees cannot?

The bar is already set. <Ref lesson="gbm-model-selection"
id="four-classes-side-by-side">The tree-ensemble lesson</Ref> tuned XGBoost on
the simulator's fifteen inputs with four prequential folds and scored the
folds' pick on four forward test weeks: ROC-AUC 0.869, average precision
0.687, card precision at 100 of 0.303. Its search took about twenty minutes
on one processor core, and on the real Belgian stream a tuned depth-6 tree
matched tuned XGBoost's card precision for a fraction of its compute.

A network brings none of the trees' indifference to how the inputs are
written. A tree asks one question of one input at a time, "is the amount
above 218.67?", so it does not care whether the amount is in euros or cents,
and it has two or three settings worth searching. A network multiplies every
input by a weight and adds them up, is trained by thousands of small
gradient steps whose size someone has to choose, keeps training as long as it
is told to, and has a dozen settings that interact. Each of those differences
is a place where the obvious choice, the one a tutorial makes, gives a
network that is slow, fragile or quietly fitted to the test week. And a
network brought level with the trees still has to justify the trouble.

## A network that outputs a fraud probability

The detector has to produce, for each payment, a number that ranks it among
the day's payments and that a decision priced in euros can read as a
probability. The
first design question is what the network's last layer computes, and with
which loss it is trained.

### One output unit, read as P(fraud)

The inputs are the fifteen numbers of each payment (<Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="five-models-scored-on-a-test-week">the five
baseline models' inputs</Ref>: the amount, the weekend and night flags, the
card's counts and mean amounts over 1, 7 and 30 days, the terminal's counts
and fraud rates over the same windows). A hidden layer of $h$ units computes,
for unit $j$,

$$
a_j = \max\Big(0,\; \sum_{i=1}^{15} w_{ij} x_i + b_j\Big),
$$

the ReLU of a weighted sum. One output unit then takes a weighted sum of the
$a_j$ and passes it through the sigmoid $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$, which
lands in $(0,1)$ and is read as $P(\text{fraud} \mid x)$.

With no hidden layer, the same construction is logistic regression: one
weighted sum of the inputs, one sigmoid. That is the model under <Ref
lesson="scorecards-woe" id="fitting-the-card-one-sign-to-check-per-line">the
scorecard</Ref>, which adds one term per variable and can therefore never
express that an input's effect depends on another's. The scorecard lesson's
last exercise found exactly such a dependence: an amount far above the
card's 30-day mean multiplies the fraud odds by about 265 among ordinary
amounts and by only about 23 among amounts above €201.82. Each hidden unit is a hinge, zero on one
side of a direction in input space and rising on the other, and a sum of
hinges can bend the log-odds surface where a sum of per-variable terms
cannot.

### Cross-entropy: the loss whose best answer is the fraud share

The network is fitted by minimising the binary cross-entropy over the
training rows,

$$
\mathrm{BCE}(y, p) = -\big(y \ln p + (1-y)\ln(1-p)\big),
$$

with $y \in \{0, 1\}$ the label and $p$ the network's output. The cost rule
of <Ref lesson="example-dependent-cost">example-dependent cost</Ref>, flag a
payment when $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}\cdot p$, needs $p$ to be a probability, not
only a score in the right order. Cross-entropy is the loss that delivers one.

<Example title="the constant that minimises cross-entropy on one group of payments">
Take the scorecard lesson's top amount bin: 200 training payments above
€201.82, of which 132 were fraud, a share of $q = 0.66$. Suppose a model must
give all 200 the same output $p$. The mean loss over the group is

$$
\overline{\mathrm{BCE}}(p) = -\big(0.66 \ln p + 0.34 \ln(1-p)\big).
$$

At $p = 0.5$ it is $0.693$; at $p = 0.9$, $0.66 \times 0.105 + 0.34 \times
2.303 = 0.852$; at $p = 0.66$, $0.66 \times 0.416 + 0.34 \times 1.079 =
0.641$, the smallest of the three. In general, setting the derivative to
zero,

$$
-\frac{q}{p} + \frac{1-q}{1-p} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = q ,
$$

so the loss is minimised by outputting the group's fraud share. A flexible
model trained by cross-entropy therefore aims, for every region of input
space, at the fraud share of the training rows there: an estimate of
$P(\text{fraud} \mid x)$ on the training distribution.
</Example>

The last three words matter. If the training rows were rebalanced, the
target is the rebalanced share, and <Ref
lesson="undersampling-calibration">the undersampling lesson's</Ref>
correction has to be applied before the cost rule reads the output.

### What the two curves say

The training loss falls from 0.035 to 0.014 and is still falling at the last
logged epoch. The test-week loss is 0.0197 at epoch 10 and 0.0194 at epoch
128: 118 more epochs, about eleven of the fourteen minutes at 5.7 seconds
an epoch, bought nothing on payments the network had not seen, while the
training loss halved.

That second fall is the network fitting rows it can memorise. A layer of
1,000 units has $15 \times 1{,}000 + 1{,}000$ weights and biases into the
hidden layer and $1{,}001$ out of it, 17,001 parameters, about 28 for every
training fraud. The [universal approximation
theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_approximation_theorem) says a single hidden
layer with enough units can approximate any continuous function on a bounded
region; a network of this width can learn the 598 frauds of one week one by
one, the way <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="what-auc-sees-in-the-fully-grown-tree">the fully grown tree</Ref> did. The overfitting is the same; what
differs is that a network does it gradually, epoch by epoch, so the number of
epochs is itself a setting that controls it.

A remark on the levels: the test-week loss lies below the training loss for
most of the first twenty epochs. Cross-entropy grows with the share of frauds, and
the test week, with known cards removed, holds fewer frauds per payment than
the training week (0.66% against 0.89%). Loss curves on different weeks are
compared by their shape, never by their level.

### A week to watch that is not the test week

The figure is informative only because it is read afterwards. Stopping
training where the test-week curve flattens would be choosing a setting on
the test week, which <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="neither-the-training-week-nor-the-test-week-can-choose">the delayed-labels
lesson</Ref> ruled out: the test number would then be the best of many noisy
looks, optimistic by construction. The loss has to be watched on a week that
plays the test week's role without being it. That is the hold-out geometry of
<Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="a-validation-week-that-stands-where-the-test-week-stands">a validation
week that stands where the test week stands</Ref>: train on 11–17 July,
leave 18–24 July as the delay, watch the loss on 25–31 July.

### Stopping when the validation loss stops improving

The validation loss is noisy from epoch to epoch (in the figure, the
test-week loss jumps from 0.0199 to 0.0286 at epoch 8 and comes straight
back), so "stop at the first epoch that does not improve" would stop on a
fluctuation. The rule tolerates a few bad epochs.

<Definition title="early stopping with patience">
After each epoch $e$, compute the validation loss $V_e$ and keep the best
epoch so far, $b = \arg\min_{e' \le e} V_{e'}$, together with a copy of the
weights at epoch $b$. A counter holds $e - b$, the number of epochs since the
best. With patience $P$, training stops at the first epoch where the counter
reaches $P + 1$, and the weights of epoch $b$ are restored.
</Definition>

Two parts of the definition are easy to drop. The counter counts epochs
since the best, not consecutive increases, so a slow drift upward with
occasional small dips below the previous epoch still stops. And the restore
matters because the weights at the stopping epoch are $P + 1$ epochs past the
best: a common implementation of the rule tracks the best loss but
never saves the weights, and returns the model of the last epoch instead.

Why watch the loss rather than card precision at 100, the metric that will
choose the model? The loss uses every validation row and moves smoothly;
card precision on one validation week moves in steps of one card in a hundred
and depends only on the top of each day's list, so a rule watching it would
react to which three or four cards happened to cross the hundredth place.

## Which optimiser: 251 epochs or 13

With the stopping rule in place the run can be left to decide its own
length. How long it takes depends on how each step is computed.

### Plain SGD at a small rate

The first trial: the same 1,000-unit network, SGD with learning rate 0.0005,
batches of 64, early stopping with patience 2 on the validation week. The
validation loss falls quickly for ten epochs, then more and more slowly,
by the end a few hundred-thousandths per epoch, but almost every epoch is a
new best, so the counter never fills. The run stops after
251 epochs, its best at epoch 248 with a validation loss of about 0.0218.

The reason lies in the size of the gradient. For the output unit, the
derivative of the cross-entropy with respect to its weighted sum is $p - y$.
On a training week where 99.1% of payments are genuine and most of them are
scored near zero after the first epochs, $p - y$ is a few thousandths for
almost every row; only the frauds, about 600 in a week, and the few genuine
payments that look like them produce gradients of order one. A batch of 64 payments holds on
average half a fraud, so the batch-mean gradient is small, and SGD's step is
the learning rate times that gradient. At 0.0005 the steps are tiny.

SGD is not slow in itself. The first run used SGD at 0.07, 140 times the
rate, and its held-out loss was at its level within ten epochs. What SGD
needs is a learning rate matched to the scale of the gradients, and that
scale depends on the base rate, the inputs and the stage of training.

### What Adam changes

[Adam](https://pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.optim.Adam.html), the optimiser from the course prerequisites, keeps for each weight a
running mean $m_t$ of its gradients and a running mean $v_t$ of their
squares, corrects both for their start at zero ($\hat m_t$, $\hat v_t$), and
steps by

$$
w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta\, \frac{\hat m_t}{\sqrt{\hat v_t} + \epsilon} .
$$

The ratio $\hat m_t / \sqrt{\hat v_t}$ is about $\pm 1$ when a weight's
gradient keeps the same sign, whatever its magnitude, and near 0 when the
sign flips from batch to batch. Each weight therefore moves by roughly $\eta$
per step in a consistent direction, whether its gradients are of order one or
of order a thousandth. On the fraud table that is the difference that
matters: the steps no longer shrink with $p - y$.

gradients, the useful values sit in a fixed range, 0.0001 to 0.001, and a

## Scaling the inputs: trees compare, networks multiply

Every run so far fed the network standardised inputs. Trees did not need
that: a strictly increasing transformation of an input leaves every split
available and sends the same payments each way, as <Ref
lesson="gbm-model-selection" id="exercises">the tree-ensemble lesson's last
exercise</Ref> showed. The question is what a network does with the inputs
as they come.

  src="./figures/input-scales.svg"

### A step proportional to the input

For a first-layer weight $w_{ij}$, the chain rule gives

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ij}} = \delta_j \, x_i ,
$$

where $\delta_j$ is the derivative of the loss with respect to unit $j$'s
weighted sum, the same for every input of that unit. The gradient on each
weight is the shared factor $\delta_j$ times the value of the input it
multiplies.

<Example title="one payment, two scales">
A payment on a card with 80 payments in the last 30 days, at a terminal
whose 7-day fraud rate is 0.6. For any hidden unit $j$, the gradient on the
weight from the 30-day count is $80\,\delta_j$, the gradient on the weight
from the terminal rate $0.6\,\delta_j$. The count's gradient is 133 times
larger, and a plain gradient step moves its weight 133 times further.

After standardising, with the simulated week's means and standard deviations
(count 77.0 and 29.5, terminal rate 0.007 and 0.067), the two inputs become
$(80 - 77.0)/29.5 = 0.10$ and $(0.6 - 0.007)/0.067 = 8.9$. Now the terminal
rate's gradient is 88 times the count's. The payment is
ordinary in its card count and extreme in its terminal rate, and after
standardising the step says so; before, the step reflected only that counts
are written in larger numbers than rates.
</Example>

The trouble is that one learning rate serves every weight, and the right
size of step along a weight depends on how sharply the loss curves along it.
Take a single logistic unit, $p = \sigma(\sum_i w_i x_i + b)$, trained by
mean cross-entropy over $n$ rows. Its gradient along $w_i$ is the mean of
$(p - y)\,x_i$; differentiating once more, with $\partial p / \partial w_i
= p(1-p)\,x_i$,

$$
\frac{\partial^2 \overline{\mathrm{BCE}}}{\partial w_i^2} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{\text{rows}} p(1-p)\, x_i^2 .
$$

The factor $p(1-p)$ is the same for every input of a row, so the curvature
along each weight is, up to that shared weighting, the mean square of its
input: about 6,800 for
the 30-day card count on the simulated week and 0.003 for the 30-day
terminal fraud rate, a ratio of more than a million. Near its minimum
$w^*$, the loss along a direction of curvature $c$ is close to the quadratic
$\tfrac{c}{2}(w - w^*)^2$, whose gradient is $c\,(w - w^*)$. A step of rate
$\eta$ therefore moves $w$ to $w - \eta c\,(w - w^*)$, which multiplies the
distance to the minimum by $(1 - \eta c)$. Once $\eta c > 1$ that factor is
negative and the step lands past the minimum; once $|1 - \eta c| > 1$, that
is once $\eta > 2/c$, the step lands further from the minimum than it
started, and every step after it further still. A rate below $2/6{,}800$, safe along the count's weight, moves
the terminal-rate weight about a millionth of the way to its minimum per
step; a rate large enough to move the terminal-rate weight sends the
count's weight back and forth across its minimum, further each time. That is
divergence.

### What happened without scaling

The experiment was run on the validation week with Adam and dropout (the
next section's regulariser), once with standardised inputs at learning rate
0.0005 and once with the raw inputs. With raw inputs the rate had to be cut
to 0.0001 to keep training from diverging, and even then the training and
validation losses were noisier and settled at worse levels than with
standardised inputs.

Adam gives every weight a step of about $\eta$, so the curvature argument
above, which is about plain gradient steps, does not apply to it directly.
What it equalises is the step in weight space. What a hidden unit computes
is its weighted sum, and a step of $\eta$ on weight $w_{ij}$ moves that sum
by $\eta\,x_i$: about $80\eta$ for a 30-day count of 80, $0.007\eta$ for a
typical terminal fraud rate. When every weight takes its step at once, a
unit's sum on a raw payment moves by up to $\eta$ times the sum of the
inputs' sizes, a few hundred, against about a dozen once the fifteen inputs
are standardised; the rate had to come down to keep those jumps in check.

The raw scales also set where training starts. The first-layer weights
begin as small random numbers, a few tenths at most, so each unit's initial
sum on a raw payment is fixed by the counts and amounts, tens each, while
the terminal rates add thousandths. Counts and amounts are all positive,
so a unit whose random weights make the counts-and-amounts part of its sum
negative is off for almost every payment; it passes back no gradient, and
Adam has nothing to normalise. The units that are on start out reading counts and amounts and
nearly blind to the inputs that carry the terminal signal. Standardising
removes both problems before the first step.

### Standardise on the training block; compress a long tail first

Standardising is a fitted step: its mean and standard deviation are
computed on the training block and then applied unchanged to the validation
and test weeks, exactly as a model's weights are. Computing them on the
whole table would let the test weeks' values shape the inputs the model was
trained on, the whole-table pitfall of <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates"
id="three-ordinary-table-operations-that-break-the-rule">the aggregates
lesson</Ref>; on prequential folds the scaler is refitted on each fold's
training block.

Standardising fixes the spread, not the shape. An input with a long right
tail, like the amount on a real card stream, where most payments are tens of
euros and a few are thousands, has its standard deviation set by the few
largest values; after dividing by it, the typical payments are squeezed into
a narrow band near zero and the network sees mostly the tail. The usual
repair is to replace the amount by $\log(1 + \text{amount})$ before
standardising.

<FigureSequence
  name="amount"
  alt="Two histograms of the same 58,957 payment amounts from one simulated week. Frame 2, log of one plus the amount in bins of 0.35: the bulk sits between 3 and 4.5 with a peak near 4, and a tail of a few hundred payments stretches down toward 0; skewness minus 0.8"

On the simulator, the transform has little to repair. Each simulated
customer draws amounts from a normal distribution around their own mean, so
the week's amounts have a moderate right tail (skewness +1.7), and the log
turns it into a left tail of about the same size (−0.8), because it stretches
the many payments of a few euros. On real amounts, which span from cents to
thousands, the log is often what makes the input usable. Which form to feed
is decided the way everything else is: on the folds.

## Dropout: an ensemble inside one network

Early stopping limits how long the network fits the training week. It does
nothing about what the 1,000 units fit while they train, and a unit can
come to depend on a handful of others to recognise a single training fraud.
The second brake changes the network that is trained at each step.

### Each batch trains a different sub-network

*Dropout* with rate $p$ sets each hidden unit's output to zero with
probability $p$, independently for each unit and each batch, and multiplies
the surviving outputs by $1/(1-p)$ so that their sum keeps its expected
size. Each batch therefore trains a different random sub-network, about
$(1-p)$ of the units, and no unit can rely on any particular other being
present. At prediction time no unit is dropped, and the full network acts
approximately as the average of the sub-networks trained along the way. It
is the forest's move, many models fitted on perturbed versions of the
problem and averaged so that what each memorised alone cancels (<Ref
lesson="gbm-model-selection"
id="random-forest-depth-stops-mattering-once-trees-are-averaged">the forest
in the tree-ensemble lesson</Ref>), carried out inside one set of weights.

### Training mode and evaluation mode

The averaging only happens if the network knows which mode it is in.
Frameworks switch dropout on and off with a training flag (in PyTorch,
`model.train()` and `model.eval()`); a validation loss computed with the
flag left in training mode scores a different random sub-network on every
batch, and comes out both noisier and worse than the network really is.

### How much dropout: a setting searched with the epochs

The rate trades two things. A larger $p$ averages more different
sub-networks and leaves each unit less room to memorise, but each unit is
also present in only a fraction $1-p$ of the batches: at $p = 0.4$ it
receives 60% of the updates a unit of the plain network would. A rate that
regularises well over a long run can therefore leave a short run
undertrained, and the best rate depends on how long the network trains. So
the rate is chosen together with the number of epochs, on validation data
like any other setting, and its candidates include 0, the plain network.

## Categorical inputs: embeddings

The fifteen inputs are all numbers. A payment also carries categories: the
terminal it was made at, the day of the week, and on real data the
merchant's category, the country, the entry mode. A network has to turn a
category into numbers before its first layer can multiply them, and for
trees <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates"
id="the-identifier-as-a-lookup-table">the aggregates lesson</Ref> already
showed what the raw identifier becomes: a lookup table of which terminals
were compromised in the training week.

### One column per value

The obvious encoding is one-hot: a column per value, 1 in the payment's
column and 0 elsewhere. For the weekday that is 7 columns. For the
simulator's 10,000 terminals it is 10,000 columns, and a first layer of 500
units would need five million weights for this one input, each trained only
by the payments made at its terminal. A training week of 67,240 payments
spread over 10,000 terminals gives each terminal about seven: 500 weights
fitted from seven payments, almost all of them genuine.

### An embedding is a linear layer on the one-hot input

<Figure
  src="./figures/embedding.svg"
  alt="A one-hot row for the weekday, seven cells Mon to Sun with a 1 under Sat, multiplied by a seven-by-three table E whose Saturday row reads minus 0.41, plus 0.35, plus 0.26, equals a one-by-three vector minus 0.41, plus 0.35, plus 0.26, which joins the fifteen inputs of the first layer. Values are illustrative; k equals the ceiling of log base 2 of 7, which is 3"
  caption="Multiplying a one-hot row by the table picks out one row of it."
/>

<Definition title="embedding">
For a categorical input with value set $C$, an *embedding of dimension $k$*
is a table $E$ of $|C|$ rows and $k$ columns. A payment with value $c$ is
represented by row $c$ of $E$, written $e_c$, which is placed beside the
numerical inputs of the first layer. The rows of $E$ are parameters of the
network, trained by the same gradient steps as every other weight.
</Definition>

If $o_c$ is the one-hot row of value $c$ (length $|C|$, a single 1 in
position $c$), then

$$
o_c\, E = e_c ,
$$

because the product adds up the rows of $E$ weighted by the entries of
$o_c$, and only row $c$ has weight 1. An embedding of dimension $k$ is
therefore exactly a linear layer without bias that takes the one-hot input
and has $k$ outputs. Nothing is computed differently; the lookup only skips
multiplying by $|C| - 1$ zeros, and its gradient touches only the rows of the
values present in the batch.

What the embedding changes is the width. With $k$ columns, the terminal input
costs $10{,}000 \times k$ parameters plus $k \times 500$ into the hidden
layer, instead of $10{,}000 \times 500$, and every value is placed in the
same $k$-dimensional space, where values that the training data treats alike
end up with similar rows. A common convention sets $k = \lceil \log_2 |C|
\rceil$, which gives 3 for the weekday and 14 for 10,000 terminals, and
starts each row with small random values, for instance drawn uniformly from
$[-0.05, 0.05]$.

<Solution>

**(b)** Embedding route: $10{,}000 \times 14 = 140{,}000$ table entries plus

route: $10{,}000 \times 500 = 5{,}000{,}000$ weights, about 34 times as many.

**(c)** Encoders reserve an "unknown" row for values not seen in training;
since no training payment had that value, the row keeps its random starting

to the identifier lookup table; only features recomputed from the terminal's
recent labelled history, like the delayed terminal fraud rate, can see it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### What the terminal and the weekday added on the simulator: nothing

Adding the terminal identifier and the weekday as two embedded inputs,
dimension 10 each, beside the fifteen numerical inputs, and training with
Adam and dropout 0.2 on the same training and validation weeks, gave a
validation loss no better than without them. It is a null
result, and it has two plain causes. The terminal's fraud history is already
among the inputs: the delayed terminal fraud rate is <Ref
lesson="point-in-time-features" id="every-statistic-of-labels-target-encoding">the
point-in-time target encoding of the terminal identifier</Ref>, so a learned
terminal vector can only add a memory of which terminals were bad in the
training week, the lookup table again. The weekday carries nothing because
the simulator's frauds ignore the calendar: no scenario depends on the day,
which is also why the weekend flag earned an information value of 0.002 in
<Ref lesson="scorecards-woe" id="the-simulators-fifteen-inputs">the
scorecard lesson</Ref>.

Embeddings earn their place where the categories carry signal that no
aggregate has summarised: on real data with many categorical fields, and in
the sequence models of a later module, where each past payment of a card
enters with its own merchant category and country.

## What the tuning costs: 162 networks on four folds

A network has more settings than any tree ensemble, and none of them has a
library default that comes from fraud data: the 1,000 units, the rate 0.0005,
the dropout of 0.2 were all choices. They have to be chosen the way <Ref
lesson="gbm-model-selection"
id="three-test-numbers-for-one-model-class">the tree-ensemble lesson</Ref>
chose an ensemble's: candidates validated on four prequential folds, the
best mean deployed, and every candidate also scored on the four forward test
weeks to see what the choice cost.

### The grid

The search crossed learning rates 0.0001, 0.0002 and 0.001; batch sizes 64,
128 and 256; 10, 20 or 40 epochs; dropout 0, 0.2 or 0.4; and one or two
hidden layers of 500 units: $3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 3 \times 2 = 162$
candidates, each trained on four folds and again on the four test-week
training blocks, with Adam. Inside the grid the number of epochs was a
setting instead of being decided by early stopping, because early stopping
would need a separate validation week inside each fold's training block.
The search took 7,350 seconds with ten fits running in parallel, against
1,251 seconds on a single core for XGBoost's eighteen candidates: six times
the wall-clock time while using ten times the processors.

<Aside>
**What the "two hidden layers" setting tested.** In the code behind this
grid, the second hidden layer was kept in a plain Python list inside the
PyTorch module. A module registers only layers assigned as modules (or held
in a `torch.nn.ModuleList`), so the extra layer's weights were never handed
to the optimiser: they stayed at their random starting values. The
"two-layer" candidates were one trained layer followed by a fixed random
layer of 500 ReLU units. Nothing in the scores flags such a bug; counting
the parameters the optimiser actually holds, `sum(p.numel() for p in
model.parameters())`, against the architecture on paper does.
</Aside>

### Batch size and epochs count the same thing

The grid's slices show the settings interacting. At 20 epochs, smaller
batches gave better average precision and card precision; at 40 epochs, the
best batch size was larger. The interaction is arithmetic. A training block
holds about 67,000 payments, so an epoch is about 1,051 updates at batch
size 64 and 263 at batch size 256. What the network learns depends mostly on
the number of updates and their size, and an epoch count means four times as
many updates at batch 64 as at batch 256.

<Exercise>

</Solution>
</Exercise>

### Dropout and epochs move together

The same slices show the trade the dropout section predicted. At learning
rate 0.001, batches of 64 and 20 epochs, a dropout rate of 0.2 gave better

the folds' picks for ROC-AUC and average precision, in the table below,
both use 0.4, with 40 epochs: twice the training let units present in 60%
of the batches catch up, and the stronger averaging then paid. Read alone,
the 20-epoch slice would have ruled out the rate the search ended up
choosing.

## The verdict, and what a network buys

After the five decisions, the network can be put beside the trees on equal
terms: the same inputs, the same folds, the same test weeks.

### A tie on the simulator, a loss below the top on real data

On the simulator the network's ROC-AUC (0.876) is 0.007 above XGBoost's,
inside both standard deviations; its average precision (0.675) is 0.012
below, about one standard deviation; its card precision equals XGBoost's,
0.303. On the real Belgian stream, searched over a smaller grid with the
same geometry as <Ref lesson="gbm-model-selection"
id="the-four-searches-on-the-real-stream">the four searches on the real
stream</Ref> (hidden layers of 500 units, dropout 0 or 0.2, rate 0.001 or
0.0001, 5, 10 or 20 epochs, batches of 64, 128 or 256: 36 candidates), the
network finds 23.6 compromised cards a day in its top hundred against
XGBoost's 23.5 and the depth-6 tree's 23.2, all inside weekly standard
deviations of four to seven cards. Below the top it loses: ROC-AUC 0.894
against 0.928, average precision 0.084 against 0.095, level with the random
forest (0.912 and 0.080).

<KeyFact>
Tuning brings the network level with the best tree ensemble exactly where
the bank works, the hundred cards a day, and nowhere better; below the top
on real payments it trails, and its search costs several times the compute.
Accuracy on a static table is therefore no reason to choose it.
</KeyFact>

A familiar expectation is that a neural network, being the more flexible
model, should beat trees once it is tuned. On these tables it does not, and
the reason is in the tables themselves. The fifteen inputs are already the
summaries that make fraud visible: the terminal's recent fraud rate, the
card's recent counts and amounts. What remains is to cut and combine a
handful of strong, unevenly scaled inputs, which a tree does with one
threshold per question, and a network has to approximate by summing smooth
hinges fitted with gradient steps. Flexibility that the data does not
demand buys nothing, and on real data, where the signal is weaker and the
noise larger, the ensemble's lead in the ranking below the top is the
measurable difference.

### Four things that come naturally to a network

If not accuracy, then what? Each of the four answers below is something a
tree ensemble can approach only by a costlier or more roundabout route,
while for a network it follows from being a fixed set of weights trained by
gradient steps.

**Updating the same model.** Both kinds of model can learn from the newest
labelled day without the nightly rebuild of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="how-often-the-model-changes-a-nightly-retrain-beside-hand-edited-rules">the
system lesson</Ref>. A boosted ensemble does it by appending trees fitted to
the current ensemble's errors on that day; the trees already there keep the
splits they chose on older data, and the ensemble grows with every update.
A network continues its gradient steps on the same weights, which carry
everything it learned before and can all move; the model stays the same
size. Either route spares the bank from rereading months of raw payments,
which data-protection law limits how long it may store. <Ref
lesson="stream-learning-and-adaptation">Stream learning and adaptation</Ref>
takes up what such updating does to a model when the stream drifts.

**Learning its own inputs.** The fifteen inputs were built by hand: someone
chose the windows, the entities and the statistics. A network can take the
card's last payments as raw input, learn its own summary of them and train
that summary and the classifier by one loss, end to end, because the
gradient passes back through every layer to the raw fields. A tree's split
is a hard threshold with no gradient to pass back, so in front of a tree
ensemble the summary has to be built separately. <Ref
lesson="sequence-models-for-transactions">Sequence models over an entity's
transactions</Ref> tries it, against the hand-built aggregates.

<Figure
  src="./figures/four-reasons.svg"
  alt="Four panels. 1, update the same model: days t minus 2, t minus 1 and t, with day t feeding a few steps into the weights; a network continues gradient steps on the same weights and stays the same size, while a boosted ensemble appends new trees and leaves the old splits as they were. 2, learn from raw sequences: five raw payments p1 to p5 feed a learned summary trained together with the classifier, in place of hand-built 1, 7 and 30-day windows; a tree's split passes no gradient back. 3, average weights across banks: banks A, B and C send weights to a mean of weights; same architecture, local data, only the weights travel, while federated trees exchange split statistics as each tree is built. 4, a partner unlike the trees: XGBoost and a network feed an average of scores; a different kind of model, partly different frauds"
  caption="What a network makes cheap or direct beside a tree ensemble; none of the four is accuracy on a static table."
/>

**Averaging across institutions.** Banks cannot pool their payment logs. If
each starts from one shared network, trains it on its own data and sends
back only the weights, the weights can be averaged into a model none of them
could have trained alone (the scheme is called federated averaging). A
weighted average of networks with the same architecture is again a network
of that architecture; an average of two trees' split thresholds is not a
tree that either bank's data chose. Boosted trees can be trained across
banks too, but the banks then have to take part in building each tree,
exchanging statistics of the candidate splits as it is grown, where the
network's banks exchange finished weights once per round.

**A partner unlike the trees.** Two models with the same card precision need
not find the same cards, and an average of two scores beats both only when
their mistakes differ. Two tree ensembles fitted to the same fifteen inputs
tend to cut on the same strong inputs and flag the same cards; a network
fits a smooth surface by gradient steps, a different route to the same
target, so it is a likelier source of different mistakes. None of the four
is measured here; they are the reasons the bank pays for the search, not
results.

## ★ Imbalance levers inside the loss

Every network above was trained on the stream's own class mix. The levers
of <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="why-every-remedy-moves-the-boundary-the-same-way">the imbalance
lesson</Ref> have direct network forms. A *weighted cross-entropy* multiplies
the fraud rows' loss by a weight $\omega$: the class weight, with the same
effect of moving the boundary into the genuine payments. A *balanced
sampler* builds each batch with equal numbers of frauds and genuine
payments, which is undersampling done batch by batch; the outputs then
estimate the fraud share of the balanced batches and need the keep-rate
correction before any rule reads them as probabilities. *Focal loss*
multiplies each row's cross-entropy by $(1 - p_t)^\gamma$, where $p_t$ is the
probability given to the row's true class, so that rows already classified
confidently, almost all of them genuine, contribute little. None of these
was run on the simulator here, and the imbalance lesson's finding, that the
best setting for average precision and card precision is no or the mildest
rebalancing, gives no reason to expect more from them than from the trees'
versions.

One more implementation detail: wrappers that make a PyTorch network behave
like a scikit-learn classifier, such as [skorch](https://skorch.readthedocs.io/),
expect two outputs with a softmax, one probability per class. A softmax over
two outputs $z_1, z_0$ gives $P(\text{fraud}) = \sigma(z_1 - z_0)$, so the
two-output network is the one-output network with a redundant parameter, and
its cross-entropy is the same loss.

<Conclusion>
You can now train a feed-forward network on transaction features and say
why each of its parts is there: a sigmoid output with
cross-entropy because the cost rule needs a probability and cross-entropy's
minimiser is the fraud share; early stopping with patience on a validation
week, with the best weights restored, because training loss keeps falling
long after held-out loss has stopped; Adam because its steps do not shrink
with the tiny gradients of a table that is 99% confidently genuine;
standardised inputs, fitted on the training block, because a first-layer
gradient is proportional to the input it multiplies; dropout and the number
of epochs searched together, and embeddings for categories that no
aggregate has summarised. Tuned on the same folds, the network ties tuned
XGBoost at the hundred-card budget on the simulator and on real data and
trails it below the top on real payments, at several times the compute. The
reasons to train it are what it makes cheap beside the trees: continuing to
train the same weights, learned representations, weight averaging across
banks and a different kind of mistake to average with.

The comparison so far has been five model classes, one at a time. <Ref
lesson="worldline-model-comparison">Eight models on Worldline data</Ref> puts
eight families, sequence networks included, through one experiment on real
card payments, where three metrics crown three different winners, and asks
what in the validation design and the compute produces that.
</Conclusion>

A bank scores each payment within its authorisation deadline. Compare the
cost of one prediction for (a) a network with 15 inputs, one hidden layer of
500 units and one output; (b) the same network with a second hidden layer of
500 units; (c) a depth-6 decision tree; (d) XGBoost with 100 trees of depth
3. Count parameters for (a) and (b) and multiply-adds or comparisons for
all four. Where, then, does the network's extra cost lie?

<Solution>
**(a)** Into the hidden layer, $15 \times 500 = 7{,}500$ weights and 500
biases; out of it, 500 weights and 1 bias: 8,501 parameters. A prediction
takes $7{,}500 + 500 = 8{,}000$ multiply-adds, 500 ReLUs and one sigmoid.

**(b)** A second layer adds $500 \times 500 + 500 = 250{,}500$ parameters,
259,001 in all, and $250{,}000$ more multiply-adds: about 258,000 per
prediction.

**(c)** A depth-6 tree compares at most 6 values per prediction.

**(d)** 100 trees of depth 3: at most 300 comparisons and 100 additions.

Even (b) is a quarter of a million operations, well under a millisecond on
one core, so the deadline is not where the network costs more. The cost is
in training: hundreds of gradient steps per epoch, epochs searched or
stopped by a rule, and a grid of 162 candidates on four folds, against
trees whose fits take seconds and whose defaults sit on a plateau.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push dropout to its edges on the 500-unit network trained for 20 epochs at
learning rate 0.001. (a) At $p = 0.9$, about how many units does each batch
train, how often is a given unit updated, and what do you predict for the
training and validation losses after 20 epochs? (b) At $p = 0$, what do you
predict if the network is instead trained for 200 epochs without early
stopping? (c) Which hypothesis of the "ensemble inside one network" picture
fails at each edge?

<Solution>
**(a)** Each batch keeps about $0.1 \times 500 = 50$ units, and a given unit
is present in about one batch in ten, so in 20 epochs it receives the
updates of about 2 epochs. The surviving outputs are multiplied by
$1/(1-0.9) = 10$, so each batch's sub-network is a small, very noisy one.
Prediction: both losses stay high and fall slowly; the network underfits.
Training loss stays close to validation loss, because there is too little
fitting for either to separate them.

**(b)** With every unit present at every step, nothing stops units from

the best epoch.

**(c)** The picture needs sub-networks that are each good enough to be worth
averaging, and enough of them to average. At $p = 0.9$ the first hypothesis
fails: fifty-unit networks trained on a tenth of the steps are each too weak,
and averaging weak models cannot add the detail every one of them lacks, the
forest of depth-5 trees again. At $p = 0$ the second fails: there is one
network, not many, so nothing averages out what it memorises. This is why
the grid includes 0 and why a rate of 0.4 was only competitive with 40
epochs.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague writes: "Standardising the inputs cannot matter for a network.
The first layer computes $\sum_i w_{ij} x_i + b_j$, and if $x_i$ is
replaced by $(x_i - \mu_i)/s_i$, the weights $w_{ij} s_i$ and a shifted
bias give exactly the same function. Whatever the network can learn from
standardised inputs, it can learn from raw ones." Say which part is true,
and name the step that fails.

<Solution>
The first part is true: for every network on standardised inputs there is a
network on raw inputs computing the same function, with weights
$w_{ij}/s_i$ and bias $b_j - \sum_i w_{ij}\mu_i/s_i$. The set of functions
the network can represent is unchanged.

The failing step is "it can learn", which is a claim about the training
procedure, not about the set of functions. Training moves each weight by
the learning rate times its gradient, and the gradient on $w_{ij}$ is
$\delta_j x_i$, proportional to the raw input. On the simulator the inputs'
standard deviations differ by a factor of about 800 and their mean squares
by more than a million, so the loss is steep along the weights of large
inputs and nearly flat along those of the terminal fraud rates. One
learning rate cannot suit both: large enough to move the fraud-rate weights,
it makes the count and amount weights overshoot and diverge; small enough
for those, it leaves the fraud-rate weights almost where they started. The
measured consequence: without scaling the rate had to be cut from 0.0005 to
0.0001, and the losses still settled at worse levels. The equivalent raw-input
network exists; gradient descent at a usable rate does not find it in the
same number of steps.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
An embedding of dimension $k$ for a categorical input with values $C$ is
followed by the first layer, whose weights from the embedding form a $k
\times h$ matrix $W$. (a) Show that, for this input, the composition
computes $o_c M$ for one $|C| \times h$ matrix $M$ of rank at most $k$, and
that every such matrix arises this way. (b) What does the rank constraint
buy, and what does it cost, for the terminal input with $|C| = 10{,}000$,
$k = 14$ and $h = 500$?

<Solution>
**(a)** The embedding gives $e_c = o_c E$ with $E$ of size $|C| \times k$,
and the first layer adds $e_c W = o_c (E W)$ to its weighted sums. So the
input contributes $o_c M$ with $M = EW$, a $|C| \times h$ matrix. Since
$\operatorname{rank}(EW) \le \min(\operatorname{rank} E, \operatorname{rank}
W) \le k$, the rank of $M$ is at most $k$. Conversely, any $|C| \times h$
matrix $M$ of rank at most $k$ factors as $M = EW$ with $E$ of size
$|C| \times k$ and $W$ of size $k \times h$ (take $E$ as $k$ columns spanning
the column space of $M$, padded with zero columns if the rank is below $k$,
and $W$ as the coefficients expressing $M$'s columns in them). So the
embedding route computes exactly the one-hot first layers whose weight
matrix has rank at most $k$.

**(b)** It buys parameters and sharing. A full one-hot layer has
$10{,}000 \times 500 = 5$ million weights; the factored one has
$140{,}000 + 7{,}000 = 147{,}000$. And because every terminal's 500 weights
must be combinations of the same 14 rows of $W$, a terminal with few payments
borrows structure from terminals the data treats alike, where in a full
one-hot layer its 500 weights would be fitted from its own seven or so
payments of the week. The cost is expressiveness: if the terminals' effects on the 500
hidden units truly needed more than 14 independent directions, the embedding
could not represent them. With $k = \lceil\log_2 10{,}000\rceil = 14$ the
convention bets that they do not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The fourth reason to train a network was diversity: tuned XGBoost and the
tuned network tie on card precision on the simulator (0.303 each), and might
still catch different cards. Design the backtest that would decide whether
averaging the two beats both, using only the simulator's four prequential
folds and four forward test weeks. Say (a) what to measure first, before any
averaging; (b) how to combine the two scores, given that the network's
outputs and XGBoost's may not be calibrated alike; (c) how the combination's
weight is chosen without touching the test weeks; (d) what result would count
as a gain.

<Solution>
**(a)** Overlap. For each test day, take the two models' top-100 cards and
count the cards in both, and among each day's detected compromised cards,
how many each model found alone. If the two lists share nearly all their
detected cards, averaging cannot find more; if each finds several cards a
day the other misses, there is something to combine.

**(b)** Average ranks instead of probabilities: replace each model's score
by its rank among the day's payments (or the fraction of payments it beats)
and take $\lambda\,r_{\text{XGB}} + (1-\lambda)\,r_{\text{NN}}$. Ranks are
insensitive to calibration; an average of probabilities would let the model
with the more spread-out scores dominate. Card precision depends only on the
order, so nothing is lost.

**(c)** Treat $\lambda$ as a setting, with candidates such as 0, 0.25, 0.5,
0.75 and 1 (the two endpoints are the single models). Fit both models on
each fold's training block, combine their validation-week scores, and pick
the $\lambda$ with the best mean card precision over the four folds, exactly
as any other setting. Then refit on the test-week training blocks and score
the chosen $\lambda$ on the four test weeks.

**(d)** Compare the combination with the better single model week by week,
on the same weeks, so that the variation each week brings cancels in the
difference. A gain counts if the mean paired difference exceeds both <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-each-disagreement-means">the two-cards-a-day noise line</Ref> and about two standard errors of the
weekly differences; with four weeks and a paired standard deviation near
0.02, that means a difference of roughly 0.02, two cards a day. Smaller
improvements may be real but cannot be told from week-to-week noise with
four weeks.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague trains the 500-unit network with a balanced sampler: every
batch holds as many frauds as genuine payments, drawn from the simulator's
training week of 67,240 payments with 598 frauds. (a) What keep rate
$\beta$ does this correspond to? (b) Turn outputs of 0.5 and 0.95 into
probabilities on the stream. (c) With an alert costing €5, which of the two
payments does the cost rule flag if each is a €200 payment, before and after
the correction? (d) Does the correction change the network's card precision
at 100?

<Solution>
**(a)** A balanced batch has training odds of fraud 1:1. The keep rate is
the stream's odds divided by the training odds: $598 / 66{,}642 = 0.00897$,
so $\beta \approx 0.009$.

**(b)** With $p' = \beta p_s / (\beta p_s - p_s + 1)$:
for $p_s = 0.5$, $p' = 0.0045 / (0.0045 + 0.5) = 0.0089$;
for $p_s = 0.95$, $p' = 0.00855 / (0.00855 + 0.05) = 0.146$.

**(c)** The rule flags when $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}\cdot p$, here when
$p \ge 5/200 = 0.025$. Read raw, both outputs (0.5 and 0.95) clear the cut
and both payments are flagged. Corrected, the first is 0.0089 and is not
flagged; the second is 0.146 and is. The raw reading overstates every
probability by the factor that <Ref lesson="undersampling-calibration"
id="two-tasks-with-the-same-β">the undersampling lesson</Ref> located below
the bias peak, close to $1/\beta$ for small $p$.

**(d)** No. The correction is strictly increasing, so the order of the
day's payments, the top hundred cards and the card precision are unchanged.
What changes is every decision that reads the probability's value.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Drop the fifteen engineered inputs. For each payment, build an input vector
by concatenating the raw fields of the card's last five payments before it
(amount, time since the previous payment, terminal fraud rate as of that
payment) with the current payment's own, 18 numbers, and train the same
500-unit network. (a) How many first-layer weights does the network spend on
"the amount of the payment three steps back"? (b) A card's burst can start
at any position in its last five payments. What does the network have to
learn to recognise a burst wherever it starts? (c) A card with only two
earlier payments: what goes in the empty slots, and what does the network
learn from that? (d) What kind of model removes these three problems?

<Solution>
**(a)** 500 weights, one per hidden unit, for that one slot; and another
500 for the amount two steps back, and so on. The network treats "amount
three steps back" and "amount two steps back" as unrelated inputs.

**(b)** A burst, several payments minutes apart, shows up as short
time gaps in positions 1–2, or 2–3, or 3–4. With separate weights per
position, the network must learn the pattern separately at each position
it can occur in, from the frauds that happen to show it there; nothing
carries what was learned at position 2 over to position 4.

**(c)** Padding values, typically zeros, which the network cannot tell from
real zero-amount payments unless a separate indicator says the slot is
empty; and the number of filled slots becomes a feature the network can
learn from (new cards look different), whether or not that is wanted.

**(d)** A model that applies the same weights to every position and reads
the payments in order, so that a pattern learned at one position is
recognised at any other and a sequence of any length can be read: a
convolution over the sequence or a recurrent network. That is the problem
<Ref lesson="sequence-models-for-transactions">sequence models over an
entity's transactions</Ref> takes up, together with the question of whether
the raw sequence finds anything the hand-built aggregates do not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

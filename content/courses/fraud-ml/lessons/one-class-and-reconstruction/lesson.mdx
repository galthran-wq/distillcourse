If no labels exist, a model can still be fitted to the normal payments alone: a boundary that encloses them, or a network trained to reproduce them. A payment outside the boundary, or one the network reproduces badly, is suspect. How are the boundary and the reconstruction trained, how good are their scores on transactions with no labels at all, and does adding such a score to a supervised model help?

<Ref lesson="isolation-forest">The isolation forest</Ref> scored a payment
by how few random cuts separate it from the rest, and on the simulator
<Ref lesson="isolation-forest" id="which-frauds-reach-the-top">its
label-free version reached an average precision of 0.03</Ref>, against 0.63
for a logistic regression trained with labels. A boundary and a
reconstruction are two other ways of saying "unlike normal", and each comes
with an obvious first attempt that does not work.

A boundary is what a classifier learns, and a classifier learns it between
two classes. Here there is one: the payments believed normal, and a support
vector machine given only genuine payments has nothing to put on the other
side of its hyperplane. A network trained to reproduce its input has an
easy way out: copy it. A network that copies reproduces every payment
perfectly, the fraud included, and its error ranks nothing.

Both repairs exist, and both train without a single label. Their numbers
in this chapter come from <Ref lesson="isolation-forest" id="the-setup">the
stream the forest was measured on</Ref>: a simulator run whose training
days 42 to 48 hold 68,898 payments, used without their labels, and whose
test days 56 to 62, after known-card removal, hold 60,212 payments of which
440 are fraud, a base rate of 0.73%. Two input sets are compared there too:
the fifteen inputs, and the twelve label-free ones that leave out the
terminals' three delayed fraud rates, which are built from labels. A number
from any other file says so.

## A boundary with only one class: the origin stands in for the missing class

The support vector machine of classical-ml, recalled in one sentence: it
finds the hyperplane that separates two classes with the widest margin,
lets a few training points fall on the wrong side at a price (the slack
variables), and works in a feature space $\varphi(x)$ that it never builds,
because the optimisation needs only inner products
$k(x, x') = \varphi(x)\cdot\varphi(x')$, the kernel. To use it with one
class, something must play the second class.

### Every payment lands on one patch of a sphere

Take the Gaussian kernel on standardised inputs,
$$
k(x, x') = e^{-\gamma\,\lVert x - x' \rVert^2},
$$
with a width parameter $\gamma > 0$. Two of its properties fix where the
payments sit in feature space.

First, $k(x, x) = e^0 = 1$, so $\lVert \varphi(x) \rVert = 1$: every
payment is mapped onto the unit sphere. Second, $k(x, x') > 0$ for every
pair, so the angle between any two images is below 90°. Take $\gamma = 1/15$,
one over the number of the simulator's fifteen inputs, because on
standardised inputs the squared difference of two random payments averages
2 in each coordinate (the difference of two independent values of variance
1 has variance $1 + 1 = 2$), a squared distance of about $2p$ over $p$
inputs, and
$\gamma = 1/p$ then puts a typical pair at $k \approx e^{-2} = 0.14$: far
from 1, where every payment would look the same, and far from 0, where each
would be alone. It is also the library default. Two payments at
standardised distance 1 have $k = e^{-1/15} = 0.94$, an angle of 21°; two
payments at distance 5, five standard deviations apart on one input, have $k = e^{-25/15} = 0.19$, an angle of 79°. No pair reaches 90°.

Angles below 90° between every pair are enough to put a hyperplane between
all the payments and the origin. Take the mean image
$m = \frac1n\sum_j \varphi(x_j)$ of $n$ training payments. Its inner
product with any one of them is
$$
m\cdot\varphi(x_i) = \frac1n\sum_{j=1}^{n} k(x_i, x_j) > 0 ,
$$
a mean of positive numbers, so every image lies strictly on the positive
side of the hyperplane through the origin normal to $m$. Shift that
hyperplane a little towards the payments, by less than the smallest of
these inner products, and it separates every image from the origin. The
origin can therefore play the second class: one point that a hyperplane can
always split off from all the payments. A hyperplane that cuts the sphere
close to the payments, as far from the origin as possible while leaving
them on its far side, encloses them; a payment on the origin's side of it
lies outside.

<Figure
  src="./figures/origin.svg"
  alt="Two panels, each showing a quarter of the unit sphere around the origin with payment images along the arc: most are blue dots in the middle of the arc, four amber squares near its two ends. Left: an amber line, labelled w·φ(x) = ρ, cuts the arc so that the blue dots lie beyond it and the amber squares on the origin's side; an arrow from the origin to the line is labelled distance ρ over the norm of w, made as large as possible. Right: an amber circle with centre a and radius R encloses exactly the same blue dots and leaves the same amber squares outside."
  caption="In feature space every payment sits on the unit sphere; a plane far from the origin and a small sphere around the data cut off the same cap."
/>

<Definition title="one-class SVM">
Given $n$ training payments $x_1, \dots, x_n$, a kernel $k$ with feature
map $\varphi$, and $\nu \in (0, 1]$, the *one-class SVM* solves
$$
\min_{w,\,\rho,\,\xi}\ \ \tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2 \;-\; \rho \;+\; \frac{1}{\nu n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_i
\qquad\text{subject to}\quad w\cdot\varphi(x_i) \ge \rho - \xi_i,\ \ \xi_i \ge 0 .
$$
A payment $x$ is inside the fitted region when $w\cdot\varphi(x) - \rho \ge 0$
and outside otherwise.
</Definition>

Each term has one job. The hyperplane $w\cdot\varphi = \rho$ lies at
distance $\rho/\lVert w\rVert$ from the origin, and the first two terms
together push that distance up. The slack $\xi_i$ lets payment $i$ fall on
the origin's side, and the last term charges $1/(\nu n)$ per unit of slack:
a small $\nu$ makes slack expensive and keeps almost everything inside, a
large $\nu$ lets more payments out in exchange for a hyperplane further from
the origin, a tighter region.

### What ν controls

The same problem in its dual form, which is what a solver works on, has one
weight $\alpha_i$ per training payment, the price of payment $i$'s
constraint. Setting the derivative of the Lagrangian with respect to $w$ to
zero gives $w = \sum_i \alpha_i\,\varphi(x_i)$, the two-class expansion
without the labels $y_i$, because every constraint here has the same sign:
each payment must lie on the far side of the hyperplane. Then
$w\cdot\varphi(x) = \sum_i \alpha_i\,k(x_i, x)$, and the feature map
never has to be built. Setting the derivative with respect to $\rho$
to zero gives $\sum_i \alpha_i = 1$, because $\rho$ appears once in the
objective, with coefficient $-1$, and once in each constraint, weighted by
$\alpha_i$; and the price
$1/(\nu n)$ of a unit of slack becomes a cap on each weight, as $C$ did in
the two-class dual. The weights are therefore constrained by
$$
0 \le \alpha_i \le \frac{1}{\nu n}, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i = 1,
$$
and the fitted rule is
$$
f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_i\,k(x_i, x) \;-\; \rho ,
$$
inside when $f(x) \ge 0$. The payments with $\alpha_i > 0$ are the support
vectors. The optimality conditions of classical-ml's SVM dual carry over:
a payment strictly outside ($\xi_i > 0$) has its weight at the cap,
$\alpha_i = 1/(\nu n)$; a payment strictly inside has $\alpha_i = 0$.

<Theorem title="ν counts the payments left outside">
At the solution, the fraction of training payments outside the region is at
most $\nu$, and the fraction of training payments that are support vectors
is at least $\nu$.
<Proof>
**Outside.** Let $m$ payments lie strictly outside. Each has
$\alpha_i = 1/(\nu n)$, and all weights are non-negative and sum to 1, so
$m/(\nu n) \le 1$, that is $m/n \le \nu$.

**Support vectors.** The weights sum to 1 and none exceeds $1/(\nu n)$, so
at least $\nu n$ of them must be non-zero: at least a fraction $\nu$ of the
payments are support vectors.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The theorem turns $\nu$ into a number a practitioner can set: the share of
the training payments the region is allowed to leave out. Fitted on 10,000
random payments of training days 42 to 48, with $\gamma = 1/15$:

| $\nu$ | training payments outside | support vectors |
|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | 1.3% | 2.1% |
| 0.05 | 5.0% | 5.3% |
| 0.20 | 20.0% | 20.1% |

At $\nu = 0.01$ the count of outside payments slightly exceeds the bound.
Support vectors on the boundary itself have $f(x) = 0$ only up to the
solver's tolerance, and a few of them land a hair on the outside.

### A sum of bumps around the support vectors

The rule $f$ reads more simply than the hyperplane that produced it. With
the Gaussian kernel, each support vector contributes a bump
$\alpha_i e^{-\gamma\lVert x - x_i\rVert^2}$ centred on itself, and a
payment is inside where the bumps add up to at least $\rho$. In input space
the region is a level set of a smoothed sum over a few hundred training
payments: at $\nu = 0.05$, 527 of the 10,000.

The verdict "outside" is too coarse for a team that calls a hundred cards a
day. On the test week's 60,212 payments the $\nu = 0.05$ region left 4.7%
outside: 68% of the week's frauds and 4.3% of its genuine payments, about
400 payments a day. The usable output is the ranking by $-f(x)$, how far a
payment falls short of the level $\rho$.

### The smallest sphere describes the same region

A second construction asks directly for the smallest sphere in feature space
that holds the payments, again with slack for a few left out: centre $a$,
radius $R$, payment $x$ inside when
$\lVert\varphi(x) - a\rVert^2 \le R^2$. It is called support vector data
description, SVDD. Expanding the square with $\lVert\varphi(x)\rVert = 1$,
$$
\lVert\varphi(x) - a\rVert^2 \le R^2
\iff 1 - 2\,a\cdot\varphi(x) + \lVert a\rVert^2 \le R^2
\iff a\cdot\varphi(x) \ge \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \lVert a\rVert^2 - R^2\bigr),
$$
which is a hyperplane condition with $w$ along $a$. On a sphere, the inside
of a smaller sphere is a cap, and a cap is what a hyperplane cuts off. For
the Gaussian kernel, and any kernel with constant $k(x, x)$, the two
methods fit the same family of regions; for other kernels they differ.

<KeyFact>
The origin can stand in for the missing class only because the kernel puts
every payment at the same distance from it ($k(x, x)$ constant) and on the
same side of it ($k > 0$); the Gaussian does both. Then $\nu$ bounds how
many training payments are left outside: at most a share $\nu$ of them.
Which payments fall outside, and the shape of the region, depend on
$\gamma$ and on the training sample as well as on $\nu$.
</KeyFact>

## What a boundary cannot see: its width and its training sample

$\nu$ bounds the count; what else decides which payments end up outside?
Two settings nobody can check without anomalies: the kernel width and the
payments the region is fitted to.

### The width decides between a ring and islands

<Ref lesson="anomaly-detection-framing" id="three-planar-experiments">The
framing lesson's panel (b)</Ref> put eight tight clouds of normal points on
a circle and a few anomalies at its centre, and a one-class boundary drawn
around the whole ring called the centre normal. Whether the boundary is
drawn around the whole ring is a setting. A wide kernel, small $\gamma$,
makes each bump broad, the bumps merge, and the region is one disc. A
narrow kernel, large $\gamma$, keeps each bump local, and the region breaks
into islands.

<Figure
  src="./figures/ring-width.svg"
  alt="Two panels with the same eight tight clouds of black points on a circle and three amber squares at the centre. Left, wide kernel gamma 0.05: one dashed blue disc covers the whole ring, centre included; caption one region: the centre is inside. Right, narrow kernel gamma 2: eight small dashed blue islands, one around each cloud, and the centre outside all of them; caption eight islands: the centre is outside. Footnote: the same 320 normal points, nu 0.05."
  caption="The same 320 normal points and the same ν = 0.05: the kernel width alone decides whether the anomalies at the centre are inside."
/>

Both fits leave about 5% of the training points outside, 4.7% and 5.3%,
because $\nu$ is the same. Nothing in the normal points says which width
is right: both regions describe them equally well, and only an anomaly can
show that one of them is wrong. Without labels the width is a guess about
the anomalies you expect.

On payments the narrow extreme is easy to reach. With $\gamma = 1$ on the
fifteen standardised inputs, 64% of the 10,000 training payments became
support vectors and 65% of the test week's genuine payments fell outside:
each training payment had become an island of its own. The verdict is then
worthless. The ranking by $-f(x)$ survives better, an average precision of
0.27 against 0.32 at $\gamma = 1/15$, because how far a payment falls short
of $\rho$ still grows with its distance from the training payments.

### Fraud inside the training sample is enclosed

The region is fitted to whatever it is given. In <Ref
lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="where-normal-comes-from-three-label-regimes">the label regimes</Ref>,
fitting it to every payment of the training week is the unsupervised
regime, and fitting it to the week's genuine payments only, which needs the
training week's labels, is the semi-supervised one. The training week holds
about 0.8% fraud, a share small enough that $\nu = 0.05$ could leave all of
it outside. It does not.

Scored on the test week, by average precision against a base rate of 0.007:

| one-class SVM, $\nu = 0.05$ | fitted to all payments | fitted to genuine payments |
|---|---|---|
| fifteen inputs | 0.32 | 0.53 |
| twelve label-free inputs | 0.13 | 0.14 |

With the fifteen inputs, dropping the training week's frauds raises the
average precision by two thirds. Most of those frauds are payments at
compromised terminals, and they share one trait: a high value of <Ref
lesson="point-in-time-features"
id="the-delayed-terminal-risk-a-window-that-ends-δ-days-ago">the
terminal's delayed fraud rate</Ref>. Dozens of payments in the same corner
of the input space are a dense group, and a dense group earns support
vectors and bumps of its own, so the region grows to cover the corner. Fit
without them, the corner is outside. This is panel (c) of the framing
lesson met by a boundary: an anomaly that comes in a crowd is normal to any
model fitted to the crowd.

With the twelve label-free inputs the frauds form no such corner, and
cleaning the sample changes almost nothing. The gain on the fifteen inputs
also used labels twice: once to clean the sample, and once inside the
terminal rates, which are built from labels.

### What the boundary costs

Fitting the dual takes time quadratic in the number of training payments,
since every pair enters through $k(x_i, x_j)$. On one core, 10,000 payments
took 0.47 seconds and 20,000 took 1.9, four times as long for twice the
data. A full week of 68,898 payments would take about 20 seconds, and a
month of a real issuer's twelve million payments is out of reach without
sub-sampling, the same wall <Ref lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
id="what-the-score-costs-every-payment-against-every-other">the neighbour
scores</Ref> hit. Scoring a new payment costs one kernel evaluation per
support vector.

## A network that reproduces its input

The second way to say "unlike normal" is a model that has learned to
reproduce normal payments and fails on the others. Closing the copying
shortcut of the introduction is not enough on its own: a network that
cannot copy must also fail on the right payments, and what decides which
payments it reproduces well turns out not to be its width.

### What stops the copying

The classical repair forces the payment through a narrow layer. A
*replicator network*, also called an *autoencoder*, has three parts: an
*encoder* that maps the $p$ inputs to a short vector, the *code*; a
*decoder* that maps the code back to $p$ outputs $o$; and training that
makes $o$ close to $x$ on the training payments. If the code holds fewer
numbers than the payment, the network cannot copy and must keep what
describes the training payments best, so a payment unlike them comes back
distorted.

<Definition title="reconstruction error">
For a payment $x$ with $p$ standardised inputs and its reconstruction $o$,
the *reconstruction error* is
$$
\varepsilon(x) = \frac{1}{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\,(x_j - o_j)^2 ,
$$
and it is used as the anomaly score: the larger, the more suspect.
</Definition>

The error is a mean of squared differences across inputs, so the inputs
must share a scale. Unscaled, a €5 difference in the amount would outweigh
any difference in a fraud rate between 0 and 1, the problem <Ref
lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
id="the-columns-have-to-share-a-scale">the neighbour distances</Ref> had.
Every input is standardised with the training week's mean and standard
deviation, as <Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="standardise-on-the-training-block-compress-a-long-tail-first">for the
supervised network</Ref>.

### The network used on the simulator

The network used on the simulator's fifteen inputs has the layer widths
15, 100, 20, 100, 15: a hidden layer of 100 ReLU units, a code of 20 ReLU
units, another hidden layer of 100, and a linear output of 15.

<Example title="the error of one payment">
The figure's payment is a training payment, one made at night (its
standardised night flag is 2.18), after training. The largest difference is
on the card's one-day count, $-0.3003$ in and $-0.3158$ out:
$(0.0155)^2 = 24.0\times10^{-5}$. The next is the terminal's one-day
count, $-0.9810$ against $-0.9684$: $15.9\times10^{-5}$. The fifteen
squared differences sum to $70.9\times10^{-5}$, and their mean is
$$
\varepsilon(x) = \frac{70.9\times10^{-5}}{15} = 4.7\times10^{-5}.
$$
</Example>

The code has 20 units, more than the 15 inputs, so the bottleneck argument
above does not apply, and on the figure's payment
the network nearly copies. It still cannot copy everywhere, because a ReLU
unit outputs zero for every negative input to it. Each code unit is a
hinge, flat on one side of a direction in input space and rising on the
other, and on the flat side it passes on nothing. Copying a signed input
everywhere takes two hinges, $x = \max(0, x) - \max(0, -x)$, so copying
all fifteen inputs everywhere would take about 30 code units. Twenty units
copy only inside the region where enough of their hinges are on their
rising side, and training places and tilts the hinges so that this region
covers the training payments. Where there were none, nothing corrected the
pieces, and a payment that falls there comes back wrong. The training data,
not the code, decides where the network is accurate.

### Training it: the supervised pipeline with the label replaced by the input

Everything else is <Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors">the supervised
network's</Ref> pipeline with the target changed from the label to the
payment itself: mean squared error instead of cross-entropy, <Ref
lesson="neural-tabular-detectors" id="what-adam-changes">Adam</Ref> at
learning rate 0.0001 on batches of 64, and <Ref
lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="stopping-when-the-validation-loss-stops-improving">early stopping</Ref>
with patience 2. The loss it watches needs no labels: the reconstruction
error of a held-out tenth of the training payments. The weights kept are
the last epoch's, not the best's: with patience 2 the run stops three
epochs after its best, so the scores below come from weights three epochs
past it, no better on the held-out tenth and possibly worse. The two
regimes of the boundary apply unchanged: train on every payment of the week, or on its
genuine payments only.

On training days 42 to 48, the network trained on every payment ran 49
epochs in 45 seconds on one processor. Its mean error on the test week fell from
0.96 with random weights to 0.00007.

Training separates the two classes by a factor of 50 between medians,

factor of 10 was there before any training. An untrained network's outputs
are small next to its standardised inputs, so its error is close to the
mean square of those inputs: the squared distance of the payment from the training
week's mean. Frauds, with large amounts and high terminal fraud rates, are
far from that mean.

That makes the untrained network a fair baseline, and an uncomfortable
one. Ranking the test week by the mean square of the standardised inputs,
with no network at all, gives an average precision of 0.31. The trained
network, over three training seeds, gives 0.21 to 0.29.

<KeyFact>
On the simulator's fifteen inputs, the trained autoencoder ranks no better
than the payment's squared distance from the training mean, which needs no
model. Training taught the network to reproduce what the training week
held, and the training week held fraud.
</KeyFact>

The network trained on genuine payments only, the semi-supervised regime,
is the other half of that sentence: its average precision is 0.52. Like the
boundary, it is accurate wherever its training payments were, and once the
frauds are taken out of them, the network was never trained where they lie.

labels, and kept the last epoch's weights. The two autoencoder runs
therefore stop on different signals: the lab run on a held-out tenth of its
own training payments, this one on the week it is then scored on. Beside it, an isolation forest of only 10 trees, on samples of 256
(the library default), is built on the fifteen inputs of the same 11 to 17
July payments without labels and scores the same week.

Its mean error on the scored week fell from 0.93 with random weights to
0.00008 after training, and the frauds' mean error was 27 times the genuine
payments', $1.8\times10^{-3}$ against $6.9\times10^{-5}$.

| scored on 25–31 July 2018 | ROC-AUC | AP | CP@100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| autoencoder, all payments | 0.836 | 0.18 | 0.217 |
| isolation forest, 10 trees | 0.808 | 0.164 | 0.19 |
| network trained on labels | 0.859 | 0.646 | 0.28 |

The labelled network here has 1,000 ReLU units and dropout 0.2, and it was
stopped on the cross-entropy of 25 to 31 July with that week's labels, so
its row is slightly optimistic: the week it is scored on also chose its
length. The picture repeats: the reconstruction and the forest are close,
their AUC is near the supervised one, and their average precision is about
a quarter of it.

## The code as a picture

The reconstruction error is one number per payment: it says how unusual a
payment is, not in which way. The code is what the network kept of the
payment, and drawing it answers a question the error cannot: do frauds of
different kinds leave the mass of the week in different directions, so that
payments could be grouped by kind without a label? A code of 20 numbers
cannot be drawn. A code of 2 can, at a price: a 15-100-2-100-15 network
trained the same way stopped at a mean error of 0.36, five thousand times
the 20-unit network's, because two numbers cannot hold a payment.

<Figure
  src="./figures/code2d.svg"
  alt="Scatter plot of the two code coordinates, both from 0 to beyond 30. Blue dots, 4,000 sampled genuine test payments, are packed in a dense wedge near the origin, below coordinate 1 of about 5. Ten k-means clusters fitted to the training week's codes put five centres
inside the dense corner and five out on the rays. Three of the five far
clusters are mostly fraud, 35 of 36, 68 of 124 and 92 of 176 test payments,
and the other two hold 25 of 139 and 17 of 160: all five run at 11% fraud
or more, against a base rate of 0.73%.

What lies on each ray is read off the payments of its clusters. The 36
payments on the steep ray hold 35 leaked-card frauds, the scenario whose
amounts the simulator multiplies by five; their mean standardised amount is
10.7 and their card's one-day average amount 8.8, while every terminal input
stays within half a unit of zero. The four clusters on the shallow rays
hold 202 frauds, every one of them at a compromised terminal, and in each
of the four the largest of the terminal's three delayed fraud rates, over
one, seven or thirty days, sits on average between 3 and 14 standard
deviations above the training week's mean, while the amount and the card's
inputs stay within half a unit of zero. Their genuine
payments carry the same high rates: they are honest payments at the same
terminals, which is why two of the four clusters are mostly genuine. The
26 frauds of the amount-over-220 scenario reach no ray. So the code does
separate kinds of fraud, along the inputs that set them apart: the inflated
amount for the leaked cards, and for the compromised terminals the rates
built from labels.

## Does an unlike-normal score help a model trained on labels?

Where labels exist, the reconstruction error does not have to compete with
the supervised network; it can be handed to it. The supervised network
learns only the fraud its labels show, and the error might carry what they
miss.

### "Semi-supervised", a second meaning

In anomaly detection, <Ref lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="where-normal-comes-from-three-label-regimes">semi-supervised</Ref>
means training on normal payments only. Combining an unsupervised score
with a supervised model, using unlabelled payments and labelled ones
together, is also called semi-supervised, and that is the meaning in this
section. There are three ways to wire it.

<Figure
  src="./figures/fusion.svg"
  alt="Three wiring diagrams. 1, average the two scores: the 15 inputs feed a blue labelled net giving P(fraud) and an amber autoencoder giving an error; both feed a box mean of ranks; note two models joined at the output. 2, the error as a 16th input: the 15 inputs feed the autoencoder, whose error joins the 15 inputs into the labelled net, which outputs P(fraud); note two models joined at the input. 3, one code, two heads: the 15 inputs feed an encoder to a code, which feeds a decoder trained by squared error and a fraud head trained by cross-entropy; every row trains the decoder, labelled rows also the head; both losses shape the code."
  caption="Three ways to combine a reconstruction error with labels."
/>

1. **Average the scores**: rank the week by the supervised probability and
   by the error separately, and order payments by the mean of their two
   ranks.
2. **The error as an input**: compute each payment's error with the trained
   autoencoder and give it to the supervised network as a sixteenth input.
3. **One code, two heads**: one encoder feeds both a decoder, trained to
   reconstruct every payment, and a fraud output, trained on the labelled
   ones; the sum of the two losses shapes the shared code.

### What the sixteenth input added

On the 2018 file, the error added as a sixteenth input moved the labelled
network from 0.859, 0.646 and 0.28 to 0.861, 0.651 and 0.276. That
comparison changed two things at once, though: the network with the extra
input had 100 hidden units, the one without it 1,000.

On the forest's simulator run each arrangement was trained with five
seeds, 0 to 4, on training days 42 to 48 and scored on the test week. On
the simulator every training payment carries a label, so every row trains
the supervised part of each arrangement. The error used is that of one
autoencoder, the 15-100-20-100-15 network of seed 0 trained on every
payment.

- **Network trained on labels**: the 500-unit network of the comparison
  table, dropout 0.2, Adam at 0.001, batches of 64, a fixed 20 epochs.
- **Average of the two ranks**: each seed's supervised network, its ranks
  averaged with the ranks of that one autoencoder's error.
- **Error as a sixteenth input**: the same network and training with a
  sixteenth input, the logarithm of the error (the error spans several
  powers of ten) standardised on the training week.
- **One code, two heads**: the autoencoder's encoder (15 to 100 to a code
  of 20) and decoder (20 to 100 to 15), plus a fraud head, one sigmoid unit
  reading the code. The loss is the mean squared reconstruction error plus
  the cross-entropy, added with equal weight on every batch; Adam at 0.001,
  batches of 64, a fixed 20 epochs, no early stopping.

| arrangement | ROC-AUC | AP | CP@100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| network trained on labels | 0.894 ± 0.004 | 0.699 ± 0.014 | 0.343 ± 0.006 |
| average of the two ranks | 0.891 ± 0.001 | 0.548 ± 0.019 | 0.312 ± 0.005 |
| error as a sixteenth input | 0.901 ± 0.003 | 0.704 ± 0.007 | 0.343 ± 0.003 |
| one code, two heads | 0.895 ± 0.005 | 0.689 ± 0.015 | 0.339 ± 0.006 |

The ± is the standard deviation over the five seeds. Averaging the ranks
costs 0.15 of average precision and three cards a day: half the weight
goes to a score whose top is full of honest surprises. The sixteenth input
raised the ROC-AUC in every seed, by 0.004 to 0.009, and left average
precision and card precision where the seeds already scatter them. The
shared code changed nothing measurable.

The gain shows where it is least useful, in the bulk of the ranking. The
reason it is small is that the error adds no information about a payment:
it is a function of the same fifteen inputs the supervised network already
reads, computed by another network. It can help only by handing over a
summary the supervised network would not find by itself from its labels.
On the simulator, the labels already cover every scripted kind of fraud.

<KeyFact>
An unlike-normal score adds most where the supervised model is blind: fraud
of a kind its labels do not hold, or far more unlabelled payments than
labelled ones to fit the reconstruction on. A simulator whose three fraud
scripts all appear in the labelled week, and whose every training payment
carries a label, has neither, and there the score moved the ROC-AUC and
nothing an investigator sees.
</KeyFact>

<Conclusion>
You can now fit a boundary to one class: map the payments onto one patch of
the unit sphere with a Gaussian kernel, cut it with a hyperplane as far from
the origin as possible, and read $\nu$ as a bound on the share of training
payments left outside and on the share of support vectors. You can show
that the smallest enclosing sphere gives the same region for that kernel,
explain why the kernel width decides whether a ring of normal groups
encloses its centre, and why fraud inside the training sample gets enclosed.
You can build an autoencoder, score a payment by its mean squared
reconstruction error, and say why a 20-unit ReLU code on 15 inputs copies
only where the training payments were. And you can read the scores honestly: with no labels anywhere they
find a handful of compromised cards a day, about as many as the squared
distance from the mean; their larger numbers lean on labels in the inputs
or in the cleaning of the training sample; and added to a supervised
network they moved only its AUC.

reconstruction and a cluster rule on real card data, the ULB set, where no
input is built from labels and the frauds are not scripted, and asks what
they deliver at the investigators' budget.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A one-input network computes $o(x) = \max(0,\ x + 2) - 2$: one ReLU hinge,
trained on payments whose standardised value lies between $-1.5$ and $2$.
(a) Compute the reconstruction error of $x = 1$, $x = -1.5$ and $x = -5$.
(b) Which of the three is flagged, and what property of the training
payments decided where the hinge sits? (c) The same network is retrained on
data that includes payments down to $-6$; where must the hinge move for the
error to stay zero on all of them, and what happens to the error of $-5$?

<Solution>
**(a)** For $x \ge -2$ the hinge is active and $o(x) = x + 2 - 2 = x$: the
error is 0 at $x = 1$ and at $x = -1.5$. For $x = -5$ the ReLU outputs 0,
so $o = -2$ and the error is $(-5 - (-2))^2 = 9$ (with $p = 1$, the mean is
the single square).

**(b)** Only $x = -5$. The hinge sits at $-2$, below every training value,
so the network is the identity wherever training payments were and constant
below $-2$, where none were. Training put the bend just outside the data
because nothing in the data asked for the identity further down.

**(c)** The bend must move to $-6$ or lower, $o(x) = \max(0, x + 6) - 6$, and
the error of $-5$ drops to 0. This is the lesson's point in one dimension:
a value is reconstructed badly only if no training payment was near it, so
a fraud pattern present in the training data stops being flagged.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push $\nu$ to its edges in the dual constraints
$0 \le \alpha_i \le 1/(\nu n)$, $\sum_i \alpha_i = 1$. (a) For $\nu = 1$,
what are the weights, what is $f(x)$, and what kind of score is it? (b) As
$\nu \to 0$, which constraint stops binding, and what does the region do on
a training sample that contains frauds? (c) On a training week with 0.8%
fraud that you want left outside, which of the two edges is the wrong
choice, and why does setting $\nu$ exactly at 0.008 still not guarantee
that the frauds end up outside?

<Solution>
**(a)** With $\nu = 1$ the cap is $1/n$, and $n$ weights of at most $1/n$
summing to 1 must all equal $1/n$. Then
$f(x) = \frac1n\sum_i e^{-\gamma\lVert x - x_i\rVert^2} - \rho$: the mean of
Gaussian bumps on every training payment, which is a kernel density
estimate, minus a level. Every payment is a support vector, and the
theorem allows up to all of them outside: the level sits so that a large
share of the training payments fall below it.

**(b)** As $\nu \to 0$ the cap $1/(\nu n)$ grows past 1 and stops binding:
slack becomes infinitely expensive and the region must hold every training
payment, frauds included. It becomes the smallest region around all of
them, which on a polluted sample means one drawn around the fraud as well.

**(c)** $\nu \to 0$ is wrong: it encloses the 0.8% by construction. Setting
$\nu = 0.008$ allows that share outside but does not choose which payments
are left out. The solver leaves out the payments whose exclusion moves the
plane furthest from the origin, those in sparse places. Frauds that form a
dense group, like the compromised-terminal payments with high terminal
fraud rates, are cheaper to enclose than to leave out, and stay inside; the
budget is then spent on rare genuine payments.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
"An autoencoder trained on all payments, 0.8% of them fraud, learns to
reconstruct the fraud as well as the genuine payments, so its error cannot
separate them." Refute the claim with the lesson's numbers, name the
assumption that makes it fail, and say what kind of fraud would make it
true.

<Solution>
**The numbers.** Trained on all payments of training days 42 to 48, the
network's median error on the test week's frauds was $6.4\times10^{-4}$
against $1.3\times10^{-5}$ for genuine payments, a factor of 50, with an
average precision of 0.26 against a base rate of 0.007. On the 2018 file
the frauds' mean error was 27 times the genuine one.

**The assumption.** Training minimises the mean error over all payments, so
each region of the input space is fitted in proportion to how many training
payments it holds. A kind of fraud that is a few payments in a sparse
region contributes almost nothing to the loss and is reconstructed badly.
That is <Ref lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="the-rarity-assumption-and-where-it-breaks">the rarity assumption</Ref>
of the unsupervised regime: normal payments dominate whatever sample the
model is fitted to.

**When the claim is true.** For fraud that is dense in the training sample:
a bulk anomaly such as a card-testing night at one shop, or, on the
simulator, the compromised-terminal payments that share high terminal fraud
rates. The claim is partly true even here: dropping the training week's
frauds raised the average precision from 0.26 to 0.52, so the network
trained on all payments had learned to reconstruct part of the fraud.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A *linear* autoencoder has no ReLU: code $c = Ax$ with $A$ of size
$q \times p$, output $o = Bc$, trained to minimise
$\sum_i \lVert x_i - BAx_i \rVert^2$ on centred inputs. (a) Show that for
any fixed $A$ and $B$ the outputs $o$ lie in a subspace of dimension at
most $q$, and that the best $o$ for each $x$ in a given subspace $S$ is the
orthogonal projection of $x$ onto $S$. (b) Conclude, using what classical-ml
proved about principal components, that the optimal subspace is the span of
the first $q$ principal components and the reconstruction error is the
squared distance off that span. (c) Which score of <Ref
lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
id="where-the-rare-combinations-live-principal-coordinates">the principal
coordinates section</Ref> is this, and what does a nonlinear autoencoder add
to it?

<Solution>
**(a)** $o = BAx$ is $B$ applied to a vector of $q$ numbers, so it lies in
the column space of $B$, of dimension at most $q$. For a subspace $S$, the
point of $S$ closest to $x$ in Euclidean distance is its orthogonal
projection $Px$; any output in $S$ has error at least
$\lVert x - Px\rVert^2$, and a linear map achieves exactly $Px$ for every
$x$ (take $B$ an orthonormal basis of $S$ and $A = B^\top$).

**(b)** The training problem therefore reduces to choosing the
$q$-dimensional subspace that minimises the total squared distance of the
training points from it. Classical-ml showed that this subspace is spanned
by the $q$ leading principal components, the eigenvectors of the covariance
with the largest eigenvalues. The error of a payment is then
$\lVert x - Px\rVert^2 = \sum_{i > q} y_i^2$ in principal coordinates
$y_i$.

**(c)** It is the minor-component score $\sum_{i > q} y_i^2/\lambda_i$
without the division by the variances $\lambda_i$: distance off the leading
subspace. On the simulator, <Ref lesson="isolation-forest"
id="exercises">the forest lesson's PCA exercise</Ref> found that with $q =
2$ it already matched the forest. A nonlinear autoencoder replaces the flat
subspace by a curved surface that can follow normal payments more closely,
and its pieces are fitted only where training payments lie, which is also
why it reconstructs well whatever the training sample held, fraud included.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The 2018-file result quoted in the lesson reads: with the reconstruction
error as a sixteenth input, the labelled network went from 0.859, 0.646 and
0.28 to 0.861, 0.651 and 0.276; the network without the extra input had
1,000 hidden units, the one with it 100. (a) What can and cannot be
concluded from this comparison? (b) Design the experiment that would
measure the effect of the input alone, say how many training seeds and
weeks it needs, and what difference in average precision you would accept
as a gain, using the seed spread in the lesson's own table. (c) The
lesson's table shows a gain in ROC-AUC in every seed. Why does that not
justify adding the input in deployment?

<Solution>
**(a)** Two settings changed at once, the input set and the hidden width.
If one helped and the other hurt, the near-zero difference would hide both;
the comparison cannot attribute anything to the input. It also rests on a
single seed and a single week, while <Ref lesson="worldline-model-comparison"
id="how-much-four-weeks-move-by-themselves">weeks differ by more than
seeds</Ref>.

**(b)** Hold the network fixed (same width, dropout, rate, epochs), train
with and without the error input from the same seeds, and score both on the
same weeks: prequential folds for choosing, several forward test weeks for
judging, and compare the paired differences, week by week and seed by seed.
The lesson's five seeds put the standard deviation of a single network's
average precision at 0.007 to 0.014 on one week; a mean difference of 0.005
over five seeds is inside it. A gain worth acting on should exceed about
two standard errors of the paired differences across weeks, not across
seeds alone.

**(c)** The ROC-AUC weighs every false-positive rate equally, most of them
far beyond a hundred calls a day; the deployment rule reads CP@100, where
the five seeds showed no change. The AUC gain says the error reorders the
bulk of the week, which the team never sees.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the forest's simulator run, a 15-100-2-100-15 autoencoder trained on all
payments of days 42 to 48 maps the test week (days 56 to 62) to two-number
codes, and ten k-means clusters are fitted to the training week's codes.
The five clusters away from the dense corner hold, on the test week:

| cluster | test payments | frauds |
|---|---|---|
| A | 36 | 35 |
| B | 124 | 68 |
| C | 176 | 92 |
| D | 139 | 25 |
| E | 160 | 17 |

The other five clusters hold 59,577 payments and 203 frauds. (a) If the
team alerts every payment of cluster A, what are the precision, the number
of alerts a day, and the share of the week's 440 frauds caught? (b) Add
clusters B and C, then D and E: give the precision and alerts a day after
each step. (c) The clusters were ranked by their test-week fraud share.
Why is that optimistic, and what would a deployable version of the rule
use instead? (d) Compare the payments-a-day of (b)'s last step with the
budget, and say what a cluster rule cannot do that a score can.

<Solution>
**(a)** Precision $35/36 = 0.97$; $36/7 \approx 5$ alerts a day; $35/440 =
8\%$ of the frauds.

**(b)** A, B, C: $35 + 68 + 92 = 195$ frauds among $36 + 124 + 176 = 336$

**(c)** The fraud shares were read off the week being scored, so the

**(d)** At 91 payments a day the five clusters fit the hundred calls, but

<Exercise>
The autoencoder's error on the fifteen inputs, trained on genuine payments,
reaches an average precision of 0.52. A product manager wants to show
investigators "the probability of fraud" next to each payment and proposes
the error itself, rescaled to lie between 0 and 1. (a) Why is the error not
a probability, and why does rescaling not make it one? (b) Would <Ref
lesson="undersampling-calibration">the undersampling correction</Ref>
$p' = \beta p_s / (\beta p_s - p_s + 1)$ apply? (c) Describe a calibration
that would turn the error into a probability, the data it needs, and what
it does to the average precision and CP@100.

<Solution>
**(a)** The error is a squared distance between a payment and its
reconstruction; nothing in its training involved the fraud label, so no
value of it is tied to a fraud frequency. Rescaling to $[0, 1]$ is a
monotone map chosen without labels: a payment at 0.9 is not fraud nine
times in ten, and the value depends on the most extreme payment in the
week.

**(b)** No. That correction undoes a known change in the class mix of a
model trained with cross-entropy on labelled data, mapping one probability
to another. The autoencoder's output was never a probability on any class
mix, so there is nothing to correct.

**(c)** Fit a monotone map from the error to the fraud frequency on
labelled payments that the autoencoder was not trained on: Platt scaling (a
logistic regression on the error, or on its logarithm) or isotonic
regression, fitted on a labelled validation week in the hold-out geometry.
It needs labels, so the calibrated number exists only where labels arrive.
Being monotone, the map leaves the order of payments unchanged, and the
average precision and CP@100 stay exactly where they were; only the values
shown to the investigators change.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For a kernel whose $k(x, x)$ varies with $x$, for instance the polynomial
kernel $k(x, x') = (1 + x\cdot x')^2$, redo the expansion of the lesson's
SVDD section. (a) Show that the sphere condition becomes
$a\cdot\varphi(x) \ge \tfrac12\bigl(k(x, x) + \lVert a\rVert^2 - R^2\bigr)$.
(b) Why is this not a hyperplane condition, and how does it change which
payments fall outside compared with the one-class SVM? (c) What does this
say about choosing the kernel when the two methods are expected to agree?

<Solution>
**(a)** $\lVert\varphi(x) - a\rVert^2 = \varphi(x)\cdot\varphi(x) - 2a\cdot\varphi(x) + \lVert a\rVert^2 = k(x, x) - 2a\cdot\varphi(x) + \lVert a\rVert^2$,
and $\le R^2$ rearranges to the stated condition.

**(b)** The right-hand side now depends on $x$ through $k(x, x)$, so the
threshold moves with the payment: the condition compares $a\cdot\varphi(x)$
with a quantity that grows with $\lVert\varphi(x)\rVert^2$. With the
polynomial kernel, $k(x, x) = (1 + \lVert x\rVert^2)^2$ grows with the
payment's size, so a payment far from the origin of input space must
project further along $a$ to stay inside. SVDD then penalises large
payments that the one-class SVM's hyperplane, which ignores
$\lVert\varphi(x)\rVert$, would keep inside.

**(c)** The two methods agree exactly when every image has the same norm,
which holds for kernels of the form $k(x, x') = g(\lVert x - x'\rVert)$
such as the Gaussian. With such a kernel the choice between them is a
choice of software, not of model.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

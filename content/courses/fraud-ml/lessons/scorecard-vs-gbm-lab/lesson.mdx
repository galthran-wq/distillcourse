Two detectors built by the learner — a points scorecard assembled table by table and a boosted tree tuned by random search — scored by the same forward-in-time referee, with the choice between them argued in money.
The module compared model classes on other people's runs and found the gap
between them smaller than the gap between metrics, with a readable card
costing something a tree does not. Neither detector exists yet as your own
code, and no comparison in the module was priced in euros.

Every step has been stated already. Equal-frequency bins, the weight of
evidence $\ln\big((g_i/N_g)/(f_i/N_f)\big)$, the information value, the χ²
that chose between two groupings of product categories, and the scale that
turns log-odds into points, 28.85 points per unit of log-odds and an offset
of 212.87 for 100 points at fraud odds of 1 to 50, come from
<Ref lesson="scorecards-woe" />. The default, best-estimated and optimal
scores of a search, and random search over the boosted-tree grid of depths
3, 6 and 9, 25 to 100 trees and learning rates 0.1 and 0.3, come from
<Ref lesson="gbm-model-selection" />. The prequential folds, average
precision, card precision at $k$ and savings are the harness of
<Ref lesson="cost-aware-evaluation-lab" />, shipped complete; the cost
rule $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}\cdot p$ is <Ref lesson="example-dependent-cost" />'s.
The habit of reading one table three ways before crowning a winner is
<Ref lesson="worldline-model-comparison" />'s.

You write six pieces, one per checkpoint: `equal_frequency_bins`, `woe_iv`,
`chi2_groupings`, `scorecard_points`, the `fit` and `woe_matrix` methods of
a `Scorecard` class, and `random_search_backtest`. The first four are
checked against the scorecard lesson's worked tables — the 2,000-row age
table with its information value of 0.6502, the product groupings at χ²
≈ 584 and ≈ 663, the amount line's 7 and 65 points — and then against
inputs built to catch the usual bugs: equal-width cuts, a repeated
quantile kept as an empty bin, a flipped sign, shares taken over all rows,
the offset added whole to every line, a base-10 logarithm. The class
composes them into a card, which the notebook fits on a week of a
simulated card log of about 118,000 payments — the point-in-time lab's
generator, with sixteen point-in-time features and three categories —
after an information-value shortlist and a sign check that drops every
line pointing against its own counts. The search draws eight of the
eighteen boosted-tree settings, picks on validation folds, and is
backtested on test folds the selection never saw, so that its cost of
selecting is a number.

The two detectors then meet on those test folds: average precision, card
precision at 20 calls a day, and savings when every alert costs the
investigator $C_a$, from €1 to €20. The written answer recommends one of
them in money and names what the other buys.

The lab runs as a Jupyter notebook — open it in Colab with the button above,
or in any Jupyter. Pair the notebook with the code from this page; each
checkpoint submits your function's outputs for verification, and the
checklist above tracks what stands. The six exact checkpoints are the
required set. The written answer and the open task are optional: the open
task grades a ROC-AUC over the card-days of a hold-out week from a second
run of the generator, where the card falls short of the bar. The platform
grades rankings, not money, so the savings of your submitted model are
drawn locally beside it. Expect about four hours. When a check goes red,
the verdict often names the bug, so read it before rereading your code.

What exists at the end is a scorecard whose every number is yours, a tuned
tree with its backtest, and a recommendation argued in euros. The card's
points return as an explanation of each alert in
<Ref lesson="alert-explanations" />, and the tuned tree as the detector the
capstone retrains in <Ref lesson="pipeline-capstone-lab" />.

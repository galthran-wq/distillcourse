A regulator, an investigator and a customer all need to see why a payment scored high, and a model with hundreds of interacting splits cannot show them. A scorecard gives each value of each variable a number of points and adds them up. How are the points derived from data so that the card is both a valid classifier and a table a reviewer can check line by line?

The simplest model the course has used is already close to a sum. The
logistic regression of <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="five-models-scored-on-a-test-week">the five-model test week</Ref>
scores a payment by a weighted sum of its fifteen inputs, and on that week
it had the best card precision at 100 of the five. Its explanation of one payment is a
list of products, coefficient times input, one per variable. A reviewer
cannot do much with that list. A coefficient on the amount is a slope:
it claims that every extra euro moves the fraud odds by the same factor,
whether the payment is €20 or €200, and nothing in the data is counted at
"every extra euro". What a reviewer can check is a group of payments: the
200 payments above €201.82 in the training week, of which 132 were fraud.
A line of the explanation should point at such a group, its direction
should agree with what the group's counts say, and the lines should add
up to the score.

The regression on raw inputs meets only the last of the three, and the
other two cannot be added after the fit: they constrain what the model
is allowed to look like. The card has to be built so that all three
hold, and then scored on the same test week as the five models, to see
what they cost.

## A reviewer checks groups, not slopes: cutting variables into bins

Start with the amount, the input every fraud analyst looks at first. In
the training week of the simulator (25 to 31 July, 67,240 payments, 598
fraud), the fraud rate barely moves with the amount over most of its
range and then jumps.

### What one slope does with a jump

Cut the training week's amounts at €160, €183 and €202. Below €160 sit
66,169 payments with 444 frauds, 0.67%. The next two slices hold 652
payments at 2.1% and 219 at 3.7%. Above €201.82 sit 200 payments, and 132
of them are fraud, 66%. That last slice is the simulator's scripted rule
that every payment above €220 is fraud (<Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="three-scripted-frauds">the three scripted frauds</Ref>), plus the
inflated payments of leaked cards that reach the same range.

<Figure
  src="./figures/amount-rate.svg"
  alt="Fraud rate on a log scale from 0.1% to 100% against payment amount from 0 to 400 euros, training week of the simulator. Ten thin grey horizontal segments show the ten equal-frequency slices: between 0.45% and 0.9% up to 110 euros, then one segment at 2.9% spanning 110 euros to the right edge. Four amber segments show four bins: 0.67% up to 160 euros, 2.1% from 160 to 183, 3.7% from 183 to 202 and 66% above 202 euros, labelled 132 of 200 fraud"
  caption="The fraud rate is flat over most of the amount range and jumps above €202. The top tenth of payments by amount mixes the jump with thousands of ordinary payments."
/>

A logistic regression puts the amount into the log-odds as one term,
$\beta\,\mathrm{Amt}$. The fitted β has to serve the flat stretch and the
jump at once: a slope steep enough to reach 66% above €202 claims that a
€150 payment is far riskier than a €20 one, which the counts deny, and a
slope that respects the flat stretch never reaches the jump. Whatever β
comes out, the line "amount: β × €150" corresponds to no group of
payments anyone counted.

### A category has no slope at all

Some variables have no order to put a slope on. A merchant's category or
a payment's country takes dozens of values with no arithmetic between
them, and a regression needs one indicator column per value but one, 49
coefficients for 50 countries, each estimated from the few frauds that
value holds.

Both problems have one answer: replace the variable by a handful of
groups of its values, and let each group carry its own number.

<Definition title="coarse classification">
*Coarse classification* (binning) replaces a variable by a small number
of bins: ranges of values for a number, groups of values for a category.
Every value falls in exactly one bin. A bin with no value of its own, such
as "missing", is allowed.
</Definition>

Each bin is a group of payments with a count of frauds and a count of
genuine payments, which is what a reviewer can look up. The reviewer's
question "why did this €250 payment score high?" now has a line to point
at: "it is in the amount bin above €201.82, where 132 of the 200 training
payments were fraud".

## Where to cut: bins that separate the classes

Binning is only worth doing if the bins differ in fraud rate. Cutting
the amount at €50 and €100 would produce three groups of payments, all
near 0.7% fraud, and lose the jump. A cut is good when the groups it
makes differ, and that can be measured.

### Grouping categories: how far the counts sit from independence

Take an online shop's 10,000 orders in six product categories, a sample
in which 1,000 orders are fraud.

| category | clothes | books | music | electronics | games | movies | all |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| genuine | 6,000 | 1,600 | 350 | 950 | 90 | 10 | 9,000 |
| fraud | 300 | 400 | 140 | 100 | 50 | 10 | 1,000 |
| genuine : fraud | 20 : 1 | 4 : 1 | 2.5 : 1 | 9.5 : 1 | 1.8 : 1 | 1 : 1 | 9 : 1 |

Six groups are too many for 140 frauds in music and 10 in movies. Two
candidate groupings into three:

- **Option 1**: clothes | books and music | the other three.
- **Option 2**: clothes | electronics | the other four.

If a grouping told nothing about fraud, each group would hold frauds in
the population's proportion, one in ten. That gives the counts expected
under independence: a group of 6,300 orders expects
$6{,}300 \times 9{,}000/10{,}000 = 5{,}670$ genuine and 630 fraud. The
further the observed counts sit from those, the more the grouping
separates the classes. The χ² statistic sums the squared gaps, each
scaled by the expected count:
$$
\chi^2 = \sum_{\text{cells}} \frac{(\text{observed} - \text{expected})^2}{\text{expected}} .
$$

<Example title="two groupings of the product categories">
**Option 1.** Observed counts, genuine / fraud: clothes 6,000 / 300;
books and music 1,950 / 540; others 1,050 / 160. Expected:
5,670 / 630; 2,241 / 249; 1,089 / 121.
$$
\chi^2 = \tfrac{330^2}{5670} + \tfrac{330^2}{630} + \tfrac{291^2}{2241}
+ \tfrac{291^2}{249} + \tfrac{39^2}{1089} + \tfrac{39^2}{121}
= 19 + 173 + 38 + 340 + 1 + 13 \approx 584 .
$$

**Option 2.** Observed: clothes 6,000 / 300; electronics 950 / 100;
others 2,050 / 600. Expected: 5,670 / 630; 945 / 105; 2,385 / 265.
$$
\chi^2 = 19 + 173 + 0.03 + 0.24 + \tfrac{335^2}{2385} + \tfrac{335^2}{265}
= 19 + 173 + 0 + 0 + 47 + 423 \approx 663 .
$$

Each term is shown rounded to a whole number; the totals are the exact
sums, 583.9 and 662.9, rounded.

Option 2 sits further from independence and is the better grouping.
Both options have three groups, so the two statistics have the same
degrees of freedom and can be compared directly.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/product-groupings.svg"
  alt="Two panels side by side. Left, option 1: three groups clothes, books and music, others, each with a bar pair of observed fraud count in amber and fraud count expected under independence in grey outline: 300 against 630, 540 against 249, 160 against 121; tally chi-squared 584. Right, option 2: clothes, electronics, others: 300 against 630, 100 against 105, 600 against 265; tally chi-squared 663"
  caption="Observed fraud counts against the counts independence predicts, for the two groupings."
/>

Option 2 wins although its middle group, electronics, is almost exactly
at the population's odds. What it gains is a pure high-risk group: books,
music, games and movies together run at 3.4 genuine per fraud, against
9 for the population. Option 1 splits those risky categories between two
groups and dilutes both.

### Cutting a number: the edge that matters

For a number the candidate groupings are cut points. Two rules need no
labels. *Equal-width* bins split the range into equal intervals; on the
amount they would put almost every payment into the first bins and leave
the upper ones nearly empty. *Equal-frequency* bins put the same number
of payments in each. The figure above shows what ten of them do: the top
tenth of the training week runs from €110 to €789 and holds 6,723
payments, 194 of them fraud, 2.9%. The 200 payments above €201.82, at
66%, are inside it, mixed with 6,523 ordinary ones. Neither rule looks at
the label, so neither can find the edge where the rate changes.

The search that finds it tries every cut and keeps the one whose two
sides differ most in fraud rate, the separation χ² measured for the
product groupings. Repeated inside each side, that search is a decision
tree with the variable as its only input; its split criterion is a
different measure of the same separation. It needs two brakes.

- **A minimum bin size.** A bin's number will be estimated from its
  frauds, and a bin of 30 payments with 2 frauds gives a number that is
  mostly noise. The simulator card below requires at least 200 training
  payments per bin and at most five bins per variable.
- **One direction.** A bin riskier than both its neighbours needs a
  story a reviewer will accept; without one it is more likely noise than
  fact. The card merges neighbouring bins until the fraud rate rises (or
  falls) steadily across them.

On the amount, the tree's cuts are €160.34, €182.62 and €201.82, the
four bins of the figure, and they already run in one direction.

<KeyFact>
The minimum bin size sets how fine the card can see. The bin above
€201.82 holds exactly 200 training payments, so a cut at €220, where the
scripted rule sits, would leave bins of 77 and 123 and is not allowed:
the card prints one line for a €205 payment and a €600 one, though every
payment above €220 is fraud and 9 of the 77 between €202 and €220 were.
</KeyFact>

## One number per bin: the weight of evidence

A binned variable still has to enter the regression. The obvious way is
one indicator per bin, the dummy columns of the categorical case. The
amount's four bins need three coefficients, a card of seven variables
about twenty, each fitted from the frauds of one bin, and nothing makes
the coefficients of neighbouring bins line up with their fraud rates. The
card would be back to many numbers per variable, none of which a reviewer
can tie to a count. What is wanted is one number per bin, computed from
that bin's counts, so that the variable enters the regression as one
column with one coefficient.

### The definition, on a table of applications

The cleanest case to compute by hand is a new-card application form. A
fraudster applying for a card in a made-up or stolen identity is the
subscription fraud of <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="superimposed-and-subscription-fraud">the typology</Ref>, and the
applicant's stated age is one of the few fields the form offers. Take
2,000 applications, 194 of them later found fraudulent, binned by age:

| age | applications | genuine | share of genuine | fraud | share of fraud |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| missing | 50 | 42 | 2.33% | 8 | 4.12% |
| 18–22 | 200 | 152 | 8.42% | 48 | 24.74% |
| 23–26 | 300 | 246 | 13.62% | 54 | 27.84% |
| 27–29 | 450 | 405 | 22.43% | 45 | 23.20% |
| 30–34 | 500 | 475 | 26.30% | 25 | 12.89% |
| 35–43 | 350 | 339 | 18.77% | 11 | 5.67% |
| 44+ | 150 | 147 | 8.14% | 3 | 1.55% |
| all | 2,000 | 1,806 | 100% | 194 | 100% |

The two share columns are distributions over the bins: 24.74% says that a
quarter of all fraudulent applications came from 18-to-22-year-olds. It is
not a fraud rate. The fraud rate of that bin is 48 of 200, 24%, close to
24.74% by coincidence; in the 44+ bin the share of fraud is 1.55% and the
rate is 2%.

<Definition title="weight of evidence">
For a bin $i$ holding $g_i$ of the $N_g$ genuine cases (payments, or
here applications) and $f_i$ of the $N_f$ frauds, the *weight of evidence* is
$$
\mathrm{WOE}_i = \ln\frac{g_i/N_g}{f_i/N_f} ,
$$
the log of the bin's share of the genuine payments over its share of the
frauds. Positive means the bin holds relatively more genuine payments
than frauds; negative, relatively more frauds. (Some texts use the
inverse ratio and the opposite sign; this course keeps this one.)
</Definition>

<Example title="weight of evidence of two age bins">
**18–22.** $\mathrm{WOE} = \ln(0.0842/0.2474) = \ln 0.340 = -1.078$. The
bin holds three times its share of the frauds, so it is a risky bin.

**44+.** $\mathrm{WOE} = \ln(0.0814/0.0155) = \ln 5.25 = +1.661$.

The whole column, from missing to 44+: $-0.573$, $-1.078$, $-0.715$,
$-0.034$, $+0.713$, $+1.197$, $+1.661$.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/age-woe.svg"
  alt="Bar chart of weight of evidence by age bin. A separate bar for missing at minus 0.57, then 18–22 at minus 1.08, 23–26 at minus 0.72, 27–29 at minus 0.03, 30–34 at plus 0.71, 35–43 at plus 1.20 and 44+ at plus 1.66. Negative bars are amber and labelled riskier than average, positive bars blue and labelled safer than average"
  caption="Weights of evidence of the age bins. They rise steadily with age, and the missing bin, which has no place on the age axis, gets a number of its own: riskier than average."
/>

The missing bin shows the second thing bins buy. A blank age cannot enter
a slope, and filling it with the average age would put it among the
safest applicants. As a bin it gets its own weight, $-0.57$: applicants
who leave the age blank are riskier than average.

<Exercise>
Compute the weight of evidence of the 35–43 bin from its counts, 339
genuine and 11 fraud, and say in words what the number means.

<Solution>
Shares: $339/1{,}806 = 0.1877$ of the genuine applications and
$11/194 = 0.0567$ of the frauds. $\mathrm{WOE} = \ln(0.1877/0.0567) =
\ln 3.31 = 1.197$. The bin holds 3.3 times as large a share of the
genuine applications as of the fraudulent ones: an applicant in it is
safer than average.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### What the weight of evidence is: the bin's log-odds against the population's

Why a log of shares, and not the bin's fraud rate? Write the bin's fraud
odds and pull the population's odds out of them:
$$
\ln\frac{f_i}{g_i}
= \ln\frac{N_f}{N_g} + \ln\frac{f_i/N_f}{g_i/N_g}
= \ln\frac{N_f}{N_g} - \mathrm{WOE}_i .
$$
The weight of evidence is the amount by which the bin's log-odds of fraud
fall below the population's. In the 18–22 bin, $\ln(48/152) = -1.153$;
the population's is $\ln(194/1{,}806) = -2.231$; the gap is $1.078$, the
bin's WOE with the sign flipped. Odds multiply where log-odds add: the
bin's fraud odds are $e^{1.078} \approx 2.9$ times the population's.

So a logistic regression with one input, the WOE of one binned variable,
has a known answer. Its log-odds $\beta_0 + \beta_1\,\mathrm{WOE}_i$ can
reproduce every bin's observed log-odds exactly with $\beta_1 = -1$ and
$\beta_0 = \ln(N_f/N_g)$, and a maximum-likelihood fit that can match
every group exactly does so. Fitted on the simulator's amount bins, the
regression returns $\beta_1 = -1.00$ and $\beta_0 = -4.71$, against
$\ln(598/66{,}642) = -4.71$.

With several WOE columns in one regression, nothing pins the
coefficients at $-1$. Each is fitted alongside the others, and where two
variables carry the same evidence the fit can split it between them,
leave one near zero, or push one above zero. A coefficient can land
anywhere, which is why its sign has to be checked. What the coding fixes
is the sign it should have:

- **The expected sign is the same for every variable.** A bin with
  relatively more frauds has a negative WOE and must raise the fraud
  odds, so every $\beta_j$ should be negative, whatever shape the
  variable's fraud rate has in its raw values. A positive $\beta_j$ gives
  the variable's riskiest bins the fewest points, against the variable's
  own counts.
- **With a negative coefficient, the bins keep the order of their
  weights of evidence.** A bin with a lower WOE adds more to the fraud
  odds than one with a higher WOE, whatever the other variables do.

## Which variables earn a line: information value

A card with every available input on it is long, and most of the lines
would carry nothing. A card fraud model typically ends up with ten to
fifteen variables. Before any fit, a cheap measure of how much each
binned variable separates the classes on its own sorts the candidates.

### One number per variable

The weight of evidence measures each bin; the variable's separation adds
the bins up, each weighted by how different its two shares are.

<Definition title="information value">
For a binned variable with bins $i$,
$$
\mathrm{IV} = \sum_i \left(\frac{g_i}{N_g} - \frac{f_i}{N_f}\right)\mathrm{WOE}_i .
$$
</Definition>

Each term is at least zero: when the bin's genuine share exceeds its
fraud share, both the difference and the log of their ratio are positive;
when it falls short, both are negative. A term is zero only when the two
shares are equal. So the IV is zero exactly when the variable's bins hold
the genuine payments and the frauds in the same proportions, and it grows
as the two distributions pull apart.

<Example title="information value of age">
Term by term, from missing to 44+:

| age | share genuine − share fraud | WOE | term |
|---|---|---|---|
| missing | −0.0179 | −0.573 | 0.0103 |
| 18–22 | −0.1632 | −1.078 | 0.1760 |
| 23–26 | −0.1422 | −0.715 | 0.1016 |
| 27–29 | −0.0077 | −0.034 | 0.0003 |
| 30–34 | +0.1341 | +0.713 | 0.0957 |
| 35–43 | +0.1310 | +1.197 | 0.1568 |
| 44+ | +0.0659 | +1.661 | 0.1095 |

IV = 0.6502. The 27–29 bin contributes nothing: it holds 22% of each
class.
</Example>

A rule of thumb from credit scoring, a convention and not something
derived, reads the number as follows:

- below 0.02, the variable separates nothing worth a line;
- 0.02 to 0.1, weak;
- 0.1 to 0.3, medium;
- above 0.3, strong.

### The simulator's fifteen inputs

On the simulator's training week, binned as described above, the fifteen
inputs of the five-model week fall into three groups.

<Figure
  src="./figures/iv-inputs.svg"

Terminal fraud rate over 7 days 2.38, over 30 days 1.87, over 1 day 1.36, amount 1.28, card average amount over 1 day 0.82, over 7 days 0.63, over 30 days 0.26, just left of the 0.3 line; then card count over 7 days 0.057, terminal count over 30 days 0.036, card count over 30 days 0.032, terminal count over 7 days 0.016, card count over 1 day 0.014, night 0.003, weekend 0.002, terminal count over 1 day 0.001. The ordering is the simulator's script read back. The weekend and night
flags score 0.002 and 0.003 because the script sets every customer's
payment times around noon and uses neither in any fraud scenario. The
counts score below 0.06 because no scenario changes how often a card
pays: a leaked card's fraudulent payments are its owner's payments with
the amount multiplied by five. The three terminal fraud rates score
above 1.3 because a compromised terminal makes every payment there fraud
for 28 days. The values leave a gap: seven inputs at 0.26 or above,
eight at 0.057 or below. Where in that gap, or above it, the cut-off
goes depends on what the filter can and cannot see.

### What the filter cannot see

The information value looks at one variable at a time. The card's three
average-amount windows all sit above the gap, but they describe the same
card's spending at three horizons, and a card can carry most of what
they share in one line. Whether one of them adds anything once the
others are in is a question only the fit can answer.

The number also belongs to the binning: the same variable cut into
different bins gets a different IV, and a variable that IV rates as
useless may carry signal that only appears in combination with another.
The filter shortlists; it does not decide.

That blindness sets where the cut-off goes. The two ways of erring are
not alike: a variable the filter keeps can still be weakened or thrown
out by the fit, while a variable it drops never reaches the fit at all.
So the cut-off goes low: at 0.1, the rule of thumb's line between weak
and medium, rather than at its line for strong, 0.3, which would drop
the card's 30-day average at 0.26 before the fit could say what it does
in the company of the others. The 0.1 line keeps seven inputs:

- the amount;
- the card's average amount over 1, 7 and 30 days;
- the terminal's fraud rate over 1, 7 and 30 days.

The same sum, computed between this week's bin shares and the shares of
the week a card was built on, measures how far a variable's distribution
has moved since; it returns as a stability check in <Ref
lesson="model-risk-monitoring">model risk monitoring</Ref>.

## Fitting the card: one sign to check per line

Fitted together, the seven variables share the evidence the filter
judged one at a time, so each line has to be checked for whether it
still points the way its own counts say.

### The first fit: one line pointing the wrong way

A logistic regression on the WOE columns of the seven inputs that passed
the filter, fitted on the training week, gives six negative
coefficients. The seventh, on the card's 30-day
average amount, came out at $+0.22$. Its WOE column runs from $+0.20$ in
the lowest bin to $-2.38$ in the highest, where 42 of 479 training
payments were fraud, the riskiest group the variable has. With a
positive coefficient, the card gives that group the fewest points of the
line. A reviewer comparing the line with its own counts sees a
contradiction.

The fit is not wrong. Given the 1-day and 7-day averages and the amount,
a high 30-day average says that the card has always spent a lot, so a
large payment is its owner's habit. The variable entered the fit as a
reference point for the others, which is the comparison that <Ref
lesson="transaction-aggregates" id="exercises">the aggregates
lesson</Ref> found the strongest single feature for leaked cards: the
amount divided by the card's 30-day average. A card cannot print "this
line counts against the others", but it can print the comparison
itself. Binned the same way, the ratio has an information value of
1.86, above every one of the card's spending inputs. Replacing the
30-day average by it and fitting again gives $\beta_0 = -4.90$ and seven
coefficients from $-0.22$ to $-1.02$, all negative. That second fit is
the card the rest of the lesson prints.

### Why the check is one rule for every line

On a raw input, a fraud rate that rises with the value calls for a
positive slope, because larger values should raise the log-odds: the
opposite sign to the WOE convention, where the riskier bin is the one
with the lower weight of evidence.

Fitted on the card's own seven raw inputs, the ratio in place of the
30-day average, the regression gives negative slopes to the amount and
to the terminal's 30-day fraud rate. The amount's training fraud rate
rises steadily, from 0.67% to 66% across its four bins, so its slope
should be positive, and anyone who knows those counts sees that the
negative one runs against them. That knowledge is what a raw slope
needs: it has an expected sign only for a variable whose fraud rate
rises or falls steadily in its raw values. The terminal's 30-day fraud
rate has none, so its negative slope can be neither confirmed nor
refuted: cut by the tree before any merging, its bins climb from 0.5% to
56% fraud and then fall to 5% in the top one. A category's indicator
columns have none, and a missing value has no raw value to put a slope
on. The WOE columns need none of this, because the coding has already
ordered every variable's bins by their own counts.

## From log-odds to points

The fitted card gives every payment a log-odds of fraud,
$$
\ln\frac{p}{1-p} = \beta_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{J} \beta_j\,\mathrm{WOE}_j(x),
$$
where $\mathrm{WOE}_j(x)$ is the weight of evidence of the bin that
payment $x$ falls in on variable $j$. That is already a sum with one term
per variable. But a log-odds of $-0.14$ means nothing to an investigator,
and terms like $-0.806 \times (-5.216)$ are not something a reviewer
reads off a table. The last step rescales the sum into whole points.

### A linear scale, fixed by two anchors

Any increasing linear function of the log-odds keeps the ranking exactly,
so the scale can be chosen for readability alone:
$$
S = \mathrm{Offset} + \mathrm{Factor}\cdot\ln\frac{p}{1-p} .
$$
Two numbers fix it, and two conventions supply them. One anchor: a
payment with fraud odds of 1 to 50 scores 100 points. One rate: every
doubling of the fraud odds adds 20 points, the *points to double the
odds*. The direction is chosen so that a higher score means more
suspicious, the order in which the alert queue is read.

The rate fixes the factor. Doubling the odds adds $\ln 2$ to the
log-odds, and that must add 20 points:
$$
\mathrm{Factor}\cdot \ln 2 = 20
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathrm{Factor} = \frac{20}{\ln 2} = 28.85 .
$$
The anchor fixes the offset: $100 = \mathrm{Offset} + 28.85\,\ln(1/50)$,
so $\mathrm{Offset} = 100 + 28.85 \times 3.912 = 212.87$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/score-line.svg"
  alt="Score on the vertical axis from 0 to 220 against fraud odds on a log-2 horizontal axis from 1 to 400 up to 1 to 1. An amber straight line rises from 40 points at 1:400 to 213 points at 1:1. Two marked anchor points: 1:50 at 100 points and 1:25 at 120 points, with a step bracket labelled one doubling, plus 20 points. Small labels on the line at 1:100 (80) and 1:12.5 (140)"
  caption="Score = 212.87 + 28.85 ln(odds). Every doubling of the fraud odds adds 20 points, so the points of a card can be read back as odds."
/>

The scale reads both ways. A payment with 120 points has fraud odds of
1 to 25; one with 80 points, 1 to 100; and two payments 40 points apart
differ in odds by a factor of four.

### Points per line

The score is a sum over variables, apart from the intercept and the
offset, which belong to no variable. The convention spreads both evenly
over the $J$ lines:
$$
S = \sum_{j=1}^{J}\Big[\mathrm{Factor}\,\big(\beta_j\,\mathrm{WOE}_j(x) + \tfrac{\beta_0}{J}\big) + \tfrac{\mathrm{Offset}}{J}\Big],
$$
and the bracket is the number printed on the card for the bin payment
$x$ falls in. Bin $i$ of variable $j$ carries
$$
\mathrm{points}_{ij} = \mathrm{Factor}\,\Big(\beta_j\,\mathrm{WOE}_{ij} + \frac{\beta_0}{J}\Big) + \frac{\mathrm{Offset}}{J} .
$$
Scoring a payment is then a lookup per variable and one addition.

### The simulator card

The simulator card is the second fit: seven lines, the ratio in place of
the 30-day average, $\beta_0 = -4.90$. Its weights of evidence are
computed with 0.5 added to every bin's genuine and fraud count, a guard
against a bin with no frauds; on bins that hold frauds it moves the
number little, on the amount line by at most 0.06. With $J = 7$, every
bin's points are
$28.85\,(\beta_j\,\mathrm{WOE}_{ij} - 0.70) + 30.41$.

<Example title="points of the amount line">
The amount's coefficient is $\beta = -0.352$. Its top bin, above
€201.82, holds 68 of the 66,642 genuine training payments and 132 of the
598 frauds; with 0.5 added to each count its WOE is
$\ln\big((68.5/66{,}642)/(132.5/598)\big) = \ln(0.001028/0.2216) = -5.373$.
Its points:
$$
28.85\,\big((-0.352)(-5.373) - 0.700\big) + 30.41 = 28.85 \times 1.191 + 30.41 = 64.8 .
$$
The bottom bin, WOE $+0.283$, gets $28.85\,(-0.100 - 0.700) + 30.41 = 7.3$.
</Example>

Two lines carry a negative number of points. That follows from spreading
the intercept evenly: the safest bins of the two strongest lines fall a

## The card on the test week: what the readable form costs

### The card and the regression on one test week

Every model below is fitted on the training week and scored on the test
week of <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="five-models-scored-on-a-test-week">the five-model setup</Ref>: 8 to
14 August, 58,264 payments, 385 frauds after the known cards are
removed. All are logistic regressions at the library's default settings;
the cards differ only in their inputs.

| model | ROC-AUC | AP | CP@100 |
|---|---|---|---|
| regression on all fifteen raw inputs (the five-model baseline) | 0.871 | 0.606 | 0.291 |
| regression on the card's seven raw inputs | 0.861 | 0.636 | 0.291 |
| first card: seven inputs passing the filter | 0.827 | 0.441 | 0.284 |
| **the card**: 30-day average replaced by the ratio | 0.868 | 0.522 | 0.290 |
| the card, terminal 30-day line left as the tree cut it | 0.868 | 0.604 | 0.279 |

Against the regression on the same seven inputs, the card ties at the
team's own number: card precision at 100 of 0.290 against 0.291, a tenth
of a card a day, far inside the two-cards-a-day noise of <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="what-each-disagreement-means">the
alert-budget metrics</Ref>. It ties on ROC-AUC. It loses on average
precision, 0.52 against 0.64, several times the spread of about 0.02 to
0.03 that average precision showed across weeks in <Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="prequential-folds-a-spread-across-weeks">the delayed-labels
lesson</Ref>.

### Where the average precision goes

Average precision is a mean over the frauds of the precision at each
fraud (<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-the-team-sees-at-each-fraud">the alert-budget metrics</Ref>), so
it splits into the shares of groups of frauds. Split the 385 test frauds
by the simulator's scenario and the loss sits in one place: the frauds at
compromised terminals, whose share of the average precision is 0.34 for
the card and 0.45 for the regression. The
card's terminal lines are where the one-direction rule did its merging.

<Figure
  src="./figures/terminal30.svg"
  alt="Two rows of bars for the terminal's 30-day fraud rate, training week. Top row, the five bins the tree found before merging: up to 0.07 at 0.5% fraud, 0.07 to 0.11 at 4%, 0.11 to 0.40 at 43%, 0.40 to 0.67 at 56%, and above 0.67 at 5%, the last bar amber and labelled 12 of 229. Bottom row, the card's three monotone bins: 0.5%, 4%, and 35% for everything above 0.11, labelled 242 of 697"
  caption="The terminal's 30-day fraud rate, before and after the one-direction rule. The top bin, terminals whose whole month was fraud, is almost clean, and merging it hides that."
/>

This is the variable whose tree bins rise and then fall: terminals whose
fraud rate over the window exceeded 0.67 had 12 frauds in 229 training
payments, 5%, against 56% in the bin below. The one-direction rule merged
that top bin into its neighbours. With the line left as the tree cut it, the
top bin on its own, the card's average precision returns to 0.604, at a card precision of 0.279, within
noise of the others. The regression's negative coefficient on the same
variable is the same fact, found without anyone being told.

Whether to allow the bin is the reviewer's decision, and the card makes
it one. A line that says "terminals with the highest 30-day fraud rate
get fewer points than those with a moderate one" needs a reason. On this
simulator there is one, in the script; whether a real fraudster's terminal behaves the same way is the
question the reviewer has to ask before signing.

### What else a reviewer checks

The sign of each line is one check. Two more come from outside the data.

- **When the input exists.** Every line of a card that scores payments
  after authorisation must be computable from the payment's own fields,
  earlier payments and labels already arrived. All seven lines here meet
  that condition; the terminal rates meet it because they read only
  labels at least seven days old, the delayed ones of <Ref
  lesson="point-in-time-features">point-in-time features</Ref>. A card printed with names makes
  an input that reads the future easier to spot than a column in a
  matrix, though no easier to prove; the proof is still the pipeline's.
- **Whether the input may be used at all.** A card names every variable
  it uses, so a reviewer sees at once if one of them is an applicant's
  age, nationality or a stand-in for something the law protects. Which
  data a bank may process for fraud detection, and under what conditions,
  is the subject of <Ref lesson="gdpr-financial-crime-data">the data
  protection rules</Ref>; the card's contribution is that the question
  cannot be missed.

<Conclusion>
You can now build a points scorecard from labelled data: cut each
variable into bins that separate the classes, with a minimum size and,
where the reviewer wants it, one direction; encode each bin by its
weight of evidence, the gap between its log-odds of fraud and the
population's; shortlist variables by information value; fit one
coefficient per variable and check that every coefficient is negative;
and rescale the fitted log-odds so that 20 points double the odds,
spreading the intercept over the lines. You can also say what the card
costs: on the simulator nothing in card precision at 100, about a fifth
of the average precision where the one-direction rule overrides the
data, and every interaction between variables that its additive form
cannot hold except through a feature built by hand, like the ratio.

The card is the readable end of the supervised detectors. At the other
end, a boosted tree combines variables freely, and whether that buys
enough card precision to give up the card's readability is measured in
<Ref lesson="gbm-model-selection">tree ensembles and prequential model
selection</Ref>. The card's points also serve as its own explanation of
each alert; what a model with no such table has to do instead is the
subject of <Ref lesson="alert-explanations">alert explanations</Ref>.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
From the age table's counts, compute the weight of evidence and the
information-value term of the 30–34 bin, and check them against the
worked table.

<Solution>
Shares: $475/1{,}806 = 0.2630$ of the genuine applications,
$25/194 = 0.1289$ of the frauds. $\mathrm{WOE} = \ln(0.2630/0.1289) =
\ln 2.041 = 0.713$. Term: $(0.2630 - 0.1289) \times 0.713 = 0.1341
\times 0.713 = 0.0957$, as in the table. The bin holds about twice its
share of the genuine applications, so it is a safe bin, and its weight
in the information value comes from the large difference between the
two shares, 13 points, multiplied by a moderate log ratio.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Suppose the three fraudulent applications in the 44+ bin had turned out
genuine: 150 genuine, 0 fraud, with the totals becoming 1,809 genuine
and 191 fraud. (a) What happens to the bin's weight of evidence and to the
variable's information value? (b) A common repair adds 0.5 to every
bin's genuine and fraud count before computing shares. Compute the bin's
weight of evidence with 0.5, and again with 0.1. (c) What do the two
answers say about the number the card would print, and what should be
done with the bin instead?

<Solution>
**(a)** The fraud share is $0/191 = 0$, so
$\mathrm{WOE} = \ln(\text{positive}/0) = +\infty$, and the bin's term
$(g/N_g - 0)\times\infty$ makes the information value infinite. A
regression on that column cannot be fitted: the coefficient would chase
an infinite input.

**(b)** With 0.5: genuine share $150.5/1{,}809 = 0.0832$, fraud share
$0.5/191 = 0.00262$, $\mathrm{WOE} = \ln 31.8 = 3.46$. With 0.1:
$150.1/1{,}809 = 0.0830$ and $0.1/191 = 0.000524$,
$\mathrm{WOE} = \ln 158 = 5.07$.

**(c)** The number moves from 3.5 to 5.1 with a constant nobody can
justify, against 1.66 for the same bin with its three frauds. The
constant decides only because the bin has no frauds: on bins that hold
some, like the simulator card's, it moves the weight of evidence little. The data
say only that frauds are rare in the bin, none in 150; how rare, the
smoothing constant decides. The fix is a minimum count of
frauds per bin, met by merging: the 35–43 and 44+ bins together hold
$339 + 150 = 489$ genuine and 11 fraud, shares $0.2703$ and $0.0576$,
$\mathrm{WOE} = \ln 4.69 = 1.55$, a number the counts support. The
simulator card's rule of at least 200 payments per bin plays the same
role.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague proposes: "Rank the candidate variables by information value
and keep the top ones; a higher IV is always a better line for the card."
Give two variables with a high information value that should not be on a
card, and name the step of the argument that fails for each. Use, for
one of them, the terminal fraud rate of <Ref
lesson="point-in-time-features" id="three-pipelines-on-one-simulator-run">the
three pipelines</Ref>.

<Solution>
**A rate computed from final labels.** The terminal fraud rate computed
on the finished training table, with every label as it is known today,
separates the training payments better than the delayed one: on that
lesson's simulator run the leaky pipeline's offline average precision
was 0.92 against 0.74 for the honest one, so its bins would show a
higher information value too. But the leaky rate reads labels that did
not exist when the payment was scored, and in deployment it reads none
of them. The step that fails: IV measures how well the variable
separates the classes in the table it is computed on, and if the table
leaks, so does the IV. Admissibility is a property of how the column was
built, which no statistic of the column can show.

**A protected attribute or a stand-in for one.** An applicant's age has
IV 0.65 on the application table. Whether a bank may score applications
by age, or by a variable that tracks a protected characteristic, is a
legal question; a strong separation makes such a variable more tempting,
not more admissible. The step that fails is the same: IV is a measure of
separation, and the card's content is also bound by what may be used.

**The transferable point.** The filter shortlists variables that are
already admissible; it cannot rule on admissibility.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Scorecards inherited from credit lending point the other way: they score
the odds of a good outcome, genuine to fraud, so that a higher score is
safer. (a) Derive the factor and offset for a card with 100 points at
genuine-to-fraud odds of 50 to 1 and 120 at 100 to 1. (b) Show that for
every payment this score $S_{\text{good}}$ and the lesson's fraud score
$S$ satisfy $S_{\text{good}} = 200 - S$. (c) Show that any two anchors,
a score $S_0$ at odds $o_0$ and a rate of $d$ points per doubling,
determine exactly one scale, and write its factor and offset.

<Solution>
**(a)** $S_{\text{good}} = \mathrm{Offset}' + \mathrm{Factor}'\ln(\text{good odds})$.
The two anchors: $100 = \mathrm{Offset}' + \mathrm{Factor}'\ln 50$ and
$120 = \mathrm{Offset}' + \mathrm{Factor}'\ln 100$. Subtracting,
$20 = \mathrm{Factor}'\ln 2$, so $\mathrm{Factor}' = 28.85$, and
$\mathrm{Offset}' = 100 - 28.85 \ln 50 = 100 - 112.87 = -12.87$.

**(b)** The good odds are the inverse of the fraud odds, so
$\ln(\text{good odds}) = -\ln(\text{fraud odds})$. Then
$S_{\text{good}} = -12.87 - 28.85 \ln(\text{fraud odds})$ and
$S = 212.87 + 28.85\ln(\text{fraud odds})$; adding them gives
$S_{\text{good}} + S = 200$. The two cards order the payments in
opposite directions and carry the same information; a payment at 208 on
the lesson's card scores −8 on the credit-style one.

**(c)** A rate of $d$ points per doubling means
$\mathrm{Factor}\cdot\ln 2 = d$, so $\mathrm{Factor} = d/\ln 2$, and the
anchor gives $\mathrm{Offset} = S_0 - \mathrm{Factor}\ln o_0$. Both are
determined, so the scale is unique: a line is fixed by one point and
its slope. For 600 points at fraud odds 1 to 30 and 50 points per
doubling: $\mathrm{Factor} = 72.13$,
$\mathrm{Offset} = 600 + 72.13 \ln 30 = 845.3$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The card's probabilities come from a logistic regression fitted on the
full training week, without undersampling, so take them as calibrated.
With an alert cost $C_a = €5$ and the amount as the cost of a miss, the
Bayes minimum-risk rule of <Ref lesson="example-dependent-cost"
id="the-decision-that-minimises-expected-cost">example-dependent
cost</Ref> flags a payment when $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}\cdot p$. (a) Turn
the rule into a minimum score $S^*(\mathrm{Amt})$ on the card. (b)
Compute it for €20, €100 and €1,000. (c) Does the rule flag the €8.65
payment that scored 208? (d) What does (c) say about using one cut-off
score for every payment?

<Solution>
**(a)** $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt}\cdot p$ is $p \ge C_a/\mathrm{Amt}$, and in
odds $p/(1-p) \ge C_a/(\mathrm{Amt} - C_a)$ for $\mathrm{Amt} > C_a$.
The score is increasing in the odds, so
$$
S^*(\mathrm{Amt}) = 212.87 + 28.85\,\ln\frac{C_a}{\mathrm{Amt} - C_a} .
$$

**(b)** €20: $\ln(5/15) = -1.099$, $S^* = 212.87 - 31.70 = 181$.
€100: $\ln(5/95) = -2.944$, $S^* = 128$.
€1,000: $\ln(5/995) = -5.293$, $S^* = 60$.

**(c)** For €8.65, $\ln(5/3.65) = 0.315$ and $S^* = 222$. The payment
scored 208, below: not flagged. In probabilities, $p \approx 0.46$ and
the expected loss $0.46 \times €8.65 = €3.98$ is below the €5 call,
though the payment was fraud.

**(d)** The cost rule's cut-off falls by about 20 points for every
doubling of the amount, so a single cut-off score is right for one amount
only, the card version of the earlier lesson's finding that a fixed
threshold suits one amount. The card does not remove the amount from
the decision; it makes the probability readable, and the rule still has
to multiply it by the euros at stake.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The regression on raw inputs gives the terminal's 30-day fraud rate a
negative coefficient, and the unmerged tree bins show the training-week
fraud rate falling from 56% to 5% at the top of that variable. Explain
the pattern from the simulator's rules (a compromised terminal turns
every payment there into fraud for 28 days; the terminal rates are
computed from labels at least seven days old), say what you would
measure on the test week to check your explanation, and decide whether
the bin belongs on a card meant for real payments.

<Solution>
**Explanation.** The 30-day rate is computed over the 30 days that end
seven days before the payment, so it reads days $t-37$ to $t-7$. A
terminal whose rate over that window is above 0.67 had fraud on at least
two thirds of its payments there, so its compromise covered about 20 or
more of those 30 days and began at least 20 days before the window's
end, 27 or more days before the payment. A compromise lasts 28 days. By
the day of the payment, most such terminals are at or past the end of
theirs, and their payments are genuine again. A moderate rate means the
compromise began late in the window, with days left to run. The fraud rate therefore rises with the 30-day rate and
then falls at the top, and the regression captures the fall with a
negative coefficient (offset by the positive one on the 7-day rate).

**Check.** The explanation makes a prediction on weeks the card was not
built on: wherever the 30-day rate is high, the fraud rate of the
payments should fall again, and it should fall at the top of the range.
On the test week, binning the 30-day rate above 0.11 into
(0.11, 0.3], (0.3, 0.5] and above 0.5 gives fraud rates of 54%, 43% and
19%: the fall is there a week later, on payments the bins were not cut
on. A sharper check uses the answer key: for each payment in the top bin,
count the days since its terminal's compromise began, which the
simulator's fraud-scenario column makes available to the analyst though
never to the model; the explanation predicts that most exceed 28.

**Decision.** The fall is a fact about the script's fixed 28-day
compromise. On real payments a compromised terminal is cleaned when it
is found, not after a fixed term, and a terminal whose month was full of
fraud is more likely still dangerous than cured. A reviewer should keep
the merged, one-direction bin on a card meant for real data, and accept
that the simulator's test week scores it lower for it. The transferable
move: before allowing a bin that breaks the direction, find what in the
data-generating process makes it break, and ask whether that process is
the one the card will meet.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

**(b)** Among amounts up to €201.82: $0.48 - (-5.10) = 5.58$. Among

A single card payment record says: card 4471, terminal 9023, EUR 184, Tuesday 14:07. Nothing in those four fields says whether it is fraud; the same payment is normal for one cardholder and alarming for another. How do you turn a card's and a terminal's past into numbers that stand next to the payment, computed only from what happened before it?

Every model in the previous module was scored against an honest referee:
a forward-in-time test week behind a delay block, judged by card precision
at 100. What the models were given went unexplained. <Ref
lesson="ulb-handbook-benchmark" id="fifteen-inputs-built-the-same-way">The
benchmark</Ref> listed fifteen inputs, among them a card's number of
payments over the last 1, 7 and 30 days, and <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="where-a-learned-model-fits-after-the-answer">the layered system</Ref>
called the step that builds such numbers feature augmentation, without
saying how they are computed.

The constraint comes with the payment. The model scores card 4471's
EUR 184 within seconds of the payment, and at that instant nothing after
it exists. Whatever stands next to the payment in the feature vector must
be computable from the payments before it, in training exactly as in
service.

## What the raw record can tell a model

The obvious first attempt hands the four fields to a learner. A decision
tree needs no scaling and splits integers as readily as amounts, so
nothing seems to stop it.

### An identifier names an entity; it does not measure one

A learner needs features that are numbers and whose order means
something: a split at a threshold sends the smaller values one way and the
larger the other. The amount qualifies. The card number and the terminal
number are numbers that do not: terminal 9023 is not more of anything than
terminal 1983. They are names of things that have a history, and the
history is where the information is.

A tree can still split on them. A deep enough tree carves the terminal
axis into intervals one terminal wide and learns which particular
terminals were fraudulent in its training week. That is a lookup table of
past labels, valid for those terminals and only as long as their status
lasts.

### An amount needs a reference point

EUR 184 is a weekly shop for one cardholder and ten times the usual
payment of another. A tree on the amount alone can only learn amounts
that are suspicious for everyone. On <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="the-transaction-simulator-a-world-whose-rules-are-known">the
transaction simulator</Ref>, whose three scripted frauds are a fixed
amount rule, compromised terminals and leaked cards, that is exactly one
scenario. The depth-2 tree that <Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="three-scripted-frauds">the canonical datasets</Ref> trained on one
week split at amount 218.67, the first scenario's line of 220. Give the
same tree the card number, the terminal number, the time of day and the
weekday as well, and it asks the same questions about the amount and
ignores the rest.

A categorical field such as the country of the payment does carry
information on its own, a rate for the whole population. In one Belgian
issuer's month of online card payments, payments made outside the European
Union were fraud about 5% of the time against 0.05% for payments in Belgium, a
hundredfold difference. But a population rate is the same for every
cardholder, so it cannot say that a payment abroad is unusual for this
card, which is what the opening asks.

<KeyFact>
Fraud is a departure from a particular card's or terminal's habit, and a
habit is a statistic over that entity's past. The raw record carries the
present payment only; the reference it has to be compared with must be
computed and placed beside it.
</KeyFact>

## Summarising a card's past: recency, frequency, monetary

A card that has paid for two years has hundreds of past payments, and a
model takes a fixed number of inputs. The history has to be cut down to a
few numbers.

### Why not the lifetime average

The first candidate is the card's mean amount over everything it ever
did. It fails on the very change it should catch. A card stolen yesterday
has two years of honest payments behind it and a day of fraud: the
lifetime mean has barely moved, and it will not move much until the fraud
has run for weeks. A summary of a habit has to forget, so that a recent
change shows.

### Three questions about a habit

A card's habit can be broken in three ways: it pays sooner after its
previous payment than it used to, it pays more often, or it pays
different amounts. Each gives one kind of summary.

<Definition title="entity and RFM aggregates">
An *entity* is anything that recurs across payments and has a history: a
card, a customer, a terminal, an account. For a payment of an entity, its
*RFM aggregates* summarise that entity's earlier payments:

- **recency**: the time since the entity's previous payment;
- **frequency**: the number of its payments in a recent period;
- **monetary value**: the amount of its payments in that period, summed
  or averaged, or the smallest, largest or most recent amount.
</Definition>

The name comes from marketing, where the same three numbers rank
customers by how recently, how often and how much they buy. Recency is one
number per payment; frequency and monetary value also need a period.

### Probe, then spend

<Ref lesson="economics-of-detection" id="one-card-five-chances-to-act">The
stolen card of the economics lesson</Ref> was tested with a €1 payment and
then used for five payments of €200 over two days. Say its owner paid
about once a day, around €40. Read the first €200 payment through the
three questions.

  src="./figures/probe.svg"

All three questions answer "unusual": a second payment within two hours
from a card that pays once a day, a most recent amount of €1, and a
current amount five times the card's 30-day mean. No field of the €200
record is odd on its own, since €200 is an ordinary purchase. The pattern
is the pair, a recent low payment and then a recent high one, and only
aggregates over the card's own past can express it.

## How far back to look: windows of several lengths

Frequency and monetary value need a period, and the obvious choice is one
period for everything, say the last 30 days. Two cards show why one
length is not enough.

### A burst and a drift

<Figure
  src="./figures/windows.svg"
  alt="Two timelines over the last 30 days, each with three nested brackets behind the current payment labelled 1 day, 7 days and 30 days. Top, a card that pays about twice a day and makes six payments in the last hour: its 1-day count is 8 against a usual 2, its 7-day count 20 against 14, its 30-day count 66 against 60. Bottom, a card that went from two payments a day to four three weeks ago: its 1-day count is 4, within its old day-to-day range; its 7-day count 28 against 14; its 30-day count 102 against 60"
  caption="A short window sees a burst that a long one dilutes; a long window sees a drift that a short one cannot tell from noise. Illustrative cards."
/>

The first card pays about twice a day and then makes six payments within
an hour, the thief's burst of <Ref lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="card-fraud-a-label-that-does-arrive">the typology</Ref>. Its count
over the last day is 8 against a usual 2; over 30 days it is 66 against a
usual 60, a change smaller than the card's ordinary month-to-month
variation. The second card has paid four times a day for the last three
weeks instead of twice. On any one day, four payments is within what a
card paying twice a day sometimes does; over 30 days its count is 102
against 60.

A short window reacts fast and is noisy; a long window is stable and
slow. Computing both and letting the model weigh them lets it read changes
at several speeds. A common choice is three windows, 1, 7 and 30 days;
another system used eight, of 1, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 72 and 168 hours; no
length is agreed on, and each set is a guess to be tested on the data. On
the real Belgian stream, <Ref lesson="ulb-handbook-benchmark"
id="a-compromised-terminal-and-a-burst">the benchmark's shallow
tree</Ref> chose the 1-day count as its second question: more than 13
payments today on a card at a terminal with recent fraud.

### A window has to fill before it means anything

The short-window noise and the long-window lag can be seen on one
simulated customer. The simulator draws each customer's habit once; for
customer 0 it drew a mean amount of €62.26 and 2.18 payments a day. Run
the 30-day window along this customer's half-year of payments.

<Figure
  src="./figures/customer0.svg"
  alt="Two panels over the 183 simulated days for customer 0. For the first 30 days the 30-day count climbs from zero, because the
window reaches back to before the data began: a feature computed there
describes the start of the file, not the customer. After a month the count
wanders around 65, the drawn rate times 30, and the mean amount stays
within a few euros of €62. The mean over the last day, a handful of
payments drawn with a spread of half the mean, scatters from a few euros to
over €120 and cannot serve as the reference by itself.

So a training set should start one longest window after the data does,
or its first rows carry half-filled features.

## Computing a window from earlier rows only

The windows are now chosen. Computing them on a table of past payments,
for training, contains a trap that the live system never meets: the
table holds the future of every row except the last.

### The rule

<Definition title="windowed aggregate">
Let payment $i$ belong to entity $e_i$, happen at time $t_i$ and have
amount $\mathrm{Amt}_i$. Over a window of length $w$, its count and sum
are taken over the same entity's payments strictly before it and less than
$w$ earlier:
$$
N_w(i) = \#\{\, j : e_j = e_i,\ t_i - w < t_j < t_i \,\},
\qquad
S_w(i) = \sum_{j :\ e_j = e_i,\ t_i - w < t_j < t_i} \mathrm{Amt}_j ,
$$
with mean amount $M_w(i) = S_w(i)/N_w(i)$, set to 0 when $N_w(i) = 0$.
</Definition>

The time $t_i$ here is the payment's timestamp, finer than the day index
$t$ of earlier lessons, and $w$ is written in hours or days. Two features
of the definition carry the time constraint. The window ends **before**
$t_i$, so the payment being judged is not part of its own reference. And
it is computed at each row's own timestamp, so a training row from 3 May
sees the card's payments up to 3 May, not up to the day the table was
built. No label enters $N_w$ or $S_w$: payments in <Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="what-the-delay-block-is-and-is-not">the delay block</Ref> may enter
them, because the bank saw those payments even though their labels had
not arrived.

### Seven payments by hand

<Example title="one card over three days, 24-hour window">
A card makes seven payments between the evening of 1 January and
midnight on 3 January, in Luxembourg and then in neighbouring Germany.
Its type is POS, a payment at a shop's card terminal, or ATM, a cash
withdrawal. For each payment, $N_{24\text{h}}$ and $S_{24\text{h}}$ count
and sum the card's payments in the 24 hours before it.

| # | time | type | country | amount (€) | $N_{24\text{h}}$ | $S_{24\text{h}}$ (€) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 Jan 18:20 | POS | Luxembourg | 250 | 0 | 0 |
| 2 | 1 Jan 20:35 | POS | Luxembourg | 400 | 1 | 250 |
| 3 | 1 Jan 22:30 | ATM | Luxembourg | 250 | 2 | 650 |
| 4 | 2 Jan 00:50 | POS | Germany | 50 | 3 | 900 |
| 5 | 2 Jan 19:18 | POS | Germany | 100 | 3 | 700 |
| 6 | 2 Jan 23:45 | POS | Germany | 150 | 2 | 150 |
| 7 | 3 Jan 00:00 | POS | Luxembourg | 10 | 3 | 300 |

**Row 1.** Nothing precedes it: every aggregate is 0. The first payment
of any history looks like this.

**Rows 2 to 4.** The window reaches back to the evening before, so it
holds every earlier payment: row 4 at 00:50 sees rows 1 to 3,
$250 + 400 + 250 = 900$.

**Row 5.** Its window opens at 1 Jan 19:18. Row 1, at 18:20, is outside;
rows 2, 3 and 4 are in: $N = 3$, $S = 400 + 250 + 50 = 700$.

**Row 6.** The window opens at 1 Jan 23:45, after row 3: rows 4 and 5
remain, $N = 2$, $S = 50 + 100 = 150$.

**Row 7.** The window opens at 2 Jan 00:00 and holds rows 4, 5 and 6:
$N = 3$, $S = 50 + 100 + 150 = 300$.
</Example>

### Three ordinary table operations that break the rule

- **A statistic over the whole table.** "The card's mean amount" computed
  with a group-by over the full file uses every payment the card ever
  made, including those after the row. In training the feature knows the
  card's future; in service it cannot.
- **A value computed once and joined to every row.** The card's 30-day
  count as of the day the table was built, attached to all of its rows,
  gives a row from May a number about September. The aggregate has to be
  evaluated at each row's own $t_i$.
- **The current payment inside its own window.** A rolling window in
  pandas, `rolling("7d")` on a time index, covers $(t_i - w,\, t_i]$ and
  so includes the payment itself; `closed="left"` excludes it. Including
  it is not a leak, since the payment is known when it is scored, but it
  mixes the payment into the reference it is meant to be compared with,
  and for a card that pays rarely the reference becomes the payment.
  The simulator benchmark's card windows included the current payment;
  the windows here exclude it, and the two give different numbers, so a
  pipeline must state which it uses.

## Conditioning the window on the kind of payment

A card pays in France every day and, today, once in Brazil. Its count of
payments over the last day is ordinary, and so is its amount. What is
unusual is a payment in Brazil, which this card has never made, and a
plain window cannot ask about that.

### The same window, a narrower question

<Definition title="conditional aggregate">
Let $g_i$ be a categorical value of payment $i$, such as its type, its
country or its merchant category. The conditional count and sum keep only
the entity's earlier payments that share the current payment's value:
$$
N^{g}_w(i) = \#\{\, j : e_j = e_i,\ g_j = g_i,\ t_i - w < t_j < t_i \,\},
$$
and $S^{g}_w(i)$ sums their amounts in the same way. Several conditions
can be combined, such as the same type **and** the same country.
</Definition>

For the seven payments, take $g$ to be the pair (type, country).

| # | type, country | amount (€) | $N^{g}_{24\text{h}}$ | $S^{g}_{24\text{h}}$ (€) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | POS, Luxembourg | 250 | 0 | 0 |
| 2 | POS, Luxembourg | 400 | 1 | 250 |
| 3 | ATM, Luxembourg | 250 | 0 | 0 |
| 4 | POS, Germany | 50 | 0 | 0 |
| 5 | POS, Germany | 100 | 1 | 50 |
| 6 | POS, Germany | 150 | 2 | 150 |
| 7 | POS, Luxembourg | 10 | 0 | 0 |

Row 3 is the card's first cash withdrawal and row 4 its first payment in
Germany: their plain counts are 2 and 3, their conditional counts 0. Row 7
returns to Luxembourg after a day in Germany; within 24 hours there is no
earlier POS payment in Luxembourg, so its conditional count is 0 while
its plain count is 3. A zero next to a busy plain window is the signature
the feature exists for: this card is active, but not like this.

### Two meanings of "at the merchant"

"The card's number of payments at the merchant in the last 24 hours" can
mean two different features.

- **At a fixed, named merchant.** One column per merchant, "payments at
  shop M in the last 24 hours", the same question for every row. It is
  zero for almost every card and almost every row, and a network with
  thousands of merchants needs thousands of such columns.
- **At the same merchant as the current payment.** One column, whose
  question changes with the row: at shop M it counts visits to M, at shop
  K visits to K. It is the conditional aggregate above, with $g$ the
  merchant.

The second is compact and answers "has this card been here before?"; the
first answers a question about one place, which is useful only for a few
places worth watching.

### How fast the grid grows

Each condition multiplies the plain features. Eight window lengths, each
with a count and a sum, plain and conditional on each of five criteria,
give $8 \times 2 \times 6 = 96$ features per payment. The five
criteria are the country; the transaction type (shop payment or cash
withdrawal); the entry mode, how the card reached the terminal (chip,
magnetic stripe, contactless, or card details typed in online); the
merchant code, the category a merchant is registered under, such as
grocery or air travel; and the merchant group, a coarser grouping of those
categories. Conditions can also
be combined, as the definition allows, and one card processor's model,
built on those eight windows and five criteria, used 280 aggregated
features in all. Every one of them must be recomputed for each payment within the
time the system has to score it, which caps the grid long before the data
runs out of conditions to try; <Ref
lesson="real-time-scoring-architecture">the real-time scoring
architecture</Ref> takes that limit up.

## The terminal's past, and the fifteen inputs

The card is one entity. The terminal that receives the payment is
another, and the same windows can run over its rows: its number of
payments over the last 1, 7 and 30 days. What these see depends on what a
compromise does to a terminal's traffic.

On the simulator a compromised terminal keeps its ordinary customers; it
is every payment there that becomes fraud for 28 days, not the number of
payments that changes. Its counts over 1, 7 or 30 days look like any
other terminal's. The counts give the terminal's scale, a busy shop
against a quiet one, and they would move under a burst of traffic, but
they do not see this scenario. What sees it is the share of the
terminal's recent payments that turned out to be fraud, and that number
needs labels. Labels have a clock, so that feature is the subject of
<Ref lesson="point-in-time-features">point-in-time features</Ref>; here it
is only reserved a place.

<Figure
  src="./figures/feature-vector.svg"
  alt="Left, the raw record: card 4471, terminal 9023, amount 184 euros, Tuesday 14:07. Arrows lead to a feature vector on the right in three groups. The payment: amount 184, weekend 0, night 0. The card's past: number of payments and mean amount over the last 1, 7 and 30 days, six features. The terminal's past: number of payments over the last 1, 7 and 30 days, three features, and below them three greyed slots for the terminal's fraud rate over 1, 7 and 30 days, labelled needs labels, built later. The card and terminal identifiers are struck through with the note used to compute the windows, not as features"
  caption="Four raw fields become a feature vector: two calendar flags, six card aggregates, three terminal counts, and three terminal fraud rates still to be built. The identifiers are used to group the history and then leave."
/>

The timestamp gives two flags of its own: whether the payment falls on a
weekend, and whether it falls at night, here before 06:00. These are
fixed calendar facts, the same for every card; a time of day that is
unusual *for this card* is the subject of <Ref
lesson="behavioural-profiles">behavioural profiles</Ref>. Together with
the amount, the six card aggregates and the three terminal counts they
make twelve numbers, all computed without a label; the terminal's three
fraud rates complete the benchmark's fifteen inputs. The card and terminal
numbers are not among them: they are needed to find each entity's
history, and <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="why-the-cards-identity-stays-out-of-the-features">the delayed-labels
lesson</Ref> showed why a card number among the inputs teaches a model
which cards were caught. What the identifier carried, a card's habit, now
enters as numbers that two cards with similar habits share, so that what
is learned on one card applies to another.

## What the aggregates buy

Whether the windows pay is a measurement, and on the simulator the answer
key makes it a precise one: it says which scenario each found fraud
belongs to.

### On the simulator: one scenario found, one not

Train a random forest of 100 trees on the simulator's training week, 25
to 31 July 2018, and score it on the test week behind a delay block, with
known cards removed, as in <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="where-the-training-set-must-end-the-delay-block">the delayed-labels
lesson</Ref>. The test week holds 385 frauds: 20 from the amount rule,
281 at compromised terminals and 84 on leaked cards. Change only the
inputs, and record, for each scenario, the share of its test frauds that
reach the top 100 payments of their day.

| amount, weekend, night, card windows | 0.21 | 0.08 | 100% | 1% | 80% |

<Task>
In the chart, find the one scenario that the card windows move and the
one that only the last input set moves. Then check the table: why does
$CP@100$ barely rise when the card windows are added, although the
leaked-card share more than doubles?

**Leaked cards.** Their fraudulent payments are the card's own amounts
multiplied by five. On the amount alone, the third of them that cross
€220 are found, the ones scenario 1's line catches anyway. With the card
windows the forest can compare the amount with the card's mean, and 80% of
them reach the top 100: the reference point the raw record lacked.

**Compromised terminals.** The card windows do not see them, because the
paying card is an ordinary customer paying ordinary amounts; neither do
the terminal counts, for the reason given above. That is why $CP@100$
barely moves: terminal frauds are 281 of the 385, and the card windows
leave them where they were.

### The identifier as a lookup table

The second row is the warning. Handing the forest the card and terminal
numbers lifts $CP@100$ from 0.06 to 0.16, and the whole gain is at
compromised terminals, 29% of whose frauds now reach the top 100. The
forest has memorised which terminals had frauds in its training week, and
on the simulator a terminal stays compromised for 28 days, so for a
terminal compromised late in the training week the memory is still right
a fortnight later. It can never be right about a terminal compromised
after the training week, and on a real stream a memorised terminal is
one that has since been investigated and repaired or closed.

The memory itself is legitimate: "this terminal's recent payments were fraud" is exactly what the terminal's
fraud rate records, for every terminal, recomputed every day from the
labels that have arrived. With that feature in place of the identifier,
the last row finds 66% of terminal fraud and $CP@100$ reaches 0.29.

Every detector gained. The plain tree went from about 19% to 39%, the

<KeyFact>
Aggregates do not add signal across the board: they add the signal of the
fraud that changes the entity they summarise. Card windows found the
leaked cards and nothing at the compromised terminals, whose signal is in
the terminal's labels.
</KeyFact>

<Conclusion>
You can now turn a raw payment record into features over its entities'
past: recency, counts and amounts over windows of several lengths,
computed for each row from the same entity's strictly earlier payments,
and conditioned on the payment's type, country or merchant where the
question is "is this kind of payment usual for this card". You can
explain why each carries signal the record lacks, the card's own
reference point, and why on the simulator they find the leaked cards and
not the compromised terminals.

The terminal's recent fraud rate, which finds those terminals, is built
from labels, and labels arrive a delay late; <Ref
lesson="point-in-time-features">point-in-time features</Ref> builds it, and
every other feature, so that each training row holds only what was known at
its own moment. What the windows cannot say is whether a payment's hour is
usual for this card, or whether the card behaves like cards similar to it.
A mean of clock times already fails there: a card that pays at 23:00 and
at 01:00 averages to noon, an hour it never pays at. <Ref
lesson="behavioural-profiles">Behavioural profiles</Ref> builds those
comparisons.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A card's very first payment has every aggregate at zero: no recency, no
counts, no amounts. (a) Show that a model given only the twelve
label-free numbers (amount, calendar flags, card windows, terminal
counts) cannot tell a brand-new card from a card that
has not paid for 31 days. (b) Why might the two deserve different scores?
(c) Name a feature that restores the distinction, and say what value it
takes on each card.

<Solution>
**(a)** For both cards every window of 1, 7 and 30 days is empty, so all
six card aggregates are 0. The amount, the calendar flags and the terminal
counts describe the payment and the terminal, not the card's age, and can
be identical. Same inputs, same score.

**(b)** A new card has no habit yet: any first payment is "unusual" for
it, and a model that scored every first payment high would flood the
queue. A dormant card that wakes with a large payment is a familiar
pattern of stolen credentials used on an account the owner no longer
watches. The two warrant different readings.

**(c)** Recency computed without a cap: the time since the card's
previous payment is undefined (or a separate flag "first payment ever")
for the new card and 31 days for the dormant one. A cap such as "30 days
or more" would merge them again, which is why a capped recency needs the
flag beside it. Equivalent alternatives: the card's age (time since its
first payment), or a count over a window longer than 30 days, 0 for the
new card and positive for the dormant one. Flags marking the first payment
in each window serve the same purpose for a linear model, which cannot
read a zero as "missing".
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
"Including the current payment in the card's 7-day mean amount is
harmless: it is one payment among many." Refute the claim with a card that
pays once a week, and say what the feature loses. Is including it a leak?

<Solution>
**The counterexample.** A card that pays once a week has, at each
payment, no other payment in the last 7 days. With the current payment
included, the 7-day mean equals the current amount, every time: a €40
payment gets a mean of €40, and a €900 fraud on the same card gets a mean
of €900. The ratio of amount to mean is 1 for both.

**What fails.** "One among many" assumes the window holds many payments.
The mean is meant to be the card's reference, and a reference that
contains the payment being judged moves toward it: by a factor of
$1/(N+1)$ of the gap for a window that held $N$ earlier payments, which is
negligible at $N = 60$ and total at $N = 0$. Rarely used cards, exactly
the ones on which a large fraud stands out, are where the feature goes
blind.

**Not a leak.** The current payment is known when it is scored, so the
feature can be computed identically in training and in service; nothing
from the future enters. The defect is lost signal, not optimism: the
model is honestly evaluated and simply weaker.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive an algorithm that computes $N_w(i)$ and $S_w(i)$ for every
payment of one entity and every $w$ in a set of window lengths
$W = \{w_1, \dots, w_m\}$, and state its cost. The payments of the entity
are sorted by time, $t_1 \le t_2 \le \dots \le t_n$. (a) Show that a
single pointer per window, moved only forward, finds each window's first
row. (b) Use prefix sums to get the sums. (c) What does the conditional
version $N^{g}_w$ cost?

<Solution>
**(a)** For window $w$, let $\ell(i)$ be the first index with
$t_{\ell(i)} > t_i - w$. As $i$ increases, $t_i - w$ does not decrease,
so $\ell(i) \ge \ell(i-1)$: the pointer only moves forward. Start
$\ell = 1$; for each $i$, advance $\ell$ while $t_\ell \le t_i - w$. The
rows in the window are $\ell(i), \dots, r(i)$, where $r(i)$ is the last
row with $t_{r(i)} < t_i$ (it is $i - 1$ unless an earlier row shares the
timestamp $t_i$, which a second forward pointer handles). So
$N_w(i) = r(i) - \ell(i) + 1$ when $r(i) \ge \ell(i)$, else 0.

**(b)** With prefix sums $P_0 = 0$, $P_k = \mathrm{Amt}_1 + \dots +
\mathrm{Amt}_k$, the sum is $S_w(i) = P_{r(i)} - P_{\ell(i) - 1}$.

**Cost.** Each pointer moves at most $n$ times in total, so one window
costs $O(n)$ for the entity, all of $W$ costs $O(mn)$, plus $O(n)$ for the
prefix sums. Over all entities, after one sort by (entity, time) costing
$O(N \log N)$ for $N$ payments, the work is $O(mN)$. (Binary search for
$\ell(i)$ instead of the pointer gives $O(mN \log N)$ and is what a
vectorised implementation does.)

**(c)** Group by (entity, value of $g$) instead of entity and run the
same sweep within each group: still $O(mN)$ after an $O(N \log N)$ sort.
Each further condition is one more pass of the same cost, which is how
the grid of the conditional section turns into recomputation time.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the simulator's test week, restricted to genuine payments and the 84
frauds on leaked cards (base rate 0.0014), each single feature ranks the
payments on its own. Average precision of each:

| feature | average precision |
|---|---|
| card's payments in the last day | 0.001 |
| card's mean amount over the last 30 days | 0.005 |
| amount | 0.34 |
| amount ÷ card's mean amount over the last 7 days | 0.15 |
| amount ÷ card's mean amount over the last 30 days | 0.61 |

Explain each number from what a leaked card does in the simulator: a third
of its payments, for 14 days, have their amount multiplied by five. In
particular: why is the count below the base rate, why does the 30-day mean
alone carry almost nothing, and why does the 30-day ratio beat the 7-day
one?

<Solution>
**Count, 0.001.** A leak does not change how often the card pays: the
fraudulent payments are the owner's own payments with inflated amounts.
The count is unrelated to the label, so its ranking is random, and AP of a
random ranking sits near the base rate. Being just under it is noise.

**30-day mean, 0.005.** The mean describes the card, not the payment.
Leaked cards were drawn at random, so their usual amounts are like
anyone's; the inflated payments of the previous days raise the mean only
a little. A high mean marks a big spender, not a fraud.

**Amount, 0.34.** Five times an amount is often large in absolute terms,
so large amounts are enriched in fraud, but a big spender's genuine
payments compete with a small spender's inflated ones.

**Ratios.** Dividing by the card's own mean removes that competition: an
inflated payment sits near five times its card's mean whatever the card.
The 30-day ratio reaches 0.61. The 7-day ratio does worse because its
reference is contaminated: after a few days of compromise, a third of the
payments in the last 7 days are themselves inflated, which multiplies the
7-day mean by $\tfrac23 + \tfrac13 \times 5 = \tfrac73 \approx 2.3$ and
pulls the ratio of the next inflated payment from 5 to about 2. A 30-day
window holds at most 14 compromised days among 30, so its mean grows by at
most $\tfrac{16}{30} + \tfrac{14}{30} \times \tfrac73 \approx 1.6$ and
the ratio stays above 3.

**The transferable point.** An aggregate carries signal through its
comparison with the current payment. A tree can learn the comparison from
the amount and the mean together, given depth; writing the ratio as a
feature hands it over in one split.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague reads the simulator table of the last section and proposes keeping
the card and terminal numbers among the inputs: "the identifiers lift
$CP@100$ from 0.06 to 0.16, and that difference is far beyond the noise
line." (a) Is the difference beyond the noise line, by the rule of the
metrics lesson? (b) Of the test week's 281 terminal frauds, 150 are at
terminals that already had frauds in the training week. What is the most
of the terminal fraud a lookup of identifiers could ever find, and how
close did the forest come? (c) Give two
reasons from earlier lessons why the lift would shrink or vanish in a
bank, and say what replaces the identifier.

<Solution>
**(a)** Yes. A $CP@100$ difference of 0.10 is ten compromised cards a
day, five times the two-cards-a-day line under which <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="what-each-disagreement-means">the
metrics lesson</Ref> treats a one-week difference as noise. The gain is
real on this test week; the question is what produces it.

**(b)** A lookup of training-week labels can only flag terminals that had
fraud in the training week. Of the 281 test-week terminal frauds, 150 sit
at such terminals, so at most 150 of 281, about 53%, are reachable; the
forest found 29% of the 281, about half of the reachable ones. The other
131 are at terminals compromised after the training week, which no lookup
can know.

**(c)** First, the simulator keeps a compromised terminal fraudulent for a
fixed 28 days and keeps its identifier; a real terminal that was used for
fraud is investigated, repaired or closed, so the memorised identifiers
point at terminals whose status has changed. Second, the card number
teaches the model which cards were caught, and the bank blocks those
cards, so in a backtest it is credited for frauds that never happen, the
argument of <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="why-the-cards-identity-stays-out-of-the-features">the delayed-labels
lesson</Ref>. What replaces the identifier is the same information as a
feature valid for every terminal and every day: the terminal's fraud rate
over a recent window of labelled days, which in the table's last row finds
66% of terminal fraud.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A bank wants a feature that flags a card paying at many merchants it has
never used before, as happens when stolen card details are tried at one
online shop after another. (a) Which of the two meanings of "at the
merchant" does the feature need, and what value does it take on a
first visit? (b) Build from it a single card-level count that rises with
the pattern. (c) Why would the other meaning fail here, even with one
column per merchant?

<Solution>
**(a)** The same merchant as the current payment: $N^{g}_{w}(i)$ with $g$
the merchant, over a long window such as 30 days. On a first visit to a
merchant it is 0, whatever the card's overall activity.

**(b)** Over the last 24 hours, count the card's payments whose own
same-merchant 30-day count was 0: the number of first visits today. A
card that pays at its two usual shops scores 0; a card tried at six new
shops in an hour scores 6. It is a windowed count, conditioned on an
indicator computed by another window, and it still uses only earlier
payments.

**(c)** A fixed-merchant column answers "how often at shop M", the same
question on every row. The pattern is not about any particular shop: each
new shop's column is 0 before the visit, like the columns of thousands of
shops the card never visits, so nothing distinguishes "new here" from
"never here", and the model would need one column per shop to see the
visit at all.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the simulator, a compromised terminal turns every payment it receives
into fraud for 28 days, and its traffic is steady. Define a terminal's
30-day fraud rate as the share of its payments in a 30-day window that are
fraud. (a) For a terminal compromised $d$ days ago, with $0 \le d \le 28$,
write the rate over the 30 days before today when every label is known,
and the rate over the 30 days ending 7 days ago. (b) On a given day the
simulator holds 56 compromised terminals, two drawn on each of the last 28
days. How many of them have a rate of 0 under the second definition? (c)
Which of the two definitions can the bank compute on the morning of day
$t$, and what happens to a model trained with the other one?

<Solution>
**(a)** With steady traffic the rate is the fraction of the window that
falls after the compromise began. All labels known: the last 30 days
contain $d$ compromised days, so the rate is $d/30$. Window ending 7 days
ago: it covers days $t - 37$ to $t - 7$, which contain $\max(0, d - 7)$
compromised days, so the rate is $\max(0, d - 7)/30$.

**(b)** The rate is 0 when $d \le 7$: the terminals drawn in the last
seven days, $2 \times 7 = 14$ of the 56, a quarter of them.

**(c)** Labels arrive a week late, so on the morning of day $t$ the bank
knows labels only up to about a week back: it can compute the second
definition, not the first. A model trained with the first learns that a
rate of $1/30$ to $7/30$ marks a fresh compromise; in service those
terminals show 0, and the rule never fires. That fresh quarter, the most
valuable to catch, is where the training feature and the served feature
part. Building every feature so that training and service see the same
value is the problem of <Ref lesson="point-in-time-features">point-in-time
features</Ref>, which computes this rate over $[t-(d+w),\,t-d]$ as the
difference of two rolling sums.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

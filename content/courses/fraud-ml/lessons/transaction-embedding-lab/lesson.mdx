The lab builds the encoder the module described, on real anonymised bank histories with product-purchase targets and no fraud label: slices and a margin loss trained on CPU in minutes, an embedding read for every client at every month's cut without a later event, aggregates by prefix sums as the opponent, and a bootstrap that says whether the two differ — at all the labels and at a tenth of them.

The last four lessons described the contrastive task, the loss, the fusion of a
learned vector with hand-built features and the questions to ask of any claim
that one beats the other. Each of those claims rests on code that can be wrong
without raising an error: a slice that runs past its history, a row paired
with itself, a state read one event after the cut, a bootstrap that treats a
client's four months as four witnesses. A wrong line still prints a plausible
ROC-AUC, and the leak at the cut prints a better one.

So you write the pipeline and check each part: a seeded slice sampler; the
margin loss on a batch of unit vectors, and the same loss restricted to each
slice's hard negatives; the forward pass of a GRU encoder over packed
sequences; the embedding of every client at every month's cut from one pass
over its history; the windowed aggregates over 30 days, 90 days and the whole
history by prefix sums; a paired bootstrap of the difference in ROC-AUC that
resamples clients, not rows; the set of clients and events the encoder may
train on; and the training loop. Then your encoder is trained on about 5,700
clients' histories and judged against your aggregates on clients and months
it has never seen.

Every definition comes from an earlier lesson. Slices, the margin loss, hard
negatives and the identity shortcut come from
<Ref lesson="contrastive-event-embeddings" />; the next-event model with public
weights from <Ref lesson="generative-pretraining-and-tokenisation" />; the rank
average from <Ref lesson="transaction-foundation-models" />; the checklist from
<Ref lesson="reading-foundation-model-claims" />; windowed aggregates and the
point-in-time cut from <Ref lesson="transaction-aggregates" /> and
<Ref lesson="point-in-time-features" />, built once already in
<Ref lesson="point-in-time-feature-lab" />. PyTorch is used at the level of
`nn.Embedding`, `nn.GRU` and an optimiser step; no sequence library.

The data is a cut of the public Multimodal Banking Dataset: 11,837 clients,
4.7 million card events from July 2021 to December 2022, and for each client
and month whether they bought each of four products. It has no fraud label,
its dates carry noise within the day, and half its clients were chosen for
having bought something, so its scores are neither fraud results nor
comparable with the benchmark's published ones. The notebook says this where
the numbers appear.

The lab runs as a Jupyter notebook — open it in Colab with the button above, or
in any Jupyter. It needs no GPU and downloads about 21 MB of data. The
pretraining takes about four minutes on four CPU threads and ten to fifteen on
a two-core Colab machine; the whole notebook, about eight minutes on the former.
Pair the notebook with the code from this page; each checkpoint submits your
function's outputs for verification, and the checklist above tracks what
stands. The nine exact checkpoints are the required set. When a check goes
red, the verdict often names the bug — a length clipped instead of drawn, the
diagonal paired, a same-client slice taken as a hard negative, the padding
read as the final state, the state after the cut, a prefix array without its
zero row, rows resampled instead of clients, a test client in the pretraining
set, gradients never cleared — so read it before rereading your code.

The written answer (what your three intervals license and forbid) and the
open task are optional. The open task scores the graded fold's four months
for all four products with labels of only a tenth of the training clients,
to a stacked ROC-AUC above 0.654. The boosted tree on the aggregates reaches
0.630 there, the logistic regression on the embedding 0.626, and their rank
average 0.650, just short of the bar: clearing it takes a change that buys
something, and a plot of your scores beside those three, product by product,
shows what each change bought. In the reference run the embedding did not beat the aggregates
with all labels, the interval for the rank average spanned zero, and at a
tenth of the labels both intervals cleared it; at 3% they cleared it with one
pretraining seed and spanned it with another. An optional last
section sets out how to bring the released next-event weights and a
synthetic card set with a fraud label into the same comparison.

The embedding is one more feature computed as of a cut. How such features are
computed, stored and served inside a payment's deadline is the subject of
<Ref lesson="real-time-scoring-architecture" />.

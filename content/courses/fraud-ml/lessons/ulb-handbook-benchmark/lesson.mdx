Five standard classifiers — logistic regression, a shallow tree, an unpruned tree, a random forest and a boosted ensemble — are trained on a simulated week and on a week of real card payments, each scored three ways. Which one is best? The answer depends on the metric and the data, and the module's results should say exactly why.

Half of this comparison is already familiar. <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="three-numbers-for-three-questions">The alert-budget metrics</Ref>
ranked the same five models on the simulator's test week and found a
different leader under each of ROC-AUC, average precision (AP) and card
precision at 100 ($CP@100$). The other half is a week of real Belgian
e-commerce payments, the stream of <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="a-hundred-cases-a-day-the-models-output-is-a-ranking">the layered system</Ref>, about 400,000
payments and 300 compromised cards a day. There nearly every model
changes place, and every number drops.

The obvious way to read fifteen numbers is a league table: average each
model's rank, or take the winner of the most familiar metric, and ship
it. That throws away what the table holds. A reversal between two models
is a measurement: either two metrics are reading different parts of the
ranked list, or the two streams let a model learn different things. The
work is to account for every reversal with a result from this module, or
with a fact about the data that can be checked, and to see what is left
over.

## What the comparison holds fixed, and what it does not

A ranking reversal can only be pinned on a cause if everything else was
held still. Three things are held still here, on both streams.

### One cut of the history

The split is the one <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="where-the-training-set-must-end-the-delay-block">the delayed-labels
lesson</Ref> derived: a training week, a delay week whose labels are not
yet known, and a test week from which each day's payments on cards
already known to be compromised are removed. On the simulator that is
25 to 31 July 2018 for training, 67,240 payments with 598 frauds, and 8
to 14 August for testing, 58,264 payments with 385 frauds after removal,
a base rate of 0.66%. On the real stream the training week holds
2,858,663 payments with 6,605 frauds (0.23%), and after removal the test
week's base rate is about 0.2%.

### Fifteen inputs, built the same way

Every model sees the same fifteen numbers for each payment:

- the **amount**, and whether the payment fell on a weekend or at night;
- for the **card**: its number of payments and their average amount over
  the last 1, 7 and 30 days;
- for the **terminal**: its number of payments and its fraud rate over
  windows of 1, 7 and 30 days, the rate counting only labels the bank
  already held when the payment was scored. How that is done without
  leaking future labels is the problem of <Ref
  lesson="point-in-time-features">point-in-time features</Ref>.

Nothing else enters: no merchant category, no country, no card
identifier.

### Five models at their library defaults

- **Logistic regression**, a linear score on the fifteen inputs.
- **A depth-2 tree**: three splits, four leaves; a payment's score is the
  fraud share of its leaf in training.
- **An unpruned tree**: depth unlimited, split until every leaf holds
  training payments of one class only, so its scores are 0 and 1.
- **A random forest**: 100 unpruned trees, each grown on a bootstrap
  sample of the training week with a random subset of inputs tried at
  each split; the score is the average of the trees' outputs.
- **XGBoost**: 100 boosted trees of depth up to 6, each fitted to the
  errors left by the ones before it.

No hyperparameter is tuned and nothing is rebalanced.

### What changes between the streams

Within one stream, two rows of the results differ only by the model.
Between the streams, the stream itself changes, and in more than one way
at once:

| | simulator | real stream |
|---|---|---|
| payments a day | about 8,300 | about 400,000 |
| test-week base rate | 0.66% | about 0.2% |
| frauds per compromised card, per day | about 1.1 | about 3 |
| compromised cards a day, after removal | about 40 to 55 | 200 to 350 |

A fifth difference does not fit in a table: how far the fifteen inputs
separate fraud from genuine payments. It is measured below, from the
leaves of the depth-2 tree.

## The simulator: half the fraud in two leaves

On the simulator's test week every one of the five models reaches 47 to
100 times the base rate in AP: logistic regression's 0.606 is 92 times
0.66%, the unpruned tree's 0.309 is 47 times. The simulator's two
reversals are already settled: the unpruned tree's two scores win AUC
and lose AP (<Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="what-auc-sees-in-the-fully-grown-tree">what AUC sees in the fully
grown tree</Ref>), and the three leaders sit within two cards a day of
each other on $CP@100$ (<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-each-disagreement-means">what each disagreement means</Ref>).
What is not settled is why the high multiples are possible at all, for
models that learn their training week by heart as much as for those
that do not.

### What the training week says

Which of the five learned the training week by heart is a question the
training week itself can answer: a model that has memorised its labels
scores near perfectly on the data it was fitted to, and keeps on a later
week only as much of that score as the memorised patterns recur. Scored on the week they were trained on:

| | training AUC | training AP | training $CP@100$ | test AUC | test AP | test $CP@100$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| logistic regression | 0.892 | 0.663 | 0.419 | 0.871 | 0.606 | 0.291 |
| depth-2 tree | 0.802 | 0.586 | 0.394 | 0.763 | 0.496 | 0.241 |
| unpruned tree | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.576 | 0.788 | 0.309 | 0.243 |
| random forest | 1.000 | 1.000 | 0.576 | 0.867 | 0.658 | 0.287 |
| XGBoost | 1.000 | 0.995 | 0.574 | 0.862 | 0.639 | 0.273 |

The three models that can carve the training week into pure regions,
the unpruned tree, the forest of unpruned trees and a hundred boosted
trees, score perfectly on it: these three are the memorisers. Logistic
regression loses 0.021 of AUC from training to test, the depth-2 tree
0.039, the memorisers 0.13 to 0.21. Yet on the test week the forest and
XGBoost still come within 0.01 of logistic regression's AUC and beat it
on AP. Much of what they memorised recurred, so the regions their trees were cut around in training must
still have been fraudulent a fortnight later. Which regions those are, a
tree small enough to read shows.

The root split finds terminals at which more than half of the payments
in a recent week, one whose labels the bank already held, were fraud:
the terminals that <Ref

second scenario</Ref> compromises for 28 days, during which every

frauds of the other scenarios lie just under 220, and taking them costs
leaf 2 its two genuine payments.

Leaf 2 is not only the first scenario's, though. That scenario adds about
five frauds a day, some 37 in a week, and leaf 2 holds 127. The third
scenario, a leaked card used for a third of its owner's payments,

above 220 (<Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"

large, and the other half of the third scenario, the multiplied payments
still under 220, falls in leaf 1.

|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 57,968 | 182 | 0.31% |
| 2 | 50 | 47 | 94% |
| 3 | 106 | 19 | 18% |

Leaves 2 and 4 together hold 184 of the week's 385 frauds in 190
payments: 48% of the fraud at 97% precision. The other half sits in leaf
1, at 0.31%, below the week's base rate.

</Example>

### Every number against its floor

Each metric has its own score for a ranking with no information: 0.5
for AUC, the base rate for AP (<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-a-ranking-with-no-information-scores">the floor of average
precision</Ref>), and for $CP@100$ the share of a day's cards that are
compromised, about 0.0025 here. Against those floors every model has
learned something, and the one that has learned least is the unpruned
tree. (The depth-2 tree's 0.077 is the week's average; the "about 0.10"
quoted for it in the alert-budget lesson was read off its daily chart.)

AP divided by the base rate puts both streams on one scale: how many
times better than chance the model concentrates fraud at the top.

<Figure
  src="./figures/ap-multiples.svg"
  alt="Dot plot on a log axis of average precision divided by the test week's base rate, from 1 to 200 times. For each model a blue dot marks the simulator and an amber dot the real stream, joined by a grey line: logistic regression 92 times and 18 times; depth-2 tree 75 and 12; unlimited-depth tree 47 and 1.5; random forest 100 and 8; XGBoost 97 and 18. A dashed line at 1 times is labelled chance"
  caption="AP as a multiple of the base rate. Every model falls from 47–100× to 18× or less; the unpruned tree falls to 1.5×, the forest from first to fourth."
/>

A lower base rate alone cannot produce this fall. At a cut with recall
$\mathrm{TPR}$ and false-positive rate $\mathrm{FPR}$, precision as a
multiple of the base rate is
$$
\frac{\text{precision}}{\pi} = \frac{\mathrm{TPR}}{\pi\,\mathrm{TPR} + (1-\pi)\,\mathrm{FPR}} ,
$$
and its denominator shrinks as $\pi$ falls whenever
$\mathrm{TPR} > \mathrm{FPR}$. So a model with the same ROC curve on a
rarer stream has a higher multiple at every cut, and a higher AP
multiple. The multiples drop instead, by a factor of 5 to 30: the inputs
separate real fraud less well than scripted fraud.

The drop is uneven. Logistic regression falls by a factor of 5, the
depth-2 tree by 6, the forest by 13 and the unpruned tree by 30.
XGBoost, perfect on the simulator's training week like the other two,
falls by 5, no more than logistic regression. A perfect training score
on the simulator therefore does not predict the drop, and neither does
combining a hundred trees: the forest and XGBoost both do that.

### What separates the two ensembles: a hypothesis

What does differ is the size of the leaves. The unpruned tree and each
tree of the forest split until every leaf is pure, so a leaf around a
fraud can hold a handful of training payments. A tree of depth 6 has at
most 64 leaves for 2.9 million training payments, some 45,000 a leaf
on average, so a score built from such leaves leans on many training
payments at once. The real training week fits this:
there only the unpruned tree and the forest score perfectly, and
XGBoost's depth-6 trees stop short of a pure fit. The
**hypothesis** is that the two worst-hit models rank first the payments
that fall in small regions cut around a few training frauds, and that on
real payments such regions are rarely fraud again. The unpruned tree's
collapse, derived below, is what the hypothesis predicts for a single
tree; for the forest it is a candidate mechanism, not a measurement.

### The unpruned tree falls to chance

The unpruned tree still outputs two scores, so the two formulas of the
delayed-labels lesson apply: with $T$ the recall of the block scored 1,
$F$ its false-positive rate, $P_1$ its precision and $\pi$ the base
rate,
$$
\mathrm{AUC} = \tfrac12 (1 + T - F), \qquad \mathrm{AP} = T\,P_1 + (1-T)\,\pi .
$$

<Example title="what the unpruned tree's top block holds on real payments">
On the real week $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.545$, $\mathrm{AP} = 0.003$ and
$\pi \approx 0.002$. The AP is printed to one significant figure, so
instead of solving for exact values, bound them.

**Recall of the top block.** $T - F = 2 \times 0.545 - 1 = 0.09$, and
$F \ge 0$, so $T \ge 0.09$.

**Precision of the top block.** Rearrange the AP formula:
$T\,(P_1 - \pi) = \mathrm{AP} - \pi \approx 0.001$. With $T \ge 0.09$,
$P_1 - \pi \le 0.001/0.09 \approx 0.011$, so $P_1 \le 0.013$. Even if
the printed 0.003 were 0.0035, $P_1$ would stay under 0.02.

**Reading.** On the simulator the same model's top block was 53% fraud
(the delayed-labels lesson's inversion). On real payments at most one in
75 payments the tree scores 1 is fraud. Each of its pure leaves was cut
around a few training frauds; on the simulator a later payment landing
in such a region is usually fraud of the same scenario, on the real
stream it is almost always genuine.
</Example>

  src="./figures/dtinf-blocks.svg"

Simulator: a blue segment of about 58% of the bar is the top block scored 1, the rest tied at 0; precision of the top block 0.53.   caption="The unpruned tree's top block: half fraud on the simulator, at most 1.3% fraud on real payments."

The team calls cards, not payments, so the bound must be turned into a
share of cards before it can be held against $CP@100$. The hundred cards
are drawn from the tied top block: it holds at least 0.09 of the day's
roughly 800 test frauds at no more than 1.3% fraud, so at least 5,500
payments a day, far more than a hundred cards.

A card's score is the highest of its payments' scores, so a card is in
the block if any one of its payments is. A compromised card makes about
three fraud payments a day, each a chance to land there; a genuine card
makes about three payments a day too (400,000 payments over some 120,000
cards, the count at which 300 compromised cards are the 0.0025 share),
each a chance as well. Assume each payment lands independently of the
card's other payments, and that each chance is small. Then a compromised card is about three times as likely to be
in the block as one of its fraud payments, a genuine card about three
times as likely as one of its genuine payments, and the two factors
cancel: the block's share of compromised cards is about its share of
fraud payments, at most 1.3%, or 2% allowing for the rounding of AP.
The owner's own payments on a compromised card are extra chances and
can only raise that share. So the bound allows one or two compromised
cards among the hundred. The measured $CP@100$, 0.017, is 1.7 cards:
consistent with the bound, at its upper edge, not a value it pins down.

The independence assumption fits this tree. Its pure leaves are small
regions cut around a few training frauds each, so a card's three frauds
fall in different regions and each lands or misses on its own. Had a
card's frauds landed together, with each genuine card contributing one
payment, the card share would be about a third of the payment share
(the card-share exercise at the end derives it), under one card in a
hundred, and the measured 0.017 would contradict the bound. The
unpruned tree's three real numbers tell one story: it memorised the
training week, and the memory does not transfer.

### The forest: an average of memorisers

A random forest averages a hundred unpruned trees, and averaging blunts
memorisation: instead of two scores it produces a graded list, and on
the simulator that was enough to give it the best AP of the five, 0.658.
Even there, its list ends in a large tied block. Re-running the
simulator split shows 48,229 of the 58,264 test payments (83%) scored
exactly 0, every tree voting genuine, and 83 of the 385 frauds among
them.

On the real stream its AP falls below the depth-2 tree's, 0.015 against
0.024, while its AUC (0.780 against 0.626) and $CP@100$ (0.104 against
0.077) stay above. Where on the list does the forest lose AP?

The two-score bounds cannot say: they work for the unpruned tree because
two scores turn AUC and AP into two equations in $T$ and $P_1$, and the
forest's scores are graded. The real week's precision–recall curves can
(<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="what-a-ranking-with-no-information-scores">the floor of average
precision</Ref> showed all five hugging the floor). Over the first 10% of recall the forest's
precision runs from about 6% down to 4%, while the depth-2 tree, after
a short spike from its best leaf, holds about 9%, and logistic
regression runs from 20% down to 12%. The forest loses its AP at the
top of its list, among the payments it is most sure of.

Why its top is diluted is the leaf-size hypothesis, not a measurement:
the highest averaged scores would go to payments that many trees placed
in small leaves cut around training frauds, regions that on real
payments are rarely fraud. Testing it would need the real test payments
themselves, which are confidential.

The $CP@100$ gap needs no hypothesis. It is 0.027, under three cards a
day, on a stream whose weekly $CP@100$ varies by 0.03 to 0.07 (<Ref
lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited" id="six-ensembles-two-streams">the
ensembles on two streams</Ref>). One week does not establish that the
forest calls more compromised cards than the shallow tree.

### XGBoost: a lead where the team never works

XGBoost leads the real AUC column by 0.11 over every other model, ties
logistic regression on AP at 0.035, and is third on $CP@100$, 0.090
against 0.150.

The AUC lead can be located exactly. A hundred cards a day among about
400,000 payments is a false-positive rate below 0.1% (<Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="how-much-of-the-curve-a-hundred-alerts-can-reach">how much of the
curve a hundred alerts can reach</Ref>). The part of the ROC area that
lies over $\mathrm{FPR} \in [0, 0.001]$ is at most $0.001 \times 1 =
0.001$, whatever the model. So at most 0.001 of XGBoost's 0.111 lead
over logistic regression can come from the operating points the team
uses; the rest is earned at false-positive rates no budget reaches.

Where the lead comes from is a separate question, and the published
numbers do not answer it. A plausible mechanism is the one <Ref
lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="boosting-without-rebalancing-near-best-not-the-pick">boosting without
rebalancing</Ref> described: each round fits the errors of the last, so
frauds the ensemble scores low keep pulling on the next trees until they
rise. That would lift frauds out of the bottom of the list, which is
what AUC counts, without filling the top hundred cards. What is
measured is the top hundred itself: XGBoost's holds 6 compromised cards
a day fewer than logistic regression's, above the two-card line and
about one weekly standard deviation, suggestive rather than settled.

### A compromised terminal and a burst

The root split finds terminals at which more than 7.5% of the payments
in a 30-day window of already-labelled days were fraud. Among those, the tree splits on the
card's number of payments in the last 24 hours, at 13.5. The best leaf is
a payment at a terminal with recent fraud, on a card that has already
paid more than 13 times today: a thief using a card as fast as possible
before it is blocked, the card-history signal of <Ref
lesson="financial-crime-typology"
id="card-fraud-a-label-that-does-arrive">the typology</Ref>. No split
uses the amount; the simulator's amount rule was a script.

### How much of the fraud the leaves reach

| | fraud in the best leaves | precision there | fraud left in the largest leaf | its fraud share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| simulator, training | 349 of 598 (58%) | 95% or more | 236 (39%) | 0.35% |
| real, training | 256 of 6,605 (4%) | 64% | 4,740 (72%) | 0.17% |

On the real week nearly three frauds in four end in a leaf that holds
99.4% of the payments at a fraud share below the stream's 0.23%. This
measures the overlap that <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="the-real-stream">the rebalancing lesson</Ref> could only infer, as a
sign, from AP's lower multiple on real data. The tie
convention turns the four training leaves into AUC 0.64 and AP 0.063.
On the test week the same tree scores AUC 0.626, close to its training
value, and AP 0.024. The AUC barely moves: it is low because most frauds
are tied at the bottom with everything else, in training and in testing
alike, not because the tree overfits. The AP falls by more than half, so
the precision of the top leaves does not carry over to the test week in
full, part of the drop being the lower base rate after known-card
removal.

## Accounting for every reversal

The question from the start was whether each reversal can be pinned on
something checkable. Here is each one with its cause, and how firmly
the cause is held: derived from a result of this module, measured on
the data, or only a mechanism that fits the numbers.

| reversal | cause | how firmly |
|---|---|---|
| simulator: unpruned tree above depth-2 tree on AUC, below on AP, tied on $CP@100$ | two scores; AUC credits the top block's corner, AP charges the tied 42% at the base rate; the budget sees neither | derived: <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential" id="what-auc-sees-in-the-fully-grown-tree">two scores, one corner</Ref> |
| simulator: logistic regression, forest and XGBoost each lead one metric | gaps under two cards a day | derived: <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="what-each-disagreement-means">the noise line</Ref> |
| simulator: training week ranks the three memorisers first | they fit the labels they were trained on | measured: training table |
| real: every multiple of the floor falls | the inputs separate real fraud less; 4% of frauds in the best leaf against 58% | measured: the two depth-2 trees |
| real: unpruned tree and forest drop most, XGBoost no more than logistic regression | the two worst-hit models rank first payments in small leaves cut around a few training frauds; depth-6 leaves hold many payments | hypothesis |
| real: unpruned tree near chance | its top block is at most 1.3% fraud | derived: the two-score bounds |
| real: forest below the depth-2 tree on AP | its precision is lower at the top of its list (about 5% against 9%) | measured: the PR curves |
| | why its top is diluted: the same leaf-size mechanism | hypothesis |
| | its $CP@100$ edge over the depth-2 tree is inside the weekly spread | derived: <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited" id="six-ensembles-two-streams">weekly spread</Ref> |
| real: XGBoost first on AUC, third on $CP@100$ | at most 0.001 of its AUC lead lies in the reachable strip | derived: <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="how-much-of-the-curve-a-hundred-alerts-can-reach">the reachable region</Ref> |
| | where the rest of the lead comes from: boosting lifting low-scored frauds | hypothesis |

### So which model should the bank ship?

The obvious answer is logistic regression: it leads $CP@100$, the
deciding metric, on both streams. The benchmark cannot support even
that. Its gaps at the top come from one week and sit within or near the
noise on both streams, and every model ran at default settings. Tuned
on prequential folds and scored over four weeks, a depth-6 tree reaches
$CP@100$ 0.232 on the same real stream (<Ref
lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited" id="the-real-stream">the real
stream</Ref>) and XGBoost 0.235 against its 0.090 here (<Ref
lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="six-ensembles-two-streams">the ensembles on two streams</Ref>):
tuning and a longer test period together move a model's number further
than any gap in this table. And the models do not cost the same: on the simulator the depth-2
tree trains in 0.08 seconds, logistic regression in 1.6, the forest in
2.1 and XGBoost in 7.0; on the real stream the forest and XGBoost took
about a hundred times as long as logistic regression on a 20-core
server.

What the benchmark delivers is the referee: a split that reproduces
deployment, three metrics with their floors, and a way to tell a metric
artefact from a difference in what the models learned. The champion is
chosen with the referee, by $CP@100$ on prequential folds over tuned
candidates, which is the problem of <Ref
lesson="gbm-model-selection">model selection for boosted trees</Ref>.

## Where the benchmark departs from deployment

Every number above carries the simplifications of the setup that
produced it, and each is a problem a later part of the course takes up.

<Figure
  src="./figures/departures.svg"
  alt="Checklist table, what each benchmark puts in front of the models, with columns simulator week and real week. Rarity: 0.66%, three times real (amber) and 0.2% (green). Class overlap: weak, scripted (amber) and present (green). Label delay: fixed 7 days on both (amber). Drift: not tested on both (grey). Adversary: absent and not tested (grey). Alert feedback: absent and not modelled (grey). Entity history: 12 aggregates on both (amber). Scoring speed: training time only on both (grey). Re-runnable: yes (green) and no (grey)"
  caption="What each half of the benchmark keeps from deployment, simplifies or leaves out."
/>

- **The delay is one fixed week.** Real labels trickle in over days and
  weeks; the split treats every label as arriving exactly seven days
  later, the simplification <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
  id="what-the-delay-block-is-and-is-not">the delayed-labels lesson</Ref>
  flagged.
- **The labels are used as if unselected.** On a real card stream part of
  the labels comes through investigators calling the cards an earlier model
  had alerted, <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
  id="the-fast-labels-are-a-sample-the-model-chose">a sample the model
  chose</Ref>; the benchmark trains on them as a plain sample. The
  simulator has no investigators at all, and its labels are complete and
  instant.
- **Nothing changes and nobody adapts.** One training week and one test
  week cannot show drift, and the simulator's three scripts never react
  to what is caught.
- **One week, one run, defaults.** No spread across weeks, no tuning,
  fifteen inputs.
- **The real half cannot be re-run.** The payments are confidential; its
  numbers can be read and checked against each other, as the bounds
  above did, but not reproduced.

<Conclusion>
You can now read a benchmark table as a set of measurements rather than
a league: put each number against its floor, locate where on the list
each metric reads, invert a two-score model's AUC and AP into the
precision of its top block, read a shallow tree's leaves for how much of
the fraud the inputs isolate, and attribute every reversal to a metric,
to noise, or to the stream, saying which causes are measured and which
are hypotheses. On the simulator half the fraud sits in two leaves; on
real payments the best leaf holds 4% of it, and the models whose top
scores are pure leaves or averages of them, the unpruned tree and the
forest, lose most, while XGBoost, as perfect on the simulator's training
week, loses no more than logistic regression; that leaf size separates
them is a hypothesis.

The evaluation code behind all of it, the delay-aware split with
known-card removal, average precision, card precision with detected-card
removal, savings and the probability correction, is what <Ref
lesson="cost-aware-evaluation-lab">the evaluation lab</Ref> builds and
uses to settle a choice of tree depth on the simulator.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Express each real-stream model's $CP@100$ as a multiple of the random
0.0025, and each simulator model's as a multiple of the simulator's
random 0.017 (simulator $CP@100$: logistic regression 0.291, depth-2 tree
0.241, unpruned tree 0.243, forest 0.287, XGBoost 0.273). (a) Rank the
real models by their AP multiple and by their $CP@100$ multiple; which
pairs swap? (b) AP multiples fall from the simulator to the real stream;
what do $CP@100$ multiples do, and why?

<Solution>
**Real multiples.** $CP@100$: logistic regression $0.150/0.0025 = 60$,
forest 42, XGBoost 36, depth-2 tree 31, unpruned tree 7. AP (floor
0.002): logistic regression and XGBoost 17.5, depth-2 tree 12, forest
7.5, unpruned tree 1.5.

**(a)** By AP: logistic regression = XGBoost, depth-2 tree, forest,
unpruned tree. By $CP@100$: logistic regression, forest, XGBoost, depth-2
tree, unpruned tree. Two pairs swap: the forest and the depth-2 tree, and
the forest and XGBoost; logistic regression and XGBoost come apart from
a tie.

**(b)** Simulator multiples: logistic regression $0.291/0.017 \approx
17$, forest 17, XGBoost 16, unpruned and depth-2 trees 14. The real
multiples are higher, 7 to 60 against 14 to 17, the opposite of AP. The
random $CP@100$ is the share of a day's cards that are compromised:
about 1 in 60 on the simulator, 1 in 400 on the real stream. And the
simulator's ceiling is low: with about 50 findable cards a day, no
model can reach more than about 0.5, so its multiple is capped near 30;
the
real stream has more compromised cards than slots, ceiling 1. A multiple
of the floor measures distance from chance on one stream; across streams
it mixes the model with the floor and the ceiling.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break the claim: "On the real stream XGBoost is the best of the five
models, because its AUC exceeds every other model's by at least 0.11."
Give the argument that limits how much of an AUC difference the team can
see, compute the limit, and name the step in the claim that fails.

<Solution>
**The limit.** The team calls 100 cards a day among about 400,000
payments. Even counting every payment of those cards as an alert, the
false-positive rate stays below 0.1%. The ROC curve lies under height 1,
so the area over $\mathrm{FPR} \in [0, 0.001]$ is at most $0.001$ for any
model. The difference between two models' areas over that strip is
therefore at most 0.001. XGBoost's lead over logistic regression is
0.111; at least 0.110 of it lies at false-positive rates no budget of a
hundred cards reaches.

**What the reachable strip shows.** $CP@100$ measures exactly the strip,
in cards: logistic regression 0.150, XGBoost 0.090, six cards a day in
logistic regression's favour; AP ties them at 0.035.

**The failing step.** "Higher AUC, so better" assumes AUC weighs the
operating points by how much they matter. It weighs every
false-positive rate equally, and 99.9% of that axis is out of the team's
reach. On these numbers XGBoost is not shown to be the best at the
budget; on one week, it is not shown to be the worst of the three either,
since six cards a day is about one weekly standard deviation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take a simulator with only the first scenario: every payment over 220 is
fraud and every other payment genuine, about five such frauds a day,
each on a different card. (a) What does the unpruned tree learn, and
what test AUC and AP should it reach? (b) What is the highest $CP@100$
any model can reach on this stream, and what is its normalised card
precision? (c) The unpruned tree fell to chance on real payments. Which
property of the real stream did that collapse need, and why is it absent
here?

<Solution>
**(a)** One split, amount above 220 (from the data, at the midpoint
between the largest genuine and smallest fraud amount in training),
already gives pure leaves, so the unpruned tree stops there or adds
splits that change nothing. Every test fraud lands in the fraud leaf and
no genuine payment does, except one between the learned threshold and
220, if any. AUC and AP are 1 or within a hair of it.

**(b)** With about five compromised cards a day and 100 slots, a perfect
ranking finds all five: $CP@100 \le 5/100 = 0.05$. The normalised card
precision divides by the ceiling $\min(1, \gamma_t/k) = 0.05$ and is 1.
A perfect model with a $CP@100$ of 0.05 is a stream property, not a
model property.

**(c)** Overlap. A pure leaf transfers to new data when the region it
was cut around stays pure. Here the fraud region is a half-line in one
input and holds no genuine payment in any week, so memorising it costs
nothing. On real payments the regions cut around training frauds are
full of genuine payments in the next week, which is what the bound
$P_1 \le 0.013$ measured. Memorisation is harmless exactly when the
classes do not overlap, and the simulator's first scenario is the
extreme of that.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Estimate the real depth-2 tree's $CP@100$ from its leaves, and say what
would count as agreement with the measured 0.077. (a) A tied block holds
payments of which a share $s$ are fraud. Every compromised card in it
contributes $c$ fraud payments and no genuine one; every genuine card
contributes one genuine payment. Show that the share of the block's
cards that are compromised is
$$
q = \frac{s/c}{s/c + 1 - s} .
$$
(b) Apply it to leaf 3 ($s = 0.144$) with the stream's $c = 3$, and to
leaf 4 ($s = 0.645$). (c) Leaf 4 holds about 57 payments a day, all on
cards that have already paid more than 13 times that day. What does that
say about how many of the 100 slots it fills, and which leaf fills the
rest? (d) Bound the expected $CP@100$ and compare. List the assumptions.

<Solution>
**(a)** Take a block of $n$ payments: $sn$ fraud payments on $sn/c$
compromised cards, $(1-s)n$ genuine payments on as many genuine cards.
The compromised share of cards is $\frac{sn/c}{sn/c + (1-s)n}$, and $n$
cancels.

**(b)** Leaf 3: $q = 0.048/(0.048 + 0.856) \approx 0.053$. Leaf 4:
$q = 0.215/(0.215 + 0.355) \approx 0.38$.

**(c)** A card enters leaf 4 only from its 14th payment of the day, and
every later payment of that day joins it, so 57 payments are a handful
of cards, each contributing several. Leaf 4 fills a few slots; leaf 3,
about 1,000 payments a day, fills nearly all the rest as one tied block.

**(d)** If leaf 4 supplies up to about 10 cards at 38% and leaf 3 the
remaining 90 at 5.3%, the expected count is about
$10 \times 0.38 + 90 \times 0.053 \approx 8.6$ cards, $CP@100 \approx
0.09$; with fewer leaf-4 cards it falls towards 0.05. The measured 0.077
lies inside 0.05 to 0.09, which counts as agreement. A value of 0.2 or
0.02 would not: it would mean the training-week leaf shares did not
describe the test week. **Assumptions**: the training week's leaf shares
hold in the test week (the test week's base rate is lower after removal,
which pushes the estimate down); compromised cards carry three frauds
and no genuine payment in a leaf; genuine cards one payment each;
detected-card removal ignored.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Price the simulator benchmark in money (<Ref lesson="example-dependent-cost"

rule</Ref>). On the test week the fraud amounts total €33,301. Re-running
the split and flagging each payment when $C_a \le \mathrm{Amt} \cdot
\text{score}$ with $C_a = €5$ gives:

| model | flags | frauds caught | cost | savings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| logistic regression | 787 | 235 | €10,754 | 67.7% |
| depth-2 tree | 224 | 187 | €12,117 | 63.6% |

(a) The unpruned tree has the lowest AP of the four and the second-best
savings. Explain. (b) Its scores of 1 are not probabilities: its top
block is 53% fraud. Would calibrating them change its decisions much? (c)
Should savings pick between the unpruned tree and logistic regression?

<Solution>
**(a)** Savings price one cut, AP the whole order. The tree's top block,

missed amounts, which every model also misses in part.

**(b)** With the calibrated 0.53 the rule flags a top-block payment when

other payments score 0 either way.

frauds (about €86 each); one large fraud more or less in the test week

rule, so the choice is made by $CP@100$ on prequential folds, with
savings reported beside it for the chosen model, as the cost lesson
concluded.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
A team selects among the five models by $CP@100$ on the training week,
where the unpruned tree and the forest score 0.576, XGBoost 0.574,
logistic regression 0.419 and the depth-2 tree 0.394. Diagnose the
procedure, and say what it would have cost on the simulator's test week
had it picked the unpruned tree.

<Solution>
**Diagnosis.** A model with pure leaves scores every training fraud 1:
its training ranking is its memory of the labels, not a prediction. The
training week cannot choose (<Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="neither-the-training-week-nor-the-test-week-can-choose">the
delayed-labels lesson</Ref>); the ranking it produces puts the three
memorisers first by construction.

**Cost.** The unpruned tree's test $CP@100$ is 0.243 against logistic
regression's 0.291: about five compromised cards a day fewer, well beyond
the two-card line below which another week could reverse a gap, and its AP is half of
logistic regression's (0.309 against 0.606). On the real stream the
unpruned tree and the forest are again both perfect on the training
week, so the procedure faces the same tie and nothing in it breaks the
tie: it picks a model with $CP@100$ 0.017 or one with 0.104, against
0.150 for logistic regression, about 13 or about 5 compromised cards a
day lost. A procedure whose outcome turns on how a tie is broken is not
choosing.

**Fix.** Select on a validation week placed where the test week stands,
or on prequential folds.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

On the simulator the five models trained in 1.57 s (logistic

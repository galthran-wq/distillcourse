On the public two-day credit-card set, a k-means cluster picked for its fraud share is 71% fraud on the test split, against a base rate of 0.2%. The rule built around it, which alerts every payment in the clusters whose training rows were mostly fraud, still floods the queue. What do isolation, reconstruction and clustering scores actually deliver on real card data, judged by the metrics investigators use?

<Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"
id="the-ulb-card-set-two-days-with-the-card-removed">The ULB card set</Ref>
holds two days of September 2013: 284,807 online card payments of European
cardholders, 492 of them fraud, each described by 28 anonymous principal
components `V1` to `V28`, the seconds since the file's first payment and the
amount. There is no card column, and nothing in it was built from labels.
The module has scored it twice already, both times on the whole file: <Ref
lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
id="on-real-card-data-fraud-comes-in-clumps">a fraud's ten nearest rows are
59% fraud</Ref>, so the frauds sit in small clumps, and <Ref
lesson="isolation-forest"
id="when-the-frauds-come-in-small-clumps-a-small-sample-buys-nothing">isolation
forests built on all of it</Ref> ranked the frauds at an AUC of 0.95 and an
average precision of 0.20 to 0.28. Those forests scored the rows they were
built from, were read by average precision alone, and had no model trained
on labels beside them to say how much they left on the table. The
autoencoder and the small-cluster results came from the simulator and from
PaySim, whose frauds follow scripts.

What none of those runs did is the test a deployed scorer faces: built on
one day, judged on the next, read at the investigators' budget of a hundred
calls, and set beside a model trained on the same day's labels.

The same file is where most people first try unsupervised fraud detection,
because it is a standard coursework exercise: cluster the payments with
k-means and say which clusters are fraud; train an autoencoder and report
its F1 score. One submitted run of the clustering exercise produced the 71%
cluster. Read at face value it is a detector that never saw a label and
finds fraud at about 400 times the base rate, and the conclusion it invites
is that clustering finds fraud on real data.

## The coursework pipeline, run as written

The 71% and the flood are outputs of one short pipeline, and neither can be
read without its steps.

### Eight steps from a split to an alert

The file is split at random: 70% of the payments for training (199,364)
and 30% for testing (85,443, of which 154 were fraud in the run). The
training split is then resampled, because a clustering of a set that is
0.2% fraud is not expected to give the frauds a cluster of their own. The
run did it two ways.

**SMOTE** is <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited"
id="synthetic-frauds-smote">the synthetic oversampler</Ref>: each new fraud
lies on the segment between a real fraud and one of its five nearest fraud
neighbours. The version used makes two synthetic frauds per real one and
then keeps twice as many genuine payments as it made synthetic frauds. The
run's 338 training frauds became 1,014 and its 199,026 genuine payments were
cut to 1,352: a set of 2,366 rows, 43% fraud.

**ROSE** draws a whole new training set of the same size as the split, half
fraud and half genuine. Each row is a randomly chosen real row of the drawn
class plus Gaussian noise, with a width per column proportional to that
column's spread within the class. Every row it returns is synthetic, in both
classes.

Each resampled set then has `Time` and `Class` dropped and is standardised
with its own mean and standard deviation. K-means is run for 1 to 20
clusters, the within-cluster sum of squares is plotted, and the number of
clusters $K$ is read off the bend of the curve, the *elbow*: 3 for SMOTE, 4
for ROSE, each fitted from 25 random starts. The clusters are named by the
classes of their training rows: a cluster made up mostly of frauds is a
fraud cluster.

The test split is standardised with its own mean and standard deviation.
Each test payment is assigned to the nearest cluster centre by squared
distance, and every payment in a fraud cluster is alerted.

### What the run reported

The run printed, for each cluster, how many test payments landed in it and
how many of those were fraud.

| resampler | cluster | named | genuine | fraud | fraud share |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SMOTE | A | fraud | 24,730 | 28 | 0.1% |
| SMOTE | B | fraud | 47 | 119 | 71.7% |
| SMOTE | C | genuine | 60,512 | 7 | 0.01% |
| ROSE | 1 | fraud | 193 | 10 | 4.9% |
| ROSE | 2 | fraud | 57 | 112 | 66.3% |
| ROSE | 3 | genuine | 43,334 | 5 | 0.01% |
| ROSE | 4 | fraud | 41,705 | 27 | 0.06% |

A cluster's fraud share is the precision of a rule that alerts every
payment in it. Whether such a rule is usable also depends on how many
payments the cluster holds, and the share does not say.

<Example title="the SMOTE run read as an alert rule">
Alerting cluster B alone gives $47 + 119 = 166$ alerts, of which 119 are
fraud: precision $119/166 = 0.72$, recall $119/154 = 0.77$.

The run's rule alerts both fraud clusters, A and B:
$24{,}730 + 28 + 47 + 119 = 24{,}924$ alerts holding $28 + 119 = 147$
frauds. Precision is $147/24{,}924 = 0.0059$ and recall $147/154 = 0.95$.

To compare with a team's hundred calls a day, convert to days of the full
file. The test split is a random three tenths of two days, so a count on it
stands for $\text{count}/(0.3 \times 2)$ a day of the full file, which
carries about 142,000 payments a day. Cluster B alone becomes
$166/0.6 \approx 280$ alerts a day. The rule becomes
$24{,}924/0.6 \approx 41{,}500$ alerts a day, 29% of all payments, against
100 calls.
</Example>

### The run is not a fluke

Rerunning the pipeline on five other random splits gives the same two
results. With SMOTE and the test split standardised by its own statistics,
the purest cluster is 68% to 80% fraud and holds 122 to 163 test payments;
the rule that alerts every fraud cluster flags between 16,000 and 25,000
test payments in four of the five splits, and 237 in the fifth.

## Where the 71% comes from: the resampler writes the labels into the clusters

K-means never sees a label, so one would expect its clusters to be
indifferent to fraud on a set that is 0.18% fraud. The clusters of the run
are not indifferent, because the labels went in before the clustering
started.

### Without resampling, no cluster is mostly fraud

Run the same pipeline with step 2 left out, so that k-means sees the
training split as it is. With $K = 3$ the purest test cluster holds 6,138
payments, 37 of them fraud: 0.6%, three and a half times the base rate.
Across the five splits the purest cluster stays between 0.3% and 0.7%. A
rule that alerts it sends about 10,000 payments a day to the team, and it
is a flood without the resampler.

### The elbow belongs to the resampler

On the training split as it is, each added cluster removes about 3% of the
within-cluster sum of squares, whatever the number already fitted: no
number of clusters is special, and the curve has no elbow. With SMOTE the
first split removes 30%, and it cuts off a group of 334 rows that are all
fraud. The curves come from one of the five reruns, whose training split
holds 349 frauds, so SMOTE gives it 1,047 fraud rows; the group cut off is a
third of them. The curve bends where the separate
fraud groups have each received a centre. The elbow the run read off was a
property of the resampled set, in which frauds are 43% of the rows and
k-means has to spend centres on them.

### A classifier trained on labels

After resampling, the centres sit where the labels put the mass. Naming the
clusters reads the labels a second time, and assigning a test payment to
the nearest centre and reading off the centre's name is then a
nearest-centroid classifier fitted on labelled data. Among <Ref
lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="where-normal-comes-from-three-label-regimes">the label regimes</Ref>
this is the supervised one. The 71% measures how well labelled centres
separate frauds, not how unlike normal the frauds are.

<KeyFact>
Resampling is a use of the labels. Any score fitted after it belongs among
the supervised rows of a comparison, beside the models trained on the same
labels; so does any score fitted after the training sample was cleaned by
label, which is where the comparison further down puts the autoencoder
trained on genuine payments.
</KeyFact>

## Where the flood comes from: test payments placed in other coordinates

If the clusters are a classifier with 72% precision in cluster B, why does
the rule alert 29% of the test split? Step 6 is the one step that treats the
test payments differently from the training rows. The centres were fitted
in coordinates set by the resampled set's mean and standard deviation; the
test split is standardised with its own. Each of the two statistics moves
the test payments in its own way, and the two can be undone one at a time.

### The mean: the genuine cloud is moved toward the frauds

Standardising subtracts a mean, so whatever point that mean is becomes the
origin. The resampled set is 43% fraud, and its mean lies between its
genuine rows and its frauds. In the coordinates the centres live in, the
origin is therefore that mixed point, and the genuine payments sit off it,
on the side away from the frauds. The figure below takes another of the
reruns, the split whose test part holds 139 frauds. There, on `V14` and
`V17` the genuine centre G sits at $(0.6, 0.5)$ training standard
deviations and the fraud centre F2 at $(-1.0, -0.6)$.

The test split is 99.8% genuine, so its mean is where its genuine payments
are: at $(0.68, 0.52)$ on the same two columns, next to G. Centring the test
split on its own mean moves that point to the origin, and with it the whole
genuine cloud, by about 0.7 and 0.5 training standard deviations toward F2.
Across all 29 columns the cloud's centre goes from 0.2 away from G to 2.0
from G and 2.3 from F2: from the middle of the genuine cluster to nearly
the boundary between the two.

### The spread: the cloud is stretched around its new centre

On the columns where frauds differ from genuine payments, the resampled
set's spread is dominated by the gap between its two classes, and it is
large. The test split's spread on the same columns is that of genuine
payments alone: its standard deviation is 0.218 times the training set's
on `V14` and 0.156 times on `V17`. Dividing by it multiplies the test
values, in training units, by $1/0.218 = 4.6$ on `V14` and
$1/0.156 = 6.4$ on `V17`. The re-centred cloud, already close to the
boundary, is stretched across it.

### Placing the test split with the training ruler

The repair is the rule <Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="standardise-on-the-training-block-compress-a-long-tail-first">the
supervised network followed</Ref>: the scaler is fitted on the training
block and applied unchanged to every later payment. With that one change,
on the same five splits, the SMOTE rule alerts between 81 and 156 test
payments, 75% to 82% of them fraud, and catches between 43% and 83% of the
test frauds. ROSE gives the same picture, 129 to 152 alerts at about 80%.

<KeyFact>
A test payment has to be placed in the coordinates its model was fitted
in. Standardising the test split by its own statistics cost the run about
25,000 false alerts on 85,443 payments.
</KeyFact>

Repaired, the rule is a supervised classifier with about 80% precision on
135 to 260 alerts a day of the full file. Inside its alert set every
payment is equal, so a team with 100 calls takes any 100 of them, and
nothing in the rule says which. It is also still a model trained on labels.

## Scores at the budget: trained on day 1, judged on day 2

Setting the label-free scores beside models trained on labels needs one
test that is honest for all of them, and on this file the split is the hard
part. The coursework split at random, which mixes later payments into the
training set and puts payments of one card on both sides, so a model is
tested on frauds whose siblings on the same card it was trained on: <Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="a-shuffled-split-tests-a-model-that-cannot-exist">a test of a model that
cannot exist</Ref>. Keeping each card on one side would remove the second
leak, but the file has no card column to do it with. A cut in time removes
the first leak and needs no card: every model is built on what came before
and scores what comes after, as a deployed one does.

### The split, the inputs, the metrics

Day 1 is $\text{Time} < 86{,}400$ seconds, 144,786 payments with 281 frauds (0.19%),
and day 2 is the rest, 140,021 payments with 211 frauds (0.15%). There is no
delay block, because the file records no label dates: the models train on
day 1's final labels, which makes every supervised number slightly
optimistic.

The inputs are `V1` to `V28` and $\log(1 + \text{Amount})$, the logarithm
because amounts have a long tail, all standardised with day 1's mean and
standard deviation. `Time` is dropped, since every value on day 2 lies
beyond day 1's range.

The metrics are ROC-AUC, average precision (AP), and the precision of the
day's 100 highest-scored payments, <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="precision-at-k-the-metric-cut-where-the-team-cuts">P@100</Ref>. Payments,
not cards, because there are no cards. Day 2 has 211 frauds, more than 100,
so a perfect ranking reaches 1. The coursework's own metric, F1 at a
threshold, scores <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="a-threshold-metric-judges-a-cut-nobody-chose">one cut nobody
chose</Ref>.

### The scorers

- **distance to the day-1 mean**: the length of a payment's vector of
  standardised inputs, with no model. Below, "distance" always means this
  length, in day-1 standard deviations; its square, the sum of the squared
  inputs, ranks the payments in the same order;
- **isolation forest**: 100 trees on samples of $\psi = 256$ (five seeds)
  and of $\psi = 4{,}096$ (three seeds);
- **autoencoder**: 29-100-20-100-29 with ReLU units, trained <Ref
  lesson="one-class-and-reconstruction"
  id="training-it-the-supervised-pipeline-with-the-label-replaced-by-the-input">exactly
  as in the last lesson</Ref>, early stopping on a held-out tenth of the
  training payments and the last epoch's weights kept, either on all of
  day 1 or on day 1's genuine payments only; three seeds each;
- **k-means**: $K = 8$ centres fitted on day 1 without resampling, three
  seeds, read two ways from <Ref lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
  id="clustering-comparing-with-a-few-centroids-instead-of-every-neighbour">the
  clustering assumptions</Ref>: distance to the nearest centre, and
  smallest cluster first, and inside a cluster the payment farther from its
  centre first.
  $K = 8$ is the setting <Ref lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
  id="does-the-small-cluster-score-find-organised-fraud">the small-cluster
  score was run with on PaySim</Ref>, fixed before day 2 was looked at;
  the elbow cannot supply one, since without resampling the curve has no
  bend;
- **supervised**: a logistic regression at library defaults and a random
  forest of 100 trees (three seeds), trained on day 1's labels.

Where a scorer was run with several seeds, each seed's run is scored
separately and the table gives the mean of their metrics.

## The small cluster: an alert set that fits the budget, at the right K

Without resampling, $K = 3$ gave no fraud cluster at all. At $K = 8$ the
smallest cluster is almost all fraud. What is in it, and why did it not
appear before?

### What sits in it

The smallest of the eight clusters holds 185 day-1 payments, 145 of them
fraud. On day 2, 67 to 70 payments are assigned to it, depending on the
seed, and every one of them is fraud: a third of the day's 211. Its centre
lies at a distance of 31 from the day-1 mean (squared, about 950), where the
median genuine payment of day 2 lies at 4.8 (squared, 23): these are frauds
that are extreme together, in the same directions. Being the smallest
cluster, it heads the ranking; the next 30 places go to the second-smallest cluster, a few thousand payments
among which fraud is rare.

This is the small-cluster score of <Ref lesson="density-and-distance-scores"
id="does-the-small-cluster-score-find-organised-fraud">the distance
lesson</Ref>, which turned the repetition of organised fraud into signal on
PaySim, here finding a group of frauds that repeat one pattern.

### A window of K that no label-free criterion finds

Refitted at every $K$ from 2 to 40, three seeds each, the small-cluster
score works only inside a window. From $K = 5$ to $K = 18$ the fraud
group is the smallest cluster and the score puts 62 to 70 frauds in the top 100. From $K = 24$ on, some honest
group of a few dozen or a few hundred payments gets its own cluster, smaller
still, and is ranked first: the top 100 holds 3 to 15 frauds on average.
Below $K = 5$ the outcome depends on the random start. The value 8,
carried over from PaySim, happened to fall inside the window; nothing on
this file short of labels would have warned against 32, since the elbow
curve without resampling has no elbow. Choosing $K$ needs labelled days,
and it becomes a setting chosen on labelled folds, like the forest's
sample size.

### Same centres, opposite verdicts

The same eight centres read as distances give the worst score in the table,
8 frauds in the top 100, fewer than the plain distance from the mean finds.
The fraud group has a centre of its own. Its day-2 members lie at a
median distance of 43 from the day-1 mean (squared, about 1,860), farther
out than the day-1 centre at 31: they are not scattered around that centre
but beyond it, a median 40 along its direction from the mean and 12
across. That is far enough for the distance from the mean to rank 34 of
them in its top 100. From their own centre they lie at a median 17, and a
place in the top 100 by distance to the nearest centre takes 38 to 39.
Genuine payments that are unusual in directions where no centre went keep
their full distance and take the places. This is <Ref
lesson="anomaly-detection-framing" id="three-planar-experiments">panel (c)
of the framing lesson</Ref> on real data: a group of anomalies that earns a
centre looks normal to a near-centroid score.

<KeyFact>
The same eight centres put 8 frauds in the day's top 100 when read as
distances and 69 when read as cluster sizes. Which reading works depends on
whether the frauds form their own group, and on this file only labels can
say that they do.
</KeyFact>

The histogram is drawn in squared distance, which spreads out the far
tail. The frauds' median is 271, twelve times the genuine payments' 23
(as distances, 16.5 and 4.8). 98% of the frauds lie above the genuine
median and 88% above the genuine 95th percentile. The ROC-AUC is the probability that a random
fraud outscores a random genuine payment, so it comes out at 0.96.

The team reads only the top 100 of 140,021 payments, beyond the 99.93rd
percentile, where the budget reaches <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="how-much-of-the-curve-a-hundred-alerts-can-reach">a false-positive rate
of at most $100/139{,}810 \approx 0.0007$</Ref>: a squared distance
beyond about 1,860, a distance of 43. There 66 genuine payments
take places from the frauds, and all 34 frauds that do reach the top are
members of the far fraud group. Only 18% of the frauds lie above the genuine
99.9th percentile; for the logistic regression the share is 70%. Measured
the other way, 1,039 genuine payments score above the median fraud by
distance from the mean, and 41 by the logistic regression.

The reason is in what the distance adds up. The components are directions
of a cloud of almost entirely genuine payments, and the squared distance
adds the squared departures along all 29 inputs with equal weight. A genuine payment
far out along any one of them scores as high as a fraud. Fraud departs along
particular directions: the logistic regression learns from labels which ones
and how much each counts, and an unlabelled score has no way to. This is
<Ref lesson="anomaly-detection-framing"
id="outside-normal-is-not-toward-fraud">"outside normal is not toward
fraud"</Ref>, measured on real payments.

## What the forward split confirms, and what the file cannot test

The module's results were first measured in three kinds of place: on the
simulator and PaySim, whose frauds follow scripts; on planar toys; and, for
the forest's sample size, on the whole ULB file scored in place. Day 2 is
the first forward test for all of them:

| claim from this module | lesson | first measured on | on day 2 of the ULB file |

| an autoencoder fitted to a sample with fraud learns that fraud | <Ref lesson="one-class-and-reconstruction" id="three-readings-of-the-table">reconstruction</Ref> | simulator | held, more weakly: AUC 0.86 (0.75 to 0.92 across seeds) against the no-model distance's 0.96, AP level with it |

columns, and that is all the file can test.

**No card.** There is no card precision, no card-history feature of <Ref
lesson="transaction-aggregates">module 3</Ref>, no removal of cards already
known. A random split also leaks one card's frauds across it: the logistic
regression scores an average precision of 0.74 on a random 70/30 split and
0.64 on the day split, the random forest 0.82 and 0.77. The two test sets
differ, so the gap is the leak plus a different mix of days, and without
cards the file cannot separate the two.

**Two days.** One test day of 211 frauds means one draw: at a true
precision of 0.7, the top 100 of a single day has a standard deviation of
about $\sqrt{0.7 \times 0.3/100} \approx 0.05$, so the table separates
0.34 from 0.81 but not 0.34 from 0.38. There is no room for a delay block,
no drift, and no retraining cadence to test.

**Anonymous columns.** No alert can be explained in words, and no feature
can be built from knowledge of payments.

**Final, undated labels.** No label clock and no feedback from alerts. What
the file tests is a score; a detection system is tested on the
simulator and the Belgian stream.

<Conclusion>
You can now take apart the coursework result. The 71% cluster exists
because SMOTE made frauds 43% of the training set, which turned k-means into
a classifier fitted on labels; the flood exists because the test split was
standardised with its own statistics: its own mean moved the genuine cloud
from the genuine centre to the resampled set's mixed mean, beside a fraud
centre, and its own spread stretched the cloud across. Repaired, the rule
is a supervised model at about 80% precision whose alerts cannot be ordered. You can run every scorer of
the module on a forward split of the real file and read it at the budget:
label-free distance and isolation scores reach the supervised AUC and a
tenth to a third of its average precision, because
genuine payments that are unusual in any direction fill the top 100; the
small-cluster score finds a pure fraud group that fits the budget, at
numbers of clusters only labels can choose; and an autoencoder fitted to
data containing fraud does no better than no model.

<Ref lesson="anomaly-scorers-lab">The lab</Ref> builds the isolation
forest and the autoencoder from their formulas and scores day 2 of this
file against the supervised ceiling measured here. After it, <Ref
lesson="fraud-as-a-graph">the next module</Ref> takes up what this file
removed first: the links between cards, accounts and merchants, where
organised fraud is visible as structure rather than as a far group of rows.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
The SMOTE run's rule has, on the test split, 147 true positives, 7 false
negatives, 24,777 false positives and 60,512 true negatives. Its software
printed a precision of 0.0059 and a recall of 0.955. A write-up of the run
defines recall as $TP/(TP + FP)$ and precision as $TP/(TP + FN)$.
(a) Compute precision, recall, F1 and the specificity $TN/(TN + FP)$ with
the correct definitions. (b) Which definitions did the software use? What
would a reader who trusts the written formulas conclude from the printed
"recall 0.955"? (c) How many alerts a day does the rule send on the full
file, and what share of the stream is that?

<Solution>
**(a)** Precision $147/(147 + 24{,}777) = 147/24{,}924 = 0.0059$. Recall
$147/(147 + 7) = 0.955$. F1 $= 2 \times 0.0059 \times 0.955/(0.0059 +
0.955) = 0.0117$. Specificity $60{,}512/(60{,}512 + 24{,}777) = 0.71$.

**(b)** The correct ones: the printed values match (a). The written
formulas are swapped, so a reader who trusts them reads "recall 0.955" as
$TP/(TP + FP)$, that is, as 95.5% of the alerts being fraud. The true
statement is the opposite: 0.6% of the alerts are fraud. A swapped
definition turns a flood into an apparently excellent detector.

**(c)** The test split is three tenths of two days, so
$24{,}924/0.6 \approx 41{,}500$ alerts a day of the full file, about 29% of
its 142,000 daily payments.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push the cluster rule to the ends of $K$, keeping SMOTE and the naming step
("a cluster made up mostly of frauds is a fraud cluster"). (a) With
$K = 1$, what is the one cluster's fraud share, how is it named, and what
does the rule alert? (b) With $K$ equal to the number of resampled training
rows, every row its own centre: what does the rule become, and what part do
the synthetic frauds play? (c) Why does no $K$ in between let the team cut
the alerts at exactly 100 a day?

<Solution>
**(a)** One cluster holds every resampled row, so its fraud share is the
set's, 43%. That is not mostly fraud, so it is named genuine and the rule
alerts nothing. (Had the rule been "fraud share above the set's", 43% is
not above 43%, and the answer would be the same.)

**(b)** Each test payment takes the class of its nearest resampled row: the
rule is a one-nearest-neighbour classifier trained on the resampled set.
Two thirds of its fraud rows are synthetic points on segments between real
frauds, so they fill in the space between frauds and widen the region in
which a test payment's nearest row is a fraud.

**(c)** At every $K$ the rule is a set of regions, each wholly alerted or
wholly not. Its alert count is the number of test payments that fall in
the fraud regions, set by the clustering, and every payment inside a region
is equal, so there is no order in which to stop at 100. Cutting at a budget
needs a score that differs inside a region: a distance to the centre, a
classifier's probability, or a cluster's size with ties broken by
distance.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
"Without labels, k-means finds no fraud on this file: at the elbow the
purest cluster is 0.6% fraud. Clustering is useless as a label-free fraud
detector." Refute the claim with the lesson's numbers, name the step of
the argument that fails, and say which part of it survives.

<Solution>
**The counterexample.** Fitted on day 1 without labels at $K = 8$, the
smallest cluster receives 67 to 70 day-2 payments, every one of them fraud,
and the small-cluster score puts 69 frauds in day 2's top 100, against 81
for a logistic regression trained on labels.

**The failing step.** "At the elbow": without resampling the elbow curve
has no elbow, so $K = 3$ was not chosen by the data. The claim also reads
clusters only by their fraud share, which needs labels, and ignores the
label-free reading by cluster size.

**What survives.** The success holds only for $K$ from 5 to 18. From
$K = 24$ on, a small honest group outranks the fraud cluster and the top
100 holds 3 to 15 frauds on average. Nothing without labels marks the
window, so the method is label-free in its scoring and needs labels to set
$K$. The same eight centres read as distances put only 8 frauds in the top
100.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
In one rerun with the training scaling, the repaired SMOTE rule alerts 133
of 85,443 test payments, 106 of them fraud, while the test split holds 139
frauds. Treat the rule as <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="two-scores-one-corner">a two-score model</Ref>: alerted payments score
1, the rest 0. (a) Compute $T$, $F$, $P_1$ and $\pi$, then the ROC-AUC
$\tfrac12(1 + T - F)$ and the average precision $T P_1 + (1 - T)\pi$.
(b) A logistic regression on another random split of the file reaches an
AUC of 0.97 and an average precision of 0.74. What does the comparison say
about the rule as a classifier, and what can it not do that the regression
can?

<Solution>
**(a)** $T = 106/139 = 0.763$. $F = (133 - 106)/(85{,}443 - 139) =
27/85{,}304 = 0.00032$. $P_1 = 106/133 = 0.797$. $\pi = 139/85{,}443 =
0.0016$. ROC-AUC $= \tfrac12(1 + 0.763 - 0.0003) = 0.88$. Average precision
$= 0.763 \times 0.797 + 0.237 \times 0.0016 = 0.608 + 0.0004 = 0.61$.

**(b)** On average precision the rule is a reasonable supervised classifier,
0.61 against 0.74; its AUC is lower because the 24% of frauds outside the
alerted block are tied with every genuine payment and reached at the base
rate. What it cannot do is order: its alerts form one block, so the team
cannot take the 100 most suspicious, and at more than 100 alerts a day it
cannot trade recall for precision at all. The regression's probability can
be cut anywhere.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The lab after this lesson asks for a ranking of day 2 that uses no labels
in what it scores and beats an average-precision bar, using an isolation
forest, an autoencoder trained on genuine day-1 rows, or a combination. The
second of these is fitted to a sample cleaned by label, so by the lesson's
own rule it sits among the supervised rows of the table. (a) From the table, which two scores are
the obvious candidates, and what are their average precisions? (b) Ranking

**(a)** The forest with $\psi = 4{,}096$ (0.19) and the autoencoder trained

<Exercise>
The SMOTE step of one rerun turns a training split of 199,364 payments, 349
of them fraud, into 1,047 fraud rows and 1,396 genuine rows. Suppose a
logistic regression were trained on that set instead of k-means. (a) Using
<Ref lesson="undersampling-calibration" id="reading-β-off-a-training-set">the
keep rate read off the class shares</Ref>,
$\beta = \pi(1 - \pi_s)/\big(\pi_s(1 - \pi)\big)$, compute $\beta$. (b)
Which probability $p'$ on the training split's base rate does an output
$p_s = 0.5$ correspond to? (c) Why is the correction only approximate
here, when it was exact for plain undersampling?

<Solution>
**(a)** $\pi = 349/199{,}364 = 0.00175$, $\pi_s = 1{,}047/2{,}443 =
0.4286$. Then
$\beta = 0.00175 \times 0.5714/(0.4286 \times 0.99825) = 0.00234$. It is
smaller than the genuine keep rate $1{,}396/199{,}015 = 0.0070$ by the
factor 3 by which the frauds were multiplied: the formula reads only the
two class shares, and both changes move them.

**(b)** $p' = \beta p_s/(\beta p_s - p_s + 1) = 0.00117/(0.00117 + 0.5)
= 0.0023$. A payment the model calls even odds is fraud about once in 430.

**(c)** The correction assumes that rows entered the training set by a coin
that looked only at the label. Two thirds of the fraud rows are new points
placed between real frauds, so the training set's fraud rows have a
different distribution in the inputs from the stream's frauds: <Ref
lesson="undersampling-calibration"
id="when-the-coin-looks-at-the-payment">the coin looks at the
payment</Ref>. The class shares, and with them $\beta$, are right; the
model's view of where frauds lie is not, and a reliability check on
unresampled payments is the only way to see how far off it is.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

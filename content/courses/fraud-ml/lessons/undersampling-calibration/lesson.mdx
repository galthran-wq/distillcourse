To train on a stream with one fraud in five hundred payments you throw away most of the genuine payments and keep all the frauds. The model then says 'probability of fraud 0.5' for a payment that, on the real stream, is fraud perhaps once in fifty times. Can the number it outputs be turned back into the real probability, and does doing so change which payments it ranks highest?

The cut is drastic. At a fraud rate of 0.4%, making a day's training chunk
half fraud discards about 99.6% of its genuine payments, and teams do it
routinely: training gets faster, and some learners only find the minority
class at all once it is less rare.

You already have the general answer to both questions. The lesson on
imbalance and leakage in the classical-ml course proved that a resampler
which keeps each class at its own rate, without looking at the features,
multiplies every payment's posterior odds by the ratio of the two keep
rates, $s_+/s_-$. Here every fraud is kept, $s_+ = 1$, and each genuine
payment is kept with probability $\beta$, so the factor is $1/\beta$ and
every log-odds rises by the same $\ln(1/\beta)$. Write $p = P(\text{fraud}
\mid x)$ for the probability on the stream and $p_s$ for what a model
fitted to the kept rows estimates. Then
$$
\frac{p_s}{1-p_s} = \frac{1}{\beta}\cdot\frac{p}{1-p},
\qquad
p_s = \frac{p}{p + \beta(1-p)},
\qquad
p' = \frac{\beta\,p_s}{\beta\,p_s - p_s + 1},
$$
where $p'$, the corrected probability, divides the odds back by $1/\beta$.
The map from $p_s$ to $p'$ is increasing, so the ranking, and every metric
computed from the ranking alone, stays put.

For the opening's numbers: one genuine payment in fifty kept is
$\beta = 0.02$; a payment with $p = 0.02$, odds about 1:49, reaches the
model with odds about 1:1 and $p_s \approx 0.505$. Read backwards, an
output of 0.5 corrects to $0.01/0.51 \approx 0.0196$, one in 51.

The proof takes β as given and assumes the rows were kept by a coin that
saw only the label.

## The keep rate on a card stream, and when there is one

A fraud team holding a trained model can be sure of neither. Its pipeline
usually logged a class share, "we balanced the classes" or "the training
set was 20% fraud", not a keep rate, and its rows may have been chosen in
ways no single β describes.

### Reading β off a training set

Take 100,000 payments at one fraud in 500: 200 frauds and 99,800 genuine
payments. With $\beta = 0.1$ about 9,980 genuine payments remain and the
training set is 2% fraud; with $\beta = 0.02$, the opening's cut, 1,996
remain and the set is 9.1% fraud, one row in eleven. A balanced set needs
as many genuine rows as frauds, $\beta = 200/99{,}800 \approx 0.002$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/bars.svg"
  alt="Four horizontal bars drawn as shares, for beta = 1, 0.1, 0.02 and 0.002. The amber fraud segment grows from a sliver to half the bar. Beside each bar: 100,000 rows, 0.2% fraud; 10,180 rows, 2.0% fraud; 2,196 rows, 9.1% fraud; about 400 rows, 50% fraud"
  caption="Lowering β shrinks only the genuine part, so the training set gets smaller and its fraud share rises."
/>

In general, out of $n$ payments with base rate $\pi$, the cut keeps $\pi n$
frauds and $\beta(1-\pi)n$ genuine payments, so the training set's base
rate is
$$
\pi_s = \frac{\pi}{\pi + \beta(1-\pi)},
\qquad\text{and solving for }\beta,\qquad
\beta = \frac{\pi\,(1 - \pi_s)}{\pi_s\,(1 - \pi)} .
$$
The second form turns the two reported shares, before and after the cut,
into β. For a chunk at 0.4% fraud made half fraud,
$\beta = 0.004/0.996 \approx 0.004$.

β is a keep rate, not a ratio of classes. Read as odds, the second form
divides the stream's ratio of frauds to genuine payments, $\pi/(1-\pi)$,
by the training set's, $\pi_s/(1-\pi_s)$. At the balanced cut the training
set's ratio is 1, so there β equals the stream's class ratio, 1:499 on a
one-in-500 stream. That is how the bug enters: every pipeline already logs
the stream's class ratio, it is right for the default balanced cut, and it
goes on being passed after someone moves the cut to 20% fraud, where it
corrects every probability by the wrong factor.

### When the coin looks at the payment

The proof needs each row kept or dropped by a coin whose bias depends on
the row's label and nothing else. Two common ways of assembling fraud
training sets break that.

- **Keeping only small genuine payments.** If the genuine rows kept are
  those under €50, the selection looks at the amount: a €30 and a €300
  genuine payment were treated differently, so no single number describes
  the distortion.
- **Training on alerted payments.** <Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system"
  id="the-fast-labels-are-a-sample-the-model-chose">The investigators' fast
  labels</Ref> exist only for the cards yesterday's model ranked highest.
  Whether a payment enters such a training set depends on its features
  through that model's score, so the keep rate varies with $x$.

The second case is the tempting one. A training set built from
investigated alerts is mostly genuine payments with a few frauds, looks
like an undersampled stream, and invites the same correction. There is no
β to undo; <Ref lesson="alert-feedback-selection-bias">alert feedback and
selection bias</Ref> takes up what can be done instead.

<KeyFact>
Whether a β exists is settled by reading the code that built the training
set, not by looking at the set: if anything besides the label and a random
draw decides whether a row is kept, there is none. The only test that
catches a wrong correction afterwards is a reliability check on payments
drawn from the stream with no selection at all, because a set built by the
same selection would share its distortion.
</KeyFact>

## How large the distortion is, and where

The odds factor is the same $1/\beta$ for every payment; the gap in
probability is not. An output near 1 has nowhere to go, and at
$\beta = 0.02$ a payment at $p = 0.0001$ moves only to 0.005, while the
opening's payment moved from 0.02 to 0.5. A fraud team needs to know where
along the scale the gap $p_s - p$ is largest.

### The size of the bias as a function of p

Write $p_s - p = p/(\beta + (1-\beta)p) - p$. Its derivative in $p$ is
$\beta/(\beta + (1-\beta)p)^2 - 1$, which is zero when $\beta +
(1-\beta)p = \sqrt{\beta}$, that is at
$$
p^* = \frac{\sqrt{\beta}}{1 + \sqrt{\beta}}, \qquad
p_s(p^*) = \frac{1}{1 + \sqrt{\beta}}, \qquad
\max_p\,(p_s - p) = \frac{1 - \sqrt{\beta}}{1 + \sqrt{\beta}} .
$$
At $\beta = 0.02$, $\sqrt{\beta} \approx 0.141$: the bias peaks at 0.75,
for payments whose real probability is about 0.12 and which the model
calls 0.88.

<Figure
  src="./figures/bias.svg"
  alt="Plot of the bias p_s minus p against p from 0 to 1, for three keep rates. Grey, beta = 0.5: a low hump, largest 0.17 at p = 0.41. Blue, beta = 0.11: largest 0.50 at p = 0.25. Amber, beta = 0.02: a tall hump rising steeply from zero, largest 0.75 at p = 0.12. All curves are zero at p = 0 and p = 1"
  caption="The harder the cut, the taller the hump and the further it sits toward small p."
/>

Payments with such intermediate posteriors sit where fraud and genuine
payments look alike enough that neither is certain: a task whose classes
overlap a lot has many of them, a well-separated task almost none.

### Two tasks with the same β

Take one feature $x$. Genuine payments have $x$ normal with mean 0 and
standard deviation 3; frauds, 10% of the data, have the same spread around
a mean μ. With $\mu = 3$ the classes overlap heavily; with $\mu = 15$ they
barely touch. Both posteriors are known exactly, and both can be cut at
$\beta = 0.5$ and at $\beta = 1/9$, which balances a set that is 10%
fraud.

<Task>
In the last frame, compare where the black and the amber curves cross
0.5 in each panel. Before reading on, predict which panel's crossing
moves further if β is lowered once more, and why the answer depends on
how far apart the two humps sit.
</Task>

For two normal classes with a common spread σ, the log-odds of fraud is a
straight line in $x$ with slope $\mu/\sigma^2$ (the last exercise but one
derives it). The cut adds $\ln(1/\beta)$ to that line, which slides the
point where it crosses zero, the 0.5 cut, by
$$
\Delta x = \frac{\sigma^2 \ln(1/\beta)}{\mu} .
$$
At $\beta = 1/9$ and $\sigma = 3$: $9 \times 2.20/3 = 6.6$ for $\mu = 3$,
and $9 \times 2.20/15 = 1.3$ for $\mu = 15$. The shift in log-odds is the
same in both tasks; the overlapping task's shallow line turns it into a
long slide along $x$. Counting the payments between the old and the new
crossing, the cut changes the 0.5 decision for 34% of all rows in the
overlapping task and for about half a percent in the separated one.

Card fraud is the overlapping kind. A stolen card used at a supermarket
looks like its owner, so many of the payments an investigator might open
have real probabilities of a few percent to a few tens of percent, under
the hump of the bias figure, where at $\beta = 0.02$ the raw output is off
by most of the probability scale.

Below the hump the gap in probability shrinks, but the gap in proportion
does not. The ratio $p_s/p = 1/(\beta + (1-\beta)p)$ climbs toward
$1/\beta$ as $p$ falls: about 7 at $p = 0.12$, 34 at $p = 0.01$, 46 at the
stream's base rate $p = 0.002$. The bulk of a card stream sits down there,
so any number that adds raw outputs over many payments, an expected fraud
count or loss, comes out inflated by a large multiple.

### What the distortion looks like on a simulated card stream

Simulate 500,000 training and 500,000 test payments at one fraud in 500,
with two standard normal features shifted by $(2, 2)$ for frauds, and fit a
logistic regression once on all rows and once at $\beta = 0.02$ (969
frauds among 11,165 kept rows). On the test payments the undersampled
model has ROC-AUC 0.980 and average precision 0.395 before and after
correction. Its raw outputs average 0.032, sixteen times the real fraud
rate. Their Brier score, the mean squared gap
between stated probability and label that the classical-ml course used to
grade calibration, is 0.0111: worse than the 0.0020 of a model that says
0.002 for every payment. After correction it is 0.0015, the same as the
model fitted on all rows.

The bands are cut by rank because rank is what the investigators work
down. In ranks 2,001 to 10,000 the raw model says 0.575 on average, better
than even, and 3.1% of those payments are fraud; the corrected value is
0.031. A staffing decision made with <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="the-value-of-the-next-call">the value of the next call</Ref>, which
keeps calling while the next card's probability exceeds $C_a/V$, would read
0.575 against a threshold of a few percent and keep calling far past the
point where calls stop paying.

<KeyFact>
A monitoring report built from <Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="three-numbers-for-three-questions">the alert-budget metrics</Ref>
alone, ROC-AUC, average precision and card precision at $k$, cannot tell
whether anyone applied the correction: they read only the order. Only a
Brier score or a reliability check shows the miscalibration.
</KeyFact>

## Decisions that read the value of the probability

If the bank only ever called the top hundred cards, the distortion would
cost nothing: the order is right. Many of its decisions, though, compare a
probability with a price, and there the value matters.

### A threshold priced in euros

<Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="what-an-alert-costs-the-call-and-sometimes-the-customer">The
economics of detection</Ref> priced an alert at $C_a$, whether the payment
is fraud or not, and a missed fraud at $c_{FN}$. Flagging a payment with
real probability $p$ costs $C_a$; leaving it costs $p\,c_{FN}$ in
expectation. Flag when
$$
p \ge \tau = \frac{C_a}{c_{FN}} ,
$$
the same comparison as that lesson's next-call rule. With an
alert at €10 and a miss at €500, $\tau = 0.02$.

A threshold does not need the correction. The correction $g$ is
increasing, so a cut $\tau_s$ on the raw output flags exactly the payments
that the matched cut $\tau' = g(\tau_s) =
\beta\tau_s/((\beta-1)\tau_s + 1)$ flags on the corrected one, as the
classical-ml lesson's exercise on stated scores showed.

The danger is the cut nobody computed. That same lesson showed that 0.5
on a balanced set is a cut at the base rate, so on a one-in-500 stream the
0.5 cut flags every payment with $p \ge 0.002$, not $p \ge 0.02$. The costs
would ask for that only if a miss cost 500 alerts: €5,000 at €10 a call,
ten times the €500 the bank actually loses. At the opening's milder
$\beta = 0.02$ the 0.5 cut sits at $p = 0.0196$, a miss priced at about 51
alerts, which matches €10 and €500 only because of β. Set the threshold
from the costs, on $p'$.

### An amount-weighted queue reverses

The ★ section of <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection">the economics of
detection</Ref> ordered alerts by expected fraud amount, probability times
amount, because a probable €10 fraud matters less than a possible €10,000
one. Here no threshold can stand in for the correction.

<Example title="two payments, two orders">
A model trained at $\beta = 0.02$ scores two payments.

| | amount | $p_s$ | $p_s \times$ amount | $p'$ | $p' \times$ amount |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | €100 | 0.99 | €99 | 0.664 | €66 |
| B | €200 | 0.60 | €120 | 0.029 | €6 |

**Raw.** B puts €120 at stake against A's €99, so B goes first.

**Corrected.** Multiply each output's odds by $\beta$: A's odds 99 become
1.98, so $p'_A = 1.98/2.98 \approx 0.664$; B's odds 1.5 become 0.03, so
$p'_B = 0.03/1.03 \approx 0.029$. A puts €66 at stake, B €6, and A goes
first by a factor of eleven.

The correction did not reorder the probabilities, only their products
with amounts: multiplying by each payment's own amount is not one
increasing function applied to every payment alike, and the correction
compresses 0.60 far more than 0.99.
</Example>

<Exercise>
Three payments from the same model: C, €50 at $p_s = 0.995$; D, €500 at
$p_s = 0.80$; E, €2,000 at $p_s = 0.30$. Order them by expected fraud
amount on the raw and on the corrected outputs.

<Solution>
**Raw.** C: $0.995 \times 50 = €49.75$; D: $0.80 \times 500 = €400$; E:
$0.30 \times 2{,}000 = €600$. Order E, D, C.

**Corrected.** Odds times 0.02. C: odds 199 → 3.98, $p' = 3.98/4.98
\approx 0.799$, €40.0. D: odds 4 → 0.08, $p' = 0.08/1.08 \approx 0.074$,
€37.0. E: odds 0.429 → 0.00857, $p' \approx 0.0085$, €17.0. Order C, D,
E: the raw queue upside down. The raw outputs let a large amount buy its
way to the top with a probability the cut had inflated more than
twentyfold.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Expected losses, the value of the next call and <Ref
lesson="example-dependent-cost">cost rules that depend on the amount</Ref>
are all built from probability values rather than their order, so they
need $p'$, not $p_s$.

## After deployment: the base rate the correction assumes

The correction returns the probability of fraud on the stream the training
rows were drawn from: in the proof, $p$ was the posterior of that stream,
with its base rate $\pi$. A card stream does not hold its base rate still.
A fraud wave doubles it for a few weeks; a quiet month halves it. What
does $p'$ mean when the model scores a stream whose base rate is $\pi_t$?

### Correcting to a different base rate

Suppose only the base rate changes: within each class, payments look as
they did in training. Bayes' rule in odds form, as <Ref
lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="bayes-rule-and-why-the-error-rates-cannot-rescue-precision">the
economics of detection</Ref> wrote it, says posterior odds are what the
evidence contributes times the prior odds. The evidence term is the same on
both streams, so dividing one stream's equation by the other's cancels it:
$$
\frac{p_t}{1-p_t}
= \frac{\pi_t/(1-\pi_t)}{\pi_s/(1-\pi_s)}\cdot\frac{p_s}{1-p_s} .
$$
When $\pi_t = \pi$ the factor in front equals β, since
$\pi_s/(1-\pi_s) = \pi/(\beta(1-\pi))$, and the formula is the correction.
When the base rate has moved, β is the wrong factor.

<Example title="a fraud wave">
The opening's model, trained at $\beta = 0.02$ on a stream at $\pi =
0.002$, so $\pi_s = 0.091$ with odds 0.1002. A fraud wave doubles the base
rate to $\pi_t = 0.004$, odds 0.004016. The factor is $0.004016/0.1002
\approx 0.040$, twice β.

| $p_s$ | $p'$ (assumes 0.002) | $p_t$ (base rate 0.004) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.0196 | 0.039 |
| 0.9 | 0.153 | 0.265 |

During the wave the β correction understates every payment's probability
by about half, and a cost threshold on $p'$ flags too few.
</Example>

### Where the current base rate comes from

Nobody knows $\pi_t$, the base rate of the payments being scored now. <Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential" id="the-label-clock">The label
clock</Ref> applies to it as to everything else: fraud labels arrive days
to weeks after the payment, so the latest base rate a team can measure is
that of the last fully labelled week, a delay block behind the stream.

Fitting a calibration map on recent labelled data, the empirical route the
classical-ml course offered for misspecified models, does not escape this.
It too is fitted on labelled weeks, so it targets their base rate, and at
one fraud in 500 a calibration set of 10,000 payments holds about 20
frauds to fit it from. On a card stream the closed form plus a tracked
base rate is the cheaper of the two, and a held-out reliability check
tests both.

The formula covers only one kind of change. If what fraud looks like
changes, a new attack pattern at a new kind of merchant, the evidence term
itself has moved, and no prior factor repairs it. That is <Ref
lesson="concept-drift">concept drift</Ref>, which the module on sequences,
streams and drift takes up.

<KeyFact>
The two parts of the correction live in different places. β is exact
and a property of the model artifact: record it when the training set is cut and
store it with the model, because nothing logged later recovers it reliably.
The base rate is an estimate as old as the labels, a monitoring quantity:
re-estimate it from the latest
fully labelled week on the monitoring cadence, and re-apply the prior
factor whenever it moves.
</KeyFact>

## What undersampling still costs: precision at the top

The correction removes the bias of the cut; it does not give back the
deleted rows. A model fitted on 2% of the genuine payments is a noisier
estimate than one fitted on all of them, and the classical-ml lesson
priced that in general: cutting the genuine class a hundredfold inflates
the standard error of anything estimated from it about tenfold. On a fraud
stream the question is where that noise lands.

An error in the raw output reaches the corrected value multiplied by the
correction's slope. That slope at $p_s$ is
$\beta/(1 - (1-\beta)p_s)^2$ (the second exercise derives it). At the
bottom of the scale it is β, so errors shrink; it passes 1 at
$p_s \approx 0.88$ and approaches $1/\beta$ as $p_s \to 1$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/slope.svg"
  alt="Log-scale plot of the slope of the correction against the raw output p_s from 0 to 1, for beta = 0.1 in blue and beta = 0.02 in amber. The amber curve starts at 0.02, crosses the dashed slope-1 line at p_s = 0.88 and rises to 45 at p_s = 0.999, just under a dashed line at 1/beta = 50; the blue curve runs from 0.1 to 10"
  caption="The correction shrinks errors at the bottom of the scale and multiplies them by up to 1/β at the top."
/>

At $\beta = 0.02$ an estimation error of 0.001 in a raw output of 0.999
becomes an error of about 0.05 in the corrected value. The payments the
model calls nearly certain are the ones the investigators open first, and
the genuine payments in those suspicious regions are few to begin with; a
hard cut keeps one in fifty of them, so the raw output there is estimated
from a handful of rows. The corrected probability is least precise exactly
where the alert decisions are made. And since the ranking is the noisy
model's own, the correction cannot fix a mistake in the order either (the
two-region exercise builds one).

<Conclusion>
You can now read an undersampled fraud model's output on a card stream:
recover β from the reported class shares, check that the selection looked
at the label alone, find where the distortion is largest, keep every
threshold and amount-weighted queue on the corrected scale, and correct to
the current base rate. Undersampling trades faster training, and for
some learners a better ranking, against variance at the top of the list:
calibration comes back exactly; the deleted rows do not.

Still open is whether undersampling, or any other rebalancing remedy,
ranks better at all once judged by average precision and card precision on
forward-in-time folds; <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">rebalancing
under honest metrics</Ref> runs that comparison.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
A fraud team resampled a portfolio in which 1% of cases are fraud so that
the training set was 20% fraud, keeping every fraud. Its report lists six
customers' model outputs and their "recalibrated" values: 0.1 → 0.0011,

correction expects the keep rate.

**(b)** From $\beta = \pi(1-\pi_s)/(\pi_s(1-\pi))$ with $\pi = 0.01$ and
$\pi_s = 0.2$: $\beta = 0.01 \times 0.8/(0.2 \times 0.99) = 0.008/0.198 =
4/99 \approx 0.0404$, four times the factor used. Multiply each output's
odds by $4/99$:

| $p_s$ | odds | $\times\,4/99$ | $p'$ | reported |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.111 | 0.00449 | 0.0045 | 0.0011 |
| 0.3 | 0.429 | 0.0173 | 0.017 | 0.0043 |
| 0.5 | 1 | 0.0404 | 0.039 | 0.0100 |
| 0.6 | 1.5 | 0.0606 | 0.057 | 0.0149 |
| 0.85 | 5.67 | 0.229 | 0.186 | 0.054 |
| 0.9 | 9 | 0.364 | 0.267 | 0.083 |

Every reported value understates the odds fourfold, in the same order,
as two increasing maps must give, so no ranking report would have exposed
the bug.

**(c)** The report flags the customers at 0.85 and 0.9; the corrected
values flag the customer at 0.6 as well (0.057), whom the report leaves
out.

**(d)** The class ratio equals the keep rate only when the training set's
own ratio of frauds to genuine cases is 1: a balanced set. Code written
for the balanced default and kept after the cut moved to 20% produces
exactly this report.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push β to its edges. (a) Show that as $\beta \to 0$, $p_s \to 1$ for every
payment with $p > 0$. (b) Show that the slope of the correction at $p_s$
is $\beta/(1 - (1-\beta)p_s)^2$, evaluate it at $p_s = 0.999$ for $\beta =
0.02$ and $\beta = 0.002$, and say what that does to a model whose raw
outputs carry estimation errors of about 0.001. (c) Why does the setup
require $\beta > 0$ strictly?

<Solution>
**(a)** $p_s = p/(p + \beta(1-p))$, and with $p > 0$ fixed the term
$\beta(1-p)$ goes to 0, so $p_s \to p/p = 1$.

**(b)** Differentiating $g(u) = \beta u/(1 - (1-\beta)u)$ by the quotient
rule gives $\beta(1 - (1-\beta)u) + \beta u(1-\beta)$ over the squared
denominator, which simplifies to $\beta/(1 - (1-\beta)u)^2$. At $u =
0.999$:

- $\beta = 0.02$: denominator $1 - 0.98 \times 0.999 = 0.02098$, squared
  $0.000440$; slope $0.02/0.000440 \approx 45$.
- $\beta = 0.002$: denominator $1 - 0.998 \times 0.999 = 0.002998$,
  squared $8.99 \times 10^{-6}$; slope $\approx 222$.

An error of 0.001 in the raw output becomes about 0.045 or 0.22 in the
corrected one. With β near 0 the outputs cluster near 1, and what
separates a real 0.1 from a real 0.5 sits in the third or fourth decimal
of numbers estimated from a handful of genuine rows: the formula stays
exact, but what it amplifies is noise.

**(c)** At $\beta = 0$ the training set has no genuine payments, $p_s = 1$
for everything, and the map from $p$ to $p_s$ is no longer one-to-one, so
there is nothing to invert.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The opening's model ($\beta = 0.02$, trained on a stream at $\pi = 0.002$)
outputs 0.9 for a payment. (a) What is the payment's probability of fraud
in a quiet month with base rate 0.001? (b) During a fraud wave the base
rate this week is 0.004, but the last fully labelled week shows 0.003 and
the team corrects to that. What does it report, and how far is that from
the truth and from the plain β correction?

<Solution>
The training set's prior odds are $\pi_s/(1-\pi_s) = \pi/(\beta(1-\pi)) =
0.002/(0.02 \times 0.998) \approx 0.1002$, and the output's odds are 9.

**(a)** Deployment prior odds $0.001/0.999 \approx 0.001001$; factor
$0.001001/0.1002 \approx 0.00999$, half of β. Odds $9 \times 0.00999
\approx 0.0899$, so $p_t \approx 0.082$, against the β correction's 0.153.

**(b)** With 0.003: odds $0.003009$, factor $\approx 0.0300$, odds $0.270$,
reported $0.213$. The truth at 0.004 is $0.265$ (factor 0.0401, odds
0.361), and the plain β correction gives 0.153. Correcting to the lagged
base rate closes about half of the gap; the rest is the label delay.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague writes: "Once you correct with $p'$, the undersampled model
is equivalent to the model trained on all the data: same probabilities,
so the same average precision." Refute it with a case, and name the step
that fails. Use two regions of feature space, A and B, each holding 500
training payments, with 5 frauds in A and 15 in B, undersampled at
$\beta = 0.02$; suppose the coin keeps 4 of A's genuine payments and 16
of B's, both well within the spread of a draw whose mean is about 10.

<Solution>
**The full model.** It sees $5/500 = 0.010$ in A and $15/500 = 0.030$ in
B, and ranks B above A, correctly.

**The undersampled model.** It sees $5/9 \approx 0.556$ in A and $15/31
\approx 0.484$ in B, and ranks A above B.

**After correction.** $p'_A = 0.02 \times 0.556/(1 - 0.98 \times 0.556)
\approx 0.0111/0.455 \approx 0.0244$ and $p'_B = 0.02 \times
0.484/(1 - 0.98 \times 0.484) \approx 0.00968/0.526 \approx 0.0184$.
A is still above B. The correction is increasing, so it keeps the
undersampled model's order, including its mistake.

**The failing step.** "Same probabilities" holds for the exact posteriors:
the odds identity relates true $p_s$ and $p$. A fitted model estimates
$p_s$ from the rows the coin happened to keep; the correction removes the
systematic factor $1/\beta$ from that estimate and leaves the noise of a
fit to 2% of the genuine rows untouched. Average precision reads only the
order, so the corrected model keeps the undersampled model's average
precision, which can be below the full model's.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Combine the cost of an alert with the cut. A stream has one fraud in
500; an alert costs €10, a missed fraud €500, and the trainer balanced
the set, $\beta = 1/499 \approx 0.002$. (a) What threshold on the real
probability do the costs ask for? (b) What threshold on the raw output
$p_s$ makes the same decisions? (c) Which flags more payments, the cut of
(b) or the habitual cut at 0.5 on $p_s$, and what does each extra flag
cost the bank in expectation?

<Solution>
**(a)** Flag when $p \ge C_a/c_{FN} = 10/500 = 0.02$.

**(b)** Send 0.02 through the undersampling map, the inverse of the
correction: $\tau_s = 0.02/(0.02 + 0.002 \times 0.98) = 0.02/0.02196
\approx 0.91$. On the raw output the cut belongs at 0.91, not 0.5.

**(c)** On a balanced set the 0.5 cut is a cut at the base rate, $p \ge
0.002$, so it flags strictly more. Every extra payment it flags has $0.002
\le p < 0.02$: its call costs €10 and saves in expectation $p \times
\text{€}500$, between €1 and €10, so each loses money, up to €9 at the
low end. Through <Ref lesson="economics-of-detection"
id="what-share-of-the-alerts-are-fraud-the-base-rate-decides">the
base-rate funnel</Ref>, they also drag the queue's precision toward the
base rate. Nobody chose this cut; balancing and the habit of 0.5 produced
it together.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the line behind the two Gaussian tasks, then push the cut harder.
Genuine payments have $x \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$, frauds $x \sim N(\mu,
\sigma^2)$, and frauds are a share $\pi$ of the stream. (a) Show that the
log-odds of fraud is linear in $x$ with slope $\mu/\sigma^2$, and find
where it crosses zero. (b) With $\sigma = 3$, $\pi = 0.1$ and a cut at
$\beta = 0.02$, how far does the 0.5 crossing move in the task with
$\mu = 3$ and in the task with $\mu = 15$?

<Solution>
**(a)** The log-odds is the log prior odds plus the log ratio of the two
densities. The normal constants cancel because the spreads are equal:
$$
\ln\frac{p}{1-p} = \ln\frac{\pi}{1-\pi} - \frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} +
\frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2} = \ln\frac{\pi}{1-\pi} + \frac{\mu x}{\sigma^2} -
\frac{\mu^2}{2\sigma^2} ,
$$
a line in $x$ with slope $\mu/\sigma^2$. It is zero at
$x_0 = (\sigma^2/\mu)\ln((1-\pi)/\pi) + \mu/2$: for $\pi = 0.1$,
$\sigma = 3$, this is $3 \times 2.197 + 1.5 = 8.1$ when $\mu = 3$ and
$0.6 \times 2.197 + 7.5 = 8.8$ when $\mu = 15$, the crossings in the
figure's first frame.

**(b)** The cut adds $\ln 50 = 3.91$ to the line, so the crossing moves
left by $\sigma^2 \ln 50/\mu$: $9 \times 3.91/3 = 11.7$ for $\mu = 3$, to
$x \approx -3.6$, below the mean of the genuine payments; and $9 \times
3.91/15 = 2.3$ for $\mu = 15$, to $x \approx 6.5$. In the overlapping
task the raw 0.5 cut now flags most genuine payments, about 89% of all
rows changing decision; in the separated task about 1.4% do.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the experiment that the next lesson's question rests on. Simulate
100,000 training and 100,000 test rows: label fraud with probability
0.01, two features drawn as independent standard normals, shifted by
$(1.5, 1.5)$ for frauds. Fit a logistic regression on all training rows,
and another on the frauds plus a random 5% of the genuine rows
($\beta = 0.05$). On the test rows compute ROC-AUC, average precision and
the balanced accuracy (the mean of the recall on frauds and on genuine
payments) of the cut at 0.5, for three scores: the full model, the raw
undersampled model and its correction. Which metric moves, and why?

<Solution>
With seed 0 in numpy and scikit-learn's default logistic regression
(other seeds move the third decimal):

| score | ROC-AUC | average precision | balanced accuracy at 0.5 | flagged |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| full model | 0.938 | 0.312 | 0.56 | 165 |
| undersampled, raw | 0.938 | 0.312 | 0.79 | 3,799 |
| undersampled, corrected | 0.938 | 0.312 | 0.56 | 142 |

**Ranking metrics.** Identical for the raw and corrected scores, by
monotonicity; nearly identical to the full model's here, because a
logistic regression on two features needs few genuine rows.

**Balanced accuracy.** It jumps from 0.56 to 0.79 for the raw score and
returns to 0.56 once corrected. Nothing about the ranking improved: the
0.5 cut on the raw score is a cut at about 0.05 on the real probability,
so it flags 23 times as many payments, catching more frauds at little
cost in genuine recall, which balanced accuracy weights equally with fraud
recall. A metric at a fixed cut rewards whatever moves the cut into the
genuine class; <Ref lesson="imbalance-techniques-revisited">rebalancing
under honest metrics</Ref> finds the same pattern across the whole
toolkit, while the ranking the investigators work from stays put.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Eight model families are trained and tuned on the same weeks of real card payments and scored by three metrics. XGBoost, the attention-LSTM and the feed-forward network each top one of the three leaderboards. What in the validation design, the metrics and the compute spent produces three different winners from one experiment?

Five of the eight rows are already familiar. The tuned decision tree,
logistic regression, random forest and XGBoost are <Ref
lesson="gbm-model-selection" id="the-four-searches-on-the-real-stream">the
four searches on the real stream</Ref>, and the feed-forward network is the
one <Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors"
id="a-tie-on-the-simulator-a-loss-below-the-top-on-real-data">the network
lesson</Ref> brought level with them at the budget. Those lessons compared
two or three of them at a time. The three new rows are networks that read
the card's earlier payments as well as the current one.

Put all eight in one table and the obvious move is to ship the winner of the
team's own number, card precision at 100: the feed-forward network, at
0.236. Its lead over XGBoost is 0.001, a tenth of a compromised card a day,
and the families that top the other two columns are different again. Before
any top place can be read as a property of a model family it has to survive
three questions. Does the gap exceed what four weeks of real fraud move by
themselves? Which part of each day's ranking does the metric read? And how
much search did each family get?

## One stream, one referee, eight grids

A leaderboard means something only if every row went through the same
procedure. Compare models from different studies and the data, the split and
the metric all change at once; nothing in the gap can then be attributed to
the model. This experiment holds everything fixed except the family.

### What is held fixed

**The stream.** The Belgian e-commerce payments of 2018 that <Ref
lesson="fraud-detection-system"
id="a-hundred-cases-a-day-the-models-output-is-a-ranking">the layered
system</Ref> introduced: about 400,000 payments a day, about 1,000 of them
fraud, on a little over 300 compromised cards. The data is confidential, so
every number below can be quoted but not re-run.

**The inputs.** The same fifteen numbers per payment as <Ref
lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="five-models-scored-on-a-test-week">the five
baseline models</Ref>: the amount, the weekend and night flags, the card's
counts and mean amounts over 1, 7 and 30 days, and the terminal's counts and
delayed fraud rates over the same windows.

**The referee.** Each candidate setting is validated on four prequential
folds, each a training week, a delay week and a validation week (<Ref
lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"
id="prequential-folds-a-spread-across-weeks">prequential folds</Ref>). For
each metric the candidate with the best mean is picked, refitted on the
training block that ends a delay before each of four forward test weeks, and
scored there. Every number in the table is the mean over those four test
weeks, and every ± is the standard deviation across them.

### Three rows that read the card's past

The feed-forward network sees the fifteen numbers of the payment it scores.
The three new models see the same fifteen numbers for each of the card's
last few payments, the current one included, in time order (five payments in
the simulator version of the same models). They differ in how they read that
short sequence:

- a **one-dimensional convolutional network (CNN)** slides one small set of
  weights along the sequence, over each pair of neighbouring payments, so a
  pattern learned at one position is recognised at any other;
- an **LSTM** (long short-term memory network) reads the payments one at a
  time, oldest first, and updates a vector of state after each, which carries
  what it has read so far; the final state feeds a hidden layer and a sigmoid
  output;
- an **LSTM with attention** keeps the state after every payment and lets a
  learned score weight them, so the classifier can lean on any earlier
  payment instead of only on the final state.

The last exercise of the network lesson arrived at the need for the first two:
<Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors" id="exercises">concatenating the last
five payments</Ref> gives every position its own weights and cannot
recognise a burst wherever it starts. How these models work, and when the
order of a card's payments carries signal at all, is the problem of <Ref
lesson="sequence-models-for-transactions">sequence models over an entity's
transactions</Ref>. Here they are three rows of a table.

The rule is the one <Ref lesson="delayed-labels-prequential"

weeks could reverse them. Here the standard deviations are taken across the

is the stricter of the two possible tests: a family whose weeks happen to be
quiet cannot be called ahead of a rival whose weeks swing widely on the
strength of its own small spread.

## How much four weeks move by themselves

Every verdict above rests on the standard deviations, and the ones in the
team's column are large: 0.03 to 0.07, three to seven cards a day. Where
they come from decides what would shrink them. If they are the luck of which
cards happened to land in 700 places a week, more test days from the same
weeks would sharpen the comparison. If the weeks themselves differ, only
more weeks, or comparing models on the same weeks, can.

### The sampling floor

<Example title="the sampling floor of a week's card precision">
A test week's $CP@100$ is the mean of seven daily values, each the number of
compromised cards among that day's top hundred divided by 100. Over the week
that is $X/700$, where $X$ counts the compromised cards among 700 card
places.

Suppose every place holds a compromised card independently with the same
probability $p$, the assumption under which one week differs from another
only by chance. Then $X$ is
binomial with $n = 700$ and

$$
\mathrm{sd}\!\left(\frac{X}{700}\right) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{700}} .
$$

At the table's level, $p = 0.23$:
$\sqrt{0.23 \times 0.77 / 700} = \sqrt{0.000253} = 0.016$, about 1.6 cards
a day.

The measured spreads are 0.03 to 0.07, two to four times that floor. A
standard deviation computed from four values is itself rough, but even its
smallest values here sit at twice the floor. Most of the week-to-week
movement is therefore not sampling: the four weeks differ in what they
contain.
</Example>

### What makes one week unlike another

What they differ in is visible in the stream itself. On the real test days
the number of compromised cards not yet found ranges from about 200 to 370
(<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="on-real-card-data">the real-data
counts</Ref>), and the easy cases that fill the top of every model's list,
compromised terminals and bursts on one card, vary in number from week to
week. A week rich in easy cards lifts every model at once. That shared lift
has been seen directly on a longer record: ten months of European online
card payments, from August 2014 to May 2015, scored every day by three
random-forest detectors (one trained once and never updated, one retrained
daily, one retrained daily with the investigators' latest findings). The
daily card precision of all three peaked together in February 2015, the
month with the most compromised cards, and sank together in October 2014,
the month with the fewest.

That is also why the comparison between two models is sharper than their
spreads suggest. Two models scored on the same four weeks share each week's
supply of easy cards, and their weekly differences cancel it. The tree-ensemble lesson's
exercises sized such a paired comparison (<Ref lesson="gbm-model-selection"
id="exercises">a paired difference across weeks</Ref>): at a paired
standard deviation of 0.02, a gap of 0.003 needs about 180 weeks to show,
a gap of 0.02 about four.

how the simulator makes fraud. Its generator runs the same scripts every

durations (<Ref lesson="canonical-datasets"

The real stream's spreads at two to four times the floor measure exactly

</Solution>
</Exercise>

## What the card's history adds, and where it shows

The average-precision column is the one place where the three new rows lead,
and the table contains a comparison that isolates why. The feed-forward
network and the LSTM receive the same fifteen numbers for the payment they
score; the LSTM also receives the fifteen numbers of the card's previous
payments. Up to the difference in their layers, whatever separates them is
what the history adds.

### One comparison that changes one thing

On the real stream the history moves average precision from 0.084 (the
feed-forward network) to 0.104 (LSTM) and 0.105 (attention), with the CNN in
between at 0.093. By the rule the LSTM's gap of 0.020 is inside its
standard deviation of 0.03 and does not separate; the attention-LSTM's 0.021
clears the larger deviation of its pair, 0.02, by 0.001. Only one of the two
separates, and barely, but all three sequence models fall on the same side.
ROC-AUC barely moves (0.894 against 0.882 to 0.898), and card precision at
100 does not move at all (0.236 against 0.220 to 0.232).

From the simulator one would have expected no gain. The same four networks,
tuned on the simulator's folds and scored on its four test weeks as in <Ref
lesson="neural-tabular-detectors" id="three-numbers-for-the-network">the
network lesson's benchmark</Ref>, gave average precision 0.675
for the feed-forward network, 0.665 for the LSTM, 0.667 with attention and
0.599 for the CNN: the history added nothing and the CNN lost. The
simulator's frauds are scripts whose whole signature is already in the
aggregates, a terminal's recent fraud rate or an amount far above the card's
own mean (<Ref lesson="ulb-handbook-benchmark"
id="the-simulator-half-the-fraud-in-two-leaves">two leaves of a shallow tree
hold half of them</Ref>), and a sequence of the same fifteen numbers has
nothing left to add. On real payments something in the order of a card's
payments survives the 1-, 7- and 30-day windows. Which property of real
fraud that is, and on which kinds of payment it matters, is what <Ref
lesson="sequence-models-for-transactions">sequence models over an entity's
transactions</Ref> takes up.

### Average precision up, card precision flat: two readings

How can a model raise average precision by a quarter and put no more
compromised cards into the day's hundred? Two different changes in the
ranking would both do it, and the table cannot tell them apart.

Average precision counts payments: every fraudulent payment adds the
precision at its rank. Card precision counts each card once, at its
highest-scored payment. A compromised card on this stream carries about
three fraudulent payments a day, so one card can fill several payment places
near the top (<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics" id="on-real-card-data">the
alert-budget lesson</Ref> noted that real cards fill up to three of the
hundred).

**Reading 1: later payments of cards already near the top rise.** In frame 2
the second frauds of cards A and C climb. Average precision rises from 0.63
to 0.77, because two fraudulent payments are now reached at higher
precision. The called cards are the same three. A model that reads the
card's previous payments is placed to do exactly this: when it scores a
card's second or third payment of a burst, the first payments are in its
input.

**Reading 2: a card just below the cut rises.** In frame 3 card G's frauds
climb to ranks 4 and 5. Average precision rises to the same 0.77, and the
called cards are again the same, because G is the fourth card and the
budget is three. With a budget of four, frame 3 would find a third
compromised card and frame 2 would not.

On the real stream the two readings make different promises. Under the
first, the sequence models spend top places on cards the team would call
anyway; under the second, they hold cards in reserve just below the
hundredth place, worth something the day the team grows. Which reading
holds is a measurement on the scored test weeks, designed in <Ref
lesson="worldline-model-comparison" id="exercises">the exercises</Ref>.

<KeyFact>
Payment-level average precision cannot tell the two readings apart: frames 2
and 3 both score 0.77. Only a quantity that counts each card once, such as
average precision over the day's card ranking or card precision at a larger
budget, can.
</KeyFact>

### What the compute bought

The compute each family consumed is only partly on record. On the real
stream the four classic searches took 695 seconds (regression), 1,617 (tree),
3,616 (forest) and 8,789 (XGBoost) on a 40-core server, as <Ref
lesson="gbm-model-selection" id="what-the-compute-buys">the tree-ensemble
lesson</Ref> priced them. The networks' real-stream grids, 36 candidates
each, have no recorded times.

The simulator, where every grid was timed, gives the scale. With ten fits
running at once, the feed-forward network's 162 candidates took about two
hours; the LSTM's 162 took 4.1 hours, the attention-LSTM's 162 took 4.6, and
the CNN's 216 took 7.5. Per candidate, a sequence network cost two to three
times a feed-forward network, and XGBoost's eighteen candidates took 21
minutes on one core. On the real stream the networks' grids were cut to 36
candidates, and the LSTM's picks show the cut landed short of the family's
optimum. The compute did not buy a single card a day at the budget. What it
bought was the sequence networks' place at the top of the average-precision
band, and that place was measured whichever of the two readings above
holds. What the readings decide is its worth: under reading 1 it buys
nothing the team can call, under reading 2 it buys cards in reserve that
pay only if the budget grows.

## Models that tie do not catch the same frauds

A tie in card precision says that two models find about the same number of
compromised cards a day. It does not say they find the same cards, and if
they find different ones, the useful answer to "which family?" may be two.

### Measuring the overlap

The eight-model experiment did not record which cards each family found.
Another card stream did. On three months of a European processor's 2015
payments, with e-commerce and face-to-face payments modelled separately, a
random forest and an LSTM were trained on March and April and tested on
May, each cut at the score where it had caught a fifth of the test frauds,
so that both caught the same number. The overlap of their caught frauds was
measured by the Jaccard index, the number caught by both divided by the
number caught by either.

For two sets of the same size $n$ sharing $s$ elements, the union holds
$2n - s$, so

$$
J = \frac{s}{2n - s} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{s}{n} = \frac{2J}{1 + J} .
$$

On e-commerce the forest and the LSTM had $J = 0.35$: each model shared
$0.70/1.35 = 52\%$ of its catches with the other, and the other 48% were its
own. On face-to-face payments $J = 0.25$, a 40% share. Even two forests
built on different input sets shared only 64% ($J = 0.475$), and two LSTMs
67% ($J = 0.508$).

### What the overlap means for the eight families

Half of each model's catches are its own. Two such models tied in card
precision are not interchangeable; an average of their rankings can find
cards neither finds alone, as long as the budget makes room. The backtest
that decides whether it does was designed in <Ref
lesson="neural-tabular-detectors" id="exercises">the network lesson's
exercises</Ref>: measure the overlap of each day's detected cards first,
average ranks, choose the weight on the folds, compare week by week. On the
eight-model table the natural pair is the family with the firm ROC-AUC lead
and a family from the average-precision band that reads the history,
XGBoost and an LSTM. Whether they disagree on this stream as much as on the
2015 one was not measured.

## Where this experiment departs from the bank

The table is a backtest under one set of simplifications, and each bears on
how far its three leaderboards carry to a deployed detector.

| the experiment | the bank | what it bears on |
|---|---|---|
| every training payment carries its label as finally known; the delay enters only as the gap in the split | labels arrive over weeks, and the fast ones are the cards the model itself chose to alert (<Ref lesson="fraud-detection-system" id="the-fast-labels-are-a-sample-the-model-chose">the system lesson</Ref>) | how well each family learns from labels its own alerts selected: <Ref lesson="alert-feedback-selection-bias">alert feedback and selection bias</Ref> |
| four forward test weeks of one summer | months of drift, weeks unlike the folds | whether any order holds beyond a season: <Ref lesson="drift-strategies-on-worldline">drift strategies on Worldline data</Ref> |
| e-commerce payments only | card-present payments too | where the history pays: <Ref lesson="sequence-models-for-transactions">sequence models</Ref> |
| fifteen hand-built numbers, no merchant category or country | dozens of categorical fields | embeddings had nothing to embed (<Ref lesson="neural-tabular-detectors" id="what-the-terminal-and-the-weekday-added-on-the-simulator-nothing">the null result</Ref>) |
| grids of 4 to 36 candidates, the LSTM's pick on the edge, network times unrecorded | a search budget chosen in advance | how much of each row is the family and how much the grid |
| one refit per test week | a nightly retrain | how often the compute bill is paid |

None of these invalidates the table. They mark what it measured: eight
families, on one season of one channel, each searched once, with labels a
deployed system never has.

<Conclusion>
You can now read a multi-model leaderboard as a measurement and say what
produced each top place. ROC-AUC's lead belongs to the tree ensembles and is
earned below the hundredth card. Average precision's top is a band of five
families, in which the networks that read the card's earlier payments sit
highest, consistent with the history carrying signal on real payments that
it did not carry on the simulator, and the gain is either more payments of
cards already called or more cards just below the cut. Card precision at
100, the team's own number, ties all eight inside 2.5 cards a day against
weekly spreads of three to seven, two to four times the sampling floor
because the weeks themselves differ, and the folds' choice of candidate
inside a family moves it as much as the choice of family. Models that tie
can still catch different frauds, so the reply to the table is to combine
and measure, not to crown.

What none of the eight rows can show is what the choice between two
detectors is worth in money, the measure a deployment decision is finally
argued in. <Ref lesson="scorecard-vs-gbm-lab">The scorecard and
boosted-tree lab</Ref> builds a scorecard and a boosted tree under the same
referee and argues that choice in savings.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Suppose the investigators could call 1,000 cards a day instead of 100. (a)
On days with 200 to 370 compromised cards not yet found, what is the ceiling
of $CP@1000$, and what does it now depend on? (b) Which of the table's
columns would you expect $CP@1000$ to resemble more, and why might the
eight-way tie break? (c) Push $k$ to every card that pays that day, about
120,000. What does every model score?

<Solution>
**(a)** The ceiling is $\min(1, \gamma_t/k)$ with $\gamma_t$ the day's
compromised cards not yet found (<Ref lesson="alert-budget-metrics"
id="reading-a-day-against-its-ceiling">reading a day against its
ceiling</Ref>). At $k = 1{,}000$ it is $\gamma_t/1{,}000$, between 0.20 and
0.37: for the first time on this stream the ceiling binds, and it moves with
each day's supply of fraud. Comparisons across weeks would then need the
normalised version, $CP@k$ divided by its ceiling, or a paired comparison on
the same weeks.

**(b)** $CP@1000$ reads the cards ranked 101 to 1,000, the region where the
table's average-precision differences can live and the top of the region
ROC-AUC reads. It should look more like the average-precision column: if the
sequence networks' gain is reading 2 (cards just below the hundredth), it
shows up here. The tie can also break for a statistical reason: 7,000 card
places a week give a sampling floor of about
$\sqrt{0.1 \times 0.9 / 7{,}000} \approx 0.004$ at a precision near 0.1, a
quarter of the 100-card floor. The week-to-week waves remain, which is why
the comparison should be paired.

**(c)** With every card called, the top $k$ cards are all cards, and
$CP@k$ is the share of compromised cards among them, about
$300 / 120{,}000 \approx 0.0025$ (taking the course's working assumption of
about 120,000 cards a day), the same for every model. The metric has
stopped reading the ranking at all: at the two extremes of $k$, a budget of
one card and a budget of every card, it says least about the model, and
between them it reads a different part of the list for each $k$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague writes: "The attention-LSTM is the best model for this bank: it
tops average precision, which summarises the whole top of the list, and it
is the most sophisticated model we tried." Refute the claim, naming the step
that fails.

<Solution>
**What is true.** The attention-LSTM has the highest average precision,
0.105.

**First failure: the top place is not separated.** Its gap to the LSTM
(0.104), XGBoost (0.095 ± 0.03), the CNN (0.093) and the forest
(0.080 ± 0.03) is smaller than the larger standard deviation of each pair.
On another four weeks the order inside that band could reverse.

**Second failure, the decisive one: average precision is not the bank's
metric.** The team calls a hundred cards a day, and on card precision at 100
the attention-LSTM scores 0.232, inside a band where all eight families
tie. The step "tops AP, therefore best for this bank" fails because average
precision counts payments and reads beyond the hundredth card. Its gain may
be later payments of cards the team calls anyway (reading 1), which is worth
nothing to a team that calls cards, or cards just below the cut (reading 2),
worth something only if the budget grows.

**Third: the row is a lower bound with an unknown price.** Its grid's picks
lean toward the slow edge, so the family might do better with a longer
search, and its real-stream search time was not recorded; on the simulator a
sequence grid ran four to seven and a half hours against XGBoost's 21
minutes on one core. "Most sophisticated" is a cost, not a credential.

The defensible statement is narrower: among the eight, the sequence networks
sit at the top of an average-precision band, which matters if the bank
needs the ranking below the hundredth card and is worth testing in
combination with a tree ensemble.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design the measurement that tells reading 1 from reading 2 for the
attention-LSTM against the feed-forward network, using only the scored test
weeks. (a) Name two quantities, computed from each model's scores and the
test labels, that move under one reading and not the other. (b) Compute both
on the three frames of the toy day. (c) Say what result on the four real
test weeks would count as evidence for reading 2.

<Solution>
**(a)** Two quantities that ignore repeated payments of a card:

- **Card-level average precision.** Collapse each day to cards as card
  precision does, each card scored by its highest payment and labelled
  compromised if any payment is fraud, then compute average precision over
  the ranked cards. Reading 1 reorders payments of cards that already lead,
  so the card order, and this number, stay put; reading 2 moves a
  compromised card up the card order and raises it.
- **$CP@k$ for $k$ above 100**, say 150, 200 and 300 cards a day. Reading 2
  puts compromised cards just below the hundredth place, so these rise;
  reading 1 leaves them where they were.

**(b)** Frame 1: the card order is A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, J, with
compromised cards at card ranks 1, 3 and 7, so card-level AP is
$\tfrac13(1 + \tfrac23 + \tfrac37) = 0.698$, and $CP@4 = 2/4 = 0.50$
(A, B, C, D). Frame 2: the card order is unchanged (A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H,
J): card-level AP 0.698, $CP@4 = 0.50$. Frame 3: the order is A, B, C, G,
D, …, compromised at ranks 1, 3 and 4: card-level AP
$\tfrac13(1 + \tfrac23 + \tfrac34) = 0.806$, $CP@4 = 3/4 = 0.75$. Payment AP
was 0.77 in both frames 2 and 3; the card-level quantities separate them.

**(c)** Compute both quantities for both models on each of the four test
weeks and take the weekly differences, LSTM minus network. Evidence for
reading 2 is a mean difference in card-level AP and in $CP@200$ above two
standard errors of those weekly differences, with the same sign every week.
Evidence for reading 1 is a payment-level AP gain with card-level AP and
$CP@200$ flat; then the history is ranking more of each burst, which helps
a system that blocks per payment and not a team that calls per card.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two models each call 100 cards a day and find 23 compromised cards. Their
sets of found cards have Jaccard index $J$. (a) Show that the number of
distinct compromised cards found by either is $46/(1+J)$, and evaluate it at
$J = 0$, $0.35$ and $1$. (b) If the team calls the union of the two lists,
how many cards is that, and between which values does the union's precision
lie at $J = 0.35$? (c) The budget stays at 100. What is the most a combined
list can find, and why is that only a bound?

<Solution>
**(a)** With $n = 23$ found by each and $s$ by both, $J = s/(46 - s)$, so
$s = 46J/(1+J)$ and the union holds $46 - s = 46/(1+J)$ compromised cards.
At $J = 0$: 46, the lists find disjoint fraud. At $J = 0.35$: $46/1.35 =
34.1$. At $J = 1$: 23, the second model adds nothing.

**(b)** At $J = 0.35$ the two lists share $s = 11.9$ compromised cards, and
may share up to all 77 genuine cards of the smaller list as well. The lists
therefore share between 11.9 and $11.9 + 77 = 88.9$ cards, and their union
holds between $200 - 88.9 = 111$ and $200 - 11.9 = 188$ cards. Its
precision is between $34.1/188 = 0.18$ and $34.1/111 = 0.31$, against 0.23
for either list alone: calling both lists raises precision only if the
models also agree on most of their false alarms.

**(c)** At most $\min(100, 34.1) = 34$ compromised cards, 11 more than either
model, if the combined list kept every card found by either and dropped
only genuine ones. Nothing tells the combination which cards those are; an
average of ranks keeps the cards both models rank high, and its result lies
somewhere between 23 and 34. Where it lies is the backtest's question; the
Jaccard index only sets the ceiling.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A compliance officer asks to ship the points scorecard of <Ref
lesson="scorecards-woe">the scorecard lesson</Ref> instead of any of the
eight families, because a reviewer can read it line by line. (a) Which row
of the table stands in for the scorecard, and what supports that? (b) What
does the bank give up at a budget of 100 cards, in cards a day and, if the
gap were real, in euros at €360 saved per compromised card found? (c) What
does it give up below the top hundred? (d) Which part of your answer can the
data not support?

<Solution>
**(a)** Logistic regression: the scorecard is a logistic regression on
weight-of-evidence columns, and on the simulator's test week it tied the
plain regression on card precision at 100 (0.290 against 0.291) while
losing average precision, 0.52 against 0.61, where the one-direction rule
merged the terminal's 30-day fraud rate. No scorecard was fitted on the real
stream, so the regression row is the closest measured stand-in, and an upper
bound on the card's average precision if the same merge applies.

**(b)** The regression's $CP@100$ is 0.211 against the best 0.236: 2.5 cards a
day. At €360 a card that would be €900 a day, about €330,000 a year. But
2.5 cards is inside weekly standard deviations of 3 to 7 cards (0.03 for
the regression, 0.04 for the network), and the regression is not separated
from any family by the rule.

**(c)** Below the top it loses clearly: ROC-AUC 0.816 against 0.928,
average precision 0.054 against 0.105 or 0.095, half of the best. Those gaps
exceed the standard deviations. The bank pays for the scorecard if it

**(d)** The euro figure: the 2.5-card gap is not separated from zero on

<Exercise>
The bank can afford one more grid search on the real stream, about 36
candidates. Which family's grid would you extend, in which direction, and
why not the decision tree's? How would you spend the 36 candidates?

<Solution>
**Which family.** The LSTM (or the attention-LSTM): its picks all use the
fewest epochs (5), and its best scores came at the smaller learning rate
(0.0001) and the largest batch (256), all three edges on the side of less
training. A pick on the boundary says the grid may not contain the family's
optimum, so its row is a lower bound. The tree's grid has candidates on
both sides of its peak at depth 6, with test scores falling steeply on each
side, so extending it can only add candidates the folds would reject.

**Which direction.** The three edges are one dimension, the number and size
of updates: fewer epochs and larger batches mean fewer steps, a smaller rate
smaller ones. Extend along that dimension: 2 and 3 epochs, batches of 512,
rates of 0.00005 and 0.00002, and keep the dropout values, which did not sit
on an edge.

**How to spend the candidates.** Draw them at random over the extended
ranges instead of crossing a few values of each, because random draws try
more distinct values along whichever setting matters (<Ref
lesson="gbm-model-selection"
id="why-random-search-beats-a-grid-when-settings-are-ranges">why random
search beats a grid</Ref>). Validate on the same four folds and judge by
the same rule: a new pick counts as an improvement only if its fold mean
beats the old pick's by more than a fold standard deviation; the extended
search may also confirm that the corner was already the optimum.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The sequence networks here saw only e-commerce payments. Card-present
payments, at a shop's terminal, are a different stream. Predict on which of
the two a model that reads the order of a card's payments should gain more
over the 1-, 7- and 30-day aggregates, and argue from what constrains a
card's sequence of payments in each channel.

<Solution>
**Card-present.** A card paying at terminals has to be physically there:
consecutive payments are tied to places and to the time it takes to move
between them, and a cardholder's shop payments follow routines of places
and hours. A counterfeit or stolen card breaks that order in ways that live
in consecutive payments: a terminal in another city twenty minutes after the
last one, a run of terminals the card has never used. A count or a mean over
a window loses which payment followed which; a sequence model keeps it.

**E-commerce.** Moving between merchants online costs nothing, so a
genuine card's consecutive payments are already irregular, and the order
carries less that a window misses. The aggregates, how many payments and
how much money recently, catch most of what a burst of online fraud looks
like.

The prediction is a larger gain on card-present payments and a small or
null one on e-commerce, which makes this lesson's average-precision gain on
an e-commerce stream the harder case. Whether the prediction holds, and
whether hand-built features that encode the order close the gap, is what
<Ref lesson="sequence-models-for-transactions">sequence models over an
entity's transactions</Ref> measures.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

# Adversarial classification: evasion as a knapsack and the arms race — research brief

Lesson: `adversarial-classification` (module: adversary, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Formulate evasion against a cost-sensitive classifier as a minimum-cost camouflage problem, derive the adversary-aware classifier, and explain why the repeated game oscillates instead of converging
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A spam filter is published, and within weeks spammers are inserting ordinary words until each message passes. A fraud detector is used, and fraudsters learn to keep amounts under what it flags. If the adversary knows the classifier and pays a cost per feature changed, what is their cheapest way through, what should the classifier do knowing that, and does the back-and-forth ever settle?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
A classifier is trained on last month's spam (or last month's fraud) and deployed. The people it is built to stop can observe what passes and what does not, and they are the ones producing next month's positives. In module 7 the reader learned to treat a changing stream as drift: watch the error, retrain. Here the change is not weather; it is a reply to the classifier. Retraining alone chases a target that moves because the classifier moved. The lesson's question is what the reply looks like when it is cheapest for the adversary, what a classifier that expects it should do, and whether the two settle.
Terms the newcomer does not yet have and must not meet before they are defined: utility (as opposed to cost), log-odds and the log threshold, the gap, camouflage / minimum-cost camouflage, knapsack, pre-image, Nash equilibrium (named once, then set aside), adversary-aware.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **How to write down "the adversary replies".** Obvious attempt: treat the post-deployment data as a new distribution and model it as drift. Why it fails: the new distribution depends on the classifier; change the classifier and the distribution changes again, so there is nothing stationary to re-estimate. Answer: two players with utilities. The classifier picks C and a feature subset X_C to maximise U_C = Σ P(x,y)·U_C(C(A(x)), y) − Σ V_i (measurement costs). The adversary picks a transformation A to maximise U_A = Σ P(x,y)·[U_A(C(A(x)), y) − W(x, A(x))], paying W per feature changed. Only positives are altered; negatives are untouched. Because: this is the smallest model that makes "the adversary's move depends on the classifier's move" explicit.
2. **Which solution concept.** Obvious attempt: solve for a Nash equilibrium. Why it fails: the strategy spaces are doubly exponential in the number of features and equilibrium assumes optimal play by both; a polynomial-time equilibrium exists only for the binary (unit/infinite) edit-cost model (Theorem 2.1). Answer: single-shot sequential play with complete information — the classifier trains as if there were no adversary, the adversary best-responds, the classifier best-responds to that. Because: it is tractable, it matches what happens in deployment (one side moves at a time), and it already shows the mechanism.
3. **The classifier's baseline.** Obvious attempt: any classifier. Answer: cost-sensitive naive Bayes — predict the y_C maximising Σ P(y|x)·U_C(y_C, y) (Eq. 4), which for two classes is "positive iff log-odds LO_C(x) > LT(U_C) = ln[(U_C(−,−) − U_C(+,−)) / (U_C(+,+) − U_C(−,+))]" (Eq. 6). Because: naive Bayes decomposes the log-odds into one additive term per feature, which is what makes the adversary's problem a knapsack; and the threshold moves with the utility matrix (penalty 10 / 100 / 1000 for a false positive), which ties the lesson to `example-dependent-cost`.
4. **The adversary's cheapest way through.** Define gap(x) = LO_C(x) − LT, positive for a message the filter catches. Obvious attempt: change the most suspicious feature. Why it fails: it may be expensive, and the cheapest set of edits may be several cheap ones. Answer: the minimum-cost camouflage MCC(x): choose feature changes x_i → x_i' whose log-odds reductions ΔLO_i sum to at least gap(x) at minimum Σ W_i — a 0-1 knapsack (reduction stated, NP-hard). Then Eq. 7: act only if W(x, MCC(x)) < ΔU_A, the utility gained by passing. Because: an adversary who pays more than the pass is worth leaves the message alone, and the classifier will exploit exactly that.
5. **Computing the knapsack.** Answer: discretise log-odds to a grid, run the dynamic program FindMCC over (feature index, discretised log-odds reduction) with cost O(W · Σ_i |X_i|) — pseudo-linear; two prunings — Lemma 4.1 (if max_i ΔLO_i/W_i < gap(x)/ΔU_A, no affordable set exists, A(x) = x) and keep only the best-gain tuple per cost. In the Add Words cost model (unit cost per word) the optimum is greedy: insert words in decreasing order of |log-odds|. Because: the DP is what makes the game computable at all; the greedy case is what the spammers actually do.
6. **What the classifier should do knowing this.** Obvious attempt: retrain on the camouflaged data. Why it fails: the training set is untainted by assumption (Assumption 4), and retraining only moves the threshold the adversary will re-cross. Answer: change the likelihood, not the data. Under Assumptions 3–5 (the adversary plays its optimum; training data untainted; W is a semi-metric obeying the triangle inequality), only P(x'|+) changes under attack: P_A(x'|+) = Σ_{x: A(x)=x'} P(x|+) = Σ_{x ∈ X_A'(x')} P(x|+) + I(x')·P(x'|+) (Eqs. 8–10), where I(x') = 1 if the observed x' would not have been camouflaged (classified negative already, or too costly). Algorithm 3 classifies x' by comparing utilities with P_A in place of P. Lemma 5.1 says which original features could have been changed to reach x'; Theorem 5.2 (if x' = MCC(x_A) then x' is the MCC of every partially reverted instance — proved by the triangle inequality on W); Corollary 5.3 / Theorem 5.4 give the lower and upper bound on the pre-image mass, the lower bound being what is used. Because: the adversary's optimal strategy is a deterministic map, so its pre-images are computable, and summing their mass is the Bayes-correct likelihood of seeing x'.
7. **Does it work.** Answer: on Ling-Spam with U_C(+,−) = −10, plain naive Bayes under the Add Words attack passes all 481 spam (481 FN, versus 94 untampered); the adversary-aware classifier gets 93 FN (Add Words), 94 (Add Length), 164 (Synonym) with essentially zero false positives; NB-SYN lets 408–414 of 481 through. Utility of the aware classifier stays roughly flat across penalties 10/100/1000 while naive Bayes degrades. Because: an adversary who alters messages removes exactly the ambiguous ones from the negative side, which is why false positives even fall.
8. **Does the back-and-forth settle.** Obvious expectation: both sides converge. The record shows the opposite: in the repeated game (fixed-form adversary re-learns the classifier each round) utility alternates between two values with period 2 and never reaches equilibrium (Fig. 3). Because: "good" words the adversary inserts lose their negative log-odds when the classifier retrains on them, so the adversary drops them, so they regain it. The fraud instances: paying the first bills before ramping up once "big volume on a new account" became a flag; calling dens moving into vans after location models; the $10,000 reporting rule producing $9,900 deposits. Hand-off: a model's own alerts also change what gets labelled — the next lesson.
9. **What the law now expects.** One paragraph: AI Act Art 15(5) requires resilience to data poisoning, model poisoning, adversarial examples / model evasion and confidentiality attacks for high-risk systems; the lesson's game is the "evasion" entry. Forward to `eu-ai-act-fraud`.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- Utility vs cost: the paper maximises utilities with negative entries for errors; module 2 minimised costs. Say once that U = −cost and keep one sign convention in the lesson.
- "Gap" is log-odds minus log threshold, not a probability difference; define LT from the utility matrix first (step 3) so that the penalty 10/100/1000 experiments make sense.
- Knapsack: define it as "choose items under a weight budget to maximise value" in one sentence before the reduction; the reader may not have met it.
- Semi-metric / triangle inequality on W: state it as "editing a feature twice costs at least editing it once to the final value" — it is what makes Theorem 5.2 hold.
- Complete information (Assumption 1) is false in practice; say so when the fraud anecdotes arrive — fraudsters probe to learn the classifier, which is the realistic substitute.
- Naive Bayes is from classical-ml; the lesson uses its additive log-odds without re-deriving Bayes' rule.
- The paper's experiments are spam, not fraud; the lesson is honest that the fraud instances are anecdotal (Provost) and that the computational fraud replay is the module's last lesson.

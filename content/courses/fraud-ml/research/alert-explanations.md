# Explaining an alert: attributions, rules and reason codes — research brief

Lesson: `alert-explanations` (module: governance, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Produce an explanation for an individual alert — Shapley attributions, extracted rules, attention weights, scorecard points — and judge which satisfies an investigator, a customer and a supervisor
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): An alert that says 'score 0.93' tells the investigator nothing to act on, tells the customer nothing they can contest, and tells the supervisor nothing about the logic. What form of explanation does each of the three need, which methods produce them from a boosted ensemble, a network or a scorecard, and which explanations are honest about what the model did?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The previous lesson routed an alert to a person. The person opens it and sees a number. To act, the investigator needs to know what about this payment made it score — which of the hundreds of features, by how much, against what baseline. The customer whose payment was declined has, under GDPR, a right to meaningful information about the logic, and under the AI Act (if the system is high-risk) to the main elements of the decision. The supervisor who audits the bank wants a record of why. Three audiences, three different objects, and a boosted ensemble of hundreds of trees that computes none of them natively. The reader has SHAP from classical-ml and will reach for it; the lesson's job is to say what SHAP gives (an additive attribution), what it does not (a reason the customer can contest, a rule the supervisor can check), and what else exists.
Terms the newcomer does not yet have: attribution (as a per-alert decomposition), baseline/expected value, reason code, rule extraction (decompositional, pedagogical), fidelity, two-stage model, Hinton diagram, attention weight (met in module 7 as a mechanism, not as an explanation).

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **What is missing from "0.93".** Obvious attempt: show the score and the top feature values. Why it fails: a value is not a reason — 14 transactions today means nothing without the customer's usual 2 and the terminal's risk against the base rate 0.003. Answer: the alert restated as deviations with contributions: "terminal risk over 30 days 0.075 where the base is 0.003 (+0.4), 14 transactions today against a usual 2 (+0.3)". Because: an investigator decides by comparing to normal, which is what the module-3 features encode and what an attribution against a baseline makes explicit.
2. **How to decompose an ensemble's score.** Obvious attempt: feature importance of the model. Why it fails: importance is global (which features matter on average), the investigator needs local (which features mattered here). Answer: Shapley attributions — restated, not re-derived: the three axioms (efficiency, symmetry, dummy) and additivity, so the contributions sum from the baseline to the score; sampled Shapley as the cloud default; the top-5 attributions a service returns per prediction. Because: additivity is what lets the investigator read "+0.4 from the terminal" as a share of the decision.
3. **What an attribution does not say.** The claim to state plainly: an attribution is a decomposition of the model's output, not a cause and not a rule; two correlated features share credit arbitrarily; the baseline choice (training mean, the customer's own history) changes every number. Because: the supervisor's and customer's questions are about the logic, not this model's arithmetic.
4. **The scorecard answer.** Answer: with a points scorecard (module 4) the explanation is the card: each attribute's points are the contribution, the baseline is the offset, and the three audiences read the same table. Because: the monotone one-coefficient-per-variable form was chosen in module 4 for this reason; here it pays.
5. **Explaining a network or a forest by a model of it.** Obvious attempt: inspect the weights. Why it fails: a Hinton diagram of 4 hidden × 5 inputs can show a droppable variable (Income) but not a decision. Answer: rule extraction — decompositional (rules from the parts: neurons, trees) or pedagogical (train a tree on the model's own predictions) — judged by accuracy, conciseness and fidelity = (a + d)/(a + b + c + d), the share of cases where the rules and the model agree; backward elimination for variable selection; the two-stage model Score = LR + NN-on-residuals, where the readable part carries most of the score and the opaque part a correction (0.68 + 0.14 = 0.82). Because: a supervisor can check a rule set and cannot check a forest; fidelity says how much of the model the rules represent.
6. **Sequence models.** Answer: attention weights over the customer's last transactions are a per-prediction weighting the model actually used — near one-hot on the last transaction when the context is the last hidden state, near uniform when it is random — so they explain which history counted, not why. Because: the weights are part of the forward pass, which makes them honest about the model and silent about the world.
7. **Graph models and sets of suspects.** Answer: for propagated scores the explanation is the path — the subgraph connecting a suspect to the confirmed frauds (connection subgraphs; for several suspects, a centre-piece subgraph); investigators review about 60 cases, so the explanation is the bottleneck, not the score. Because: a random-walk score has no features to attribute to; its evidence is where the mass came from.
8. **Three audiences, three objects.** The investigator needs a narrative of deviations with contributions (steps 1–2); the customer needs meaningful information about the logic and the main elements of the decision — a reason code built from the top attributions in plain words, checked for what it may not reveal (tipping off, module 10); the supervisor needs the decision record: model version, inputs, attributions, action, who overrode what — and, under PSD2 Art 72(2), more than "the instrument was used" to show authorisation. Because: the law names the audiences and the record, and the previous lesson built the case the record attaches to.
9. **Which explanations are honest.** Answer: an explanation is honest when it reports what the model computed (attribution, attention, points) and says what it does not know (causes, correlated credit, baseline dependence); a pedagogical rule set is honest only with its fidelity attached; a reason code that names a feature the decision did not use is not an explanation. Because: the supervisor will ask whether the explanation would have differed had the model differed.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- SHAP is a prerequisite: restate axioms and additivity in one paragraph, never re-derive the Shapley value; name sampled Shapley as an approximation.
- Baseline: say which (training mean vs the customer's own history) before any number; the investigator's narrative wants the customer baseline, the supervisor's record the model's.
- Correct the fidelity misprint (a+d)/(b+c) → (a+d)/(a+b+c+d) silently in the lesson (the brief records the erratum).
- "Reason code" is a customer-facing object; it must not disclose a suspicion of laundering (tipping-off, module 10) — one sentence, forward.
- Attention weights are not attributions: they weight history positions, not features.
- The legal articles are quoted, not taught; module 10 does that.

## EXERCISE PLAN
1. [warm-up] Given a baseline of 0.007 and five attributions (+0.41, +0.33, +0.12, +0.09, −0.02), write the investigator's two-sentence narrative and check the additivity to 0.93.
2. [boundary probe] Two perfectly correlated features (terminal risk and a copy of it): show that the Shapley attributions split the credit equally and that dropping the copy doubles the other's attribution — what does the reason code say in each case, and which is honest?
3. [claim to refute] "A pedagogical rule set with 95% accuracy on the test set explains the model." Refute: accuracy against labels is not fidelity against the model; construct a case where the rules are accurate and unfaithful.
4. [derivation skipped] Show that for a points scorecard the per-attribute points are exactly the Shapley attributions of the log-odds with the offset as baseline (additive model, no interactions), and state where the equivalence breaks for a boosted ensemble.
5. [research-shaped] On the simulator's test week, extract a pedagogical tree of depth 3 from the XGBoost model's predictions; report fidelity, conciseness (number of leaves) and the recall at k = 100 of the tree alone.
6. [mixed review, E3] For a payment scored by the exposure score of `guilt-by-association`, say why an attribution over features is empty, and build the explanation from the walk instead (the three highest-mass paths).
7. [research-shaped] Compare the attention weights of a trained LSTM on ten simulated customers with the hand-built aggregate that fired: how often does the position the attention weights point at coincide with the window the aggregate summarised?

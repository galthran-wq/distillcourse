# Alert-feedback selection bias and learning from feedbacks plus delayed samples — research brief

Lesson: `alert-feedback-selection-bias` (module: adversary, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Analyze the sample selection bias created by labelling only alerted transactions, show why importance weighting cannot remove it, and derive the feedback-plus-delayed-sample learning strategy
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): The freshest labels a detector gets are the investigators' verdicts on the hundred payments it ranked highest yesterday. Training on them means training on a sample the model chose, in which fraud is far more common and the features look nothing like the stream. Can the bias be weighted away, and if not, how should yesterday's verdicts and last week's complete labels be combined?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
Module 1 established the loop: the model ranks the day, investigators open about a hundred cards, their verdicts come back the same day, and everything else is labelled a week or more later by disputes or by silence. Module 7 said the model must be retrained on a cadence. The question nobody has asked yet: retrained on what? The hundred verdicts are the only fresh labels, and they were chosen by the model. The newcomer has to see three things before any formula: that the chosen hundred have a fraud share of tens of percent where the stream has 0.2%; that their features are the features the model thinks are fraud; and that these two facts are not fixable by the undersampling correction of module 2, because the selection depended on the label through the model.
Terms the newcomer does not yet have: feedbacks, delayed samples, selection variable s, covariate shift / prior shift / complete bias, importance weight, aggregation of posteriors, NCP_k.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **What the labelled data actually is.** Obvious attempt: "the labelled transactions of the last month". Why it fails: on day t only two kinds of labels exist. Answer: alerts A_t = the k top-ranked cards' transactions; feedbacks F_t = all recent transactions of those cards, labelled at once by the investigators (a fraudulent card's other recent transactions are usually fraudulent too); delayed samples D_{t−δ} = every transaction of day t − δ, labelled by disputes, with silence read as genuine; F_{t−δ} ⊂ D_{t−δ}. Because: the loop produces exactly these two streams and nothing else (Fig. 2 timeline, δ = 7).
2. **Why the metric is CP_k, not AUC.** Recalled from `alert-budget-metrics`: P_k = |TP_k|/k; CP_k counts a card once; NCP_k = CP_k / min(1, γ_t/k) with γ_t the day's fraudulent cards, usable only in backtests because γ_t is known after the latency. Worked: 40 of 100 cards caught, 50 fraudulent cards that day → CP 0.4, NCP 0.8. Because: the investigators' day is the unit, and AUC rewards ranking the 399,900 unalerted payments.
3. **Is the feedback set a fair sample.** Obvious attempt: treat F_t as recent labelled data and pool it with D. Why it fails: F_t was chosen by yesterday's classifier as the most fraud-like; its class prior is far higher, its feature distribution is the model's high-score region, and the rows are clustered by card (not independent). Answer: name the three kinds of selection bias with the selection variable s — class-prior bias p(s|y), feature bias p(s|x) (covariate shift), complete bias p(s|x,y). Alert selection is the third because the model's score is a function of x trained to track y. Because: the module-2 correction assumed p(s|y) only — undersampling by design — and that assumption is exactly what alerts violate.
4. **Can the bias be weighted away.** Obvious attempt: importance weighting, w = P(s=1)/P(s=1|x,y) (Eq. 10), which corrects covariate shift when p(y|x) is unchanged by selection. Why it fails here: the selection probability is correlated with P(+|x) itself, so the weights would have to be estimated from the same classifier that caused the bias, and p(y|x) in the selected region is not the stream's. The record: Table IV — weighted F does no better than unweighted F (P_k 0.68 vs 0.73); the sanity check that selects all transactions with amount > 500 at random (pure covariate shift) shows the weighting does work there. Because: a weight can undo a selection that depends on x; it cannot undo one that depends on y.
5. **Why not ask the investigators for informative labels.** Obvious attempt: active learning — query the transactions that would reduce uncertainty most. Why it fails: investigators must check the most suspicious transactions, because every query spent on an informative-but-probably-genuine payment is a fraud not stopped; the loop's objective is detection, not model improvement. Because: the budget k is spent on precision at k, which is the system's reason to exist.
6. **How to use both streams.** Obvious attempt: pool F (last Q days) and D (last M days) and train one model W. Why it fails: D outnumbers F by orders of magnitude, so pooling drowns the fresh, biased-but-relevant feedbacks in stale, unbiased data; and a model trained on F alone has the best alert precision but the worst AUC (Table III, 0.87). Answer: train F_t on Q days of feedbacks and D_t on M days of delayed samples separately and average posteriors, P_A(+|x) = α·P_F(+|x) + (1 − α)·P_D(+|x), α = 0.5 (Algorithm 1; Q = δ + M). Because: averaging gives the feedback model weight it would lose in a pool, and alerts only what both consider fraud — the biased model's confidence is checked by the unbiased one.
7. **Does it work and what it costs.** Table III: AW beats W and E on P_k and CP_k on both datasets (2014–15: P_k 0.77 vs 0.61, CP_k 0.37 vs 0.25); F alone beats W on P_k but is last on AUC; the ideal R (all labels at once) has the best AUC 0.96 and is mid-table on P_k — the best global ranker is not the best top-k ranker. Fig. 5: CP_k swings with the number of fraudulent cards per day, so comparisons use ranks (Friedman) not means. Tables V–VI: δ = 15 with Q = 30 still improves; NCP_k rises from 0.40 to 0.48 as k goes 100 → 300 — the hiring argument. Adaptive α: mean optimal α_t 0.52, so 0.5 stays. Because: the operational metric and the global metric disagree, and the strategy is tuned to the operational one.
8. **A leak the loop creates.** Obvious attempt: keep the card ID as a feature. Why it fails: after the first detected fraud the card is blocked, so an offline backtest that keeps it scores the model on cards the bank would have stopped. Answer: use the card ID only to compute aggregates (module 3), never as a feature; remove known cards from test days (`delayed-labels-prequential`). Because: the backtest must reproduce what the live system could have done.
9. **The general statement.** Closed loops break replay evaluation: a model's alerts change which labels exist, so a history replayed through a new model is not the history that model would have produced (Gama Sec 5). The same loop appears in astronomy (experts label the top-scored objects) and in insurance (small claims never labelled). Hand-off: the module's application replays it on the simulator; `label-maturation` builds the ledger that records what was alerted.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Feedback" means investigator verdicts on alerted cards, not customer feedback; define at first use with F_t.
- "Delayed samples" are not "delayed feedbacks": D is all of day t − δ, F ⊂ D.
- Keep the three bias types apart; the module-2 undersampling is class-prior bias by design, alert selection is complete bias — say so explicitly, because the reader will reach for the β correction.
- The importance weight formula needs P(s=1|x,y), which is unobservable here; say why the sanity check is possible (selection on amount is known) and the real case is not.
- NCP_k is for backtests only; P_k and CP_k are the running metrics.
- Overlapping training windows make daily ranks dependent (the paper's own footnote 3); report ranks and paired tests, do not claim independence.
- The paper's datasets are confidential; the module's application replays the strategy on the simulator.

## EXERCISE PLAN
1. [warm-up] A day has 60 fraudulent cards; the model alerts 100 cards of which 36 are fraudulent. Compute P_k over 150 alerted transactions with 50 true positives, CP_k and NCP_k.
2. [boundary probe] Let k equal the number of transactions in the day (everything is alerted): show F_t = D_t, the selection bias vanishes, and AW reduces to W — which term of the selection model collapsed?
3. [claim to refute] "Feedbacks are just an undersampled training set with a high fraud rate, so the β correction of module 2 restores their probabilities." Refute: write p(s|x,y) for alert selection, show it is not a function of y alone, and name the step of the β derivation that fails.
4. [derivation skipped] Derive the importance weight P(s=1)/P(s=1|x,y) as the factor that makes the selected-sample expectation equal the stream expectation, then show that when s depends on y through a model trained to predict y, estimating the denominator requires the quantity being estimated.
5. [research-shaped] Design the experiment that distinguishes "AW wins because feedbacks are fresh" from "AW wins because averaging two models reduces variance": specify the control (two models on random halves of D, averaged) and what result would decide.
6. [mixed review, E3] Place the feedback-only model F in the drift taxonomy of `concept-drift`: the stream did not change, yet F's training distribution moves every day — which of p(x), p(y), p(y|x) moved, and is it drift?
7. [research-shaped] From Table VI's NCP_k at k = 100/150/300, estimate the marginal fraudulent cards caught per extra investigator-day and state what cost figure from `economics-of-detection` would make hiring break even.
8. FORWARD → `score-to-action`: the alert queue is a top-k cut; compute, on a toy day, how many alerts a probability × amount frontier would raise instead, and which high-probability low-amount alerts it drops — the reader builds the inspection frontier before it is named.

# The AML rulebook II: automated decisions, outsourcing and the supervisor — research brief

Lesson: `aml-automated-decisions-and-supervision` (module: eu-regulation, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Classify what a model may decide alone under the AML Regulation, which decisions can never be outsourced to a vendor, what the financial intelligence unit and the new EU authority may demand to see, and what a breach costs
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A bank buys a vendor's model, lets it close alerts automatically and refuse customers it scores high. Which of those steps can a model make at all under the AML rules, which can never be handed to a vendor, what can the financial intelligence unit and the new EU authority demand to see, and what does a breach cost?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The pipeline has three automatic steps a product owner wants: (a) alerts under a score are closed without a person looking; (b) applicants above a score are refused; (c) the model itself, the rules and the scoring are bought from a vendor who retrains it. The previous lesson said the Art 69(2) assessment is the bank's judgment. The newcomer now needs: whether the law lets a model make any of (a)–(c); if a vendor runs the model, who is liable and what the vendor may never do; what the FIU sends back after a report (the loop's missing half); what a supervisor — national, or the new EU authority AMLA — can open; and the price of getting it wrong. Terms to define at first use: profiling (GDPR Art 4(4), one clause — module-10 lesson 5 does the rest), meaningful human intervention, outsourcing vs purchase (Rec 47), AMLA (Authority for Anti-Money Laundering, Frankfurt, directly supervising up to 40 high-risk groups from 2028), supervisor vs FIU (the one that fines vs the one that receives reports), periodic penalty payment.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **May a model decide at all?** — Obvious attempt: the GDPR right not to be subject to an automated decision forbids it. Why it fails: Rec 150 says the entity "should be able to adopt processes that enable automated individual decision-making, including profiling, as set out under Article 22" with the Regulation's own safeguards on top; Art 76(5) permits "decisions resulting from automated processes, including profiling … or from processes involving AI systems". Answer: yes, on three conditions: (a) the data is "limited to data obtained pursuant to Chapter III" (CDD data), (b) any decision "to enter or refuse to enter into or maintain a business relationship … or to carry out or refuse to carry out an occasional transaction … or to increase or decrease the extent of the customer due diligence measures" is "subject to meaningful human intervention", (c) the customer "may obtain an explanation of the decision … and may challenge that decision, except in relation to a report". Because: the law distinguishes decisions that touch the customer (relationship, transaction, diligence level) from internal triage. Map: auto-closing low-score alerts (a) is not in the (b) list and is lawful triage; refusing an applicant (b) is in the list and needs a human; a model that raises a customer's diligence tier needs a human; the explanation and challenge channels of module 9 are mandatory except for the report itself.
2. **What does "meaningful" rule out?** — Obvious attempt: a person clicks approve on every model refusal. Why it fails: the text says intervention "to ensure the accuracy and appropriateness of such a decision"; Art 21(3) requires the grounds of a refusal recorded; Rec 152 forbids decisions "solely on the basis of" special-category data irrelevant to risk; Rec 53 forbids AML grounds as cover for commercial decisions and refusals to civil-society organisations for geography alone. Answer: the human must be able to see why (the attribution of module 9), to overrule (module 9's referral zone) and to leave a recorded ground. Because: the record is what the supervisor audits. Map: the referral band must exist for every relationship decision; the ledger row holds the human's ground; the AI Act's automation-bias wording (Art 14) will return in lesson 4 of this module.
3. **The vendor's model: who is responsible?** — Obvious attempt: outsourcing transfers the duty. Why it fails: Rec 47: "the responsibility for complying … should remain entirely with the obliged entity" and buying software or using a screening service "are not considered to be outsourcing" — so no notification, but also no transfer; Art 18(2): the entity must "demonstrate to the supervisor that it understands the rationale behind the activities carried out by the service provider"; Art 18(5): outsourcing may not impair the supervisor's ability "to monitor and retrace" compliance. Answer: a bought model is the bank's model; it must be explainable to the bank, and the bank must be able to show the supervisor what it does. Map: the model documentation and the parity checks of modules 9 and 11 are owed by the buyer, not the seller; a black box the vendor will not open fails Art 18(2).
4. **What can never be handed over?** — Obvious attempt: everything but the final signature. Why it fails: Art 18(3): "shall not be outsourced under any circumstances": (a) the business-wide risk assessment, (b) approval of policies, (c) "decision on the risk profile to be attributed to the customer", (d) "the decision to enter into a business relationship or carry out an occasional transaction", (e) "the reporting to FIU", (f) "the approval of the criteria for the detection of suspicious or unusual transactions". Answer: the thresholds, the scenario rules and the model's operating point — the "criteria for the detection" — are approved in-house; the customer's risk tier and the onboarding decision are made in-house; the report is filed in-house. Because: these are the judgments the entity is liable for. Map: a vendor may propose a model and a threshold; the bank's model board (module 9's life cycle) approves the criteria and owns the threshold; the alert queue's cut is a bank decision.
5. **Before a new model goes live** — Obvious attempt: ship, then monitor. Why it fails: Art 10(1) last paragraph: risks of "new products, new business practices, including new delivery channels, and the use of new or developing technologies" are assessed "before" launch. Answer: a risk assessment entry for the model precedes deployment. Map: the champion–challenger stage of module 9 is where that assessment is written.
6. **What comes back from the FIU?** — Obvious attempt: nothing; reports go into a void. Why it fails: AMLD6 Art 28: feedback "at least once per year" on "the quality of the information provided, the timeliness of reporting, the description of the suspicion and the documentation"; Art 24: suspension of a transaction up to 10 working days and of an account up to 5; Art 25: monitoring orders for named persons; Art 26: alerts on risky typologies (max 6 months) and annual typologies; Art 23: the FIU tells the supervisor about report quality. Answer: the loop closes — slowly, aggregated, and in the currency of report quality, not labels. Map: FIU feedback is a label source with a one-year clock (module 11's label-maturation); a monitoring order is a forced alert for a named entity; the typology alerts are feature requests.
7. **Sharing scores between banks** — Obvious attempt: pool alerts with peers to see rings across banks. Why it fails: Art 75 allows partnerships only with safeguards: shared information is never the sole basis for a decision (Rec 147), "information generated through the use of artificial intelligence, machine learning technologies or algorithms may only be shared where those processes were subject to adequate human oversight" (75(4)(g)), a DPIA first (75(4)(h)), every sharing instance recorded. Answer: cross-bank graph signal (module 6's shared-graph result) is lawful inside a notified partnership with human oversight on anything a model produced. Map: an exposure score may be shared only after review; the ledger records each share.
8. **What can the supervisor open?** — Obvious attempt: the policy documents. Why it fails: AMLD6 Art 37(7): unannounced inspection, access to "any software, databases, IT tools or other electronic means of recording information", interviews of outsourcee staff; AMLA Art 18(1)(d): "documents and information relating to decision-making processes, including those developed by algorithms or other digital processes"; AMLA Art 18(1)(c): the same software and databases. Answer: the model, its training data, its thresholds and its logs are inspectable in statute. Because: the Regulation's risk-based approach is "not an unduly permissive option" (Rec 52) and the supervisor checks the evidence. Map: documentation that a new team could continue from (module 9's documentation test) is the supervisory deliverable.
9. **The price** — Obvious attempt: a fine is a cost of doing business. Why it fails: AMLD6 Art 55(3): for a bank at least EUR 10 000 000 or 10% of total annual turnover (consolidated), whichever is higher; Art 57: periodic penalties up to 3% of average daily turnover for 6 months, extendable 6; AMLA Art 22 and Annex I for directly supervised groups: base EUR 500 000–2 000 000 or 1% of turnover, cumulative multipliers (repeat 1.1, over six months 1.5, systemic weaknesses 2.2, intentional 3, no remedial action 1.7, management non-cooperation 1.5; mitigating 0.7 / 0.4 / 0.6), capped at 10% of turnover. Answer: carry the arithmetic: a group with EUR 2 bn turnover, base 1% = EUR 20 m, systemic 2.2 × no remedial action 1.7 = EUR 74.8 m, under the EUR 200 m cap. Map: the model risk of module 9 has a price tag; the sanction factors of Art 53 (gravity, duration, repetition, cooperation, prior breaches) are a rubric.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- AMLR Art 76(5) cites the AI Act as "Regulation (EU) 2024/xxx" with footnote 45 "not yet published" — the staged text predates the AI Act's OJ number; cite 2024/1689 and say the cross-reference is in the footnote.
- Art 76(5)(c)'s exception "except in relation to a report" means the customer cannot obtain an explanation of, or challenge, the filing of a report — do not read it as an exception to human intervention.
- Keep "outsourcing" (Art 18, notification, written agreement, controls) apart from "reliance" (Arts 48–50, another entity's CDD) and from "purchase" (Rec 47).
- The AMLD6 fines are minimum maxima ("at least"); Member States may go higher (Art 55(4)); AMLA's Art 22 applies only to selected entities.
- Dates: AMLA tasks from 1 July 2025, first selection by 1 July 2027, direct supervision 2028, AMLA guidelines on outsourcing by 10 July 2027 — original OJ dates.
- No AMLA guidelines, EBA opinions or case law are in the dossier; the lesson may name the Art 18(8) guidelines as pending, nothing more.
- "Meaningful human intervention" is not defined in the text; the lesson must not invent a test beyond the clause's own purpose ("accuracy and appropriateness") and Art 21(3)'s recorded grounds.

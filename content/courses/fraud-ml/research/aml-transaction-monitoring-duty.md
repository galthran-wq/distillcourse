# The AML rulebook I: what must be monitored, reported and recorded — research brief

Lesson: `aml-transaction-monitoring-duty` (module: eu-regulation, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Identify in the AML Regulation every obligation that defines what a transaction-monitoring system must watch, what counts as suspicion, what must be reported to the financial intelligence unit and when, and what record must exist for every alert
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): From July 2027 every bank, payment firm and crypto exchange in the Union is bound by one regulation to watch its customers' transactions and report suspicion. What exactly does that duty require a monitoring system to look at, what counts as suspicion, what must be filed and when, and what record must exist for every alert, including the ones closed as nothing?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The engineer has a pipeline: features as of each transaction, a model, a ranked alert queue cut at k, investigators who close alerts, labels that come back late. They now learn that from 10 July 2027 one directly applicable Regulation, not 27 national transpositions of a Directive, binds every bank, payment institution, e-money issuer and crypto-asset service provider in the Union as an "obliged entity" (the gatekeeper the law conscripts). The questions the pipeline cannot answer on its own: which transactions must be looked at at all (all of them? only large ones?), what the law means by "suspicion" (is a score of 0.93 a suspicion?), who the report goes to and how fast, what the bank may do with the transaction meanwhile, and what has to be written down about an alert the investigator closed as nothing.

Legal terms the newcomer does not have, each defined at first use: obliged entity (Art 3; the bank as gatekeeper); customer due diligence, CDD (Art 20: identify, verify, understand purpose, monitor); business relationship (Art 2(1)(19): an element of repetition or duration); financial intelligence unit, FIU (AMLD6 Art 19: the single national unit that receives reports and analyses them); suspicious transaction report, STR (the Art 69 report; the course uses the common acronym); recital vs article (a recital states purpose and is interpretive; an article binds); directive vs regulation (transposed vs directly applicable); AMLA (the new EU authority, Regulation 2024/1620 — one sentence here, its powers next lesson).

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **Which transactions must the system watch?** — Obvious attempt: watch the big ones; the CDD thresholds (EUR 10 000 occasional, EUR 1 000 transfers, EUR 3 000 cash) look like a monitoring threshold. Why it fails: those thresholds trigger identification, not monitoring; Art 26(1) says monitoring covers "transactions undertaken by the customer throughout the course of a business relationship" and Rec 72 says the system "should in principle cover all services and products". Answer: every transaction of every relationship enters the system; but "not all transactions need to be scrutinised individually" — intensity is risk-based, "designed around precise and relevant criteria". Because: the law separates coverage (total) from scrutiny (proportionate), which is exactly the architecture of modules 1 and 9 — everything is scored, k are opened. Map: the scoring job reads the whole stream; the queue cut and the zone thresholds are the "intensity".
2. **Against what is a transaction judged?** — Obvious attempt: against the population (global outliers). Why it fails: Art 26(1) requires transactions to be "consistent with the obliged entity's knowledge of the customer, the customer's business activity and risk profile", and Art 25 requires the bank to have obtained, at onboarding, the purpose, "estimated activity" and source and destination of funds. Answer: the baseline is the customer's own declared and observed profile — the per-entity context of module 3 and the contextual-anomaly framing of module 5, now a legal requirement. Because: the law was written around the "know your customer" idea; a model that lacks per-customer features cannot satisfy it. Map: the feature store must hold the expected-activity fields from onboarding beside the behavioural aggregates; group-wide information (Art 26(1) third subparagraph) must be joinable.
3. **What counts as suspicion?** — Obvious attempt: a score above a threshold. Why it fails: Art 69(1)(a) binds on "knows, suspects or has reasonable grounds to suspect … regardless of the amount"; Art 69(2) says what a suspicion is based on: "the characteristics of the customer and their counterparts, the size and nature of the transaction or activity or the methods and patterns thereof, the link between several transactions or activities, the origin, destination or use of funds, … including the consistency of the transaction or activity with the information obtained pursuant to Chapter III including the risk profile of the client." Art 34(2) adds the four triggers for examination: complex; unusually large; unusual pattern; no apparent economic or lawful purpose. Answer: suspicion is a human judgment formed on an assessment; a score is one input to the assessment, and the assessment itself (Art 69(2)) is the thing the law names. Because: the report is filed by the compliance officer (Art 69(6)) on the entity's judgment, not by a model. Map: the alert explanation of module 9 is what the investigator's assessment reads; Art 69(2)'s list is a feature taxonomy — counterparty characteristics, size, patterns, links between transactions, origin/destination — and every one of those is already in the course (graph features, aggregates, sequence, conditional sums).
4. **How does the law see structuring?** — Obvious attempt: thresholds are per transaction. Why it fails: Art 2(1)(20) defines "linked transactions" (identical or similar origin, destination, purpose or characteristics over a specific period) and Art 19(1) applies thresholds to "a single transaction or linked transactions"; Rec 136 says suspicion covers "patterns of transactions". Answer: the legal object includes the pattern; the pipeline must aggregate. Because: the reporting threshold is exactly the adversary's target (module 1's smurfing, module 8's cheapest edit). Map: windowed sums per counterparty pair and the laundering motifs of module 6.
5. **What happens when a suspicion is formed?** — Obvious attempt: block the account and tell the customer. Why it fails: Art 71(1): refrain from the transaction "until they have submitted a report", then may proceed if no contrary instruction "within 3 working days"; Art 71(2): if refraining is impossible or "would be likely to frustrate efforts to pursue the beneficiaries", execute and inform immediately after; Art 73(1): never disclose to the customer that an assessment or analysis is or was being carried out. Answer: a report, a hold with a three-working-day clock, silence toward the customer; the FIU can extend the hold (AMLD6 Art 24: up to 10 working days for a transaction, 5 for an account). Because: the report protects the investigation, and good faith shields the bank (Art 72). Map: the action router of module 9 gets a fourth action, "hold and report", with a timer; the customer-facing explanation of module 9 must not reveal an AML assessment (contrast with a fraud decline, which may be explained).
6. **How fast, and to whom?** — Obvious attempt: a monthly batch of reports. Why it fails: "promptly" (Art 69(1)); FIU requests answered "within 5 working days", "less than 24 hours" when urgent (Art 69(1) third subparagraph); the report goes to the FIU of the Member State of establishment (Art 69(6)); the format will be an AMLA technical standard by 10 July 2026 (Art 69(3)). Answer: a per-case workflow with clocks, not a batch. Map: the case-management queue of module 9 with SLA timers; the transaction-record format must be exportable in the standard format.
7. **What must be written down?** — Obvious attempt: keep the reports. Why it fails: Art 77(1)(b): "a record of the assessment undertaken pursuant to Article 69(2), including the information and circumstances considered and the results of such assessment, whether or not such assessment results in a suspicious transaction report being made" plus a copy of any report; records "are not redacted"; kept 5 years from the end of the relationship (Art 77(3)), up to 5 more on request; Art 78: systems able to answer in full and speedily whether a relationship existed in the prior five years. Answer: every alert's disposition — including false positives — is a legal record with the facts considered. Because: the supervisor and the FIU reconstruct the bank's judgment from it. Map: the alert ledger (module 9's audit trail; module 11's alert-disposition record) is the Art 77(1)(b) record; the "circumstances considered" are the explanation attached to the alert; the feedback label and the legal record are the same row.
8. **How often must the profile itself be refreshed?** — Obvious attempt: once at onboarding. Why it fails: Art 26(2): updates at most every 1 year for higher-risk customers and 5 years for others; Art 26(3): event-driven review (changes in jurisdictions, value or volume, new products); Art 33(5): simplified diligence stops when monitoring contradicts the low-risk assumption. Answer: the customer's risk tier is itself a time series fed by the monitor. Map: a drift-like trigger from monitoring back into the customer risk profile — the module 7 loop at the customer level.

### Set pieces and worked examples to recreate
- The one-sentence coverage rule: "should in principle cover all services and products … not all transactions need to be scrutinised individually" set beside the course's "score everything, open k" — the architecture of module 1 restated by the legislator (AMLR Rec 72; regulationeu-s1-amlr-recitals.txt line 163).
- Art 69(2) as a feature taxonomy: lay the sentence out as a table mapping each clause to a course feature family — counterparty characteristics (module 6 node features), size and nature (raw and aggregate), methods and patterns (sequence, module 7), links between transactions (graph, linked transactions), origin/destination/use (travel-rule fields, next-but-one lesson), consistency with the profile (module 3 deviations) (AMLR Art 69(2); regulationeu-s4-amlr-ch4-end.txt line ~300).
- The Art 34(2) four triggers as four anomaly types already met: complex (collective), unusually large (point, relative to the profile), unusual pattern (sequence/contextual), no apparent purpose (the one no feature computes) (AMLR Art 34(2); regulationeu-s3-amlr-ch2-3.txt line 521).
- The clocks on one timeline: suspicion → hold → report "promptly" → 3 working days of silence allowed before executing (Art 71(1)) → FIU may suspend a transaction up to 10 working days or an account up to 5 (AMLD6 Art 24) → FIU request answered within 5 working days, or under 24 h (Art 69(1)); application date 10 July 2027 (Art 90); formats by 10 July 2026 (Art 69(3)); indicators guidelines by 10 July 2027 (Art 69(5)).
- Thresholds that are CDD triggers, not monitoring thresholds: EUR 10 000 occasional, EUR 1 000 transfers and crypto, EUR 3 000 cash, EUR 2 000 gambling (AMLR Art 19; regulationeu-s3-amlr-ch2-3.txt), with "single or linked" (Art 2(1)(20)) — and the EUR 10 000 cash cap of Art 80, a limit rather than a trigger.
- The Art 77(1)(b) record built from a module-9 alert: score, attributions, action, investigator's note, date — then the counterfactual: delete the closed-as-nothing rows and show which Art 77 clause is breached and what the FIU can no longer ask under Art 78.
- "A suspicious transaction is not an indication of criminal activity" (Rec 145) beside the module-2 precision numbers: at CP@100 ≈ 0.24 three alerts in four are false, and the law expects it — which is why the tipping-off ban exists.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- Recitals are not binding; quote operative articles for every "must" and recitals only for purpose (Rec 72 explains Art 26, it does not add duties).
- Do not call the report a "SAR": the Regulation's object is the Art 69 report of a suspicion; the course calls it a suspicious transaction report (STR) and says once that national practice uses other names.
- Art 69 has no amount threshold; the Art 19 thresholds are CDD triggers and the Art 74 thresholds (EUR 250 000 vehicles, EUR 7 500 000 watercraft and aircraft) are a separate goods-traders duty — never conflate the three.
- "Linked transactions" is a defined term (Art 2(1)(20)); the period over which linking is judged is not fixed in the text.
- Dates are the original OJ dates (application 10 July 2027; Art 3(3)(n),(o) entities from 10 July 2029); the human checks for amendments at write time.
- The dossier holds no AMLA guidelines, no FATF text and no case law; the lesson may say only that AMLA guidelines on monitoring intensity (Rec 72) and on indicators (Art 69(5)) are due, not what they will say.
- Art 73's tipping-off ban applies to AML assessments; a card-fraud decline under PSD2 is a different regime and may be explained to the customer — keep the two apart or the module-9 explanation lesson contradicts this one.
- The staged texts have flattened lists (lettered points on their own lines) — quote with the letters restored.

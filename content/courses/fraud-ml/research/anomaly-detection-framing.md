# Anomaly types, label regimes and the assumption behind each detector — research brief

Lesson: `anomaly-detection-framing` (module: anomaly, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Classify anomalies as point, contextual or collective and detectors by label regime and key assumption, and predict from the assumption which detector fails on which data geometry
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A supervised detector finds the kinds of fraud it was shown. A new scheme produces payments unlike any labelled fraud, and the only thing known about them is that they are unlike the normal ones. What does 'unlike normal' mean for a payment, what has to be assumed about normal data to detect departures from it, and when does each assumption break?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader has a supervised detector from module 4 that, on the simulator, catches the three scripted scenarios and, on Worldline data, scores 0.23 card precision at 100. A fraud ring starts a scheme nobody has labelled — say card testing with EUR 1 payments on a merchant the model has never seen flagged. Every labelled fraud the model learned from looks different, so the model scores the new scheme low. The investigators find it a month later, by hand. The bank asks: could the detector have raised it earlier, with no label, on the grounds that it looked like nothing the cardholder or the merchant normally does? That question needs three things the newcomer does not yet have: a definition of "unlike normal" that a computer can evaluate (not yet: point / contextual / collective anomaly); a statement of which data a detector is allowed to learn "normal" from (not yet: label regime); and, for each way of scoring departure, the one assumption it makes about what normal data looks like (not yet: detector assumption). Terms that must not appear before they are defined: anomaly score, contextual attribute, semi-supervised, one-class.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **What is an anomaly, for a payment?** — Obvious attempt: "a payment with an unusual value", i.e. an outlier in one column (amount above the 99.9th percentile). Why it fails: most frauds in the card data are not extreme in any column (the handbook's scenario 2 frauds have ordinary amounts; the PaySim frauds have no extreme amounts); and genuine December spending is extreme without being fraud. Answer: three kinds of departure — a *point* anomaly (the instance differs from everything else), a *contextual* anomaly (it differs only given a context: EUR 1,000 a week is normal in December for this cardholder and anomalous in July; the same value normal for one cardholder and alarming for another), a *collective* anomaly (a group of related instances is anomalous together though no member is: a run of EUR 1 payments at one merchant, a sustained low stretch in an ECG). Because: fraud in a card stream is almost always contextual or collective — the cardholder, the terminal, the hour are the context — which is why module 3's profile features already turned contextual anomalies into point anomalies in feature space.
2. **Where does the definition of "normal" come from?** — Obvious attempt: learn it from labelled genuine rows as a classifier learns. Why it fails: the labelled genuine rows are labelled "genuine" only because nobody complained (verification latency, module 1) — some are undiscovered fraud; and a classifier trained on two classes finds only the second class it saw. Answer: three label regimes. Supervised: labels for both classes (the module-4 detectors; needs representative fraud labels, which new schemes never have). Semi-supervised: labels for normal only — model normal, flag departures; the most widely applicable because a bank can always say what the last 90 days of unflagged behaviour looked like. Unsupervised: no labels at all, with the implicit assumption that normal is far more frequent than anomalous. Because: the regime decides what the detector can learn and what it will miss; the unsupervised assumption fails when anomalies are bulk (worm traffic; a flood of card testing).
3. **Which cardholder is the yardstick?** — Obvious attempt: one global model of normal payments. Why it fails: Breiman's cardholder G.B.S. — his normal weeks form a fuzzy ball; a boundary around *everyone's* normal is so wide that his takeover week sits inside it. Answer: by-owner detection (a profile per cardholder: expensive central lookup, catches account takeover) vs by-operation detection (anomalies among the transactions at one location or merchant: cheap, catches a compromised terminal); both are contextual anomaly detection with a different context. Because: fraud is activity monitoring — the departure is from *this* entity's pattern — which is what module 3's peer-group and break-point features encoded (recall with Ref, do not re-teach).
4. **The two fuzzy balls: what an unsupervised boundary buys and loses.** — Obvious attempt: once normal is modelled, anything outside is fraud. Why it fails: outside-normal is not toward-fraud; a point outside the normal ball may be a holiday purchase. Answer: the supervised picture draws a second ball (fraud weeks) and discriminates between the two — fewer false alarms, but it assumes future fraud resembles past fraud and other accounts' fraud; the unsupervised picture draws one ball and flags everything outside it — it finds the new scheme and also every honest surprise. Because: that is exactly the trade the module's numbers will show (AUC decent, precision poor, lesson 4–5), so state it as the expectation now.
5. **Every detector family rests on one assumption, and the assumption is its failure condition.** — Obvious attempt: pick the method with the best benchmark number. Why it fails: the benchmark's geometry decides the winner. Answer: nearest-neighbour methods assume normal instances occur in dense neighbourhoods and anomalies far from their neighbours; clustering methods assume normal instances belong to large dense clusters; statistical methods assume normal instances sit in high-probability regions of a fitted model; classification (one-class) methods assume a boundary around normal can be learned; spectral methods assume a lower-dimensional subspace separates them. The three 2-D thought experiments: (a) a tight cluster plus distant anomalies — everything works; (b) normal points on a ring of tight clusters with anomalies at the centre — a one-class circular boundary encloses the centre and misses them, neighbour and clustering methods find them; (c) a tight anomalous *cluster* at the centre, of significant size — neighbour and clustering methods call it normal. Because: each assumption names the geometry on which it is wrong, so the lesson's deliverable is a table (family, assumption, fails when) the reader can apply before running anything.
6. **Score or label?** — Obvious attempt: output "anomalous / not". Why it fails: the investigators have a budget of k (module 1–2); a binary flag either floods them or hides what they could have checked. Answer: a score and a ranking, cut at k, with the module-2 metrics. Because: that is the only output the loop can consume.
7. **Why anomalies are not fraud, and what follows.** — Answer: an anomaly is a reason to look, not a verdict; descriptive (unsupervised) finds, predictive (supervised) explains and confirms; the adoption order in practice is rules → descriptive → predictive → network. Because: this sets up lesson 4's fusion question and module 6.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Anomaly", "outlier", "novelty", "noise" are not synonyms: noise is to be removed or accommodated, a novelty gets folded into the normal model, an anomaly is of interest in itself — define in the first section.
- "Semi-supervised" here means normal-only labels, not the classical-ml meaning (few labels plus many unlabelled); say so at first use.
- "Contextual attribute" vs "behavioural attribute": the cardholder id and the hour are contextual, the amount is behavioural — introduce in sub-problem 1, since the whole of module 3 was the act of turning contextual attributes into features.
- "Rarity assumption": the newcomer will assume anomaly detection needs no assumptions; the lesson's point is that unsupervised detection assumes normal dominates.
- Do not name the detector methods (LOF, Isolation Forest, autoencoder) before lesson 2–4; here they are families with assumptions.

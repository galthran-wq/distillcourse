# Contrastive embeddings of event sequences — research brief

Lesson: `contrastive-event-embeddings` (module: learned-representations, type: core, status: planned). NEW — no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Construct a self-supervised training task for a sequence encoder from unlabelled histories — random slices of one customer as positives, other customers' slices as negatives under a margin loss with hard negatives — and explain why the task fails when the encoder can recognise the customer from a static field
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): There are two years of events for two million customers and no label for the task at hand. A network can only be trained to do something that the data itself can grade. What can it be asked to do with a customer's history alone, so that what it learns about the history is useful later for a task nobody has named yet, and what is the cheap way for it to cheat?

Status: CoLES is peer-reviewed (SIGMOD 2022, code released for the public tasks); the Temporal Contrastive Transformer is an industry preprint (NICE Actimize, arXiv 2605.21490) on one proprietary split with no intervals; pytorch-lifestream is the reference implementation (IJCAI 2025 demo paper plus repository docs). The lab's spike (research/representations/feasibility-spike.py) is this session's own single-seed run.

## Sources to consult
- CoLES (research/texts/coles.txt): Sec 3.1 (each customer as a latent process of which one finite sequence is observed, so there are no ready-made positive pairs), Sec 3.2 and Algorithm 1 (random slices: k slices, length drawn uniformly, kept only if within [m, M], start uniform; discard rather than clip), Fig 2 / Sec 4.0.2 (the KL test: event-type histograms of two slices of the same customer are closer than slices of different customers; text posts show no such gap — the negative control), Sec 3.3 (batch of N customers × K slices; the loss L = ½Y d² + ½(1−Y) max(0, ρ − d)²; unit-norm embeddings so d² = 2 − 2 c_u·c_v and the batch loss is a similarity matrix; margin ρ = 0.5), Sec 3.4 (event encoder = per-categorical embeddings concatenated with batch-normed numerics; GRU with learnt c₀; the last state is the embedding), Table 2 (slices beat non-contiguous samples and disjoint segments on all four tasks), Table 3 (GRU ≈ LSTM; a transformer encoder worse — churn 0.780 vs 0.812–0.823, assess 0.542 vs ~0.62), Table 4 (the margin loss matches or beats binomial deviance, histogram, triplet), Table 5 (hard negatives beat random: age 0.639 vs 0.626, assess 0.618 vs 0.593), Fig 3 (quality saturates with embedding size), Sec 4.0.4 (64 customers × 5 slices × ~90 events ≈ 28,800 events per batch, 142 ms on one P100), Sec 4.3.1 (the GRU updates a stored embedding from the new event alone; 4-bit quantisation shrinks 256 floats to 128 bytes).
- Temporal Contrastive Transformer (research/texts/temporalcontr-arxiv-2605.21490.txt): Sec 3.3 and 6.3 (the identity shortcut: static entity attributes let the contrastive loss be solved by recognising the entity; the fix was dynamic fields only), Sec 3.4 (predictive contrastive coding: predict the next window's code from the context and contrast it against in-batch negatives — the second family of contrastive task), Table 1 (XGBoost on 30 raw features 0.9245 ROC-AUC; raw + embeddings 0.9205; embeddings alone 0.8644; one chronological split, proprietary business-payment data, no intervals), Sec 6.2 (their own reading: the embedding recaptures what the aggregates already held).
- pytorch-lifestream (research/texts/pytorch-lifestream-docs.txt and -ijcai2025.txt): the event-per-row → sequence-per-row reshaping and `PaddedBatch`; the `target_` prefix pitfall (an array not marked as a target is sliced and shuffled by the augmentations); Table 1 of the paper (CoLES 0.7921 / 0.640 / 0.841 on scoring / age / churn against hand-made 0.7792 / 0.629 / 0.827; CPC 0.792 and RTD 0.771 on churn, below hand-made features); the `coles-emb.ipynb` and `coles-catboost.ipynb` demo notebooks the lab is modelled on.

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The previous lesson promised a vector per customer learned without labels and left the "how" open. The newcomer knows a loss as something computed against a label. The lesson must build, from scratch, the idea of a task the data grades itself: what the positives are when there is one sequence per customer, why the obvious tasks (predict the label, reconstruct the row) are either unavailable or wrong, what keeps the solution from collapsing, why negatives must be chosen, and why a static field is the cheap way to cheat. It ends with the frozen vector handed to a boosted tree, scored on the same benchmark as last lesson.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **What can the data grade?** — Obvious attempt: reconstruct each transaction from itself (an autoencoder, module 5). Why it fails: it learns the row, not the customer; module 5 showed the reconstruction error adds nothing to a supervised model. Second attempt: predict the next event (the next lesson's family). Answer for this lesson: ask whether two pieces of history come from the same customer. Because: that question has a correct answer in the data, with no label, and answering it requires knowing what is characteristic of a customer — which is what a downstream task wants.
2. **Where do the pairs come from when each customer has one sequence?** — Obvious attempt: compare a customer with themself (trivial). Why it fails: no learning. Answer: cut the one sequence into random contiguous slices — length drawn at random, kept only within [m, M], start at random, k slices per customer — and call slices of the same customer a positive pair, slices of different customers a negative pair. Because: a person's behaviour repeats, so two windows of their life look alike — and that is a checkable assumption: the event-type histograms of two slices of the same customer sit closer in KL divergence than slices of two customers, while two halves of a text post do not. Contrast the alternatives measured: non-contiguous random samples and disjoint segments both do worse; an augmentation that changes nothing (dropout only) teaches nothing.
3. **What loss turns pairs into training?** — Obvious attempt: pull positives together. Why it fails: every embedding goes to one point and the loss is zero. Answer: ½Y d² for positives, ½(1−Y) max(0, ρ − d)² for negatives — negatives are pushed only until they are ρ apart and then released. Because: the margin is what forbids collapse and what stops the model spending capacity on negatives already far away. Worked example: four slices from two customers, distances in a 2×2 table, the loss computed line by line for ρ = 0.5.
4. **How is the whole batch scored at once?** — Answer: restrict embeddings to the unit sphere; then d² = 2 − 2 c_u·c_v and a batch of N × K slices needs one matrix product for every pairwise distance. Because: this is what makes the next step cheap. Batch arithmetic: 64 customers × 5 slices of ~90 events ≈ 28,800 events per step.
5. **Which negatives?** — Obvious attempt: all pairs in the batch. Why it fails: most negatives are already beyond the margin and contribute nothing. Answer: for each slice take the nearest other-customer slices as negatives (hard negatives), read off the same distance matrix. Because: the loss concentrates on the confusable customers; measured, hard negatives beat random ones on every public task (0.639 vs 0.626; 0.618 vs 0.593).
6. **What reads the slice?** — Answer: an event encoder (an embedding per categorical field, the numerics normalised, concatenated) feeding a GRU; the last state is the slice's vector. Because: a recurrent cell carries a state that one new event updates — in production the stored embedding is advanced by the new transaction without re-reading the history; a transformer must re-read it. Measured, GRU and LSTM tie and a transformer encoder is worse on these tasks (0.780 vs 0.81–0.82 on churn) — a small-data, long-sequence result, not a law; name the quantisation to 128 bytes per customer as the storage argument.
7. **How can the encoder cheat?** — Answer: if a static field (customer type, region, an age bucket) is fed in, two slices of the same customer share it exactly and the loss is solved by reading the field — the identity shortcut. The embedding then encodes who the customer is, not what they do, and adds nothing on a downstream task. Fix: dynamic fields only. Because: a self-supervised task is only as good as the shortcuts it forbids — the same logic as the label column left out of pretraining in the next lesson.
8. **What is the vector worth?** — Answer: frozen and handed to a boosted tree on the previous lesson's benchmark: 0.773 against the aggregates' 0.783 at full labels and 0.785 against 0.781 on the 69K-client cut; on the public tasks it beats hand-crafted features on churn (0.843 vs 0.825), ties on three and loses on one; on a business-payment set with 30 raw features it adds nothing (0.9205 vs 0.9245, with 0.8644 alone — a real signal that is redundant). Because: the embedding recaptures what windows over counts, recency and novelty already express; it adds where they do not. Which is the regime rule of the previous lesson, now with the mechanism visible.
9. **Does the order matter to this encoder?** — Answer: on the lab's data, shuffling each customer's event order before encoding moves the embedding's score from 0.689 to 0.678 and the rank-average from 0.713 to 0.707 — little. Because: the published dates are jittered with only the hour kept, so local order is already noise there, and the contrastive task asks "same customer?", which a bag of events answers almost as well; module 7 showed where order carries signal (card-present bursts). The reader must not conclude order never matters.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Positive pair" and "negative pair" are new; define before the loss and never as "anchor/positive/negative" triplet vocabulary unless the triplet loss is being contrasted.
- "Collapse": name it as what happens with no negative term, then show the margin fixing it; a reader who has only seen supervised losses will not expect a trivial minimiser.
- "Hard negative": define as a distance rank, not as a difficult customer.
- The transformer-worse result is on four small public datasets with sequences in the hundreds; the next two lessons use transformers at scale with other objectives. Say both, once.
- The TCT null result is one split of proprietary data with ROC-AUC only; quote the three numbers with that frame and do not call it fraud-on-cards (it is business-payment anomaly detection).
- Keep "contrastive" (same vs different customer) and "predictive contrastive" (predict the next window's code) apart; the second is one paragraph, not a section.
- The KL test uses event-type histograms: say which field.

# Distance and density scores: kth-neighbour distance, LOF and Mahalanobis — research brief

Lesson: `density-and-distance-scores` (module: anomaly, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Compute kth-nearest-neighbour, local-outlier-factor and Mahalanobis anomaly scores and explain the varying-density failure that LOF repairs
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): The simplest way to score how unusual a payment is: measure how far it sits from its nearest neighbours in feature space. A point at the edge of a sparse but legitimate group then scores higher than a true outlier beside a dense group. How do you score distance relative to the local crowd, and what do the statistical versions of the same idea assume?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader has the simulator's 14 standardised features per transaction and no labels. They try the first thing: for each payment, the distance to its nearest other payments; far from everything means unusual. On the real stream there are cardholders who pay at the same shop every morning (a dense knot of near-identical rows) and cardholders who travel (a wide, sparse cloud). A stolen card used once beside the morning-shop knot sits at distance 0.3 from its neighbours; the traveller's ordinary Lisbon purchase sits at distance 3 from hers. The global distance ranks the traveller first. What the newcomer lacks: a notion of density that is *local* (not yet: local reachability density, LOF), a sense of what the number k does, and a way to read the familiar z-score and box-plot rules as the same idea under a distributional assumption (not yet: Mahalanobis distance, Grubbs' test).

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **A first score from distances.** — Obvious attempt: distance to the single nearest neighbour. Why it fails: a duplicate transaction (a retry) has distance 0 to its twin and hides; a pair of frauds hide each other. Answer: the distance to the kth nearest neighbour with k > 1 (or, dually, the count of neighbours within a radius d — fix d and use 1/count, or fix the count and use 1/d). Because: a single twin cannot mask a point once k exceeds the size of any small anomalous group; and the radius form is a crude density estimate, which names what we are really measuring.
2. **What this costs.** — Obvious attempt: compute all pairwise distances. Why it fails: O(N²) — 400,000 payments a day is 1.6×10¹¹ distances, not computable before the next day. Answer: pruning (abandon a point's neighbour search once its current kth distance drops below the best score found so far), partition-based bounds for a top-n list, sub-sampling (O(MN)), and the warning that tree indexes degrade with dimension. Because: anomaly scores only need the top of the ranking (module 2's k), and that is what pruning exploits — and the cost argument is what the next lesson's method removes entirely.
3. **The varying-density failure.** — Walk the two-cluster picture: a sparse cluster C1 and a dense cluster C2; p1 far from everything is caught; p2 just outside C2 has a kth-neighbour distance smaller than any point inside C1, so it ranks below honest C1 points. Why: the score compares distances across regions whose typical spacing differs by a factor of ten. Answer: compare each point's density to its neighbours' densities — the local outlier factor, LOF = (average local density of the k neighbours) / (own local density), with local density = k / (volume of the smallest ball holding the k neighbours). LOF ≈ 1 inside any cluster however sparse, large for p2. Because: the ratio cancels the region's own scale; the obvious global threshold cannot. Carry the arithmetic on the toy: p2's density vs the C2 points' density gives a ratio of several; a C1 edge point's ratio stays near 1.
4. **Variants and what the family still cannot see.** — COF follows a line-shaped structure (connectivity instead of a ball); ODIN uses in-degree in the kNN graph; LOCI finds anomalous micro-clusters. What all miss: a dense cluster of anomalies larger than k — card testing on one merchant creates exactly that. Because: the family's assumption is "anomalies are far from their neighbours", and a ring of fraudsters are each other's neighbours.
5. **Clustering as the same idea at a coarser grain.** — Obvious attempt: run k-means, call the smallest clusters anomalous. Why it fails in one form and works in another: assumption (1) normal points belong to a cluster and anomalies to none — needs an algorithm that leaves points unassigned (DBSCAN); (2) normal points lie near their centroid, score = distance to centroid — fails when anomalies form their own cluster; (3) normal points lie in large dense clusters, anomalies in small or sparse ones (CBLOF combines cluster size and distance). Because: clustering evaluates a point against its cluster, kNN against its neighbourhood; test-time cost is a handful of centroids instead of N — the price is that any algorithm forcing every point into a cluster hides anomalies inside large clusters (module-final lesson's k-means result is this).
6. **The statistical version: when "far" has units.** — Obvious attempt: a point is anomalous if its z-score exceeds 3. Why it is the same idea: under a Gaussian model, μ ± 3σ holds 99.7% of the data, the 1.5-IQR box-plot fence holds 99.3% — the two textbook rules coincide on Gaussian data, and both are "low probability under the fitted model". Grubbs' test makes it a hypothesis test: z = |x − x̄|/s against a t-distribution critical value at α/(2N), iterating by removal. Multivariate: Mahalanobis (x − x̄)ᵀ S⁻¹ (x − x̄) is the z-score with the covariance removed — the ellipsoid, not the sphere; the 500-vs-505 standardisation lesson is the special case S = diag. Because: a distributional assumption buys a score with a confidence level and no k, and loses everything when the data are not Gaussian (heavy-tailed amounts) or high-dimensional.
7. **Profiles and subspaces.** — Histogram profiles per attribute (the NIDES-style frequency score) miss the rare *combination* of common values — a common merchant at a common hour for an uncommon cardholder; the robust-PCA score Σ_{i≤q} y_i²/λ_i over the leading q components is χ²_q-distributed and equals Mahalanobis when q = p, so spectral detection is the statistical family in a subspace; on the ULB set's 28 PCA components this is literally what the features are. Because: the fraud of this course is the rare combination, which is why module 3 built joint features and why single-column rules are weak.
8. **Reading the family's assumption off the thought experiments.** — Return to lesson 1's three panels: neighbour and clustering methods succeed on (a) and (b), fail on (c); statistical methods need low dimension and a valid model. Because: that closes the lesson on "how do we know it works".

### Set pieces and worked examples to recreate
- The C1/C2 two-cluster picture with p1 and p2: global kth-NN distance ranks p2 below C1's edge points; LOF repairs it (Chandola Sec. 5.2, Fig. 7).
- 3σ vs 1.5-IQR: 99.7% vs 99.3% on Gaussian data — one rule, two dialects (Chandola Sec. 7.1.1, Fig. 9).
- Euclidean 500 vs Manhattan 505: amount swamping recency, standardise first; SMC 2/5 vs Jaccard 1/4 on red-flag binaries, and why 0-0 matches must not count when 5 of 100 flags are raised (Baesens Ch3 Distance metrics, Fig. 3.8).
- Grubbs' iterative test with the t-based critical value; Mahalanobis as its multivariate form (Baesens Ch3; Chandola Eq. 1–3).
- Robust-PCA score as Mahalanobis in a subspace, χ²_q threshold (Chandola Sec. 9, Eq. 5–6).
- kNN's masking failure on ORCA: AUC 0.36 on Http, 0.33 on Mulcross, k = 150 did not help — because anomaly clusters exceed k (Liu et al. 2008 Sec. 5.1; forward pointer).

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Local density" — define as k over the volume of the smallest ball holding the k neighbours before writing LOF; the newcomer will read LOF as a distance.
- "k" plays two roles (neighbour count in kNN scores; cluster count in k-means) — say so where k-means enters.
- "Extreme value" vs "anomaly": extremes sit in the tail of the same distribution, anomalies may come from another distribution (footnote in the survey) — define at the statistical section.
- Distances need standardisation and a metric fit for the data type (binary red flags → Jaccard); the lesson states this before any distance is computed.
- Do not call the box-plot rule or z-score "Tukey's" or cite anyone in the body (L8a).

# The AI Act and fraud detection: the high-risk line and what sits on each side — research brief

Lesson: `eu-ai-act-fraud` (module: eu-regulation, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Classify a financial-crime model under the AI Act — the Annex III credit-scoring entry and its fraud carve-out, the Article 6(3) derogation and its profiling override — and map the high-risk obligations onto the pipeline
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): The AI Act lists AI that scores the creditworthiness of natural persons as high-risk, and in the same line excepts AI 'used for the purpose of detecting financial fraud'. Is a transaction-fraud model high-risk, and an account-opening-fraud model, and a laundering model that profiles customers? And when a system is high-risk, which documents, logs, metrics and human-oversight controls does the pipeline owe?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The bank runs three models: a transaction-fraud scorer (declines card payments), an application-fraud scorer (refuses account openings it suspects are synthetic identities), and a laundering risk model that assigns each customer a tier. A lawyer asks which of them is "high-risk AI". The newcomer needs: what the Act counts as an AI system (a boosted tree yes, a hand-written rule no); how the Act decides high-risk (a product route and a list route, Annex III); what the credit-scoring entry says and how narrow its fraud exception is; the Art 6(3) way out and the profiling rule that closes it; and, for a system that is high-risk, which of the Arts 9–15 requirements become which artefacts in the pipeline, with what a bank is excused from. Terms to define at first use: provider vs deployer (the one who builds and places on the market vs the one who uses under its authority — a bank that trains its own model is both); intended purpose; profiling (GDPR Art 4(4) by reference: automated evaluation of personal aspects — economic situation, reliability, behaviour); market surveillance authority, MSA; conformity assessment (internal control, for Annex III points 2–8); fundamental rights impact assessment, FRIA; post-market monitoring; general-purpose AI (one sentence: out of scope here).

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **Is a fraud model an AI system?** — Obvious attempt: only neural networks count. Why it fails: Rec 12 rests the definition on the capability "to infer" models from data and excludes systems "based solely on rules defined by natural persons". Answer: every learned detector of modules 4–7 is in scope; the blocking rules of module 1 alone are not; a scorecard fitted from data is. Map: the rule layer and the model layer get different treatment, so the system boundary matters.
2. **How does a system become high-risk?** — Obvious attempt: a risk score of the model's own harm. Why it fails: Art 6(1) (a safety component of a listed product — not finance) and Art 6(2): "AI systems referred to in Annex III shall be considered to be high-risk" — a list, not an assessment. Answer: read Annex III; the entries that can touch financial crime are 5(a) public-benefit eligibility (no fraud exception), 5(b) creditworthiness of natural persons "with the exception of AI systems used for the purpose of detecting financial fraud", 5(c) life and health insurance pricing (no exception), 1(a)'s exclusion of biometric verification whose sole purpose is confirming identity, and the law-enforcement and migration headings (6, 7) a bank does not occupy. Because: the legislator listed areas of fundamental-rights exposure. Map: the transaction-fraud scorer is not in any entry unless it is also used to decide credit; the application-fraud scorer sits at the boundary of 5(b) (is refusing an account "creditworthiness"?); the AML tier model is in no entry.
3. **How narrow is the carve-out?** — Obvious attempt: "fraud detection is exempt". Why it fails: the exception is a clause inside 5(b) only — it removes fraud-detection use from the creditworthiness entry; it does not exempt a system from 5(a) or 5(c), and it is purpose-based ("used for the purpose of detecting financial fraud"): a credit model that also catches fraud is not covered by it. Rec 58's version is narrower still: "provided for by Union law for the purpose of detecting fraud in the offering of financial services" — which invites the reading that the exception covers what PSD2 and the AMLR require. Rec 42 adds that risk analytics "on the basis of suspicious transactions" are not prohibited profiling of individuals. Answer: a transaction-fraud model is outside Annex III; a credit-decision model with a fraud module is high-risk; the Commission's guidelines under Art 6(5) (due 2 February 2026) and Art 96 are where the boundary cases get settled — nothing in the dossier says how. Map: the classification decision is a documented judgment; the lesson models it as a flow.
4. **The derogation and its override** — Obvious attempt: "our model only assists a human, so Art 6(3) takes us out". Why it fails: Art 6(3) exempts an Annex III system that "does not pose a significant risk of harm … including by not materially influencing the outcome of decision making", when it performs a narrow procedural task, improves a completed human activity, detects deviations from prior decision patterns without replacing the human assessment, or performs a preparatory task — but "shall always be considered to be high-risk where the AI system performs profiling of natural persons". Rec 53's examples: structuring unstructured data, document classification, duplicate detection, a grading-deviation check. Answer: a duplicate-alert detector is (c)/(d); a customer-risk model built on personal features is profiling and stays high-risk if it is in Annex III at all; and under Art 6(4) and Art 49(2) a provider claiming the derogation documents the assessment before launch and registers the system with "the condition relied on and a short summary of the grounds" (Annex VIII Section B) in the public database (Art 71); Art 80 lets the MSA re-evaluate and Art 99(7) fines a misclassification. Map: the derogation is a filing, not a private opinion.
5. **If high-risk, what does the pipeline owe?** — Obvious attempt: a compliance document. Why it fails: Arts 9–15 are engineering requirements. Answer, each mapped: Art 9 risk management "continuous iterative" over the lifecycle, 9(8) testing "against prior defined metrics and probabilistic thresholds" → a pre-registered evaluation protocol (module 2's metrics, module 9's backtest); Art 10 data governance — 10(2)(f) bias examination "especially where the outputs influence inputs", 10(3) representative and "to the best extent possible, free of errors and complete", 10(4) the deployment setting, 10(5) special-category data strictly for bias detection → a datasheet with the feedback-loop analysis of module 8 and the label provenance of module 11; Art 11 + Annex IV → "what the system is designed to optimise for" (the cost matrix and threshold), "labelling procedures", metrics incl. "potentially discriminatory impacts", the post-market plan; Art 12 → automatic event logs (the alert ledger); Art 13(3) → instructions stating accuracy metrics and "performance on specific groups"; Art 14 → oversight by people who "remain aware of … automation bias", can "decide not to use", "override or reverse", "stop" (module 9's referral band with a real override); Art 15 → declared accuracy, "feedback loops" from biased outputs addressed (15(4)), resilience to "data poisoning, model poisoning, adversarial examples or model evasion, confidentiality attacks" (15(5)) — module 8's adversary in statute. Because: each requirement is the legislator's name for a failure the course has already seen.
6. **What is a bank excused from?** — Obvious attempt: nothing. Why it fails: Art 17(4): a financial institution's quality-management duty is "deemed to be fulfilled" by financial-services governance rules "with the exception of paragraph 1, points (g), (h) and (i)" (risk management, post-market monitoring, incident reporting); Art 18(3), 19(2), 26(5)–(6), 72(4) likewise fold documentation, logs and monitoring into financial governance; Rec 158 and Art 74(6)–(7): the prudential supervisor is the market-surveillance authority, reporting to the ECB. Answer: the bank's existing model-risk function carries most of it; three things are not deemed. Map: module 9's life cycle is the deemed part; the post-market plan (Art 72) and the incident clock (Art 73: 15 days, 2 days for widespread infringement, 10 for death) are new.
7. **What does the customer get?** — Obvious attempt: nothing beyond GDPR. Why it fails: Art 26(11): deployers inform natural persons subject to a high-risk decision; Art 86: a person adversely affected by a decision "taken by the deployer on the basis of the output from a high-risk AI system listed in Annex III" may obtain "clear and meaningful explanations of the role of the AI system in the decision-making procedure and the main elements of the decision" — "only to the extent that the right … is not otherwise provided for under Union law" (86(3)). Answer: a reason-code channel with the system's role stated; where GDPR Art 22 already gives it, that governs. Map: module 9's three audiences gain a fourth text — the role of the system in the decision.
8. **Retraining and the law of modification** — Obvious attempt: every retrain is a new system. Why it fails: Art 43(4) and Rec 128: changes "pre-determined by the provider and assessed at the moment of the initial conformity assessment" to a continuously learning system are not a substantial modification; Annex IV 2(f) requires those pre-determined changes to be documented. Answer: the retraining cadence of module 7 is written into the technical documentation as a pre-determined change, with its bounds. Map: a daily retrain within declared bounds is one system; a new architecture or purpose is a new one (Art 25).
9. **The price** — Art 99: EUR 35 m or 7% for prohibited practices; EUR 15 m or 3% for provider/deployer obligations (Arts 16, 26) — note Arts 9–15 are fined through Art 16; EUR 7.5 m or 1% for misleading information; SMEs pay the lower of amount and percentage; ten factors including "financial benefits gained, or losses avoided" and self-notification. Dates: application 2 August 2026 for Annex III systems; prohibitions from 2 February 2025; legacy systems caught on significant design change (Art 111(2)).

### Set pieces and worked examples to recreate
- Annex III 5(b) quoted whole and dissected clause by clause: the entry, the exception, "natural persons", "for the purpose of"; beside it Rec 58's "provided for by Union law for the purpose of detecting fraud in the offering of financial services" — two wordings, one narrower (regulation-2024-1689-oj.txt lines 2391 and 136).
- The three models classified: transaction-fraud decline (outside Annex III; AML and PSD2 "provide for" it; Art 95 voluntary code route), synthetic-identity application fraud (boundary of 5(b) — is refusal a creditworthiness decision? and 1(a)'s verification exclusion for the selfie match), customer AML tier (not in Annex III; but it is profiling — so if it were, Art 6(3) could not save it) — presented as a flow, with the uncertain branch labelled "Commission guidelines, Art 6(5)".
- Rec 53's examples on the alert queue: duplicate-alert suppression (Art 6(3)(c)/(d)), OCR of a disputed invoice ((a)), a reviewer-consistency check ((c)) — and the one that fails: a tier model on personal features (profiling override) (line 653–663).
- Arts 9–15 as a table of pipeline artefacts: risk register; datasheet with feedback-loop and label-provenance sections; model card stating optimisation target, threshold, per-group metrics; event logs = alert ledger; oversight UI with override and stop; adversarial test suite — each cell naming the course lesson that built it. Inspired by the dossier's "table mapping recitals 65–76 and Arts 9–15 to ML lifecycle artefacts".
- The deeming grid for a bank: QMS (deemed except (g),(h),(i)), documentation (18(3)), logs (19(2)), deployer monitoring (26(5)–(6)), post-market plan (72(4)) — with the three not-deemed items highlighted (line 927 ff).
- The Art 73 incident ladder: immediately after a causal link, in any event 15 days; 2 days for widespread infringement; 10 days for a death; initial incomplete report allowed; do not alter the system in a way that affects evaluation without informing the authority.
- Art 99 arithmetic: a bank with EUR 20 bn turnover: 3% = EUR 600 m vs EUR 15 m, higher applies; an SME with EUR 10 m turnover: lower of EUR 15 m and EUR 300 000.
- Art 15(5)'s list beside module 8's adversary: "adversarial examples or model evasion" is the minimum-cost camouflage; "data poisoning" is the feedback loop poisoned; 10(2)(f) and 15(4) are the alert-feedback selection bias in statute.
- The retraining boundary: a daily retrain on a 90-day window declared in Annex IV 2(f) is a pre-determined change (Art 43(4)); switching from a tree to a GCN is a new system (Rec 128).

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- The carve-out belongs to 5(b) only; never write "fraud detection is exempt from the AI Act" — a fraud system in 5(a) (public benefits) or 5(c) (insurance pricing) has no exception, and a system outside Annex III is simply not high-risk, which is different from exempt.
- Rec 58 ("provided for by Union law") and Annex III 5(b) ("used for the purpose of") differ; the article binds, the recital interprets — say both and that the Commission's Art 6(5) guidelines (due 2 February 2026) are not in the dossier.
- Art 6(3)'s override: "shall always be considered to be high-risk where the AI system performs profiling of natural persons" — it applies to Annex III systems; it does not pull a non-Annex-III system in.
- Arts 9–15 are not directly fined; Art 99(4) lists Art 16 (providers) and Art 26 (deployers).
- Dates are the original OJ dates (2 Feb 2025, 2 Aug 2025, 2 Aug 2026, 2 Aug 2027 for Art 6(1)); the dossier flags that later omnibus changes are not included — the human checks.
- Rec 136 duplicates Rec 120 and Art 101(1) carries OJ typos — do not quote those passages.
- No Commission guidelines, harmonised standards or case law are in the dossier; the lesson may say where they would come from (Art 6(5), Art 96, Art 40) and nothing about their content.
- "High-risk" does not mean prohibited (Rec 63: classification does not make use lawful or unlawful under other law); keep the three regimes — prohibited (Art 5), high-risk (Annex III), the rest — distinct.

## EXERCISE PLAN
1. [warm-up] Compute the maximum Art 99(4) fine for a provider with EUR 800 m worldwide turnover and for an SME with EUR 12 m, applying 99(6).
2. [research-shaped] Classify, with the article for each step, a system that (i) scores card payments for decline, (ii) scores loan applications and includes a fraud sub-model, (iii) ranks AML alerts for an analyst, (iv) assigns customers a risk tier from occupation, country and transaction history. State which of the four needs an Art 49(2) registration and which a FRIA under Art 27.
3. [claim to refute] "Our AML tier model assists a compliance officer, so Art 6(3)(c) takes it out of high-risk." Refute in two steps: it is not in Annex III in the first place; and if it were, the last subparagraph of Art 6(3) would apply because it profiles.
4. [boundary probe] A system that only flags alerts whose features duplicate another open alert on the same card — under Art 6(3) which condition fits, and what one added feature (the cardholder's age) would do to the classification.
5. [derivation skipped] From Art 10(2)(f) ("outputs influence future inputs") and Art 15(4), write the one-paragraph bias examination a datasheet must contain for a detector trained on alert feedback, using module 8's result that importance weighting cannot remove the bias.
6. [mixed review, E3] Take module 2's finding that validation ROC-AUC selects a depth-50 tree while AP selects depth 4, and write the Annex IV 2(b) sentence "what the system is designed to optimise for" and the Art 13(3)(b) declared metric so that a reviewer could not be misled (Rec 74's "without misleading statements").
7. FORWARD → `gdpr-financial-crime-data`: write the Art 86 explanation for a declined payment, then ask whether the decline was "solely automated" and on what legal basis the features were processed — the reader hits Art 22 and Art 6(1)(f), which the next lesson settles.

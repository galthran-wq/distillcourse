# Designing an end-to-end EU transaction-monitoring system — research brief

Lesson: `eu-transaction-monitoring-design` (module: system, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block. Module-final APPLICATION.
Goal: Design a transaction-monitoring system for an EU payment institution by reading a cloud reference architecture and a graph-analytics workflow against every result and obligation of this course, naming each component's clock, data, metric and legal hook
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A reference architecture from a cloud vendor and a graph-analytics workflow from a database vendor each show a working skeleton. Neither has a delay block, an alert budget, a feedback classifier, a graph propagator, a fraud-rate controller or an audit record. Design the monitoring system of an EU payment institution by adding each one and saying which result or which article requires it.

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader has every part: metrics (M2), features and the store (M3, M11), detectors (M4–M7), the loop's biases (M8), decisions, explanations and monitoring (M9), the law (M10), serving and labels (M11). Two vendor skeletons show a running system with most of those parts missing. The task is design: place each part, give it a clock, a data source, a metric and the article or result that requires it. The newcomer's difficulty is not a new concept but the join: which component answers which obligation.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **What does the skeleton already get right?** — Answer: events on a queue → a warehouse → batch (daily) and streaming (minutes) features → one store → a point-in-time training table → a registered model → an endpoint → a serving handler → logs → a monitor; and, on the graph side, the method explore → hypothesise → encode → resolve → label → validate. Because: this is the serving architecture of the previous lessons and the graph workflow of module 6; the lesson credits it before it adds.
2. **Where is the delay block and the budget?** — Obvious attempt: the pipeline's "time-travel to yesterday" and a 0.5 threshold. Why it fails: labels have not settled by yesterday (M11), and a threshold is not a budget. Answer: a training-set builder as of t − δ with known-card removal, and a daily top-k queue with one alert per card scored by CP@k. Because: module 2's validation geometry and module 1's budget are the system's first two parts, and the accuracy-0.95 gate that a never-alert model passes is replaced by an AP/CP@k gate on a prequential fold.
3. **Where does the feedback go?** — Answer: the daily loop — score, alert top k, receive dispositions now and delayed labels after δ, retrain on both as two models averaged, never pooled; the card id out of the features. Because: module 8's selection bias and module 11's label clocks.
4. **Where is the graph?** — Answer: a nightly job that builds the entity graph with shared-identifier edges under a degree cap, runs components for rings and a restart walk for exposure scores, and writes the scores to the store for the next day; a cold-start update for new pairs at request time. Because: module 6's propagator is a daily batch feature (APATE's midnight recomputation), and components on ER edges are the auditable ring definition the law's "pattern of transactions" asks for.
5. **Where are the detectors on the stream?** — Answer: a stability index per period on the score and feature distributions (inputs), a change detector on the per-day budget-normalised metric (outputs), calibration backtests on probability pools, feeding a champion–challenger stage and a retraining trigger. Because: module 7's error-stream detectors and module 9's backtests are the monitor the vendor's skew/drift thresholds approximate on 9 of 25 features.
6. **Where is the law?** — Answer, component by component: detection criteria, the risk profile, the onboarding and transaction decisions and the report stay in-house (Art 18(3)) — a vendor may supply the model, not the decision; every automated refusal or exit routes through meaningful human intervention with an explanation and a challenge path (Art 76(5); GDPR Art 22 and Recital 71); every alert, reported or not, has a five-year assessment record (Art 77) — the ledger of M11; monitoring covers all products at risk-based intensity against the customer's profile (Art 26, Rec 72) with Art 34(2)'s four triggers as named rule or feature families and "linked transactions" aggregated; the report leaves within the three-working-day rule and answers the unit within five days or 24 hours (Arts 69, 71); for remote card payments the per-transaction risk score, the 90-day value-weighted rate per band with its two-quarter switch and the quarterly breakdown (RTS Arts 18–21) and a yearly audit of the model (Art 3(2)); timestamps on a unified reference (RTS Art 29); the AI Act's artefacts — a risk register, a data sheet with the feedback-loop bias check, logs, declared per-group metrics, an oversight design that names automation bias, robustness to evasion — kept whether or not the fraud carve-out is claimed, because a profiling system is high-risk regardless and the claim itself must be documented and registered (Art 6(3)–(4), Art 49(2)); a DPIA and a minimisation audit of the store (GDPR Arts 35, 5(1)(c)); a new model risk-assessed before launch (AMLR Art 10(1)). Because: each is an obligation on a specific component with a specific clock, and the design must show where it is met.
7. **What is the resulting system?** — Answer: the layered diagram with the clocks — rules (ms), score with exposure and sequence features (sub-second), graph and features (nightly), retraining on feedbacks and delayed samples (daily), ledger and reports (days), monitors and the fraud-rate controller (quarterly), audit and the AI Act artefacts (yearly) — every box labelled with its metric and its article. Because: that is the course's argument as a machine.
8. **What does this design not do?** — Answer: it runs on a vendor stack the lesson does not endorse, on synthetic data that scores AUC 1.0; it has no adversary model beyond retraining; the graph is a nightly batch, not a streaming one; the fraud-rate controller's hysteresis is the law's, not a design choice; the AI Act's narrow carve-out is unsettled until the Commission's guidelines; nothing here is legal advice.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- This is design, not compliance advice: say so once, at the departures step, in the dry register.
- Vendor names stay out of the body ("a cloud vendor's reference pipeline", "a graph database vendor's workshop"); the artefacts are named in References.
- "Risk-based intensity" (AMLR) vs "risk score" (RTS) vs "high-risk system" (AI Act): three uses of "risk", each defined where it recurs.
- The AI Act carve-out is narrow and purpose-based; the design keeps the artefacts regardless — do not let the lesson read as "fraud models are exempt".
- The ledger is one table serving three readers (feedback, investigator, supervisor); a reader will design three.
- Clocks: write them on every box; a component without a clock is unplaced.

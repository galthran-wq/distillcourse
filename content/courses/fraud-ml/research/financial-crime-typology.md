# A typology of financial crime as learning problems — research brief

Lesson: `financial-crime-typology` (module: problem, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Classify the major financial-crime types — card fraud, account takeover, laundering, mules, sanctions evasion — by the unit that carries the label, where the label comes from, how late it arrives and whether the signal is in one record, one entity's history or the connections between entities
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): Stolen card numbers used online, a customer's own account emptied by someone who took it over, cash from drug sales turned into apartment purchases, a student paid to let money pass through their account, a sanctioned company paying through a cousin's firm: all of these land on a bank as 'financial crime'. Which of them can a classifier learn from examples, which show up only as a change in one person's habits, which are visible only in the connections between accounts, and which arrive with no reliable labels at all?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader has one picture from the previous lesson: a model ranking payments for a hundred investigators, labelled by calls and complaints. The opening lists five crimes that all arrive at the same bank. The newcomer has no vocabulary for them and no reason to think they differ for a classifier. The question is which of the loop's parts — the unit scored, the label source, the delay, the structure of the signal — changes from crime to crime, so that "fraud detection" turns out to be five different learning problems. Terms not yet available: card-present / card-not-present, account takeover, superimposition, mule, placement/layering/integration, structuring, suspicious transaction report, sanctions screening.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **What makes any of these hard for a classifier?** — Obvious attempt: treat fraud as a rare class and apply the imbalance playbook. Why it fails: rarity is only one of five traits. Fraud is uncommon (few labels), well considered (it looks like a normal payment on purpose), imperceptibly concealed (camouflage), time-evolving (it changes when caught) and organised (several accounts act together). Answer: each trait names a difficulty the detector inherits — scarce labels, overlap with the genuine class, drift, relational structure — and the typology sorts crimes by which traits dominate. Because: a method that handles rarity does nothing about camouflage or organisation.
2. **Card fraud: what is the unit and where does the label come from?** — Obvious attempt: "a card is stolen, the card is the unit". Why it fails: the bank decides per payment, and a stolen card number is used online for a few payments before the cardholder notices; the label comes per payment from the cardholder's dispute or the investigator's call. Answer: unit = transaction; label = investigator feedback (days) or dispute (weeks); near-complete because the cardholder eventually sees the statement. Card-present fraud (lost, stolen, counterfeit) is shrinking; card-not-present fraud (stolen credentials used remotely) is over half the losses on under a sixth of the volume. Because: a chip at a terminal is hard to copy, a card number typed into a website is not.
3. **Account takeover: whose behaviour is the signal?** — Obvious attempt: same as card fraud, a transaction classifier. Why it fails: the account is the customer's own; the fraudster's transactions are mixed into a legitimate stream (superimposition), and the only thing abnormal is the departure from that customer's habits — nothing in the payment is abnormal in itself. Contrast with subscription fraud, where the whole account was opened to defraud. Answer: unit = the account's stream; signal = deviation from the entity's own profile (module 3's features); label = the customer's complaint, often weeks late. Because: the same payment is normal for one account and alarming for another.
4. **Laundering: why is there almost no label?** — Obvious attempt: find the dirty money by its amount. Why it fails: the three stages — placement (cash into the system), layering (moved through transactions to hide its origin), integration (spent on apartments) — are built to look like ordinary commerce; a single transaction is unremarkable, the pattern is the crime (four levels: transaction, account, business, ring); discovery takes years and the only label is a filed report, so most laundering is never labelled. Answer: unit = account or ring; signal = the pattern across accounts (module 6); label = a suspicious transaction report, incomplete and late. Because: the launderer's success is measured by not being noticed, and no customer complains.
5. **Mules and structuring: the adversary's reply.** — Obvious attempt: a reporting threshold (cash above $10,000 must be reported) catches large movements. Why it fails: launderers split amounts below the threshold (structuring, smurfing) and recruit mules — a student paid to let money pass through — so the money moves in many small, legitimate-looking accounts. Answer: the detector must aggregate linked transactions and look at the entity and its connections, not the single row. Because: the rule changed the crime; whatever the detector does next, the crime will change again.
6. **Sanctions evasion: a matching problem, not a classification.** — Obvious attempt: a classifier. Why it fails: the question is whether a party is on a designated list, possibly under another name or through a related company — a screening of names and identifiers with a hard legal duty to freeze, and no learned threshold to tune. Answer: screening against lists at every transfer, with the travel-rule fields as inputs (module 10); ML only helps with matching and with finding the related party in the graph. Because: the obligation is rule-based, not risk-based.
7. **The table.** — Each crime as (unit, label source, delay, structure, dominant trait). Card fraud: transaction / dispute or feedback / days–weeks / per-row + entity history / uncommon, concealed. Account takeover: account stream / complaint / weeks / entity history / concealed, superimposed. Laundering: account or ring / STR / years or never / graph / organised, concealed. Mules and structuring: linked transactions / STR / late / graph + aggregation / time-evolving. Sanctions: counterparty / list / immediate / matching / rule-based. Because: this is what the rest of the course fills in — modules 2–3 live in the first two rows, module 6 in the third and fourth, module 10 in the last.

### Set pieces and worked examples to recreate
- The five-trait definition sentence, with one difficulty per trait. (Baesens Ch1 p. 3; APATE Sec. 2.1.)
- CNP vs card-present: 54% of losses on < 15% of volume; SEPA CNP share growing to 79% by 2018; EUR 1.8bn SEPA card fraud in 2018. (Handbook Ch2.2.)
- The "Bandit" call records: calls at 1–2 a.m., 34–39 seconds, each unremarkable alone, abnormal for this subscriber — superimposition made concrete. (Baesens Table 1.2, after Fawcett and Provost.)
- Structuring: reports reached over 10 million in 1994, about 0.5% flagged; the pessimistic view that only incompetent launderers get caught. (Bolton & Hand Sec. 4.)
- The erythromycin trade-price anomaly ($1,694 a gram imported vs $0.08 exported) as laundering through invoices. (Bolton & Hand Sec. 4.)
- The label contrast: cardholder feedback gives near ground truth for card fraud; nothing equivalent exists for laundering; a synthetic world labels transitively ($100 → $50 → $25 all tainted). (AMLworld Sec. 7 and 3.4.)
- Telecom: subscription fraud (whole account) vs superimposed fraud (calls mixed in), tumbling and cloning as the pre-card version of account takeover. (Bolton & Hand Sec. 5.)

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- Define card-not-present at first use as "the card number used remotely, no chip, no PIN"; the newcomer may read it as a lost card.
- "Superimposition" is a telecom word; introduce it through the Bandit calls and then apply it to account takeover.
- "Structuring" / "smurfing": define as splitting below a reporting threshold, with the $10,000 US rule as the historical instance and the EU "linked transactions" notion as a forward pointer.
- STR vs SAR: the EU term is suspicious transaction report; the US term is suspicious activity report; use STR and say they are the same document.
- Do not let "money laundering" read as a transaction class; it is a pattern-level label.
- Sanctions screening is not a learned classifier; say so, or the reader will try to apply the course's metrics to it.

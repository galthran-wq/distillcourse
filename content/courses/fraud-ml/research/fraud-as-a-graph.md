# Transactions as a graph: nodes, edges, shared identifiers and laundering motifs — research brief

Lesson: `fraud-as-a-graph` (module: graphs, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Construct a bipartite or tripartite graph from a transaction log, add edges for shared identifiers, and classify laundering typologies as motifs in it
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): Eight rows of a transfer table look ordinary one at a time; drawn as accounts and arrows, they show money leaving A, passing through C and D and returning to A. Fraud is organised, and a per-row feature cannot see the organisation. How do you turn a transaction log into a graph, what should be a node and an edge, what do shared cards, devices and addresses add, and which shapes in the graph are the laundering patterns investigators look for?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader's features so far are per row: a payment plus its card's and its terminal's history. A laundering ring moves EUR 100,000 from A to B, C and D in five transfers and back to A in three; each transfer, with its own history features, is a normal-looking business payment. The signal is the shape the eight rows make together. What the newcomer lacks: how to decide what a node and an edge are for a payment log (not yet: bipartite, tripartite, multigraph, adjacency matrix and list); how the age of a transaction enters (not yet: time-decayed weights); how two cardholders who share a card or a device become one object (not yet: entity resolution, identifier nodes); and a vocabulary of shapes to look for (not yet: motif, fan-in, cycle, scatter-gather). Terms not to use before definition: adjacency, egonet, homophily (next lesson), propagation (lesson 4).

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **What is a node and what is an edge?** — Obvious attempt: accounts as nodes, a transfer as a directed edge — the AML case, a directed multigraph with timestamped parallel edges. For card payments the obvious node set has two kinds: cardholders and merchants, with a payment as an edge between a cardholder and a merchant and no edges within a kind — a bipartite graph G = (V1, V2, E). Why one choice is not enough: a card payment carries the fraud label, and a label on an *edge* cannot be propagated by methods that pass mass between nodes. Answer: the tripartite transformation — promote each transaction to a node with exactly one cardholder and one merchant neighbour; Q_tri = [[0, M],[Mᵀ, 0]] over (c + m + t) nodes. Because: the thing with the label must be a node for the next lessons to start from it.
2. **How does time enter?** — Obvious attempt: an unweighted edge per payment. Why it fails: a cardholder's payment at a merchant three years ago says nothing about a payment today. Answer: edge weight w = exp(−γh) with h the age; γ chosen so the weight is near zero after a day, a week or a month (three graphs for three horizons). Because: the graph is rebuilt nightly over a sliding window and the horizon is a design parameter like the delay δ. (The source prints α where γ is meant in one place; the lesson uses γ throughout.)
3. **Representation and size.** — Adjacency matrix vs adjacency list (weight matrix / weight list): the matrix is n² of mostly zeros; the list is O(edges); the matrix is what the mathematics of lessons 2 and 4 is written in, the list is what runs. The toy sociogram with its four representations. Because: a million cardholders is a 10¹² matrix on paper and a few million edges in memory.
4. **One person, many accounts: shared identifiers.** — Obvious attempt: treat each account as a separate node. Why it fails: a fraudster opens ten accounts; spread across ten nodes, each looks ordinary. Answer: make identifiers (card number, device, IP, address, phone) nodes too — the heterogeneous graph User / Card / Device / IP with HAS_CC, USED, HAS_IP — and let two users become one object when they share an identifier: entity resolution as new edges. The telco case: a fraudster disconnected and refounded cannot resist calling the same family; the spider construction in social-security fraud where a bankrupt employer's staff, equipment and address reappear under a new name. Because: identifiers are the only thing a fraudster must reuse. The caveat that a shared coffee-shop IP links everyone is named and deferred to the communities lesson.
5. **The shapes that are laundering.** — Obvious attempt: look for large transfers. Why it fails: structuring splits amounts under thresholds; the laundering is in the pattern, not the row. Answer: the eight motifs with their graph definitions — fan-out (a node with k ≥ 2 distinct out-neighbours), fan-in, gather-scatter (fan-in then fan-out at the same node), scatter-gather (fan-out from v and fan-in to u over the same intermediates), simple cycle, random (a cycle-free chain without return), bipartite, stack (a bipartite with an extra layer); how placement, layering and integration (module 1) map onto them. Because: these are what rules and graph features look for — and the honesty check: only 19% of the laundering in a synthetic world that plants them follows one; the rest hides as payroll and supplier payments, so pattern matching caps recall.
6. **Why the graph is hard to fool.** — A fraudster can fake an IP, a login time, an age; they cannot see or reshape their position in the global network, and organised fraud by construction creates links among its members. Because: this is the module's reason to exist, stated as a claim with its limit (a lone opportunist leaves no structure).
7. **How do we know it works.** — The 5-hop chain query between flagged users and the shared-card query on the 204k-user P2P set as the first evidence that shapes exist in real data; the transitive-taint example (A pays B 100 illicit, B pays C 50, C pays D 25 — all laundering) as what complete labels would look like against the incomplete labels real banks have.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Bipartite", "tripartite", "multigraph", "heterogeneous graph" — define each with the card instance at first use; the newcomer will read "bipartite" as "two parts of one graph".
- Direction: card payments are naturally undirected cardholder–merchant edges; transfers are directed; say which graph is which before any matrix is written.
- "Entity resolution" in this course means adding edges for shared identifiers, not deduplicating records — define narrowly.
- APATE's α/γ typo; "weights ≈ 0 after a week" with γ = 0.004 per hour is actually 0.51 — the lesson states the decay and lets the reader check it (exercise).
- Motif definitions are informal in the source; the lesson gives the k ≥ 2 definitions and does not claim they are standard beyond that paper.
- Do not introduce degree, triangles or homophily here (next lesson).

## EXERCISE PLAN
1. [warm-up] Write the adjacency matrix, adjacency list and weight list of a 5-node, 6-edge weighted toy, and its tripartite Q matrix.
2. [derivation skipped] Show that the tripartite transformation preserves every cardholder–merchant path length up to a factor of two and that a random walk on Q_tri alternates between the entity side and the transaction side — the property lesson 4 relies on.
3. [boundary probe] γ → 0 and γ → ∞ in w = exp(−γh): what graph results in each limit, and why neither can carry a fraud signal; then compute h at which the week-horizon weight is 0.5 and compare with "near zero after a week".
4. [claim to refute] "Because fraud is organised, every fraudulent transaction sits in a motif, so a motif detector has recall 1 on laundering." Refute with the 19% figure and the payroll camouflage; name the recall ceiling.
5. [research-shaped] On a PaySim subset, build the account multigraph for one day and count fan-out nodes (k ≥ 5 distinct out-neighbours in 6 hours) and 2-cycles; report what share of the flagged TRANSFER→CASH_OUT frauds sit in either.
6. FORWARD → `neighbourhood-and-centrality`: on the P2P-style toy with 12 users (4 fraudulent), count the edges joining a fraudulent to a legitimate user and compare with 2·l·f·M; the reader computes the homophily statistic the next lesson names and tests.
7. [mixed review] Using `point-in-time-features`: specify the graph that exists at time t — which edges, which labels on transaction nodes — so that a feature read from it at t uses only labels known by t − δ.

# The layered fraud detection system — research brief

Lesson: `fraud-detection-system` (module: problem, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Explain why a payment network's deadline, its investigators' capacity and the way labels arrive force a fast rule stage before authorisation, a learned model after it, and a feedback loop that only ever labels what was alerted
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A payment network sees about four hundred thousand card payments a day and must answer each one within a second or two. Around a thousand of those payments are fraud, the team that can phone a cardholder and check can handle about a hundred cases a day, and whether a payment really was fraud is only known days or weeks later, if anyone ever says. Where in that flow can a learned model sit, what can it be asked to do, and what does it get back?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader knows classifiers and has never seen a payment system. Start from the three numbers in the opening, each with its frame: 400,000 payments a day and a one-to-two-second answer; about 1,000 frauds on about 300 cards; about 100 cases a day the investigators can phone about; a label that comes back in days to weeks, from the investigator's call or the customer's complaint, and otherwise never. Each number is a constraint, and the question is what shape of system they force. Terms the newcomer does not have yet and must not meet before they are built: "layer", "blocking rule", "scoring rule", "alert", "feedback", "delayed sample", "investigator budget", "verification latency". Do not open with the five-layer diagram; derive it.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **Where does the deadline put a learned model?** — Obvious attempt: score every payment with the model before answering it. Why it fails: the authorisation answer is due in one to two seconds (six seconds end to end in another description), and the model needs features computed over the card's history; some checks must run in milliseconds and cannot wait. Answer: split by clock speed. Checks that are instant and precise — is the card expired, is the PIN right, does a hard rule fire ("internet transaction AND unsecured website → deny") — run before the answer and can block it; the model's score is computed in near real time (under a second) from precomputed features and is not what blocks the payment. Because: a block before authorisation must be fast AND very precise, since a wrong block is a lost sale, and a learned score cannot guarantee either on its own.
2. **What can the model's output be used for, if not blocking?** — Obvious attempt: treat the score as a yes/no fraud decision. Why it fails: a thousand frauds a day and a hundred investigators' cases mean nine in ten frauds cannot be looked at even if every alert were right; a yes/no on 400,000 payments produces thousands of "yes" with nobody to act. Answer: the model produces a ranking, and the top k (about 100) become alerts; the investigators phone the cardholders of those. Because: the capacity is fixed by people, not by the model; what the bank can buy with a better model is a more precise top hundred, not more alerts.
3. **Where do rules written by people fit beside the model?** — Obvious attempt: replace all rules with the model. Why it fails: a known pattern ("previous transaction in a different continent and less than one hour ago → score 0.95") is cheaper to state than to learn, and investigators can read it; the model is retrained, never edited by hand. Answer: scoring rules next to the model, both feeding the alert ranking; blocking rules ahead of both. Because: rules are precise and editable, the model is general and not editable — two tools with different maintenance.
4. **Where do the labels come from?** — Obvious attempt: assume every payment gets labelled. Why it fails: nobody checks 400,000 payments; the investigators' calls label the alerted cards' recent payments within a day; everything else gets a label only if the customer complains (days to weeks, up to 13 months under EU law — forward pointer only), and a payment nobody complained about is read as genuine. Answer: two kinds of label with two clocks — feedbacks (fast, for alerted cards) and delayed samples (slow, for everything, with silence as genuine). Because: the only people who produce labels are the investigators (who see what the model showed them) and the customers (who notice late or never).
5. **What is wrong with the fast labels?** — Obvious attempt: retrain on the feedbacks, they are freshest. Why it fails: the alerted cards were chosen by yesterday's model; the fast labels cover only what it ranked highest, so they are not a sample of the stream. Answer (stated, not solved — the solution is module 8): the loop labels what it chose to show; every later lesson has to live inside that. Because: the investigators cannot be asked to check a random sample instead — their job is the most suspicious hundred.
6. **How often does the model change?** — Obvious attempt: train once. Why it fails: the labelled data arrive daily and the fraud changes; the handbook's system retrains the model once a day at end of day, and rules are added by hand whenever a pattern is found. Answer: a retraining cadence (daily) and a rule-editing process beside it. Because: the model's knowledge is only as fresh as its last training set, and the delayed labels keep arriving.

### Set pieces and worked examples to recreate
- The three-speed timeline: terminal checks in milliseconds → blocking rules → scoring rules and the model in under a second → alerts → investigators over the day → feedback the next day, disputes weeks later. (Dal Pozzolo 2018 Fig. 1; Handbook Ch2.3 Fig. 1.)
- The two example rules verbatim: blocking "IF internet transactions AND unsecured website THEN deny"; scoring "IF previous transaction in a different continent AND less than one hour from previous THEN fraud score = 0.95". (Dal Pozzolo 2018 Sec. II.)
- The day's arithmetic: 400,000 payments, ~1,000 frauds, ~300 compromised cards (about 3 frauds per card), 100 alerts; so at most a third of the compromised cards can be looked at even with a perfect ranker. (Handbook Ch3 RealWorld, Ch4.5.)
- The label timeline for one day t: feedbacks F_t on the alerted cards' recent transactions at once; D_{t−δ} for the whole of day t − δ once δ days have passed, with δ = 7 in the paper. (Dal Pozzolo 2018 Fig. 2, Eqs. 1–3.)
- Provost's arithmetic: a million accounts at 1% false alarms a day is 10,000 calls a day — why only very strong evidence is acted on automatically. (Bolton & Hand 2002, Provost comment p. 250.)
- The fraud cycle: detection → investigation → confirmation → prevention, with the feedback arrow into the training data. (Baesens Ch1 Fig. 1.3.)

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Alert" vs "block": an alert is a request for a human to look; a block refuses the payment. Define both at the point where the deadline splits them.
- "Feedback" means the investigators' verdicts on alerted cards, not generic model feedback; "delayed sample" means a day's complete labels after the delay. Define each when it is derived in sub-problem 4.
- "Silence is read as genuine": say explicitly that an unlabelled payment is treated as negative, and that this is a modelling decision with consequences (label noise) — do not let the reader assume all negatives are verified.
- The investigators' k = 100 is the handbook's convention; real teams differ. Give the number with its frame (per day, cards not transactions).
- Do not present "five layers" as a list; the count is a consequence, not a fact. The APATE description has four boxes with different names (acceptance check, online and offline sanity checks); say that the shape, not the count, is what the constraints force.

## EXERCISE PLAN
1. [warm-up] From 400,000 payments, 1,000 frauds on 300 cards and 100 alerts a day, compute the best card precision a perfect ranker can reach and the share of compromised cards it can never reach.
2. [boundary probe] Set the investigators' capacity to zero and then to 400,000: what does the model's output mean in each case, and which constraint in the lesson disappears? Name the one that stays (the delayed label).
3. [claim to refute] "Since the model scores every payment in under a second, it can replace the blocking rules." Refute with the precision requirement on a block and the cost of a wrong refusal; name the step (the block runs before the answer, the score after).
4. [research-shaped] Design the measurement that would tell whether a bank's "silence is genuine" convention is safe: which quantity, on which sample, over which horizon (customer disputes up to 13 months).
5. [derivation skipped] Show that with δ-day delayed labels and daily retraining, the model trained on day t has seen complete labels only up to day t − δ, and compute how many of the previous week's frauds it has never seen for δ = 7 at 1,000 frauds a day.
6. FORWARD → `alert-feedback-selection-bias`: simulate ten days in which only the top 100 of 10,000 scored rows get labels and the model is retrained on them; plot the fraud share of the training set over days against the stream's 1% — the reader watches the training set drift from the stream before the mechanism is named.

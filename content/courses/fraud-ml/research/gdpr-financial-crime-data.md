# GDPR for fraud data: lawful basis, Article 22 and minimisation against feature richness — research brief

Lesson: `gdpr-financial-crime-data` (module: eu-regulation, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Classify a fraud-scoring decision by its lawful basis, its Article 22 status and its information duties, and audit a feature set against data minimisation and special-category rules
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A fraud model declines a payment with no human involved, using a feature store that holds everything the bank knows about the customer. GDPR gives the customer a right not to be subject to such a decision, requires every feature to be necessary, and forbids some features outright. On what legal basis does the model run, when may it decide alone, and which of its features must go?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The pipeline's feature store (module 3, module 11) holds the card's history, the device, the IP, the merchant, the customer's age, nationality and occupation from onboarding, and labels from investigators and disputes. The decline model fires on a score with no person in the loop. A data-protection officer asks three things: under what provision of GDPR the bank processes all that for scoring; whether a decline with no human is a decision the customer "has the right not to be subject to"; and which columns in the store are not "necessary" or are special-category data. Terms to define at first use: personal data, controller and processor (the bank; a cloud vendor), processing, profiling (Art 4(4): automated evaluation of "economic situation, … reliability, behaviour, location"), lawful basis (one of the six in Art 6(1)), legitimate interest, data subject, special categories (Art 9(1): racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religion, trade-union membership, genetic, biometric for identification, health, sex life or orientation), data protection impact assessment (DPIA, Art 35), supervisory authority (the national data-protection authority — not the payments supervisor of the previous lessons).

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **On what basis may the bank score at all?** — Obvious attempt: consent. Why it fails: consent can be withdrawn and a fraud model cannot stop; Art 6(1) lists six bases and three fit: (b) "necessary for the performance of a contract" (the payment itself), (c) "necessary for compliance with a legal obligation" (AML monitoring under the AMLR, SCA monitoring under the RTS — the obligation must be in law), (f) "necessary for the purposes of the legitimate interests pursued by the controller" unless overridden by the data subject's rights. Rec 47 sentence 6: "The processing of personal data strictly necessary for the purposes of preventing fraud also constitutes a legitimate interest of the data controller concerned." PSD2 Art 94(1) adds a sector permission "when necessary to safeguard the prevention, investigation and detection of payment fraud". Answer: fraud scoring runs on (f) with Rec 47, or on (c) where a law requires it; AML scoring runs on (c). Because: the bases differ in what they permit and what must be disclosed — Art 13(1)(d) and 14(2)(b) require the legitimate interest to be named to the customer. Map: the model's documentation names its basis per use; "strictly necessary" and the balancing test (Rec 47 sentences 1–4: reasonable expectations) are the first minimisation filter.
2. **May the model reuse data collected for something else?** — Obvious attempt: data in the warehouse is data. Why it fails: Art 5(1)(b) purpose limitation; Art 6(4) compatibility test: the link between purposes, the context, the nature of the data (Art 9/10), the consequences, and safeguards "such as encryption or pseudonymisation". Answer: onboarding data reused for fraud scoring passes or fails the Art 6(4) test per field. Map: each feature in the store carries its collection purpose; Art 4(5) pseudonymisation (the technical key of module 11's design) is the named safeguard.
3. **Is a lone decline a decision the customer can refuse?** — Obvious attempt: a decline is a product decision. Why it fails: Art 22(1): "the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her"; Rec 71 names "automatic refusal of an online credit application" as the model case. Answer: a solely automated decline with significant effect is in Art 22(1) unless one of 22(2)'s exceptions applies: (a) "necessary for entering into, or performance of, a contract", (b) "authorised by Union or Member State law … which also lays down suitable measures to safeguard", (c) explicit consent. Rec 71 sentence 3 names the (b) route: "including for fraud and tax-evasion monitoring and prevention purposes conducted in accordance with the regulations, standards and recommendations of Union institutions or national oversight bodies and to ensure the security and reliability of a service provided by the controller". Because: the law lets fraud decisions be automated through an authorising law, not through a recital. Map: a card decline argues (a) (the payment contract) or (b) (PSD2 Art 97's SCA regime, the AMLR's Art 76(5)); the AMLR's own human-intervention clause is the "suitable measure" for AML decisions.
4. **What safeguards attach?** — Obvious attempt: an appeal email. Why it fails: Art 22(3): for (a) and (c), "at least the right to obtain human intervention on the part of the controller, to express his or her point of view and to contest the decision"; Rec 71 sentence 4 adds "an explanation of the decision reached after such assessment"; Art 13(2)(f), 14(2)(g), 15(1)(h): "meaningful information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences". Answer: a human review channel, a contest channel, a reason-code text, and a general statement of the logic in the privacy notice. Map: module 9's customer-facing explanation is the Art 15(1)(h) object; module 9's referral band is the human-intervention channel; and when a decision is a card decline the module-10 tipping-off ban does not apply, whereas an AML hold cannot be explained (Art 76(5)(c) AMLR excepts the report).
5. **Which features may never be in the decision?** — Obvious attempt: anything predictive. Why it fails: Art 22(4): automated decisions "shall not be based on special categories of personal data referred to in Article 9(1), unless point (a) or (g) of Article 9(2) applies"; Art 9(1) lists them; Rec 71 sentence 6: prevent "discriminatory effects … on the basis of racial or ethnic origin, political opinion, religion or beliefs, trade union membership, genetic or health status or sexual orientation"; AMLR Art 76(2)(c) adds "no decisions that would lead to biased and discriminatory outcomes" and Rec 152 no decisions solely on irrelevant special-category data. Answer: no special-category feature in an automated decision; proxies (a merchant category that reveals health or religion; a biometric liveness score, which is Art 4(14) biometric data under Art 9(1) when used to identify) are audited as such. Map: the feature-set audit — each column tagged (not personal / personal / special category / proxy) and the model's inputs checked against the tag.
6. **Which features are not necessary?** — Obvious attempt: richer is better (module 3 said so). Why it fails: Art 5(1)(c): "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary in relation to the purposes"; 5(1)(e) storage limitation; 5(1)(d) accuracy — "every reasonable step" to rectify inaccurate data, which includes wrong fraud labels; Art 5(2) accountability. Answer: minimisation is a test the feature store must pass per column and per retention period, and the course's own ablations (module 3: which groups add savings; module 4: embeddings adding nothing) are the evidence of necessity. Because: a feature that does not move the metric is not necessary. Map: the module-3 feature groups with their savings lift are the minimisation file; AMLR Art 77's five-year retention and PSD2's 13-month dispute clock set storage limits for labels.
7. **When is a DPIA required?** — Obvious attempt: for new systems only. Why it fails: Art 35(3)(a): "a systematic and extensive evaluation of personal aspects … based on automated processing, including profiling, and on which decisions are based that produce legal effects" — the Art 22 trigger nearly word for word; 35(7): description and purposes incl. the legitimate interest, necessity and proportionality, risks, measures; 35(11): review when risk changes. Answer: a fraud scoring system with automated declines presumptively needs a DPIA, reviewed when the model changes. Map: the DPIA is the module-9 documentation test extended with the Art 35(7) sections; the AI Act's FRIA (previous lesson) complements it; AMLR Art 75(4)(h) requires one before sharing.
8. **What must the customer be told, and when may the bank stay silent?** — Obvious attempt: tell everything. Why it fails: Art 13/14 require the basis, the legitimate interest, the existence of automated decision-making and the logic; Art 14(5)(b) excuses notice where it "would be likely to render impossible or seriously impair the achievement of the objectives"; and the AMLR's Art 73 tipping-off ban overrides for AML. Answer: fraud declines are explained; AML assessments are not. Map: module 9's reason code has two modes.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- The staged GDPR text is the gdpr-info.eu mirror with paragraph numbers dropped and page chrome ("Suitable Recitals") included; cite as Art N(para) from EUR-Lex and never quote the mirror's unofficial recital titles ("Overriding Legitimate Interest" is the mirror's label, not the Regulation's).
- Rec 47 is a recital: it supports an Art 6(1)(f) argument, it is not itself a basis; Rec 71's fraud carve-out needs an authorising law under Art 22(2)(b) — the lesson must keep "recital says fraud is fine" and "article requires a law" apart.
- Art 22(1) is phrased as a right ("shall have the right not to be subject"); do not call it a prohibition.
- Art 22(3)'s safeguards attach to exceptions (a) and (c); for (b) the safeguards are in the authorising law (hence AMLR Art 76(5)'s own list).
- The dossier holds no EDPB/WP29 guidelines and no CJEU case law (the SCHUFA scoring ruling is named in the reader's notes as outside the dossier) — the lesson may say that guidance on "solely", "significant effect" and "meaningful information" comes from the EDPB and the Court, and must not state what they held.
- "Supervisory authority" here is the data-protection authority; the previous lessons' supervisors are the AML and payments supervisors.
- Biometric data is special-category only when "processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying a natural person" (Art 9(1)); a behavioural-biometrics fraud signal is a boundary case the lesson flags, not settles.

# GDPR for fraud data: lawful basis, Article 22 and minimisation against feature richness — research brief

Lesson: `gdpr-financial-crime-data` (module: eu-regulation, core, planned). NEW.
Goal: Classify a fraud-scoring decision by its lawful basis, its Article 22 status and its information duties, and audit a feature set against data minimisation and special-category rules.

## Sources (primary legal text; staged from the gdpr-info.eu mirror — paragraph numbers dropped there; cite from EUR-Lex)
- Art 4 definitions: (1) personal data, (2) processing, (4) profiling (automated processing to evaluate personal aspects — performance, economic situation, health, preferences, reliability, behaviour, location), (5) pseudonymisation (not attributable without separately kept information), (7)/(8) controller/processor, (11) consent, (14) biometric data.
- Art 5(1)(a)–(f) and 5(2): lawfulness/fairness/transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation ("adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary"); accuracy (inaccurate data erased/rectified); storage limitation (identifiable form no longer than necessary); integrity/confidentiality; accountability.
- Art 6(1)(a)–(f) (consent; contract; legal obligation; vital interests; public task; legitimate interests unless overridden — not for public authorities in their tasks); 6(4) (compatibility test for further processing: link, context, nature of data, consequences, safeguards incl. encryption or pseudonymisation).
- Rec 47 (legitimate interest: careful assessment; reasonable expectations; "The processing of personal data strictly necessary for the purposes of preventing fraud also constitutes a legitimate interest of the data controller concerned"); Rec 60 (information duties incl. profiling).
- Art 9(1)–(2), 9(4): special categories prohibited unless an exception (9(2)(a) explicit consent, (g) substantial public interest …); national limits on biometric/genetic/health data.
- Art 13(1)(d), 13(2)(f); Art 14(2)(b), 14(2)(g), 14(5)(b)–(d); Art 15(1)(h): the legitimate interests pursued must be disclosed; automated decision-making incl. profiling under Art 22(1) and (4) with "meaningful information about the logic involved, as well as the significance and the envisaged consequences"; Art 14(5)(b) disproportionate effort / seriously impairing the objectives; one-month window for Art 14.
- Art 22(1) (right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, producing legal or similarly significant effects); 22(2) (exceptions: (a) necessary for a contract, (b) authorised by Union/Member State law with suitable safeguards, (c) explicit consent); 22(3) (for (a) and (c): right to human intervention, to express a view, to contest); 22(4) (no special-category data unless 9(2)(a) or (g) with safeguards).
- Rec 71 ("automatic refusal of an online credit application or e-recruiting practices without any human intervention"; decisions authorised by law "including for fraud and tax-evasion monitoring and prevention purposes … and to ensure the security and reliability of a service provided by the controller"; safeguards — specific information, human intervention, an explanation of the decision reached, challenge; not children; appropriate mathematical or statistical procedures, measures to correct inaccuracies and minimise errors, prevent discriminatory effects on protected grounds); Rec 72 (profiling subject to the ordinary rules; EDPB guidance).
- Art 35(1), 35(3)(a), 35(7), 35(11): DPIA before high-risk processing; specifically a systematic and extensive automated evaluation (profiling) on which decisions with legal or similarly significant effect are based; contents (description and purposes incl. legitimate interest, necessity/proportionality, risks, measures); review when risk changes.
- Cross-statute links: AMLR Art 76(5) and Rec 150 (automated decisions under GDPR Art 22 permitted with the AMLR's safeguards — AMLR as the "Union law" of 22(2)(b)); AMLR Art 76(4) (purpose limitation, no commercial reuse), Art 77 (five-year retention), Rec 152 (no decisions solely on irrelevant special-category data), Art 75(4)(h) (DPIA before a partnership); PSD2 Art 94 (processing for fraud prevention permitted; explicit consent for other data), Art 73(1) (the reasonable-grounds refund delay communicated in writing); AI Act Art 86 (explanation "based mainly"; yields to other Union law), Art 10(5) (special-category data for bias detection under conditions), Rec 140 (sandbox is no Art 22(2)(b) basis), Art 27 (FRIA complements the DPIA; Annex VIII Section C); Reg. 2023/1113 Art 25–26 (travel-rule data only for AML/CFT; five years).
- Baesens Ch7 pp.317–326 (anonymisation by aggregation/discretisation/value distortion/generalisation; technical keys random not sequential — "incrementing IDs leak founding order"; SQL views; LBAC; the 1995 directive — dated) and Ch4 p.136 (legal/discrimination constraints on variables); Dal Pozzolo 2015 Sec. VI and the ULB release (PCA as the anonymisation of a public fraud set); Jurgovsky Sec. 4.1 (identifiers removed "for generalization").

## Key points
1. The lawful basis for scoring: Art 6(1)(f) with Rec 47's sentence — "strictly necessary" plus the balancing test and reasonable expectations; 6(1)(c) where AMLR/PSD2 impose the processing (AMLR Art 26, PSD2 RTS Art 2); 6(1)(b) for the payment contract; the basis must be disclosed (13(1)(d)); 6(4) for reusing transaction logs to train — the compatibility factors, with pseudonymisation as the named safeguard.
2. Article 22 on the course's decisions: a decline or an account block with no human = a decision "based solely on automated processing" with "similarly significant effect" (Rec 71's credit-refusal example is the nearest analogue); the three exceptions — contract necessity (the issuer's argument), law with safeguards (AMLR Art 76(5), PSD2 Art 68/97 as the "Union law"), explicit consent; the safeguards of 22(3) and Rec 71 (human intervention, explanation, challenge); 22(4)'s special-category bar even under an exception; the referral band of score-to-action as the design that keeps most decisions outside 22(1).
3. Rec 71's two requirements on the model itself: "appropriate mathematical or statistical procedures" and "prevent discriminatory effects" — the course's calibration (module 2), bias measurement (score-to-action's per-group rates) and the AI Act's Art 10 read as the same instruction in three texts.
4. Information duties: Art 13/14/15's "meaningful information about the logic involved, the significance and the envisaged consequences" — what a reason code must carry (alert-explanations) and what it must not (tipping-off under AMLR Art 73; Art 14(5)(b)'s "seriously impair the objectives" as the fraud-investigation carve-out debated); AI Act Art 86's narrower "role and main elements" and its yielding clause.
5. The feature audit: Art 5(1)(c) against the feature store — each feature adequate, relevant, necessary; the purpose-limitation test for reused data; storage limitation against the five-year AML retention (two clocks: the model's features vs the record); Art 9 against proxies (postcode as ethnicity, device as disability, merchant category as health or religion) — the exclusion list; Art 22(4)'s bar; the accuracy principle against stale labels (a false fraud flag is inaccurate personal data to be rectified — the dispute as a GDPR event).
6. The DPIA: Art 35(3)(a) mirrors Art 22's trigger, so a fraud scorer with consequential automated decisions presumptively needs one; its contents map onto the AI Act's FRIA (Art 27) and AMLR's partnership DPIA (Art 75(4)(h)); one document, three statutes.
7. Anonymisation for research and sharing: PCA'd features (ULB), identifiers removed (Jurgovsky), generalisation to country/EU/ROW flags (Baesens), random technical keys; pseudonymised cross-matching (AMLA's hit/no-hit) — what a public dataset can carry and why module 1's datasets look the way they do.
8. ★ Starred: Art 6(4)'s five factors in full; Art 14's one-month clock; the SCHUFA scoring judgment named as outside the dossier (the human may stage it); Baesens' pre-GDPR frame as a datedness note.

## Set pieces & gems
- Rec 47 sentence 6 — one line that grounds the whole pipeline's lawful basis.
- Rec 71: "automatic refusal of an online credit application" and the fraud carve-out in the same recital.
- Art 22(1) as a right, 22(2)(b) requiring an actual law with safeguards — AMLR Art 76(5) as that law.
- Art 22(4): no special-category data even under an exception.
- Art 35(3)(a) ≈ Art 22's trigger.
- Art 15(1)(h) vs AI Act Art 86 — two explanation duties, two scopes.
- "Incrementing IDs leak company founding order."

## EXERCISE PLAN
1. Warm-up: classify five processing steps (training on last year's logs; real-time scoring; a human-reviewed decline; an automated decline; sharing exposure scores with a partner bank) by their Art 6 basis and their Art 22 status.
2. Derivation the lesson skipped: show that a decision taken inside the referral band of score-to-action (an analyst decides) is not "based solely on automated processing" only if the intervention is meaningful (Rec 71; AMLR Art 76(5)(b)); write the condition as a property of the analyst's authority and information (can override; sees the attribution), and name the automation-bias clause (AI Act Art 14(4)(b)) that threatens it.
3. Boundary probe: a customer disputes a transaction the model flagged as fraud, and the dispute is upheld — which GDPR principle is engaged (accuracy), what must happen to the stored label, and what happens to a model trained on the uncorrected label.
4. Claim to refute: "Fraud prevention is a legitimate interest, so any feature that improves detection is lawful to process." Refute with "strictly necessary", the balancing test, Art 5(1)(c) and Art 9/22(4); name the proxy test (does the feature encode a special category?) and the necessity test (does detection fail without it?).
5. Research-shaped: audit the simulator's 14 features plus Bahnsen's aggregates and APATE's region flags against Art 5(1)(c) and Art 9: for each, state basis, necessity evidence (the savings/AP delta from modules 3–4 with and without it) and proxy risk.
6. FORWARD (E7): for a crypto-asset transfer, list the originator and beneficiary fields a provider must process and retain regardless of the data-minimisation audit — the reader meets a statute that fixes the fields → lands on `transfer-of-funds-crypto` (extra) and, for the system, `eu-transaction-monitoring-design`.
7. Mixed review (E3): the DPIA's "risk to rights" section for the alert-feedback loop (alert-feedback-selection-bias): write the risk (self-reinforcing selection of groups) and the measure (per-group rates monitored — model-risk-monitoring; AI Act Art 10(2)(f)).

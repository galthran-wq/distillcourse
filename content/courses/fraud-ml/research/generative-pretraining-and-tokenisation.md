# Generative pretraining and the tokenisation of a transaction — research brief

Lesson: `generative-pretraining-and-tokenisation` (module: learned-representations, type: core, status: planned). NEW — no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Design the conversion of a transaction into tokens — binned amounts, per-field vocabularies or key–value pairs, a soft-log time gap, hashed merchant ids — and the next-event and masked-field objectives trained on them, and explain which parts of each objective carry the downstream signal
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A transaction is a row with a timestamp, an amount with a long tail, a dozen categorical codes and a merchant id drawn from ten million. A language model reads a sequence of tokens from a fixed vocabulary and learns by predicting the next one. How does a row become tokens without losing the size of the amount or the gap since the last event, what should the model be asked to predict, and what in that prediction is useful for a task it was never trained on?

Status: TabBERT/TabGPT is a 4-page conference paper (ICASSP 2021) with a released synthetic dataset; FATA-Trans is CIKM 2023; UniTTab is Machine Learning 2025; NPPR is ICAIF '23 (public half reproducible, fraud half proprietary); PRAGMA, TransactionGPT and the Nubank paper are industry preprints with no released data; EventGPT is a preprint with released weights (CC BY 4.0); the NVIDIA post is a vendor developer example. Say which is which in References.

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The previous lesson's task was "same customer or not?"; it needed no decoder and no vocabulary. The forward exercise hit the wall: to predict the next event, the amount has nothing to predict it with. The newcomer knows cross-entropy over a categorical and MSE over a number, knows an embedding of a categorical id, and knows a GRU; they do not know what a token is in this setting, why a row is not simply serialised as text, how time enters, how ten million merchants fit, or what "predict the next event" and "fill in the hidden field" each teach the model. The lesson builds the tokeniser field by field, then the two objectives, then asks what each is worth downstream.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **Why not write the row as a sentence?** — Obvious attempt: "2024-03-12 14:07 EUR 184.00 at merchant 9023, MCC 5411" into a text tokeniser. Why it fails: field names and delimiters become most of the tokens (~39–55 per transaction), digits split so that 184 and 1840 share pieces and magnitude is lost, and a 4,092-token window then holds ~100 transactions where a purpose-built vocabulary holds ~315. Answer: give each field its own small vocabulary — a domain tokeniser of ~12–14 tokens per transaction. Because: the model's context is a budget, and a token that encodes a comma buys nothing.
2. **How does an amount become a token?** — Obvious attempt: round to the euro (a vocabulary of a million). Why it fails: too many classes, each rare. Answer: bin into quantiles of the amount's distribution — ~20 learned percentile buckets plus a zero bucket — one token per bin; or, if the amount stays numeric, feed sin/cos at doubling frequencies of the log amount (a frequency embedding) and predict its quantile bin as a class with label smoothing over neighbouring bins. Because: a bin id is a class a cross-entropy can grade, and smoothing over neighbours keeps the bins ordered; the frequency embedding separates nearby amounts that a plain scalar input cannot (and is sharp: L = 8 pairs good, L = 10 worse).
3. **How does time enter?** — Obvious attempt: position in the sequence. Why it fails: two events a minute apart and two a month apart get the same positions. Answer: the gap since the last event as 8·ln(1 + Δt/8) — linear for recent gaps, logarithmic for long ones — plus calendar fields (hour, weekday, day of month) as their own tokens or as cyclical features; in a transformer the gap also enters the positional term (a learned mix w_p·i + w_t·t_i, or rotary positions on log-time). Because: a soft-log gap makes a minute-to-hour step and a day-to-month step the same size, which is what habits look like. Measured: with a positional term, relative time adds ~0.01 PR-AUC; absolute timestamps without a positional term hurt (the reading of that table is the claims lesson's job; here only the design lesson).
4. **How do ten million merchants fit?** — Obvious attempt: an embedding row per merchant: 10M × 128 = 1.3B parameters, and a new merchant has no row. Answer: hash the id with k functions into m ≪ n slots and pool the k vectors; reserve slot 0 for the unseen; tie the output head to the input table so the merchant logits need no second table. Because: the collision cost is small (−0.6% to +1.9% on the authors' metric) against a 74% memory cut, and an unseen merchant is handled at scoring time.
5. **One token per field, or key and value?** — Answer: per-field vocabularies (each column its own token set, so "true" in column A is a different token from "true" in column B) work when every row has the same fields; key–value pairs (a key token from ~60 keys, a value token from ~28k values) work when events of different kinds — a card payment, a top-up, a login — carry different fields, and a profile of first-occurrence events can be added as more key–values. Because: the vocabulary design follows the heterogeneity of the stream.
6. **What should the model predict — the next event?** — Answer: from the history up to t, predict every field of event t+1: cross-entropy on the categorical tokens, squared error on the log amount and the log gap, summed; a GRU or a causal transformer reads the history; the state after the last event is the customer's embedding. Because: every position supervises, so one history yields hundreds of graded predictions, and the state must hold what predicts behaviour — which is what a downstream task wants. Worked: the random-guess loss ln(6251) ≈ 8.74 falling to ~6 in 30 steps on the synthetic card set.
7. **Does predicting the future need the far past?** — Obvious attempt: next-event alone. Why it fails for some tasks: churn is a decline in velocity that the last few events do not show. Answer: add a second head that reconstructs a past event x_{t−k} given the state and the gap back to it, weighted exp(−δ/λ) with λ about two months. Because: the ablation shows both heads beat either alone on all four public tasks, and past-reconstruction alone wins churn (0.833 vs 0.814 next-event alone). Pooling follows the same logic: average the per-event embeddings for a stable trait (+6.1% on age), keep the last for a recent change (−0.5% on churn if averaged).
8. **Or hide a field and fill it in?** — Answer: mask 15% of the tokens of a window of rows — at token, whole-event and whole-key level — and predict them from both directions; the row's own field-level encoder and the sequence-level encoder form a hierarchy. Because: a bidirectional reader sees the whole window at once, which suits a fixed-window representation of recent rows; the measured difference between the families is small and task-dependent (on the course's benchmark the generative transformer scored 0.802 and the masked one 0.762, both below the supervised GRU). Two shortcuts to forbid: the label column leaves the pretraining data (else the representation encodes it); a static field repeated in every row is read off a neighbour when masked, so static fields are encoded once.
9. **Can the pretrained model answer without a label?** — Answer: yes, for a question of the form "will the customer do X next month": score each customer by the model's probability of event-kind X — 0.79–0.92 ROC-AUC on five first-occurrence targets with zero labels, but only after excluding customers who already have the habit (without that rule 0.91 → 0.72). Because: a next-event model predicts habits first; a first occurrence is what it finds hardest, and that is the targets' definition. A sober comparison: a Markov chain over event kinds times recent activity scores 0.79–0.88 on the same targets.
10. **What is in the embedding that aggregates miss, and what is not?** — Answer: merchant categories cluster by industry from purchase order alone (Lufthansa next to British Airways and Air France; Hilton next to Doubletree and Hampton), so the vector carries merchant semantics no count can; on the synthetic card set the embedding alone has AP 0.012 against 0.124 for thirteen raw features, and together they reach 0.176 at ~0.1% fraud. Because: the generative state is a summary of behaviour, not a detector; it adds to features, which is the module's claim again. Note what is not in it: anything about the counterparties' own histories (the network, next lessons).

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Token", "vocabulary", "mask", "causal" are new to a reader who has not met language models; define each at first use in this domain and never assume GPT/BERT knowledge — the course's prerequisite is an MLP.
- Quantile binning vs equal-width binning: the reader met coarse classification in module 4 (scorecards); point there and say why quantiles here.
- The label column leaving pretraining and the static field encoded once are two instances of the previous lesson's shortcut idea; say so with a <Ref>, do not re-explain shortcuts.
- The TabFormer dataset is synthetic with rule-generated fraud; the TabBERT fraud F1 (0.83 → 0.86) is on upsampled classes; the NVIDIA AP numbers are on a stratified 100k holdout at the natural rate. Frame each number in its sentence.
- Do not present the generative-vs-masked contest as settled; the measured gap is task-dependent and small, and the one head-to-head on the course's benchmark put both below the supervised GRU.
- The zero-shot 0.79–0.92 is on product-first-purchase targets, not fraud; the Markov baseline sits a few hundredths below it.
- Keep "embedding of a merchant id" (a table row) and "embedding of a customer history" (an encoder state) apart; both words appear in this lesson.

# Rebalancing under honest metrics — research brief

Lesson: `imbalance-techniques-revisited` (module: rare-positives, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Evaluate class weights, resampling, synthetic oversampling and balanced ensembles on card data under average precision and card precision at k, and explain why a technique that raises ROC-AUC lowers precision
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): The standard toolkit for rare classes — class weights, oversampling, synthetic minority points, undersampling, rebalanced ensembles — was mostly judged by ROC-AUC and balanced accuracy. Under precision at the top hundred and average precision, on a forward-in-time split, which of those techniques still help a fraud detector, and why would a technique raise one metric while lowering another?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader knows the imbalance playbook (class weights, SMOTE, undersampling, balanced ensembles) from classical-ml and has just learned what undersampling does to a posterior. They expect the playbook to help; the opening says it was judged by metrics the course has set aside. The lesson runs the playbook through the honest referee (AP, CP@100, prequential folds) on the simulator and on real card data and explains the pattern that comes out: AUC up, AP down, almost every time. Terms not yet available: imbalance ratio IR, class weight, balanced bagging, scale_pos_weight, pipeline resampling.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **What does every rebalancing technique do, geometrically?** — Obvious attempt: "it makes the classes equal". Why it fails: that is the training set; the question is the decision surface. Answer: on the 2-D toy (5,000 points, 95/5, overlapping), class weighting, oversampling, SMOTE, undersampling and balanced bagging all move the boundary away from the minority points into the majority region — the minority region grows. Because: every technique raises the cost or the count of minority errors, and the cheapest way to pay is to predict more positives in the overlap.
2. **Which metrics see that move?** — Obvious attempt: all of them. Why it fails: the boundary move changes what is above a threshold, not the order of the scores (much). Answer: balanced accuracy jumps (LR 0.641 → 0.899 from one class_weight line) while AUC and AP stay put; AUC measures order, AP measures order near the top, balanced accuracy measures the threshold. Because: a threshold metric rewards moving the boundary; a ranking metric does not.
3. **On transactions, why does AUC go up and AP go down?** — Obvious attempt: a better AUC is a better ranking, so AP should follow. Why it fails: AUC integrates over the whole false-positive range; rebalancing lifts the middle of the ROC curve (more positives ranked above middling negatives) while pushing genuine rows from the overlap into the top of the ranking, which is where precision lives. Answer: on the simulator the decision tree's AUC rises with every technique (0.81 → 0.845 SMOTE) while AP falls (0.600 → 0.573) and CP@100 barely moves; on real data the same (DT AUC .869 → .881–.892; AP and CP@100 fall; SMOTE+RUS worst at AP .045, CP@100 .119). Because: the technique manufactures false positives in exactly the region the budget reaches.
4. **Is there a best imbalance ratio?** — Obvious attempt: 50/50. Why it fails: the optimum depends on the metric: for AP the best IR is the natural one (0.02 on the simulator, 0.01 on real data — i.e. do nothing), for AUC near 0.5, for CP@100 near 0.1 for balanced bagging. Answer: treat IR (or the class weight) as a hyperparameter selected by the metric you care about, on prequential folds, with the sampler inside the pipeline. Because: it is a knob with no universal setting.
5. **Then why do balanced ensembles look good?** — Obvious attempt: rebalancing works when ensembled. Why it fails: balanced bagging and balanced RF gain AUC on both datasets and a little CP@100 on real data (.248, .254 vs .209, .227) — but the gain appears for all ratios and tracks tree diversity, not the balance; a plain random forest already gets most of it. Answer: the gain comes from fitting many different trees on different subsamples, which the undersampling supplies cheaply; XGBoost without any rebalancing matches or beats them (.928/.095/.235), and its weighted version helps only for a small scale_pos_weight (1 → 5). Because: boosting on residuals already upweights the minority implicitly — each round fits the rows the previous rounds got wrong, which are mostly positives.
6. **Where may resampling run?** — Obvious attempt: resample the whole table, then split. Why it fails: synthetic positives land in validation and test, and oversampled duplicates straddle the split; the estimate is inflated. Answer: the sampler is a pipeline step fitted on training folds only, inside the prequential loop. Because: the validation set must look like the stream, not like the training trick.
7. **What survives?** — Answer: for a fraud detector judged by AP and CP@k, do nothing to the ratio unless the learner needs it; if a learner needs undersampling, correct the probabilities (previous lesson) and prefer ensembles of undersamples for the variance; prefer XGBoost; choose any ratio by the metric on prequential folds. Resampling also fails on cost (next lesson) because it distorts the cost distribution. Because: the referee changed, and the winners changed with it.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Imbalance ratio" is positives/negatives here (0.02), not the reverse; say the convention once.
- Balanced accuracy is a threshold metric; the newcomer will read its rise as a better model.
- "Balanced bagging" = RUS per bootstrap; "balanced RF" the same with feature subsampling; define at first use.
- scale_pos_weight is a cost on FN relative to FP = 1; set to 1/IR by the heuristic, but the data choose 1–5.
- Samplers inside the pipeline: the canonical bug is resampling before the split; name it.
- Do not present a league table of techniques; the lesson's claim is about the metric, not a winner.

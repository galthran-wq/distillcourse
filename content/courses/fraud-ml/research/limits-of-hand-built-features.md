# The limits of hand-built features: when learning the representation pays — research brief

Lesson: `limits-of-hand-built-features` (module: learned-representations, type: core, status: planned). NEW — module opener added after the anomaly/graph/sequence modules; no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Explain when a representation of an entity's history learned from unlabelled events is worth training instead of hand-built aggregates — by label volume, by the task's aggregation key and by the cost of maintaining features per issuer — and predict from the label regime which of the two wins
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): Fifteen windowed aggregates per card took a module to design, and they were designed for one task on one issuer's data. A second issuer has different fields, a laundering team needs a different grouping key, and for a new product line there are forty labelled cases. Each time, someone writes new features by hand. A customer's history is already a record of their behaviour; could a model learn what to summarise from the events themselves, before any label exists, and when would such a learned summary beat the hand-built one?

Status of every number below: MBD is a peer-reviewed benchmark paper (arXiv 2409.17587, with a CC BY 4.0 dataset); CoLES is peer-reviewed (SIGMOD 2022); NPPR is a workshop paper (ICAIF '23); the Sber foundation model is KDD '26 applied track; UniTTab is Machine Learning 2025; pytorch-lifestream is an IJCAI demo paper; EventGPT and the Nubank paper are preprints. Mark each as such in References; in the body the numbers stand on their own with their frame.

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader has built aggregates (module 3), watched them carry every supervised detector (module 4), seen an LSTM add signal only where hand-built aggregates were absent (module 7), and seen graph features that no per-row aggregate reaches (module 6). Each of those feature sets was designed by a person for one task. The newcomer does not yet have: the idea of an encoder trained with no label, the word "embedding" as a learned summary of a history, the label regimes in which that could pay, or any number comparing the two. The lesson gives all four and ends with a rule for choosing, not a verdict for either side.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **What does a hand-built feature set actually cost?** — Obvious attempt: features are a one-off. Why it fails: every window length, key and condition was a human choice (module 3 made about fifteen of them for one task); a second issuer's fields differ, a laundering team's unit is a counterparty pair with no obvious group-by key, and a new product's labels are a handful; the features must also be kept point-in-time correct and in parity between training and serving (module 3). Answer: the cost is per task, per issuer and forever — feature-engineering debt. Because: a feature encodes an assumption about what matters, and assumptions are owned by someone.
2. **Could the summary be learned?** — Obvious attempt: train a network end to end on the task (module 4's feed-forward detector, module 7's LSTM). Why it fails where it fails: it needs the task's labels, and the lesson's three cases (new issuer, new unit, forty positives) are exactly the ones with few. Answer: train an encoder on the events alone to produce a vector per customer — an event-sequence representation — and hand that vector to any downstream model as if it were a block of aggregates. Because: the history carries structure (habits repeat, amounts and merchants co-occur) that a model can be trained to capture without knowing the eventual question. The lesson names the object and defers the "how" to the next two lessons.
3. **What is the fair opponent?** — Answer: the aggregation baseline every paper in this module uses — sums, means and standard deviations of the numeric fields and a count per categorical value over the whole history, into a boosted tree — which on two million real bank clients with product-purchase targets scores 0.783 mean ROC-AUC over four targets, within 0.02–0.04 of the best deep model. Because: a learned representation is only worth its cost against the strongest cheap baseline, and this one is cheap.
4. **With labels for everyone, what wins?** — Answer, same benchmark, same metric: a supervised GRU trained end to end on the labels, 0.819; a generatively pretrained transformer, 0.802; the aggregates, 0.783; a contrastive embedding, 0.773. Because: when every client is labelled, nothing beats fitting the task directly — the pretrained objective is a proxy for the task and a proxy loses to the thing itself. Set the experiment up before the numbers (L0g): the task, the twelve monthly dates, the strictly-earlier-events rule, the five client folds, the metric.
5. **With few labels, what wins?** — Answer: on the 69K-client cut of the same data the ranking flips — the supervised GRU falls to last (0.745) and the embedding and the aggregates tie near 0.78; on the public contrastive benchmarks the gain of pretraining over supervised-only grows as the labelled fraction shrinks; on 478 labelled bank clients a boosted tree beats every un-pretrained transformer and loses to the pretrained one; a boosted tree on 114 engineered features needs tens to hundreds of thousands of labelled client-months — about 50–400 positives — to match a score that used no labels at all. Because: the pretraining data is the whole stream, the task's labels are a sliver of it; the representation borrows statistical strength from the unlabelled events. This is the label-scarcity regime, and it is where the module's methods live.
6. **Added or instead?** — Answer: at Sber scale the embedding alone loses to a mature engineered-feature model (0.787 vs 0.792 mean ROC-AUC on four marketing tasks) and the two together reach 0.801; in the contrastive paper the hybrid is best or tied on every bank task, including one where the embedding alone loses badly (retail churn 0.65 vs 0.74 baseline, 0.76 together); scaling the encoder from 2M to 42M parameters moves the embedding alone from 0.750 to 0.787 and the combination only from 0.792 to 0.801. Because: the aggregates already carry most of the signal; the embedding adds what windows and keys miss and duplicates the rest. The module's standing claim: a learned representation is a feature block, mostly added, rarely a replacement.
7. **Where does the learned summary have an edge by construction?** — Answer: where the grouping key is unclear — transfers between legal entities, where a hand-built aggregate must pick sender or receiver and the encoder learns the aggregation level itself (the bank's largest gains were on legal-entity tasks); and where sequence order carries signal the windows lose (module 7's card-present result). Because: a window is an assumption about which rows belong together; an encoder is not forced to make it.
8. **Which of the opening's three cases is which?** — Answer: a second issuer with different fields is a new vocabulary, not a label problem — pretraining transfers only across tasks inside one institution (hands off to `transaction-foundation-models`); the laundering unit is the unclear-key case, and also the network case the module will show pretraining cannot fix (hands off to `reading-foundation-model-claims`); forty positives is the scarcity case, where the module's methods pay. Because: the rule for choosing is a function of labels, key and vocabulary, not of model fashion.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Embedding" has appeared once (module 4's entity embedding of a categorical id, a linear layer on a one-hot). Say that an event-sequence representation is a different object: a vector computed from a whole ordered history by a trained encoder, read as of a time.
- "Self-supervised" and "pretraining": define as training on a task the data itself grades, before the downstream task; do not use "foundation model" yet.
- Every MBD number is product-purchase propensity on a Russian bank's anonymised data with jittered dates and no fraud label; say so where the table first appears and do not let the reader carry 0.819 into a fraud setting.
- The CoLES fraud result (0.82 / 0.84 / 0.85 AUROC on internal money-transfer monitoring) carries no precision, no prevalence and no released data: quote it as what it is — a modest internal AUROC gain — or leave it to the claims lesson.
- UniTTab's XGBoost collapse (0.779 → 0.349 F1 at 50-row windows) is a weak-baseline artefact (tsfresh aggregates, no tuning parity); use it as a caution about baselines, never as evidence against trees.
- Keep ROC-AUC's blindness at low prevalence (module 2) in view: every benchmark here is scored by ROC-AUC on tasks with 2–20% positives, not 0.1%.
- The distinction "supervised sequence model trained end to end" (module 7) vs "pretrained encoder + frozen embedding" is the lesson's axis; name both at first contrast.

3. [boundary probe] A customer with one event. What does a windowed aggregate return (module 3's all-zeros first row), what can an encoder return, and why does the labels-needed curve say nothing about this customer?

5. [derivation skipped] From the Sber scaling table, fit the embedding-alone and combined curves against log parameters and extrapolate the model size at which the combination would reach 0.81; state the assumption the extrapolation makes about the engineered features.
6. [mixed review] A laundering team's unit is a pair of accounts. Using <Ref> `fraud-as-a-graph`, write the two group-by keys a hand-built aggregate could use and what each loses; then state what an encoder over one account's history still cannot see (the doorstep of this module's last core lesson).
7.

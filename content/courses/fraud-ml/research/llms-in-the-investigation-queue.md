# Large language models in the investigation queue — research brief

Lesson: `llms-in-the-investigation-queue` (module: governance, type: core, status: planned). NEW — added to the governance module between the explanation lesson and model-risk monitoring; no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Design where a large language model may sit in a detection pipeline — triage against retrieved evidence, narratives from a tree's attributions, draft reports — from the measured record of frontier models on real payment data, and specify the grounding checks, the cost routing and the decisions that stay with people
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): An investigator opens an alert: a score, ten feature values and a page of transactions. A model that can read and write plain language could be asked to decide the alert, to explain it, or to draft the report that follows. Each of the three has been tried and measured. Which can be handed over, what must check the output before an investigator reads it, and which decisions may never leave a person?

Status: the frontier-model benchmark is a preprint (HKUST et al., arXiv 2601.05578, proprietary data, one run); the retrieval study is a preprint (arXiv 2512.13040, public data, three runs); the AML triage system is a preprint on synthetic data (arXiv 2604.19755); the mule pipeline is a KDD '26 workshop paper from a bank (OCBC); the report-drafting system is a preprint from a payments company (arXiv 2509.08380); Financial Crime Bench is a vendor-run benchmark post (Bretton Labs); the AMLA draft guidelines are a regulator's consultation paper (draft, not adopted). Mark each in References.

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader can produce an attribution for an alert and route a score into an action. They have heard that language models are being put into fraud and compliance work and have, from the previous module, a checklist for reading claims. They do not know: how such a model performs when asked to score a payment, why it answers at the base rate, what retrieval does and does not fix, what a narrative from a tree's attributions looks like and how it fails, what "61% time saved" was, what a benchmark of analyst calls shows, and what the law and the draft guidance say about an automated closure. The lesson derives the model's place from measurements, then the checks from the failure modes, then the boundary from the rules.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **Can the model score the payment?** — Obvious attempt: give a frontier model the order record and ask "fraud?". Measured: on 5,000 real orders at 9.6% fraud, eight frontier and large open models score F1 0.12–0.18 with precision 0.09–0.11 — the base rate; the same family sits at opposite thresholds (one flags 84% of fraud with 21% specificity, another 22% with 80%). Answer: the models recognise the signals (a proxy IP, a long shipping distance) and have no threshold for this stream; their ranking is near chance. Because: a boosted tree learned the stream's base rate and its thresholds from labels; a text model learned neither. Reinforcement fine-tuning on the stream's labels doubles F1 (0.36) — still with precision 0.31, and the larger model's explanations start fabricating evidence (hallucination pass rate down 46.7 points). No tree baseline is reported in that study; module 4's trees on real card data are the reader's reference.
2. **Does showing it labelled neighbours fix that?** — Answer: retrieve the twenty most similar labelled transactions (importance-ranked features, a categorical filter, cosine over z-scored numerics) and put them in the prompt: a 14B model goes from F1 0.04 to 0.62 on a public e-commerce set where XGBoost scores 0.74 and a tabular network 0.82; on the ULB card set from 0.00 to 0.31 against XGBoost's 0.89. Because: the neighbours carry the labels and the model mostly votes on them — on a set with 13 test positives whose neighbours are all positive, every model's recall is 0.85 (11/13); the retrieval is a k-nearest-neighbour classifier with a text model as the tally. Latency: prompts 10–13× longer; 1–1.6 s per query for a non-reasoning model and 8–45 s for a reasoning one, retrieval excluded — against module 11's sub-second budget. Rule: scoring stays with the trees; the model does not rank payments.
3. **Then what does it do after detection?** — Answer: three jobs with measured records. Triage: given an alert and a bundle of retrieved evidence (policy clauses, customer file, transactions, similar cases — filtered by access rights before the model sees it), return dismiss / monitor / escalate with a rationale that cites evidence ids. On synthetic AML data the model alone scores PR-AUC 0.52 below a boosted tree's 0.63 and a graph network's 0.69; with retrieval 0.71; with retrieval and counterfactual checks 0.75 at precision 0.53 vs the GNN's 0.46 at similar recall. Because: the gain over the tree is a system — retrieval, a verifier, checks — not the model; the baseline comparisons there are on synthetic data and the lesson says so.
4. **How does a rationale lie?** — Answer: by citing evidence the decision does not depend on — a decorative citation — and by inventing policy references: the model alone has citation validity 0.62 (four in ten cited ids do not exist in the bundle) and a policy hallucination rate of 0.14; with retrieval and a verifier 0.96–0.98 and 0.02–0.05. The test: remove the cited item and check that the disposition and the citations change; propose a minimal edit to the facts and check the call flips. Because: an audit record with one fabricated policy reference is void (module 10's Art 77 record), so faithfulness is a measured property, not a style.
5. **Narratives from a tree.** — Answer: the bank's design — 280 features into LightGBM, TreeSHAP's top ten attributions with their percentiles, a self-hosted model that may cite only those ten into a fixed JSON of red flags. Measured on 20 confirmed alerts by blinded raters: the fastest model best on accuracy at 5.7 s, the slowest worst at 40 s; failure modes — attribution values exaggerated, occasional 10× magnitude errors, fabricated attribution values, a ratio's direction misread. The investigators adopted the narrative and had not adopted the attribution plots. Live: yield 89% vs 61% for the rule system over one month with three analysts, alerts up from 211 to 302 — a measured operational number with its small frame stated. Because: module 9's explanation lesson showed the attribution is the honest object; the narrative is its translation and the translation has an error rate.
6. **What checks the output?** — Answer: a grounding verifier independent of the generator — every amount, date, identity, attribution value and percentile in the draft re-read against the structured record it came from; a mismatch sends the draft back with the specific failure ("you referenced an evidence id not in the bundle"; "your total conflicts with evidence T3"). Plus: identities masked before any external model and unmasked only in the investigator's draft; the model's top-ten constraint; periodic human sampling. Because: the generator cannot be its own verifier (the fine-tuned model that reasoned better also fabricated more), and the structured record is ground truth the pipeline already holds.
7. **What does "61% time saved" measure?** — Answer: six investigators rated "efficiency" 3.06 on a 1–5 scale; 3.06/5 = 61%; the conclusion reports it as time saved. No stopwatch, no control arm, no count of drafts; the same paper's own observation of plain prompting was that correcting hallucinated detail "nullified any time benefits"; the only real time figure is the regulator's 25–315 minutes per report. Because: the previous module's checklist applies to benefit claims as to accuracy claims — a perceived benefit renormalised to a percentage is a rating, and a measured one needs the review cost of the draft on the other side of the ledger.
8. **Can it make the analyst's call?** — Answer: a benchmark of 79 analyst-decided AML cases: the best configuration passes 42% of rollouts (the right call AND a reasoning score ≥ 0.8), pooled 28%; 42–66% of right calls fail the reasoning check; 8–21× more reasoning tokens buy −1 to +5 points; most models lean lenient (clearing each red flag alone — the 81-year-old's case dismissed by every rollout of seven models); three rollouts agree on 79% of cases, so the failures are systematic. Because: a right call with a wrong record is the record the next reviewer inherits — and it is vendor-run, with the methodology unpublished, so the lesson reads it as one measurement.
9. **What may never leave a person?** — Answer: under the AML Regulation the detection criteria, the risk profile, the onboarding decision and the suspicious-transaction report are the bank's own and cannot be outsourced (Art 18(3)); an automated refusal, exit or CDD change needs meaningful human intervention, an explanation and a challenge route (Art 76(5)) — both read in full in module 10; the draft AMLA guidelines (consultation closed September 2026; final text expected Q4 2026 — quote as draft) say AI is "neither mandatory nor, in itself, an indicator of effectiveness", that outputs must be reviewable and challengeable without "full technical interpretability", that a third-party tool the bank cannot explain must not materially influence outcomes, and that automated closure is allowed only where no indicator remains and never for higher-risk customers. The bank's own paper says the narrative is "not an independent basis for adverse action". Because: the model drafts; a person decides and signs; the log records both.
10. **Where in the queue, then, and at what cost?** — Answer: after the score and the attribution; before the investigator; routed only to the top x% or the ambiguous band (module 9's referral zone), with counterfactual checks capped at one or two per alert; self-hosted where data residency requires; the drafting and the review cost both on the ledger. Because: the design falls out of the measurements — the model's rank is chance, its narration is useful with an error rate, its benefit is unmeasured until timed, and the law fixes the signature.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Large language model", "prompt", "retrieval", "fine-tuning", "rollout" — define each in plain words at first use; the course assumes no language-model background.
- F1 is threshold-dependent; say so once and read every F1 beside its precision and the base rate (module 2's lesson, recalled).
- Keep the three data regimes apart in every sentence: real payment orders (proprietary, one run), public tabular sets (three runs, small test samples), synthetic AML data (illustrative). Never pool their numbers.
- The 89%-yield number is real and small-framed (one month, three analysts, 20 narratives rated); the 61% is a rating; the 42% is vendor-run. Each carries its frame in its sentence.
- The AMLA guidelines are a DRAFT; the lesson quotes them as supervisory expectations under consultation and the EU module's AML brief carries the same caveat.
- No model or vendor names in prose (L8a) beyond what is needed to distinguish "a frontier model", "a 14B open model", "a bank's self-hosted model"; names live in References.
- Do not re-teach SHAP, reason codes or the referral zone; one <Ref> each.

## EXERCISE PLAN

3. [boundary probe] A triage model with citation validity 1.0 and decorative citations throughout. Show that the provenance metric is satisfied and the record is still void; derive which counterfactual check catches it and why the other does not.
4. [derivation skipped] Write the ledger a measured time-saving study would need — drafting time, review and correction time per draft, the control arm, the 25–315-minute baseline — and show the condition under which a 70%-complete draft saves no time at all (the paper's own Sec 3.2 observation, made quantitative).
5.

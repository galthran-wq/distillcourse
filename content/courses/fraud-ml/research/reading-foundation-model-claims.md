# Reading a foundation-model claim: baselines, prevalence, leakage and the network — research brief

Lesson: `reading-foundation-model-claims` (module: learned-representations, type: core, status: planned). Module-final APPLICATION (L16): the artifact is the published record itself — seven claims read against the course's results. NEW — no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Evaluate a published or vendor claim about a pretrained transaction model by locating its baseline, its metric's frame, its test prevalence, its pretraining data and its blindness to the network, and restate each headline number in the course's own terms
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A vendor reports a detection rate rising from 59% to 97% overnight; a research team reports a 22.5% improvement on a business-critical metric; a workshop paper reports a precision–recall area of 0.958. None of these says what was measured against what, on how many frauds in how many payments. Given the course's results so far, what questions turn each of these numbers into something that can be compared with a boosted tree on windowed aggregates — or shown to be incomparable?

Status, per claim: Stripe — vendor blog; TransactionGPT — industry preprint (Visa Research); FraudTransformer — ICAIF '25 workshop paper; Temporal Contrastive Transformer — industry preprint (NICE Actimize); EventGPT — preprint, public data and released weights; PRAGMA — industry preprint (Revolut); NPPR — ICAIF '23; NVIDIA — vendor developer post; UniTTab — Machine Learning 2025. The two AML pretraining papers (GCPAL; GT-SSL) were NOT obtained — see the AML section below.

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The module has given the reader the methods and the module's claim; the field's public record consists mostly of numbers that cannot be checked. The newcomer has every tool needed — the base-rate funnel, AP's prevalence dependence, the delay block, point-in-time leakage, the graph module — but has never been asked to turn them on a headline. The lesson is the module's application: seven claims, each read with one question from the course until it either becomes a number the reader can compare with module 4's boosted tree or is shown to be incomparable, and a short section on what no claim yet covers (AML pretraining). It takes no position on the papers' worth beyond what the numbers allow (L17 does not apply: nothing here is a verdict, every reading is a computation or a missing quantity).

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **Against what?** — Claim: "+22.5% on a business-critical anomaly detection metric against a competitive production model." Obvious reading: a quarter better at catching fraud. Why it fails: the metric is proprietary ("accuracy under an operational threshold"), the baseline's absolute value is never given, the production model trained on complete rows while the new one trained on rows with missing fields, and the paper never calls the task fraud. Answer: the number is the top of an ablation ladder (−90.5 without features, +7.7 for a single-transaction model with no sequence, +7.3 for the sequence model with features, +19.2 for the full model, +22.5 with text-initialised merchant embeddings); the lesson reads the ladder: the sequence added nothing over the single-transaction model until a third encoder fused the ~400 features, and without features the pretrained model is useless on the metric. Because: a relative gain hides its denominator; the one thing the ladder does establish is the module's claim — the features carry the metric. Rule 1: find the baseline's absolute score and what it was given; if absent, the gain is unevaluable.
2. **At what prevalence?** — Claim: "PR-AUC 0.958, above LightGBM's 0.945." Obvious reading: a 0.013 lead on the metric module 2 said to use. Why it fails: the test set is 43% fraud by undersampling genuine rows; a random ranker's PR-AUC equals the prevalence (module 2), so the floor is 0.43 and every number sits in a compressed band; at the stream's 0.1–0.4% the same ranking would give an AP in the tenths at best (module 4's real-data APs sit near 0.10). Answer: recompute what a 43%-prevalence PR-AUC can and cannot say — ROC-AUC is prevalence-free (0.9672 vs 0.9621, a gap of 0.005), precision is not; the lead over the trees is one run of deterministic trees given the same binned fields rather than aggregates over the 500-event history; the largest gain in the table is a plain positional encoding (+0.027), the time encoder adds ~0.01; the abstract's "fewer missed frauds and lower false alarms" names no operating point. Because: the undersampled posterior (module 2's β) shifts every precision-type number; without the real prevalence nothing here reaches module 2's P@100. Rule 2: find the test prevalence; a precision-type metric at a shifted prevalence is a different quantity.
3. **Is parity a win?** — Claim: "embeddings approximate domain features without manual engineering." Numbers: 0.9245 raw / 0.9205 raw + embeddings / 0.8644 alone, one chronological split, no intervals, train AUC 0.99 against test 0.92. Answer: a null result — the embedding carries real signal (0.86 alone is not noise) that the thirty hand-built features already express; a −0.004 on one split is not evidence of harm either. The paper's own Sec 6.2 says so; its abstract does not. Because: signal is not incremental signal, which is the module's mechanism from lesson 2. Rule 3: read the table before the abstract, and treat one split without intervals as one draw (module 4's prequential variance is 3–8× the same-period estimate).
4. **Did pretraining see the answer?** — Claim: an embedding scored on clients whose events were in the pretraining stream. Obvious reading: leakage, discard. Why that fails: leakage is a quantity, and here it was measured — pretraining only on events before the test period (54% of the tokens) scores 0.789 against 0.793, matched for exposure 0.787; the overlap is worth 0.004–0.006 and not significant. Answer: ask whether the pretraining cut is before the test period and whether the vocabulary and bins were fitted on pretraining folds only; where the authors did not say (the synthetic-card post: 13 unnamed features, pretraining period unstated; the TabBERT protocol: test sequences in pretraining; stride-5 windows overlapping train and test moving an RMSE from 20 to 25), the number is a ceiling. Also the label-construction trap: a month with no transactions makes 99.1% of its rows "churned", and a model that can date the month scores 0.91 instead of 0.83 — module 3's leak through time in a new costume. Because: the course's point-in-time rule applies to pretraining data exactly as to features. Rule 4: find the pretraining cut and the vocabulary's fitting set; measure the overlap if you can.
5. **What is a before/after on one slice?** — Claim: "59% to 97% overnight." Answer: a deployment-time comparison on "attacks on large users", with no definition of detection rate, no false-positive rate, no baseline model, no sample size, no window; set beside "attacks down 80% on Stripe while up industry-wide", which the same post attributes to features, labelling and retraining — two claims that must not be conflated. The logic is sound (card testing hides in a large merchant's volume, so a per-payment score sees nothing and a sequence does), and sound logic is not a measurement. Because: a detection rate without its denominator and its false-positive cost is the hit rate module 1 said is not a target. Rule 5: a vendor number is a hypothesis to test on your own stream, with your P@k.
6. **Relative to a 2012 baseline?** — Claim: "up to 140% VDR uplift at a 5:1 false-positive ratio." Answer: the frame is good — three hold-out issuers at 0.04–0.11% fraud, split by time and by cardholder, the metric value-weighted at a stated operating point (module 2's cost view) — and the baseline is a fourteen-aggregate feature set from 2012; the absolute VDR is in a plot (about 0.05–0.17 for the baseline at 2:1–5:1 on the first issuer), and "up to" is the best issuer at one ratio. Because: a relative lift against a weak baseline is large by construction; the public-benchmark half of the same paper puts the margins among good methods at ~0.03 AUC. Rule 6: ask how strong the baseline was, and read "up to".
7. **Does the embedding see the network?** — Claim, by the authors themselves: a linear probe on the 1B model's embedding scores −47.1% F0.5 against the production network-aware AML baseline. Answer: the authors give two reasons — the AML dataset is large enough that the baseline learns alone, and AML is relational: the signal is in cross-record network features a per-history encoder never forms. Because: module 6 showed nine propagated exposure features alone reach AUC 0.92 and a random forest on neighbour aggregates beats a graph network on Elliptic; a customer's history is one node's own rows, and laundering is a shape across nodes. Network blindness is the module's limit, stated by its strongest advocate. Rule 7: ask what the embedding cannot see by construction.
8. **What does a well-made claim look like?** — Answer: the anonymisation check (model ranking on private vs public data, Kendall τ 0.94), the out-of-time pretraining run, the staleness test (events inside the p99.9 propagation delay dropped, ≤ 0.2% movement), the clustered standard error over twelve correlated rows per client, two seeds reported as a 0.022 spread, the aggregation-baseline row in every table. Because: the module's own numbers (the lab's confidence interval) must meet the same bar.
9. **AML pretraining — what is not known yet.** — Two papers propose self-supervised pretraining for anti-money-laundering on graphs (GCPAL, Lu and Wang, Int. J. Comput. Intell. Syst. 17, 2024, doi 10.1007/s44196-024-00720-4; GT-SSL, Shang, Discover AI 6, 2026, doi 10.1007/s44163-026-02264-2). Neither could be read for this brief (Springer's bot check; recorded in sources.yaml as not obtained; the human will download them). The section may quote their numbers only after they are read. What can be said now: no head-to-head of pretraining against a strong supervised GNN or boosted trees on graph features has been found so far; PRAGMA's −47.1% is the one measured comparison of a history encoder against a network-aware production model, and it went the other way. The section is one paragraph until the papers are read, and must not grow by inference.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- Do not call TransactionGPT's metric fraud; the paper does not. Say "anomaly detection on an undisclosed product" and let the reader see the inference.
- "Relative improvement" must be defined once as (x/baseline − 1) and then flagged every time a number is one.
- VDR and FP-ratio are new names for module 2's quantities (value-weighted recall; false positives per true positive); define by the course's terms.
- F0.5 weights precision over recall; say so where the AML number appears.
- The lesson must not read as a prosecution: every source is credited where its design is good (the NPPR frame, the EventGPT disclosure, the PRAGMA staleness test, the MBD check), and the checklist is applied to the lab's own number too.
- No author names in prose (L8a); the claims are referred to by what they are ("a payment processor's post", "a card network's report", "a workshop paper on a bank's data").
- Keep the AML paragraph to what is known; the two unread papers are named only in References, with "not yet read" removed once they are.

# Entity history aggregates: RFM, windows and conditional sums — research brief

Lesson: `transaction-aggregates` (module: features, type: core, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Construct windowed count and amount aggregates over a cardholder's and a terminal's earlier transactions, overall and conditional on type and country, and explain why each one carries signal the raw record does not
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A single card payment record says: card 4471, terminal 9023, EUR 184, Tuesday 14:07. Nothing in those four fields says whether it is fraud; the same payment is normal for one cardholder and alarming for another. How do you turn a card's and a terminal's past into numbers that stand next to the payment, computed only from what happened before it?

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader has the loop (module 1) and the referee (module 2): a model scores each payment, the top hundred go to investigators, and the score is judged by card precision on a forward-in-time test week. What has not been said is what the model is given. A payment arrives as a row: identifiers (card, terminal), an amount, a timestamp. The identifiers are large integers with no order; a tree splitting on `terminal_id > 5012` learns nothing a human would keep. The amount means nothing by itself: EUR 184 is a weekly shop for one card and ten times the usual for another. The timestamp is one instant. The hard constraint, inherited from module 2's delay block: whatever is computed for this row may use only rows that came before it, because the live system scores the payment before anything after it exists. Terms the newcomer does not yet have: "aggregate", "window", "RFM", "entity", "feature store" (not this lesson), "terminal risk" (next lesson).

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **Why the raw row cannot be the input** — Obvious attempt: hand the four fields to a boosted tree, which is scale-free and handles integers. Why it fails: the model needs features that are both numerical and ordered; a card id is numerical but not ordered, so any split is a lookup table over cards seen in training, which is memorisation of the cards, not of fraud; the amount has no reference point; the simulator's depth-2 tree on raw fields recovers only the amount > 220 rule and nothing of scenarios 2 and 3. Answer: replace identifiers by summaries of the entity's past; make the amount relative to the card's own past. Because: fraud is a departure from this card's habit, and a habit is a statistic over its history.
2. **Which summaries of a history** — Obvious attempt: the mean amount over everything the card ever did. Why it fails: a three-year mean hides last week's change; a compromised card looks like itself until the window ends. Answer: recency, frequency, monetary (RFM) — time since the last payment, count of payments in a period, amount summed or averaged in a period — at one or more windows. Because: the three questions "when did they last pay, how often, how much" are the three ways a card's habit can be violated; the probe-then-spend pattern reads as recent-low then recent-high on the monetary axis.
3. **Which period** — Obvious attempt: pick one window (a month). Why it fails: a burst of five payments in an hour is invisible in a 30-day count and glaring in a 1-day count; a card that doubled its monthly spend is invisible in a 1-day window. Answer: several windows at once — the handbook's 1/7/30 days, or hours (1, 3, 6, 12, 18, 24, 72, 168) in the published grid; there is no consensus on the lengths, so they are chosen per data and kept few. Because: windows of different lengths see different speeds of change, and the model can weigh them.
4. **Computing it without reading the future** — Obvious attempt: `groupby(card).rolling('7d')` on the sorted table. Why it fails if done carelessly: a rolling window that includes the current row leaks the current amount into its own feature (harmless for the count, wrong for "amount of the previous week"); a window computed over the whole table reads rows after the payment. Answer: sort by time, roll over strictly earlier rows (the worked table's first row is all zeros), one entity at a time; the handbook's rolling window includes the current row for count and mean and the lesson must say which convention it uses. Because: the feature must equal what the live system could have computed at the row's timestamp.
5. **The worked table** — seven payments of one card over three days; for each, count and sum over the previous 24 h, then count and sum over the previous 24 h restricted to the same type and the same country: (0,0,0,0), (1,250,1,250), (2,650,0,0), (3,900,0,0), (3,700,1,50), (2,150,2,150), (3,400,0,0). Carry the arithmetic through row by row. Because: a reader who can reproduce this table by hand can write the lab.
6. **Conditioning the window** — Obvious attempt: counts over the card's whole activity. Why it fails: a card that pays in France daily and once in Brazil has an ordinary daily count; the anomaly is "how often has this card paid in this country". Answer: the same window restricted to rows sharing the current row's categorical value (type, country, merchant group) — agg2 in the published notation, Eqs. 9–11. Because: the feature asks "is this kind of payment usual for this card", which is a contextual question the plain window cannot ask. Note the two semantics: a count at a fixed named merchant (mostly zero, consistent) versus a count at the same merchant as the current payment (changes meaning per row).
7. **The terminal's past** — the same windows over the terminal's rows (count over 1/7/30 days); the terminal's fraud rate is deferred to the next lesson because it uses labels. Because: a compromised terminal is the simulator's scenario 2, and a count feature alone does not see it.
8. **Does it pay** — Obvious attempt: assume more features help. Answer: on 236,735 real transactions, aggregates over raw fields raise savings by about 201%; adding the conditional aggregates on top adds to 252%; conditional aggregates alone (raw+agg2) do worse than raw+agg1. Because: the conditional sums add information only relative to the unconditional baseline they are compared against.
9. **What leaves the feature vector** — the card id and the terminal id as raw columns: a blocked card never transacts again, so an id-keyed memory makes offline evaluation optimistic; the aggregates carry what the id carried. ★ Starred: 60-variable RFM grids at five aggregation levels; region dummies (ROW 5.36% fraud vs Belgium 0.05%); currency features neutralised when the euro dominates; the latency warning that an unbounded feature list cannot be recomputed per payment within the authorisation deadline (forward to module 11).

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Aggregate", "window", "RFM", "entity" (a card, a terminal, an account — the thing with a history): define at first use, in that order.
- "Strictly earlier rows": say whether the current row is in the window, per feature; the handbook's count includes it, the worked table excludes it.
- Handbook erratum: the night flag is described as "0pm to 6am" and coded `hour <= 6`; state one definition (hour < 6) and use it.
- "Merchant" vs "terminal" vs "merchant category": the sources use three granularities; the simulator has terminals only.
- Do not use "feature store", "as-of join", "terminal risk", "label delay" here beyond a forward pointer; they are the next lesson's answers.
- Units: amounts in EUR, windows in hours or days — write both every time.

## EXERCISE PLAN
1. [warm-up] Extend the seven-row table with an eighth payment (04/01/15 09:10, same type, a new country, EUR 300) and compute its four aggregates by hand.
2. [boundary probe] A card's very first payment has every aggregate at zero; show that a model trained on aggregates alone cannot distinguish a new customer from a dormant one, and name which raw field or flag restores the distinction.
3. [claim to refute] "Including the current row in the 7-day mean amount is harmless because it is one row among many." Refute with a card that pays once a week: the current amount is the mean, and the feature equals the target of the question it is supposed to answer.
4. [derivation skipped] Show that count and sum over a window of length t_p for every t_p in a nested set can be computed in one pass over the sorted rows with a two-pointer sweep, and state the cost per entity.
5. [research-shaped] On the simulator, measure how the 1-day customer count and the 30-day mean amount separate scenario-3 frauds (×5 amounts for 14 days) from genuine payments, as AP of each feature alone; which window finds scenario 3 and which cannot.
6. [mixed review] Compute card precision at 100 (`alert-budget-metrics`) for a depth-2 tree on raw fields and on the 14 features on one simulator week, with the delay block of `delayed-labels-prequential`; report the lift.
7. FORWARD → `point-in-time-features`: compute a terminal's fraud rate over the last 30 days with today's labels and again with labels only up to seven days ago; plot the difference per day — the reader sees the feature the live system cannot compute before the next lesson names the fix.

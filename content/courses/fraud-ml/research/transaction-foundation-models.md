# Transaction foundation models: pretrain once, adapt to every task — research brief

Lesson: `transaction-foundation-models` (module: learned-representations, type: core, status: planned). NEW — no draft exists, so no REVISION block.
Goal: Analyze the design of an institution-scale transaction foundation model — hierarchical encoders, profile state, training cost, probe versus low-rank fine-tuning, fusion with tabular features and serving at authorisation time — and attribute where its gains come from
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A bank runs forty scoring models, each with its own feature pipeline, each retrained from its own labels. Twenty-six million customers' histories sit in one store. Could one model be trained once on all of them and then fitted to each task in hours, what would it cost to train and to serve inside a payment's deadline, and where would it get its gains — from the history alone, or only beside the features each team already has?

Status: every institution-scale source here is an industry report — PRAGMA (Revolut, arXiv 2604.08649), TransactionGPT (Visa Research, arXiv 2511.08939), nuFormer (Nubank, arXiv 2507.23267), the Sber model (KDD '26 applied track, arXiv 2607.09955), NPPR (Featurespace, ICAIF '23) — on proprietary data, mostly with relative gains against internal baselines and without intervals; EventGPT is a single-author preprint with released weights on public data. The lesson reads them for design and cost; the next lesson reads them for claims.

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
The reader can now tokenise a transaction and name two pretraining objectives, and has seen them on a small encoder. The newcomer does not know what changes at twenty-six million customers and 24 billion events: how a history of thousands of events fits a model at all, what a "profile" is, what the training costs in hardware and days, how the one model is fitted to forty tasks, how its vector meets the features each team already has, and how it is served when a payment must be answered in under a second. The lesson derives each from its constraint, and ends by attributing the gains with the one controlled ablation table the sources offer.

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **How does a 6,500-event history fit a model?** — Obvious attempt: one token sequence, 8.6 tokens per event, ~56,000 tokens; attention is quadratic. Why it fails: cost and memory. Answer: a hierarchy — a per-event encoder turns each event's tokens into one vector, a history encoder reads the sequence of event vectors; attention within an event and across events are separate (2.5–4× throughput over a flat decoder at 2,048–4,096 tokens); local attention of width w where the far past is not needed; sequence packing by event count for rectangular batches. Because: the sequence model should read events, not sub-tokens, and the intra-event structure is the field encoder's job. Trade-off: compressing an event to one vector bottlenecks it — emit a few vectors per event if the task needs width.
2. **What does truncation lose, and what is a profile?** — Obvious attempt: keep the most recent 6,500 events. Why it fails: account-age milestones — first top-up, first card, first login from abroad — fall off the window for an active customer. Answer: a profile state of first-occurrence events with their timestamps, encoded once and prepended. Because: the largest single ablation in the external-fraud task is removing it — precision −46.8%, recall −85.6% relative at the smallest size — and the mechanism is that fraud on a new account looks like fraud by its age, not by its last events. Static fields encoded once is the previous lesson's shortcut rule seen from the other side.
3. **What does it cost to train?** — Answer: at 207B tokens, bf16, Muon/AdamW: a 10M model about two days on 16 H100s, 100M and 1B models about two weeks each on 16 and 32 H100s; at the small end, 332M tokens train in 1.5–2 hours on one L4; a 203M model on another bank's data took seven days on 8 A100s. Because: the cost is tokens × parameters, paid once per institution; the comparison is forty feature pipelines and their retraining, not one boosted tree. Frame every figure as wall-clock at a stated size.
4. **Does scale pay?** — Answer: task-dependent — credit scoring's relative PR-AUC gain rises from +70% to +130% from 10M to 1B parameters while lifetime value stays flat and the smallest model is already competitive on recurring-transaction detection; on the four marketing tasks, 2M → 42M lifts the embedding alone from 0.750 to 0.787 and the combination with features only from 0.792 to 0.801; longer context (256 → 1024 events) adds ~0.005. Because: scale buys what the features lack, and the features already hold most of it. Write the lesson's rule: spend on scale where the embedding stands alone, not where it sits beside a mature feature set.
5. **How is one model fitted to forty tasks?** — Answer: two ways. A probe — standardise the frozen [USR] vector, fit a logistic regression in minutes (a boosted tree on 192 dimensions works but tunes slower); or low-rank adapters (rank 8 on the attention and MLP weights, 2–4% of parameters) trained on the task's labels in about one eighth of the pretraining time. Measured on external fraud: LoRA beats the probe by +30.8% precision / +27.4% recall at size S, and beats the same architecture trained from scratch on the task by +8.0% / +17.0% at size M. Because: a probe can only re-weight what the pretraining kept; the adapters can move the representation toward the task, and the pretraining is what the from-scratch model lacks. Which to choose: a probe for a team with hundreds of positives and an afternoon; adapters for a task with labels to spend and a GPU-day.
6. **How does the vector meet the features the team already has?** — Answer: three fusions. Early: the frozen embedding and the features into one boosted tree (the lab's design; the ensemble that adds +0.014–0.018 ROC-AUC on every fold of the public set is this, by rank averaging, and dropping the embedding into the tree as columns did not help there). Late: fine-tune the encoder on the task, then a tree on embedding plus features (+0.97% relative AUC on credit default). Joint: end to end, with a neural branch over the tabular features (+1.25%) — a tree cannot join because nothing flows back through it, and the neural branch has to be made to match a boosted tree first (periodic numeric embeddings, a cross network, weight decay: −0.09% → +0.08%). Because: the gain of joint over late is about a quarter of a point of relative AUC, bought with a tabular network that had to be tuned to parity — the fusion choice is an engineering budget, not a modelling truth. The module's claim measured a fourth time: embeddings alone 0.787 < features 0.792 < both 0.801; and ~90% of redundant hand-built transaction features became removable once the fusion was in place.
7. **How is it served inside the deadline?** — Obvious attempt: run the encoder over the history at authorisation. Why it fails: the history is thousands of events and the budget is sub-second. Answer: compute the embedding ahead of time and store it per customer as a feature — a GRU state advanced by each new event (one step, not a re-read), or a transformer re-run in a daily batch (about 5 hours on 60 A100s for 100M+ customers) with delta scoring so only customers with a new event or a changed silence bucket are rescored; quantise the stored vector (256 floats → 128 bytes). The embedding carries an as-of time — the "now" event with the silence since the last event — and embeddings from two checkpoints are not comparable, so the store records the checkpoint. Because: the embedding is a feature, and features obey module 3's rules — point-in-time, parity, versioned. Robustness check: dropping events inside the event bus's p99.9 propagation delay moves fraud precision/recall by ≤ 0.2% relative, so a slightly stale embedding is safe at scoring time.
8. **Does one model transfer?** — Answer: across tasks inside one institution, yes (that is the point); across issuers inside one processor's data, frozen embeddings roughly matched issuer-specific fine-tuning on three hold-out issuers in countries absent from pretraining; across institutions, no — the vocabulary is the bank's own integer codes; only the time tokens are universal. Because: tokens are an institution's language. This closes the opening's "second issuer" case from the module's first lesson.
9. **Where do the gains come from?** — Answer, from the one ablation the sources allow: the profile state (largest), then pretraining over training from scratch (+8/+17 on fraud; +0.004 mean ROC-AUC on marketing where labels are plentiful), then adapters over a probe, then scale — and all of it additive to features. Because: a controlled comparison is the only thing that attributes a gain; the next lesson shows what happens when the baseline is not controlled.
10. **What did the online test measure?** — Answer: a two-month A/B with 10% treatment against production boosted trees, +1% total net present value across products, reported in total because one optimiser allocates contact capacity across the products (the arms interfere). Because: an offline AUC is not the business number, and the business number has its own design problems.

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- "Foundation model" is named here for the first time: one encoder pretrained once on the institution's stream and adapted to many tasks; do not use it earlier in the module.
- "LoRA", "probe", "[USR] token", "profile state", "sequence packing", "delta scoring" — define each at first use in plain words; the reader has an MLP and a GRU, not a transformer course.
- Every PRAGMA number is a relative percentage against an internal baseline whose absolute value is unknown; say "relative" in the same sentence every time, and keep the fraud task's name ("external fraud", binary, precision/recall) as the source gives it.
- nuFormer's task is credit default, Sber's are marketing responses, NPPR's three issuers are fraud with VDR at an FP-ratio: never pool them into one claim about fraud.
- The joint-fusion gain over late fusion (~0.28 points of relative AUC) is small; do not let the diagram imply joint is the answer.
- The serving section must not re-teach the feature store or the latency budget (module 3; module 11 ahead); one <Ref> each and the new thing only — an embedding is a feature with an as-of time and a checkpoint.
- The AML −47.1% appears here in one sentence as the limit of a per-customer history; its full reading belongs to the next lesson.

# The Transfer of Funds Regulation and the crypto travel rule — research brief

Lesson: `transfer-of-funds-crypto` (module: eu-regulation, type: extra, status: planned). NEW — full re-synthesis, no draft exists, so no REVISION block. Off the core path: no non-extra lesson may require it.
Goal: Identify the originator and beneficiary data that must travel with a funds or crypto transfer under Regulation 2023/1113, the threshold logic, and the missing-information checks a receiving institution must run
Opening (verbatim, the lesson must start with it): A EUR 900 transfer inside the Union needs only two account numbers to travel with it; a EUR 50 crypto transfer must carry the sender's name, address and ledger address, and a transfer to a wallet nobody custodies obliges the exchange to find out who controls it. Which fields the law makes travel with money, and what the receiving institution must check, are the inputs of a screening model.

## EXPLANATION PLAN
### The problem as the newcomer meets it
Module 6 built graph features from counterparties; module 10's first lesson said suspicion rests partly on "the origin, destination or use of funds". The newcomer asks where the counterparty's identity comes from at all when money crosses institutions: a bank sees its own customer, and the other end is a name and an account number sent along in the payment message — if they are sent. The questions: which fields the law makes travel with a transfer, for banks and for crypto exchanges; why the amounts differ (EUR 1 000 for funds, nothing for crypto); what the receiving institution must check and may do when a field is missing; and what a wallet with no custodian does to all of this. Terms to define at first use: payer/payee and originator/beneficiary (the funds and crypto vocabularies), payment service provider and intermediary PSP, crypto-asset service provider (CASP, by reference to MiCA), distributed-ledger address, self-hosted address (Art 3(20): not held by a CASP), unique transaction identifier (UTI), legal entity identifier (LEI), batch file transfer, restrictive measures (sanctions), FATF Recommendation 16 (one clause: the international "travel rule" the Regulation implements).

### Chain of sub-problems
1. **What must travel with a bank transfer?** — Obvious attempt: the account numbers suffice. Why it fails: Art 4(1): the payer's name, payment account number, "address including the name of the country, official personal document number and customer identification number, or, alternatively, the payer's date and place of birth", and the LEI where the field exists; 4(2): the payee's name and account number (and LEI if provided); 4(3): a UTI when there is no account; 4(4): verified before the transfer; 4(6): no execution before compliance. Answer: five fields out, two in, verified. Because: a receiving institution can only screen a name it was given. Map: the payment message's fields are the screening model's inputs; a missing field is a feature in itself.
2. **Why does the EUR 900 transfer need less?** — Obvious attempt: small transfers are exempt. Why it fails: Art 5(1): when all PSPs in the chain are in the Union, "at least the payment account number of both the payer and the payee" travels — because every PSP in the Union already holds verified CDD on its customer; Art 5(2): on request, within three working days, full information for transfers "exceeding EUR 1 000, whether such transfers are carried out in a single transaction or in several transactions which appear to be linked", and at least names and account numbers below; Art 6: outside the Union, batch files and the ≤ EUR 1 000 unlinked case; verification is waived below EUR 1 000 unless cash or anonymous e-money or suspicion. Answer: the data exists on both ends; the law lets it stay there and be fetched. Because: Rec 29's trade-off — too low a line drives transactions underground, too high a line misses small terrorist-financing transfers. Map: the "appear to be linked" clause is the structuring hook again (module 8's adversary; the AMLR's linked transactions); a screening model must aggregate across transfers before deciding whether EUR 1 000 was crossed.
3. **Why does crypto get no threshold?** — Obvious attempt: same rule, smaller amounts. Why it fails: Rec 30: crypto transfers run "at a larger scale and higher speed", with "structuring a large transaction into smaller amounts, using multiple seemingly unrelated DLT addresses, including one-time use DLT addresses, and using automated processes", and volatility "makes the calculation of linked transactions more uncertain"; so "the same requirements regardless of their amount". Art 14(1)–(2): the originator's name, DLT address (and account number where used) or account number, address or document number or customer id or date and place of birth, LEI; the beneficiary's name, DLT address and/or account, LEI. Answer: the full set, every time, domestic or not. Because: the linked-transaction defence fails when addresses are free and prices move. Map: the adversary's cheapest edit (module 8) is a new address; the regulator removed the threshold the edit would exploit.
4. **Does the information go on the chain?** — Obvious attempt: it must be in the transaction. Why it fails: Art 14(4): submitted "in advance of, or simultaneously or concurrently with, the transfer", securely and GDPR-compliant — it "does not need to be attached to or embedded in" the on-chain transfer (Rec 43, 50 technology neutrality). Answer: an off-chain message between CASPs. Map: the screening pipeline consumes two streams — the ledger and the messaging channel — and must join them by UTI or transaction hash.
5. **A wallet nobody custodies** — Obvious attempt: out of scope. Why it fails: Art 2(4) excludes only person-to-person transfers with no CASP; when a CASP is on one end, Art 14(5): obtain and hold the originator and beneficiary information and make the transfer "individually identified"; and "in the case of a transfer of an amount exceeding EUR 1 000 to a self-hosted address, the crypto-asset service provider of the originator shall take adequate measures to assess whether that address is owned or controlled by the originator"; Art 16(2) mirrors it on receipt; Rec 39: no verification of the other user is required in principle; Art 38 adds Directive Art 19a (identify, additional origin/destination information, enhanced monitoring) and the Art 18(6) attention to mixers, tumblers, privacy wallets, with DLT analytics named (Rec 17). Answer: a self-hosted leg is in scope through the CASP, with an ownership assessment above EUR 1 000. Map: ownership assessment is a graph problem — module 6's components and the exposure score run on the ledger, now with a legal trigger.
6. **What must the receiving institution check?** — Obvious attempt: that the message arrived. Why it fails: Art 7(1)/16(1): "effective procedures … to detect whether the fields relating to the information … have been filled in using characters or inputs admissible in accordance with the conventions of the messaging or payment and settlement system" — a data-quality validation written into law; Art 7(2)–(3)/16: detect missing or incomplete information, including by ex-post monitoring; verify the payee before crediting above EUR 1 000 (or linked). Answer: a field validator, a completeness detector and a verification step, in that order. Map: module 1's blocking-rule layer gains a statutory rule set; "admissible characters" is a parser, not a model.
7. **What may it do when something is missing?** — Obvious attempt: reject. Why it fails: Art 8(1)/17(1): "effective risk-based procedures" to decide whether to execute, reject, return or suspend and whether to request the information before or after crediting; Art 8(2)/17(2): a PSP or CASP that "repeatedly fails" gets warnings and deadlines, then rejection, restriction or termination, and a report to the authority; Art 9/18: missing information is a factor in the suspicion assessment and in FIU reporting. Answer: a decision ladder per counterparty, with a repeat-offender counter. Because: the law treats the sending institution's behaviour as the risk, not only the transfer's. Map: a counterparty-institution risk score (module 9's decision table with an escalation state); missing-information rate per counterparty as a drift signal.
8. **Sanctions screening** — Art 23 (with Rec 18): internal policies, procedures and controls to implement Union and national restrictive measures when performing transfers — screening names and addresses against designation lists; EBA guidelines by 30 December 2024. Map: the name-matching layer in front of the model; a false positive here is a frozen payment, so precision costs are module 9's friction costs.
9. **Records and clocks** — Art 26: five years, deletable after, one further five on national law; Art 24: answer authorities "fully and without delay"; Art 40: applies from 30 December 2024; reviews 1 July 2026 (self-hosted) and 30 June 2027 (general).

### Pitfalls and terms to define
- Two vocabularies: payer/payee for funds, originator/beneficiary for crypto — the Regulation uses both; the lesson picks one per chapter and says so once.
- Art 2(5) lets a Member State exempt domestic payments up to EUR 1 000 for goods and services — a national option, not the rule.
- The EUR 1 000 figure appears three times with three meanings: the Art 5/6 data-set split for funds; the Art 14(5)/16(2) self-hosted ownership assessment; and the AMLR Art 19(2)–(3) CDD trigger for transfers and crypto — never merge them into "the EUR 1 000 threshold".
- Crypto-asset and CASP are defined by reference to MiCA (Regulation 2023/1114), which is not in the dossier; the lesson may name it only as the source of the definitions.
- "DLT analytics" and "know your transaction" appear in Rec 17 and Art 37(3)(b) as expectations and a review item, not as a defined duty; do not present a KYT model as required by the text.
- No EBA guidelines (Art 36, Art 23) are in the dossier; the lesson may only say they are due or issued, not what they say.
- Dates are the OJ's; the human checks for the Art 37 reports at write time.

A noisy channel is a conditional distribution p(y|x), and its capacity is
the largest mutual information any input distribution can push through it:
C = max over p(x) of I(X;Y). This lesson defines the discrete memoryless
channel as a transition table, shows that the information crossing it
depends on the input distribution as well as on the noise, defines capacity
as the maximum of $I(X;Y)$ over inputs, and computes it in full for the two
canonical binary channels: $C = 1 - H(p)$ for the symmetric channel and
$C = 1 - e$ for the erasure channel.

## A channel is a transition table

A bit is sent down a wire. Noise flips it with probability $0.1$ and leaves
it intact with probability $0.9$, each transmission independently of every
other. The complete physics of the wire, as far as information is
concerned, fits in a $2 \times 2$ table of conditional probabilities:

$$
p(y \mid x):\qquad
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & y = 0 & y = 1 \\ \hline
x = 0 & 0.9 & 0.1 \\
x = 1 & 0.1 & 0.9
\end{array}
$$

Each row is a conditional distribution: fix the input $x$, and the row
gives the probabilities of every possible output. Nothing else about the
medium enters the theory: copper, fiber, or radio with the same table are
the same channel.

<Definition title="discrete memoryless channel">
A **discrete memoryless channel** consists of a finite input alphabet
$\mathcal{X}$, a finite output alphabet $\mathcal{Y}$, and for each input
$x \in \mathcal{X}$ a conditional distribution $p(y \mid x)$ over
$\mathcal{Y}$. *Memoryless* means uses are independent: over $n$ uses,
$p(y_1 \ldots y_n \mid x_1 \ldots x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i \mid x_i)$,
whatever the inputs.
</Definition>

<Definition title="binary symmetric channel">
The **binary symmetric channel** $\mathrm{BSC}(p)$ has
$\mathcal{X} = \mathcal{Y} = \{0, 1\}$ and flips its input with the
*crossover probability* $p$:
$p(1 \mid 0) = p(0 \mid 1) = p$ and
$p(0 \mid 0) = p(1 \mid 1) = 1 - p$.
</Definition>

The general channel is the same picture with more rows and columns: one
row per input symbol, one conditional distribution per row. The rows need
not be permutations of each other, and $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$
need not match; the erasure channel below has three outputs for two
inputs.

<KeyFact>
A discrete memoryless channel is fully specified by its transition table
$p(y \mid x)$: one conditional distribution per input symbol, independent
across uses.
</KeyFact>

## Information through the channel depends on the input

The receiver sees $Y$ and wants $X$. The quantity that measures what the
output tells about the input is the mutual information of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>,
$$
I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X),
$$
and both terms have channel readings. $H(Y \mid X)$ is the **noise**: the
uncertainty the channel adds to a known input, computed row by row from
the transition table. $H(Y)$ is the total variety of the output. What gets
through is the difference.

One term is missing from the table: $I(X;Y)$ is a functional of the
*joint* distribution $p(x)\,p(y \mid x)$, and the channel supplies only
the second factor. The input distribution $p(x)$ is chosen by the sender.
Different choices push different amounts of information through the same
wire.

### Two inputs on the same wire: 0.531 against 0.211 bits

<Example title="uniform and biased inputs on BSC(0.1)">
Fix the channel $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$ and write $\pi = P(X = 1)$.

**Noise term, any input.** Given $X = 0$ the output is distributed
$(0.9, 0.1)$; given $X = 1$, $(0.1, 0.9)$. Both rows have the binary
entropy $H(0.1) \approx 0.469$ bits
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>), so the weighted average is
$H(Y \mid X) = 0.469$ bits regardless of $\pi$.

**Uniform input, $\pi = 0.5$.** Then
$P(Y = 1) = 0.5 \cdot 0.9 + 0.5 \cdot 0.1 = 0.5$: the output is uniform,
$H(Y) = 1$ bit, and
$$
I(X;Y) = 1 - 0.469 = 0.531 \text{ bits per use}.
$$

**Biased input, $\pi = 0.9$.** Then
$P(Y = 1) = 0.9 \cdot 0.9 + 0.1 \cdot 0.1 = 0.82$, so
$H(Y) = H(0.82) \approx 0.680$ bits and
$$
I(X;Y) \approx 0.680 - 0.469 = 0.211 \text{ bits per use}.
$$

Same channel, same noise term, less than half the information: a sender
who almost always transmits $1$ gives the receiver little uncertainty
worth removing.
</Example>

The uniform case has been computed before: the joint table of
$\mathrm{BSC}(p)$ under a uniform input is exactly the dependence-dial
family of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> with agreement probability
$q = 1 - p$, and its mutual information $1 - H(q) = 1 - H(p)$ matches the
$0.531$ bits computed there at $q = 0.9$.

<KeyFact>
$I(X;Y)$ through a channel depends on the channel and on the input
distribution: the noise $H(Y \mid X)$ is set by the transition table, but
how much information crosses is partly the sender's choice.
</KeyFact>

## Capacity: the maximum over input distributions

The curve above has a well-defined peak, and the peak deserves the name.

<Definition title="channel capacity">
The **capacity** of a discrete memoryless channel $p(y \mid x)$ is
$$
C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y),
$$
the maximum over all input distributions on $\mathcal{X}$, measured in
bits per channel use.
</Definition>

The maximum exists: $I(X;Y)$ is continuous in $p(x)$, and the input
distributions form a closed and bounded set. Two bounds pin down the
possible values.

<Theorem title="range of capacity">
For every discrete memoryless channel,
$$
0 \le C \le \min\bigl(\log_2 |\mathcal{X}|,\ \log_2 |\mathcal{Y}|\bigr).
$$

<Proof>
Nonnegativity: $I(X;Y) \ge 0$ for every input distribution
(<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, nonnegativity theorem), so the
maximum is $\ge 0$. Upper bound: for every input,
$I(X;Y) \le \min(H(X), H(Y))$ (the upper-bound theorem of the same
lesson), and entropy is at most the log of the alphabet size
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>, range theorem):
$H(X) \le \log_2 |\mathcal{X}|$ and $H(Y) \le \log_2 |\mathcal{Y}|$.
Every value entering the maximization obeys the bound, hence so does the
maximum. This is [Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
§7.3.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

For a binary-input channel, $C \le 1$ bit per use: no input distribution
carries more than one bit into a two-symbol alphabet.

### A channel where the maximum is visible: the noisy typewriter

<Example title="four-key noisy typewriter">
Inputs and outputs are $\{A, B, C, D\}$, arranged in a cycle. Each key
prints itself with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and the next letter with
probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ ($D$ wraps to $A$).

**Noise term.** Every row of the table is a fifty-fifty split over two
outputs: $H(Y \mid X) = 1$ bit for any input distribution.

**Output term.** $H(Y) \le \log_2 4 = 2$ bits, attained when $Y$ is
uniform; a uniform input achieves that. So
$$
C = 2 - 1 = 1 \text{ bit per use}.
$$

**The maximizer read as a strategy.** A uniform input over $\{A, C\}$
alone also attains the maximum: $A$ produces an output in $\{A, B\}$, $C$
an output in $\{C, D\}$, the two output sets are disjoint, and the
receiver recovers the input exactly. One error-free bit per use, matching
$C$. Choosing an input distribution includes choosing which symbols to
use at all.
</Example>

The typewriter is the exceptional case where capacity is reached with
zero error by symbol-by-symbol signaling. On the BSC no such disjoint
output sets exist; what the number $C$ promises there is the subject of
the <Ref lesson="noisy-channel-coding">next lesson</Ref>, and this lesson
only computes it.

<Remark title="Shannon's formulation">
[Shannon's 1948 paper](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf)
(Part II, §12) defines the rate of transmission as
$R = H(x) - H_y(x)$: source entropy minus the *equivocation*, his name
for $H(X \mid Y)$, the receiver's leftover uncertainty about what was
sent. By the three-forms theorem of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, $H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = I(X;Y)$, so his
$C = \max R$ and the modern $C = \max I$ are the same number.
</Remark>

<Aside>
Computing $C$ in general. For a fixed channel, $I(X;Y)$ is a concave
function of the input distribution, so the maximization is a convex
program in $|\mathcal{X}| - 1$ variables with no spurious local maxima;
the [Blahut–Arimoto algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blahut%E2%80%93Arimoto_algorithm)
(1972) solves it by alternating maximization and is the standard tool
when no symmetry helps. When symmetry does help, it helps completely:
for a *symmetric* channel, where every row of the transition table is a
permutation of every other row and likewise every column, the uniform
input is optimal and
$C = \log_2 |\mathcal{Y}| - H(\text{row})$, the entropy of any single
row (Cover & Thomas §7.2). The BSC and the noisy typewriter are both
instances: $1 - H(p)$ and $2 - 1$.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Capacity is not a property of the noise alone: $C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y)$
optimizes over the input distribution, which the sender designs. The
channel fixes the constraint; the sender picks the point on it.
</KeyFact>

<Theorem title="capacity of the BSC">
The binary symmetric channel with crossover probability $p$ has
$$
C = 1 - H(p) \text{ bits per use},
$$
attained by the uniform input, where
$H(p) = -p \log_2 p - (1-p) \log_2(1-p)$ is the binary entropy function.

<Proof>
For any input distribution, each row of the transition matrix is a
permutation of $(1-p,\ p)$, so every conditional output entropy is
$H(p)$ and $H(Y \mid X) = H(p)$: the noise term does not depend on the
input. Hence $I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(p) \le 1 - H(p)$, since $Y$ is binary
and $H(Y) \le 1$. Equality requires $Y$ uniform; with $\pi = P(X = 1)$,
$$
P(Y = 1) = \pi(1 - p) + (1 - \pi)p = p + \pi(1 - 2p),
$$
which equals $\tfrac{1}{2}$ at $\pi = \tfrac{1}{2}$ (for $p \ne
\tfrac{1}{2}$, only there). The bound is attained, so
$C = 1 - H(p)$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### Three points on the curve: p = 0, 0.1, 0.5

- $p = 0$: $C = 1 - H(0) = 1$ bit. A noiseless binary channel carries
  exactly one bit per use.
- $p = 0.1$: $C = 1 - 0.469 = 0.531$ bits. Ten percent noise costs
  nearly half the channel.
- $p = 0.5$: $C = 1 - H(0.5) = 1 - 1 = 0$. Then
  $P(Y = 1) = 0.5 + \pi \cdot 0 = 0.5$ for every $\pi$: the output is a
  fair coin regardless of the input, $Y$ is independent of $X$, and no
  input distribution shares a single bit with the output.

<Remark title="a 90% flipper is a good channel">
$C = 1 - H(p)$ is symmetric under $p \leftrightarrow 1 - p$, since
$H(p) = H(1-p)$: a channel that flips with probability $0.9$ has the
same $0.531$-bit capacity as one that flips with probability $0.1$. The
receiver inverts every received bit and faces a $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$. At
$p = 1$ the channel is a deterministic inverter and $C = 1$: perfectly
wrong is perfectly informative.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
A noisy channel and a useless channel are different things, and the
boundary is $C = 0$: the BSC has positive capacity at every
$p \ne \tfrac{1}{2}$, and exactly at $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ the arriving
bits are independent of the sent ones — noise maximal not when errors
are certain, but when they are as likely as not.
</KeyFact>

## The binary erasure channel: C = 1 − e

The second canonical channel loses symbols instead of flipping them: with
probability $e$ the receiver gets an erasure mark $?$, and with
probability $1 - e$ the input arrives exactly. Unlike a flipped bit, an
erased bit is a failure the receiver can see.

### The receiver's ledger at e = 0.2

Take $e = 0.2$ and a uniform input, and count leftover uncertainty
through $I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$, the decomposition from the input
side. Before transmission, $H(X) = 1$ bit. After: if the output is $0$ or
$1$ (probability $0.8$), it equals the input and zero uncertainty
remains; if it is $?$ (probability $0.2$), the erasure says nothing about
which bit was sent, and the full $1$ bit remains. So
$$
H(X \mid Y) = 0.8 \cdot 0 + 0.2 \cdot 1 = 0.2, \qquad
I(X;Y) = 1 - 0.2 = 0.8 \text{ bits per use}.
$$
The channel delivers exactly the fraction of symbols that survive.

<Theorem title="capacity of the BEC">
The binary erasure channel with erasure probability $e$ has
$$
C = 1 - e \text{ bits per use},
$$
attained by the uniform input.

<Proof>
Let $\pi = P(X = 1)$, so $H(X) = H(\pi)$. Condition on the output. If
$Y \ne \ ?$, then $X = Y$ exactly and the conditional entropy is $0$. If
$Y = \ ?$, an event of probability $e$ for either input, the posterior is
$P(X = 1 \mid Y = ?) = \pi e / e = \pi$: the erasure leaves the prior
untouched, with entropy $H(\pi)$. Averaging,
$H(X \mid Y) = e\,H(\pi)$, hence
$$
I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = (1 - e)\,H(\pi) \le 1 - e,
$$
with equality iff $\pi = \tfrac{1}{2}$. So $C = 1 - e$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The derivation ran through $H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$ rather than
$H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$ because conditioning on the output is trivial here:
either the input is known exactly or nothing was learned. Exercise 7
reruns it from the output side and lands on the same $(1-e)H(\pi)$.

### Flipping against erasing: the same 0.2, nowhere near the same cost

Set the two noise parameters equal, $p = e = 0.2$. The erasure channel
keeps $C = 0.8$ bits per use. The symmetric channel keeps
$C = 1 - H(0.2) = 1 - 0.722 = 0.278$ bits per use: not $1 - 0.2$,
because a flip costs $H(0.2) = 0.722$ bits of output entropy, not $0.2$.

The gap has one cause. The erasure channel tells the receiver *which*
symbols failed; the twenty percent that arrive as $?$ are known losses,
and the rest are certainties. The symmetric channel corrupts silently:
every received bit, correct or not, looks the same, and the doubt spread
over all of them is what $H(0.2)$ prices.

<KeyFact>
At the same noise level, erasure is far cheaper than flipping:
$C_{\mathrm{BEC}} = 1 - e$ against $C_{\mathrm{BSC}} = 1 - H(p)$, and at
$0.2$ that is $0.8$ against $0.28$ bits per use. A channel that flags
its own failures loses only the failed symbols.
</KeyFact>

## The second of Shannon's two limits

Both canonical channels happened to be maximized by the uniform input,
because both are symmetric. That is a property of the examples, not of
the definition: for an asymmetric channel the optimal input is generally
non-uniform (Exercise 6 shows a channel whose maximizer puts probability
$0.4$ on one symbol), and in general $C$ is found numerically as the
concave maximization in the Aside above. The lesson on
<Ref lesson="neural-coding"/> applies exactly this quantity to a
biological channel, as the denominator of coding efficiency.

Capacity closes a symmetry that the course opened with entropy. Both
limits are extreme values of the same functional, mutual information:

side literally as a minimization of $I(X;\hat X)$ over representations.
On the transmission side, this lesson produced the number
$C = \max I(X;Y)$ — and so far only the number. That every rate below
$C$ is actually achievable with vanishing error, and every rate above it
is not, is the noisy-channel coding theorem, the
<Ref lesson="noisy-channel-coding">next lesson</Ref>; nothing in this
one assumes it. For continuous-valued channels the same maximization
gives the Gaussian-channel formula
$C = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(1 + P/N)$, stated and used, not derived, in
<Ref lesson="neural-coding"/>.

<KeyFact>
Entropy and capacity are the two extreme points of mutual information:
$H(X)$ is the floor reached by minimizing over representations, $C$ the
ceiling reached by maximizing over channel inputs. Units for both sides
of this lesson: bits per channel use, under $\log_2$.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Compute the capacity of $\mathrm{BSC}(0.2)$. Show the noise term, the
output ceiling, and the subtraction, as in the derivation frames. Use
$\log_2 5 \approx 2.3219$.

<Solution>
**Noise term.** Each row of the transition matrix is a permutation of
$(0.8, 0.2)$, so for any input
$H(Y \mid X) = H(0.2) = -0.2 \log_2 0.2 - 0.8 \log_2 0.8$. With
$\log_2 0.2 = -\log_2 5 \approx -2.3219$ and
$\log_2 0.8 = \log_2 4 - \log_2 5 \approx -0.3219$:
$$
H(0.2) \approx 0.2 \cdot 2.3219 + 0.8 \cdot 0.3219
= 0.4644 + 0.2575 = 0.7219 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Output ceiling.** $Y$ is binary, so $H(Y) \le 1$ bit, with equality at
the uniform input ($P(Y=1) = 0.2 + 0.5 \cdot 0.6 = 0.5$).

**Subtract.** $C = 1 - H(0.2) \approx 1 - 0.7219 = 0.278$ bits per use.
A twenty-percent flip rate destroys nearly three quarters of the
channel.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Compute the capacity of $\mathrm{BSC}(0.9)$ and of $\mathrm{BSC}(1)$.
For each, state the decoding move that makes the capacity usable.

<Solution>
$C = 1 - H(0.9) = 1 - H(0.1) \approx 1 - 0.469 = 0.531$ bits per use,
by the symmetry $H(p) = H(1-p)$: the same capacity as
$\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$. The receiver inverts every received bit; the
composite channel from input to inverted output flips with probability
$0.1$.

$\mathrm{BSC}(1)$: $C = 1 - H(1) = 1 - 0 = 1$ bit per use. The channel
inverts deterministically; inverting again recovers the input exactly.
A channel is useless not when it is usually wrong but when it is
uninformative: capacity falls to zero only at $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Compute the capacity of $\mathrm{BEC}(0.4)$ and of $\mathrm{BSC}(0.4)$,
and take the ratio. Use $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ and
$\log_2 5 \approx 2.3219$.

<Solution>
Erasure channel: $C = 1 - e = 0.6$ bits per use.

Symmetric channel: $H(0.4) = -0.4 \log_2 0.4 - 0.6 \log_2 0.6$. With
$\log_2 0.4 = 1 - \log_2 5 \approx -1.3219$ and
$\log_2 0.6 = \log_2 3 - \log_2 5 \approx -0.7370$:
$$
H(0.4) \approx 0.4 \cdot 1.3219 + 0.6 \cdot 0.7370
= 0.5288 + 0.4422 = 0.9710 \text{ bits},
$$
so $C = 1 - 0.9710 = 0.029$ bits per use.

Ratio: $0.6 / 0.029 \approx 21$. At forty percent noise the erasure
channel still delivers most of a bit per use, while the symmetric
channel is within three hundredths of a bit of useless: silent
corruption at $p = 0.4$ leaves the output nearly independent of the
input.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A six-key noisy typewriter has inputs and outputs $\{A, \ldots, F\}$ in
a cycle; each key prints itself or the next letter with probability
$\tfrac{1}{2}$ each. Compute its capacity and give an explicit zero-error
signaling scheme that attains it.

<Solution>
Every row of the transition table is a fifty-fifty split over two
outputs, so $H(Y \mid X) = 1$ bit for any input. The output alphabet has
six symbols, so $H(Y) \le \log_2 6$, attained at the uniform input
(each output is reached from two inputs with total probability
$\tfrac{1}{6}$). Hence
$$
C = \log_2 6 - 1 = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585 \text{ bits per use}.
$$

Zero-error scheme: use only $\{A, C, E\}$, uniformly. Their output sets
are $\{A, B\}$, $\{C, D\}$, $\{E, F\}$: pairwise disjoint, so the
receiver identifies the input exactly and one of three symbols arrives
error-free, worth $\log_2 3$ bits per use, which equals $C$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
An input with $P(X = 1) = 0.2$ feeds $\mathrm{BSC}(0.25)$. Build the
joint table $p(x, y)$, compute both marginals, then compute $I(X;Y)$ as
$H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$ using the recipes of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>. Compare with the
capacity of the channel. Use $H(0.25) \approx 0.8113$ and
$H(0.35) \approx 0.9341$.

<Solution>
**Joint table.** $p(x, y) = p(x)\,p(y \mid x)$:
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & y = 0 & y = 1 \\ \hline
x = 0 & 0.8 \cdot 0.75 = 0.60 & 0.8 \cdot 0.25 = 0.20 \\
x = 1 & 0.2 \cdot 0.25 = 0.05 & 0.2 \cdot 0.75 = 0.15
\end{array}
$$

**Marginals.** Rows: $P(X = 0) = 0.8$, $P(X = 1) = 0.2$ (as designed).
Columns: $P(Y = 0) = 0.60 + 0.05 = 0.65$, $P(Y = 1) = 0.20 + 0.15
= 0.35$.

**Conditional entropy.** Each row of $p(y \mid x)$ is a permutation of
$(0.75, 0.25)$, so
$H(Y \mid X) = 0.8 \cdot H(0.25) + 0.2 \cdot H(0.25) = H(0.25)
\approx 0.8113$ bits.

**Mutual information.**
$H(Y) = H(0.35) \approx 0.9341$ bits, so
$$
I(X;Y) \approx 0.9341 - 0.8113 = 0.123 \text{ bits per use}.
$$

**Against capacity.** $C = 1 - H(0.25) \approx 1 - 0.8113 = 0.189$ bits
per use. The biased input realizes $0.123$ of the possible $0.189$
bits; the remaining $0.066$ is lost to the sender's choice, not to the
channel.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The *Z-channel* transmits $0$ faithfully but flips $1$ to $0$ with
probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$: $p(0 \mid 0) = 1$,
$p(0 \mid 1) = p(1 \mid 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Write $\pi = P(X = 1)$.
(a) Compute $I(X;Y)$ at the uniform input. (b) Show
$I(\pi) = H(\pi/2) - \pi$ and find the maximizing $\pi$. (c) Compute
$C$ and compare with (a). Use $H(0.25) \approx 0.8113$ and
$H(0.2) \approx 0.7219$.

<Solution>
**(a)** At $\pi = 0.5$: $P(Y = 1) = 0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.25$, so
$H(Y) = H(0.25) \approx 0.8113$. The rows have entropies $H(0) = 0$
(input $0$, deterministic) and $H(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 1$ (input $1$), so
$H(Y \mid X) = 0.5 \cdot 0 + 0.5 \cdot 1 = 0.5$. Hence
$I \approx 0.8113 - 0.5 = 0.311$ bits.

**(b)** In general $P(Y = 1) = \pi/2$, so $H(Y) = H(\pi/2)$, and
$H(Y \mid X) = (1 - \pi) \cdot 0 + \pi \cdot 1 = \pi$. Thus
$I(\pi) = H(\pi/2) - \pi$. Setting the derivative to zero, with
$H'(q) = \log_2 \tfrac{1-q}{q}$ and the chain rule factor
$\tfrac{1}{2}$:
$$
\tfrac{1}{2} \log_2 \frac{1 - \pi/2}{\pi/2} - 1 = 0
\;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{2 - \pi}{\pi} = 4
\;\Longrightarrow\; \pi = 0.4.
$$

**(c)** $C = I(0.4) = H(0.2) - 0.4 \approx 0.7219 - 0.4 = 0.322$ bits
per use, against $0.311$ at the uniform input. The asymmetric noise
wants an asymmetric input: the symbol $1$ is unreliable, so the
maximizer uses it less. Uniform inputs are optimal for symmetric
channels, not in general.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Rerun the BEC capacity derivation from the output side: for
$\mathrm{BEC}(e)$ with $\pi = P(X = 1)$, compute $H(Y \mid X)$ and
$H(Y)$ directly, and verify $I(X;Y) = (1 - e)\,H(\pi)$.

<Solution>
**Noise term.** Given $X = x$, the output is $x$ with probability
$1 - e$ and $?$ with probability $e$: a two-outcome distribution with
entropy $H(e)$, the same for both inputs. So $H(Y \mid X) = H(e)$.

**Output term.** $Y$ takes the values $0$, $?$, $1$ with probabilities
$(1-e)(1-\pi)$, $e$, $(1-e)\pi$. Expanding the entropy sum and
splitting each $\log$ of a product:
$$
H(Y) = -(1-e)\bigl[(1-\pi)\log_2(1-\pi) + \pi \log_2 \pi\bigr]
- (1-e)\log_2(1-e) - e \log_2 e,
$$
which is $H(Y) = (1-e)\,H(\pi) + H(e)$.

**Subtract.**
$$
I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) = (1-e)\,H(\pi) + H(e) - H(e)
= (1-e)\,H(\pi),
$$
matching the input-side derivation, and $\le 1 - e$ with equality at
$\pi = \tfrac{1}{2}$. The erasure entropy $H(e)$ appears in both terms
and cancels: the visible fact *that* an erasure happened carries no
information about *what* was sent. Picking the decomposition whose
conditional term is easy — here either works; on the BSC only the
output side is clean — is most of the labor.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

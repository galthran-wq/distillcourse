A noisy channel is a conditional distribution p(y|x), and its capacity is
the largest mutual information any input distribution can push through it:
C = max over p(x) of I(X;Y). This lesson defines the discrete memoryless
channel as a transition table, shows that what crosses the table is decided
by the sender's input distribution as well as by the noise, and computes the
maximum in full for the three channels the rest of the module uses.

This is the first move of the course's sixth step, the second of Shannon's
two limits. The argument so far has priced uncertainty and then priced shared
uncertainty: <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> defined
$I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$ as the bits one variable removes from another's
uncertainty, and <Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/> showed that no
function of the data can raise that number. Both took the joint distribution
as given. A channel supplies only half of one: it fixes $p(y \mid x)$ and
leaves $p(x)$ to whoever is transmitting, so the channel's own figure of merit
has to be the largest $I(X;Y)$ any sender can arrange. This lesson computes
that maximum. It does not establish what the number is for. Capacity is
defined here as a maximisation and nothing else; the claim that it is exactly
the rate at which reliable communication becomes possible belongs to
<Ref lesson="noisy-channel-coding">the next lesson</Ref> and is not assumed
anywhere in this one.

The route: the transition table and what memorylessness costs; the input
dependence of $I(X;Y)$; capacity defined, bounded, and read off a demo; the
two binary channels computed in full; Shannon's own worked example, in which
a one percent error rate costs eight percent of the channel; and a channel
whose best input is not the uniform one.

## A channel is a transition table

The object to be measured is a physical link, and the first task is to throw
away everything about it that the theory does not use. A bit is sent down a
wire. Noise flips it with probability $0.1$ and leaves it intact with
probability $0.9$, each transmission independently of every other. The
complete physics of the wire, as far as information is concerned, fits in a
$2 \times 2$ table of conditional probabilities:

$$
p(y \mid x):\qquad
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & y = 0 & y = 1 \\ \hline
x = 0 & 0.9 & 0.1 \\
x = 1 & 0.1 & 0.9
\end{array}
$$

Each row is a conditional distribution: fix the input $x$, and the row gives
the probabilities of every possible output. Copper, fibre, or radio with the
same table are the same channel, and every result below is a statement about
the table.

<Definition title="discrete memoryless channel">
A **discrete memoryless channel** consists of a finite input alphabet
$\mathcal{X}$, a finite output alphabet $\mathcal{Y}$, and for each input
$x \in \mathcal{X}$ a conditional distribution $p(y \mid x)$ over
$\mathcal{Y}$. *Memoryless* means uses are independent: over $n$ uses,
$p(y_1 \ldots y_n \mid x_1 \ldots x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i \mid x_i)$,
whatever the inputs.
</Definition>

<Definition title="binary symmetric channel">
The **binary symmetric channel** $\mathrm{BSC}(p)$ has
$\mathcal{X} = \mathcal{Y} = \{0, 1\}$ and flips its input with the
*crossover probability* $p$:
$p(1 \mid 0) = p(0 \mid 1) = p$ and
$p(0 \mid 0) = p(1 \mid 1) = 1 - p$.
</Definition>

### Four channels against the definition

A definition is learned as a boundary. Four cases fix where this one runs.

- **The canonical case.** $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$, the table above: two inputs,
  two outputs, both rows a permutation of $(0.9, 0.1)$.
- **A different surface, same structure.** An *erasure* channel, worked in
  full below, replaces the input with a third output symbol $?$ instead of
  corrupting it. Its output alphabet is larger than its input alphabet, which
  the definition permits: nothing requires $\mathcal{X}$ and $\mathcal{Y}$ to
  match, only that each row be a distribution over $\mathcal{Y}$.
- **The degenerate case.** The useless channel $p(y \mid x) = p(y)$, whose
  rows are all identical. It is a legitimate discrete memoryless channel; its
  output tells nothing about its input, and the arithmetic below returns zero
  for it rather than breaking.
- **The near miss.** A wire whose errors arrive in bursts, so that a flip at
  use $i$ makes a flip at use $i+1$ more likely. Each single use still has a
  transition table, and that table can be estimated. What fails is exactly one
  clause: $p(y_1 \ldots y_n \mid x_1 \ldots x_n)$ is no longer the product of
  the rows, so the per-use table no longer determines the behaviour of the
  link over $n$ uses.

Every real link fails the memoryless clause somewhere: fading, thermal drift,
and interference all correlate errors in time. The standard repair is to
treat a block of $k$ uses as one use of an *extended channel* with
$|\mathcal{X}|^k$ inputs, which is memoryless if the correlations do not
reach past $k$, and to apply the same theory to that.

## What crosses depends on the input, not only on the noise

The transition table alone does not determine how much the receiver learns.
The receiver sees $Y$ and wants $X$, and the quantity that measures what the
output says about the input is mutual information
(<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>), the drop in uncertainty about one
variable caused by observing the other:

$$
I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X).
$$

Both terms have channel readings. $H(Y \mid X)$ is the **noise**: the
uncertainty a known input still leaves in the output, computed row by row
from the transition table. $H(Y)$ is the total variety of the output. What
crosses is the difference.

Only one of the two is fixed by the channel. $I(X;Y)$ is a functional of the
*joint* distribution $p(x)\,p(y \mid x)$, and the table supplies only the
second factor. The input distribution $p(x)$ is the sender's to choose, and
different choices push different amounts through the same wire.

<Example title="uniform and biased inputs on BSC(0.1)">
Fix the channel $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$ and write $\pi = P(X = 1)$ for the input
bias.

**Noise term, any input.** Given $X = 0$ the output is distributed
$(0.9, 0.1)$; given $X = 1$, $(0.1, 0.9)$. Both rows have the binary entropy
$H(0.1) \approx 0.469$ bits (<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>,
where $H(q) = -q\log_2 q - (1-q)\log_2(1-q)$ was computed and plotted), so
the weighted average is $H(Y \mid X) = 0.469$ bits regardless of $\pi$.

**Uniform input, $\pi = 0.5$.** Then
$P(Y = 1) = 0.5 \cdot 0.9 + 0.5 \cdot 0.1 = 0.5$: the output is uniform,
$H(Y) = 1$ bit, and
$$
I(X;Y) = 1 - 0.469 = 0.531 \text{ bits per use}.
$$

**Biased input, $\pi = 0.9$.** Then
$P(Y = 1) = 0.9 \cdot 0.9 + 0.1 \cdot 0.1 = 0.82$, so
$H(Y) = H(0.82) \approx 0.680$ bits and
$$
I(X;Y) \approx 0.680 - 0.469 = 0.211 \text{ bits per use}.
$$

Same channel, same noise term, less than half the information. A sender who
almost always transmits $1$ leaves the receiver little uncertainty worth
removing.
</Example>

The uniform case has appeared before. The joint table of $\mathrm{BSC}(p)$
under a uniform input is exactly the dependence-dial family of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, a pair of bits that agree with
probability $q$, at $q = 1 - p$; its mutual information $1 - H(q)$ matches the
$0.531$ bits computed there at $q = 0.9$.

## Capacity: the maximum over input distributions

The curve has a well-defined top, and the top is the only number on it that
belongs to the channel alone.

<Definition title="channel capacity">
The **capacity** of a discrete memoryless channel $p(y \mid x)$ is
$$
C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y),
$$
the maximum over all input distributions on $\mathcal{X}$, measured in bits
per channel use.
</Definition>

The maximum is attained rather than merely approached: $I(X;Y)$ is continuous
in $p(x)$, and the input distributions form a closed and bounded subset of
$\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{X}|}$. Two bounds pin the possible values.

<Theorem title="range of capacity">
For every discrete memoryless channel,
$$
0 \le C \le \min\bigl(\log_2 |\mathcal{X}|,\ \log_2 |\mathcal{Y}|\bigr).
$$

<Proof>
Nonnegativity: $I(X;Y) \ge 0$ for every input distribution
(<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, nonnegativity theorem), so the maximum of
a set of nonnegative numbers is nonnegative.

Upper bound: for every input, $I(X;Y) \le \min(H(X), H(Y))$ (the upper-bound
theorem of the same lesson, which follows from
$I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$ and $H(X \mid Y) \ge 0$), and an entropy is at
most the log of its alphabet size
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>, range theorem), so
$H(X) \le \log_2 |\mathcal{X}|$ and $H(Y) \le \log_2 |\mathcal{Y}|$. Every
value entering the maximisation obeys the bound, hence so does the largest of
them.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

For a binary-input channel this reads $C \le 1$ bit per use: no input
distribution carries more than one bit into a two-symbol alphabet, however
clean the wire. The bound by $\log_2|\mathcal{Y}|$ is the one that bites when
the output alphabet is the smaller of the two, as when a sixteen-level signal
is read by a comparator that reports only its sign.

<KeyFact>
Capacity is bits per **channel use**, not bits per second. A link that carries
$0.5$ bits per use and is clocked at $10^6$ uses per second carries
$5 \times 10^5$ bits per second; the same channel clocked slower carries
proportionally less, with the same capacity. Every number in this lesson is
per use, and the conversion needs a symbol rate the transition table does not
contain.
</KeyFact>

### The maximiser read as a strategy

A distribution over inputs that puts zero probability on some symbols is still
an input distribution. Choosing $p(x)$ therefore includes choosing which
symbols to use at all, and on some channels that choice is the whole story.

<Example title="four-key noisy typewriter">
Inputs and outputs are $\{A, B, C, D\}$ arranged in a cycle. Each key prints
itself with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and the next letter with probability
$\tfrac{1}{2}$, with $D$ wrapping to $A$.

**Noise term.** Every row of the table is a fifty-fifty split over two
outputs, so $H(Y \mid X) = 1$ bit for any input distribution.

**Output term.** $H(Y) \le \log_2 4 = 2$ bits, attained when $Y$ is uniform,
which a uniform input achieves: each output symbol is reached from exactly two
inputs, each contributing $\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot\tfrac{1}{2}$. So
$$
C = 2 - 1 = 1 \text{ bit per use}.
$$

**A second maximiser.** The uniform distribution over $\{A, C\}$ alone attains
the same value: $H(Y \mid X)$ is still $1$, and $Y$ is still uniform over four
symbols. Read as a strategy it is stronger than the number suggests. $A$
produces an output in $\{A, B\}$ and $C$ produces one in $\{C, D\}$; the two
output sets are disjoint, so the receiver recovers the input exactly, and the
scheme delivers one error-free bit per use with no coding of any kind.
</Example>

The maximum is therefore attained by more than one distribution, and one of
them happens to be error-free. That coincidence is fragile. Enlarge the same
construction to 27 keys, the letters $A$ through $Z$ plus a space arranged in
a cycle, each printing itself or its successor with probability
$\tfrac{1}{2}$: the noise term is still $1$ bit, the output term is still
maximised by the uniform input, and

$$
C = \log_2 27 - 1 = \log_2 13.5 \approx 3.755 \text{ bits per use}.
$$

The error-free strategy no longer reaches it. Two inputs are confusable
exactly when they are adjacent in the cycle, so a set of inputs with disjoint
output sets is a set of letters no two of which are neighbours, and an odd
cycle of length 27 admits at most 13 such letters. Signalling with them gives
$\log_2 13 \approx 3.700$ bits per use, error-free, and $0.055$ bits per use
short of $C$. Nothing said so far explains what that last sliver of capacity
is, or whether anything can collect it. That question stays open until
<Ref lesson="noisy-channel-coding">the next lesson</Ref>; the rest of this one
computes the number, not its meaning.

## The binary symmetric channel: C = 1 − H(p)

The worked example did $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$ at two input distributions. The
maximisation over all of them takes three lines: the noise term is the same
for every input, so only the output term is left to maximise.

<Theorem title="capacity of the BSC">
The binary symmetric channel with crossover probability $p$ has
$$
C = 1 - H(p) \text{ bits per use},
$$
attained by the uniform input, where
$H(p) = -p \log_2 p - (1-p) \log_2(1-p)$ is the binary entropy function.

<Proof>
The plan is direct: fix the noise term, cap the output term, subtract, then
check that the cap is reached.

**The noise term is constant.** Each row of the transition matrix is a
permutation of $(1-p,\ p)$, so every conditional output entropy equals $H(p)$
and their average is $H(Y \mid X) = H(p)$, whatever $p(x)$ is.

**The output term is capped.** $Y$ takes two values, so $H(Y) \le 1$ bit
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>, range theorem), and therefore
$I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(p) \le 1 - H(p)$ for every input.

**The cap is attained.** Equality needs $Y$ uniform. With $\pi = P(X = 1)$,
$$
P(Y = 1) = \pi(1 - p) + (1 - \pi)p = p + \pi(1 - 2p),
$$
which equals $\tfrac{1}{2}$ at $\pi = \tfrac{1}{2}$. For $p \ne \tfrac{1}{2}$
the coefficient $1 - 2p$ is nonzero, so $\pi = \tfrac{1}{2}$ is the only
solution; at $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ every $\pi$ gives $P(Y=1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and
the bound $1 - H(\tfrac12) = 0$ is attained everywhere. The upper bound is
achieved, so it is the maximum.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The equality condition has a geometric reading. The map
$\pi \mapsto P(Y = 1) = p + \pi(1 - 2p)$ sends the unit interval of input
biases onto the interval $[p, 1-p]$ of output biases: noise contracts the
sender's reach toward the middle by the factor $1 - 2p$. At $p = 0.1$ the
reachable outputs run from $0.1$ to $0.9$ and comfortably include the uniform
point. At $p = 0.5$ the image collapses to the single point $\tfrac{1}{2}$,
every input produces the same output distribution, and no choice the sender
makes is visible at the far end.

<Figure
  src="./figures/bsc-output-squeeze.svg"
  alt="Three copies of the input-bias interval mapping onto output-bias intervals for crossover 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5: the image shrinks from the interval 0.1 to 0.9, to 0.3 to 0.7, to the single point one half"
  caption="Noise contracts the reachable output distributions by 1 − 2p. At p = ½ the image is one point, which is why C = 0 there and only there."
/>

Three points fix the shape of $C = 1 - H(p)$.

- $p = 0$: $C = 1 - H(0) = 1$ bit. A noiseless binary channel carries exactly
  one bit per use, the alphabet bound.
- $p = 0.1$: $C = 1 - 0.469 = 0.531$ bits. Ten percent noise costs nearly half
  the channel.
- $p = 0.5$: $C = 1 - 1 = 0$. The output is a fair coin whatever is sent, $Y$
  is independent of $X$, and no input distribution shares a single bit with
  the output.

<Remark title="a 90% flipper is a good channel">
$C = 1 - H(p)$ is symmetric under $p \leftrightarrow 1-p$, because
$H(p) = H(1-p)$. A channel that flips with probability $0.9$ therefore has the
same $0.531$-bit capacity as one that flips with probability $0.1$: the
receiver inverts every bit it receives and is looking at a $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$.
At $p = 1$ the channel is a deterministic inverter and $C = 1$. Reliably wrong
is as good as reliably right, and the relabelling that converts one into the
other costs nothing.
</Remark>

<Exercise>
Compute the capacity of $\mathrm{BSC}(0.2)$ by the three moves of the proof:
the noise term, the output ceiling, and the subtraction. Write the noise term
down before opening the solution. Use $\log_2 5 \approx 2.3219$.

<Solution>
**Strategy.** Both rows are permutations of $(0.8, 0.2)$, so the first move is
to evaluate one binary entropy, and the other two moves need no arithmetic
particular to this channel.

**Noise term.** $H(Y \mid X) = H(0.2) = -0.2\log_2 0.2 - 0.8\log_2 0.8$. With
$\log_2 0.2 = -\log_2 5 \approx -2.3219$ and
$\log_2 0.8 = 2 - \log_2 5 \approx -0.3219$,
$$
H(0.2) \approx 0.2 \cdot 2.3219 + 0.8 \cdot 0.3219 = 0.4644 + 0.2575
= 0.7219 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Output ceiling.** $Y$ is binary, so $H(Y) \le 1$ bit, attained at the
uniform input, where $P(Y=1) = 0.2 + 0.5 \cdot 0.6 = 0.5$.

**Subtract.** $C = 1 - 0.7219 \approx 0.278$ bits per use. A twenty percent
flip rate destroys nearly three quarters of the channel, which is the fact the
next section explains.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyFact>
A noisy channel and a useless channel are different objects, and the boundary
between them is $C = 0$. The BSC has positive capacity at every
$p \ne \tfrac{1}{2}$, including $p = 0.999$; capacity vanishes only where the
output is statistically independent of the input. Noise is worst not when
errors are certain but when they are as likely as not.
</KeyFact>

## Equivocation: an error costs more than the bit it destroyed

The last computation returned $0.278$ bits from a channel that delivers 80% of
its bits intact, and the discrepancy is not an artefact of the example. It is
the point Shannon spends most of §12 of the 1948 paper establishing, against
the natural accounting that a link corrupting a fraction $p$ of its bits
delivers the remaining $1 - p$ of them.

That accounting is wrong, and its failure is visible at the extreme. Take a
channel so noisy that the output is independent of the input, sending $1000$
binary symbols per second. Half of the received symbols are correct, by
chance; the naive ledger credits the system with 500 bits per second. Shannon
disposes of it in one sentence: "equally good transmission would be obtained
by dispensing with the channel entirely and flipping a coin at the receiving
point." A number that a coin can match is not measuring transmission.

The correction is to subtract not the errors but the uncertainty they leave.
Shannon's name for $H(X \mid Y)$, the receiver's leftover uncertainty about
what was sent, is the **equivocation**, and his rate of transmission is
$R = H(x) - H_y(x)$ in the notation of the paper: source entropy minus
equivocation. That is $H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = I(X;Y)$, the same quantity this
lesson maximises, reached from the input side rather than the output side
(<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, three-forms theorem).

<Example title="Shannon's 1000 bits per second at 1% error">
A source emits $1000$ equiprobable binary symbols per second into a channel
that flips each one with probability $0.01$, independently. What rate is
actually transmitted?

**The naive figure.** One percent of the symbols are wrong, so 990 arrive
correct: 990 bits per second.

**The equivocation.** Receive a $0$. By symmetry of the channel and of the
source, the posterior probability that a $0$ was sent is $0.99$ and that a $1$
was sent is $0.01$, and the reversed figures hold for a received $1$. So the
leftover uncertainty per symbol is
$$
H(X \mid Y) = -[\,0.99 \log_2 0.99 + 0.01 \log_2 0.01\,] = H(0.01)
\approx 0.081 \text{ bits},
$$
or $81$ bits per second.

**The rate.** $R = 1000 - 81 = 919$ bits per second, not $990$. The one
percent of symbols that are wrong cost eight percent of the channel.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/equivocation-919.svg"
  alt="A bar of 1000 bits per second split into 919 bits per second of transmitted rate and 81 bits per second of equivocation, with the naive figure of 990 marked against it as wrong"
  caption="1000 bits per second at a 1% error rate: 81 bits per second of equivocation, not 10, and 919 transmitted."

The multiplier grows as the error rate falls. At $p = 0.01$ the equivocation
$H(p) \approx 0.081$ is about eight times $p$; at $p = 0.001$ it is
$0.0114$, about eleven times $p$. The reason is in the dominant term
$p \log_2 (1/p)$: the receiver's uncertainty is not about *how many* symbols
were corrupted, which it knows on average, but about *which* ones, and naming
a rare position costs $\log_2(1/p)$ bits per error.

Shannon's Theorem 10 makes that reading exact: a side channel of capacity
exactly $H(X \mid Y)$ suffices to let the receiver correct every error, and no
smaller one does, so the equivocation is literally the price of a list of the
corrupted positions. Halving an error rate therefore does not halve the loss:
from $p = 0.01$ to
$p = 0.005$ the equivocation falls only by a factor of $1.8$, to $0.045$ bits,
because each remaining error is rarer and so more expensive to name.

## The binary erasure channel: C = 1 − e

One channel escapes the previous section's verdict, and it is the one whose
failures announce themselves. Instead of flipping a symbol, it deletes it:
with probability $e$ the receiver gets an erasure mark $?$, and with
probability $1 - e$ the input arrives exactly. No input is ever received as
the other input. (Here $e$ is the erasure probability, not the base of the
natural logarithm; every logarithm in this lesson is base 2.)

<Figure
  src="./figures/bec-transition.svg"
  alt="The binary erasure channel drawn as inputs 0 and 1 mapping to outputs 0, question mark and 1, with straight arrows labelled 1 minus e and arrows into the question-mark node labelled e"
  caption="BEC(e): with probability e the symbol is replaced by a visible erasure mark, never by the wrong symbol."
/>

Count the leftover uncertainty at $e = 0.2$ with a uniform input, working from
the input side through $I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$. Before transmission,
$H(X) = 1$ bit. After it: if the output is $0$ or $1$, an event of probability
$0.8$, the output equals the input and nothing is left uncertain; if it is
$?$, an event of probability $0.2$, the erasure says nothing about which bit
was sent and the full bit remains. So
$$
H(X \mid Y) = 0.8 \cdot 0 + 0.2 \cdot 1 = 0.2, \qquad
I(X;Y) = 1 - 0.2 = 0.8 \text{ bits per use}.
$$
The channel delivers exactly the fraction of symbols that survive, which is
the answer the naive accounting of the previous section would have given for a
20% failure rate. Here it is correct.

<Theorem title="capacity of the BEC">
The binary erasure channel with erasure probability $e$ has
$$
C = 1 - e \text{ bits per use},
$$
attained by the uniform input.

<Proof>
Let $\pi = P(X = 1)$, so $H(X) = H(\pi)$, and condition on the output.

If $Y \ne\ ?$ then $X = Y$ exactly, and the conditional entropy is $0$.

If $Y =\ ?$, an event of probability $e$ for either input, Bayes gives
$P(X = 1 \mid Y = ?) = \pi e / e = \pi$: the erasure leaves the prior
untouched, and the conditional entropy is $H(\pi)$.

Averaging over the two cases, $H(X \mid Y) = e\,H(\pi)$, so
$$
I(X;Y) = H(\pi) - e\,H(\pi) = (1 - e)\,H(\pi) \le 1 - e,
$$
using $H(\pi) \le 1$, with equality exactly at $\pi = \tfrac{1}{2}$. The bound
is attained, so $C = 1 - e$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof ran from the input side because conditioning on the output is
trivial here: either the input is known exactly or nothing was learned. On the
BSC the reverse holds, and the output-side decomposition is the clean one.
Choosing the decomposition whose conditional term collapses is most of the
labour in any capacity computation, and Exercise 4 runs the erasure channel
the hard way to show what the choice saves.

### Matched noise, unmatched capacity

Set the two noise parameters equal at $p = e = 0.2$. The erasure channel keeps
$0.8$ bits per use; the symmetric channel keeps $0.278$. The same fraction of
symbols fails, and the cost differs by a factor of nearly three.

The gap has one cause. The erasure channel tells the receiver *which* symbols
failed, so its equivocation is only the content of the erased symbols
themselves; nothing has to be spent locating them. The symmetric channel
corrupts silently, every received bit looks the same whether or not it is
correct, and the doubt spread over all of them is what $H(p)$ prices. The
straight line and the curve in the figure are the same subtraction with and
without that piece of side information.

Which of the two models a given link obeys is therefore an engineering
decision with a factor-of-three consequence, and it is settled by asking
whether the hardware flags its own failures: a
checksummed frame that is discarded on failure is an erasure channel, and the
same frame delivered unflagged is a symmetric one.

## The Z channel: the best input is not uniform

Both channels so far were maximised by the uniform input, and from two
examples one would take the uniform input for a general rule. It is not one.
It is a consequence of a symmetry both examples happen to have, and the
smallest asymmetric channel breaks it.

<Definition title="Z channel">
The **Z channel** with error probability $f$ transmits $0$ faithfully and
corrupts $1$ into $0$ with probability $f$:
$p(0 \mid 0) = 1$, $p(0 \mid 1) = f$, $p(1 \mid 1) = 1 - f$. Its transition
diagram has three arrows rather than four, in the shape of the letter.
</Definition>

The two rows now have different entropies, $H(Y \mid X = 0) = 0$ and
$H(Y \mid X = 1) = H(f)$, so the noise term $H(Y \mid X) = \pi H(f)$ depends
on the input bias $\pi = P(X = 1)$. The sender is no longer choosing only how
much output variety to create; it is choosing how much noise to admit, by
deciding how often to use the unreliable symbol.

<Example title="Z channel at f = 0.15 (MacKay 9.11)">
Write $\pi = P(X = 1)$. Only the input $1$ can produce the output $1$, and it
does so with probability $1 - f$, so $P(Y = 1) = \pi(1 - f)$ and
$$
I(\pi) = H\bigl(\pi(1-f)\bigr) - \pi H(f).
$$

**Differentiate.** With $\tfrac{d}{dq}H(q) = \log_2\frac{1-q}{q}$ and the
chain-rule factor $1 - f$, setting $I'(\pi) = 0$ gives
$$
(1-f)\log_2 \frac{1 - \pi(1-f)}{\pi(1-f)} = H(f).
$$
Writing $z = \pi(1-f)$ for the output bias, this rearranges to
$\frac{1-z}{z} = 2^{H(f)/(1-f)}$, so
$$
z^{*} = \frac{1}{1 + 2^{H(f)/(1-f)}}, \qquad
\pi^{*} = \frac{z^{*}}{1-f}.
$$

**Evaluate at $f = 0.15$.** $H(0.15) = 0.610$ bits, so the exponent is
$0.610/0.85 = 0.717$ and $2^{0.717} = 1.644$. Then $z^* = 1/2.644 = 0.378$
and $\pi^* = 0.378/0.85 = 0.445$. The capacity is
$$
C = H(0.378) - 0.445 \cdot 0.610 = 0.957 - 0.271 = 0.685 \text{ bits per use},
$$
against $I(0.5) = H(0.425) - 0.5 \cdot 0.610 = 0.984 - 0.305 = 0.679$ bits at
the uniform input. The optimal sender transmits $1$ slightly less than half
the time, because that symbol is the only one that can be corrupted.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/z-channel.svg"
  alt="The Z channel transition diagram beside its mutual information plotted against the input bias for f = 0.15: an asymmetric hump peaking at 0.685 bits at bias 0.445, with the uniform input marked slightly lower at 0.679"
  caption="Z channel at f = 0.15: the curve is no longer symmetric about ½, and its peak sits at π = 0.445."
/>

Two readings of the number matter more than the number. First, asymmetric
noise wants an asymmetric input: the maximiser uses the unreliable symbol
less, and on channels with more skew the effect is larger. Second, the penalty
for ignoring this is small here, $0.006$ bits per use or under one percent,
which is why the uniform input survives as a default in practice even where it
is not optimal. Substituting the general $z^*$ back into $I$ gives the closed
form
$$
C_{\mathrm{Z}}(f) = \log_2\Bigl(1 + (1-f)\,f^{\,f/(1-f)}\Bigr),
$$
which returns $\log_2 1.25 = 0.322$ bits at $f = 0.5$ and tends to $1$ bit as
$f \to 0$, where the channel becomes the identity.

<Task>
Return to the dial above, switch it to the Z channel, and raise $f$ from $0$
towards $0.99$ while watching the marked peak. Report which way the optimal
bias moves, what value it approaches, and what the capacity readout does over
the same range.
</Task>

Both limits sit in the closed form. As $f \to 1$ the capacity falls to zero,
since $(1-f)\,f^{\,f/(1-f)} \to 0$. The optimal bias does not follow it down.
Write $u = 1 - f$ and expand the binary entropy for small $u$ as
$H(f) \approx u\log_2(1/u) + 1.443\,u$: the exponent $H(f)/u$ becomes
$\log_2(1/u) + 1.443$, so $2^{H(f)/u} \approx 2.718/u$ and
$$
\pi^{*} = \frac{z^{*}}{u} \approx \frac{1}{1 + 2.718/u}\cdot\frac{1}{u}
\longrightarrow 0.368 ,
$$
the reciprocal of Euler's number, written numerically because $e$ is the
erasure probability in this lesson. A symbol corrupted 99 times in 100 is
still worth sending on more than a third of the uses: on the hundredth it
arrives unambiguously, since only the input $1$ can produce the output $1$.

<KeyFact>
$I(X;Y)$ is concave in $p(x)$ for a fixed channel, so the maximisation has no
local maximum that is not global, and hill climbing from any starting
distribution finds $C$. That is what replaces symmetry when a channel has none,
and it is why capacity is computable at all on alphabets too large to reason
about by hand.
</KeyFact>

## ★ When no symmetry helps

Everything computed above used a symmetry to guess the maximiser and an
inequality to prove it optimal. This section states the general machinery for
when neither is available. Nothing later depends on it.

**The symmetric shortcut, stated properly.** Call a channel *symmetric* when
the rows of $p(y \mid x)$ are permutations of each other and the columns are
permutations of each other, and *weakly symmetric* when the rows are
permutations of each other and all column sums are equal. For a weakly
symmetric channel the uniform input is optimal and
$$
C = \log_2|\mathcal{Y}| - H(\text{row}),
$$
the entropy of any single row. The BSC ($1 - H(p)$), the four-key typewriter
($2 - 1$) and the 27-key typewriter ($\log_2 27 - 1$) are all instances, and
the derivations above are the general argument specialised.

**What replaces symmetry.** For any fixed channel, $I(X;Y)$ is a concave
function of $p(x)$, so the maximisation is a convex program in
$|\mathcal{X}| - 1$ variables. Its optimum is characterised by an equal-
distance condition: writing $p_Y^*$ for the output distribution induced by an
optimal input,
$$
D\bigl(p(\cdot \mid x)\,\Vert\, p_Y^*\bigr) = C \quad
\text{for every } x \text{ the optimal input uses},
$$
and $\le C$ for the rest, where $D$ is the Kullback–Leibler divergence
(<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>), the expected extra bits paid for coding with
the wrong distribution. Geometrically $C$ is the radius of the smallest divergence
ball containing all the rows, and $p_Y^*$ is its unique centre. The optimal
input distribution need not be unique; the output distribution it induces
always is.

**A check on the Z channel.** At $f = 0.5$ the maximiser is $\pi^* = 0.4$
(Exercise 5), so $p_Y^* = (0.8, 0.2)$. The row for input $0$ is $(1, 0)$, at
divergence $\log_2 \tfrac{1}{0.8} = 0.322$ bits from the centre; the row for
input $1$ is $(0.5, 0.5)$, at divergence
$0.5\log_2\tfrac{0.5}{0.8} + 0.5\log_2\tfrac{0.5}{0.2} = 0.322$ bits. The two
rows are equidistant from $p_Y^*$, and their common distance is the capacity.

**Computing it.** When the alphabet is large and no structure helps, the
Blahut–Arimoto algorithm alternates between the input distribution and the
output distribution and converges to $C$ from any interior start. It is the
standard tool, and it is the concavity above that guarantees the fixed point
it reaches is the global maximum.

## What capacity does not yet mean

Four channels, four arrow patterns, four capacities. The transition table
determines the number completely, and the numbers differ by more than the
noise levels do.

<Figure
  src="./figures/channel-gallery.svg"
  alt="Four small transition diagrams side by side with their capacities: the binary symmetric channel at 1 minus H(p), the erasure channel at 1 minus e, the four-key noisy typewriter at 1 bit, and the Z channel at 0.685 bits for f = 0.15"
  caption="Four arrow patterns, four capacities: only the pattern decides the number."
/>

Established: a discrete memoryless channel is a transition table and a
memorylessness assumption; the information crossing it depends on the input
distribution as well as on the table, so the channel's figure of merit is the
maximum $C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y)$, which lies between $0$ and
$\min(\log_2|\mathcal{X}|, \log_2|\mathcal{Y}|)$; the symmetric channel has
$C = 1 - H(p)$ and reaches zero only at $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$; the erasure
channel has $C = 1 - e$; the cost of silent errors is an entropy rather than
an error rate; and the uniform input is optimal exactly where the channel is
symmetric.

What is not established is what $C$ is worth. The 27-key typewriter already
showed the gap: the best zero-error scheme visible by inspection falls $0.055$
bits per use short of its capacity, and on the symmetric channel no zero-error
scheme exists at all, since every output is reachable from every input. So far
$C$ is the top of a curve. That every rate below it can be achieved with error
probability driven as close to zero as desired, and that no rate above it can,
is the noisy-channel coding theorem, proved in
<Ref lesson="noisy-channel-coding">the next lesson</Ref> out of Fano's
inequality and the data-processing bound already in hand.

The maximisation also closes a symmetry the course opened with. On the
compression side, <Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/> made $H(X)$ the
fewest bits per symbol a lossless code can average, and
<Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/> will write that side explicitly as a
*minimisation* of $I(X;\hat{X})$ over representations. On the transmission
side, capacity is the *maximisation* of $I(X;Y)$ over inputs. Both of
Shannon's limits are extreme values of the same functional.

<KeyFact>
The ceiling gets used as a denominator later. <Ref lesson="neural-coding"/>
divides a recorded neuron's transmitted information by the capacity of the same
stimulus-response channel, and the ratio is a coding efficiency between $0$ and
$1$: how much of the channel the cell in front of the electrode actually uses.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A link is measured at $0.5$ bits per use, and the failure mode has not been
diagnosed. Give the crossover probability of a symmetric channel and the
erasure probability of an erasure channel consistent with that measurement,
say whether either answer is unique, and state the ratio between the two
parameters. Then repeat for a link measured at $0.9$ bits per use, and say
what the ratio does as the measured rate approaches $1$ bit per use, and why.
Use $H(0.11) \approx 0.500$ and $H(0.013) \approx 0.100$.

<Solution>
**At $0.5$ bits per use.** The erasure channel needs $1 - e = 0.5$, so
$e = 0.5$, uniquely. The symmetric channel needs $H(p) = 0.5$, so $p = 0.11$ —
and also $p = 0.89$, since $H(p) = H(1-p)$; the two are the same channel up to
inverting the received bit, so the honest answer is that the crossover
probability is determined only up to that reflection. Taking the branch below
$\tfrac12$, the ratio is $0.5 / 0.11 = 4.5$.

**At $0.9$ bits per use.** The erasure channel needs $e = 0.1$. The symmetric
channel needs $H(p) = 0.1$, which gives $p = 0.013$. The ratio is
$0.1 / 0.013 = 7.7$.

**The trend.** The ratio grows without bound as the measured rate approaches
$1$. For small $p$ the binary entropy is dominated by
$H(p) \approx p \log_2(1/p)$, so matching the two channels means
$e \approx p\log_2(1/p)$ and
$$
\frac{e}{p} \approx \log_2 \frac{1}{p},
$$
which diverges as $p \to 0$. At $0.99$ bits per use the numbers are
$e = 0.01$ against $p \approx 0.00086$, a ratio near $12$.

**The reading.** A silent-error link must be an order of magnitude quieter
than a self-reporting one to deliver the same rate, and the gap widens the
cleaner the link gets, because each rare error costs $\log_2(1/p)$ bits to
locate. The transferable move: capacity is invertible, so a measured rate
constrains the noise parameter — but only once the failure mode is known.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Compute the capacity of $\mathrm{BSC}(0.5)$ and of $\mathrm{BSC}(1)$. Explain
why the two answers differ although the second channel is wrong far more
often, name the physical operation that rescues the second case, and say which
hypothesis of the BSC capacity proof fails at $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

<Solution>
$C = 1 - H(0.5) = 1 - 1 = 0$ bits per use. $C = 1 - H(1) = 1 - 0 = 1$ bit per
use.

**Why they differ.** Capacity measures dependence between output and input,
not agreement. At $p = 1$ the output determines the input exactly, by
inversion; at $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ the output is a fair coin whatever was sent,
and $Y$ is independent of $X$. Being always wrong is a perfect code with a
relabelled alphabet; being right half the time by chance is no code at all.

**The operation.** The receiver inverts every bit it receives. The composite
channel from input to inverted output is noiseless, so the inversion is a
zero-cost relabelling of the output alphabet, and relabelling cannot change
$I(X;Y)$, which depends only on the joint distribution up to bijections of
each variable.

**The hypothesis that fails.** Not the bound $I \le 1 - H(p)$, which still
holds and is still attained. What fails is the uniqueness argument in the
attainment step: $P(Y = 1) = p + \pi(1 - 2p)$ has coefficient $1 - 2p = 0$ at
$p = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so every input attains the maximum instead of only the
uniform one. The maximiser stops being unique exactly where the maximum
becomes zero.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim, which is the accounting the equivocation section rejects.
"A channel that corrupts 10% of the bits sent through it delivers 0.9 bits per
use: you lose exactly what you corrupt." Give the true capacity of the channel
the claim describes, name the step that fails, and then produce a channel for
which the claimed figure is exactly right and state what makes it different.

<Solution>
**The true number.** For $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$, $C = 1 - H(0.1) = 1 - 0.469 =
0.531$ bits per use, not $0.9$. The claim overstates the capacity by $0.37$
bits per use, or 70% of the true value.

**The step that fails.** The claim subtracts the error *probability* where the
correct subtraction is the *entropy* that the errors leave behind:
$I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$ with $H(X \mid Y) = H(p)$ for a uniform input,
and $H(0.1) = 0.469$, not $0.1$. The extra $0.369$ bits are the cost of not
knowing which received bits are the corrupted ones; there are about
$\binom{n}{0.1n} \approx 2^{0.469n}$ plausible error patterns in $n$ uses, and
naming one of them is what the receiver cannot do.

**Where the claim is right.** $\mathrm{BEC}(0.1)$ has $C = 1 - e = 0.9$ bits
per use exactly. The difference is that its failures are labelled: the
receiver sees a $?$ and knows precisely which uses were lost, so there is no
error pattern left to identify and the equivocation is just the content of the
missing symbols. The claim is not a bad approximation to the symmetric
channel; it is the exact answer to a different channel.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The erasure capacity was derived from the input side. Derive it twice more.
(a) From the output side: compute $H(Y \mid X)$ and $H(Y)$ directly for
$\mathrm{BEC}(e)$ with $\pi = P(X = 1)$ and verify $I(X;Y) = (1-e)H(\pi)$.
(b) By conditioning on the erasure indicator $E = \mathbf{1}\{Y = \,?\}$,
which the receiver can compute from $Y$. State which step of (a) the second
argument replaces.

<Solution>
**(a) Output side.** Given $X = x$, the output is $x$ with probability $1 - e$
and $?$ with probability $e$: a two-outcome distribution of entropy $H(e)$,
the same for both inputs, so $H(Y \mid X) = H(e)$.

$Y$ takes the values $0$, $?$, $1$ with probabilities $(1-e)(1-\pi)$, $e$,
$(1-e)\pi$. Splitting each logarithm of a product,
$$
H(Y) = -(1-e)\bigl[(1-\pi)\log_2(1-\pi) + \pi\log_2\pi\bigr]
- (1-e)\log_2(1-e) - e\log_2 e = (1-e)H(\pi) + H(e).
$$
Subtracting, $I(X;Y) = (1-e)H(\pi) + H(e) - H(e) = (1-e)H(\pi)$, maximised at
$\pi = \tfrac12$ to give $C = 1 - e$.

**(b) Erasure indicator.** $E$ is a function of $Y$, so $(Y, E)$ carries
exactly the information $Y$ does and $I(X;Y) = I(X;Y,E)$. Condition on $E$:
$$
I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y, E)
= H(X) - \bigl[(1-e)\cdot 0 + e \cdot H(X)\bigr] = (1-e)H(X),
$$
since given $E = 0$ the receiver knows $X$ exactly, and given $E = 1$ it knows
nothing beyond the prior. The channel is a mixture: a noiseless binary channel
a fraction $1 - e$ of the time and a disconnected wire the rest of the time.

**What is replaced.** The expansion of $H(Y)$ into $(1-e)H(\pi) + H(e)$, the
only nontrivial algebra in (a), and with it the cancellation of $H(e)$ against
the noise term. The indicator argument never creates the $H(e)$ that has to
cancel, because it separates the *fact* of an erasure, which is visible and
carries no information about the input, from the *content* that was lost. The
transferable move: when part of the output is a function of the output, split
on it before expanding anything.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Maximise the Z channel at $f = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so that $p(0 \mid 0) = 1$ and
$p(0 \mid 1) = p(1 \mid 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$. Report $I$ at the uniform input,
the optimal bias $\pi^*$, and $C$; then confirm the general closed form
$C = \log_2(1 + (1-f)f^{f/(1-f)})$ on this case. Use $H(0.25) \approx 0.8113$
and $H(0.2) \approx 0.7219$.

<Solution>
**Uniform input.** $P(Y = 1) = 0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 0.25$, so
$H(Y) = H(0.25) \approx 0.8113$. The rows have entropies $H(0) = 0$ and
$H(\tfrac12) = 1$, so $H(Y \mid X) = 0.5 \cdot 0 + 0.5 \cdot 1 = 0.5$ and
$I(0.5) \approx 0.8113 - 0.5 = 0.311$ bits per use.

**The optimum.** In general $I(\pi) = H(\pi/2) - \pi$ here, since
$P(Y=1) = \pi/2$ and $H(Y \mid X) = \pi \cdot 1$. Differentiating with
$\tfrac{d}{dq}H(q) = \log_2\frac{1-q}{q}$ and the chain-rule factor

$$
\tfrac{1}{2}\log_2\frac{1 - \pi/2}{\pi/2} - 1 = 0
\;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{2-\pi}{\pi} = 4 \;\Longrightarrow\; \pi^* = 0.4.
$$

**Capacity.** $C = I(0.4) = H(0.2) - 0.4 \approx 0.7219 - 0.4 = 0.322$ bits
per use, against $0.311$ at the uniform input: the optimal sender transmits
the corruptible symbol $1$ only 40% of the time.

**The closed form.** With $f = \tfrac12$, $H(f) = 1$ and $f/(1-f) = 1$, so
$1 + (1-f)f^{f/(1-f)} = 1 + 0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 1.25$ and
$\log_2 1.25 = 0.3219$, matching. The transferable move is that the derivative
condition always reduces to a statement about the *output* bias
$z = \pi(1-f)$, which is why the closed form exists at all.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
An input with $P(X = 1) = 0.2$ feeds a binary symmetric channel with
crossover $0.25$. Decide first which of the three expressions for $I(X;Y)$ is
cheapest to evaluate here and say what cue decides it. Then build the joint
table $p(x,y)$, compute both marginals, evaluate $I(X;Y)$, and compare with
the channel's capacity. Use $H(0.25) \approx 0.8113$ and
$H(0.35) \approx 0.9341$.

<Solution>
**Which expression.** $H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$. The cue is that the conditional
term is the one the transition table hands you directly: every row of a BSC
has the same entropy, so $H(Y \mid X) = H(0.25)$ with no averaging to do.
$H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$ would require inverting the channel with Bayes for each
output value, and $H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y)$ would require a four-term joint
entropy. The rule generalises: condition on the variable whose conditional
distribution you were given (<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>).

**Joint table.** $p(x,y) = p(x)p(y \mid x)$:
$$
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & y = 0 & y = 1 \\ \hline
x = 0 & 0.8 \cdot 0.75 = 0.60 & 0.8 \cdot 0.25 = 0.20 \\
x = 1 & 0.2 \cdot 0.25 = 0.05 & 0.2 \cdot 0.75 = 0.15
\end{array}
$$

**Marginals.** $P(X) = (0.8, 0.2)$ by construction;
$P(Y = 0) = 0.60 + 0.05 = 0.65$ and $P(Y = 1) = 0.20 + 0.15 = 0.35$.

**Mutual information.** $H(Y \mid X) = H(0.25) \approx 0.8113$ and
$H(Y) = H(0.35) \approx 0.9341$, so
$$
I(X;Y) \approx 0.9341 - 0.8113 = 0.123 \text{ bits per use}.
$$

**Against capacity.** $C = 1 - H(0.25) \approx 0.189$ bits per use. This
sender realises $0.123$ of the available $0.189$ bits; the missing $0.066$ is
lost to the choice of input distribution, not to the channel. The channel is
identical in both cases, which is the whole reason capacity is defined as a
maximum.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Measure a real link and report its capacity with an honest error range. Pick
something you can run a few hundred trials through: a serial line at a
deliberately high baud rate, a UDP path with sequence numbers, a QR code
photographed at increasing angles, a radio link at the edge of range. Decide
from the failure mode whether a BSC or a BEC is the right model, estimate the
parameter, propagate the uncertainty to $C$, and state a test that would show
the memoryless model is wrong.

<Solution>
**Model choice.** If failures arrive labelled — a dropped packet, a
checksum-flagged frame, an unreadable module — the channel is an erasure
channel and the parameter is the erasure rate. If failures arrive as
plausible-looking wrong values, it is a symmetric channel and the parameter is
the crossover rate. The distinction is exactly the one the equivocation
section priced, and getting it wrong changes the answer by a factor of two or
more.

**Estimate.** With $k$ failures in $n$ independent trials, $\hat{p} = k/n$
with standard error $\sqrt{\hat p (1-\hat p)/n}$. For a concrete case, $k =
23$ failures in $n = 500$ gives $\hat p = 0.046 \pm 0.009$.

**Propagate.** For the erasure channel $C = 1 - e$, so the error bar carries
straight across: $C = 0.954 \pm 0.009$ bits per use. For the symmetric
channel, $\frac{dC}{dp} = -\log_2\frac{1-p}{p}$, which at $\hat p = 0.046$ is
$-4.37$, so a $0.009$ uncertainty in $p$ becomes $0.041$ in $C$:
$C = 1 - H(0.046) = 0.731 \pm 0.041$ bits per use. Report two significant
figures at most; the derivative blows up as $p \to 0$, so a clean link needs
many more trials for the same precision in $C$.

**Testing memorylessness.** Split the trials into blocks of 20 and compare the
variance of the per-block failure counts against the binomial prediction
$np(1-p)$. Clustered failures give a variance well above it. Equivalently,
compare the observed distribution of gaps between failures with the geometric
distribution the model implies. If either test fails, the number computed is
the capacity of a channel you do not have, and the honest report is the
per-use failure rate plus the observed burst statistics.

**What counts as agreement.** Split the data in half, compute $C$ separately
on each half, and check the two values agree within the combined error bars.
Two halves that disagree by more than that indicate drift in the link during
the measurement, which is another failure of the memoryless assumption.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A binary symmetric channel with $p = 0.11$ has capacity $0.5$ bits per use, so
half a bit per use is not obviously out of reach. Try to reach it by
repetition: send each message bit $n$ times and decode by majority vote.
Compute the probability that a message bit is decoded wrongly for $n = 3$,
$5$, and $7$, and the rate of each scheme in bits per channel use. Report what
the error probability and the rate are each doing as $n$ grows, and what that
implies about the pair you would want.

<Solution>
**Error probability.** A majority vote over $n$ uses fails when more than
$n/2$ of the $n$ transmissions flip. With $p = 0.11$:
$$
n = 3:\quad \binom{3}{2}p^2(1-p) + p^3 = 3(0.0121)(0.89) + 0.00133 = 0.0336.
$$
$$
n = 5:\quad \textstyle\sum_{k=3}^{5}\binom{5}{k}p^k(1-p)^{5-k}
= 0.01054 + 0.00065 + 0.00002 = 0.0112.
$$
$$
n = 7:\quad \textstyle\sum_{k=4}^{7}\binom{7}{k}p^k(1-p)^{7-k}
= 0.00361 + 0.00027 + 0.00001 = 0.00389.
$$

**Rate.** One message bit per $n$ channel uses: $0.333$, $0.200$, $0.143$ bits
per use.

**The two trends.** The error probability falls, roughly by a factor of three
per two extra repetitions, and drives towards zero. The rate also falls
towards zero, as $1/n$. Repetition buys reliability only by spending rate, and
every scheme in the family is strictly worse than the last on the axis that
matters: at $n = 7$ the error is $0.4\%$ and the rate is $0.143$ bits per use,
less than a third of the $0.5$ the channel is worth.

**What you would want.** A family whose error probability goes to zero while
the rate stays fixed at some $R < C$. Nothing here suggests such a family
exists; repetition makes the opposite trade, and the same is true of every
scheme built by adding redundancy to one bit at a time. That such families do
exist for every $R < 0.5$ on this channel, and for no $R > 0.5$, is the
noisy-channel coding theorem of
<Ref lesson="noisy-channel-coding">the next lesson</Ref>, which gets its rate
by coding over long blocks rather than over single bits.
</Solution>

Cross-entropy decomposes as H(p,q) = H(p) + KL(p||q); minimizing
cross-entropy loss minimizes the divergence of the model from the data
distribution. This lesson identifies the training loss of probabilistic
classifiers and language models with the coding cost of
<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/> and works out the consequences: the loss has a
floor the model cannot lower, the part the model controls is exactly
$D(p\,\|\,q)$, and the unique minimizer is $q = p$. The route: the
single-example loss as the model's surprisal at the truth, cross-entropy as
its average, the decomposition into entropy plus divergence, the collapse to
negative log-likelihood under one-hot labels, and the unbounded penalty for
confident wrong predictions.

## The loss on one example: the model's surprisal at the truth

A three-class image classifier ends its forward pass in a probability
distribution over labels. On one input it outputs $q = (0.70,\ 0.20,\ 0.10)$
over $\{\text{cat}, \text{dog}, \text{fox}\}$. The true label is then
revealed, and training charges the model for that one outcome: the *log-loss*
on the example is $-\log_2 q(\text{truth})$. Nothing else in $q$ is billed.

<Example title="one prediction, three possible bills">
The output is $q = (0.70,\ 0.20,\ 0.10)$; the loss depends on which label is
true.

- Truth = cat: $-\log_2 0.70 \approx 0.51$ bits. Confident and right: near
  zero.
- Truth = dog: $-\log_2 0.20 \approx 2.32$ bits.
- Truth = fox: $-\log_2 0.10 \approx 3.32$ bits. The model rated the true
  label least likely and pays the most.

The loss is small exactly when the model put high probability on what
happened; the probabilities assigned to the labels that did not occur enter
only through normalization.
</Example>

The formula is not new. <Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/> defined the
surprisal of an event with probability $p(x)$ as $I(x) = -\log_2 p(x)$; the
log-loss is the same curve evaluated at the **model's** probability of the
observed outcome. The loss on one example is the model's surprisal at the
truth: how surprised $q$ is that this label occurred.

<KeyFact>
The log-loss on one example is $-\log_2 q(x_{\text{true}})$: the surprisal
the model assigns to the outcome that occurred. The model is billed by its
own probability for what actually happened.
</KeyFact>

## Cross-entropy: the expected log-loss under the truth

One example gives one bill. Averaging the bill over the data distribution
gives the quantity that training actually minimizes.

The average appeared already in <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>: the expected
bits spent when events from $p$ are coded with the exact code for $q$ is
$\sum_x p(x)(-\log_2 q(x))$, and on the running source
$p = (.5, .25, .15, .1)$ the uniform model $q = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14,
\tfrac14, \tfrac14)$ pays $2.000$ bits per symbol against the entropy
$H(p) \approx 1.743$. That expected cost gets the name.

<Definition title="cross-entropy">
For probability distributions $p$ and $q$ on a finite set $\mathcal{X}$, the
**cross-entropy** of $q$ relative to $p$ is

$$
H(p, q) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p(x) \log_2 q(x) \quad \text{bits,}
$$

the expected surprisal under the model $q$ when outcomes are drawn from $p$.
</Definition>

Three immediate readings of the same sum:

- **Coding:** the expected codeword length when $p$-data is coded with $q$'s
  code (<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>).
- **Learning:** the expected log-loss of model $q$ on examples drawn from
  the data distribution $p$.
- **Sanity check:** $H(p, p) = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x) = H(p)$: a model that
  matches the truth pays exactly the entropy.

**Direction convention.** Cross-entropy is not symmetric:
$H(p, q) \ne H(q, p)$ in general, because the first argument supplies the
expectation weights and the second supplies the surprisals. The loss is
always $H(\text{data}, \text{model})$ — data first, model second, the same
order as $D(p\,\|\,q)$ in <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>. Exercise 6
computes both directions on a concrete pair.

## The decomposition: entropy floor plus divergence

The gap between the uniform model's $2.000$ bits and the floor $1.743$ was
computed in <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/> to be $D(p\,\|\,q) = 0.257$ bits.
That accounting is fully general, and it is the lesson's headline identity.

<Theorem title="cross-entropy decomposition">
For probability distributions $p$ and $q$ on a finite set,

$$
H(p, q) = H(p) + D(p \,\|\, q).
$$

<Proof>
Split the logarithm inside the definition:
$\log_2 \tfrac{1}{q(x)} = \log_2 \tfrac{1}{p(x)} + \log_2
\tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ for every $x$ with $p(x) > 0$. Multiplying by $p(x)$ and
summing,

$$
-\sum_x p(x) \log_2 q(x)
= \underbrace{-\sum_x p(x) \log_2 p(x)}_{H(p)}
+ \underbrace{\sum_x p(x) \log_2 \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}}_{D(p \,\|\, q)},
$$

with the conventions of <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/> covering $p(x) = 0$
terms (they contribute $0$ to every sum) and $q(x) = 0$ terms (both sides
are $+\infty$).
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### The minimizer: training targets the divergence

The two terms have different owners. $H(p)$ is a property of the data
distribution alone; no choice of $q$ moves it. $D(p\,\|\,q)$ is the model's
mismatch, and Gibbs' inequality (<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>) states
$D(p\,\|\,q) \ge 0$ with equality if and only if $q = p$. Therefore

$$
\argmin_q H(p, q) = \argmin_q D(p \,\|\, q) = p :
$$

minimizing cross-entropy over models and minimizing model–data divergence
are the **same optimization**, and its unique solution is the data
distribution itself. A model trained to convergence on cross-entropy loss is
being pushed, in bits, toward $q = p$.

<KeyFact>
$H(p)$ does not depend on the model, so minimizing cross-entropy minimizes
$D(p\,\|\,q)$, and the unique minimizer is $q = p$: cross-entropy training
is divergence minimization under another name.
</KeyFact>

### The floor is not zero: reading a loss value

A common misreading: "the loss is $0.9$, so the model is still wrong."
Whether $0.9$ is bad depends entirely on $H(p)$, and $H(p) > 0$ whenever the
labels carry genuine randomness.

Concrete case: a binary task where annotators genuinely disagree, with
soft label $p = (0.7, 0.3)$ on some input. The hedging model
$q = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ pays $H(p,q) = 1.000$ bit. The **perfect** model
$q = p$ still pays $H(p) = 0.7 \log_2 \tfrac{1}{0.7} + 0.3 \log_2
\tfrac{1}{0.3} \approx 0.881$ bits. Only $0.119$ of the hedger's bill was
its own fault; the remaining $0.881$ bits are the label noise, and no model
recovers them. A loss above zero is not evidence of a bad model — the
model's true score is the distance above the floor, $D(p\,\|\,q)$, not the
loss itself.

<Aside>
Sometimes the floor is raised on purpose. *Label smoothing* (Szegedy et al.,
["Rethinking the Inception Architecture"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.00567),
2016) replaces the one-hot target $(1, 0, \ldots, 0)$ with the softened
$(1-\varepsilon, \tfrac{\varepsilon}{k-1}, \ldots)$, deliberately making
$H(p) > 0$. The decomposition explains both effects at once: the achievable
loss floor rises from $0$ to $H(p_{\text{smooth}})$, and the minimizer moves
from the point mass to the smoothed target, so the model is no longer pushed
toward infinite logits on the true class. The identity, not the training
loop, is what changed.
</Aside>

## One-hot labels: cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood

Classification datasets rarely ship soft labels. The recorded target is a
single class $c$, encoded as the point mass $p = (0, \ldots, 1, \ldots, 0)$
with the $1$ at position $c$. The general sum then collapses: every term with
$p(x) = 0$ vanishes, leaving

$$
H(p, q) = -\log_2 q(c),
$$

the plain log-loss of the first section. The decomposition sharpens
accordingly: a point mass is the certain distribution, so $H(p) = 0$
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>), and

$$
H(p, q) = D(p \,\|\, q) :
$$

for hard labels the cross-entropy **is** the divergence. There is no floor,
the minimum loss is $0$, and it is reached only at $q(c) = 1$.

### Over a dataset: minimizing the loss is maximizing likelihood

Training averages the per-example loss over a dataset $x_1, \ldots, x_N$:

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl(-\log_2 q(x_i)\bigr)
= \sum_{x} \hat{p}(x) \bigl(-\log_2 q(x)\bigr) = H(\hat{p}, q),
$$

where $\hat{p}(x)$ is the empirical distribution, the fraction of the
dataset equal to $x$. The training objective is literally a cross-entropy,
with the empirical distribution in the data slot. The same sum is
$-\tfrac{1}{N}\log_2 \prod_i q(x_i)$: minimizing the average log-loss is
**maximizing the likelihood** $\prod_i q(x_i)$ of the dataset. Cross-entropy
minimization, KL minimization, and maximum-likelihood estimation are one
procedure in three vocabularies.

<Remark title="nats and the softmax gradient">
Two implementation facts. Libraries compute the loss with natural
logarithms, so reported values are in nats: multiply by
$1/\ln 2 \approx 1.443$ to get bits, the same unit change as in
<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>. And when $q$ is produced by a softmax
layer with logits $z$, the gradient of the (natural-log) loss on a one-hot
target $p$ is $\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial z_j = q_j - p_j$ — prediction
minus target, derived in Bishop, *Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning*,
§4.3.4. The clean form is one reason softmax and cross-entropy are paired.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
For one-hot labels, $H(p) = 0$ and the cross-entropy equals both
$D(p\,\|\,q)$ and the negative log-likelihood $-\log_2 q(\text{true
class})$: the three standard descriptions of the classification loss are
the same number.
</KeyFact>

## Confident and wrong: the blowup

The log in the loss is gentle near $q(\text{true}) = 1$ and violent near
$0$. The same table that priced rare events in
<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/> now prices predictions.

<Example title="the price of confidence">
Per-example loss $-\log_2 q(\text{true})$ as the model's probability on the
truth falls:

| $q(\text{true})$ | loss (bits) |
| ---------------- | ----------- |
| $0.9$            | $0.152$     |
| $0.5$            | $1$         |
| $0.1$            | $3.32$      |
| $0.01$           | $6.64$      |
| $10^{-6}$        | $19.9$      |

The scale is logarithmic in both directions. Being right confidently is
cheap: $131$ predictions at $q(\text{true}) = 0.9$ cost about as much as
**one** prediction at $q(\text{true}) = 10^{-6}$
($131 \times 0.152 \approx 19.9$). A model that is nearly always confidently
right and once confidently wrong can lose, on average, to a model that
merely hedges — exercise 5 runs the numbers.
</Example>

The endpoint $q(\text{true}) = 0$ is the missing-outcome disaster of
<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/> arriving in training: a model that assigns an
occurring outcome probability zero takes an infinite loss on that single
example, and no accuracy elsewhere compensates. This is why a softmax never
emits an exact zero and why count-based language models are smoothed — the
support condition of the previous lesson, enforced by the loss itself on
every gradient step.

<KeyFact>
The loss on one example is unbounded as $q(\text{true}) \to 0$:
overconfidence on a wrong prediction is punished by the same logarithm that
makes rare events highly surprising, and an exact zero on a real outcome
costs $\infty$.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Let $p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18)$ and let $q$ be uniform on
the four symbols. Compute $H(p, q)$, $H(p)$, and $D(p\,\|\,q)$ term by term,
and verify the decomposition $H(p,q) = H(p) + D(p\,\|\,q)$.

<Solution>
Cross-entropy: every symbol has $-\log_2 q(x) = \log_2 4 = 2$ bits, so
$H(p, q) = 2 \sum_x p(x) = 2.000$ bits regardless of $p$.

Entropy: $H(p) = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot 2 + \tfrac18 \cdot 3 +
\tfrac18 \cdot 3 = 1.75$ bits.

Divergence terms:
$\tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac{1/2}{1/4} = 0.5$;
$\tfrac14 \log_2 \tfrac{1/4}{1/4} = 0$;
$\tfrac18 \log_2 \tfrac{1/8}{1/4} = -0.125$, twice.
Sum: $D(p\,\|\,q) = 0.5 + 0 - 0.25 = 0.25$ bits.

Verification: $H(p) + D(p\,\|\,q) = 1.75 + 0.25 = 2.000 = H(p,q)$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A classifier outputs $q = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1)$ over three classes. Compute the
log-loss in bits for each possible true class, then convert the three values
to nats.

<Solution>
Bits: $-\log_2 0.7 \approx 0.515$; $-\log_2 0.2 \approx 2.322$;
$-\log_2 0.1 \approx 3.322$.

Nats (multiply by $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$, or compute $-\ln q$ directly):
$-\ln 0.7 \approx 0.357$; $-\ln 0.2 \approx 1.609$;
$-\ln 0.1 \approx 2.303$.

Check on the first value: $0.515 \times 0.693 \approx 0.357$ nats.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A dataset $x_1, \ldots, x_N$ has empirical distribution
$\hat{p}(x) = \tfrac{1}{N}\,\#\{i : x_i = x\}$. Show that the average
log-loss $\tfrac{1}{N} \sum_i (-\log_2 q(x_i))$ equals $H(\hat{p}, q)$, and
conclude that minimizing it over $q$ is equivalent to maximizing the
likelihood $\prod_i q(x_i)$.

<Solution>
Group the sum by distinct values: each $x$ appears $N \hat{p}(x)$ times, so

$$
\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl(-\log_2 q(x_i)\bigr)
= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{x} N \hat{p}(x) \bigl(-\log_2 q(x)\bigr)
= \sum_x \hat{p}(x)\bigl(-\log_2 q(x)\bigr) = H(\hat{p}, q).
$$

For the likelihood: $-\log_2 \prod_i q(x_i) = \sum_i (-\log_2 q(x_i)) =
N \cdot H(\hat{p}, q)$. Since $-\log_2$ is strictly decreasing and $N$ is a
positive constant, minimizing $H(\hat{p}, q)$ over $q$ and maximizing
$\prod_i q(x_i)$ over $q$ have identical solutions: cross-entropy
minimization is maximum-likelihood estimation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On a binary task, human annotators genuinely disagree on some input: the
soft label is $p = (0.8, 0.2)$. State the smallest cross-entropy loss any
model can achieve on this input, name the model that achieves it, and
explain why a reported loss of $0.75$ bits here would signal a nearly
optimal model rather than a poor one.

<Solution>
By the decomposition, $H(p, q) = H(p) + D(p\,\|\,q) \ge H(p)$ with equality
iff $q = p$. The floor is

$$
H(p) = 0.8 \log_2 \tfrac{1}{0.8} + 0.2 \log_2 \tfrac{1}{0.2}
= 0.8 \cdot 0.322 + 0.2 \cdot 2.322 \approx 0.722 \text{ bits,}
$$

achieved by the model $q = (0.8, 0.2)$ that reports the annotator
distribution exactly. A loss of $0.75$ bits is only
$0.75 - 0.722 = 0.028$ bits of divergence above the irreducible label
noise: the model is nearly perfect. Loss values are meaningful only
relative to $H(p)$, which is zero exclusively for deterministic (one-hot)
labels.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two models process the same $10$ test examples with one-hot labels. Model A
assigns probability $0.9$ to the true class on $9$ examples and $10^{-6}$ on
the remaining one. Model B assigns $0.6$ to the true class on all $10$.
Compute each model's average loss in bits and compare.

<Solution>
Model A: nine examples cost $-\log_2 0.9 \approx 0.152$ bits each, the
tenth costs $-\log_2 10^{-6} = 6 \log_2 10 \approx 19.93$ bits. Average:
$(9 \times 0.152 + 19.93)/10 \approx (1.37 + 19.93)/10 \approx 2.13$ bits.

Model B: every example costs $-\log_2 0.6 \approx 0.737$ bits. Average:
$0.737$ bits.

Model B wins by a factor of almost three, although Model A is confidently
correct $90\%$ of the time: the single confident miss contributes $19.93$
bits, more than $14$ times the total cost of Model A's nine successes. The
log-loss average is dominated by the worst confident error, which is exactly
the incentive it is designed to create.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Let $p = (0.9, 0.1)$ and $q = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$. Compute both
directions $H(p, q)$ and $H(q, p)$, and state which one is the training
loss when the data is $90/10$ and the model hedges fair.

<Solution>
Forward: $H(p, q) = 0.9 \cdot (-\log_2 0.5) + 0.1 \cdot (-\log_2 0.5) = 1$
bit: the fair-coin code spends exactly one bit whatever happens.

Reverse: $H(q, p) = 0.5 \cdot (-\log_2 0.9) + 0.5 \cdot (-\log_2 0.1)
= 0.5 \cdot 0.152 + 0.5 \cdot 3.322 \approx 1.737$ bits.

The two differ: cross-entropy is not symmetric. The training loss is
$H(\text{data}, \text{model}) = H(p, q) = 1$ bit: the data distribution
supplies the expectation weights (reality decides how often each label
occurs), the model supplies the surprisals.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

size in bytes to which the model could losslessly compress the text, and
compare with the $8$ bits per character of raw ASCII. Given the estimate
from <Ref lesson="entropy-of-english"/> that English carries roughly $1$ bit
per letter, bound the part of the model's rate that is mismatch rather than
irreducible content.

<Solution>
Coding at the model's cross-entropy costs $1.2 \times 10^6$ bits total,
i.e. $1.2 \times 10^6 / 8 = 150{,}000$ bytes, against $10^6$ bytes for
ASCII: a $6.7{:}1$ compression ratio.

With the entropy rate of English near $1$ bit per character, the floor for
this text is about $10^6$ bits $= 125{,}000$ bytes. The model's excess is
about $1.2 - 1.0 = 0.2$ bits per character: its divergence from the true
text distribution, roughly $17\%$ of its rate. The next lesson
(<Ref lesson="compression-is-prediction"/>) makes the model-to-compressor
construction explicit.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A forecaster privately believes the outcome distribution is $p$ but reports
a distribution $r$, and is penalized $-\log_2 r(x)$ when outcome $x$ occurs.
Using Gibbs' inequality from <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>, prove that the
expected penalty is uniquely minimized by reporting $r = p$.

<Solution>
The expected penalty under the forecaster's belief is

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\bigl[-\log_2 r(x)\bigr] = H(p, r) = H(p) +
D(p \,\|\, r).
$$

$H(p)$ is fixed by the belief, and Gibbs' inequality gives
$D(p\,\|\,r) \ge 0$ with equality if and only if $r = p$. The expected
penalty is therefore at least $H(p)$, with the minimum attained exactly at
the honest report $r = p$. The log penalty is a *strictly proper scoring
rule*: it makes truthful reporting of probabilities the unique optimal
strategy, which is the decision-theoretic face of using cross-entropy as a
training loss.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

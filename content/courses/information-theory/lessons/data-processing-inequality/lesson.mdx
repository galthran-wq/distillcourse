For any variable T computed from X, no processing can increase information
about a third variable Y: if Y, X, T form a Markov chain, then
I(T;Y) \<= I(X;Y). This lesson watches the loss happen on a four-symbol
table, states the Markov condition that makes "computed from X" precise,
proves the inequality by expanding one quantity two ways with the chain
rule, extends it along a pipeline of arbitrary depth, characterizes the
equality case as sufficiency, and separates what the theorem bounds from
what it does not.

The course's argument has reached the point where two variables are
compared. <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> defined $I(X;Y)$ as the bits
that observing one variable removes from the other's uncertainty, and
fixed its range: never negative, zero exactly under independence, never
above $\min(H(X), H(Y))$. What that lesson leaves open is whether the
quantity can be manufactured. $I(X;Y)$ is a functional of a joint
distribution, and nothing said so far constrains what happens when one of
the two variables is replaced by something computed from it: a summary, a
quantizer, a hash, a network layer. This lesson closes that question with
a bound that holds for every processing rule and with the exact condition
under which the bound is met with equality. The uncertainty the bound
leaves behind, $H(Y \mid T)$, becomes an error probability in
<Ref lesson="fano-inequality">the next lesson</Ref>.

## A merge that loses bits: I drops from 0.811 to 0.467

The claim is about every processing rule. One rule on a table the course
has already computed sets the scale of the effect: merging two of four
symbols costs $0.344$ of the $0.811$ bits available.

<Task>
Answer these from memory before reading on. (1) Write $I(X;Y)$ in three
equivalent ways using entropies. (2) Name the joint tables with
$I(X;Y) = 0$. (3) State the largest value $I(X;Y)$ can take for a given
pair, and the condition that attains it.
</Task>

<Aside>
(1) $I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) = H(X) + H(Y) -
H(X,Y)$. (2) Exactly the tables in which $X$ and $Y$ are independent,
$p(x,y) = p(x)p(y)$ in every cell. (3) $I(X;Y) \le \min(H(X), H(Y))$,
with $I(X;Y) = H(Y)$ exactly when $Y$ is determined by $X$, that is
$H(Y \mid X) = 0$. All three are proved in
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>.
</Aside>

### The setup: a four-symbol source with a binary label

Take the four-symbol source used since
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>: $X \in \{a,b,c,d\}$ with
probabilities $\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}$, so
$H(X) = 1.75$ bits. Attach a binary label $Y$: $Y = 1$ if
$X \in \{c, d\}$, else $Y = 0$. The joint table has one nonzero cell per
column:

| $p(x,y)$ | $x = a$ | $x = b$ | $x = c$ | $x = d$ | $p(y)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $y = 0$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $\tfrac{1}{4}$ | $0$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{3}{4}$ |
| $y = 1$ | $0$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{1}{8}$ | $\tfrac{1}{8}$ | $\tfrac{1}{4}$ |
| $p(x)$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $\tfrac{1}{4}$ | $\tfrac{1}{8}$ | $\tfrac{1}{8}$ | $1$ |

$Y$ is a function of $X$: every column is certain, so
$H(Y \mid X) = 0$, the determinism endpoint of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>. The marginal is
$p(Y{=}1) = \tfrac{1}{8} + \tfrac{1}{8} = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so
$H(Y) = H_2(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$ bits, writing $H_2$ for the
binary entropy function, and
$$
I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \approx 0.811 - 0 = 0.811 \text{ bits}.
$$
$X$ resolves the label completely; $0.811$ bits is the entire budget in
play.

### The processing step: collapse b and c into one symbol

Define $T = f(X)$ with $f(a) = a$, $f(b) = f(c) = m$, $f(d) = d$, a
quantizer that merges the two middle symbols. $T$ is computed from $X$
alone; it never sees $Y$.

<Example title="mutual information after the merge">
Compute $I(T;Y)$ for the merged variable.

**Step 1: distribution of $T$.** Merging adds probabilities:
$p(a) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $p(m) = \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{8} =
\tfrac{3}{8}$, $p(d) = \tfrac{1}{8}$.

**Step 2: label given $T$.** Given $a$: $Y = 0$, certain. Given $d$:
$Y = 1$, certain. Given $m$: the merged column holds $b$'s mass
$\tfrac{1}{4}$ with $Y = 0$ and $c$'s mass $\tfrac{1}{8}$ with $Y = 1$,
so $p(Y{=}1 \mid m) = \tfrac{1/8}{3/8} = \tfrac{1}{3}$.

**Step 3: conditional entropy.** Only the $m$ slice is uncertain, with
$H_2(\tfrac{1}{3}) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.918$ bits:
$$
H(Y \mid T) = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0 + \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot 0.918
+ \tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 0 \approx 0.344 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Step 4: subtract.**
$$
I(T;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid T) \approx 0.811 - 0.344 = 0.467
\text{ bits} \;<\; 0.811 = I(X;Y).
$$
</Example>

## The Markov condition: what "computed from X" means

The merge is one instance of a general shape: $T$ is produced from $X$
by a fixed rule, possibly with added randomness, and without access to
$Y$. The probabilistic statement of "without access to $Y$" is
conditional independence.

<Definition title="Markov chain">
Random variables $Y$, $X$, $T$ form a **Markov chain**, written
$Y \to X \to T$, if
$$
p(t \mid x, y) = p(t \mid x) \quad \text{whenever } p(x,y) > 0.
$$
Equivalently, the joint factorizes as
$$
p(y, x, t) = p(y)\, p(x \mid y)\, p(t \mid x),
$$
with no factor in which $t$ and $y$ appear together, and equivalently
again, $Y$ and $T$ are conditionally independent given $X$:
$p(y, t \mid x) = p(y \mid x)\, p(t \mid x)$.
</Definition>

Three consequences fix the scope of the hypothesis.

- **Deterministic maps qualify.** $T = f(X)$ gives
  $p(t \mid x, y) = \mathbf{1}\{t = f(x)\}$, which does not involve
  $y$: every function of the data, including the merge above, forms a
  Markov chain.
- **Randomized processing qualifies.** If $T$ is computed from $X$ and
  fresh noise independent of $(X,Y)$, for instance $T = X \oplus N$, the
  condition still holds. The proof below uses the Markov property only,
  never determinism.
- **The chain reverses.** The factorization
  $p(y,x,t) = p(x)\, p(y \mid x)\, p(t \mid x)$ is symmetric in $y$ and
  $t$, so $Y \to X \to T$ holds exactly when $T \to X \to Y$ does. One
  hypothesis serves both reading directions.

### The condition is about the joint, not about the code

The hypothesis is checkable only against the joint distribution the data
was generated by, and the commonest way to lose it is to build $T$ with
the label in hand. Let $X$ be a customer's region, two values, uniform
and independent of a fair binary label $Y$, so $I(X;Y) = 0$. Replace the
region by the *target-encoded* feature $T$, defined as the average label
among rows sharing that region. Computed on a held-out fold, $T$ is a
function of $X$ and past data, and the chain holds. Computed on the same
rows, with a single row per region, the average label of a region *is*
that row's label: $T = Y$, hence $I(T;Y) = H(Y) = 1$ bit while
$I(X;Y) = 0$. The bound is violated, because $p(t \mid x, y) =
\mathbf{1}\{t = y\}$ depends on $y$ and the chain never held.

<Figure
  src="./figures/chain-broken.svg"
  alt="The three-node chain Y, X, T with an extra amber arrow curving directly from Y to T, labelled as the encoder reading the realised label, and the failed condition p(t given x,y) not equal to p(t given x)"
  caption="Label leakage draws the extra arrow: T depends on the realised Y, the factorization fails, and the theorem says nothing."
/>

One distinction is worth stating precisely, because it trips practised
readers. *Choosing* the function $f$ by inspecting the labels does not
break the chain: for a fixed $f$, $T = f(X)$ is a function of $X$ alone
whatever inspired it, the joint factorizes, and $I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$
still holds for the population distribution. What breaks the chain is
$T$ depending on the *realised* $Y$ of the same sample, as leave-one-in
target encoding, test-set-informed normalisation, and any label-derived
column do. The first is a selection effect that inflates estimates from
finite samples; the second is a violated hypothesis.

<KeyFact>
A stage built without the labels is bounded against every target at
once. The conditional law of $T = f(X)$ contains no $y$, so
$Y \to X \to T$ holds for any variable jointly distributed with $X$,
including targets nobody has measured — one pipeline audit covers all of
them. The exercise below that encodes the source with a prefix code turns
that universality into an equality when $f$ is invertible.
</KeyFact>

## The proof: expand I(Y; X,T) two ways

The proof needs one quantity and one identity, both assembled from
entropies the course already owns. Conditional mutual information
appeared in <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> only in passing, defined
for the XOR counterexample in which conditioning on $Z = X \oplus Y$
raised the shared information between two independent bits from $0$ to
$I(X;Y \mid Z) = 1$ bit. Here it carries the proof, so it is stated in
full.

### Conditional mutual information, stated in full

<Definition title="conditional mutual information">
The **conditional mutual information** of $Y$ and $T$ given $X$ is
$$
I(Y;T \mid X) = H(Y \mid X) - H(Y \mid X, T)
= \sum_{x} p(x)\, I(Y;T \mid X{=}x),
$$
where $I(Y;T \mid X{=}x)$ is the mutual information of the conditional
joint distribution $p(y,t \mid x)$.
</Definition>

Each slice $I(Y;T \mid X{=}x)$ is an ordinary mutual information, hence
nonnegative by the nonnegativity theorem of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>; the weighted average inherits the
bound: $I(Y;T \mid X) \ge 0$, with equality exactly when $Y$ and $T$
are independent inside every slice. That equality condition is the
Markov property, restated.

Both conditional informations are computable directly on the running
table. Given $X = b$, the value $T = m$ is certain, and a constant is
independent of everything: every slice of $I(Y;T \mid X)$ is zero, so
$I(Y;T \mid X) = 0$. The mirror quantity $I(Y;X \mid T)$ behaves
differently. The slices $T = a$ and $T = d$ determine $Y$, contributing
zero; the slice $T = m$ holds $X \in \{b, c\}$ with probabilities
$(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$ and $Y$ follows $X$ inside it, so its
mutual information is $H_2(\tfrac{1}{3}) \approx 0.918$ bits. Averaging,
$$
I(Y;X \mid T) = \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot 0.918 \approx 0.344 \text{ bits},
$$
which is numerically the loss $I(X;Y) - I(T;Y)$ observed in the first
section. The chain rule turns that coincidence into an identity.

### The chain rule for mutual information

<Theorem title="chain rule for mutual information (Cover &amp; Thomas 2.5.2)">
For any discrete random variables $Y$, $X$, $T$,
$$
I(Y;\, X, T) = I(Y;X) + I(Y;T \mid X) = I(Y;T) + I(Y;X \mid T).
$$

<Proof>
The pair $(X,T)$ is one discrete variable, so
$I(Y;\, X,T) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X, T)$. Insert $H(Y \mid X)$:
$$
I(Y;\, X,T)
= \bigl[H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)\bigr]
+ \bigl[H(Y \mid X) - H(Y \mid X,T)\bigr]
= I(Y;X) + I(Y;T \mid X).
$$
The first bracket is the definition of $I(Y;X)$; the second is the
definition of $I(Y;T \mid X)$. The pair enters only as a pair, so the
same insertion with $H(Y \mid T)$ in place of $H(Y \mid X)$ gives the
second equality.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The identity reads: the information the pair $(X,T)$ carries about $Y$
can be tallied $X$-first or $T$-first, and both tallies equal the same
total. Neither tally has used any hypothesis about how $T$ arose.

### The theorem

<Theorem title="data-processing inequality (Cover &amp; Thomas 2.8.1)">
If $Y \to X \to T$, then
$$
I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y),
$$
with equality if and only if $I(Y;X \mid T) = 0$.

<Proof>
Expand $I(Y;\, X,T)$ both ways with the chain rule:
$$
I(Y;X) + I(Y;T \mid X) = I(Y;\, X,T) = I(Y;T) + I(Y;X \mid T).
$$
**The Markov step.** $Y$ and $T$ are conditionally independent given
$X$, so $I(Y;T \mid X) = 0$; this is the only step of the proof that
uses the hypothesis. The left side collapses to $I(Y;X)$:
$$
I(Y;X) = I(Y;T) + I(Y;X \mid T).
$$
Since $I(Y;X \mid T) \ge 0$, the right side is at least $I(Y;T)$, which
is the inequality; equality holds exactly when the residual term
$I(Y;X \mid T)$ vanishes.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The second check has the answer that matters: only the $X$-first

$I(Y;X,T) = I(Y;T) + I(Y;X \mid T)$ holds for every joint distribution,

<Remark title="an average, not a pointwise bound">
The theorem compares averages over the joint tables. It does not order
individual events: for a particular outcome, observing $T = t$ may
shift the conditional distribution of $Y$ more than observing some
uninformative value $X = x$ would. As with the pointwise mutual
information of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, only the expectation
obeys the inequality.
</Remark>

## Every stage is a ratchet: pipelines of any depth

One processing step was enough to prove the theorem; real systems apply
dozens. Because the hypothesis is closed under composition, the bound
extends along the whole pipeline without a new argument.

<Theorem title="the layered corollary">
Let $Y \to X \to T_1 \to T_2 \to \cdots \to T_L$, meaning that each stage
is computed from the previous one, deterministically or with fresh
independent randomness. Then
$$
I(X;Y) \;\ge\; I(T_1;Y) \;\ge\; I(T_2;Y) \;\ge\; \cdots \;\ge\;
I(T_L;Y).
$$
In particular $I(f(X);Y) \le I(X;Y)$ for every function $f$.

<Proof>
Fix a stage $k$. Everything $T_{k+1}$ depends on is contained in $T_k$
and in randomness independent of the rest, so
$p(t_{k+1} \mid t_k, y) = p(t_{k+1} \mid t_k)$ and
$Y \to T_k \to T_{k+1}$ is a Markov chain. The theorem gives
$I(T_{k+1};Y) \le I(T_k;Y)$. Chaining these $L$ inequalities, with
$T_0 = X$, gives the displayed sequence. For the function case,
$T = f(X)$ satisfies $p(t \mid x,y) = \mathbf{1}\{t = f(x)\} =
p(t \mid x)$, so $Y \to X \to T$ and the theorem applies directly.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The corollary covers every post-processing step: a hash, a quantizer, a
cropped or downsampled image, a summary statistic, a network layer, an
argmax. Each is computed from its input,
so each can only shed information about any fixed target.

### A cascade with numbers

Repeating one weak noise stage makes the sequence quantitative: with a
$10\%$ flip per stage, each stage keeps roughly three fifths of what
reached it.

<Example title="three noisy stages in a row">
Let $Y$ be a fair bit and let each stage add an independent
$\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.1)$ flip: $X = Y \oplus N_1$,
$T_1 = X \oplus N_2$, $T_2 = T_1 \oplus N_3$, $T_3 = T_2 \oplus N_4$.

**Effective flip probability.** Composing $k$ independent flips of
probability $\delta$ leaves the total flipped with probability
$p_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(1 - (1-2\delta)^k\bigr)$, since each flip
multiplies the bias $1 - 2p$ by $1 - 2\delta$. With $\delta = 0.1$:
$p_1 = 0.1$, $p_2 = 0.18$, $p_3 = 0.244$, $p_4 = 0.2952$.

**Information at each stage.** Every stage is a fair bit, so
$H(\cdot) = 1$, and stage $k$ differs from $Y$ with probability $p_k$,
so $I(\text{stage}_k;Y) = 1 - H_2(p_k)$:
$$
I(X;Y) = 0.531,\quad I(T_1;Y) = 0.320,\quad I(T_2;Y) = 0.198,\quad
I(T_3;Y) = 0.125 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Reading.** The first stage keeps $53\%$ of the one bit in $Y$, and
each further stage keeps between $60\%$ and $63\%$ of what reached it.
Nothing in the pipeline can reverse the direction, and the losses
compound geometrically rather than additively.
</Example>

### The ceiling over a learned representation

A trained network is such a pipeline, with the raw input at the bottom
and the prediction at the top, so its every layer sits under the same
ceiling: $I(\text{layer};Y) \le I(X;Y)$. No amount of training moves the
ceiling, because the ceiling is a property of the data-generating joint
distribution, not of the model. Only measuring something new about the
world, another sensor or another column, raises it.

The contrapositive is a working debugging rule. A learned feature that
scores higher on $I(\cdot\,;Y)$ than the raw input did is not a better
feature; it is a broken chain. Somewhere the pipeline read $Y$ or a
proxy for it, and the audit follows the arrow that the theorem forbids.
The one benign explanation is estimation error, which is systematic and
upward for small samples, examined in
<Ref lesson="estimating-mutual-information"/>.

<Aside>
The same machinery prices what a learning algorithm memorizes. For a
training set $S$ and learned weights $W$,
[Xu & Raginsky (2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07809) bound the
expected generalization gap of any algorithm with a $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian
loss by
$$
\left|\, \mathbb{E}[\text{gen}(S,W)] \,\right|
\le \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\, I(S;W)}{n}},
$$
where $n$ is the number of training examples: an algorithm whose output
shares few bits with its training set cannot overfit it. Because
training is itself a Markov chain $S \to W \to W'$ for any $W'$ computed
from the weights, the layered corollary applies to the bound as well: no
post-processing of a trained model (pruning, quantisation, distillation)
can increase its mutual information with the training set.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Monotone is not the same as strictly decreasing: the corollary permits a
stage to lose nothing at all, which is the equality case of the next
section. Strict loss needs a condition on the stage itself — for the
$10\%$ flip of the cascade every stage must shed at least $36\%$ of what
reached it, a contraction the theorem above does not supply and the
starred section records.
</KeyFact>

## Equality: sufficient statistics

The proof localizes the entire loss in one term, so it also says exactly
when nothing is lost: $I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$ precisely when
$I(Y;X \mid T) = 0$, that is, when the original $X$ carries no further
information about $Y$ once $T$ is known. In that case $Y \to T \to X$ is
a Markov chain as well, and $T$ stands fully between the data and the
target.

<Definition title="sufficient statistic">
A variable $T$ with $Y \to X \to T$ is a **sufficient statistic** for
$Y$ if
$$
I(T;Y) = I(X;Y),
$$
equivalently, if $X$ and $Y$ are conditionally independent given $T$.
</Definition>

<Example title="a merge with equality">
On the same four-symbol table, merge the other pair: $T' = g(X)$ with
$g(a) = a$, $g(b) = b$, $g(c) = g(d) = e$.

**Step 1: distribution of $T'$.** $p(a) = \tfrac{1}{2}$,
$p(b) = \tfrac{1}{4}$, $p(e) = \tfrac{1}{8} + \tfrac{1}{8} =
\tfrac{1}{4}$.

**Step 2: label given $T'$.** Given $a$ or $b$: $Y = 0$, certain.
Given $e$: both merged columns carry $Y = 1$, so $Y = 1$, certain.
Hence $H(Y \mid T') = 0$.

**Step 3: subtract.**
$I(T';Y) = H(Y) - 0 \approx 0.811$ bits $= I(X;Y)$: equality. $T'$ is
a sufficient statistic for $Y$.

**Compression check.** $T'$ is strictly coarser than $X$:
$H(T') = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{4}
\cdot 2 = 1.5$ bits, against $H(X) = 1.75$. The merge discarded a
quarter bit of $X$, all of it irrelevant to $Y$.
</Example>

### The classical case: a count is enough

Statistics reached the same condition from the other side, with the
target a model parameter rather than a label. A statistic $T(X)$ is
called sufficient for a family $p(x;\theta)$ when the conditional law of
$X$ given $T$ does not involve $\theta$, which is the Markov chain
$\theta \to T(X) \to X$, and therefore the equality case of this
lesson's theorem for every prior on $\theta$.

<Example title="the head count of three tosses">
Toss a coin three times independently, writing $\theta$ for the
probability of heads. Take $\theta$ to be $\tfrac{1}{4}$ or
$\tfrac{3}{4}$ with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ each, and let
$T = X_1 + X_2 + X_3$ be the number of heads.

**Why $T$ is sufficient.** Given $T = k$, all $\binom{3}{k}$ sequences
with $k$ heads have the same probability
$\theta^k(1-\theta)^{3-k}$, so each has conditional probability
$1 / \binom{3}{k}$ whatever $\theta$ is. The conditional law of the
sequence given the count does not involve $\theta$: the chain
$\theta \to T \to X^3$ holds.

**The two informations.** The posterior odds on $\theta = \tfrac{3}{4}$
after $k$ heads are $3^k : 3^{3-k}$, giving posteriors
$\tfrac{1}{28}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{27}{28}$ for
$k = 0,1,2,3$, and those counts occur with probabilities
$\tfrac{28}{128}, \tfrac{36}{128}, \tfrac{36}{128}, \tfrac{28}{128}$.
Averaging the posterior entropies,
$$
H(\theta \mid T) = \tfrac{56}{128} H_2(\tfrac{1}{28})
+ \tfrac{72}{128} H_2(\tfrac{1}{4})
\approx 0.4375 \cdot 0.222 + 0.5625 \cdot 0.811 = 0.554 \text{ bits},
$$
so $I(\theta;T) = 1 - 0.554 = 0.446$ bits. The full sequence gives the
same posterior for each $k$, so its conditional entropy is the same
average and $I(\theta;X^3) = 0.446$ bits: equality, as sufficiency
requires.

**The price in data.** $H(X^3) = 2.880$ bits against $H(T) = 1.989$
bits. The count throws away $0.891$ bits of the sequence, namely the
order of the tosses, and none of the $0.446$ bits about $\theta$.
</Example>

  src="./figures/sufficiency-coin.svg"
  alt="Three coin tosses collapsing to a head count, with entropy bars falling from 2.880 to 1.989 bits while the two information bars about theta stay equal at 0.446 bits"
  caption="Lossy about the data, lossless about the parameter: the count discards 0.891 bits of the sequence and none of the 0.446 bits about θ."
/>

<Remark title="sufficiency is relative to the target">
$T'$, the merge of $c$ and $d$, is sufficient for that table's $Y$. For
the target $X$ itself the same merge is lossy: $I(T';X) = H(T') = 1.5$
bits, below $I(X;X) = H(X) = 1.75$ bits (self-information,
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>). A statistic sufficient for one
target can destroy information about another, so sufficiency is always a
property of the pair (statistic, target), never of the statistic alone.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Equality does not mean invertibility. The head count reconstructs
nothing about which toss came first, discarding $0.891$ of $2.880$ bits
of the data, and still meets the bound with equality. It is also
fragile: the exercise below that perturbs the sufficient merge leaks
$5\%$ of one symbol across it and loses $0.052$ bits, an order of
magnitude more than the leak's own
probability mass would suggest.
</KeyFact>

## What the theorem does not say

From "processing cannot increase information about $Y$" one would expect
that processing cannot help a predictor of $Y$ either, and that the
best thing to do with data is to keep all of it. Both conclusions are
false, and the second is a live methodological error: it argues against
feature engineering, dimensionality reduction, and representation
learning from a theorem that says nothing about any of them.

The theorem bounds one functional of the joint distribution. It does not
bound accuracy, loss, sample efficiency, or the performance of any
particular model class, because none of those is a function of
$I(\cdot\,;Y)$ alone. A representation can hold strictly fewer bits
about $Y$ and still support a strictly better predictor, whenever the
bits it dropped were inaccessible to the model class and the bits it
kept were put in a form the class can read.

<Example title="fewer bits, twenty accuracy points">
Let $Y$ be a fair bit. The raw record is $X = (A, B, Z)$, built as
follows: $A$ is an independent fair bit, $B = A \oplus S$ where
$S = Y \oplus E_1$ with $E_1 \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.1)$, and
$Z = Y \oplus E_2$ with $E_2 \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.4)$. So the
strong evidence about $Y$ is the parity $A \oplus B$, corrupted $10\%$
of the time, and the weak evidence is the single coordinate $Z$,
corrupted $40\%$ of the time.

**Information in the raw record.** Given $Y$, the two pieces of evidence
are independent, so the posterior odds multiply. When $S$ and $Z$ agree
their combined likelihood ratio is $\tfrac{0.9 \cdot 0.6}{0.1 \cdot 0.4}
= 13.5$; when they disagree it is $\tfrac{0.9 \cdot 0.4}{0.1 \cdot 0.6}
= 6$. Agreement has probability $0.58$ and leaves posterior
$\tfrac{0.04}{0.58} = 0.069$; disagreement has probability $0.42$ and
leaves $\tfrac{0.06}{0.42} = 0.143$. Then
$$
H(Y \mid X) = 0.58\,H_2(0.069) + 0.42\,H_2(0.143) \approx 0.458,
\qquad I(X;Y) \approx 0.542 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Information in the feature.** Let $T = A \oplus B = S$, discarding
$Z$. Then $I(T;Y) = 1 - H_2(0.1) = 0.531$ bits, strictly less than
$0.542$: the processing destroyed $0.011$ bits, as it must.

**Accuracy under a linear model.** A rule that thresholds a weighted sum
$w_1A + w_2B + w_3Z$ cannot read the parity, because the parity is not a
linear function of $A$ and $B$; enumerating all such rules on the eight
possible records, the best reaches $70\%$ correct. The single rule
"guess $Y = T$" on the processed feature reaches $90\%$.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/usability-panels.svg"
  alt="Left panel: the four A,B cells in a checkerboard label pattern that no straight line separates, plus a weak Z strip, marked 70 percent correct and 0.542 bits; right panel: the XOR feature splitting cleanly at one threshold, marked 90 percent correct and 0.531 bits"
  caption="The same data twice: 0.542 bits and 70% accuracy on the raw coordinates, 0.531 bits and 90% on the derived feature."
/>

The replacement model is a two-part account. Mutual information measures
how many bits about $Y$ are *present* in a representation; a model class
determines how many of those bits are *reachable*. The data-processing
inequality governs the first quantity only, and every practical gain
from preprocessing is a gain in the second. The theorem is therefore a
constraint on the measure rather than advice about pipelines: it fixes
what $I(\cdot\,;Y)$ can do, and $I(\cdot\,;Y)$ is not the quantity a
predictor consumes.

<KeyFact>
The gap between the two quantities belongs to the model class, not to
the data. Against an unrestricted predictor it closes: the Bayes error
on $T$ is at least the Bayes error on $X$, since any rule reading $T$ is
a rule reading $X$, so no preprocessing can help a Bayes-optimal
learner. Every reported gain from feature engineering is a gain against
a class that cannot read the raw coordinates — which is why the same
trick stops paying as capacity grows.
</KeyFact>

## ★ The divergence form and a stronger inequality

The mutual-information statement is a corollary of a more general one,
in which no pair of variables appears and only two distributions and one
channel do. The core path does not need it. It is the form under which
the inequality holds for every divergence-like functional, and the form
in which the contraction coefficient at the end of this section is
stated.

Write $D(P \Vert Q) = \sum_z P(z) \log_2 \tfrac{P(z)}{Q(z)}$ for the
divergence between two distributions on the same alphabet, the quantity
that <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> already produced as
$I(X;Y) = D\bigl(p(x,y) \Vert p(x)p(y)\bigr)$ and that
<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/> develops as the price of coding with the
wrong model. Let $K$ be a channel, that is, a conditional distribution
$K(t \mid x)$, and write $KP$ for the output distribution when the input
is drawn from $P$.

<Theorem title="data processing for divergence (Polyanskiy &amp; Wu 2.2)">
For any channel $K$ and any input distributions $P$, $Q$ on its input
alphabet,
$$
D(KP \Vert KQ) \le D(P \Vert Q).
$$
Equality holds exactly when the output is a sufficient statistic for
deciding between $P$ and $Q$.
</Theorem>

Two lines recover the mutual-information version. Apply the theorem with
the channel that acts on the first coordinate of a pair,
$(x,y) \mapsto (t,y)$ with $t$ drawn from $p(t \mid x)$, to the two
inputs $P = p(x,y)$ and $Q = p(x)p(y)$. The channel sends $P$ to
$p(t,y)$ and, because it leaves $y$ alone and the input factorizes, it
sends $Q$ to $p(t)p(y)$. Therefore
$$
I(T;Y) = D\bigl(p(t,y) \Vert p(t)p(y)\bigr)
\le D\bigl(p(x,y) \Vert p(x)p(y)\bigr) = I(X;Y).
$$
The proof of the divergence form itself uses the same two-way expansion
as the proof above, with divergences in place of mutual informations,
and is left to the exercise below that proves the divergence form.

### A noisy channel contracts by a definite factor

The inequality can be strengthened from "does not increase" to
"shrinks by a definite factor". For many channels there is a constant
$\eta_{\mathrm{KL}} < 1$, the *contraction coefficient*, with
$D(KP \Vert KQ) \le \eta_{\mathrm{KL}}\, D(P \Vert Q)$ for all $P, Q$,
and hence $I(U;T) \le \eta_{\mathrm{KL}} I(U;X)$ for every chain
$U \to X \to T$ through that channel. For the binary symmetric channel —
a channel that flips each transmitted bit independently with probability
$\delta$, the noise model behind the XOR counterexample of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> and the stage of the cascade above —
$\eta_{\mathrm{KL}} = (1 - 2\delta)^2$.

The cascade above is exactly such a chain with $\delta = 0.1$, so
$\eta_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0.64$ and each stage must lose at least $36\%$ of
what reached it:

| stage | $I(\text{stage};Y)$ | bound $0.64 \times$ previous |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $X$ | $0.531$ | |
| $T_1$ | $0.320$ | $0.340$ |
| $T_2$ | $0.198$ | $0.205$ |
| $T_3$ | $0.125$ | $0.127$ |

The measured values sit just under the strong bound at every stage,
which is what makes the geometric decay of the cascade a theorem rather
than an observation about this particular noise level.

## Where this inequality is load-bearing

Four later results in the course are consequences of the theorem proved
here, which is why it is worth carrying the Markov hypothesis around in
full.

- <Ref lesson="fano-inequality"/> converts the residual conditional
  entropy $H(Y \mid T)$ into a lower bound on the error probability of
  any decoder, so the bits destroyed here become errors there.
- The converse of the
  <Ref lesson="noisy-channel-coding">noisy-channel coding theorem</Ref>
  applies the inequality to the chain
  message $\to$ codeword $\to$ channel output $\to$ decoded message and
  then Fano's bound, which is what makes capacity an impossibility
  result rather than a target.
- In <Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/>, the reconstruction is processed
  from the compressed description, so the rate needed for a fidelity
  level is a *minimum* of $I(X;\hat{X})$ over test channels: the
  inequality is what forbids any cleverer decoder from beating it.
- The <Ref lesson="information-bottleneck"/> objective trades $I(X;T)$
  against $I(T;Y)$ over encoders $X \to T$, and its entire feasible
  region lies under the ceiling $I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$ established here;
  the layered corollary is what lets it be applied one network layer at
  a time.

Established: for every $T$ computed from $X$ without access to $Y$,
$I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$, with the deficit equal to $I(Y;X \mid T)$ and
equality exactly at sufficiency; the bound composes along a pipeline of
any depth. What remains open is the operational meaning of the leftover
$H(Y \mid T)$. It is measured in bits, while the quantity an engineer is
judged on is an error rate.
<Ref lesson="fano-inequality">The next lesson</Ref> converts one into
the other.

<Exercise>
A binary target $Y$ is uniform. The data $X$ takes three values with
$p(x \mid Y{=}0) = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1)$ and
$p(x \mid Y{=}1) = (0.1, 0.2, 0.7)$ over $x \in \{0,1,2\}$. The
processed variable $T$ is binary, produced by the channel
$p(T{=}1 \mid x) = 0, \tfrac{1}{2}, 1$ for $x = 0, 1, 2$. Compute
$I(X;Y)$ and $I(T;Y)$ and verify the inequality. Use
$H_2(0.125) = 0.544$ and $H_2(0.2) = 0.722$.

<Solution>
**$I(X;Y)$.** The marginal of $X$ is
$\tfrac{1}{2}(0.7,0.2,0.1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(0.1,0.2,0.7) = (0.4, 0.2,
0.4)$. Rather than entropies of $X$, condition the other way: the
posterior on $Y$ given $x = 0$ is $\tfrac{0.7}{0.8} = 0.875$, given
$x = 2$ it is $0.125$, and given $x = 1$ it is $\tfrac{1}{2}$. So
$$
H(Y \mid X) = 0.4\,H_2(0.125) + 0.2 \cdot 1 + 0.4\,H_2(0.125)
= 0.8 \cdot 0.544 + 0.2 = 0.635 \text{ bits},
$$
using $H_2(0.125) = 0.544$, and $I(X;Y) = 1 - 0.635 = 0.365$ bits.

**$I(T;Y)$.** The channel is a randomized threshold, so
$p(T{=}1 \mid Y{=}0) = 0.7 \cdot 0 + 0.2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} + 0.1
\cdot 1 = 0.2$, and by symmetry $p(T{=}1 \mid Y{=}1) = 0.8$. Then
$T$ is uniform, $H(T) = 1$, and $H(T \mid Y) = H_2(0.2) = 0.722$, so
$I(T;Y) = 1 - 0.722 = 0.278$ bits.

**Check.** $0.278 \le 0.365$. The randomized middle value is where the
loss happens: $x = 1$ was already uninformative, but the coin flip also
scatters the informative values $x = 0$ and $x = 2$ into a two-symbol
alphabet that cannot record how extreme the evidence was.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Work the boundary of the equality case on the four-symbol table of the
lesson. (a) Find a $T$ that is neither constant nor a bijection and for
which $I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$ exactly, and prove sufficiency by exhibiting
$H(Y \mid T)$. (b) Now perturb the channel: with probability
$\varepsilon$ the symbol $c$ is routed into the merged bucket instead of
its own. Compute $I(T;Y)$ at $\varepsilon = 0.05$ and state how the loss
scales as $\varepsilon \to 0$.

<Solution>
**(a)** Merge $a$ and $b$ into one symbol $g$, leaving
$T \in \{g, c, d\}$ with $p(g) = \tfrac{3}{4}$,
$p(c) = p(d) = \tfrac{1}{8}$. Both merged columns carry $Y = 0$, so
$Y = 0$ is certain given $g$; $c$ and $d$ each determine $Y = 1$. Hence
$H(Y \mid T) = 0$ and $I(T;Y) = H(Y) = 0.811 = I(X;Y)$. $T$ is not
constant (three values) and not a bijection ($H(T) = 1.061$ bits against
$H(X) = 1.75$), so it discards $0.689$ bits of $X$ while remaining
sufficient for $Y$.

**(b)** With the leak, $p(g) = \tfrac{3}{4} + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{8}$
and the bucket now holds $Y = 1$ mass $\tfrac{\varepsilon}{8}$, so
$$
p(Y{=}1 \mid g) = \frac{\varepsilon/8}{3/4 + \varepsilon/8}.
$$
At $\varepsilon = 0.05$: $p(g) = 0.75625$ and
$p(Y{=}1 \mid g) = 0.00826$, with $H_2(0.00826) = 0.069$, so
$H(Y \mid T) = 0.75625 \cdot 0.069 = 0.052$ bits and
$I(T;Y) = 0.811 - 0.052 = 0.759$ bits, a loss of $0.052$ bits.

**Scaling.** The loss is $\tfrac{3}{4}H_2(\varepsilon/6)$ to leading
order, which behaves like $\tfrac{\varepsilon}{8}\log_2
\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}$: superlinear in $\varepsilon$, with infinite
derivative at $0$. Numerically the loss per unit $\varepsilon$ rises as
$\varepsilon$ falls, from $0.92$ at $\varepsilon = 0.1$ to $1.75$ at
$\varepsilon = 0.001$. Sufficiency is an exact condition and its
neighbourhood is not flat: a $1\%$ leak already costs more than $1\%$ of
a bit.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Encode the lesson's source with the prefix code
$a \to 0$, $b \to 10$, $c \to 110$, $d \to 111$ (the optimal code for
$\{\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}\}$ from
<Ref lesson="huffman-by-hand"/>), and let $T$ be the codeword. Compute
the expected codeword length, then prove that $I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$ for
**every** random variable $Y$, using the data-processing inequality
twice.

<Solution>
**Expected length.**
$\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 3 +
\tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 3 = 1.75$ bits per symbol, equal to $H(X)$: the
code is complete and the source probabilities are powers of two, so the
code meets the entropy floor of
<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/> exactly.

**Equality of information.** $T$ is a function of $X$, so
$Y \to X \to T$ holds for any $Y$ and the inequality gives
$I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$. The code is prefix-free, hence uniquely decodable
(<Ref lesson="prefix-codes"/>): there is a decoding function $g$ with
$X = g(T)$. Then $X$ is a function of $T$, so $Y \to T \to X$ also
holds and the inequality gives the reverse bound
$I(X;Y) = I(g(T);Y) \le I(T;Y)$. Both directions together force
$I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$.

The transferable move: to prove an equality from an inequality, find the
second chain. An invertible map is a sufficient statistic for every
target, which is the information-theoretic content of "lossless": the
representation changed, the information about anything did not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim, with a concrete classification example of your own
and a precise statement of what the theorem does and does not bound.
"Feature engineering cannot help, because the data-processing inequality
says processing only loses information."

<Solution>
**The example.** Two coordinates $A, B$ are independent fair bits and
the label is $Y = A \oplus B$ with probability $0.9$, flipped
otherwise. Add a third coordinate $Z$ that agrees with $Y$ $60\%$ of the
time, independently of everything else given $Y$. On the raw
coordinates, the best rule that thresholds a weighted sum reaches $70\%$
accuracy, because the parity is not linearly separable and the only
linearly usable evidence is the weak $Z$. Feed the same model the single
derived feature $T = A \oplus B$ and it reaches $90\%$, although
$I(T;Y) = 0.531 < 0.542 = I(X;Y)$: the derived feature is strictly
poorer in information and strictly better in accuracy.

**What the theorem bounds.** It bounds $I(T;Y)$, a functional of the
joint distribution of $(T,Y)$, by $I(X;Y)$, and nothing else. It does
not bound the accuracy of a model class on $T$ by its accuracy on $X$,
nor the sample complexity, nor the loss.

**Why the argument fails.** It substitutes "information about $Y$" for
"usable evidence for a given model class", which are different
quantities: the parity bit is present in $(A,B)$ but unreachable by a
linear rule, and computing it moves the same evidence into reachable
form. The only claim the theorem licenses is the negative one: no
feature can make a *Bayes-optimal* predictor better than the raw data
allows, since the Bayes error is determined by the joint distribution.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague reports: "adding the derived column `ratio = spend / visits`
raised validation accuracy from $0.81$ to $0.86$, so it must have
increased $I(\text{features};\,\text{label})$." Identify which
hypothesis of the theorem the argument needs, decide whether it holds,
and give the correct account of the accuracy gain. Then state the one
circumstance in which the information really did increase, and what that
would imply about the reported number.

<Solution>
**The hypothesis.** The relevant fact is that the new column is a
function of the existing ones: $R = g(X)$ with $X$ the original feature
vector. Then $(X, R) = (X, g(X))$ is a bijective image of $X$, so
$Y \to X \to (X,R)$ and $Y \to (X,R) \to X$ are both Markov chains, and
applying the inequality in both directions gives
$$
I\bigl((X,R);\,Y\bigr) = I(X;Y)
$$
exactly. Adding a derived column changes the information by zero, not by
a positive amount.

**The correct account.** Accuracy rose because the hypothesis class or
the optimizer changed, not the information. A gradient-boosted tree
splits on axis-aligned thresholds, and a ratio is a curved boundary in
the original coordinates that would need many splits to approximate. The
engineered column moves reachable evidence into a form the class reads
in one split.

**The exception.** If the column was computed using the label, for
example a target encoding or a normalisation fitted on the validation
fold, then $R$ is not a function of $X$ alone, the chain fails, and
$I((X,R);Y)$ genuinely exceeds $I(X;Y)$. In that case the reported
$0.86$ is not an estimate of anything: the quantity being validated used
the labels it is being scored against. The check is mechanical: recompute
the column from training rows only and re-score.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Prove the divergence form of the inequality, which the lesson stated
without proof. For distributions $P, Q$ on the same alphabet, write
$D(P \Vert Q) = \sum_z P(z)\log_2 \tfrac{P(z)}{Q(z)}$, and for a channel
$K(t \mid x)$ write $KP$ for the induced output distribution. You may
use the chain rule for divergence, which for joint distributions
$P_{XT}, Q_{XT}$ reads
$$
D(P_{XT} \Vert Q_{XT}) = D(P_X \Vert Q_X) + \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P_X}
D\bigl(P_{T \mid X=x} \Vert Q_{T \mid X=x}\bigr),
$$
and the fact that divergence is nonnegative. Prove
$D(KP \Vert KQ) \le D(P \Vert Q)$, then derive the mutual-information
form in two lines.

<Solution>
**Setup.** Build two joint distributions on $(X,T)$ using the *same*
channel: $P_{XT} = P(x)K(t \mid x)$ and $Q_{XT} = Q(x)K(t \mid x)$.

**Expansion one, $X$ first.** The conditional laws
$P_{T \mid X=x}$ and $Q_{T \mid X=x}$ are both $K(\cdot \mid x)$, so
every term of the expectation is $D(K(\cdot \mid x) \Vert
K(\cdot \mid x)) = 0$ and
$$
D(P_{XT} \Vert Q_{XT}) = D(P \Vert Q).
$$
This is the step that uses the hypothesis that one channel produced
both outputs, the exact counterpart of the Markov property in the
mutual-information proof.

**Expansion two, $T$ first.** The same chain rule with the roles of the
coordinates exchanged gives
$$
D(P_{XT} \Vert Q_{XT}) = D(KP \Vert KQ) + \mathbb{E}_{t \sim KP}
D\bigl(P_{X \mid T=t} \Vert Q_{X \mid T=t}\bigr)
\;\ge\; D(KP \Vert KQ),
$$
since each divergence in the expectation is nonnegative. Combining the
two expansions, $D(P \Vert Q) \ge D(KP \Vert KQ)$, with equality exactly
when the posterior over the input given the output is the same under $P$
and $Q$.

**The mutual-information form.** Take the alphabet to be pairs $(x,y)$,
the two inputs to be $P = p(x,y)$ and $Q = p(x)p(y)$, and the channel to
be "draw $t$ from $p(t \mid x)$ and pass $y$ through unchanged". It
sends $P$ to $p(t,y)$ and $Q$ to $p(t)p(y)$, because it does not touch
$y$ and the product structure survives. Then
$I(T;Y) = D(p(t,y) \Vert p(t)p(y)) \le D(p(x,y) \Vert p(x)p(y)) =
I(X;Y)$.

The transferable move is the same in both proofs: expand one quantity
along two orders, kill the term the hypothesis forces to zero, and drop
the term nonnegativity bounds.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take any tabular dataset you have with a label column and a row
identifier, for instance an incrementing `id`, an ingestion timestamp,
or a filename index. Estimate $I(\text{label};\,\texttt{id})$ and
$I(\text{label};\,\texttt{id} \bmod 10)$ by plug-in counting on the
whole table. Report both, explain the first number before you compute it,
and state which Markov chain has to hold for the second to be a valid
summary of the first.

<Solution>
**Predict the first number.** If identifiers are unique, every row is
its own cell of the joint table, so the plug-in estimate is
$\hat{I}(\text{label};\,\texttt{id}) = \hat{H}(\text{label})$ exactly:
the identifier "determines" the label in the sample, whatever the
truth. On a balanced binary label that is $1.000$ bit, on a
ten-class balanced label $3.322$ bits. This is a property of counting
with one observation per cell, not evidence of leakage, and it is the
reason a raw identifier can never be audited by a plug-in estimate.

**The bucketed number.** $\texttt{id} \bmod 10$ has ten values, so with
$n$ rows each cell holds about $n/10$ observations and the estimate is
meaningful. On data collected in a shuffled order it lands near zero,
typically a few hundredths of a bit of upward bias. On data collected
class by class, or written by ten parallel workers with class-correlated
shards, it is materially positive, and that is the finding worth
reporting: a periodic collection order is leakage that survives
shuffling of the rows but not of the identifiers.

**The chain.** $\texttt{id} \bmod 10$ is a function of $\texttt{id}$,
so $\text{label} \to \texttt{id} \to (\texttt{id} \bmod 10)$ is a
Markov chain automatically, and the theorem gives
$I(\text{label};\,\texttt{id} \bmod 10) \le
I(\text{label};\,\texttt{id})$. The bucketed value is therefore a lower
bound on the leakage, never a certificate that there is none: a positive
value proves a problem, a near-zero value proves nothing. What counts as
agreement: rerun on a random permutation of the identifier column and
check that the bucketed estimate falls to the same small positive
baseline; if it does not, the collection order is the cause.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
In a pipeline $X \to T_1 \to T_2$ a decoder reads $T_2$ and outputs a
guess $\hat{Y}$ of a binary target $Y$, wrong with probability $P_e$.
Argue that $P_e$ cannot be pushed below a floor determined by
$H(Y \mid T_2)$, and write that floor down as explicitly as you can.
Then evaluate it for the cascade of this lesson's example, where
$I(T_2;Y) = 0.198$ bits and $H(Y) = 1$ bit.

<Solution>
**The chain.** $\hat{Y}$ is computed from $T_2$, so
$Y \to T_2 \to \hat{Y}$ is a Markov chain and
$I(\hat{Y};Y) \le I(T_2;Y)$, equivalently
$H(Y \mid \hat{Y}) \ge H(Y \mid T_2)$. Any decoder inherits at least the
uncertainty its input left.

**Turning uncertainty into errors.** Let $E = \mathbf{1}\{\hat{Y} \ne
Y\}$, so $P(E{=}1) = P_e$. Expanding $H(E, Y \mid \hat{Y})$ two ways
gives $H(Y \mid \hat{Y}) \le H(E) + P_e \cdot \log_2(|\mathcal{Y}| - 1)$,
which for a binary target is simply
$$
H(Y \mid T_2) \;\le\; H(Y \mid \hat{Y}) \;\le\; H_2(P_e).
$$
Since $H_2$ is increasing on $[0, \tfrac{1}{2}]$, this inverts to a
floor: $P_e \ge H_2^{-1}\bigl(H(Y \mid T_2)\bigr)$, where $H_2^{-1}$
takes the value in $[0, \tfrac{1}{2}]$.

**The number.** $H(Y \mid T_2) = H(Y) - I(T_2;Y) = 1 - 0.198 = 0.802$
bits. Solving $H_2(P_e) = 0.802$ gives $P_e \ge 0.244$: no decoder
reading the third stage of that cascade can be right more than about
three times in four, which matches the effective flip probability
$p_3 = 0.244$ computed directly, so the bound is tight here.

The inequality $H(Y \mid \hat{Y}) \le H_2(P_e) + P_e\log_2(|\mathcal{Y}|
-1)$ is Fano's inequality, proved and used in
<Ref lesson="fano-inequality"/>; combined with the data-processing
inequality it is the whole converse machinery of the course: bits lost
along a chain become errors that no decoder can undo.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

For any variable T computed from X, no processing can increase information
about a third variable Y: if Y, X, T form a Markov chain, then
I(T;Y) \<= I(X;Y). This lesson watches the loss happen on a four-symbol
table, states the Markov condition that makes "computed from X" precise,
proves the inequality by expanding one quantity two ways with the chain
rule, characterizes the equality case as sufficiency, and reads the result
as a ceiling over every learned representation: no feature of X knows more
about a target than X does.

## A merge that loses bits: I drops from 0.811 to 0.467

### The setup: a four-symbol source with a binary label

Take the four-symbol source used since
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>: $X \in \{a,b,c,d\}$ with
probabilities $\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}$, so
$H(X) = 1.75$ bits. Attach a binary label $Y$: $Y = 1$ if
$X \in \{c, d\}$, else $Y = 0$. The joint table has one nonzero cell per
column:

| $p(x,y)$ | $x = a$ | $x = b$ | $x = c$ | $x = d$ | $p(y)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $y = 0$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $\tfrac{1}{4}$ | $0$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{3}{4}$ |
| $y = 1$ | $0$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{1}{8}$ | $\tfrac{1}{8}$ | $\tfrac{1}{4}$ |
| $p(x)$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $\tfrac{1}{4}$ | $\tfrac{1}{8}$ | $\tfrac{1}{8}$ | $1$ |

$Y$ is a function of $X$: every column is certain, so
$H(Y \mid X) = 0$, the determinism endpoint of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>. The marginal is
$p(Y{=}1) = \tfrac{1}{8} + \tfrac{1}{8} = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so
$H(Y) = H_2(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$ bits, and by the definition of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information">mutual information</Ref>,
$$
I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \approx 0.811 - 0 = 0.811 \text{ bits}.
$$
$X$ resolves the label completely; $0.811$ bits is the entire budget in
play.

### The processing step: collapse b and c into one symbol

Process the data: define $T = f(X)$ with $f(a) = a$,
$f(b) = f(c) = m$, $f(d) = d$, a quantizer that merges the two middle
symbols. $T$ is computed from $X$ alone; it never sees $Y$.

<Example title="mutual information after the merge">
Compute $I(T;Y)$ for the merged variable.

**Step 1: distribution of $T$.** Merging adds probabilities:
$p(a) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $p(m) = \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{8} =
\tfrac{3}{8}$, $p(d) = \tfrac{1}{8}$.

**Step 2: label given $T$.** Given $a$: $Y = 0$, certain. Given $d$:
$Y = 1$, certain. Given $m$: the merged column holds $b$'s mass
$\tfrac{1}{4}$ with $Y = 0$ and $c$'s mass $\tfrac{1}{8}$ with $Y = 1$,
so $p(Y{=}1 \mid m) = \tfrac{1/8}{3/8} = \tfrac{1}{3}$.

**Step 3: conditional entropy.** Only the $m$ slice is uncertain, with
$H_2(\tfrac{1}{3}) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.918$ bits:
$$
H(Y \mid T) = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0 + \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot 0.918
+ \tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 0 \approx 0.344 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Step 4: subtract.**
$$
I(T;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid T) \approx 0.811 - 0.344 = 0.467
\text{ bits} \;<\; 0.811 = I(X;Y).
$$
</Example>

The merge cost $0.344$ bits because it fused a pure-$Y{=}0$ column with a

</KeyFact>

## The Markov condition: what "computed from X" means

The merge is one instance of a general shape: $T$ is produced from $X$
by a fixed rule, possibly with added randomness, and without access to
$Y$. The probabilistic statement of "without access to $Y$" is
conditional independence.

<Definition title="Markov chain">
Random variables $Y$, $X$, $T$ form a **Markov chain**, written
$Y \to X \to T$, if
$$
p(t \mid x, y) = p(t \mid x) \quad \text{whenever } p(x,y) > 0;
$$
equivalently, if $Y$ and $T$ are conditionally independent given $X$:
$p(y, t \mid x) = p(y \mid x)\, p(t \mid x)$.
</Definition>

Three consequences fix the scope of the hypothesis:

- **Deterministic maps qualify.** $T = f(X)$ gives
  $p(t \mid x, y) = \mathbf{1}\{t = f(x)\}$, which does not involve
  $y$: every function of the data, including the merge above, forms a
  Markov chain.
- **Randomized processing qualifies.** If $T$ is computed from $X$ and
  fresh noise independent of $(X,Y)$, e.g. $T = X \oplus N$, the
  condition still holds. The proof below uses the Markov property only,
  never determinism.
- **The chain reverses.** Factoring
  $p(y,x,t) = p(x)\, p(y \mid x)\, p(t \mid x)$ is symmetric in $y$ and
  $t$, so $Y \to X \to T$ holds exactly when $T \to X \to Y$ does. One
  hypothesis serves both reading directions.

<Remark title="the arrow direction is the hypothesis">
If $T$ is built with access to $Y$ itself, the condition
$p(t \mid x,y) = p(t \mid x)$ fails and the conclusion can fail with it:
Exercise 3 constructs a $T$ with $I(T;Y) = 1$ bit while $I(X;Y) = 0$.
Checking the Markov property is not a formality; it is the entire
content of "$T$ was computed from $X$".
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
$Y \to X \to T$ states exactly that all of $T$'s dependence on $Y$
routes through $X$: determinism is not required, fresh randomness is
allowed, access to $Y$ is not.
</KeyFact>

## The proof: expand I(Y; X,T) two ways

The proof needs one new quantity and one identity, both assembled from
entropies the course already owns.

### Conditional mutual information: the missing primitive

<Definition title="conditional mutual information">
The **conditional mutual information** of $Y$ and $T$ given $X$ is
$$
I(Y;T \mid X) = H(Y \mid X) - H(Y \mid X, T)
= \sum_{x} p(x)\, I(Y;T \mid X{=}x),
$$
where $I(Y;T \mid X{=}x)$ is the mutual information of the conditional
joint distribution $p(y,t \mid x)$.
</Definition>

Each slice $I(Y;T \mid X{=}x)$ is an ordinary mutual information, hence
nonnegative by the nonnegativity theorem of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>; the weighted average inherits the
bound: $I(Y;T \mid X) \ge 0$, with equality exactly when $Y$ and $T$
are independent inside every slice. That equality condition is the
Markov property, restated.

On the running table both conditional informations are computable
directly. Given $X = b$, the value $T = m$ is certain, and a constant
is independent of everything: every slice of $I(Y;T \mid X)$ is zero,
so $I(Y;T \mid X) = 0$. The mirror quantity $I(Y;X \mid T)$: the
slices $T = a$ and $T = d$ determine $Y$, contributing zero; the slice
$T = m$ holds $X \in \{b, c\}$ with probabilities
$(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$ and $Y$ follows $X$ inside it, so its
mutual information is $H_2(\tfrac{1}{3}) \approx 0.918$ bits. Averaging:
$$
I(Y;X \mid T) = \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot 0.918 \approx 0.344 \text{ bits},
$$
which is numerically the loss $I(X;Y) - I(T;Y)$ observed in the first
section. The chain rule turns this coincidence into an identity.

### The chain rule for mutual information

<Theorem title="chain rule for mutual information">
For any discrete random variables $Y$, $X$, $T$,
$$
I(Y;\, X, T) = I(Y;X) + I(Y;T \mid X) = I(Y;T) + I(Y;X \mid T).
$$

<Proof>
The pair $(X,T)$ is one discrete variable, so
$I(Y;\, X,T) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X, T)$. Insert $H(Y \mid X)$:
$$
I(Y;\, X,T)
= \bigl[H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)\bigr]
+ \bigl[H(Y \mid X) - H(Y \mid X,T)\bigr]
= I(Y;X) + I(Y;T \mid X).
$$
The first bracket is the definition of $I(Y;X)$; the second is the
definition of $I(Y;T \mid X)$. The pair enters only as a pair, so the
same insertion with $H(Y \mid T)$ in place of $H(Y \mid X)$ gives the
second equality. This is the chain rule for information,
[Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
Theorem 2.5.2, in the two-variable case.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The identity reads: the information the pair $(X,T)$ carries about $Y$
can be tallied $X$-first or $T$-first, and both tallies equal the same
total.

### The theorem

<Theorem title="data-processing inequality">
If $Y \to X \to T$, then
$$
I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y),
$$
with equality if and only if $I(Y;X \mid T) = 0$.

<Proof>
Expand $I(Y;\, X,T)$ both ways with the chain rule:
$$
I(Y;X) + I(Y;T \mid X) = I(Y;\, X,T) = I(Y;T) + I(Y;X \mid T).
$$
The Markov property makes $Y$ and $T$ conditionally independent given
$X$, so $I(Y;T \mid X) = 0$ and the left side collapses to $I(Y;X)$:
$$
I(Y;X) = I(Y;T) + I(Y;X \mid T).
$$
Since $I(Y;X \mid T) \ge 0$, the right side is at least $I(Y;T)$, which
is the inequality; equality holds exactly when the residual term
$I(Y;X \mid T)$ vanishes. This is
[Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
Theorem 2.8.1, with the chain relabeled $Y \to X \to T$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Remark title="an average, not a pointwise bound">
The theorem compares averages over the joint tables. It does not order
individual events: for a particular outcome, observing $T = t$ may
shift the conditional distribution of $Y$ more than observing some
uninformative value $X = x$ would. As with the pointwise mutual
information of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, only the
expectation obeys the inequality.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
For a Markov chain, $I(X;Y) = I(T;Y) + I(Y;X \mid T)$: the information
about $Y$ splits exactly into the part that survives in $T$ plus the
part the processing destroyed, and the destroyed part cannot be
negative.
</KeyFact>

## No function of the data beats the data

<Theorem title="corollary: functions and pipelines">
For any function $f$ and any $Y$,
$$
I(f(X);\,Y) \le I(X;Y).
$$
More generally, if each stage of a pipeline
$X \to T_1 \to T_2 \to \cdots$ is computed from the previous stage
(deterministically or with independent randomness), then
$I(T_k;Y)$ is non-increasing in $k$.

<Proof>
$T = f(X)$ satisfies $p(t \mid x,y) = \mathbf{1}\{t = f(x)\}
= p(t \mid x)$, so $Y \to X \to T$ and the theorem applies. For the
pipeline, each stage $T_{k+1}$ depends on $Y$ only through $T_k$, so
$Y \to T_k \to T_{k+1}$ is a Markov chain and
$I(T_{k+1};Y) \le I(T_k;Y)$; chaining the inequalities gives the
monotone sequence.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The corollary covers every post-processing step in an engineer's
toolbox: a hash, a quantizer, a cropped or downsampled image, a summary
statistic, a neural network layer. Each is a $T$ computed from its
input, so each can only shed information about any fixed target.

### What representation learning does under the ceiling

$I(X;Y)$ counts bits, not the ease of extracting them. Reading the
label from raw pixels may require exactly the computation a trained
network performs; reading it from the last-layer feature needs one
linear map. Training a representation rearranges a fixed information
budget into usable form; it never grows the budget.

The contrapositive is a working debugging rule: a learned feature that
appears to know more about $Y$ than the raw input did is evidence of
leakage: somewhere, the pipeline touched $Y$ or a proxy for it
directly, and the Markov chain assumed in evaluation does not hold.

<Aside>
The same machinery prices what a learning algorithm memorizes. For a
training set $S$ and learned weights $W$,
[Xu & Raginsky (2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07809) bound the
expected generalization gap of any algorithm with a $\sigma$-sub-Gaussian
loss by
$$
\left|\, \mathbb{E}[\text{gen}(S,W)] \,\right|
\le \sqrt{\frac{2\sigma^2\, I(S;W)}{n}},
$$
where $n$ is the number of training examples: an algorithm whose output
shares few bits with its training set cannot overfit it. The mutual
information between data and hypothesis plays the same gating role that
$I(X;Y)$ plays across a Markov chain.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Every stage of a pipeline lives under one ceiling:
$I(\text{stage};\, Y) \le I(X;Y)$. A representation can concentrate the
bits about $Y$ into accessible form; only measuring new data raises the
ceiling.
</KeyFact>

## Equality: sufficient statistics

The proof localizes the entire loss into one term:
$I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$ exactly when $I(Y;X \mid T) = 0$, that is, when,
given $T$, the original $X$ carries no further information about $Y$.
In that case $Y \to T \to X$ is also a Markov chain: $T$ stands fully
between the data and the target.

<Definition title="sufficient statistic">
A variable $T$ with $Y \to X \to T$ is a **sufficient statistic** for
$Y$ if
$$
I(T;Y) = I(X;Y),
$$
equivalently, if $X$ and $Y$ are conditionally independent given $T$.
</Definition>

<Example title="a merge with equality">
On the same table, merge the other pair: $T' = g(X)$ with $g(a) = a$,
$g(b) = b$, $g(c) = g(d) = e$.

**Step 1: distribution of $T'$.** $p(a) = \tfrac{1}{2}$,
$p(b) = \tfrac{1}{4}$, $p(e) = \tfrac{1}{8} + \tfrac{1}{8} =
\tfrac{1}{4}$.

**Step 2: label given $T'$.** Given $a$ or $b$: $Y = 0$, certain.
Given $e$: both merged columns carry $Y = 1$, so $Y = 1$, certain.
Hence $H(Y \mid T') = 0$.

**Step 3: subtract.**
$I(T';Y) = H(Y) - 0 \approx 0.811$ bits $= I(X;Y)$: equality. $T'$ is
a sufficient statistic for $Y$.

**Compression check.** $T'$ is strictly coarser than $X$:
$H(T') = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{4}
\cdot 2 = 1.5$ bits, against $H(X) = 1.75$. The merge discarded a
quarter bit of $X$, all of it irrelevant to $Y$.
</Example>

<Remark title="sufficiency is relative to the target">
$T'$ is sufficient for this $Y$. For the target $X$ itself the same
merge is lossy: $I(T';X) = H(T') = 1.5$ bits, below
$I(X;X) = H(X) = 1.75$ bits (self-information,
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>). A statistic sufficient for one
target can destroy information about another; sufficiency is a property
of the pair (statistic, target). Exercise 5 works the comparison.
</Remark>

Classical statistics states the same notion with the target a model
parameter: $T(X)$ is sufficient for a family $p(x;\theta)$ when
$\theta \to T(X) \to X$, i.e. the conditional law of $X$ given $T$ does
not involve $\theta$, and
[Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
§2.9 shows this coincides with the information-preservation condition
$I(\theta; T) = I(\theta; X)$: a sufficient statistic is one at the
DPI's equality case for every prior on $\theta$.

<KeyFact>
Equality in the data-processing inequality is exactly sufficiency: $T$
may be far smaller than $X$, provided the discarded bits are ones $Y$
never touched.
</KeyFact>

## Where the inequality operates next

Three later results are applications of this theorem, each in one
sentence:

- The converse of the
  <Ref lesson="noisy-channel-coding">noisy-channel coding theorem</Ref>
  runs the same machinery through Fano's inequality (Cover & Thomas
  §2.10), turning "information was lost across the chain
  message $\to$ channel $\to$ decoder" into an explicit lower bound on
  decoding error.
- In <Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/>, the reconstruction $\hat{X}$ is
  processed from the compressed description of $X$, so the DPI pins the
  information any reconstruction can retain to what the bit budget let
  through.
- The <Ref lesson="information-bottleneck"/> objective trades $I(X;T)$
  against $I(T;Y)$ over encoders $X \to T$; its entire feasible region
  sits under the ceiling $I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$ established here.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Let $X$ be uniform on $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ and $Y = 1$ if $X \ge 3$, else
$Y = 0$. Consider two merges: $T_1$ maps $\{1,2\} \to s$ and
$\{3,4\} \to t$; $T_2$ maps $\{1,3\} \to u$ and $\{2,4\} \to v$.
Compute $I(X;Y)$, $I(T_1;Y)$, and $I(T_2;Y)$, verify the
data-processing inequality for both merges, and identify which merge is
a sufficient statistic for $Y$.

<Solution>
**$I(X;Y)$.** $Y$ is a function of $X$, so $H(Y \mid X) = 0$;
$p(Y{=}1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so $H(Y) = 1$ bit and $I(X;Y) = 1$ bit.

**$T_1$.** Given $s$, $X \in \{1,2\}$, so $Y = 0$ certain; given $t$,
$Y = 1$ certain. $H(Y \mid T_1) = 0$ and
$I(T_1;Y) = H(Y) = 1$ bit $= I(X;Y)$: equality, so $T_1$ is a
sufficient statistic. The merge respects the $Y$-boundary.

**$T_2$.** Given $u$, $X$ is $1$ or $3$ with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$
each, so $p(Y{=}1 \mid u) = \tfrac{1}{2}$; likewise given $v$. Every
slice of $Y$ is a fair coin: $H(Y \mid T_2) = 1$ bit, so
$I(T_2;Y) = 1 - 1 = 0$ bits.

**DPI check.** $1 \le 1$ and $0 \le 1$: both merges obey the bound.
$T_2$ crosses the $Y$-boundary in both merged pairs and destroys all
information about the label while keeping a full bit of $X$
($H(T_2) = 1$): how many bits survive and which bits survive are
different questions.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the lesson's table with the lossy merge $T$ ($b, c \to m$), compute
$I(Y;\, X, T)$ directly, then verify both chain-rule expansions
numerically: $I(Y;X) + I(Y;T \mid X)$ and $I(Y;T) + I(Y;X \mid T)$.

<Solution>
**Direct.** $T = f(X)$, so the pair $(X,T)$ takes the same values as
$X$ with the same probabilities: $H(Y \mid X, T) = H(Y \mid X) = 0$,
and $I(Y;\, X,T) = H(Y) - 0 = H_2(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$ bits.

**$X$-first.** $I(Y;X) \approx 0.811$ bits (lesson, first section).
Given any $X = x$, the value $T = f(x)$ is certain, so every slice of
$I(Y;T \mid X)$ is zero: $I(Y;T \mid X) = 0$. Sum:
$0.811 + 0 = 0.811$ bits.

**$T$-first.** $I(Y;T) \approx 0.467$ bits (worked example). For
$I(Y;X \mid T)$: slices $T = a$ and $T = d$ determine $Y$, contributing
$0$; the slice $T = m$ (probability $\tfrac{3}{8}$) holds
$X \in \{b,c\}$ with conditional probabilities
$(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$, and within the slice $Y$ is determined
by $X$, so the slice's mutual information is
$H_2(\tfrac{1}{3}) \approx 0.918$ bits. Average:
$\tfrac{3}{8} \cdot 0.918 \approx 0.344$ bits. Sum:
$0.467 + 0.344 = 0.811$ bits.

Both expansions match the direct value, and the $T$-first expansion
exhibits the loss as the residual term $I(Y;X \mid T) = 0.344$ bits.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Construct random variables $Y$, $X$, $T$ for which
$I(T;Y) > I(X;Y)$, and state which hypothesis of the data-processing
inequality your construction violates.

<Solution>
Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent fair bits and set $T = Y$.

Then $I(X;Y) = 0$ (independence, the nonnegativity equality case of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>), while
$I(T;Y) = I(Y;Y) = H(Y) = 1$ bit: the "processed" variable carries one
full bit more than $X$.

The violated hypothesis is the Markov condition: here
$p(t \mid x, y) = \mathbf{1}\{t = y\}$ depends on $y$, so
$p(t \mid x, y) \ne p(t \mid x)$ and $Y \to X \to T$ fails. $T$ was
not computed from $X$; it was computed from $Y$ directly. The
inequality bounds processing of the data, not arbitrary side
information.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Let $Y$ be a fair bit, $X = Y \oplus N_1$, and $T = X \oplus N_2$,
where $N_1, N_2$ are independent $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.1)$ flips, also
independent of $Y$. Verify that $Y \to X \to T$ is a Markov chain, then
compute $I(X;Y)$ and $I(T;Y)$ and check the data-processing inequality.
Use $H_2(0.1) \approx 0.469$ and $H_2(0.18) \approx 0.680$.

<Solution>
**Markov.** $T$ is computed from $X$ and the noise $N_2$, which is
independent of $(Y, X)$: $p(t \mid x, y) = p(t \mid x)$, so
$Y \to X \to T$ holds. Randomized processing qualifies.

**$I(X;Y)$.** Given $Y$, $X$ differs from it with probability $0.1$:
$H(X \mid Y) = H_2(0.1) \approx 0.469$ bits. By symmetry $X$ is a fair
bit, $H(X) = 1$. So
$I(X;Y) = 1 - 0.469 = 0.531$ bits (the $q = 0.9$ dial value of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, Exercise 3).

**$I(T;Y)$.** $T = Y \oplus N_1 \oplus N_2$ differs from $Y$ when
exactly one flip fires:
$0.1 \cdot 0.9 + 0.9 \cdot 0.1 = 0.18$. So
$H(T \mid Y) = H_2(0.18) \approx 0.680$, $H(T) = 1$, and
$I(T;Y) = 1 - 0.680 = 0.320$ bits.

**Check.** $0.320 \le 0.531$: the second noisy stage destroyed a
further $0.211$ bits. Cascading channels degrades information at every
stage, exactly as the pipeline corollary requires.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The lesson showed that $T'$ (the merge $c, d \to e$) is a sufficient
statistic for $Y$. Show that the same $T'$ is **not** sufficient when
the target is $X$ itself: compute $I(T';X)$ and $I(X;X)$ and compare.

<Solution>
$T'$ is a function of $X$, so $H(T' \mid X) = 0$ and
$I(T';X) = H(T') - H(T' \mid X) = H(T')$. From the worked example,
$H(T') = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{4}
\cdot 2 = 1.5$ bits.

The benchmark is $I(X;X) = H(X) = 1.75$ bits: a variable shares its
whole entropy with itself (self-information,
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>).

Comparison: $I(T';X) = 1.5 < 1.75 = I(X;X)$. Taking the target to be
$X$, the chain $X \to X \to T'$ gives $I(T';X) \le I(X;X)$ with a
strict $0.25$-bit gap: the merge cannot distinguish $c$ from $d$, and
that distinction is worth
$\tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 1 = 0.25$ bits of $X$. Sufficient for $Y$, lossy
for $X$: sufficiency is relative to the target.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Encode the lesson's source with the prefix code
$a \to 0$, $b \to 10$, $c \to 110$, $d \to 111$ (the optimal code for
$\{\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}\}$ from
<Ref lesson="huffman-by-hand"/>), and let $T$ be the codeword. Compute
the expected codeword length, and prove that $I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$ for
**every** random variable $Y$, using the data-processing inequality
twice.

<Solution>
**Expected length.**
$\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 3 +
\tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 3 = 1.75$ bits per symbol, equal to $H(X)$: the
code is complete and the source probabilities are powers of two, so the
code meets the entropy floor of
<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/> exactly.

**Equality of information.** $T$ is a function of $X$, so
$Y \to X \to T$ holds for any $Y$ and the DPI gives
$I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$. The code is prefix-free, hence uniquely decodable
(<Ref lesson="prefix-codes"/>): there is a decoding function $g$ with
$X = g(T)$. Then $X$ is a function of $T$, so $Y \to T \to X$ also
holds and the DPI gives the reverse bound $I(X;Y) = I(g(T);Y) \le
I(T;Y)$. Both directions together force $I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$.

An invertible map is a sufficient statistic for every target: lossless
compression changes the representation of the data, never its
information content about anything.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For a Markov chain $Y \to X \to T$, show that conditioning on the
processed output can only reduce the dependence between the endpoints:
$$
I(Y;X \mid T) \le I(Y;X).
$$
Then evaluate both sides on the lesson's table with the lossy merge
$T$.

<Solution>
Expand $I(Y;\, X,T)$ both ways, using the Markov property
$I(Y;T \mid X) = 0$ as in the theorem's proof:
$$
I(Y;X) = I(Y;T) + I(Y;X \mid T)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
I(Y;X \mid T) = I(Y;X) - I(Y;T).
$$
Since $I(Y;T) \ge 0$ (nonnegativity of mutual information), the right
side is at most $I(Y;X)$, which is the claim.

On the table: $I(Y;X \mid T) \approx 0.344$ bits and
$I(Y;X) \approx 0.811$ bits, and indeed
$0.344 = 0.811 - 0.467 \le 0.811$. Observing $T$ "explains away"
$0.467$ of the $0.811$ bits of dependence between $X$ and $Y$; only
the part $T$ failed to capture remains.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Entropy extends surprisal from single events to whole distributions: it is
the expected surprisal of one draw. <Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/> fixed
the information content of a single event at $-\log_2 p$, forced by the
requirement that independent events add. That quantity is attached to an
outcome, so a source emitting many different symbols has no single
information content, only one number per symbol it might emit. Averaging
over the source closes the course's first step, *information has a size*:
entropy is the number a source carries, as surprisal is the number an event
carries.

This lesson defines $H(X)$ and computes it, shows that many visibly
different distributions share one entropy value, establishes the range
$0 \le H \le \log_2 n$ with its exact equality conditions, derives the
grouping property that splits entropy along a two-stage choice, and
evaluates the binary entropy function $H(p)$, the curve this course reuses
more often than any other.

## The probability-weighted average of surprisal

A source emits a different outcome on different draws, so it produces a
different surprisal each time. The quantity that describes the source
rather than the draw is the long-run average of those surprisals, and the
weights in that average are not equal.

Take tomorrow's weather with three outcomes: sunny with probability
$\tfrac{1}{2}$, cloudy $\tfrac{1}{4}$, rainy $\tfrac{1}{4}$. "Sunny"
carries $-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ bit, "cloudy" and "rainy" carry $2$ bits
each. The unweighted average of those three values,
$(1 + 2 + 2)/3 \approx 1.67$ bits, counts every outcome once, while half of
all days produce the 1-bit report. Weighting each surprisal by the
frequency of its outcome gives
$H = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2
= 1.5$ bits per day, and it is the weighted figure that a year of weather
reports would actually average out to.

<Definition title="entropy">
The **entropy** of a discrete random variable $X$ with probability mass
function $p$ is
$$
H(X) = \sum_x p(x)\, I(x) = -\sum_x p(x) \log_2 p(x)
     = \mathbb{E}\bigl[-\log_2 p(X)\bigr],
$$
measured in **bits**. Terms with $p(x) = 0$ contribute nothing, by the
convention $0 \log_2 0 = 0$.
</Definition>

That convention is a limit, not an evaluation of $\log_2 0$: as
$p \to 0^{+}$ the surprisal $-\log_2 p$ grows without bound while the weight
$p$ shrinks faster, and the product $-p \log_2 p$ tends to $0$. An outcome
that never occurs contributes nothing to the average of what does occur.

Every term of the sum uses only its own outcome's probability. Entropy
therefore depends on the multiset of probability values alone: renaming or
reordering outcomes leaves $H$ unchanged.

### The three-step computation: surprisal, weight, sum

<Example title="entropy of a four-symbol source">
A source emits one of four symbols with probabilities
$A: \tfrac{1}{2}$, $B: \tfrac{1}{4}$, $C: \tfrac{1}{8}$, $D: \tfrac{1}{8}$.
Compute its entropy.

**Step 1: surprisal of each outcome.**
$I(A) = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ bit,
$I(B) = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{4} = 2$ bits,
$I(C) = I(D) = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{8} = 3$ bits.

**Step 2: weight each surprisal by its probability.**
$\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 = 0.5$, $\;\tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 = 0.5$,
$\;\tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 3 = 0.375$, and again $0.375$ for $D$.

**Step 3: sum.**
$H = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 = 1.75$ bits.

**Bounds check.** The result lies between the smallest surprisal ($1$ bit)
and the largest ($3$ bits), and closer to the small end, because the
outcome with the smallest surprisal carries the largest weight.
</Example>

Dyadic probabilities make every surprisal a whole number of bits. Decimal
probabilities do not, and the arithmetic then runs through
$\log_2 10 \approx 3.322$.

<Example title="entropy from decimal probabilities">
Four symbols with probabilities $0.4$, $0.3$, $0.2$, $0.1$. Each surprisal
is $\log_2 10$ minus the log of the numerator:
$-\log_2 0.4 = 3.322 - 2 = 1.322$ bits,
$-\log_2 0.3 = 3.322 - 1.585 = 1.737$ bits,
$-\log_2 0.2 = 3.322 - 1 = 2.322$ bits,
$-\log_2 0.1 = 3.322$ bits.

Weighting and summing:
$$
H \approx 0.4(1.322) + 0.3(1.737) + 0.2(2.322) + 0.1(3.322)
  = 0.529 + 0.521 + 0.464 + 0.332 = 1.846 \text{ bits.}
$$

The four contributions are nearly equal, which is what a near-uniform
distribution looks like from inside the sum: rising surprisal almost
exactly cancels falling weight.
</Example>

<KeyFact>
Entropy describes the source, not any message it produces: $n$ draws carry
$nH$ bits on average, and a particular sequence can carry far more or far
less. Which sequences stay near the average is the subject of the typical
set in <Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>.
</KeyFact>

## Same entropy, different shape

From a formula that reads every probability in the distribution, one would
expect the output to nearly determine the input: two sources with the same
entropy ought to differ at most by a relabelling of outcomes. They need not
agree even on how many outcomes are possible.

Count the degrees of freedom. A distribution over four outcomes is fixed by
three free numbers, since the fourth is forced by normalization. Requiring
$H = 1.50$ imposes one equation on those three numbers, so the solutions
form a two-dimensional surface inside the set of distributions: a whole
sheet of distributions, not an orbit of relabellings.

<Example title="three distributions at 1.50 bits">
$P_1 = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, 0)$. Three outcomes are
possible and one never occurs:
$H = 0.5(1) + 0.25(2) + 0.25(2) = 1.500$ bits exactly.

$P_2 = (0.445, 0.445, 0.055, 0.055)$. Two heavy outcomes and two rare ones:
$-\log_2 0.445 = 1.168$ and $-\log_2 0.055 = 4.185$, so
$H = 2(0.445)(1.168) + 2(0.055)(4.185) = 1.040 + 0.460 = 1.500$ bits.

$P_3 = (0.64, 0.16, 0.10, 0.10)$. One dominant outcome:
$H = 0.64(0.644) + 0.16(2.644) + 2(0.10)(3.322)
= 0.412 + 0.423 + 0.664 = 1.499$ bits.

The three differ in support size (three outcomes against four), in the
largest probability ($0.50$ against $0.64$), and in the number of outcomes
that carry appreciable mass. The single number $H$ separates none of it.
</Example>

<Remark title="entropy is not the information in a message">
Because entropy is measured in bits and files are measured in bits, it is
commonly read as "the amount of information in a message". It is not: $H$
is a functional of a probability distribution, and a message is one draw
from it. The reading fails because the map runs the wrong way. A
distribution assigns a surprisal to *every* possible message, and entropy
averages them; no single message can be recovered from, or is described by,
that average. The replacement is the one the definition already gives: a
message of $n$ symbols from a source of entropy $H$ costs about $nH$ bits
*in expectation over messages*. The discriminating case is $P_1$ and $P_3$
above. Both sit at $1.50$ bits, but a message from $P_1$ never contains the
symbol $D$ at all, while a message from $P_3$ contains it about a tenth of
the time.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Entropy is many-to-one: it fixes neither the support size nor the
probabilities. That is why every coding result ahead is stated in terms of
$p$ itself and only its *bounds* in terms of $H$.
</KeyFact>

## The extremes: certainty and even spread

The three distributions above sit at $1.50$ bits. The edges of the scale
are the two cases where the weighted average collapses to something
computable by inspection.

**Full concentration.** All probability on one outcome. The only outcome
that ever occurs has surprisal $-\log_2 1 = 0$, so $H = 1 \cdot 0 = 0$
bits; the impossible outcomes contribute nothing by the convention in the
definition. A zero-entropy source delivers nothing per draw.

**Even spread.** With $n$ outcomes at probability $\tfrac{1}{n}$ each,
every outcome has the same surprisal $\log_2 n$, and the average of
identical values is that value: $H = \log_2 n$. A fair coin is the $n = 2$
case at $1$ bit; four equally likely outcomes give $2$ bits.

Between them, moving mass toward one outcome always lowers $H$, and the
mechanism is a race the rare outcomes lose. Halving an outcome's
probability adds only one bit to its surprisal while halving the weight
that surprisal enters with. At $p = 0.05$ an outcome is worth
$-\log_2 0.05 \approx 4.32$ bits, more than twice what any outcome of a
uniform four-way source is worth, but its contribution to the average is
capped at $0.05 \times 4.32 = 0.216$ bits.

## The range theorem: 0 ≤ H ≤ log₂ n

The two extremes just computed are the endpoints of the entire range, and
the conditions under which they are attained are narrower than the
statement of the bounds suggests.

<Theorem title="range of entropy">
Let $X$ be a discrete random variable over an alphabet of $n$ outcomes.
Then
$$
0 \le H(X) \le \log_2 n.
$$
$H(X) = 0$ if and only if $X$ takes a single value with probability 1, and
$H(X) = \log_2 n$ if and only if $X$ is uniform over all $n$ outcomes.

<Proof>
The lower bound is termwise; the upper bound is one application of
[Jensen's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen%27s_inequality)
to the concave function $\log_2$, followed by a check of when Jensen is
tight.

*Lower bound.* For $p(x) \in [0,1]$ each term $-p(x)\log_2 p(x)$ is
non-negative, so $H(X) \ge 0$. A sum of non-negative terms vanishes exactly
when every term does, which requires each $p(x)$ to be $0$ or $1$; for a
probability distribution that means one outcome carries all the mass.

*Upper bound.* Restrict the sum to the **support**, the outcomes with
$p(x) > 0$, and write entropy as an expectation of
$\log_2 \tfrac{1}{p(X)}$. Because $\log_2$ is concave, Jensen's inequality
moves the expectation inside:
$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log_2 \tfrac{1}{p(X)}\right]
\le \log_2 \mathbb{E}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{p(X)}\right]
= \log_2 \sum_{x:\, p(x) > 0} p(x) \cdot \tfrac{1}{p(x)}
= \log_2 |\mathrm{supp}(p)| \le \log_2 n.
$$
The middle equality is where the argument becomes general: the sum counts
one unit for each supported outcome regardless of what its probability is.

*Equality.* $\log_2$ is strictly concave, so Jensen is tight only when
$\tfrac{1}{p(X)}$ takes a single value with probability 1, that is, when
$p$ is uniform *on its support*. The second inequality is tight only when
the support is the whole alphabet. Both are needed for $H = \log_2 n$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof produces a sharper statement than the theorem quotes. What Jensen
bounds is $\log_2$ of the support size; the alphabet enters only in the
last step, and only as an upper bound on the support.

<Example title="the ceiling of a distribution with an impossible symbol">
$P_1 = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, 0)$ from the previous
section lives on a four-symbol alphabet, so the theorem caps it at
$\log_2 4 = 2$ bits. The proof caps it at $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits,
because $|\mathrm{supp}(P_1)| = 3$.

The sharper cap is the attainable one. Every distribution with this support
has the form $(a, b, c, 0)$, and the first Jensen step is tight only at
$a = b = c = \tfrac{1}{3}$, giving $H = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits. No
choice of $a, b, c$ reaches $2$ bits, because the second inequality of the
proof is strict whenever the support is smaller than the alphabet.

So $P_1$ at $1.500$ bits sits $0.085$ bits under its real ceiling and
$0.500$ under its nominal one. Reporting the gap against $\log_2 4$ would
overstate by a factor of six how much room the source has left.
</Example>

<KeyFact>
The ceiling is set by the support, not by the declared alphabet: a source
over 256 byte values that only ever emits 10 of them is capped at
$\log_2 10 \approx 3.32$ bits per symbol, not 8.
</KeyFact>

<Aside>
A second proof of the upper bound, two lines long, is available once
relative entropy exists. For the uniform $u(x) = 1/n$,
$$
D(p \,\|\, u) = \sum_x p(x) \log_2 \frac{p(x)}{1/n} = \log_2 n - H(X),
$$
and $D \ge 0$ with equality only at $p = u$. That is Cover & Thomas's
Theorem 2.6.4, and it is worth returning to after
<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/> defines $D$: the same inequality then also
prices what a wrong distribution costs, which the Jensen argument does not.
</Aside>

expected number of yes/no questions, needed to identify one draw. The
$1.75$ bits of the four-symbol example is not an average of a convenient
quantity but a code length no encoder beats.
</Remark>

## The grouping property: entropy splits along a two-stage choice

The range theorem bounds $H$ but relates no two distributions to each
other. One relation does, and it is the one every later argument about
codes leans on: revealing an outcome in two stages costs the same total as
revealing it in one.

<Example title="Shannon's three-way choice, split in two">
Take $p = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{6})$. Directly,
$$
H = \tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{1}{3}(1.585) + \tfrac{1}{6}(2.585)
  = 0.500 + 0.528 + 0.431 = 1.459 \text{ bits.}
$$

Now reveal the outcome in two stages. First ask whether the first outcome
occurred: a fair binary question, worth $H(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 1$ bit. If the
answer is no, which happens half the time, a second question separates the
remaining two, whose probabilities conditioned on "no" are
$\tfrac{1/3}{1/2} = \tfrac{2}{3}$ and $\tfrac{1/6}{1/2} = \tfrac{1}{3}$,
carrying $H(\tfrac{2}{3}) = 0.918$ bits.

The second stage runs only half the time, so it is charged at half weight:
$$
1 + \tfrac{1}{2}(0.918) = 1 + 0.459 = 1.459 \text{ bits,}
$$
the direct value.
</Example>

The general form runs in the merging direction. Collapse two outcomes into
one and entropy drops by exactly the entropy of the choice you stopped
making, charged at the weight of the merged group.

<Theorem title="grouping property">
Let $p$ be a distribution containing two outcomes of probability $p_1$ and
$p_2$, and let $p'$ be the distribution obtained by replacing them with a
single outcome of probability $s = p_1 + p_2$, all other probabilities
unchanged. Then
$$
H(p) = H(p') + s\, H\!\left(\tfrac{p_1}{s}\right),
$$
where $H(\cdot)$ on a single argument is the binary entropy function of the
next section.
</Theorem>

Applied to the weather distribution, merging "cloudy" and "rainy" into
"not sunny" produces $(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$ with $H = 1$ bit, and
the term dropped is $s\,H(p_1/s) = \tfrac{1}{2}H(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 0.5$ bits.
The original $1.5$ bits is recovered exactly. The proof is three lines of
algebra and is left as an exercise below.

<KeyFact>
Entropy does not depend on how the outcome is revealed: any sequence of
questions that eventually identifies it distributes the same total $H$
across its stages. That is what makes a code tree's expected depth
comparable with $H$ at all (<Ref lesson="prefix-codes"/>), and it is the
invariant the Huffman merge step preserves (<Ref lesson="huffman-by-hand"/>).
</KeyFact>

## ★ Shannon's uniqueness theorem: three requirements force the formula

Entropy was introduced above as the average of a quantity already derived.
It can also be derived from scratch, from requirements on what an
uncertainty measure should do, with no mention of surprisal. The core path
does not use this result; it is here because it explains why no competing
formula is in circulation.

<Theorem title="uniqueness of H (Shannon 1948, Appendix 2)">
Suppose $H(p_1, \ldots, p_n)$ is (1) continuous in the $p_i$, (2)
monotonically increasing in $n$ when all $p_i = 1/n$, and (3) satisfies the
grouping property of the previous section. Then
$$
H(p_1, \ldots, p_n) = -K \sum_{i=1}^n p_i \log p_i
$$
for some constant $K > 0$.

<Proof>
Write $A(n) = H(\tfrac{1}{n}, \ldots, \tfrac{1}{n})$. Grouping decomposes a
choice among $s^m$ equally likely outcomes into $m$ successive choices
among $s$, so $A(s^m) = m A(s)$, and likewise $A(t^n) = n A(t)$.

Fix $t$ and $s$, take $n$ arbitrarily large, and pick $m$ with
$s^m \le t^n < s^{m+1}$. Taking logarithms and dividing by $n \log s$:
$$
\frac{m}{n} \le \frac{\log t}{\log s} < \frac{m}{n} + \frac{1}{n}.
$$
Requirement (2) makes $A$ increasing, so the same sandwich holds for it:
$m A(s) \le n A(t) \le (m+1) A(s)$, hence
$\left| \tfrac{m}{n} - \tfrac{A(t)}{A(s)} \right| \le \tfrac{1}{n}$.
Both $\log t / \log s$ and $A(t)/A(s)$ lie within $1/n$ of $m/n$ for every
$n$, so they are equal: $A(t) = K \log t$.

For rational $p_i = n_i / \sum_j n_j$, group a uniform choice among
$\sum_j n_j$ outcomes into $n$ groups of sizes $n_i$. Grouping gives
$K \log \sum_j n_j = H(p) + K \sum_i p_i \log n_i$, which rearranges to
$H(p) = -K \sum_i p_i \log p_i$. Requirement (1) extends the result from
rational to all $p_i$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The constant $K$ is the choice of logarithm base, which is the choice of
unit. Shannon states the theorem and then declines to lean on it: the
assumptions "are in no way necessary for the present theory", and "the real
justification of these definitions... will reside in their implications."
The implication he means arrives in <Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>,
where $H$ turns out to be a code length rather than a plausible average.

## The binary entropy function

Entropy has been evaluated one distribution at a time. For two outcomes the
whole family collapses to a single curve, because a two-outcome
distribution is determined by one number: the probability $p$ of one
outcome fixes the other at $1 - p$.

<Example title="entropy of a 90/10 coin">
A coin lands heads with probability $0.9$. The two surprisals are
$-\log_2 0.9 \approx 0.152$ bits and $-\log_2 0.1 \approx 3.322$ bits, so
$$
H = 0.9(0.152) + 0.1(3.322) = 0.137 + 0.332 = 0.469 \text{ bits.}
$$

The rare outcome supplies more than twice as much of the total as the
common one ($0.332$ against $0.137$ bits) despite occurring a ninth as
often: each occurrence carries $3.32$ bits, enough to outweigh its
frequency. A fair coin delivers $1$ bit per flip, so a 90/10 bias has
already cost more than half of it.
</Example>

<Exercise>
Compute $H(0.75)$ to three decimals, using $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$. Then
state, without computing anything further, the value of $H(0.25)$ and the
property of entropy that decides it.

<Solution>
Surprisals: $-\log_2 0.75 = 2 - \log_2 3 \approx 0.415$ bits and
$-\log_2 0.25 = 2$ bits. Weighted:
$0.75(0.415) + 0.25(2) = 0.311 + 0.500 = 0.811$ bits.

$H(0.25) = 0.811$ bits as well. The distribution $(0.25, 0.75)$ is
$(0.75, 0.25)$ with the two outcomes renamed, and entropy depends only on
the multiset of probability values, so relabelling cannot change it.

Check the value against the two computed neighbours: $0.811$ lies between
the fair coin's $1$ bit and the 90/10 coin's $0.469$, as it must, since
$0.75$ lies between $0.5$ and $0.9$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Definition title="binary entropy function">
The **binary entropy function** is
$$
H(p) = -p \log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2 (1-p), \qquad p \in [0, 1],
$$
the entropy of a source with two outcomes of probabilities $p$ and $1-p$
(a Bernoulli($p$) source). The convention $0 \log_2 0 = 0$ gives
$H(0) = H(1) = 0$. A scalar argument distinguishes it from the entropy
$H(X)$ of a random variable.
</Definition>

### Three properties, all forced by earlier results

- **Endpoints.** $H(0) = H(1) = 0$: a coin that always lands the same way
  is the certain source of the range theorem.
- **Maximum.** $H(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 1$ bit, attained only at
  $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$: the $n = 2$ case of the range theorem, ceiling
  $\log_2 2 = 1$.
- **Symmetry.** $H(p) = H(1-p)$: substituting $1-p$ for $p$ swaps the two
  terms. This is relabelling invariance, so $H(0.1) = H(0.9) = 0.469$ bits.

### Steep at the ends, flat in the middle

The asymmetry between the middle and the ends is quantitative, and it is
where the curve stops behaving like a smooth interpolation. The derivative
is
$$
H'(p) = \log_2 \frac{1-p}{p},
$$
which grows without bound as $p \to 0$ or $p \to 1$: the curve leaves both
endpoints vertically, so near certainty $H$ changes faster than any
multiple of the change in $p$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/binary-entropy-zoom.svg"
  alt="Zoom of the binary entropy curve on p between 0.9 and 1: the curve dives to zero at p = 1, where its tangent is vertical, with the point H(0.99) ≈ 0.081 bits marked"
  caption="H(p) near p = 1: the tangent turns vertical at the endpoint, and one percentage point from certainty already carries H(0.99) ≈ 0.081 bits."
/>

Numerically: $H(0.99) \approx 0.081$ bits, eight times the one-point shift
in $p$ that produced it. At the other end $H(0.51) \approx 0.9997$ bits,
within $0.0003$ of the maximum, for the same one-point shift.

<KeyFact>
Moving one percentage point off certainty buys $0.081$ bits; moving one
point off fairness costs $0.0003$. The same asymmetry later prices a 1%
channel error rate at 8% of a bit lost per transmission, since a binary
symmetric channel's capacity is $1 - H(p)$
(<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>).
</KeyFact>

This curve is the most reused object in the course. It returns as the
capacity of the binary symmetric channel, $C = 1 - H(p)$, and as the
rate-distortion function of a Bernoulli source, $R(D) = H(p) - H(D)$
(<Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/>). Both are read off the same inverted U.

## Entropies of common sources

Every source in this course gets measured against its own $\log_2 n$
ceiling, because the distance to that ceiling is what a compressor has to
work with. For uniform sources the distance is zero and there is nothing to
win.

<Figure
  float="right"
  width="s"
  src="./figures/ceiling.svg"
  alt="Bars for a fair coin (1 bit), fair die (2.585 bits), English letters (4.11 bits, below their 4.75-bit ceiling), and a uniform byte (8 bits), each with a dashed log₂ n ceiling"
  caption="Uniform sources touch the log₂ n ceiling; English letters sit 0.64 bits below theirs."
/>

| Distribution | $H$ | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fair coin | $1$ bit | $n = 2$ |
| Fair die | $\log_2 6 \approx 2.585$ bits | between four outcomes ($2$ bits) and eight ($3$ bits) |
| Uniform random byte | $\log_2 256 = 8$ bits | a random byte delivers exactly what a byte can store |
| Shuffled 52-card deck (order) | $\log_2 52! \approx 225.58$ bits | about $28$ bytes to specify one shuffle |
| English letters (monograms) | $\approx 4.11$ bits/char | below the $\log_2 27 \approx 4.75$ ceiling |

<Example title="the entropy of a shuffled deck, computed">
All $52!$ orders of a deck are equally likely, so the distribution is
uniform and $H = \log_2 52!$ by the even-spread case. The factorial is too
large to evaluate directly, and two routes give the number.

**By decomposition.** A particular order has probability
$\tfrac{1}{52}\cdot\tfrac{1}{51}\cdots\tfrac{1}{1}$, since each successive
card is uniform over those remaining. Surprisals of independent successive
choices add, so the order's surprisal is
$\log_2 52 + \log_2 51 + \cdots + \log_2 1$, and since every order has the
same surprisal, the entropy equals it.

**By Stirling.** $\ln n! \approx n \ln n - n + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi n)$.
With $n = 52$ and $\ln 52 = 3.9512$:
$$
\ln 52! \approx 52(3.9512) - 52 + \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(326.7)
        = 205.46 - 52 + 2.89 = 156.35,
$$
so $H = 156.35 / \ln 2 = 156.35 / 0.6931 \approx 225.6$ bits. Naming one
shuffle therefore costs about $28$ bytes, which is why a 32-byte random
seed is more than enough to address every shuffle a card program will ever
produce.
</Example>

The letters of English are the standard non-uniform case. Over the
27-symbol alphabet a–z plus space, the single-character (*monogram*)
frequencies of English give
$H \approx 4.11$ bits per character against the uniform ceiling
$\log_2 27 \approx 4.75$: the skew toward space and *e* leaves $0.64$ bits
per character unused by the source.

That $4.11$ is not the entropy of English. It is the entropy of a source
that draws letters independently from English frequencies, which produces
strings like `ocro hli rgwr`. Real text carries far less per letter once
context is charged, and <Ref lesson="entropy-of-english"/> measures how
much less.

<KeyFact>
A fixed-width encoding spends $\lceil \log_2 n \rceil$ bits per symbol
whatever the distribution; entropy is what a distribution-aware code can
spend instead. The $0.64$-bit gap is the entire prize available to
letter-frequency compression of English (<Ref lesson="huffman-by-hand"/>),
and it is small next to what context yields.
</KeyFact>

<Remark title="units">
Base 2 is a unit choice here exactly as it was for surprisal
(<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>, where the base fixed bits against nats
and hartleys). Natural logarithms give
$H_{\text{nats}} = -\sum_x p(x) \ln p(x) = H_{\text{bits}} \cdot \ln 2$, so
one nat is $1/\ln 2 \approx 1.443$ bits and the weather source's $1.5$ bits
is $1.04$ nats. Libraries that compute entropies and log-losses with
natural logarithms report nats, which is the commonest source of a factor
of $0.693$ in a reproduced number.
</Remark>

Established: entropy is the probability-weighted average surprisal of one
draw, it lies in $[0, \log_2 k]$ for a support of size $k$ with equality
only at a point mass and at the uniform distribution, it is invariant under
relabelling and splits along a two-stage choice, and for two outcomes it is
the curve $H(p)$. All of it concerns one variable observed in isolation.
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/> takes up two variables at
once, where observing one changes the distribution of the other and the
grouping property generalizes into the chain rule.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Three sources: a fair six-sided die; a loaded die with
$P(1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and each of the other five faces at
$\tfrac{1}{10}$; and a two-outcome source with $p = 0.9$. Order the three
by entropy *before* computing anything, using the range theorem and the
binary curve, then compute all three in bits and check your ordering.

<Solution>
**Ordering by inspection.** The two-outcome source is capped at $1$ bit and
is far from fair, so it is lowest. The fair die attains its ceiling
$\log_2 6$; the loaded die has the same alphabet but is not uniform, so it
sits strictly below the fair die. Predicted order: biased coin, loaded die,
fair die.

**Fair die.** Uniform over six outcomes:
$H = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585$ bits.

**Loaded die.** $H = \tfrac{1}{2}(1) + 5 \cdot \tfrac{1}{10}(\log_2 10)
= 0.5 + 0.5(3.322) = 0.5 + 1.661 = 2.161$ bits.

**Biased coin.** $H(0.9) = 0.469$ bits, computed in the body.

Order: $0.469 < 2.161 < 2.585$, as predicted. The loaded die loses $0.42$
bits to its ceiling; the biased coin loses $0.53$ of a much lower ceiling,
which is why ranking by "how skewed does it look" alone is unreliable
across different alphabet sizes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A distribution over an 8-symbol alphabet assigns probability zero to three
of the symbols. Compute the largest entropy it can attain, and name which
clause of the equality condition in the range theorem's proof produces that
answer instead of $\log_2 8 = 3$ bits. Then state what would have to change
about the distribution for $3$ bits to become attainable.

<Solution>
The support has $8 - 3 = 5$ symbols. Jensen's step in the proof bounds
$H$ by $\log_2 |\mathrm{supp}(p)| = \log_2 5 \approx 2.322$ bits, and that
bound is attained: put $\tfrac{1}{5}$ on each supported symbol, and every
surprisal equals $\log_2 5$, so the average does too.

The clause responsible is the *second* inequality of the upper-bound proof,
$\log_2|\mathrm{supp}(p)| \le \log_2 n$. It is strict whenever the support
is a proper subset of the alphabet, and no choice of probabilities on five
symbols can close it. The first inequality (Jensen itself) is already tight
at the uniform-on-support distribution, so nothing is left to gain there.

For $3$ bits to be attainable the support would have to grow to all eight
symbols, and then uniformity over those eight is additionally required. Both
conditions, not either one.

The move to carry forward: an alphabet size is a declaration, while a
support size is a property of the distribution, and only the second bounds
anything.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the following claim, with numbers: "Adding an outcome to a
distribution always increases its entropy, because there is more to be
uncertain about." Give a counterexample and name the step of the reasoning
that fails.

<Solution>
**Counterexample.** Start from the fair coin $(0.5, 0.5)$ with $H = 1$ bit.
Add a third outcome and redistribute mass as $(0.98, 0.01, 0.01)$:
$$
H = 0.98(0.029) + 2(0.01)(6.644) = 0.029 + 0.133 = 0.161 \text{ bits.}
$$
Three outcomes, one sixth of the entropy of the two-outcome source. Even
the degenerate addition refutes the claim in its "always" form: appending
an outcome of probability zero leaves $H$ at exactly $1$ bit, by
$0\log_2 0 = 0$.

**The failing step.** "More to be uncertain about" is a statement about the
*ceiling*: $n \to n+1$ raises $\log_2 n$ to $\log_2(n+1)$, and the range
theorem does say the maximum attainable entropy rises. It says nothing
about where inside $[0, \log_2 n]$ a particular distribution sits. Entropy
is a function of the probabilities, not of their count, and adding an
outcome necessarily takes mass from the existing ones, which can concentrate
the distribution far faster than the extra symbol spreads it.

The general lesson matches the boundary probe above from the other side: an
alphabet's size bounds entropy and never determines it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Prove the grouping property stated in the body: if two outcomes of
probability $p_1$ and $p_2$ are merged into one of probability
$s = p_1 + p_2$, entropy drops by exactly $s\,H(p_1/s)$, where $H(\cdot)$
is the binary entropy function. Then use it twice to recompute the entropy
of $\{\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}\}$ without
evaluating any logarithm beyond $\log_2 2$.

<Solution>
**Proof.** Every term of $H$ except those for $p_1$ and $p_2$ is identical
in the two distributions, so the difference is
$$
\Delta = \bigl[-p_1 \log_2 p_1 - p_2 \log_2 p_2\bigr]
       - \bigl[-s \log_2 s\bigr].
$$
Write $p_1 = sa$ and $p_2 = s(1-a)$ with $a = p_1/s$. Then
$-p_1\log_2 p_1 = -sa(\log_2 s + \log_2 a)$ and
$-p_2\log_2 p_2 = -s(1-a)(\log_2 s + \log_2(1-a))$. The two
$\log_2 s$ pieces carry coefficients $sa$ and $s(1-a)$, which sum to $s$:
$$
\Delta = -s\log_2 s - s\bigl[a\log_2 a + (1-a)\log_2(1-a)\bigr] + s\log_2 s
       = s\,H(a).
$$
The $-s\log_2 s$ terms cancel, leaving exactly the binary entropy of the
split, weighted by the mass being split. The step where the argument
becomes general is the factorization $p_i = s \cdot (p_i/s)$, which needs
only $s > 0$.

**Applied twice.** Merge $C$ and $D$ (both $\tfrac{1}{8}$) into one outcome
of mass $\tfrac{1}{4}$:
$$
H\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac18,\tfrac18\bigr)
= H\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac14\bigr)
+ \tfrac14 H\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)
= H\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac14\bigr) + 0.25.
$$
Merge the two $\tfrac{1}{4}$ outcomes into one of mass $\tfrac{1}{2}$:
$$
H\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac14,\tfrac14\bigr)
= H\bigl(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\bigr) + \tfrac12 H\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr)
= 1 + 0.5 = 1.5.
$$
Total: $1.5 + 0.25 = 1.75$ bits, matching the worked example. Only
$H(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 1$ was ever evaluated.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Pick any text of at least a few hundred words that you have in a file.
Case-fold it and strip everything but letters and spaces. Compute (a) the
entropy of its single-character distribution and (b) the entropy of its
word-length distribution. Report both in bits, state which is larger, and
say what a difference of half a bit between two such numbers would and
would not tell you about the text.

<Solution>
**A worked instance.** On the Gettysburg Address (272 words, 1,420
characters after folding to lowercase and reducing every run of
non-letters to a single space):

- Character distribution: 24 distinct symbols occur (23 letters plus
  space; *j*, *q*, *x* and *z* never appear). $H \approx 3.97$ bits per
  character, against a ceiling of $\log_2 24 \approx 4.58$.
- Word-length distribution: lengths 1 through 11 occur, with 3-letter words
  commonest (60 of 272). $H \approx 2.91$ bits per word, against a ceiling
  of $\log_2 11 \approx 3.46$.

The character entropy is larger, by $1.06$ bits. Your text will differ:
short samples have too few counts per symbol for the plug-in estimate to
settle, and the $3.97$ here is below the usual $\approx 4.11$ for English
partly because four letters are simply absent from this sample.

**What half a bit would tell you.** Only a per-symbol cost, within one
distribution and one alphabet: a code built on this word-length
distribution would average about half a bit less per word than a code built
on some comparison distribution.

**What it would not tell you.** Nothing about which stream is harder to
predict overall, because the two are counted in different units over
different alphabets: 1,420 characters against 272 words. Multiplying
through, the characters carry $1420 \times 3.97 \approx 5{,}640$ bits and
the word lengths $272 \times 2.91 \approx 790$, and the second is not a
smaller description of the same thing but a description of much less.
Neither number is the entropy of English: both treat successive symbols as
independent draws, which <Ref lesson="entropy-of-english"/> shows is off by
a factor of about four.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For $\{A: \tfrac{1}{2}, B: \tfrac{1}{4}, C: \tfrac{1}{8}, D: \tfrac{1}{8}\}$,
assign each symbol $x$ a codeword whose length $\ell(x)$ in bits is its
surprisal $-\log_2 p(x)$. Write out the four lengths, then compute
$\sum_x 2^{-\ell(x)}$ over the four codewords. Report the value, and say
what would have to be true of a distribution for this sum to come out below
$1$.

<Solution>
Lengths: $\ell(A) = 1$, $\ell(B) = 2$, $\ell(C) = \ell(D) = 3$.

$$
\sum_x 2^{-\ell(x)} = 2^{-1} + 2^{-2} + 2^{-3} + 2^{-3}
= 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.125 + 0.125 = 1.
$$

The sum is exactly $1$, and not by accident: $\ell(x) = -\log_2 p(x)$ makes
$2^{-\ell(x)} = p(x)$ term by term, so the sum is the total probability.
Any distribution gives $1$ under this assignment.

The sum falls below $1$ only when some codeword is *longer* than its
surprisal, which is what happens as soon as lengths are rounded up to whole
numbers: $-\log_2 p$ is an integer only for dyadic probabilities, and every
rounding up halves that symbol's share of the sum or more.

This sum has a name and a meaning. It is the Kraft budget: a codeword of
length $\ell$ claims $2^{-\ell}$ of a total of one, a set of lengths is
realizable by a decodable code exactly when the sum is at most $1$, and the
computation above shows the ideal lengths spend the budget precisely to the
last unit. See <Ref lesson="kraft-inequality"/>, and
<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/> for why that exactness is the
reason $H$ is a floor.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

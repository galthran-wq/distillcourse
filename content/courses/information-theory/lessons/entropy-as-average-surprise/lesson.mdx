Entropy extends surprisal from single events to whole distributions: it is
the expected surprisal of one draw. This lesson defines the entropy $H(X)$
of a discrete distribution, computes it in three steps, establishes its
range from $0$ at certainty to $\log_2 n$ at the uniform distribution, and
evaluates it for the two-outcome family and for several standard sources.

## The probability-weighted average of surprisal

Consider tomorrow's weather with three outcomes: sunny with probability
$\tfrac{1}{2}$, cloudy $\tfrac{1}{4}$, rainy $\tfrac{1}{4}$. Surprisal
assigns each outcome a value on its own: "sunny" carries
$-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ bit, "cloudy" and "rainy" carry $2$ bits each.
The quantity of interest is the average information per report over a long
run of days.

The unweighted average of the three surprisals,
$(1 + 2 + 2)/3 \approx 1.67$ bits, is not that quantity: it counts every
outcome equally, while half of all days produce the 1-bit report. The
long-run average weights each surprisal by the frequency of its outcome:
$H = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 = 1.5$
bits per day.

<Figure
  src="./figures/weighted-average.svg"
  alt="Three weather outcomes with probability and surprisal bars, combined into the probability-weighted sum H = 1/2·1 + 1/4·2 + 1/4·2 = 1.5 bits"
  caption="Entropy: each outcome's surprisal weighted by its probability."
/>

This computation defines entropy: weight each outcome's surprisal by its
probability, then sum.

<Definition title="entropy">
The **entropy** of a discrete random variable $X$ with probability mass
function $p$ is
$$
H(X) = \sum_x p(x)\, I(x) = -\sum_x p(x) \log_2 p(x),
$$
measured in **bits**. Terms with $p(x) = 0$ contribute nothing, by the
convention $0 \log_2 0 = 0$ (justified by $p \log_2 p \to 0$ as
$p \to 0$).
</Definition>

### Expected surprisal: no new primitive

Entropy is the expected value of the surprisal defined in
<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>: in expectation notation,
$H(X) = \mathbb{E}[I(X)] = \mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p(X)]$, the mean surprisal
of the outcome that occurred.
[Shannon's 1948 paper](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf)
(§6) introduces exactly this sum as the measure of a source's uncertainty.

Each term of the sum uses only the outcome's probability, so $H$ depends on
the multiset of probability values alone: relabeling or reordering the
outcomes leaves entropy unchanged. This invariance returns below as the
symmetry of the binary entropy function.

<KeyFact>
Entropy is the probability-weighted average of surprisal,
$H(X) = \mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p(X)]$: the frequent outcomes dominate the
average, however small their surprisal.
</KeyFact>

### The three-step computation: surprisal, weight, sum

<Example title="entropy of a four-symbol source">
A source emits one of four symbols with probabilities
$A: \tfrac{1}{2}$, $B: \tfrac{1}{4}$, $C: \tfrac{1}{8}$, $D: \tfrac{1}{8}$.
Compute its entropy.

**Step 1: surprisal of each outcome.**
$I(A) = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ bit,
$I(B) = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{4} = 2$ bits,
$I(C) = I(D) = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{8} = 3$ bits.

**Step 2: weight each surprisal by its probability.**
$\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 = 0.5$, $\;\tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 = 0.5$,
$\;\tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 3 = 0.375$, and again $0.375$ for $D$.

**Step 3: sum.**
$H = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 = 1.75$ bits.

**Bounds check.** The result lies between the smallest surprisal ($1$ bit)
and the largest ($3$ bits), closer to the small end: the outcome with the
smallest surprisal carries the largest weight.
</Example>

  caption="The three-step computation on {½, ¼, ⅛, ⅛}: probabilities, then surprisals, then the weighted sum H = 1.75 bits."
/>

<Task>
Step through the three frames. In frame 3, check which outcomes contribute
most to $H$: $A$ and $B$ tie at $0.5$ bits each, while the two rarest
outcomes contribute only $0.375$ bits apiece.
</Task>

Probabilities in practice are decimals rather than powers of two; the same
three steps apply, with $\log_2 10 \approx 3.322$ converting decimal
probabilities to bits.

<Example title="entropy from decimal probabilities">
A source emits four symbols with probabilities $0.4$, $0.3$, $0.2$, $0.1$.
Compute its entropy to three decimals, using $\log_2 10 \approx 3.322$ and
$\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$.

**Step 1: surprisals.**
$-\log_2 0.4 = \log_2 10 - \log_2 4 \approx 3.322 - 2 = 1.322$ bits;
$-\log_2 0.3 = \log_2 10 - \log_2 3 \approx 3.322 - 1.585 = 1.737$ bits;
$-\log_2 0.2 = \log_2 10 - 1 \approx 2.322$ bits;
$-\log_2 0.1 = \log_2 10 \approx 3.322$ bits.

**Step 2: weight each surprisal by its probability.**
$0.4 \cdot 1.322 \approx 0.529$; $\;0.3 \cdot 1.737 \approx 0.521$;
$\;0.2 \cdot 2.322 \approx 0.464$; $\;0.1 \cdot 3.322 \approx 0.332$.

**Step 3: sum.**
$H \approx 0.529 + 0.521 + 0.464 + 0.332 = 1.846$ bits.

**Bounds check.** The value lies below the four-outcome maximum
$\log_2 4 = 2$ bits established in the next section, and not far below it:
the distribution is close to uniform.
</Example>

## The range of entropy: 0 to log₂ n

For a distribution over $n$ outcomes, $H$ ranges from $0$ to $\log_2 n$.
Both extremes are worth computing directly before the general result.

### The extremes: certainty and even spread

The minimum is full concentration: all probability on a single outcome.
The only outcome that ever occurs has surprisal $-\log_2 1 = 0$, so
$H = 1 \cdot 0 = 0$ bits; the impossible outcomes contribute nothing by
the convention in the definition. A zero-entropy source delivers no
information per draw.

The maximum is even spread. With $n$ outcomes at probability $\tfrac{1}{n}$
each, every outcome has the same surprisal $\log_2 n$, and the average of
identical values is that value: $H = \log_2 n$. The fair coin is the
two-outcome case, $\log_2 2 = 1$ bit; four equally likely outcomes give
$\log_2 4 = 2$ bits.

No four-outcome distribution exceeds $2$ bits: concentrating mass lowers
the surprisal of the frequent outcomes, and the larger surprisal of the
rare outcomes enters the average with weights too small to compensate.

### The theorem: Jensen's inequality does the work

The upper bound is one application of
[Jensen's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen%27s_inequality)
for concave functions; the argument below follows
[Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X),
Theorem 2.6.4.

<Theorem title="range of entropy">
Let $X$ be a discrete random variable over $n$ outcomes. Then
$$
0 \le H(X) \le \log_2 n.
$$
Moreover $H(X) = 0$ if and only if $X$ takes a single value with
probability 1, and $H(X) = \log_2 n$ if and only if $X$ is uniform over
the $n$ outcomes.

<Proof>
Sketch; Jensen's inequality for concave functions is used without proof.

*Lower bound.* Each term $-p(x)\log_2 p(x)$ is non-negative for
$p(x) \in [0,1]$, so $H(X) \ge 0$. The sum vanishes exactly when every
term does, that is, when every $p(x)$ is $0$ or $1$; for a probability
distribution this means a single outcome carries probability 1.

*Upper bound.* Restrict the sum to outcomes with $p(x) > 0$ and write
entropy as an expectation: $H(X) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\log_2 \tfrac{1}{p(X)}\right]$.
The logarithm is concave, so Jensen's inequality gives
$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log_2 \tfrac{1}{p(X)}\right]
\le \log_2 \mathbb{E}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{p(X)}\right]
= \log_2 \sum_{x:\, p(x) > 0} p(x) \cdot \tfrac{1}{p(x)}
= \log_2 |\mathrm{supp}(p)| \le \log_2 n.
$$
Because the logarithm is strictly concave, the first inequality is an
equality only when $\tfrac{1}{p(X)}$ is constant, i.e. $p$ is uniform on
its support; equality with $\log_2 n$ then forces the support to be all
$n$ outcomes.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<KeyFact>
Entropy is maximal exactly when you know least: among distributions over
$n$ outcomes, only the uniform one attains $H = \log_2 n$, and only a
point mass attains $0$.
</KeyFact>

<Remark>

<Ref lesson="prefix-codes"/> and <Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>

</Remark>

## The binary entropy function

A distribution over two outcomes is determined by a single number: the
probability $p$ of one outcome fixes the other at $1 - p$. Its entropy is
therefore a function of $p$ alone, and that function recurs throughout
information theory. The concrete case first.

<Example title="entropy of a 90/10 coin">
A biased coin lands heads with probability $0.9$ and tails with probability
$0.1$. Compute the entropy of one flip.

**Step 1: surprisals.**
$I(\text{heads}) = -\log_2 0.9 \approx 0.152$ bits;
$I(\text{tails}) = -\log_2 0.1 \approx 3.322$ bits.

**Step 2: weight each surprisal by its probability.**
$0.9 \cdot 0.152 \approx 0.137$; $\;0.1 \cdot 3.322 \approx 0.332$.

**Step 3: sum.**
$H \approx 0.137 + 0.332 = 0.469$ bits.

**Interpretation.** A fair coin delivers $1$ bit per flip; the 90/10 coin
delivers less than half a bit. The rare outcome contributes more than twice
as much to the average as the frequent one ($0.332$ against $0.137$ bits):
tails occurs in only a tenth of flips, but each occurrence carries $3.32$
bits, enough to outweigh its low frequency.
</Example>

<Definition title="binary entropy function">
The **binary entropy function** is
$$
H(p) = -p \log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2 (1-p), \qquad p \in [0, 1],
$$
the entropy of a source with two outcomes of probabilities $p$ and $1-p$
(a Bernoulli($p$) source). The convention $0 \log_2 0 = 0$ gives
$H(0) = H(1) = 0$. The scalar argument distinguishes it from the entropy
$H(X)$ of a random variable.
</Definition>

The worked example above evaluates one point of this function:
$H(0.9) \approx 0.469$. The full curve is Shannon's fig. 7, recreated
below.

<Figure
  src="./figures/binary-entropy.svg"
  alt="The binary entropy function H(p) on [0,1]: an inverted-U curve, zero at p = 0 and p = 1, maximal at 1 bit for p = 0.5, with the 90/10 coin marked at H(0.9) ≈ 0.47"
  caption="The binary entropy function. Maximum 1 bit at the fair coin; steep near the endpoints."
/>

### Three properties: endpoints, maximum, symmetry

All three are visible in the plot and follow from the definition.

- **Endpoints.** $H(0) = H(1) = 0$: a coin that always lands the same way
  is the certain source of the range theorem and delivers nothing.
- **Maximum.** $H(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 1$ bit, attained only at
  $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$: the $n = 2$ case of the range theorem, with maximum
  $\log_2 2 = 1$.
- **Symmetry.** $H(p) = H(1-p)$: substituting $1-p$ for $p$ swaps the two
  terms of the sum. This is the relabeling invariance noted after the
  definition of entropy: a 90/10 coin and a 10/90 coin differ only in
  which side is called heads, so $H(0.1) = H(0.9) \approx 0.469$ bits.

### Steep at the ends, flat in the middle

The steepness near the endpoints is quantitative. The derivative is
$H'(p) = \log_2 \tfrac{1-p}{p}$, which grows without bound as $p \to 0$
or $p \to 1$: the curve leaves both endpoints vertically.

<Figure
  src="./figures/binary-entropy-zoom.svg"
  alt="Zoom of the binary entropy curve on p between 0.9 and 1: the curve dives to zero at p = 1, where its tangent is vertical, with the point H(0.99) ≈ 0.081 bits marked"
  caption="H(p) near p = 1: the tangent turns vertical at the endpoint, and one percentage point from certainty already carries H(0.99) ≈ 0.081 bits."
/>

Numerically, $H(0.99) \approx 0.081$ bits: moving one percentage point of
mass away from certainty already buys $0.08$ bits of uncertainty. Near the
middle the curve is almost flat: $H(0.51) \approx 0.9997$ bits, within
$0.0003$ of the maximum.

<KeyFact>
Small deviations from certainty add uncertainty fast, small deviations
from fairness change it hardly at all: $H(0.99) \approx 0.081$ bits
against $H(0.51) \approx 0.9997$.
</KeyFact>

## Entropies of common distributions

<Figure
  float="right"
  width="s"
  src="./figures/ceiling.svg"
  alt="Bars for a fair coin (1 bit), fair die (2.585 bits), English letters (4.11 bits, below their 4.75-bit ceiling), and a uniform byte (8 bits), each with a dashed log₂ n ceiling"
  caption="Uniform sources touch the log₂ n ceiling; English letters sit 0.64 bits below theirs."
/>

The uniform case $H = \log_2 n$ covers most distributions named in
everyday computing; the range theorem puts every non-uniform source
strictly below its $\log_2 n$ ceiling.

| Distribution | $H$ | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fair coin | $1$ bit | $n = 2$ |
| Fair die | $\log_2 6 \approx 2.585$ bits | between four outcomes ($2$ bits) and eight ($3$ bits) |
| Uniform random byte | $\log_2 256 = 8$ bits | a random byte delivers exactly what a byte can store |
| Shuffled 52-card deck (order) | $\log_2 52! \approx 225.6$ bits | about $28$ bytes to specify one shuffle |
| English letters (monograms) | $\approx 4.11$ bits/char | non-uniform: below the $\log_2 27 \approx 4.75$ ceiling |

<Aside>
The deck value decomposes. A particular order has probability
$\tfrac{1}{52} \cdot \tfrac{1}{51} \cdots \tfrac{1}{1}$: each successive
card is uniform over those remaining, so the order's surprisal is the sum
$\log_2 52 + \log_2 51 + \dots + \log_2 1 = \log_2 52!$. Every order has
this same surprisal, so the entropy equals it: $225.6$ bits, or about
$28$ bytes to specify one shuffle.
</Aside>

The letters of English text are the standard non-uniform example. Over the
27-symbol alphabet a–z plus space, the single-letter (monogram)
frequencies of English give $H \approx 4.11$ bits per character
([MacKay](https://www.inference.org.uk/itprnn/book.pdf), ch. 2), against
the uniform ceiling $\log_2 27 \approx 4.75$ bits: the skew toward
frequent characters such as space and e costs about $0.64$ bits per
character.

The monogram figure ignores all context between letters;
<Ref lesson="entropy-of-english"/> measures how far context pushes the
per-character entropy below it.

<KeyFact>
Uniform sources attain the $\log_2 n$ ceiling exactly; real sources are
non-uniform and sit strictly below it, English monograms at $4.11$ of a
possible $4.75$ bits.
</KeyFact>

<Remark title="units">
Base 2 is a choice of unit, exactly as it was for surprisal (the units
discussion of <Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/> applies unchanged).
Natural logarithms measure entropy in *nats*; the conversion is the
constant factor $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$.
</Remark>

<Aside>
The conversion in formulas:
$$
H_{\text{nats}} = -\sum_x p(x) \ln p(x) = H_{\text{bits}} \cdot \ln 2,
$$
since $\ln p = \log_2 p \cdot \ln 2$. One nat is
$1/\ln 2 \approx 1.443$ bits. The weather distribution's $1.5$ bits is
$1.5 \ln 2 \approx 1.04$ nats. Libraries that compute entropies and
log-losses with natural logarithms report nats.
</Aside>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A source emits one of five symbols with probabilities
$\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}$.
Compute its entropy, following the three steps of the worked example.

<Solution>
**Step 1: surprisals.** The three outcomes at $\tfrac{1}{4}$ each carry
$-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{4} = 2$ bits; the two at $\tfrac{1}{8}$ each carry
$-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{8} = 3$ bits.

**Step 2: weight by probability.**
$\tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 = 0.5$ for each of the three quarter-probability
outcomes; $\tfrac{1}{8} \cdot 3 = 0.375$ for each of the two
eighth-probability outcomes.

**Step 3: sum.**
$H = 3 \cdot 0.5 + 2 \cdot 0.375 = 1.5 + 0.75 = 2.25$ bits.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For the weather distribution $\{\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}\}$,
the unweighted average of the three surprisals is
$(1 + 2 + 2)/3 \approx 1.67$ bits, while the entropy is $1.5$ bits. State
what the unweighted average gets wrong and why the probability weights fix
it.

<Solution>
The unweighted average treats the three outcomes as equally frequent, but
over a long run of days half of all reports are the 1-bit "sunny" report,
and only a quarter each are the 2-bit reports. The average information per
report is a long-run frequency-weighted average, and the frequency of each
outcome is its probability. Weighting each surprisal $I(x)$ by $p(x)$
yields exactly the expected value
$H = \sum_x p(x) I(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 +
\tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 = 1.5$ bits, pulled below $1.67$ because the
low-surprisal outcome occurs most often.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A source always emits the same symbol out of an alphabet of four. Compute
its entropy directly from the definition, accounting for every term of the
sum.

<Solution>
The certain symbol has $p = 1$ and contributes
$-1 \cdot \log_2 1 = 0$. Each of the three impossible symbols has $p = 0$
and contributes $-0 \cdot \log_2 0 = 0$ by the convention
$0 \log_2 0 = 0$. The sum is $H = 0$ bits: a source whose output is known
in advance delivers no information.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Three distributions over four outcomes:
$P_1 = (\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$,
$P_2 = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8})$,
$P_3 = (0.7, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1)$.
Rank them by entropy using the range theorem, then verify by computing all
three values (two decimals suffice for $P_3$; use
$\log_2 10 \approx 3.32$ and $\log_2 0.7 \approx -0.51$).

<Solution>
Ranking: $P_1$ is uniform, so it attains the four-outcome maximum;
$P_3$ concentrates the most mass on one outcome, so it sits lowest;
$P_2$ lies between. Verification:
$H(P_1) = \log_2 4 = 2$ bits.
$H(P_2) = 1.75$ bits: the worked example.
$H(P_3) = -0.7 \log_2 0.7 - 3 \cdot 0.1 \log_2 0.1
\approx 0.7 \cdot 0.51 + 0.3 \cdot 3.32 \approx 0.36 + 1.00 = 1.36$ bits.
So $H(P_3) < H(P_2) < H(P_1) = 2$, consistent with the theorem: the closer
to uniform, the higher the entropy, and none exceeds $\log_2 4$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
One card is drawn from a shuffled 16-card pile; it is the single marked
card. Compute the surprisal of this event, and the entropy of the draw
when all 16 cards are equally likely. State why the two numbers coincide.

<Solution>
Surprisal of the event: $p = \tfrac{1}{16}$, so
$I = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{16} = \log_2 16 = 4$ bits. Entropy of the uniform
draw: all 16 outcomes have the same surprisal $4$ bits, and the
probability-weighted average of identical values is that value, so
$H = \log_2 16 = 4$ bits. The numbers coincide because under a uniform
distribution every outcome is equally surprising: the average surprisal
equals each individual surprisal.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A coin lands heads with probability $0.75$. Compute the entropy of one
flip to three decimals, following the steps of the 90/10 worked example
(use $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$), then express the result in nats.

<Solution>
**Step 1: surprisals.**
$I(\text{heads}) = -\log_2 0.75 = 2 - \log_2 3 \approx 2 - 1.585 = 0.415$
bits; $I(\text{tails}) = -\log_2 0.25 = 2$ bits.

**Step 2: weight by probability.**
$0.75 \cdot 0.415 \approx 0.311$; $\;0.25 \cdot 2 = 0.5$.

**Step 3: sum.**
$H(0.75) \approx 0.311 + 0.5 = 0.811$ bits.

In nats: $0.811 \cdot \ln 2 \approx 0.811 \cdot 0.693 \approx 0.562$ nats.
The value sits between the fair coin's $1$ bit and the 90/10 coin's
$0.469$ bits, as the plot of $H(p)$ requires: $0.75$ lies between $0.5$
and $0.9$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Show from the definition that the binary entropy function satisfies
$H(p) = H(1-p)$ for every $p \in [0, 1]$, and state the general property
of entropy that this symmetry instantiates.

<Solution>
Substitute $1-p$ for $p$ in the definition:
$$
H(1-p) = -(1-p)\log_2 (1-p) - \bigl(1-(1-p)\bigr)\log_2 \bigl(1-(1-p)\bigr)
= -(1-p)\log_2 (1-p) - p\log_2 p,
$$
which is $H(p)$ with its two terms in the opposite order; addition is
commutative, so $H(1-p) = H(p)$. At $p \in \{0, 1\}$ both sides equal $0$
by the convention $0\log_2 0 = 0$. The general property: entropy depends
only on the collection of probability values, not on which outcome carries
which. The sum $H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x)$ is invariant under
relabeling the outcomes, and swapping heads with tails is a relabeling.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

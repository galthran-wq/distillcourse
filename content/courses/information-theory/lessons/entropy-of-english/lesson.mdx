English text carries roughly one bit of information per letter while ASCII
spends eight; the gap is redundancy, and Shannon's guessing game measures
it. This lesson establishes that number in four steps: the ladder of
conditional entropies $F_N$, which drops as context grows; the *entropy
rate*, defined as its limit; the guessing game of
[Shannon's 1951 paper](https://www.princeton.edu/~wbialek/rome/refs/shannon_51.pdf),
which turns a reader's next-letter guesses into upper and lower bounds on
the rate; and the resulting redundancy of roughly 75%. Throughout, the
alphabet is the 27 symbols a–z plus space, with case and punctuation
dropped; every number below is tied to that choice.

## Context buys bits: the ladder of conditional entropies

The single-letter frequencies of English give an entropy of about $4.1$
bits per character, computed in
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>. That number treats each letter
as an independent draw. English letters are not independent: after the
letter *q*, the next letter is almost always *u*, and the conditional
distribution of the next letter collapses nearly to a point. Its entropy,
$H(X_{n+1} \mid X_n = q)$, is close to zero bits, against $4.1$ without
the context.

Averaging over all contexts is exactly the conditional entropy of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>. Shannon organized these
averages into a sequence: the *N-gram entropy* $F_N$ is the conditional
entropy of the next letter given $N-1$ preceding letters,

$$
F_N = H(X_N \mid X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{N-1}),
$$

with $F_1 = H(X_1)$, the monogram entropy, and $F_0 = \log_2 27 \approx
4.76$ bits by convention: the cost of a letter with no statistics at all.

### Measured rungs: Shannon's 1951 table

From letter, digram, and trigram frequency tables, and from word
frequencies via [Zipf's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipf%27s_law),
Shannon computed the first rungs for the 27-letter alphabet:

| $F_N$ | conditions on | bits per letter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $F_0$ | nothing: 27 equiprobable symbols | $4.76$ |
| $F_1$ | letter frequencies | $4.03$ |
| $F_2$ | one preceding letter (digrams) | $3.32$ |
| $F_3$ | two preceding letters (trigrams) | $3.1$ |
| $F_{\text{word}}$ | word frequencies (Zipf fit) | $2.14$ |

Each added letter of context buys bits: $0.73$ from $F_1$ to $F_2$,
another $0.2$ to $F_3$. The word-level figure comes from an entropy of
$11.82$ bits per word divided by $5.5$ symbols per word (average word
length $4.5$ letters plus one space). The ladder stops at $N = 3$ for a
mechanical reason: an $N$-gram table has $27^N$ entries, and no corpus of
1951 (or a printed table) supported reliable frequencies past trigrams.

The monogram values differ across sources: Shannon's counts give
$F_1 = 4.03$ bits, while the table in
[MacKay](https://www.inference.org.uk/mackay/itila/) §2.1, used in
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>, gives $4.11$. Letter
frequencies are corpus estimates, and every rung inherits their
uncertainty; the shape of the ladder, not the second decimal, is the
result.

<KeyFact>
The $4.1$-bit monogram figure is not the entropy of English: it prices
letters as independent draws and ignores all structure between them. Each
letter of conditioning context strictly lowers the per-letter cost.
</KeyFact>

<Aside>
Shannon's 1948 paper, [§3](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf),
runs the same ladder in the generative direction: sample text *from* each
approximation and watch it approach English. A trigram-level sample reads
"IN NO IST LAT WHEY CRATICT FROURE BIRS GROCID…", and a second-order word
approximation produces "THE HEAD AND IN FRONTAL ATTACK ON AN ENGLISH
WRITER THAT THE CHARACTER OF THIS POINT IS THEREFORE ANOTHER METHOD…":
locally plausible, globally empty. The better a model's sample text looks,
the fewer bits per letter the model needs; the ladder read in either
direction is the same fact.
</Aside>

## The entropy rate: the limit of the ladder

The measured rungs decrease: $4.76, 4.03, 3.32, 3.1, \ldots$ The sequence
cannot decrease forever, since every conditional entropy is at least $0$.
A decreasing sequence bounded below converges; the limit is the number the
opening paragraph promised.

<Definition title="entropy rate">
For a stationary source $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ over a finite alphabet, the
**entropy rate** is
$$
H_{\text{rate}} = \lim_{N \to \infty} H(X_N \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1})
= \lim_{N \to \infty} F_N,
$$
in bits per symbol: the per-letter uncertainty given unlimited preceding
context. For stationary sources this limit exists and equals
$\lim_{N \to \infty} \tfrac{1}{N} H(X_1, \ldots, X_N)$
([Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X),
Theorem 4.2.1); Exercise 3 connects the two forms.
</Definition>

*Stationary* means the statistics do not depend on position: the
distribution of $(X_{k+1}, \ldots, X_{k+n})$ is the same for every shift
$k$. English prose is treated as approximately stationary: letter
statistics in chapter 12 match those in chapter 1.

<Theorem title="the ladder never rises">
For a stationary source, $F_{N+1} \le F_N$ for every $N \ge 1$, and the
limit $H_{\text{rate}}$ exists.

<Proof>
Fix the shorter context $(X_2, \ldots, X_N)$. For each of its values,
adding the observation $X_1$ cannot raise the average uncertainty about
$X_{N+1}$: this is conditioning-reduces-entropy from
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>, applied under each context
value and then averaged over contexts. Hence
$$
F_{N+1} = H(X_{N+1} \mid X_1, \ldots, X_N)
\le H(X_{N+1} \mid X_2, \ldots, X_N).
$$
By stationarity, shifting all indices down by one leaves the right side
unchanged: $H(X_{N+1} \mid X_2, \ldots, X_N) = H(X_N \mid X_1, \ldots,
X_{N-1}) = F_N$. The sequence $(F_N)$ is non-increasing and bounded below
by $0$, so it converges.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

## Shannon's guessing game: the speaker is the model

Extending the table method to $F_{100}$ requires the joint statistics of
$27^{100}$ contexts. No corpus fills that table. What is needed is the
conditional distribution of the next letter given a long context; where it
lives is in the head of every fluent reader, who has absorbed the words,
grammar, and idioms of the language. Shannon's method queries that
implicit model directly.

The protocol: show a subject $N-1$ letters of an unfamiliar text. The
subject guesses the next letter; if wrong, guesses again, until correct.
Record the number of guesses, reveal the letter, advance one position, and
repeat. The output is one *guess rank* per position: a $1$ means the
first guess was right, a $17$ means sixteen wrong guesses came first.

  caption="The guessing game on a five-symbol text. Each position yields one number: how many guesses the correct letter took."

<Task>
Step through the five frames. Check that context is doing the work: the
first position, guessed blind, costs 4 tries, while every position with a
word underway costs 1 or 2. The output of the whole game is only the tally
row: ranks $4, 2, 1, 2, 1$.
</Task>

Shannon's demonstration runs show how strong human prediction is. On a
129-letter passage, his subject guessed 89 of the letters (69%) right on
the first try. In the guess-until-correct variant on a 102-symbol text, 79
positions took one guess, 8 took two, 3 took three, 2 each took four and
five, and only 8 needed more. For the systematic experiment he drew 100
passages from a novel and had the subject play at every context length
$N-1 = 0, 1, \ldots, 14$, and $99$.

### The reduced text: ranks carry the same information

The rank sequence looks like a loss of information; it is not. Suppose a
second subject exists with **exactly the same prediction model**:
Shannon's "identical twin", in practice the same person following a fixed
guessing rule. Given the ranks alone, the twin replays the game: at each
position the twin generates guesses in the same order, and the rank says
which guess is the true letter. The text reconstructs perfectly; Exercise
6 runs this decoding on a toy predictor.

The predictor is therefore an invertible transducer: original text in,
*reduced text* (the rank sequence) out, and back again with the same
predictor. Both directions look only at the current symbol and the $N-1$
preceding text letters, so the transformation preserves $N$-gram
statistics, and the reduced text has the same entropy per symbol as
English itself. What the translation changes is the *shape* of the
frequencies: complicated constraints among letters become a single wildly
skewed distribution over ranks, mass piled on rank 1.

<KeyFact>
Text and rank sequence are two encodings of the same information: given
the predictor, each reconstructs the other. Measuring the ranks measures
English.
</KeyFact>

## Bounds from the guess-rank frequencies

Let $q_i$ denote the fraction of positions resolved on guess $i$ when the
subject knows $N-1$ preceding letters, with $q_1 \ge q_2 \ge \cdots \ge
q_{27}$ and $q_{28} = 0$. For an ideal predictor — one that always guesses
in order of decreasing conditional probability — the rank frequencies pin
$F_N$ between two computable numbers.

<Theorem title="Shannon's guessing bounds">
For the ideal predictor with $N-1$ known letters,
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{27} i\,(q_i - q_{i+1}) \log_2 i
\;\le\; F_N \;\le\;
-\sum_{i=1}^{27} q_i \log_2 q_i .
$$
The upper bound holds for any predictor whose guessing order is a fixed
function of the known context, a human included; the lower bound is proved
only for the ideal predictor.

<Proof>
Sketch of the upper bound. The rank sequence has the same $N$-gram entropy
as the text (previous section). By the ladder theorem, its per-symbol
conditional entropy $F_N$ is at most its $F_1$, and $F_1$ of the rank
sequence is exactly the entropy of the rank distribution,
$-\sum_i q_i \log_2 q_i$: ignoring inter-rank structure can only
overestimate. Any deterministic guesser yields a valid rank encoding, so
the bound needs no ideal predictor.

The lower bound is Shannon's rectangular-decomposition argument
([1951](https://www.princeton.edu/~wbialek/rome/refs/shannon_51.pdf), §5):
among all sources whose ideal-prediction rank frequencies equal $q$, the
least uncertain one splits into blocks of equally probable guesses, and
its entropy is the left-hand sum. Equality requires every context to offer
a set of equally probable next letters; real English does not, which is
one reason the two bounds stay apart.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The coding reading of the upper bound: build a prefix code for the rank
alphabet matched to $q$, per <Ref lesson="prefix-codes"/>. The rank stream
then costs about $-\sum_i q_i \log_2 q_i$ bits per symbol
(<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>), and the rank stream
reconstructs the text. A concentrated guess distribution is a cheap code
for English; the peaked histogram *is* the low entropy.

### Shannon's measured bounds

Applying the bounds to the guess tallies at each context length (100
samples per column, smoothed) gave:

| known letters | 0 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 9 | 14 | 99 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| upper bound | $4.03$ | $3.42$ | $3.0$ | $2.7$ | $2.1$ | $2.1$ | $1.3$ |
| lower bound | $3.19$ | $2.50$ | $2.1$ | $1.7$ | $1.0$ | $1.2$ | $0.6$ |

The zero-context column reproduces the monogram $F_1 = 4.03$ exactly: with
no context the ideal guesser works from letter frequencies alone. At 99
letters of context the estimate lands between $0.6$ and $1.3$ bits per
letter. The bumps along the way (the lower bound rising from $1.0$ to
$1.2$) are sampling error from 100-sample columns, flagged by Shannon
himself.

<Remark title="what the estimate measures">
Three qualifications. The bounds coincide only in idealized limits; a
finite game gives a range, not a value. The upper bound measures the
*predictor's model* of English, which can only overestimate the true rate:
a better predictor produces a more concentrated rank distribution and a
lower bound on the same text.
[Cover and King (1978)](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1055912/)
sharpened the protocol by having subjects bet on their guesses, estimating
approximately $1.3$ bits per symbol. And every figure is relative to the
declared 27-symbol alphabet: restoring case and punctuation changes each
number. <Ref lesson="compression-is-prediction"/> replaces the human with
a trained language model and reads its loss as exactly this game's score.
</Remark>

## A worked estimate from one short game

<Example title="from tally to bounds">
Suppose a 40-position game on an unfamiliar passage produces the tally

| guess rank $i$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| positions | 26 | 6 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| $q_i$ | $.65$ | $.15$ | $.10$ | $.05$ | $.025$ | $.025$ |

**Upper bound: the entropy of the rank distribution.** Surprisals:
$-\log_2 .65 = 0.621$, $-\log_2 .15 = 2.737$, $-\log_2 .10 = 3.322$,
$-\log_2 .05 = 4.322$, $-\log_2 .025 = 5.322$. Weighted:

$$
.65 \times 0.621 + .15 \times 2.737 + .10 \times 3.322
+ .05 \times 4.322 + 2 \times .025 \times 5.322
$$
$$
= 0.404 + 0.411 + 0.332 + 0.216 + 0.266 = 1.63 \text{ bits.}
$$

**Lower bound: $\sum_i i\,(q_i - q_{i+1}) \log_2 i$**, with $q_7 = 0$.
The $i = 1$ term vanishes ($\log_2 1 = 0$), and $i = 5$ vanishes
($q_5 = q_6$):

$$
2(.15 - .10)(1) + 3(.10 - .05)(1.585) + 4(.05 - .025)(2)
+ 6(.025)(2.585)
$$
$$
= 0.100 + 0.238 + 0.200 + 0.388 = 0.93 \text{ bits.}
$$

**Estimate.** The game places the per-letter entropy between $0.93$ and
$1.63$ bits, consistent with Shannon's $0.6$–$1.3$ from a hundred times
the data. Forty positions is a small sample; the bounds carry error bars
of the same order as the interval itself.

**A non-estimate, for contrast.** The average number of guesses is
$(26 \cdot 1 + 6 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 4 + 1 \cdot 5 + 1 \cdot 6)/40
= 69/40 \approx 1.7$ guesses per letter. That number is not an entropy;
only the distribution of ranks, pushed through the bounds, is.
</Example>

<Task>
Run the game yourself, with a partner and a paragraph the guesser has not
read. Use the 27-symbol alphabet: lowercase everything, drop punctuation,
keep spaces. Play 40 positions, tally the ranks into counts, convert to
frequencies $q_i$, and compute both bounds as in the example. Expect an
upper bound between roughly $1$ and $2.5$ bits depending on the text and
the guesser.
</Task>

## Redundancy: what the gap is worth

A raw fixed-width encoding of the 27-symbol alphabet spends $\log_2 27
\approx 4.76$ bits per letter; ASCII spends 8. If the entropy rate is
about $1.2$ bits per letter, the fraction of the raw encoding that carries
no information is

$$
\text{redundancy} = 1 - \frac{H_{\text{rate}}}{\log_2 27}
\approx 1 - \frac{1.2}{4.76} \approx 0.75.
$$

Shannon's statement: long-range statistics reduce the entropy of ordinary
literary English to about one bit per letter, "with a corresponding
redundancy of roughly 75%". His 1948 estimate, using only statistics out
to about eight letters, was $2.3$ bits per letter and roughly 50%
redundancy; the extra 25 points came from the long-range structure only
the guessing game could reach.

By the source coding theorem of
<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>, the entropy rate is the floor
for lossless compression of English, and the redundancy is exactly the
compressible fraction: a perfect compressor would shrink ASCII text by a
factor near $8/1.2 \approx 7$. Reaching the floor requires a coder driven
by a model as good as the human predictor: the subject of
<Ref lesson="compression-is-prediction"/>, and the reason
[MacKay](https://www.inference.org.uk/mackay/itila/) (ch. 6) builds stream
codes around a predictive model.

<KeyFact>
Redundancy $= 1 - H_{\text{rate}} / \log_2 27 \approx 75\%$: roughly
three quarters of printed English is determined by structure rather than
free choice, and that fraction is what lossless compression can remove.
</KeyFact>

<Aside>
Redundancy is why damaged English survives. Shannon's 1948 discussion
notes that a language of zero redundancy would make any letter string
valid text: errors would be undetectable, and (his example) any
two-dimensional letter array would be a valid crossword. English's
redundancy is what lets a reader repair "th_ r__m w_s n_t v_ry l_ght",
and what crossword constructors fight against: too much redundancy and
few grids fill; none, and every grid does. The same margin is what
error-correcting codes will spend deliberately in the channel-coding
module.
</Aside>

<Exercise>
A toy language over the alphabet $\{t, h, e\}$ has adjacent-pair joint
distribution

| $p(x_1, x_2)$ | $x_2 = t$ | $x_2 = h$ | $x_2 = e$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x_1 = t$ | $0$ | $3/8$ | $1/8$ |
| $x_1 = h$ | $0$ | $0$ | $1/4$ |
| $x_1 = e$ | $1/8$ | $1/8$ | $0$ |

Compute the marginal entropy $H(X_2)$ and the conditional entropy
$H(X_2 \mid X_1)$ by the weighted-average form, and state the toy
language's first ladder step $F_1 \to F_2$. Use
$\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$.

<Solution>
**Marginals.** Rows: $p(t) = \tfrac12$, $p(h) = \tfrac14$,
$p(e) = \tfrac14$. Columns: $p(t) = \tfrac18$,
$p(h) = \tfrac38 + \tfrac18 = \tfrac12$,
$p(e) = \tfrac18 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac38$.

**Marginal entropy.**
$H(X_2) = \tfrac18 \cdot 3 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac38 \log_2
\tfrac83 = 0.375 + 0.5 + 0.375 \times 1.415 \approx 1.41$ bits.

**Row entropies.** Given $t$: $(0, \tfrac34, \tfrac14)$, entropy
$H(\tfrac14) \approx 0.811$ bits. Given $h$: $(0, 0, 1)$, entropy $0$.
Given $e$: $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0)$, entropy $1$ bit.

**Weighted average.**
$H(X_2 \mid X_1) = \tfrac12 \times 0.811 + \tfrac14 \times 0 +
\tfrac14 \times 1 \approx 0.656$ bits.

**Ladder step.** $F_1 \approx 1.41$ bits, $F_2 \approx 0.66$ bits: one
letter of context removes $0.75$ bits, more than half the per-letter
uncertainty: the toy version of English's $4.03 \to 3.32$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A 20-position guessing game yields the tally: rank 1 fourteen times,
rank 2 three times, rank 3 twice, rank 4 once. Compute Shannon's upper
and lower bounds on the per-letter entropy. Use $\log_2 .7 \approx
-0.515$, $\log_2 .15 \approx -2.737$, $\log_2 .10 \approx -3.322$,
$\log_2 .05 \approx -4.322$, $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$.

<Solution>
Frequencies: $q_1 = .7$, $q_2 = .15$, $q_3 = .10$, $q_4 = .05$,
$q_5 = 0$.

**Upper bound.**
$-\sum q_i \log_2 q_i = .7 \times 0.515 + .15 \times 2.737 + .10 \times
3.322 + .05 \times 4.322 = 0.360 + 0.411 + 0.332 + 0.216 \approx 1.32$
bits.

**Lower bound.** $\sum i (q_i - q_{i+1}) \log_2 i$: the $i = 1$ term is
$0$; $i = 2$: $2(.15 - .10)(1) = 0.100$; $i = 3$:
$3(.10 - .05)(1.585) = 0.238$; $i = 4$: $4(.05 - 0)(2) = 0.400$. Sum
$\approx 0.74$ bits.

The 20-position game brackets the entropy between $0.74$ and $1.32$
bits per letter, with error bars the size of the interval: the tally is
ten times smaller than one column of Shannon's experiment.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Using the two-variable chain rule of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/> repeatedly, show that
$$
H(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_N) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} F_k ,
$$
and conclude that $\tfrac{1}{N} H(X_1, \ldots, X_N)$ — the per-letter
cost of describing a block of $N$ letters — is the running average of
the ladder, and therefore also converges to $H_{\text{rate}}$ for a
stationary source.

<Solution>
**Chain.** The two-variable rule $H(A, B) = H(A) + H(B \mid A)$ with
$A = (X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1})$ and $B = X_N$ gives
$$
H(X_1, \ldots, X_N) = H(X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1})
+ H(X_N \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1}).
$$
The last term is $F_N$. Applying the same step to
$H(X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1})$, and so on down to $H(X_1) = F_1$, unrolls the
joint entropy into $\sum_{k=1}^N F_k$.

**Average.** Dividing by $N$,
$\tfrac1N H(X_1, \ldots, X_N) = \tfrac1N \sum_{k=1}^N F_k$: the mean of
the first $N$ rungs. If $F_k \to H_{\text{rate}}$, then for any
$\varepsilon$ all but finitely many terms lie within $\varepsilon$ of
the limit; the finitely many exceptional terms contribute at most a
constant divided by $N$, which vanishes. Hence the running mean
converges to the same limit (the Cesàro-mean argument), which is
Theorem 4.2.1 of Cover & Thomas: block description cost per letter and
conditional-entropy limit agree.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

A novel contains $500{,}000$ characters. Compute its size in ASCII
(8 bits per character), its information content at an entropy rate of
$1.2$ bits per character, the ideal compression factor, and the
redundancy relative to a fixed-width 27-symbol encoding
($\log_2 27 \approx 4.755$).

<Solution>
ASCII: $500{,}000 \times 8 = 4{,}000{,}000$ bits $= 500{,}000$ bytes,
about $0.5$ MB. Information content: $500{,}000 \times 1.2 = 600{,}000$
bits $= 75{,}000$ bytes. Ideal compression factor:
$8 / 1.2 \approx 6.7$. Redundancy:
$1 - 1.2/4.755 \approx 1 - 0.252 = 0.748 \approx 75\%$.

The entropy rate is a floor, not an achieved figure: reaching $75$ kB
requires a coder whose predictive model matches a fluent reader's;
dictionary compressors with short memories stop well short of it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Suppose a "language" produces 27 symbols independently and uniformly at
random. Determine the ideal predictor's guess-rank frequencies $q_i$,
and evaluate both of Shannon's bounds. State what the result says about
flat versus peaked rank histograms.

<Solution>
Every conditional distribution is uniform, so every guessing order is
ideal, and each guess is correct with probability $\tfrac{1}{27}$
regardless of rank: $q_i = \tfrac{1}{27}$ for $i = 1, \ldots, 27$.

**Upper bound.** $-\sum_{i=1}^{27} \tfrac{1}{27} \log_2 \tfrac{1}{27}
= \log_2 27 \approx 4.75$ bits.

**Lower bound.** For $i < 27$, $q_i - q_{i+1} = 0$; only $i = 27$
survives: $27 \times \tfrac{1}{27} \times \log_2 27 = \log_2 27$.

The bounds coincide at $\log_2 27$: for a memoryless uniform source the
game measures the entropy exactly, and prediction buys nothing. A
perfectly flat rank histogram certifies incompressibility; the entire
entropy saving of English is visible as the peak of its histogram at
rank 1.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A toy predictor over $\{A, B, C\}$ guesses in a fixed order determined
by the previous letter: after $A$ it guesses $B, A, C$; after $B$:
$A, C, B$; after $C$: $A, B, C$; at the start of the text: $A, B, C$.
Decode the rank sequence $2, 1, 1, 3, 1$ into text, then re-encode your
text into ranks to verify the round trip. State what this shows about
the information content of the reduced text.

<Solution>
**Decode.** Position 1: start order $(A, B, C)$, rank 2 $\to B$.
Position 2: after $B$, order $(A, C, B)$, rank 1 $\to A$. Position 3:
after $A$, order $(B, A, C)$, rank 1 $\to B$. Position 4: after $B$,
order $(A, C, B)$, rank 3 $\to B$. Position 5: after $B$, order
$(A, C, B)$, rank 1 $\to A$. Text: $B\,A\,B\,B\,A$.

**Re-encode.** $B$ at the start is guess 2; $A$ after $B$ is guess 1;
$B$ after $A$ is guess 1; $B$ after $B$ is guess 3; $A$ after $B$ is
guess 1. Ranks: $2, 1, 1, 3, 1$. The round trip closes.

Given the predictor, text and ranks determine each other, position by
position; the maps are mutually inverse. Two sequences related by an
invertible, context-respecting recoding carry the same information and
have the same entropy rate. This is why measuring the rank distribution
in the guessing game measures English itself.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

English text carries roughly one bit of information per letter while ASCII
spends eight; the gap is redundancy, and Shannon's guessing game measures
it.

Every quantity in the course so far has been computed from a distribution
supplied in advance: surprisal from one probability
(<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>), entropy from a full table
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>), and conditional entropy from
a joint table, where <Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>
established that observing a second variable never raises average
uncertainty. Applied once to a real source that result says a letter costs
less when the letter before it is known; applied without limit it defines
the per-symbol cost of the source itself, the entropy rate. This lesson
defines that quantity, measures it for printed English by the experiment
Shannon built for the purpose, and closes the module by naming where the
measurement departs from the theory it applies.

Four steps: the ladder of conditional entropies $F_N$; its limit; the
guessing game, which reaches context lengths no frequency table can; and
the redundancy that the resulting number implies. Unless stated otherwise
the alphabet is the 27 symbols a–z plus space, with case and punctuation
dropped, and every number below is tied to that choice.

## Four bits a letter is the wrong number

The entropy of English is the average cost of one letter to a reader who
has already read everything before it. That is the quantity this lesson
measures, and it is not the quantity most often quoted.

The figure in circulation comes from a letter-frequency table. Over the
27-symbol alphabet the monogram frequencies give about $4.11$ bits per
letter (<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>, from MacKay's table;
Shannon's own counts give $4.03$ for the same alphabet, the gap being
corpus variation rather than error), and that number is widely repeated as
the entropy of English. It is wrong, and the reason is structural rather
than numerical.

### What a frequency table cannot see

A frequency table records how often each symbol occurs and nothing else.
An entropy computed from it is the entropy of a source that draws letters
independently from those frequencies: a source free to emit `qz`, `hh` or
`tttt` as readily as `the`. Every constraint the
language imposes between adjacent letters is invisible to that table,
because the table has already averaged the context away. English is not
that source. After `q` the next symbol is `u` in almost every English word;
after a space, `e` and `x` are not remotely equally likely; and a reader
who knows the previous six letters is rarely in doubt about more than two
or three candidates.

The replacement is a sequence rather than a single number. Write $F_N$ for
the conditional entropy of the next letter given the $N-1$ letters before
it:

$$
F_N = H(X_N \mid X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_{N-1}),
$$

so that $F_1 = H(X_1)$ is the monogram entropy, and $F_0 = \log_2 27
\approx 4.76$ bits by convention, the cost of a letter under no statistics
at all. The misquoted $4.1$ is $F_1$: the second rung of a ladder, reported
as its bottom.

### Measured rungs

Letter, digram and trigram frequency tables give the first few rungs
directly. Shannon computed them for both of the usual alphabets:

| alphabet | $F_0$ | $F_1$ | $F_2$ | $F_3$ | $F_{\text{word}}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 26 letters, no space | $4.70$ | $4.14$ | $3.56$ | $3.3$ | $2.62$ |
| 27 symbols, space included | $4.76$ | $4.03$ | $3.32$ | $3.1$ | $2.14$ |

  caption="Each rung conditions on more of the past, and every rung is cheaper than the one above it."

One letter of context is worth $0.71$ bits on the 27-symbol alphabet
($4.03 \to 3.32$); a second is worth another $0.22$. That is the
discriminating comparison: the digram entropy is computed from the same
corpus by the same formula as the monogram entropy, and it is lower, so no
appeal to measurement error rescues $F_1$ as an estimate of the whole.

The ladder stops at $N = 3$ for a counting reason. A tetragram table has
$27^4 = 531{,}441$ cells, and no frequency table of the period filled it
reliably; even $F_3$ carries a caveat, since the trigram table used counted
only trigrams inside words and had to be corrected by hand for those
bridging a word boundary. The word-level rung comes from a different route:
word frequencies plotted against frequency rank fall on a line of slope
$-1$ on log-log axes, the relationship
[Zipf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipf%27s_law) had reported across many
languages, and assuming $p_n = 0.1/n$ out to the rank at which the
probabilities exhaust one gives $11.82$ bits per word. Divided by $5.5$
symbols per word (an average of $4.5$ letters plus one space) that is
$2.14$ bits per letter.

## The entropy rate: the limit of a decreasing ladder

Five computed rungs are five numbers, and the counting argument above says
a sixth will not arrive by the same route. What makes the sequence more
than a list is that it is monotone, and therefore has a limit the list
itself never reaches.

<Theorem title="the ladder never rises">
For a stationary source, $F_{N+1} \le F_N$ for every $N \ge 1$, and
$\lim_{N \to \infty} F_N$ exists.

<Proof>
The plan: drop the oldest symbol from the conditioning set, apply
conditioning-reduces-entropy, then use stationarity to recognise the result
as the previous rung. The direction of the inequality is the whole content;
convergence follows from a standard fact about bounded monotone sequences.

**Step 1, shorten the context.** Compare $F_{N+1} = H(X_{N+1} \mid X_1,
\ldots, X_N)$ with the same quantity conditioned on one symbol fewer,
$H(X_{N+1} \mid X_2, \ldots, X_N)$. The first conditions on everything the
second does and on $X_1$ besides. Conditioning on an extra variable cannot
raise average uncertainty: <Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>
proved $H(Y \mid X) \le H(Y)$ by one application of Jensen's inequality to
the concave $\log_2$. Applying that theorem separately inside each value of
$(X_2, \ldots, X_N)$ and averaging over those values gives the conditional
form needed here. Hence
$$
F_{N+1} = H(X_{N+1} \mid X_1, \ldots, X_N)
\;\le\; H(X_{N+1} \mid X_2, \ldots, X_N).
$$

**Step 2, shift the indices.** Stationarity says the joint distribution of
a block does not depend on where the block starts, so shifting every index
down by one changes nothing:
$H(X_{N+1} \mid X_2, \ldots, X_N) = H(X_N \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1}) = F_N$.
This is the only step that uses stationarity, and it is the step the
measurement in the last section will strain.

**Step 3, converge.** $(F_N)$ is non-increasing and bounded below by $0$,
since every conditional entropy is an average of entropies and no entropy
is negative. A non-increasing sequence bounded below converges to its
infimum.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Definition title="entropy rate">
For a stationary source $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ over a finite alphabet, the
**entropy rate** is
$$
H_{\text{rate}} = \lim_{N \to \infty} H(X_N \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1})
= \lim_{N \to \infty} F_N,
$$
in bits per symbol: the per-letter uncertainty given unlimited preceding
context. The same limit is reached by the block description cost
$\tfrac{1}{N} H(X_1, \ldots, X_N)$, which the exercise below on the two definitions of the entropy rate derives.
</Definition>

Four sources fix what the definition covers.

- **The canonical case.** Printed English, whose rate the rest of this
  lesson measures at roughly one bit per letter against a $4.76$-bit
  alphabet.
- **A structurally different case.** A source drawing symbols
  independently from a fixed distribution. Context tells nothing, so every
  rung equals $F_1$: the ladder is flat and the rate is the ordinary
  entropy of one symbol. The misquoted $4.1$ is the correct answer to this
  question about a different source.
- **The degenerate case.** The periodic source `ABABAB…`. Here $F_1 = 1$
  bit, because half the symbols are `A`; but one letter of context settles
  the next completely, so $F_2 = 0$ and the rate is $0$. A source can have
  positive symbol entropy and zero information rate.
- **The near miss.** A source whose statistics drift with position violates
  exactly one clause, stationarity, and Step 2 of the proof fails with it.
  The rungs of such a source may rise, and the limit need not exist.

<KeyFact>
Monotonicity is a theorem about stationary sources, not about text. English
is not stationary: letter statistics differ between a dictionary, a novel
and this page, and a single document drifts as its subject changes. Every
number reported below inherits that assumption, which is why the estimate
is quoted for "ordinary literary English" and not for English.
</KeyFact>

## Shannon's guessing game: the model is in the reader's head

The limit is a statement about $N = 100$ and beyond; the table method dies
at $N = 4$. What is needed at $N = 100$ is the conditional distribution of
the next letter given the preceding ninety-nine, and one exists in usable
form: every fluent reader carries it, in the words, spellings, idioms and
grammar that let them repair a smudged word or finish someone else's
sentence. Shannon's method queries that implicit model instead of
tabulating a corpus.

**The protocol.** Show a subject the first $N-1$ letters of a text they
have not read. The subject names the next letter; if wrong, names another,
and continues until correct. Record how many guesses it took, reveal the
letter, advance one position, repeat. Each position yields one number, its
*guess rank*: a $1$ means the first guess was right, a $17$ means sixteen
wrong guesses came before it.

<FigureSequence
  name="guessing-game"
  alt="Five frames of the guessing game on the text IT IS: each frame reveals one position, lists the subject's guesses until the correct letter, and appends the guess count to a tally row that ends 4, 2, 1, 2, 1"
  caption="The guessing game on a five-symbol text. Each position yields one number: how many guesses the correct letter took."
/>

<Task>
Step through the five frames and check where the cost falls. Position 1,
guessed blind, takes 4 tries; every position with a word already underway
takes 1 or 2. The entire output of the game is the tally row $4, 2, 1, 2,
1$, and everything computed later is computed from a row like it.
</Task>

Human scores on ordinary prose are high. On a 129-letter passage, Shannon's
subject named 89 letters (69%) correctly on the first attempt, with errors
concentrated at the beginnings of words, where the line of thought can
branch. In the guess-until-correct variant on a 102-symbol passage from
*Jefferson the Virginian*, a 1948 biography chosen because the subject had
not read it, 79 positions took one guess, 8 took two, 3 took three, 2 each
took four and five, and only 8 needed more than five. For the systematic
experiment, 100 fifteen-letter samples were drawn from one book and played
at every context length from 0 to 14 known letters, and a further set at 99
known letters.

### The reduced text carries the same information

Replacing the letters by their ranks looks like discarding the text. It is
not, and the argument is worth following, because the whole measurement
rests on it.

<Figure
  float="right"
  width="m"
  src="./figures/reduced-text.svg"
  alt="Two rows: text into a predictor produces the rank sequence 4 2 1 2 1; the same rank sequence into the same predictor reproduces the text, with a dashed link marking that both boxes hold one model"
  caption="Given the predictor, text and ranks rebuild each other. Measuring the ranks measures English."
/>

Suppose a second predictor exists that guesses exactly as the first did:
Shannon's "identical twin", or in practice a fixed rule rather than a
person. Hand the twin the rank sequence alone. At position 1 the twin
generates its guesses in its own fixed order, and the rank says which of
them is the true letter; knowing that letter, the twin generates its order
for position 2, and so on. The text reconstructs exactly. The exercise below on the toy fixed-order predictor runs
this decoding on a three-letter toy predictor.

So the predictor is an invertible transducer: text in, *reduced text* (the
rank sequence) out, and back again through the same machine. Both
directions look only at the current symbol and the $N-1$ letters before it,
so the recoding preserves $N$-gram statistics, and the reduced text has the
same $F_N$ as English itself. What the recoding changes is the shape of the
frequencies. Constraints spread across many letters, invisible in any
single-symbol table, become one wildly skewed distribution over the ranks
$1$ to $27$, with most of the mass on rank 1. The redundancy has been moved
from the correlations into the marginal, where a single-symbol calculation
can see it.

## Bounds from the guess-rank frequencies

The rank frequencies are a distribution over 27 symbols, so an entropy can
be computed from them directly. What that entropy bounds, and in which
direction, is the content of Shannon's estimate.

Write $q_i$ for the fraction of positions resolved on guess $i$ when the
subject knows $N-1$ preceding letters, ordered $q_1 \ge q_2 \ge \cdots \ge
q_{27}$, with $q_{28} = 0$.

<Theorem title="entropy bounds from prediction frequencies">
For the ideal predictor with $N-1$ known letters, which guesses in order of
decreasing conditional probability,
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{27} i\,(q_i - q_{i+1}) \log_2 i
\;\le\; F_N \;\le\;
-\sum_{i=1}^{27} q_i \log_2 q_i .
$$
The upper bound holds for any predictor whose guessing order is a fixed
function of the known context, a human included. The lower bound is proved
only for the ideal predictor.

<Proof>
**Upper bound.** The reduced text has the same $F_N$ as the original
(previous section). By the ladder theorem applied to the reduced text,
$F_N \le F_1$ of that text, and $F_1$ of the reduced text is exactly the
entropy of the rank distribution, $-\sum_i q_i \log_2 q_i$. In words:
pricing the ranks as independent draws overcharges, and the overcharge is
in the safe direction. Nothing here requires the predictor to be ideal,
only that its guessing order be reconstructible from the known context.

**Lower bound.** Arrange the $q_i$ as a staircase of decreasing heights and
decompose it into rectangles: the $i$-th rectangle spans the first $i$ ranks
at height $q_i - q_{i+1}$, carries total probability $i(q_i - q_{i+1})$, and
is uniform on $i$ outcomes, so its own entropy is $\log_2 i$. The left-hand
sum is the entropy of that decomposition. Shannon's argument shows that any
source whose ideal-prediction frequencies are $q$ has $F_N$ at least this
value, because the rectangular system is reached from the source's true
conditional distributions by a series of steps each of which decreases or
preserves entropy. Equality demands that every context offer a set of
equally probable next letters and assign zero to all others. English does
not, which is one reason the two bounds stay apart.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Figure
  src="./figures/rank-histogram.svg"
  alt="Histogram of guess ranks from a 40-position game: 65% of letters guessed on the first try, 15% on the second, 10% on the third, and a thin tail out to rank six"
  caption="Guess-rank frequencies from the 40-position game worked below. The mass piled on rank 1 is what drives both bounds down."
/>

The upper bound has a coding reading that <Ref lesson="kraft-inequality"/>
and <Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/> will make exact later in the
course: a code matched to the rank distribution spends about
$-\sum_i q_i \log_2 q_i$ bits per rank, and the rank stream reconstructs
the text, so English can be stored at that price. A concentrated rank
histogram is a cheap code for English, and the peak at rank 1 is the low
entropy made visible.

### A worked estimate from one short game

<Example title="from tally to bounds">
A 40-position game on an unfamiliar passage produces this tally.

| guess rank $i$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| positions | 26 | 6 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| $q_i$ | $.65$ | $.15$ | $.10$ | $.05$ | $.025$ | $.025$ |

**Upper bound: the entropy of the rank distribution.** The surprisals are
$-\log_2 .65 = 0.621$, $-\log_2 .15 = 2.737$, $-\log_2 .10 = 3.322$,
$-\log_2 .05 = 4.322$ and $-\log_2 .025 = 5.322$ bits. Weighting each by
its own frequency,

$$
.65 (0.621) + .15 (2.737) + .10 (3.322)
+ .05 (4.322) + 2 \times .025 (5.322)
$$
$$
= 0.404 + 0.411 + 0.332 + 0.216 + 0.266 = 1.63 \text{ bits.}
$$

**Lower bound: $\sum_i i\,(q_i - q_{i+1}) \log_2 i$**, with $q_7 = 0$. Two
terms drop out before any arithmetic: $i = 1$ because $\log_2 1 = 0$, and
$i = 5$ because $q_5 = q_6$ makes its difference zero. What remains is

$$
2(.15 - .10)(1) + 3(.10 - .05)(1.585) + 4(.05 - .025)(2)
+ 6(.025)(2.585)
$$
$$
= 0.100 + 0.238 + 0.200 + 0.388 = 0.93 \text{ bits.}
$$

**The estimate.** The game brackets the per-letter entropy between $0.93$
and $1.63$ bits, on the 27-symbol alphabet, at whatever context length the
guesser was given.

**A non-estimate, for contrast.** The mean number of guesses is
$(26 + 12 + 12 + 8 + 5 + 6)/40 = 69/40 \approx 1.7$ guesses per letter.
That number is not an entropy and does not bound one: it is a weighted
average of the ranks themselves, not of their surprisals, and two tallies
with the same mean rank can have different rank entropies. Only the
distribution, pushed through the two bounds, gives bits.
</Example>

<Exercise>
Run the same computation on a different tally. A 20-position game gives
rank 1 fourteen times, rank 2 three times, rank 3 twice and rank 4 once.
Compute both bounds. Write down the two $q$ vectors you need (the
frequencies, and the differences $q_i - q_{i+1}$) before opening the
solution. Useful values: $\log_2 .7 \approx -0.515$,
$\log_2 .15 \approx -2.737$, $\log_2 .10 \approx -3.322$,
$\log_2 .05 \approx -4.322$, $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$.

<Solution>
**Strategy.** The upper bound is an ordinary entropy of the $q$ vector. The
lower bound needs the successive differences and the convention
$q_{i+1} = 0$ past the last non-empty rank; the $i = 1$ term always
vanishes.

Frequencies: $q_1 = .7$, $q_2 = .15$, $q_3 = .10$, $q_4 = .05$, and
$q_5 = 0$. Differences: $q_1 - q_2 = .55$, $q_2 - q_3 = .05$,
$q_3 - q_4 = .05$, $q_4 - q_5 = .05$.

**Upper bound.**
$$
-\sum_i q_i \log_2 q_i = .7(0.515) + .15(2.737) + .10(3.322) + .05(4.322)
$$
$$
= 0.360 + 0.411 + 0.332 + 0.216 \approx 1.32 \text{ bits.}
$$

**Lower bound.** The $i = 1$ term is $0$. Then $i = 2$ gives
$2(.05)(1) = 0.100$; $i = 3$ gives $3(.05)(1.585) = 0.238$; $i = 4$ gives
$4(.05)(2) = 0.400$. Sum: $\approx 0.74$ bits.

The interval is $[0.74, 1.32]$ bits per letter, narrower at the top than
the worked example's $[0.93, 1.63]$ because more mass sits on rank 1
($.70$ against $.65$). Twenty positions is a demonstration, not a
measurement: the tally is a fiftieth of one column of Shannon's experiment,
and moving a single letter from rank 1 to rank 3 moves the upper bound by
about $0.13$ bits.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyFact>
Only the upper bound is proved for a human guesser. The lower bound assumes
prediction in exact order of conditional probability, which no person
achieves, so $0.6$ is not a proved floor under English. Shannon's defence
is a compensation argument: a human's failure to predict ideally pushes the
estimate up, while the failure of real English contexts to offer equally
probable alternatives pushes the true $F_N$ above the rectangular bound by
at least as much.
</KeyFact>

## ★ Why ideal prediction is the right yardstick

The lower bound is stated for the ideal predictor, and the choice is not
arbitrary. Among all predictors that are functions of the previous $N-1$
letters and instantaneously reversible, ideal prediction concentrates the
rank distribution more than any other. Formally, its partial sums dominate:
$\sum_{i \le S} q_i \ge \sum_{i \le S} r_i$ for every $S$, where $r$ is the
rank distribution of any competing predictor. The reason is a counting
argument on a table with one row per $(N-1)$-gram and one column per next
letter: the ideal predictor's first $S$ ranks collect the $S$ largest
entries of each row, and reversibility forbids any competitor from
collecting more, since no two entries in a row may map to the same output
symbol.

A distribution dominating another in every partial sum is said to
*majorize* it, and majorization implies lower entropy. So the ideal
predictor gives the smallest possible rank entropy, which is to say the
tightest upper bound obtainable this way. A human predictor sits strictly
below ideal, and the estimate suffers accordingly in one known direction.

The same table argument shows the partial sums increase with $N$: knowing
more letters can only raise the probability of being right within the first
$S$ guesses, for every $S$. Both bounds are therefore non-increasing in $N$,
which is what licences reading the last column of the experiment as the
best available approximation to the limit.

<Remark title="prediction backwards">
Nothing in $F_N$ prefers one direction of the text. Written in terms of
block probabilities, $F_N = H(X_1, \ldots, X_N) - H(X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1})$,
and both terms are unchanged by reversing the sequence, so a language and
its reversal have identical $N$-gram entropies. Shannon checked this against
a subject asked to guess the letter *preceding* a known passage. On two
101-letter samples the forward run took one guess 70 times and the reverse
run 66 times, with the reverse run's tail slightly heavier. The task feels
much harder and scores barely worse, which is what the identity predicts and
intuition does not.
</Remark>

## Redundancy: three quarters of English is structure

A rate of about one bit per letter is a statement about prediction. Divided
by the cost of the raw encoding it becomes a statement about text: what
fraction of what is written was never a free choice.

$$
\text{redundancy} = 1 - \frac{H_{\text{rate}}}{\log_2 27}
\approx 1 - \frac{1.2}{4.76} \approx 0.75 .
$$

Shannon's conclusion, quoted in the 1951 paper's own terms, is that
long-range statistical effects up to 100 letters reduce the entropy of
ordinary literary English to something of the order of one bit per letter,
"with a corresponding redundancy of roughly 75%". Three quarters of printed
English is fixed by the structure of the language; the remaining quarter is
choice.

### Reconciling 50% with 75%

The 1948 paper reports the redundancy of English as roughly 50%, and the
1951 paper as roughly 75%. Both are correct, and the difference is entirely
in the two conventions, which is why a redundancy figure without its
convention is unusable.

- **Context length.** The 1948 figure counts statistical effects reaching
  no further than about eight letters, and puts the entropy at $2.3$ bits
  per letter. The 1951 figure counts effects reaching 100 letters. The
  extra 25 points of redundancy are precisely the long-range structure that
  only the guessing game could reach.
- **Alphabet.** The 1948 estimate is against the 26-letter alphabet
  ($2.3 / 4.70 \approx 0.51$); the 1951 estimate is against the 27-symbol
  alphabet. Because the space is almost entirely predictable once whole
  words are in view, the 27-symbol rungs run at about $4.5/5.5 = 0.818$ of
  the 26-letter rungs at large $N$: the same language, two bookkeeping
  systems.

Against ASCII neither figure applies. ASCII spends 8 bits on a symbol set
five times larger than the one English uses, so its 85% redundancy measures
the encoding's slack as much as the language's.

### What redundancy buys: crosswords

{/* img mackay-crosswords: two completed crossword grids side by side, one American-style (every square in both a horizontal and a vertical word) and one British-style (about half the squares in one word only), both fully filled with real interlocking words so the reader can see that consistent solutions exist in quantity; candidate images.yaml id `informationt-s2-crossword` — MacKay, Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms, CUP 2003, Figure 18.1, printed p. 260 (PDF p. 272), https://www.inference.org.uk/itprnn/book.pdf; placement float-right, width s, beside the crossword paragraphs */}

The clearest test of a redundancy figure is that it predicts which language
games are possible. A crossword grid demands that a string of letters read
correctly in two directions at once. In a language of zero redundancy every
letter string is valid text, so every array of letters is a valid crossword
and constructing them is free. At the other extreme a language constrained
enough that almost no string is a word admits almost no grids. Redundancy
sets where between those extremes a language sits: assuming the language's
constraints behave like random ones, large two-dimensional crosswords are
just possible at 50% redundancy, and at 33% three-dimensional crosswords
would be.

The counting behind the claim is worth stating, because it converts an
existence observation into a bound. Model the language as a dictionary of
$W$ words all of length $L$, letters drawn from a monogram source of
entropy $H_0$. A grid of $S$ squares holds about $f_w S$ words on $f_1 S$
occupied squares, with $f_w$ and $f_1$ fixed by the grid style. Filling the
squares at random gives $2^{f_1 S H_0}$ typical fillings; the chance that
any one word comes out valid is $W / 2^{L H_0}$; so the log of the number of
valid grids grows linearly in $S$ with a coefficient that is positive only
if the dictionary is large enough. With a monogram entropy of $4.2$ bits, a
$4{,}000$-word vocabulary and five-letter words, the condition is
comfortably met for British-style grids, where about half the squares lie in
one word only, and *only just* met for American-style grids, where every
square lies in two words. American constructors reaching for obscure
vocabulary is the visible consequence of a bound that is nearly tight.

<KeyFact>
The redundancy is not waste. It is the margin that lets a reader repair
`th_ r__m w_s n_t v_ry l_ght`, and the same margin is what the channel
module later spends on purpose: an error-correcting code is redundancy
inserted deliberately, at a rate the designer chooses, after the source
coder has removed the language's own.
</KeyFact>

## The measurement and its four departures

The one-bit figure is the output of an experiment, and this section states
what the experiment assumed that reality does not supply. This is where the
module's results get cashed out: surprisal (<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>)
supplies the $-\log_2 q_i$ of each rank, entropy as an expectation
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>) supplies the upper bound,
conditional entropy (<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>) supplies
the definition of each rung, and the chain rule makes the rungs a decreasing
sequence with a limit worth measuring. Four things then go wrong, and all
four bias the answer in directions that can be named.

**1. The source is assumed stationary and ergodic, and is neither.** Step 2
of the ladder proof shifts every index by one and requires the statistics to
be unchanged. English fails this: a technical paper, a novel and a shopping
list have different letter statistics, and a single novel drifts as its
subject does. Shannon's samples all come from one book, which makes the
assumption locally defensible and the result a statement about that register
rather than about the language.

**2. The model is a person, so the estimate measures the guesser.** The
upper bound is the entropy of the guesser's rank distribution, and a better
predictor produces a more concentrated distribution and a lower bound on the
same text. The measurement therefore bounds English from above and measures
the predictor exactly. Replacing the human with a trained language model and
reading its loss as this game's score is the subject of
<Ref lesson="compression-is-prediction"/>.

**3. The sample is small and the bounds are wide.** Each column of the
experiment rests on 100 guesses; a home run of 40 positions rests on 40. The
worked example above returned an interval $0.70$ bits wide from 40
positions, which is comparable to the quantity being estimated, and moving
one letter between ranks shifts a bound by over $0.1$ bits. A tally is a
demonstration; only a corpus is a measurement. A later sharpening of the
protocol had 12 subjects bet on their guesses over a 75-letter excerpt of
the same book, converting the odds directly into bits, and reported $1.34$
bits per letter.

**4. Every number depends on the alphabet.** The same passage yields $4.03$
or $4.14$ bits at the monogram rung according to whether the space is a
symbol, $2.14$ or $2.62$ at the word rung, and different numbers again if
case and punctuation are restored. No per-letter figure for English means
anything without its symbol set attached, which is why this lesson fixes 27
symbols in its third paragraph and repeats the convention at every number.

<Task>
Run the experiment. Take a paragraph the guesser has not read, lowercase it,
drop punctuation and keep spaces. Play 40 positions of guess-until-correct,
tally the ranks into counts, convert to frequencies $q_i$, and compute both
bounds as in the worked example. Report the interval, not a value. Expect an
upper bound between roughly $1$ and $2.5$ bits depending on the text and the
guesser; a result outside that range usually means the passage was familiar
(too low) or the guesser gave up early on hard positions (too high).
</Task>

What is now established: a real source's information content is its entropy
rate, the per-symbol entropy given unlimited context; for printed English on
a 27-symbol alphabet that rate is on the order of one bit per letter against
a $4.76$-bit encoding, leaving about 75% redundancy. What is not established
is what a bound of one bit per letter is a bound *on*. The claim that no
lossless code can average fewer bits per symbol than the entropy has been
used here and proved nowhere. The coding module takes it up, beginning with
<Ref lesson="prefix-codes"/> and reaching the bound itself in
<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>; the number measured here returns
in <Ref lesson="dictionary-coding"/>, where a compressor attains the entropy
rate without being told the source, and in
<Ref lesson="compression-is-prediction"/>, where the guesser is replaced by a
model.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A guessing game is played on the same passage twice: once with the guesser
told nothing, and once with the guesser told the whole preceding paragraph.
Both games are on a text of the single letter `a` repeated 200 times.
Predict the tally in each case, compute both of Shannon's bounds from it,
and state what the game reports. Then do the same for a text of 200 letters
drawn uniformly and independently from the 27-symbol alphabet. Say which
property of the source each answer detects.

<Solution>
**Repeated letter.** After the first position the context makes `a` certain,
so an ideal guesser names it first every time: $q_1 = 1$ and $q_i = 0$ for
$i \ge 2$ (the first position is one letter in 200 and does not move the
frequencies at this precision).

Upper bound: $-1 \log_2 1 = 0$ bits. Lower bound: the only non-zero
difference is $q_1 - q_2 = 1$, and its term carries $\log_2 1 = 0$, so the
sum is $0$ bits. Both bounds are $0$: the game reports that the source
carries no information per symbol, which is correct. Note that $F_1$ of this
source is also $0$, since the marginal is a point mass, so this text does
*not* discriminate between a monogram model and a context model.

**Uniform random letters.** Every conditional distribution is uniform over
27 symbols, so every guessing order is ideal and each guess is right with
probability $\tfrac{1}{27}$ whatever its rank: $q_i = \tfrac{1}{27}$ for
$i = 1, \ldots, 27$.

Upper bound: $-\sum_{i=1}^{27} \tfrac{1}{27} \log_2 \tfrac{1}{27} =
\log_2 27 \approx 4.75$ bits. Lower bound: $q_i - q_{i+1} = 0$ for
$i < 27$, so only $i = 27$ survives, giving
$27 \times \tfrac{1}{27} \times \log_2 27 = \log_2 27$. The bounds
coincide.

**What each detects.** The bounds pinch together exactly at the two
extremes: a flat rank histogram certifies that prediction buys nothing and
the source is incompressible, and a rank histogram concentrated on 1
certifies that it is free. Between them the gap is the game's admission that
rank frequencies alone do not determine an entropy. The repeated-letter text
detects predictability of any kind; the uniform text detects its complete
absence; neither separates monogram structure from context structure, which
is why the real experiment must be run on English.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim, which is repeated often enough to be worth dismantling
precisely. "English has about 1 bit per letter of entropy, so a perfect
compressor could store any 1000-letter English text in about 1000 bits."
Two distinct things are wrong with it. Identify both, and for each name the
step in the lesson that fails.

<Solution>
**First error: expectation versus individual strings.** The entropy rate is
an average over the source. A code achieving one bit per letter *on average*
must spend more than 1000 bits on some 1000-letter texts and fewer on
others, and the Kraft budget of <Ref lesson="kraft-inequality"/> makes this
exact: the short codewords are a finite resource. Concretely, there are
$27^{1000}$ strings over the alphabet, and at most $2^{1001} - 1$ distinct
binary strings of length $\le 1000$, so the overwhelming majority of
1000-letter strings cannot be stored in 1000 bits by any code whatever. The
step that fails is treating $H_{\text{rate}}$, defined as a limit of
*average* conditional entropies, as a per-string guarantee. An unusual text,
a random-letter string or a passage in transliterated Finnish, costs far
more.

**Second error: who holds the model.** The guessing game measures the
entropy of a text against a fluent reader's model of English. A compressor
that achieves the same rate must contain a model of comparable quality, and
either it ships that model or it learns it from the data it is compressing.
Shipping it charges the model's size against the file; learning it costs
bits early in the stream, before there is anything to learn from. The
one-bit figure is a floor for a compressor that already knows English as
well as a person, not a promise about any shipped program. The step
that fails is reading a bound on $F_N$, a property of the source and an
idealised predictor, as a bound on any particular program's output.

**What survives.** One bit per letter remains a correct statement about a
long stream of ordinary literary English compressed by an ideal
model-carrying coder, and it correctly predicts that a megabyte of prose has
a few hundred kilobits of content. It predicts nothing about any single
1000-letter string.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The definition of the entropy rate asserted that the block description cost
$\tfrac{1}{N} H(X_1, \ldots, X_N)$ has the same limit as the ladder, and did
not prove it. Prove it. Use the two-variable chain rule of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/> repeatedly to show
$$
H(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_N) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} F_k ,
$$
then conclude that $\tfrac{1}{N} H(X_1, \ldots, X_N)$ is the running mean of
the ladder and converges to $H_{\text{rate}}$. State also which of the two
quantities is larger at finite $N$, and why.

<Solution>
**Unrolling the chain.** The two-variable rule $H(A,B) = H(A) + H(B \mid A)$
with $A = (X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1})$ and $B = X_N$ gives
$$
H(X_1, \ldots, X_N) = H(X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1})
+ H(X_N \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1}),
$$
whose last term is $F_N$. Apply the same step to $H(X_1, \ldots, X_{N-1})$,
and so on down to $H(X_1) = F_1$. The joint entropy unrolls into
$\sum_{k=1}^{N} F_k$: the cost of a block is the sum of the per-position
costs, each conditioned on everything to its left.

**The running mean.** Dividing by $N$ gives
$\tfrac{1}{N} H(X_1, \ldots, X_N) = \tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^N F_k$. If
$F_k \to H_{\text{rate}}$, fix $\varepsilon > 0$ and choose $K$ with
$|F_k - H_{\text{rate}}| < \varepsilon$ for all $k > K$. Splitting the mean
at $K$, the first $K$ terms contribute at most $K F_1 / N$, which vanishes
as $N$ grows, and the remainder lies within $\varepsilon$ of
$H_{\text{rate}}$. Hence the mean converges to the same limit. (This is the
Cesàro-mean argument: the average of a convergent sequence converges to the
same value.)

**Which is larger.** The mean of a non-increasing sequence is at least its
current term, so $\tfrac{1}{N} H(X_1, \ldots, X_N) \ge F_N$ for every $N$.
The block cost per symbol overstates the marginal cost of the next symbol,
because it still carries the expensive early positions where little context
was available. Both descend to the same limit, and the conditional form
descends faster: it is the better finite-$N$ estimate of the rate, which is
why the ladder and not the block cost is what the guessing game measures.

**The transferable move:** telescoping a joint quantity into conditional
increments turns a statement about blocks into a statement about a sequence,
and averages of a convergent sequence inherit its limit.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A toy language over $\{t, h, e\}$ has the adjacent-pair joint distribution

| $p(x_1, x_2)$ | $x_2 = t$ | $x_2 = h$ | $x_2 = e$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x_1 = t$ | $0$ | $3/8$ | $1/8$ |
| $x_1 = h$ | $0$ | $0$ | $1/4$ |
| $x_1 = e$ | $1/8$ | $1/8$ | $0$ |

Compute $F_1$ and $F_2$ for this language by the weighted-average form of
conditional entropy, then convert each into a redundancy against the
language's own alphabet. State which rung buys more bits, and what would
have to be true of the language for a third rung to buy nothing further.
Use $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$.

<Solution>
**Marginals.** Summing rows: $p(t) = \tfrac12$, $p(h) = \tfrac14$,
$p(e) = \tfrac14$. Summing columns: $p(t) = \tfrac18$,
$p(h) = \tfrac38 + \tfrac18 = \tfrac12$,
$p(e) = \tfrac18 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac38$.

**$F_1 = H(X_2)$.**
$\tfrac18 (3) + \tfrac12 (1) + \tfrac38 \log_2 \tfrac83
= 0.375 + 0.5 + 0.375 (1.415) \approx 1.41$ bits.

**$F_2 = H(X_2 \mid X_1)$.** Row conditionals: given $t$, the distribution
is $(0, \tfrac34, \tfrac14)$ with entropy $0.811$ bits; given $h$ it is
$(0,0,1)$ with entropy $0$; given $e$ it is $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0)$ with
entropy $1$ bit. Weighting by the row marginals,
$\tfrac12 (0.811) + \tfrac14 (0) + \tfrac14 (1) \approx 0.656$ bits.

**Redundancies.** The alphabet has three symbols, so
$F_0 = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits. At monogram order the redundancy is
$1 - 1.41/1.585 \approx 11\%$; at digram order it is
$1 - 0.656/1.585 \approx 59\%$.

**Which rung buys more.** The first rung buys $1.585 - 1.41 = 0.18$ bits
and the second buys $1.41 - 0.66 = 0.75$ bits, so almost all the structure
of this toy language lives in the adjacency and almost none in the symbol
frequencies. English behaves the same way in direction and differs in
magnitude: its first rung buys $0.73$ bits and its second $0.71$, and the
rungs keep paying out to at least a hundred letters of context.

**When the ladder would stop.** $F_3 = F_2$ exactly when the letter two
positions back is irrelevant given the letter one back, that is, when the
language is a first-order Markov chain. The table above specifies only pair
probabilities and so does not settle the question; a language could match
this table and still have $F_3 < F_2$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Reproduce the experiment and report it honestly. Play 60 positions of
guess-until-correct on a passage chosen by someone else from a book you
have not read, using the 27-symbol convention. Tally the ranks, compute both
bounds, and report the interval together with a statement of how wide it
would have to be to be consistent with Shannon's $0.6$–$1.3$ bits per letter
at 99 letters of context and with the $1.34$ bits per letter from the
12-subject betting experiment. State in advance what would count as
agreement and what would count as a real discrepancy.

<Solution>
**Protocol details that matter.** The guesser must not have read the
passage: familiarity collapses the ranks and returns an entropy far below
English. Give the guesser the text from the start of the passage forward, so
that by position 60 they have 59 letters of context; the published 99-letter
figures are not comparable to a game whose context averages 30 letters.
Count a space as a symbol and let it be guessed like any other.

**What to expect.** A competent guesser on ordinary prose lands near
$q_1 \approx 0.6$–$0.75$ with a tail out to rank 5 or so, giving an upper
bound between about $1.0$ and $2.0$ bits and a lower bound between about
$0.5$ and $1.1$.

**What counts as agreement.** Three conditions, all necessary. (i) The
intervals overlap: your $[\text{lower}, \text{upper}]$ intersects
$[0.6, 1.3]$. (ii) Your upper bound is not below $0.6$; a bound below
Shannon's lower bound means the passage was recognised, not predicted. (iii)
Your interval is *wider* than his, since 60 positions is under a tenth of
one of his columns. An interval narrower than $[0.6, 1.3]$ from 60 positions
indicates a tally too concentrated to be credible, usually because the
guesser stopped guessing hard letters and was told the answer.

**What counts as a discrepancy.** An upper bound above about $2.5$ bits at
50 letters of context, reproduced across two passages and two guessers, is a
real disagreement rather than noise. The likely explanation is the text:
scientific prose, poetry and proper nouns all score worse than the literary
English Shannon sampled, and the correct conclusion is that the entropy rate
is a property of a register, not of a language.

**Comparison with the betting estimate.** The $1.34$ bits per letter from
the 12-subject experiment used a 75-letter excerpt and sequential betting at
27-for-1 odds, which converts a subject's stated odds directly into bits and
removes the guess-rank bounds entirely. It sits inside Shannon's upper bound
and above his lower bound, which is the expected relationship: it is an
upper bound too, estimated from a better-instrumented but equally human
predictor.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A toy predictor over $\{A, B, C\}$ guesses in a fixed order determined by
the previous letter: after $A$ it guesses $B, A, C$; after $B$ it guesses
$A, C, B$; after $C$ it guesses $A, B, C$; at the start of the text it
guesses $A, B, C$. Decode the rank sequence $2, 1, 1, 3, 1$ into text, then
re-encode your text into ranks to verify the round trip. Then state what
would break if the predictor's order depended on the *following* letter
instead of the preceding one.

<Solution>
**Decode.** Position 1: start order $(A, B, C)$, rank 2 gives $B$.
Position 2: after $B$ the order is $(A, C, B)$, rank 1 gives $A$.
Position 3: after $A$ the order is $(B, A, C)$, rank 1 gives $B$.
Position 4: after $B$ the order is $(A, C, B)$, rank 3 gives $B$.
Position 5: after $B$ the order is $(A, C, B)$, rank 1 gives $A$. The text
is $B\,A\,B\,B\,A$.

**Re-encode.** $B$ at the start is guess 2; $A$ after $B$ is guess 1; $B$
after $A$ is guess 1; $B$ after $B$ is guess 3; $A$ after $B$ is guess 1.
Ranks: $2, 1, 1, 3, 1$. The round trip closes.

**Why it closes.** Both directions read only the preceding letters, which
are already decoded when they are needed. The maps are mutually inverse
position by position, so text and ranks carry the same information and have
the same entropy rate: measuring the rank distribution measures English.

**If the order depended on the following letter,** decoding would need a
letter it has not yet recovered. The transducer would no longer be
instantaneously reversible, the two rank orders at a position could
collide, and the recoding would stop preserving $N$-gram statistics. The
upper-bound proof uses exactly this property and nothing else about the
predictor, which is why it holds for a human guesser but not for an
arbitrary transformation of the text.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take the entropy rate of English as $1.2$ bits per letter on the 27-symbol
alphabet. A 1 MB file of English prose is stored as ASCII, one byte per
character. Compute the file's information content, the size a coder
achieving the entropy rate would produce, and the implied compression
factor. Then compress a real 1 MB text file with `gzip` and report the
factor it achieves. Account for the gap in one sentence.

<Solution>
**The ideal figure.** One megabyte of ASCII is $10^6$ characters at 8 bits,
so $8 \times 10^6$ bits. At $1.2$ bits per character the content is
$1.2 \times 10^6$ bits $= 150{,}000$ bytes, about $150$ kB. The implied
factor is $8 / 1.2 \approx 6.7$, and the redundancy relative to the
language's own $4.76$-bit alphabet is $1 - 1.2/4.76 \approx 75\%$.

**The measured figure.** `gzip` on English prose returns roughly $2.5$ to
$3.5$ times, that is around $300$–$400$ kB for the same megabyte, or about
$2.5$ bits per character. Modern general-purpose compressors do better:
`xz` and `zstd --ultra -22` typically reach $3.5$ to $4.5$ times on the same
text. None reaches $6.7$.

**The gap.** The ideal coder is charged nothing for knowing English; every
real compressor must pay for the model it uses, and the models these
programs can afford see a few thousand bytes of recent text rather than a
lifetime of reading.

This is the entire agenda of the next two modules.
<Ref lesson="dictionary-coding"/> shows why a compressor that learns the
source while reading it reaches the entropy rate at all, and why the
convergence is asymptotic, so a megabyte is nowhere near enough text.
<Ref lesson="deflate-and-zstd"/> reads `gzip`'s format field by field and
locates the $2.5$ bits per character in specific design decisions: a 32 kB
match window, per-block rather than per-file statistics, and whole-bit
codewords. The measured $2.5$ against the ideal $1.2$ is the price of not
knowing the source, and it is quantified exactly in
<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

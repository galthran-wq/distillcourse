Every mutual information in this module was computed from a known joint
distribution; estimating one from a finite sample is a different problem, and
the naive estimator reports shared information between independent variables.

This lesson closes the module on shared information.
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> defined $I(X;Y)$ as the bits one variable
removes from the other's uncertainty, the
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality">data-processing inequality</Ref>
put a ceiling over every stage of a pipeline, and
<Ref lesson="fano-inequality"/> converted the leftover bits into an error
floor. All three take the joint table as given. In practice nobody is given a
joint table: there are $n$ rows of data, and the table has to be built from
them. This lesson establishes what that substitution costs, and then reads one
published dispute end to end, in which the cost was the entire measurement.

The route: the plug-in estimator and its bias on independent data, the closed
form of that bias and its correction, the dependence of the whole answer on
bin width, and the deep-network information plane of 2017 to 2019, where a
quantity that provably cannot change was plotted changing.

## Independent variables score 0.29 bits

The estimator everyone writes first is the obvious one: build the empirical
table, then apply the definition to it as though the empirical table were the
truth.

alphabet. (2) State the largest value $H(X)$ can take when $X$ has $k$
possible outcomes. (3) State what $I(X;Y) = 0$ says about the joint table.
</Task>

<Aside>
(1) $I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$, and
$I(X;Y) = D\bigl(p(x,y) \,\Vert\, p(x)p(y)\bigr)$, the divergence between the

only for the uniform distribution
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>). (3) That $X$ and $Y$ are
independent: $p(x,y) = p(x)p(y)$ in every cell.
</Aside>

### The estimator

<Definition title="plug-in estimator">
Given $n$ independent draws $(x_1,y_1), \dots, (x_n,y_n)$ sorted into an
$r \times c$ table of cell counts $n_{ij}$, write
$\hat{p}_{ij} = n_{ij}/n$ for the empirical joint and $\hat{p}_{i\cdot}$,
$\hat{p}_{\cdot j}$ for its marginals. The **plug-in estimate** of mutual
information is
$$
\hat{I} \;=\; \sum_{i,j} \hat{p}_{ij}
\log_2 \frac{\hat{p}_{ij}}{\hat{p}_{i\cdot}\,\hat{p}_{\cdot j}},
$$
with cells of count zero contributing nothing. It is also called the
maximum-likelihood or naive estimator.
</Definition>

$\hat{I}$ is a mutual information: it is the divergence between the empirical
joint and the product of the empirical marginals, computed on a genuine
probability table. Divergence is zero only when the two arguments agree
exactly, so $\hat{I} = 0$ requires $n_{ij} = n_{i\cdot} n_{\cdot j} / n$ in
**every** cell at once. Finite samples do not do that.

### What it returns on data with nothing in it

Draw $n = 1000$ pairs $(x, y)$ with both coordinates uniform on $[0,1]$ and
drawn independently, so the true mutual information is exactly $0$ bits. Bin
each coordinate into $20$ equal intervals and apply the definition. Averaged
over $2{,}000$ independent repetitions the estimate is $0.293$ bits, and no
single repetition in that run came out below $0.22$ bits.

reported "shared information" grew by a factor of thirty.

<KeyFact>
The failure is not noise. A noisy estimator would scatter around the truth;
this one sits above it with probability essentially one, because the quantity
it computes is a divergence and divergences are non-negative. Averaging over
repeated experiments makes it more certain, not more accurate.
</KeyFact>

## The bias has a closed form: (r−1)(c−1) / (2n ln 2)

The estimate on independent data is not an arbitrary positive number. Its
expectation is fixed to leading order by the shape of the table and the sample
size, and by nothing else.

<Theorem title="plug-in bias under independence">
Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent, taking values in an $r \times c$ table with
every cell probability strictly positive, and let $\hat{I}_n$ be the plug-in
estimate from $n$ independent draws. Then
$$
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\hat{I}_n\bigr] \;=\;
\frac{(r-1)(c-1)}{2n \ln 2} \;+\; o\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)
\quad \text{bits}.
$$

<Proof>
**Step 1, the estimator is a divergence.** By construction
$\hat{I}_n = D\bigl(\hat{p}_{ij} \,\Vert\, \hat{p}_{i\cdot}\hat{p}_{\cdot j}\bigr)$,
the divergence of the empirical joint from the product of its own marginals.

**Step 2, expand.** For two distributions that differ by a small amount,
$D(P \Vert Q) = \frac{1}{2\ln 2}\sum_z \frac{(P(z)-Q(z))^2}{Q(z)} + O(\|P-Q\|^3)$,
which is the local quadratic behaviour of divergence. Applying it with
$P = \hat{p}_{ij}$ and $Q = \hat{p}_{i\cdot}\hat{p}_{\cdot j}$,
$$
\hat{I}_n \;\approx\; \frac{1}{2n \ln 2}\;
\underbrace{n \sum_{i,j}
\frac{\bigl(\hat{p}_{ij} - \hat{p}_{i\cdot}\hat{p}_{\cdot j}\bigr)^2}
     {\hat{p}_{i\cdot}\hat{p}_{\cdot j}}}_{\textstyle \chi^2}.
$$
The braced quantity is Pearson's statistic for the hypothesis of
independence.

**Step 3, quote the law.** Under independence, Pearson's statistic converges
in distribution to $\chi^2$ with $(r-1)(c-1)$ degrees of freedom, and the mean
of that law equals its degrees of freedom. The count is an accounting of free
parameters: a table on $rc$ cells has $rc - 1$ of them, and fitting the two
marginals uses $(r-1) + (c-1)$, leaving
$rc - 1 - (r-1) - (c-1) = (r-1)(c-1)$. Dividing by $2n \ln 2$ converts nats to
bits and gives the stated expectation.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Three readings of the formula, in order of how often each is needed.

- **The bias falls like $1/n$ and grows like the cell count.** Doubling the
  sample halves it; doubling the bins per axis roughly quadruples it.
- **It does not depend on the distribution**, only on the table's shape. That
  is what makes it a usable correction rather than a warning.
- **It is a floor on how much data a table needs.** To hold the artefact under
  $0.01$ bits with a $20 \times 20$ table takes $n \approx 26{,}000$.

<Example title="the headline number, predicted">
For the experiment of the previous section, $r = c = 20$ and $n = 1000$:
$$
\frac{(20-1)(20-1)}{2 \cdot 1000 \cdot \ln 2}
= \frac{361}{1386.3} = 0.260 \text{ bits}.
$$
The simulation returned $0.293$ bits, about $13\%$ higher. The gap is the
theorem's asymptotics failing, not the theorem failing: $1000$ points spread
over $400$ cells give an expected count of $2.5$ per cell, and the $\chi^2$
limit needs expected counts of roughly $5$ or more. Raising the sample to
$n = 3000$ puts $7.5$ points in each cell, and prediction and simulation land
at $0.087$ and $0.089$ bits.
</Example>

<Exercise>
A $5 \times 5$ table is built from $n = 200$ independent draws of two
independent variables. Predict the plug-in estimate, then state how large $n$
must be before the prediction drops below $0.01$ bits, and say which of the
two answers you should trust less and why.

<Solution>
**Prediction.** $(5-1)(5-1) = 16$ degrees of freedom, so
$\hat{I} \approx 16 / (2 \cdot 200 \cdot \ln 2) = 16/277.3 = 0.058$ bits. A
simulation of this exact setup returns $0.060$ bits: the expected count is
$200/25 = 8$ per cell, comfortably inside the regime the $\chi^2$ limit needs.

**Sample size.** Solve $16/(2n \ln 2) < 0.01$ for $n$:
$n > 16/(0.02 \ln 2) = 1154$. Round up to $n \approx 1200$.

**Which to trust less.** The first, marginally. The formula is an asymptotic
statement in $n$, and $n = 200$ is small; but with $8$ expected counts per
cell it is already accurate, as the simulation confirms. What deserves
suspicion is the same formula applied to a table with expected counts below
about $5$: the $20 \times 20$ table at $n = 1000$ understates the artefact by
$13\%$, and at $n = 100$, with a quarter of a point per cell, it predicts
$2.60$ bits against a measured $1.95$ and overshoots by a third. Neither
direction of error is safe, since the formula is used as a correction and a
correction is subtracted.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### The Miller–Madow correction removes most of it

The plug-in entropy estimate is biased too, and in the opposite direction:
$\mathbb{E}[\hat{H}] \approx H - (\hat{m}-1)/(2n \ln 2)$ bits, where $\hat{m}$
counts the occupied bins. Entropy is *underestimated* because an empirical
distribution is always more concentrated than the truth; mutual information is
*overestimated* because it is a divergence. Adding the entropy correction to
each of the three entropies in
$\hat{I} = \hat{H}(X) + \hat{H}(Y) - \hat{H}(X,Y)$ gives the standard
first-order fix.

<Definition title="Miller–Madow corrected estimate">
With $\hat{m}_{xy}$ occupied cells, $\hat{r}$ occupied rows and $\hat{c}$
occupied columns,
$$
\hat{I}_{\mathrm{MM}} \;=\; \hat{I}
\;-\; \frac{\hat{m}_{xy} - \hat{r} - \hat{c} + 1}{2n \ln 2} .
$$
When every cell is occupied the subtracted term is exactly
$(r-1)(c-1)/(2n \ln 2)$, cancelling the leading bias.
</Definition>

On the $n = 1000$, $20 \times 20$ experiment, $367$ of the $400$ cells are
occupied on average and all rows and columns are, so the correction subtracts
$0.237$ bits and leaves $\hat{I}_{\mathrm{MM}} = 0.057$ bits. That is a factor
of five better and still not zero. At $n = 3000$ the residue falls to $0.003$
bits. The correction removes the leading term of an expansion whose remaining
terms are $O(1/n^2)$ with coefficients that grow with the table, so it helps
most exactly where it is least needed.

<KeyFact>
The correction that always works is the shuffle. Permute the $y$ column
against the $x$ column and recompute: the shuffled pairs are independent by
construction, so the estimates they produce are a sample from the estimator's
own null distribution, whatever the table shape, sample size and cell
occupancy. A reported $\hat{I}$ means nothing until it is quoted against that
null, and the practice costs one line of code.
</KeyFact>

## Shrink the bins and the estimate climbs to log₂ n

Continuous variables have no cells until somebody makes some. The previous
section priced the choice of table for a fixed alphabet; here the choice of
table is the entire modelling decision, and the estimate moves through the
whole range of possible answers as it varies.

Fix one sample of $n = 4096$ pairs, drawn once and never redrawn:
$x$ uniform on $[0,1]$ and $y = (x + u)/2$ with $u$ an independent uniform
draw. This pair has an exactly computable mutual information,
$1/(2\ln 2) = 0.721$ bits. Sweep the bins per axis from $2$ to $1024$ and
recompute.

### Coarse bins lose the signal, fine bins invent it

Two errors run in opposite directions.
Coarse bins throw away the distinction the estimate is meant to detect: the
binned pair is a processed version of the original, so the
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality">data-processing inequality</Ref>
caps what any bin count can recover, and at $2$ bins per axis the true
information of the binned pair is only $0.189$ bits. Fine bins add the
artefact of the previous section, which grows without limit. Between them sits
a band, here roughly $8$ to $32$ bins per axis, inside which the estimate stays
within a quarter of the true $0.721$ bits and, at $32$ bins, within $5\%$.

<Example title="the two errors, added up">
The discretized truth can be computed exactly by integrating the joint density
over cells; the artefact is $(r-1)^2/(2n\ln 2)$ with $n = 4096$. At $8$ bins
per axis, the discretized truth is $0.546$ bits and the artefact $0.009$ bits,
predicting $0.555$; the measurement is $0.556$. At $16$ bins the two terms are
$0.628$ and $0.040$, predicting $0.668$ against a measured $0.664$. At $64$
bins the account fails, predicting $0.697 + 0.699 = 1.396$ against a measured
$1.079$, because one point per cell is far outside the $\chi^2$ regime.

Nothing in this two-term account is available to someone who does not already
know the answer. Both terms need the true joint distribution: the first to
integrate, the second to know that the table is well enough filled for the
formula to hold.
</Example>

### The ceiling is log₂ n, and the climb to it is slow

The climb has a hard stop, and knowing where it is turns the whole diagnosis
into arithmetic. With $n$ samples the empirical marginals put mass on at most
$n$ distinct values each, so
$\hat{I} \le \min\bigl(\hat{H}(X), \hat{H}(Y)\bigr) \le \log_2 n$ by the
uniform bound of <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>. Here that
ceiling is $\log_2 4096 = 12$ bits. It is attained when no two samples share a
row or a column: the empirical table is then a perfect matching, the empirical
$x$ determines the empirical $y$, and the estimate reports the maximum
regardless of what generated the data.

That condition is far stricter than one sample per cell. At $64$ bins per axis
the $4096$ cells hold one point each on average, and this sample scores
$1.089$ bits — under a tenth of the ceiling, because $64$ rows still hold $64$
points apiece. Emptying the rows is a birthday problem, so the bin count per
axis has to run past $n^2$: $4096$ bins per axis gives $10.15$ bits,
$65{,}536$ gives $11.85$, and coming within $0.01$ bits of $12$ takes about a
million bins per axis. The climb is monotone but slow through the middle of
the range — doubling this sample's bins from $8$ to $16$ to $32$ moves the
estimate by under $20\%$ each time — so a local sweep that looks stable is no
evidence that the bin width is right.

### Nearest neighbours remove the bin width, not the dimension

The grid is not the only way to get a number out of a continuous sample. The
standard alternative is the
[$k$-nearest-neighbour estimator of Kraskov, Stögbauer and Grassberger](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0305641),
usually called KSG: for each point take the distance to its $k$-th nearest
neighbour in the joint space, count how many points lie within that distance
in the $x$ coordinate alone and in the $y$ coordinate alone, and combine the
two counts through digamma functions. Resolution is then set locally by the
data — fine where points are dense, coarse where they are sparse — and the
only free parameter is the integer $k$, typically $2$ to $6$.

On the pair above it works. Over forty fresh samples of $4096$ points, KSG at
$k = 4$ returns $0.699$ bits with a standard deviation of $0.017$, against the
true $0.721$: a residual bias of $-3\%$, with nothing tuned. The plug-in on the
same forty samples returns $0.55$ bits at $8$ bins per axis, $0.65$ at $16$,
$0.76$ at $32$ and $1.09$ at $64$ — a spread the reader cannot resolve without
already knowing the answer.

What the method does not remove is the dimension. Run the same construction
with $X$ uniform on $[0,1]^d$ and each coordinate of $Y$ the average of the
matching coordinate of $X$ with fresh noise, so the truth is
$d/(2 \ln 2)$ bits and grows linearly in $d$. At $n = 4096$ samples throughout,
KSG recovers $96\%$ of the truth at $d = 1$, $60\%$ at $d = 4$ and $36\%$ at
$d = 8$: the estimate stalls near $2$ bits while the quantity being estimated
passes $5$. The sample size needed to keep up is exponential in $d$, and that
is a property of the problem rather than of this estimator — the same wall
appears at the end of this lesson as a constant growing like $c^{\,d}$ in the
error bound of a quite different estimator.

<KeyFact>
Where the pair is continuous and one variable determines the other exactly,
the true mutual information is infinite. The runaway estimate is then not a
failure of the estimator: it is converging on the correct answer, and no bin
width is the right one. The rest of this lesson examines a published case in
which exactly that sequence of numbers was read as a measurement of a system.
</KeyFact>

## The information plane: the 2017 claim

The module's results now get cashed out on a published artefact. Between 2017
and 2019 a specific claim about how neural networks learn was made,
challenged, and resolved, entirely through the quantity this module has been
building. Three of the module's results are load-bearing in what follows: the
definition of $I(X;Y)$, the layered chain
$I(X;T_1) \ge \dots \ge I(X;T_L)$ from the
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality">data-processing inequality</Ref>, and
the entropy bound
$H \le \log_2 n$ just used as a ceiling.

### The experiment: a seven-layer tanh classifier on 4096 points

The setup is a fully connected classifier with tanh activations and layer
widths $12$–$10$–$7$–$5$–$4$–$3$–$2$, trained by stochastic gradient descent
on a synthetic binary classification task with $12$-dimensional inputs and a
dataset of $n = 2^{12} = 4096$ points. For each hidden layer $T_\ell$ and each
sampled training epoch, two numbers are computed: the information the layer
retains about the input, and the information it carries about the label. Each
layer's pair of numbers traces a curve across training in the plane with
$I(X;T_\ell)$ on the horizontal axis and $I(T_\ell;Y)$ on the vertical. That
plane is the *information plane*.

### Every trajectory turns: a fitting phase, then a long compression phase

The curves have an elbow. Early in training both coordinates increase: the
layer learns about the label, which was expected, and about the input, which
was not being asked for. Then every trajectory turns and spends the remaining
epochs moving left, decreasing $I(X;T_\ell)$ while holding $I(T_\ell;Y)$
roughly constant. The two segments were named the *fitting* and *compression*
phases, and the second occupies almost all of training.

### The reading: compression explains generalization, and SGD causes compression

The reading offered was that the network sheds information about the input
that is irrelevant to the label, that this shedding explains why deep networks
generalize, and that stochastic gradient descent causes it: the compression
phase was reported to begin at the epoch where the ratio of the gradient's
mean to its standard deviation drops, dividing training into a directed phase
and a diffusive one. The layers also sit in the right order, deeper ones
further left, as the
data-processing inequality along the chain
$Y \to X \to T_1 \to \dots \to T_L$ requires.

## The rebuttal: compression tracks the activation function

If compression were a property of learning, changing the nonlinearity should
not remove it. It does.

### Saturation on both sides, not learning, produces the elbow

Rerunning the same architecture with ReLU units in place of tanh removes the
compression phase: $I(X;T_\ell)$ increases monotonically in every ReLU layer,
and only the final sigmoidal layer bends. The same contrast appears on a
$784$–$1024$–$20$–$20$–$20$–$10$ network trained on MNIST, and on the
soft-sign against soft-plus pair. The separating property is saturation on
both sides: activations bounded above and below produce the elbow, activations
bounded on one side do not.

### Compression is detached from generalization and from gradient noise

Two further claims come apart under the same treatment. Compression was
detached from generalization in both directions. A deep *linear* network in a
student–teacher setup, where the generalization error and the information
terms can both be computed in closed form, generalizes well and shows no
compression at all; sized to match the number of samples so that it badly
overfits, its information-plane trajectory does not change. In the other
direction, the original tanh network retrained on $30\%$ of the data overfits
substantially and still compresses. And compression was detached from
stochastic gradients: training the same networks with full-batch gradient
descent, which has no gradient noise to diffuse on, produces trajectories that
look the same as the stochastic ones. The high-to-low gradient signal-to-noise
transition does occur, in every configuration tried, but it occurs
independently of whether any compression appears. Throughout, the rebuttal
accepted the published measurements and attacked the interpretation put on
them.

## The plotted quantity does not depend on the weights

Both papers plot a curve against training epoch and read its shape as a
statement about the network. For that reading to be available, the quantity
has to depend on the network's parameters. It does not.

A deterministic network computes $T_\ell = \varphi_\ell(X)$, a fixed function
of the input for fixed weights. Two cases exhaust the modelling choices, and
neither leaves anything to measure.

### Continuous input: the quantity is infinite at every layer

<Theorem title="vacuous mutual information in deterministic networks (Amjad &amp; Geiger 2019, Theorem 1)">
Let $X$ be a $d$-dimensional random variable whose distribution has an
absolutely continuous component with a density that is continuous on a compact
subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$, and let the activation function be bi-Lipschitz, or
continuously differentiable with strictly positive derivative. Then for every
layer $\ell$ and almost every choice of weight matrices,
$$
I(X;T_\ell) = \infty .
$$
</Theorem>

The reason is the one from the previous section, applied to a network instead
of a bin grid, and it is not injectivity. The layers here contract dimension at
every stage, $12$ inputs to $10$ to $7$ and down to $2$, so no layer map is
invertible and $T_\ell$ does not determine $X$. What the hypotheses buy is
weaker and sufficient: the input's absolutely continuous component has positive
correlation dimension, a bi-Lipschitz or strictly increasing map cannot drive
that dimension to zero, and so $T_\ell$ stays spread over a continuum instead of
collapsing onto countably many points, however narrow the layer. Cut the layer's
range into $k$ cells and record which cell the representation lands in: the
record $Q_k(T_\ell)$ is a discrete variable, and $H(Q_k(T_\ell))$ grows without
bound as the cells shrink, at a rate set by that dimension. It is
also a deterministic function of $X$, so $I(X;Q_k(T_\ell)) = H(Q_k(T_\ell))$,
and it is a processed version of $T_\ell$, so the
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality">data-processing inequality</Ref> gives
$I(X;T_\ell) \ge I(X;Q_k(T_\ell)) = H(Q_k(T_\ell))$ for every $k$ at once. That
puts $I(X;T_\ell)$ above every finite number. Nothing about
training enters, and $\infty$ does not vary with the weights.

### Input uniform on the dataset: the quantity is 12 bits at every layer

The alternative modelling choice, the one actually used in the experiments,
makes $X$ uniform on the $n$ points of the dataset. That caps the quantity at
$H(X) = \log_2 n$ and creates the opposite problem. Here injectivity does hold,
because the map to be inverted is a map between finite sets: two of the $4096$
points collide at layer $\ell$ only on a measure-zero set of weight matrices,
so the $4096$ representations are distinct for almost every weight setting even
though the layer is two units wide. An injective map is a sufficient statistic
for every target, the equality case of the
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality">data-processing inequality</Ref>.
Hence
$$
I(X;T_\ell) = H(X) = \log_2 4096 = 12 \text{ bits},
\qquad I(T_\ell;Y) = I(X;Y),
$$
for every layer, every epoch, and every weight setting. The horizontal axis of
the information plane holds still at $12$ bits for the entire run.

  caption="The chain is flat until noise is added: injective layers all sit at H(X) = 12 bits, and only the noisy versions move."
/>

The data-processing inequality is not violated by any of this. It says the
sequence is non-increasing, and a constant sequence is non-increasing; the
layered corollary permits a stage to lose nothing, which is exactly what an
injective stage does. The published ordering of layers from left to right was
read as the inequality biting, and the inequality was in fact silent.

<KeyFact>
The correct statement is that the curves are measurements of the measuring
apparatus. A quantity that is constant in the weights cannot produce a
trajectory across training; every wiggle in the published plots therefore
comes from the estimator, and the useful question is what the estimator was
responding to.
</KeyFact>

## What the published curves measure: the entropy of a quantizer

The experiments never computed $I(X;T_\ell)$. They computed a quantized proxy,
and the proxy has a closed form that identifies it exactly.

Let $Q_m[T_\ell]$ cut the layer's bounded range, the hypercube $[-1,1]^{d_\ell}$
for tanh units, into $m$ equal intervals per coordinate, and replace each
representation by the cell it falls in. Because $T_\ell$ is a deterministic
function of $X$, so is $Q_m[T_\ell]$, and for a deterministic function
$H(Q_m[T_\ell] \mid X) = 0$. Therefore
$$
I\bigl(X; Q_m[T_\ell]\bigr)
= H\bigl(Q_m[T_\ell]\bigr) - H\bigl(Q_m[T_\ell] \mid X\bigr)
= H\bigl(Q_m[T_\ell]\bigr),
$$
computed by counting how many of the $4096$ inputs land in each cell. The
plotted quantity is an entropy of a histogram of representations, and $m$ is
chosen by the experimenter: no quantizer exists anywhere in the network being
studied.

<Figure
  src="./figures/bin-size-artifact.png"
  alt="Four side-by-side panels plotting the quantized proxy I(X;Q_m[T_ell]) against training epoch on a log axis from 1 to 10^4, one panel per quantization cell size 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01 and 0.1, with five coloured curves for layers 1 to 5 in each. At cell size 0.0001 all five layers lie flat just above 8 for almost every epoch, layer 5 dropping only at the very end; as the cell size coarsens the deeper layers peel away and develop dips and rises, and at cell size 0.1 layers 3, 4 and 5 start near 0 to 4 and wander across the whole range"
  caption="Four measurements of one training run, differing only in the experimenter's bin size. At cell size 0.0001 every layer sits flat at the saturation value, about 8.3 nats; the compression elbow appears only as the bins coarsen."
/>

### Four bin sizes, four different pictures of one training run

Four bin sizes give four qualitatively different pictures of the same training
run. At the finest setting, cell size $10^{-4}$, every layer sits flat across
almost all epochs at $\ln(2^{12}) \approx 8.3$ nats, which is $12$ bits: the
quantizer has become fine enough to separate all $4096$ points, the proxy has
converged to the true value, and the true value is the constant of the
previous section. At coarser settings the curves develop the elbow. The
compression phase appears and disappears as a function of a parameter of the
measurement.

<Remark title="the proxy does not inherit the inequality">
The layered ordering that made the published plots look theoretically
constrained does not survive quantization. The data-processing inequality
applies to $I(X;T_\ell)$, which is constant; the quantized proxies
$I(X;Q_m[T_\ell])$ are entropies of separately quantized variables and obey no
such chain, so a deeper layer scoring higher than a shallower one would
falsify nothing.
</Remark>

## The resolution: compression is clustering

The proxy is not measuring mutual information. It is measuring something, it
moves in a consistent direction late in training, and the identity
$I(X;Q_m[T_\ell]) = H(Q_m[T_\ell])$ says what.

### The proxy falls exactly when representations concentrate

An entropy of a histogram falls when the histogram concentrates. Uniform
occupancy over $k$ cells gives the maximum $\log_2 k$
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>); mass piled into a few cells
gives much less. So $H(Q_m[T_\ell])$ falls exactly when representations that
were spread across many cells move into few, which is to say when points in
the layer's representation space cluster.

<Figure
  src="./figures/clustering-entropy.svg"
  alt="Two panels of 128 two-class points under the same 16 by 16 grid: spread points occupy 83 cells and give H = 6.20 bits, clustered points occupy 13 cells and give H = 3.10 bits"
  caption="The same 128 points, the same grid: pulling same-class points together empties 70 cells and drops the tallied entropy by 3.1 bits."
/>

### Injected noise makes the quantity well posed, and it agrees with the scatter plots

Confirming that this is what the networks do required making the information
quantity well posed first. Replacing each layer's output by
$T_\ell^{(\sigma)} = T_\ell + Z_\ell$ with $Z_\ell$ isotropic Gaussian noise of
scale $\sigma$, and training the network with the noisy activations rather
than adding noise only at measurement time, turns the map from input to
representation into a genuine stochastic channel. Then $I(X;T_\ell^{(\sigma)})$
is finite, depends on the weights, and is worth estimating. At $\sigma$ of
order $10^{-2}$ such a network performs like its deterministic counterpart and
learns closely related representations.

<Figure
  src="./figures/compression-is-clustering.png"
  alt="Left: mutual information in nats for five hidden layers against training epoch on a log axis — layers 1 to 3 flat near 8 nats, layer 4 in olive and layer 5 in cyan rising to peaks near epochs 28 and 541 and then falling, layer 5 reaching about 2 nats by epoch 10,000 — with train and test loss plotted below falling from 0.8 to near 0; four grey arrows mark epochs 28, 80, 541 and one near 9,000. Right: four scatter plots of the fifth hidden layer's representation at those four epochs, drawn in a 3-D axis box with grey shadow projections, points coloured orange and blue by class — smeared along one continuous band at epochs 28 and 541, collapsed into a few tight same-class clumps at epoch 80 and at the final epoch."
  caption="Read the arrows across. Layer 5's information (cyan) is high at epochs 28 and 541, where the two classes lie smeared along a single continuous band, and low at epoch 80 and at the end of training, where the same points have collapsed into a few tight same-class clumps. Train and test loss fall throughout: the information tracks the clustering, not the learning."
/>

Measured this way, the compression phase is real: $I(X;T_\ell^{(\sigma)})$
does undergo a long decrease late in training. Set the estimate beside scatter
plots of the same layer's representations at the marked epochs and the two
line up. Same-class representations draw together; the noisy channel from
representation to observation can no longer tell them apart; the mutual
information falls. The mechanism is geometric, and the information-theoretic
reading was tracking it through a proxy that happened to be sensitive to the
same thing.

### The price: risk exponential in the layer's width

The estimator that makes this measurable carries the cost of the whole
enterprise in its error bound. Exploiting the known law of the injected noise,
the estimation risk for a layer of width $d_\ell$ satisfies
$$
\sup_{P_X} \mathbb{E}\left|\, I(X;T_\ell^{(\sigma)}) - \hat{I} \,\right|
\;\le\; \frac{8 c^{\,d_\ell} + d_\ell \log\bigl(1 + \sigma^{-2}\bigr)}{4\sqrt{n}},
$$
for a constant $c$ independent of the sample and the distribution. The rate in
$n$ is the usual $n^{-1/2}$; the constant is exponential in the width of the
layer, and the matching lower bound shows this is not an artefact of the
method. Estimating a mutual information in high dimension is not merely hard,
it is provably exponentially expensive in the dimension, which is why every
experiment in this literature uses layers a handful of units wide.

<KeyFact>
Refuted: the identification of the plotted curve with $I(X;T)$, and with it
every inference from the curve's shape to a network shedding input
information. Survived: that trained classifiers progressively cluster
same-class representations, at a rate set by the activation function and
movable in either direction relative to generalization — a real phenomenon
that had no name before the dispute.
</KeyFact>

## What to report instead

Three responses are available, and they differ in cost. A better estimator is
not among them: the rebuttal recomputed its information planes with
$k$-nearest-neighbour and kernel-density estimators alongside the quantizer,
and changing estimator addresses the error in the measurement rather than the
target of it. For a deterministic network the target is infinite under a
continuous input and constant under the dataset input, and no accuracy makes
an infinite or constant quantity move with training.

- **Make the quantity well posed.** Injecting noise into the system, not into
  the measurement, gives a mutual information that is finite,
  weight-dependent and estimable. The price is that the network being studied
  is no longer the network being deployed, and the estimator's constant grows
  exponentially in layer width.
- **Bound it instead of estimating it.** A lower bound computed from samples
  sidesteps the density estimation entirely, which is how mutual information
  is handled in modern representation learning; the construction and its
  ceiling are the subject of <Ref lesson="infonce">the InfoNCE bound</Ref>.
- **Report what was computed, under its own name.** $H(Q_m[T])$ at a stated
  $m$, with the shuffle control beside it, is an honest measurement of
  clustering. Relabelled $I(X;T)$ it is a claim about a quantity that was
  never computed.

Established: the plug-in estimator of mutual information is biased upward by
$(r-1)(c-1)/(2n\ln 2)$ bits, the bias is removable to first order and never
fully, the answer for continuous variables is a function of the bin width and
climbs to $\log_2 n$ as bins shrink, and in a deterministic network the
quantity the information plane plots is a constant that the quantizer, not the
training, was making move. That closes the module on what one variable says
about another. <Ref lesson="channel-capacity">The next lesson</Ref> stops
measuring mutual information and starts maximizing it: over all input
distributions to a fixed noisy channel, the largest achievable $I(X;Y)$ is
that channel's capacity.

<Exercise>
The table below counts $n = 160$ observations of two four-valued variables.
Compute the plug-in estimate $\hat{I}$ and the Miller–Madow corrected value,
and report both.

| $n_{ij}$ | $j=1$ | $j=2$ | $j=3$ | $j=4$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $i=1$ | $20$ | $10$ | $6$ | $4$ |
| $i=2$ | $10$ | $20$ | $6$ | $4$ |
| $i=3$ | $5$ | $5$ | $15$ | $15$ |
| $i=4$ | $5$ | $5$ | $15$ | $15$ |

<Solution>
**Marginals.** Rows: $40, 40, 40, 40$, so $\hat{p}_{i\cdot} = 1/4$ each and
$\hat{H}(X) = 2$ bits. Columns: $40, 40, 42, 38$, so
$\hat{p}_{\cdot j} = 0.25, 0.25, 0.2625, 0.2375$ and
$\hat{H}(Y) = 1.9991$ bits.

**Joint entropy.** The distinct cell probabilities are $20/160 = 0.125$ (twice),
$10/160 = 0.0625$ (twice), $6/160 = 0.0375$ (twice), $4/160 = 0.025$ (twice),
$5/160 = 0.03125$ (four times) and $15/160 = 0.09375$ (four times), and their
$-p\log_2 p$ contributions are $0.750$, $0.500$, $0.355$, $0.266$, $0.625$ and
$1.281$ respectively:
$$
\hat{H}(X,Y) = 0.750 + 0.500 + 0.355 + 0.266 + 0.625 + 1.281
= 3.777 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Plug-in.** $\hat{I} = 2 + 1.9991 - 3.777 = 0.222$ bits.

**Miller–Madow.** All $16$ cells, all $4$ rows and all $4$ columns are
occupied, so the correction is
$$
\frac{16 - 4 - 4 + 1}{2 \cdot 160 \cdot \ln 2} = \frac{9}{221.8}
= 0.041 \text{ bits},
$$
giving $\hat{I}_{\mathrm{MM}} = 0.222 - 0.041 = 0.181$ bits. The correction is
$18\%$ of the raw value: with $10$ expected counts per cell the table is well
filled, which is exactly the regime in which the correction is small and
trustworthy. A table with the same total spread over $30 \times 30$ cells
would have a correction larger than the estimate.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two independent fair coins are tossed $n = 20$ times and the results tabulated
in a $2 \times 2$ table. (a) Compute the expected plug-in estimate from the
bias formula. (b) Now imagine replacing the coins by two continuous variables
and increasing the number of bins per axis toward $n$. State the value the
plug-in estimate approaches, and explain why the limit is
$\min\bigl(\hat{H}(X), \hat{H}(Y)\bigr)$ rather than $0$. Write down what the
empirical table looks like at that limit before opening the solution.

<Solution>
**(a)** $(2-1)(2-1) = 1$ degree of freedom, so
$\hat{I} \approx 1/(2 \cdot 20 \cdot \ln 2) = 0.036$ bits. Small, but the true
value is $0$ and the estimate is above it with probability one unless the
table comes out exactly balanced.

**(b)** The estimate approaches $\log_2 20 \approx 4.32$ bits, the largest
value any estimate from $20$ samples can take.

Once the bins are fine enough that no two of the $20$ samples share an $x$-bin
or a $y$-bin, the empirical table has exactly one occupied cell in each
occupied row and each occupied column: a perfect matching. In that table the
empirical $x$ determines the empirical $y$ with certainty, so
$\hat{H}(Y \mid X) = 0$ and $\hat{I} = \hat{H}(Y) = \log_2 20$, and by the same
argument $\hat{I} = \hat{H}(X)$. Independence of the underlying variables plays
no part: the estimator sees a table, and this table is a bijection.

The reason the limit is not $0$ is that the plug-in estimator has no access to
anything but the empirical table, and every sample is a distinct pair. To
report $0$ it would need cells with counts matching the product of the
marginals, and with one sample per cell there is nothing for the counts to
agree with. The maximum is $\min(\hat{H}(X), \hat{H}(Y))$ rather than
$\log_2 n$ in general because a tie in either coordinate merges two rows or
two columns, and merging can only reduce the entropy of that marginal.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim. "In the published information plane, layer $4$ has a lower
$I(X;T)$ than layer $2$ at every epoch, so the network is discarding
information about the input as it goes deeper, and the discarded bits are the
ones irrelevant to the label." State the two separate conditions under which
this reading is invalid, say what quantity the plotted numbers actually
describe, and name one measurement that would distinguish the two readings.

<Solution>
**Condition one: the network is deterministic.** For a deterministic network
with strictly monotone activations and a discrete input uniform on the
dataset, every layer map is injective almost surely, so
$I(X;T_\ell) = H(X) = \log_2 n$ for every $\ell$. The true quantity is equal
across layers, not decreasing, and it is a constant of the dataset rather than
a property of the network. For a continuous input the same hypotheses give
$I(X;T_\ell) = \infty$ at every layer.

**Condition two: the plotted numbers are quantized proxies.** What was
computed is $I(X;Q_m[T_\ell]) = H(Q_m[T_\ell])$ for a user-chosen bin size
$m$. The data-processing inequality governs $I(X;T_\ell)$ and says nothing
about the quantized versions, so the observed ordering across layers is not an
instance of the theorem and carries no theoretical warrant.

**What the numbers describe.** The entropy of the histogram of the layer's
representations under the chosen quantizer: a measure of how many cells the
representations occupy and how evenly. It falls when same-class
representations cluster.

**The discriminating measurement.** Recompute the entire plot at two bin sizes
separated by two orders of magnitude. A genuine mutual information is a
property of the joint distribution and cannot depend on the observer's bin
size, so agreement between the two plots is what the information reading
predicts; the published curves instead change shape, and at the finest bin
size flatten onto $\log_2 n$. A second, sharper test is to compare the
trajectory of an accurately estimated $I(X;T^{(\sigma)})$ in a network trained
with intrinsic noise against scatter plots of the same layer's
representations.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Carry out the degeneracy argument the lesson quoted. (a) Prove that for a
discrete $X$ and an injective map $T = f(X)$, $I(X;T) = H(X)$ exactly,
independent of $f$. (b) State what changes when $f$ is many-to-one, and give
the exact expression for the loss. (c) State what changes when $X$ is
continuous and $f$ is injective and continuous, and say which step of part (a)
breaks.

<Solution>
**(a)** $T = f(X)$ is a function of $X$, so $H(T \mid X) = 0$. Injectivity
gives the reverse: $X = f^{-1}(T)$ is a function of $T$, so $H(X \mid T) = 0$.
Then
$$
I(X;T) = H(X) - H(X \mid T) = H(X) - 0 = H(X),
$$
and symmetrically $I(X;T) = H(T)$, so $H(T) = H(X)$ as well. Nothing about $f$
beyond injectivity entered, so relabelling the outcomes in any invertible way
leaves the quantity fixed. This is the equality case of the data-processing
inequality (<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>): an invertible map is
a sufficient statistic for every target.

**(b)** With $f$ many-to-one, $H(X \mid T)$ is no longer zero, and
$$
I(X;T) = H(X) - H(X \mid T)
= H(X) - \sum_t p(t)\, H\bigl(X \mid T = t\bigr).
$$
Each merged group contributes the entropy of the conditional distribution of
$X$ inside that group, weighted by the group's probability. A map that merges
two outcomes of equal probability $q$ loses $2q$ bits when $q$ is the mass of
each, since the conditional distribution inside the merged symbol is a fair
coin: the loss is the entropy destroyed, not the number of symbols removed.

**(c)** With $X$ continuous, $H(X)$ is not defined as a finite number; the
step that breaks is the very first one, since discrete entropy has no
continuous counterpart with the same properties. The conclusion becomes
$I(X;T) = \infty$: for each $k$, quantizing $X$ to $k$ levels gives a discrete
$X_k$ that $T$ still determines, so $I(X;T) \ge I(X_k;T) = H(X_k)$, and
$H(X_k) \to \infty$ as the quantization refines. The mutual information is
above every finite bound.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Reproduce the bin-size artefact yourself. Generate $4096$ samples from any
two-dimensional distribution you like, push them through a fixed random map
into two dimensions, and compute $I(X;Q_m[T])$ for four bin widths spanning
three orders of magnitude. Report all four numbers alongside $\log_2 4096$,
and state in advance what agreement with the published information-plane
claim would look like.

<Solution>
**What to compute.** With $X$ uniform on the $4096$ generated points and $T$
their images under the fixed map, $Q_m[T]$ is the cell index and
$I(X;Q_m[T]) = H(Q_m[T])$, computed by tallying how many of the $4096$ points
fall in each cell and taking $-\sum_k (n_k/4096)\log_2 (n_k/4096)$. No model
fitting and no density estimation is involved: it is a histogram.

**What to expect.** At the coarsest width, cells wide enough that a handful of
them hold everything, a value of a few bits. At the finest, every point in its
own cell, exactly $\log_2 4096 = 12$ bits. In between, a monotone climb. The
four numbers will span most of the range from $2$ to $12$ bits on the same
data and the same map.

**What agreement would look like.** The claim under test is that the plotted
quantity measures $I(X;T)$, a functional of the joint distribution of $X$ and
$T$. A functional of the joint distribution does not depend on a parameter the
observer chose, so agreement means the four numbers coincide to within
estimation noise. They will not. The correct conclusion is not that the
computation is wrong but that it is a computation of $H(Q_m[T])$, which is a
perfectly good measurement of how spread out the representations are, at the
resolution $m$.

**The control to run alongside.** Shuffle the assignment of points to images
and recompute. Here the shuffle leaves $H(Q_m[T])$ unchanged, since it is a
function of the multiset of images alone, and that invariance is itself
informative: a quantity that a shuffle cannot move is not measuring an
association between two variables.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague reports $I(\text{feature};\text{label}) = 0.8$ bits on a dataset
of $500$ rows, where the feature is categorical with $30$ levels and the label
is binary, and proposes to drop every other feature on that basis. Decide
whether to believe the number, showing the calculations that drive the
decision. Use $H_2(0.031) = 0.20$.

<Solution>
**Bias check.** The table is $30 \times 2$, so
$$
\frac{(30-1)(2-1)}{2 \cdot 500 \cdot \ln 2} = \frac{29}{693.1}
= 0.042 \text{ bits}.
$$
Estimation bias accounts for about $5\%$ of the reported figure, so the number
is not an artefact of the estimator. The expected count per cell is
$500/60 = 8.3$, just inside the regime where that formula is reliable; if the
levels are unbalanced, many cells will be nearly empty and the true bias will
be larger, but not by a factor of twenty.

**Ceiling check.** $I \le \min(H(\text{feature}), H(\text{label}))$, and the
label is binary, so $H(\text{label}) \le 1$ bit. A reported $0.8$ bits says
the feature removes four fifths of all the uncertainty there is.

**Fano check: the accuracy ceiling.** Take the label balanced, so $H(\text{label}) = 1$
bit and $H(\text{label} \mid \text{feature}) = 1 - 0.8 = 0.2$ bits exactly.
Fano's inequality for a binary target reads
$H(\text{label} \mid \text{feature}) \le H_2(P_e)$
(<Ref lesson="fano-inequality"/>), so $H_2(P_e) \ge 0.20$ and therefore
$P_e \ge 0.031$. Fano runs in the impossibility direction only: it says no rule
computed from this feature, lookup table or boosted tree or anything else, beats
about $97\%$ accuracy. It is the obstacle, not a licence, and it never asserts
that any rule attains the floor.

**The accuracy floor, from the other side.** A guarantee that accuracy is high has to
come from a different inequality. For a binary target the majority-label rule
errs with $P_e \le H(\text{label} \mid \text{feature})/2$ (the Hellman–Raviv
bound), so $H(\text{label} \mid \text{feature}) \le 0.2$ bits forces
$P_e \le 0.1$. If the $0.8$ bits are real, predicting the majority label inside
each of the $30$ levels reaches at least $90\%$ accuracy on the population the
sample came from. That is a testable consequence, and the interval $90$–$97\%$
is what the reported number commits the colleague to.

**Decision.** Do not believe it without one further measurement, and the
measurement is not information-theoretic. Fit the trivial classifier that
predicts the majority label within each of the $30$ levels, and score it on
held-out rows. If it lands in the $90$–$97\%$ band, the estimate is real and the
colleague's proposal is sound. If it lands near chance, the $0.8$ bits is
memorization of a small sample by a high-cardinality feature, and the shuffle
control will confirm it: permute the label column and recompute, and compare
the reported $0.8$ against the distribution of shuffled values.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
You need $I(X;Y)$ where $X$ is a photograph and $Y$ is its caption.
(a) Explain why binning is hopeless here, quantitatively. (b) Design a scheme
that estimates a *lower* bound instead: train a scoring function to pick the
true caption for an image out of one true and $N-1$ impostor captions, and say
what a low error rate would certify. (c) Work out the largest bound such a
scheme could ever produce, and state what happens to a very good
representation once it exceeds that.

<Solution>
**(a)** A modest image is $224 \times 224 \times 3$ bytes, so $X$ lives in a
space of about $150{,}000$ dimensions. Binning each coordinate into even $2$
cells gives $2^{150000}$ cells, against a dataset of at most $10^9$ images:
the expected count per cell is zero to hundreds of decimal places, every
sample occupies its own cell, and the plug-in estimate returns
$\log_2 n$ exactly, as the boundary exercise above showed. The number would be
the logarithm of the dataset size and nothing else. The estimator's error
constant makes the same point in reverse: the risk bound of this lesson grows
like $c^{\,d}$ in the dimension $d$, so the sample size required is
exponential in $150{,}000$.

**(b)** Take a batch of $N$ image–caption pairs. For an image $x$, score it
against all $N$ captions with a learned function $f(x, y)$, and train the
softmax over those $N$ scores to select the caption that truly accompanied
$x$. The loss is an ordinary $N$-way cross-entropy
(<Ref lesson="cross-entropy-loss"/>), computable from samples with no density
estimation anywhere. Low loss certifies that image and caption share enough
information to identify each other among $N$ candidates: the shared
information is at least what is needed to win that discrimination.

**(c)** A classifier choosing among $N$ equiprobable options can carry at most
$\log_2 N$ bits, since the entropy of the uniform distribution on $N$ labels
is $\log_2 N$ (<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>). Even a scheme
that never errs certifies only that much, so the bound is capped at $\log_2 N$
and a batch of $256$ certifies at most $8$ bits. Beyond that point a strong
representation and a merely adequate one produce the same saturated number,
and the estimate stops distinguishing them. This is the InfoNCE bound
$I(X;Y) \ge \log_2 N - L_N$, derived and its saturation examined in
<Ref lesson="infonce">the InfoNCE lesson</Ref>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

A classifier trained to pick the true partner of a context out of N
candidates yields a mutual-information estimate: I(X;Y) >= log2 N - L_N,
where L_N is the contrastive cross-entropy loss.

The course's argument reached mutual information as a number computed from
a joint table, and then established that the table is exactly what a
practitioner does not have: <Ref lesson="estimating-mutual-information"/>
showed that plug-in estimates of $I(X;Y)$ from binned samples manufacture
bits that are not there, and that the sample cost of getting them right
grows exponentially with dimension. What stands open is whether anything
about $I(X;Y)$ can be certified for images, audio or text at all. This
lesson answers with a construction that touches no density: pose the
estimation as a classification problem, train a critic, and read a floor
under $I(X;Y)$ off the loss. The floor is genuine and the instrument is
narrow, and the second fact is the more useful one.

The route: what a sample-only estimator has to work with, the $N$-way
contrastive task and its loss, the proof that the loss-minimizing critic
scores candidates by the density ratio $p(y \mid x)/p(y)$, an exact
derivation of the bound with its slack in closed form, the numbers on a toy
pair, the $\log_2 N$ ceiling that no amount of dependence can lift, and the
separation between what the bound measures and what a representation is
worth.

## What a sample-only estimator has to work with

The obstacle is not that the joint table is large. It is that it does not
exist. Let $X$ be a $32 \times 32$ RGB image and $Y$ a sentence describing
it: $X$ alone ranges over $256^{3072}$ values, so the table has
astronomically more cells than the observable universe has atoms
($10^{80}$). Estimating a cell's probability by counting requires observing
that cell many times, and no dataset visits more than a vanishing fraction
of cells even once. Binning the space coarsely enough to fill the cells
does not rescue the estimate; it replaces it with a measurement of the bin
width.

What a dataset does provide is two streams of samples, and every method in
this lesson is built from them alone.

- **Paired draws.** The recorded pairs $(x_i, y_i) \sim p(x,y)$: an image
  with its caption, a waveform window with the window that follows it.
- **Shuffled draws.** Detaching each $y_i$ from its own context turns it
  into a draw from the marginal $p(y)$, because a caption picked at random
  from the dataset is exactly that. Marginal samples are free.

The estimation problem is therefore: build, out of paired and shuffled
samples plus a trainable function, a computable number that says something
true about $I(X;Y)$. "Something true" will turn out to mean a lower bound,
and the restriction is not a weakness of the particular construction below.
Any procedure that certifies a lower bound on mutual information from $n$
samples with high confidence, without assuming anything about the
distribution, is capped at about $\log n$ bits
([McAllester & Stratos, 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04251)).

<KeyFact>
The $\log_2 N$ ceiling derived later in this lesson is one instance of that
limit rather than a defect of the particular construction, which is why a
reported "30 bits of mutual information" between high-dimensional variables
is a statement about the method used, not about the data.
</KeyFact>

## The contrastive task: one true partner among N candidates

The two sample streams admit exactly one natural game: mix them and ask
which is which. Present a context together with $N$ candidates, one of them
its recorded partner and the rest shuffled in, and require the true one to
be identified.

### One round: sample, shuffle, classify

One round runs in three steps.

1. **Draw the positive.** One pair $(x, y_{\text{pos}}) \sim p(x,y)$: the
   context and its true partner.
2. **Draw the negatives.** $N-1$ candidates $y_1, \ldots, y_{N-1}$, each an
   independent draw from the marginal $p(y)$.
3. **Classify.** Present $x$ and the $N$ candidates in random order; the
   classifier names the position it believes holds the partner.

A classifier for this task is built from a *critic*: a function
$f(x, y) > 0$ scoring how well $y$ fits $x$. The $N$ scores are normalized
by a softmax into a distribution over positions, and the round bills the
classifier the surprisal of the weight it placed on the true position. That
is a one-hot cross-entropy with $N$ classes, the loss of
<Ref lesson="cross-entropy-loss"/>, computed on a label the dataset
generates for itself.

### The loss, and what chance costs

<Definition title="InfoNCE loss">
Given a joint distribution $p(x,y)$, a candidate count $N \ge 1$, and a
critic $f(x,y) > 0$, the **InfoNCE loss** is
$$
L_N[f] = \mathbb{E}\left[
  -\log_2 \frac{f(x, y_{\text{pos}})}
  {f(x, y_{\text{pos}}) + \sum_{j=1}^{N-1} f(x, y_j)}
\right] \quad \text{bits},
$$
the expectation running over the positive pair
$(x, y_{\text{pos}}) \sim p(x,y)$ and the negatives
$y_1, \ldots, y_{N-1} \sim p(y)$, drawn independently.
</Definition>

**Chance baseline:** a critic that has learned nothing, a constant $f$,
makes the softmax uniform, puts weight $\tfrac1N$ on every candidate, and
pays $L_N = \log_2 N$ bits per round. Any smaller loss is evidence that the
critic tells recorded pairs from shuffled ones.

### The running toy pair

The running example is the dependence-dial table of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> at agreement $q = 0.75$: binary $X$ and
$Y$ with uniform marginals, diagonal cells $\tfrac38$, off-diagonal cells
$\tfrac18$. Its conditionals are $p(y{=}x \mid x) = 0.75$ and its mutual
information is $I(X;Y) = 1 - H_2(0.75) \approx 0.189$ bits. It is small
enough to enumerate exactly, so every claim below can be checked by hand
rather than sampled.

<Remark title="the negatives are not a free design choice">
Everything derived below identifies ratios **against $p(y)$**, so the
negatives must be marginal draws, which in practice means real partners
borrowed from other pairs in the batch. This is the single hypothesis every
result in this lesson is conditional on. Sampling the negatives from a
convenient noise model $q$ instead changes what the loss bounds, and
changes it upward: the certificate then converges on a value that *exceeds*
$I(X;Y)$ by the mismatch between $q$ and $p(y)$, so the one-way guarantee
that makes the number safe to report is gone. The closing exercises price
the mismatch exactly. This is also the difference between InfoNCE and
generic noise-contrastive estimation, where the noise distribution is
chosen by the designer to make an unnormalized model fittable.
</Remark>

## The optimal critic is a density ratio

The loss is defined for any critic. What the classifier is forced to learn
is fixed by which critic minimizes it, and the answer is neither of the
distributions involved.

### Bayes on one concrete round

Take the toy pair at $N = 2$, context $x = 0$, and suppose the two
candidates presented are $y_1 = 0$ and $y_2 = 1$. Over the two position
hypotheses, with a uniform prior, Bayes' rule gives

$$
p(\text{pos}{=}1 \mid \text{data})
= \frac{p(0 \mid x{=}0)\, p(1)}{p(0 \mid x{=}0)\, p(1) + p(1 \mid x{=}0)\, p(0)}
= \frac{0.75 \cdot 0.5}{0.75 \cdot 0.5 + 0.25 \cdot 0.5} = 0.75.
$$

Each hypothesis contributes one conditional factor (for the candidate
claimed to be the partner) and one marginal factor (for the other).
Dividing numerator and denominator by $p(y_1)\,p(y_2) = 0.25$ rewrites the
posterior in a portable form:

$$
p(\text{pos}{=}1 \mid \text{data})
= \frac{p(0 \mid 0)/p(0)}{\,p(0 \mid 0)/p(0) + p(1 \mid 0)/p(1)\,}
= \frac{1.5}{1.5 + 0.5} = 0.75.
$$

The posterior depends on the candidates only through the ratios
$p(y \mid x)/p(y)$: the per-cell departure from independence, whose
logarithm is the pointwise mutual information of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>. Write

$$
r(x,y) = \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)},
$$

so that $r = 1.5$ on the toy's agreeing cells and $r = 0.5$ on the
disagreeing ones.

<Theorem title="the optimal critic is a density-ratio softmax">
In one round with candidates $y_1, \ldots, y_N$, the positive at a
uniformly random position, the posterior probability that position $i$
holds the true partner is
$$
p(\text{pos}{=}i \mid x, y_1, \ldots, y_N)
= \frac{r(x, y_i)}{\sum_{k=1}^{N} r(x, y_k)},
$$
and $L_N[f]$ is minimized exactly when the score softmax equals this
posterior. Every critic of the form $f(x,y) = h(x)\, r(x,y)$ with $h > 0$
is optimal, for every $N$ simultaneously.

<Proof>
**The posterior.** Given $\text{pos} = i$ and the context $x$, the
candidate values have density
$p(y_i \mid x) \prod_{j \ne i} p(y_j)$: the positive comes from the
conditional, the negatives from the marginal. The prior over $i$ is
uniform, so Bayes' rule gives
$$
p(\text{pos}{=}i \mid x, y_{1..N})
= \frac{p(y_i \mid x) \prod_{j \ne i} p(y_j)}
{\sum_{k=1}^{N} p(y_k \mid x) \prod_{j \ne k} p(y_j)}.
$$
Divide numerator and denominator by $\prod_{j=1}^{N} p(y_j)$, a positive
constant not depending on $i$: each term
$p(y_k \mid x) \prod_{j \ne k} p(y_j)$ becomes
$p(y_k \mid x)/p(y_k) = r(x, y_k)$, which is the stated softmax.

**Minimality.** Condition on $x$ and on the candidate values. The true
position is then distributed by the posterior $\pi$, and the classifier's
softmax $s_f$ is billed $-\log_2 s_f(i)$ when the truth is $i$, so the
conditional expected loss is
$\sum_i \pi(i)\bigl(-\log_2 s_f(i)\bigr) = H(\pi, s_f)$: a cross-entropy,
minimized uniquely at $s_f = \pi$ by Gibbs' inequality
(<Ref lesson="cross-entropy-loss"/>). Averaging over rounds preserves the
minimizer. A softmax is unchanged by rescaling every score by one factor,
and $h(x)$ is constant across the candidates of a round, so $f = h\,r$
attains the posterior. Since $r$ does not depend on $N$, one critic is
optimal at every candidate count.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Two consequences of the $h(x)$ freedom are worth stating, because both are
routinely misread. The critic's numerical scale carries no information:
$f = 1000\,r$ and $f = r$ are the same classifier. And no amount of
training recovers $p(y \mid x)$ from a trained critic, because the critic
never separated the conditional from the marginal in the first place.

<Exercise>
On the toy pair, take context $x = 1$ and a round with $N = 3$ whose
candidates are $y_1 = 1$, $y_2 = 1$, $y_3 = 0$, with the true partner at
position 1. Write the three ratios, the optimal critic's softmax weights,
and the bits this round costs. Then say what the round would have cost had
the true partner sat at position 3.

<Solution>
**Ratios.** With $x = 1$, agreement means $y = 1$: $r(1,1) = 1.5$ and
$r(1,0) = 0.5$. So the three candidates carry $r = 1.5,\ 1.5,\ 0.5$ and
their total is $3.5$.

**Softmax.** The optimal weights are $1.5/3.5 = 0.4286$,
$1.5/3.5 = 0.4286$, and $0.5/3.5 = 0.1429$.

**Cost.** The true partner is at position 1, so the round costs
$-\log_2 0.4286 \approx 1.222$ bits. Two candidates are indistinguishable
by value here, and no critic can do better than split the weight between
them.

**If the partner were at position 3**, the round would cost
$-\log_2 0.1429 \approx 2.807$ bits. The same critic pays far more, and
correctly: a partner sitting on a cell that occurs *less* often than
independence predicts is genuinely hard to pick out. Averaged over rounds
these two cases weight themselves, which is what the loss computes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyFact>
Non-identifiability has a downstream price: $h(x)$ can inflate every score
for one context and leave the loss unchanged, so a trained critic's numbers
are comparable within one lineup and meaningless across contexts. It is
therefore neither a density model for $y$ nor a calibrated retrieval score
— it can rank captions for one image and cannot set a single threshold
across images — and no post-hoc rescaling repairs this, because the loss
never constrained the scale in the first place. This is one half of the
division of labour the closing section draws: the loss is a training signal
and its value a diagnostic, and neither role requires the critic to be a
probability.
</KeyFact>

## ★ The ratio is a likelihood ratio

The ratio the critic converges to is a standard object of hypothesis
testing, and that reading accounts for the coincidence at the heart of the
lesson: that a classification loss can bound an information quantity.
Deciding
whether a presented pair was recorded together or shuffled together is a
hypothesis test between $p(x,y)$ and $p(x)p(y)$, and the
[Neyman–Pearson lemma](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
(Cover & Thomas §11.7) says the optimal test thresholds the likelihood
ratio

$$
\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)} = \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)} = r(x,y).
$$

The critic is therefore the optimal test statistic for "is this pair
real?", and the $N$-way lineup is that test posed as a multiple choice.
The Chernoff-Stein lemma (§11.8) supplies the payoff: given $n$
independent pairs, the best test's error probability falls exponentially in
$n$ at a rate per pair equal to the KL divergence between the two
hypotheses, which here is
$D\bigl(p(x,y) \,\|\, p(x)p(y)\bigr) = I(X;Y)$. Mutual information is the
error exponent of exactly the discrimination the critic is trained to
perform, which is why an accuracy on that task converts into bits.

## The bound: log₂ N minus the loss

The optimal critic learns $r$, and $r$ is also what mutual information is
made of. <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> proved the divergence form
$I(X;Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}$, and since
$\tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} = r(x,y)$ this reads

$$
I(X;Y) = \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\bigl[\log_2 r(x, y)\bigr]:
$$

mutual information is the expected log ratio at true pairs. The loss is an
expectation of the same logarithms over the same objects, so the two are
comparable, and the comparison is an inequality in one direction only.

<Theorem title="InfoNCE bound (exact form: Poole et al. 2019)">
For every critic $f > 0$ and every $N \ge 1$, with $L_N$ measured in bits,
$$
I(X;Y) \;\ge\; \log_2 N - L_N[f].
$$

<Proof>
Three moves: replace an arbitrary critic by the optimal one; recognize the
optimal loss as a conditional entropy, which turns the certificate into a
mutual information about the lineup; and bound that mutual information by
$I(X;Y)$, which is where Jensen's inequality enters. Write $D$ for the
position of the true partner, uniform on $\{1, \ldots, N\}$ by
construction, and $W = (x, y_1, \ldots, y_N)$ for everything the classifier
sees.

**Step 1: the optimal critic suffices.** By the previous theorem
$L_N[f] \ge L_N[r]$, so proving the bound for $r$ proves it for every $f$.

**Step 2: the optimal loss is a conditional entropy.** With $f = r$ the
softmax is the true posterior $p(D \mid W)$, and the expected surprisal of
a distribution under itself is its entropy, so
$L_N[r] = H(D \mid W)$. Since $D$ is uniform, $H(D) = \log_2 N$, and
$$
\log_2 N - L_N[r] = H(D) - H(D \mid W) = I(D; W).
$$
The certificate *is* the information the lineup carries about which
position is the true one.

**Step 3: what that information is.** Conditioned on $D = i$, the lineup
has density $p(x)\, p(y_i \mid x) \prod_{j \ne i} p(y_j) =
p(x) \bigl(\prod_j p(y_j)\bigr)\, r(x,y_i)$. Averaging over the uniform
$D$ gives $p(W) = p(x)\bigl(\prod_j p(y_j)\bigr) A$, where
$$
A = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} r(x, y_k)
$$
is the lineup's average ratio. Hence
$p(W \mid D)/p(W) = r(x, y_D)/A$ and
$$
I(D;W) = \mathbb{E}\left[\log_2 \frac{r(x,y_D)}{A}\right]
= I(X;Y) - \mathbb{E}\bigl[\log_2 A\bigr],
$$
the first term because $(x, y_D)$ is a draw from the joint, which is the
divergence form above.

**Step 4: the slack is non-negative (Jensen).** Let $Q$ be the reference
draw in which *all* $N$ candidates come from the marginal, $D$ still
uniform and now independent of everything. The task's law $P$ differs from
$Q$ in one factor, the positive being conditional rather than marginal, so
$\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}Q = r(x, y_D)$. For any function $g$ of the lineup,
$\mathbb{E}_P[g] = \mathbb{E}_Q[r(x,y_D)\, g]$, and under $Q$ the position
$D$ is independent of the candidates, so averaging over it replaces
$r(x,y_D)$ by $A$. With $g = \log_2 A$,
$$
\mathbb{E}_P\bigl[\log_2 A\bigr] = \mathbb{E}_Q\bigl[A \log_2 A\bigr].
$$
Under $Q$ every candidate is a marginal draw, so for each $k$ and each $x$,
$\mathbb{E}_Q[r(x,y_k)] = \sum_y p(y)\,\tfrac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)} =
\sum_y p(y \mid x) = 1$, giving $\mathbb{E}_Q[A] = 1$. The map
$t \mapsto t \log_2 t$ is convex, so Jensen's inequality gives
$$
\mathbb{E}_Q\bigl[A \log_2 A\bigr] \;\ge\;
\mathbb{E}_Q[A]\, \log_2 \mathbb{E}_Q[A] = 0.
$$

Combining steps 2 to 4, $\log_2 N - L_N[r] = I(X;Y) -
\mathbb{E}_Q[A \log_2 A] \le I(X;Y)$, and step 1 extends it to every
critic.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof hands over more than the inequality: it names the gap. For the
optimal critic,

$$
\underbrace{I(X;Y)}_{\text{what is true}}
\;-\;
\underbrace{\bigl(\log_2 N - L_N[r]\bigr)}_{\text{what is certified}}
\;=\;
\mathbb{E}_Q\bigl[A \log_2 A\bigr],
$$

the slack being an expectation of $A \log_2 A$ over lineups drawn entirely
from the marginal. It vanishes exactly when $A = 1$ with probability one.
Since $A$ is an average of $N$ independent unit-mean terms, the law of
large numbers pushes it to $1$ as the lineup grows, and the certificate
climbs toward the truth. That is the mechanism behind "larger $N$ is
tighter", and it is checkable on the toy pair below.

### Reading the inequality: a budget minus a bill

Each of the three quantities in $I(X;Y) \ge \log_2 N - L_N$ has an
operational reading. $\log_2 N$ is the cost of naming one candidate out of
$N$ with no information at all, which is what the chance classifier pays:
the budget of the task. $L_N$ is what the trained classifier actually pays.
The difference, its edge over chance, is a certified floor under the mutual
information, because nothing except shared bits can produce an edge.

The certificate is one-way, and the asymmetry is the whole practical
content. A low loss proves high mutual information. A high loss proves
nothing at all: it is equally consistent with near-independent variables
and with a critic that failed to train. Reporting $\log_2 N - L_N$ as "the
mutual information" therefore overstates twice, once by ignoring the slack
and once by ignoring the critic.

<Remark title="bits, nats, and reported losses">
The paper and every deep-learning framework compute $L_N$ with natural
logarithms, so the bound is usually written $I \ge \ln N - L_N$ in nats.
Divide nats by $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ to get bits
(<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>). A loss of $2.30$ nats at $N = 1024$
certifies $10 - 2.30/0.693 \approx 6.7$ bits. Mixing the conventions is the
commonest arithmetic error in reported InfoNCE numbers, and it is worth
about $1.44\times$.
</Remark>

<Aside>
The derivation above is not the one in the source. van den Oord, Li &
Vinyals reach the bound (Appendix A.1, eqs. 6-11) by two informal steps:
replacing the negatives' ratio sum by $(N-1)$ times its mean, and applying
$1 + (N-1)/\rho \ge N/\rho$ inside an expectation where $\rho < 1$ is
possible. Both steps are accurate for large $N$ and neither is needed:
[Poole et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06922) place InfoNCE in a
family of variational mutual-information bounds and prove the inequality
exactly, as does the $A \log_2 A$ argument above. The same survey maps the
trade the family makes: estimators like
[MINE](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04062), built on the Donsker-Varadhan
representation, are not capped at $\log_2 N$ but pay for it in variance,
and InfoNCE sits at the low-variance, ceiling-limited end.
</Aside>

## Exact numbers: the toy pair at N = 2, 4, 16

An inequality with an unknown gap is not yet an instrument. The toy pair is
small enough to evaluate $L_N$ exactly for the optimal critic, with no
sampling and no approximation, so the gap can be measured rather than
guessed. The ratio table has two values: $r = \tfrac{0.75}{0.5} = 1.5$ on
agreeing cells and $r = \tfrac{0.25}{0.5} = 0.5$ on disagreeing ones.

<Example title="the bound at N = 2, enumerated">
One positive and one negative. The positive's ratio $\rho$ is $1.5$ with
probability $0.75$ (the pair agrees) and $0.5$ with probability $0.25$. The
negative is a marginal draw independent of the pair, so its ratio $r_2$ is
$1.5$ or $0.5$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. Four cases:

| $\rho$ | $r_2$ | prob. | softmax $\tfrac{\rho}{\rho + r_2}$ | loss (bits) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1.5$ | $1.5$ | $0.375$ | $0.5$ | $1$ |
| $1.5$ | $0.5$ | $0.375$ | $0.75$ | $0.415$ |
| $0.5$ | $1.5$ | $0.125$ | $0.25$ | $2$ |
| $0.5$ | $0.5$ | $0.125$ | $0.5$ | $1$ |

$$
L_2 = 0.375 \cdot 1 + 0.375 \cdot 0.415 + 0.125 \cdot 2 + 0.125 \cdot 1
\approx 0.906 \text{ bits}.
$$

Certified: $\log_2 2 - L_2 \approx 1 - 0.906 = 0.094$ bits, against the
true $I \approx 0.189$. The bound holds and captures about half the
information.
</Example>

The first row explains where the other half went. With probability
$\tfrac12$ the negative takes the same value as the positive: the two
candidates are then identical, no critic can beat the coin flip, and a full
bit is paid. Collisions between a marginal draw and the conditional are
unavoidable on a two-symbol alphabet, and they are exactly what the slack
formula prices. Evaluating $\mathbb{E}_Q[A \log_2 A]$ on this lineup, where
$A$ takes the values $1.5, 1, 0.5$ with probabilities
$\tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac14$, gives
$\tfrac14(1.5)(0.585) + 0 + \tfrac14(0.5)(-1) = 0.0943$ bits, which is
exactly the gap between the true $0.1887$ and the certified $0.0944$.

<Example title="the bound at N = 4">
One positive, three negatives. Let $k$ be the number of negatives whose
value agrees with $x$; each agrees independently with probability
$\tfrac12$, so $k \sim \mathrm{Bin}(3, \tfrac12)$ with weights
$\tfrac18, \tfrac38, \tfrac38, \tfrac18$. The softmax denominator is
$\rho + 1.5k + 0.5(3-k)$.

**Branch $\rho = 1.5$** (probability $0.75$): denominator $3 + k$, losses
$-\log_2 \tfrac{1.5}{3+k}$ for $k = 0 \ldots 3$, that is $1$, $1.415$,

$\tfrac18(1) + \tfrac38(1.415) + \tfrac38(1.737) + \tfrac18(2) = 1.557$

$$
L_4 = 0.75 \cdot 1.557 + 0.25 \cdot 2.760 \approx 1.858 \text{ bits},
\qquad
\log_2 4 - L_4 \approx 2 - 1.858 = 0.142 \text{ bits}.
$$
</Example>

The same enumeration at $N = 16$ gives $L_{16} \approx 3.823$ bits and a
certificate of $0.177$ bits. The loss itself **rises** with $N$, since
there are more distractors to be confused by, but the budget $\log_2 N$
rises faster, and the slack falls from $0.094$ to $0.046$ to $0.011$ bits
as the lineup average $A$ concentrates on $1$.

## The ceiling: nothing above log₂ N is certifiable

From a stronger dependence one expects a stronger reading, the way a longer
ruler measures a longer object. This instrument does not behave that way.
Step 2 of the proof already contains the reason: the certificate equals
$I(D;W)$, an information about a variable $D$ that is uniform on $N$
positions, and no information about such a variable can exceed its entropy.
Hence

$$
\log_2 N - L_N \;=\; I(D;W) \;\le\; H(D) \;=\; \log_2 N
$$

for every pair, every critic and every dependence strength. With
$N = 256$ candidates the largest certificate that can ever be printed is
$8$ bits. A representation carrying $8$ bits about the target and one
carrying $20$ are, to this instrument, the same representation.

<Example title="a perfect critic, saturated">
Let $X$ be uniform on four symbols and $Y = X$ in lockstep, so
$I(X;Y) = H(X) = 2$ bits. The ratio is $r = 4$ when $y = x$ and $0$
otherwise, a limiting case of the strictly positive critics the definition
allows, with the softmax still well defined because the positive always
scores $4$. The optimal critic therefore answers every round in which no
negative collides with the positive, and the whole loss comes from
collisions. With
$k$ colliding negatives the weight on the positive is
$\tfrac{4}{4(1+k)} = \tfrac{1}{1+k}$, and
$k \sim \mathrm{Bin}(N-1, \tfrac14)$, so $L_N = \mathbb{E}[\log_2 (1+k)]$:

| $N$ | ceiling $\log_2 N$ | $L_N$ (bits) | certified | true $I$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $2$ | $1$ | $0.250$ | $0.750$ | $2$ |
| $4$ | $2$ | $0.676$ | $1.324$ | $2$ |
| $16$ | $4$ | $2.145$ | $1.855$ | $2$ |
| $256$ | $8$ | $6.008$ | $1.992$ | $2$ |

At $N = 2$ the ceiling is $1$ bit, below the true $2$: more than half the
dependence is invisible however perfect the critic. At $N = 256$ the same
pair, read with the same critic, gives back $1.992$ of its $2$ bits.
Nothing about the variables changed between the rows. Only the question
did.
</Example>

### The ceiling as a design constraint

The ceiling explains an otherwise strange fact about contrastive training:
these methods are hungry for batch size in a way that supervised training
is not. Implementations take the negatives from the batch itself, which
makes $N$ the batch size, which makes the batch a cap on the bits the loss
can see. A batch of $256$ can pursue at most $8$ bits of dependence; to
pursue $20$ bits requires over a million candidates.

<Figure
  src="./figures/batch-ceiling.svg"
  alt="Certifiable bits against candidate count on a log axis: a straight line log2 N marked at 64 candidates for a CPC minibatch (6 bits), 8,191 for SimCLR (13 bits), 32,768 for CLIP (15 bits), and 65,536 for the MoCo queue (16 bits)"
  caption="One bit per doubling: the deployed candidate counts of published contrastive systems buy 6 to 16 bits of ceiling."
/>

The published numbers sit exactly where the constraint predicts.
[SimCLR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709) trains at a batch of $4{,}096$
images, giving $8{,}190$ negatives per positive and a ceiling near $13$
bits; [CLIP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020) uses a minibatch of
$32{,}768$ image-text pairs, a $15$-bit ceiling;
[MoCo](https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.05722) keeps a queue of $65{,}536$
encoded negatives precisely so that $N$ can exceed what fits in a batch,
buying a $16$-bit ceiling on ordinary hardware. The returns are
logarithmic, which is the honest reading of a decade of scaling the batch:
going from $256$ to $32{,}768$ candidates, a factor of $128$ in compute per
step, raises the ceiling by $7$ bits.

<KeyFact>
The ceiling belongs to the question, not to the data: an $N$-way
multiple-choice test cannot reveal more than $\log_2 N$ bits, so a
saturated bound carries no information about which of two good
representations is better. Ranking representations by their InfoNCE value
past saturation is ranking them by nothing.
</KeyFact>

## Bound quality and representation quality are different measurements

The saturated regime is where contrastive systems are actually trained, so
the previous section's verdict has a consequence for how their numbers are
read. The InfoNCE value certifies bits of dependence. It does not measure
how usable a representation is, and the two are separated by more than the
ceiling.

### The claim to reject

The prevalent reading in the literature is that a higher InfoNCE value
means a better representation, and it fails for two independent reasons.

The first is the ceiling: past $\log_2 N$ the value stops responding to the
quantity it is supposed to track, so differences between strong
representations are invisible by construction.

The second survives even when the bound is tight, and it is a property of
mutual information itself rather than of the estimator. Mutual information
is invariant under invertible transformations: if $T' = \phi(T)$ for an
invertible $\phi$, then $I(X;T') = I(X;T)$ exactly. A representation and
any scrambling of it are indistinguishable to any mutual-information
criterion, while a *linear probe*, a linear classifier trained on the
frozen representation and the standard way of asking whether it is usable,
reads one and fails on the other.
[Tschannen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.13625) make this concrete:
over a family of invertible encoders the true $I$ is constant by
construction, yet downstream classification accuracy rises steadily during
training, and among that same family there exist encoders that maximize $I$
while scoring no better than raw pixels.

<Figure
  src="./figures/two-questions.svg"
  alt="Two panels: a representation whose two classes are linearly separable and an invertible scramble of it whose classes are interleaved, both carrying an identical 8-bit InfoNCE certificate"
  caption="An invertible scramble preserves I(X;T) exactly, so the certificate is identical while the linear probe collapses."
/>

The same paper closes the loop from the other side: higher-capacity critics
give tighter bounds and worse representations. Comparing a bilinear, a
separable and an MLP critic on the same encoders, the MLP critic reaches
the highest estimator value while its representations barely beat the
pixel-space baseline. Tightening the bound and improving the representation
are not merely different objectives; on this evidence they can conflict.

### What to conclude instead

The replacement model is a division of labour. The loss is a **training
signal**, whose gradient shapes the encoder and whose usefulness comes from
the architecture and the choice of what counts as a positive pair. The
value $\log_2 N - L_N$ is a **diagnostic** with a hard ceiling, useful for
detecting a critic that has not trained and useless for ranking
representations. Answering "how many bits does this pair share?" and "is
this representation any good?" requires two measurements, and only the
second is answered by a probe on the downstream task.

None of which makes the objective a poor one. Trained across four
modalities with nothing but this loss, contrastive predictive coding
reaches $64.6\%$ accuracy at labelling which speech sound is present in a
window of LibriSpeech audio, read off its representations by a linear
probe. The comparisons are $39.7\%$ for MFCC features, the hand-designed
spectral descriptors that preceded learned ones, and $74.6\%$ for a
network trained on the labels directly: most of the supervised gap closed
without labels. On ImageNet the same recipe gives a linear probe $48.7\%$
top-1 accuracy. Those runs drew their negatives from minibatches of eight
examples, so the candidate count was in the tens and the certificate was
capped near $6$ bits, below any plausible figure for what a waveform shares
with its own future. The representations are good anyway: the objective's
usefulness never depended on the bound being tight.

<KeyFact>
A reported InfoNCE value is unreadable without two companions: the
candidate count $N$ it was measured at, and how the value moves when $N$
doubles. Without both, two such values cannot be compared, not even between
two runs of the same system.
</KeyFact>

What is established: a mutual information can be lower-bounded from paired
and shuffled samples alone, by training an $N$-way classifier and reading
$\log_2 N - L_N$; the bound is exact, its slack is
$\mathbb{E}_Q[A \log_2 A]$, and its ceiling is $\log_2 N$ whatever the
data. That is the form in which information theory enters current
representation learning, and the ceiling is why the field reports
downstream accuracies rather than bits. What remains is to spend the bits
rather than count them.
<Ref lesson="learned-image-compression"/> takes up a system that does:
a learned codec trains an encoder, a quantizer, an entropy model and a
decoder against rate plus $\lambda$ times distortion, where the rate term
is once again a cross-entropy standing in for an information quantity that
cannot be computed directly.

<Exercise>
A contrastive model trained with $N = 64$ candidates reports a loss of
$1.2$ nats. Convert the certificate to bits. Then report the certificate
for the same loss at $N = 256$, and state which of the two runs, on this
evidence alone, has the better critic.

<Solution>
In nats the bound reads $I \ge \ln N - L_N$; converting through
$1 \text{ bit} = \ln 2 \approx 0.693$ nats gives a loss of
$1.2/0.693 \approx 1.73$ bits.

At $N = 64$: the budget is $\log_2 64 = 6$ bits, so
$I \ge 6 - 1.73 \approx 4.27$ bits.

At $N = 256$: the budget is $8$ bits, so $I \ge 8 - 1.73 \approx 6.27$
bits.

**Which critic is better?** The question cannot be answered from these
numbers. The second run certifies two bits more purely because it was asked
a harder question, and an equal loss on a harder task is in fact the
stronger performance, but "better critic" is a claim about the loss at a
fixed $N$, not about the certificate. Comparing certificates across
different $N$ compares budgets.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push the construction to its two degenerate ends. (a) Evaluate the bound at
$N = 1$: compute $L_1$ for an arbitrary critic and state what the bound
then asserts. (b) Evaluate it at $L_N = 0$ for $N \ge 2$: state what the
critic must be doing, and what the bound asserts. In each case name the
hypothesis of the theorem that is doing the work.

<Solution>
**(a) $N = 1$.** With one candidate the softmax has a single term, so the
weight on the true partner is $f/f = 1$ regardless of the critic, and
$L_1 = -\log_2 1 = 0$ bits for every $f$. The bound reads
$I(X;Y) \ge \log_2 1 - 0 = 0$, which is true and empty. Nothing broke: the
theorem holds at $N = 1$, and the derivation shows why it must be vacuous.
The certificate is $I(D;W)$ with $D$ constant, so $H(D) = 0$ and there is
nothing to learn about the position. A task with no alternatives asks no
question.

**(b) $L_N = 0$.** A zero loss means the softmax puts weight $1$ on the
true partner in every round, so the critic assigns zero score to every
negative that is ever drawn. The bound then certifies the full budget,
$I(X;Y) \ge \log_2 N$. This is attainable: for $X$ uniform on $M$ symbols
with $Y = X$ and $N \le M$ small enough that collisions never occur,
$L_N$ is genuinely $0$. In general collisions make $L_N > 0$ whenever a
negative can take the positive's value, which is why the lockstep example
pays $0.250$ bits at $N = 2$ rather than nothing.

**The hypothesis at work** in both cases is that the negatives are drawn
from $p(y)$, independently of the positive. It is what makes collisions
occur with the marginal's own probability, and thus what ties the
attainable loss to the true dependence rather than to the critic's skill.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim, from a paper's results table: "Our method achieves an
InfoNCE value of $7.9$ bits with $N = 256$, so our representation captures
$7.9$ bits about the target." State precisely what is wrong, and then
design the experiment that would distinguish a representation carrying $8$
bits from one carrying $30$.

<Solution>
**What is wrong.** Three things, in increasing severity.

1. *Direction.* $7.9$ bits is a lower bound, not a measurement: the true
   $I$ is at least $7.9$ bits and may be anything above it.
2. *Saturation.* The ceiling at $N = 256$ is $\log_2 256 = 8$ bits, and
   $7.9$ sits $0.1$ bits below it. A value pinned to its ceiling is
   consistent with $I = 8$ bits and with $I = 300$ bits. The number
   reported is a property of the batch size.
3. *Estimation.* $L_N$ is itself estimated from finite batches, so the
   quantity is an estimate of a bound. On a given batch it can fluctuate
   above its own target.

**The experiment.** Raise $N$ and watch. Retrain (or merely re-evaluate the
frozen critic) at $N = 2^{10}, 2^{12}, 2^{14}, 2^{16}$ and plot the
certificate against $\log_2 N$. A pair with $I = 8$ bits produces a curve
that flattens near $8$ and stops responding. A pair with $I = 30$ bits
produces a curve that keeps climbing roughly one bit per doubling, still
pinned near its ceiling at $16$ bits. The diagnostic is the *slope* of the
certificate in $\log_2 N$: near $1$ means still saturated, near $0$ means
the bound has found the true value. Reporting a single $N$ discards exactly
this information.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the converse claim: "a lower InfoNCE bound means a worse
representation." Give the two independent causes of a low value, and
construct a case where the representation with the *lower* bound is the
better one for a downstream classifier.

<Solution>
**Two causes of a low value.** (i) The dependence really is small, so
$I(X;Y)$ itself is low. (ii) The critic is weak or undertrained, so
$L_N[f] > L_N[r]$ and the certificate falls short of what the same data
could support. The bound is one-way: a low value distinguishes neither
case, since $\log_2 N - L_N[f]$ is a floor under $I$ and a floor can be
arbitrarily far below.

A third, quieter cause is a small $N$: at $N = 2$ nothing above $1$ bit can
be reported at all.

**A case where lower is better.** Take the critic comparison of
[Tschannen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.13625): with the encoder
fixed, a high-capacity MLP critic reaches a higher estimator value than a
bilinear or separable critic, yet the representations trained with the MLP
critic barely beat raw pixels on a linear probe while the simpler critics
clearly beat it. The mechanism is that the critic is not only a measuring
device but also the source of the gradient reaching the encoder: a critic
powerful enough to exploit any arrangement of features imposes no pressure
on the encoder to arrange them usefully. Constructing the case by hand is
easier still: take any representation $T$ and any invertible scramble
$T' = \phi(T)$. Both have identical $I(X;T)$ and therefore identical
attainable bounds, while a linear probe separates one and not the other.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A derivation the lesson skipped. Suppose the negatives are drawn not from
$p(y)$ but from some other distribution $q(y)$ with full support, everything
else unchanged. Show that the Bayes-optimal critic becomes
$f(x,y) \propto p(y \mid x)/q(y)$, that the expected log of this ratio at
true pairs equals
$$
\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\log_2 \frac{p(y \mid x)}{q(y)}\right]
= I(X;Y) + D\bigl(p_Y \,\|\, q\bigr),
$$
with $D$ the KL divergence of <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>, and conclude
what happens to the certificate.

<Solution>
**The critic.** Rerun the posterior derivation with negatives from $q$:
given $\text{pos} = i$, the candidates have density
$p(y_i \mid x) \prod_{j \ne i} q(y_j)$. Dividing numerator and denominator
of Bayes' rule by $\prod_j q(y_j)$ leaves
$$
p(\text{pos}{=}i \mid x, y_{1..N})
= \frac{p(y_i \mid x)/q(y_i)}{\sum_k p(y_k \mid x)/q(y_k)},
$$
so the optimal critic is the ratio against the wrong reference,
$r'(x,y) = p(y \mid x)/q(y)$.

**The expectation.** Split the logarithm:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\log_2 \frac{p(y \mid x)}{q(y)}\right]
= \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\log_2 \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)}\right]
+ \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\log_2 \frac{p(y)}{q(y)}\right].
$$
The first term is $I(X;Y)$ by the divergence form. In the second the
integrand depends on $y$ alone, so the expectation collapses onto the
marginal: $\sum_y p(y) \log_2 \tfrac{p(y)}{q(y)} = D(p_Y \| q)$.

**The conclusion.** Every step of the bound's proof goes through with $q$
in place of $p_Y$, and the quantity bounded is the inflated
$I(X;Y) + D(p_Y \| q) \ge I(X;Y)$. So for large $N$ the reported
$\log_2 N - L_N$ converges to a value that *exceeds* the true mutual
information by exactly the marginal mismatch, and the one-way guarantee is
gone: the number can now overstate. This is why implementations build
negatives by borrowing real partners from the batch rather than sampling
from a convenient noise model, and it is the sharpest practical difference
between InfoNCE and noise-contrastive estimation generally.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Here is a joint distribution: $X$ is the sky (clear or overcast), $Y$ is
the ground (dry or wet), with
$p(\text{clear},\text{dry}) = \tfrac12$,
$p(\text{clear},\text{wet}) = 0$, and
$p(\text{overcast},\text{dry}) = p(\text{overcast},\text{wet}) = \tfrac14$.
Decide which quantity of this lesson is computable here without any
training at all, compute it for every cell, and use it to obtain
$I(X;Y)$ by a route other than the entropy difference. Then name the cell
an optimal critic would score highest. Use $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$.

<Solution>
**Which quantity.** With the joint table in hand the density ratio
$r(x,y) = p(y \mid x)/p(y)$ is computable cell by cell, and the divergence
form $I(X;Y) = \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}[\log_2 r]$ then gives the mutual
information without computing $H(Y)$ and $H(Y \mid X)$ separately.

**Marginals and conditionals.** $p(\text{dry}) = \tfrac34$,
$p(\text{wet}) = \tfrac14$. Clear implies dry
($p(\text{dry} \mid \text{clear}) = 1$); overcast splits evenly.

**Ratios.** $r(\text{clear}, \text{dry}) = \tfrac{1}{3/4} = \tfrac43$;
$r(\text{overcast}, \text{dry}) = \tfrac{1/2}{3/4} = \tfrac23$;
$r(\text{overcast}, \text{wet}) = \tfrac{1/2}{1/4} = 2$. The cell
(clear, wet) has probability zero and never enters the average.

**Mutual information.**
$$
\tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac43 + \tfrac14 \log_2 \tfrac23 + \tfrac14 \log_2 2
= \tfrac12 (0.415) + \tfrac14 (-0.585) + \tfrac14 (1)
\approx 0.311 \text{ bits},
$$
matching the value computed by entropies in
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>.

**Highest-scoring cell.** (overcast, wet), with $r = 2$: it is the cell
that occurs twice as often as independence predicts. Note that
(overcast, dry) scores *below* $1$ even though it is the more probable
outcome, which is the distinction between a conditional and a ratio: the
critic ranks surprise-relative-to-the-marginal, not frequency.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
A research-shaped task. Choose a joint whose $I(X;Y)$ you can compute
exactly (the dependence dial at any $q$, or $X$ uniform on $M$ symbols with
$Y$ a noisy copy). Evaluate the optimal-critic loss $L_N$ by enumeration or
Monte Carlo at $N = 4, 16, 64, 256$, and plot the certificate against
$\log_2 N$ alongside the true value. Report the $N$ at which the
certificate stops improving by more than $0.01$ bits per doubling, and
compare that $N$ with $2^{I(X;Y)}$. State in advance what agreement would
look like.

<Solution>
**Setup.** For a discrete joint the loss needs no training: the optimal
critic is $r(x,y) = p(y \mid x)/p(y)$, available in closed form, so
$L_N$ is either an exact enumeration (small alphabets) or an average over
sampled lineups (a few $10^5$ rounds gives three decimals).

**What to expect.** The certificate rises with $\log_2 N$ and levels off
just below $I(X;Y)$. The knee sits near $N \approx 2^{I(X;Y)}$, and this is
the prediction to test: the ceiling $\log_2 N$ must clear $I$ before the
bound can be tight, so the transition happens where $\log_2 N \approx I$.
On the dependence dial at $q = 0.75$, with $I = 0.189$ bits, the knee is
essentially immediate: the certificate is already $0.177$ at $N = 16$ and
gains $0.01$ bits or less per doubling after that. On a $16$-symbol
lockstep pair with $I = 4$ bits, the certificate is still climbing at
$N = 16$ and flattens only past $N \approx 64$.

**What agreement looks like.** The certificate at the knee should reach
$80$-$90\%$ of the true $I$, and doubling $N$ past it should add under
$0.05$ bits while the ceiling still rises by a full bit. Disagreement in
one specific direction is the informative failure: if the certificate keeps
climbing one bit per doubling with no sign of a knee, the pair's $I$ is
larger than every $N$ tried, and no run at these candidate counts can
report its value.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A learned image codec must decide how many bits to spend on each part of an
image. Write down a training objective for it, in the same shape as the
losses of this course: the rate term is a cross-entropy of the true
distribution over codes against a learned model of that distribution, and
the fidelity term is a distortion between the input and its
reconstruction. State the units of each term, say what the trade-off
parameter multiplies, and compare the objective's shape with
$R(D)$ from <Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/>.

<Solution>
**The objective.** With an encoder producing a discrete code $\hat{z}$ from
an image $x$, a learned distribution $q(\hat{z})$ over codes, and a decoder
producing $\hat{x}$:
$$
\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigl[-\log_2 q(\hat{z})\bigr]}_{\text{rate, bits}}
\;+\; \lambda \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigl[d(x, \hat{x})\bigr]}_{\text{distortion}}.
$$
The rate term is the expected code length an entropy coder achieves against
the model $q$, which is the cross-entropy of
<Ref lesson="cross-entropy-loss"/>: it exceeds the true entropy of the code
distribution by the divergence between the model and the truth
(<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>), so improving the entropy model is
literally shortening the file. Its units are bits (per image, or divided
through by pixel count, bits per pixel). The distortion term carries
whatever units $d$ has, squared error or one minus a similarity index, and
$\lambda$ multiplies it, converting distortion into bits so that the sum is
meaningful.

**Comparison with $R(D)$.** Sweeping $\lambda$ traces a curve of achieved
(rate, distortion) pairs, which is the same construction as the
rate-distortion function of <Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/>, with two
differences. $R(D)$ is an infimum over all test channels, attained by no
particular algorithm; the sweep here is over one parametric family, so the
achieved curve lies on or above $R(D)$. And $R(D)$ measures rate as a
mutual information $I(X;\hat{X})$, whereas the objective above measures it
as a cross-entropy against a learned model, which is a computable quantity
for the same reason $L_N$ is: an information quantity has been replaced by
a loss whose optimum recovers it.

**The shape.** $\mathcal{L} = \text{rate} + \lambda \cdot \text{distortion}$
is a Lagrangian for the constrained problem "minimize rate subject to a
distortion budget", the same algebraic form as the bottleneck objective and
the same form the entire codec of
<Ref lesson="learned-image-compression"/> is trained against. That lesson
identifies each of its four learned components with one of this course's
results.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

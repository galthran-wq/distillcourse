A classifier trained to pick the true partner of a context out of N
candidates yields a mutual-information estimate: I(X;Y) >= log2 N - L_N,
where L_N is the contrastive cross-entropy loss. This lesson turns the
mutual information of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> into a quantity
trainable from samples. The route: why joint-table estimation fails at
scale, the N-way contrastive task and its loss, the proof that the optimal
classifier scores candidates by the density ratio $p(y \mid x)/p(y)$, the
derivation of the bound, exact numbers on a toy pair, the $\log_2 N$
ceiling that forces large candidate counts, and the loss's role in
contrastive representation learning.

## Joint tables do not scale: the estimation problem

Every mutual information computed in this course came out of a complete
joint table: the $2\times 2$ weather table of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>, the $2\times 4$ source of
<Ref lesson="information-bottleneck"/>. The recipe reads $p(x,y)$ off
every cell, takes marginals by summing and conditionals by renormalizing,
and evaluates $I(X;Y)$ by the formulas of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>.

For the pairs modern learning cares about, the table does not exist. Let
$X$ be a $32 \times 32$ RGB image and $Y$ a sentence describing it: $X$
alone ranges over $256^{3072}$ values, so the joint table has more cells
than there are atoms in the observable universe (about $10^{80}$).
Estimating a cell probability by counting requires observing that cell
many times; no dataset visits more than a vanishing fraction of cells even
once. The counting recipe is not merely expensive at this scale: it is
undefined.

What a dataset does provide is **samples**. It provides draws from the
joint: the recorded pairs $(x_i, y_i) \sim p(x,y)$. And it provides draws
from the marginal for free: shuffling the pairing detaches each $y_i$ from
its context, giving samples of $p(y)$ alone. The estimation problem is to
build, from these two kinds of samples, a computable quantity whose value
certifies something about $I(X;Y)$.

The problem is exactly where the previous lesson stopped:
<Ref lesson="information-bottleneck"/> defined the objective
$I(X;T) - \beta\, I(T;Y)$ and noted that estimating its mutual
informations from high-dimensional samples gates every practical use.
[Contrastive predictive coding](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748)
(van den Oord, Li & Vinyals, 2018) supplies the tool this lesson derives;
its motivating observation (§2.1 of the paper) is that mutual information
is the right training objective for representations, and that modeling
$p(y \mid x)$ generatively (reconstructing every pixel of $y$) spends
model capacity on detail the shared information does not contain.

<KeyFact>
Every $I(X;Y)$ so far came from a full joint table; for images, audio,
and text the table is unavailable. InfoNCE replaces it with a quantity
computable from two things every dataset has: paired samples and shuffled
samples.
</KeyFact>

## The contrastive setup: one true partner among N candidates

### The running toy pair

The toy joint for the whole lesson is the dependence-dial table of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> at agreement $q = 0.75$: binary $X$ and
$Y$ with uniform marginals, diagonal cells $\tfrac38$, off-diagonal cells
$\tfrac18$. Its conditionals are $p(y{=}x \mid x) = 0.75$, and its mutual
information is $I(X;Y) = 1 - H_2(0.75) \approx 0.189$ bits. Small enough
to enumerate exactly, so every claim below can be checked by hand.

### The protocol: sample, shuffle, classify

One round of the contrastive task is built from the two available sample
types:

1. **Draw the positive.** One pair $(x, y_{\text{pos}}) \sim p(x,y)$: the
   context and its true partner.
2. **Draw the negatives.** $N-1$ candidates $y_1, \ldots, y_{N-1}$,
   independent draws from the marginal $p(y)$.
3. **Classify.** Present $x$ and the $N$ candidates in random order; the
   classifier must identify which candidate is the partner.

A classifier for this task is built from a *critic*: a function
$f(x, y) > 0$ scoring how well $y$ fits $x$. The scores over the $N$
candidates are normalized by a softmax into a distribution over positions,
and the round bills the classifier the surprisal of the softmax weight it
placed on the true partner: a one-hot cross-entropy, the exact loss of
<Ref lesson="cross-entropy-loss"/> with $N$ classes.

<Definition title="InfoNCE loss">
Given a joint distribution $p(x,y)$, a candidate count $N \ge 2$, and a
critic $f(x,y) > 0$, the **InfoNCE loss** is
$$
L_N[f] = \mathbb{E}\left[
  -\log_2 \frac{f(x, y_{\text{pos}})}
  {f(x, y_{\text{pos}}) + \sum_{j=1}^{N-1} f(x, y_j)}
\right] \quad \text{bits},
$$
the expectation running over the positive pair
$(x, y_{\text{pos}}) \sim p(x,y)$ and the negatives
$y_1, \ldots, y_{N-1} \sim p(y)$, drawn independently.
</Definition>

**Chance baseline:** a critic that has learned nothing, a constant
$f$, makes the softmax uniform, weight $\tfrac1N$ on every candidate,
and pays $L_N = \log_2 N$ bits. Any smaller loss is evidence that the
critic distinguishes true pairs from shuffled ones; the bound below turns
that evidence into a certified number of bits.

<Remark title="negatives must be marginal draws">
The negatives' distribution is not a free design choice: everything
derived below identifies ratios **against $p(y)$**, so the negatives
must come from the marginal itself. In practice this is what a training
batch provides: for each context, the other pairs' partners are marginal
draws. This is the point of contrast with generic noise-contrastive
estimation ([Gutmann & Hyvärinen, 2010](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v9/gutmann10a.html)),
which discriminates data from a designer-chosen noise distribution to fit
unnormalized models. Exercise 7 prices the mismatch when negatives come
from the wrong distribution.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The InfoNCE loss is an $N$-way cross-entropy: one candidate from
$p(y \mid x)$, $N-1$ from $p(y)$, and the classifier is billed
$-\log_2$ of the softmax weight it put on the true partner. Chance costs
$\log_2 N$ bits.
</KeyFact>

## The optimal critic recovers the density ratio

### Bayes on one concrete round

Take the toy pair at $N = 2$, context $x = 0$, and suppose the two
candidates presented are $y_1 = 0$ and $y_2 = 1$. Bayes' rule identifies
the partner, over the two position hypotheses with a uniform prior:

$$
p(\text{pos}{=}1 \mid \text{data})
= \frac{p(0 \mid x{=}0)\, p(1)}{p(0 \mid x{=}0)\, p(1) + p(1 \mid x{=}0)\, p(0)}
= \frac{0.75 \cdot 0.5}{0.75 \cdot 0.5 + 0.25 \cdot 0.5} = 0.75.
$$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $p(y_1)\,p(y_2) = 0.25$ rewrites
the same posterior in a more portable form:

$$
p(\text{pos}{=}1 \mid \text{data})
= \frac{p(0 \mid 0)/p(0)}{\,p(0 \mid 0)/p(0) + p(1 \mid 0)/p(1)\,}
= \frac{1.5}{1.5 + 0.5} = 0.75.
$$

The posterior depends on the candidates only through the ratios
$p(y \mid x)/p(y)$: the per-cell departure from independence, which
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> met as the pointwise mutual
information's argument. The general statement:

<Theorem title="the optimal critic is a density-ratio softmax">
In one round with candidates $y_1, \ldots, y_N$ (the positive at a
uniformly random position), the posterior probability that position $i$
holds the true partner is
$$
p(\text{pos}{=}i \mid x, y_1, \ldots, y_N)
= \frac{r(x, y_i)}{\sum_{k=1}^{N} r(x, y_k)},
\qquad
r(x,y) = \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)},
$$
and $L_N[f]$ is minimized exactly when the score softmax equals this
posterior. In particular every critic of the form
$f(x,y) = h(x)\, r(x,y)$ with $h > 0$ is optimal, for every $N$
simultaneously.

<Proof>
**The posterior.** Given $\text{pos} = i$ and the context $x$, the
candidate values have density
$p(y_i \mid x) \prod_{j \ne i} p(y_j)$: the positive is drawn from the
conditional, the negatives from the marginal. The prior over $i$ is
uniform, so Bayes' rule gives
$$
p(\text{pos}{=}i \mid x, y_{1..N})
= \frac{p(y_i \mid x) \prod_{j \ne i} p(y_j)}
{\sum_{k=1}^{N} p(y_k \mid x) \prod_{j \ne k} p(y_j)}.
$$
Divide numerator and denominator by $\prod_{j=1}^{N} p(y_j)$, a positive
constant not depending on $i$: each term
$p(y_k \mid x) \prod_{j \ne k} p(y_j)$ becomes
$p(y_k \mid x)/p(y_k) = r(x, y_k)$, which is the stated softmax.

**Minimality.** Condition on $x$ and the candidate values. The true
position is distributed by the posterior $\pi$, and the classifier's
softmax $s_f$ is billed $-\log_2 s_f(i)$ when the truth is $i$, so the
conditional expected loss is $\sum_i \pi(i)(-\log_2 s_f(i)) =
H(\pi, s_f)$: a cross-entropy, minimized uniquely at $s_f = \pi$
(Gibbs' inequality, as in <Ref lesson="cross-entropy-loss"/>). Averaging
over rounds preserves the minimizer. The softmax is unchanged by
rescaling $f$ by any factor constant in the candidate, so
$f = h(x)\, r$ attains it; $r$ does not depend on $N$, so one critic is
optimal at every $N$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The theorem is the mechanism's core. Training the classifier drives the
critic toward the ratio $p(y \mid x)/p(y)$, and the ratio is obtained
**without modeling either distribution**. No density over images, no
density over sentences: only a scoring function whose softmax matches an
$N$-way posterior. That is what makes the construction usable exactly
where the joint table is not.

<KeyFact>
The loss-minimizing critic scores candidates by the density ratio
$p(y \mid x)/p(y)$, the joint's cell-by-cell departure from independence,
learned without ever modeling $p(y \mid x)$ or $p(y)$ separately.
</KeyFact>

## The bound: log₂ N minus the loss never overstates I

The last ingredient is the identity the whole derivation turns on.
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> proved the divergence form
$I(X;Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}$; since
$\tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} = \tfrac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)} = r(x,y)$, this
reads: **mutual information is the expected log ratio at true pairs**,
$$
I(X;Y) = \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\bigl[\log_2 r(x, y)\bigr]
$$
([Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
§2.3). The critic has just learned $r$; the loss it pays is therefore an
expectation of the same logarithms, and comparing the two gives the
bound.

<Theorem title="InfoNCE bound">
For every critic $f$ and every $N \ge 2$, with $L_N$ in bits,
$$
I(X;Y) \;\ge\; \log_2 N - L_N[f].
$$

<Proof>
Derivation at the level of rigor of
[van den Oord et al., Appendix A.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.03748);
two steps are approximations, flagged where they occur and discussed in
the remark below.

**Step 1: reduce to the optimal critic.** By the previous theorem,
$L_N[f] \ge L_N[r]$ for every $f$: a lower bound established for the
optimal critic transfers to all critics.

**Step 2: the optimal loss.** With $f = r$, writing $\rho = r(x,
y_{\text{pos}})$ for the positive's ratio and
$S = \sum_{j=1}^{N-1} r(x, y_j)$ for the negatives' total,
$$
L_N[r] = \mathbb{E}\left[\log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{\rho}\right)\right].
$$

**Step 3: the negatives sum to about $N-1$.** Each negative is an
independent draw from $p(y)$, and the ratio has unit mean under the
marginal:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim p(y)}\bigl[r(x, y)\bigr]
= \sum_y p(y)\, \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)} = \sum_y p(y \mid x) = 1
$$
(Exercise 3). So $\mathbb{E}[S] = N - 1$, and the first approximation
replaces $S$ by its mean (the relative fluctuation of a sum of $N-1$
independent unit-mean terms shrinks as $N$ grows):
$$
L_N[r] \approx \mathbb{E}\left[\log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{N-1}{\rho}\right)\right].
$$

**Step 4: round $N-1$ up to $N$.** Pointwise,
$1 + (N-1)/\rho \ge N/\rho$ exactly when $\rho \ge 1$; the second
informal step applies it inside the expectation wholesale:
$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{N-1}{\rho}\right)\right]
\;\ge\; \mathbb{E}\left[\log_2 \frac{N}{\rho}\right].
$$

**Step 5: the divergence form finishes.**
$$
\mathbb{E}\left[\log_2 \frac{N}{\rho}\right]
= \log_2 N - \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\bigl[\log_2 r(x, y_{\text{pos}})\bigr]
= \log_2 N - I(X;Y),
$$
since the positive pair is a draw from the joint. Chaining:
$L_N[f] \ge L_N[r] \gtrsim \log_2 N - I(X;Y)$, which rearranges to the
statement.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Remark title="what the derivation assumes">
Two steps were informal: replacing the negatives' sum by its mean
(step 3, accurate for large $N$), and applying the $\rho \ge 1$
inequality under the expectation (step 4; $\rho < 1$ occurs whenever the
positive pair is an anti-correlated cell). The conclusion survives both:
[Poole et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06922) prove
$I(X;Y) \ge \log_2 N - L_N[f]$ exactly, for every critic and every $N$,
placing InfoNCE in a family of variational mutual-information bounds.
The paper-level derivation is kept here because it shows where each term
comes from; the exact enumerations below use no approximation at all.
</Remark>

### Reading the inequality: a budget minus a bill

The three quantities in $I(X;Y) \ge \log_2 N - L_N$ each have an
operational reading. $\log_2 N$ is the bits needed to name one candidate
out of $N$, and it is exactly what the chance classifier pays: the
budget of the task. $L_N$ is what the trained classifier actually pays.
The difference, the classifier's edge over chance, is a certified floor
under the mutual information: the partner can only be found faster than
chance if $x$ and $y$ share information, and every bit of edge is backed
by a shared bit.

The inequality is a **one-way certificate**. A low loss proves high
mutual information: nothing but shared bits can produce an edge over
chance. A high loss proves nothing: it may mean the variables are nearly
independent, or only that the critic is bad. The direction matters in
practice: a trained InfoNCE loss reported as $\log_2 N - L_N$ bits is a
floor under $I$, never a measurement of it.

<Remark title="bits, nats, and reported losses">
The paper and every deep-learning framework compute $L_N$ with natural
logarithms; the bound there reads $I \ge \ln N - L_N$ in nats. The
conversion is the usual factor of <Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>:
divide nats by $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ to get bits. A reported InfoNCE
loss of $2.30$ nats at $N = 1024$ certifies
$10 - 2.30/0.693 \approx 6.7$ bits (Exercise 6).
</Remark>

<Aside>
InfoNCE is one point in a family of sample-based mutual-information
estimators. [MINE](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.04062) (Belghazi et al.,
2018) bounds $I$ through the Donsker–Varadhan representation of the KL
divergence, training a single unnormalized critic without the $N$-way
softmax; its estimates can exceed $\log_2 N$ but carry high variance.
[Poole et al. (2019)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.06922) unify both: the
trade is variance against bias, with InfoNCE the low-variance,
ceiling-limited end. The limits are not an artifact of these particular
estimators: [McAllester & Stratos (2020)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.04251)
prove that any distribution-free high-confidence lower bound on mutual
information computed from $n$ samples cannot certify more than
$O(\log n)$ bits. Certifying many bits is intrinsically expensive.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
$I(X;Y) \ge \log_2 N - L_N$ for every critic and every $N$: the
certificate can undershoot but never overstate. Low loss proves high
mutual information; high loss proves nothing.
</KeyFact>

## Exact numbers: the bound on the toy pair

The toy pair is small enough to evaluate $L_N$ exactly, with no sampling
and no approximation, using the optimal critic $f = r$. The ratio table has two
values: $r = \tfrac{0.75}{0.5} = 1.5$ on agreeing cells,
$r = \tfrac{0.25}{0.5} = 0.5$ on disagreeing cells.

<Example title="the bound at N = 2, enumerated">
One positive and one negative. The positive's ratio $\rho$ is $1.5$ with
probability $0.75$ (the pair agrees) and $0.5$ with probability $0.25$.
The negative is a uniform marginal draw, independent of the pair: its
ratio $r_2$ is $1.5$ or $0.5$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. Four
cases:

| $\rho$ | $r_2$ | prob. | softmax $\tfrac{\rho}{\rho + r_2}$ | loss (bits) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1.5$ | $1.5$ | $0.375$ | $0.5$ | $1$ |
| $1.5$ | $0.5$ | $0.375$ | $0.75$ | $0.415$ |
| $0.5$ | $1.5$ | $0.125$ | $0.25$ | $2$ |
| $0.5$ | $0.5$ | $0.125$ | $0.5$ | $1$ |

$$
L_2 = 0.375 \cdot 1 + 0.375 \cdot 0.415 + 0.125 \cdot 2 + 0.125 \cdot 1
\approx 0.906 \text{ bits}.
$$

Certified: $\log_2 2 - L_2 \approx 1 - 0.906 = 0.094$ bits, against the
true $I = 0.189$. The bound holds and captures about half the
information.
</Example>

The first row explains the slack. With probability $\tfrac12$ the
negative **equals** the positive's value: the two candidates are
identical, no classifier can beat the coin flip, and a full bit is paid.
Even the exact ratio critic pays for collisions between the marginal and
the conditional.

<Example title="the bound at N = 4">
One positive, three negatives. Let $k$ be the number of negatives whose
value agrees with $x$; each negative agrees independently with
probability $\tfrac12$, so $k \sim \mathrm{Bin}(3, \tfrac12)$ with
weights $\tfrac18, \tfrac38, \tfrac38, \tfrac18$. The denominator is
$\rho + 1.5k + 0.5(3-k)$.

**Branch $\rho = 1.5$** (probability $0.75$): denominator $3 + k$,
losses $-\log_2 \tfrac{1.5}{3+k}$ for $k = 0..3$: $1$, $1.415$, $1.737$,
$2$ bits. Weighted:
$\tfrac18(1) + \tfrac38(1.415) + \tfrac38(1.737) + \tfrac18(2) = 1.557$
bits.

**Branch $\rho = 0.5$** (probability $0.25$): denominator $2 + k$,
losses $2$, $2.585$, $3$, $3.322$ bits. Weighted: $2.760$ bits.

$$
L_4 = 0.75 \cdot 1.557 + 0.25 \cdot 2.760 \approx 1.858 \text{ bits},
\qquad
\log_2 4 - L_4 \approx 2 - 1.858 = 0.142 \text{ bits}.
$$
</Example>

The same enumeration at $N = 16$ gives $L_{16} \approx 3.823$ bits and a
certificate of $0.177$ bits. The loss itself **rises** with $N$, since the
task has more distractors, but the budget $\log_2 N$ rises faster, and
the certified difference climbs toward the true $0.189$:

## Saturation: nothing above log₂ N is certifiable

The softmax weight on the true partner is at most $1$, so the loss is
never negative, and
$$
\log_2 N - L_N \;\le\; \log_2 N
$$
always. An $N$-way question can certify at most the bits it takes to
answer it. When the true mutual information exceeds $\log_2 N$, the
classification task is simply too easy to be informative: the classifier
answers nearly perfectly, the loss sits near its floor, and the
certificate pins at the ceiling whatever the surplus dependence.

<Example title="a perfect critic, saturated">
Let $X$ be uniform on four symbols and $Y = X$ in lockstep:
$I(X;Y) = H(X) = 2$ bits. The ratio is $r = 4$ when $y = x$ and $0$
otherwise, so the optimal critic solves every round in which no negative
collides with the positive: the loss comes entirely from collisions.
With $k$ colliding negatives the softmax weight on the positive is
$\tfrac{4}{4(1+k)} = \tfrac{1}{1+k}$, and
$k \sim \mathrm{Bin}(N-1, \tfrac14)$:

$$
L_N = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\log_2 (1 + k)\bigr].
$$

| $N$ | $L_N$ (bits) | certified $\log_2 N - L_N$ | true $I$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $2$ | $0.250$ | $0.750$ | $2$ |
| $4$ | $0.676$ | $1.324$ | $2$ |
| $16$ | $2.145$ | $1.855$ | $2$ |
| $256$ | $6.008$ | $1.992$ | $2$ |

At $N = 2$ the ceiling is $\log_2 2 = 1$ bit and the certificate cannot
even reach that: half the true information is invisible. Only at
$N = 256$ does the certificate approach the true $2$ bits.
</Example>

More negatives raise the ceiling, at the rate of one extra bit per
doubling of $N$. Certifying $8$ bits requires $N > 256$; certifying the
$\sim\!10$ bits a rich image–caption pair might share requires
candidate counts in the thousands.

$I$ approaches $\log_2 N$.
</Task>

<Remark title="an estimate of a bound, not an estimator of I">
In practice $L_N$ is itself estimated from a finite batch, so
$\log_2 N - \hat L_N$ is a noisy estimate of a lower bound, not an
unbiased estimator of $I$, and on any given batch it can fluctuate above
the true value even though its target sits below. The two qualifications
compound: the quantity is (an estimate of) (a floor). Reporting it as
"the mutual information" overstates on both counts.
</Remark>

<Aside>
The ceiling is why contrastive training runs at extreme batch sizes.
In-batch negatives put $N$ equal to the batch size, so the batch caps
the bits the loss can see:
[SimCLR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05709) (Chen et al., 2020) trains
with batches up to $4096$ ($\log_2 4096 = 12$ bits of ceiling), and
[CLIP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020) (Radford et al., 2021) uses
$32{,}768$ ($15$ bits). The gain per doubling is a single bit, which is
also why returns diminish: the jump from $256$ to $32{,}768$ negatives
buys $7$ bits of ceiling, not a hundredfold improvement.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
$\log_2 N - L_N \le \log_2 N$ always: an $N$-way classification can
certify at most the bits needed to answer it, so dependence beyond the
ceiling stays invisible until $N$ grows. This is why contrastive methods
use large batches.
</KeyFact>

## The payoff: one loss across audio, vision, text, and RL

The bound was derived for representation learning, and the deriving
system shows where it sits in practice. Contrastive predictive coding
(CPC) processes a signal (raw speech, image patches read as a sequence,
text, an agent's observations) with an encoder producing latents $z_t$,
summarizes the past into a context $c_t$ with an autoregressive network,
and scores future latents $z_{t+k}$ against negatives from other
positions with a critic $f_k(z_{t+k}, c_t)$. The InfoNCE loss on this
lineup is the system's **only training signal**: minimizing it maximizes
a certified floor under $I(z_{t+k}; c_t)$, forcing the context to carry
the bits that predict the future, without reconstructing a single
sample of it.

The same loss, unchanged, works across modalities: the paper's
experiments (§3) train the identical objective on four domains. On
LibriSpeech speech, a linear classifier on CPC representations reaches
$64.6\%$ phone accuracy against $39.7\%$ for MFCC features and $74.6\%$
for a fully supervised network: most of the supervised gap closed with
no labels. On ImageNet, a linear probe on CPC features reaches $48.7\%$
top-1 accuracy; in reinforcement learning, adding the loss as an
auxiliary objective improves A2C agents on DeepMind Lab tasks. The
critic's density-ratio trick, with the pair $(x, y)$ instantiated as
context–future, image–image view, or image–caption, is the shared core
of the contrastive representation learning that followed, SimCLR and
CLIP among it.

The lesson also closes the arc of this module.
<Ref lesson="information-bottleneck"/> prescribed what a good
representation should optimize: keep $I(T;Y)$, spend little $I(X;T)$.
Both terms were uncomputable outside toy tables. InfoNCE makes the relevance
term trainable: $\log_2 N - L_N$ is a differentiable floor under a
mutual information, maximized by gradient descent on the critic and
encoder jointly. A prescription became a loss function.

<KeyFact>
One InfoNCE loss trains representations in speech, vision, text, and RL;
the density-ratio critic is the common core of contrastive
representation learning, and the bound is the trainable surrogate for
the bottleneck's relevance term $I(T;Y)$.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Repeat the $N = 2$ enumeration on the dependence-dial table at
$q = 0.9$ (diagonal cells $0.45$, off-diagonal $0.05$; the table of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, Exercise 3, with
$I(X;Y) \approx 0.531$ bits). Write the ratio table, enumerate the four
cases for the optimal critic, compute $L_2$ and the certificate
$\log_2 2 - L_2$, and compare with the true $I$. Use
$-\log_2 0.9 \approx 0.152$ and $\log_2 10 \approx 3.322$.

<Solution>
**Ratios.** Diagonal: $r = 0.9/0.5 = 1.8$; off-diagonal:
$r = 0.1/0.5 = 0.2$.

**Cases.** The positive's ratio is $1.8$ with probability $0.9$, $0.2$
with probability $0.1$; the negative's is $1.8$ or $0.2$ with
probability $\tfrac12$ each, independently:

| $\rho$ | $r_2$ | prob. | softmax | loss (bits) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1.8$ | $1.8$ | $0.45$ | $0.5$ | $1$ |
| $1.8$ | $0.2$ | $0.45$ | $0.9$ | $0.152$ |
| $0.2$ | $1.8$ | $0.05$ | $0.1$ | $3.322$ |
| $0.2$ | $0.2$ | $0.05$ | $0.5$ | $1$ |

$$
L_2 = 0.45 + 0.45 \cdot 0.152 + 0.05 \cdot 3.322 + 0.05 \cdot 1
\approx 0.734 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Certificate.** $1 - 0.734 = 0.266$ bits, against the true
$I \approx 0.531$: the bound holds and captures about half the
information. As in the lesson's toy, the dominant slack is the
collision row ($\rho = r_2 = 1.8$), where the candidates are identical
and a full bit is paid regardless of the critic.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the optimal posterior for $N = 2$ directly. One candidate pair
$(y_1, y_2)$ is presented for context $x$; with probability $\tfrac12$
the partner is $y_1$ (then $y_2 \sim p(y)$), otherwise $y_2$. Show from
Bayes' rule that
$$
p(\text{pos}{=}1 \mid x, y_1, y_2)
= \frac{r(x,y_1)}{r(x,y_1) + r(x,y_2)},
$$
and verify the formula on the lesson's concrete round ($x = 0$,
$y_1 = 0$, $y_2 = 1$, toy table at $q = 0.75$).

<Solution>
The two hypotheses have likelihoods
$p(y_1 \mid x)\, p(y_2)$ and $p(y_2 \mid x)\, p(y_1)$, each with prior
$\tfrac12$. Bayes' rule:
$$
p(\text{pos}{=}1 \mid x, y_1, y_2)
= \frac{p(y_1 \mid x)\, p(y_2)}
{p(y_1 \mid x)\, p(y_2) + p(y_2 \mid x)\, p(y_1)}.
$$
Divide numerator and denominator by $p(y_1)\, p(y_2) > 0$:
$$
= \frac{p(y_1 \mid x)/p(y_1)}
{p(y_1 \mid x)/p(y_1) + p(y_2 \mid x)/p(y_2)}
= \frac{r(x,y_1)}{r(x,y_1) + r(x,y_2)}.
$$
On the concrete round: $r(0,0) = 1.5$, $r(0,1) = 0.5$, so the posterior
is $\tfrac{1.5}{1.5 + 0.5} = 0.75$, matching the direct Bayes
computation in the lesson: $\tfrac{0.75 \cdot 0.5}{0.75 \cdot 0.5 +
0.25 \cdot 0.5} = 0.75$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Prove the unit-mean identity used in step 3 of the bound's derivation:
for every $x$,
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim p(y)}\left[\frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)}\right] = 1,
$$
and state in one sentence why it makes the negatives' score sum
concentrate near $N - 1$.

<Solution>
Expand the expectation over the marginal:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{y \sim p(y)}\left[\frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)}\right]
= \sum_y p(y)\, \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)}
= \sum_y p(y \mid x) = 1,
$$
since $p(y \mid x)$ is a probability distribution over $y$ for each
fixed $x$ (terms with $p(y) = 0$ contribute nothing: such $y$ is never
drawn). The negatives' total $S = \sum_{j=1}^{N-1} r(x, y_j)$ is a sum
of $N-1$ independent terms each with mean $1$, so
$\mathbb{E}[S] = N-1$ and the relative fluctuation of $S$ around $N-1$
shrinks as $N$ grows: the justification for replacing $S$ by $N-1$ in
the derivation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Prove that the certificate never exceeds the budget:
$\log_2 N - L_N[f] \le \log_2 N$ for every critic $f$. Then compute the
saturation exactly for a binary lockstep pair ($X$ uniform on
$\{0,1\}$, $Y = X$, so $I(X;Y) = 1$ bit) at $N = 2$ with the optimal
critic, and state what fraction of the true information is certified.

<Solution>
**Ceiling.** The softmax weight on the true partner is a term of a
distribution, hence at most $1$, so every round's bill
$-\log_2(\text{weight}) \ge 0$ and $L_N[f] \ge 0$. Subtracting,
$\log_2 N - L_N \le \log_2 N$.

**Binary lockstep at $N = 2$.** The ratio is $r = \tfrac{1}{1/2} = 2$
when $y = x$ and $0$ otherwise; the positive always has $\rho = 2$. The
single negative is a fair marginal draw: with probability $\tfrac12$ it
collides ($y = x$), making the candidates identical: softmax
$\tfrac{2}{2+2} = \tfrac12$, loss $1$ bit. With probability $\tfrac12$
it differs: softmax $\tfrac{2}{2+0} = 1$, loss $0$.
$$
L_2 = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0 = 0.5 \text{ bits},
\qquad
\log_2 2 - L_2 = 0.5 \text{ bits}.
$$
The certificate is $0.5$ of the true $1$ bit: exactly half. Even a
perfect critic loses a full bit whenever the marginal draw duplicates
the positive, and at $N = 2$ that happens half the time. (Raising $N$
recovers the rest: the ceiling $\log_2 N$ grows while collisions cost
only $\log_2(1+k)$.)
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Review of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>: for the weather table
($p(\text{clear},\text{dry}) = \tfrac12$,
$p(\text{clear},\text{wet}) = 0$,
$p(\text{overcast},\text{dry}) = p(\text{overcast},\text{wet}) =
\tfrac14$, with $X$ the sky and $Y$ the ground), compute the ratio
$r(x,y) = p(y \mid x)/p(y)$ for each nonzero cell and verify the
identity the bound's derivation turns on:
$\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}[\log_2 r] = I(X;Y) \approx 0.311$ bits. Use
$\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$.

<Solution>
Marginals: $p(\text{dry}) = \tfrac34$, $p(\text{wet}) = \tfrac14$.
Conditionals given the sky: clear $\Rightarrow$ dry certain
($p(\text{dry} \mid \text{clear}) = 1$); overcast $\Rightarrow$ each
with probability $\tfrac12$.

Ratios:
$r(\text{clear}, \text{dry}) = \tfrac{1}{3/4} = \tfrac43$;
$r(\text{overcast}, \text{dry}) = \tfrac{1/2}{3/4} = \tfrac23$;
$r(\text{overcast}, \text{wet}) = \tfrac{1/2}{1/4} = 2$. The
zero-probability cell (clear, wet) never occurs and is skipped.

Expected log ratio, weighted by the joint:
$$
\tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac43 + \tfrac14 \log_2 \tfrac23 + \tfrac14 \log_2 2
= \tfrac12 (0.415) + \tfrac14 (-0.585) + \tfrac14 (1)
\approx 0.208 - 0.146 + 0.250 = 0.311 \text{ bits},
$$
matching $I(X;Y) \approx 0.311$ from the mutual-information lesson: the
log ratios are the pointwise mutual informations, and their
joint-weighted average is $I$. An optimal contrastive critic on this
table would score (overcast, wet) highest ($r = 2$) and
(overcast, dry) below $1$: the cell that occurs less often than
independence predicts.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A contrastive model trained with $N = 1024$ candidates reports an
InfoNCE loss of $2.30$ nats. Convert the certificate to bits. Then state
what a reported loss of $\ln 1024 \approx 6.93$ nats would certify, and
why.

<Solution>
In nats the bound reads $I \ge \ln N - L_N$. Converting via
$1 \text{ bit} = \ln 2 \approx 0.693$ nats
(<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>): the loss is
$2.30 / 0.693 \approx 3.32$ bits, the budget is
$\log_2 1024 = 10$ bits, so
$$
I(X;Y) \ge 10 - 3.32 \approx 6.7 \text{ bits}.
$$
(Equivalently in nats: $6.93 - 2.30 = 4.63$ nats $= 6.7$ bits.)

A loss of $\ln 1024$ nats is exactly the chance loss $\log_2 1024$ bits:
the certificate is $10 - 10 = 0$ bits. It certifies nothing, consistent
both with independent variables and with a critic that has learned
nothing, which is the one-way direction of the bound.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Suppose the negatives are drawn not from the marginal $p(y)$ but from
some other distribution $q(y)$ with full support. Show that the
Bayes-optimal critic becomes $f(x,y) \propto p(y \mid x)/q(y)$, and
that the expected log of this ratio at true pairs is
$$
\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\log_2 \frac{p(y \mid x)}{q(y)}\right]
= I(X;Y) + D\bigl(p_Y \,\|\, q\bigr),
$$
where $p_Y$ is the true marginal and $D$ is the KL divergence of
<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>. Conclude what goes wrong with the
certificate.

<Solution>
**The critic.** Rerun the posterior derivation with negatives from $q$:
given $\text{pos} = i$, the candidates have density
$p(y_i \mid x) \prod_{j \ne i} q(y_j)$. Dividing numerator and
denominator of Bayes' rule by $\prod_j q(y_j)$ leaves
$$
p(\text{pos}{=}i \mid x, y_{1..N})
= \frac{p(y_i \mid x)/q(y_i)}{\sum_k p(y_k \mid x)/q(y_k)},
$$
so the optimal critic is the ratio against the **wrong reference**,
$r'(x,y) = p(y \mid x)/q(y)$.

**The expectation.** Split the logarithm:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\log_2 \frac{p(y \mid x)}{q(y)}\right]
= \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\log_2 \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)}\right]
+ \mathbb{E}_{p(x,y)}\left[\log_2 \frac{p(y)}{q(y)}\right].
$$
The first term is $I(X;Y)$ (divergence form). In the second, the
integrand depends only on $y$, so the expectation collapses to the
marginal: $\sum_y p(y) \log_2 \tfrac{p(y)}{q(y)} = D(p_Y \| q)$.

**Conclusion.** The role that $I$ played in the derivation is now
played by $I(X;Y) + D(p_Y \| q) \ge I(X;Y)$: for large $N$ the quantity
$\log_2 N - L_N$ approaches the inflated value, exceeding the true
mutual information by exactly the marginal mismatch. The one-way
certificate is broken: the reported number can overstate $I$. This is
why implementations build negatives by shuffling real partners (exact
marginal draws) rather than sampling from a convenient noise model.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

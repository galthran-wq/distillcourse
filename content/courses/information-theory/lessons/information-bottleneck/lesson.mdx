The information bottleneck makes relevant compression precise: choose a
stochastic encoder mapping X to T that minimizes I(X;T) - beta I(T;Y),
keeping the bits of X that inform the target Y and discarding the rest.
This lesson replaces rate-distortion's hand-picked distortion measure with
a second mutual information, computes the trade-off in full on a
four-symbol source with a binary label, maps every candidate encoder into
the information plane under two data-processing ceilings, states the
Gibbs-form optimal encoder whose distortion measure emerges as a KL
divergence, and derives the exchange rate $\beta$ that prices a relevant
bit in bits of rate.

## Relevance replaces distortion: the flaw the bottleneck fixes

<Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/> priced lossy compression as
$R(D) = \min I(X;\hat X)$ subject to $\mathbb{E}[d(x, \hat x)] \le D$, and
its closing observation was that one ingredient is not derived: someone
must pick the distortion measure $d$, and that choice silently decides
which differences matter. A codec judged by squared error spends bits on
deviations no user of the data cares about.

[Tishby, Pereira & Bialek (1999)](https://arxiv.org/abs/physics/0004057)
remove the hand-picked ingredient by changing what the constraint asks
for. Instead of "reconstruct $X$ within tolerance $d$", the requirement
becomes "keep information about a **target variable** $Y$": predict a
label from an input, a disease from a scan, the next word from a context.
Which bits of $X$ are *relevant* is then defined by the joint distribution
$p(x, y)$ itself, not by a designer. The reconstruction term
$\mathbb{E}[d(x, \hat x)]$ is replaced by a second mutual information,
$I(T;Y)$, measured in the same currency as the rate.

The information bottleneck is rate-distortion with the distortion measure

becomes "inform about $Y$", and $p(x,y)$ decides which bits are relevant.

## The objective: compress X, keep Y

### The pieces: a fixed joint, a chosen encoder

Two distributions play different roles. The joint $p(x, y)$ is **given**:
it is the statistics of the data. The encoder is **chosen**: a
*stochastic encoder* is a conditional distribution $p(t \mid x)$ mapping
each input $x$ to a distribution over representation values $t$, with
deterministic maps $T = f(X)$ as the special case of one-point
conditionals.

The encoder reads $x$ only. $T$ never sees $Y$, so the three variables
form the Markov chain $Y \to X \to T$ of
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>: all of $T$'s dependence on
$Y$ routes through $X$. The arrow direction is a hypothesis, not a
decoration. $T$ is a compressed code of $X$, not a hidden cause
generating $Y$; Tishby, Pereira & Bialek flag exactly this reading in a
footnote, and every figure in this lesson keeps the encoder arrow
pointing out of $X$.

<Definition title="information bottleneck objective">
Given a joint distribution $p(x,y)$ and a trade-off parameter
$\beta \ge 0$, the **information bottleneck** problem is
$$
\min_{p(t \mid x)} \; \mathcal{L}\bigl[p(t \mid x)\bigr]
= I(X;T) \;-\; \beta\, I(T;Y),
$$
the minimum over stochastic encoders $p(t \mid x)$ with a fixed number
$|\mathcal{T}|$ of representation values, where $T$ obeys the Markov
chain $Y \to X \to T$.
</Definition>

### The two extremes of β

The parameter $\beta$ prices relevant bits in units of rate, and its
extremes bracket the behavior:

- **$\beta \to 0$**: relevance is worthless, and the objective reduces to
  minimizing $I(X;T)$ alone. The minimum is $0$, attained by collapsing
  every $x$ to one constant value of $T$: maximal compression, zero
  information about anything.
- **$\beta \to \infty$**: rate is free, and the objective reduces to
  maximizing $I(T;Y)$. Copying, $T = X$, attains the maximum the next
  section shows is $I(X;Y)$: all the relevance $X$ ever had, at full
  rate $H(X)$.

Every finite $\beta$ buys a point between these extremes. The rest of
the lesson computes what the intermediate points look like, first by
brute force on a toy source, then in general.

<KeyFact>
$\mathcal{L} = I(X;T) - \beta\, I(T;Y)$ trades bits kept about $X$
against bits kept about $Y$: $\beta \to 0$ collapses $T$ to a constant,
$\beta \to \infty$ makes $T$ a copy of $X$, and finite $\beta$ prices
every position in between.
</KeyFact>

## The trade-off computed: four symbols, one binary label

### The source: same rate, different relevance

Let $X$ be uniform on $\{a, b, c, d\}$, $p(x) = \tfrac14$ each, with a
binary label $Y$ attached through the conditionals
$$
p(1 \mid a) = 0, \quad
p(1 \mid b) = \tfrac14, \quad
p(1 \mid c) = \tfrac34, \quad
p(1 \mid d) = 1,
$$
writing $p(1 \mid x)$ for $p(Y{=}1 \mid X{=}x)$. The joint table:

| $p(x,y)$ | $x = a$ | $x = b$ | $x = c$ | $x = d$ | $p(y)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $y = 0$ | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac{3}{16}$ | $\tfrac{1}{16}$ | $0$ | $\tfrac12$ |
| $y = 1$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{1}{16}$ | $\tfrac{3}{16}$ | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac12$ |
| $p(x)$ | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac14$ | $1$ |

<Example title="the budget in play: I(X;Y)">
The label's marginal is fair, $H(Y) = 1$ bit. Given $X$, the label is
certain for $a$ and $d$ and a $\tfrac14$-coin for $b$ and $c$
($H_2(\tfrac14) = H_2(\tfrac34) \approx 0.811$):
$$
H(Y \mid X) = \tfrac14\bigl(0 + 0.811 + 0.811 + 0\bigr) \approx 0.406
\text{ bits},
$$
so by <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>,
$$
I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \approx 1 - 0.406 = 0.594 \text{ bits}.
$$
$X$ carries $0.594$ bits about the label, out of $H(X) = 2$ bits of
description: less than a third of the input is relevant.
</Example>

### Three hard 2-cluster encoders, priced

Take the simplest nontrivial encoders: deterministic merges of the four
symbols into two clusters of two, $T = f(X)$ with
$|\mathcal{T}| = 2$. There are three such pairings. For each,
$I(X;T) = H(T) - H(T \mid X) = H(T) = 1$ bit, since the clusters are
equiprobable and $T$ is a function of $X$: **all three cost the same
rate**, so the comparison isolates relevance.

<Example title="the aligned merge: ab | cd">
Cluster $t_1 = \{a, b\}$, $t_2 = \{c, d\}$, each with $p(t) = \tfrac12$.

**Step 1: label given cluster.** The mass with $Y = 1$ inside $t_1$ is
$p(a)\, p(1 \mid a) + p(b)\, p(1 \mid b) = 0 + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac14 =
\tfrac{1}{16}$, so
$p(1 \mid t_1) = \tfrac{1/16}{1/2} = \tfrac18$. Symmetrically
$p(1 \mid t_2) = \tfrac78$.

**Step 2: conditional entropy.** Both clusters are $\tfrac18$-coins:
$H(Y \mid T) = H_2(\tfrac18) \approx 0.544$ bits.

**Step 3: subtract.**
$$
I(T;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid T) \approx 1 - 0.544 = 0.456 \text{ bits}.
$$
The merge keeps $0.456$ of the $0.594$ available bits; the loss is
$0.138$ bits.
</Example>

The other two pairings, computed the same way (Exercise 1 works the
first in full):

- **$\{a,c\} \mid \{b,d\}$**: cluster conditionals
  $p(1 \mid t) = \tfrac38$ and $\tfrac58$, so
  $H(Y \mid T) = H_2(\tfrac38) \approx 0.954$ and
  $I(T;Y) \approx 0.046$ bits.
- **$\{a,d\} \mid \{b,c\}$**: both cluster conditionals equal
  $\tfrac12$, so $H(Y \mid T) = 1$ and $I(T;Y) = 0$ bits: one bit of
  rate spent, nothing about the label kept.

The ranking is decided entirely by **which conditionals get fused**.
Merging $a$ with $b$ works because $p(y \mid a)$ and $p(y \mid b)$ are
similar: inside the cluster, the label distribution barely blurs.
Merging $a$ with $d$ fuses opposite conditionals into a fair coin and
destroys everything. The Gibbs-form optimum below turns this observation
into the exact assignment rule.

One caveat scopes the enumeration: hard clusterings are a strict subset
of stochastic encoders. The definition optimizes over all
$p(t \mid x)$, and the true optimum at a given rate can strictly beat
every hard partition; the hard points computed here are candidates, not
the frontier itself.

<KeyFact>
At equal rate $I(X;T) = 1$ bit, the three 2-cluster encoders keep
$0.456$, $0.046$, and $0$ bits about $Y$: relevance is decided by how
similar the fused conditionals $p(y \mid x)$ are, not by how much of
$X$ survives.
</KeyFact>

## The information plane: two ceilings cap every encoder

### Both bounds are the data-processing inequality

Plot every encoder as a point with coordinates
$\bigl(I(X;T),\, I(T;Y)\bigr)$: rate spent against relevance kept. This
coordinate system is the *information plane*, and two lines cap it, both
inherited from <Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>.

The Markov chain $Y \to X \to T$ reverses to $T \to X \to Y$ (the
factorization is symmetric, as established there), and the DPI applied
to each reading direction gives one ceiling:

- **The relevance ceiling.** From $Y \to X \to T$:
  $\;I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$. No encoder can know more about the target than
  the input did; for the toy source, no point rises above $0.594$ bits.
- **The rate diagonal.** From $T \to X \to Y$:
  $\;I(T;Y) \le I(X;T)$. A representation cannot say more about $Y$
  than it contains about anything: relevance never exceeds rate, so
  every point lies on or below the $45°$ line.

### The frontier: what β sweeps out

For each rate the interesting quantity is the **largest** relevance any
encoder achieves; the resulting upper boundary is the *frontier*, traced
by solving the bottleneck problem as $\beta$ sweeps from $0$ to
$\infty$. Three facts locate it:

- It starts at $(0, 0)$: zero rate forces $T$ independent of $X$, hence
  of $Y$ (the $\beta \to 0$ collapse).
- It ends on the ceiling: at rate $H(X) = 2$ bits the identity encoder
  reaches $I(T;Y) = I(X;Y) = 0.594$, and more rate buys nothing.
- It is concave, and there is one such curve per cardinality
  $|\mathcal{T}|$: allowing more clusters relaxes the optimization, so
  the curves nest, each approaching the $|\mathcal{T}| = |\mathcal{X}|$
  master curve from below.

The toy's hard encoders sit **under** the frontier at their rates,
except where they happen to touch it; the stochastic optimum at rate
$1$ bit is at least the $0.456$ bits of the aligned merge.

<KeyFact>
The feasible region of the bottleneck is bounded by two applications of
one theorem: $I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$ and $I(T;Y) \le I(X;T)$, the DPI read
along the chain in its two directions.
</KeyFact>

## The optimal encoder: the distortion measure emerges as a KL

### Exactly what a merge loses: an identity

The toy ranking suggested that the cost of assigning $x$ to a cluster
$t$ is the mismatch between $p(y \mid x)$ and the cluster's aggregate
conditional $p(y \mid t)$. That is an exact accounting, not a
heuristic.

<Theorem title="relevance loss is an expected KL divergence">
For any encoder $p(t \mid x)$ with $Y \to X \to T$,
$$
I(X;Y) - I(T;Y)
= \mathbb{E}_{p(x,t)}\Bigl[\, D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t)\bigr) \Bigr]
\;\ge\; 0,
$$
the average, over inputs and their cluster assignments, of the KL
divergence of <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/> between the input's label
conditional and its cluster's label conditional.

<Proof>
Expand the expectation:
$$
\mathbb{E}\bigl[D\bigr]
= \sum_{x,t} p(x,t) \sum_y p(y \mid x)
\log_2 \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y \mid t)}.
$$
The Markov chain gives $p(y \mid x, t) = p(y \mid x)$, so
$p(x,t)\,p(y \mid x) = p(x,t,y)$, and the sum splits into two full
expectations over the joint. The numerator term is
$\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(y \mid x) = -H(Y \mid X)$. The denominator
term is
$-\sum_{x,t,y} p(x,t,y) \log_2 p(y \mid t)
= -\sum_{t,y} p(t,y) \log_2 p(y \mid t) = H(Y \mid T)$. Hence
$$
\mathbb{E}\bigl[D\bigr] = H(Y \mid T) - H(Y \mid X)
= \bigl[H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)\bigr] - \bigl[H(Y) - H(Y \mid T)\bigr]
= I(X;Y) - I(T;Y),
$$
and nonnegativity of each KL term makes the loss nonnegative, which is
the relevance ceiling again.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

**Numeric check** on the aligned merge: the four assignment costs are
$D\bigl((1,0) \,\|\, (\tfrac78, \tfrac18)\bigr) = \log_2 \tfrac87
\approx 0.193$ bits for $a$, $\approx 0.083$ bits for $b$, and by
symmetry $0.083$ and $0.193$ for $c$ and $d$. Their average,
$\tfrac14(0.193 + 0.083 + 0.083 + 0.193) \approx 0.138$ bits, is
exactly the loss $0.594 - 0.456$ computed by brute force (Exercise 3
verifies the four divergences).

### Theorem 4: the Gibbs form

The identity says the objective is rate plus $\beta$ times an expected
KL "distortion". Minimizing it yields the central result of Tishby,
Pereira & Bialek, their Theorem 4, stated here without its Lagrange
derivation.

<Theorem title="optimal bottleneck encoder (Tishby–Pereira–Bialek)">
The stationary encoders of
$\mathcal{L} = I(X;T) - \beta\, I(T;Y)$ satisfy
$$
p(t \mid x) = \frac{p(t)}{Z(x, \beta)}
\exp\!\Bigl(-\beta\, D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t)\bigr)\Bigr),
$$
where $Z(x, \beta)$ normalizes over $t$, and the cluster statistics are
determined self-consistently by the encoder:
$$
p(t) = \sum_x p(x)\, p(t \mid x),
\qquad
p(y \mid t) = \frac{1}{p(t)} \sum_x p(x, y)\, p(t \mid x).
$$
</Theorem>

The exponent is the theorem's point: the quantity playing the role that
$d(x, \hat x)$ played in rate-distortion is
$D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t)\bigr)$, **derived** from the
objective rather than assumed. Relevance defines its own distortion
measure: the cost of putting $x$ into cluster $t$ is how differently
$x$ and $t$ predict the target.

Three readings of the form:

- **It explains the toy ranking.** The aligned merge won because its
  assignment costs ($0.193$ and $0.083$ bits) are the smallest any
  2-cluster partition can offer; the $\{a,d\}$ merge charges
  $D\bigl((1,0) \,\|\, (\tfrac12,\tfrac12)\bigr) = 1$ bit per certain
  symbol.
- **It is a soft assignment.** At finite $\beta$ the encoder is
  genuinely stochastic, splitting borderline inputs between clusters
  in proportion to $e^{-\beta D}$; hard partitions reappear only as
  $\beta \to \infty$. This is how the frontier dominates the hard
  points.
- **It is rate-distortion's own solution form.** The Gibbs encoder is
  the standard rate-distortion optimality condition with $d(x, t)$
  replaced by the emergent KL, and the self-consistent equations are
  solved by alternating updates exactly as
  [Blahut–Arimoto](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
  (Cover & Thomas §10.8) solves $R(D)$: Tishby, Pereira & Bialek's
  Theorem 5 proves the iteration converges, though not to a unique
  optimum; the algorithm itself is out of scope here.

<KeyFact>
The optimal encoder is
$p(t \mid x) \propto p(t)\, e^{-\beta D(p(y \mid x) \| p(y \mid t))}$:
the distortion measure rate-distortion required as an input is here an
output of the objective, and it is a KL divergence between label
conditionals.
</KeyFact>

## Along the frontier: one relevant bit costs β bits of rate

### The slope, derived from the Lagrangian

Write the frontier as a function: $I_Y^\ast(R)$ is the largest
relevance any encoder achieves at rate $I(X;T) = R$. A $\beta$-optimal
encoder minimizes $\mathcal{L} = R - \beta I_Y$, so at its frontier
point a small move **along the frontier** cannot change the objective
to first order:
$$
\delta \mathcal{L} = \delta R - \beta\, \delta I_Y = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\delta R}{\delta I_Y} = \beta,
\qquad
\frac{d I_Y^\ast}{d R} = \frac{1}{\beta}.
$$
Each additional bit of relevance costs exactly $\beta$ bits of rate at
the operating point $\beta$ selects; equivalently the frontier's slope
in the plane is $1/\beta$. Conventions differ across write-ups (some
papers put $\beta$ on the rate term, flipping the slope to $\beta$),
which is why the relation is derived here from the stated
$\mathcal{L}$ rather than quoted.

Because the frontier is concave, its slope falls monotonically:
**cheap relevant bits are bought first**. Near the origin, one bit of
rate buys nearly one bit of relevance (small $\beta$ suffices); near
the ceiling, each remaining sliver of relevance costs many bits of rate
(large $\beta$).

<Example title="where each encoder wins">
Compare three toy encoders by
$\mathcal{L} = I(X;T) - \beta\, I(T;Y)$ as $\beta$ varies:
$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{collapse}} = 0, \qquad
\mathcal{L}_{ab|cd} = 1 - 0.456\,\beta, \qquad
\mathcal{L}_{T=X} = 2 - 0.594\,\beta.
$$

**Collapse vs. merge.** $1 - 0.456\,\beta < 0$ exactly when
$\beta > 1/0.456 \approx 2.19$: below this price, relevance is too
cheap to bother paying a bit of rate for, and total collapse wins.

**Merge vs. copy.** $2 - 0.594\,\beta < 1 - 0.456\,\beta$ exactly when
$0.138\,\beta > 1$, i.e. $\beta > 1/0.138 \approx 7.25$: the extra bit
of rate from merge to copy buys only $0.138$ bits of relevance, so it
pays only when a relevant bit is worth more than $7.25$ rate bits.

Among these three candidates: collapse for $\beta < 2.19$, the aligned
merge for $2.19 < \beta < 7.25$, the copy for $\beta > 7.25$. The
crossover values are exactly the inverse slopes
$\Delta R / \Delta I_Y$ between consecutive points, the exchange-rate
relation read off discretely (Exercise 7 proves this in general).
</Example>

### The β → ∞ limit: minimal sufficient statistics

At $\beta \to \infty$ the objective keeps **all** the relevance,
$I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$, at the least possible rate. That is the equality
case of the DPI: $T$ is a sufficient statistic for $Y$
(<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>), and among sufficient
statistics the bottleneck selects a minimal one, the smallest $I(X;T)$
that still loses nothing
([Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
§2.9). The bottleneck at large $\beta$ is therefore a variational
construction of minimal sufficiency; at finite $\beta$ it relaxes
sufficiency into a continuum of lossy summaries. On the toy source the
four conditionals $p(y \mid x)$ are pairwise distinct, so no merge is
sufficient and the minimal sufficient statistic is $X$ itself: the
frontier reaches the ceiling only at full rate. Exercise 6 modifies
the source so a genuine 1-bit sufficient statistic exists.

### What the objective does not give you

Everything above assumed the joint $p(x, y)$ is known and small enough
to enumerate. Estimating the two mutual informations from samples of
high-dimensional data is its own hard problem, and it gates every
practical use of the objective: the trainable route, a variational
bound optimized with neural encoders, is
[deep variational IB](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.00410) (Alemi et al.,
2017), and the general tool for bounding mutual information from
samples is the InfoNCE bound of the next lesson,
<Ref lesson="infonce"/>.

<Aside>
Two adjacent literatures border the objective without being part of
it. First, the famous "information plane trajectories of deep
networks": [Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby (2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.00810)
plotted $\bigl(I(X;T), I(T;Y)\bigr)$ for hidden layers $T$ during SGD
training and reported a fitting phase followed by a compression phase;
[Saxe et al. (2018)](https://openreview.net/forum?id=ry_WPG-A-) found
the compression phase absent for non-saturating activations and
sensitive to the mutual-information estimator. The 1999 paper contains
none of these claims (and zero figures); this lesson teaches the
objective, on which the dynamics debate has no bearing. Second, the
"keep few bits of the data" idea reappears in generalization theory:
[Xu & Raginsky (2017)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.07809) bound the
expected generalization gap of a learning algorithm by
$\sqrt{2\sigma^2 I(S;W)/n}$, where $I(S;W)$ is the mutual information
between training set and learned weights, the quantity the aside of
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/> introduced: an algorithm
that keeps few bits about its sample cannot overfit it, a sibling of
the bottleneck's compression term.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Moving along the frontier, one additional bit of $I(T;Y)$ costs
$\beta$ bits of $I(X;T)$; $\beta \to \infty$ recovers minimal
sufficient statistics, and finite $\beta$ prices the lossy summaries
in between.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Work the $\{a,c\} \mid \{b,d\}$ merge of the toy source in full:
compute the cluster conditionals $p(1 \mid t)$, the conditional entropy
$H(Y \mid T)$, and $I(T;Y)$, and state why this pairing loses almost
all relevance at the same rate as the aligned merge. Use
$H_2(\tfrac38) \approx 0.954$.

<Solution>
Clusters $t_1 = \{a, c\}$ and $t_2 = \{b, d\}$, each with
$p(t) = \tfrac12$.

**Cluster conditionals.** Mass with $Y = 1$ in $t_1$:
$p(a)\,p(1 \mid a) + p(c)\,p(1 \mid c) = 0 + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac34
= \tfrac{3}{16}$, so $p(1 \mid t_1) = \tfrac{3/16}{1/2} = \tfrac38$.
In $t_2$: $\tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 \cdot 1 = \tfrac{5}{16}$,
so $p(1 \mid t_2) = \tfrac58$.

**Conditional entropy.** Both clusters have entropy
$H_2(\tfrac38) = H_2(\tfrac58) \approx 0.954$ bits, so
$H(Y \mid T) \approx 0.954$ bits.

**Relevance.** $I(T;Y) = 1 - 0.954 = 0.046$ bits, against $0.456$ for
the aligned merge at the identical rate $I(X;T) = 1$ bit.

The pairing fuses distant conditionals: $0$ with $\tfrac34$, and
$\tfrac14$ with $1$. Each cluster's aggregate conditional lands near
$\tfrac12$, blurring the label almost to a fair coin; relevance is
decided by the similarity of the fused $p(y \mid x)$, not by the
cluster sizes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Price the unequal split $T$ with clusters $\{a\}$ and $\{b, c, d\}$:
compute $I(X;T)$ and $I(T;Y)$, then verify both information-plane
ceilings from the data-processing inequality. Use
$H_2(\tfrac14) \approx 0.811$ and $H_2(\tfrac13) \approx 0.918$.

<Solution>
**Rate.** $T$ is a function of $X$ with
$p(t_1) = \tfrac14$, $p(t_2) = \tfrac34$:
$I(X;T) = H(T) = H_2(\tfrac14) \approx 0.811$ bits.

**Relevance.** Given $t_1 = \{a\}$: $Y = 0$ certain, entropy $0$.
Given $t_2$: mass with $Y = 1$ is
$\tfrac{1}{16} + \tfrac{3}{16} + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$, so
$p(1 \mid t_2) = \tfrac{1/2}{3/4} = \tfrac23$, entropy
$H_2(\tfrac23) = H_2(\tfrac13) \approx 0.918$ bits. Average:
$$
H(Y \mid T) = \tfrac14 \cdot 0 + \tfrac34 \cdot 0.918 \approx 0.689
\text{ bits},
\qquad
I(T;Y) = 1 - 0.689 = 0.311 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Ceilings.** Relevance ceiling: $0.311 \le 0.594 = I(X;Y)$, the DPI
along $Y \to X \to T$. Rate diagonal: $0.311 \le 0.811 = I(X;T)$, the
DPI along the reversed chain $T \to X \to Y$. Both hold; the point
$(0.811, 0.311)$ sits inside the feasible region, below the frontier.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Verify the relevance-loss identity on the aligned merge
$\{a,b\} \mid \{c,d\}$: compute the four assignment costs
$D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t(x))\bigr)$ from the definition of
KL divergence, average them under $p(x)$, and check the result against
$I(X;Y) - I(T;Y) = 0.594 - 0.456$. Use $\log_2 \tfrac87 \approx 0.193$
and $\log_2 \tfrac67 \approx -0.222$.

<Solution>
The cluster conditionals are
$p(y \mid t_{ab}) = (\tfrac78, \tfrac18)$ and
$p(y \mid t_{cd}) = (\tfrac18, \tfrac78)$, ordered $(y{=}0, y{=}1)$.

**Symbol $a$**, $p(y \mid a) = (1, 0)$:
$$
D = 1 \cdot \log_2 \frac{1}{7/8} + 0 = \log_2 \tfrac87 \approx 0.193
\text{ bits}.
$$
(The $y{=}1$ term contributes $0$ by the convention
$0 \log \tfrac{0}{q} = 0$.)

**Symbol $b$**, $p(y \mid b) = (\tfrac34, \tfrac14)$:
$$
D = \tfrac34 \log_2 \frac{3/4}{7/8} + \tfrac14 \log_2 \frac{1/4}{1/8}
= \tfrac34 \log_2 \tfrac67 + \tfrac14 \cdot 1
\approx -0.167 + 0.25 = 0.083 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Symbols $c$ and $d$** mirror $b$ and $a$ against $t_{cd}$:
$0.083$ and $0.193$ bits.

**Average.** Under the uniform $p(x)$:
$$
\tfrac14 (0.193 + 0.083 + 0.083 + 0.193) \approx 0.138 \text{ bits},
$$
which matches $I(X;Y) - I(T;Y) = 0.594 - 0.456 = 0.138$ bits: the
relevance the merge destroys is exactly the average KL divergence
between each symbol's label conditional and its cluster's.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
At $\beta = 3$, rank the following toy encoders by the objective
$\mathcal{L} = I(X;T) - \beta\, I(T;Y)$, using the coordinates computed
in this lesson: collapse $(0, 0)$; the three 2-cluster merges
$(1, 0.456)$, $(1, 0.046)$, $(1, 0)$; the split $\{a\} \mid \{b,c,d\}$
at $(0.811, 0.311)$; the identity $(2, 0.594)$. State the winner and
check it against the crossover prices of the worked example.

<Solution>
Evaluate $\mathcal{L} = I(X;T) - 3\, I(T;Y)$:

- collapse: $0 - 0 = 0$
- $\{a,b\} \mid \{c,d\}$: $1 - 3 \cdot 0.456 = -0.369$
- $\{a,c\} \mid \{b,d\}$: $1 - 3 \cdot 0.046 = 0.863$
- $\{a,d\} \mid \{b,c\}$: $1 - 0 = 1$
- $\{a\} \mid \{b,c,d\}$: $0.811 - 3 \cdot 0.311 = -0.122$
- identity: $2 - 3 \cdot 0.594 = 0.217$

Ranking (best first): the aligned merge $(-0.369)$, then
$\{a\} \mid \{b,c,d\}$ $(-0.122)$, collapse $(0)$, identity $(0.217)$,
$\{a,c\} \mid \{b,d\}$ $(0.863)$, $\{a,d\} \mid \{b,c\}$ $(1)$.

The winner is the aligned merge, consistent with the worked example's

</Solution>

<Exercise>
Prove from the data-processing inequality of
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/> that every encoder
$p(t \mid x)$ with $Y \to X \to T$ satisfies both plane bounds,
$$
I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
I(T;Y) \le I(X;T),
$$
stating explicitly which Markov chain each application uses.

<Solution>
**Relevance ceiling.** The hypothesis is the chain $Y \to X \to T$
directly: $T$ depends on $Y$ only through $X$. The DPI (Cover & Thomas
Theorem 2.8.1, as proved in the prerequisite lesson) applied to this
chain gives $I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$.

**Rate diagonal.** The Markov factorization
$p(y, x, t) = p(x)\, p(y \mid x)\, p(t \mid x)$ is symmetric in $y$
and $t$, so $Y \to X \to T$ holds exactly when $T \to X \to Y$ does
(the chain-reversal fact of the prerequisite lesson). Apply the DPI to
$T \to X \to Y$, reading $T$ as the source variable and $Y$ as the
processed one: the information the endpoint carries about $T$ is at
most what the middle carries, $I(Y;T) \le I(X;T)$.

Both bounds are one theorem applied to one hypothesis read in its two
directions; no property of the bottleneck objective enters. Every
encoder, optimal or not, lives in the region under both lines.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Modify the toy source: keep $X$ uniform on $\{a,b,c,d\}$ but set
$p(1 \mid a) = p(1 \mid b) = 0$ and $p(1 \mid c) = p(1 \mid d) = 1$.
Compute $I(X;Y)$, then show that the merge
$T$ with clusters $\{a,b\}$ and $\{c,d\}$ is a sufficient statistic
for $Y$, and give the information-plane point at which the
$\beta \to \infty$ solutions accumulate.

<Solution>
**Budget.** $Y$ is now a deterministic function of $X$
($Y = 1$ exactly on $\{c,d\}$), so $H(Y \mid X) = 0$; the marginal is
fair, $H(Y) = 1$, hence $I(X;Y) = 1$ bit.

**Sufficiency of the merge.** The clusters have
$p(1 \mid t_1) = 0$ and $p(1 \mid t_2) = 1$: given $T$, the label is
certain, $H(Y \mid T) = 0$, so
$$
I(T;Y) = H(Y) - 0 = 1 = I(X;Y),
$$
the equality case of the DPI, which is precisely the definition of a
sufficient statistic from the prerequisite lesson. Equivalently, every
assignment cost vanishes:
$D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t(x))\bigr) = 0$ for all four
symbols, so the relevance-loss identity gives loss zero.

**The large-β point.** The merge costs $I(X;T) = H(T) = 1$ bit, and no
cheaper encoder is sufficient: $I(T;Y) = 1$ requires
$I(X;T) \ge I(T;Y) = 1$ by the rate diagonal. So the minimal
sufficient statistic costs exactly $1$ bit, and as
$\beta \to \infty$ the optima accumulate at $(1, 1)$, not at the
identity's $(2, 1)$: the second bit of $X$ (which symbol within the
cluster) is pure irrelevance, and the bottleneck discards it at any
price.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two encoders occupy plane points $(R_1, I_1)$ and $(R_2, I_2)$ with
$I_2 > I_1$. Show that their objectives tie,
$\mathcal{L}_1 = \mathcal{L}_2$, at exactly
$$
\beta^\ast = \frac{R_2 - R_1}{I_2 - I_1},
$$
the inverse slope of the segment joining the points, and evaluate
$\beta^\ast$ for the aligned merge $(1, 0.456)$ against the identity
$(2, 0.594)$. State how this connects to the frontier's slope
relation.

<Solution>
Set the objectives equal:
$$
R_1 - \beta I_1 = R_2 - \beta I_2
\;\Longleftrightarrow\;
\beta (I_2 - I_1) = R_2 - R_1
\;\Longleftrightarrow\;
\beta^\ast = \frac{R_2 - R_1}{I_2 - I_1}.
$$
For $\beta < \beta^\ast$ the cheaper encoder wins ($R$ dominates); for
$\beta > \beta^\ast$ the more informative one wins ($I$ dominates).

For the given pair:
$$
\beta^\ast = \frac{2 - 1}{0.594 - 0.456} = \frac{1}{0.138} \approx
7.25,
$$
the merge-vs-copy crossover of the worked example.

Connection: on the smooth frontier, the optimum at price $\beta$ sits
where $dI_Y^\ast/dR = 1/\beta$, i.e. $\delta R / \delta I_Y = \beta$.
The tie price $\beta^\ast$ is the discrete version of the same
statement: it is the inverse slope $\Delta R / \Delta I_Y$ between two
candidate points, the average price per relevant bit of upgrading from
one encoder to the other. As the candidates crowd the frontier, the
discrete exchange rate converges to the derivative relation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

The information bottleneck makes relevant compression precise: choose a
stochastic encoder mapping X to T that minimizes I(X;T) - beta I(T;Y),
keeping the bits of X that inform the target Y and discarding the rest.

The course has now priced compression three times, and every price was quoted
against something supplied by hand. Entropy priced exact description against a
known distribution; $R(D)$ priced lossy description against a distortion
measure $d(x, \hat x)$ that <Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/> could define but
never derive; description length priced a model against a code for its own
parameters. This lesson removes the hand-picked ingredient from the lossy
case: the fidelity constraint is replaced by a second mutual information about
a target variable, and the distortion measure returns as a *consequence* of
the objective rather than an input to it. What stands open at the end is
computation, since both mutual informations require the joint distribution
$p(x,y)$ that no one has for images and captions.

The route: the flaw stated on a speech signal, the objective and what its
representation variable is not, the trade-off computed by hand on a
four-symbol source, the two data-processing ceilings that bound the
information plane, the KL divergence that emerges in the optimal encoder,
$\beta$ as an exchange rate, and the pair of variational bounds that make the
whole construction trainable.

## One signal, three notions of relevance

Rate-distortion left one ingredient underived, and the cleanest place to see
what that costs is a signal with more than one use.

name the object that has to be supplied before it can be evaluated. (2) State
what the data-processing inequality asserts for a Markov chain
$Y \to X \to T$. (3) Write $D(p \,\|\, q)$ and state its sign. Then open the
fold.
</Task>

<Aside>
(1) $R(D) = \min I(X;\hat X)$ over test channels $p(\hat x \mid x)$ subject to
$\mathbb{E}[d(x,\hat x)] \le D$; the distortion measure $d$ must be supplied
(<Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/>). (2) $I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$: processing $X$
without access to $Y$ cannot increase what is known about $Y$
(<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>). (3)
$D(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_i p_i \log_2 (p_i / q_i) \ge 0$, with equality exactly
when $p = q$ (<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>).
</Aside>

A microphone samples speech at 16 kHz. Coding that waveform losslessly costs
its entropy rate; coding the words that were said costs orders of magnitude
less, because a transcript is a far smaller object than the pressure signal
that carried it. Somewhere between the two lies every practical summary, and
which summary is right depends entirely on what the summary is for. A speech
recogniser needs the phoneme sequence and can throw away the speaker's pitch.
A speaker-identification system needs the pitch and timbre and can throw away
the words. A grammar checker needs only each word's part of speech, and can
throw away which word it was.

<Figure
  src="./figures/three-relevances.svg"
  alt="One speech waveform with three arrows to three targets: the transcript, the speaker's identity, and the part-of-speech tag, each listing what a good representation keeps and discards"
  caption="One waveform, three targets: each choice of Y makes different features of the same signal worth keeping."

In the rate-distortion account these three tasks differ only in $d(x, \hat x)$,
and the difference has to be written down before any rate can be computed. No
one knows the distortion measure on acoustic waveforms that scores two signals
as close exactly when they carry the same words: that measure would have to
encode what the auditory system discards, which is the thing the recogniser
was supposed to learn. Choosing $d$ is choosing which features survive, and in
the words of Tishby, Pereira and Bialek, "an arbitrary choice of the
distortion function is in fact an arbitrary feature selection" — a modelling
decision disguised as a technical parameter, made before the data is
consulted.

The three tasks do differ in something observable. For each, there is a
variable that says what mattered: a transcript, a speaker label, a tag
sequence. Each comes paired with the signal in a corpus of recordings, so each
defines a joint distribution with $X$. If relevance can be read off that joint
distribution, the modelling decision moves from an unknowable function on
waveforms to a variable someone has already measured.

## The objective: a second mutual information in place of the budget

The fix keeps the shape of the rate-distortion problem and replaces exactly
one term in it.

Two distributions play different roles, and keeping them apart is most of the
notation. The joint $p(x, y)$ is **given**: it is the statistics of the data,
$X$ the signal and $Y$ the target, and it is the one thing the problem is
allowed to assume. The encoder is **chosen**: a *stochastic encoder* is a
conditional distribution $p(t \mid x)$ that maps each input $x$ to a
distribution over values of a representation variable $T$, with deterministic
maps $T = f(X)$ as the special case in which every conditional puts all its
mass on one value. The number of values $|\mathcal{T}|$ is fixed in advance,
exactly as the reconstruction alphabet is fixed in rate-distortion.

<Definition title="information bottleneck objective">
Given a joint distribution $p(x,y)$, a cardinality $|\mathcal{T}|$ and a
trade-off parameter $\beta \ge 0$, the **information bottleneck** problem is
$$
\min_{p(t \mid x)} \; \mathcal{L}\bigl[p(t \mid x)\bigr]
= I(X;T) \;-\; \beta\, I(T;Y),
$$
minimized over stochastic encoders $p(t \mid x)$, where $T$ obeys the Markov
chain $Y \to X \to T$ of <Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>: the
encoder reads $x$ and nothing else.
</Definition>

Both terms are measured in bits, which is what makes their difference
meaningful; $\beta$ is a pure number, the price in bits of rate that the
designer is willing to pay for one bit of relevance. The two ends of its range
bracket everything in between. At $\beta = 0$ relevance is worthless and the
problem is to minimize $I(X;T)$ alone: the minimum is zero, attained by
sending every $x$ to the same value of $T$. As $\beta \to \infty$ rate is free
and the problem is to maximize $I(T;Y)$: copying, $T = X$, attains the largest
value available, which the next section shows is $I(X;Y)$.

$I(X;T)$ is a rate in the operational sense of the coding theorems, not a
regularizer that happens to be measured in bits: it is the number of bits per
symbol a real code spends, in a coding problem whose fidelity criterion is
$I(T;Y)$.

<Remark title="the objective is a coding problem in disguise">
In *remote source coding*, an encoder does not see the source $Y^n$ it must
convey; it sees a noisy observation $X^n$, and the decoder must reproduce
$Y^n$ from the transmitted index. Score the reproduction by logarithmic loss,
so that the decoder outputs a distribution $\hat y_i$ over $\mathcal{Y}$ for
each position and pays $\log_2 (1 / \hat y_i(y_i))$ bits, the code length of
<Ref lesson="compression-is-prediction"/>. The optimal decoder output is the
posterior $p(y \mid t)$, its expected loss is $H(Y \mid T)$, and the
achievable rate at distortion $D$ is
$$
R(D) = \min_{p(t \mid x) \,:\, H(Y \mid T) \le D} I(X;T).
$$
Writing $D = H(Y) - \alpha$ turns the constraint into $I(T;Y) \ge \alpha$,
which is the bottleneck problem with $\beta$ as its Lagrange multiplier. So
$I(X;T)$ is bits per symbol that a real code can achieve, and $I(T;Y)$ is a
fidelity budget in the one distortion measure that needs no design.
</Remark>

## What T is not: the arrow points out of X

The Markov chain in the definition is a hypothesis about how $T$ is made, and
it is the piece most often read backwards.

The correct statement is that $T$ is computed from $X$, possibly with fresh
randomness, and never from $Y$. The joint therefore factorizes as
$p(y, x, t) = p(x)\, p(y \mid x)\, p(t \mid x)$, with no factor in which $t$
and $y$ appear together, and every conditional of $Y$ given a value of $T$ is
an average of data conditionals,
$p(y \mid t) = \sum_x p(x \mid t)\, p(y \mid x)$, not a modelling choice.

The frequent misreading is that $T$ is a latent variable generating the data,
as in a variational autoencoder: a hidden cause drawn from a prior $p(t)$,
which then produces $x$ through a decoder $p(x \mid t)$. That is a different
factorization, $p(t, x, y) = p(t)\, p(x \mid t)\, p(y \mid x)$, and under it
the bottleneck's machinery stops making sense. The encoder would be a
posterior to be inferred rather than an object to be chosen; $p(y \mid t)$
would be a modelling assumption rather than a weighted average of the data's
own conditionals; and the ceiling $I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$, which the next section
gets for free from the chain, would need a separate argument. The 1999 paper
raises exactly this reading in a footnote and rejects it: the quantization "is
not used as a hidden variable in a model of the data."

<Figure
  src="./figures/markov-direction.svg"
  alt="Left panel: X at the top with arrows to Y and T, the encoder p(t given x), and the factorization p(x) p(y given x) p(t given x). Right panel, crossed out: T generating X which generates Y, with the factorization p(t) p(x given t) p(y given x)"
  caption="The arrow out of X is the definition: T is computed from the data. Reverse it and the object is a latent-variable model, which the bottleneck is not."
/>

A second reversal is subtler and worth separating from the first. Nothing in
the setup assumes the chain $X \to T \to Y$, in which $T$ carries all of $X$'s
information about the target. That statement is *sufficiency*, and it is what
the objective tries to buy at large $\beta$, not what it assumes at any
$\beta$. The distinction matters when reading claims about trained networks: a
hidden layer is a valid $T$ under $Y \to X \to T$ whatever it has learned,
including nothing.

<KeyFact>
Every bound in this lesson follows from the chain $Y \to X \to T$ alone, so it
holds for untrained networks, random projections and hash functions equally.
Nothing that follows is evidence that a representation is good; the bounds
constrain what any representation could be.
</KeyFact>

## The trade-off computed: four symbols, one binary label

With the objective fixed, the trade-off can be worked out completely on a
source small enough to enumerate.

Let $X$ be uniform on $\{a, b, c, d\}$, $p(x) = \tfrac14$ each, and attach a
binary label $Y$ through the conditionals
$$
p(1 \mid a) = 0, \quad
p(1 \mid b) = \tfrac14, \quad
p(1 \mid c) = \tfrac34, \quad
p(1 \mid d) = 1,
$$
writing $p(1 \mid x)$ for $p(Y{=}1 \mid X{=}x)$. Unlike the deterministic label
of <Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>, this one is noisy for two of the
four symbols, which is what makes the trade-off nontrivial. The joint table:

| $p(x,y)$ | $x = a$ | $x = b$ | $x = c$ | $x = d$ | $p(y)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $y = 0$ | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac{3}{16}$ | $\tfrac{1}{16}$ | $0$ | $\tfrac12$ |
| $y = 1$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{1}{16}$ | $\tfrac{3}{16}$ | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac12$ |
| $p(x)$ | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac14$ | $\tfrac14$ | $1$ |

<Example title="the budget in play: I(X;Y)">
The label's marginal is fair, $H(Y) = 1$ bit. Given $X$, the label is certain
for $a$ and $d$ and a $\tfrac14$-coin for $b$ and $c$, with
$H_2(\tfrac14) = H_2(\tfrac34) \approx 0.811$ bits:
$$
H(Y \mid X) = \tfrac14\bigl(0 + 0.811 + 0.811 + 0\bigr) \approx 0.406
\text{ bits},
$$
so by <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>,
$$
I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \approx 1 - 0.406 = 0.594 \text{ bits}.
$$
Describing $X$ exactly costs $H(X) = 2$ bits, and only $0.594$ of them say
anything about the label: less than a third of the input is relevant.
</Example>

### Three merges at one price

Take the simplest nontrivial encoders: deterministic merges of the four
symbols into two clusters of two, $T = f(X)$ with $|\mathcal{T}| = 2$. There
are three such pairings, and each costs the same rate. Since $T$ is a function
of $X$, $H(T \mid X) = 0$, and since the clusters are equiprobable,
$I(X;T) = H(T) - H(T \mid X) = H(T) = 1$ bit for all three. The comparison
therefore isolates relevance.

<Example title="the aligned merge: ab | cd">
Cluster $t_1 = \{a, b\}$ and $t_2 = \{c, d\}$, each with $p(t) = \tfrac12$.

**Step 1: label given cluster.** The mass with $Y = 1$ inside $t_1$ is
$p(a)\, p(1 \mid a) + p(b)\, p(1 \mid b) = 0 + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac14 =
\tfrac{1}{16}$, so $p(1 \mid t_1) = \tfrac{1/16}{1/2} = \tfrac18$. By symmetry
$p(1 \mid t_2) = \tfrac78$.

**Step 2: conditional entropy.** Both clusters are $\tfrac18$-coins, so
$H(Y \mid T) = H_2(\tfrac18) \approx 0.544$ bits.

**Step 3: subtract.**
$$
I(T;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid T) \approx 1 - 0.544 = 0.456 \text{ bits}.
$$
The merge keeps $0.456$ of the $0.594$ available bits, at a rate of $1$ bit
against the $2$ bits an exact description costs. The loss is $0.138$ bits.
</Example>

The other two pairings run identically and land elsewhere. Merging
$\{a,c\}$ against $\{b,d\}$ gives cluster conditionals $\tfrac38$ and
$\tfrac58$, hence $H(Y \mid T) = H_2(\tfrac38) \approx 0.954$ and
$I(T;Y) \approx 0.046$ bits. Merging $\{a,d\}$ against $\{b,c\}$ gives both
cluster conditionals equal to $\tfrac12$, hence $H(Y \mid T) = 1$ and
$I(T;Y) = 0$: one full bit of rate spent, nothing about the label kept.

One caveat scopes the whole enumeration. Hard clusterings are a strict subset
of the stochastic encoders the definition ranges over, so these six points are
candidates, not the frontier. At rate $1$ bit the best stochastic encoder does
at least as well as $0.456$ bits, and at rates between the enumerated ones it
does strictly better than anything a partition can offer.

<KeyFact>
No distortion measure on $X$ alone can produce this ranking. All three
encoders cut the four symbols into two pairs and charge one bit, so any
$d(x, \hat x)$ that separates them must already encode which conditionals
$p(y \mid x)$ lie close together — the arbitrary feature selection of the
opening section, smuggled back in. Relevance is a property of the pair
$(X, Y)$, and no function of $X$ and its reconstruction can see it.
</KeyFact>

## The information plane: two ceilings from one theorem

Every encoder, optimal or not, is now a point with two coordinates, and the
region those points can occupy is bounded on two sides by a theorem already
proved.

Plot rate spent horizontally, $I(X;T)$, against relevance kept vertically,
$I(T;Y)$. This coordinate system is the *information plane*. Both of its
bounds are the data-processing inequality of
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>, applied to the same hypothesis
read in its two directions. That lesson established that the factorization
$p(y,x,t) = p(x)\,p(y \mid x)\,p(t \mid x)$ is symmetric in $y$ and $t$, so
$Y \to X \to T$ holds exactly when $T \to X \to Y$ does.

- **The relevance ceiling.** From $Y \to X \to T$: $\;I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$. No
  encoder knows more about the target than the input did. For this toy source
  no point rises above $0.594$ bits, whatever the rate.
- **The rate diagonal.** From $T \to X \to Y$: $\;I(T;Y) \le I(X;T)$. A
  representation cannot say more about $Y$ than it contains about anything at
  all, so every point lies on or below the $45°$ line.

<Figure
  src="./figures/information-plane.svg"
  alt="The information plane with rate I(X;T) horizontal and relevance I(T;Y) vertical: a dashed ceiling at 0.594 bits, the dashed diagonal I(T;Y) at most I(X;T), the six enumerated toy encoders as points, and the concave frontier curve from the origin flattening into the ceiling at rate 2"
  caption="Every encoder is a point under two data-processing lines. The enumerated merges sit at or below the frontier; the frontier itself is what sweeping β traces."
/>

The upper boundary of the shaded region is the *frontier*: for each rate, the
largest relevance any encoder of the allowed cardinality achieves. Four facts
locate it. It starts at the origin, since zero rate makes $T$ independent of
$X$ and therefore of $Y$. It is concave once the cardinality is free to grow,
because two encoders can be mixed by flipping an independent coin and
appending its outcome to $T$: both coordinates then average, so the whole
chord joining two achievable points is achievable, at the cost of an alphabet
as large as the two put together. It ends
on the ceiling: at rate $H(X) = 2$ bits the identity encoder reaches
$I(T;Y) = I(X;Y) = 0.594$ bits, and more rate buys nothing. And there is one
such curve per cardinality $|\mathcal{T}|$, each lying under the next, since
allowing more clusters only relaxes the minimization.

The frontier's left end is pinned by the diagonal rather than by any property
of the objective: relevance cannot exceed rate, so reaching $90\%$ of a
source's $I(X;Y)$ requires at least $0.9\,I(X;Y)$ bits of rate no matter how
the encoder is designed. On the toy source that floor is $0.535$ bits, and the
frontier does not reach $0.535$ bits of relevance until about $1.45$ bits of
rate, nearly three times the floor: the diagonal is a bound, not a
prediction.

<KeyFact>
Both plane bounds hold for any encoder whatsoever, so a reported point above
the diagonal or above the ceiling is a fact about the estimator, not about the
representation. Mutual informations of high-dimensional representations are
estimated, not computed, and
<Ref lesson="estimating-mutual-information"/> shows how far the naive
estimates drift.
</KeyFact>

## The distortion measure that was not assumed

The toy comparison suggested that the cost of putting $x$ into a cluster is
the mismatch between $p(y \mid x)$ and the cluster's aggregate conditional.
That is exact accounting, not a heuristic, and it is what turns the objective
into something more than a reparameterization.

<Theorem title="relevance loss is an expected KL divergence">
For any encoder $p(t \mid x)$ with $Y \to X \to T$,
$$
I(X;Y) - I(T;Y)
= \mathbb{E}_{p(x,t)}\Bigl[\, D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t)\bigr) \Bigr]
\;\ge\; 0,
$$
the average over inputs and their cluster assignments of the KL divergence of
<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/> between the input's label conditional and its
cluster's.

<Proof>
Expand the expectation:
$$
\mathbb{E}\bigl[D\bigr]
= \sum_{x,t} p(x,t) \sum_y p(y \mid x)
\log_2 \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y \mid t)}.
$$
The Markov chain gives $p(y \mid x, t) = p(y \mid x)$, so
$p(x,t)\,p(y \mid x) = p(x,t,y)$ and the sum splits into two expectations over
the full joint. The numerator term is
$\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(y \mid x) = -H(Y \mid X)$. The denominator term is
$-\sum_{x,t,y} p(x,t,y) \log_2 p(y \mid t)
= -\sum_{t,y} p(t,y) \log_2 p(y \mid t) = H(Y \mid T)$. Hence
$$
\mathbb{E}\bigl[D\bigr] = H(Y \mid T) - H(Y \mid X)
= \bigl[H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)\bigr] - \bigl[H(Y) - H(Y \mid T)\bigr]
= I(X;Y) - I(T;Y),
$$
and each KL term is non-negative, which is the relevance ceiling again.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The relevance term is therefore already a distortion: minimizing
$I(X;T) - \beta I(T;Y)$ is minimizing $I(X;T) + \beta \,\mathbb{E}[D]$ up to
the constant $\beta\, I(X;Y)$, which is the rate-distortion Lagrangian with an
expected divergence in the distortion slot.

<Example title="the aligned merge's loss, symbol by symbol">
The cluster conditionals are $p(y \mid t_{ab}) = (\tfrac78, \tfrac18)$ and
$p(y \mid t_{cd}) = (\tfrac18, \tfrac78)$, ordered $(y{=}0, y{=}1)$.

Symbol $a$ has $p(y \mid a) = (1, 0)$, so
$D = 1 \cdot \log_2 \tfrac{1}{7/8} = \log_2 \tfrac87 \approx 0.193$ bits, the
$y{=}1$ term vanishing by the convention $0 \log \tfrac{0}{q} = 0$.

Symbol $b$ has $p(y \mid b) = (\tfrac34, \tfrac14)$, so
$$
D = \tfrac34 \log_2 \frac{3/4}{7/8} + \tfrac14 \log_2 \frac{1/4}{1/8}
= \tfrac34 \log_2 \tfrac67 + \tfrac14
\approx -0.167 + 0.25 = 0.083 \text{ bits}.
$$

Symbols $c$ and $d$ mirror $b$ and $a$ against $t_{cd}$, giving $0.083$ and
$0.193$ bits. Averaging under the uniform $p(x)$:
$$
\tfrac14 (0.193 + 0.083 + 0.083 + 0.193) \approx 0.138 \text{ bits},
$$
which is exactly the loss $0.594 - 0.456$ computed by brute force.
</Example>

### The optimal encoder is Gibbs in that divergence

Minimizing the objective over $p(t \mid x)$, subject only to each conditional
summing to one, gives a stationarity condition with the divergence in the
exponent.

<Theorem title="optimal bottleneck encoder (Tishby-Pereira-Bialek, Theorem 4)">
The stationary points of $\mathcal{L} = I(X;T) - \beta\, I(T;Y)$ satisfy
$$
p(t \mid x) = \frac{p(t)}{Z(x, \beta)}\,
2^{-\beta\, D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t)\bigr)},
$$
where $Z(x, \beta)$ normalizes over $t$, and the cluster statistics are fixed
self-consistently by the encoder itself:
$$
p(t) = \sum_x p(x)\, p(t \mid x),
\qquad
p(y \mid t) = \frac{1}{p(t)} \sum_x p(x, y)\, p(t \mid x).
$$
</Theorem>

The exponent is the theorem's content. The quantity playing the role that
$d(x, \hat x)$ played in rate-distortion is
$D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t)\bigr)$, and nothing in the setup put it
there: the objective was written with two mutual informations and a Lagrange
multiplier, and the divergence appeared when the derivative was taken. In the
authors' words it "emerged as the relevant effective distortion measure from
our variational principle but is not assumed otherwise anywhere". The closing
exercise on the Lagrangian carries out the differentiation and marks the line
at which the divergence appears.

Three readings of the form matter later. It **explains the toy ranking**: the
aligned merge won because its assignment costs, $0.193$ and $0.083$ bits, are
the smallest any two-cluster partition offers, while the $\{a,d\}$ merge
charges a full bit for each certain symbol. It is a **soft assignment**: at
finite $\beta$ the encoder is genuinely stochastic, splitting borderline
inputs between clusters in proportion to $2^{-\beta D}$, and hard partitions
reappear only as $\beta \to \infty$. And it is **rate-distortion's own
solution form**, the standard optimality condition with the emergent
divergence in place of $d(x, \hat x)$, which is why the equations are solved
the way $R(D)$ is solved.

### Solving the equations: alternating minimization, and its limits

The three conditions of the theorem are each easy to enforce given the other
two, which suggests iterating them: update $p(t \mid x)$ from the current
$p(t)$ and $p(y \mid t)$, then recompute $p(t)$, then recompute
$p(y \mid t)$. That iteration is the three-distribution generalization of the
[Blahut-Arimoto algorithm](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
(Cover & Thomas §10.8) used for $R(D)$, and the 1999 paper's Theorem 5 proves
each step decreases the same functional, which is bounded below.

Convergence is not optimality. The functional is convex in each of the three
distributions separately and not in the product of them, exactly as in EM, so
different initializations reach different fixed points. On the toy source at
$\beta = 6.9$ the iteration from random starts lands on three distinct
solutions, with objective values $-2.150$, $-2.197$ and $-2.244$: the first is
the two-cluster merge $ab \mid cd$, the other two split those clusters four
ways — one asymmetrically, one symmetrically — and only the symmetric
four-cluster solution is optimal. All three are stable: an iteration that
lands on the first will stay there forever.

<KeyFact>
The optimal encoder is a fixed point, not a formula: $p(y \mid t)$ inside the
exponent is itself computed from $p(t \mid x)$, so nothing can be evaluated in
one pass. Two consequences travel with every use of the objective: the
cardinality $|\mathcal{T}|$ must be chosen in advance, and the solution
reported for a given $\beta$ is a local optimum.
</KeyFact>

## β prices a relevant bit in bits of rate

The multiplier has been treated as a dial so far. Its exact meaning is a
slope, and the slope is what makes $\beta$ readable.

Write $I_Y^\ast(R)$ for the frontier: the largest relevance any encoder
achieves at rate $I(X;T) = R$. An encoder optimal for $\beta$ minimizes
$\mathcal{L} = R - \beta I_Y$, so at its own frontier point a small move along
the frontier cannot change the objective to first order:
$$
\delta \mathcal{L} = \delta R - \beta\, \delta I_Y = 0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{d I_Y^\ast}{d R} = \frac{1}{\beta}.
$$
One additional bit of relevance costs exactly $\beta$ bits of rate at the
operating point $\beta$ selects. Conventions differ across the literature —
some write the multiplier on the rate term instead, which inverts the slope —
so the relation is derived here from the stated $\mathcal{L}$ rather than
quoted.

<Example title="reading the slope off the computed frontier">
Solving the fixed-point equations numerically on the toy source, with
$|\mathcal{T}| = 4$, gives frontier points $(R, I_Y)$ at each $\beta$. Taking
a central difference between $\beta - 0.05$ and $\beta + 0.05$:

| $\beta$ | $\Delta I_Y / \Delta R$ | $1/\beta$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $3$ | $0.3335$ | $0.3333$ |
| $4$ | $0.2501$ | $0.2500$ |
| $5$ | $0.2000$ | $0.2000$ |

The agreement is to three decimal places, and it is a check on the whole
apparatus: the numbers come from iterating the Gibbs encoder, the prediction
comes from differentiating a Lagrangian, and nothing was fitted.

Because the frontier is concave, its slope falls monotonically, so cheap
relevant bits are bought first. At $\beta = 3$ one bit of rate still buys a
third of a bit of relevance; at $\beta = 20$ it buys a twentieth.
</Example>

The same arithmetic run on discrete candidates gives crossover prices. Among
the three encoders computed by hand, the objective of collapse is $0$, of the
aligned merge $1 - 0.456\,\beta$, and of the identity $2 - 0.594\,\beta$. The
merge beats collapse once $\beta > 1/0.456 \approx 2.19$, and the identity
beats the merge once $\beta > 1/0.138 \approx 7.25$: each crossover is the
inverse slope $\Delta R / \Delta I_Y$ between two points, the discrete form of
the frontier relation.

Both crossover prices overstate where the optimum actually moves, because a
soft encoder can buy a little relevance for a little rate instead of a whole
bit at once. Solving over all stochastic encoders, the optimum leaves the
origin at $\beta \approx 1.60$, well below $2.19$, and abandons the aligned
merge at $\beta \approx 5.11$, well below $7.25$. From
$\beta = 0$ up to $1$ no such purchase is possible for any source: the rate
diagonal gives $I(T;Y) \le I(X;T)$, so $\mathcal{L} \ge (1-\beta) I(X;T) \ge 0$,
and collapse attains $\mathcal{L} = 0$. Between $\beta = 1$ and the source's
own transition point, the answer depends on the joint distribution.

<KeyFact>
$\beta$ has no units and no natural scale, so a value reported for one problem
means nothing on another: it is a slope in bits per bit, and the same $\beta$
sits at different fractions of $I(X;Y)$ depending on $H(X)$, on
$|\mathcal{T}|$, and on which convention put the multiplier on which term. The
transferable quantity is the point on the frontier, never the $\beta$ that
produced it.
</KeyFact>

The number of clusters the optimum uses changes in steps. Up to
$\beta \approx 1.60$ the optimum is the constant encoder. Above it a
two-cluster solution takes over and climbs toward $(1,\ 0.456)$. At
$\beta \approx 5.11$ each of those clusters splits in two: a new cluster peels
off to hold the certain symbol $a$ alone, its mirror holds $d$, and both start
with zero mass and fill as $\beta$ rises. By $\beta = 6.9$ symbol $a$ goes to
its own cluster with probability $0.725$ and the point has reached
$(1.49,\ 0.541)$. Both coordinates cross the transition continuously — the
frontier has no gap, and every point on it is selected by some $\beta$ — but
$dI(X;T)/d\beta$ jumps by a factor of about fifteen at the critical value,
from $0.03$ bits per unit of $\beta$ below it to $0.45$ above. Solutions of
different cardinality separating at critical values of $\beta$ is the general
behaviour the 1999 paper describes.

Annealing in one direction is not enough to find the transition. The
two-cluster solution remains a stable fixed point up to $\beta \approx 7$,
long after it stops being optimal, so *deterministic annealing* — raising
$\beta$ in small steps and initializing each solution from the previous one —
stays on it and reports a rate of $0.99$ bits where the optimum costs $1.49$,
a local optimum and a wrong frontier for nearly two units of $\beta$. The
curve drawn above and the demo's readouts come from annealing in both
directions and keeping, at each $\beta$, whichever fixed point has the smaller
$\mathcal{L}$.

At the far end the objective buys sufficiency. As $\beta \to \infty$ the
solution keeps all the relevance, $I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$, at the least rate that
allows it, which is the equality case of the data-processing inequality: $T$
is a sufficient statistic for $Y$, and among sufficient statistics the
bottleneck selects a minimal one
(<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>, and Cover & Thomas §2.9). On this
toy source the four conditionals $p(y \mid x)$ are pairwise distinct, so no
merge is sufficient and the minimal sufficient statistic is $X$ itself: the
frontier reaches the ceiling only at the full $2$ bits. Change the source so
that two symbols share a conditional and the picture changes at once: the
closing exercise on the ends of the frontier builds that case, where one bit
of rate buys the entire budget and the second bit of $X$ is discarded at any
price.

## ★ The Gaussian case: the bottleneck is canonical correlation analysis

One family of joint distributions admits a closed form, and in it the
bottleneck turns out to be a method statisticians have used since 1936. The
core path continues without this section.

Let $X$ and $Y$ be jointly Gaussian, centred, with covariances $\Sigma_X$,
$\Sigma_Y$ and cross-covariance $\Sigma_{XY}$. The optimal $T$ is then
Gaussian too, so it can be written $T = A X + Z$ with
$Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ independent of $(X, Y)$, and the whole problem
reduces to choosing the matrix $A$. Let $\Sigma_{X \mid Y} = \Sigma_X -
\Sigma_{XY}\Sigma_Y^{-1}\Sigma_{YX}$ be the error covariance of estimating $X$
from $Y$, and let $\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots$ be the eigenvalues of
the normalized matrix $\Sigma_{X \mid Y}\Sigma_X^{-1}$, with eigenvectors
$v_1, v_2, \dots$. A small eigenvalue means that direction of $X$ is well
predicted by $Y$, so it is a direction worth keeping.

The solution is a projection onto the leading such directions, with the number
of them set by $\beta$: for
$$
\beta_i^\ast = \frac{1}{1 - \lambda_i},
$$
the optimal $A$ uses the first $i$ eigenvectors when
$\beta \in [\beta_i^\ast, \beta_{i+1}^\ast)$, scaled by factors that grow with
$\beta$, and $A = 0$ below $\beta_1^\ast$. Two readings follow immediately.
The eigenvectors are the *canonical correlation analysis* basis — the pair of
linear transforms that maximize correlation between two vector variables,
introduced by Hotelling in 1936 and standard in statistics ever since — so the
Gaussian bottleneck is CCA with a rank set by $\beta$. And the rank
jumps: as $\beta$ increases continuously, the dimension of $T$ increases in
steps at the critical values $\beta_i^\ast$, the exact structure the toy
source showed numerically.

## Making it trainable: two variational bounds

Everything so far assumed the joint $p(x,y)$ is known and small enough to
enumerate. For an $X$ of images and a $Y$ of captions it is neither, and both
terms of the objective become unavailable at once.

The repair replaces each mutual information by a bound that only needs
samples and a network. For the relevance term, introduce a *decoder*
$q(y \mid t)$, any distribution the modeller can evaluate. Because
$D\bigl(p(y \mid t) \,\|\, q(y \mid t)\bigr) \ge 0$, replacing the true
posterior by $q$ can only lower the score:
$$
I(T;Y) \;\ge\; H(Y) + \mathbb{E}_{p(t,y)}\bigl[\log_2 q(y \mid t)\bigr]
\;=\; H(Y) - \mathbb{E}\bigl[-\log_2 q(y \mid t)\bigr].
$$
The bracket is the decoder's cross-entropy loss in bits
(<Ref lesson="cross-entropy-loss"/>), so **a classifier that predicts $Y$ from
$T$ well certifies that $T$ is relevant**, and the slack in the bound is
exactly how bad the decoder is. For the rate term, introduce a *marginal*
$r(t)$ in place of the intractable $p(t) = \sum_x p(x) p(t \mid x)$. Because
$D\bigl(p(t) \,\|\, r(t)\bigr) \ge 0$,
$$
I(X;T) \;\le\; \mathbb{E}_{p(x)}\Bigl[ D\bigl(p(t \mid x) \,\|\, r(t)\bigr)
\Bigr],
$$
a quantity computable in closed form when the encoder and $r$ are Gaussian.
Both bounds point the right way: one lower-bounds what is maximized, the other
upper-bounds what is minimized, so their combination is an upper bound on
$\mathcal{L}$ and minimizing it is safe.

Putting them together, dropping the constant $H(Y)$, and dividing by $\beta$
gives a loss that a network can be trained on directly:
$$
J = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\bigl[-\log_2 q(y \mid t)\bigr]}_{\text{decoder loss}}
\;+\; \frac{1}{\beta}\,
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{p(x)}\bigl[D\bigl(p(t \mid x)\,\|\,r(t)\bigr)\bigr]}
_{\text{rate bound}} .
$$
Sampling $t$ from $p(t \mid x)$ inside the first term is what the
reparameterization of a Gaussian encoder makes differentiable. Alemi et al.
write the same objective with their multiplier on the KL term, so their
$\beta$ is $1/\beta$ here; their reported operating point $\beta = 10^{-3}$ is
$\beta = 1000$ in this lesson's convention, far out on the high-relevance end
of the frontier.

That last identity is not a metaphor. Run the same two bounds with the
"target" taken to be the identity of the sample itself, so that the decoder
must reproduce $x$, and the resulting objective is a variational autoencoder's
with an arbitrary weight on the KL term, equal to the standard one at
$\beta = 1$. The algebra is shared and the claim is not: in the autoencoder,
$p(t)$ is a prior and $q(t \mid x)$ approximates a posterior; in the
bottleneck, $p(t \mid x)$ is the object being designed and $r(t)$ is the
approximation.

Trained this way, the objective is reported to do more than compress. On
permutation-invariant MNIST with a 256-dimensional bottleneck, a network
trained with the variational bottleneck loss reached $1.13\%$ test error
against $1.38\%$ for the identical architecture without it, and its
predictions were harder to flip with adversarial perturbations. Those are
results on specific benchmarks with specific architectures, not theorems, and
they are the kind of claim the estimation problems of
<Ref lesson="estimating-mutual-information"/> make hard to sharpen: the
trained quantities are bound values, and the gap between a bound and the
mutual information it bounds is unknown.

<KeyFact>
The trainable objective never evaluates a mutual information; it evaluates one
bound on each. A reported $\bigl(I(X;T), I(T;Y)\bigr)$ for a trained network
is therefore a pair of bounds whose slack depends on the decoder and the
prior, and the relevance bound in particular is capped by what the decoder's
task can certify at all — the ceiling that <Ref lesson="infonce"/> makes
explicit.
</KeyFact>

What is established: replacing a fidelity budget with a second mutual
information removes the distortion measure from the designer's hands and
returns it as $D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t)\bigr)$, derived from the
objective; the achievable pairs $\bigl(I(X;T), I(T;Y)\bigr)$ lie under two
data-processing lines; sweeping $\beta$ traces a concave frontier whose slope
is $1/\beta$ and whose solutions change cardinality at critical values; and
two variational bounds turn the whole construction into a loss for gradient
descent. What is not established is how tight the relevance bound can be when
$Y$ is a caption rather than a label. <Ref lesson="infonce"/> answers that
with a contrastive bound that certifies at most $\log_2 N$ bits from $N$
candidates, and <Ref lesson="learned-image-compression"/> runs the same
Lagrangian end to end with $Y$ taken to be the image itself.

the optimal encoder and the value of both mutual informations. (b) As

$$
\mathcal{L} = I(X;T) - \beta I(T;Y) \ge I(X;T) - \beta I(X;T)
= (1-\beta)\, I(X;T) \ge 0
\quad\text{for } \beta \le 1 .
$$
The constant encoder attains $\mathcal{L} = 0$, hence is optimal. The bound
used is the rate diagonal, the data-processing inequality applied to the
reversed chain $T \to X \to Y$; the relevance ceiling
$I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$ says nothing here, because it does not compare relevance
to rate. Note what the result does *not* say: the transition need not happen
at $\beta = 1$. On the toy source collapse persists to $\beta \approx 1.60$,
and the gap between $1$ and the true transition point depends on the joint.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim. "The bottleneck finds the representation that keeps all the
information about $Y$ and none about $X$." Show that the described object
cannot exist except in a degenerate case, name the inequality that rules it
out, and state what the objective actually returns.

<Solution>
The claim asks for $I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$ together with $I(X;T) = 0$. The rate
diagonal $I(T;Y) \le I(X;T)$, the data-processing inequality on

$X$ and $Y$ are independent, in which case both quantities are zero and there
was nothing to keep.

The error is treating "information about $X$" and "information about $Y$" as
separable stocks. Every bit $T$ holds about $Y$ is a bit it holds about $X$,
because $T$ sees $Y$ only through $X$: the relevant bits are a *subset* of
the bits of $X$, not a different currency. What the objective returns is the
cheapest description of $X$ that carries a given amount about $Y$ — a point on
the frontier, always on or below the diagonal, and equal to the diagonal only
when every bit spent is a bit about $Y$. On the toy source the frontier's
initial slope is below $1$, so even the first bit of rate buys less than a
full bit of relevance.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break a second claim. "$T$ is a latent variable that generates $X$, so
$p(x \mid t)$ is the model and $p(t \mid x)$ is its posterior." State the
factorization this assumes, contrast it with the bottleneck's, and identify
which quantity in the Gibbs-form encoder would have to be reinterpreted.

<Solution>
The claim assumes $p(t, x, y) = p(t)\, p(x \mid t)\, p(y \mid x)$: $T$ is
drawn from a prior and the data is generated from it, so $p(t \mid x)$ is
obtained by Bayes' rule and is not free to choose. The bottleneck assumes
$p(y, x, t) = p(x)\, p(y \mid x)\, p(t \mid x)$: the data comes first and $T$
is computed from it, so $p(t \mid x)$ is the only free object and $p(t)$,
$p(y \mid t)$ are consequences of it.

The quantity that changes meaning is $p(y \mid t)$ in the exponent of the
optimal encoder. In the bottleneck it is the average
$\sum_x p(x \mid t) p(y \mid x)$ of the data's own conditionals, recomputed at
every iteration from the current encoder; under the generative reading it
would be a modelled conditional, chosen by the modeller, and the theorem's
self-consistency conditions would have nothing to determine. The
distortion measure would then be assumed again, which is exactly what the
construction was built to avoid.

Two further consequences are worth naming. First, the ceiling
$I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$ follows from the bottleneck's factorization and not from
the generative one, so the information plane would lose its boundary. Second,
the algebraic coincidence with a variational autoencoder is real but does not
transfer the interpretation: the same loss can be reached from either
direction, and which variable is modelled and which is designed is a
statement about the problem, not about the formula.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Carry out the derivation the lesson quoted. Minimize
$\mathcal{L} = I(X;T) - \beta I(T;Y)$ over $p(t \mid x)$ with multipliers
$\lambda(x)$ enforcing $\sum_t p(t \mid x) = 1$, using the Markov relations
$\partial p(t)/\partial p(t \mid x) = p(x)$ and
$\partial p(t \mid y)/\partial p(t \mid x) = p(x \mid y)$. Show that the
stationarity condition is the Gibbs form, and identify the step at which the
KL divergence appears. Write down the constraint and both mutual informations
in explicit sums before differentiating.

<Solution>
Write both terms as sums over the free variable:
$$
\mathcal{L} = \sum_{x,t} p(x) p(t \mid x) \log_2 \frac{p(t \mid x)}{p(t)}
- \beta \sum_{t,y} p(t, y) \log_2 \frac{p(t \mid y)}{p(t)}
- \sum_{x,t} \lambda(x) p(t \mid x).
$$
Differentiating with respect to $p(t \mid x)$ for one pair $(x,t)$, the first
term gives $p(x)\bigl[c + \log_2 p(t \mid x)\bigr]$ from the numerator and
$-p(x)\bigl[c + \log_2 p(t)\bigr]$ through $p(t)$; the second gives
$-\beta \sum_y p(x \mid y) p(y)\bigl[c + \log_2 p(t \mid y)\bigr]$ through
$p(t \mid y)$ and $+\beta\, p(x)\bigl[c + \log_2 p(t)\bigr]$ through $p(t)$,
where $c = 1/\ln 2$ is the constant from differentiating $u \log_2 u$. Every
occurrence of $c$ is independent of $t$ and absorbs into the multiplier. Using
$p(x \mid y)p(y) = p(x)p(y \mid x)$ and dividing by $p(x)$:
$$
\log_2 \frac{p(t \mid x)}{p(t)}
- \beta \sum_y p(y \mid x) \log_2 \frac{p(t \mid y)}{p(t)}
- \tilde\lambda(x) = 0 .
$$
Now rewrite the middle sum. By Bayes' rule
$p(t \mid y)/p(t) = p(y \mid t)/p(y)$, so
$$
\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log_2 \frac{p(t \mid y)}{p(t)}
= \sum_y p(y \mid x) \log_2 \frac{p(y \mid t)}{p(y)}
= \underbrace{\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log_2 \frac{p(y \mid x)}{p(y)}}
_{\text{depends on } x \text{ only}}
- \; D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t)\bigr),
$$
by adding and subtracting $\log_2 p(y \mid x)$ inside the sum. **This is the
step at which the divergence appears**: it is the residue left after the
$x$-only term, which is absorbed into $\tilde\lambda(x)$ because it does not
depend on $t$. What remains is
$$
\log_2 \frac{p(t \mid x)}{p(t)} = -\beta D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\,
p(y \mid t)\bigr) + \text{const}(x),
$$
and exponentiating, with the constant fixed by normalization over $t$,
$$
p(t \mid x) = \frac{p(t)}{Z(x,\beta)}\,
2^{-\beta D(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t))}.
$$
The transferable move is that the divergence was never inserted: it is what a
log-ratio of conditionals becomes once the part that cannot influence the
assignment is absorbed into a normalizer.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two claims about the ends of the frontier. (a) Show that $T = X$ achieves
$I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$ at rate $H(X)$, and that no encoder with
$I(X;T) < I(X;Y)$ reaches the ceiling. (b) Modify the toy source so that
$p(1 \mid a) = p(1 \mid b) = 0$ and $p(1 \mid c) = p(1 \mid d) = 1$, keeping
$X$ uniform. Compute $I(X;Y)$, find the cheapest encoder that reaches the
ceiling, and give the point at which the $\beta \to \infty$ solutions
accumulate.

<Solution>
**(a)** With $T = X$ the chain is trivial and $I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$ by definition,
while $I(X;T) = I(X;X) = H(X)$. For the second half, the rate diagonal gives
$I(T;Y) \le I(X;T)$; any encoder reaching the ceiling has
$I(T;Y) = I(X;Y)$, hence $I(X;T) \ge I(X;Y)$. So the frontier cannot touch the
ceiling at any rate below $I(X;Y)$ bits, and it leaves the origin with slope at
most $1$.

**(b)** The label is now a function of $X$, so $H(Y \mid X) = 0$; the marginal
is fair, $H(Y) = 1$, hence $I(X;Y) = 1$ bit.

The merge $t_1 = \{a,b\}$, $t_2 = \{c,d\}$ has $p(1 \mid t_1) = 0$ and
$p(1 \mid t_2) = 1$, so $H(Y \mid T) = 0$ and $I(T;Y) = 1 = I(X;Y)$: it is
sufficient, the equality case of the data-processing inequality
(<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>). Equivalently every assignment
cost $D\bigl(p(y \mid x) \,\|\, p(y \mid t(x))\bigr)$ is zero, so the
relevance-loss identity gives loss zero. Its rate is
$I(X;T) = H(T) = 1$ bit, and by part (a) no encoder reaching the ceiling costs
less than $I(X;Y) = 1$ bit, so this is minimal.

The $\beta \to \infty$ solutions therefore accumulate at $(1, 1)$, not at the
identity encoder's $(2, 1)$. The second bit of $X$ — which symbol within the
cluster — is pure irrelevance, and the bottleneck discards it at every price.
Compare with the original source, where the four conditionals were distinct
and the accumulation point was $(2, 0.594)$: whether compression is free
depends entirely on whether the conditionals coincide.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Three requests arrive from different teams. For each, decide what kind of
problem it is — a rate-distortion problem, a bottleneck problem, or neither as
stated — name the words in the request that decide it, and then
produce the one number the framework yields. (i) "Store these fair, independent
bits at half a bit each; how many will come back wrong?" (ii) "We have 40,000
recordings, each with a speaker label; how few bits per recording can we keep
and still identify the speaker as well as the raw audio does?" (iii) "Compress
these images so they look good." Useful values: $H(0.11) \approx 0.5$,
$H(0.25) \approx 0.811$.

<Solution>
**(i) Rate-distortion.** The deciding words are "wrong" applied to individual
bits, which names Hamming distortion, and a rate given in advance. With
$R = \tfrac12$ and $R(D) = 1 - H(D)$ for a fair binary source
(<Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/>), solve $1 - H(D) = \tfrac12$, i.e.
$H(D) = \tfrac12$, giving $D \approx 0.11$: about $11\%$ of the bits come back
wrong.

**(ii) Bottleneck.** The deciding words are the paired label and the target
"as well as the raw audio does": relevance is defined by another variable and
the fidelity criterion is information about it, not reconstruction of the
audio. The number the framework yields is the ceiling: the least rate that
keeps everything is at least $I(X;Y)$ bits, and $I(X;Y) \le H(Y) \le
\log_2 40{,}000 \approx 15.3$ bits per recording if every recording had a
distinct speaker — with, say, 200 speakers appearing equally often, at most
$\log_2 200 \approx 7.6$ bits. The audio's megabytes are irrelevant to this
bound; only the label's entropy is.

**(iii) Neither, as stated.** "Look good" names no distortion measure and no
target variable, so neither framework can be evaluated. It becomes a
rate-distortion problem the moment someone fixes $d$, and a bottleneck problem
the moment someone supplies a $Y$ that stands in for looking good, such as
recorded human preference judgements. The point of the exercise is that the
missing ingredient is the same missing ingredient in both cases, and the
frameworks differ only in who supplies it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A team has $X$ = photographs and $Y$ = their captions, so $p(x,y)$ is
unavailable and both mutual informations must be bounded rather than computed.
They train a decoder $q(y \mid t)$ from a representation $T$ and measure its
average loss. (a) Write the resulting lower bound on $I(T;Y)$ and state which
quantity in it they cannot evaluate. (b) They replace the task by a
multiple-choice one: given $T$, pick the true caption from $N$ candidates
drawn from the caption pool. Write the bound this yields and state the largest
value it can ever certify.

<Solution>
**(a)** The decoder bound derived above is
$$
I(T;Y) \;\ge\; H(Y) - \mathbb{E}\bigl[-\log_2 q(y \mid t)\bigr],
$$
relevance at least the label entropy minus the decoder's cross-entropy loss in
bits. The unevaluable quantity is $H(Y)$: the entropy of natural-language
captions is not known, and estimating it is the problem of
<Ref lesson="entropy-of-english"/> at the level of sentences. Without it the
bound has an unknown offset, so the measured loss ranks representations but
certifies no number of bits.

**(b)** In the multiple-choice task the answer variable is uniform on $N$
options, so the unknown entropy is replaced by a known one, $\log_2 N$. If
$L_N$ is the average loss in bits of the $N$-way classifier,
$$
I(T;Y) \;\ge\; \log_2 N - L_N .
$$
A perfect classifier has $L_N = 0$, so the bound certifies at most $\log_2 N$
bits: with $N = 256$ candidates, no more than $8$ bits, however informative
the representation actually is. This is the InfoNCE bound, and its ceiling is
why a strong representation and an adequate one can look identical once the
loss saturates: <Ref lesson="infonce"/> derives it and works out what the
saturation costs in practice.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

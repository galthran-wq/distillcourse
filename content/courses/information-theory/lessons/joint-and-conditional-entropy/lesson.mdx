Conditional entropy H(Y|X) is the uncertainty remaining in Y once X is
observed; the chain rule decomposes joint uncertainty into sequential
parts.

The course has so far priced one variable at a time.
<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/> fixed the information content of a single
event at $-\log_2 p$, and <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>
averaged that quantity over a distribution to get $H(X)$, the bits one draw
from a source carries. Both numbers describe a variable observed in
isolation, and no real source is observed that way: a letter of English
arrives after the letters before it, and those change what it is worth.
This lesson builds the second step of the course's argument, that context
destroys uncertainty. It defines the entropy of a pair, the entropy of one
variable given another, and the identity that links them, and it proves
that observing a second variable never raises average uncertainty.
<Ref lesson="entropy-of-english"/> then turns that machinery on English
text and measures the drop.

The route: the joint table and its marginals; joint entropy; conditional
entropy computed two equivalent ways on one table; the chain rule in both
orders; and the inequality $H(Y \mid X) \le H(Y)$ with its equality case,
its two endpoints, and the pointwise statement that is false.

## The joint table: one probability per pair

Two variables observed together are one random object, and its
distribution is a table rather than a list. Take one day of weather with
the sky $X \in \{\text{clear}, \text{overcast}\}$ and the ground
$Y \in \{\text{dry}, \text{wet}\}$: the pair $(X, Y)$ has four possible
values, and the distribution assigns a probability to each.

| $p(x,y)$ | $y = \text{dry}$ | $y = \text{wet}$ | $p(x)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x = \text{clear}$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $0$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ |
| $x = \text{overcast}$ | $\tfrac{1}{4}$ | $\tfrac{1}{4}$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ |
| $p(y)$ | $\tfrac{3}{4}$ | $\tfrac{1}{4}$ | $1$ |

The four cells sum to 1. Summing each row recovers the distribution of $X$
alone, $p(\text{clear}) = \tfrac{1}{2} + 0 = \tfrac{1}{2}$; summing each
column recovers the distribution of $Y$,
$p(\text{dry}) = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{3}{4}$. Those row and
column sums are the *marginals*, the distributions written in the margins
of the table.

<Figure
  src="./figures/joint-table.svg"
  alt="The 2×2 joint table for sky and rain with cell probabilities 1/2, 0, 1/4, 1/4; row sums 1/2 and 1/2 give p(x), column sums 3/4 and 1/4 give p(y); the highlighted cell (clear, dry) contributes 1/2 times 1 bit to the joint entropy"
  caption="The joint table: cells are pair probabilities, row and column sums are the marginals, and each nonzero cell contributes weight × surprisal to H(X,Y)."
/>

The zero cell says that a clear sky above wet ground never occurs. It
contributes nothing to any entropy computed below, by the convention
$0 \log_2 0 = 0$ that <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/> adopted
for outcomes of probability zero.

The direction of the arithmetic is worth fixing now, because the whole
lesson depends on it. Cells determine margins, since the marginals are
sums of cells. Margins do not determine cells. The same
$p(x) = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$ and
$p(y) = (\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$ are also produced by the table with
cells $\tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}$, in which
rain and sky have nothing to do with each other, and by every table between
the two.

<KeyFact>
The marginals do not determine the joint table. Everything this lesson
adds beyond <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/> lives in the
difference between two tables with identical margins, and Exercise 5 shows
how wide that difference can be.
</KeyFact>

## Joint entropy: the entropy formula, applied to pairs

The uncertainty of the whole day is the entropy of the pair, and no new
formula is needed to compute it. A pair-valued variable is still a discrete
variable; its outcomes are the four cells. Reading the table as one flat
distribution $\{\tfrac{1}{2}, 0, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}\}$ over
pair-outcomes reduces the problem to the three-step computation of
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>: surprisal of each outcome,
weight by probability, sum.

<Example title="joint entropy of the weather table">
Compute $H(X,Y)$ for the table above.

**Step 1: surprisal of each nonzero cell.**
$(\text{clear}, \text{dry})$: $-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ bit;
$(\text{overcast}, \text{dry})$ and $(\text{overcast}, \text{wet})$:
$-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{4} = 2$ bits each. The zero cell is skipped.

**Step 2: weight each surprisal by its cell probability.**
$\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 = 0.5$; $\;\tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 = 0.5$;
$\;\tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 2 = 0.5$.

**Step 3: sum.** $H(X,Y) = 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.5$ bits.

**Bounds check.** The value lies below $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits, the
ceiling for a distribution supported on three outcomes.
</Example>

<Definition title="joint entropy">
The **joint entropy** of a pair of discrete random variables $(X, Y)$ with
joint probability mass function $p(x,y)$ is
$$
H(X,Y) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(x,y)
       = \mathbb{E}\bigl[-\log_2 p(X,Y)\bigr],
$$
measured in bits. Cells with $p(x,y) = 0$ contribute nothing.
</Definition>

Joint entropy is **symmetric**: $H(X,Y) = H(Y,X)$, because transposing the
table permutes the same multiset of cell values, and entropy depends on
that multiset alone.

One number is worth carrying forward. The marginal entropies of this table
are $H(X) = 1$ bit, a fair coin, and $H(Y) = H(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$
bits, the binary entropy value computed in
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>. Their sum is
$1.811$ bits, while the pair costs $1.5$ bits. Pricing the two variables
separately overcharges by $0.311$ bits, and the rest of the lesson accounts
for that gap.

## Conditional entropy: an average over what the observation could be

A forecaster who has seen the sky needs a number for the uncertainty still
left about the ground. There is no single such number until the question is
made precise, because the sky can show two different things and they leave
different amounts of work to do.

### Each row, renormalized, is a conditional distribution

Dividing a row of the joint table by its row sum $p(x)$ gives the
conditional distribution $p(y \mid x)$ of the ground given that sky.

- Given clear, the row $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 0)$ renormalizes to $(1, 0)$:
  certainty. The leftover uncertainty is
  $H(Y \mid X{=}\text{clear}) = 0$ bits.
- Given overcast, the row $(\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$ renormalizes to
  $(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$: a fair coin, so
  $H(Y \mid X{=}\text{overcast}) = 1$ bit.

Each is an ordinary entropy of a two-outcome distribution, evaluated on the
binary entropy curve $H(p)$ of
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>. Two numbers come out, one per
value of $X$, and neither of them is the answer to the forecaster's
question, which was about a day whose sky has not been seen yet.

### One number: weight the rows by how often they occur

The summary the question asks for weights each row's entropy by the
probability of landing in that row:
$$
H(Y \mid X) = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 = 0.5
\text{ bits}.
$$
Half of all days the sky settles the question completely; the other half it
leaves a fair coin. On average, half a bit of uncertainty about the ground
survives the observation.

  caption="Conditional entropy in three steps: row entropies, row weights, weighted sum H(Y|X) = 0.5 bits."

<Task>

$\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 = 0.5$, so $H(Y \mid X) = 0.5$ bits. Then compute

stay $0$ and $1$, and only the weights move.
</Task>

<Definition title="conditional entropy">
The **conditional entropy** of $Y$ given $X$ is
$$
H(Y \mid X) = \sum_{x} p(x)\, H(Y \mid X{=}x)
            = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(y \mid x),
$$
measured in bits, where
$H(Y \mid X{=}x) = -\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log_2 p(y \mid x)$ is the entropy
of the row at $x$. Values $x$ with $p(x) = 0$ are skipped: $p(y \mid x)$ is
undefined there, and such rows carry weight zero.
</Definition>

Four symbols now share a letter $H$ and differ in what follows it. The
distinctions are the ones every later chapter of this course relies on.

| Symbol | Read as | What it is | Confusion to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $p(y \mid x)$ | *p of y given x* | a distribution over $y$, one per row | not a number |
| $H(Y \mid X{=}x)$ | *entropy of Y given the observation x* | one number per row | not a bound on anything |
| $H(Y \mid X)$ | *conditional entropy of Y given X* | the $p(x)$-weighted average of those numbers | not a function of $X$, and not any single row |
| $H(X,Y)$ | *joint entropy of X and Y* | entropy of the flattened table | not $H(X) + H(Y)$ unless the variables are independent |

The third row is where errors concentrate. $H(Y \mid X)$ is a functional of
the joint distribution and a plain number, unlike the conditional
expectation $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X]$ of probability theory, which is a random
variable. Averaging over $X$ is built into the definition, not applied to
it afterwards.

### The two forms are the same sum

The second expression in the definition drops the renormalization step.
Substituting $H(Y \mid X{=}x) = -\sum_y p(y \mid x) \log_2 p(y \mid x)$
into the first form and using $p(x)\, p(y \mid x) = p(x,y)$ merges each
row's weight into its cell probabilities, giving
$-\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(y \mid x)$ term by term.

<Example title="the same 0.5 bits, computed cell by cell">
Recompute $H(Y \mid X)$ for the weather table from
$-\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(y \mid x)$, one nonzero cell at a time. Each
term uses the *cell* probability as a weight and the *conditional*
probability inside the logarithm, which is the only subtlety.

$(\text{clear}, \text{dry})$: $p(y \mid x) = 1$, so the term is
$-\tfrac{1}{2} \log_2 1 = 0$.

$(\text{overcast}, \text{dry})$: $p(y \mid x) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so the term
is $-\tfrac{1}{4} \log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{4}$.

$(\text{overcast}, \text{wet})$: likewise $\tfrac{1}{4}$.

Sum: $0 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5$ bits, matching the weighted-average form. The
cell form is the faster route on a given table; the weighted-average form
is the one that shows what the number means.
</Example>

<Exercise>
A three-column table: $X \in \{a, b\}$, $Y \in \{1,2,3\}$, with
$p(a,1) = \tfrac{1}{4}$, $p(a,2) = \tfrac{1}{4}$, $p(a,3) = 0$,
$p(b,1) = \tfrac{1}{8}$, $p(b,2) = \tfrac{1}{8}$,
$p(b,3) = \tfrac{1}{4}$. Compute $H(Y \mid X)$ by the weighted-average
form. Write the two renormalized rows down before opening the solution.
Use $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$.

<Solution>
**Row weights.** $p(a) = \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4} + 0 = \tfrac{1}{2}$,
and $p(b) = \tfrac{1}{8} + \tfrac{1}{8} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

**Row $a$.** $(\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, 0)$ divided by $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is
$(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, 0)$, a fair two-way choice with the third
outcome impossible: $H(Y \mid X{=}a) = 1$ bit.

**Row $b$.** $(\tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{4})$ divided by
$\tfrac{1}{2}$ is $(\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{2})$:
$H(Y \mid X{=}b) = \tfrac{1}{4}(2) + \tfrac{1}{4}(2) + \tfrac{1}{2}(1)
= 1.5$ bits.

**Average.** $H(Y \mid X) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(1.5) = 1.25$
bits.

**Check by the cell form.** The nonzero terms are
$-\tfrac{1}{4}\log_2\tfrac{1}{2} = 0.25$ twice,
$-\tfrac{1}{8}\log_2\tfrac{1}{4} = 0.25$ twice, and
$-\tfrac{1}{4}\log_2\tfrac{1}{2} = 0.25$: five terms of $0.25$, total
$1.25$ bits. The transferable move is that the zero cell drops out of both
forms, so a row with an impossible outcome is a shorter sum, never a
special case.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### The near miss: averaging the rows before taking the entropy

One clause of the definition carries most of its content, and swapping the
order of two operations violates exactly that clause. Averaging the rows
first, then taking one entropy, gives
$$
\tfrac{1}{2}(1, 0) + \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right)
= \left(\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}\right),
$$
whose entropy is $0.811$ bits. That is $H(Y)$, the marginal entropy, and it
is not $H(Y \mid X) = 0.5$. The average of the rows is the marginal
distribution by construction, so this calculation cannot mention the
conditioning at all. Entropy must be applied row by row, *before* the
weights are used.

The gap between $0.811$ and $0.5$ is not an artifact of this table. Mixing
the rows into one distribution and then measuring uncertainty never gives
less than measuring each row and then mixing the numbers, because $H$ is a
concave function of the distribution it is fed. That statement is
$H(Y) \ge H(Y \mid X)$, proved as a theorem two sections below; the near
miss computes its left-hand side.

<KeyFact>
$H(Y \mid X)$ is a number computed from the whole joint table, not a
function of $X$ and not a bound on any particular observation. Every
theorem in this course that conditions on data bounds this average and
nothing finer, which is why <Ref lesson="fano-inequality"/> can convert
leftover conditional entropy into an unavoidable error *rate* but never
into a guarantee about one received message.
</KeyFact>

## The chain rule: one total, two splits

Three numbers have now been computed from the same table: $H(X) = 1$ bit,
$H(Y \mid X) = 0.5$ bits, and $H(X,Y) = 1.5$ bits. They satisfy an exact
identity,
$$
H(X) + H(Y \mid X) = 1 + 0.5 = 1.5 = H(X,Y),
$$
with the reading that total uncertainty about the day equals uncertainty
about the sky plus the average uncertainty about the ground once the sky is
known. Learn $X$, then learn $Y$ given $X$.

### The other order costs the same total

Nothing in the setup privileged the sky. Running the split the other way,
with columns playing the rows' part: given dry, probability $\tfrac{3}{4}$,
the column renormalizes to
$p(x \mid \text{dry}) = (\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$ with entropy
$\log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.918$ bits; given wet, the column is
$(0, 1)$ with entropy $0$. So
$$
H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac{3}{4} \cdot 0.918 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 0
\approx 0.689 \text{ bits},
\qquad
H(Y) + H(X \mid Y) \approx 0.811 + 0.689 = 1.5.
$$
The two conditional entropies differ, $0.5$ against $0.689$, and the two
totals agree.

<Figure
  src="./figures/chain-bars.svg"
  alt="A 1.5-bit bar for H(X,Y) split two ways: as H(X) = 1 plus H(Y|X) = 0.5, and as H(Y) = 0.811 plus H(X|Y) = 0.689"
  caption="The chain rule: the same 1.5-bit total splits as H(X) + H(Y|X) or as H(Y) + H(X|Y)."
/>

<Theorem title="chain rule (Cover &amp; Thomas 2.2.1)">
For discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$,
$$
H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X) = H(Y) + H(X \mid Y).
$$

<Proof>
The goal is an identity, not an inequality, so the proof is direct: take
the product rule for probabilities cell by cell, turn products into sums
with a logarithm, and average.

**Cell by cell.** For every cell with $p(x,y) > 0$, the product rule
$p(x,y) = p(x)\, p(y \mid x)$ gives, after applying $-\log_2$,
$$
-\log_2 p(x,y) = -\log_2 p(x) - \log_2 p(y \mid x).
$$

**Average with weights $p(x,y)$.** The left side becomes $H(X,Y)$ by
definition. The first term on the right becomes
$$
\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \bigl(-\log_2 p(x)\bigr)
= \sum_x \Bigl(\sum_y p(x,y)\Bigr) \bigl(-\log_2 p(x)\bigr)
= \sum_x p(x) \bigl(-\log_2 p(x)\bigr) = H(X),
$$
where the inner sum over $y$ collapses to the marginal. This is the step
where the argument stops depending on the particular table: it uses only
that the row sums are $p(x)$. The second term is the cell form of
$H(Y \mid X)$.

Exchanging the roles of $X$ and $Y$ throughout gives the second equality.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The identity is one line of algebra, and it is also the averaged version of
a rule already established: $I(A \cap B) = I(A) + I(B \mid A)$ for the
surprisal of single events (<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>). Exercise 4
runs the induction that extends it to $n$ variables.

<Remark title="conditioning is asymmetric, and by how much">
$H(Y \mid X) = 0.5$ while $H(X \mid Y) \approx 0.689$: only the total
$H(X,Y)$ is symmetric. Subtracting the two forms of the chain rule fixes
the difference exactly,
$$
H(X \mid Y) - H(Y \mid X) = H(X) - H(Y),
$$
here $1 - 0.811 = 0.189$ bits. The asymmetry of the parts is entirely
inherited from the asymmetry of the marginals, which is a stronger
statement than "the two are not equal": knowing $H(X)$ and $H(Y)$ lets you
convert either conditional entropy into the other without touching the
table.
</Remark>

### The area picture: five quantities on one interval

Because the same total splits two ways, all five quantities fit a single
additive diagram. Lay $H(X,Y)$ out as an interval of length $1.5$ bits.
Place $H(X)$ as a sub-interval at the left end and $H(Y)$ as a sub-interval
at the right end. Since $H(X) + H(Y)$ exceeds the total by $0.311$ bits,
the two sub-intervals overlap by that much, and each conditional entropy is
the part of one marginal lying outside the overlap.

  alt="Aligned bars on a common 1.5-bit axis: H(X,Y) spans the whole interval, H(X) covers the left 1.0 bit, H(Y) the right 0.811 bits; their 0.311-bit overlap is shaded, with the crescents H(X|Y) = 0.689 and H(Y|X) = 0.5 outside it"
  caption="The additive diagram: H(X) and H(Y) tile H(X,Y) with a 0.311-bit overlap; the crescents are the conditional entropies."

The overlap has width $H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \approx 0.311$ bits, the
uncertainty counted in both marginals but only once in the total. It is the
gap noted after the joint-entropy definition, and it stays unnamed here.
The diagram is a mnemonic for the chain rule rather than a theorem about
sets, and the next section shows what it costs to forget that.

<Aside>
The chain rule holds inside a conditioning as well:
$H(X,Y \mid Z) = H(X \mid Z) + H(Y \mid X, Z)$, proved by running the same
argument on the table of $(X,Y)$ given each value $z$ and averaging over
$z$ with weights $p(z)$. This conditional form is what
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/> expands in two different orders
to prove that processing cannot create information.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Applied repeatedly, the chain rule turns the entropy of a long sequence
into a sum of per-symbol conditional entropies,
$H(X_1, \ldots, X_n) = \sum_i H(X_i \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1})$. A source's
information per symbol is therefore an average of conditional entropies,
not of marginal ones, which is the definition
<Ref lesson="entropy-of-english"/> measures with.
</KeyFact>

## ★ Why the area diagram is a mnemonic and not an identity

The two-variable diagram is exact: the three regions are $H(X \mid Y)$,
the overlap, and $H(Y \mid X)$, and all three are entropies or differences
of entropies that cannot be negative. Drawing the same picture for three
variables suggests that a seven-region version would also have
non-negative areas. It does not, and one three-bit example settles it. The
core path does not use this section.

Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent fair bits and let $Z = X \oplus Y$, their
sum modulo 2. Every one of the three variables is a fair bit, so
$H(X) = H(Y) = H(Z) = 1$ bit. Every *pair* is a pair of independent fair
bits, because knowing $X$ says nothing about $Z$ and knowing $Y$ says
nothing about $Z$, so $H(X,Y) = H(X,Z) = H(Y,Z) = 2$ bits. All three
together carry no more, since any two of them determine the third:
$H(X,Y,Z) = 2$ bits.

In the picture, the region belonging to all three circles is forced by
inclusion-exclusion to be
$$
H(X) + H(Y) + H(Z) - H(X,Y) - H(X,Z) - H(Y,Z) + H(X,Y,Z)
= 3 - 6 + 2 = -1 \text{ bit}.
$$

<Figure
  src="./figures/venn-warning.svg"
  alt="Left: two overlapping circles for H(X) and H(Y) with regions H(X|Y) = 0.689, overlap 0.311 and H(Y|X) = 0.5, all positive. Right: three overlapping circles for X, Y and Z with the central region shaded and labelled minus 1 bit, under the caption Z = X XOR Y"
  caption="Two circles: three non-negative regions. Three circles: a centre region that must hold −1 bit."
/>

No assignment of areas to regions can hold, because areas are not negative.
The underlying fact is not a drawing defect. $X$ and $Y$ are independent,
so before $Z$ is seen they share nothing; after $Z$ is seen they determine
each other, since $Y = Z \oplus X$. Conditioning created a dependence that
was not there, and a Venn diagram has no way to draw that.
[MacKay](https://www.inference.org.uk/itprnn/book.pdf) makes the same point
with the same example in §8.4 (his figures 8.2 and 8.3), and adds the
reason the case is not exotic: a noisy channel with input $X$, noise $Y$
and output $Z = X \oplus Y$ has exactly this structure.

Two-variable diagrams remain safe, and this course keeps using them. The
warning matters from three variables on, which is where
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> and the conditional mutual information
of <Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/> operate.

## Conditioning reduces entropy on average

In the weather table $H(Y \mid X) = 0.5$ bits against
$H(Y) \approx 0.811$: seeing the sky removed $0.311$ bits of uncertainty
about the ground. The near miss of the conditional-entropy section already
named the reason, the concavity of $H$. What remains is to prove the
inequality for every joint distribution and to pin down exactly when it is
tight.

<Theorem title="conditioning reduces entropy (Cover &amp; Thomas 2.6.5)">
For discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$,
$$
H(Y \mid X) \le H(Y),
$$
with equality if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

<Proof>
The plan: write the difference as one expectation of a logarithm, move the
expectation inside the logarithm with
[Jensen's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen%27s_inequality)
for the concave $\log_2$, and evaluate what is left. Jensen is used as in
the range theorem of <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>. All sums
run over cells with $p(x,y) > 0$.

**The difference as one sum.** Using the cell form of $H(Y \mid X)$ and
$\sum_x p(x,y) = p(y)$ for the marginal term,
$$
H(Y \mid X) - H(Y)
= \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p(y)}{p(y \mid x)}.
$$

**Jensen.** The sum is the expectation of $\log_2$ of the random quantity
$p(Y)/p(Y \mid X)$, and $\log_2$ is concave, so
$$
\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p(y)}{p(y \mid x)}
\le \log_2 \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\, \frac{p(y)}{p(y \mid x)}.
$$

**Evaluate.** Inside the logarithm, $p(x,y) = p(x)\,p(y \mid x)$ cancels
the denominator and leaves $\sum_{x,y} p(x)\,p(y)$, a sum over the
supported cells only. Summing $p(x)p(y)$ over a subset of all pairs is at
most $1$, so the whole expression is at most $\log_2 1 = 0$.

**Equality.** $\log_2$ is strictly concave, so the Jensen step is tight
only when $p(y)/p(y \mid x)$ takes one value on the support; the final
step is tight only when the support is every pair with $p(x)p(y) > 0$.
Together they force that constant to be $1$, that is
$p(y \mid x) = p(y)$ wherever $p(x) > 0$, which is independence.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Two boundary cases are worth computing rather than asserting, because they
fix the two ends of the range $0 \le H(Y \mid X) \le H(Y)$.

<Example title="the equality case: an independent table with the same margins">
Build the table with cells $p(x,y) = p(x)\,p(y)$ and the weather table's
marginals $p(x) = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$,
$p(y) = (\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$. Both rows are then
$(\tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8})$.

Each row renormalizes to $(\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$, so each row
entropy is $H(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$ bits, and the weighted average
of two equal numbers is that number:
$H(Y \mid X) = 0.811 = H(Y)$ bits.

Directly from the cells, with $\log_2 \tfrac{8}{3} \approx 1.415$,
$$
H(X,Y) = 2 \cdot \tfrac{3}{8}(1.415) + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8}(3)
= 1.061 + 0.75 = 1.811 \text{ bits} = H(X) + H(Y).
$$
Joint entropy is additive exactly for independent variables, which is the
distribution-level version of the additivity of surprisal over independent
events in <Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>.
</Example>

<Example title="the other endpoint: Y is a function of X">
Roll a fair die $X$ and report only $Y = 1$ if $X \ge 5$, else $Y = 0$.

$H(X) = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585$ bits, a uniform six-outcome draw.
$Y$ is $1$ with probability $\tfrac{2}{6} = \tfrac{1}{3}$, so
$H(Y) = H(\tfrac{1}{3}) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.918$ bits.

Every row of the joint table is certain, because the value of $X$ fixes
$Y$: each $H(Y \mid X{=}x) = 0$, so $H(Y \mid X) = 0$ and the chain rule
collapses to $H(X,Y) = H(X) \approx 2.585$ bits.

The reverse conditional does not vanish. By the other order,
$H(X \mid Y) = H(X,Y) - H(Y) \approx 2.585 - 0.918 = 1.667$ bits, and
directly: given $Y = 1$, probability $\tfrac{1}{3}$, $X$ is uniform on
$\{5,6\}$ with entropy $1$ bit; given $Y = 0$, uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$
with entropy $2$ bits; the average is
$\tfrac{1}{3}(1) + \tfrac{2}{3}(2) = \tfrac{5}{3} \approx 1.667$ bits.

Determinism is one-sided: $Y = f(X)$ drives $H(Y \mid X)$ to zero and says
nothing about $H(X \mid Y)$, which measures how much the function threw
away.
</Example>

## The bound is on the average, and only on the average

From "observation never raises uncertainty" one would expect each
individual observation to help, or at worst to leave things unchanged. It
does not, and the table used throughout this lesson is already a
counterexample. Before looking at the sky, uncertainty about the ground is
$H(Y) \approx 0.811$ bits. After seeing an overcast sky it is
$H(Y \mid X{=}\text{overcast}) = 1$ bit: the observation moved the ground's
distribution from $(\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$ to a fair coin, which is
closer to uniform and therefore *more* uncertain.

<Figure
  src="./figures/pointwise.svg"
  alt="A blue bar for H(Y) = 0.811 bits with a dashed guide; below it an empty bar for H(Y|X = clear) = 0 and an amber bar for H(Y|X = overcast) = 1 bit that extends past the guide; at the bottom the weighted average H(Y|X) = 0.5 bits, well short of the guide"
  caption="One row above the H(Y) line, one row far below it: the theorem bounds only the weighted average of the two."
/>

The theorem is untouched, because it bounds the average
$\tfrac{1}{2}(0) + \tfrac{1}{2}(1) = 0.5$ bits and not the rows. What the
overcast row spends above $H(Y)$, the clear row more than repays. The
weather table keeps the effect small; the standard sharper instance makes
it hard to miss.

<Example title="an observation that nearly doubles the uncertainty">
Take $X, Y \in \{1,2\}$ with cells $p(1,1) = 0$, $p(2,1) = \tfrac{3}{4}$,
$p(1,2) = \tfrac{1}{8}$, $p(2,2) = \tfrac{1}{8}$.

Marginals: $p(x) = (\tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{7}{8})$ and
$p(y) = (\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$. So
$H(X) = H(\tfrac{1}{8}) = \tfrac{1}{8}(3) + \tfrac{7}{8}(0.193)
\approx 0.544$ bits.

Columns: given $Y = 1$ the column is $(0, \tfrac{3}{4})$, renormalizing to
$(0,1)$, so $H(X \mid Y{=}1) = 0$ bits. Given $Y = 2$ the column is
$(\tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8})$, renormalizing to a fair coin, so
$H(X \mid Y{=}2) = 1$ bit.

Observing $Y = 2$ therefore takes uncertainty about $X$ from $0.544$ bits
to $1$ bit, nearly doubling it, and it happens a quarter of the time. The
average is $H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac{3}{4}(0) + \tfrac{1}{4}(1) = 0.25$ bits,
less than half of $H(X)$.
</Example>

The distinction matters wherever data arrives one observation at a time.
Evidence is guaranteed to help only in aggregate: a single measurement can
leave the observer strictly less certain than before it arrived, and
nothing in the theorem forbids that. What the theorem forbids is a source
of observations that does it on balance.

<KeyFact>
Nothing caps the excess. Give $X$ a rare value $b$ whose row is uniform
over $n$ outcomes and put the rest of the mass on a single certain row:
$H(Y \mid X{=}b) = \log_2 n$ while $H(Y)$ can be pushed as close to zero
as desired by shrinking $p(b)$. A single observation can therefore raise
uncertainty by an unbounded number of bits, and no weakened pointwise
version of the theorem survives.
</KeyFact>

## The drop H(Y) − H(Y|X): what the next two lessons take up

Across the three tables of the conditioning sequence above, the drop
$H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$ takes the values $0$, $0.311$, and $0.811$ bits: the
uncertainty about rain that seeing the sky removes, from nothing to
everything. By the chain rule the drop also equals
$H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y)$, which is the overlap width in the area diagram, so
the two routes measure one quantity.

Established: a joint table carries information its marginals do not;
$H(X,Y)$ is the ordinary entropy of the flattened table; $H(Y \mid X)$ is
the $p(x)$-weighted average of the row entropies and equals
$-\sum p(x,y) \log_2 p(y \mid x)$; the chain rule splits the same total two
ways, with $H(Y \mid X) = 0$ exactly when $Y$ is a function of $X$; and
$H(Y \mid X) \le H(Y)$ holds on average, with equality exactly under
independence and no pointwise version.

<Ref lesson="entropy-of-english"/> takes the chain rule's telescoping form
and pushes the conditioning back over many preceding symbols, turning
"entropy per letter" into a limit of conditional entropies and measuring it
for English. <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> gives the drop
$H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$ its name and its properties.

<Exercise>
For the table with $X \in \{a,b,c\}$, $Y \in \{0,1\}$ and cells
$p(a,0) = \tfrac{1}{4}$, $p(a,1) = \tfrac{1}{4}$,
$p(b,0) = \tfrac{1}{4}$, $p(b,1) = \tfrac{1}{8}$, $p(c,0) = 0$,
$p(c,1) = \tfrac{1}{8}$: compute $H(X)$, $H(Y)$, $H(X,Y)$, $H(Y \mid X)$
and $H(X \mid Y)$, and verify both forms of the chain rule. Use
$\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ and $\log_2 \tfrac{8}{3} \approx 1.415$.

<Solution>
**Marginals.** Row sums: $p(a) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $p(b) = \tfrac{3}{8}$,
$p(c) = \tfrac{1}{8}$. Column sums: $p(0) = \tfrac{1}{2}$,
$p(1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

**Marginal entropies.** $H(Y) = 1$ bit, a fair two-way split. For $X$,
$$
H(X) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{3}{8}(1.415) + \tfrac{1}{8}(3)
= 0.5 + 0.531 + 0.375 = 1.406 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Joint entropy.** The nonzero cells are
$\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}$:
$$
H(X,Y) = 3 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4}(2) + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8}(3)
= 1.5 + 0.75 = 2.25 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Rows.** $a$ renormalizes to $(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$, entropy $1$;
$b$ to $(\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})$, entropy
$\log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.918$; $c$ to $(0,1)$, entropy $0$.
Weighted:
$H(Y \mid X) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{3}{8}(0.918) + \tfrac{1}{8}(0)
= 0.5 + 0.344 = 0.844$ bits.

**Columns.** $0$ renormalizes to
$(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, 0)$, entropy $1$; $1$ renormalizes to
$(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$, entropy
$\tfrac{1}{2}(1) + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4}(2) = 1.5$. Weighted:
$H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(1.5) = 1.25$ bits.

**Chain rule, both forms.**
$H(X) + H(Y \mid X) = 1.406 + 0.844 = 2.25$, and
$H(Y) + H(X \mid Y) = 1 + 1.25 = 2.25$. Both equal $H(X,Y)$, and the
difference check holds as well:
$H(X \mid Y) - H(Y \mid X) = 0.406 = H(X) - H(Y)$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Construct a joint distribution with $H(Y \mid X) = 0$ and
$H(X \mid Y) = 1$ bit exactly. State the relation between $X$ and $Y$ that
forces $H(Y \mid X) = 0$, explain what the value of $H(X \mid Y)$ is
measuring, and say what would have to change about that relation to drive
both conditional entropies to zero.

<Solution>
**A table that works.** Let $X$ be uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$ and set
$Y = 0$ for $X \in \{1,2\}$ and $Y = 1$ for $X \in \{3,4\}$. The four
nonzero cells each carry $\tfrac{1}{4}$.

$H(X) = \log_2 4 = 2$ bits, and $H(Y) = 1$ bit since $Y$ is a fair bit.
Every row of the table is certain, so $H(Y \mid X) = 0$ and
$H(X,Y) = H(X) = 2$ bits. The other order gives
$H(X \mid Y) = H(X,Y) - H(Y) = 2 - 1 = 1$ bit, confirmed directly: each
value of $Y$ leaves $X$ uniform on two possibilities, worth $1$ bit, and
the average of two equal numbers is that number.

**What forces each value.** $H(Y \mid X) = 0$ holds exactly when $Y$ is a
function of $X$, since a row entropy vanishes only for a certain row.
$H(X \mid Y)$ measures what the function discards: here $f$ maps four
inputs onto two outputs two-to-one, so one bit of $X$ is not recoverable
from $Y$, and $H(X \mid Y)$ reports exactly that bit.

**Driving both to zero.** The function must also be injective, that is, a
relabelling of the same four outcomes. Then $X$ is a function of $Y$ as
well, both conditionals vanish, and the chain rule collapses to
$H(X,Y) = H(X) = H(Y)$. Any two-to-one collapse anywhere in the map leaves
$H(X \mid Y) > 0$, so "both zero" and "bijection" are the same condition.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim with a specific table: "Conditioning reduces entropy, so
$H(Y \mid X{=}x) \le H(Y)$ for every value $x$." Give the numbers, and
name the step at which the argument silently replaced one quantity by
another.

<Solution>
**A table that breaks it.** $X \in \{a,b\}$, $Y \in \{0,1\}$ with
$p(a,0) = 0.9$, $p(a,1) = 0$, $p(b,0) = 0.05$, $p(b,1) = 0.05$.

Marginals: $p(x) = (0.9, 0.1)$ and $p(y) = (0.95, 0.05)$, so
$H(Y) = H(0.05) = 0.05(4.322) + 0.95(0.074) \approx 0.286$ bits.

Rows: $a$ renormalizes to $(1, 0)$, so $H(Y \mid X{=}a) = 0$ bits; $b$
renormalizes to $(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$, so
$H(Y \mid X{=}b) = 1$ bit. The second row exceeds $H(Y)$ by a factor of
about $3.5$, which refutes the claim.

The theorem survives: $H(Y \mid X) = 0.9(0) + 0.1(1) = 0.1$ bits, well
below $0.286$.

**The failing step.** The theorem's left-hand side is
$H(Y \mid X) = \sum_x p(x) H(Y \mid X{=}x)$, an average. The claim reads
the same symbol as though the sum were absent, which replaces a weighted
average by each of its terms. That replacement is legal only when all the
terms are equal, and equal row entropies are not what the proof
establishes. The general shape of the error is that averaging destroys
information about the spread of what is being averaged, and the whole
content of this table is spread: one row at $0$, one row at $1$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The body proved the chain rule for two variables. Prove the general form
$$
H(X_1, \ldots, X_n)
= \sum_{i=1}^{n} H(X_i \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1})
$$
by induction from the two-variable case, then use it together with the
theorem of the previous section to prove
$H(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \le \sum_i H(X_i)$, and state when equality holds.
Write down what plays the role of $X$ and what plays the role of $Y$ in
the two-variable rule before opening the solution.

<Solution>
**Induction.** The base case $n = 1$ reads $H(X_1) = H(X_1)$. Assume the
identity for $n - 1$ variables. The two-variable chain rule applies to any
pair of discrete variables, and a tuple is a discrete variable, so take
$X = (X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1})$ and $Y = X_n$:
$$
H(X_1, \ldots, X_n)
= H(X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1}) + H(X_n \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1}).
$$
Applying the induction hypothesis to the first term and appending the
second gives the claimed sum. This is the only step that needs care: the
two-variable rule is being used with a vector-valued first argument, which
is legitimate because its proof used nothing about the alphabet of $X$
beyond its row sums being $p(x)$.

**Subadditivity.** Conditioning reduces entropy, applied with the
conditioning variable $(X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1})$, gives
$H(X_i \mid X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1}) \le H(X_i)$ for each $i$. Summing the $n$
inequalities and using the chain rule on the left,
$$
H(X_1, \ldots, X_n) \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} H(X_i).
$$

**Equality.** A sum of inequalities is tight only when every one of them
is, so equality requires $X_i$ to be independent of the block
$(X_1, \ldots, X_{i-1})$ for every $i$, which is exactly joint
independence of the $n$ variables. Pairwise independence is weaker and does
not suffice: the triple $X$, $Y$, $Z = X \oplus Y$ of the starred section
is pairwise independent, yet $H(X,Y,Z) = 2 < 3 = H(X) + H(Y) + H(Z)$.

The transferable move is that every multi-variable entropy identity in this
course is the two-variable one applied to tuples, then unrolled.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A colleague reports three numbers from a joint distribution they will not
show you: $H(X) = 1.5$ bits, $H(Y) = 1.0$ bits, $H(X,Y) = 2.1$ bits.
Decide whether $X$ and $Y$ are independent. Then state the largest and the
smallest value $H(X,Y)$ could have taken given only the two marginal
entropies, and say which joint distribution attains each. Finally, using
the range theorem of <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>, give the
smallest alphabet $Y$ can have and say whether $H(Y) = 1.0$ bits is
attainable on it.

<Solution>
**Independence.** No. Independence holds if and only if
$H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y) = 2.5$ bits, and the reported total is $2.1$. The
shortfall of $0.4$ bits is the drop $H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$, so knowing $X$
removes $0.4$ bits of the $1.0$ bits of uncertainty in $Y$.

**Largest.** $2.5$ bits, by the subadditivity bound of Exercise 4, attained
by the product table with these marginals.

**Smallest.** $1.5$ bits. The chain rule gives
$H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X) \ge H(X)$ because conditional entropies are
non-negative, and likewise $H(X,Y) \ge H(Y)$, so the total is at least
$\max(1.5, 1.0) = 1.5$ bits. It is attained exactly when
$H(Y \mid X) = 0$, that is when $Y$ is a function of $X$ — consistent here,
since $H(Y) = 1.0 \le 1.5 = H(X)$ leaves room for such a function. The
reported $2.1$ sits between the two bounds, as it must.

**Alphabet of $Y$.** The range theorem caps entropy at $\log_2 n$ for $n$
outcomes, so $H(Y) = 1.0$ needs $\log_2 n \ge 1$, that is $n \ge 2$. On
$n = 2$ the value is attainable, but only at the ceiling, which the theorem
reaches only for the uniform distribution: $Y$ would have to be a fair
binary variable. Any two-outcome $Y$ that is not fair has $H(Y) < 1$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take an English text of a few thousand characters that you have to hand.
Case-fold it and reduce every run of non-letters to a single space, giving
a 27-symbol alphabet. Tabulate the joint distribution of the pair (current
letter, next letter) over the whole file, then compute
$H(\text{next} \mid \text{current})$ from that table. Compare it with the
single-letter entropy $H(\text{next})$ computed from the same file, report
the drop in bits, and state what would count as agreement with published
figures.

<Solution>
**Procedure.** The joint table has $27 \times 27 = 729$ cells; cell
$(u,v)$ holds the count of the ordered pair $uv$ divided by the number of
pairs. Row sums give the current-letter distribution. Then
$H(\text{next} \mid \text{current}) = -\sum_{u,v} p(u,v) \log_2 p(v \mid u)$
with $p(v \mid u) = p(u,v)/p(u)$, summing over nonzero cells only, exactly
as in the worked cell-form example.

**What to expect.** The single-letter entropy over 27 symbols lands near
$4.0$–$4.1$ bits per character, against the uniform ceiling
$\log_2 27 \approx 4.75$. The conditional entropy should land somewhere
around $3.3$–$3.6$ bits, a drop of roughly half a bit to three quarters of
a bit. Almost all of that drop comes from a handful of rows: after `q` the
next symbol is nearly always `u`, and after a space the distribution is far
from the overall letter frequencies.

**What counts as agreement.** Cover & Thomas (§6.4) quote $4.03$ bits per
letter for a first-order model of English and $2.8$ bits for a
fourth-order model on the same 27-symbol alphabet. A digram estimate that
falls clearly between those two, and clearly below your own single-letter
number on the same file, agrees. A number outside that interval usually
means the alphabet was built differently — punctuation retained, case kept,
or spaces dropped — and the alphabet must be stated with the figure for it
to mean anything.

**A caution about sample size.** A few thousand characters give a few
thousand pairs spread over 729 cells, so most cells hold $0$ or $1$. A
plug-in entropy computed from sparse counts is biased *downward*, because
unobserved pairs are treated as impossible rather than rare. Expect your
number to be a little optimistic, and expect it to fall further as you
shorten the file, which is the wrong direction for a real trend.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Extend the previous exercise: on the same file, compute
$H(\text{next} \mid \text{previous two})$ by tabulating the joint
distribution of (two-letter context, next letter). Plot the three numbers
you now have against the amount of context used — $0$, $1$ and $2$
preceding letters — and extrapolate the sequence by eye. State what the
sequence is monotonically doing and which result of this lesson forces
that, then say what the limit would be a measurement of.

<Solution>
**The three numbers.** With zero letters of context the answer is the
single-letter entropy, near $4.0$ bits; with one letter, near $3.4$; with
two, near $3.0$ to $3.2$. Your exact values will differ with the file, but
the ordering will not.

**Why it can only fall.** Each step conditions on strictly more, and
$H(Y \mid X, Z) \le H(Y \mid Z)$ — the conditional form of the theorem
proved in this lesson — applies with $Z$ the previous letter and $X$ the
one before it. The sequence is non-increasing for every source, not just
for English. It is also bounded below by $0$, so it converges.

**What the limit measures.** The limit of per-symbol conditional entropies
as the context grows without bound is the **entropy rate** of the source:
the bits a letter carries given everything written before it.
Extrapolating three points by eye is crude, and the curve flattens slowly,
so an honest eyeball extrapolation lands somewhere between $1$ and $2$ bits
per letter. That is the quantity <Ref lesson="entropy-of-english"/>
measures properly, using a human predictor instead of a table, and the
answer there is about one bit per letter against a $4.75$-bit alphabet.

**Why the table runs out.** A two-letter context needs $27^2 = 729$ rows
and $19{,}683$ cells, which a few thousand characters cannot fill; a
three-letter context needs half a million. The downward bias flagged in
the previous solution grows with each step, so past two letters of context
the table method measures its own sparsity rather than the language. That
failure is the reason the entropy rate of English was first estimated by
asking people to guess.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Among all distributions consistent with known expectation constraints,
exactly one maximizes entropy, and it has exponential form; choosing any
other distribution assumes information you do not have. This lesson derives
that distribution. The route: an underdetermined inference problem (the
Brandeis dice), the exponential solution found numerically, the general
theorem proved with Gibbs' inequality from <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>,
and the two best-known instances of the solution, the Boltzmann
distribution of statistical mechanics and the softmax layer of machine
learning.

## One constraint, infinitely many distributions

A six-sided die is tossed a great many times and the results average
$4.5$, not the $3.5$ of a fair die. The task: assign probabilities
$p_1, \dots, p_6$ to the faces using this fact and nothing else. This is
the *Brandeis dice problem*, the running example of
[Jaynes' 1962 Brandeis lectures](https://bayes.wustl.edu/etj/articles/brandeis.pdf).

The evidence supplies two equations for six unknowns:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{6} p_i = 1, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{6} i\, p_i = 4.5 .
$$

Four degrees of freedom remain, so infinitely many pmfs fit the data.
Three of them, with their entropies from
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>:

| candidate $(p_1, \dots, p_6)$ | mean | $H$ (bits) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $(0, 0, .25, 0, .75, 0)$ | $4.5$ | $0.811$ |
| $(0, 0, 0, .5, .5, 0)$ | $4.5$ | $1.000$ |
| $(0, 0, .25, .25, .25, .25)$ | $4.5$ | $2.000$ |

Every candidate satisfies the constraints; every candidate also **asserts
more** than the constraints contain. The first declares faces 1, 2, 4, and
6 impossible, an impossibility claim nowhere in the evidence. The second
rules out four faces on the strength of a single average. Even the third
zeroes two faces the data never excluded.

### The principle: keep all the uncertainty the data does not remove

Entropy ranks the candidates by exactly the property at stake: $H(p)$
measures the uncertainty a distribution admits, so the candidate with
maximal $H$ is the one that concedes the most ignorance while honoring the
data. Adopting any lower-entropy candidate narrows the possibilities
further than the constraints justify.

<Definition title="maximum entropy principle">
Given constraints on a distribution $p$ (normalization plus known
expectation values), the **maximum entropy principle** adopts the
distribution
$$
p^\ast = \argmax_{p\ \text{satisfying the constraints}} H(p),
$$
the entropy maximizer over the *feasible set* of all distributions
consistent with the constraints.
</Definition>

<Remark title="Jaynes' formulation">
[Jaynes (1957)](https://bayes.wustl.edu/etj/articles/theory.1.pdf),
§2: the maximum-entropy distribution "is the least biased estimate
possible on the given information; i.e., it is maximally noncommittal
with regard to missing information." The principle does not manufacture
knowledge; it refuses to pretend to knowledge that is absent.
</Remark>

## Solving the dice: tilt exponentially, then tune

The maximization runs over a four-parameter set, but the search collapses
to one dimension. The theorem of the next section states that the
maximizer is **exponential** in the constrained quantity; the constrained
quantity here is the face value $f(i) = i$, so the solution has the form

$$
p_i = \frac{e^{-\lambda i}}{Z(\lambda)}, \qquad
Z(\lambda) = \sum_{i=1}^{6} e^{-\lambda i},
$$

with a single unknown $\lambda$. Writing $x = e^{-\lambda}$ gives
$p_i \propto x^i$: successive faces carry probabilities in a fixed ratio
$x$, a geometric ladder. One knob remains, and one constraint remains to
set it.

### Tuning the knob

The mean $\sum_i i\,p_i$ increases monotonically in $x$, so the equation
"mean $= 4.5$" has exactly one solution.

<Example title="the Brandeis dice, solved">
**$x = 1$:** the ladder is flat, $p_i = \tfrac16$, mean $3.5$. Too low.

**$x = 1.2$:** $Z = \sum_i 1.2^i = 11.916$, giving
$p = (.101, .121, .145, .174, .209, .251)$ and mean
$\sum_i i\,p_i = 4.02$. Still too low.

**Solving numerically** (bisection on $x$): the mean hits $4.5$ at
$x = 1.4493$, i.e. $\lambda = -\ln 1.4493 = -0.371$. Then
$Z(\lambda) = 26.66$ and

$$
p^\ast = (.054,\ .079,\ .114,\ .165,\ .240,\ .347).
$$

**Checks.** Mean: $\sum_i i\,p^\ast_i = 0.054 + 0.158 + 0.343 + 0.662 +
1.199 + 2.085 = 4.50$. Entropy:
$H(p^\ast) = -\sum_i p^\ast_i \log_2 p^\ast_i = 2.328$ bits, below the
unconstrained maximum $\log_2 6 = 2.585$ but above every candidate in the
table: $0.811$, $1.000$, and $2.000$ all fall short.
</Example>

## The general theorem: exponential in the constrained quantities

The dice used one constraint function, $f(i) = i$. The general statement
allows any finite list. Throughout, exponentials are base $e$ and
entropies are reported in bits; changing base rescales $\lambda$ by
$\ln 2$ and nothing else.

<Definition title="feasible set">
Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a finite alphabet, $f_1, \dots, f_m$ real-valued
functions on $\mathcal{X}$, and $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m$ real numbers.
The **feasible set** is the set of pmfs $p$ on $\mathcal{X}$ with
$$
\sum_{x} p(x)\, f_r(x) = \alpha_r, \qquad r = 1, \dots, m .
$$
</Definition>

The claimed maximizer generalizes the dice solution: one multiplier
$\lambda_r$ per constraint, all sitting in one exponent.

<Theorem title="maximum entropy under expectation constraints">
Suppose $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$ can be chosen so that
$$
q(x) = \frac{e^{-\sum_{r} \lambda_r f_r(x)}}{Z(\lambda)},
\qquad
Z(\lambda) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} e^{-\sum_{r} \lambda_r f_r(x)},
$$
lies in the feasible set. Then $q$ uniquely maximizes $H$ over the
feasible set: $H(p) \le H(q)$ for every feasible $p$, with equality only
at $p = q$.

<Proof>
Let $p$ be any feasible pmf. Insert $q$ into the entropy and split the
logarithm:

$$
H(p) = -\sum_x p(x) \log_2 p(x)
     = -\sum_x p(x) \log_2 \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} - \sum_x p(x) \log_2 q(x)
     = -D(p \,\|\, q) - \sum_x p(x) \log_2 q(x).
$$

By the information inequality of <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>,
$D(p\,\|\,q) \ge 0$, so

$$
H(p) \;\le\; -\sum_x p(x) \log_2 q(x).
$$

The right side is where the exponential form pays: $\log_2 q(x) =
\bigl(-\sum_r \lambda_r f_r(x) - \ln Z(\lambda)\bigr)/\ln 2$ is a linear
combination of the constrained functions plus a constant, so

$$
-\sum_x p(x) \log_2 q(x)
= \frac{\sum_r \lambda_r \sum_x p(x) f_r(x) + \ln Z(\lambda)}{\ln 2}
= \frac{\sum_r \lambda_r \alpha_r + \ln Z(\lambda)}{\ln 2},
$$

a number that depends on $p$ only through the constrained expectations
$\alpha_r$, hence the same for every feasible $p$. In particular $q$ is
itself feasible, so this number equals $-\sum_x q(x) \log_2 q(x) = H(q)$.
Therefore $H(p) \le H(q)$, with equality exactly when
$D(p\,\|\,q) = 0$, i.e. $p = q$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof is [Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)'
Theorem 12.1.1, and it is Gibbs' inequality doing the work one more time:
the same $D \ge 0$ that priced wrong-code overhead and floored the coding
cost now certifies a maximizer, with no calculus required. The one
structural fact used is that $\log_2 q$ is **linear in the constraints**,
which pins the bound $-\sum p \log_2 q$ to a single value across the
whole feasible set.

### Tuning the multipliers: the partition function does the bookkeeping

For the dice, one bisection found $\lambda$. In general the *partition
function* $Z(\lambda)$ organizes the tuning: differentiating $\ln Z$ with
respect to $\lambda_r$ brings down $-f_r$ inside the sum, giving

$$
\langle f_r \rangle
= \sum_x q(x) f_r(x)
= -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \lambda_r},
$$

so the $m$ constraint equations $\langle f_r \rangle = \alpha_r$ are the
$m$ equations $-\partial \ln Z / \partial \lambda_r = \alpha_r$ in the
$m$ multipliers ([Jaynes 1957](https://bayes.wustl.edu/etj/articles/theory.1.pdf),
eqs. 2-6 to 2-9). Dice check: at $\lambda = -0.371$,
$\ln Z = \ln 26.66 = 3.283$, and the numerical derivative gives
$-\partial \ln Z/\partial\lambda = 4.500$, the constrained mean.

<Aside>
Higher derivatives of $\ln Z$ generate higher moments:
$\partial^2 \ln Z / \partial \lambda_r^2 = \operatorname{Var}(f_r)$,
which is Jaynes' variance formula and, in the statistical-mechanics
reading below, the heat capacity. The historical derivation of the
exponential form is also worth knowing: introduce *Lagrange multipliers*
$\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$ for the constraints and set the
gradient of $-\sum p \ln p - \lambda_0 \sum p - \sum_r \lambda_r \sum p f_r$
to zero; stationarity in each $p(x)$ gives
$\ln p(x) = -1 - \lambda_0 - \sum_r \lambda_r f_r(x)$, the exponential
form. The multiplier route finds the stationary point; the Gibbs route
above is what proves it is the global maximum, and it needs neither
calculus of variations nor second-order conditions.
</Aside>

<Remark title="when the machinery can fail">
The constraints must be mutually satisfiable over the alphabet: no pmf on
$\{1, \dots, 6\}$ has mean $7$, and an empty feasible set has no
maximizer. Separately, for some constraint sets on infinite alphabets the
supremum of $H$ is not attained by any distribution (Cover & Thomas
§12.3 exhibit such anomalous cases); both situations are out of scope
here.
</Remark>

With no constraints at all ($m = 0$), the theorem's $q$ is
$e^{0}/Z = 1/|\mathcal{X}|$, the uniform distribution: the range theorem
$H \le \log_2 n$ of <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/> is the
constraint-free special case of maximum entropy.

<KeyFact>
The maximum-entropy distribution under expectation constraints is
exponential in the constrained quantities,
$p^\ast(x) = e^{-\sum_r \lambda_r f_r(x)} / Z(\lambda)$, and Gibbs'
inequality proves no feasible distribution beats it: the entropy any
other choice loses is exactly $D(p \,\|\, p^\ast)$.
</KeyFact>

## Boltzmann and softmax: one solution, two vocabularies

The exponential solution predates its information-theoretic derivation by
some eighty years in physics, and postdates it by a few decades in
machine learning. Both are the theorem with one constraint list.

### Physics: the energy constraint

Take $\mathcal{X}$ to be the microstates $i$ of a physical system, each
with energy $E_i$, and impose one constraint: the mean energy is a known
value $U$. The theorem returns

$$
p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z(\beta)}, \qquad
Z(\beta) = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i},
$$

with a single multiplier $\beta$ tuned so
$\langle E \rangle = -\partial \ln Z / \partial \beta = U$. This is the
*Boltzmann distribution*, and physics names the multiplier: $\beta = 1/kT$,
inverse temperature. High temperature means a weak multiplier and a
near-uniform spread over microstates; low temperature concentrates the
distribution on the low-energy states.

[Jaynes' 1957 paper](https://bayes.wustl.edu/etj/articles/theory.1.pdf)
(§3) turns this observation into a program: equilibrium statistical
mechanics *is* maximum-entropy inference with an energy constraint, and
the standard machinery (partition function identities, thermodynamic
derivatives) follows from the inference, not from any dynamical
assumption such as ergodicity. Whether this founds statistical mechanics
is Jaynes' claim, and remains his to defend; what is theorem is that the
energy constraint alone forces the Boltzmann form. The physical price of
these bits is the subject of
<Ref lesson="thermodynamics-of-information"/>.

### Machine learning: the softmax layer

A classifier head turns a vector of logits $z_1, \dots, z_k$ into a
distribution by

$$
p_i = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_{j} e^{z_j / T}},
$$

the *softmax* with temperature $T$ (training uses $T = 1$). Matching
symbols against the theorem: the constraint function is the negative
logit, $f(i) = -z_i$, and the multiplier is $1/T$. Softmax is the
maximum-entropy distribution whose expected logit is pinned; temperature
rescales the multiplier exactly as it does for Boltzmann. The
identification is standard in NLP, where
[Berger, Della Pietra & Della Pietra (1996)](https://aclanthology.org/J96-1002/)
built classifiers by imposing feature-expectation constraints and derived
the exponential (log-linear) model as the unique maximum-entropy
solution; training the weights is tuning the multipliers so the model's
feature expectations match the data's, and the loss minimized is the
cross-entropy of <Ref lesson="cross-entropy-loss"/>.

### Temperature in numbers

<Example title="softmax at two temperatures">
Logits $z = (2.0,\ 1.0,\ 0.1)$.

**$T = 1$:** exponentials $e^{2} = 7.389$, $e^{1} = 2.718$,
$e^{0.1} = 1.105$; normalizer $Z = 11.213$; distribution
$p = (0.659,\ 0.242,\ 0.099)$. Entropy: $H = 1.222$ bits.

**$T = 2$:** exponentials $e^{1} = 2.718$, $e^{0.5} = 1.649$,
$e^{0.05} = 1.051$; normalizer $Z = 5.418$; distribution
$p = (0.502,\ 0.304,\ 0.194)$. Entropy: $H = 1.481$ bits.

Doubling $T$ halves the multiplier: the constraint bites half as hard,
the distribution moves toward uniform, and the entropy climbs toward the
three-outcome ceiling $\log_2 3 = 1.585$ bits. As $T \to \infty$ the
output is uniform regardless of the logits; as $T \to 0$ all mass
collapses onto the largest logit.
</Example>

<KeyFact>
The Boltzmann distribution and the softmax layer are the same
maximum-entropy solution: an expectation constraint on energy in physics
vocabulary, on logits in ML vocabulary, with temperature as the inverse
multiplier in both.
</KeyFact>

## A method of reasoning, and its limits

Jaynes' own assessment
([1957](https://bayes.wustl.edu/etj/articles/theory.1.pdf),
§6) is the right frame: maximum entropy is "a method of reasoning", not a
law of physics. It converts exactly the stated constraints into a
distribution, adding nothing; the output is a description of the state of
knowledge encoded in the constraints, not a claim about long-run
frequencies. Whether such subjective probabilities also match observed
frequencies is a further question Jaynes treats separately (§4) and this
course leaves aside.

Two operational consequences follow. First, the output is only as good as
the constraints fed in: a max-ent die built from the mean alone will be
revised, not patched, when a second moment arrives, by adding the
constraint and re-solving. Second, constraints are never free: adding one
shrinks the feasible set, so the achievable maximum entropy can only
decrease, exactly as the dice dropped from $2.585$ to $2.328$ bits when
the mean was pinned.

### The continuous counterpart, with a caveat

<Remark title="the Gaussian as a maximum-entropy distribution">
Among probability densities on $\mathbb{R}$ with fixed variance
$\sigma^2$, the Gaussian maximizes the *differential entropy*
$h(f) = -\int f \ln f$; the result is property 5 of
[Shannon's 1948 paper](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf),
§20. The solution is again exponential in the constrained quantity: the
constraint is on $x^2$, and $e^{-\lambda x^2}$ normalized is the
Gaussian. The caveat: differential entropy is not the discrete entropy of
this course. It can be negative, and its value changes under a change of
coordinates (Shannon, §20), so the discrete statements proved above do
not transfer silently; the continuous theory is out of this course's
scope.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Maximum entropy turns exactly the stated constraints into a distribution,
and nothing else: its output is only as good as the constraints fed in,
and every added constraint can only lower the achievable entropy.
</KeyFact>

<Exercise>
Apply the theorem with no constraints ($m = 0$) to a distribution over
$n$ outcomes, and state which earlier result this recovers.

<Solution>
With $m = 0$ the exponent is empty: $q(x) = e^{0}/Z = 1/Z$, and
normalization forces $Z = n$, so $q$ is uniform. The theorem then says
$H(p) \le H(q) = \log_2 n$ for every pmf $p$, with equality only at the
uniform distribution: the upper half of the range theorem of the entropy
lesson, re-derived. The proof specializes to
$H(p) = \log_2 n - D(p\,\|\,\text{uniform})$, so the entropy deficit from
the ceiling is exactly a KL divergence.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Solution>
On a two-point alphabet, normalization and the mean pin the distribution
completely: $p(1) = 0.3$, $p(0) = 0.7$. The feasible set is a single
point, so the maximization is trivial and maximum entropy adds nothing.

The exponential form still represents it:
$q(1)/q(0) = e^{-\lambda} = 0.3/0.7 = 3/7$, so
$\lambda = \ln(7/3) \approx 0.847$. Check:
$q(1) = e^{-0.847}/(1 + e^{-0.847}) = 0.429/1.429 = 0.3$. The principle
has content only when the constraints leave freedom; here every feasible
distribution is the solution.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Find the maximum-entropy distribution on the non-negative integers
$\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ subject to mean $2$, and compute its entropy in
bits.

<Solution>
The constrained quantity is $f(k) = k$, so the solution is
$q_k \propto e^{-\lambda k} = \theta^k$ with $\theta = e^{-\lambda} \in
(0,1)$: a geometric distribution $q_k = (1-\theta)\theta^k$.

Tune $\theta$ to the mean: $\sum_k k\,q_k = \theta/(1-\theta) = 2$ gives
$\theta = 2/3$ (and $\lambda = \ln \tfrac{3}{2} \approx 0.405$), so
$q_k = \tfrac13 \left(\tfrac23\right)^k$.

Entropy: $-\log_2 q_k = -\log_2 \tfrac13 - k \log_2 \tfrac23$, so
$$
H = \mathbb{E}[-\log_2 q_K]
= \log_2 3 + \mathbb{E}[K] \log_2 \tfrac32
= 1.585 + 2 \times 0.585 = 2.755 \text{ bits.}
$$
No distribution on $\{0, 1, \dots\}$ with mean $2$ has larger entropy.
(This is Cover & Thomas' example 12.2.4; on a countable alphabet the
existence caveat applies, but here the geometric maximizer exists.)
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A system has two states with energies $E_0 = 0$ and $E_1 = \varepsilon$,
at temperature $T$ with $\varepsilon = kT \ln 3$. Compute the Boltzmann
distribution and its entropy, and give the limiting distributions as
$T \to 0$ and $T \to \infty$ at fixed $\varepsilon$.

<Solution>
With $\beta = 1/kT$, the Boltzmann weights are $e^{-\beta E_0} = 1$ and
$e^{-\beta E_1} = e^{-\varepsilon/kT} = e^{-\ln 3} = \tfrac13$. So
$Z = 1 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac43$ and
$$
p_0 = \frac{1}{4/3} = \frac34, \qquad p_1 = \frac{1/3}{4/3} = \frac14 .
$$
Entropy: $H = -\tfrac34 \log_2 \tfrac34 - \tfrac14 \log_2 \tfrac14 =
0.811$ bits.

$T \to 0$: $\beta \to \infty$, $e^{-\beta\varepsilon} \to 0$, so
$p \to (1, 0)$: all mass on the ground state, $H \to 0$. $T \to \infty$:
$\beta \to 0$, both weights tend to $1$, so $p \to (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$
and $H \to 1$ bit: an infinitely weak multiplier leaves the uniform
maximum-entropy distribution.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Logits $z = (1.5,\ 0.5,\ -0.5)$. Compute the softmax distribution and its
entropy at $T = 1$, then show that adding a constant $c$ to every logit
leaves the softmax unchanged, and connect that invariance to the
partition function.

<Solution>
$T = 1$: exponentials $e^{1.5} = 4.482$, $e^{0.5} = 1.649$,
$e^{-0.5} = 0.607$; $Z = 6.737$;
$p = (0.665,\ 0.245,\ 0.090)$. Entropy:
$H = -(0.665 \log_2 0.665 + 0.245 \log_2 0.245 + 0.090 \log_2 0.090)
= 0.391 + 0.497 + 0.313 = 1.201$ bits.

Shift invariance: replacing $z_i$ by $z_i + c$ multiplies every
exponential by $e^{c/T}$, so
$$
\frac{e^{(z_i + c)/T}}{\sum_j e^{(z_j + c)/T}}
= \frac{e^{c/T}\, e^{z_i/T}}{e^{c/T} \sum_j e^{z_j/T}}
= \frac{e^{z_i/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}} .
$$
The normalizer absorbs the shift: $Z$ is multiplied by $e^{c/T}$ and the
ratio is untouched. In multiplier language, $c$ shifts the constraint
function by a constant, which changes only the bookkeeping term
$\ln Z$, never the distribution; only logit differences carry
information. (These logits are $z = (2.0, 1.0, 0.0)$ shifted by $-0.5$;
the worked example's logits $(2.0, 1.0, 0.1)$ differ in the third entry,
which is why its $p = (0.659, 0.242, 0.099)$ is close but not equal.)
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Show that for any pmf $p$ on $\{1, \dots, 6\}$,
$H(p) = \log_2 6 - D(p \,\|\, u)$ where $u$ is the uniform distribution,
and evaluate $D(p^\ast \|\, u)$ for the Brandeis solution
($H(p^\ast) = 2.328$ bits). Interpret the number.

<Solution>
Expand the divergence against the uniform $u_i = \tfrac16$:
$$
D(p \,\|\, u) = \sum_i p_i \log_2 \frac{p_i}{1/6}
= \sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i + \log_2 6 \sum_i p_i
= \log_2 6 - H(p),
$$
which rearranges to the claim. For the Brandeis solution:
$D(p^\ast \|\, u) = 2.585 - 2.328 = 0.257$ bits.

Interpretation: $0.257$ bits is the entropy the mean-$4.5$ constraint
removed, measured as the coding overhead of the tilted die relative to a
code built for a fair one. The constraint's information content and the
KL divergence from the unconstrained maximizer are the same number, and
the identity also shows that maximizing $H$ over a feasible set is
minimizing $D(p \,\|\, u)$ over it: maximum entropy is minimum divergence
from uniform.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

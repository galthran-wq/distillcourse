Among all distributions consistent with known expectation constraints,
exactly one maximizes entropy, and it has exponential form; choosing any
other distribution assumes information you do not have.

Until now entropy has been a measurement. Surprisal, code length, divergence
and capacity are all readings taken off a distribution somebody handed over,
and even <Ref lesson="kolmogorov-complexity">the shortest-program
length</Ref>, which dispensed with distributions altogether, was reconciled
at the end by showing that its average equals the entropy of one. This
lesson runs the machinery backwards. The distribution is now the unknown, a
handful of average values is all that is given, and entropy stops being a
measurement and becomes an objective function whose maximizer *is* the
answer. That inversion is the organising move of this module, and the
physical price of the bits it selects is the subject of
<Ref lesson="thermodynamics-of-information"/>.

The route: an underdetermined inference problem with a documented history
(Jaynes' Brandeis dice), its solution found numerically, the general theorem
proved in three lines with Gibbs' inequality, the catalogue of families the
theorem generates, two independent checks on the answer, and the two
best-known instances of it.

## One constraint, infinitely many distributions

A die is tossed a great many times and the results average $4.5$, not the
$3.5$ of a fair die. The task: assign probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_6$ to the
faces using this fact and nothing else. This is the *Brandeis dice problem*,
stated in almost these words at the opening of the
[1962 Brandeis summer-school lectures](https://bayes.wustl.edu/etj/articles/brandeis.pdf)
of E. T. Jaynes, the physicist who spent the previous decade arguing that
statistical mechanics is a form of inference. It is not a problem about
dice: it is the smallest case of assigning a distribution when the evidence
is a handful of averages.

<Task>
Before reading on, recall three facts this lesson runs on. (a) The largest
entropy any distribution over six outcomes can have, and which distribution
attains it. (b) What $D(p \,\|\, q) \ge 0$ says about the cost of coding
$p$'s output with a code built for $q$. (c) The condition under which
$D(p \,\|\, q) = 0$. The answers are $\log_2 6 = 2.585$ bits at the uniform
distribution (<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>); the wrong code
never costs less than the right one, and the excess is $D$
(<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>); and equality holds only at $p = q$. That
is the entire toolkit needed below.
</Task>

The evidence supplies two equations for six unknowns:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{6} p_i = 1, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{6} i\, p_i = 4.5 .
$$

Four degrees of freedom remain, so infinitely many probability mass
functions fit the data. Three of the candidates Jaynes draws, in his order
of increasing reasonableness, together with the answer this lesson derives,
and their entropies from
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise">the entropy lesson</Ref>:

| candidate $(p_1, \dots, p_6)$ | how it was chosen | $H$ (bits) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $(0, 0, 0, .5, .5, 0)$ | put the mass on the two faces straddling $4.5$ | $1.000$ |
| $(0, 0, .25, .25, .25, .25)$ | spread it over the faces above the fair mean | $2.000$ |
| $p_n = (12n - 7)/210$ | a straight ramp: equal steps between neighbours | $2.302$ |
| $(.054, .079, .114, .165, .240, .347)$ | the answer derived below | $2.328$ |

Every candidate satisfies both constraints. Every candidate except the last
also **asserts more** than the constraints contain. The first declares four
faces impossible on the strength of a single average. The second still rules
out faces 1 and 2, which the data never excluded. The third assigns every
face positive probability and looks unimpeachable, and it is the one worth
dwelling on, because its plausibility rests on a smoothness criterion that
has no solution at all once the target mean exceeds $4.67$.

### Smoothness is not a criterion

The third candidate is the unique assignment whose successive differences
$p_{n+1} - p_n$ are constant, and smoothness of that kind has a hard
ceiling. A straight ramp through the origin, $p_n = c\,(n-1)$, is the
steepest admissible line, since any steeper one drives $p_1$ negative, and
its mean is

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{6} n \cdot \frac{n-1}{15} = \frac{70}{15} = 4.67 .
$$

So no straight-line assignment reaches a mean of $4.7$. Had the die averaged
$4.7$ instead of $4.5$, the whole family of candidates would have been
empty, while the reasoning that produced it would have been unchanged. A
criterion that runs out of solutions at an arbitrary point in the data range
is not a criterion.

### The principle: keep all the uncertainty the data does not remove

Entropy ranks the candidates by exactly the property at stake. $H(p)$
measures the uncertainty a distribution admits, so the feasible distribution
with the largest $H$ is the one that concedes the most ignorance while
honouring the data. Adopting any lower-entropy candidate narrows the
possibilities further than the constraints justify, and the gap in the table
measures by how much.

<Definition title="maximum entropy principle">
Given constraints on a distribution $p$ (normalization plus known
expectation values), the **maximum entropy principle** adopts
$$
p^\ast = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{p\ \text{satisfying the constraints}} H(p),
$$
the entropy maximizer over the *feasible set* of all distributions
consistent with the constraints.
</Definition>

Geometrically the feasible set is flat. Normalization confines every
candidate to the simplex of probability vectors; each expectation constraint
$\sum_x p(x) f(x) = \alpha$ is linear in $p$, so it cuts that simplex with a
hyperplane. What is left is a convex slice, and entropy is a strictly
concave function on it, which is why the maximizer is a single point rather
than a face.

<Figure
  src="./figures/simplex.svg"
  alt="The triangle of all distributions over three outcomes with entropy level sets drawn as rings around the uniform centre; a straight constraint line cuts the triangle, and the maximum-entropy distribution sits where the highest ring touches that line"
  caption="Constraints select a slice; entropy selects the one point of it that the highest level set reaches."
/>

Jaynes states the claim more narrowly than the principle is usually
reported. The maximum-entropy distribution, in his words,
"is uniquely determined as the one which is maximally
noncommittal with regard to missing information", and it "assigns positive
weight to every situation that is not absolutely excluded by the given
information". Neither sentence claims the distribution is true.

<KeyFact>
The definition is silent about what "assumes least" means in absolute terms.
Entropy measures spread against an implicit reference, the uniform
distribution, which exists because the alphabet is finite; on a continuum
there is no uniform reference and the phrase "maximum entropy" changes
meaning under a change of variable. That failure and its repair arrive when
continuous examples do.
</KeyFact>

## Solving the dice: tilt exponentially, then tune

The maximization runs over a four-parameter set, but the search collapses to
one dimension. The theorem of the next section states that the maximizer is
**exponential** in the constrained quantity. The constrained quantity here
is the face value $f(i) = i$, so the solution has the form

$$
p_i = \frac{e^{-\lambda i}}{Z(\lambda)}, \qquad
Z(\lambda) = \sum_{i=1}^{6} e^{-\lambda i},
$$

with a single unknown $\lambda$. Writing $x = e^{-\lambda}$ gives
$p_i \propto x^i$: successive faces carry probabilities in a fixed ratio
$x$, a geometric ladder. The divisor $Z(\lambda)$, the *partition function*,
is whatever number makes the six values sum to one, so it carries no freedom
of its own. One knob remains, and one constraint remains to set it.

### Tuning the knob

The mean $\sum_i i\,p_i$ increases monotonically in $x$, from $1$ as
$x \to 0$ to $6$ as $x \to \infty$, so the equation "mean $= 4.5$" has
exactly one solution, and so does the same equation for any target strictly
between $1$ and $6$. The linear family's ceiling of $4.67$ has no analogue
here.

<Example title="the Brandeis dice, solved">
**$x = 1$:** the ladder is flat, $p_i = \tfrac16$, mean $3.5$. Too low.

**$x = 1.2$:** $Z = \sum_i 1.2^i = 11.916$, giving
$p = (.101, .121, .145, .174, .209, .251)$ and mean
$\sum_i i\,p_i = 4.02$. Still too low.

**Solving numerically** (bisection on $x$): the mean hits $4.5$ at
$x = 1.4493$, that is $\lambda = -\ln 1.4493 = -0.371$. Then
$Z(\lambda) = 26.664$ and

$$
p^\ast = (.054,\ .079,\ .114,\ .165,\ .240,\ .347).
$$

**Checks.** Mean: $\sum_i i\,p^\ast_i = 0.054 + 0.158 + 0.343 + 0.662 +
1.199 + 2.085 = 4.50$. Entropy:
$H(p^\ast) = -\sum_i p^\ast_i \log_2 p^\ast_i = 2.328$ bits, below the
unconstrained ceiling $\log_2 6 = 2.585$ and above every candidate in the
table.
</Example>

## The general theorem: exponential in the constrained quantities

The dice used one constraint function, $f(i) = i$. The general statement
allows any finite list of them, and the proof is no harder for $m$
constraints than for one. Throughout, exponentials are base $e$ and
entropies are reported in bits; changing base rescales every $\lambda$ by
$\ln 2$ and nothing else.

<Definition title="feasible set">
Let $\mathcal{X}$ be a finite alphabet, $f_1, \dots, f_m$ real-valued
functions on $\mathcal{X}$, and $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m$ real numbers.
The **feasible set** is the set of pmfs $p$ on $\mathcal{X}$ with
$$
\sum_{x} p(x)\, f_r(x) = \alpha_r, \qquad r = 1, \dots, m .
$$
</Definition>

The claimed maximizer generalizes the dice solution: one multiplier
$\lambda_r$ per constraint function, all sitting in one exponent, and one
partition function normalizing the result.

<Theorem title="maximum entropy under expectation constraints (Cover & Thomas 12.1.1)">
Suppose $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$ can be chosen so that
$$
q(x) = \frac{e^{-\sum_{r} \lambda_r f_r(x)}}{Z(\lambda)},
\qquad
Z(\lambda) = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} e^{-\sum_{r} \lambda_r f_r(x)},
$$
lies in the feasible set. Then $q$ uniquely maximizes $H$ over the feasible
set: $H(p) \le H(q)$ for every feasible $p$, with equality only at $p = q$.

<Proof>
Direct, in three moves: split the entropy against $q$, drop the divergence
term, then show what is left is the same number for every feasible $p$.

**Split.** Let $p$ be any feasible pmf. Insert $q$ into the entropy and
split the logarithm:

$$
H(p) = -\sum_x p(x) \log_2 p(x)
     = -\sum_x p(x) \log_2 \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} - \sum_x p(x) \log_2 q(x)
     = -D(p \,\|\, q) - \sum_x p(x) \log_2 q(x).
$$

**Drop.** By the information inequality of <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>,
$D(p\,\|\,q) \ge 0$, so

$$
H(p) \;\le\; -\sum_x p(x) \log_2 q(x).
$$

**Evaluate.** Taking the logarithm of $q$ turns its exponent into a sum,
$\log_2 q(x) = \bigl(-\sum_r \lambda_r f_r(x) - \ln Z(\lambda)\bigr)/\ln 2$,
which is a linear combination of the constrained functions plus a constant.
Hence

$$
-\sum_x p(x) \log_2 q(x)
= \frac{\sum_r \lambda_r \sum_x p(x) f_r(x) + \ln Z(\lambda)}{\ln 2}
= \frac{\sum_r \lambda_r \alpha_r + \ln Z(\lambda)}{\ln 2},
$$

a number that depends on $p$ only through the constrained expectations
$\alpha_r$, hence the same for every feasible $p$. The distribution $q$ is
itself feasible by hypothesis, so evaluating at $p = q$ identifies that
number as $-\sum_x q(x) \log_2 q(x) = H(q)$. Therefore $H(p) \le H(q)$, with
equality exactly when $D(p\,\|\,q) = 0$, that is when $p = q$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Calculus locates stationary points and then owes a second-order argument;
the divergence term is non-negative with a known equality case, so one
inequality delivers optimality and uniqueness together. The one structural
fact used is that $\log_2 q$ is **linear in the
constrained functions**, which pins the bound $-\sum p \log_2 q$ to a single
value across the whole feasible set. Any other functional form would leave
that bound varying with $p$, and the argument would collapse.

### Tuning the multipliers: the constraint equations are derivatives of $\ln Z$

For the dice, one bisection found $\lambda$. In general the partition
function organizes the tuning. Differentiating $\ln Z$ with respect to
$\lambda_r$ brings down a factor of $-f_r$ inside the sum and divides by
$Z$, which is exactly an expectation under $q$:

$$
\langle f_r \rangle
= \sum_x q(x) f_r(x)
= -\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \lambda_r},
$$

so the $m$ constraint equations $\langle f_r \rangle = \alpha_r$ become $m$
equations $-\partial \ln Z / \partial \lambda_r = \alpha_r$ in the $m$
unknown multipliers. For $m > 1$ these are solved numerically; there is no
closed form in general, and the multipliers are not free parameters that may
be set to taste. Dice check: at $\lambda = -0.371$, $\ln Z = \ln 26.664 =
3.2833$, and the central difference
$-[\ln Z(\lambda + 0.01) - \ln Z(\lambda - 0.01)]/0.02 = 4.4999$ recovers
the constrained mean to four figures.

<Remark title="when the machinery has nothing to return">
The theorem needs two hypotheses. First, the constraints must be mutually
satisfiable over the alphabet: no pmf on $\{1, \dots, 6\}$ has mean $7$, and
an empty feasible set has no maximizer. Second, some $\lambda$ must actually
place $q$ inside the feasible set. On a finite alphabet this holds whenever
the target lies strictly inside the range of achievable values, and fails at
the endpoints, where the maximizer is a point mass that no finite $\lambda$
produces. On infinite alphabets the failure can be worse: for some
constraint sets the supremum of $H$ is finite but attained by no
distribution at all (Cover & Thomas §12.3 constructs one).
</Remark>

With no constraints at all ($m = 0$) the theorem's $q$ is
$e^{0}/Z = 1/|\mathcal{X}|$, the uniform distribution, and the conclusion
$H(p) \le \log_2 n$ is the range theorem of
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise">the entropy lesson</Ref>, which
bounded entropy above by $\log_2 n$ with equality only at the uniform
distribution. That theorem is the constraint-free special case of this one.

<KeyFact>
The proof prices the alternatives as well as ranking them: for any feasible
$p$, $H(p) = H(p^\ast) - D(p \,\|\, p^\ast)$, so the entropy a wrong choice
gives up is exactly the divergence that <Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/> showed
is the price of coding with the wrong model. Choosing badly here and coding
badly there are the same arithmetic.
</KeyFact>

## Which constraint picks which family

The theorem was proved for an arbitrary list of constraint functions, and
each list returns a distribution that already has a name. The exponent is
always linear in what is constrained, so the constrained functions decide
the family and their values decide which member of it; the table below is
five applications of the one theorem, not five separate results.

<Figure
  src="./figures/families.svg"
  alt="A five-row table pairing what is constrained with the resulting maximum-entropy family and a sketch of its shape: nothing gives the uniform, a mean gives the geometric on the non-negative integers or the exponential on the half-line, a mean and variance give the Gaussian, and a constrained mean of log x gives a power law"
  caption="Five applications of one theorem: the exponent is linear in whatever is constrained, and the support decides the rest."

<Example title="mean 2 on the non-negative integers">
Constrain $f(k) = k$ on $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$ with $\langle k \rangle = 2$.
The solution is $q_k \propto e^{-\lambda k} = \theta^k$ with
$\theta = e^{-\lambda}$, and normalization needs $\theta < 1$, giving the
geometric distribution $q_k = (1-\theta)\theta^k$.

Tune $\theta$ to the mean: $\sum_k k\,q_k = \theta/(1-\theta) = 2$ gives
$\theta = 2/3$, so $\lambda = \ln\tfrac32 = 0.405$ and
$q_k = \tfrac13 (\tfrac23)^k$.

Entropy, computed from the surprisal directly:
$-\log_2 q_k = \log_2 3 + k \log_2 \tfrac32$, so
$$
H = \mathbb{E}[-\log_2 q_K]
= \log_2 3 + \langle k \rangle \log_2 \tfrac32
= 1.585 + 2 \times 0.585 = 2.755 \text{ bits},
$$
and no distribution on $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ with mean $2$ has larger entropy.
The alphabet is infinite, so the existence caveat applies, but here the
maximizer exists and the entropy is finite.
</Example>

The continuous rows of the table are the ones that need care, and the care
is not about the algebra. On $\mathbb{R}$ the object being maximized is the
*differential entropy* $h(f) = -\int f \ln f$, not the entropy of this
course. Under a variance constraint the maximizer is the Gaussian, proved in
[Shannon's §20 as property 5](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf)
and again by the Gibbs route in the exercises below; under a mean constraint
on $[0,\infty)$ it is the
exponential density $f(x) = \tfrac1\mu e^{-x/\mu}$; under a mean constraint
on all of $\mathbb{R}$ there is no maximizer, because Gaussians of growing
variance push $h$ up without bound while keeping the mean fixed.

<Remark title="on a continuum, maximum entropy is minimum divergence">
Differential entropy is not invariant under a change of variable. Substitute
$y = g(x)$ and $h$ changes by $\mathbb{E}[\ln |g'(x)|]$, so a density that
maximizes $h$ in the coordinate $x$ generally does not maximize $h$ in the
coordinate $\log x$ or $1/x$. "The maximum-entropy density" is therefore
underdetermined until a coordinate is named, and naming a coordinate is
smuggling in exactly the kind of assumption the principle was meant to
avoid.

The invariant repair is to state a reference density $m$ and minimize
$D(f \,\|\, m)$ over the feasible set instead. That quantity does not change
under a change of variable, because the Jacobian cancels between $f$ and
$m$. On a finite alphabet with $m$ uniform the two statements coincide:
$H(p) = \log_2 n - D(p \,\|\, u)$, so maximizing $H$ and minimizing
$D(p \,\|\, u)$ are the same instruction. The finite case hides the choice
of $m$ rather than avoiding it, and every continuous application of maximum
entropy in the literature has a reference measure in it, stated or not.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Changing $\alpha_r$ moves along a family; changing $f_r$ changes the family.
Fitting a max-entropy model to data is therefore two decisions, not one, and
only the second is a modelling choice with content: which functions to
constrain.
</KeyFact>

## The maximum-entropy distribution is also the most numerous

Everything so far has justified $p^\ast$ by what it refrains from assuming,
which is an argument about the state of knowledge and not about dice.
There is a second, entirely different justification, and it was Boltzmann's:
under one added assumption the entropy maximizer is the composition that the
most arrangements produce.

Throw $n$ dice at once and record only how many show each face, a vector
$(n_1, \dots, n_6)$ summing to $n$. Call that the *macrostate*; the full
record of which die showed what is the *microstate*. The number of
microstates realizing a given macrostate is the multinomial coefficient
$\binom{n}{n_1,\dots,n_6}$, and Stirling's approximation turns its logarithm
into $n$ times an entropy:

$$
\frac{1}{n}\log_2 \binom{n}{n_1, \dots, n_6}
\;\longrightarrow\; H\!\left(\frac{n_1}{n}, \dots, \frac{n_6}{n}\right).
$$

Maximizing the count subject to $\sum_i i\,n_i = n\alpha$ is therefore, up
to the approximation, maximizing $H$ subject to $\sum_i i\,p_i = \alpha$,
which is the problem already solved. The most numerous macrostate is
$(n p_1^\ast, \dots, n p_6^\ast)$.

Sixty dice showing $270$ spots in total, the finite version of the Brandeis
constraint, admit $28{,}862$ macrostates. Exact enumeration picks out
$(3, 5, 7, 10, 14, 21)$ as the one realized by the most arrangements, and
$60\,p^\ast = (3.26, 4.73, 6.85, 9.93, 14.39, 20.85)$ rounds to precisely
those six integers. Two hand-picked compositions meeting the same
270-spot total — an even split between 4s and 5s, $(0,0,0,30,30,0)$, which
is the opening table's first candidate scaled up, and fifteen 3s with
forty-five 5s, $(0,0,15,0,45,0)$ — are realized in $10^{17.1}$ and
$10^{13.7}$ arrangements against $10^{38.2}$: a shortfall of twenty-one and
twenty-four decades on a problem with only sixty dice. The drop is that
steep only away from $p^\ast$. Compositions adjacent to the modal one are
almost as numerous — $(3,5,6,11,15,20)$ reaches $10^{38.10}$, five
hundredths of a decade below the maximum — so the arrangements pile up on a
neighbourhood of $p^\ast$ rather than on a single vector.

The counting statement is stronger than "most probable". As $n$ grows, the
macrostates within any fixed distance of $p^\ast$ carry not merely the
largest share of the probability but a share approaching one, conditional on
the constraint holding (Cover & Thomas §12.2 states this, and proves it in
ch. 11 by the same typicality machinery that
<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite">the source coding theorem</Ref>
runs on). At $n = 60$ the concentration is already visible in the ratios
above; at $n = 10^{23}$ it is absolute.

<KeyFact>
The counting argument buys its conclusion with an assumption the inference
argument never makes: that all microstates are equally likely. Where that
holds, $p^\ast$ is a statement about what the world does; where it does not,
$p^\ast$ is only a statement about what the evidence supports. Physics
usually asserts the first and gets the second thrown in.
</KeyFact>

## A prediction that can be checked: the exponential atmosphere

Both justifications so far are internal. The principle also makes a
prediction about a measurable profile, and the prediction is sharp enough to
be wrong.

Take the height $h$ of an air molecule above the ground as the random
variable, and take as the single constraint the mean potential energy
$\langle mgh \rangle$, which is fixed once the gas has a temperature. The
constrained function is $f(h) = mgh$ on the support $[0, \infty)$, so the
maximum-entropy density is exponential in height,

$$
f(h) = \frac{1}{H_s} e^{-h/H_s},
\qquad
H_s = \frac{kT}{mg},
$$

with the multiplier $\lambda = 1/kT$ fixed by the temperature, and $H_s$ the
scale height, the altitude over which density falls by a factor of $e$. For
air at $T = 250$ K, a representative temperature for the first 30 km, the
mean molecular mass gives $H_s = 7.3$ km. Nothing here is fitted to the
atmosphere: one thermometer reading and two constants produce the number.

  caption="The line is the prediction, the points are the standard atmosphere; the bow between 5 and 15 km is the single-temperature assumption failing."

Measured against the U.S. Standard Atmosphere, a mid-latitude annual-mean
profile assembled from rocket and satellite soundings, air density falls
from $1.225$ to $0.0184$ kg/m³ between sea level and 30 km, a factor of
$66$. The unfitted exponential reproduces that fall to within a quarter
everywhere: it is $24\%$ low at 10 km, exact at 25 km, $10\%$ high at 30 km.

The residual is a systematic bow rather than scatter, and its source is
identifiable. The model assumed one temperature, and the real atmosphere
runs from 288 K at the surface to 217 K in the lower stratosphere. Layer by
layer the observed density falls by one factor of $e$ per $9.2$ km over
0–10 km, per $6.5$ km over 10–20 km, and per $6.3$ km over 20–30 km, while a
single $\lambda$ can only produce one rate.

<KeyFact>
The prediction under test is not that the atmosphere maximizes entropy. It
is that a system with a fixed mean potential energy and nothing else pinned
has an exponential height profile, so agreement is evidence that the
constraint list is complete, and the informative part of the plot is the
disagreement below 10 km, where temperature varies most.
</KeyFact>

## Boltzmann and softmax: one solution, two vocabularies

The exponential solution predates its information-theoretic derivation by
some eighty years in physics and postdates it by a few decades in machine
learning. Both are this theorem with one constraint.

### Physics: the energy constraint

Take $\mathcal{X}$ to be the microstates $i$ of a physical system, each with
energy $E_i$, and impose one constraint: the mean energy is a known value
$U$. The theorem returns

$$
p_i = \frac{e^{-\beta E_i}}{Z(\beta)}, \qquad
Z(\beta) = \sum_i e^{-\beta E_i},
$$

with a single multiplier $\beta$ tuned so
$\langle E \rangle = -\partial \ln Z / \partial \beta = U$. This is the
*Boltzmann distribution*, and physics names the multiplier: $\beta = 1/kT$,
inverse temperature, where $k = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K is Boltzmann's
constant. High temperature means a weak multiplier and a near-uniform spread
over microstates; low temperature concentrates the distribution on the
low-energy states.

Jaynes' 1957 paper turns the observation into a programme. Equilibrium
statistical mechanics, on his account, *is* maximum-entropy inference under
an energy constraint, and the standard machinery follows from the inference
rather than from any dynamical assumption such as ergodicity: identify
$\lambda_1$ with $1/kT$ and the free energy with $-kT \ln Z$, and the
thermodynamic relations drop out of the partition-function identities
already proved. Whether that founds statistical mechanics remains his claim
to defend. What is theorem is narrower and not in dispute: an energy
constraint alone forces the Boltzmann form.

### Machine learning: the softmax layer

A classifier head turns a vector of logits $z_1, \dots, z_k$ into a
distribution by

$$
p_i = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_{j} e^{z_j / T}},
$$

the *softmax* with temperature $T$, which is set to $1$ during training and
varied at inference. Matching symbols against the theorem: the constraint
function is the negative logit, $f(i) = -z_i$, and the multiplier is $1/T$.
Softmax is the maximum-entropy distribution whose expected logit is pinned,
and temperature rescales the multiplier exactly as it does for Boltzmann.

The identification is algebraic. Softmax entered neural networks as a

obtained the log-linear model as the unique maximum-entropy solution, so
that training the weights *is* tuning the multipliers.

### Temperature in numbers

<Example title="softmax at two temperatures">
Logits $z = (2.0,\ 1.0,\ 0.1)$.

**$T = 1$:** exponentials $e^{2} = 7.389$, $e^{1} = 2.718$,
$e^{0.1} = 1.105$; normalizer $Z = 11.213$; distribution
$p = (0.659,\ 0.242,\ 0.099)$. Entropy: $H = 1.222$ bits.

**$T = 2$:** exponentials $e^{1} = 2.718$, $e^{0.5} = 1.649$,
$e^{0.05} = 1.051$; normalizer $Z = 5.418$; distribution
$p = (0.502,\ 0.304,\ 0.194)$. Entropy: $H = 1.481$ bits.

Doubling $T$ halves the multiplier, so the constraint bites half as hard,
the distribution moves toward uniform, and the entropy climbs toward the
three-outcome ceiling $\log_2 3 = 1.585$ bits. As $T \to \infty$ the output
is uniform regardless of the logits; as $T \to 0$ all mass collapses onto
the largest logit.
</Example>

<KeyFact>
Temperature scaling at inference, the standard trick for calibrating a
classifier's confidence, is a change of multiplier and nothing else. It
moves the output's entropy along the same curve the die's $\lambda$ traced,
and it cannot change which logit is largest, so it alters confidence without
altering any prediction.
</KeyFact>

## ★ Maximum entropy is maximum likelihood in the exponential family

Two procedures that sound unrelated turn out to have the same fixed point,
and the identity explains why max-entropy models are fitted with ordinary
likelihood code.

Fix the constraint functions $f_1, \dots, f_m$ and consider the parametric
family $P(x \mid w) = \tfrac{1}{Z(w)}\exp\bigl(\sum_r w_r f_r(x)\bigr)$,
with the weights $w$ unknown. Given data $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}$, setting
the derivative of the log likelihood with respect to $w_r$ to zero gives the
stationarity condition

$$
\sum_x P(x \mid w_{\text{ML}}) f_r(x) = \frac1N \sum_n f_r\bigl(x^{(n)}\bigr),
$$

that is: each function's average under the fitted model equals its average
in the data. But that is precisely the constraint the maximum-entropy
problem imposes, with $\alpha_r$ set to the empirical average. Maximum
likelihood in the exponential family and maximum entropy under matched
empirical constraints are two descriptions of one set of equations, solved
for the same $w = -\lambda$.

The consequence is practical. Nobody implements a maximum-entropy fit by
maximizing entropy; they run gradient ascent on a log likelihood, which is
gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss of
<Ref lesson="cross-entropy-loss"/>, and read off the multipliers at
convergence.

<Aside>
The historical derivation of the exponential form, for completeness.
Introduce Lagrange multipliers $\lambda_0, \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_m$ for
normalization and the $m$ constraints, and set the gradient of
$-\sum_x p \ln p - \lambda_0 \sum_x p - \sum_r \lambda_r \sum_x p f_r$ to
zero. Stationarity in each $p(x)$ separately gives
$\ln p(x) = -1 - \lambda_0 - \sum_r \lambda_r f_r(x)$, which is the
exponential form, with $\lambda_0$ absorbed into $\ln Z$. This is the route
Jaynes takes in §2 of the 1957 paper and Cover & Thomas call "Approach 1".
It finds a stationary point and owes a second-variation argument that the
Gibbs route never incurs.

Higher derivatives of $\ln Z$ generate higher moments:
$\partial^2 \ln Z / \partial \lambda_r^2 = \operatorname{Var}(f_r)$, which
for the Brandeis die evaluates to $2.298$ and in the statistical-mechanics
reading is the heat capacity.
</Aside>

## A method of reasoning, and its limits

The principle has now been justified three ways: it assumes least, it counts
most, and in one case it predicts a measured profile. None of those makes it
a law, and its author was explicit that it is not one.

Jaynes' position is that maximum entropy is a rule of inference whose output
is a description of a state of knowledge, and that its relation to physics
is empirical rather than definitional. In the Brandeis lectures he insists
that information entropy and experimental thermodynamic entropy "are
entirely different concepts", and that the job is "to deduce whatever
relations we can from known mathematical and physical facts" rather than to
postulate any. The 1957 sequel states the falsification condition directly:
maximum-entropy inference "can never lead us astray, for any quantity which
it is incapable of predicting will betray that fact by yielding a broad
probability distribution", and if the class of phenomena it predicts ever
differs from the class of experimentally reproducible ones, "that fact would
demonstrate the existence of new laws of physics, not presently known". A
disagreement is a report about the constraint list, not a refutation of the
arithmetic. That is what makes the atmosphere a confirmation and not a
proof.

### The standing objection

The principle is not uncontested where it is used as a general inference
rule. MacKay, raising this exact die problem with a mean of $2.5$, rejects
it: maximum entropy "can give silly answers", the correct way to solve an
inference problem is Bayes' theorem, and he does not advocate it for
assigning priors either. The disagreement is real and it is narrow. Nobody
disputes the theorem or any of the arithmetic above. What is disputed is
whether
"the constraints are all I know" is ever an honest description of a state of
knowledge, given that the mean of a die was itself estimated from data that
a likelihood could have used in full.

Two operational consequences hold regardless of which side of that argument
a reader lands on. First, the output is only as good as the constraint list:
a max-entropy die built from the mean alone is revised, not patched, when a
second moment arrives, by adding the constraint and re-solving. Second,
constraints are never free. Adding one shrinks the feasible set, so the
achievable maximum entropy can only fall, exactly as the die dropped from
$2.585$ to $2.328$ bits when the mean was pinned.

<KeyFact>
Maximum entropy converts a constraint list into a distribution and does
nothing else. It cannot detect that a constraint is missing; only a
comparison against data can, and the signature is a systematic residual of
the kind the atmosphere shows below 10 km.
</KeyFact>

What is established: among the distributions consistent with given
expectation constraints there is exactly one entropy maximizer; it is
exponential in the constrained quantities, normalized by a partition
function whose logarithmic derivatives return the constrained averages; and
every other feasible distribution falls short by exactly its divergence from
that maximizer. What is not established is any price for the choice. The
Boltzmann distribution arrived here as an inference and its multiplier was
identified with $1/kT$ by matching formulas, which leaves open whether the
bits so counted have physical consequences.
<Ref lesson="thermodynamics-of-information">The next lesson</Ref> settles
that by erasing one bit and measuring the heat.

**Re-tuning fails.** The exponent is linear in whatever is constrained, so
with $f_1(i) = i$ and $f_2(i) = i^2$ the solution is

its mean is pinned at $4.5$ that case forces $\langle i^2 \rangle = 22.55$.
The second measurement is precisely the information a one-knob family cannot
carry.

**Bisection fails.** The two constraint equations are coupled: changing
$\lambda_2$ moves the mean as well as the second moment, so neither equation
is a monotone function of one unknown on its own. What replaces bisection is
Newton's method on the pair, with Jacobian
$\partial \langle f_r \rangle / \partial \lambda_s = -\operatorname{Cov}_q(f_r, f_s)$,
the matrix version of the
$\partial^2 \ln Z/\partial \lambda^2 = \operatorname{Var}(f)$ identity. That
matrix is also why the $m$-dimensional solve is well behaved: a covariance
matrix is positive definite, so $\ln Z$ is convex in $\lambda$ and the root
is unique.

**The solve.** Start from the one-constraint answer, $\lambda_1 = -0.371$ and
$\lambda_2 = 0$, where the mean is already right and the second moment is
short by $1.45$. The covariance matrix of $(i, i^2)$ there is
$$
\operatorname{Cov} =
\begin{pmatrix} 2.30 & 17.92 \\ 17.92 & 144.71 \end{pmatrix},

and four Newton steps converge to
$$
\lambda_1 = 1.688, \qquad \lambda_2 = -0.274, \qquad Z = 1.495,

$$
Checks: mean $0.163 + 0.137 + 0.150 + 0.252 + 0.687 + 3.112 = 4.50$; second
moment $0.163 + 0.274 + 0.449 + 1.007 + 3.433 + 18.675 = 24.0$. Entropy

**The cost, and the change.** Entropy falls from $2.328$ bits under the mean
alone to $2.042$, so the second constraint costs $0.285$ bits, and the two
together sit $0.543$ bits below the uniform ceiling — which is
$D(p^\ast \| u)$. The shape changed as well: $\lambda_2 < 0$ makes the
exponent convex in $i$, so the distribution is U-shaped, $.519$ on face 6 and
$.163$ on face 1 with a trough of $.050$ at face 3. No monotone ladder
produces that: adding a constraint function moved the answer to a different
family, not to another member of the same one.

**Where it would break.** At mean $4.5$ the achievable second moments run

distribution, which has zero-probability faces and so is reached by no finite

The transferable move: one multiplier per constraint, and the dimension of
the root-find is the number of constraints, not the size of the alphabet.

**The tilt runs away.** Numerically: $\mu = 5.5$ gives $x = 2.965$ and

it is not delivered by the theorem: it is the limit of the theorem's answers
rather than one of them.

<Exercise>
Break this claim, then rescue as much of it as survives. "The
maximum-entropy distribution is the most likely one." State precisely the

<Solution>
**The two claims.** The first is about a distribution: "$p^\ast$ is the
distribution most likely to be the true one." The second is about
compositions: "among the face counts consistent with the constraint, the one
closest to $n p^\ast$ is realized by the most microstates." The claim as
stated is the first; what the mathematics delivers is the second.

**Why the first is false.** "Most likely to be the true one" requires a
probability distribution over candidate distributions, that is a prior, and
maximum entropy supplies none. Two constraint lists that describe the same
state of knowledge but name different functions produce different $p^\ast$,
which no likelihood ranking could do. And in the Brandeis problem the die
either is or is not loaded in a particular way; the evidence given is
compatible with every candidate in the opening table, and nothing in the
derivation makes one of them a better estimate of the physical die's bias.

**The sense in which it is nearly true.** With $n = 60$ dice and 270 spots,
the entropy-maximizing counts $(3,5,7,10,14,21)$ are realized in $10^{38.2}$

arrangements are equally likely, the maximum-entropy macrostate is the modal
one, outnumbering that composition by twenty-one orders of magnitude (though
only by a factor of $1.1$ against its immediate neighbours), and as $n$ grows the
macrostates near $p^\ast$ carry a conditional probability tending to $1$.
For a large system, "nearly all arrangements look like $p^\ast$" is an
accurate statement.

**The assumption, and where it fails.** The rescue needs all microstates to
be equally likely. That is a real physical hypothesis, not a triviality, and
it is exactly what the counting argument assumes and the inference argument
does not. It is obviously false for a physically loaded die, whose faces are
not exchangeable, and equally false for English text: the letter counts of a
page of English are nowhere near the entropy-maximizing composition, because
the strings are not drawn uniformly from those with the given counts
(<Ref lesson="entropy-of-english"/>). Where microstates are not
equiprobable, $p^\ast$ remains the honest summary of the evidence and stops
being a prediction about frequencies.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The lesson stated the Gaussian result and did not prove it. Prove it by the
Gibbs route rather than by variational calculus: among densities on
$\mathbb{R}$ with $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \sigma^2$, show
that the Gaussian $\varphi$ of variance $\sigma^2$ maximizes the
differential entropy $h(f) = -\int f \ln f$. Then state what the maximizer
becomes if the support is restricted to $[0, \infty)$ with only the mean
constrained, and why the two answers differ.

<Solution>
**Setup.** Let $g$ be any density on $\mathbb{R}$ with the two moments
above, and let
$\varphi(x) = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2} e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}$. The relative
entropy between densities is
$D(g\,\|\,\varphi) = \int g \ln (g/\varphi) \ge 0$, with equality only if
$g = \varphi$ almost everywhere; this is the same inequality as in the
discrete case.

**Split and drop.** Exactly as in the theorem,
$$
h(g) = -\int g \ln g = -D(g \,\|\, \varphi) - \int g \ln \varphi
\;\le\; -\int g \ln \varphi .
$$

**Evaluate.** The point of the Gaussian is that $\ln \varphi$ is a
polynomial in the constrained quantities:
$\ln \varphi(x) = -\tfrac12 \ln(2\pi\sigma^2) - x^2/2\sigma^2$. Therefore
$$
-\int g \ln \varphi
= \tfrac12 \ln(2\pi\sigma^2) + \frac{\mathbb{E}_g[X^2]}{2\sigma^2}
= \tfrac12 \ln(2\pi\sigma^2) + \tfrac12
= \tfrac12 \ln(2\pi e \sigma^2),
$$
using $\mathbb{E}_g[X^2] = \sigma^2$, which is where the constraint enters.
The right-hand side does not depend on $g$, and substituting $g = \varphi$
shows it equals $h(\varphi)$. Hence $h(g) \le h(\varphi)$, with equality
only at $g = \varphi$. Numerically $h(\varphi) = \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e
\sigma^2) = 2.047$ bits at $\sigma = 1$.

**On the half-line.** With support $[0,\infty)$ and only
$\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu$ constrained, the exponent must be linear in $x$, and
the maximizer is $f(x) = \tfrac1\mu e^{-x/\mu}$. The same three moves
work verbatim with $\ln f$ linear rather than quadratic.

**Why they differ.** The family is set by which function is constrained and
on what support, not by any general preference for bell shapes. A quadratic
constraint gives a quadratic exponent and a Gaussian; a linear constraint
gives a linear exponent and an exponential density; and on $[0,\infty)$ a
zero-mean Gaussian is not even admissible. Note also that both statements
are about differential entropy and are therefore coordinate-dependent: they
say the maximizer in the coordinate $x$, with Lebesgue measure as the
reference.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Prove the identity used but not derived in the text: for the exponential
family $q(x) = e^{-\sum_r \lambda_r f_r(x)}/Z(\lambda)$,
$$
-\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \lambda_r} = \mathbb{E}_q[f_r].
$$
Then verify it numerically on the Brandeis die at $\lambda = -0.371$ using
$\ln Z(-0.361) = 3.2384$ and $\ln Z(-0.381) = 3.3284$, and say what the
second derivative $\partial^2 \ln Z/\partial\lambda_r^2$ computes.

<Solution>
**Derivation.** Write $S(x) = \sum_r \lambda_r f_r(x)$, so
$Z = \sum_x e^{-S(x)}$. Differentiating term by term,
$$
\frac{\partial Z}{\partial \lambda_r}
= \sum_x \frac{\partial}{\partial \lambda_r} e^{-S(x)}
= -\sum_x f_r(x)\, e^{-S(x)} .
$$
Divide by $Z$, which is the chain rule for the logarithm:
$$
\frac{\partial \ln Z}{\partial \lambda_r}
= \frac{1}{Z}\frac{\partial Z}{\partial \lambda_r}
= -\sum_x f_r(x) \frac{e^{-S(x)}}{Z}
= -\sum_x f_r(x) q(x)
= -\mathbb{E}_q[f_r].
$$
The whole content is that dividing by $Z$ converts the weights $e^{-S(x)}$
into the probabilities $q(x)$.

**Numerical check.** The central difference at spacing $h = 0.01$ is
$$
-\frac{\ln Z(-0.361) - \ln Z(-0.381)}{0.02}
= -\frac{3.2384 - 3.3284}{0.02} = 4.500 ,
$$
which is the constrained mean, as required.

**The second derivative.** Differentiating once more gives
$\partial^2 \ln Z/\partial \lambda_r^2 = \mathbb{E}_q[f_r^2] -
\mathbb{E}_q[f_r]^2 = \operatorname{Var}_q(f_r)$. For the Brandeis die,
$\mathbb{E}[i^2] = 22.548$ and $\mathbb{E}[i]^2 = 20.25$, so the variance is
$2.298$. Because a variance is non-negative, $\ln Z$ is convex in every
$\lambda_r$, which is the reason the constraint equation
$-\partial \ln Z/\partial \lambda_r = \alpha_r$ has at most one solution: a
convex function has a monotone derivative. That is the structural fact
behind the "tune one knob" step of the dice calculation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Show that $H(p) = \log_2 n - D(p \,\|\, u)$ for any pmf $p$ on an alphabet
of size $n$, where $u$ is uniform. Evaluate $D(p^\ast \| u)$ for the
Brandeis solution and interpret the number in bits-per-toss terms. Then say
what the identity implies about which of the two phrases, "maximum entropy"
and "minimum divergence from uniform", is the more portable instruction.

<Solution>
**The identity.** Expand the divergence against $u_i = 1/n$:
$$
D(p \,\|\, u) = \sum_i p_i \log_2 \frac{p_i}{1/n}
= \sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i + \log_2 n \sum_i p_i
= \log_2 n - H(p),
$$
using $\sum_i p_i = 1$ in the last step. Rearranging gives the claim.

**The number.** For the Brandeis solution,
$D(p^\ast \| u) = 2.585 - 2.328 = 0.257$ bits. Read as a coding cost
(<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>): a code built for a fair die, applied to
this loaded one, wastes $0.257$ bits per toss. Equivalently, the mean
constraint carries $0.257$ bits of information about the die, since that is
the entropy it removed.

**Which phrase is portable.** "Minimum divergence from uniform" is, because
it names its reference. The identity shows the two instructions coincide
whenever a uniform distribution exists, which is to say on a finite
alphabet. On a continuum no uniform density exists, "maximum entropy" is
coordinate-dependent, and only the divergence form survives, with $u$
replaced by a reference density that must be stated. The transferable move:
whenever an objective is written as an entropy, look for the measure it is
an entropy with respect to.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Fit a maximum-entropy model to real data. Take any numeric column of at
least 500 values you have to hand (file sizes, response times, a sensor log,
one column of a public dataset), impose the two constraints
$\langle x \rangle$ and $\langle x^2 \rangle$ at their empirical values,
report the fitted multipliers $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$, and overlay the fitted
density on a histogram of the data. State in advance what agreement would
look like, and what shape of discrepancy would tell you a constraint is
missing rather than that maximum entropy is the wrong tool.

<Solution>
**What the fit is.** Two constraints with $f_1(x) = x$ and $f_2(x) = x^2$
give an exponent quadratic in $x$, so the family is Gaussian and no
numerical root-finding is needed: matching the two moments gives
$\hat\mu = \langle x \rangle$ and $\hat\sigma^2 = \langle x^2 \rangle -
\langle x \rangle^2$. Reading the multipliers off
$-\lambda_1 x - \lambda_2 x^2 = -(x-\hat\mu)^2/2\hat\sigma^2 + \text{const}$
gives $\lambda_2 = 1/2\hat\sigma^2$ and
$\lambda_1 = -\hat\mu/\hat\sigma^2$. Reporting them, rather than $\hat\mu$
and $\hat\sigma$, is the exercise: they are the quantities that generalize
when a third constraint is added.

**What agreement looks like.** State it before plotting. With $N$ samples in
a bin of expected count $c$, sampling noise alone moves the bar by about
$\sqrt{c}$, so agreement means the histogram tracks the curve within roughly
$\pm\sqrt{c}$ per bin, with residuals of alternating sign across the range.
A quantile-quantile plot is the sharper version of the same check.

**Discrepancy that indicates a missing constraint.** A *systematic* residual
with a shape: one tail consistently above the curve and the other below
(skew, which the constraint list cannot represent because a quadratic
exponent is symmetric, and which is fixed by adding $\langle x^3 \rangle$);
both tails above and the centre below (excess kurtosis, calling for a
heavier-tailed family, which on $[1,\infty)$ means constraining
$\langle \log x \rangle$ instead and obtaining a power law); or two humps
(the sample is a mixture, and no single-exponential-family fit will do). All
three are the atmosphere's systematic bow in another guise.

**Discrepancy that indicts the method instead.** Residuals with no structure
but far larger than $\sqrt{c}$ point at the data, not the model: dependent
samples, a drifting source, or a quantized measurement. Maximum entropy
assumes the constraint values are known; if they are estimates from a small
or non-stationary sample, the fitted density inherits that uncertainty and
the whole apparatus is answering a question that was not asked.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A system has two microstates with energies $E_0 = 0$ and $E_1 = \varepsilon$
and a fixed average energy. (a) Write the maximum-entropy distribution and
identify the multiplier in physical units. (b) Take
$\varepsilon = kT \ln 3$ and compute the distribution and its entropy in
bits. (c) The system is now forced into the state $0$ with certainty, by
whatever means. Compute the change in Shannon entropy, convert it to
thermodynamic entropy using $S = k \ln 2$ per bit, and give the heat that
must leave the system at $T = 300$ K if its energy is unchanged.

<Solution>
**(a)** One constraint, $f(i) = E_i$, so
$p_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z$ with $Z = 1 + e^{-\beta\varepsilon}$ and $\beta$
tuned to the average energy. Physics names the multiplier $\beta = 1/kT$.

**(b)** With $\varepsilon = kT\ln 3$, the weights are $e^{0} = 1$ and
$e^{-\ln 3} = \tfrac13$, so $Z = \tfrac43$ and
$$
p_0 = \frac{1}{4/3} = \frac34, \qquad p_1 = \frac{1/3}{4/3} = \frac14 .
$$
Entropy: $H = -\tfrac34\log_2\tfrac34 - \tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14 = 0.811$
bits.

**(c)** Afterwards the distribution is $(1,0)$ with $H = 0$, so the Shannon
entropy falls by $0.811$ bits. In thermodynamic units that is
$\Delta S = -0.811 \times k \ln 2 = -0.811 \times 9.57\times10^{-24}
= -7.76 \times 10^{-24}$ J/K. Entropy that leaves a system has to appear
somewhere, and at fixed energy it leaves as heat: $Q = T|\Delta S| =
300 \times 7.76\times10^{-24} = 2.33\times10^{-21}$ J.

For the fully symmetric case, a two-state system at $p = (\tfrac12,
\tfrac12)$ driven to a known state, the same arithmetic gives exactly one
bit and $Q = kT\ln 2 = 2.87\times10^{-21}$ J at $300$ K. That number is
Landauer's bound, the minimum heat dissipated when one bit is erased, and it
is the subject of <Ref lesson="thermodynamics-of-information"/>: the
identification $\beta = 1/kT$ made in this lesson by matching formulas turns
out to have a measurable consequence in joules.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

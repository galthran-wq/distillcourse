Mutual information I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y) quantifies, in bits, how much
observing one variable reduces uncertainty about the other. This lesson
computes that drop on the weather table of the previous lesson, proves it
symmetric, bounds it between zero and $\min(H(X), H(Y))$, rewrites it as a
divergence between the joint distribution and the product of its marginals,
and closes with two standard readings of it that fail: correlation as a test
of dependence, and the area diagram at three variables.

This is the first move of the course's fifth step, which asks what one
variable says about another. Everything so far has priced uncertainty:
<Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/> fixed the content of a single event at
$-\log_2 p$, <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/> averaged it into
$H(X)$, and <Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/> showed that
observing a second variable never raises the average, $H(Y \mid X) \le H(Y)$,
leaving a $0.311$-bit gap on its weather table that it left unnamed. This
lesson names that gap and establishes what it can do.

The route: the drop named and defined; three expressions that all evaluate to
it, which forces a symmetry the definition does not show; the range from zero
to $\min(H(X), H(Y))$; a one-parameter family sweeping that range; the
rewrite as a divergence from independence; and two places where the standard
mental pictures break.

## Naming the drop: I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y)

<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/> ended on an unnamed
$0.311$-bit gap. Its weather table, sky
$X \in \{\text{clear}, \text{overcast}\}$ and ground
$Y \in \{\text{dry}, \text{wet}\}$ with cells
$p(\text{clear},\text{dry}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$,
$p(\text{clear},\text{wet}) = 0$ and
$p(\text{overcast},\text{dry}) = p(\text{overcast},\text{wet}) = \tfrac{1}{4}$,
produced five entropies: $H(X) = 1$, $H(Y) \approx 0.811$, $H(X,Y) = 1.5$,
$H(Y \mid X) = 0.5$ and $H(X \mid Y) \approx 0.689$ bits.

Before the ground is observed, uncertainty about the sky is $H(X) = 1$ bit.
After it is observed, an average of $H(X \mid Y) \approx 0.689$ bits remains.
The observation **removed**

$$
H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \approx 1 - 0.689 = 0.311 \text{ bits}.
$$

<Definition title="mutual information">
The **mutual information** between discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ is
$$
I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y),
$$
measured in bits: the average reduction in the uncertainty of $X$ produced by
observing $Y$.
</Definition>

No new primitive enters. Every term comes from the joint table by the previous
lesson's recipes: marginals from row and column sums, conditional entropy as
the $p(y)$-weighted average of the column entropies. The units follow the log
base of the entropies, bits under $\log_2$ and nats under $\ln$; machine
learning papers usually report nats, and one nat is $1/\ln 2 \approx 1.443$
bits.

The semicolon in $I(X;Y)$ is not a comma, and the difference is not
cosmetic.

| Symbol | Read as | What the punctuation does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $H(X,Y)$ | *joint entropy of X and Y* | the comma **pools**: $(X,Y)$ is one variable with four outcomes |
| $I(X;Y)$ | *mutual information between X and Y* | the semicolon **separates**: two sides sharing something |
| $I(X,Y;Z)$ | *information the pair carries about Z* | both at once: the pooled pair $(X,Y)$ against $Z$ |

<KeyFact>
$I(X;Y)$ is a functional of the joint distribution, not a statistic of data.
Every number in this lesson assumes the table is the truth. Computed from $N$
samples instead, the same formula returns a positive value even when the
variables are independent, and that gap is the subject of
<Ref lesson="estimating-mutual-information"/>.
</KeyFact>

## One quantity, three routes to it

The definition is one-sided. It measures what $Y$ does to the uncertainty of
$X$, and nothing in $H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$ suggests that the reverse
computation, what $X$ does to the uncertainty of $Y$, returns the same
number. The two conditional entropies are certainly not equal here:
$H(X \mid Y) \approx 0.689$ against $H(Y \mid X) = 0.5$. Subtracting each
from its own marginal, however,

$$
H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \approx 0.811 - 0.5 = 0.311 \text{ bits},
$$

which is the first drop again. A third combination of the same five numbers
lands in the same place: $H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \approx 1 + 0.811 - 1.5 =
0.311$ bits.

  caption="Three different subtractions on the same five numbers, one answer: I(X;Y) = 0.311 bits."

The coincidence is not a property of this table. The chain rule of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>, which splits the same total
$H(X,Y)$ as $H(X) + H(Y \mid X)$ or as $H(Y) + H(X \mid Y)$, forces it for
every joint distribution.

<Theorem title="three forms of mutual information (Cover &amp; Thomas 2.4.1)">
For discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$,
$$
I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)
       = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y),
$$
and in particular $I(X;Y) = I(Y;X)$.

<Proof>
The goal is an identity, so the proof is direct substitution from the chain
rule, twice.

**Eliminate the conditional entropy.** The chain rule in the second order is
$H(X,Y) = H(Y) + H(X \mid Y)$, so $H(X \mid Y) = H(X,Y) - H(Y)$. Substituting
into the definition,
$$
I(X;Y) = H(X) - \bigl(H(X,Y) - H(Y)\bigr) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y).
$$

**Read the result.** The right side contains no conditional entropy and is
unchanged by swapping $X$ and $Y$, since $H(X,Y) = H(Y,X)$: the entropy of a
table does not depend on which variable is written first. This is the step
that produces the symmetry.

**Return by the other order.** Using $H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X)$ in the
same expression gives $H(X) + H(Y) - H(X) - H(Y \mid X) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The symmetry is worth stating as a fact about the world rather than about the
algebra. The sky and the ground are not symmetric objects: the sky determines
the ground half the time and the ground determines the sky a quarter of the
time, the conditional entropies differ by $0.189$ bits, and a forecaster
reasoning in one direction faces a different problem from one reasoning in the
other. The number of bits transferred is nevertheless identical in the two
directions. Sharing has one size, not two.

The third form completes the additive diagram left open in the previous
lesson. $H(X)$ and $H(Y)$ overlap inside $H(X,Y)$, and the overlap, counted in
both marginals but once in the total, is exactly $I(X;Y)$. For two variables
this picture is exact, because all three regions are entropies or differences
of entropies that cannot be negative. The last section of this lesson shows
what happens to it at three variables.

<Remark title="entropy is self-information">
Setting $Y = X$ gives $I(X;X) = H(X) - H(X \mid X) = H(X)$, since knowing $X$
leaves no uncertainty about $X$. A variable shares its entire entropy with
itself, which is why entropy is also called *self-information*. The identity
is also the reason $I$ cannot be a distance: a distance from a thing to itself
is zero, and this one is $H(X)$.
</Remark>

<Exercise>
For the joint table $p(a,c) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $p(a,d) = \tfrac{1}{4}$,
$p(b,c) = \tfrac{1}{8}$, $p(b,d) = \tfrac{1}{8}$, compute $I(X;Y)$ by two of
the three routes, taking one route through a conditional entropy computed
row by row. Write down the two renormalized rows before opening the solution.
Use $\log_2 5 \approx 2.3219$ and $\log_2 3 \approx 1.5850$.

<Solution>
**Marginals.** Row sums $p(a) = \tfrac{3}{4}$, $p(b) = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so
$H(X) = H(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.8113$ bits. Column sums
$p(c) = \tfrac{5}{8}$, $p(d) = \tfrac{3}{8}$, with
$-\log_2 \tfrac{5}{8} = 3 - \log_2 5 \approx 0.6781$ and
$-\log_2 \tfrac{3}{8} = 3 - \log_2 3 \approx 1.4150$:
$$
H(Y) = \tfrac{5}{8}(0.6781) + \tfrac{3}{8}(1.4150) \approx 0.4238 + 0.5306
= 0.9544 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Route 1, through the rows.** Row $a$ is
$(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4})$, renormalizing to $(\tfrac{2}{3},
\tfrac{1}{3})$ with entropy $\log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.9183$ bits.
Row $b$ is $(\tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8})$, renormalizing to a fair coin,
entropy $1$ bit. Weighted,
$H(Y \mid X) = \tfrac{3}{4}(0.9183) + \tfrac{1}{4}(1) \approx 0.9387$ bits,
so $I = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \approx 0.9544 - 0.9387 = 0.0157$ bits.

**Route 2, through the joint.** The flattened table
$\{\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}\}$ has
$H(X,Y) = \tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{1}{4}(2) + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8}(3) = 1.75$
bits, so $I = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \approx 0.8113 + 0.9544 - 1.75 = 0.0157$
bits: agreement.

The table is nearly independent. The variables share about $0.016$ bits, some
fifty times less than $\min(H(X), H(Y)) \approx 0.81$, which is what "nearly
independent" means quantitatively. Note what the eye cannot do here: the
cells $\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}$ look
lopsided, and lopsided cells are not what dependence is. Dependence is a
mismatch between the cells and the product of the margins, and this table
almost matches its own product.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## The range: from zero to min(H(X), H(Y))

A quantity that will be maximised and minimised for the rest of the course
needs its range pinned down. Both ends follow in one line each from results
already proved.

<Theorem title="nonnegativity">
$I(X;Y) \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

<Proof>
Conditioning reduces entropy on average
(<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>): $H(X \mid Y) \le H(X)$, with
equality exactly under independence. Subtracting,
$I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \ge 0$, with equality in the same case.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Theorem title="upper bound (Weissman EE376A 2.3)">
$I(X;Y) \le \min\bigl(H(X), H(Y)\bigr)$. Equality $I(X;Y) = H(X)$ holds if
and only if $H(X \mid Y) = 0$, that is, $X$ is a deterministic function of
$Y$; equality $I(X;Y) = H(Y)$ holds if and only if $Y$ is a deterministic
function of $X$.

<Proof>
Conditional entropy is an average of entropies, hence nonnegative:
$H(X \mid Y) \ge 0$. So $I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \le H(X)$, with equality
exactly when $H(X \mid Y) = 0$, which happens exactly when every value of $Y$
that occurs pins $X$ down. The bound $I(X;Y) \le H(Y)$ follows from the
symmetric form $I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The bound has an operational reading. Observing $Y$ cannot teach more about
$X$ than the total uncertainty $H(X)$ there was to remove, and cannot teach
more than $H(Y)$ either, because $Y$ carries only $H(Y)$ bits in the first
place.

### Four cases that fix the two bounds

- **The canonical case.** The weather table: $I \approx 0.311$ against
  $\min(1, 0.811) = 0.811$. Both bounds are slack, and neither variable
  determines the other.
- **The degenerate case.** Any product table $p(x,y) = p(x)\,p(y)$ has
  $I = 0$ with both marginals arbitrary. Zero shared information is
  compatible with any amount of uncertainty on each side.
- **A case with a different surface.** Roll a fair die $X$ and report only
  $Y = 1$ if $X \ge 5$, else $Y = 0$, the die-and-indicator example worked in
  <Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>, where
  $H(X) = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585$, $H(Y) \approx 0.918$ and
  $H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac{5}{3}$ bits. Here
  $I = H(Y) - 0 = 0.918$ bits, and the $H(Y)$ bound is attained.
- **The near miss.** In that same example the $H(X)$ bound is nowhere near
  attained: $0.918$ against $2.585$. Determinism in one direction saturates
  one bound and says nothing about the other. Reading "$Y$ is a function of
  $X$" as "$I = H(X)$" violates exactly the clause that names which
  conditional entropy vanishes.

<KeyFact>
The $H(Y)$ half of the bound is what makes channel capacity finite. A channel
whose output is a single bit cannot deliver more than $1$ bit per use about
its input, whatever alphabet the input is drawn from and however the input
distribution is chosen, which is why the maximisation in
<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/> is over a bounded quantity.
</KeyFact>

## The dependence dial: independent to lockstep

The two endpoints of the range, and everything in between, fit inside one
family of tables. Let $X$ and $Y$ be binary with uniform marginals, coupled
only through the *agreement probability* $q = P(Y = X)$: each diagonal cell
holds $q/2$ and each off-diagonal cell $(1-q)/2$. Every marginal stays fixed
at a fair coin while $q$ moves, so any change in $I$ is caused by the coupling
and by nothing else.

<Task>

</Task>

<Example title="mutual information at q = 0.75">
The table has diagonal cells $\tfrac{3}{8}$ and off-diagonal cells
$\tfrac{1}{8}$; row and column sums are all $\tfrac{1}{2}$, so
$H(X) = H(Y) = 1$ bit.

**Step 1: condition on each value of $Y$.** Given $Y = 0$, probability
$\tfrac{1}{2}$, the column $(\tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8})$ renormalizes to
$p(x \mid y{=}0) = (\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$, with entropy
$H(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$ bits. Given $Y = 1$, the column renormalizes
to $(\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{3}{4})$: the same entropy.

**Step 2: average.** $H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.811) +
\tfrac{1}{2}(0.811) = 0.811$ bits.

**Step 3: subtract.** $I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \approx 1 - 0.811 = 0.189$
bits.

**Cross-check via the joint form.** The flattened table
$\{\tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}\}$ has
$H(X,Y) = 2 \cdot \tfrac{3}{8}(1.415) + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8}(3) \approx 1.811$
bits, and $H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \approx 1 + 1 - 1.811 = 0.189$ bits.
</Example>

## The whole dial in one formula: I = 1 − H₂(q)

Step 1 of that example did not use the value $0.75$. Every column of the
table renormalizes
$X$ to $(q, 1-q)$ or its mirror image, so $H(X \mid Y) = H_2(q)$, the binary
entropy function of <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>, and

$$
I(X;Y) = 1 - H_2(q).
$$

At $q = \tfrac{1}{2}$ every cell equals $\tfrac{1}{4}$, the product of the
marginals, so the variables are independent and $I = 0$. At $q = 1$ they are
in lockstep, $H(X \mid Y) = 0$, and $I = 1$ bit, the full $H(X)$: the upper
bound is attained. The formula is symmetric under
$q \leftrightarrow 1-q$, so perfect **disagreement** carries exactly as much
information as perfect agreement. Relabelling the outcomes of $Y$ changes no
conditional distribution's entropy, and mutual information sees only those
entropies, never the labels.

The shape of that curve is the practical content of the family. Moving from
$q = 0.5$ to $q = 0.75$, halfway to determinism, buys only $0.189$ bits of the
available $1$; the remaining $0.811$ bits are all packed into the last quarter
of the range. Nearly-deterministic couplings are where mutual information
lives, and weak couplings are worth much less than their correlation
coefficients suggest.

<Exercise>

<Solution>
**(a) The equation.** $I = 1 - H_2(q)$, so half a bit shared means
$H_2(q) = \tfrac{1}{2}$: half of $H(X)$ removed, half left over. $H_2$
combines $q$ with $\log_2 q$ additively, and no elementary function inverts
that combination, so the root has to be bracketed and squeezed rather than
isolated.

**(b) The bracket.** On $[\tfrac{1}{2}, 1]$ the binary entropy falls strictly
from $1$ to $0$, so exactly one root lies there. The worked example at
$q = 0.75$ produced $H(X \mid Y) = H_2(0.75) \approx 0.811$, above
$\tfrac{1}{2}$; the $q = 0.95$ frame read $I = 0.714$, that is
$H_2(0.95) \approx 0.286$, below it. The root is in $(0.75, 0.95)$. One
evaluation tightens it:
$$
H_2(0.85) = -0.85(-0.2345) - 0.15(-2.7370) \approx 0.199 + 0.411
= 0.610 > \tfrac{1}{2},
$$
so the root is in $(0.85, 0.95)$.

**(c) Which side interpolation falls on.** The chord across the wide bracket
has slope $(0.286 - 0.811)/0.20 = -2.625$ bits per unit $q$ and reaches
$\tfrac{1}{2}$ at $q \approx 0.75 + 0.311/2.625 = 0.868$. The chord across the
tight bracket has slope $(0.286 - 0.610)/0.10 = -3.24$ and reaches it at
$q \approx 0.85 + 0.110/3.24 = 0.884$. $H_2$ is concave, so on any interval
its graph lies above the chord; the chord therefore crosses $\tfrac{1}{2}$
while $H_2$ is still above it, and a linear estimate always lands **below**
the true root. Both do: the root is $q^\star \approx 0.890$ ($0.88997$ to
five figures), and the tighter bracket, whose chord hugs the curve over a
shorter span, is the closer of the two.

**(d) The mirror root.** $H_2(q) = H_2(1-q)$, so
$q = 1 - 0.890 = 0.110$ shares the same half bit — the perfect-disagreement
side of the dial, where $Y$ predicts $X$ just as well with its labels
swapped.

**(e) Sensitivity.** $(0.515 - 0.485)/0.01 = 3.0$ bits per unit $q$. Exactly,
$$
\frac{dI}{dq} = -H_2'(q) = \log_2 \frac{q}{1-q},
$$
the log-odds of $q$ in bits, which at $q = 0.89$ is
$\log_2(0.89/0.11) \approx 3.02$ — the two-point estimate is right to its
digits. Holding $I$ within $0.001$ of half a bit therefore needs $q$ set to
within $0.001/3.02 \approx 0.0003$, finer than the dial's step of $0.005$,
which is why its readout jumps $0.485$, $0.500$, $0.515$ with nothing in
between.

**The transferable move.** The slope of $I$ in this family is the log-odds of
$q$: zero at $q = \tfrac{1}{2}$, which is why the curve is flat near
independence, and unbounded as $q \to 1$: the last fifth of the sweep, from
$q = 0.9$ to lockstep, delivers $0.469$ bits, nearly as much as the whole
first four fifths. Inverting an entropy is always
this shape of work — monotonicity for uniqueness of the root, two evaluations
for a bracket, concavity for the sign of the error.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Mutual information is a divergence from independence

Nonnegativity was proved by inheritance, and inheritance does not explain
much: $I \ge 0$ came out of $H(X \mid Y) \le H(X)$, which itself came out of
concavity. There is a second route that explains both, and it changes what
mutual information is a measure *of*. Independence is the case
$p(x,y) = p(x)\,p(y)$, so the natural thing to measure is the discrepancy
between the true joint table and the product table built from its own
marginals.

<Theorem title="divergence form (Cover &amp; Thomas §2.3)">
$$
I(X;Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}.
$$

<Proof>
Write both terms of the definition as sums over the joint table, using
$\sum_y p(x,y) = p(x)$ to turn the marginal entropy into a cell sum:
$$
I(X;Y) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(x)
         + \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(x \mid y)
       = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p(x \mid y)}{p(x)}.
$$
Multiplying numerator and denominator inside the logarithm by $p(y)$ and
using $p(x \mid y)\,p(y) = p(x,y)$ gives the stated ratio. Cells with
$p(x,y) = 0$ carry weight zero and are skipped, by the convention of
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Each cell contributes its own probability times the logarithm of how much
more likely it is than independence would make it. Running that tally on the
weather table gives the number a second derivation.

### The sum has a name

The quantity in the theorem is not specific to joint tables. For any two
distributions $p$ and $q$ over the same outcomes,
$\sum_i p_i \log_2 (p_i / q_i)$ is the
**Kullback-Leibler divergence** $D(p \Vert q)$, and
<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/> proves that it is nonnegative, zero only at
$p = q$, and equal to the extra bits paid for coding $p$ with a code built for
$q$. Mutual information is that quantity evaluated on one particular pair:

$$
I(X;Y) = D\bigl(p(x,y) \,\Vert\, p(x)\,p(y)\bigr).
$$

Two facts already used fall out of this identity rather than being separate
results. $I \ge 0$ because a divergence is nonnegative, and $I = 0$ exactly
under independence because a divergence vanishes only when its two arguments
agree, which here means $p(x,y) = p(x)p(y)$ in every cell. The identity is
also the form the rest of the course uses:
<Ref lesson="information-bottleneck"/> and <Ref lesson="infonce"/> both
manipulate $I$ as a divergence, not as a difference of entropies, because the
entropies of a high-dimensional representation are not available and the
ratio inside the logarithm is.

<Remark title="the per-cell terms carry a sign">
The quantity $\operatorname{pmi}(x,y) = \log_2 \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}$, the
*pointwise mutual information*, is positive where the pair occurs more often
than independence predicts and negative where it occurs less. The weather
cell (overcast, dry) has $\operatorname{pmi} = \log_2 \tfrac{2}{3} \approx
-0.585$ bits, and the tally above weights it by $\tfrac{1}{4}$. Nonnegativity
is a statement about the weighted average and not about the cells, so an
individual observation can lower the observer's information about $X$ even
though observations cannot on average. Word-association and collocation
scores in natural language processing are computed from these per-cell terms
rather than from $I$.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The divergence form is the definition that survives when the entropies do
not. For a variable with a density the discrete entropy $H(X)$ is not
defined, and the differential entropy that stands in for it can be negative
and shifts when the units change. The ratio $p(x,y)/p(x)p(y)$ is a pure
number, so $D(p_{XY} \Vert p_X p_Y)$ stays well defined, nonnegative and
unit-free. Every mutual information reported for a continuous signal or a
network activation is that divergence, not a difference of entropies.
</KeyFact>

## ★ The nearest product distribution

The divergence form compares the joint against one specific product table,
the one built from its own marginals. Nothing so far says that this is the
best product approximation available. It is, and the proof is three lines.
The core path does not use this section.

<Theorem title="mutual information as distance to the product distributions (Polyanskiy &amp; Wu, Thm 3.4 in §3.2)">
For any distributions $q_X$ and $q_Y$ that are positive wherever $p_X$ and
$p_Y$ are,
$$
D\bigl(p(x,y) \Vert q_X(x) q_Y(y)\bigr)
= I(X;Y) + D(p_X \Vert q_X) + D(p_Y \Vert q_Y),
$$
so $I(X;Y) = \min_{q_X, q_Y} D\bigl(p(x,y) \Vert q_X q_Y\bigr)$, attained
only at $q_X = p_X$ and $q_Y = p_Y$.

<Proof>
Split the logarithm by inserting the true marginals:
$$
\log_2 \frac{p(x,y)}{q_X(x) q_Y(y)}
= \log_2 \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} + \log_2 \frac{p(x)}{q_X(x)}
+ \log_2 \frac{p(y)}{q_Y(y)}.
$$
Take the expectation under $p(x,y)$. The first term gives $I(X;Y)$. In the
second, the summand depends on $x$ alone, so summing over $y$ collapses
$p(x,y)$ to $p(x)$ and leaves $\sum_x p(x) \log_2 \frac{p(x)}{q_X(x)} =
D(p_X \Vert q_X)$; the third is the same computation on $y$. Both divergences
are nonnegative and vanish only at $q_X = p_X$, $q_Y = p_Y$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Among all the ways of pretending that two variables are independent, keeping
their true marginals is the cheapest, and its price is the mutual
information. The decomposition also gives a computable upper bound: pick any
product distribution at all, evaluate one divergence, and the result exceeds
$I(X;Y)$. Bounds of exactly this shape, with a tractable $q$ standing in for
an intractable marginal, are how mutual information is estimated in practice
when the joint distribution is unavailable.

## What correlation misses

Zero mutual information is now known to mean independence, with nothing left
over. The familiar diagnostic for "these two variables are unrelated" is not
mutual information but the correlation coefficient, and it certifies far
less. The claim that zero correlation means independence is false, and it is
false in the direction that matters: a pair can be deterministically coupled,
one variable a function of the other, and still have correlation exactly
zero.

The reason is that covariance $\mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y]$ is
one number computed from products of values. It measures how well a straight
line fits the joint distribution and it discards everything a straight line
cannot express. Mutual information is computed from the whole table of
probabilities and refers to no fitted shape at all.

<Example title="dependent but uncorrelated">
Let $X$ be uniform on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $Y = X^2$.

**Correlation.** $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] =
\tfrac{1}{3}(-1 + 0 + 1) = 0$, so
$\operatorname{cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0$
and the correlation coefficient is exactly zero.

**Mutual information.** $Y$ is a function of $X$, so $H(Y \mid X) = 0$ and
$I(X;Y) = H(Y)$. Since $Y = 1$ with probability $\tfrac{2}{3}$ and $Y = 0$
with probability $\tfrac{1}{3}$,
$$
I(X;Y) = H(Y) = H\bigl(\tfrac{1}{3}\bigr) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3}
\approx 0.918 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Cross-check from the other side.** $H(X) = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits.
Given $Y = 1$, $X$ is uniform on $\{-1, 1\}$: $1$ bit. Given $Y = 0$,
$X = 0$: $0$ bits. So $H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac{2}{3}(1) + \tfrac{1}{3}(0) =
\tfrac{2}{3}$, and $I = 1.585 - 0.667 = 0.918$ bits, the same value, as
symmetry requires.
</Example>

The replacement rule is short. Correlation zero rules out linear dependence
and nothing else. Mutual information zero rules out dependence of every kind,
because it is a divergence and a divergence vanishes only when the two tables
agree cell by cell. When a feature-selection pipeline drops a column because
its correlation with the target is near zero, it is discarding any
relationship shaped like this example, which is why mutual-information
screening finds predictors that a correlation filter throws away.

<KeyFact>
The reflex this section refutes is sound in exactly one family: for a jointly
Gaussian pair, zero correlation does imply independence, and the two
quantities determine each other,
$I(X;Y) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(1 - \rho^2)$ bits — zero at $\rho = 0$, half a
bit at $\rho \approx 0.707$, unbounded as $\rho \to \pm 1$
(Cover &amp; Thomas 8.5.1). Jointly Gaussian is the one common family whose
dependence a single coefficient describes completely, which is where the habit
comes from and why it survives the counterexamples.
</KeyFact>

<Remark title="mutual information is not a distance, but a distance is nearby">
$I$ fails every axiom a metric needs: $I(X;X) = H(X) \neq 0$, and it obeys no
triangle inequality. The quantity that does satisfy the axioms is built from
it. The *variation of information*
$$
D_H(X,Y) = H(X,Y) - I(X;Y) = H(X \mid Y) + H(Y \mid X)
$$
is nonnegative, symmetric, zero when $X$ and $Y$ determine each other, and
satisfies the triangle inequality; the proof is a worthwhile exercise in
entropy inequalities. Note the reversal it forces on the intuition:
independent variables are the *farthest* apart under $D_H$, at
$D_H = H(X) + H(Y)$, not the closest.
</Remark>

## Where the overlap picture stops working

The area diagram was called exact for two variables, and it is: three
regions, three nonnegative widths, one identity per reading. The natural next
step is to draw three overlapping circles and treat every region as an amount
of information. That picture is false, and the counterexample is three fair
bits.

Let $X$ and $Y$ be independent fair bits and let $Z = X \oplus Y$, their sum
modulo 2, as in the starred section of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>, which showed that the central
region of the three-circle diagram would have to hold $-1$ bit. In terms of
mutual information the same fact reads as follows. Before $Z$ is observed,
$X$ and $Y$ are independent, so $I(X;Y) = 0$. After $Z$ is observed, $X$ and
$Y$ determine each other, since $Y = Z \oplus X$, so the conditional mutual
information $I(X;Y \mid Z)$, defined by conditioning every entropy in the
definition on $Z$, is $1$ bit.

<Figure
  src="./figures/conditioning-creates-information.svg"
  alt="Left, the joint table of two independent fair bits with all cells 1/4 and I(X;Y) = 0; right, the same pair conditioned on Z = 0, with mass 1/2 on the two diagonal cells and I(X;Y|Z=0) = 1 bit"
  caption="X and Y share nothing until Z = X ⊕ Y is observed, after which they share a full bit: conditioning created the dependence."
/>

An overlap of two regions cannot both be empty and hold a bit depending on
what a third region is doing, so no assignment of nonnegative areas
represents these three variables. The quantity a Venn diagram would call the
triple intersection is
$$
I(X;Y) - I(X;Y \mid Z) = 0 - 1 = -1 \text{ bit},
$$
and negative areas do not exist. The case is not exotic. It is a binary
symmetric channel, a channel that flips each transmitted bit independently
with some fixed probability, written here with input $X$, noise $Y$ and
output $Z = X \oplus Y$; that channel is the running example of
<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>.

<KeyFact>
Conditioning moves mutual information in either direction. $I(X;Y \mid Z)$
can exceed $I(X;Y)$, as here, or fall below it, as when $Z$ contains
everything $X$ and $Y$ share. No inequality holds between the two in general,
which is exactly why the theorem of the next lesson is stated about a Markov
chain and not about conditioning.
</KeyFact>

Established: the drop $H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$ is called mutual information;
three expressions compute it and force it to be symmetric; it lies between
$0$ and $\min(H(X), H(Y))$, hitting the bottom exactly at independence and
the top exactly at determinism; it equals the divergence
$D(p(x,y) \Vert p(x)p(y))$ of the joint from the independent product with the
same marginals; correlation is a strictly weaker test than $I = 0$; and the
two-variable area picture does not extend to three variables.

<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/> takes the quantity forward by
asking what happens to it under processing. If $T$ is computed from $X$
alone, then $I(T;Y) \le I(X;Y)$ for every $Y$: no function of the data can
manufacture information about anything, which turns the number defined here
into a hard constraint on every pipeline that processes a signal.

<Exercise>
You must report $I(X;Y)$ for a $27 \times 27$ table of English letter pairs,
where many cells are zero, a rare-letter row may be empty altogether, and the
answer is expected to be a few hundredths of a bit. Three routes are
available: (i) $H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y)$; (ii) $H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$ row by row;
(iii) the cell sum $\sum p(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}$. Say what each
route does at a zero cell and at an empty row, and choose one. Then use the
nearly-independent table already worked above ($H(X) \approx 0.8113$,
$H(Y) \approx 0.9544$, $H(X,Y) = 1.75$, $I \approx 0.0157$ bits) to say how
many decimal places of the three entropies route (i) needs to deliver $I$ to
two significant figures.

<Solution>
**Behaviour at the degenerate entries.** A zero cell contributes nothing to
any of the three, by the $0 \log 0 = 0$ convention: route (i) skips it inside
$H(X,Y)$, route (ii) skips it inside a row entropy, route (iii) skips the
whole term, and in route (iii) that is essential, since the ratio
$p(x,y)/p(x)p(y)$ is $0/0$ whenever an empty cell sits in an empty row. An
empty row is worse for route (ii) than for the others: renormalizing it means
dividing by $p(x) = 0$. The guard is to skip rows with $p(x) = 0$, which is
legitimate because such a row enters $H(Y \mid X)$ with weight zero. Routes
(i) and (iii) never form the quotient and need no guard beyond the convention.

**Precision, which is the real constraint.** Route (i) subtracts numbers of
order $1$ to produce an answer of order $0.01$, so it loses the leading digits
to cancellation. On the worked table, $0.8113 + 0.9544 - 1.75 = 0.0157$: four
decimals in, three digits gone, two significant figures out. Rounding those
entropies to two decimals instead gives $0.81 + 0.95 - 1.75 = 0.01$ — one
digit, and it is wrong in the second place. To keep two significant figures of
an answer near $0.0157$ each entropy must be carried to about four decimals,
that is two more places than the answer itself has. Route (iii) has no such
loss: it accumulates the small quantity directly, term by term, and every term
is already of the answer's own size.

**The choice.** Route (iii), the divergence form, on both counts: it is one
pass over the nonzero cells with the marginals precomputed, it needs no
per-row renormalization, and its arithmetic does not cancel. Route (i) is the
right choice when $I$ is a substantial fraction of the entropies — and it
returns $H(X)$, $H(Y)$ and $H(X,Y)$ as by-products, which are worth reporting
for a digram table. Route (ii) earns its place only when the conditional
distributions are what you have, for example when they come from a model
rather than from counts.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
$X$ takes four values and $Y$ is binary. Construct a joint distribution
making $I(X;Y)$ as large as it can be. Report the value, state which of the
two upper bounds is binding, and say what feature of the construction the
binding bound is responding to. Then state what changes if $X$ is allowed
sixteen values instead of four.

<Solution>
**The construction.** Let $X$ be uniform on $\{1,2,3,4\}$ and set
$Y = 1$ for $X \in \{3,4\}$, else $Y = 0$. Then $Y$ is a fair bit,
$H(Y) = 1$ bit, and $Y$ is a function of $X$, so $H(Y \mid X) = 0$ and
$$
I(X;Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) = 1 \text{ bit}.
$$

**Which bound binds.** $H(X) = 2$ bits and $H(Y) = 1$ bit, so
$\min(H(X), H(Y)) = H(Y) = 1$ and the $H(Y)$ side is binding and attained.
The $H(X)$ side is slack by a full bit: $H(X \mid Y) = 1$ bit survives the
observation, because each value of $Y$ leaves two values of $X$ equally
likely.

**What the bound responds to.** It responds to the alphabet of $Y$, not to
the coupling. $I \le H(Y) \le \log_2 2 = 1$ holds for every joint
distribution with a binary $Y$, however $X$ is distributed and however
tightly the two are coupled. The equality condition of the upper-bound
theorem says the maximum is reached exactly when $H(Y \mid X) = 0$, that is
when $Y$ is a deterministic function of $X$, and $H(Y) = 1$ additionally
requires $Y$ to be a fair bit, which is what forces the two-two split of the
four values of $X$.

**Sixteen values.** Nothing changes: the maximum is still $1$ bit, attained
by any function of $X$ that is a fair bit. Enlarging the alphabet of $X$
raises $H(X)$ to $4$ bits and leaves $I$ where it was, which is the
degenerate case the $\min$ in the bound exists to cover. A single binary
observation cannot carry more than one bit about anything.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "if $I(X;Y) = 0$ and $I(Y;Z) = 0$ then $I(X;Z) = 0$;
sharing nothing is transitive." Give a counterexample with explicit
distributions, name the property of $I$ the claim silently assumes, and then
check whether the XOR triple $X$, $Y$, $Z = X \oplus Y$ of independent fair
bits satisfies the claim's hypotheses.

<Solution>
**Counterexample.** Let $X$ be a fair bit, let $Z = X$, and let $Y$ be an
independent fair bit. Then $I(X;Y) = 0$ and $I(Y;Z) = 0$, since $Y$ is
independent of both. But $I(X;Z) = I(X;X) = H(X) = 1$ bit, which is the
maximum available. The claim fails by the widest possible margin.

**The assumption.** The claim reads $I = 0$ as "distance zero" and then
applies a triangle inequality: if $X$ is at distance $0$ from $Y$ and $Y$ at
distance $0$ from $Z$, then $X$ and $Z$ coincide. Mutual information is not a
distance in either direction. It is zero at maximal *dis*-similarity rather
than at identity, and $I(X;X) = H(X)$ shows the self-distance axiom fails
too. The quantity built from $I$ that does satisfy the axioms is the
variation of information $D_H(X,Y) = H(X,Y) - I(X;Y)$, and on this example it
behaves correctly: $D_H(X,Y) = 2$, $D_H(Y,Z) = 2$, $D_H(X,Z) = 0$, and
$0 \le 2 + 2$.

**The XOR triple.** All three hypotheses hold, and so does the conclusion:
each pair of $X$, $Y$, $Z = X \oplus Y$ is a pair of independent fair bits,
so all three pairwise mutual informations are zero. The triple is therefore
not a counterexample to the claim, but it refutes the stronger statement the
claim is usually confused with. Pairwise zero mutual information does not
make three variables jointly independent: here any two of them determine the
third, and $I(X;Y \mid Z) = 1$ bit. Zero pairwise sharing constrains pairs
and says nothing about triples.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The lesson proved $I(X;Y) \ge 0$ by inheriting it from "conditioning reduces
entropy". Prove it instead directly from the divergence form, using
[Jensen's inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen%27s_inequality)
for the concave $\log_2$, and derive the equality condition. Then prove
$I(X;Y) \le H(X)$ from the same starting point and characterise its equality
case. State which of the two inequalities in your first proof needs *strict*
concavity and why.

<Solution>
**Nonnegativity.** Work with $-I$ so that the logarithm's argument is the
one being averaged. Summing over the cells with $p(x,y) > 0$,
$$
-I(X;Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p(x)\,p(y)}{p(x,y)}.
$$
This is the expectation of $\log_2$ of the random quantity
$p(X)p(Y)/p(X,Y)$. Since $\log_2$ is concave, Jensen's inequality moves the
expectation inside:
$$
-I(X;Y) \le \log_2 \sum_{x,y: \, p(x,y) > 0} p(x,y)\,
\frac{p(x)\,p(y)}{p(x,y)} = \log_2 \!\!\sum_{x,y: \, p(x,y) > 0}\!\! p(x)p(y)
\le \log_2 1 = 0,
$$
the last step because summing $p(x)p(y)$ over a subset of all pairs cannot
exceed summing it over all of them, which gives $1$. Hence $I \ge 0$.

**Equality.** Both inequalities must be tight. The second is tight only when
every pair with $p(x)p(y) > 0$ also has $p(x,y) > 0$: the support of the
joint is the full rectangle. The first is tight only when
$p(x)p(y)/p(x,y)$ takes a single value $c$ across the support. Then
$p(x,y) = p(x)p(y)/c$ on the full rectangle, and summing both sides over all
cells gives $1 = 1/c$, so $c = 1$ and $p(x,y) = p(x)p(y)$ everywhere:
independence.

**Which step needs strict concavity.** The equality analysis of the Jensen
step. A merely concave function can be affine on an interval, and Jensen
would then be tight for a random quantity spread across that interval; strict
concavity forces the quantity to be constant. The inequality $I \ge 0$
itself needs only concavity.

**The upper bound.** From the definition, $I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$, and
$H(X \mid Y) = \sum_y p(y) H(X \mid Y{=}y)$ is a weighted average of
entropies, each nonnegative, so $H(X \mid Y) \ge 0$ and $I \le H(X)$.
Equality needs $H(X \mid Y) = 0$, hence $H(X \mid Y{=}y) = 0$ for every $y$
with $p(y) > 0$: each such column is a point mass, so $X$ is a deterministic
function of $Y$ on the values of $Y$ that occur. The transferable move is
that both ends of the range come from the same subtraction, read once with
$H(X \mid Y) \le H(X)$ and once with $H(X \mid Y) \ge 0$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take $X, Y \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ with cells $p(-1,-1) = p(-1,1) = p(1,-1) =
p(1,1) = \tfrac{1}{6}$ and $p(0,0) = \tfrac{1}{3}$, all other cells zero.
Compute the correlation coefficient and, using the row-average form of
conditional entropy from <Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>,
compute $I(X;Y)$. Then construct a second table with the same two marginals
and the same correlation but a different mutual information, and report both
numbers for it.

<Solution>
**Marginals.** Row sums: $p(X = -1) = \tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{1}{6} =
\tfrac{1}{3}$, $p(X = 0) = \tfrac{1}{3}$, $p(X = 1) = \tfrac{1}{3}$. By
symmetry the column sums are the same, so both marginals are uniform on three
values and $H(X) = H(Y) = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits.

**Correlation.** $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}[Y] = 0$ by symmetry, and
$$
\mathbb{E}[XY] = \tfrac{1}{6}(1) + \tfrac{1}{6}(-1) + \tfrac{1}{3}(0)
+ \tfrac{1}{6}(-1) + \tfrac{1}{6}(1) = 0,
$$
so the covariance and the correlation coefficient are both zero.

**Mutual information.** Row $X = -1$ is
$(\tfrac{1}{6}, 0, \tfrac{1}{6})$, renormalizing to
$(\tfrac{1}{2}, 0, \tfrac{1}{2})$ with entropy $1$ bit. Row $X = 1$ is the
same, $1$ bit. Row $X = 0$ is $(0, \tfrac{1}{3}, 0)$, renormalizing to a
point mass, entropy $0$. Weighted by the uniform row probabilities,
$$
H(Y \mid X) = \tfrac{1}{3}(1) + \tfrac{1}{3}(0) + \tfrac{1}{3}(1)
= \tfrac{2}{3} \text{ bits},
$$
so $I(X;Y) = 1.585 - 0.667 = 0.918$ bits. Uncorrelated, and yet more than
half of $H(Y)$ is shared: $X = 0$ and $Y = 0$ always occur together, and the
sign of $Y$ carries no linear signal because the two signs are equally likely
whenever $X \neq 0$.

**The second table.** Take the product of the marginals: every cell
$\tfrac{1}{9}$. The marginals are unchanged by construction, the correlation
is again zero because independent variables have zero covariance, and
$I(X;Y) = 0$ bits exactly, since the joint equals the product.

**What the pair shows.** Two tables, identical marginals and identical
correlation, mutual informations $0.918$ and $0$ bits. Correlation and the
marginals together carry no information about how much the variables share.
Only the cells do, which is the direction-of-arithmetic point from
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>: cells determine margins,
margins do not determine cells.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take a real tabular dataset with a categorical target: a churn flag, a
survival flag, an income bracket. Pick one numeric feature and bin it into
about ten equal-count bins. Build the two-way count table of (binned feature,
target), normalise it, and compute $I$ in bits. Then shuffle the target
column uniformly at random, rebuild the table, and recompute $I$. Report both
numbers, state what the shuffled value would be if you had the true
distributions rather than counts, and say what an $I$ close to $H(\text{target})$
would tell you about the feature.

<Solution>
**Procedure.** With counts $n_{ij}$ over $r$ feature bins and $c$ target
classes and $N = \sum_{ij} n_{ij}$, set $\hat{p}_{ij} = n_{ij}/N$ and
evaluate the divergence form directly,
$$
\hat{I} = \sum_{ij} \hat{p}_{ij} \log_2
\frac{\hat{p}_{ij}}{\hat{p}_{i\cdot}\,\hat{p}_{\cdot j}},
$$
skipping empty cells. Equal-count bins rather than equal-width bins keep the
row marginal uniform, which stops a single dense bin from dominating.

**What to expect.** For a binary target, $H(\text{target})$ is at most $1$
bit and is usually well under it for an imbalanced flag. A genuinely
predictive single feature typically removes a small fraction of that: values
in the hundredths of a bit are normal, and a tenth of a bit is a strong
feature.

**The shuffled value.** Shuffling breaks any relationship between the columns,
so the distribution the shuffled data is drawn from is exactly the product of
the two marginals, whose mutual information is $0$ by the nonnegativity
theorem's equality case. The computed $\hat{I}$ will nonetheless come out
positive. It is a sum of $rc$ terms fitted to noise, and the formula has no
way to tell a small genuine dependence from a small accidental one.

**Reading a large value.** If $\hat{I}$ lands close to $H(\text{target})$,
the feature nearly determines the target, since $I = H(\text{target})$ holds
exactly when $H(\text{target} \mid \text{feature}) = 0$. On real data that
usually means leakage: the feature was derived from the target or recorded
after it. The bound is doing diagnostic work here, and the honest check is to
ask how the column was produced.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Continue the previous exercise. Subsample the dataset to $N = 10$, $100$ and
$1000$ rows, and at each size recompute the *shuffled* estimate a few dozen
times, keeping the average. Plot the average shuffled $\hat{I}$ against $N$
on log axes. Report the shape, predict the value at $N = 10{,}000$ before
computing it, and say which of your three points does not lie on the line
and why.

<Solution>
**What the plot shows.** The average shuffled estimate is positive at every
$N$ and, over the upper part of the range, falls in proportion to $1/N$: a
straight line of slope $-1$ on log-log axes. With $r = 10$ bins and $c = 2$
classes, expect roughly $0.065$ bits at $N = 100$ and $0.0065$ at
$N = 1000$, so the prediction at $N = 10{,}000$ is about $0.00065$ bits.

**Where those numbers come from.** Under true independence, the plug-in
estimator has an average value of about
$$
\mathbb{E}[\hat{I}] \approx \frac{(r-1)(c-1)}{2N \ln 2} \text{ bits},
$$
which for $r = 10$, $c = 2$ is $9 / (2N \ln 2) \approx 6.5/N$ bits. The bias
is set by the *shape of the table*, not by the data: more bins means more
apparent information, at no cost in evidence. Doubling the number of bins
roughly doubles the reading obtained from pure noise.

**The point off the line.** $N = 10$. With ten rows spread over twenty
cells, almost every occupied cell holds a single row, so the binned feature
appears to determine the shuffled target and $\hat{I}$ saturates near
$H(\text{target})$, close to $1$ bit for a balanced binary target rather
than the $0.65$ the formula predicts. The $1/N$ law is asymptotic and needs
$N$ comfortably larger than the cell count $rc$; below that, the estimator
reports the resolution of the table rather than anything about the data.

**What this is.** The gap between $\hat{I}$ and $I$ is the plug-in bias of
mutual-information estimation, and it is upward: a finite sample always looks
more dependent than the distribution it came from, because unobserved cells
are counted as impossible rather than as rare. Every reported mutual
information between two high-dimensional objects, including the ones plotted
for neural network layers, has to be read against this floor.
<Ref lesson="estimating-mutual-information"/> takes the estimator apart, and
the $1/N$ line you have just plotted is the leading term of its bias.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Mutual information I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y) quantifies, in bits, how much
observing one variable reduces uncertainty about the other. This lesson
computes that drop on the weather table of the previous lesson, defines
mutual information, proves it symmetric with range $0$ to
$\min(H(X), H(Y))$, rewrites it as a divergence from independence, and
closes with a dependent pair that correlation cannot detect.

## Naming the drop: I(X;Y) = H(X) − H(X|Y)

<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/> ended with an unnamed
$0.311$-bit gap. Its weather table, sky
$X \in \{\text{clear}, \text{overcast}\}$ and ground
$Y \in \{\text{dry}, \text{wet}\}$, produced five entropies: $H(X) = 1$,
$H(Y) \approx 0.811$, $H(X,Y) = 1.5$, $H(Y \mid X) = 0.5$, and
$H(X \mid Y) \approx 0.689$ bits.

Before the ground is observed, uncertainty about the sky is $H(X) = 1$
bit. After, an average of $H(X \mid Y) \approx 0.689$ bits remains. The
observation **removed**
$$
H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \approx 1 - 0.689 = 0.311 \text{ bits}.
$$

The same drop appears in the other direction. Observing the sky removes
$H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \approx 0.811 - 0.5 = 0.311$ bits of uncertainty
about the ground. One number measures the coupling from either end.

<Definition title="mutual information">
The **mutual information** between discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$
is
$$
I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y),
$$
measured in bits: the average reduction in the uncertainty of $X$
produced by observing $Y$.
</Definition>

No new primitive enters: every term comes from the joint table by the

conditional entropy as the weighted average of row entropies. The units
follow the log base of the entropies: bits under $\log_2$, nats under

<KeyFact>
$I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$ is the average number of bits that
observing one variable removes from the uncertainty of the other:
$0.311$ bits in the weather table, from either direction.
</KeyFact>

## Symmetry: Y says as much about X as X says about Y

The two drops agreeing at $0.311$ bits is not a feature of the weather
table. The chain rule of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/> forces it for every joint
distribution.

<Theorem title="three forms of mutual information">
For discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$,
$$
I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)
       = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y),
$$
and in particular $I(X;Y) = I(Y;X)$.

<Proof>
The chain rule gives $H(X,Y) = H(Y) + H(X \mid Y)$, so
$H(X \mid Y) = H(X,Y) - H(Y)$. Substituting into the definition:
$$
I(X;Y) = H(X) - \bigl(H(X,Y) - H(Y)\bigr) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y).
$$
The right side is symmetric in $X$ and $Y$: it is unchanged by swapping
them, since $H(X,Y) = H(Y,X)$. Applying the same substitution with the
roles exchanged, using $H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X)$, yields
$H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$. This is
[Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X),
Theorem 2.4.1.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

**Check on the weather table:**
$H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \approx 1 + 0.811 - 1.5 = 0.311$ bits, matching
both drops.

The third form completes the area diagram left open in the previous
lesson: $H(X)$ and $H(Y)$ overlap inside $H(X,Y)$, and the overlap,
counted in both marginals but once in the total, is exactly $I(X;Y)$.
The picture is the bar form of
[MacKay's](https://www.inference.org.uk/itprnn/book.pdf) figure 8.1.

<Remark title="entropy is self-information">
Setting $Y = X$ gives $I(X;X) = H(X) - H(X \mid X) = H(X)$, since
knowing $X$ leaves no uncertainty about $X$: $H(X \mid X) = 0$. A
variable shares its entire entropy with itself, which is why entropy is
also called *self-information*.
</Remark>

<Aside>
The overlap picture is an aid, not a theory. Mutual information is not a
distance: $I(X;X) = H(X) \ne 0$, so "zero iff identical" fails in the
metric direction, and $I$ satisfies no triangle inequality. The picture
also stops at two variables: the natural three-variable analogue of the
overlap, the interaction information $I(X;Y;Z)$, can be negative, so no
Venn diagram with nonnegative areas represents three general variables.
Both points are beyond this course's scope; Cover & Thomas §2.5 treats
the multivariate extensions that do work (chain rules for mutual
information).
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Mutual information is symmetric: $Y$ reveals exactly as many bits about
$X$ as $X$ reveals about $Y$, whatever the joint distribution.
</KeyFact>

## The range: zero at independence, min(H(X), H(Y)) at determinism

Two theorems pin down the possible values of $I(X;Y)$. Both are one-line
consequences of results already proved.

<Theorem title="nonnegativity">
$I(X;Y) \ge 0$, with equality if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are
independent.

<Proof>
Conditioning reduces entropy on average
(<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>, after Cover & Thomas
Theorem 2.6.5): $H(X \mid Y) \le H(X)$, with equality exactly under
independence. Subtracting, $I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \ge 0$, with
equality in the same case.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Theorem title="upper bound">
$I(X;Y) \le \min\bigl(H(X), H(Y)\bigr)$. Equality $I(X;Y) = H(X)$ holds
if and only if $X$ is determined by $Y$ (that is, $H(X \mid Y) = 0$),
and symmetrically for $H(Y)$.

<Proof>
Conditional entropy is an average of entropies, hence nonnegative:
$H(X \mid Y) \ge 0$. So $I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \le H(X)$, with
equality exactly when $H(X \mid Y) = 0$: every value of $Y$ pins down
$X$. The bound $I(X;Y) \le H(Y)$ follows by symmetry.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The bound has an operational reading: observing $Y$ cannot teach more
about $X$ than the total uncertainty $H(X)$ that exists to remove. In
the weather table, $I \approx 0.311 \le \min(1, 0.811)$, and the bound
is slack: neither variable determines the other.

<KeyFact>
$0 \le I(X;Y) \le \min(H(X), H(Y))$: zero exactly at independence, the
full marginal entropy exactly when one variable determines the other.
</KeyFact>

## The dependence dial: independent to lockstep

The two endpoints, and everything between, fit in one family of tables.
Let $X$ and $Y$ be binary with uniform marginals, coupled only through
the *agreement probability* $q = P(Y = X)$: each diagonal cell holds
$q/2$, each off-diagonal cell $(1-q)/2$.

### One table worked: q = 0.75

<Example title="mutual information at q = 0.75">
The table has diagonal cells $\tfrac{3}{8}$ and off-diagonal cells
$\tfrac{1}{8}$; row and column sums are all $\tfrac{1}{2}$, so
$H(X) = H(Y) = 1$ bit.

**Step 1: condition on each value of $Y$.** Given $Y = 0$ (probability
$\tfrac{1}{2}$), the column $(\tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8})$ renormalizes
to $p(x \mid y{=}0) = (\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$, with entropy
$H(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$ bits. Given $Y = 1$, the column
renormalizes to $(\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{3}{4})$: the same entropy.

**Step 2: average.**
$H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.811 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.811
= 0.811$ bits.

**Step 3: subtract.**
$I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \approx 1 - 0.811 = 0.189$ bits.

**Cross-check via the joint form.** The flattened table
$\{\tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}\}$ has
$H(X,Y) = 2 \cdot \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot 1.415 + 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8}
\cdot 3 \approx 1.811$ bits, and
$H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \approx 1 + 1 - 1.811 = 0.189$ bits.
</Example>

### The whole dial in one formula

The computation generalizes: every column conditions $X$ to the
distribution $(q, 1-q)$, so $H(X \mid Y) = H_2(q)$, the binary entropy
function, and
$$
I(X;Y) = 1 - H_2(q).
$$
At $q = \tfrac{1}{2}$ every cell equals $\tfrac{1}{4}$, the product of
the marginals: independence, $I = 0$. At $q = 1$ the variables are in
lockstep and $I = 1$ bit, the full $H(X)$, hitting the upper bound. The
formula is symmetric under $q \leftrightarrow 1-q$: perfect
**disagreement** is as informative as perfect agreement, since swapping
the labels of $Y$ changes no conditional distribution's entropy.

## Divergence from independence

Since $I = 0$ characterizes independence, mutual information should
measure how far the joint distribution sits from the independent one
with the same marginals. Rewriting the definition makes this exact.

<Theorem title="divergence form">
$$
I(X;Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)}.
$$

<Proof>
Write both terms of the definition as sums over the joint table, using
$\sum_y p(x,y) = p(x)$ for the first:
$$
I(X;Y) = -\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(x)
         + \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 p(x \mid y)
       = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y) \log_2 \frac{p(x \mid y)}{p(x)}.
$$
Multiplying numerator and denominator by $p(y)$ and using
$p(x \mid y)\, p(y) = p(x,y)$ gives the stated ratio. Cells with
$p(x,y) = 0$ contribute nothing, by the usual convention. This is the
definition Cover & Thomas §2.3 takes as primary.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The right side compares the true joint $p(x,y)$ against the product
$p(x)\,p(y)$, the table the variables would have if independent with
the same marginals. A later lesson,
<Ref lesson="kl-divergence"/>, names this comparison for arbitrary
distribution pairs: the KL divergence $D(p \Vert q)$, the expected
extra bits paid for coding $p$ with a code built for $q$. Mutual
information is the special case
$I(X;Y) = D\bigl(p(x,y) \,\Vert\, p(x)\,p(y)\bigr)$: the coding price
of pretending the variables are independent.

<Remark title="pointwise terms can be negative">
The per-cell quantity
$\operatorname{pmi}(x,y) = \log_2 \tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}$, the
*pointwise mutual information*, carries a sign: positive where the pair
occurs more often than independence predicts, negative where it occurs
less. In the weather table the cell (overcast, dry) has
$\operatorname{pmi} = \log_2 \tfrac{2}{3} \approx -0.585$ bits.
$I(X;Y)$ is the $p(x,y)$-weighted average of these terms and is never
negative; Exercise 7 runs the full tally. Do not read the nonnegativity
of $I$ as a statement about individual cells.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
$I(X;Y) = D\bigl(p(x,y) \Vert p(x)p(y)\bigr)$: mutual information is
the divergence of the joint from the independent product, which is why
it vanishes exactly at independence and never below zero.
</KeyFact>

## What correlation misses

Zero mutual information means statistical independence. The familiar
weaker diagnostic, the correlation coefficient, certifies much less:
zero correlation rules out only *linear* dependence. A pair can be
deterministically coupled and still uncorrelated.

<Example title="dependent but uncorrelated">
Let $X$ be uniform on $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and $Y = X^2$.

**Correlation.** $\mathbb{E}[X] = 0$ and
$\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X^3]
= \tfrac{1}{3}(-1 + 0 + 1) = 0$, so
$\operatorname{cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,
\mathbb{E}[Y] = 0$: the correlation is exactly zero.

**Mutual information.** $Y$ is a function of $X$, so
$H(Y \mid X) = 0$ and $I(X;Y) = H(Y)$. Since $Y = 1$ with probability
$\tfrac{2}{3}$ and $Y = 0$ with probability $\tfrac{1}{3}$,
$$
I(X;Y) = H(Y) = H\bigl(\tfrac{1}{3}\bigr)
= \log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.918 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Cross-check from the other side.** $H(X) = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585$
bits. Given $Y = 1$, $X$ is uniform on $\{-1, 1\}$: $1$ bit. Given
$Y = 0$, $X = 0$: $0$ bits. So
$H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot 0 =
\tfrac{2}{3}$, and
$I = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \approx 1.585 - 0.667 = 0.918$ bits: the same
value, as symmetry requires.
</Example>

Correlation projects the dependence onto a straight line, and this
dependence is a parabola: the projection vanishes. Mutual information
works on the joint table itself, so no functional shape hides from it.

<KeyFact>
Zero correlation rules out only linear dependence; zero mutual
information rules out **all** dependence. Independence is the stronger,
cleaner condition, and mutual information is its exact witness.
</KeyFact>

This exactness is why mutual information, not correlation, is the
working currency of what follows: the next lesson,
<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>, proves that no processing of
data can increase it, and later lessons build on it as an objective —
<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/> maximizes it over channel inputs,
<Ref lesson="information-bottleneck"/> trades it between compression and
relevance, and <Ref lesson="infonce"/> bounds it from raw samples.

<Exercise>
For the weather table ($p(\text{clear},\text{dry}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$,
$p(\text{clear},\text{wet}) = 0$,
$p(\text{overcast},\text{dry}) = p(\text{overcast},\text{wet}) =
\tfrac{1}{4}$), compute $I(X;Y)$ three ways: as $H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$,
as $H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$, and as $H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y)$. Use the
entropies quoted in the lesson.

<Solution>
From the previous lesson: $H(X) = 1$, $H(Y) \approx 0.811$,
$H(X,Y) = 1.5$, $H(Y \mid X) = 0.5$, $H(X \mid Y) \approx 0.689$ bits.

Route 1: $I = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \approx 1 - 0.689 = 0.311$ bits.
Route 2: $I = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \approx 0.811 - 0.5 = 0.311$ bits.
Route 3: $I = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \approx 1 + 0.811 - 1.5 = 0.311$
bits.

All three agree, as the three-forms theorem requires: they are the same
overlap read off different pairs of bars.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
For the joint table $p(a,c) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, $p(a,d) = \tfrac{1}{4}$,
$p(b,c) = \tfrac{1}{8}$, $p(b,d) = \tfrac{1}{8}$ (the table of the
previous lesson's Exercise 1, with $H(X) \approx 0.8113$,
$H(X,Y) = 1.75$, $H(Y \mid X) \approx 0.9387$ bits), compute $H(Y)$ and
then $I(X;Y)$ by two routes. Use $\log_2 5 \approx 2.3219$ and
$\log_2 3 \approx 1.5850$.

<Solution>
**Marginal of $Y$.** Column sums: $p(c) = \tfrac{5}{8}$,
$p(d) = \tfrac{3}{8}$. Then
$-\log_2 \tfrac{5}{8} = 3 - \log_2 5 \approx 0.6781$ and
$-\log_2 \tfrac{3}{8} = 3 - \log_2 3 \approx 1.4150$, so
$$
H(Y) = \tfrac{5}{8} \cdot 0.6781 + \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot 1.4150
\approx 0.4238 + 0.5306 = 0.9544 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Route 1.** $I = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \approx 0.9544 - 0.9387 = 0.0157$
bits.

**Route 2.** $I = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) \approx 0.8113 + 0.9544 - 1.75
= 0.0157$ bits.

The table is nearly independent: the variables share only about
$0.016$ bits, two orders of magnitude below $\min(H(X), H(Y))
\approx 0.81$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Write out the dependence-dial table at $q = 0.9$ and compute $I(X;Y)$.
Use $H_2(0.9) \approx 0.469$ bits, the entropy of the $90{:}10$ coin
from <Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>. Compare with the
lockstep value at $q = 1$.

<Solution>
Diagonal cells $q/2 = 0.45$, off-diagonal cells $(1-q)/2 = 0.05$;
marginals uniform, $H(X) = 1$ bit.

Each column of the table renormalizes $X$ to $(0.9, 0.1)$ (or its
mirror), so every conditional entropy is $H_2(0.9) \approx 0.469$ bits
and $H(X \mid Y) = 0.469$ bits. Therefore
$$
I(X;Y) = 1 - H_2(0.9) \approx 1 - 0.469 = 0.531 \text{ bits}.
$$
At $q = 1$ the off-diagonal cells vanish, $H(X \mid Y) = 0$, and
$I = 1$ bit: lockstep attains the upper bound $I = H(X)$. The last ten
percentage points of agreement are worth $0.469$ bits, nearly as much
as the first forty ($0.531$ bits): mutual information rises steeply
near determinism.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Let $X$ be uniform on $\{-2, -1, 1, 2\}$ and $Y = X^2$. Show that $X$
and $Y$ are uncorrelated, then compute $I(X;Y)$ by two routes.

<Solution>
**Correlation.** $\mathbb{E}[X] = \tfrac{1}{4}(-2 - 1 + 1 + 2) = 0$ and
$\mathbb{E}[XY] = \mathbb{E}[X^3] = \tfrac{1}{4}(-8 - 1 + 1 + 8) = 0$,
so $\operatorname{cov}(X,Y) = 0 - 0 = 0$.

**Route 1.** $Y$ is a function of $X$, so $H(Y \mid X) = 0$ and
$I = H(Y)$. $Y$ takes values $1$ and $4$ with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$
each: $H(Y) = 1$ bit, so $I(X;Y) = 1$ bit.

**Route 2.** $H(X) = \log_2 4 = 2$ bits. Given $Y = 1$, $X$ is uniform
on $\{-1, 1\}$: $1$ bit; given $Y = 4$, uniform on $\{-2, 2\}$: $1$
bit. So $H(X \mid Y) = 1$ and $I = 2 - 1 = 1$ bit.

The pair shares a full bit, the identity of $|X|$, while the
correlation sees nothing: the dependence is symmetric about zero, so
its linear shadow vanishes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Prove $I(X;X) = H(X)$ from the definition, and evaluate it for one
fair die roll. State why the result names entropy "self-information".

<Solution>
$I(X;X) = H(X) - H(X \mid X)$. Given $X = x$, the value of $X$ is
certain: each conditional distribution is a point mass with entropy
$0$, so the average $H(X \mid X) = 0$ and $I(X;X) = H(X)$.

For a fair die, $I(X;X) = H(X) = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585$ bits.

Reading: mutual information measures the bits one variable carries
about another, and a variable carries about itself exactly its entire
uncertainty. Entropy is the information a variable shares with itself,
hence self-information. This also shows the upper bound
$I \le \min(H(X), H(Y))$ is tight.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Roll a fair die $X$ and set $Y = 1$ if $X \ge 5$, else $Y = 0$ (the
construction of the previous lesson's Exercise 6, where
$H(X) = \log_2 6 \approx 2.585$, $H(Y) \approx 0.918$, and
$H(X \mid Y) = \tfrac{5}{3}$ bits were computed). Compute $I(X;Y)$ by
two routes and verify that it attains the upper bound
$\min(H(X), H(Y))$.

<Solution>
**Route 1.** $Y$ is a function of $X$, so $H(Y \mid X) = 0$ and
$I = H(Y) - 0 = H(\tfrac{1}{3}) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3}
\approx 0.918$ bits.

**Route 2.** $I = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \approx 2.585 - 1.667 = 0.918$
bits: agreement.

**Bound.** $\min(H(X), H(Y)) = \min(2.585, 0.918) = 0.918 = I$. The
bound is attained on the $H(Y)$ side because $Y$ is determined by $X$:
all of $Y$'s uncertainty is shared. It is far from attained on the
$H(X)$ side: knowing $Y$ still leaves $\tfrac{5}{3}$ bits of $X$
unresolved.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Compute the pointwise mutual information
$\operatorname{pmi}(x,y) = \log_2 \tfrac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}$ for each
nonzero cell of the weather table, identify the negative cell, and
verify that the $p(x,y)$-weighted average equals $I(X;Y) \approx 0.311$
bits. Use $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$.

<Solution>
Marginals: $p(\text{clear}) = p(\text{overcast}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$;
$p(\text{dry}) = \tfrac{3}{4}$, $p(\text{wet}) = \tfrac{1}{4}$.

$(\text{clear}, \text{dry})$: product $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{3}{4}
= \tfrac{3}{8}$; ratio $\tfrac{1/2}{3/8} = \tfrac{4}{3}$;
$\operatorname{pmi} = \log_2 \tfrac{4}{3} = 2 - \log_2 3 \approx
0.415$ bits.

$(\text{overcast}, \text{dry})$: product $\tfrac{3}{8}$; ratio
$\tfrac{1/4}{3/8} = \tfrac{2}{3}$;
$\operatorname{pmi} = 1 - \log_2 3 \approx -0.585$ bits — the
negative cell: overcast-and-dry occurs less often than independence
predicts.

$(\text{overcast}, \text{wet})$: product $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot
\tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{8}$; ratio $2$; $\operatorname{pmi} = 1$ bit.

$(\text{clear}, \text{wet})$ has $p = 0$: weight zero, skipped.

**Weighted average:**
$$
\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 0.415 + \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot (-0.585)
+ \tfrac{1}{4} \cdot 1
\approx 0.208 - 0.146 + 0.250 = 0.311 \text{ bits} = I(X;Y).
$$
Individual cells go negative; the average, mutual information, does
not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

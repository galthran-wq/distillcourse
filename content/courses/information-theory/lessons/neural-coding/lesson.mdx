A neuron is a noisy channel from stimulus to spike train, and the mutual
information I(S;R) = H(R) - H(R|S) measures, in bits, how much its response
reveals about what it saw.

This is an off-path chapter inside the course's sixth step, the second of
Shannon's two limits. <Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/> defined a channel as a
transition table $p(y \mid x)$ and its capacity as the largest mutual
information any input distribution can push through that table, and
<Ref lesson="estimating-mutual-information"/> showed that every such quantity
computed from a finite sample is biased upward. Both were stated for links
somebody built. This chapter runs the same machinery on a system nobody
designed, where the transition table has to be measured rather than specified,
and reports what the measurements say. Nothing later in the course depends on
it: <Ref lesson="deployed-codes"/> closes the module on engineered codes and
uses none of what follows.

The route: the discretisation that turns a spike train into a channel output;
the direct method computed end to end on a small table; bits per spike and the
measured values it is compared against; the bin width, which is a choice the
experimenter makes and the answer depends on; coding efficiency and the
question of what belongs in its denominator; and the claims the numbers do and
do not support.

## A spike train becomes a channel output by discretisation

The channel formalism needs a finite output alphabet, and a spike train is a
list of times. The standard conversion is mechanical. Fix a bin width
$\Delta t$ and a window length $T$; cut the record into $L = T/\Delta t$ bins;
write $1$ for a bin containing a spike and $0$ for a bin without one. The
response $R$ is the resulting binary word, one of $2^L$ possibilities.

Take the smallest case, $L = 1$. With $\Delta t = 5$ ms the response is a
single bit: the neuron fired in this bin, or it did not. Present a weak drive
$s_0$ and a strong drive $s_1$ equally often, repeat each many times, and count
how often a spike appears. Suppose the counts come out at one bin in eight
under $s_0$ and seven in eight under $s_1$:

$$
p(r \mid s):\qquad
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & r = 0 & r = 1 \\ \hline
s = s_0 & 7/8 & 1/8 \\
s = s_1 & 1/8 & 7/8
\end{array}
$$

  src="./figures/neuron-as-channel.svg"
  alt="A neuron drawn as a channel: stimulus nodes s0 weak and s1 strong connect to response nodes 0 silent and 1 spike, with reliable arrows carrying 7/8 and crossing noise arrows carrying 1/8, beside the same 2 by 2 transition matrix"
  caption="The neuron as a transition table: the crossing arrows are the trial-to-trial variability, and their entropy is the noise term."
/>

Each row is the response distribution for one stimulus, and the rows are what
the whole of the biophysics reduces to. Membrane currents, synaptic weights and
ion-channel kinetics enter only through these four numbers. The table is the
discrete memoryless channel of <Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/> with the
stimulus as input and the binned response as output, and it happens to be the
symmetric channel $\mathrm{BSC}(1/8)$; nothing below uses the symmetry, and an
asymmetric neuron gives unequal rows and the same arithmetic.

### Where the discretisation holds and where it breaks

<Definition title="binned response word">
For a bin width $\Delta t$ and a window $T$, the **response word** of a trial
is the string $R = (r_1, \ldots, r_L)$ with $L = T/\Delta t$, where $r_i = 1$
if the neuron spiked in bin $i$ and $0$ otherwise. The response alphabet has
$2^L$ elements.
</Definition>

Four cases fix what the definition covers.

- **The canonical case.** $L = 1$, the table above: one bin, two outcomes.
- **A different surface, same structure.** $L = 6$ at $\Delta t = 5$ ms, a
  30 ms window with $2^6 = 64$ possible words. The alphabet is larger and every
  formula below is unchanged.
- **The degenerate case.** $\Delta t = T$, one bin covering the whole window.
  The word collapses to "spiked or not", and any structure in the timing of the
  spikes inside the window is invisible to it. This is the failure mode worked
  out in the bin-width section.
- **The near miss.** A bin width smaller than the neuron's refractory period,
  the dead time after a spike during which the cell cannot fire again, about
  1 ms in most cells. Every clause of the definition still holds and the
  word is still well formed, but consecutive bins are no longer even
  approximately independent: a $1$ forbids a $1$ in the next bin. Nothing
  breaks in the accounting; what breaks is the temptation to treat the $L$ bins
  as $L$ independent uses of the same channel.

<KeyFact>
Everything computed from here on is conditional on $\Delta t$ and $T$, which
the experimenter chooses and the neuron does not. A reported information rate
without its bin width is not a measurement of a neuron; it is a measurement of
a neuron and a discretisation together.
</KeyFact>

## The direct method: total variety minus noise

The table above is a joint distribution, and the quantity to extract from it is
the one <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> defined: $I(S;R) = H(R) - H(R \mid S)$,
the drop in uncertainty about the response caused by knowing the stimulus,
which by symmetry is also the drop in uncertainty about the stimulus caused by
seeing the response. Computing it from binned spike trains rather than from an
assumed model of the neuron is called the **direct method**, and it has exactly
two estimation steps.

**Total response entropy $H(R)$.** Pool the trials from both stimuli and count
words. With equally likely stimuli,
$$
p(R = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{8} + \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{7}{8}
         = \tfrac{1}{2},
$$
so the pooled response is a fair coin and $H(R) = 1$ bit. This is the whole
variety the output has available.

**Noise entropy $H(R \mid S)$.** Hold the stimulus fixed and count words within
that stimulus alone. Each row is a $(7/8, 1/8)$ split, whose binary entropy is
$$
H(\tfrac{1}{8}) = \tfrac{1}{8}\log_2 8 + \tfrac{7}{8}\log_2 \tfrac{8}{7}
= 0.375 + 0.169 = 0.544 \text{ bits},
$$
and both rows give the same value, so $H(R \mid S) = 0.544$ bits. This is the
variety that survives even when the stimulus is known, which is what noise
means here.

**Subtract.**
$$
I(S;R) = H(R) - H(R \mid S) = 1 - 0.544 = 0.456 \text{ bits per bin}.
$$

  caption="The direct method: bin the record, count words within a stimulus for the noise term, pool for the total term, subtract."
/>

<Task>
Step through the four frames and check each number against the raster. The
$s_0$ block holds 4 spikes in 32 bins and the $s_1$ block 28 in 32, which are
the $1/8$ and $7/8$ of the table; the pooled count is 32 spikes in 64 bins,
which is the fair coin behind $H(R) = 1$ bit; and $1 - 0.544 = 0.456$ bits is
what one binned response reveals.
</Task>

At $\Delta t = 5$ ms a second holds 200 bins, so this cell would transmit
$200 \times 0.456 = 91$ bits per second while firing at 100 spikes per second.
Both figures are reported in the literature and both are needed: the rate says
how fast, the per-bin figure says how efficiently.

<KeyFact>
The noise term is what repetition buys. $H(R \mid S)$ can only be estimated
from many trials of the *same* stimulus, so the direct method requires a
stimulus that can be presented again, exactly. Under a stimulus that never
repeats, which is the natural case, neither term is available and the estimate
has to come from a model instead.
</KeyFact>

<Example title="the same number from the stimulus side">
Mutual information is symmetric, so $I(S;R) = H(S) - H(S \mid R)$ must return
$0.456$ bits as well. The stimulus prior is uniform, so $H(S) = 1$ bit.
Condition on the response instead of on the stimulus. Since
$p(R = 0) = p(R = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, Bayes' rule gives, for $R = 0$,
$$
p(s_0 \mid R = 0) = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{7}{8}}{\tfrac{1}{2}}
= \tfrac{7}{8},
$$
so the posterior over stimuli is $(7/8, 1/8)$, with entropy $0.544$ bits;
$R = 1$ is the mirror image, also $0.544$ bits. Averaging over the two
responses, $H(S \mid R) = 0.544$, and $I(S;R) = 1 - 0.544 = 0.456$ bits.

The two routes agree, and they say different things. From the response side,
$0.456$ bits is the part of the cell's output variability that the stimulus
explains. From the stimulus side, it is the part of the observer's uncertainty
about the stimulus that one binned response removes.
</Example>

<Exercise>
A recording gives a neuron with rows $p(r \mid s_0) = (\tfrac{4}{5},
\tfrac{1}{5})$ and $p(r \mid s_1) = (\tfrac{1}{5}, \tfrac{4}{5})$ over
$r \in \{0,1\}$, stimuli equally likely. Decide first which of the two routes
above is cheaper here and name the cue that decides it; then compute $I(S;R)$.
Write down the cue before opening the solution. Use
$H(\tfrac{1}{5}) \approx 0.722$ bits.

<Solution>
**Which route.** The response side, $H(R) - H(R \mid S)$. The cue is that the
experiment hands you the rows of $p(r \mid s)$ directly, so the conditional
term needs no inversion: it is an average of row entropies. The stimulus side
requires a Bayes step per response value before anything can be averaged. The
rule generalises beyond this example: condition on the variable whose
conditional distribution you were given
(<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>).

**Noise term.** Both rows are permutations of $(\tfrac{4}{5}, \tfrac{1}{5})$,
so $H(R \mid S) = H(\tfrac{1}{5}) \approx 0.722$ bits.

**Total term.** $p(R = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{5} +
\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{4}{5} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so $H(R) = 1$ bit.

**Subtract.** $I(S;R) = 1 - 0.722 = 0.278$ bits per bin, against $0.456$ for
the cell in the text. A crossover of $1/5$ rather than $1/8$ costs about 40%
of the transmitted information.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### What a tuning curve throws away

Classical neurophysiology summarises a cell by its tuning curve: mean response
against stimulus, plotted with an error bar. The mean is a function of the row,
so the curve of means is a lossy summary of the table, and the loss is not
cosmetic. Two cells can agree in their mean response at every stimulus and
differ in transmitted information by a factor of 2.6.

<Example title="two neurons with the same tuning curve">
Let the response be a spike count $r \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ per bin and the two
stimuli be equally likely.

**Neuron A.** $p(r \mid s_0) = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, 0)$ and
$p(r \mid s_1) = (0, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$. Pooling gives
$p(R) = (\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4})$, so
$H(R) = 2\cdot\tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 1 = 1.5$ bits. Each row
is uniform on two outcomes, so $H(R \mid S) = 1$ bit and
$I(S;R) = 0.5$ bits.

**Neuron B.** $p(r \mid s_0) = (\tfrac{3}{4}, 0, \tfrac{1}{4})$ and
$p(r \mid s_1) = (\tfrac{1}{4}, 0, \tfrac{3}{4})$. Pooling gives
$p(R) = (\tfrac{1}{2}, 0, \tfrac{1}{2})$, so $H(R) = 1$ bit; each row has
entropy $H(\tfrac{1}{4}) = 0.811$ bits, so
$I(S;R) = 1 - 0.811 = 0.189$ bits.

**The tuning curves.** Neuron A responds with mean $0.5$ spikes to $s_0$ and
$1.5$ to $s_1$. So does neuron B. The two cells are indistinguishable in the
mean at both stimuli, and they differ by a factor of $2.6$ in bits, because
B spreads its mass onto counts that both stimuli produce while A splits its
mass onto counts that only one stimulus produces.

**What the error bar sees.** The collapse is of the mean alone. The customary
error bar is not blind to the difference: A's spread is $0.5$ spikes at each
stimulus and B's is $0.87$, so the plot already hints that something differs.
What the hint cannot do is price it. A spread measures how far the mass sits
from the mean, while the information depends on which counts the two rows
share, and two rows can agree in mean and variance and still differ in bits.

</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/tuning-vs-distributions.svg"
  alt="Two panels of response histograms for the same pair of stimuli: neuron A with tight adjacent distributions transmitting 0.50 bits, neuron B with broad overlapping distributions transmitting 0.19 bits, both with means 0.5 and 1.5 spikes"
  caption="Identical means at both stimuli, different codes: mutual information prices the whole distribution, and the mean cannot see the difference."
/>

The same argument runs one level deeper. A cell whose spikes carry information
in their *placement* rather than their number has a flat tuning curve at every
stimulus and still transmits, and the direct method finds that information
without being told to look for it, because the word distribution already
contains it. The bin-width section builds such a cell.

## Bits per spike

An absolute figure of $0.456$ bits per bin does not compare across experiments,
because bins and firing rates differ. Two normalisations do. Dividing by
elapsed time gives bits per second, and dividing by the mean number of spikes
gives **bits per spike**, which is what most of the measured literature reports.
For the running cell the mean count per bin is
$\mathbb{E}[R] = p(R = 1) = 0.5$ spikes, so
$$
\frac{I(S;R)}{\mathbb{E}[R]} = \frac{0.456}{0.5} \approx 0.91
\text{ bits per spike}.
$$

Nothing forces this ratio to land near one. Its ceiling is
$H(R)/\mathbb{E}[R]$, the response entropy per spike, which grows without bound
as the firing gets sparser, since a rare spike locates itself in time as well
as occurring. At the other end a cell can burn a spike per bin and transmit
almost nothing: neuron B above
spends a mean of $1.0$ spike per bin to deliver $0.189$ bits, which is $0.19$
bits per spike, against neuron A's $0.5$ from the same spike budget.

<Figure
  src="./figures/bits-per-spike-dotplot.svg"
  alt="A dot plot of bits per spike from Borst and Theunissen's Table 2: fly H1 at about 1, monkey MT at 0.6 to 1.5, salamander retina at 1.6, cricket cercal afferents from 0.6 to 3.2, electric fish P-afferents up to 1.2, and frog auditory nerve moving from 1.4 under broadband noise to 7.8 under conspecific calls"
  caption="Measured bits per spike across preparations: within a factor of a few of one bit, and moved fivefold in one cell by the stimulus ensemble."
/>

Across preparations the measured values run from about $0.6$ bits per spike to
a few bits, within a factor of a few of the toy's $0.91$; they are collected in
[Table 2 of Borst and
Theunissen](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn1199_947). The fly
motion-sensitive interneuron H1 — a single cell in the lobula plate, the
motion-processing layer of the fly's visual system — reports wide-field
horizontal image motion and carries about $1$ bit per spike under a randomly
moving grating. Motion-sensitive cells in monkey area MT carry $0.6$ bits per
spike by a decoding estimate and $1.5$ by the direct method on the same
preparation, a
gap the closing section charges to the decoder rather than to the cell. At those
values a spike is not a small contribution to a smoothed rate: one bit halves
the number of stimuli still in contention, so a single spike settles about half
of an ideal observer's uncertainty.

Each of those numbers names a preparation, a stimulus ensemble, and a bin
width, and it has to. The most direct evidence is a single row of the same
table: primary auditory afferents of the frog carry $1.4$ bits per spike at
$46$ bits per second under broadband noise, and $7.8$ bits per spike at $133$
bits per second when the stimulus is a recorded conspecific call, a vocalisation
of the frog's own species
([Rieke, Bodnar and Bialek 1995](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rspb.1995.0204)).
Same cells, same recording technique, a factor of five in bits per spike. The
mutual information is an average over the stimulus distribution, and changing
that distribution changes the answer as surely as changing the cell would.

<KeyFact>
"One bit per spike" is a statement about an ideal observer of the spike train:
it bounds what a decoder with unlimited computation could recover about the
stimulus. It is not a claim that any downstream neuron extracts that bit, and
it is not a claim about what the animal perceives. The data-processing
inequality (<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>) makes the gap
one-directional: everything downstream has at most this much to work with, and
usually less.
</KeyFact>

## The bin width is a choice, and the answer moves with it

The discretisation was introduced as bookkeeping. It is not neutral. A neuron
can place the same number of spikes in different arrangements, and whether
those arrangements are visible depends entirely on $\Delta t$.

<Example title="a code the spike count cannot see">
A cell responds to a 10 ms window with exactly one spike, always. Under $s_0$
that spike falls in the first half of the window with probability $3/4$; under
$s_1$ it falls in the second half with probability $3/4$. At $\Delta t = 5$ ms
the window is two bins and the response word is $10$ or $01$:
$$
p(w \mid s):\qquad
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & w = 10 & w = 01 \\ \hline
s = s_0 & 3/4 & 1/4 \\
s = s_1 & 1/4 & 3/4
\end{array}
$$
The pooled word distribution is $(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$, so $H(R) = 1$
bit; each row has entropy $H(\tfrac{1}{4}) = 0.811$ bits; and
$I(S;R) = 0.189$ bits per window, all of it carried by one spike.

Now set $\Delta t = 10$ ms, one bin for the whole window. Every trial under
every stimulus produces the word $1$. The pooled distribution is a point mass,
$H(R) = 0$, the rows are point masses, $H(R \mid S) = 0$, and
$$
I(S;R) = 0 - 0 = 0 \text{ bits}.
$$
The firing rate is $100$ spikes per second under both stimuli and carries
nothing. The same recording yields $0.189$ bits or $0$ depending on a number
the experimenter typed.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/timing-code-words.svg"
  alt="A raster of trials under two stimuli in which every trial has exactly one spike, early under s0 and late under s1, with the word table and two readouts: 0.189 bits at a 5 ms bin width and 0 bits at a 10 ms bin width"
  caption="Rate-blind, timing-informative: with one bin per window the code is invisible, and the invisibility is a property of the analysis."
/>

The dependence is also a measuring instrument. A coarse bin can only hide
structure, so the estimate should rise as $\Delta t$ falls and then stop rising
once the bins are finer than the precision the cell actually controls. Sweeping
$\Delta t$ and finding where the curve flattens is the standard way to measure
the *limiting spike-timing resolution* of a code, and for H1 it lands at
roughly one millisecond.

What makes the sweep hard is that the alphabet grows as $2^{T/\Delta t}$ while
the data does not. Over a $T = 30$ ms window, $\Delta t = 10$ ms gives $r = 8$
possible words, and $\Delta t = 3$ ms gives $r = 1024$. Plug-in estimates of
mutual information are biased upward by about $(r-1)(c-1)/(2n \ln 2)$ bits with
$r$ response categories, $c$ stimuli and $n$ samples
(<Ref lesson="estimating-mutual-information"/>, which derives the bias and the
corrections for it). With $c = 2$ stimuli and $n = 500$ trials:

$$
\Delta t = 10 \text{ ms}: \frac{7}{2 \cdot 500 \cdot \ln 2} = 0.010
\text{ bits}, \qquad
\Delta t = 3 \text{ ms}: \frac{1023}{2 \cdot 500 \cdot \ln 2} = 1.48
\text{ bits}.
$$

<Figure
  src="./figures/bin-width-sweep.svg"
  alt="Estimated information per 30 ms word against bin width, with a true curve that rises and plateaus at the code's timing resolution and a plug-in curve at 500 trials that keeps rising and leaves the top of the plot at a bin width of 3 milliseconds"
  caption="Two curves that agree at coarse bins and separate at fine ones: the truth plateaus, the plug-in estimate does not, because the bias grows with the alphabet."
/>

At $\Delta t = 3$ ms the artefact is larger than any true value the experiment
could report, since with two stimuli the true $I$ cannot exceed $H(S) = 1$ bit.
Holding the bias below $0.05$ bits at that resolution needs
$n \ge 1023 / (2 \cdot 0.05 \cdot \ln 2) \approx 14{,}800$ repetitions of the
identical stimulus, which is a different experiment from the one that produced
the recording.

Read the two curves at $\Delta t = 10$ ms and at $\Delta t = 3$ ms and compare

</Task>

## Coding efficiency: the ratio, and the fight over its denominator

Bits per spike normalises against the cell's firing. The other normalisation is
against what the cell could have transmitted, which is a ceiling and therefore
requires saying which ceiling.

<Definition title="coding efficiency">
For a neuron with capacity $C$, driven by a stimulus ensemble under which it
transmits $I(S;R)$ bits, the **coding efficiency** is
$$
\eta = \frac{I(S;R)}{C},
$$
the fraction of the channel's capacity the code realises, with
$0 \le \eta \le 1$.
</Definition>

For the running table this is computable exactly. $\mathrm{BSC}(1/8)$ has
capacity $C = 1 - H(\tfrac{1}{8}) = 0.456$ bits per bin, attained by the
uniform input (<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>), and the running ensemble is
uniform, so $\eta = 0.456/0.456 = 1$. A symmetric toy driven by its
capacity-achieving input sits at its ceiling by construction, which says
nothing about neurons and everything about how the example was built.

Change the ensemble and the ratio drops. Present the strong stimulus nine times
as often, $p(s_1) = 0.9$. The rows are unchanged, so $H(R \mid S) = 0.544$
still, but the pooled response is now
$p(R = 1) = 0.9 \cdot \tfrac{7}{8} + 0.1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8} = 0.8$, so
$H(R) = H(0.8) = 0.722$ and
$$
I(S;R) = 0.722 - 0.544 = 0.178 \text{ bits}, \qquad
\eta = \frac{0.178}{0.456} = 0.39 .
$$
The neuron is unchanged and uses under 40% of itself, because an ensemble that
rarely visits the silent response wastes the half of the alphabet that
distinguishes it.

### The denominator an experiment can actually compute

The capacity in that calculation was available because the whole table was
given. In a recording it is not. Capacity is a maximisation over stimulus
ensembles, and an experimenter has one ensemble, the one presented; the rows of
the table for a $2^{L}$-word response were never estimated in the first place.
The practical substitute is a different ceiling: the largest entropy the spike
train could carry at the firing rate and timing resolution observed, with
$I(S;R)$ compared against that. For a single binary bin the maximum entropy at
a fixed mean rate is just the entropy of the observed marginal, so the
substitute ratio is
$$
\eta_H = \frac{I(S;R)}{H(R)} = 1 - \frac{H(R \mid S)}{H(R)} ,
$$
which for the running cell is $0.456 / 1 = 0.46$, not $1$, and for the biased
ensemble $0.178 / 0.722 = 0.25$, not $0.39$. The same recording is 100%
efficient against one denominator and 46% against the other, and the difference
is not an error in either: they answer different questions. The
capacity ratio asks how well the *stimulus ensemble* is chosen for the cell;
the entropy ratio asks how much of the cell's own output variability is signal
rather than noise.

<Exercise>
A cell is measured as $\mathrm{BSC}(1/4)$ under an ensemble with
$p(s_1) = 0.8$. Compute $I(S;R)$, then compute both efficiencies, and say which
denominator a paper reporting "this neuron uses 65% of its channel" must have
used. Use $H(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$ and $H(0.35) \approx 0.934$ bits.

<Solution>
**Transmitted information.** The rows are permutations of
$(\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$, so $H(R \mid S) = 0.811$ bits whatever the
ensemble. Pooled: $p(R = 1) = 0.8 \cdot \tfrac{3}{4} + 0.2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{4}
= 0.65$, so $H(R) = H(0.35) = 0.934$ and $I(S;R) = 0.934 - 0.811 = 0.123$ bits
per bin.

**Capacity ratio.** $C = 1 - H(\tfrac{1}{4}) = 0.189$ bits per bin, so
$\eta = 0.123/0.189 = 0.65$.

**Entropy ratio.** $\eta_H = 0.123/0.934 = 0.13$.

**Which one.** The capacity ratio: $65\%$ is $\eta$, not $\eta_H$. The report
therefore depends on knowing $C$, which here was available only because the
transition table was handed over complete. The transferable move is to read any
published efficiency as a fraction *of a stated ceiling*, and to ask which
ceiling before comparing two efficiencies to each other.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

  alt="Four bars showing transmitted information as a fraction of the response-entropy ceiling: the toy neuron at 46 percent under a uniform ensemble and 25 percent under a biased one, and frog auditory nerve fibres at about 20 percent under broadband noise and about 90 percent under conspecific calls"
  caption="Efficiency is a property of the pairing, not of the cell: the frog bars are the same fibres under two stimulus ensembles."

Measured efficiencies are high and stimulus-dependent in the same way the toy
is. Cercal afferents of the cricket, which report air motion sensed by filiform
hairs, reach about $50\%$ of the entropy their spike
trains could carry at the observed rate, and electric fish P-afferents, which
encode amplitude modulations of the animal's own electric field, reach about
the same. The frog auditory fibres quoted above reach about $20\%$ under
broadband noise and about $90\%$ under conspecific calls. Borst and Theunissen
call $50\%$ a high number, on the ground that the ceiling counts every possible
arrangement of the spikes and a cell attaining half of it is placing its spikes
close to as informatively as their number allows.

<KeyFact>
Efficiency near $90\%$ under natural stimuli and near $20\%$ under artificial
ones is the same statement the capacity lesson made about the maximising input,
observed in a cell rather than chosen by a sender. What the numbers do not
establish is causation: the cell may be matched to its natural inputs, or the
natural inputs may simply be lower-bandwidth. Distinguishing those requires the
experiment, not the arithmetic.
</KeyFact>

## ★ The continuous route this chapter does not take

Everything above kept the response discrete, which is what makes it computable
by hand and consistent with the rest of the course. The literature's main
alternative treats the response as a continuous waveform and prices it with
Gaussian-channel formulas. This section states what that machinery is and what
it buys; nothing else in the chapter depends on it.

### The upper bound: signal-to-noise per frequency

Repeat one stimulus many times, average the responses to get the signal, and
call each trial's deviation from that average the noise. Fourier-transform
both, form the signal-to-noise ratio $\mathrm{SNR}(f)$ per frequency, and
integrate
$$
C = \int \log_2\bigl(1 + \mathrm{SNR}(f)\bigr)\, df .
$$
This is the continuous-channel capacity formula whose single-band form appeared
in <Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>. What it returns is a capacity, the most
any stimulus could push through a response with those power fluctuations, and
it overshoots the recorded ensemble twice over: once through the maximisation,
and once through the Gaussian assumption, which credits the response with the
largest entropy any distribution of that variance could have.

### The lower bound: reconstruct the stimulus and price the reconstruction

Fit a linear filter that reconstructs the stimulus from the spike train, and
compute the information between the stimulus and the reconstruction. Because
the reconstruction is computed from the response, the data-processing
inequality caps it by the true value, so the number is a floor.
For a fly HS cell, a graded-potential motion-sensitive neuron in the same
lobula plate as H1, the pair comes out at $21$ bits per second from below and
$73$ from above on one recording (Borst and Theunissen, Fig. 5, p. 952). Where
the gap between the two goes is readable from the coherence, the per-frequency
correlation between stimulus and reconstruction: below about 10 Hz the
coherence the response noise alone would allow is $0.9$ and the coherence the
linear decoder actually attains is $0.6$, so of the $0.4$ shortfall from a
perfect representation, $0.1$ is response noise and $0.3$ is the cell's
nonlinearity, which the linear decoder cannot follow (same source, p. 955).

  src="./figures/coherence-bounds.svg"
  alt="A stacked bar of coherence from 0 to 1 for a fly HS cell below 10 Hz: 0.6 recovered by a linear filter giving the 21 bits per second lower bound, 0.3 lost to nonlinearity, and 0.1 lost to response noise, with the noise-only level of 0.9 giving the 73 bits per second upper bound"
  caption="Most of the gap between the two bounds is the decoding model, not the cell's noise: nonlinearity accounts for three times what noise does."

### Why the chapter stays discrete

Two reasons keep this off the main path. It needs differential entropy, which
this course uses only where a named result requires it, and it replaces the
direct method's single assumption — a choice of temporal resolution — with a
stack of them: Gaussian signal, Gaussian additive noise, stationarity, and, for
the lower bound, linear decoding. The discrete route makes fewer commitments
and pays for it in data.

## What the numbers can be held to

Three limits apply to every figure quoted above, and they are limits on the
claim, not caveats about precision.

### The estimate is biased upward, and the shuffle measures it

Plug-in entropies computed from empirical frequencies combine so that
$\hat{I}$ sits above $I$, by roughly the $(r-1)(c-1)/(2n \ln 2)$ bits already
used (<Ref lesson="estimating-mutual-information"/>). The diagnostic that
requires no theory is a **shuffled control**: pair each response with a stimulus
drawn independently, destroying any real dependence, and re-run the estimator.
The true mutual information of shuffled data is exactly zero, so whatever comes
back is bias at that sample size and alphabet, and only the excess of the real
estimate over the shuffled one is defensible as signal.

<Figure
  src="./figures/shuffled-control.svg"
  alt="Three horizontal bars in bits per word: a raw estimate at 0.56, a shuffled control at 0.11 whose true value is zero by construction, and their difference at 0.45, which coincides with a dashed line marking the true value"
  caption="The shuffled run is the estimator's reading on data with nothing to read: at 32 words and 200 trials it returns the 0.11 bits the bias formula predicts."
/>

### A decoder gives a floor, not a value

The alternative to counting words is to fit a model that reconstructs the
stimulus $\hat{S}$ from the response and
report $I(S;\hat{S})$. Since $\hat{S}$ is computed from $R$ alone, the chain
$S \to R \to \hat{S}$ is Markov and
$$
I(S;\hat{S}) \le I(S;R)
$$
by the data-processing inequality (<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>).
Improving the decoder can only raise the estimate toward the truth, never past
it, which is why the monkey MT row of the table has $0.6$ bits per spike by
decoding and $1.5$ by the direct method on the same cells: the gap is the
decoder's, not the neuron's.

<KeyFact>
The two error directions do not cancel. A word-counting estimate is biased
*upward* by the finite sample; a decoding estimate is biased *downward* by the
decoder. A published pair therefore brackets the truth and a single published
number does not, which is the case for reporting both. The floor also improves
by a route the direct method has no access to: a better decoder, rather than
more repetitions of a stimulus that may not be repeatable at all
(<Ref lesson="infonce"/>).
</KeyFact>

### The number is defined only relative to the ensemble

$I(S;R)$ averages over $p(s)$, so an absolute bit count without its stimulus
distribution is not a
measurement. This is not a technicality about experimental design; it is the
same fact <Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/> stated when it defined capacity as a
maximum over inputs, met from the other side. A neuron plus an ensemble is a
joint distribution; a neuron alone is a table with no distribution on its rows.

### What this chapter establishes

A spike train becomes a channel output once a bin width and a window are fixed;
the direct method reads $I(S;R)$ off the word
counts as a total entropy minus a noise entropy; normalising by spikes gives
values within a factor of a few of one bit per spike across a range of
preparations, moving fivefold in one cell when the stimulus ensemble changes;
efficiency is that
information over a ceiling that must be named to be interpreted; and every
figure is a plug-in estimate biased upward by an amount that grows with the
alphabet the bin width creates. The formalism needed no adjustment for the
channel being made of protein, which is the point of running it here. Nothing
downstream requires the result: the module's argument continues at
<Ref lesson="deployed-codes"/> with codes that were designed rather than
measured, and the estimation problem met here returns in
<Ref lesson="infonce"/>, where the stimuli are too high-dimensional for any
word count to be attempted.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A neuron fires up to two spikes per bin, with
$p(r \mid s_0) = (\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, 0)$ and
$p(r \mid s_1) = (0, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{3}{4})$ over $r \in \{0, 1, 2\}$,
stimuli equally likely. Before computing anything, name the response outcomes
that identify the stimulus outright and the fraction of trials they account
for, and predict $I(S;R)$ from that fraction alone. Then compute $H(R)$,
$H(R \mid S)$, $I(S;R)$ and the bits per spike, and say whether the prediction
was exact or only an approximation. Use $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ and
$H(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$.

<Solution>
**The prediction.** A response $r = 0$ can only have come from $s_0$ and
$r = 2$ only from $s_1$, so those two outcomes name the stimulus with
certainty; $r = 1$ has the same probability $\tfrac{1}{4}$ under both stimuli,
so it says nothing. The conclusive outcomes carry $\tfrac{3}{8} + \tfrac{3}{8}
= \tfrac{3}{4}$ of the response mass, and each removes the whole $1$ bit of
prior uncertainty about the stimulus, giving
$\tfrac{3}{4}\cdot 1 + \tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 0 = 0.750$ bits.

**Pool.** $p(R) = (\tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{3}{8})$. With
$\log_2 \tfrac{8}{3} = 3 - 1.585 = 1.415$,
$$
H(R) = 2 \cdot \tfrac{3}{8} \cdot 1.415 + \tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 2
= 1.061 + 0.500 = 1.561 \text{ bits}.
$$

**Noise.** Each row is a permutation of $(\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, 0)$,
whose entropy is $H(\tfrac{1}{4}) = 0.811$ bits, so $H(R \mid S) = 0.811$.

**Subtract.** $I(S;R) = 1.561 - 0.811 = 0.750$ bits per bin.

**Bits per spike.** $\mathbb{E}[R \mid s_0] = \tfrac{1}{4}$ and
$\mathbb{E}[R \mid s_1] = \tfrac{1}{4} + 2\cdot\tfrac{3}{4} = 1.75$, so the
mean count is $1.0$ spike and the rate is $0.750$ bits per spike.

**Exact, and why.** The prediction matched the entropy arithmetic to the digit
because every response here is either conclusive or independent of the
stimulus, which is the erasure structure: the trial-by-trial accounting has no
partially informative case to average over. The running cell of the text has no
such split — under $\mathrm{BSC}(1/8)$ a response leaves a $(7/8, 1/8)$
posterior rather than a certainty — so there the counting shortcut is
unavailable and the entropies have to be computed.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A cell fires exactly one spike in every 10 ms window under both stimuli, so its
firing rate carries zero information. Construct a two-bin table under which the
timing carries a full bit, verify the three entropies, and state the largest
bin width at which the code is visible and what the estimate returns above it.
Then say what happens to the true $I$ as $\Delta t$ is made smaller than 5 ms.

<Solution>
**The table.** Split the window into two 5 ms bins and let the cell be
deterministic: $p(10 \mid s_0) = 1$ and $p(01 \mid s_1) = 1$, stimuli equally
likely.

**The entropies.** The pooled word distribution is
$(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$ over $\{10, 01\}$, so $H(R) = 1$ bit. Each row
is a point mass, so $H(R \mid S) = 0$. Hence $I(S;R) = 1 - 0 = 1$ bit per
window, carried by a single spike, and the mean count is $1$ under both
stimuli, so the count-based estimate of the same code is
$H(\text{count}) - H(\text{count} \mid S) = 0 - 0 = 0$.

**The largest visible bin width.** $\Delta t = 5$ ms, which is exactly half the
window. At any $\Delta t \ge 10$ ms the whole window is one bin, every trial
gives the word $1$, both entropies are zero, and the estimate returns $0$ bits.
The transition is sharp rather than gradual because the code here is
deterministic; with the $3/4$ version in the text the estimate at $\Delta t = 5$
ms is $0.189$ bits and still $0$ at $10$ ms.

**Finer bins.** Below 5 ms the true $I$ cannot fall, since a finer word
determines the coarser one and processing cannot add information
(<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>). Here it does not rise either: the
extra bins add response entropy and noise entropy in equal measure, because
where inside its half the spike falls is stimulus-independent by construction.
The plateau of the true curve therefore starts at $\Delta t = 5$ ms, and that
plateau is what a sweep is looking for. An uncorrected plug-in estimate would
keep climbing past it, which is why the plateau is only meaningful after the
bias is subtracted.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim. "We measured this neuron at 1.2 bits per spike, so it carries
1.2 bits per spike about any stimulus." Name the quantity that must be
specified for the claim to be well posed, give a concrete case in which the
same cell yields a different value, and state whether the claim can be repaired
by reporting bits per second instead.

<Solution>
**The step that fails.** $I(S;R)$ is an average over the stimulus distribution
$p(s)$: it is a functional of the joint $p(s)\,p(r \mid s)$, and the experiment
supplies only the second factor. Without a stated ensemble, "1.2 bits per
spike" names no quantity at all, and it certainly does not transfer to a
stimulus set the cell never saw.

**The concrete case.** Frog primary auditory afferents measured under
broadband noise give $1.4$ bits per spike; the same fibres under recorded
conspecific calls give $7.8$. Reporting either number as a property of the
fibre would misstate the other by a factor of five.

**Bits per second does not repair it.** That normalisation divides by time
instead of by spikes, and the ensemble dependence sits in the numerator, which
neither divisor touches: the same frog fibres go from $46$ to $133$ bits per
second across the same two ensembles. What makes a report well posed is naming
the ensemble, the bin width, and the window, and quoting bits per spike or
efficiency alongside the rate so that another laboratory can tell which factor
its own disagreement comes from.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The direct method used $I = H(R) - H(R \mid S)$ without deriving it from the
definition of mutual information as a divergence between the joint and the
product of marginals. Do that derivation, and obtain the other two forms in the
same pass: show that
$$
I(S;R) = H(R) - H(R \mid S) = H(S) - H(S \mid R) = H(S) + H(R) - H(S,R).
$$

<Solution>
Start from the definition (<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>):
$$
I(S;R) = \sum_{s,r} p(s,r) \log_2 \frac{p(s,r)}{p(s)\,p(r)} .
$$

**First form.** Write $p(s,r) = p(s)\,p(r \mid s)$ in the numerator and cancel
the $p(s)$:
$$
I = \sum_{s,r} p(s,r) \log_2 \frac{p(r \mid s)}{p(r)}
= \sum_{s,r} p(s,r)\log_2 p(r \mid s) - \sum_{s,r} p(s,r)\log_2 p(r).
$$
The first sum is $-H(R \mid S)$ by definition of conditional entropy. In the
second, the summand does not depend on $s$, so summing over $s$ marginalises
$p(s,r)$ to $p(r)$ and the sum is $-H(R)$. Hence $I = H(R) - H(R \mid S)$.

**Second form.** The ratio is symmetric under exchanging the roles: write
$p(s,r) = p(r)\,p(s \mid r)$ instead and repeat the same two steps to get
$I = H(S) - H(S \mid R)$.

**Third form.** Split the logarithm of the quotient directly:
$$
I = \sum_{s,r} p(s,r)\log_2 p(s,r) - \sum_{s,r} p(s,r)\log_2 p(s)
- \sum_{s,r} p(s,r)\log_2 p(r) = -H(S,R) + H(S) + H(R).
$$

**What the three forms are for.** They are the same number reached from three
sides, and an experiment picks whichever side its data supplies. Repeated
presentations of a fixed stimulus give the rows, hence $H(R \mid S)$, hence the
first form, which is the direct method. A decoding experiment gives a posterior
over stimuli, hence the second. The third needs the full joint table and is the
one an estimator with limited data can least afford.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Reproduce the bin-width artefact on a simulated cell. Take two stimuli, equally
likely, each driving a homogeneous Poisson neuron whose rate is constant within
the trial and set by the stimulus: $20$ spikes per second under $s_0$ and $80$
under $s_1$, over a $T = 100$ ms window. Estimate $I(S;R)$ by the direct method on a
grid: bin widths $\Delta t \in \{50, 20, 10\}$ ms crossed with trial counts
$n \in \{50, 200, 1000\}$ per stimulus. Report the nine numbers, subtract the
predicted bias from each, and state which axis of the grid moves the raw
estimate more. State what would count as agreement.

<Solution>
**What to compute.** For each cell of the grid, bin every trial into
$L = T/\Delta t$ bins, giving $r = 2^L$ possible words: $r = 4$, $32$ and
$1024$ for the three bin widths. Build the word histogram within each stimulus
for $H(R \mid S)$, pool for $H(R)$, and subtract.

**Predicted bias.** With $c = 2$ and bias $(r-1)(c-1)/(2n \ln 2)$ bits:

$$
\begin{array}{c|ccc}
 & n = 50 & n = 200 & n = 1000 \\ \hline
r = 4 & 0.043 & 0.011 & 0.002 \\
r = 32 & 0.447 & 0.112 & 0.022 \\
r = 1024 & 14.8 & 3.69 & 0.738
\end{array}
$$

**Reading the grid.** The bin-width axis moves the raw estimate far more than
the trial-count axis, because $r$ enters the bias linearly while $n$ enters as
$1/n$, and $r$ doubles with every halving of $\Delta t$. The entry to inspect is
$r = 1024$, $n = 50$: the predicted bias of $14.8$ bits exceeds the largest
value the quantity can take, since with two stimuli $I \le H(S) = 1$ bit. That
is the formula announcing its own domain: it is an asymptotic expansion valid
for $n \gg r$, and at $n < r$ almost every observed word is unique, the pooled
and per-stimulus histograms are equally spiky, and the plug-in estimate
saturates near $1$ bit whatever the neuron does. Subtracting a bias larger than
the ceiling returns nonsense, which is the correct signal that the cell of the
grid should not be reported at all.

**What counts as agreement.** Three checks. First, a shuffled control at the
same $(r, n)$ should return approximately the bias predicted by the formula
wherever $n \gg r$, and it should return roughly $1$ bit wherever $n \lesssim r$.
Second, the corrected estimates in the columns with $n \gg r$ should agree
across trial counts to within their run-to-run spread over repeated
simulations. Third, there is an exact anchor, and it uses the constant rate. For a Poisson
process of constant rate the spike
times given the count are uniform over the window, so they are
stimulus-independent and the whole train
carries exactly what the count carries, and every binned word carries at most
that (<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>). The count values are Poisson
with means $2$ and $8$ over the window, whose mixture entropy is $3.72$ bits
and average conditional entropy $3.00$, so the ceiling is $0.72$ bits. Corrected
estimates should rise toward $0.72$ as $\Delta t$ shrinks — the coarse bins sit
below it because binarisation clips multi-spike bins, and at $\Delta t = 10$ ms
about a fifth of the strong-stimulus bins hold two or more spikes — and must
never pass it. Anything above $0.72$ bits is bias that survived the correction.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Now make the stimulus naturalistic: a 30-minute movie shown to the animal, in
which no frame sequence repeats. The direct method is unavailable, because
$H(R \mid S)$ needs many trials of the same stimulus. Design an estimate that
survives this, along these lines: take the recorded response to a segment,
present a classifier with the true segment and $N-1$ segments drawn from
elsewhere in the movie, and train it to pick the true one. Argue that a
classifier which succeeds establishes a lower bound on $I(S;R)$, and compute
the largest number of bits this scheme could ever certify with $N = 64$.

<Solution>
**Why it is a lower bound.** The classifier's output is computed from the
response and the candidate list, so any information it exhibits about which
candidate is true came through $R$: the chain $S \to R \to \hat{S}$ is Markov
and the data-processing inequality gives $I(S;\hat{S}) \le I(S;R)$. A
classifier that performs above chance therefore certifies that $I(S;R)$ is at
least what its performance implies, and a better classifier can only raise the
floor.

**The ceiling.** The task the classifier solves is to name one of $N$
candidates. That answer is a variable on an alphabet of size $N$, so it carries
at most $\log_2 N$ bits however perfectly the classifier performs. With
$N = 64$ the scheme cannot certify more than $\log_2 64 = 6$ bits per segment,
no matter how informative the neuron is: a cell carrying 6 bits per segment and
a cell carrying 60 produce the same perfect score. Raising the bound requires
raising $N$, and the certificate grows only logarithmically in the number of
impostors.

**Where this goes.** This is the InfoNCE bound, and the exact statement is
$I(S;R) \ge \log_2 N - L_N$, where $L_N$ is the classifier's cross-entropy loss
on the $N$-way task; the saturation at $\log_2 N$ is derived, and the estimator
studied, in <Ref lesson="infonce"/>. The neural case is where the need is
sharpest: no word histogram over naturalistic stimuli will ever be filled in,
and a bound that only requires a trainable discriminator does not need one.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

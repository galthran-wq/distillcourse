A neuron is a noisy channel from stimulus to spike train, and the mutual
information I(S;R) = H(R) - H(R|S) measures, in bits, how much its
response reveals about what it saw. This lesson builds a small
stimulus-response table, computes $I(S;R)$ by the direct method as a total
response entropy minus a noise entropy, divides the result by the mean spike
count to read off bits per spike, measures the code against the channel's
capacity as coding efficiency, and states honestly what a finite sample of
spikes can and cannot certify.

## A neuron is a noisy channel

Present a stimulus to a sensory neuron and record its spikes; present the
same stimulus again and the spike pattern differs. The variability is the
channel's noise. Fix a short time bin and record whether the neuron fired at
least once (a **spike**, $R = 1$) or stayed silent ($R = 0$). Two stimuli, a
weak drive $s_0$ and a strong drive $s_1$, presented with equal frequency,
produce a two-row transition table:

$$
p(r \mid s):\qquad
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & r = 0 & r = 1 \\ \hline
s = s_0 & 7/8 & 1/8 \\
s = s_1 & 1/8 & 7/8
\end{array}
$$

Each row is the response distribution for one stimulus: given $s_0$ the
neuron is usually silent, given $s_1$ it usually fires. Nothing about the
biophysics enters beyond this table: membrane currents, synapses, and ion
channels matter only through the numbers $p(r \mid s)$. This is exactly the
<Ref lesson="channel-capacity">discrete memoryless channel</Ref> of the
channel lessons, relabelled: the stimulus is the input, the binned spike
count is the output.

The table is symmetric (the neural reading of $\mathrm{BSC}(1/8)$), but

<KeyFact>
A neuron is specified, for information purposes, by its transition table
$p(r \mid s)$: one response distribution per stimulus. The trial-to-trial
variability within a row is the channel's noise.
</KeyFact>

## The direct method: I(S;R) = H(R) − H(R|S)

The tuning curve of classical neurophysiology records only the mean response
per stimulus: here $\mathbb{E}[R \mid s_0] = 1/8$ and
$\mathbb{E}[R \mid s_1] = 7/8$. Two means do not say how reliably a downstream
neuron, seeing one spike count, can tell the stimuli apart. Mutual
information does, and it is computed from the same table by the **direct
method**: estimate the total response entropy $H(R)$ and the average
per-stimulus noise entropy $H(R \mid S)$, then subtract.

**Total response entropy $H(R)$.** Marginalize the table over the two equally
likely stimuli:
$$
p(R = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{8} + \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{7}{8}
         = \tfrac{1}{2},
$$
so the pooled response is a fair coin and $H(R) = 1$ bit. This is the full
variety the output could carry.

**Noise entropy $H(R \mid S)$.** Each row is a $(7/8, 1/8)$ split, whose
binary entropy is
$$
H(\tfrac{1}{8}) = -\tfrac{1}{8}\log_2\tfrac{1}{8}
- \tfrac{7}{8}\log_2\tfrac{7}{8} = 0.375 + 0.169 = 0.544 \text{ bits}.
$$
Both rows give the same value, so the weighted average is
$H(R \mid S) = 0.544$ bits: the response entropy that survives even when the
stimulus is known, which is noise by definition.

**Subtract.**
$$
I(S;R) = H(R) - H(R \mid S) = 1 - 0.544 = 0.456 \text{ bits per bin}.
$$

<Task>
Step through the three frames and check the arithmetic at each stop: the
pooled response is $(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})$ so $H(R) = 1$; each row
carries $H(\tfrac{1}{8}) = 0.544$ so $H(R \mid S) = 0.544$; the difference
$1 - 0.544 = 0.456$ bits is what one binned response reveals about the
stimulus.
</Task>

The recipe is the entropy and <Ref lesson="mutual-information">mutual-information</Ref>
machinery applied verbatim, with $S$ and $R$ in place of the weather
variables. Its neural name, the *direct method*, is due to
[Strong et al. (1998)](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.80.197),
who estimated both entropies straight from binned spike trains of the fly
motion-sensitive neuron H1 rather than from any model of the neuron.

<Example title="the same number from the stimulus side">
Mutual information is symmetric, so the same $0.456$ bits must come out of
$I(S;R) = H(S) - H(S \mid R)$. The stimulus prior is uniform, $H(S) = 1$ bit.
Condition on the response instead. Since $p(R = 0) = p(R = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}$,
Bayes' rule gives, for $R = 0$,
$$
p(s_0 \mid R = 0) = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{7}{8}}{\tfrac{1}{2}}
= \tfrac{7}{8},
$$
so the posterior over stimuli is $(7/8, 1/8)$ with entropy $0.544$ bits;
$R = 1$ is the mirror image, also $0.544$ bits. Averaging,
$H(S \mid R) = 0.544$, and
$$
I(S;R) = 1 - 0.544 = 0.456 \text{ bits},
$$
matching the response-side computation. A downstream neuron that sees one
spike bin resolves $0.456$ of the $1$ bit of stimulus uncertainty.
</Example>

<KeyFact>
The direct method computes $I(S;R) = H(R) - H(R \mid S)$ straight from the
response table: total response entropy minus the average within-stimulus
(noise) entropy — no model of the neuron required.
</KeyFact>

## Mutual information beats the tuning curve

The tuning curve reports a mean and, at best, an error bar per stimulus. Two
neurons with identical tuning curves can carry very different information: if
the trial-to-trial response distributions overlap heavily, a single response
barely distinguishes the stimuli even when the means are far apart; if they
are tight and separated, one response nearly settles the question. The mean
cannot see this; the full distributions $p(r \mid s)$ can, and $I(S;R)$ reads
it off directly.

Mutual information also needs no assumption that the code is a firing rate. A
neuron that signals with precise spike *timing* rather than average rate has
a response distribution over timing patterns, and the direct method applies
unchanged once those patterns are binned — which is why
[Strong et al.](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.80.197) could
measure the information in H1's spike timing at millisecond resolution
without committing to any rate code.

<KeyFact>
The tuning curve keeps means; mutual information keeps whole response
distributions, and so measures discriminability the mean cannot — including
information carried by spike timing rather than rate.
</KeyFact>

## Bits per spike

An absolute figure of $0.456$ bits per bin is hard to compare across neurons
and experiments, because bins and firing rates differ. Dividing by the mean
number of spikes normalizes it. In the running example the mean count per bin
is
$$
\mathbb{E}[R] = p(R = 1) = 0.5 \text{ spikes},
$$
so the code delivers
$$
\frac{I(S;R)}{\mathbb{E}[R]} = \frac{0.456}{0.5} \approx 0.91 \text{ bits per spike}.
$$
Each spike, in this idealized neuron, is worth close to one bit: an
information-bearing symbol, not merely a tick contributing to an average
rate.

The value $0.91$ is a property of this constructed table. What makes it worth
computing is that real recordings land in the same neighbourhood.
[Borst & Theunissen (1999)](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn0999_947)
collect measurements across species in their Table 2: bits per spike
clustered near **1**, with a reported range of roughly $0.6$ to $3.2$ across
preparations. The number is an empirical regularity of recorded neurons, not
a theorem: it depends on the stimulus ensemble, the bin width, and the
species. Its order of magnitude is nonetheless robust, and it is the reason
spike trains are read as information-bearing codes rather than as smoothed
rates.

<Example title="a neuron that wastes its spikes">
Not every neuron approaches a bit per spike. Take a neuron whose two
stimulus rows are $p(r \mid s_0) = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$
and $p(r \mid s_1) = (\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{2})$ over spike
counts $r \in \{0, 1, 2\}$, with uniform stimuli. Pooling gives
$p(R) = (\tfrac{3}{8}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{3}{8})$, so
$$
H(R) = 2\cdot\tfrac{3}{8}\log_2\tfrac{8}{3} + \tfrac{1}{4}\cdot 2
\approx 1.56 \text{ bits}.
$$
Each row is a permutation of $(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$
with entropy $1.5$ bits, so $H(R \mid S) = 1.5$ and
$I(S;R) \approx 0.06$ bits. The mean count is
$\tfrac{1}{2}(0.75 + 1.25) = 1$ spike, so this neuron spends a full spike per
bin to transmit $0.06$ bits: about $0.06$ bits per spike. High firing, almost
no information: the metric exposes a code that a tuning curve, showing a
clean difference in means, would flatter.
</Example>

<KeyFact>
Dividing $I(S;R)$ by the mean spike count gives bits per spike; recorded
single neurons cluster near one bit per spike (Borst & Theunissen, Table 2),
an empirical range across preparations, not a universal constant.
</KeyFact>

## Coding efficiency: I(S;R) / C

The neuron's channel has a ceiling. Its
<Ref lesson="channel-capacity">capacity</Ref> is the largest mutual
information any stimulus ensemble could push through the fixed table,
$C = \max_{p(s)} I(S;R)$, and **coding efficiency** is the fraction of that
ceiling a given code actually uses.

<Definition title="coding efficiency">
For a neuron with capacity $C$ driven by a stimulus ensemble that transmits
$I(S;R)$ bits, the **coding efficiency** is
$$
\eta = \frac{I(S;R)}{C},
$$
the fraction of the channel's capacity the code realizes, $0 \le \eta \le 1$.
</Definition>

For the symmetric running table, $\mathrm{BSC}(1/8)$, the capacity is
$C = 1 - H(\tfrac{1}{8}) = 1 - 0.544 = 0.456$ bits per bin, attained by the
uniform stimulus prior (<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>). The running
example uses exactly that prior, so it sits at the ceiling by construction:
$\eta = 0.456 / 0.456 = 1$. This is an artifact of a symmetric toy driven by
its capacity-achieving input, not a claim that neurons choose optimal codes.

Change the ensemble and efficiency drops. Suppose the strong stimulus $s_1$
is presented nine times as often, $p(s_1) = 0.9$. The noise term is
unchanged, $H(R \mid S) = 0.544$, but the pooled response is now
$p(R = 1) = 0.9\cdot\tfrac{7}{8} + 0.1\cdot\tfrac{1}{8} = 0.8$, so
$H(R) = H(0.8) = 0.722$ and
$$
I(S;R) = 0.722 - 0.544 = 0.178 \text{ bits}, \qquad
\eta = \frac{0.178}{0.456} \approx 0.39.
$$
The same neuron now uses under $40\%$ of its channel: a stimulus set that
rarely exercises the silent response wastes the capacity.

Real measurements show the same stimulus dependence far more sharply.
[Borst & Theunissen](https://www.nature.com/articles/nn0999_947) review cases
in which a neuron encodes a naturalistic stimulus with efficiency approaching
$90\%$ while encoding a white-noise stimulus of matched power at a fraction of
that — on the order of $20\%$ in bullfrog auditory afferents. Sensory neurons
appear tuned to the statistics of their natural inputs, and efficiency, unlike
an absolute bit count, is comparable across those conditions.

<Aside>
Capacity for a continuous response. Real spike trains are not binary, and
their capacity is usually estimated through the Gaussian-channel bound rather
than a discrete maximization. Treating the response as a signal plus
independent Gaussian noise across frequency bands gives
$$
C = \int \log_2\!\bigl(1 + \mathrm{SNR}(f)\bigr)\, df,
$$
the continuous-channel formula whose single-band form
$C = \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2(1 + P/N)$ was cited in
<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>. Borst & Theunissen's Box 3 develops the
coherence-based upper and lower bounds built on it. The continuous derivation
is out of scope here; this course stays with the discrete table, where the
capacity is the same maximization the channel lessons already ran.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Coding efficiency $\eta = I(S;R) / C$ is the fraction of channel capacity a
code uses. It is a property of the stimulus ensemble, not of the neuron
alone: naturalistic stimuli raise it, and mutual information is only
meaningful relative to a stated ensemble.
</KeyFact>

## What the estimate can and cannot claim

Every number above assumed the true probabilities $p(r \mid s)$. In an
experiment they are estimated from finitely many trials, and that introduces
a bias with a definite sign.

**Finite samples overestimate.** Entropy estimated by plugging empirical
frequencies into $-\sum \hat{p}\log_2\hat{p}$ is biased, and for mutual
information the biases combine so that the plug-in estimate sits *above* the
true value. The clean diagnostic is a **shuffled control**: randomly repair
each response with a stimulus drawn independently, destroying any real
dependence, and re-estimate. The true mutual information of shuffled data is
zero, so whatever the estimator returns, often a visibly nonzero number, is
pure bias at that sample size. Corrections that extrapolate the bias away
(for instance a jackknife over subsets of the data) exist; the point here is
that a raw positive estimate is not, by itself, evidence of a code.

**A decoder only lower-bounds the information.** A common alternative to the
direct method fits a model that reconstructs the stimulus from the response
and reports the information in the reconstruction. Because the reconstruction
$\hat{S}$ is computed from $R$ alone, the chain $S \to R \to \hat{S}$ is
Markov, and the <Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality">data-processing
inequality</Ref> gives
$$
I(S; \hat{S}) \le I(S; R).
$$
Any decoding-based estimate is therefore a *lower* bound on the true mutual
information: a better decoder can only raise it toward $I(S;R)$, never past
it. Borst & Theunissen (p. 949) make exactly this point in separating the
direct method from reconstruction methods.

**The number is relative to the ensemble.** Because $I(S;R)$ averages over
$p(s)$, it is defined only once the stimulus ensemble is fixed. Absolute bit
counts do not transfer between experiments with different stimuli; efficiency
and bits per spike, which normalize against capacity and spike count, are the
quantities that compare. The finite-sample MI-estimation problem is the same
obstacle that <Ref lesson="infonce">InfoNCE</Ref> sidesteps for
high-dimensional data by replacing a plug-in entropy estimate with a
sample-based bound that stays valid without ever filling in a joint table.

<KeyFact>
Finite-sample mutual information is biased upward (a nonzero shuffled control
is bias, not signal); decoding-based estimates only lower-bound the truth by
the data-processing inequality; and every value is relative to the stimulus
ensemble it was averaged over.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A neuron has the binned response table $p(r \mid s_0) = (\tfrac{3}{4},
\tfrac{1}{4})$ and $p(r \mid s_1) = (\tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{3}{4})$ over
$r \in \{0, 1\}$, with equally likely stimuli. Compute $I(S;R)$ by the direct
method and the bits per spike. Use $H(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$ bits.

<Solution>
**Pool.** $p(R = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{2}
\cdot\tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so $H(R) = 1$ bit.

**Noise.** Each row is a $(\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4})$ split, entropy
$H(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$; averaging, $H(R \mid S) = 0.811$.

**Subtract.** $I(S;R) = 1 - 0.811 = 0.189$ bits per bin.

**Bits per spike.** $\mathbb{E}[R] = p(R = 1) = 0.5$ spikes, so
$0.189 / 0.5 = 0.377$ bits per spike. This noisier neuron (crossover
$\tfrac{1}{4}$ against the lesson's $\tfrac{1}{8}$) carries less than half the
information per spike of the running example.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A different neuron behaves as $\mathrm{BSC}(1/5)$ (crossover $\tfrac{1}{5}$)
with equally likely stimuli. Compute $I(S;R)$ from the stimulus side, as
$H(S) - H(S \mid R)$ using the posterior over stimuli, and confirm it equals
the response-side value $H(R) - H(R \mid S)$. Use $H(\tfrac{1}{5}) \approx
0.722$ bits. This is the symmetry of mutual information from
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>.

<Solution>
**Stimulus side.** $H(S) = 1$ bit (uniform prior). The pooled response is
uniform, $p(R = 0) = p(R = 1) = \tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{4}{5} +
\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{5} = \tfrac{1}{2}$. By Bayes, for $R = 0$,
$p(s_0 \mid R = 0) = (\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{4}{5}) / \tfrac{1}{2} =
\tfrac{4}{5}$, so the posterior is $(\tfrac{4}{5}, \tfrac{1}{5})$ with entropy
$H(\tfrac{1}{5}) = 0.722$ bits; $R = 1$ is the mirror image, also $0.722$.
Averaging, $H(S \mid R) = 0.722$, hence $I(S;R) = 1 - 0.722 = 0.278$ bits.

**Response side.** $H(R) = 1$ bit (pooled response uniform); each row is a
$(\tfrac{4}{5}, \tfrac{1}{5})$ split, so $H(R \mid S) = H(\tfrac{1}{5}) =
0.722$, and $H(R) - H(R \mid S) = 1 - 0.722 = 0.278$ bits. The two routes
agree, as the symmetry of mutual information guarantees.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Treat a neuron as $\mathrm{BSC}(1/4)$ (crossover $\tfrac{1}{4}$). (a) Compute
its capacity $C$. (b) Under the biased ensemble $p(s_1) = 0.8$, compute
$I(S;R)$ and the coding efficiency $\eta = I(S;R)/C$. Use
$H(\tfrac{1}{4}) \approx 0.811$ and $H(0.35) \approx 0.934$ bits. This uses
the capacity recipe of <Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>.

<Solution>
**(a)** For a symmetric channel the noise term is $H(\tfrac{1}{4}) = 0.811$
regardless of input, and the uniform stimulus maximizes $H(R)$ at $1$ bit, so
$C = 1 - 0.811 = 0.189$ bits per bin.

**(b)** Noise term unchanged: $H(R \mid S) = 0.811$. Pooled response:
$p(R = 1) = 0.8\cdot\tfrac{3}{4} + 0.2\cdot\tfrac{1}{4} = 0.6 + 0.05 = 0.65$,
so $H(R) = H(0.35) \approx 0.934$. Then $I(S;R) = 0.934 - 0.811 = 0.123$
bits, and $\eta = 0.123 / 0.189 \approx 0.65$. The biased ensemble uses about
$65\%$ of the channel; the uniform ensemble would reach $\eta = 1$ because it
is the capacity-achieving input for this symmetric neuron.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A decoder observes the binned response $R$ of the running example and outputs
a stimulus guess $\hat{S}$. (a) For the identity decoder $\hat{S} = s_1$ if
$R = 1$ else $s_0$, compute $I(S; \hat{S})$. (b) For the constant decoder
$\hat{S} = s_1$ always, compute $I(S; \hat{S})$. (c) State which theorem
guarantees neither exceeds $I(S;R) = 0.456$ bits.

<Solution>
**(a)** With $\hat{S}$ a relabelling of the binary $R$, the joint table of
$(S, \hat{S})$ is the table of $(S, R)$ with $r = 1 \mapsto s_1$,
$r = 0 \mapsto s_0$. So $I(S; \hat{S}) = I(S;R) = 0.456$ bits: for a binary
response, the identity decoder loses nothing, because $R$ is already a
sufficient statistic.

**(b)** A constant $\hat{S}$ is independent of $S$, so
$I(S; \hat{S}) = 0$ bits.

**(c)** The chain $S \to R \to \hat{S}$ is Markov ($\hat{S}$ depends on $S$
only through $R$), so the data-processing inequality gives
$I(S; \hat{S}) \le I(S;R) = 0.456$. Both decoders respect it: $0.456 \le
0.456$ and $0 \le 0.456$. A decoding-based estimate is a lower bound on the
true information.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
An experimenter estimates $\hat{I}(S;R) = 0.30$ bits from a small number of
trials, then repeats the estimate after randomly shuffling which response
goes with which stimulus and obtains $\hat{I}_{\text{shuffled}} = 0.08$ bits.
Interpret the shuffled value, and give a corrected estimate of the true
mutual information.

<Solution>
Shuffling destroys any real stimulus-response dependence, so the true mutual
information of the shuffled data is exactly $0$. The estimate
$\hat{I}_{\text{shuffled}} = 0.08$ bits is therefore entirely the positive
bias of the plug-in estimator at that sample size. A first-order correction
subtracts it: $\hat{I}_{\text{corrected}} \approx 0.30 - 0.08 = 0.22$ bits.
The raw $0.30$ overstated the code by roughly a third; only the excess of the
estimate over its shuffled control is defensible as signal, and even that is
best confirmed with more data or a jackknife extrapolation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A neuron fires up to two spikes per bin, with
$p(r \mid s_0) = (\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8})$ and
$p(r \mid s_1) = (\tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{3}{4})$ over
$r \in \{0, 1, 2\}$, with uniform stimuli. Compute $H(R)$, $H(R \mid S)$,
$I(S;R)$, and the bits per spike. Use $\log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ and
$\log_2 7 \approx 2.807$.

<Solution>
**Pool.** $p(R) = (\tfrac{7}{16}, \tfrac{1}{8}, \tfrac{7}{16})$. With
$\log_2\tfrac{16}{7} = 4 - 2.807 = 1.193$:
$H(R) = 2\cdot\tfrac{7}{16}\log_2\tfrac{16}{7} + \tfrac{1}{8}\log_2 8
= 2\cdot 0.4375\cdot 1.193 + 0.125\cdot 3 = 1.044 + 0.375 = 1.419$ bits.

**Noise.** Each row is a permutation of $(\tfrac{3}{4}, \tfrac{1}{8},
\tfrac{1}{8})$ with entropy
$\tfrac{3}{4}\log_2\tfrac{4}{3} + 2\cdot\tfrac{1}{8}\log_2 8
= 0.75\,(2 - 1.585) + 2\cdot 0.125\cdot 3 = 0.311 + 0.75 = 1.061$ bits, so
$H(R \mid S) = 1.061$.

**Subtract.** $I(S;R) = 1.419 - 1.061 = 0.358$ bits per bin.

**Bits per spike.** $\mathbb{E}[R \mid s_0] = \tfrac{1}{8} + 2\cdot\tfrac{1}{8}
= 0.375$, $\mathbb{E}[R \mid s_1] = \tfrac{1}{8} + 2\cdot\tfrac{3}{4} = 1.625$,
mean $1.0$ spike. So $0.358 / 1.0 = 0.358$ bits per spike: by placing its
spikes to distinguish the two stimuli, this neuron gets about a third of a bit
from each spike, far above the wasteful neuron of the earlier example.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Explain, in two or three sentences, why "this neuron carries $2.4$ bits" is an
incomplete claim, and what two normalized quantities make information measures
comparable across experiments.

<Solution>
Mutual information averages over the stimulus prior $p(s)$, so a raw bit count
is defined only relative to the stimulus ensemble used; the same neuron
reports different $I(S;R)$ under different ensembles, and the number does not
transfer to another experiment's stimuli. The comparable quantities are bits
per spike ($I / \mathbb{E}[R]$), which normalizes against firing, and coding
efficiency ($I / C$), which normalizes against the neuron's own channel
capacity.
</Solution>

Reliable communication is possible at every rate below the channel capacity
and impossible above it: capacity is a sharp threshold, not a soft
trade-off. This lesson states the theorem, gives the counting argument
behind each half of it, and reports what the argument costs at blocklengths
anyone can build.

The course now has a limit on each side of a communication link.
<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/> fixed the source side: entropy is
the floor on bits per symbol, forced by the budget that codeword lengths
spend and matched from above by the count of typical sequences.
<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/> fixed the channel side only as a number,
$C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y)$, an optimization over a transition table with no
statement attached about what a communication system can do. This lesson
attaches the statement, and in doing so gives mutual information its
operational meaning: a rate, not a descriptive statistic. It also
establishes that the proof exhibits no code, which is the premise of
<Ref lesson="hamming-codes"/> and <Ref lesson="deployed-codes"/>.

The route: the repetition code and the wrong law it suggests, the
definitions of code, rate and achievability, the theorem, the
joint-typicality counting that proves the achievable half, the three-line
converse, and the finite-blocklength correction that says what the
threshold costs at $n = 100$.

## Repetition buys reliability only by spending the whole rate

Fix the channel of <Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>: $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$,
each transmitted bit flipped independently with probability $f = 0.1$,
capacity $C = 1 - H_2(0.1) \approx 0.531$ bits per use. Sent raw, one data
bit in ten arrives wrong. The oldest countermeasure is repetition:
transmit every data bit three times, decode by majority vote.

<Example title="the repetition code R3 on BSC(0.1)">
Each data bit becomes three channel bits; the decoder outputs the majority
of the three received copies. The vote errs exactly when at least two of
the three copies flip:
$$
p_b = 3f^2(1 - f) + f^3 = 3 \cdot 0.01 \cdot 0.9 + 0.001 = 0.028.
$$
Per-bit error falls from $0.1$ to $0.028$, a factor of $3.6$. The price is
paid in rate: three channel uses now carry one data bit, $R = \tfrac{1}{3}$
against the raw code's $R = 1$.
</Example>

Repeating $N$ times continues the pattern. The majority vote fails only
when more than half the copies flip, an event whose probability falls
exponentially in $N$, while the rate falls only as $1/N$. Exponential
against linear looks like a good trade until a reliability target is named.
For $p_b \le 10^{-15}$, the error rate a disk drive is sold against,
$N = 61$ copies are needed, at rate $\tfrac{1}{61} \approx 0.016$: sixty-one
noisy drives to build one reliable one.

  caption="Repetition on BSC(0.1): every decade of reliability costs most of the remaining rate, and the family converges on rate zero."
/>

The family suggests a law, and the law is the one the communication
engineering of the 1940s took for granted: **reliability is bought with
rate**, and driving error to zero drives rate to zero with it. Shannon
opens his §13 by stating the expectation in those words: "one would
expect, however, that to make this probability of errors approach zero,
the redundancy of the encoding must increase indefinitely, and the rate of
transmission therefore approach zero." His next sentence answers it: "This
is by no means true."

The inference generalizes from one family of codes to all codes,
and the failure is specific: repetition spends its redundancy on each data
bit separately, so the decoder never gets to average a decision over many
uses of the channel. A code that spreads one message across a long block
gets to exploit the law of large numbers. The fraction of flipped
positions in a long block is close to $f$ with overwhelming probability,
and a code can be built to distinguish messages that differ in far more
than $fn$ positions. The replacement model is the theorem below: the
boundary between reliable and unreliable sits at $R = C = 0.531$, not at
$R = 0$, and the entire band between $0.016$ and $0.531$ that repetition
never enters is achievable at any error level.

<KeyFact>
On this channel the theorem asserts a code of rate $0.45$ with block error
$10^{-15}$, twenty-seven times the rate repetition needs for the same
reliability. It asserts this without producing one, and no code family
came within a decibel of the boundary until the 1990s
(<Ref lesson="deployed-codes"/>).
</KeyFact>

## Codes, rates, and what "achievable" means

R3 is one instance of a general shape: it assigns $M = 2$ messages the
codewords $000$ and $111$ of length $n = 3$ and maps each received triple
back to a message. Replacing $2$ and $3$ by parameters gives the object the
theorem quantifies over.

<Definition title="block code and rate">
An **$(M, n)$ block code** for a channel $p(y \mid x)$ consists of an
encoder assigning each message $w \in \lbrace 1, \ldots, M \rbrace$ a
codeword $x^n(w) \in \mathcal{X}^n$, and a decoder
$g : \mathcal{Y}^n \to \lbrace 1, \ldots, M \rbrace$. Its **rate** is
$$
R = \frac{\log_2 M}{n} \text{ bits per channel use},
$$
so a rate-$R$ code carries $M = 2^{nR}$ distinct messages on $n$ uses.
</Definition>

Four instances fix the boundaries of the definition. R3 is the $(2,3)$ code
of rate $\tfrac{1}{3}$. A structurally different instance with the same
shape is the $(16, 7)$ Hamming code of <Ref lesson="hamming-codes"/>, which
carries four message bits on seven uses at rate $\tfrac{4}{7} \approx
0.571$ and corrects any single flip. The degenerate case is $M = 1$: one
message, rate $0$, and error probability zero for any channel whatsoever,
which is why the theorem's content is entirely about positive rates. The
near miss is a *variable*-rate scheme in which the encoder stops when it
judges the message delivered; that is not an $(M,n)$ code, because $n$ is
fixed in advance, and treating it as one is the commonest way to
misread the theorem's scope.

Two error probabilities are in play, and the difference matters in the
proof. The **average** block error $P_e^{(n)}$ averages over a uniformly
chosen message; the **maximal** block error $\lambda^{(n)} = \max_w
\Pr\lbrace g(Y^n) \ne w \mid W = w \rbrace$ takes the worst message. A code
can have tiny average error while one unlucky message fails half the time,
so the guarantee worth having is the maximal one.

<Definition title="achievable rate">
A rate $R$ is **achievable** if there is a sequence of $(2^{nR}, n)$ codes,
one for each $n$, whose maximal block error probability $\lambda^{(n)}$
tends to $0$ as $n \to \infty$.
</Definition>

The quantifier order is the whole content of the definition. The rate is
held fixed while $n$ grows, and only then is the error required to vanish.
The repetition family fails this test not because its errors are large but
because its rate is not fixed: $R_N$ drives $p_b \to 0$ along a sequence
whose rate $1/N$ also tends to $0$, so it witnesses nothing about any
positive rate.

<Remark title="arbitrarily small is not zero">
Achievability asks for error tending to zero, never for error equal to
zero at some finite $n$. On $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$ every output sequence has
positive probability under every input sequence, so no two codewords can
ever be told apart with certainty: the *zero-error* capacity of the BSC is
$0$, while its capacity is $0.531$. Zero-error capacity is a different
quantity, generally smaller and, for most channels, still unknown.
</Remark>

## The theorem: the achievable rates are exactly the rates below C

Both halves are now statable. One says that the counting works out
whenever $R < C$; the other says it fails whenever $R > C$.

<Theorem title="noisy-channel coding theorem (Shannon 1948, Theorem 11)">
Let a discrete memoryless channel have capacity $C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y)$.

**Achievability.** Every rate $R < C$ is achievable: there exist
$(2^{nR}, n)$ codes with $\lambda^{(n)} \to 0$.

**Converse.** No rate above $C$ is achievable: every $(2^{nR}, n)$ code
with $R > C$ has average block error
$$
P_e^{(n)} \;\ge\; 1 - \frac{C}{R} - \frac{1}{nR},
$$
which is bounded away from $0$ as $n \to \infty$.
</Theorem>

> **Theorem 11.** Let a discrete channel have the capacity $C$ and a discrete
> source the entropy per second $H$. If $H \le C$ there exists a coding system
> such that the output of the source can be transmitted over the channel with
> an arbitrarily small frequency of errors (or an arbitrarily small
> equivocation). If $H > C$ it is possible to encode the source so that the
> equivocation is less than $H - C + \epsilon$ where $\epsilon$ is arbitrarily
> small. There is no method of encoding which gives an equivocation less than
> $H - C$.
>
> — Shannon 1948, §13

The two directions are separate arguments with separate tools, and neither
implies the other. Achievability is a probabilistic existence proof: it
constructs a random object and shows the average behaves, which forces some
instance to behave. The converse is an accounting identity: it counts
information along the pipeline and shows the books do not balance above
$C$. Shannon's own statement uses equivocation rather than error
probability, and adds the exact floor for the impossible side: above
capacity the leftover uncertainty per use cannot be pushed below $H - C$,
however the code is designed.

## Achievability: a random codebook wins a counting race

The obstacle to proving achievability is scale. At $n = 1000$ and
$R = 0.4$ a codebook assigns $2^{400} \approx 10^{120}$ messages their
thousand-bit codewords; no explicit construction of that size was known in
1948, and analyzing any particular one is hopeless. The proof sidesteps
both problems in three moves: a decoder whose failure analysis needs only
counts of typical sequences, an average over every codebook in place of any
particular one, and a discard step that converts the average guarantee into
a worst-case one.

### The counts: how many inputs could have produced this output

<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/> established the typical set: for
large $n$, a source of entropy $H$ concentrates its probability on about
$2^{nH}$ roughly equiprobable sequences, and essentially none elsewhere.
The same statement applies to the pair $(X^n, Y^n)$ drawn through the
channel, and produces three counts rather than one.

<Example title="the three counts on BSC(0.1)">
Take the capacity-achieving input, uniform bits. Then $H(X) = 1$ and
$H(Y) = 1$, so there are about $2^{n}$ typical input sequences and about
$2^{n}$ typical output sequences. The noise contributes
$H(Y \mid X) = H_2(0.1) = 0.469$, so
$H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X) = 1.469$ and there are about $2^{1.469 n}$
typical *pairs*.

Now pick a typical $x^n$ and a typical $y^n$ independently. The chance
that this unrelated pair happens to look like a genuine
input-output pair is the number of typical pairs divided by the number of
ways to pick the two sequences separately:
$$
\frac{2^{nH(X,Y)}}{2^{nH(X)} \, 2^{nH(Y)}} = 2^{-n[H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)]}
= 2^{-nI(X;Y)} = 2^{-0.531\,n}.
$$
At $n = 1000$ that is $2^{-531}$, about $10^{-160}$. Equivalently: given a
received $y^n$, only about $2^{nH(X \mid Y)} = 2^{0.469 n}$ of the $2^{n}$
typical inputs could plausibly have produced it.
</Example>

The exponent that appeared is mutual information, and it appeared as a
count. That is the whole reason $I(X;Y)$ turns out to be the operational
rate: it measures how much a received sequence narrows the field of
candidate inputs, in bits per channel use.

### The union bound fixes the condition R < I(X;Y)

The decoder is now forced. Given $y^n$, output $\hat w$ if $x^n(\hat w)$ is
the **unique** codeword jointly typical with $y^n$; if there is none, or
more than one, declare failure. Two events cause failure, and both are
countable.

First, the transmitted pair $(x^n(w), y^n)$ may itself fail to be jointly
typical. The law of large numbers sends that probability to $0$ as $n$
grows, for any fixed input distribution.

Second, some wrong codeword may be jointly typical with $y^n$. This is what
the random construction buys: if the $2^{nR}$ codewords are drawn
independently from $p(x)$, then a wrong codeword is statistically unrelated
to the received $y^n$, so it lands in the candidate set with probability
about $2^{-nI(X;Y)}$, the count just computed. There are $2^{nR} - 1$
wrong codewords, and the union bound charges for all of them at once:
$$
(2^{nR} - 1)\, 2^{-n I(X;Y)} \;<\; 2^{-n\,(I(X;Y) - R)}
\;\longrightarrow\; 0
\qquad \text{whenever } R < I(X;Y).
$$
Choosing the input distribution that attains the maximum makes
$I(X;Y) = C$, so every rate below capacity is covered.

<Example title="the safety exponent at n = 1000">
On $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$ at $R = 0.4$, the exponent is
$n(C - R) = 1000 \times 0.131 = 131$, so the wrong-codeword probability is
below $2^{-131} \approx 10^{-39}$. The competitors are not few: there are
$2^{400}$ of them. Each one's chance of interfering is $2^{-531}$, and the
race is decided by the difference of the two exponents, $131$.
</Example>

<Exercise>
Repeat the computation at $R = 0.53$, a hair below capacity, at
$n = 1000$. Then find the blocklength at which the $R = 0.53$ bound
matches the $R = 0.4$, $n = 1000$ bound of $2^{-131}$, and state what the
comparison implies about operating near capacity.

<Solution>
At $R = 0.53$ the exponent is $n(C - R) = 1000 \times 0.001 = 1$, so the
bound is $2^{-1} = 0.5$: useless at this blocklength.

The bound is $2^{-n(C-R)}$, so matching an exponent of $131$ at
$C - R = 0.001$ needs $n \times 0.001 = 131$, that is $n = 131{,}000$.

The safety margin is the product $n(C - R)$ of blocklength and distance
from capacity, and only that product. Halving the distance to capacity
doubles the blocklength required for the same guarantee; running $0.001$
bits below $C$ instead of $0.131$ below costs a factor of $131$ in
blocklength, and therefore in latency and in decoder work. The
transferable move: whenever an exponential bound has the form
$2^{-n \cdot \text{margin}}$, the design variable is the product, not
either factor.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

The same counts admit a geometric reading. Each codeword's plausible
outputs form a cloud of about $2^{nH(Y \mid X)}$ sequences inside the
$2^{nH(Y)}$ typical outputs. Decoding succeeds when the clouds do not
overlap, so the number of codewords that fit is at most
$2^{nH(Y)} / 2^{nH(Y \mid X)} = 2^{nI(X;Y)}$, and capacity is a packing
number: how many disjoint noise clouds fit in the output space, per
channel use.

### Averaging over codebooks, then discarding the worst half

Nothing so far names a code. What the union bound controls is the error
probability averaged over all codebooks, since the codewords were treated
as random draws. The remaining step is the oldest argument of the
probabilistic method: a quantity cannot be below its own average
everywhere, so if the average error over codebooks tends to $0$, then for
each $n$ at least one specific codebook does at least as well as the
average. Existence follows. Which codebook it is, the argument does not
say.

One gap remains. The controlled quantity is the average over messages
within a codebook as well, so the winning codebook is only guaranteed a
small $P_e^{(n)}$, not a small $\lambda^{(n)}$. The repair is to sort the
codewords by their individual error probability and delete the worse half.
Every survivor then has conditional error below twice the average, and the
message count halves, which costs $\tfrac{1}{n}$ in rate and vanishes.
This step is called **expurgation**, and without it the theorem's
worst-case guarantee is not proved.

<Remark title="existence without construction">
The proof is non-constructive twice over. It exhibits no codebook, only the
goodness of an average; and the objects it averages over could not be used
if they were handed to you, since a codebook drawn at random has no
structure for an encoder or a decoder to exploit. Neither defect touches
the theorem's claim, which is only that a good code exists at every rate
below capacity; both are properties of the route taken to it.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Joint-typicality decoding is not optimal: maximum-likelihood decoding has
lower error probability at every blocklength, and the threshold is reached
anyway. So the boundary is not an artifact of the decoding rule, and no
better decoder can move it either, because the converse below never
mentions a decoder at all.
</KeyFact>

## The converse: nR bits cannot cross a link of width nC

The converse must rule out every conceivable code above $C$, including
codes no one has imagined, so it cannot examine decoders. It counts
information along the pipeline $W \to X^n \to Y^n \to \hat W$ and shows the
count fails when $R > C$. Two prerequisites supply the counting, and both
are already proved.

The first is Fano's inequality of <Ref lesson="fano-inequality"/>: for any
estimate $\hat W$ of a message $W$ drawn from a set of size $M$, with error
probability $P_e$, the uncertainty the estimate leaves behind obeys
$H(W \mid \hat W) \le H_2(P_e) + P_e \log_2(M - 1) \le 1 + P_e \log_2 M$.
Read forwards it says that a good decoder leaves little uncertainty; read
backwards, which is how the converse uses it, it says that unresolved
uncertainty has to be paid for in errors.

The second is the <Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>: along a
Markov chain, information only decreases. The encoder makes $X^n$ a
function of $W$ and the decoder makes $\hat W$ a function of $Y^n$, so the
chain applies twice and gives $I(W; \hat W) \le I(X^n; Y^n)$.

### n uses of a memoryless channel pass at most nC

The one step this lesson owns is the middle link. Memorylessness means the
noise acts independently on each use, so
$H(Y^n \mid X^n) = \sum_i H(Y_i \mid X_i)$, and then
$$
I(X^n;Y^n) = H(Y^n) - \sum_{i=1}^n H(Y_i \mid X_i)
\;\le\; \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl[ H(Y_i) - H(Y_i \mid X_i) \bigr]
= \sum_{i=1}^n I(X_i;Y_i) \;\le\; nC .
$$
The first inequality is subadditivity, $H(Y^n) \le \sum_i H(Y_i)$, which is
the chain rule plus the fact that conditioning does not increase entropy
(<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>). The last holds because
each $I(X_i;Y_i)$ is a mutual information across one use of the channel
and therefore at most the maximum $C$ defining capacity. Whatever
dependence the encoder builds across the block, $n$ uses pass at most $n$
capacities of information.

### The arithmetic of impossibility

Take $W$ uniform over the $M = 2^{nR}$ messages, so $H(W) = nR$ bits.
Splitting that uncertainty into the part the decoder resolves and the part
it does not, using the definition of mutual information
(<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>), and applying the two bounds:
$$
nR = H(W) = I(W;\hat W) + H(W \mid \hat W)
\;\le\; \underbrace{nC}_{\text{capacity}} + \underbrace{1 + P_e^{(n)}\, nR}_{\text{Fano}} .
$$
Divide by $nR$ and rearrange:
$$
P_e^{(n)} \;\ge\; 1 - \frac{C}{R} - \frac{1}{nR}.
$$
For $R \le C$ the bound is negative and says nothing. For $R > C$ it
converges to $1 - C/R > 0$ as $n$ grows. On $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$ at
$R = 0.6$, at least $11.5\%$ of blocks decode wrongly under every code that
will ever be written; at $R = 1$, uncoded transmission of one bit per use,
at least $46.9\%$ do. The average error never exceeds the maximal error, so
a floor under the average is a floor under the maximum as well, and the
bound rules out both kinds of guarantee.

<Exercise>
The binary erasure channel of <Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/> replaces a
transmitted bit with an erasure symbol with probability $e$ and has
capacity $C = 1 - e$. A design calls for rate $R = 0.75$ over
$\mathrm{BEC}(0.4)$. Give the asymptotic error floor, and state what
changes about the floor if the receiver is additionally told, free of
charge, which positions were erased.

<Solution>
$C = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6$, so
$$
P_e^{(n)} \ge 1 - \frac{C}{R} = 1 - \frac{0.6}{0.75} = 0.2
$$
asymptotically: at least one block in five fails under every code.

Telling the receiver where the erasures fell changes nothing, because the
receiver of a BEC already knows: an erasure is a visible symbol, not a
silent flip. The genie's announcement is a function of $Y^n$, so
$I(W; \hat W) \le I(X^n; Y^n) \le nC$ still holds and the floor is
unmoved. The transferable move: side information that is computable from
what the receiver already has cannot raise capacity, by the same
data-processing step that produced the bound.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Above capacity the situation is worse than this floor. The **strong
converse**, proved for discrete memoryless channels by Wolfowitz in 1957,
says that at any fixed $R > C$ the block error of any code sequence tends
to $1$, not merely to $1 - C/R$. The cliff is two-sided: below $C$ the
error can be driven to zero, above $C$ it is driven to certainty, and the
weak converse above understates the second side.

<KeyFact>
Fano's inequality is attained with equality by a specific conditional
distribution (<Ref lesson="fano-inequality"/>), so the converse loses
nothing at that step. The boundary at $C$ is not an artifact of a lossy
bound, and no future argument moves it.
</KeyFact>

## Finite blocklength: the cliff is a limit, and at n = 100 it is a slope

Both halves of the theorem are statements about $n \to \infty$. Nothing has
been promised at any particular blocklength, and the demo's task already
exposed the gap: at $n = 1000$ the largest rate holding block error under
$10^{-3}$ is not $0.531$.

The correction has a known shape. For a fixed error tolerance
$\varepsilon$, the largest number of messages $M^*(n, \varepsilon)$ that
length-$n$ codes can carry satisfies
$$
\log_2 M^*(n, \varepsilon) \;=\; nC - \sqrt{nV}\, Q^{-1}(\varepsilon)
+ O(\log n),
$$
where $Q^{-1}$ is the inverse Gaussian tail function and $V$, the
**channel dispersion**, is the variance of the per-use information density
under the capacity-achieving input. Capacity is a mean; dispersion is its
variance, and the deficit is the price of the fluctuation. For
$\mathrm{BSC}(f)$, $V = f(1-f)\log_2^2\!\bigl((1-f)/f\bigr)$, which at
$f = 0.1$ is $0.9 \cdot 0.1 \cdot (3.17)^2 = 0.90$ bits² per use.

<Example title="what n buys on BSC(0.1) at ε = 10⁻³">
$Q^{-1}(10^{-3}) = 3.09$, so the rate deficit is
$\sqrt{V/n} \cdot 3.09 = 2.94/\sqrt{n}$ bits per use:

- $n = 100$: deficit $0.294$, best rate $\approx 0.24$ — under half of
  capacity;
- $n = 1000$: deficit $0.093$, best rate $\approx 0.44$;
- $n = 10^4$: deficit $0.029$, best rate $\approx 0.50$;
- $n = 10^5$: deficit $0.009$, best rate $\approx 0.52$.

Closing the last $0.01$ bits costs two orders of magnitude in blocklength,
because the deficit shrinks only as $1/\sqrt{n}$.
</Example>

</KeyFact>

## ★ Communicating above capacity, with errors allowed

Everything so far demanded vanishing error. Dropping that demand extends
the achievable region past $C$, and the extension has a closed form worth
knowing, because it is where channel coding meets lossy compression.

Fix a tolerated bit-error rate $p_b$ and ask for the largest rate. A naive
scheme sends a fraction $C/R$ of the source bits reliably and lets the
receiver guess the rest, giving $p_b = \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - C/R)$, that is
$R = C/(1 - 2p_b)$. Spreading the damage evenly over all bits instead of
concentrating it on a discarded subset does better, and the best possible
is
$$
R = \frac{C}{1 - H_2(p_b)},
$$
reached by using a capacity-achieving code's decoder backwards as a lossy
compressor: compress the source to the code's message space, accepting the
$p_b$ of distortion the code would have corrected, then send the compressed
bits reliably.

<Figure
  src="./figures/above-capacity.svg"
  alt="Tolerated bit-error rate against rate on BSC with f equals 0.1: Shannon's optimum curve R equals C over one minus binary entropy of p b, the dashed discard-and-guess curve to its left, and the marked point at rate 1 with bit error 0.1"
  caption="Allowing errors extends the region past C; the optimum passes exactly through uncoded transmission at R = 1."
/>

The curve passes through one point that checks itself. On
$\mathrm{BSC}(f)$, capacity is $1 - H_2(f)$, so at $p_b = f$ the formula
gives $R = (1 - H_2(f))/(1 - H_2(f)) = 1$ exactly. Rate $1$ with bit-error
rate $f$ is uncoded transmission, which anyone can implement. The theory
says that at rate $1$ on this channel nothing whatsoever beats sending the
bits raw, and the naive discard-and-guess scheme at rate $1$ would suffer
$p_b = 0.235$ instead of $0.1$. The general curve $R(p_b)$ is the
rate-distortion function of a binary source under Hamming distortion,
divided by capacity, and <Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/> derives it from
the other direction.

## Feedback adds nothing, and separation costs nothing

Two corollaries bound the scope of what has been proved, and both are
negative results that justify a standard design practice.

**Feedback.** Suppose the encoder sees every channel output before choosing
the next input. The capacity does not change: $C_{\text{FB}} = C$ for a
discrete memoryless channel. The converse above survives almost unchanged,
because $I(W;Y^n) \le nC$ still holds when $X_i$ depends on
$(W, Y^{i-1})$. What feedback changes is engineering cost, sometimes
enormously: on the erasure channel, an encoder with feedback simply
retransmits every erased bit, achieving capacity $1 - e$ with a trivial
code and no decoding error at all, where the forward-only design needs a
capacity-approaching block code.

**Separation.** A source of entropy rate $H$ can be sent over a channel of
capacity $C$ with vanishing error if $H < C$, and cannot if $H > C$; and
the optimal way to do it is the obvious two-stage one, compress first with
the machinery of <Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>, then
channel-code the compressed bits. Nothing is lost by ignoring the source
while designing the channel code, which is why compression and error
correction are separate modules of this course and separate layers of every
deployed stack. The result is asymptotic and single-user: it fails for some
multi-user channels, where joint source-channel designs beat the
decomposition.

## What the proof withholds: no code

What the theorem delivers is the exact frontier, in both directions, for
every discrete memoryless channel, plus the operational meaning of mutual
information: $C$ bits per use, achievable and unimprovable. What it
withholds is a code. The achievability argument averages over codebooks
that cannot be stored or searched, the converse examines no code at all,
and neither says how to build anything.

The gap was not rhetorical. Forty-five years passed between the theorem and
the first practical code family to come within a decibel of the boundary on
a real channel, and the intervening designs were built around a different
and weaker figure of merit, the number of errors a code is guaranteed to
correct. <Ref lesson="hamming-codes"/> constructs a code around that figure,
evaluates it, and shows the figure to be the wrong target.
<Ref lesson="deployed-codes"/> then measures how close the codes actually
flown and standardized come to the number proved here.

<KeyFact>
Three conditions are assumed silently wherever $H$ and $C$ are quoted as
the operational limits. **Asymptotic:** both are limits over $n \to \infty$,
and at packet-length blocks the finite-blocklength deficit is real and
compressing before channel-coding is no longer optimal. **Single-user:**
the counterexample is two senders with *correlated* sources sharing one
channel, where transmitting the correlation jointly delivers pairs that no
pair of separate compress-then-code stages can. **Memoryless:** with memory the per-use
ceiling is not the single-letter maximum $\max_{p(x)} I(X;Y)$ but the limit
of $\tfrac{1}{n} \max I(X^n;Y^n)$, which no single transition table
determines.
</KeyFact>

<Exercise>
On $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$, R3 reaches $p_b = 0.028$ at rate $\tfrac{1}{3}$.
Compute the decoded bit-error probability and rate of R5 under majority
decoding, then convert the pair of points into the two factors that
describe the family's trajectory (what one step costs in rate, what it
buys in error), and say which of the two decides whether the family can
witness a positive achievable rate.

<Solution>
**R5.** The vote errs when at least $3$ of $5$ copies flip:
$$
p_b = \binom{5}{3} f^3(1-f)^2 + \binom{5}{4} f^4(1-f) + f^5
= 10(0.001)(0.81) + 5(0.0001)(0.9) + 10^{-5},
$$
so $p_b = 0.0081 + 0.00045 + 0.00001 \approx 0.0086$, at
$R = \tfrac{1}{5} = 0.2$.

Error improves by a factor of $3.3$ while rate falls by a factor of $1.7$.
The error factor is the attractive one and it is not the one that decides:
achievability asks for a *fixed* rate with vanishing error, so what settles
the question is the rate factor, which is bounded away from $1$ at every
step. The family's error tends to $0$ only along a sequence whose rate
tends to $0$ with it, and a sequence of rates converging to $0$ witnesses
no positive achievable rate at all. The theorem asserts the same
reliability is available at any fixed rate below $0.531$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A channel has capacity $C = 0$ (for instance $\mathrm{BSC}(0.5)$, which
outputs a fair coin flip whatever is sent). State which rates the theorem
declares achievable. Then evaluate the converse for a fixed $R > 0$: give
the floor it produces at finite $n$ and in the limit, and say precisely
whether the conclusion is "error is large" or "error tends to $1$",
justifying the difference.

<Solution>
**Achievable rates.** The set $\lbrace R : R < C \rbrace$ is empty. Only
$R = 0$ is achievable, and trivially: a code with $M = 1$ message has
error probability zero and rate $\log_2 1 / n = 0$. No positive rate is
achievable.

**The converse at $C = 0$.** Substituting into
$P_e^{(n)} \ge 1 - C/R - 1/(nR)$ with $C = 0$ gives
$$
P_e^{(n)} \;\ge\; 1 - \frac{1}{nR},
$$
which tends to $1$ as $n \to \infty$. So here the weak converse already
delivers the strong conclusion: the error does not merely fail to vanish,
it converges to certainty. The reason is that the $C/R$ term, which is
what leaves room for a floor strictly between $0$ and $1$ at rates just
above capacity, has vanished.

**Why the distinction matters.** At a general $R > C > 0$ the weak
converse only gives $P_e \ge 1 - C/R$, a floor short of $1$; concluding
"error tends to $1$" there requires Wolfowitz's strong converse, a separate
theorem. At $C = 0$ no extra theorem is needed. The direct check confirms
it: with $2^{nR}$ equally likely messages and an output independent of the
input, the best decoder guesses, and $P_e = 1 - 2^{-nR} \to 1$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim. "The theorem is proved with random codebooks, so a
randomly chosen codebook will work in practice: draw $2^{nR}$ codewords,
ship it, and the theorem guarantees the error." Give two independent
reasons the claim fails, one about the cost of using such a code and one
about what averaging over codebooks actually establishes. Then state the
one thing a random draw does deliver.

<Solution>
**Reason 1: the code cannot be used.** A codebook drawn at random has no
structure. Storing it takes $n \cdot 2^{nR}$ bits and decoding compares the
received word against all $2^{nR}$ codewords; at $n = 1000$, $R = 0.5$ that
is $2^{500} \approx 10^{150}$ entries. Both costs are exponential in $n$,
while the whole point of a long blocklength is that $n$ must be large. Even
restricting to random *linear* codes, which need only a $k \times n$
generator matrix and so solve the storage problem, leaves decoding
intractable: maximum-likelihood decoding of a general linear code is
NP-hard.

**Reason 2: the average is not the guarantee.** The union bound controls
the error averaged over codebooks *and* over messages. Two conclusions
follow, and only one of them is about a particular codebook: some codebook
achieves at most the average, and (by Markov's inequality) a codebook drawn
at random has average-message error below twice the mean with probability
at least $\tfrac{1}{2}$. Neither statement controls the *maximal* error of
the drawn codebook, which is the guarantee a communication system needs. A
random codebook typically contains a few confusable pairs, and the messages
in those pairs fail often. Expurgation repairs this by deleting the worst
half of the codewords, and expurgation is not something a random draw does
for you: it requires evaluating each codeword's error probability, which is
the expensive computation the proof was designed to avoid.

**What is true.** A random draw with high probability gives a codebook
whose *average-message* error is within a constant factor of the proved
bound. That is a real fact, and it is why random-coding arguments are
useful for computing achievable rates. It is not a construction, and the
distance from it to a shippable code is the subject of
<Ref lesson="deployed-codes"/>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the converse in its rate form. Starting from $H(W) = nR$ for a
uniformly chosen message, use Fano's inequality and the data-processing
inequality to obtain
$$
R \;\le\; \frac{C}{1 - P_e^{(n)}} + \frac{1}{n\,(1 - P_e^{(n)})},
$$
and take $P_e^{(n)} \to 0$. Name the step at which each of the two
inequalities enters, and state which step fails if the channel has memory.

<Solution>
**Setup.** $W$ is uniform on $2^{nR}$ messages, so $H(W) = nR$. Split it by
the definition of mutual information:
$$
nR = H(W) = I(W;\hat W) + H(W \mid \hat W).
$$

**Data processing enters here.** $W \to X^n \to Y^n \to \hat W$ is a Markov
chain, so $I(W;\hat W) \le I(X^n;Y^n)$; memorylessness plus subadditivity
then give $I(X^n;Y^n) \le \sum_i I(X_i;Y_i) \le nC$.

**Fano enters here.** With $M = 2^{nR}$,
$H(W \mid \hat W) \le 1 + P_e^{(n)} \log_2 M = 1 + P_e^{(n)} nR$.

**Combine and solve.**
$$
nR \le nC + 1 + P_e^{(n)} nR
\;\Longrightarrow\;
nR\bigl(1 - P_e^{(n)}\bigr) \le nC + 1,
$$
and dividing by $n(1 - P_e^{(n)})$, which is positive whenever
$P_e^{(n)} < 1$,
$$
R \le \frac{C}{1 - P_e^{(n)}} + \frac{1}{n\,(1 - P_e^{(n)})}.
$$

**The limit.** If the code sequence is to be achievable then
$P_e^{(n)} \to 0$, and both correction terms collapse: the first factor
tends to $C$ and the second to $0$. Hence $R \le C$. Rearranging the same
inequality for $P_e^{(n)}$ instead of $R$ returns the floor
$P_e^{(n)} \ge 1 - C/R - 1/(nR)$ stated in the theorem.

**With memory.** Data processing and Fano both survive: neither says
anything about the channel. The step that fails is
$I(X^n;Y^n) \le \sum_i I(X_i;Y_i) \le nC$, which used
$H(Y^n \mid X^n) = \sum_i H(Y_i \mid X_i)$ — exactly the memorylessness
assumption. For a channel with memory the per-use maximum $C$ is not the
right ceiling, and capacity has to be redefined as the limit of
$\tfrac{1}{n}\max I(X^n;Y^n)$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the achievability counts on a channel and an input the lesson did not
count. Take $\mathrm{BEC}(0.4)$: each bit is delivered intact or replaced
by an erasure symbol with probability $e = 0.4$, and $C = 1 - e = 0.6$.

(a) With a uniform input, give $H(X)$, $H(Y)$, $H(Y \mid X)$ and $H(X,Y)$,
hence the collision probability $2^{-nI(X;Y)}$. Then explain why on this
channel the set of inputs compatible with a received $y^n$ can be counted
exactly, with no typicality argument, and which feature of the erasure
channel makes that possible.

(b) Now draw the codebook i.i.d. $\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.11)$ instead of
uniform. Recompute $I(X;Y)$ and the collision exponent, and give a rate
that lies below capacity yet is not covered by the union bound.

(c) At that rate, name which of the proof's two error events is the one
that fails, which survives untouched, and what the proof does about it.

<Solution>
**(a) The four entropies.** The output alphabet is
$\lbrace 0, 1, ? \rbrace$ with $\Pr\lbrace ? \rbrace = 0.4$ and
$\Pr\lbrace 0 \rbrace = \Pr\lbrace 1 \rbrace = 0.3$, so
$$
H(X) = 1, \quad
H(Y) = H_2(0.4) + (1 - 0.4)\cdot 1 = 0.971 + 0.6 = 1.571,
$$
the split being one bit for the erase/deliver coin plus one bit whenever
the symbol survives. The channel's own uncertainty is the erasure coin
alone: $H(Y \mid X) = H_2(0.4) = 0.971$, and never more, because a
delivered symbol is determined by the input. Hence
$H(X,Y) = H(X) + H(Y \mid X) = 1.971$ and
$$
2^{n[H(X,Y) - H(X) - H(Y)]} = 2^{n(1.971 - 1 - 1.571)} = 2^{-0.6\,n}
= 2^{-nI(X;Y)},
$$
matching $I = C = 0.6$. The count structure differs from the BSC in one
place: there $H(Y) = H(X) = 1$ and all the asymmetry sat in
$H(Y \mid X)$; here the output space is larger than the input space,
$H(Y) > H(X)$, and the mutual information still comes out as the same
difference of exponents.

**Why the count is exact.** A received $y^n$ carrying $k$ erasures is
compatible with exactly $2^{k}$ inputs, since the delivered positions are
known with certainty and each erased position is free, and under a uniform
input those $2^k$ candidates are exactly equiprobable. Only $k$ itself needs the
law of large numbers, concentrating at $ne = 0.4n$, which returns the
candidate count $2^{nH(X \mid Y)}$ with $H(X \mid Y) = e$. The feature
responsible is that the erasure channel's confusion sets *partition* the
input space: an output either pins a coordinate or says nothing about it,
and no input is ever a merely improbable explanation of $y^n$. On the BSC
every input remains possible for every output, so its candidate set is a
typical-set estimate and nothing sharper.

**(b) A codebook drawn from the wrong distribution.** With
$\Pr\lbrace X = 1 \rbrace = q = 0.11$, $H(X) = H_2(0.11) = 0.500$, and
since the channel erases regardless of the input,
$H(Y \mid X) = H_2(0.4) = 0.971$ as before while
$H(Y) = H_2(0.4) + (1-e)H_2(q) = 0.971 + 0.6 \times 0.500 = 1.271$.
The exponent is
$$
H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y) = 0.500 + 1.271 - (0.500 + 0.971) = 0.300,
$$
so an unrelated codeword collides with probability $2^{-0.300\,n}$ instead
of $2^{-0.6\,n}$: the square root of the correct figure, and at $n = 1000$
larger by $2^{300}$. In general $I(X;Y) = (1-e)H_2(q)$ on the BEC, and
$q = 0.11$ throws away half of it. The union bound therefore covers only
$R < 0.300$. At $R = 0.45$, comfortably below $C = 0.6$, so that the
theorem promises some code achieves it, the bound reads
$2^{n(0.45 - 0.300)} = 2^{0.15 n} \to \infty$ and proves nothing.

**(c) Which event fails.** The first error event, the transmitted pair
$(x^n(w), y^n)$ failing to be jointly typical, survives untouched: it
vanishes by the law of large numbers for *any* fixed input distribution,
since $(X^n, Y^n)$ is drawn i.i.d. from that distribution and the channel.
The second event, some wrong codeword landing in the candidate set, is the
one that fails, and it fails because the codebook's own distribution set the
collision exponent at $I(X;Y) = 0.300$, not because the channel changed.
So $R < I(X;Y)$ is the condition the argument actually delivers, and
$R < C$ is what it becomes only after the free parameter is set well; the
proof's response is to choose $p(x)$ uniform, restoring $I = C = 0.6$ and
covering $R = 0.45$.

The restriction is not an artifact of a loose bound. Mutual information is
concave in $p(x)$ for a fixed channel, so running the lesson's converse on
a code whose codewords all have bias $0.11$ replaces the ceiling $nC$ with
$\sum_i I(X_i;Y_i) \le 0.300\,n$: such codes really cannot carry more,
whatever decoder is attached. The transferable move: in a random-coding argument the input
distribution is a design variable with a converse attached, so a bad choice
costs real rate rather than merely weakening the proof.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

$\varepsilon \approx 0.45$. Including the usual $\tfrac{1}{2}\log_2 n
= 1.79$ bits as the correction gives $Q^{-1}(\varepsilon) = 0.66$, hence
$\varepsilon \approx 0.26$. The measurement lands on the second of these,
$0.256$ against $0.2556$, and nowhere near the uncorrected $0.455$.

**What counts as agreement.** Landing inside the band the two versions of
the expansion bracket, $0.25$ to $0.45$, is agreement; landing at $0.05$ or
at $0.8$ would not be. Three caveats keep the comparison honest, and the
near-exact hit on the corrected value is not evidence that any of them can
be dropped. The expansion is asymptotic and $n = 12$ is not, so the
$O(\log n)$ term is not pinned down: $\tfrac{1}{2}\log_2 n$ is a
conventional choice of coefficient, and another choice moves the prediction
anywhere inside the band. $M^*$ refers to the best code of that
blocklength, while the simulation averages over random ones, so the
measurement should if anything sit above the prediction rather than on it.
And nearest-neighbour decoding is maximum-likelihood here only because
$f < \tfrac{1}{2}$ makes fewer flips more likely; on a channel where that
fails, the same simulation would measure a different decoder.

**The reading.** At $n = 12$ the threshold picture is worthless: a rate
$6\%$ below capacity yields a $26\%$ block error, not a small one. Capacity
describes what long blocks can do.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design a code by hand. Find seven-bit codewords for $16$ messages, so that
every single-bit flip in a block is corrected: four message bits, three
extra bits, and a decoder that recovers the message whenever at most one of
the seven bits flips. State your parity equations and the rule your decoder
uses. If you cannot make it work, report the constraint that blocked you.
Before opening the solution, count how many distinct error patterns the
decoder must distinguish and compare that with how many distinct settings
three extra bits can signal.

<Solution>
**The count first.** The decoder must distinguish "no error" from a flip in
each of the $7$ positions: $8$ outcomes. Three binary checks produce
$2^3 = 8$ patterns. The budget matches exactly, with nothing to spare, so a
solution can exist and can have no slack.

**A construction.** Write the message as $s_1 s_2 s_3 s_4$ and set
$$
t_5 = s_1 \oplus s_2 \oplus s_3, \qquad
t_6 = s_2 \oplus s_3 \oplus s_4, \qquad
t_7 = s_1 \oplus s_3 \oplus s_4,
$$
transmitting $s_1 s_2 s_3 s_4 t_5 t_6 t_7$ at rate $\tfrac{4}{7} \approx
0.571$. The decoder recomputes the three parities on the received block;
the triple of pass/fail results is the *syndrome*. Each of the seven
positions occurs in a distinct subset of the three checks: $s_1$ in checks
1 and 3, $s_2$ in 1 and 2, $s_3$ in all three, $s_4$ in 2 and 3, and each
$t_j$ in its own check alone. So each single flip produces its own
syndrome, and the all-pass syndrome means no error.

**Check it.** Encode $s = 1000$: $t_5 = 1$, $t_6 = 0$, $t_7 = 1$,
transmitted $1000101$. Flip $s_2$: received $1100101$. Check 1
($s_1, s_2, s_3, t_5$) sums to $3$, odd, fails; check 2
($s_2, s_3, s_4, t_6$) sums to $1$, fails; check 3 ($s_1, s_3, s_4, t_7$)
sums to $2$, holds. The unique bit inside checks 1 and 2 but outside check
3 is $s_2$. Flip it back.

**Where it stops.** Because the syndrome budget is exactly spent, every one
of the eight syndromes is already claimed, by "no error" or by one of the
seven single flips. Two flips give the sum of two single-flip syndromes,
which is nonzero, so something is visibly wrong, but that sum is itself
the syndrome of some third position, and this decoder, which always flips
the bit its syndrome names, corrects that third bit and returns a wrong
codeword. The failure is the decoder's, not the code's: a double error is
detectable, and the same code used purely as a detector would report both
flips. What no rule can do is both, since correcting every single flip
means spending each nonzero syndrome on a single-flip verdict.

This is the Hamming $(7,4)$ code, and <Ref lesson="hamming-codes"/> builds
it as a null space, derives the syndrome rule from the parity-check matrix,
and then makes the point this lesson has been setting up: guaranteed
correction of one error in seven is a far weaker property than what the
coding theorem promises at rate $0.571$, and minimum distance is the wrong
figure of merit for approaching capacity.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

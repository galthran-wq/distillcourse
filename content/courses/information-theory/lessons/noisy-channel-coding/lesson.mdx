Reliable communication is possible at every rate below the channel capacity
and impossible above it: capacity is a sharp threshold, not a soft
trade-off. This lesson defines block codes and their rates, states the
noisy-channel coding theorem, sketches both halves of the argument
(achievability by random codebooks with joint-typicality decoding, the
converse by Fano's inequality combined with the data-processing
inequality), and works one finite code, the Hamming (7,4), by hand.

## Brute redundancy: reliability at a vanishing rate

Fix the channel of <Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>: $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$,
each transmitted bit flipped independently with probability $f = 0.1$,
capacity $C = 1 - H(0.1) \approx 0.531$ bits per use. Sending data bits raw
corrupts one in ten. The oldest countermeasure is repetition: transmit
every data bit three times and decode by majority vote.

<Example title="the repetition code R3 on BSC(0.1)">
Each data bit becomes three channel bits; the decoder outputs the majority
of the three received copies. The vote errs exactly when at least two of
the three copies flip:
$$
p_b = 3f^2(1 - f) + f^3 = 3 \cdot 0.01 \cdot 0.9 + 0.001 = 0.028.
$$
Per-bit error falls from $0.1$ to $0.028$. The price is paid in rate: three
channel uses now carry one data bit, $R = \tfrac{1}{3}$ against the raw
code's $R = 1$.
</Example>

Repeating $N$ times continues the pattern: the majority vote fails only
when half the copies flip, an event whose probability falls exponentially
in $N$, while the rate falls as $1/N$.
[MacKay](https://www.inference.org.uk/mackay/itila/) (ch. 1) runs the
numbers for a target of $p_b \le 10^{-15}$, the reliability a disk drive
needs: $N = 61$ copies, rate $\tfrac{1}{61} \approx 0.016$. The pattern
suggests a general law: error tends to zero only if rate does. The theorem
of this lesson says the suggestion is false, and that the true boundary
sits at $R = 0.531$, not at $R = 0$.

<KeyFact>
Repetition buys reliability only by spending the entire rate: driving its
error to zero drives $R \to 0$. That trade is a defect of the code, not a
law of the channel.
</KeyFact>

## Codes and rates: the objects the theorem quantifies

R3 is one instance of a general shape. It assigns $M = 2$ messages the
codewords $000$ and $111$ of length $n = 3$, and maps each received triple
back to a message. The general object replaces $2$ and $3$ by parameters.

<Definition title="block code and rate">
An **$(M, n)$ block code** for a channel $p(y \mid x)$ consists of an
encoder assigning each message $w \in \{1, \ldots, M\}$ a codeword
$x^n(w) \in \mathcal{X}^n$, and a decoder
$g : \mathcal{Y}^n \to \{1, \ldots, M\}$. Its **rate** is
$$
R = \frac{\log_2 M}{n} \text{ bits per channel use},
$$
so a rate-$R$ code carries $M = 2^{nR}$ distinct messages on $n$ uses.
</Definition>

<Definition title="achievable rate">
The **maximal block error probability** of a code is
$\lambda^{(n)} = \max_w \Pr\{g(Y^n) \ne w \mid W = w\}$: the worst case
over messages of decoding to anything but the sent message. A rate $R$ is
**achievable** if a sequence of $(2^{nR}, n)$ codes exists with
$\lambda^{(n)} \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$.
</Definition>

The question the theorem answers: for a given discrete memoryless channel,
which rates are achievable. The limit $n \to \infty$ is part of the
question. At any fixed $n$ some error probability remains; achievability
asks whether a *sequence* of codes drives it to zero at fixed rate.

## The theorem: the achievable rates are exactly the rates below C

<Theorem title="noisy-channel coding theorem">
Let a discrete memoryless channel have capacity
$C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y)$.

**Achievability.** Every rate $R < C$ is achievable: there exist
$(2^{nR}, n)$ codes with $\lambda^{(n)} \to 0$.

**Converse.** No rate above $C$ is achievable: every $(2^{nR}, n)$ code
with $R > C$ has average block error
$$
P_e \;\ge\; 1 - \frac{C}{R} - \frac{1}{nR},
$$
which is bounded away from $0$ as $n \to \infty$.
</Theorem>

This is Theorem 11 of
[Shannon's 1948 paper](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf)
(Part II, §13). The two directions are separate arguments with separate
tools, sketched in the next two sections: a probabilistic existence proof
for achievability, an information-counting bound for the converse. Neither
direction implies the other.

## Finite blocklength: the cliff is the n → ∞ limit

At any finite $n$ there is no discontinuity. The error-versus-rate curve
of the best length-$n$ codes is an S-shaped slope through the
neighborhood of $C$; the theorem describes the limit of these slopes, and
the wall at $C$ is what they steepen into.

<Aside>
The $1/\sqrt{n}$ width has an exact form. For error tolerance
$\varepsilon$, the largest number of messages $M^*(n, \varepsilon)$
carried by length-$n$ codes satisfies
$$
\log_2 M^*(n, \varepsilon) \;\approx\; nC - \sqrt{nV}\, Q^{-1}(\varepsilon),
$$
where $V$ is the *channel dispersion*, a variance-like constant of the
channel, and $Q^{-1}$ is the inverse Gaussian tail function. The
best usable rate at finite $n$ sits below $C$ by a deficit shrinking as
$\sqrt{V/n}$: the normal approximation of
[Polyanskiy & Wu](https://people.lids.mit.edu/yp/homepage/data/itlectures_v5.pdf)
(ch. 20, §20.3), sharpening a line of analysis going back to Strassen
(1962). For $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$ at $n = 1000$ and
$\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$, the deficit is on the order of $0.03$ bits per
use: the theorem's promise is honest, but blocklength is the currency
that pays for it.
</Aside>

## Achievability: random codebooks, typicality decoding

The task: show that for every $R < C$ codes with vanishing error exist.
The obstacle is scale. At $n = 1000$ and $R = 0.4$ a codebook assigns
$2^{400} \approx 10^{120}$ messages their thousand-bit codewords; no
explicit construction of that size was known in 1948, and analyzing any
particular one is hopeless. Shannon's argument sidesteps both problems
with two moves: a decoder whose analysis needs only typicality counts,
and an average over *all* codebooks in place of any particular one.

### The decoder: joint typicality

<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/> established the typical set:
for large $n$, the sequences a source actually produces concentrate on
$\approx 2^{nH}$ roughly equiprobable *typical* sequences. Apply this to
the pair $(X^n, Y^n)$ under the capacity-achieving input distribution
(uniform, for the BSC): a pair is *jointly typical* when the input is
typical, the output is typical, and the pair looks like $n$ draws from
the joint distribution $p(x)\,p(y \mid x)$.

The counts for $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$ with uniform input: $H(X) = 1$,
$H(Y) = 1$, $H(X,Y) = 1 + H(0.1) = 1.469$, so
$H(X \mid Y) = 0.469$. Given a received $y^n$, the inputs jointly typical
with it number $\approx 2^{0.469\,n}$, out of $\approx 2^{n}$ typical
inputs: a fraction
$$
\frac{2^{nH(X \mid Y)}}{2^{nH(X)}} = 2^{-n I(X;Y)} = 2^{-0.531\,n}.
$$

The decoder: output $\hat w$ if $x^n(\hat w)$ is the *unique* codeword
jointly typical with the received $y^n$; declare an error otherwise.

### Two failure modes, one union bound

The decoder fails in exactly two ways. First, the true pair
$(x^n(w), y^n)$ falls outside the jointly typical set; the law of large
numbers sends this probability to $0$. Second, some *wrong* codeword is
jointly typical with $y^n$. A wrong codeword, drawn independently of the
transmission, hits the jointly typical shell of $y^n$ with probability
$\approx 2^{-nI(X;Y)}$; there are $2^{nR} - 1$ wrong codewords, and the
union bound charges them all:
$$
2^{nR} \cdot 2^{-n I(X;Y)} = 2^{-n\,(I(X;Y) - R)} \;\longrightarrow\; 0
\qquad \text{whenever } R < I(X;Y).
$$
Choosing the input distribution that attains the maximum makes
$I(X;Y) = C$, so every $R < C$ is covered. At $n = 1000$, $R = 0.4$:
$2^{400}$ competitors against an exponent of $2^{-531}$ leaves total wrong-codeword
probability $\approx 2^{-131} \approx 10^{-39}$.

### The random-codebook trick

The union bound computed an *average*: codewords were treated as drawn
independently and uniformly at random, so the quantity bounded is the
error probability averaged over all codebooks. The final step is the
oldest argument in the probabilistic method: if the average over
codebooks of the error tends to $0$, then for each $n$ at least one
specific codebook does at least as well as the average. Existence
follows; no codebook is named. (The average controls the *average-over-messages*
error; discarding the worse half of each codebook's messages converts
the guarantee to the maximal error $\lambda^{(n)}$ at a rate cost of
$\tfrac{1}{n}$, which vanishes —
[Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
§7.7.)

<Remark title="existence without construction">
The proof is non-constructive twice over. It exhibits no codebook, only
the goodness of an average; and the objects it averages over are
unusable, since a random codebook has no structure to exploit: storing
it takes $n \cdot 2^{nR}$ bits and typicality decoding means searching
all $2^{nR}$ codewords per received block. The gap between this
existence proof and codes that run on hardware stayed open for
decades; the closing section returns to it.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Random codes beat the noise for every $R < C$: a wrong codeword is
jointly typical with the output with probability $\approx 2^{-nC}$, and
even $2^{nR}$ competitors lose that race whenever $R < C$. If the
average codebook succeeds, some codebook does.
</KeyFact>

## The converse: Fano's inequality and the data-processing bound

The converse must rule out every conceivable code at rates above $C$,
including codes no one has imagined. The argument therefore cannot
examine decoders; it counts information along the pipeline
$W \to X^n \to Y^n \to \hat W$ and shows the count fails when $R > C$.
Two bounds do all the work.

### Fano: low error forces low conditional entropy

If the decoder is usually right, then given its output $\hat W$ little
uncertainty about $W$ can remain. Fano's inequality makes the
quantifier exact.

<Theorem title="Fano's inequality">
Let $W$ take values in a set of size $M$ and let $\hat W$ be any estimate
of $W$ (any function of any data), with error probability
$P_e = \Pr\{\hat W \ne W\}$. Then
$$
H(W \mid \hat W) \;\le\; H_2(P_e) + P_e \log_2 (M - 1)
\;\le\; 1 + P_e \log_2 M .
$$

<Proof>
Let $E = \mathbf{1}\{\hat W \ne W\}$, the error indicator. Expand
$H(E, W \mid \hat W)$ both ways with the chain rule of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>:
$$
H(W \mid \hat W) + H(E \mid W, \hat W)
= H(E \mid \hat W) + H(W \mid E, \hat W).
$$
The second term on the left is $0$: $E$ is a function of $(W, \hat W)$.
On the right, $H(E \mid \hat W) \le H(E) = H_2(P_e)$, since conditioning
does not increase entropy. For the last term, split on $E$: given
$E = 0$, $W = \hat W$ exactly, contributing $0$; given $E = 1$, $W$ is
one of the $M - 1$ values other than $\hat W$, so its conditional
entropy is at most $\log_2(M-1)$ (the range bound of
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>). Hence
$$
H(W \mid \hat W) \le H_2(P_e) + P_e \log_2 (M - 1),
$$
and the weaker form follows from $H_2(P_e) \le 1$ and
$\log_2(M-1) \le \log_2 M$. This is
[Cover & Thomas](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/047174882X)
Theorem 2.10.1.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The reading direction matters: Fano converts an assumed error rate into
an upper bound on leftover uncertainty, so, contraposed, a *large*
leftover uncertainty forces a *large* error. The converse manufactures
exactly that large leftover.

### The code is a Markov chain, and n uses carry at most nC

Encoder, channel, and decoder form the chain
$W \to X^n \to Y^n \to \hat W$: each stage is computed from the one
before it. The <Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/> applies twice
and gives $I(W; \hat W) \le I(X^n; Y^n)$. Memorylessness then caps the
middle link:
$$
I(X^n;Y^n) = H(Y^n) - \sum_{i=1}^n H(Y_i \mid X_i)
\;\le\; \sum_{i=1}^n \bigl[ H(Y_i) - H(Y_i \mid X_i) \bigr]
= \sum_{i=1}^n I(X_i;Y_i) \;\le\; nC .
$$
The equality uses independence of the noise across uses
($H(Y^n \mid X^n) = \sum_i H(Y_i \mid X_i)$); the first inequality is
subadditivity of entropy, from the chain rule plus conditioning
(<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>); the last holds because
each $I(X_i;Y_i)$ enters the maximization defining $C$. Whatever
dependence the encoder builds across the block, $n$ uses of the channel
pass at most $n$ capacities of information.

### The arithmetic of impossibility

Take $W$ uniform over the $M = 2^{nR}$ messages, so $H(W) = nR$; the
average error $P_e$ under uniform messages lower-bounds the maximal
error, so a floor here floors both. Split $H(W)$ with the
three-forms identity of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> and apply the
two bounds:
$$
nR = H(W) = I(W;\hat W) + H(W \mid \hat W)
\;\le\; nC + 1 + P_e\, nR .
$$
Solving for $P_e$:
$$
P_e \;\ge\; 1 - \frac{C}{R} - \frac{1}{nR}.
$$
For $R \le C$ the bound is vacuous. For any $R > C$ it converges to
$1 - C/R > 0$: at $R = 0.6$ on $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$, at least $11.5\%$ of
blocks decode wrongly whatever the code; at $R = 1$ (uncoded
transmission of a full bit per use), at least $46.9\%$. No decoder
design enters the computation. The floor binds every code because it is
an accounting identity about entropy, not an analysis of a mechanism.

<Aside>
Two refinements, both out of scope. **Strong converse:** above capacity
the situation is worse than the Fano floor: for a DMC, the block error
of any code sequence at fixed $R > C$ tends to $1$, not merely to
$1 - C/R$ (Wolfowitz, 1957). The cliff is two-sided: error $\to 0$
below, error $\to 1$ above. **Feedback:** the bound
$I(X^n;Y^n) \le nC$ as derived assumes the encoder does not see the
channel outputs. Shannon proved in 1956 that even a perfect feedback
link changes nothing for a DMC: $C_{\text{feedback}} = C$. Feedback
shortens delays and simplifies codes; it adds no capacity.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
The converse is bookkeeping, not engineering: $nR$ message bits must
pass a link that carries at most $nC$, and Fano's inequality converts
the shortfall into a hard error floor $P_e \ge 1 - C/R - \tfrac{1}{nR}$.
Above capacity, no code, present or future, escapes it.
</KeyFact>

## Hamming (7,4): three parity checks localize one error

The theorem names no code; this section builds the classic small one.
The [Hamming (7,4) code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamming%287%2C4%29)
(Hamming 1950, already exhibited in §17 of Shannon's 1948 paper)
protects $4$ data bits $s_1 s_2 s_3 s_4$ with $3$ parity bits computed
as
$$
t_5 = s_1 \oplus s_2 \oplus s_3, \qquad
t_6 = s_2 \oplus s_3 \oplus s_4, \qquad
t_7 = s_1 \oplus s_3 \oplus s_4,
$$
and transmits the $7$-bit block $s_1 s_2 s_3 s_4 t_5 t_6 t_7$: rate
$R = \tfrac{4}{7} \approx 0.571$. Each parity bit makes the total number
of ones in its group even, and the three groups overlap in the pattern
of a three-circle Venn diagram.

<Example title="encode, corrupt, correct">
**Encode** $s = 1000$. The parities:
$t_5 = 1 \oplus 0 \oplus 0 = 1$, $t_6 = 0 \oplus 0 \oplus 0 = 0$,
$t_7 = 1 \oplus 0 \oplus 0 = 1$. Transmitted block: $1000101$.

**Corrupt.** The channel flips $s_2$: received $1100101$.

**Recompute the three checks** on the received block (each sums its
circle, and should be even):
- check 1 ($s_1, s_2, s_3, t_5$): $1 + 1 + 0 + 1 = 3$, odd — **fails**;
- check 2 ($s_2, s_3, s_4, t_6$): $1 + 0 + 0 + 0 = 1$, odd — **fails**;
- check 3 ($s_1, s_3, s_4, t_7$): $1 + 0 + 0 + 1 = 2$, even — holds.

**Localize.** The error lies in every failing circle and in no holding
circle: inside checks 1 and 2, outside check 3. One bit occupies that
region of the Venn diagram: $s_2$. Flip it back; the data $1000$ is
recovered exactly.
</Example>

The pattern of check results is the *syndrome*, and the count works out
exactly: $3$ binary checks give $2^3 = 8$ syndromes, which is one for
"no error" plus one for each of the $7$ possible single-bit flips. Every
single error in a block, in data or parity bits alike, is corrected;
blocks with two or more flips decode to the wrong codeword.

### The payoff against repetition: structure beats brute redundancy

At $f = 0.01$, a block fails when at least two of its $7$ bits flip:
$$
1 - (1-f)^7 - 7f(1-f)^6 \approx 0.0020,
$$
against $0.039$ for $4$ unprotected bits. Repetition R3 protecting the
same $4$ data bits spends $12$ channel uses (rate $\tfrac{1}{3}$) for a
block failure probability of $\approx 0.0012$. The Hamming code reaches
a comparable failure rate, within a factor of two, while carrying
$71\%$ more data per channel use. Both codes correct any single error
per block; Hamming simply pays $3$ parity bits for $4$ data bits where
repetition pays $8$ for $4$.

<Remark title="one point, not the theorem">
Hamming (7,4) is a single $(16, 7)$ code at one fixed rate: its block
error on $\mathrm{BSC}(f)$ is a fixed number, not a sequence tending to
$0$, and at $f = 0.01$ its rate $0.571$ sits far below the capacity
$C = 1 - H(0.01) \approx 0.919$ that the theorem makes achievable with
vanishing error. Shannon's §17 shows the code attains the capacity of a
different, specially constrained channel, one guaranteed to corrupt at
most one symbol per block of seven; on the BSC no capacity claim
attaches to it. The code demonstrates that structured redundancy works;
the theorem says how far such structure can be pushed.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Three parity checks pinpoint any single error in seven bits because the
counting is exact: $2^3 = 8$ syndromes cover $7$ error positions plus
"no error". Redundancy arranged to intersect beats redundancy merely
piled up.
</KeyFact>

## From existence to constructions: LDPC, turbo, polar

What the theorem delivers is the exact frontier, in both directions, for
every discrete memoryless channel. What it withholds is a code:
the achievability proof averages over codebooks that cannot be stored or
searched, and for decades no explicit family provably approached $C$
with practical decoding.

The gap closed in stages.
[Gallager's low-density parity-check codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low-density_parity-check_code)
(1963) generalize Hamming's idea to large sparse random check
matrices; they were computationally ahead of their time, forgotten, and
rediscovered in the 1990s (MacKay & Neal, 1996), whereupon iterative
decoding carried them to within fractions of a decibel of capacity.
Turbo codes (Berrou, Glavieux & Thitimajshima, 1993) reached similar
performance empirically and made near-capacity coding an engineering
commodity. The proof came last:
[Arıkan's polar codes](https://arxiv.org/abs/0807.3917) (2009) are the
first explicit family with a theorem: they achieve the capacity of every
symmetric binary-input DMC with encoding and decoding complexity
$O(n \log n)$. Later standardization work built on that result: the 5G
cellular standard (3GPP, 2016–2018) adopted polar codes for its control
channels, with LDPC codes carrying the data channels.

One boundary of scope: capacity is a statement per channel use, and
matching a *source* of entropy rate $H$ to a channel of capacity $C$ is
a further theorem, source-channel separation, which states that
compressing first (<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>) and
channel-coding second loses nothing for a DMC. Shannon's §14 already
notes the mechanism behind it: natural sources arrive with redundancy of
their own, and coding redistributes it.

<KeyFact>
The pair of coding theorems gives each course quantity its operational
meaning: entropy is the fewest bits per symbol a source can be
compressed to, and capacity, the maximum of mutual information, is the
most message bits per use a channel can carry reliably. Mutual
information is not a descriptive statistic; it is a rate.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Compute the per-bit error probability of the five-fold repetition code
R5 on $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$ under majority decoding, and its rate. Compare
both numbers with R3's ($p_b = 0.028$ at $R = \tfrac{1}{3}$), and state
what the comparison illustrates about repetition.

<Solution>
Majority-of-five errs when at least $3$ of the $5$ copies flip:
$$
p_b = \binom{5}{3} f^3 (1-f)^2 + \binom{5}{4} f^4 (1-f) + f^5
= 10 \cdot 0.001 \cdot 0.81 + 5 \cdot 0.0001 \cdot 0.9 + 10^{-5},
$$
so $p_b = 0.0081 + 0.00045 + 0.00001 \approx 0.0086$, at rate
$R = \tfrac{1}{5} = 0.2$.

Against R3: error improves by a factor of $\approx 3.3$
($0.028 \to 0.0086$) while the rate drops from $0.333$ to $0.2$. Each
step of improvement costs a constant fraction of the remaining rate:
error $\to 0$ along the repetition family forces $R \to 0$. The coding
theorem asserts this trade is avoidable at every rate below
$C = 0.531$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A system must deliver $300$ message bits over $n = 1000$ uses of
$\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$; a second design calls for $600$ message bits over
the same $1000$ uses. For each design, give the rate, state what the
coding theorem says about it, and where applicable compute the error
floor from the converse.

<Solution>
First design: $R = 300/1000 = 0.3 < C = 0.531$. Achievable: by the
theorem there exist $(2^{300}, 1000)$-style code sequences whose block
error tends to $0$ (and at this margin below capacity, good codes of
blocklength $1000$ already exist in practice).

Second design: $R = 600/1000 = 0.6 > C$. Not achievable. The converse
floor:
$$
P_e \ge 1 - \frac{C}{R} - \frac{1}{nR}
= 1 - \frac{0.531}{0.6} - \frac{1}{600}
= 1 - 0.885 - 0.0017 \approx 0.113.
$$
At least $11\%$ of blocks decode wrongly under every conceivable code;
the specification cannot be met on this channel. The remedy is more
channel uses per message bit, not a cleverer code.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The achievability sketch bounds the wrong-codeword error by
$2^{-n(I(X;Y) - R)}$. On $\mathrm{BSC}(0.1)$ (so $I = C = 0.531$ at the
uniform input), evaluate the bound at $n = 1000$ for $R = 0.4$ and for
$R = 0.53$. Then find the blocklength $n$ at which the $R = 0.53$ bound
matches the $R = 0.4$, $n = 1000$ bound. What does the comparison say
about operating near capacity?

<Solution>
At $R = 0.4$: exponent $n(C - R) = 1000 \cdot 0.131 = 131$, bound
$2^{-131} \approx 10^{-39}$.

At $R = 0.53$: exponent $1000 \cdot 0.001 = 1$, bound
$2^{-1} = 0.5$ — useless at this blocklength.

Matching: the bound is $2^{-n(C-R)}$, so $n \cdot 0.001 = 131$ requires
$n = 131{,}000$.

The safety margin is the exponent $n(C - R)$: the product of blocklength
and distance from capacity. Halving the distance to capacity doubles the
blocklength needed for the same guarantee; operating within $0.001$ bits
of $C$ costs two orders of magnitude more blocklength than operating
$0.131$ bits below it. The threshold is sharp only in the limit; near
it, $n$ is the price.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A decoder for $M = 2^{500}$ messages ($n = 1000$, $R = 0.5$) achieves
$P_e = 10^{-3}$. Use Fano's inequality to bound $H(W \mid \hat W)$, and
interpret the two terms of the bound. Use
$\log_2 10^{-3} \approx -9.97$.

<Solution>
The exact form:
$$
H(W \mid \hat W) \le H_2(P_e) + P_e \log_2 (M - 1).
$$
First term: $H_2(10^{-3}) = -10^{-3} \log_2 10^{-3} - 0.999 \log_2
0.999 \approx 0.00997 + 0.00144 \approx 0.011$ bits. Second term:
$10^{-3} \cdot \log_2(2^{500} - 1) \approx 10^{-3} \cdot 500 = 0.5$
bits. Total: $H(W \mid \hat W) \le 0.511$ bits.

Interpretation: out of $H(W) = 500$ bits of message uncertainty, the
decoder's output leaves at most half a bit unresolved. The first term
prices the uncertainty about *whether* an error occurred (the indicator
$E$); the second prices *which* of the $M - 1$ wrong messages was sent
when one did, weighted by how rarely that case arises. Small error
probability forces small residual entropy: exactly the lever the
converse pulls in reverse.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Encode $s = 0110$ with the Hamming (7,4) code. The channel then flips
the parity bit $t_6$. Compute the received block, run the three checks,
localize the error from the syndrome, and state what the receiver
outputs.

<Solution>
**Encode.** $s_1 s_2 s_3 s_4 = 0110$:
$t_5 = 0 \oplus 1 \oplus 1 = 0$, $t_6 = 1 \oplus 1 \oplus 0 = 0$,
$t_7 = 0 \oplus 1 \oplus 0 = 1$. Transmitted: $0110001$.

**Corrupt.** Flipping $t_6$ gives received block $0110011$.

**Checks** (each sums its circle; even = holds):
- check 1 ($s_1, s_2, s_3, t_5$): $0 + 1 + 1 + 0 = 2$, even — holds;
- check 2 ($s_2, s_3, s_4, t_6$): $1 + 1 + 0 + 1 = 3$, odd — **fails**;
- check 3 ($s_1, s_3, s_4, t_7$): $0 + 1 + 0 + 1 = 2$, even — holds.

**Localize.** The error lies inside check 2 and outside checks 1 and 3.
The only bit in circle 2 alone is $t_6$ itself. Flip it back to get
$0110001$; the receiver outputs the data bits $0110$, which were in fact
never corrupted. Errors in parity bits are detected and corrected by
the same mechanism as errors in data bits: the syndrome does not care
which kind of bit failed.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Show that Hamming (7,4) is a *perfect* code: the radius-$1$ Hamming
balls around its $16$ codewords exactly partition the space of $2^7$
binary words. Conclude that every pattern of two or more bit errors is
decoded to a wrong codeword.

<Solution>
**Ball size.** The radius-$1$ ball around a $7$-bit word contains the
word itself plus its $7$ single-bit neighbors: $1 + 7 = 8$ words.

**Disjointness.** Single-error correction means every word at distance
$\le 1$ from a codeword is decoded to that codeword; a word inside two
different codewords' balls would have to decode to both, which is
impossible. So the $16$ balls are pairwise disjoint.

**Counting.** Disjoint balls cover $16 \cdot 8 = 128 = 2^7$ words: the
whole space, with nothing left over. The balls partition
$\{0,1\}^7$ exactly, which is the definition of a perfect code.

**Consequence.** A received word carrying $\ge 2$ errors lies at
distance $\ge 2$ from the true codeword, hence outside its ball. Being
in the partition, it lies inside exactly one other codeword's ball and
is decoded there: a wrong codeword, with certainty. Perfection means
zero slack — every syndrome is spent on single errors, so nothing is
left to even detect double ones.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The binary erasure channel $\mathrm{BEC}(e)$ of
<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/> has capacity $C = 1 - e$. (a) For
$\mathrm{BEC}(0.4)$ and rate $R = 0.75$, compute the asymptotic converse
floor. (b) Give a direct counting argument, without Fano, for why no
code at rate $R > 1 - e$ can have vanishing error: compare the number of
messages with the number a genie-aided receiver could distinguish if
the erasure locations were announced in advance.

<Solution>
**(a)** $C = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6$, and
$$
P_e \ge 1 - \frac{C}{R} = 1 - \frac{0.6}{0.75} = 0.2
$$
asymptotically: at least one block in five fails, under every code.

**(b)** Over $n$ uses, the law of large numbers puts the number of
unerased positions near $(1 - e)n$. Hand the receiver a genie that
announces the erasure pattern in advance: the transmission is then
determined entirely by the $\approx (1-e)n$ surviving bits, so the
receiver can distinguish at most $2^{(1-e)n}$ transmission outcomes.
The code carries $2^{nR}$ messages, and for $R > 1 - e$,
$$
\frac{2^{nR}}{2^{(1-e)n}} = 2^{n(R - (1-e))} \longrightarrow \infty:
$$
exponentially many messages per distinguishable outcome, so most
messages share their receivable evidence with exponentially many others
and cannot be decoded reliably. The genie only strengthens the
receiver, so the true channel does no better. This is the converse made
concrete: the channel simply does not deliver enough distinguishable
outputs to separate $2^{nR}$ messages.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

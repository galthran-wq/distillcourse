Lossy compression trades bits for fidelity, and the rate-distortion function
R(D) = min I(X;X-hat) subject to expected distortion at most D is the fewest
bits per symbol that buy reconstruction within tolerance D.

The course has priced exact description twice. Entropy is the floor on
lossless bits per symbol (<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>), and
capacity is the ceiling on reliable bits per channel use
(<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>); both are mutual informations attached to
a fixed distribution. Neither limit governs a photograph or an audio stream,
which are never reconstructed exactly and never need to be. This lesson drops
exactness, replaces it with a numerical fidelity budget, and finds that the
resulting limit is again a mutual information, minimised over channels
rather than maximised over inputs.

The route: a 100-bit game whose optimum beats the obvious scheme by more than
a factor of two, the definition of a distortion measure and of $R(D)$, the
two classical closed forms, the covering geometry that explains the game, and
the gap between the curve and a quantizer anyone can build.

## The Hamming game: 100 bits written down in 50

Before any theory, the trade can be posed as a concrete problem with a
checkable answer.

<Task>
Closed book, from the prerequisites. (1) State the entropy of one fair,
independent bit and the fewest bits per symbol any lossless code for that
source can average. (2) Write $I(X;Y)$ as a difference of entropies. (3) For
a Markov chain $X \to W \to \hat X$, state the inequality the data-processing
result of <Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/> puts on
$I(X; \hat X)$. Then open the fold and check.
</Task>

<Aside>
(1) $H(X) = 1$ bit; the source coding theorem
(<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>) puts the floor for lossless
coding at exactly $H(X) = 1$ bit per symbol, met by copying the stream.
(2) $I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$, the drop in
uncertainty about either variable on observing the other
(<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>). (3)
$I(X; \hat X) \le I(X; W)$: nothing computed from $W$ knows more about $X$
than $W$ does.
</Aside>

**The game.** One hundred fair, independent bits are shown to you. You may
write anything you like on a slip of paper that holds at most 50 bits. The
slip is then all you have: from it you must guess all 100 original bits, and
your score is the fraction you get wrong. Decide on a scheme and write down
the error fraction it achieves before reading on.

Two schemes bracket the answer. Writing nothing gives a decoder that outputs
all zeros and is wrong on half the bits. Copying 50 of the bits exactly and
guessing the other 50 is wrong on half of the guessed half: an error fraction
of $\tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$. The second scheme
is the one most readers write down, and 25% is not the optimum. The best slip
is wrong on **11%** of the bits: exactly 11% in the long-block limit that
$R(D)$ describes, and a shade above it for a single block of 100.

<Figure
  src="./figures/hamming-game-tally.svg"
  alt="Two ten-by-ten grids of 100 bits: the store-half-and-guess scheme with 25 amber squares marking wrong bits, and the optimal 50-bit description with 11 amber squares, both under the same half-bit-per-symbol budget"
  caption="Same 50-bit budget, two schemes: storing half the bits and guessing the rest is wrong on 25 bits; the long-block optimum is wrong on 11, a count a single 100-bit block approaches but does not reach."
/>

The gap is not a coding trick applied bit by bit. The naive scheme decides
each bit's fate separately, stored or guessed, and any scheme that treats
the bits one at a time is stuck at 25%. The optimum describes all 100 bits
with one joint description, and joint description is strictly stronger even
though the bits are independent and the score treats them all alike.

<KeyFact>
The gap is a property of block length rather than of effort: at $n = 1$
nothing beats 25%, and the reachable error fraction falls toward 11% only as
the block grows. Deciding one bit at a time pins the reconstruction points to
a rectangular grid, and grid cells waste more of their volume in their corners
the more dimensions they have.
</KeyFact>

## The distortion measure is chosen, not discovered

Scoring the game by the fraction of wrong bits was a choice, made silently.
The general theory refuses to make it silently: the error metric is an input
to the problem, supplied before any rate can be computed.

<Definition title="distortion measure">
A **distortion measure** on a source alphabet $\mathcal{X}$ and a
reconstruction alphabet $\hat{\mathcal{X}}$ is a function
$d : \mathcal{X} \times \hat{\mathcal{X}} \to [0, \infty)$. For blocks it is
averaged per symbol,
$d(x^n, \hat x^n) = \tfrac1n \sum_{i=1}^n d(x_i, \hat x_i)$, and the measure
is *bounded* when $\max_{x, \hat x} d(x, \hat x) < \infty$.
</Definition>

Four instances fix what the definition admits.

- **The canonical case.** *Hamming distortion* on a discrete alphabet:
  $d(x, \hat x) = 1$ if $x \ne \hat x$ and $0$ otherwise. Its average is the
  fraction of misreconstructed symbols, the game's score.
- **A different surface, the same structure.** *Squared error* on the reals:
  $d(x, \hat x) = (x - \hat x)^2$, averaging to the mean squared error. The
  alphabets are infinite and the values unbounded, but the object is the same
  two-argument function.
- **A degenerate but admissible case.** $d \equiv 0$ is a distortion measure.
  Every reconstruction meets every budget, so $R(D) = 0$ for all $D \ge 0$:
  the definition covers the case in which nothing is required and returns
  the right answer.
- **The near miss.** The log-loss "distortion"
  $d(x, \hat x) = -\log_2 \hat x(x)$, with $\hat x$ a distribution over
  $\mathcal{X}$, breaks exactly one clause — the codomain. It takes the value
  $+\infty$ wherever the reconstruction assigns probability zero to the symbol
  that occurred, and $+\infty$ is not a member of $[0, \infty)$. Restricted to
  reconstructions that give every symbol positive probability it is
  admissible, and still unbounded. Boundedness is the hypothesis the
  achievability theorem below needs: with $\max d$ infinite, one atypical
  block can carry arbitrarily large distortion and the block average stops
  concentrating.

Nothing in the definition ranks these. Squared error is used on images and
audio because it is quadratic, differentiable, and makes the Gaussian case
solvable in closed form, not because two images at equal mean squared error
look equally good; they routinely do not. Every number this lesson computes
is a statement about the measure that was fed in.

With a measure fixed, every scheme becomes a point $(D, R)$ in a plane:
distortion paid against rate spent. Writing nothing sits at
$(\tfrac12, 0)$, store-half-and-guess at $(\tfrac14, \tfrac12)$, the optimum
at $(0.11, \tfrac12)$. The subject of the rest of this lesson is the exact
boundary between the pairs some code attains and the pairs none does.

<KeyFact>
The distortion measure is the one ingredient the theory never derives.
Changing it moves the whole curve, so a rate quoted without its measure is
not a number; and it is exactly this hand-picked ingredient that
<Ref lesson="information-bottleneck"/> removes by deriving the effective
measure from a target variable instead.
</KeyFact>

## R(D): the least information the budget allows

The frontier has to be computed from the source and the measure alone,
without enumerating codes. The route is to look at what any code does to a
single symbol.

### Every scheme is a channel from source to reconstruction

Whatever machinery sits between source and reconstruction (encoder,
bitstream, decoder), its end-to-end effect on one symbol is a conditional
distribution $p(\hat x \mid x)$: for each source symbol, the probabilities of
the reconstructions it may become. That conditional distribution is called a
*test channel*. It is not hardware and not a model of the source; it is the
bookkeeping device in which the bound is stated.

The rate such a channel needs is measured by the mutual information
$I(X; \hat X)$ of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, the bits one variable
carries about the other. The compressed file is a bottleneck between $X$ and
$\hat X$: the reconstruction is computed from the file, the file is computed
from the source, and the data-processing inequality
(<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>) caps $I(X;\hat X)$ by the file's
own size. Minimising rate therefore means finding the test channel that meets
the distortion budget while letting the **least** information through.

<Definition title="rate-distortion function">
For a source $X \sim p(x)$ and a distortion measure $d$, the
**rate-distortion function** is
$$
R(D) \;=\; \min_{p(\hat x \mid x)\,:\; \mathbb{E}[d(X, \hat X)] \le D}\; I(X; \hat X),
$$
the minimum over all test channels whose expected distortion,
$\mathbb{E}[d] = \sum_{x, \hat x} p(x)\, p(\hat x \mid x)\, d(x, \hat x)$,
stays within $D$. On a finite alphabet the admissible channels form a
compact set and $I$ is continuous on it, so the minimum is attained and the
word "min" is literal.
</Definition>

### The theorem: the single-symbol minimum governs long blocks

The definition is an optimisation over distributions on one symbol. What
makes it a compression limit is that it also governs blocks of any length.

<Theorem title="rate-distortion theorem (Shannon 1959)">
For an i.i.d. source $X \sim p(x)$ and a bounded distortion measure $d$,
$R(D)$ is the boundary of achievability: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there
exist block codes of rate $R(D) + \varepsilon$ whose expected distortion is
at most $D + \varepsilon$ for all sufficiently large block lengths, and no
sequence of codes of rate below $R(D)$ meets the budget $D$.

<Proof>
Sketch in both directions; the details are Cover & Thomas §10.4 and §10.5.

**Converse.** A rate-$R$ code compresses $X^n$ into an index
$W \in \lbrace 1, \dots, 2^{nR} \rbrace$ and reconstructs $\hat X^n$ from $W$
alone, so $X^n \to W \to \hat X^n$ is a Markov chain. Data processing and the
entropy bound on $W$ give
$I(X^n; \hat X^n) \le I(X^n; W) \le H(W) \le nR$. For an i.i.d. source the
block information splits into single letters,
$I(X^n; \hat X^n) \ge \sum_{i=1}^n I(X_i; \hat X_i)$; each term is at least
$R(\mathbb{E}[d(X_i, \hat X_i)])$ because $R$ is defined as a minimum over
exactly those channels; and convexity (next subsection) pulls the average
through, giving $nR \ge n\,R(\mathbb{E}[d(X^n, \hat X^n)])$. A code meeting
budget $D$ therefore spends at least $R(D)$.

**Achievability.** Fix the minimising test channel and draw a codebook of
$2^{n(R(D) + \varepsilon)}$ reconstruction words independently from the
induced marginal $p(\hat x)$. With high probability a typical source block is
jointly typical with at least one codeword, and joint typicality forces the
per-symbol distortion between them to sit near $\mathbb{E}[d] \le D$; the
encoder transmits that codeword's index. Boundedness of $d$ is what keeps the
rare failure events from dominating the expected distortion. The count of
codewords needed is the covering argument of the geometry section below.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### Endpoints and shape

Three structural facts hold for every source before any specific curve is
computed, and they are what make the picture a curve rather than a scatter of
points.

<Theorem title="shape of R(D)">
Let $d$ satisfy $d(x, \hat x) = 0$ exactly when $\hat x = x$, on a common
alphabet. Then:

1. $R(0) = H(X)$: the zero-tolerance corner is lossless source coding.
2. $R(D)$ is non-increasing, and $R(D) = 0$ exactly when
   $D \ge D_{\max} = \min_{\hat x} \mathbb{E}[d(X, \hat x)]$, the distortion
   of the best constant reconstruction.
3. $R(D)$ is convex on $[0, D_{\max}]$.

<Proof>
**(1)** Budget $D = 0$ forces $P(X \ne \hat X) = 0$, so
$I(X; \hat X) = I(X; X) = H(X)$.

**(2)** Enlarging $D$ only enlarges the set of admissible channels, so the
minimum cannot rise. If $D \ge D_{\max}$, the constant channel
$\hat X \equiv \arg\min_{\hat x} \mathbb{E}[d(X, \hat x)]$ meets the budget
with $I = 0$. If $D < D_{\max}$, zero rate is out of reach: $I(X;\hat X) = 0$
makes $\hat X$ independent of $X$, and then
$\mathbb{E}[d] = \sum_{\hat x} p(\hat x)\, \mathbb{E}[d(X, \hat x)] \ge
D_{\max} > D$.

**(3)** Let $q_1, q_2$ attain $R(D_1), R(D_2)$. The blended channel
$q_\lambda = \lambda q_1 + (1 - \lambda) q_2$ has expected distortion exactly
$\lambda D_1 + (1-\lambda) D_2$, since $\mathbb{E}[d]$ is linear in the
channel, and mutual information is convex in $p(\hat x \mid x)$ at fixed
$p(x)$ (Cover & Thomas Theorem 2.7.4), so
$I_{q_\lambda} \le \lambda R(D_1) + (1 - \lambda) R(D_2)$. The minimum at the
blended budget is no larger.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Convexity does two jobs in this lesson: the converse above averaged its
single-letter bounds with it, and the closing section reads the curve's slope
as a price because of it. Operationally it says that time-sharing
two codes never beats the curve, because the time-shared point is a convex
combination of two points already on or above it.

<KeyFact>
$R(D)$ is a limit over long blocks, and nothing here promises it at any
finite block length. A one-shot quantizer sits strictly above the curve by a
gap that is a property of dimension rather than of effort: about a quarter
of a bit per sample even after entropy coding, as the quantizer section
computes.
</KeyFact>

## Capacity's mirror: max over inputs, min over channels

Both limits in this course are now the same functional of the same pair of
variables, $I$ of a source and its counterpart; they differ in which argument
is fixed and in the direction the optimisation runs.

<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/> defined $C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y)$: the
channel $p(y \mid x)$ is given by physics and the input distribution is the
free variable, tuned to push the most information through. Rate-distortion
exchanges both roles. The source $p(x)$ is given and the channel
$p(\hat x \mid x)$ is the free variable, tuned to let the least information
through subject to the fidelity budget. Communication maximises mutual
information over inputs; compression minimises it over channels.

The symmetry is not merely notational. Mutual information is concave in the
input at a fixed channel and convex in the channel at a fixed input, so
capacity is a concave maximisation and $R(D)$ a convex minimisation: both are
well-posed convex programs with no local traps, and the same alternating
algorithm, [Blahut–Arimoto](https://doi.org/10.1109/TIT.1972.1054855),
alternating between the channel and a marginal, computes either one
numerically for a source or channel with no closed form.

Each problem also needs its constraint for the answer to be finite and
non-trivial. Without the noise in $p(y \mid x)$, capacity would be the
alphabet's full $\log_2 |\mathcal{X}|$; without the distortion budget,
$R(D)$ would be zero, attained by the channel that ignores the source. The
budget is what stops the minimisation at a positive value, which is why
$R(D)$ is a curve and $C$ is a number.

## Bernoulli under Hamming: R(D) = H(p) − H(D)

The mirror explains what kind of object $R(D)$ is; the game asked for a
number. For a biased-bit source under Hamming distortion the minimisation has
an exact solution, and it is the first of the two classical closed forms.

<Theorem title="Bernoulli rate-distortion function (Cover & Thomas 10.3.1)">
For $X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ under Hamming distortion,
$$
R(D) =
\begin{cases}
H(p) - H(D), & 0 \le D \le \min(p,\, 1-p), \\[2pt]
0, & D \ge \min(p,\, 1-p),
\end{cases}
$$
with $H(\cdot)$ the binary entropy function of
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>.

<Proof>
Take $p \le \tfrac12$ (relabel otherwise), so $\min(p, 1-p) = p$ and
$D \le p \le \tfrac12$. The plan is a lower bound on every admissible
channel, then one channel that attains it.

**Converse: no admissible channel beats $H(p) - H(D)$.** Let
$p(\hat x \mid x)$ satisfy $P(X \ne \hat X) = \mathbb{E}[d] \le D$. Write the
error indicator $Z = X \oplus \hat X$. The pair $(X, \hat X)$ determines
$(Z, \hat X)$ and conversely, so $H(X \mid \hat X) = H(Z \mid \hat X)$;
conditioning cannot raise entropy
(<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>), so
$H(Z \mid \hat X) \le H(Z)$. The variable $Z$ is binary with
$P(Z = 1) \le D \le \tfrac12$, and $H$ increases on $[0, \tfrac12]$, so
$H(Z) \le H(D)$. Chaining:
$$
I(X; \hat X) = H(X) - H(X \mid \hat X)
\;\ge\; H(p) - H(Z) \;\ge\; H(p) - H(D).
$$
Both inequalities are equalities exactly when $Z$ is independent of $\hat X$
and $Z \sim \text{Bernoulli}(D)$, which is the recipe for the construction.

**Achievability: one channel meets the bound.** Build the joint distribution
backwards from the reconstruction. Let
$\hat X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(q)$ with $q = \tfrac{p - D}{1 - 2D}$, and let
$X = \hat X \oplus Z$ with $Z \sim \text{Bernoulli}(D)$ independent of
$\hat X$. The marginal checks out:
$P(X = 1) = q(1 - D) + (1 - q) D = D + q(1 - 2D) = p$. The distortion is
$P(X \ne \hat X) = P(Z = 1) = D$, on budget. And given $\hat X$, the only
remaining uncertainty in $X$ is the independent flip, so
$H(X \mid \hat X) = H(D)$ and
$$
I(X; \hat X) = H(X) - H(X \mid \hat X) = H(p) - H(D).
$$
For $D \le p \le \tfrac12$ the parameter $q$ lies in $[0, \tfrac12]$, so the
construction is a valid distribution and the converse bound is attained.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The construction runs against the reader's expectation and against the data
flow: one would expect the reconstruction to be the source plus quantisation
noise. The optimal test channel is the reverse. The **source** is the
reconstruction plus an independent flip, and the reconstruction is *less*
random than the source: $q < p$ for $p < \tfrac12$, so the optimal
reconstruction is more biased toward the majority symbol than the source is.
An optimal lossy coder does not scatter its output around the source; it
pulls it toward the middle of the distribution.

  caption="The optimal test channel for the binary case, drawn backwards: the source is the reconstruction plus an independent Bernoulli(D) flip."

The formula reads as a ledger. Describing the source exactly costs $H(p)$

bits of its own; the encoder pays only the difference. The refund is exact
only because the errors may be placed where the code finds them cheap, which
is again the joint-description effect of the opening game.

### The game, settled

<Example title="fair bits at rate one-half">
For $p = \tfrac12$ the curve is $R(D) = 1 - H(D)$ on $[0, \tfrac12]$.

**The 50-bit slip.** One hundred bits into 50 is a rate of
$R = \tfrac12$ bit per symbol. Solve $1 - H(D) = \tfrac12$, i.e.
$H(D) = \tfrac12$. Since
$H(0.11) = 0.11 \log_2 \tfrac{1}{0.11} + 0.89 \log_2 \tfrac{1}{0.89}
= 0.350 + 0.150 = 0.500$, the answer is $D \approx 0.11$: at this rate the
frontier permits **11 errors per 100 bits**, against the naive scheme's 25.

**The naive scheme, priced the other way.** At $D = \tfrac14$ the curve gives
$R = 1 - H(0.25) = 1 - 0.811 = 0.189$ bits per symbol. The error rate the
naive scheme bought with 50 bits is available for 19: it overspends by a
factor of $2.6$.

**Bounds check.** $R(0) = 1 - H(0) = 1 = H(X)$, the lossless rate, and
$R(\tfrac12) = 1 - H(\tfrac12) = 0$, the free all-zeros guess.
</Example>

<Example title="a biased sensor">
A sensor emits $X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.25)$, so $H(0.25) = 0.811$ bits and
$R(D) = 0.811 - H(D)$ on $[0, 0.25]$.

At $D = 0.1$: $R = 0.811 - 0.469 = 0.342$ bits per symbol, a 58% saving
against lossless coding, in exchange for errors on one symbol in ten. The
curve ends at $D_{\max} = \min(0.25, 0.75) = 0.25$, where always guessing $0$
errs exactly on the 25% of symbols that are $1$, at zero rate:
$R(0.25) = 0.811 - H(0.25) = 0$, consistent.

The optimal reconstruction at $D = 0.1$ has
$q = \tfrac{0.25 - 0.1}{1 - 0.2} = 0.1875$, noticeably more biased toward $0$
than the source.
</Example>

<Exercise>
The optimal fair-source test channel at $D = 0.25$ has the joint table
$P(X{=}0, \hat X{=}0) = P(X{=}1, \hat X{=}1) = \tfrac38$ and
$P(X{=}0, \hat X{=}1) = P(X{=}1, \hat X{=}0) = \tfrac18$. Using the recipes
of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> (marginals from row and column sums,
conditional entropy as a probability-weighted average of column entropies),
compute $\mathbb{E}[d]$ and $I(X;\hat X)$ from the table alone, then check
both against the closed form. Write the two marginals down before opening the
solution.

<Solution>
**Distortion.** The off-diagonal mass is $\tfrac18 + \tfrac18 = \tfrac14$, so
$\mathbb{E}[d] = 0.25$: on budget with equality.

**Marginals.** Every row and column sums to $\tfrac12$, so
$H(X) = H(\hat X) = 1$ bit.

**Conditional entropy.** Given $\hat X = 0$ (probability $\tfrac12$), the
column $(\tfrac38, \tfrac18)$ renormalises to $(\tfrac34, \tfrac14)$, with
entropy $H(0.25) = 0.811$ bits; the $\hat X = 1$ column is its mirror image
and gives the same value. The average is $H(X \mid \hat X) = 0.811$ bits.

**Mutual information.** $I(X;\hat X) = 1 - 0.811 = 0.189$ bits.

**Check.** $R(0.25) = 1 - H(0.25) = 0.189$: the table attains the minimum, so
this is an optimal test channel. It is the construction of the proof at
$q = \tfrac12$, $D = \tfrac14$, written as a joint table. The transferable
move is that a test channel is verified, never trusted: compute its
distortion and its mutual information from the joint table and compare with
the closed form.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyFact>
The flat tail is exact, not asymptotic: from $D = \min(p, 1-p)$ onward,
always emitting the majority symbol costs zero bits and meets the budget. Any
codec whose target quality is worse than "guess the most common value" should
be sending nothing at all, and the same condition is what makes $R(D)$ hit
zero at finite $D$ for discrete sources — and never for continuous ones.
</KeyFact>

## The Gaussian source: half a log, and 6 dB per bit

The second closed form is continuous, and it is the one every quantizer
designer uses. Its derivation follows the binary proof line for line, with
differential entropies in place of discrete ones.

<Theorem title="Gaussian rate-distortion function (Cover & Thomas 10.3.2)">
For $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ under squared-error distortion,
$$
R(D) =
\begin{cases}
\tfrac12 \log_2 \dfrac{\sigma^2}{D}, & 0 < D \le \sigma^2, \\[4pt]
0, & D \ge \sigma^2,
\end{cases}
$$
attained by the test channel $X = \hat X + Z$ with
$\hat X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 - D)$ and $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, D)$
independent.
</Theorem>

The test channel repeats the binary lesson in continuous form: the
reconstruction carries variance $\sigma^2 - D$, strictly less than the
source's, and the source is the reconstruction plus independent noise. An
optimal lossy coder shrinks.

Two flags on the statement. Differential entropy is not a bit count (it can
be negative and it changes under a change of coordinates), but $R(D)$ is a
*difference* of differential entropies and the coordinate-dependent terms
cancel, so the rate is a genuine bit count. And the endpoints behave like the
discrete case at one end only: $D_{\max} = \sigma^2$ is the squared error of
the best constant guess $\hat X = \mathbb{E}[X] = 0$, but as $D \to 0$ the
rate grows without bound.

started.

## Covering, not packing: the geometry behind the 11%

Both closed forms were reached by algebra, and neither explains why
describing 100 bits jointly beats describing them one at a time. The counting
argument behind achievability does.

Fix a block length $n$. The source blocks that matter are the typical ones
(<Ref lesson="source-coding-theorem-lite"/>, where a long block was shown to
concentrate on about $2^{nH(X)}$ sequences of near-average surprisal). Each
reconstruction word $\hat x^n$ in the codebook accounts for the source blocks
jointly typical with it, of which there are about $2^{nH(X \mid \hat X)}$.
Covering the whole typical set therefore needs about
$$
\frac{2^{nH(X)}}{2^{nH(X \mid \hat X)}} = 2^{n I(X; \hat X)}
$$
codewords, which is $2^{nR}$ at $R = I(X;\hat X)$: the definition of $R(D)$
is a count of balls needed to cover a set.

<Figure
  src="./figures/packing-vs-covering.svg"
  alt="Two panels: channel coding packs disjoint noise balls inside an output space, lossy coding covers a typical set with overlapping distortion balls, both counts equal to two to the n I"
  caption="The dual geometries: channel coding packs disjoint balls (as many as fit), lossy coding covers with overlapping balls (as few as suffice). Both counts are 2^(nI)."
/>

The channel problem of <Ref lesson="noisy-channel-coding"/> counts the same
way in the opposite direction. There, each codeword's noise ball must stay
*disjoint* from the others so the decoder never confuses two messages, and
the number that fits is about
$2^{nH(Y)} / 2^{nH(Y \mid X)} = 2^{nI(X;Y)}$. Packing wants that number as
large as possible, hence $\max I$; covering wants it as small as possible,
hence $\min I$. One exponent, two directions.

This is also the answer to the game. A scheme that decides each bit
separately places its reconstruction points on the corners of a rectangular
grid, and a grid's cells are a poor shape for covering: at fixed cell volume
they reach further into the corners than a ball would, which is wasted
distortion. Working in $n$ dimensions at once lets the code choose ball-like
cells and lets concentration discard the atypical blocks entirely; neither
saving is available one bit at a time.

<KeyFact>
The covering count is exponential in $n$ with exponent $I(X;\hat X)$, so the
11% figure is a statement about long blocks: at $n = 100$ a real code lands
near but not on it, and at $n = 1$ it is unreachable. Every gap between
theory and codec in this course is a finite-blocklength gap of this kind.
</KeyFact>

## What a real quantizer gives up

The theory says to cover a high-dimensional typical set. Shipped systems
usually quantize one sample at a time, and the distance between the two is
measurable.

### Lloyd's algorithm: alternate two conditions

A scalar quantizer with $N$ reconstruction points is fixed by two things: the
points $c_1, \dots, c_N$ and the cells assigned to them. Two conditions are
necessary at any optimum, and each is easy to enforce given the other. Every
source value must map to its **nearest** reconstruction point, which makes
the cells the intervals between midpoints; and each point must be the
**centroid** $\mathbb{E}[X \mid X \in \text{cell}]$ of its own cell, since
the mean minimises squared error over a set. Lloyd's algorithm enforces them
alternately until neither moves.

  name="lloyd"

  caption="Lloyd's algorithm: move the points to their cells' centroids, move the boundaries to the points' midpoints, repeat until neither moves."

<Task>
Track the leftmost point across the three frames. It starts at $x = -2.4$,
where the density is nearly zero, and moves to $-1.91$, the centroid of the
cell $(-\infty, -1.6)$ — toward the mass. Then confirm that frame 3 changes

</Task>

Each step can only reduce the expected squared error, so the algorithm
converges, and its fixed points are exactly the *centroidal* tessellations:
partitions in which every cell's centroid is its own reconstruction point.
Convergence is not optimality: centroidal tessellations are not unique, and
the algorithm can settle on one that is not the best. For a log-concave
density the fixed point is unique, which covers the Gaussian; even there, the
optimal points are not known in closed form beyond $N = 3$.

### The high-rate rule and the size of the gap

At high rates the optimal quantizer's design is describable even when its
points are not. Writing $\lambda(x)$ for the density of reconstruction points
and treating cells as short, the cell around $x$ has width about
$1/(N\lambda(x))$ and contributes a squared error of about one twelfth of
that width squared, giving
$$
D \;\approx\; \frac{1}{12 N^2} \int \frac{p(x)}{\lambda(x)^2}\,dx .
$$
Minimising over $\lambda$ by Hölder's inequality puts the optimal point
density at $\lambda^\ast \propto p^{1/3}$ — the **Panter–Dite** rule: spend
reconstruction points in proportion to the cube root of the source density,
which is considerably flatter than the density itself. Substituting back for
a Gaussian source gives
$$
D_{\text{scalar}}(R) \;\approx\; \frac{\sqrt{3}\,\pi}{2}\, \sigma^2 4^{-R}
\;=\; 2.72\,\sigma^2 4^{-R},
$$
against the theoretical $\sigma^2 4^{-R}$. The best possible fixed-rate
scalar quantizer is therefore $10\log_{10} 2.72 = 4.35$ dB above the curve,
which at 6 dB per bit is $0.72$ bit per sample thrown away.

Most of that loss is recoverable without leaving one dimension. A **uniform**
scalar quantizer whose output is then entropy-coded, the classical
transform-codec design, lands within $\tfrac12 \log_2 \tfrac{2\pi e}{12}
= 0.254$ bit per sample of $R(D)$ for *any* source at high rate, and no
vector quantizer of any dimension can beat it by more than that. The
remaining quarter-bit is the price of cubical cells rather than the best
space-filling shape.

<KeyFact>
Two numbers bound the whole practical field: fixed-rate scalar quantization
of a Gaussian wastes $0.72$ bit per sample, and entropy-coded uniform scalar
quantization wastes at most $0.254$ bit per sample against $R(D)$ on any
source. The entire payoff of vector quantization, lattice codes and learned
transforms is confined to that quarter of a bit — which is why real codecs
spend their effort on the transform and the entropy model, not on the
quantizer.
</KeyFact>

## ★ Reverse water-filling for parallel Gaussian sources

Real signals are not one Gaussian but many, of unequal variance: the
coefficients of a transform, the bands of a filter bank. For $m$ independent
Gaussians with variances $\sigma_1^2, \dots, \sigma_m^2$ under summed squared
error, the optimum allocates a common water level $\theta$ and sets
$D_i = \min(\theta, \sigma_i^2)$ with $\sum_i D_i = D$, giving
$$
R(D) = \sum_{i \,:\, \sigma_i^2 > \theta} \tfrac12 \log_2
\frac{\sigma_i^2}{\theta} .
$$
Every component quieter than the water level is not coded at all: its
reconstruction is the constant $0$ and it contributes its whole variance to
the distortion budget, while every louder component is coded down to exactly
$\theta$. Equalising the *residual* distortion rather than the rate is what
makes it "reverse" water-filling, against the water-filling that equalises
power in parallel Gaussian channels.

The rule is visible in any transform codec's quantization table: high-frequency
coefficients with small variance are quantized to zero wholesale, which is one
long run of zeros in the bitstream, and the bits go to the low-frequency
coefficients that carry the variance. The core path continues without this
section.

## The budget as a price: R + λD

The curve has been treated as a constraint so far: a budget $D$, a resulting
rate. Every system that implements it treats it as a price instead, and
convexity is what makes the two descriptions interchangeable.

Because $R(D)$ is convex and decreasing, each point of the curve is the
solution of an unconstrained problem: minimise
$$
R + \lambda D
$$
over all schemes, where $\lambda = -R'(D) \ge 0$ is the negated slope at that
point. Sweeping $\lambda$ from large to small walks the solution from the
low-distortion end toward the zero-rate tail; the hard budget has become a
Lagrange multiplier, the same trade made in
<Ref lesson="max-entropy-principle"/> when a constrained expectation became a
tilt parameter.

<Example title="reading the multiplier off the Gaussian curve">
For $\sigma^2 = 1$, $R(D) = -\tfrac12 \log_2 D$, so
$R'(D) = -\tfrac{1}{2D \ln 2}$ and
$\lambda = \tfrac{1}{2D\ln 2}$.

At $D = 0.25$ (rate 1 bit): $\lambda = 1/(2 \cdot 0.25 \cdot 0.693) = 2.89$
bits per unit of squared error. At $D = 0.0625$ (rate 2 bits):
$\lambda = 11.5$. Quadrupling the price of distortion moved the operating
point by exactly one bit — the same 6 dB per bit rule, read through the
multiplier.

A codec exposes $\lambda$, not $D$: the encoder decides each local choice by
comparing the bits it costs against $\lambda$ times the distortion it saves,
which requires no knowledge of the rate the file will eventually reach.
</Example>

distortion balls, the dual of the packing that gives capacity; and real
quantizers sit above it by a gap measured in fractions of a bit. What is not
established is where the distortion measure comes from. It was supplied by

information about a target variable instead. The immediate next step is
<Ref lesson="minimum-description-length"/>, which applies the same accounting
to a model rather than to a signal: a hypothesis is charged for its own
description, and model selection becomes compression.

Evaluate both curves at their two extremes. For $\text{Bernoulli}(p)$ under

<Solution>
**Discrete.** $R(0) = H(p)$ bits: with $D = 0$ the only admissible channel is
the identity, whose mutual information is $I(X;X) = H(X)$. $D_{\max} =
\min(p, 1-p)$, the error rate of the majority guess.

**Continuous.** $R(0) = \lim_{D \to 0} \tfrac12\log_2(\sigma^2/D) = \infty$.
$D_{\max} = \sigma^2$, the squared error of the constant $\hat X = 0$.

**Which hypothesis decides.** $R(0) = I(X;X)$ in both cases; the difference is
that $I(X;X)$ equals $H(X)$, a finite number of bits, only when $X$ is
discrete. A continuous variable is a draw from an uncountable set, and naming
it exactly is not a finite description at all — differential entropy is not a
bit count, and the substitute $\tfrac12\log_2(\sigma^2/D)$ diverges. The
hypothesis doing the work is the discreteness of the alphabet, the same one
the source coding theorem needed.

**A missing reconstruction symbol.** If some $x_0$ with $p(x_0) > 0$ has no
equal in $\hat{\mathcal{X}}$, then $d(x_0, \hat x) > 0$ for every
reconstruction and $\mathbb{E}[d] \ge p(x_0)\min_{\hat x} d(x_0, \hat x) > 0$
for every channel: the budget $D = 0$ is infeasible and $R(0)$ is undefined
rather than infinite. The clause "$d(x,\hat x) = 0$ exactly when
$\hat x = x$, on a common alphabet" in the shape theorem is what excludes
this, and it is the reason a quantizer's reconstruction levels must include
the source values if zero distortion is ever to be claimed.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Break this claim, which is often stated as a rule of thumb: "halving the rate
roughly doubles the distortion." Test it against both closed forms, report
one regime where it is approximately right and two where it fails badly, and
name the property of each curve that decides the outcome.

<Solution>
**Where it holds.** Fair bits, $R = 1 - H(D)$. At $R = 0.5$ the distortion is
$D = 0.11$. Halving to $R = 0.25$ needs $H(D) = 0.75$, giving $D = 0.215$.
The claim is right to within 2% here — a coincidence of the mid-range of the
binary entropy function, not a law.

**First failure: the Gaussian at any rate.** $D = \sigma^2 4^{-R}$, so
halving the rate multiplies the distortion by $4^{R/2}$, a factor that
depends on where you started. From $R = 8$ to $R = 4$ the distortion grows by
$4^4 = 256$-fold, not twofold. The claim fails because the Gaussian curve is
logarithmic in $D$: equal rate *differences*, not ratios, correspond to fixed
distortion factors.

**Second failure: the binary source near its tail.** From $R = 0.05$
($D = 0.371$) to $R = 0.025$ the required $H(D) = 0.975$ gives $D = 0.407$, a
factor of $1.1$. Here the distortion cannot double, because $D$ is a
probability bounded by $D_{\max} = \tfrac12$ and the curve flattens onto it.

**The deciding property.** The rule of thumb assumes $\log D$ is linear in
$R$ with slope $-1$ per halving. The Gaussian has $\log D$ linear in $R$ with
slope $-2$ per bit, so the multiplier compounds; the Bernoulli curve is not
log-linear at all and is clamped at $D_{\max}$. A rate-distortion rule of
thumb that does not name the source and the measure is not a rule.

<Exercise>
Derive the rate-distortion function of a source uniform on $m$ symbols under
Hamming distortion. Show that
$R(D) = \log_2 m - H(D) - D\log_2(m-1)$ for $0 \le D \le 1 - \tfrac1m$, in
both directions: bound $H(X \mid \hat X)$ above using the error indicator,
and exhibit a test channel attaining the bound. Then check the result against
$m = 2$ and at $D = D_{\max}$. Write down the error indicator before opening
the solution.

<Solution>
**Converse.** Let $E$ be the indicator of the event $X \ne \hat X$, so
$P(E = 1) = \mathbb{E}[d] \le D$. Expand $H(X, E \mid \hat X)$ two ways. Since
$E$ is a function of $(X, \hat X)$,
$$
H(X \mid \hat X) = H(X, E \mid \hat X) = H(E \mid \hat X) + H(X \mid E, \hat X).
$$
The first term is at most $H(P(E{=}1))$. In the second, $E = 0$ leaves no
uncertainty at all, and $E = 1$ leaves $X$ among the $m-1$ symbols other than
$\hat X$, worth at most $\log_2(m-1)$ bits. Hence
$H(X \mid \hat X) \le H(P(E{=}1)) + P(E{=}1)\log_2(m-1)$ — Fano's inequality
(<Ref lesson="fano-inequality"/>) in its general form. The right-hand side
increases in $P(E{=}1)$ on $[0, 1 - \tfrac1m]$, since its derivative
$\log_2\frac{(m-1)(1-D)}{D}$ is positive exactly there, so it is at most
$H(D) + D\log_2(m-1)$. Therefore
$$
I(X;\hat X) = \log_2 m - H(X \mid \hat X) \ge \log_2 m - H(D) - D\log_2(m-1).
$$

**Achievability.** Let $\hat X$ be uniform on the $m$ symbols and let the
channel from $\hat X$ to $X$ keep the symbol with probability $1 - D$ and
otherwise replace it by one of the other $m-1$ symbols uniformly. By
symmetry the induced marginal of $X$ is uniform, as required, and the
distortion is exactly $D$. Given $\hat X$, the uncertainty in $X$ is the
choice "same or different" ($H(D)$ bits) plus, when different, a uniform pick
among $m-1$ symbols ($D\log_2(m-1)$ bits). So
$H(X \mid \hat X) = H(D) + D\log_2(m-1)$ and the bound is attained.

**Checks.** At $m = 2$: $\log_2 2 - H(D) - D\log_2 1 = 1 - H(D)$, the fair
binary curve. At $D = D_{\max} = 1 - \tfrac1m$, expanding
$H(1 - \tfrac1m) = \tfrac1m\log_2 m + (1 - \tfrac1m)\log_2\tfrac{m}{m-1}$
gives $R = \log_2 m - \log_2 m = 0$, and $1 - \tfrac1m$ is indeed the error
rate of a constant guess against a uniform source.

The transferable move: every Hamming-distortion converse in this lesson is
Fano's inequality with the error probability renamed as a distortion.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

(<Ref lesson="max-entropy-principle"/>). (b) Verify that the stated test
channel attains it. (c) Convert "one extra bit" into decibels explicitly, and
report the rate needed for a 40 dB signal-to-noise ratio.

<Solution>
**(a) The bound.** Write $I(X;\hat X) = h(X) - h(X \mid \hat X)$. Since
conditioning cannot increase differential entropy and subtracting a function
of the conditioning variable does not change it,
$$
h(X \mid \hat X) = h(X - \hat X \mid \hat X) \le h(X - \hat X).
$$
The variable $X - \hat X$ has second moment at most $D$, so its variance is
at most $D$, and among all densities of variance at most $D$ the Gaussian has
the largest differential entropy: $h(X - \hat X) \le \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e D)$.
Hence
$$
I(X;\hat X) \ge \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e\sigma^2) - \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e D)
= \tfrac12\log_2\frac{\sigma^2}{D}.
$$
The $2\pi e$ factors cancel, which is why the coordinate-dependence of
differential entropy does no harm.

**(b) Attainment.** Take $\hat X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 - D)$ and
$Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, D)$ independent, with $X = \hat X + Z$. Then $X$ is
Gaussian with variance $(\sigma^2 - D) + D = \sigma^2$, as required, and
$\mathbb{E}[(X - \hat X)^2] = \mathbb{E}[Z^2] = D$: on budget. Given
$\hat X$, the residual is exactly $Z$, so
$h(X \mid \hat X) = \tfrac12\log_2(2\pi e D)$ and both inequalities in (a)
are equalities. The construction needs $D \le \sigma^2$ for the variance
$\sigma^2 - D$ to be non-negative — which is precisely the range in which
$R(D) > 0$.

**(c) Decibels.** $D(R) = \sigma^2 4^{-R}$, so the signal-to-noise ratio is
$\sigma^2/D = 4^R$ and, in decibels,
$10\log_{10} 4^R = R \cdot 10\log_{10} 4 = 6.02\,R$. One extra bit buys
$6.02$ dB; conversely $40$ dB requires $R = 40/6.02 = 6.6$ bits per sample,
which a fixed-rate scalar quantizer would not deliver until about $7.4$ bits
because of the $0.72$-bit scalar penalty.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A measurement, on a real image. Take any grayscale photograph and quantize
its pixel values uniformly to $1, 2, 3$ and $4$ bits per pixel — that is,
replace each value by the centre of one of $2^b$ equal intervals covering
$[0, 255]$ — and measure the mean squared error at each rate. Plot rate
against $10\log_{10}(\sigma^2/D)$, where $\sigma^2$ is the variance of the
image's pixel values. Report the slope, compare it against the 6 dB per bit
prediction, state where your points sit relative to
$R(D) = \tfrac12\log_2(\sigma^2/D)$, and give one reason for the gap. State
in advance what would count as agreement.

<Solution>
**What to predict before running it.** For a uniform quantizer of step
$\Delta = 256/2^b$ whose cells are short enough that the density is roughly
flat inside them, the error is uniform on $(-\Delta/2, \Delta/2)$ and
$D \approx \Delta^2/12 = 5461 \cdot 4^{-b}$. So $D$ should fall by a factor
of four per bit, and the plotted signal-to-noise ratio should rise by
$6.02$ dB per bit.

**What counts as agreement.** A fitted slope within a few tenths of a
decibel of $6.02$ over the $2 \to 4$ bit range. The $1$-bit point will sit
well below the line and should: at two levels the flat-within-a-cell
assumption fails completely, which is the high-rate condition of the
approximation failing, not a measurement error.

**Where the points sit.** Above the curve, always. With a typical natural
image variance around $\sigma^2 \approx 3000$ (a standard deviation near
$55$), $R(D)$ would allow $D = 3000 \cdot 4^{-4} \approx 12$ at 4 bits, while
uniform quantization gives $5461 \cdot 4^{-4} \approx 21$: about $2.5$ dB, or
$0.4$ bit, of loss.

**One reason for the gap** (any of these is a complete answer). The
quantizer is scalar and fixed-rate, which costs up to $0.72$ bit by the
Panter–Dite calculation, and it spends levels uniformly over $[0,255]$ rather
than in proportion to $p^{1/3}$, wasting levels on empty parts of the
histogram. A deeper reason, worth noticing: neighbouring pixels are strongly
correlated, so the i.i.d. Gaussian $R(D)$ computed from the marginal variance
is not the right bound for an image at all — the true rate-distortion
function of the image source is far lower, which is why a real codec
transforms before it quantizes and beats this comparison in both directions.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
You must compress images so that a downstream classifier still identifies the
object in them, and the bit budget is tight enough that visible degradation
is certain. Squared error is a poor proxy for this goal: it charges equally
for a change in a texture nobody classifies on and for a change in the shape
that decides the label. Write down what you would actually want the
compressed representation $T$ to preserve, express it as a quantity involving
the label $Y$, and state what the optimisation becomes when your quantity
replaces $\mathbb{E}[d(X,\hat X)] \le D$ in the definition of $R(D)$.

<Solution>
**What to preserve.** Not the pixels but the label information: the
representation should be free to discard anything about $X$ that does not
change what $Y$ is. The natural quantity is the mutual information between
the representation and the label, $I(T;Y)$, in bits — the same functional
used throughout this course, now pointed at the target rather than at the
source.

**The objective.** Keep the rate term $I(X;T)$ as the thing to minimise, and
replace the distortion constraint by a floor on preserved label information:
$$
\min_{p(t \mid x)\,:\; I(T;Y)\, \ge\, I_0} \; I(X;T),
$$
or, in the Lagrangian form this lesson's closing section derived,
$\min\, I(X;T) - \beta\,I(T;Y)$. It is the rate-distortion problem with one
substitution: an externally supplied $d$ is gone, and its role is played by a
second mutual information.

**Two facts that follow immediately.** The data-processing inequality
(<Ref lesson="data-processing-inequality"/>) caps $I(T;Y)$ at $I(X;Y)$, so
the achievable region has a hard ceiling no encoder can cross; and the
effective distortion measure is no longer chosen but *derived* — it turns out
to be a KL divergence between conditional label distributions, so two inputs
are "close" exactly when they imply the same beliefs about $Y$.

This objective is the information bottleneck, and
<Ref lesson="information-bottleneck"/> develops it, including the derivation
of that KL distortion and what the $\beta$ sweep traces out.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

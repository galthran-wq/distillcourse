Information has a physical price: Landauer's principle sets the minimum
heat dissipated when one bit is erased at kT ln 2, and Szilard's engine
shows one bit of information is worth exactly that much extractable work.
This lesson establishes both statements and balances the books between
them. The route: the unit bridge identifying Shannon's $H$ with
thermodynamic entropy, Landauer's bound derived from second-law
bookkeeping, the Szilard engine's $kT \ln 2$ computed as an integral,
Bennett's resolution of Maxwell's demon, and the feedback second law
$W \ge \Delta F - kT(\ln 2)\, I(X;M)$, which prices measured information
in the mutual-information currency of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>.

## One entropy, two units: k ln 2 per bit

A fair coin flip carries $H = 1$ bit
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>). A microscopic particle
resting in one of two wells of a symmetric trap, each side equally likely,
is described by the same half/half distribution, but a physicist reports
its entropy as
$$
S = k \ln 2 \approx 9.57 \times 10^{-24} \text{ J/K},
$$
where $k = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K is *Boltzmann's constant* (also
written $k_{\mathrm{B}}$). Same count of possibilities, different unit.

The identification is general. Boltzmann's formula assigns a system with
$W$ equally likely microstates the entropy $S = k \ln W$; the range
theorem of <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/> assigns the uniform
distribution over $W$ outcomes the Shannon entropy $H = \log_2 W$ bits.
Dividing, $S = (k \ln 2)\, H$. For non-uniform distributions the match
persists: Gibbs' entropy $S = -k \sum_i p_i \ln p_i$ is term-for-term the
Shannon sum $H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i$, with $\ln$ in place of
$\log_2$. By the units discussion of <Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>,
that substitution is the bits-to-nats conversion:

$$
S \;=\; k \cdot H_{\text{nats}} \;=\; (k \ln 2) \cdot H_{\text{bits}}.
$$

<KeyFact>
Thermodynamic entropy is Shannon entropy measured in nats and multiplied
by Boltzmann's constant, $S = (k \ln 2) H_{\text{bits}}$: one bit
corresponds to $9.57 \times 10^{-24}$ J/K.
</KeyFact>

<Remark title="statistical mechanics as inference">
The identity of the two functionals is the starting point of
[Jaynes' 1957 paper](https://bayes.wustl.edu/etj/articles/theory.1.pdf),
already met in <Ref lesson="max-entropy-principle"/>: Boltzmann's $H$ and
Shannon's $H$ are the same expression, so equilibrium statistical
mechanics can be read as maximum-entropy inference under an energy
constraint. That lesson used the reading to derive the Boltzmann
distribution; this lesson runs the bridge in the other direction, from
bits to joules.
</Remark>

<Aside>
The conversion factor explains why the thermodynamic weight of
information went unnoticed for a century. The shuffled 52-card deck of
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/> carries $\log_2 52! \approx
225.6$ bits, which converts to $225.6 \times 9.57 \times 10^{-24} \approx
2.2 \times 10^{-21}$ J/K. The thermal entropy of the cardboard itself is
of order $10^2$ J/K: the information-bearing degrees of freedom are some
$10^{23}$ times lighter than the thermal ones. Only when experiments
reached single-molecule and single-colloid scales (below) did the
information term become measurable.
</Aside>

## Landauer's principle: erasure has a heat floor

Take a one-bit memory: a physical system with two stable states, holding
$0$ or $1$ with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ each. The *reset* operation
returns it to a fixed state, say $0$, regardless of its contents. Before
the reset the memory's entropy is $1$ bit, i.e. $k \ln 2$; after, the
state is known and the entropy is $0$. The reset removes $k \ln 2$ of
entropy from the memory, and the second law forbids destroying it: the
entropy must move into the environment, as heat.

<Theorem title="Landauer's principle">
Resetting a one-bit memory holding either state with probability
$\tfrac{1}{2}$, in contact with a heat bath at temperature $T$,
dissipates at least
$$
Q \;\ge\; kT \ln 2
$$
of heat into the bath. At $T = 300$ K,
$kT \ln 2 \approx 2.87 \times 10^{-21}$ J (about $18$ meV). More
generally, resetting a memory whose contents have distribution $p$
dissipates at least $kT \ln 2 \cdot H(p)$.

<Proof>
The memory plus the bath form a closed system, so the second law gives
$\Delta S_{\text{mem}} + \Delta S_{\text{bath}} \ge 0$. The reset takes
the memory from entropy $k \ln 2$ (one bit, by the unit bridge) to $0$:
$\Delta S_{\text{mem}} = -k \ln 2$. Hence
$\Delta S_{\text{bath}} \ge k \ln 2$. A bath at fixed temperature $T$
absorbing heat $Q$ gains entropy $\Delta S_{\text{bath}} = Q/T$
(Clausius), so $Q \ge kT \ln 2$. For contents distributed as $p$ the
memory's initial entropy is $(k \ln 2) H(p)$ and the same bookkeeping
gives $Q \ge kT \ln 2 \cdot H(p)$. When the two memory states have equal
energy, the dissipated heat is supplied as work: erasure costs at least
$kT \ln 2$ of work per uniformly random bit.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### Many-to-one is what costs: erasing, writing, copying

The charge attaches to *logical irreversibility*: reset maps both inputs
$\{0, 1\}$ to the single output $0$, so the output does not determine the
input, and the lost distinction must be exported to the bath. Operations
that lose no distinction have **no such floor**:

- **NOT** maps $0 \mapsto 1, 1 \mapsto 0$: a permutation, invertible,
  no minimum dissipation.
- **Copying onto a blank**: $(b, 0) \mapsto (b, b)$ is invertible (the
  inverse blanks the copy against the intact original), so making a copy
  of a bit into a known-blank register can in principle be free.
- **Overwriting an unknown register** is erase-then-write: the old
  contents are lost, and Landauer's price applies.

[Bennett's 1973 result](https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.176.0525) sharpens the
scope: any computation can be embedded in a logically reversible one that
keeps its intermediate results, so computation as such has no minimum
energy cost. The unavoidable charge falls only on discarding information.

### A floor, not an operating point

The bound is a lower limit on dissipation approached only by slow,
carefully designed protocols.
[Bérut et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10872) realized the
double-well memory with a colloidal particle in an optical trap and
measured the mean heat of erasure approaching $kT \ln 2$ as the protocol
slowed, confirming the principle experimentally. Production hardware sits
far above the floor: no current chip operates anywhere near
$3 \times 10^{-21}$ J per erased bit, and the bound constrains only
logically irreversible operations in the quasi-static limit.

<KeyFact>
Erasing one bit at temperature $T$ dissipates at least $kT \ln 2$ of
heat, $2.87 \times 10^{-21}$ J at $300$ K; the charge is for logical
irreversibility (many-to-one maps), not for computation itself.
</KeyFact>

## The Szilard engine: one bit buys kT ln 2 of work

Landauer's principle has an exact converse, published thirty-two years
earlier. [Szilard's 1929 engine](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01341281)
converts one bit of *acquired* information into $kT \ln 2$ of work,
using a gas of exactly one molecule.

The device: a box of volume $V$ in contact with a heat bath at
temperature $T$, containing a single molecule. A thin partition drops
into the middle, trapping the molecule in one half. A measurement records
which half — one bit, since both sides are equally likely. The partition
now becomes a piston: knowing the molecule's side, the demon lets the
molecule push the partition toward the empty half, extracting work
through a reversible isothermal expansion from $V/2$ back to $V$. Then
the piston is removed and the cycle restarts.

<Task>
Step through the four frames and tally what has changed by the end of
frame 4: the weight is up by $kT \ln 2$, the bath is down $kT \ln 2$ of

bit. Every entry but the last is paid for; the next section charges it.
</Task>

### The work integral

<Example title="work extracted per cycle">
A single molecule in volume $V'$ at temperature $T$ exerts the
time-averaged pressure of an ideal gas with $N = 1$: $p = kT / V'$. The
reversible isothermal expansion from $V/2$ to $V$ delivers

$$
W = \int_{V/2}^{V} p \, dV'
  = \int_{V/2}^{V} \frac{kT}{V'} \, dV'
  = kT \ln \frac{V}{V/2}
  = kT \ln 2 .
$$

At $T = 300$ K: $W = 2.87 \times 10^{-21}$ J. The expansion is
isothermal, so the molecule's internal energy is unchanged and the energy
delivered as work is drawn from the bath as heat: $Q_{\text{in}} = W =
kT \ln 2$. The gas ends where it started, in volume $V$ at temperature
$T$.
</Example>

The measured bit is what makes the extraction possible: without knowing
the side, the demon does not know which way to release the piston, and
releasing it blindly extracts nothing on average. One measured bit is
worth exactly $kT \ln 2$ — the engine's exchange rate equals Landauer's
price.

### The apparent violation

The cycle looks like a perpetual motion machine of the second kind. The
Kelvin-Planck form of the second law forbids any cyclic process whose
only effect is converting heat from a single bath entirely into work, and
each Szilard cycle appears to do exactly that: bath heat in, lifted
weight out, gas restored. The resolution is that the cycle shown is
**not closed** — one system in the picture did not return to its initial
state.

<KeyFact>
One measured bit converts to exactly $kT \ln 2$ of isothermal work:
Szilard's engine and Landauer's erasure price the bit identically, from
opposite directions.
</KeyFact>

## Maxwell's demon: the reset balances the books

The Szilard engine is the minimal version of a much older puzzle. In his
1871 *Theory of Heat*, Maxwell imagined a molecule-sighted being
operating a frictionless trapdoor between two gas chambers,
letting fast molecules pass one way and slow ones the other. The demon
sorts the gas into hot and cold halves without doing work, creating a
temperature difference that can drive an engine: heat flows uphill, and
the second law appears to fail. Szilard distilled the puzzle to its core:
the demon's power comes entirely from *information* about individual
molecules, one bit per decision.

[Bennett's 1982 analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02084158) closed the
loophole. To run another cycle, the demon must return its memory to the
blank state — and by Landauer's principle that reset dissipates at least
$kT \ln 2$ per stored bit, exactly the work each bit yielded. Over the
full cycle, engine plus memory, the books balance:

$$
W_{\text{total}} \;=\; \underbrace{kT \ln 2}_{\text{extracted}}
\;-\; \underbrace{kT \ln 2}_{\text{reset}} \;\le\; 0
\qquad\text{per bit: no net gain.}
$$

  caption="The demon's balance sheet, one entry per frame: extraction, the unpaid bit, the Landauer charge that closes the books."

<Task>
Step through the three frames and watch the net line: $+kT \ln 2$ while
the bit sits in memory, $0$ once the reset posts. Check the charge
against the erasure theorem: the stored bit is $0$ or $1$ with
probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ each, so blanking it dissipates at least
$kT \ln 2 \cdot H(\tfrac{1}{2}) = kT \ln 2$.
</Task>

<Remark title="who pays: measurement or erasure">
The cost's location was misassigned for fifty years. Szilard, and later
Brillouin, charged the *measurement*; Bennett showed that measurement can
be performed reversibly — recording which-side is a copy onto a blank
register, one of the free operations above — and that the unavoidable
charge is the *reset* of that register. The demon is not an open problem:
its apparent violation is an accounting error, an entropy entry
(the stored bit, worth $k \ln 2$) left off the books.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Maxwell's demon does not break the second law: it converts bits to work
at $kT \ln 2$ per bit and must pay the same rate to blank them, so the
closed cycle nets $W_{\text{total}} \ge 0$.
</KeyFact>

## The feedback second law: I(X;M) is a work voucher

The Szilard analysis generalizes from one perfect bit to any measurement
of any system, and the general bound is stated in the course's own
currency. The baseline: for a system in contact with a bath at
temperature $T$, define the *Helmholtz free energy* $F = U - TS$
(internal energy minus temperature times entropy). The second law in work
form says the average work $W$ done **on** the system during an
isothermal process obeys $W \ge \Delta F$; equivalently, the extractable
work $-W$ is at most $-\Delta F$. Measurement plus feedback relaxes this
bound.

<Theorem title="second law with feedback (Sagawa–Ueda)">
Let a measurement of the system state $X$ produce outcome $M$, and let
the subsequent protocol depend on $M$ (feedback control). Then the
average work satisfies
$$
W \;\ge\; \Delta F \;-\; kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M),
$$
with $I(X;M)$ the mutual information between system state and
measurement outcome, in bits.

<Proof>
Sketch; the full proof belongs to stochastic thermodynamics
([Sagawa & Ueda 2010](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.104.090602);
[Parrondo, Horowitz & Sagawa 2015](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys3230)
review it).

The bookkeeping that produces the $I$ term is the chain rule of
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy"/>: after the measurement,
$H(X, M) = H(X) + H(M) - I(X;M)$, so the correlated pair (system,
memory) carries $I(X;M)$ fewer bits of joint entropy than its parts
counted separately — by the unit bridge, a thermodynamic entropy deficit
of $k \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M)$. Apply the ordinary second law to the joint
supersystem, for which the feedback is just an internal dependence:
its work bound is the joint free-energy change, which sits
$kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M)$ below the sum of the parts because of the
deficit. Feedback is the mechanism that cashes the correlation: acting
on $M$ steers the system through states of low conditional entropy
$H(X \mid M)$, converting the deficit into extractable work before the
correlation decays. The $W$ in the statement is an average over
measurement outcomes; individual runs fluctuate around it.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### The bound, checked on Szilard

Over one engine cycle the gas returns to its initial state, so
$\Delta F = 0$. The measurement is perfect: $M$ determines the side $X$,
so $I(X;M) = H(X) = 1$ bit
(<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, the upper bound at determinism).
The theorem gives $W \ge -kT \ln 2$: at most $kT \ln 2$ of work out,
and the reversible expansion attains the bound with equality.

<Example title="a noisy demon">
The demon's detector misreads the side with probability
$\varepsilon = 0.1$: the memory records the true side with probability
$0.9$. System and memory are then coupled exactly like the
agreement-probability tables of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> at
$q = 0.9$:

$$
I(X;M) = 1 - H_2(0.9) \approx 1 - 0.469 = 0.531 \text{ bits},
$$

using $H_2(0.9) \approx 0.469$ from
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>. With $\Delta F = 0$, the
extractable work per cycle is at most

$$
-W \;\le\; kT \ln 2 \times 0.531
\;\approx\; 2.87 \times 10^{-21} \times 0.531
\;\approx\; 1.52 \times 10^{-21} \text{ J}
$$

at $300$ K. A noisy bit is worth its mutual information, not its raw
length: the $47\%$ of the bit lost to detector noise buys nothing.
Attaining the bound requires a protocol tuned to the noise (a full
expansion trusting a wrong reading compresses against the molecule and
loses work); the review discusses optimal feedback protocols that
approach it.
</Example>

<Aside>
The demon's side of the ledger generalizes too: Sagawa and Ueda also
showed that measurement plus memory reset together cost at least
$kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M)$ of work, so the voucher is never printed for
free — whatever the feedback gains, the measuring device pays.
Feedback engines have run in the lab:
[Toyabe et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys1821) steered a
colloidal particle up a staircase potential by feedback on its measured
position, demonstrating information-to-energy conversion at about
$28\%$ of the $kT \ln 2 \cdot I$ limit.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Measured information is a thermodynamic resource priced at $kT \ln 2$
per bit: $W \ge \Delta F - kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M)$. The mutual
information built in this course, applied to a physical measurement,
buys joules.
</KeyFact>

## The physical price of computation

The course's entropy has now met physics twice, from opposite
directions. <Ref lesson="max-entropy-principle"/> ran inference toward
physics: maximizing $H$ under an energy constraint produces the
Boltzmann distribution, so the physicist's equilibrium ensembles are
Shannon-entropy maximizers. This lesson ran physics toward information:
the second law charges $kT \ln 2$ per bit erased and credits the same
per bit measured, so Shannon's $H$ carries joules. Both directions work
because the two entropies are one functional in two units.

For computing, the consequence is a hard floor with a precise scope.
Every logically irreversible step — every erased or overwritten bit — has
a thermodynamic minimum cost of $kT \ln 2$, unavoidable by any device at
temperature $T$; reversible logic is the only exemption the physics
grants. The floor is real but distant: it has been approached only in
single-bit laboratory erasures, and no production hardware operates near
it.

<KeyFact>
Landauer's bound is a floor on irreversible computation, not the
operating point of any current chip: $kT \ln 2$ per erased bit, escapable
only by computing reversibly.
</KeyFact>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A storage device erases one gigabyte ($8 \times 10^9$ bits) of uniformly
random data at $T = 300$ K. Compute the Landauer minimum heat, and
compare it with the roughly $1$ J a real drive dissipates for the same
operation.

<Solution>
Per bit: $kT \ln 2 = 2.87 \times 10^{-21}$ J. Total:
$$
Q \ge 8 \times 10^9 \times 2.87 \times 10^{-21}
\approx 2.3 \times 10^{-11} \text{ J},
$$
about $23$ picojoules. A real drive dissipating $\sim 1$ J sits roughly
$10^{10}$ to $10^{11}$ times above the floor: Landauer's bound is a hard
limit, not a description of current hardware.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Compute the Landauer bound per bit at $T = 77$ K (liquid nitrogen) and
$T = 4$ K (liquid helium). Then show that running the erasure cold buys
nothing once the heat must be pumped into a $300$ K room: compute the
total heat delivered to the room when erasure at $T_c = 4$ K is followed
by an ideal (Carnot) refrigerator moving the dissipated heat to
$T_h = 300$ K.

<Solution>
Per bit, $Q = kT \ln 2$: at $77$ K,
$Q \ge 1.381 \times 10^{-23} \times 77 \times 0.693 \approx 7.4 \times
10^{-22}$ J; at $4$ K, $Q \ge 3.8 \times 10^{-23}$ J. The floor scales
linearly in $T$.

Pumping: to move heat $Q_c = kT_c \ln 2$ out of the cold box, an ideal
refrigerator does work $W \ge Q_c (T_h/T_c - 1)$, and the room receives
$Q_c + W \ge Q_c \, T_h / T_c = k T_h \ln 2$. The room-temperature
Landauer cost reappears exactly: cooling relocates the charge, it does
not reduce it. (Cold erasure still helps devices whose *other* losses
drop with temperature; the Landauer term itself is conserved.)
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A one-bit memory holds $0$ with probability $0.9$ and $1$ with
probability $0.1$. Compute the minimum heat dissipated by resetting it
to $0$ at $T = 300$ K, and state what the reset of an already-known bit
costs.

<Solution>
The memory's entropy is $H_2(0.9) \approx 0.469$ bits
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>). The generalized bound
gives
$$
Q \ge kT \ln 2 \cdot H_2(0.9)
\approx 2.87 \times 10^{-21} \times 0.469
\approx 1.35 \times 10^{-21} \text{ J}.
$$
A known bit has $H = 0$: resetting it can in principle dissipate nothing
(the map is one-to-one on the actually occupied state). The erasure cost
is the entropy of what is forgotten, not the number of physical bits
touched.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
An asymmetric Szilard engine inserts the partition at one third of the
box: the molecule is trapped on the left (volume $V/3$) with probability
$\tfrac{1}{3}$, on the right (volume $2V/3$) with probability
$\tfrac{2}{3}$. A perfect measurement records the side and the trapped
volume expands reversibly and isothermally back to $V$. Compute the work
in each branch, the expected work per cycle, and verify that it equals
$kT \ln 2 \cdot H_2(\tfrac{1}{3})$, the measured information priced at
$kT \ln 2$ per bit.

<Solution>
Left branch: $W_L = \int_{V/3}^{V} kT/V' \, dV' = kT \ln 3 \approx
1.099 \, kT$. Right branch: $W_R = kT \ln \tfrac{3}{2} \approx 0.405 \,
kT$.

Expected work:
$$
\bar W = \tfrac{1}{3} kT \ln 3 + \tfrac{2}{3} kT \ln \tfrac{3}{2}
= kT \left( \ln 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \ln 2 \right) \approx 0.6365 \, kT .
$$

Measured information: the measurement resolves a $(\tfrac{1}{3},
\tfrac{2}{3})$ binary outcome, $H_2(\tfrac{1}{3}) = \log_2 3 -
\tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.918$ bits. Price it:
$kT \ln 2 \times 0.918 = kT \times 0.693 \times 0.918 \approx 0.6365\,
kT = \bar W$. The match is exact:
$\ln 3 - \tfrac{2}{3}\ln 2 = \ln 2 \,(\log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3})$. The
engine converts every measured bit — here a fractional $0.918$ of one —
into work at the full $kT \ln 2$ rate, saturating the feedback second
law with $\Delta F = 0$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A Szilard engine's detector reports the wrong side with probability
$\varepsilon = 0.2$. Compute the mutual information between the
molecule's side $X$ and the record $M$, and the maximum average work
extractable per cycle at $300$ K.

<Solution>
$X$ is a fair bit and $M$ agrees with it with probability $0.8$: the
agreement table of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> with $q = 0.8$,
so
$$
I(X;M) = 1 - H_2(0.8) = 1 - 0.722 = 0.278 \text{ bits},
$$
using $H_2(0.8) = -0.8 \log_2 0.8 - 0.2 \log_2 0.2 \approx 0.722$.
With $\Delta F = 0$, the feedback second law bounds the extraction:
$$
-W \le kT \ln 2 \times 0.278 \approx 2.87 \times 10^{-21} \times 0.278
\approx 8.0 \times 10^{-22} \text{ J}.
$$
Doubling the error rate from $0.1$ to $0.2$ nearly halved the voucher
($0.531 \to 0.278$ bits): work tracks mutual information, and $H_2$ is
steep in this range.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Explain, by counting preimages, why copying a bit $b$ into a register
known to hold $0$ has no Landauer cost, while writing $b$ into a
register holding an unknown bit $a$ costs at least $kT \ln 2$ when $a$
is uniformly random. Give the map each operation implements on the pair
(source, register).

<Solution>
Copy onto blank: $(b, 0) \mapsto (b, b)$. The possible inputs are
$(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$; the outputs $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ are distinct, so
the map is one-to-one on its domain and invertible — no distinction is
lost, no entropy must be exported, no heat floor.

Overwrite: $(b, a) \mapsto (b, b)$. Inputs $(b, 0)$ and $(b, 1)$ both
map to $(b, b)$: two-to-one. The register's bit $a$, worth $H(a) = 1$
bit $= k \ln 2$ of entropy when uniform, is destroyed; by Landauer's
bookkeeping the bath must absorb $Q \ge kT \ln 2$. Overwriting is
erasure of $a$ followed by a free copy, and the erasure carries the
entire charge.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A demon is issued a tape of $N$ pre-blanked bits and runs $N$ Szilard
cycles, filling the tape and extracting $N kT \ln 2$ of work from a
single bath without ever paying an erasure cost. State why this does not
violate the second law, and account for the total entropy change of
bath plus tape.

<Solution>
The blank tape is a low-entropy resource, and the demon consumes it like
fuel. Entropy audit per cycle: the bath loses heat $kT \ln 2$, so
$\Delta S_{\text{bath}} = -k \ln 2$; the tape gains one uniformly random
bit (the molecule's side), so $\Delta S_{\text{tape}} = +k \ln 2$. Total
change: $0 \ge 0$ — the second law holds with equality for the
reversible protocol. Over $N$ cycles the demon has not created work from
nothing; it has traded $N k \ln 2$ of pre-paid entropy capacity for
$N kT \ln 2$ of work, exactly the rate Landauer's principle sets. When
the tape is full, continuing requires erasing it, which returns
$N kT \ln 2$ to the bath and closes Bennett's ledger. Blank memory is as
much a thermodynamic resource as a charged battery.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

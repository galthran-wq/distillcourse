Information has a physical price: Landauer's principle sets the minimum
heat dissipated when one bit is erased at kT ln 2, and Szilard's engine
shows one bit of information is worth exactly that much extractable work.

This is an off-path chapter, and the course's argument does not depend on
it. What the argument has established is the ninth spine step, entropy as
a choice rule: <Ref lesson="max-entropy-principle"/> showed that
maximizing $H$ under a mean-energy constraint returns the Boltzmann
distribution, so a physicist's equilibrium ensemble is an entropy
maximizer. That identification is the only thing licensing traffic in the
other direction, from Shannon's $H$, defined over any distribution
whatever, to a quantity a calorimeter can read. This lesson runs that
direction and prices it: what a bit costs to destroy, what a bit is worth
to acquire, and where the mutual information of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> turns up inside an inequality measured
in joules. Nothing later in the course requires the result.

The route: the unit bridge and the condition it rests on, Landauer's
bound derived from second-law bookkeeping, the Szilard engine's
$kT \ln 2$ computed as an integral, Bennett's resolution of Maxwell's
demon, and the feedback second law
$W \ge \Delta F - kT(\ln 2)\, I(X;M)$.

## k ln 2 per bit: the bridge from H to joules per kelvin

Pricing a bit means converting an entropy into an energy, and three
results already established carry the whole conversion.

<Task>
Closed book, before reading on. (1) State the entropy of a fair coin flip
in bits, and the entropy of a uniform distribution over $W$ outcomes.
(2) State what $I(X;M)$ equals in terms of $H(X)$ and $H(X \mid M)$, and
its value when $M$ determines $X$. (3) State the distribution that
maximizes $H$ subject to a fixed mean energy, and name the multiplier
physics attaches to the energy constraint.
</Task>

<Aside>
(1) $H = 1$ bit; $H = \log_2 W$ bits
(<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>). (2)
$I(X;M) = H(X) - H(X \mid M)$, the bits observing $M$ removes from the
uncertainty about $X$; if $M$ determines $X$ then $H(X \mid M) = 0$ and
$I(X;M) = H(X)$ (<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>). (3) The Boltzmann
distribution $p_i = e^{-E_i/kT}/Z$, whose multiplier is the inverse
temperature $\beta = 1/kT$ (<Ref lesson="max-entropy-principle"/>).
</Aside>

Start with the coin. A fair flip carries $H = 1$ bit. A microscopic
particle resting in one of two wells of a symmetric trap, each side
equally likely, is described by the same half/half distribution, and a
physicist reports its entropy as

$$
S = k \ln 2 \approx 9.57 \times 10^{-24} \text{ J/K},
$$

where $k = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K is *Boltzmann's constant* (also
written $k_{\mathrm{B}}$). Same count of possibilities, different unit,
and the unit is what makes the second number physical: entropy in
thermodynamics is defined through heat and temperature, by Clausius'
relation $\Delta S = Q/T$ for heat $Q$ absorbed reversibly at
temperature $T$.

### The formulas match, and matching formulas prove nothing

Gibbs' entropy of a distribution over microstates is
$S = -k \sum_i p_i \ln p_i$, term for term the Shannon sum
$H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i$ with $\ln$ in place of $\log_2$. By the
change-of-base rule of <Ref lesson="what-is-surprise"/>, that
substitution is exactly the bits-to-nats conversion, so

$$
S \;=\; k \cdot H_{\text{nats}} \;=\; (k \ln 2) \cdot H_{\text{bits}} .
$$

The algebra is trivial and it is not the argument. Two functionals having
the same shape does not make the physicist's $S$ and the coder's $H$ the
same quantity: $H$ is defined for any distribution over any alphabet,
while $S$ is defined for a system in a thermodynamic state, and a
one-line rewrite cannot carry a quantity from one domain into the other.

What carries it is the previous lesson's theorem. An equilibrium system
at temperature $T$ occupies microstate $i$ with probability
$p_i = e^{-E_i / kT} / Z$, and that distribution is the maximum-entropy
distribution subject to the mean energy $\langle E \rangle = U$. So the
distribution appearing in the thermodynamic $S$ and the distribution
appearing in Shannon's $H$ are not two distributions that happen to look
alike; under the max-entropy identification they are one distribution,
evaluated by one functional, reported in two units. Where the
identification fails — a system held far from equilibrium, or an
observer whose constraints are not the ones defining the ensemble — the
conversion $S = (k\ln 2)H$ has no content, because the two symbols no
longer name the same object.

<KeyFact>
Every joule priced in the rest of this lesson — the erasure floor, the
engine's yield, the feedback bound — carries that condition with it: each
is a claim about a system whose distribution its constraints determine,
never about an arbitrary distribution over an arbitrary alphabet.
</KeyFact>

Multiplying by $T$ converts an entropy into an energy: at room
temperature $T = 300$ K, one bit is worth

$$
kT \ln 2 \;=\; 9.57 \times 10^{-24} \times 300
\;\approx\; 2.87 \times 10^{-21} \text{ J} \;\approx\; 18 \text{ meV},
$$

which is $69\%$ of the thermal energy $kT \approx 26$ meV that jostles
every degree of freedom at this temperature. A bit's worth of energy is
not small compared with the noise it sits in; it is comparable to it,
which is why erasing reliably takes more than the minimum.

<Aside>
Why the thermodynamic weight of information went unnoticed for a century.
The shuffled 52-card deck of <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>
carries $\log_2 52! \approx 225.6$ bits, which converts to
$225.6 \times 9.57 \times 10^{-24} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-21}$ J/K. The
thermal entropy of the cardboard itself is of order $10^2$ J/K: the
information-bearing degrees of freedom are some $10^{23}$ times lighter
than the thermal ones they sit inside. The information term became
measurable only when experiments reached single molecules and single
colloidal particles.
</Aside>

## Landauer's principle: erasure has a heat floor

The conversion prices an entropy change, and there is one entropy change
every computer performs constantly: forgetting. Take a one-bit memory, a
physical system with two stable states, holding $0$ or $1$ with
probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$ each. The *reset* operation returns it to a
fixed state, say $0$, regardless of its contents. Before the reset the
memory's entropy is $1$ bit, that is $k \ln 2$; after, the state is known
and the entropy is $0$. The reset removes $k \ln 2$ of entropy from the
memory, and the second law forbids destroying entropy: it has to go
somewhere, and the only place available is the environment, as heat.

<Theorem title="Landauer's principle">
Resetting a one-bit memory holding either state with probability
$\tfrac{1}{2}$, in contact with a heat bath at temperature $T$,
dissipates at least
$$
Q \;\ge\; kT \ln 2
$$
of heat into the bath. At $T = 300$ K,
$kT \ln 2 \approx 2.87 \times 10^{-21}$ J. More generally, resetting a
memory whose contents have distribution $p$ dissipates at least
$kT \ln 2 \cdot H(p)$.

<Proof>
Goal: a lower bound on the heat, from entropy bookkeeping alone. Mode:
direct, in three steps: the memory's entropy change, the bath's
compensating change, and Clausius' conversion of the bath's entropy into
heat.

**The memory.** The reset takes the memory from entropy $k \ln 2$ (one
bit, converted by the bridge above) to $0$, so
$\Delta S_{\text{mem}} = -k \ln 2$.

**The bath.** Memory plus bath form a closed system, and the second law
requires $\Delta S_{\text{mem}} + \Delta S_{\text{bath}} \ge 0$. Hence
$\Delta S_{\text{bath}} \ge k \ln 2$.

**The heat.** A bath large enough to hold its temperature fixed at $T$
while absorbing heat $Q$ gains entropy $\Delta S_{\text{bath}} = Q/T$.
Substituting, $Q/T \ge k \ln 2$, so $Q \ge kT \ln 2$.

For contents distributed as $p$ the memory's initial entropy is
$(k \ln 2) H(p)$ instead of $k \ln 2$, and the same three steps give
$Q \ge kT \ln 2 \cdot H(p)$. When the two memory states have equal
energy the internal energy is unchanged, so the dissipated heat must be
supplied as work: erasure costs at least $kT \ln 2$ of work per uniformly
random bit.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The argument used nothing about the mechanism of the reset. It did not
matter whether the memory is a double-well potential, a magnetic domain
or a charged capacitor, because no step referred to the device; the bound
follows from the entropy the memory carried and from the second law.
That is why it is a floor for every implementation rather than a
statement about one.

### Many-to-one is what costs: erasing, writing, copying

The generalized bound $Q \ge kT \ln 2 \cdot H(p)$ says the charge is for
the entropy actually destroyed, not for the number of physical bits
touched, and the sharpest form of that statement is about the logic of
the operation rather than its statistics.

<Example title="four one-bit operations, priced">
Each operation acts on a register whose contents are uniform unless
stated otherwise.

**Reset**, $\{0,1\} \mapsto 0$. Two inputs, one output: the output does
not determine the input. Entropy destroyed: $1$ bit. Floor:
$kT \ln 2 \approx 2.87 \times 10^{-21}$ J at 300 K.

**NOT**, $0 \mapsto 1$ and $1 \mapsto 0$. A permutation of the state
space, so the input is recoverable from the output. Entropy destroyed:
none. Floor: zero.

**Copy onto a blank**, $(b, 0) \mapsto (b, b)$. The reachable inputs are
$(0,0)$ and $(1,0)$; the outputs $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$ are distinct, so the
map is one-to-one on its domain, invertible by blanking the copy against
the intact original. Floor: zero. This is the operation a *measurement*
performs, and its being free is what resolves Maxwell's demon below.

**Reset of a known bit.** The register holds $0$ with certainty and is
reset to $0$. Then $H(p) = 0$ and the floor is zero, even though the
operation is the same physical procedure as the first case. What is
charged is the forgetting, and there was nothing to forget.
</Example>

The pattern is that a *logically irreversible* map — one whose output
does not determine its input — destroys entropy that has to be exported,
while a logically reversible one need not. The consequence for computing
is larger than it looks: any computation can be embedded in a logically
reversible one that carries its intermediate results along instead of
discarding them, so computation as such has no minimum energy cost, and
the unavoidable charge falls only on discarding
([Bennett 1973](https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.176.0525)).

<Exercise>
Explain, by counting preimages, why copying a bit $b$ into a register
known to hold $0$ has no Landauer cost, while writing $b$ into a register
holding an unknown uniform bit $a$ costs at least $kT \ln 2$. Give the
map each operation implements on the pair (source, register), and say
which of the two the AND gate resembles.

<Solution>
**Copy onto blank:** $(b, 0) \mapsto (b, b)$. Reachable inputs $(0,0)$
and $(1,0)$ map to distinct outputs $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$. Every output has
exactly one preimage, so no distinction is lost, no entropy has to be
exported, and there is no heat floor.

**Overwrite:** $(b, a) \mapsto (b, b)$. The inputs $(b, 0)$ and $(b, 1)$
both map to $(b, b)$: two preimages per output. The register's old bit
$a$, worth $H(a) = 1$ bit $= k \ln 2$ of entropy when uniform, is
destroyed, so the bath must absorb $Q \ge kT \ln 2$. Overwriting is
erasure of $a$ followed by a free copy, and the erasure carries the
entire charge.

**AND.** The gate maps four inputs onto two outputs, and the output $0$
has three preimages, $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(1,0)$. It resembles the
overwrite: with uniform inputs the output entropy is
$H_2(\tfrac{1}{4}) = 0.811$ bits against $2$ bits in, so
$1.189$ bits are destroyed and the floor is
$1.189 \times kT\ln 2 \approx 3.4 \times 10^{-21}$ J. Two-input gates
that discard an input are the reason conventional logic is irreversible
at all.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### A floor, not an operating point

A floor is a statement about what no device can beat, and it says nothing
about what devices do. The gap is enormous, and stating it is the only
protection against the common misreading that Landauer's principle is a
live constraint on chip design.

A CMOS gate dissipates roughly $CV^2$ per switch, where $C$ is the load
capacitance it drives and $V$ the supply voltage. With
$C \approx 0.1$ fF $= 10^{-16}$ F and $V \approx 0.8$ V, that is
$10^{-16} \times 0.64 \approx 6 \times 10^{-17}$ J: about
$2 \times 10^{4}$ times the erasure floor. Moving a 32-bit word in from
DRAM costs of order a nanojoule, some $10^{10}$ times the floor per bit
carried.

  caption="Twenty-one decades of energy: the Landauer floor sits four orders of magnitude below one gate switch and ten below one DRAM access."

Both gaps are engineering facts, not fundamental ones, and neither is
shrinking quickly: switching energy is set by the voltage a transistor
needs to turn off reliably against thermal noise, and that constraint
bites at some hundreds of $kT$, not at $kT \ln 2$. The bound is a real
limit on irreversible computation and it is not the limit anyone is
currently fighting.

<KeyFact>
Landauer's bound constrains only logically irreversible operations, and
constrains them from $2 \times 10^{4}$ times below the switching energy
of a CMOS gate: reversible logic, not lower dissipation, is the only
route the physics offers around it.
</KeyFact>

## The Szilard engine: one bit buys kT ln 2 of work

Erasure destroys a bit and pays $kT \ln 2$. The converse transaction
exists, was published thirty-two years earlier, and runs at exactly the
same rate: [Szilard's 1929 engine](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01341281)
converts one bit of *acquired* information into $kT \ln 2$ of work, using
a gas of exactly one molecule.

The device: a box of volume $V$ in contact with a heat bath at
temperature $T$, containing a single molecule. A thin partition drops
into the middle, trapping the molecule in one half. A measurement records
which half, one bit, since both sides are equally likely. The partition
now becomes a piston: knowing the molecule's side, the operator lets the
molecule push the piston toward the empty half, extracting work through a
reversible isothermal expansion from $V/2$ back to $V$. The piston is
then removed and the cycle restarts.

  caption="The Szilard cycle, one step per frame: trap, measure one bit, expand. The measured bit selects which way the piston is released."
/>

<Task>
Step through the four frames and tally what has changed by the end of
frame 4: the weight is up by $kT \ln 2$, the bath is down $kT \ln 2$ of
heat, the gas is back to volume $V$, and the memory still holds one bit.
Every entry but the last is paid for.
</Task>

<Example title="work extracted per cycle">
A single molecule bouncing inside volume $V'$ at temperature $T$ exerts
the time-averaged pressure of an ideal gas with $N = 1$ particle,
$p = kT / V'$. The reversible isothermal expansion from $V/2$ to $V$
delivers

$$
W = \int_{V/2}^{V} p \, dV'
  = \int_{V/2}^{V} \frac{kT}{V'} \, dV'
  = kT \ln \frac{V}{V/2}
  = kT \ln 2 .
$$

At $T = 300$ K this is $2.87 \times 10^{-21}$ J, the same number as the
erasure floor and for the same reason: both are $k \ln 2$ of entropy
priced at temperature $T$.

The expansion is isothermal, so the molecule's mean kinetic energy is
unchanged and the energy leaving as work must enter as heat from the
bath: $Q_{\text{in}} = W = kT \ln 2$. The gas ends where it started, in
volume $V$ at temperature $T$.

The integral assumes the piston moves *quasi-statically*, slowly enough
that the molecule's pressure on it stays at the equilibrium value
$kT/V'$ throughout. A piston released suddenly is outrun by the molecule
and delivers less; $kT \ln 2$ is the supremum over protocols, not the
yield of an arbitrary one.
</Example>

### Where the work comes from: the free energy of a known side

The measurement is not decoration on the cycle. Without it, the same
apparatus extracts nothing on average, and the reason is worth computing,
because it introduces the quantity the general bound is written in.

For a system in contact with a bath at temperature $T$, the *Helmholtz
free energy* is $F = U - TS$ (internal energy minus temperature times
entropy), and the largest work an isothermal process can deliver is the
drop in $F$. Compare three states of the box:

- **No partition.** The molecule roams volume $V$.
- **Partition in, side unknown.** The molecule is confined to a half, so
  its positional entropy falls by $k \ln 2$. But which half is now a
  random variable carrying $1$ bit, worth $+k \ln 2$. The total entropy
  is unchanged, so $\Delta F = 0$ and no work is available.
- **Partition in, side known.** The measurement outcome removes the
  $k \ln 2$ of the side variable while the confinement stays. Total
  entropy is down $k \ln 2$, so $F$ is up by $kT \ln 2$.

The expansion cashes exactly that free-energy rise. Inserting the

confined-but-unknown molecule into a confined-and-known one, and the
$kT \ln 2$ was created by the bookkeeping of the third line.

<KeyFact>
Szilard's engine and Landauer's erasure are the same exchange rate run in
opposite directions: $k \ln 2$ of entropy, priced at $kT \ln 2$, bought
by a measurement and sold back by a reset.
</KeyFact>

### The apparent violation

Read as a heat engine, the cycle is a perpetual motion machine of the
second kind. The Kelvin-Planck form of the second law forbids any cyclic
process whose only effect is the complete conversion of heat from a
single bath into work, and each Szilard cycle appears to do exactly that:
heat in from one bath at $T$, a lifted weight out, gas restored to its
initial state. Nothing in the work integral is wrong and the numbers
check; the defect is that the cycle shown is not closed, because one
system in it has not returned to its initial state.

## Maxwell's demon: the reset pays, not the measurement

The unpaid entry is the memory, and the charge on it is Landauer's.

That resolution is the endpoint of a long argument about a larger
version of the same device. In his 1871 *Theory of Heat*, Maxwell stated
the second law with a qualification attached: it is "undoubtedly true as
long as we can deal with bodies only in mass, and have no power of
perceiving or handling the separate molecules of which they are made up".
He then removed the qualification, conceiving "a being whose faculties
are so sharpened that he can follow every molecule in its course".
Stationed at a frictionless trapdoor between two gas chambers, the being
opens it for fast molecules going one way and slow molecules the other,
sorting a gas into a hot half and a cold half without doing work; the
resulting temperature difference drives an engine, and heat has flowed
uphill. Maxwell's own framing already put the issue where this lesson
puts it, in what the operator can perceive. Szilard's box is the same
puzzle stripped to one molecule and one bit, which is what makes it
computable.

### The wrong resolution, and why it fails

The standard answer for half a century was that the demon fails because
*observing* a molecule costs energy: the demon must illuminate the
molecule to see it, and the photon it spends is what pays the second
law's bill. Brillouin turned this into a quantitative account in 1951,
charging every observation at least $k \ln 2$ of entropy per bit
obtained, which is precisely enough to cancel the engine's yield.

The account is wrong. It fails at the point where a claim about one
implementation is promoted into a bound: shining light on the molecule is
one way to find out where it is, and its cost is a property of that
apparatus, not of measurement. Logically, a measurement is the
copy-onto-blank of the operations table above, $(b, 0) \mapsto (b, b)$ —
a one-to-one map, which the entropy bookkeeping charges nothing for. No
step of Landauer's proof applies to it, because no entropy is destroyed.

The correct model replaces the measurement with the memory. The demon
records one bit per cycle, and a demon that records is a demon whose
memory fills. To be a *cycle* the protocol must return every part of the
apparatus to its initial state, the memory included, and blanking the
memory is many-to-one. By Landauer's principle that reset dissipates at
least $kT \ln 2$ per stored bit, exactly the work each bit yielded
([Bennett 1982](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02084158)). Over the full
cycle, engine plus memory, the books balance:

$$
W_{\text{total}} \;=\; \underbrace{kT \ln 2}_{\text{extracted}}
\;-\; \underbrace{kT \ln 2}_{\text{reset}} \;\le\; 0
\qquad\text{per bit: no net gain.}
$$

### The experiment that separates the two accounts

The two accounts differ in a checkable way, which is what makes this a
resolution rather than a preference. Issue the demon a tape of $N$
pre-blanked bits. On Brillouin's account the demon cannot run at all, or
runs at a loss from the first cycle, because every observation is charged.
On Bennett's account the demon runs at full yield for exactly $N$ cycles,
extracting $N kT \ln 2$ of work from a single bath, and then stops:
continuing requires blanking the tape, which returns the whole
$N kT \ln 2$ to the bath.

No second law is broken in the $N$ profitable cycles. The audit per
cycle: the bath loses heat $kT \ln 2$, so
$\Delta S_{\text{bath}} = -k \ln 2$; the tape gains one uniformly random
bit, so $\Delta S_{\text{tape}} = +k \ln 2$; the total is zero, which is
what the second law permits for a reversible protocol. The demon has not
created work, it has spent a resource. Blank memory is a thermodynamic
fuel with a fixed exchange rate, in the same sense as a charged battery.

<KeyFact>
Nothing in the ledger charges for **holding** a bit, only for forgetting
one, so the demon's profit can stand for as long as the tape is left
full: the second law is satisfied at the reset, not at any moment before
it.
</KeyFact>

## The feedback second law: I(X;M) is a work voucher

Every measurement so far has been perfect, which is why every bit has
been worth a full $kT \ln 2$. Real detectors misread, and the general
statement of the bound has to say what a partly reliable record is worth.
It turns out to be worth its mutual information, and the bound is stated
in exactly the currency of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>.

The baseline is the free-energy inequality already used: for an
isothermal process on a system in contact with a bath at $T$, the average
work $W$ done **on** the system obeys $W \ge \Delta F$, so the
extractable work $-W$ is at most $-\Delta F$. Measurement plus feedback
relaxes that bound, and by a computable amount.

<Theorem title="second law with feedback (Sagawa–Ueda 2010)">
Let a measurement of the system state $X$ produce outcome $M$, and let
the subsequent protocol depend on $M$ (feedback control). Then the
average work satisfies
$$
W \;\ge\; \Delta F \;-\; kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M),
$$
with $I(X;M)$ the mutual information between system state and
measurement outcome, in bits.

<Proof>
A sketch of where the $I$ term comes from; the full argument runs through
the fluctuation theorems of stochastic thermodynamics
([Sagawa & Ueda 2010](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.104.090602)).

Apply the ordinary second law not to the system but to the joint
supersystem of system plus memory, for which the feedback is merely an
internal dependence and no external agent is needed. The
inclusion-exclusion identity of <Ref lesson="mutual-information"/> writes
the overlap the two marginals count twice as
$I(X;M) = H(X) + H(M) - H(X,M)$, which rearranges to
$H(X, M) = H(X) + H(M) - I(X;M)$: a correlated pair carries $I(X;M)$
fewer bits of joint entropy than its parts counted separately, which by
the unit bridge is a thermodynamic entropy deficit of
$k \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M)$. The supersystem's work bound is its joint
free-energy change, and the deficit places that $kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M)$
below the sum of the parts' bounds. Feedback is the mechanism that cashes
the correlation: conditioning the protocol on $M$ steers the system
through states of low conditional entropy $H(X \mid M)$ before the
correlation decays. The $W$ in the statement is an average over
measurement outcomes; individual runs fluctuate around it.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### The bound, checked on Szilard

Over one engine cycle the gas returns to its initial state, so
$\Delta F = 0$. The measurement is perfect, so $M$ determines the side
$X$ and $I(X;M) = H(X) = 1$ bit. The theorem gives $W \ge -kT \ln 2$:
at most $kT \ln 2$ of work out per cycle, and the quasi-static expansion
computed above attains it with equality. Setting $I = 0$ recovers the
ordinary statement $W \ge \Delta F$, which for the Szilard cycle forbids
any work at all: the unknown-side free-energy calculation of the previous
section, this time read off the general bound.

### A noisy demon is worth its mutual information

<Example title="a detector that misreads one side in ten">
The demon's detector reports the wrong side with probability
$\varepsilon = 0.1$, so the memory records the true side with probability
$0.9$. The molecule's side $X$ is a fair bit and the record $M$ agrees
with it with probability $q = 0.9$: the agreement table of
<Ref lesson="mutual-information"/>, for which

$$
I(X;M) = 1 - H_2(0.9) \approx 1 - 0.469 = 0.531 \text{ bits},
$$

with $H_2$ the binary entropy function of
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise"/>. With $\Delta F = 0$ the
extractable work per cycle is at most

$$
-W \;\le\; kT \ln 2 \times 0.531
\;\approx\; 2.87 \times 10^{-21} \times 0.531
\;\approx\; 1.52 \times 10^{-21} \text{ J}
$$

at $300$ K. A $10\%$ error rate costs $47\%$ of the work, because $H_2$
is steep near its endpoints: the bit is still recorded in full, and
nearly half of it buys nothing.

The memory, meanwhile, still holds one full bit. Blanking it costs
$kT \ln 2 \cdot H(M) = kT \ln 2$, against $kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M)$
extracted, so the noisy demon runs at a net loss of
$kT \ln 2 \cdot H(M \mid X) \approx 0.469\, kT \ln 2$ per cycle. The
same $H(M \mid X)$ that <Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/> subtracts from
$H(M)$ to get what a noisy channel actually carries is here subtracted
from a work output, in joules.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/work-vs-detector-error.svg"
  alt="Extractable work per cycle in units of kT ln 2 plotted against detector error rate, a symmetric curve equal to one minus the binary entropy, marked at 0.531 bits for error 0.1, zero at one half, and a full bit at one"
  caption="What a noisy record is worth: 0.531 bits at a 10% error rate, nothing at ε = 1/2, and a full bit again at ε = 1, where the detector is right after relabelling."
/>

Attaining the bound requires a protocol tuned to the noise. A demon that
trusts a wrong reading and releases the piston the wrong way compresses
the gas rather than expanding it and loses work, so the optimal protocol
moves the piston only part of the way, by an amount set by the posterior
probability that the reading is right.

<KeyFact>
The voucher is never printed free: measurement and reset together cost at
least $kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M)$ of work, so whatever feedback gains, the
measuring device pays, and the closed-cycle balance is the ordinary
second law with no information term in it.
</KeyFact>

## ★ The bound in the laboratory

Both halves of the account have been measured, in systems small enough
that $kT \ln 2$ is not lost in the noise.

### Erasure: the excess heat falls as $1/\tau$

For erasure, a colloidal particle a micrometre across is held in a
double-well optical trap, the two wells standing for $0$ and $1$; a
protocol tilts and lowers the barrier over a duration $\tau$ so the
particle ends in one well whatever its start, and the heat is read off
from the measured trajectory and the force applied
([Bérut et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10872)).

Across the protocols that erase reliably, the measured mean dissipated
heat exceeds $kT \ln 2$ at every duration tested and falls toward it as
the protocol slows, with the excess decaying like $1/\tau$: a
least-squares fit over those optimised protocols gives
$\langle Q \rangle = kT(\ln 2 + B/\tau)$ with $B \approx 8$ seconds, so a
$40$-second erasure still dissipates about $0.2\,kT$ above the floor, and
a fit with the intercept left free returns $0.72\,kT$ against the
predicted $0.693\,kT$
([Bérut, Petrosyan & Ciliberto 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.06537),
figure 8). That is the shape quasi-staticity predicts: the bound is a
limit approached as $\tau \to \infty$, and every finite-speed protocol
pays the $1/\tau$ surcharge.

<Figure
  src="./figures/erasure-heat-vs-duration.svg"
  alt="Mean dissipated heat per erasure in units of kT plotted against protocol duration, with six measured points carrying error bars, a fitted ln 2 + B/τ curve, and a dashed horizontal line at the Landauer bound of 0.693"
  caption="Every optimised erasure dissipates more than ln 2, and the excess decays as 1/τ: at 40 seconds the fit is still 0.20 kT above the floor. Points and fit read from figure 8 of Bérut, Petrosyan & Ciliberto (2015); error bars ±0.15 kT."
/>

### Extraction: information into free energy at 28% of the ceiling

For extraction, a feedback experiment does the Szilard trick with a
colloidal bead on a staircase potential: the bead's position is measured,
and when thermal noise happens to carry it upward the potential is
switched to block its return. No work is done on the bead by the
switching, and the bead climbs, converting measured information into
free energy at about $28\%$ of the $kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M)$ ceiling
([Toyabe et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys1821)).

Neither result could have come out otherwise without overturning the
second law, so their value is not confirmation of the inequality but
calibration of how close a real protocol gets: a factor of about three
short of the bound for feedback, and within a few tenths of $kT$ of it at
the slowest erasure protocols tested.

## The physical price of computation

The course's entropy has now met physics twice, from opposite
directions. <Ref lesson="max-entropy-principle"/> ran inference toward
physics: maximizing $H$ under an energy constraint produces the Boltzmann
distribution, so the physicist's equilibrium ensembles are Shannon-entropy
maximizers. This lesson ran physics toward information: because that
ensemble is the entropy maximizer, the $S$ in the second law is the $H$
of this course times $k \ln 2$, and the second law then charges
$kT \ln 2$ per bit erased and credits the same per bit measured.

What is established: the conversion $S = (k \ln 2)H$ and the condition it
needs; a floor of $kT \ln 2$ on the heat from erasing one uniformly
random bit, attaching to logical irreversibility and not to computation;
a ceiling of $kT \ln 2$ on the work one measured bit can buy; the
cancellation of the two that closes Maxwell's demon; and the feedback
bound $W \ge \Delta F - kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M)$, in which a
mutual information appears with joules on both sides. What is not
established is any constraint on present hardware: at $2 \times 10^4$
gate switches' worth of margin, the floor is a limit on what is possible
and not on what is built.

This chapter sits off the course's main line and nothing later depends on
it. The argument resumes with <Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/>, which
drops the requirement of exact reconstruction and turns the coding limit
into a curve trading bits against fidelity.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Compute the work a Szilard engine extracts per cycle at $T = 300$ K and
at $T = 4$ K (liquid helium), and the minimum heat dissipated in erasing
one bit at each temperature. State the ratio between the two
temperatures' figures without recomputing it.

<Solution>
Per cycle and per bit both equal $kT \ln 2$, with
$k \ln 2 = 9.57 \times 10^{-24}$ J/K.

At $300$ K: $9.57 \times 10^{-24} \times 300 = 2.87 \times 10^{-21}$ J.

At $4$ K: $9.57 \times 10^{-24} \times 4 = 3.83 \times 10^{-23}$ J.

Extraction and erasure give the same number at each temperature, which is
the exchange rate of the lesson. The ratio is $300/4 = 75$ in both cases:
the bound is linear in $T$, with no other temperature dependence, so
every quantity in this lesson scales by the same factor.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Push the temperature to the edge. State what $kT \ln 2$ becomes as
$T \to 0$, and then show that erasing cold does not deliver free
computation: compute the total heat delivered to a $T_h = 300$ K room
when a bit is erased at $T_c = 4$ K and an ideal (Carnot) refrigerator
moves the dissipated heat from the cold stage to the room. Name the
quantity that replaces $kT \ln 2$ as the operative cost.

<Solution>
**The limit.** $kT \ln 2 \to 0$ as $T \to 0$: the entropy $k \ln 2$ still
has to leave the memory, but at low temperature carrying entropy costs
little energy, since $Q = T \Delta S$.

**The pumping cost.** An ideal refrigerator moving heat $Q_c$ from $T_c$
to $T_h$ requires work $W \ge Q_c (T_h/T_c - 1)$, and the room receives
$Q_c + W$. With $Q_c = k T_c \ln 2$,

$$
Q_c + W \;\ge\; Q_c \frac{T_h}{T_c} \;=\; k T_h \ln 2 ,
$$

which is $2.87 \times 10^{-21}$ J: the room-temperature figure, exactly.
Numerically, erasing at $4$ K dissipates $3.83 \times 10^{-23}$ J into
the cold stage and takes at least $3.83\times10^{-23} \times 74 = 2.83
\times 10^{-21}$ J of pumping work, and the two sum to the $300$ K value.

**What replaces the bound.** The operative cost is the erasure charge
referred to the temperature of the ultimate heat sink, the room. Cooling
relocates the charge, it does not reduce it, and any real cryostat
delivers a fraction of Carnot efficiency, so cold erasure is strictly
worse. Cooling still helps devices whose *other* dissipation falls with
temperature; the Landauer term is the one part of the budget it cannot
touch.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim. "A demon with enough memory beats the second law. Give
it a tape of $10^{12}$ blank bits and it never needs to erase anything,
so it extracts work from a single bath at no cost, indefinitely."
Identify the false step, and give the entropy audit for one cycle and for
the whole run.

<Solution>
The false step is "at no cost, indefinitely": the two clauses are
separately wrong in a way that hides the resource being spent.

**One cycle.** The bath supplies $kT \ln 2$ of heat, so
$\Delta S_{\text{bath}} = -k \ln 2$. One blank tape cell is overwritten
with the molecule's side, a uniformly random bit, so
$\Delta S_{\text{tape}} = +k \ln 2$. Total: $\Delta S_{\text{total}} = 0$,
which is what the second law allows for a reversible protocol. Nothing is
violated, and nothing is free either: the cycle traded one unit of the
tape's entropy capacity for the work.

**The whole run.** After $10^{12}$ cycles the demon has extracted
$10^{12} \times 2.87 \times 10^{-21} \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-9}$ J,
about three nanojoules, and the tape is full of random bits. To continue
it must blank the tape, which by Landauer's principle returns at least
the same $2.9 \times 10^{-9}$ J to the bath. "Indefinitely" is where the
claim fails: the engine is not cyclic, because the tape does not return
to its initial state, and the second law's prohibition applies only to
cyclic processes.

**The correct statement.** A blank tape is a low-entropy resource with
exactly $N k \ln 2$ of entropy capacity, worth $N kT \ln 2$ of work and
no more. The claim describes discharging a battery and calling it
perpetual motion.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
An asymmetric Szilard engine inserts the partition at one third of the
box: the molecule is trapped on the left (volume $V/3$) with probability
$\tfrac{1}{3}$ and on the right (volume $2V/3$) with probability
$\tfrac{2}{3}$. A perfect measurement records the side, and the trapped
volume expands reversibly and isothermally back to $V$. Compute the work
in each branch and the expected work per cycle, verify that it equals
$kT \ln 2 \cdot H_2(\tfrac{1}{3})$, and name the step of your derivation
that assumes quasi-static operation.

<Solution>
**Branch work.** The integral of the worked example with the lower limit
changed:
$$
W_L = \int_{V/3}^{V} \frac{kT}{V'}\,dV' = kT \ln 3 \approx 1.099\, kT,
\qquad
W_R = \int_{2V/3}^{V} \frac{kT}{V'}\,dV' = kT \ln \tfrac{3}{2}
\approx 0.405\, kT .
$$

**Expected work.**
$$
\bar W = \tfrac{1}{3} kT \ln 3 + \tfrac{2}{3} kT \ln \tfrac{3}{2}
= kT \left( \ln 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \ln 2 \right) \approx 0.6365\, kT .
$$

**The information.** The measurement resolves a
$(\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3})$ binary outcome, so
$H_2(\tfrac{1}{3}) = \log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.918$ bits.
Pricing it: $kT \ln 2 \times 0.918 = kT \times 0.693 \times 0.918
\approx 0.6365\, kT = \bar W$. The identity behind the match is
$\ln 3 - \tfrac{2}{3}\ln 2 = \ln 2\,(\log_2 3 - \tfrac{2}{3})$, so the
agreement is exact, not numerical luck. A fractional bit converts at the
same full $kT \ln 2$ per bit.

**The quasi-static step.** Writing the pressure as $p = kT/V'$ *during*
the expansion assumes the molecule is in equilibrium with the piston at
every intermediate volume. Release the piston faster than the molecule
can re-equilibrate and the pressure on it drops below $kT/V'$, so the
integral overstates the yield; the computed $\bar W$ is the supremum over
protocols.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A Szilard engine's detector reports the wrong side with probability
$\varepsilon$, independently on each cycle. Before computing anything,
say which object from an earlier lesson the pair (side, record) is, and
which formula that identification hands you. Then evaluate the
extractable work at $300$ K for $\varepsilon = 0.2$, and give the
$\varepsilon$ at which the engine's yield falls to zero.

<Solution>
**Which object.** The side $X$ is a uniform binary input, the record $M$
is its output through a channel that flips the symbol with probability
$\varepsilon$ and leaves it alone otherwise: a binary symmetric channel
(<Ref lesson="channel-capacity"/>). The cue is that both error directions
have the same probability and the input is uniform. The identification
hands over $I(X;M) = 1 - H_2(\varepsilon)$, which for the uniform input
is also the channel's capacity.

**At $\varepsilon = 0.2$.**
$H_2(0.2) = -0.2 \log_2 0.2 - 0.8 \log_2 0.8 \approx 0.722$, so
$I(X;M) \approx 0.278$ bits and
$$
-W \le kT \ln 2 \times 0.278 \approx 2.87 \times 10^{-21} \times 0.278
\approx 8.0 \times 10^{-22} \text{ J}.
$$
Doubling the error rate from $0.1$ to $0.2$ cut the voucher from $0.531$
to $0.278$ bits, nearly in half, because $H_2$ is steep away from its
maximum.

**Zero yield.** $I(X;M) = 0$ exactly when $H_2(\varepsilon) = 1$, that
is $\varepsilon = \tfrac{1}{2}$: a detector that is right half the time
records nothing about the molecule and buys no work. At
$\varepsilon = 1$ the yield returns to its full $kT \ln 2$, since
$H_2(1) = 0$ and $I = 1$ bit: a detector that is always wrong is a
correct detector with the labels swapped. The engine cares about the
mutual information, not about the agreement.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Estimate the Landauer floor for a data centre and compare it with the
real draw. Take a facility of $10^4$ servers drawing $30$ MW in total;
assume each server's processors contain about $10^9$ switching nodes
clocked at $3$ GHz, of which about $1\%$ switch in any given cycle, and
charge one bit of erasure per switch. Report the ratio to one order of
magnitude and name the assumption that most affects it.

<Solution>
**Irreversible operations per second.** Per chip:
$3 \times 10^9 \text{ s}^{-1} \times 10^9 \times 0.01 = 3 \times 10^{16}$
switches per second. Across $10^4$ servers:
$3 \times 10^{20}$ per second.

**The floor.** At one bit per switch and $2.87 \times 10^{-21}$ J per
bit,
$$
P_{\min} = 3 \times 10^{20} \times 2.87 \times 10^{-21}
\approx 0.86 \text{ W} .
$$
Under one watt, against $3 \times 10^7$ W drawn: the facility runs about
$3 \times 10^{7}$ times, call it $10^{7.5}$, above its Landauer floor.

**The dominant assumption.** The activity factor multiplied by the bits
erased per switch. The $1\%$ figure is uncertain by an order of magnitude
across workloads, and "one bit per switch" is itself a modelling choice
that could be argued up or down by a factor of a few. Everything else in
the estimate is known to well within a factor of two, so the answer is
firm at $10^{7 \pm 1}$ and no firmer. That is enough for the only
conclusion it supports: the Landauer term is invisible in a data centre's
energy budget, and the whole of that budget is the gap between real
devices and the floor.

**Cross-check.** The per-switch figures agree: $30$ MW spread over
$3\times 10^{20}$ switches is $10^{-13}$ J per switch, three orders above
the $6 \times 10^{-17}$ J of a bare gate, which is the overhead of
clocking, interconnect, memory and cooling around each logical operation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Generalize the engine to $n$ cells. Insert $n-1$ partitions dividing the
box into $n$ equal cells, so the molecule sits in a uniformly random cell
$X$.
(a) With a perfect record of $X$, compute the extractable work per cycle.
(b) The demon may now store only one bit per cycle. Design the best
one-bit record for $n = 4$, compute its extractable work, and prove no
one-bit record does better.
(c) For an arbitrary record $M$, express the extractable work in terms of
$I(X;M)$, and state the design problem "fewest recorded bits for a
required work yield" as a minimization.

<Solution>
**(a)** Knowing the cell, the molecule expands from $V/n$ to $V$:
$$
W = \int_{V/n}^{V} \frac{kT}{V'}\,dV' = kT \ln n = kT \ln 2 \cdot \log_2 n ,
$$
and $\log_2 n$ is exactly the information a perfect record carries. The
exchange rate is unchanged.

**(b)** Record which half the molecule is in: $M = 0$ for cells
$\{1,2\}$, $M = 1$ for $\{3,4\}$. Given $M$ the molecule is confined to
volume $V/2$, so the expansion yields $kT \ln 2$, and $I(X;M) = 1$ bit.
No one-bit record beats it: for a record $M$ taking two values,
$I(X;M) \le H(M) \le 1$ bit, and by part (c) the work is
$kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M) \le kT \ln 2$. An unbalanced record such as
"cell 1 or not" does worse: it yields
$\tfrac{1}{4}kT\ln 4 + \tfrac{3}{4} kT \ln \tfrac{4}{3} \approx 0.562\,kT$
against $0.693\,kT$, because $H(M) = H_2(\tfrac14) = 0.811$ bits.

**(c)** For a record $M = f(X)$, knowing $M = m$ confines the molecule to
total volume $V \cdot P(M = m)$, so the expansion to $V$ yields
$kT \ln \tfrac{1}{P(M=m)}$, and averaging gives
$$
\bar W = kT \ln 2 \cdot H(M) = kT \ln 2 \cdot I(X;M),
$$
the last equality because $M$ is a function of $X$. Fewest bits for a
required yield is therefore
$$
\min_{p(m \mid x)} I(X;M) \quad \text{subject to} \quad
\mathbb{E}[\text{forgone work}] \le D,
$$
a mutual information minimized over channels from $X$ to $M$, subject to
an expected-distortion constraint. That is the rate-distortion problem,
$R(D) = \min I(X;\hat X)$ subject to $\mathbb{E}[d] \le D$, with forgone
work as the distortion measure: <Ref lesson="rate-distortion"/> derives
the function and computes it for the binary and Gaussian cases. The
transferable move is that a memory budget and a rate are the same
constraint.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

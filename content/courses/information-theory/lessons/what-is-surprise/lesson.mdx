An event's information content is determined by its probability alone: the
less probable the event, the more information its occurrence carries. This
lesson derives the measure of that content, surprisal, from two requirements
plus an additivity axiom, fixes its unit, and computes it for concrete
events.

This is the first move of the course's argument, which claims that the number
of bits a message needs is fixed by a probability distribution and by nothing
else. Before that claim can be made about a source it has to be made about a
single event, and the per-event quantity has to be pinned down hard enough
that no other function of probability would serve. This lesson establishes
that quantity and the reason it is a logarithm rather than any other
decreasing function. What stands open at the end is the step from one event to
a whole source, which <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise">the next
lesson</Ref> takes by averaging.

The route: two constraints on a candidate measure, the additivity axiom and
the theorem it forces, the definition $I(x) = -\log_2 p(x)$ with its units,
and the behaviour of surprisal on composite events.

## Information is a property of the event, not of the report

The quantity to be measured is how much a report tells its recipient. Compare
two reports of equal length: "the sun rose this morning" and "it snowed in
Cairo yesterday", Cairo being a city on the Nile in northern Egypt where snow
is a once-in-decades event. The first event was practically certain, so the
report eliminates no uncertainty. The second rules out the overwhelmingly more
likely alternative and eliminates a great deal. Both sentences are six words
long, both arrive over the same channel, and both are in English.

So the content is not carried by the message. It is carried by the probability
of the event the message reports. Write $f(p)$ for the information delivered
by a report of an event of probability $p$; the comparison already fixes two
properties of $f$:

- $f(1) = 0$: an event that was certain to happen carries nothing when it
  happens;
- $f$ is strictly decreasing: lower probability means more information.

<Figure
  src="./figures/rarity.svg"
  alt="Three events (certain sunrise, coin flip, lottery win) ordered by probability, with information carried growing as probability shrinks"
  caption="Three events ordered by probability: as probability shrinks, information grows."
/>

Two constraints on a single unknown function look restrictive. They are not.
The candidate $f(p) = 1 - p$ satisfies both. So does $f(p) = \tfrac{1}{p} - 1$,
and so does $f(p) = -\log_2 p$. These
candidates disagree without bound: at $p = 10^{-6}$ the first returns
$0.999999$, the second returns $999999$, and the third returns about $20$. The
requirements written so far do not distinguish a bounded measure from an
unbounded one, and no further reflection on how surprising an event *feels*
will decide between them.

A third requirement has to come from somewhere else: from what the number is
going to be used for.

## The third requirement: independent surprises add

The number is going to count something that gets stored and transmitted, and
storage and channels compose by addition. Two independent fair coin flips are
reported. The pair should be worth exactly twice one flip, because the two
reports could have arrived over two separate links, each doing the same job as
the other. Probabilities of independent events multiply, so the demand is

$$
f(pq) = f(p) + f(q) \qquad \text{for independent events.}
$$

Two independent flips have joint probability
$\tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{4}$, and the demand says
$f(\tfrac{1}{4}) = 2f(\tfrac{1}{2})$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/additivity.svg"
  alt="Two coin flips: probabilities multiply along the tree while information adds"
  caption="Independent events: probabilities multiply along the tree, surprisals add along the path."
/>

This is a choice, and recording it as one matters. Nothing in probability
theory forces a measure of information to be additive over independent events;
additivity is imposed because the quantity is meant to be counted out in
hardware.
[Shannon](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~ctm/home/text/others/shannon/entropy/entropy.pdf)
defends the logarithmic measure on exactly that ground: "one feels, for
example, that two punched cards should have twice the capacity of one for
information storage, and two identical channels twice the capacity of one for
transmitting information." Every bit count later in this course, from codeword
lengths to channel capacities, inherits the decision made in this paragraph.

### Both simple candidates break on the coin pair

<Example title="testing 1 − p and 1/p − 1">
Additivity at the coin pair requires
$f(\tfrac{1}{4}) = f(\tfrac{1}{2}) + f(\tfrac{1}{2})$.

$f(p) = 1 - p$ gives $f(\tfrac{1}{2}) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so the requirement is
$f(\tfrac{1}{4}) = 1$. The candidate returns $1 - \tfrac{1}{4} = 0.75$. It
falls short, and it must: this $f$ never exceeds $1$, while $n$ independent
reports have to be worth $n$ times one report for every $n$.

$f(p) = \tfrac{1}{p} - 1$ gives $f(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 1$, so the requirement is
$f(\tfrac{1}{4}) = 2$. The candidate returns $4 - 1 = 3$. It overshoots: it
grows like $1/p$ where the requirement asks for growth like $\log (1/p)$.

$f(p) = -\log_2 p$ gives $f(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 1$ and $f(\tfrac{1}{4}) = 2$,
which is the requirement exactly.
</Example>

## Additivity forces the logarithm

The requirement is multiplicative on the left and additive on the right, which
is why it is awkward to attack directly. One substitution makes both sides
additive: replace the probability $p$ by $t = -\ln p$, which runs from $0$
when $p = 1$ up to $+\infty$ as $p$ falls to zero. Under that change of
variable, multiplying probabilities becomes adding $t$-values.

<FigureSequence
  name="cauchy"
  alt="Three frames: the map t = −ln p from the probability axis to the t axis, then one halving of p shown as a fixed step of 0.69 in t, then the solved values g = ct at each tick"
  caption="The substitution t = −ln p: halving p is a fixed step in t, so the requirement becomes additive on both sides and only a straight line survives."
/>

<Theorem title="additivity forces the logarithm">
Let $f : (0, 1] \to [0, \infty)$ be continuous and strictly decreasing with
$f(1) = 0$ and $f(pq) = f(p) + f(q)$ for all $p, q \in (0,1]$. Then
$f(p) = -c \ln p$ for some constant $c > 0$.

<Proof>
The plan has four steps: substitute to make both sides additive; solve the
additive equation on the positive integers; extend to the rationals by
dividing; extend to the reals by continuity.

**Step 1, substitute.** For $t \ge 0$ set $g(t) = f(e^{-t})$. The map
$t \mapsto e^{-t}$ sends $[0, \infty)$ onto $(0,1]$, so $g$ determines $f$ and
$f$ determines $g$. For $s, t \ge 0$,
$$
g(s + t) = f\bigl(e^{-(s+t)}\bigr) = f\bigl(e^{-s} e^{-t}\bigr)
         = f(e^{-s}) + f(e^{-t}) = g(s) + g(t),
$$
using the hypothesis in the third equality. Also $g(0) = f(1) = 0$. The
equation $g(s+t) = g(s) + g(t)$ is
[Cauchy's functional equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy%27s_functional_equation).

**Step 2, integers.** Induction on $n$: $g(1 \cdot t) = g(t)$, and if
$g(nt) = n\,g(t)$ then $g((n+1)t) = g(nt + t) = g(nt) + g(t) = (n+1)g(t)$. So
$g(nt) = n\,g(t)$ for every positive integer $n$ and every $t \ge 0$.

**Step 3, rationals.** Write $c = g(1)$. For positive integers $m, n$, Step 2
applied with $t = m/n$ gives $n\,g(m/n) = g(m) = m\,g(1) = mc$, so
$g(m/n) = c\,(m/n)$. Together with $g(0) = 0$ this is $g(t) = ct$ at every
non-negative rational $t$.

**Step 4, reals.** Every real $t \ge 0$ is the limit of a sequence of
non-negative rationals $t_k$. Since $f$ is continuous and $t \mapsto e^{-t}$ is
continuous, $g$ is continuous, so
$g(t) = \lim_k g(t_k) = \lim_k c\,t_k = ct$. This is the only step that uses
continuity.

**Back-substitution.** Putting $t = -\ln p$ gives $f(p) = g(-\ln p) = -c \ln p$
for all $p \in (0,1]$. Finally $f(\tfrac{1}{2}) = c \ln 2$ must be positive
because $f$ is strictly decreasing with $f(1) = 0$, so $c > 0$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The remaining constant is not a further degree of freedom. For any base
$b > 1$, $-\log_b p = -\ln p / \ln b$, so $-c \ln p$ is $-\log_b p$ with
$c = 1 / \ln b$: choosing $c$ and choosing the base of the logarithm are the
same act. Base 2 corresponds to $c = 1/\ln 2 \approx 1.4427$.

<Task>
Locate the two hypotheses in the proof: name the step that uses continuity and
the step that uses strict monotonicity, and confirm that neither is used
before it is named. Then state what the argument still proves if $f$ is only
known on probabilities of the form $2^{-k}$ for integer $k$: Steps 2 and 3
still run, so $f(2^{-k}) = kc \ln 2$, but nothing pins down $f$ between those
points.
</Task>

<KeyFact>
Continuity is not the only route to the conclusion: the strict monotonicity
already assumed is enough on its own, and if both are dropped, additivity by
itself admits solutions that are unbounded on every interval, however short
(the exercise below that removes the continuity assumption).
</KeyFact>

## Surprisal: −log₂ p, measured in bits

Fixing the constant fixes the unit, and the standard choice in coding and
communication is base 2.

<Definition title="surprisal">
The **surprisal** (information content) of an event $x$ with probability
$p(x) > 0$ is
$$
I(x) = -\log_2 p(x),
$$
measured in **bits**.
</Definition>

The quantity is also called the *Shannon information content*.
[MacKay](https://www.inference.org.uk/itprnn/book.pdf) (§2.4) writes it
$h(x) = \log_2 \tfrac{1}{p(x)}$, the same number; the lowercase $h$ marks the
per-event content as against the ensemble average $H$ that
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise">the next lesson</Ref> defines, and
both conventions appear in the literature.

Four cases fix what the definition does and does not cover.

- **The canonical case.** A fair coin lands heads: $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and
  $I = 1$ bit. The unit is defined by this case, not derived from it.
- **A case with a different surface.** A byte is drawn uniformly from its 256
  possible values and its value is reported: $p = 2^{-8}$ and $I = 8$ bits,
  matching the width of the register that holds it. Any uniform draw from
  $2^k$ possibilities costs $k$ bits.
- **The degenerate case.** An event of probability $1$ occurs:
  $I = -\log_2 1 = 0$ bits. The definition covers it, and returns nothing.
- **The near miss.** An event of probability $0$ does not occur, and
  $-\log_2 0$ is undefined. The clause $p(x) > 0$ excludes it. Surprisal grows
  without bound as $p$ falls towards zero, but an impossible event is not the
  limit of possible ones: it never happens, so no report of it is ever made.
  The separate convention $0 \log 0 = 0$ used in
  <Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise">the entropy lesson</Ref> concerns
  the weight attached to an outcome in an average, not the value of $I$.

### A worked check: the ace of spades

<Example title="ace of spades">
One card is drawn from a shuffled 52-card deck; it is the ace of spades.

Probability: $p = \tfrac{1}{52}$. Surprisal:
$I = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{52} = \log_2 52$. Since $2^5 = 32$ and $2^6 = 64$, the
value lies between 5 and 6 bits, and
$\log_2 52 = \ln 52 / \ln 2 = 3.951 / 0.693 \approx 5.70$ bits. The number is
not an integer, and nothing requires it to be: no rule says a report is worth
a whole number of bits.

**Additivity check.** "Ace of spades" is the conjunction of two independent
facts about the card: its suit, one of 4, and its rank, one of 13. The suit
report is worth $\log_2 4 = 2$ bits and the rank report
$\log_2 13 \approx 3.70$ bits. The parts sum to the whole:
$2 + 3.70 = 5.70$ bits, matching the direct computation. The probabilities
multiply the same way: $\tfrac{1}{4} \times \tfrac{1}{13} = \tfrac{1}{52}$.
</Example>

<Figure

  caption="The ace of spades: 5.70 bits split as 2 bits of suit plus 3.70 bits of rank."
/>

<Exercise>
A byte is drawn uniformly at random and reported to be the value `0x2A`.
Compute its surprisal directly, then recompute it as a sum over the eight
independent bit reports that make up the byte, and check that the two agree.
Then produce one event from your own work whose surprisal is between 3 and 4
bits, and name the clause of the definition that decides whether your
probability is admissible.

<Solution>
Directly: $p = \tfrac{1}{256} = 2^{-8}$, so
$I = -\log_2 2^{-8} = 8$ bits.

As a sum: the eight bits of a uniformly drawn byte are independent and each is
$0$ or $1$ with probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$, so each bit report carries
$-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ bit, and the eight reports carry $8 \times 1 = 8$
bits. The probabilities multiply, $(\tfrac{1}{2})^8 = \tfrac{1}{256}$, and the
surprisals add: the same decomposition as suit-and-rank, with eight parts
instead of two.

For the last part, any event with $\tfrac{1}{16} < p < \tfrac{1}{8}$ works,
since $-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{8} = 3$ and $-\log_2 \tfrac{1}{16} = 4$. One
admissible answer: a uniformly chosen day of the fortnight, $p = 1/14$, worth
$\log_2 14 \approx 3.81$ bits. The deciding clause is $p(x) > 0$: an event
your model assigns probability zero has no surprisal at all, not a large one.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyFact>
Every bit count reached later in this course, from the entropy of a source to
the length of a codeword to the capacity of a channel, is a sum or an average
of these per-event values. Nothing but $-\log_2 p$ enters any of them.
</KeyFact>

## Units: changing the base is changing the unit

The base was left free by the theorem, and switching it changes every value by
a single factor. For any two bases $a$ and $b$,
$\log_b p = \log_a p / \log_a b$, and the divisor $\log_a b$ does not depend on
$p$: a table of surprisals in one base becomes the table in another base by
one multiplication throughout. That is what a change of unit is.

Three bases are in use. Base 2 gives **bits**, the working unit of coding and
communication. Base $e$ gives **nats**, standard wherever the quantity is
differentiated, since $\ln$ has the cleaner derivative. Base 10 gives
**hartleys**, one per decimal digit.

Shannon's 1948 paper settles the name in one sentence: "if the base 2 is used
the resulting units may be called binary digits, or more briefly bits, a word
suggested by J. W. Tukey." The conversion to base 10 is stated there too, as
$\log_2 M = 3.32 \log_{10} M$, which makes one decimal digit worth about
$3\tfrac{1}{3}$ bits. The base-10 unit is named after
[Hartley's 1928 paper](https://archive.org/details/bstj7-3-535), which
measured the information in a message of $n$ symbols drawn from an alphabet of
$s$ as $n \log s$, twenty years before probabilities entered the account.

<Aside>
The unit taxonomy in detail. One bit is the surprisal of probability
$\tfrac{1}{2}$; the international standard names this unit the *shannon* (Sh)
to distinguish it from the bit as a unit of storage, but "bit" remains the
working term everywhere outside standards documents. One nat is the surprisal
of probability $1/e \approx 0.368$. One hartley, also called the *dit* or the
*ban*, is the surprisal of probability $\tfrac{1}{10}$: exactly one decimal
digit of information. The ban entered use at Bletchley Park as the unit in
which evidence for a cipher setting was accumulated, which is the same
arithmetic in logarithmic form.
</Aside>

### The surprisal of a letter is a property of the model

<Example title="how many bits is the letter e?">
MacKay's monogram table for English (§2.4) uses a 27-symbol alphabet: the 26
letters plus the space. In it $p(\text{e}) = 0.0913$, $p(\text{z}) = 0.0007$,
and the space is the commonest symbol at $0.1928$. The surprisals are

$$
I(\text{e}) = \log_2 \tfrac{1}{0.0913} \approx 3.45 \text{ bits},
\qquad
I(\text{z}) = \log_2 \tfrac{1}{0.0007} \approx 10.5 \text{ bits},
\qquad
I(\text{space}) \approx 2.38 \text{ bits}.
$$

In nats the first is $\ln \tfrac{1}{0.0913} \approx 2.39$, and the check
against the conversion factor holds: $3.45 \times 0.693 = 2.39$.

Now drop the space from the alphabet and renormalise the 26 letter
frequencies by dividing each by $1 - 0.1928 = 0.8072$. The letter *e* becomes
$0.0913 / 0.8072 = 0.1131$, and its surprisal falls to
$\log_2 \tfrac{1}{0.1131} \approx 3.14$ bits. A widely reproduced 26-letter
table, built from a different corpus, puts $p(\text{e})$ at $0.127$ and its
surprisal at $2.98$ bits.

So one letter has three defensible surprisals, $3.45$, $3.14$ and $2.98$
bits, and the spread is not measurement error. Part of it is the alphabet: the
same text tokenised with and without spaces gives different probabilities for
every symbol. The rest is the corpus: monogram frequencies are counts over a
particular body of text, and newspaper, fiction and technical prose differ in
the second decimal place.
</Example>

The surprisal of a symbol is therefore a statement about a model of the
source, never about the symbol. The same decision about the alphabet shifts
the headline number in
<Ref lesson="entropy-of-english">the entropy of English text</Ref>, where a
per-letter figure has to be quoted against a stated symbol set to mean
anything.

## ★ Surprisal under other names

Surprisal shows up in working systems under vocabulary that hides it. The
negative log-likelihood loss used to train probabilistic models is the model's
surprisal at the data: a model that assigned probability $q(x)$ to an outcome
$x$ that then occurred incurs loss $-\ln q(x)$, its surprisal in nats. A
confident wrong prediction, meaning small $q(x)$ on an outcome that happened,
produces a large loss for the same reason a rare event carries large
surprisal, and the loss is unbounded above for the same reason. Averaging that
loss over data gives the cross entropy of
<Ref lesson="cross-entropy-loss">a later lesson</Ref>.

Surprisal also solves a numerical problem. The probability of a long sequence
is a product of many factors below $1$, and it underflows: double-precision
arithmetic reaches zero near $10^{-308}$, which is a surprisal of about $1023$
bits, or roughly 340 characters of English at 3 bits each. Software therefore
stores log-probabilities and adds them, which is to say it computes with
surprisals and never with probabilities.

## Composite events: additivity needs independence

The units section left one piece of arithmetic unexplained. Folding the
space out of the alphabet lowered the surprisal of *e* from $3.45$ to $3.14$
bits, a drop of $0.31$ bits, and $0.31$ bits is itself a surprisal: it is
$-\log_2 0.8072$, the surprisal of the report "this character is not a space".
The renormalised number is not a different measurement. It is the surprisal of
*e* given that the character is not a space, and the two reports together
reconstruct the first number.

That is the general rule. For any two events with $p(A) > 0$,
$p(A \cap B) = p(A)\,p(B \mid A)$; taking $-\log_2$ of both sides turns the
product into a sum:

$$
I(A \cap B) = I(A) + I(B \mid A),
\qquad \text{where } I(B \mid A) = -\log_2 p(B \mid A).
$$

Independence is the special case $p(B \mid A) = p(B)$, in which the second
term collapses to $I(B)$ and the plain additivity of the axiom reappears.
Outside that case, no formula recovers $I(A \cap B)$ from $I(A)$ and $I(B)$
alone: the conditional probability is extra information that the two marginal
surprisals do not contain.

<KeyFact>
Additivity is an assumption about the events, not a property of the measure.
The general decomposition $I(A \cap B) = I(A) + I(B \mid A)$ always holds, and
its average over all outcomes is the chain rule for entropy in
<Ref lesson="joint-and-conditional-entropy">joint and conditional
entropy</Ref>.
</KeyFact>

<Example title="dependent events on a die">
Roll one fair die. Let $A$ be "the result is even" and $B$ be "the result is
at least 4". Then $p(A) = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $p(B) = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so
$I(A) = I(B) = 1$ bit each.

The joint event is $A \cap B = \{4, 6\}$, with $p = \tfrac{2}{6} =
\tfrac{1}{3}$, so $I(A \cap B) = \log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits. That is not
$I(A) + I(B) = 2$ bits. Learning the roll is even raises the probability that
it is at least 4 from $\tfrac{1}{2}$ to $p(B \mid A) = \tfrac{2}{3}$, so the
second report has less work left to do.

The conditional decomposition gives the right answer:
$I(B \mid A) = -\log_2 \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.58$ bits, and
$I(A) + I(B \mid A) = 1 + 0.58 = 1.58$ bits.
</Example>

### A chain of forty-nine reports

The decomposition applies any number of times in a row, and the telescoping it
produces is the clearest available check that the logarithm was the right
choice.

<Example title="hunting a submarine (MacKay §4.1)">
One submarine hides in one of 64 squares, all equally likely. Shots are fired
one square at a time, and each shot reports either a hit or a miss.

The first shot misses. Its probability was $\tfrac{63}{64}$, so it carries
$\log_2 \tfrac{64}{63} \approx 0.0227$ bits: almost nothing, correctly, since
almost nothing was learned. The second shot misses as well; conditioned on the
first miss, its probability was $\tfrac{62}{63}$, and it carries
$\log_2 \tfrac{63}{62} \approx 0.0230$ bits. Each successive miss is worth
slightly more than the last, because each one rules out a larger fraction of
what remains.

After 32 misses the accumulated total is
$$
\log_2 \tfrac{64}{63} + \log_2 \tfrac{63}{62} + \cdots + \log_2 \tfrac{33}{32}
= \log_2 \tfrac{64}{32} = 1 \text{ bit},
$$
because every numerator cancels the next denominator. Half the hypotheses have
been eliminated, and the accounting says one bit, exactly as a single yes/no
question would.

Suppose the hit comes on the 49th shot, with 16 squares still in play. Its
conditional probability was $\tfrac{1}{16}$, so it carries $\log_2 16 = 4$
bits. The total over the whole search is
$$
\log_2 \tfrac{64}{63} + \log_2 \tfrac{63}{62} + \cdots + \log_2 \tfrac{17}{16}
+ \log_2 \tfrac{16}{1} = \log_2 64 = 6 \text{ bits}.
$$

The same cancellation runs whichever shot lands the hit: the total is always
$\log_2 64 = 6$ bits, the surprisal of naming one square out of 64 in a single
report. How the information is split across the reports depends on the order
of the shots; how much there is in total does not.
</Example>

### Disjoint events fail additivity the other way

<Figure
  float="right"
  width="s"
  src="./figures/disjoint.svg"
  alt="Two disjoint die events with probability 1/6 and 2.58 bits each; their union has probability 1/3 and only 1.58 bits"
  caption="Disjoint events: probabilities add, so the union carries less surprisal than either part."
/>

Independence is not the only way two events can relate, and the opposite
extreme fails in the opposite direction. For disjoint $A$ and $B$ the
probabilities **add**: $p(A \cup B) = p(A) + p(B)$, so

$$
I(A \cup B) = -\log_2\bigl(p(A) + p(B)\bigr),
$$

which is *smaller* than both $I(A)$ and $I(B)$, since the union is more
probable than either part. On a fair die, $A = \{1\}$ and $B = \{2\}$ each
carry $\log_2 6 \approx 2.58$ bits, while $A \cup B$ carries
$\log_2 3 \approx 1.58$ bits, nowhere near the sum $5.17$. Surprisals add when
probabilities multiply, and the probabilities of disjoint events do not
multiply.

What is established: information content is a function of probability alone,
additivity over independent events forces that function to be $-c \log p$, the
constant is a choice of unit, and the general composition rule is
$I(A \cap B) = I(A) + I(B \mid A)$. What is not established is anything about
a source. Surprisal is defined for one event at a time, and a source emits
many, with different probabilities.
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise">The next lesson</Ref> averages
surprisal over a distribution and calls the result entropy.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
One card is drawn from a shuffled 52-card deck. Compute the surprisal, in
bits, of each of these reports: (a) the card is the queen of hearts; (b) the
card is a heart; (c) the card is red. Then identify the fourth quantity $q$
for which $I(a) = I(b) + q$, and say what report $q$ measures.

<Solution>
(a) $p = \tfrac{1}{52}$, so $I = \log_2 52 \approx 5.70$ bits.

(b) $p = \tfrac{13}{52} = \tfrac{1}{4}$, so $I = \log_2 4 = 2$ bits.

(c) $p = \tfrac{26}{52} = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so $I = \log_2 2 = 1$ bit.

The missing quantity is $q = 5.70 - 2 = 3.70$ bits $= \log_2 13$. It is the
surprisal of naming the rank once the suit is known:
$p(\text{queen} \mid \text{heart}) = \tfrac{1}{13}$. In the notation of the
composition rule, $I(a) = I(b) + I(\text{rank} \mid \text{suit})$. Note that
(b) and (c) do not combine the same way: every heart is red, so those two
reports overlap completely rather than being independent.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Examine the two ends of the range separately. State what $I(x)$ does as
$p \to 1$ and as $p \to 0$, with the rate in each case. Then explain why the
value at $p = 0$ is undefined rather than infinite, naming the clause of the
definition that rules the case out, and say what would go wrong with the
additivity axiom if $p = 0$ were admitted with the value $I = \infty$.

<Solution>
**As $p \to 1$.** $I \to 0$, and the approach is linear in the shortfall:
writing $p = 1 - \varepsilon$, $I = -\log_2(1-\varepsilon) =
\varepsilon / \ln 2 + O(\varepsilon^2) \approx 1.44\,\varepsilon$ bits. At
$p = 0.99$ the report is worth about $0.0145$ bits.

**As $p \to 0$.** $I \to \infty$, but slowly: each factor-of-two drop in $p$
adds exactly one bit, so reaching 100 bits takes $p = 2^{-100}$. Unbounded
growth here is not a defect; it is what additivity requires, since $n$
independent copies of a rare event must be worth $n$ times as much.

**At $p = 0$.** The definition requires $p(x) > 0$; $\log_2 0$ is not a real
number, so $I$ is not defined there. Nor is the limit an adequate substitute:
$I(x) \to \infty$ describes a family of events with small positive
probability, all of which can occur, while an event of probability zero never
occurs and is therefore never reported. There is no observation whose
information content the value would describe.

Admitting $I = \infty$ would break the arithmetic rather than extend it.
Additivity would give $I(A \cap B) = \infty + \infty$ for two impossible
events, which is unobjectionable, but the conditional form
$I(A \cap B) = I(A) + I(B \mid A)$ requires $p(A) > 0$ before
$p(B \mid A)$ even exists, so the composition rule loses its meaning exactly
where the infinite value would be used. Entropy handles zero-probability
outcomes by a different route, weighting rather than valuing them: the
convention $0 \log 0 = 0$ in
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise">the next lesson</Ref> assigns weight
zero to the outcome and never evaluates $I$ there.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim. "Information content is additive, so the surprisal of 'the
card is a heart and the card is the ace of hearts' is the sum of the two
surprisals, $2 + 5.70 = 7.70$ bits." Give the correct value, name the step
that fails, and state the general condition under which the claimed step would
have been legitimate.

<Solution>
The correct value is $5.70$ bits. The conjunction "the card is a heart and the
card is the ace of hearts" is the event "the card is the ace of hearts", since
every ace of hearts is a heart. Its probability is $\tfrac{1}{52}$, so its surprisal is
$\log_2 52 \approx 5.70$ bits.

The step that fails is $p(A \cap B) = p(A)\,p(B)$, which holds only for
independent events. Here $A$ is "heart" and $B$ is "ace of hearts", with
$p(A) = \tfrac{1}{4}$ and $p(B) = \tfrac{1}{52}$; the product is
$\tfrac{1}{208}$, but the true joint probability is $\tfrac{1}{52}$, four
times larger. The events are as dependent as they can be: $B$ implies $A$.

The correct decomposition is the conditional one:
$p(B \mid A) = \tfrac{1}{13}$, so
$I(A \cap B) = I(A) + I(B \mid A) = 2 + \log_2 13 = 2 + 3.70 = 5.70$ bits. The
claimed step would have been legitimate only if $p(B \mid A) = p(B)$, that is,
if learning the suit had told you nothing about which card it was. The 2 bits
of suit are counted once in the correct account and twice in the false one,
which is exactly the $2$-bit discrepancy between $7.70$ and $5.70$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The theorem assumed continuity, and Step 4 of the proof is the only place it
is used. Show that the assumption is not decoration and not irreplaceable.
(a) State what Steps 1 to 3 establish about $g$ without continuity, and why
that does not determine $g$ on the irrationals. (b) Prove that strict
monotonicity of $f$, already among the hypotheses, suffices in place of
continuity: for real $t \ge 0$, squeeze $g(t)$ between values at rationals.

<Solution>
**(a) What survives without continuity.** Steps 1 to 3 use only the additive
equation, so they still give $g(q) = cq$ for every non-negative rational $q$,
with $c = g(1)$. That is $\mathbb{Q}$-linearity, and it constrains $g$ only on
a measure-zero set. Regarding $\mathbb{R}$ as a vector space over
$\mathbb{Q}$, one may choose a basis (a Hamel basis, which requires the axiom
of choice) and define $g$ to be any $\mathbb{Q}$-linear map on it. Every such
map satisfies $g(s+t) = g(s) + g(t)$, and every one that is not of the form
$g(t) = ct$ has a graph that is dense in the plane: on every interval, however
short, it takes values arbitrarily far above and below any given number. Such
a function is not monotone, not measurable, and not bounded on any interval,
so it is ruled out by any regularity assumption at all. What it is not ruled
out by is additivity.

**(b) Monotonicity suffices.** Suppose $g$ is additive on $[0,\infty)$ with
$g(q) = cq$ for rational $q \ge 0$, and suppose $g$ is non-decreasing (which
follows from $f$ strictly decreasing, since $t \mapsto e^{-t}$ is decreasing:
larger $t$ means smaller probability means larger $f$). Fix a real $t \ge 0$
and take rationals $q_1 \le t \le q_2$. Monotonicity gives
$g(q_1) \le g(t) \le g(q_2)$, that is $c\,q_1 \le g(t) \le c\,q_2$ (using
$c > 0$). Choosing sequences of rationals $q_1 \uparrow t$ and
$q_2 \downarrow t$ squeezes both bounds to $ct$, so $g(t) = ct$.

The theorem can therefore be restated with a shorter hypothesis list: additive,
$f(1) = 0$, strictly decreasing. Continuity was never an extra requirement on
the measure, only a convenient route through the proof. The transferable move
is that additive functions are rigid: any one-sided regularity condition
(monotonicity, measurability, boundedness on an interval, continuity at a
single point) upgrades $\mathbb{Q}$-linearity to $\mathbb{R}$-linearity.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take any English text of at least 2,000 characters that you have to hand and
count its letters. Report the surprisal, in bits, of its commonest and its
rarest letter under three treatments of the alphabet: (i) all characters as
they appear, including spaces and punctuation; (ii) letters and spaces only,
case folded; (iii) the 26 letters only, case folded, punctuation and spaces
discarded. Say by how much each number moves between treatments, and state
what would count as agreement with the published monogram figures.

<Solution>
**What to expect.** The commonest letter will almost certainly be *e* and the
rarest one of *j*, *q*, *x*, *z*, though in 2,000 characters the rarest letter
is often absent altogether, in which case the estimate $\hat{p} = 0$ gives no
surprisal at all: report the rarest letter that does appear and note the
truncation. A 2,000-character sample contains roughly 250 occurrences of *e*
and rarely more than 2 of *z*, so the *e* estimate is good to a few percent
and the *z* estimate is worth no more than an order of magnitude.

**How the treatments differ.** Under (i) every symbol's probability is
diluted by the punctuation and digits, so every surprisal rises. Moving from
(ii) to (iii) removes the space, whose share of the character stream is about
$0.19$, and divides each remaining probability by about $0.81$: every letter's
surprisal drops by $-\log_2 0.81 \approx 0.31$ bits, uniformly. Predicting
that uniform shift before computing it is the check that you have understood
the conditional decomposition.

**What counts as agreement.** For treatment (ii), $I(\text{e})$ should land
within a few tenths of a bit of $3.45$; for treatment (iii), within a few
tenths of $3.0$ to $3.15$. The rare letters will not agree that closely, and
should not be expected to: a letter with true probability $0.0007$ appears
$1.4$ times on average in 2,000 characters, so the Poisson fluctuation between
1 and 3 occurrences moves the estimated surprisal by more than a bit. A useful
sharper test is the ranking: the order of the top eight letters by frequency
is stable across corpora even when the individual probabilities are not, and
disagreement there means the sample is too short or the text is unusual, for
example code or a word list.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A source emits one of four symbols with probabilities
$\{A: \tfrac{1}{2},\ B: \tfrac{1}{4},\ C: \tfrac{1}{8},\ D: \tfrac{1}{8}\}$.
Compute the surprisal of each symbol. Then compute the average of those four
numbers weighted by the probabilities of the symbols they belong to, and
separately the plain unweighted average of the four. Report both, and say
which one answers the question "how many bits does one symbol from this source
carry on average".

<Solution>
Surprisals: $I(A) = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} = 1$ bit,
$I(B) = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{4} = 2$ bits, and
$I(C) = I(D) = -\log_2 \tfrac{1}{8} = 3$ bits.

Probability-weighted average:
$$
\tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{1}{4}(2) + \tfrac{1}{8}(3) + \tfrac{1}{8}(3)
= 0.5 + 0.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 = 1.75 \text{ bits}.
$$

Unweighted average: $(1 + 2 + 3 + 3)/4 = 2.25$ bits.

The weighted number is the one that answers the question. Over a long run,
half of all emitted symbols are $A$ and carry 1 bit each, so the 1-bit case
must count for half the average; the unweighted mean counts the rare symbol
$D$ as often as the common symbol $A$ and therefore describes no run of the
source. The weighted value $1.75$ bits is the entropy of this distribution,
defined and computed in
<Ref lesson="entropy-as-average-surprise">the next lesson</Ref>; the same
lesson shows that $1.75$ is also the fewest bits per symbol any lossless code
for this source can average, which is why the weighting is not a matter of
taste.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Production features face conditions offline notebooks never do: categorical vocabularies that grow hourly, missing values from upstream schema changes, and the requirement that every transform be computable at serving time.

Module 3's claim is that training data is manufactured, and two steps of the manufacturing line are now in place: <Ref lesson="labels-and-feedback-loops">labels</Ref>, each source priced, and <Ref lesson="data-leakage-and-splitting">splits</Ref> that no longer let the offline metric lie. What those honest splits measure is the features, and the features taught in an offline curriculum assume a fixed vocabulary, a complete row, and a transform that runs once. This lesson replaces those assumptions with the production versions: hashing for vocabularies with no upper bound, crosses that buy a linear model its interactions, embeddings treated as infrastructure objects, and the lifecycle audits that decide which features stay. What it deliberately leaves open is keeping each transform identical on both sides of the train/serve boundary, which is the next lesson's subject.

The route: an encoding that fails three ways at Amazon scale; the hashing trick and the measured price of its collisions; feature crosses and their startling economics; embeddings as features with serving infrastructure attached; missing values and transform statistics; and the audits (coverage, overlap, importance, deletion) that manage a feature portfolio over time.

## The unbounded vocabulary: three encodings, three failures

The offline curriculum ends at one-hot and integer encoding, and both assume the vocabulary is known before training. Production categoricals refuse the assumption. Amazon's catalog held over two million distinct brands already in 2019, and new brands register continuously; the same shape recurs wherever the category is generated by the world rather than by a schema: user accounts, IP addresses, website domains, restaurants, product types.

The escalation below, a recommender using product brand as a feature, is the canonical three-act failure.

### Act 1: integer encoding, a partial function

Assign each of the two million brands an id from 0 to 1,999,999. The model scores well on the historical test set and is approved for 1% of live traffic, where it crashes: a brand outside the training vocabulary has no id, and the encoder throws. An encoder built from the training vocabulary is a *partial function*, and serving traffic is drawn from a larger domain than its training-set support.

### Act 2: the UNKNOWN bucket starves novelty

Reserve id 2,000,000 for every unseen brand. The crash disappears and a starvation appears: the UNKNOWN category never occurred in training, so the model has no learned behavior for it, and products from new sellers get no recommendations at all. Refining the design (encode the top 99% most popular brands, map the bottom 1% plus everything new to UNKNOWN) trains the bucket, and holds for about an hour, until twenty new brands join: luxury labels, knockoffs, and established brands entering the platform, all served identically as one averaged "unpopular" input, and click-through on recommendations drops.

### Act 3: the hashing trick, a total function

Replace the vocabulary lookup with a computation: $\text{index} = \operatorname{hash}(\text{brand}) \bmod 2^{18}$. Every string, including every brand that will register next year, maps to one of $2^{18} = 262{,}144$ indices. The encoder is now a total function of fixed output width, and it stores nothing, because the transform is code, not a table. The price is that distinct brands can land on the same index.

The hashing trick was popularized by [Vowpal Wabbit](https://vowpalwabbit.org/) and is now built into scikit-learn, TensorFlow, and gensim; its academic reputation as a hack has never dented its adoption, because it is the only encoding in the list that survives all three acts.

<KeyFact>
A production categorical encoder must be a total function over strings it has never seen, with fixed output width and no vocabulary artifact to version and ship. Integer encoding fails the first requirement, UNKNOWN buckets fail by collapsing all novelty into one systematically-shared input; hashing satisfies all three and pays in collisions that are random rather than systematic.
</KeyFact>

## The price of collisions: two probabilities, one of which matters

Collisions are the objection everyone raises against Act 3, and sizing the hash space requires separating two quantities that the word "collision" conflates. First the mechanics, because they contain a constraint that matters later.

### The transform is stateless code

The implementation is one line:

```sql
ABS(MOD(FARM_FINGERPRINT(brand), 262144))
```

The hash must be a *fingerprint* hash (FarmHash-family): deterministic, with no seed or salt, and portable, with implementations in every language the pipeline touches, because the identical computation must run in the training pipeline and in the serving path. Cryptographic hashes fail the requirement from the other side: they are deliberately slow, and salted deployments are not even deterministic. This is the same hash, for the same reason, as <Ref lesson="data-leakage-and-splitting">repeatable splitting</Ref>: a transform whose output must agree across runs, languages, and years is a pure function or it is a bug. Note what is absent: no vocabulary file, no lookup table, no artifact beyond the bucket count in the model's configuration.

### Birthday arithmetic: refusing the wrong probability

The tempting fix for collisions is to raise the bucket count until they vanish. It does not work, and the reason is the birthday problem. For $n$ keys hashed into $B$ buckets,

$$
P(\text{a collision exists somewhere}) \;=\; 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{n-1}\Bigl(1 - \tfrac{i}{B}\Bigr) \;\approx\; 1 - e^{-n(n-1)/2B},
$$

and the exponent grows with the square of the vocabulary, so "some collision, somewhere" stays likely at bucket counts that dwarf the vocabulary. But that probability is the wrong quantity to size by. The quantity a feature's quality depends on is per-key exposure: the probability that one particular key shares its bucket,

$$
P(\text{this key collides}) \;=\; 1 - \Bigl(1 - \tfrac{1}{B}\Bigr)^{n-1} \;\approx\; \tfrac{n-1}{B}.
$$

<Example title="347 airports, 100,000 buckets">
A flight-delay model encodes the departure airport; the US has 347 of them. Hash into $B = 100{,}000$ buckets, 288 buckets per airport.

Any-collision: $n(n-1)/2 = 347 \times 346 / 2 = 60{,}031$ pairs, so
$P \approx 1 - e^{-60{,}031/100{,}000} = 1 - e^{-0.60} \approx 45\%$. Even at 288× the vocabulary, the odds are near even that two airports share a bucket somewhere.

Per-key exposure: $346/100{,}000 = 0.35\%$. The expected number of colliding pairs is $60{,}031/100{,}000 = 0.6$, so, counting keys rather than pairs, the expected number of airports sitting in a shared bucket is about $1.2$ of 347.

The same table read at two scales: a collision almost certainly exists, and almost no key is in one.
</Example>

<Solution>

The rule of thumb of about five entries per bucket lands in the same range ($b = 13$ or $14$). The transferable move: size the hash space from expected occupancy, and read per-key exposure to know what regime you are in; the next question, what that regime costs in model quality, is answered by measurement below.
</Solution>

### The measured cost, and where it concentrates

Exposure numbers still need a price in model quality, and Booking.com measured it. In [Bernardi's 2018 study](https://booking.ai/dont-be-tricked-by-the-hashing-trick-192a6aae3087) on production datasets, driving the collision rate up to 50% of features raised log loss by less than 0.5%. The mechanism is the one Act 2 lacked: hash collisions are random, so a colliding key shares statistics with an arbitrary partner and the damage spreads thin, where the UNKNOWN bucket collided every new key with every other new key, systematically.

Random on average is not random for the key you care about. In the airport model at 100 buckets, Chicago O'Hare shares a bucket with Burlington, Vermont and Kansas City: ORD's 3.6 million flights against BTV's 67,000 mean the shared bucket learns Chicago's behavior and nothing else, and Burlington inherits O'Hare's taxi delays. Two standing mitigations: add an **aggregate feature** that preserves the per-key signal the hash destroys (each airport's historical on-time rate as a numeric column beside the hashed id), and treat the bucket count as a **tuned hyperparameter** rather than a constant, since the collision/quality trade lands differently on every distribution.

<KeyFact>
Per-key exposure, the sizing quantity, silently assumes a well-mixed hash: bucket assignment is then independent of popularity, however skewed the traffic; whether traffic skew changes the affected share of examples is the closing refutation exercise.
</KeyFact>

## Feature crosses: interactions a linear model can afford

Hashing compresses a categorical space; the second production technique inflates one on purpose. A *feature cross* is a synthetic categorical built from the cartesian product of two or more features, and it exists to hand a linear model the interaction terms it cannot represent on its own.

The cleanest failure it repairs is XOR-shaped data: points labeled $+$ in quadrants II and IV of the plane, $-$ in quadrants I and III. No weights $w_1, w_2, b$ on the raw coordinates separate the classes (the four sign constraints contradict; the derivation exercise below carries it through). The repair takes two steps. **Bucketize** each coordinate by sign into binary features $A/B$ and $C/D$; then **cross** them into four indicators $AC, BC, AD, BD$, one per quadrant. Each indicator gets its own weight, and the data is linearly separable in the crossed space.

Third, the four crossed cells AC, BC, AD, BD tinted by class, each cell one indicator feature with its own weight, making the data separable."

### The economics: a cross plus a linear model, versus a DNN

The benchmark that makes crosses a systems decision rather than a modeling nicety comes from the natality dataset (US birth records; the task is predicting birthweight). A linear regression given the cross of `is_male` (3 values) and `plurality` (6 values), trained in BigQuery ML, reached **RMSE 1.05 in 0.42 minutes of training**. A deep neural network on the same data without crosses reached RMSE 1.07 in 48 minutes. Equal quality, at roughly 1/100th the training time, from a convex problem with reproducible optima: with enough data, explicitly enumerated interactions plus a linear model buy what the DNN spends its depth learning. The cross is also why the funnel systems of module 7 kept linear rankers competitive for a decade.

Three rules of discipline keep the technique from destroying itself:

- **Never cross raw continuous features.** A continuous input has unbounded distinct values, so its product space is unenumerable; bucketize first (latitude and longitude into 0.01° bins, then cross the bins into a location cell).
- **Regularize the blown-up space.** Cardinalities multiply: `day_of_week` × `hour_of_day` is only $7 \times 24 = 168$, but four bucketized coordinates cross into $n^4$ cells, most holding a handful of examples that a model will memorize. L1 regularization prunes the useless cells to zero weight; L2 keeps rare cells from overfitting. And since the crossed space is itself an unbounded-ish vocabulary, the standard implementation hashes the cross, which is why the section 2 arithmetic applies: 18 `(is_male, plurality)` pairs hashed into 1,000 buckets are collision-free with probability $\approx e^{-153/1000} = 86\%$.
- **Do not cross correlated features.** The cross of $x_1$ and $x_2 = 5x_1$, bucketized by sign, produces four cells of which two are structurally empty and the other two duplicate $x_1$'s buckets: the product space adds nothing when one factor determines the other.

## Embeddings: the feature that ships as data

Hashing and one-hot share a defect that crosses do not repair: the representation carries no notion of similarity. Two one-hot vectors for distinct categories have dot product 0, whatever the categories are; a hashed pair is identical or unrelated. The `plurality` feature from the natality model makes the loss concrete: `Twins(2)` and `Triplets(3)` affect birthweight in nearly the same way, and the one-hot representation states that they are exactly as unrelated as `Single(1)` and `Quintuplets(5)`.

An *embedding* replaces the one-hot vector with a short learned dense vector: an embedding layer is a trainable lookup table, its weights fit by the same gradient descent as the rest of the model. Trained on the birthweight task with 2 dimensions, the plurality embedding's cosine-similarity matrix acquires exactly the structure one-hot destroyed: Twins·Triplets = 0.99, Quadruplets·Quintuplets = 0.99, Single vs Quadruplets = 0.06.

### Two acquisition paths and two rules of thumb

An embedding is either **co-trained** (a layer inside this model, optimized for this task, repriced at every retrain) or **pretrained** (produced elsewhere — a word2vec-style run over user sessions, another team's user embedding — and reused, cheaply, as a frozen input). The trade is task fit against cost and coupling, and the coupling is the sharp edge: [Rules of ML #17](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/guides/rules-of-ml) warns that a feature learned by an external system carries that system's objective, goes stale if you snapshot it, and silently changes meaning when its producer updates. A pretrained embedding is an *upstream dependency* with all of module 1's debt attached.

Dimension choice is honest folklore. The TensorFlow documentation's rule of thumb is $d = \sqrt[4]{D}$ for $D$ categories; another in circulation is $d = 1.6\sqrt{D}$. For $D = 625$ they say 5 and 40, an 8× disagreement, which is the correct amount of confidence to place in either: treat the pair as the bounds of a hyperparameter search, not as laws.

Operator choice at serving time is a latency decision, not only a modeling one. LinkedIn's job-matching model scores each seeker–job pair by logistic regression over the elementwise (Hadamard) product of the two embeddings; the team's stated reason: "We chose the Hadamard product over more common functions, like cosine similarity, to give the model flexibility to learn its own distance function, while avoiding a fully connected layer to reduce scoring latency in our online recommendation systems."

### The systems view: an artifact that needs a home

For this course the defining property of an embedding is not the training procedure but the deployed object: a versioned table of vocabulary → vector, learned state that must ship somewhere. Co-trained embeddings travel inside the model artifact. Precomputed entity embeddings (a user vector refreshed nightly from behavior) are features like any other, and land in <Ref lesson="feature-platform">the feature platform</Ref>: materialized to the offline store for training joins, replicated into the online key-value store for millisecond lookup at request time. Precompute-and-look-up is the standard pattern precisely because computing an embedding in the request path would spend the serving latency budget on a transform.

<KeyFact>
Hashing ships as pure code and an embedding ships as data: a versioned table that must be stored, replicated to the serving path, and kept in step with the model that expects it. Similarity search over such tables at corpus scale is its own infrastructure problem, approximate nearest-neighbor indexing, which <Ref lesson="recommender-funnel">module 7's retrieval stage</Ref> is built on.
</KeyFact>

## Serving reality: missing values, statistics, and the clock

Every technique so far assumed the feature's raw value arrives. Production revokes that assumption too, and the missing values it produces are not one phenomenon but three, distinguished by the reason the value is absent.

<Remark title="the missingness taxonomy">
**Missing not at random (MNAR)**: the absence depends on the value itself; respondents with higher incomes decline to disclose income, so the gap itself carries signal. **Missing at random (MAR)**: the absence depends on another observed variable; one gender group in a survey tends not to disclose age. **Missing completely at random (MCAR)**: no pattern, and genuinely rare; absence usually has a reason. The taxonomy decides the remedy: deleting rows with MNAR gaps deletes signal, deleting rows with MAR gaps biases the sample against the correlated group, and imputation is safest exactly when it encodes the distinction, for instance with an explicit is-missing indicator column rather than a plausible filled-in value.
</Remark>

MAR: the missing age cells concentrate in group B, so dropping rows biases the sample against that group. MCAR: missing cells scatter with no pattern, the rare case. Each column names the remedy that fails for it."
  caption="One hole in a table, three causes, and the remedy that is wrong for each. The safe default encodes the distinction instead of erasing it: impute, plus an explicit is-missing indicator."

Two operational rules follow. **Impute with values that cannot be confused with data**: filling missing children-count with 0 makes the childless and the unknown identical forever. And **expect the upstream schema to change**: the age-0 incident recalled from <Ref lesson="data-leakage-and-splitting">the leakage lesson</Ref>, where a frontend stopped collecting age and serving imputed a value training had never seen, was not a modeling error but a missing-value policy meeting an unannounced schema change. The distribution-overlap audit in the next section is the check that catches it, and the module's <Ref lesson="data-validation">validation lesson</Ref> automates that check into a pipeline gate.

Scaling statistics carry the same fragility in milder form. The min/max, mean and variance that scale numeric features are fit on the training split only, established as <Ref lesson="data-leakage-and-splitting">leakage's preprocessing route</Ref>; the production corollary is that they are artifacts, shipped with the model and applied verbatim at serving, and they age: as the live distribution drifts away from the training window, the frozen statistics scale incoming values into regions the model never saw, which is one of the quiet arguments for a regular retraining cadence.

### The clock: a feature that cannot overlap

The extreme case of a transform that serving changes is the clock itself. Netflix feeds its play-history model the raw, continuous timestamp of each historical play together with the current time at prediction, and reported an offline lift on the order of tens of percent from the pair — the one published account is secondhand and names neither the metric nor the baseline, so the size is an order of magnitude, not a measurement. Read the serving side of the feature: the production model always receives "now", a value strictly greater than every timestamp in its training data. By construction, the serving distribution of this feature has zero overlap with training; the offline gain was measured entirely on interpolation inside the logged range, and survives in production only if retraining runs often enough to keep "now" near the training window's edge, which is exactly how Netflix operates it.

A feature can be a large, genuine offline win and a serving liability at once, and telling the two apart requires reading the feature's value on the serving side rather than in the training export: the habit the forward exercise drills, and the subject the next lesson names.

## Feature lifecycles: measure, then delete

A feature that clears every hurdle so far still has to earn its place over time, and the portfolio view is where production diverges hardest from the notebook. Two checks and two budget rules govern it.

### Generalization: coverage and overlap

**Coverage** is the fraction of examples carrying a value for the feature. Low coverage is a warning (a feature present in 1% of rows rarely generalizes), with the MNAR exception cutting the other way: a feature present in 1% of rows whose presence is 90% predictive of a positive label is excellent, because the missingness is the signal. Coverage is also a monitor: it differs across slices, moves over time, and a large train/test coverage gap means the split is broken or leaking, per the last lesson.

**Distribution overlap** asks whether the values seen at serving lie inside the range seen in training. The clean failure needs no drift at all: a taxi-ETA model retrained weekly on the previous six days, using `DAY_OF_WEEK` as a feature, trains on Monday–Saturday and serves on Sunday, coverage 100%, overlap zero. `HOUR_OF_DAY` in the same model overlaps completely and generalizes fine. Between the two sits the generality/specificity dial: `IS_RUSH_HOUR` generalizes more and informs less than `HOUR_OF_DAY`.

### Importance: the concentration is extreme

Importance measurement (XGBoost's built-ins for trees, [SHAP](https://github.com/shap/shap) model-agnostically) exists in production to reveal how little most features matter. When Facebook's ads team measured feature importance in their click-through-rate model ([He et al., 2014](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2648584.2648589)), the top 10 features carried about **half** of the model's total importance, and the last 300 features together carried **under 1%**. The head justifies its infrastructure; the tail is candidate debt, kept alive by inertia.

The model-agnostic audit for the tail is **leave-one-feature-out**: retrain without a feature (or feature group) and measure the delta, run regularly rather than once. Sculley's technical-debt paper names the four ways a near-zero-delta feature got into the model: **legacy** features made redundant by later ones, **bundled** features shipped in a group under deadline, **ε-features** added for a gain too small to survive noise, and **correlated** features riding a partner that is the actually-causal one, brittle the day the world changes the correlation.

### The budget: features scale with data, and unused features are debt

[Rules of ML #21](https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/guides/rules-of-ml) states the sizing law this whole lesson has been assuming: the feature weights a linear model can learn is roughly proportional to the data. A thousand labeled examples support about a dozen features; ten million support on the order of a hundred thousand; billions support crosses of full token vocabularies at ~10 million features. The rule prices sections 2–4: hashing and regularized crosses are how a team spends a large data budget, and a small budget cannot afford them.

Rule #22 closes the loop: **clean up features you are no longer using**. An unused feature is not neutral; it is surface area for the failures this lesson catalogued (a vocabulary that grows, a schema that changes, a correlation that flips) with no offsetting gain, and deleting it is the one feature-engineering operation with guaranteed non-negative expected value. Rule #11 makes the practice organizational: every feature column has an owner and documentation, because the engineer who knew what `f_re7_v2` meant changes teams.

What is established: features that survive production are built with hashing when the vocabulary is unbounded, crosses (bucketized, regularized, hashed) when a linear model needs interactions, embeddings when similarity structure matters, missingness handled by mechanism rather than by default, and a lifecycle of coverage, overlap, importance, and deletion audits. Every one of these transforms must now run in two places, the training pipeline and the serving path, and nothing so far guarantees the two computations agree; the Netflix timestamp already showed the two sides disagreeing by construction. <Ref lesson="training-serving-skew">The next lesson</Ref> names that gap training–serving skew, decomposes it into three measurable pieces, and builds the transform architectures that close it.

Derivation the lesson skipped: for binary features $x_1, x_2 \in \{0,1\}$ and the XOR label ($y = 1$ iff $x_1 \ne x_2$), prove that no linear model $\hat y = \mathbb{1}[w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + b > 0]$ classifies all four points, then add the crossed feature $x_3 = x_1 x_2$ and exhibit weights that do.

<Solution>
The four points impose: $(0,0)\!: b < 0$; $(1,0)\!: w_1 + b > 0$; $(0,1)\!: w_2 + b > 0$; $(1,1)\!: w_1 + w_2 + b < 0$. Add the two middle inequalities: $w_1 + w_2 + 2b > 0$, so $w_1 + w_2 + b > -b > 0$ (using $b < 0$), contradicting the fourth inequality. No $(w_1, w_2, b)$ exists; XOR is not a linear function of its inputs, only of their interaction.

With $x_3 = x_1 x_2$, take $w_1 = w_2 = 1$, $w_3 = -2$, $b = -\tfrac12$: scores are $-\tfrac12, \tfrac12, \tfrac12, -\tfrac12$ on $(0,0), (1,0), (0,1), (1,1)$ — correct on all four. The crossed feature is exactly the interaction term the contradiction proved missing; the figure's quadrant version is the same argument with each sign-bucket pair as one indicator.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Research-shaped: reproduce the natality economics in spirit. For a taxi-fare model with `day_of_week` (7 values) and `hour_of_day` (24 values), estimate (a) the parameter count and per-example training FLOPs of a linear model over the two one-hot features plus their cross, and (b) the same for a DNN taking the 31 one-hot inputs through two hidden layers of 64 units. From the FLOP ratio, argue whether the published 0.42 min vs 48 min (114×) wall-clock gap is plausible, and state what would count as agreement.

<Solution>
(a) Linear with cross: $7 + 24 + 7 \times 24 = 199$ weights plus a bias, ≈200 parameters. Each example activates exactly 3 features (its day, its hour, its crossed cell), so a sparse implementation does ~3 multiply-adds forward and ~3 gradient updates backward: order 10 FLOPs per example.

(b) DNN: $31 \times 64 + 64 + 64 \times 64 + 64 + 64 + 1 \approx 6{,}300$ parameters; a dense forward pass is ≈6,100 multiply-adds, and backward roughly doubles it: order $2 \times 10^4$ FLOPs per example, about 1,000–2,000× the linear model's.

The raw FLOP ratio (~10³) exceeds the observed 114×, and the gap closes from both sides: dense DNN math vectorizes onto hardware far better than sparse scattered updates (shrinking the DNN's effective cost per FLOP), while the non-convex DNN typically needs more passes to converge than the convex linear fit (raising it). Agreement here means the right order of magnitude with the right sign, two to three orders in FLOPs supporting two in wall clock, and it holds. The economics are structural, not an artifact of one benchmark: enumerated interactions turn learned representation into a convex lookup.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Mixed review: the flight-delay model deploys with (a) the hashed `departure_airport` feature and (b) a co-trained embedding of the `day_of_week × hour_of_day` cross, plus (c) a precomputed per-airport on-time-rate aggregate refreshed nightly. Using the stores this course has built so far — offline warehouse, online key-value store, model artifact — name where each transform's state lives, and identify the one that lives nowhere.

<Solution>
(a) Nowhere: the hashed feature has no state. The transform is `ABS(MOD(FARM_FINGERPRINT(airport), B))`, pure code; the only configuration is the bucket count $B$, versioned with the model. No vocabulary is extracted, stored, or synchronized, which is precisely the property Act 3 bought.

(b) Model artifact: a co-trained embedding is a weight matrix inside the network, shipped and versioned with the rest of the parameters. It needs no external store, and it changes at every retrain.

(c) Both halves of <Ref lesson="feature-platform">the feature platform</Ref>: the nightly aggregate is computed in batch over the warehouse's history (the <Ref lesson="batch-and-stream-processing">batch path</Ref>), written to the offline store for training joins, and materialized into the online key-value store so the serving path can read one airport's row in milliseconds. It is the only one of the three with a freshness property to monitor, and keeping its training-time and serving-time values consistent is exactly the point-in-time problem the coming skew lesson closes.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Forward: the Netflix-style timestamp feature produced its large offline lift. Write down the value this feature takes at serving time for a prediction made at time $T$, compare it with the feature's range in a training set exported at time $T_0 < T$, and explain the precise sense in which the offline lift may be fictitious. What operational policy rescues the feature?

<Solution>
At serving, the feature equals the current clock, $T$; in training data exported at $T_0$, every value is $\le T_0$. So for all $T > T_0$ the serving value lies strictly outside the training range: overlap between the feature's training and serving distributions is zero by construction, the extreme case of the overlap audit. The offline evaluation, run on held-out rows whose timestamps sit *inside* the logged range, measured interpolation; production asks for extrapolation on every request. The lift is real about the past and unverified about the only regime the model will ever serve in; the further $T$ drifts from $T_0$, the less the certificate covers.

The rescue is operational, not representational: retrain on a cadence tight enough that $T - T_0$ stays small relative to the timescales the model learned, which is how Netflix runs it. The general phenomenon, a feature whose training-time and serving-time values systematically differ, is *training–serving skew*, and <Ref lesson="training-serving-skew">the next lesson</Ref> decomposes it into three measurable gaps and builds the architectures that close them.
</Solution>

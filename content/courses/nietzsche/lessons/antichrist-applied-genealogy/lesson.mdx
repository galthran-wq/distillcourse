The Antichrist is the Genealogy's method run on one case: Israel's God denaturalized by priests, a redeemer reconstructed as a physiological type, and Paul turning a practice into a doctrine of guilt and resurrection — every step borrowed from the three treatises, and several unsupported by anything but assertion. This lesson reads *The Antichrist* §§24–47, with §15, §2, §7 and §§56–62 around them, as the application the book says it is: at each step it names the *Genealogy* result being used, states what record the step rests on, and grades the step by the *Genealogy*'s own standard for a history.

The course's argument arrives here with the three treatises reconstructed and joined. <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">The priest lesson</Ref> stated the joint as one machine: the inversion from the First Treatise (I:7's priestly caste, I:10's *ressentiment*), the site from the Second (II:16's enclosed animal, II:19–21's debt to a god), the administrator and instrument from the Third (III:15's redirection, sin). <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">The will-to-truth lesson</Ref> closed the treatise on its own author and ended on what stood open: the machinery had been reconstructed from Nietzsche's general claims and never watched on a single historical record. This lesson watches it, on the record Nietzsche chose himself, and what it contributes is a scorecard: where the application is mechanism plus record, where it is mechanism plus assertion, and where a value verdict is staged as history. What the scorecard leaves for Module 5 is the "type" the book reconstructs Jesus as, which is a constitution of drives and needs the psychology of *Beyond Good and Evil* to be a causal claim at all.

The route: the artefact and the standard it sets itself; §§24–26's five stages as I:7 and II:19–21; §§27–35's redeemer as the Doctrine of Types; §§39–47's Paul as III:15 and I:7, with §45 under the sourcing drill; §15 and §§48–49 as the Four Great Errors; the is–ought slides of §§2, 7 and 62 with A §2 read two ways; §§56–57 and §61 as the method past its evidence; and the scorecard.

## The artefact: thirty days, two citations of the Genealogy, and a standard set by the Preface

*Der Antichrist* was written in Turin between 3 and 30 September 1888, immediately after *Twilight of the Idols*, and it was planned as the first of four books of a *Revaluation of All Values*; the surviving title pages record the plan being abandoned, the later one carrying the subtitle "Revaluation of All Values" crossed out and *Fluch auf das Christenthum*, "A Curse on Christianity", written in. It reached print in 1895 from the Nietzsche Archive, which places it on the rung <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> called an authorized manuscript: prepared for the printer by the author, not seen through the press by him.

The book presents itself as an application, twice, by name. §24: "In my *Genealogy of Morals* I introduced for the first time the psychology of the antithetical concepts of a noble morality and a *ressentiment* morality, the latter deriving from a denial of the former: but this latter corresponds totally to Judeo-Christian morality." §45, after quoting 1 Corinthians 1:20 ff.: "To understand this passage, a document of the very first rank for the psychology of every Chandala morality, one should read the first essay of my *Genealogy of Morals*." A text that cites the treatise as the source of its psychology asks to be graded as the treatise's application, and the standard for the grading is the treatise's own.

### The standard: grey, documented, actually there

<Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">The genealogy-as-method lesson</Ref> read Preface §7's demand: against the English genealogists' hypotheses "into the blue", what matters to a genealogist is "the grey (*das Graue*), which is to say the documented (*das Urkundliche*), the actually ascertainable (*das Wirklich-Feststellbare*), what actually existed (*das Wirklich-Dagewesene*), in short the whole long hieroglyphic script of humanity's moral past". The treatises met that demand unevenly, and the earlier lessons recorded where: etymologies and a church father's peroration in the First, law codes and a festival in the Second, an epidemiology of the sick in the Third, with the course's sourcing standard grading each as illustration or as proof.

So each step of *The Antichrist* is graded in three columns. **Mechanism**: which result of the treatises the step uses, named by section. **Record**: what document, dated event or checkable claim the step rests on, as distinct from the mechanism. **Verdict**: what value claim the step draws, and whether the premise that gets from the description to the value is stated. A step scores on all three when it is what the Preface asked for; a step that scores on the first only is a mechanism applied by assertion; and a step that scores on the third only is a verdict staged as history. The hyperbole enters the verdict column as quotation; what is graded is the premise, not the tone.

## §§24–26: Israel's God denaturalized in five stages (I:7 and II:19–21 applied)

The standard is set, and the first stretch it meets is the one where the record column should be fullest: §§24–26 are the only place in the book that tells a dated-looking history, a nation's god changed in stages by named events, and the column is empty from the third stage on. §24 states the thesis the stages serve.

Christianity "is not a counter-movement against the Jewish instinct, it is actually its logical consequence, one further conclusion of its fear-inspiring logic"; the Jews, "faced with the question of being or not being", chose "being at any price", and the price was "the radical falsification of all nature": "they inverted religion, religious worship, morality, history, psychology one after the other in an irreparable way into the contradiction of their natural values". The agent is named as a type, not a people: "for the kind of man who desires to attain power through Judaism and Christianity, the priestly kind, *décadence* is only a means". That is I:7's hinge from <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the ressentiment lesson</Ref>, the caste noble by rank and impotent in action whose one outlet is a revaluation, and §24 says so in the sentence that cites the treatise.

### The five stages, as §§25–26 tell them

§25 announces "five stages" (*fünf Thatsachen*, five facts) in "the history of Israel", offered as "a typical history of the denaturalizing of natural values". The stages as the text gives them:

1. **The Kingdom.** "Originally, above all in the period of the Kingdom, Israel too stood in a correct, that is to say natural relationship to all things." Yahweh "was the expression of their consciousness of power, of their delight in themselves, their hopes of themselves"; from him the people expected "victory and salvation", and "above all rain". "Yaweh is the God of Israel and consequently the God of justice: the logic of every nation that is in power and has a good conscience about it."
2. **Misfortune.** The state "had been tragically done away with: anarchy within, the Assyrian from without. But every hope remained unfulfilled. The old God could no longer do what he formerly could. One should have let him go."
3. **The reinterpretation.** "What happened? One altered the conception of him: at this price one retained him." The altered god "becomes an instrument in the hands of priestly agitators who henceforth interpret all good fortune as a reward, all misfortune as punishment for disobedience of God, for 'sin': that most mendacious mode of interpretation of a supposed 'moral world-order' through which the natural concept 'cause' and 'effect' is once and for all stood on its head."
4. **The past rewritten.** §26: "The entire history of Israel was useless: away with it!" The priests "translated their own national past into religious terms, that is to say they made of it a stupid salvation-mechanism of guilt towards Yaweh and punishment, piety towards Yaweh and reward"; "the great epoch in the history of Israel became an epoch of decay, the Exile, the long years of misfortune, was transformed into an eternal punishment for the great epoch".
5. **Revelation, and the priest as the sole channel.** "The 'will of God' … has to be known – to this end a 'revelation' is required. In plain words: a great literary forgery becomes necessary, a 'sacred book' is discovered." From then on "at all the natural events of life, at birth, marriage, sickness, death … there appears the holy parasite to denaturalize them"; "disobedience of God, that is to say of the priest, of 'the Law', now acquires the name 'sin'"; "the priest alone 'redeems'". The closing formula: "'God forgives him who repents' – in plain language: who subjects himself to the priest."

### The map to the treatises

Stage 3 is the mechanism of two treatises at once. The priest who reads misfortune as punishment is I:7's priestly caste doing what I:7 says it does, revaluing because it cannot act. The reading itself, reward for obedience and punishment for "sin" before a god who "demands", is II:19–21's chain from <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref>: the creditor grown to a god, the debt paid by obedience, and the "moral world-order" of §26 is II:21's moralization, the debt "pushed back into the bad conscience", stated at the scale of a nation. And "cause and effect stood on its head" is the third of *Twilight*'s Four Great Errors, the retrofitted cause, which the fifth section below takes up.

Stage 5 is III:15 before the Third Treatise's sufferer exists: the priest as the one channel to the god, sin as what needs him, redemption as submission. What §§25–26 add to the treatises is the sequence, misfortune before reinterpretation, the god retained by being changed, which is a claim about history rather than about a mechanism.

<Remark title="§§25–26 reconstructed">
*P1 (a type-fact).* A nation in power affirms itself in its god: its god is "the expression of their consciousness of power" (§25).

*P2 (a record).* Israel lost its power, "anarchy within, the Assyrian from without", and the god could no longer deliver what he had stood for (§25).

*P3 (I:7, II:19–21).* A priestly type, unable to restore the power, retained the god by reinterpreting him: fortune as reward, misfortune as punishment for sin, a "moral world-order" in place of natural causality (§25).

*P4 (I:7 on history).* The same type rewrote the nation's past in the terms of P3, the great epoch as decay and the Exile as punishment (§26).

*P5 (III:15 in embryo).* The reinterpretation was fixed by a "revelation", a sacred book, which made the priest the sole channel to the god and sin the name for disobeying him (§26).

*C.* Israel's God was denaturalized: morality became "the antithesis of life", and Christianity is the conclusion of that inversion (§24, §27).

*The premise not stated, and the record not given.* P3 and P4 are the mechanism of I:7 with the caste's impotence transferred to a defeated nation, and II:19–21's debt to a god run forward as reward and punishment. Nothing in the text dates them. "The documentation of which lies before us in a good part of the Bible" (§26) is the whole citation: the Bible is read as a priestly redaction and then cited as the evidence that priests redacted it. That reading had a scholarly base in 1888, Julius Wellhausen's *Prolegomena zur Geschichte Israels* (1878; second edition 1883), whose thesis is that the priestly law of the Pentateuch is post-exilic and was projected back onto Moses; Nietzsche's five stages follow that shape, and the text names neither the thesis nor its author. What would make P3 and P4 a "grey" history is exactly what Wellhausen's book is, a source-critical argument over datable strata; what §§25–26 give is the conclusion of such an argument stated as a psychology.
</Remark>

The grade, in the three columns: mechanism, I:7 and II:19–21, named by the text; record, none dated, the one document cited being the redaction itself; verdict, "falsification", "the most shameful act of historical falsification", which presupposes that the "natural" relationship of stage 1 is the standard, a premise the sixth section below names. On the naturalist reading the stretch renders as it should: a type (the priest), a circumstance (defeat), and a valuation explained by both. What the reading cannot supply is what the Preface demanded and the earlier lessons found the treatises supplying in part, the document that shows the mechanism at work.

<KeyFact>
The record column for §§25–26 is empty where it need not have been: the source-critical dating of the priestly strata that would fill it had been in print for ten years (Wellhausen, 1878), and the text cites neither it nor any date. That fixes the grade at "mechanism by assertion" rather than "mechanism past the available evidence", and the scorecard keeps the two apart.
</KeyFact>

## §§27–35: the redeemer type (the Doctrine of Types applied)

The stretch on Jesus is where the book changes method, and it says so. §27 gives one portrait, §28 announces "for the first time … the problem of the psychology of the redeemer" and gives another, and Walter Kaufmann's chapter on the *Antichrist* reads the two as deliberately opposed: one "from the outside – a polemical attempt at reconstructing history", one "from the inside", of which only the second is Nietzsche's own estimate.

### The external portrait, and its withdrawal

§27 places the redeemer on the soil of §§25–26. Christianity "is the Jewish instinct once more – in other words the priestly instinct which can no longer endure the priest as a reality"; the revolt Jesus is "understood or misunderstood" to have led was "a revolt against 'the good and the just', against 'the saints of Israel', against the social hierarchy", and its leader, "this holy anarchist who roused up the lowly, the outcasts and 'sinners', the Chandala within Judaism", "was a political criminal, in so far as political criminals were possible in an absurdly unpolitical society". "This is what brought him to the Cross: the proof is the inscription on the Cross. He died for his guilt – all ground is lacking for the assertion, however often it is made, that he died for the guilt of others." The *Chandala* of the sentence are the outcastes beneath the four castes of the Sanskrit law-codes, whose provisions for them *Twilight*'s "Improvers of Mankind" §3 had quoted weeks earlier; in Nietzsche's usage the word names the lowest stratum of any order, and it returns at §45 and §56.

The next section withdraws the portrait as a psychology: "It is quite another question whether he was conscious of any such antithesis – whether he was not merely *felt* to be this antithesis." Kaufmann's reading is that §27 is "designed to give offense to Christian readers" and "does not represent Nietzsche's own estimate of Jesus". The inscription on the Cross, the one record §27 cites, is left standing as evidence of what the authorities took him for, not of what he was.

### The internal portrait: a type, not a biography

§28 sets aside the discipline that could have supplied a record. Nietzsche had read David Friedrich Strauss, whose *Das Leben Jesu* (1835) treated the Gospel narratives as myth, "when I was twenty years old: now I am too serious for that. What do I care for the contradictions of 'tradition'?" The stories of saints are "the most ambiguous literature in existence: to apply to them scientific procedures when no other records are extant seems to me wrong in principle". §29 then states what replaces the record: "What I am concerned with is the psychological type of the redeemer. For it could be contained in the Gospels in spite of the Gospels, however much mutilated and overloaded with foreign traits: as that of Francis of Assisi is contained in the legends about him in spite of the legends. *Not* the truth about what he did, what he said, how he really died: but the question whether his type is still conceivable at all."

Ernest Renan's *Vie de Jésus* (1863), the century's most read life of Jesus, had used "the two most inapplicable concepts possible in this case: the concept of the genius and the concept of the hero. But if anything is unevangelic it is the concept hero. Precisely the opposite of all contending, of all feeling oneself in struggle has here become instinct: the incapacity for resistance here becomes morality ('resist not evil!': the profoundest saying of the Gospel, its key in a certain sense)". The type-word follows.

<Quote source="A §29, eKGWB; trans. Hollingdale, with the three words restored">
<Original lang="de">
Mit der Strenge des Physiologen gesprochen, wäre hier ein ganz andres Wort eher noch am Platz: das Wort Idiot. Wir kennen einen Zustand krankhafter Reizbarkeit des Tastsinns, der dann vor jeder Berührung, vor jedem Anfassen eines festen Gegenstandes zurückschaudert.
</Original>

To speak with the precision of the physiologist a quite different word would rather be in place here: the word idiot. We recognize a condition of morbid susceptibility of the sense of touch which makes it shrink back in horror from every contact, every grasping of a firm object.
</Quote>

The word is the one <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the compilation lesson</Ref> ran as a philological case: cut from every printing between 1895 and 1954, read by Josef Hofmiller in 1931 as a symptom of the author's insanity, and shown by Kaufmann to be Dostoevsky's term for the type he drew as Prince Myshkin in *The Idiot* (1868–69), a physiology of non-resistance. Here it is a diagnosis of a type and not a term of abuse, and §31 names the source it needed: "one has to regret that no Dostoyevsky lived in the neighbourhood of this most interesting *décadent*".

### From one type-fact to the whole doctrine

§30 states the physiology as two facts and derives the doctrine from them. "Instinctive hatred of reality: consequence of an extreme capacity for suffering and irritation which no longer wants to be 'touched' at all because it feels every contact too deeply. Instinctive exclusion of all aversion, all enmity, all feeling for limitation and distancing: consequence of an extreme capacity for suffering and irritation which already feels all resisting, all need for resistance, as an unbearable displeasure … and knows blessedness (pleasure) only in no longer resisting anyone or anything, neither the evil nor the evil-doer – love as the sole, as the last possibility of life." "These are the two physiological realities upon which, out of which the doctrine of redemption has grown."

The doctrine is then read off as consequences. §32: "the 'glad tidings' are precisely that there are no more opposites"; the faith "does not prove itself … it is every moment its own miracle"; everything in it is "a sign-language, a semeiotic, an occasion for metaphors", and "denial is precisely what is totally impossible for him". §33: "in the entire psychology of the 'Gospel' the concept guilt and punishment is lacking; likewise the concept reward"; what distinguishes the Christian is a practice, "he is distinguished by a different mode of acting", "all consequences of one instinct", and "the life of the redeemer was nothing else than this practice – his death too was nothing else". The section's formula: "A new way of living, not a new belief" (*eine neue Praktik*, *nicht ein neuer Glaube*). §34: the "kingdom of Heaven" is "a condition of the heart – not something that comes 'upon the earth' or 'after death'". §35: the death "not to 'redeem mankind' but to demonstrate how one ought to live"; "what he bequeathed to mankind is his practice".

§31 says what to do with the Gospel material that does not fit: the type "has been preserved to us only in a very distorted form", coarsened by "the first disciples in particular", who "had to translate a being immersed entirely in symbols and incomprehensibilities into their own crudity", and enriched "with traits which become comprehensible only with reference to warfare and the aims of propaganda": "the prophet, the Messiah, the judge who is to come, the moral preacher, the miracle-worker", and the "aggressive fanatic, the mortal enemy of theologian and priest, which Renan has wickedly glorified". "All sectarians adjust their Master into an apologia of themselves."

<Remark title="§§29–33 reconstructed as an argument from the Doctrine of Types">
*P1 (the Doctrine of Types, from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>).* A doctrine a person holds and lives is explained by that person's psycho-physical constitution together with his circumstances.

*P2 (§§29–30).* The doctrine recoverable from the Gospels "in spite of the Gospels", non-resistance, no guilt, no distance, blessedness as a present state, is the "ultimate logic" of one constitution: a morbid oversensitivity to contact that feels resistance itself as unbearable.

*P3 (§31).* The Gospel traits incompatible with that constitution, the judge, the Messiah, the fanatic, the Second Coming, are explained by the needs of the first community after the death, and so are accretions.

*C.* The historical redeemer was that type, and his teaching was that practice and nothing else: "a new way of living, not a new belief".

*Validity.* From P1 and P2 it follows that *if* the doctrine of P2 was the redeemer's, a type of that kind explains it. C needs more: that the doctrine of P2 *was* his, which is what P3 secures by discounting the contrary evidence. P3's criterion for what is accretion is the constitution of P2, so the argument's evidence is filtered by its hypothesis. §31 concedes the alternative in its own voice, "the type, as a *décadence* type, could in fact have been of a peculiar multiplicity and contradictoriness: such a possibility cannot be entirely excluded", and rejects it on the ground that then "the tradition would have to have been remarkably faithful and objective: and we have reasons for assuming the opposite", which is P3 again.

*The premise not stated.* That one doctrine expresses one type. §30 itself names Epicureanism as "closest related" to the doctrine of redemption, from "a thoroughly morbid basis" with "a considerable addition of Greek vitality", and §§20–23 give Buddhism as a second *décadence* religion with a different physiology; the same doctrine, on Nietzsche's own account, has arisen from more than one constitution. The inference from a doctrine to *the* type behind it is therefore not unique, and nothing in the stretch makes it so. This is a type-fact doing the work the protocol lesson's classification predicted, and doing it without an independent measurement of the type.
</Remark>

The grade: mechanism, the Doctrine of Types, applied exactly as <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref> found *Twilight* applying it to Cornaro's diet, a professed doctrine read as the expression of a constitution; record, the Gospels, which §28 declares unusable for "what he did, what he said, how he really died" and §44 will call "evidence of the already irresistible corruption within the first community"; verdict, none yet, the stretch being the one place in the book Tanner finds the tone "warmer and even ecstatic".

Kaufmann's summary is the fair one: the conception "does not recommend itself on purely historical grounds", and "Nietzsche was, of course, aware of the discrepancies between his picture and that found in the Gospels; but he thought that any differences could be explained in terms of the psychology of the disciples". Tanner adds the point that applies to every life of Jesus, Renan's included: "any attempt to extract an intelligible portrayal of him from the gospels is bound to be selective. What takes one by surprise is the nature of Nietzsche's selectivity."

<KeyFact>
The redeemer stretch is a type-hypothesis whose only record is a text the same book calls falsified, and whose criterion for separating original from accretion is the hypothesis itself; what survives as checkable is the two-strata reading of the Gospels, which is a philologist's claim, and §28 refuses the philologist's tools.
</KeyFact>

## §§39–47: from a practice to a doctrine (III:15 and I:7 applied)

§39 is the hinge of the book, and the sentence the course treats as its keeper.

<Quote source="A §39, eKGWB; trans. Hollingdale">
<Original lang="de">
Ich kehre zurück, ich erzähle die echte Geschichte des Christenthums. — Das Wort schon „Christenthum" ist ein Missverständniss —, im Grunde gab es nur Einen Christen, und der starb am Kreuz. Das „Evangelium" starb am Kreuz. Was von diesem Augenblick an „Evangelium" heisst, war bereits der Gegensatz dessen, was er gelebt: eine „schlimme Botschaft", ein Dysangelium.
</Original>

To resume, I shall now relate the real history of Christianity. – The word 'Christianity' is already a misunderstanding – in reality there has been only one Christian, and he died on the Cross. The 'Evangel' died on the Cross. What was called 'Evangel' from this moment onwards was already the opposite of what he had lived: 'bad tidings', a dysangel.
</Quote>

"The real history" (*die echte Geschichte*) is the Preface's word, and what follows is a mechanism in four moves. The section continues: "only Christian practice, a life such as he who died on the Cross lived, is Christian"; "not a belief but a doing"; and then the general claim on which the whole stretch rests, that "states of consciousness, beliefs of any kind … are a matter of complete indifference and of the fifth rank compared with the value of the instincts", so that "'faith' has been at all times, with Luther for instance, only a cloak, a pretext, a screen, behind which the instincts played their game". "In fact there have been no Christians at all."

### The four moves: death, blame, sacrifice, Paul

**§40, the death and the question.** "It was only the death, this unexpected shameful death, only the Cross, which was in general reserved for the *canaille* alone – it was only this terrible paradox which brought the disciples face to face with the real enigma: 'Who was that? What was that?'" "Here everything had to be necessary, meaningful, reasonable … a disciple's love knows nothing of chance. Only now did the chasm open up: 'Who killed him? who was his natural enemy?' – this question came like a flash of lightning. Answer: ruling Judaism, its upper class." "Precisely the most unevangelic of feelings, revengefulness, again came uppermost"; one "required 'retribution', 'judgement'"; the kingdom of God became "a last act, as a promise". And the exaltation: the disciples' revenge "consisted in exalting Jesus in an extravagant fashion, in severing him from themselves: just as the Jews, in revenge on their enemies, had previously separated their God from themselves and raised him on high. The one God and the one Son of God: both products of *ressentiment*".

**§41, the sacrifice.** "'How could God have permitted that?' For this question the deranged reason of the little community found a downright terrifyingly absurd answer: God gave his Son for the forgiveness of sins, as a sacrifice. All at once it was all over with the Gospel! The guilt sacrifice, and that in its most repulsive, barbaric form, the sacrifice of the innocent man for the sins of the guilty!" "For Jesus had done away with the concept 'guilt' itself." Then, "step by step: the doctrine of a Judgement and a Second Coming, the doctrine of his death as a sacrificial death, the doctrine of the Resurrection", and Paul's rationalization, "'If Christ is not resurrected from the dead our faith is vain'" (1 Corinthians 15:14), with immortality taught "as a reward".

**§42, Paul.** "In Paul was embodied the antithetical type to the 'bringer of glad tidings', the genius of hatred (*das Genie im Hass*), of the vision of hatred, of the inexorable logic of hatred. What did this dysangelist not sacrifice to his hatred! The redeemer above all: he nailed him to his Cross." "Paul simply shifted the centre of gravity of that entire existence beyond this existence – in the lie of the 'resurrected' Jesus. In fact he could make no use at all of the redeemer's life – he needed the death on the Cross and something in addition." The motive is stated as an inference: "Paul willed the end, consequently he willed the means"; "His requirement was power; with Paul the priest again sought power – he could employ only those concepts, teachings, symbols with which one tyrannizes over masses, forms herds." The instrument: "the belief in immortality – that is to say the doctrine of 'judgement'".

### The map to the treatises

The stretch uses all three treatises, in the order the priest lesson's machine put them. The disciples' demand for a guilty party (§40) is III:15's premise, and their first answer, ruling Judaism, is I:10's *ressentiment*, a No said to something outside; the exaltation of Jesus "severing him from themselves" is I:7's revaluation run on one figure. The sacrifice reading (§41) is II:21 from the debt lesson, the maximal creditor paying the debt himself, here told as the community's answer to "how could God have permitted that?": the god who pays is invented at the moment the debt becomes unpayable. And Paul (§42) is the priest of I:7 and III:15–18, a type whose requirement is power and whose instruments are guilt, judgement and the herd; "with Paul the priest again sought power" is the text's own map.

Kaufmann's chapter adds the earlier and fuller treatment. *Daybreak* §68, "The first Christian" (*Der erste Christ*, 1881), reads Paul as a man unable to fulfil the Law, for whom faith in Christ became at once "escape" and "the perfect revenge" against the Law and those who could keep it, and Kaufmann pairs it with Luther's discovery of his inability "to become the perfect man of the clerical ideal in his monastery": "faith as a way out of one's inability 'to get rid of one's sins' and faith as a screen for fanatical hatred". That is I:7's psychology, an aristocrat of the spirit denied the deed, applied to one man twice, and it is the argument §42's "genius of hatred" compresses.

<Remark title="§§39–43 reconstructed">
*P1 (§§33, 39).* The redeemer's legacy was a practice, non-resistance and blessedness in this life, and not a belief.

*P2 (§40; III:15's premise).* The shameful death produced in the disciples the demand for a meaning and for someone to blame: "why has this happened?", "who killed him?"

*P3 (§40; I:10, I:7).* Their answer, ruling Judaism, put revenge, retribution and judgement into the type of the Master, and exalted him out of their own equality with him.

*P4 (§41; II:21).* The question "how could God have permitted that?" was answered by a guilt sacrifice, the innocent for the guilty, and by resurrection, judgement and immortality, which "juggled away" the present blessedness that had been the whole content of the Evangel.

*P5 (§42; I:7, III:15–18).* Paul, a priestly type seeking power, made that answer a doctrine, shifted life's centre of gravity beyond life, and used immortality and judgement as the means of forming herds.

*C.* What has been called Christianity is the opposite of the one Christian's practice: "a dysangel".

*Validity.* The chain is the machine's: an affect (I) at P2–P3, a debt to a god (II) at P4, a priest's instrument (III) at P5. Each step follows from the treatises' results *if* the type-attributions hold.

*The premises not stated.* P5's motive. "Paul willed the end, consequently he willed the means" is an inference from the doctrine's use to its author's intention, and the text's evidence for it is 1 Corinthians 15:14 and the claim that the Damascus vision was "a hallucination" Paul himself did not believe, for which no letter is read. P1 rests on the previous section's type-hypothesis. What the stretch does establish independently is P2's shape: that a movement's account of its founder's death changes what the founder is remembered as teaching is a claim about sects in general, and §31's "all sectarians adjust their Master into an apologia of themselves" is a hypothesis with cases outside Christianity.
</Remark>

### §45 read by the sourcing drill: the verses, Nietzsche's glosses, and what each gloss assumes

§45 is the one place in the stretch where the record is on the page, and the course's sourcing drill from the ressentiment lesson applies to it as it applied to Tertullian. The metadata first: the author is Nietzsche, in September 1888; the audience is "we Hyperboreans" of §1; the purpose is stated at §57, "the critic of Christianity cannot be spared the task of making Christianity contemptible". **Records.** Thirteen passages, cited by chapter and verse, quoted (in Hollingdale's edition) in the Authorized Version; every citation checks. **Argues.** That the ten Gospel sayings are accretions, "put into the mouth of their Master", and the three Pauline ones are the inversion in its author's own voice; the ground for the first is §29's key, "resist not evil", and §33's "the concept guilt and punishment is lacking; likewise the concept reward". **Omits.** That the key is drawn from the same Gospels and selected by the same hypothesis; that no criterion independent of the type-reconstruction is offered for calling Mark 6:11 accretion and "resist not evil" original; and that the sarcasm carries the argument, since "How evangelic!" is an inference whose premise is the previous section.

**Use.** The provenance fixes what §45 can establish. It establishes that the canonical Gospels contain sayings of judgement, reward and retribution alongside sayings of non-resistance, which is a fact about the text anyone can check, and that 1 Corinthians states, in the first person, a valuation that ranks the weak above the mighty, which is the First Treatise's inversion with a signature. It cannot establish which stratum is original, because the only sorting tool on the page is the hypothesis being tested. Kaufmann's reading of the same section makes the narrow claim the verses do support: the resurrection "furnished the setting for a new doctrine of retribution", so that the faith "made it possible for men … to indulge their lust for revenge by hoping for the eventual torture and destruction of their persecutors"; that is I:15's Tertullian, and §45 supplies its earlier specimens.

§46 draws the moral and names the one figure the book respects: "one does well to put gloves on when reading the New Testament"; "there is only one solitary figure one is obliged to respect? Pilate, the Roman governor", whose "What is truth?" (*Was ist Wahrheit?*) is "the only expression which possesses value – which is its criticism, its annihilation even". The line is the will to truth of <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the previous lesson</Ref> put in a Roman's mouth against "an impudent misuse of the word 'truth'", and §47 closes the stretch with the book's formula, *Deus, qualem Paulus creavit, dei negatio*: God as Paul created him is the negation of God.

## §15 and §§48–49: imaginary causes at the scale of a religion

### §15: the third and fourth errors in a religion's vocabulary

The engine under the five stages and under Paul's doctrine is named earlier in the book, and it is *Twilight*'s. §15: "In Christianity neither morality nor religion come into contact with reality at any point. Nothing but imaginary causes ('God', 'soul', 'ego', 'spirit', 'free will' – or 'unfree will'): nothing but imaginary effects ('sin', 'redemption', 'grace', 'punishment', 'forgiveness of sins')." There follows "an imaginary psychology (nothing but self-misunderstandings, interpretations of pleasant or unpleasant general feelings, for example the condition of the *nervus sympathicus*, with the aid of the sign-language of religio-moral idiosyncrasy – 'repentance', 'sting of conscience', 'temptation by the Devil', 'the proximity of God')", and the diagnosis: "Who alone has reason to lie himself out of actuality? He who suffers from it."

<Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">The doer lesson</Ref> reconstructed the third of *Twilight*'s Four Great Errors with its case, the sleeper who hears a distant cannon-shot and wakes with a story already composed in which the shot is the last event: a felt state comes first, a cause is supplied after and experienced before. §15 is that error stated for a religion: a general feeling, pleasant or unpleasant, is the state; "sin", "grace", "the proximity of God" are the causes foisted on it. The fourth error, free will invented so that punishment can be deserved, is §26's "sin" as the priest's lever. And §25's sentence on the moral world-order, "when one has banished natural causality from the world by means of reward and punishment, one then requires an anti-natural causality", is the mechanism of the five stages stated as an error of reasoning rather than as a priest's act.

### §§48–49: Genesis read as the priest's fear of science

§§48–49 run the same engine on the first chapters of Genesis, and the passage is the method at its most fanciful, told here in one paragraph. "This priest's-book begins, as is only proper, with the priest's great inner difficulty: he has only one great danger, consequently 'God' has only one great danger." The old God is bored, invents man, then animals, then woman; "'Consequently, science too comes into the world through her'"; "science is the first sin, the germ of all sins, original sin"; expulsion, distress, war and the flood are "nothing but expedients in the struggle against science". §49 states the conclusion without the fable: "the concept of guilt and punishment, the entire 'moral world-order', was invented in opposition to science – in opposition to the detaching of man from the priest"; "the priest rules through the invention of sin".

### The grade

The grade, in the three columns. Mechanism: the third and fourth great errors, argued in *Twilight* with a case (the dream) that can be checked against anyone's experience, and with the direction-prediction the doer lesson found tested a century later. Record: none. The Genesis reading is an allegory of the text's author's fear, and it reads the text without a word on when it was written or by whom, which is the question Wellhausen's book was about. Verdict: "the greatest crime against humanity" (§49), on the bridge the next section names. The mechanism is general and defensible; the application to a particular text is by fiat, and the fable of §48 does not pretend otherwise.

## The is–ought slides: the missing premise drawn three times

The verdict column has been left half-filled at every step so far, the verdict named and its premise not, and this section fills it. §§2, 7 and 62 each move from a physiological description to a value verdict with no premise between them, the slide from "this proceeds from weakness" to "this is bad" that the book makes on nearly every page; the three sites are the ones that carry the book's weight, and the missing premise is the same at each.

Was ist gut? — Alles, was das Gefühl der Macht, den Willen zur Macht, die Macht selbst im Menschen erhöht. Was ist schlecht? — Alles, was aus der Schwäche stammt. Was ist Glück? — Das Gefühl davon, dass die Macht wächst, dass ein Widerstand überwunden wird.

### §2: a definition where an argument would be

The three questions are answered by definitions, and a definition does not slide, because it does not argue: it stipulates. The slide is in the definition's claim to be the right one. Behind it stands a descriptive premise stated at §6, "I consider life itself instinct for growth, for continuance, for accumulation of forces, for power: where the will to power is lacking there is decline", and the step from "life seeks power" to "good is what heightens power" is the step from a fact about what a kind of thing does to a standard for what it should have. The bridge, unstated, is that life-enhancement is the measure of value, and <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the will-to-truth lesson</Ref> found the same bridge in BGE 4's standard, "how far the judgment promotes and preserves life, how well it preserves, and perhaps even cultivates, the type", where it was also asserted. §2's "feeling of power" is the nearest the published works come to stating it, and it states it as a definition, which is why <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref> calls this "the definitional use of the doctrine".

### §7: pity described, then condemned

§7 opens with physiology. Pity "stands in antithesis to the tonic emotions which enhance the energy of the feeling of life: it has a depressive effect. One loses force when one pities." Then a second descriptive claim in borrowed vocabulary: "Pity on the whole thwarts the law of evolution, which is the law of selection. It preserves what is ripe for destruction; it defends life's disinherited and condemned." The verdict: "pity is practical nihilism", "nothing in our unhealthy modernity is more unhealthy than Christian pity". The bridge is the same as §2's, what preserves and enhances life is good, and the descriptive premises are of two kinds: the first, that pitying depresses the one who pities, is a claim a physiologist could test; the second, "the law of selection", is Darwin's vocabulary in the mouth of a writer who rejects Darwin's conclusion a page earlier (§4, "progress is merely a modern idea, that is to say a false idea") and in BGE 13 ("self-preservation is only one of the indirect and most frequent consequences" of the will to power). The premise is borrowed for the verdict and not held.

### §62: the curse

<Quote source="A §62, trans. Hollingdale">
I condemn Christianity, I bring against the Christian Church the most terrible charge any prosecutor has ever uttered. To me it is the extremest thinkable form of corruption, it has had the will to the ultimate corruption conceivably possible. The Christian Church has left nothing untouched by its depravity, it has made of every value a disvalue, of every truth a lie, of every kind of integrity a vileness of soul. … To abolish any state of distress whatever has been profoundly inexpedient to it: it has lived on states of distress, it has created states of distress in order to eternalize itself. … I call it the one immortal blemish of mankind.

The section's descriptive premises are the book's history: that the Church lived on and manufactured distress (§§26, 49), that "equality of souls before God" became "revolution, modern idea and the principle of the decline of the entire social order" (§43). The verdict is the curse the subtitle promised. Two bridges are missing, not one: the standard of §2, and a weighing, since §62 lists the Church's "'humanitarian' blessings" and dismisses them without saying what the higher type's loss is measured against. The course quotes the section and does not paraphrase it: its content as an argument is the three premises above and the two absent bridges, and its content as a curse is not the course's to restate.

### A §2's imperative read two ways, and the sentence that decides

§2 ends with the sentence that has done most to fix the book's reputation: "The weak and ill-constituted shall perish: first principle of our philanthropy. And one shall help them to do so." The correct claim first: the sentence states a *principle of value* whose object, to whom the philanthropy is directed, is not fixed by the sentence, and the two readings that fix it differently are decided, as far as they are decided, by §7.

The misreading, stated once: *A §2 announces a breeding programme, a policy of eliminating the weak to improve the stock of the strong.* Bernard Reginster calls this "the most common interpretation": the weak corrupt the strong by teaching pity, so the philanthropy is toward the strong or toward mankind, and removing the weak serves "the highest power and splendor actually possible to the type man" (GM Preface §6). Reginster's objection is that the premise does not reach the conclusion: that the weak endanger the strong licenses, at most, "segregation of the weak from the strong", which Nietzsche himself sometimes prescribes, and not their destruction.

The replacement reading is Reginster's. The philanthropy "is directed instead to the weak themselves. It is a matter of philanthropy to help them perish because no life can be worth living for them, even by their own lights": a life-negating valuation ends in nihilistic despair, and the ethics of power, adopted by the constitutionally weak, ends in "relentless frustration, and breed[s] more despair". So "Nietzsche's principle of philanthropy advocates not eugenics but euthanasia".

The discriminating sentence is §7's close: "To be physician here, to be inexorable here, to wield the knife here – that pertains to us, that is our kind of philanthropy." A physician's object is the patient, which is the second reading's direction; and the book's other use of "breed" tells against the first. §3 asks "what type of human being one ought to breed (*züchten*), ought to will, as more valuable", and says at once that the higher type "has existed often enough already: but as a lucky accident, as an exception, never as willed", while "the reverse type has been willed, bred, achieved: the domestic animal, the herd animal, the sick animal man – the Christian". In *Twilight*'s "Improvers of Mankind" §§1–2 "breeding" and "taming" are the two things every morality has meant by "improvement", the Church's included; the word names what a valuation does to the people who hold it, not a biological programme peculiar to its author.

What the refutation does not remove is what Reginster concedes in the same pages, and the course records it as the residue. "Even so, however, the view continues to disturb": "his advocacy of euthanasia does sometimes appear to serve a troubling eugenic program (see, for example, TI, IX 36)"; "he often acts as if weakness is a matter of 'constitution', as if it is therefore 'incurable', and as if he simply knows when an individual is thus 'ill-constituted'"; and "when, for example, does a physiological state form an incurable constitutional defect?" The Doctrine of Types, which bounds a life without fixing it, is exactly what cannot answer that last question from the outside, and the sentence's residue is the doctrine's.

The defence of the bridge is not this lesson's. That life-enhancement can be a standard at all is the question of <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the normativity lesson</Ref>; that "feeling of power" names something a psychology can measure is <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref>'s, where A §2's definition is read beside BGE 36 and Reginster's three conditions. Here the bridge is named, drawn three times, and left.

## §§56–57 and §61: the method past its evidence

Two passages near the end show what the machinery produces when it is run with no record and no mechanism. They are not applications that failed; they are verdicts the application never entered, and the scorecard keeps that distinction: these rows bound the book, not the method.

### Manu against Corinthians: two holy lies compared by their effect on the body

§56 begins with the criterion: "Ultimately the point is to what end a lie is told. That 'holy' ends are lacking in Christianity is my objection to its means." The *Law-Book of Manu*, the Sanskrit code of caste duties, is "an incomparably spiritual and superior work, so much as to name which in the same breath as the Bible would be a sin against the spirit": "the means by which the noble orders, the philosophers and the warriors, keep the mob under control; noble values everywhere". The comparison is made on one topic. "All the things upon which Christianity vents its abysmal vulgarity, procreation for example, woman, marriage, are here treated seriously, with reverence, with love and trust. How can one actually put into the hands of women and children a book containing the low-minded saying: 'To avoid fornication let every man have his own wife … for it is better to marry than burn'?" (1 Corinthians 7:2, 9). Against it: "'A woman's mouth' – it says in one place – 'a girl's breast, a child's prayer, the smoke of a sacrifice are always pure.'" §57 supplies the theory of the holy lie: a law-book "summarizes the experience, policy and experimental morality of long centuries", and secures it by "a two-fold wall", revelation and tradition, so that the way of life it fixes becomes "unconscious"; "the order of castes … is only the sanctioning of a natural order".

What the comparison can establish is that two codes sanctify different things: one treats sexuality as a concession, the other as pure. What it cannot establish is what §56 concludes from it, that one code's lie serves life and the other's poisons it, because that is a claim about *effects* and the passage supplies none.

The record that would bear on it exists, and Nietzsche had printed it weeks earlier. *Twilight*'s "Improvers of Mankind" §3 quotes Manu's provisions for the Chandala, the outcastes of §27's sentence: they may eat only garlic and onions, take water only "from the entrances to swamps and holes made by the feet of animals", may not wash, may not be helped in childbirth; "they shall have for clothing only rags from corpses, for utensils broken pots"; and the section names the harvest, "murderous epidemics, hideous venereal diseases", and says of the provisions that "perhaps there is nothing which outrages our feelings more". §56 omits its own evidence. It compares the code's treatment of the castes' women with Paul's treatment of marriage and leaves out the code's treatment of the bodies beneath the castes, which is the one effect on the body the author had documented. The Manu Nietzsche read was Louis Jacolliot's French compilation *Les législateurs religieux* of 1876, a popularization whose text and provenance Indologists have since found unreliable, so the "record" was second-hand on both sides of the comparison.

### Cesare Borgia as Pope: a counterfactual staged as history

§61 reads the Renaissance as "the revaluation of Christian values, the attempt, undertaken with every expedient, with every instinct, with genius of every kind, to bring about the victory of the opposing values, the noble values", and stages its climax: "I behold a spectacle at once so meaningful and so strangely paradoxical it would have given all the gods of Olympus an opportunity for an immortal roar of laughter – Cesare Borgia as Pope…. Am I understood?… Very well, that would have been a victory of the only sort I desire today – : Christianity would thereby have been abolished!" Then Luther: "a German monk, Luther, went to Rome", saw "the corruption of the Papacy, while precisely the opposite was palpably obvious: the old corruption, the *peccatum originale*, Christianity no longer sat on the Papal throne! Life sat there instead!" "And Luther restored the Church: he attacked it."

The Borgia the sentence names is the condottiere (1475–1507) whom <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the ressentiment lesson</Ref> found Kaufmann reading as an ideogram for unsublimated passion, praised nowhere as a model. Here he is a counterfactual pope. The *Genealogy*'s method is a real history, and a real history has nothing to say about what would have followed from an event that did not occur; "Christianity would thereby have been abolished" is not a conclusion any genealogy can reach, and §61 does not argue it, it beholds it. Tanner's comment is the historian's: Nietzsche "seems to be far too retrospectively hopeful. When one thinks of what Christianity has survived, it seems unlikely that anyone or anything could abolish it." What survives of the section as an argument is smaller and real: that the Renaissance was a revaluation "in the very seat of Christianity" is a thesis with a literature, above all Jacob Burckhardt's *Die Cultur der Renaissance in Italien* (1860), the book by Nietzsche's senior colleague at Basel that gave the period its character as a period, and the section's reading of Luther as a priestly type who "thinks only of himself" is I:7 applied once more, with *Daybreak* §68's Paul as its model.

The grade: mechanism, I:7 on Luther and nothing on the counterfactual; record, none; verdict, the whole. The composition frame belongs here: the book was written in thirty days, its subtitle changed from a "Revaluation" to a "Curse", and §§56–62 are the curse, written last, with the machinery of §§24–47 behind them and no longer in use.

## The scorecard: what the application shows about the method

The module's results were the three treatises' mechanisms with their evidence and their missing premises. The artefact is Nietzsche's own deployment of them, and the departure from the idealisation is the same at every row.

**What the application uses.** I:7 and I:16 at §§24–26 (the priest's inversion, on a nation) and again at §42 and §61 (on Paul and Luther); II:19–21 at §§25–26 (the god as creditor, reward and punishment) and at §41 (the sacrifice); III:15 at §40 (the demand for someone to blame) and §§42–43 (the herd, immortality as instrument); the Four Great Errors at §15 and §§48–49; the Doctrine of Types at §§29–31, applied to a single historical person. The text names five of these itself, at §24, §42 and §45.

**Where the artefact departs from the idealisation.** The *Genealogy*'s form, as the earlier lessons found it, was a mechanism, a record graded as illustration or proof, and a nameable missing premise. *The Antichrist* keeps the first and the third and replaces the second with a psychology: the record at every row is either absent (§§25–26, §§48–49, §61), the mechanism's own output cited as its evidence (the Bible as forgery, the Gospels as accretion), or a set of verses that establish less than they are made to (§45). Where the treatises read etymologies, law codes and a church father, the *Antichrist* reads one book and calls it falsified before reading it. The motives, Paul's above all, are inferred from the doctrine's use, "Paul willed the end, consequently he willed the means", which is the third great error's own pattern, a cause supplied after the effect, and the text does not notice. And the verdicts of §§2, 7, 56 and 62 slide from "proceeds from weakness" to "bad" on a bridge the book states once as a definition.

**What this shows about the working hypothesis.** The naturalist reading renders every row of the scorecard that tells a history as a type-explanation, and the text confirms it row by row; on that count the hypothesis has its best evidence in the corpus that this is how Nietzsche meant the *Genealogy* to be used. What the reading finds in return is that a type-explanation without a record is an assertion with a mechanism attached, and that the treatise's own standard, Preface §7, is the one the application fails. The grade is not a dismissal. Three things in the book are checkable and partly hold: §45's verses are there and carry two strata; *Daybreak* §68's Paul is a hypothesis about conversion that has a literature; and the two-strata reading of the Gospels is what the philology §28 refused was in fact doing. The audit in <Ref lesson="naturalist-reading-verdict">the closing lesson</Ref> records the row for this module as: mechanism confirmed, evidence thinnest in the corpus, missing premise the same one at every verdict.

<Aside>
Kaufmann's appendix on the "suppressed manuscripts" settles what the printed text of *The Antichrist* is. Three restorations distinguish the editions since Karl Schlechta's of 1954–56 from the Archive's of 1895: the three words "the word idiot" at §29; a few lines at §35 in which the malefactor on the Cross says "That was verily a divine man, a child of God!" and the redeemer answers "If thou feelest this, thou art in Paradise", omitted, Kaufmann judges, because the exchange is not in the Gospels that way (Luke 23:40–43 gives different words, and the centurion, not the thief, calls him a son of God, Matthew 27:54); and the word "young" before "prince" at §38, cut because it pointed at Kaiser Wilhelm II. Kaufmann calls the reaction to the second deletion "as misguided as the deletion itself": an inexact Gospel quotation from memory, in a book finished in thirty days, is the kind of thing Peter Gast had authority to fix in proof, and "it is misleading to speak of 'suppression'". Erich Podach's 1961 edition added a one-page "Law Against Christianity" in seven propositions after §62, from a sheet found with the manuscript; Kaufmann's article argues the placement is not warranted by the manuscript, and Hollingdale's edition, the course's, omits it.
</Aside>

What is established. *The Antichrist* §§24–47 is the three treatises' machinery run on one case, and the text names the sources: I:7 and II:19–21 on Israel's God, the Doctrine of Types on the redeemer, III:15 with I:7 and II:21 on the disciples and Paul, the Four Great Errors as the engine. At each step the mechanism is legible and the record is not what Preface §7 demanded: no dated document for the five stages, the Gospels filtered by the hypothesis for the type, Paul's motive inferred from his doctrine's use, thirteen verses that show two strata and not their order. The verdicts of §§2, 7 and 62 run on one unstated bridge, life-enhancement as the standard, which BGE 4 asserts and this book defines; A §2's imperative is underdetermined between a breeding programme and Reginster's euthanasia-philanthropy, §7's physician decides toward the second, and a residue survives. §§56–57 and §61 are the verdict with the mechanism removed: a comparison that omits its author's own evidence, and a counterfactual no history can reach.

What stands open is the type. §§29–31 explain a doctrine by a constitution, "two physiological realities" with one dominant sensitivity, and the argument is causal only if a psychology says what a constitution is made of and how one sensitivity comes to rule the rest. BGE 12 reopens the soul as "mortal soul", "subject-multiplicity", "a society constructed out of drives and affects", and BGE 19 dissects willing as command among under-wills; <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the next lesson</Ref> begins there, and the Doctrine of Types, which this lesson applied to a single person with no record, is stated there in full with the evidence Knobe and Leiter bring for it.

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "*The Antichrist* proves that Christianity was invented by Paul." Distinguish the mechanism (III:15's redirection, I:7's inversion, argued in the *Genealogy*) from the historical claim (asserted at §42, with the New Testament as its only cited record); state what §45's verse readings do establish and what §42's "genius of hatred" does not; and name the step of the claim that fails.

<Solution>
**The two things run together.** (a) A mechanism: a movement whose founder dies shamefully will look for someone to blame (III:15's premise), will exalt the founder and put its own revenge into his mouth (I:7, I:10), and a priestly type will turn the result into an instrument of power over a herd (III:15–18). That is argued in the *Genealogy* as a general psychology, with the evidence and the missing premises the earlier lessons graded. (b) A history: that this is what happened between the Crucifixion and the letters of Paul, and that Paul did it knowingly, "willed the end, consequently … willed the means". (b) is not entailed by (a); (a) says what kind of thing could have happened, and (b) says it did, to named people, from a named motive.

**What §45 establishes.** That the canonical Gospels contain sayings of judgement, retribution and reward (Mark 6:11, 9:42, 9:47–8; Matthew 6:15; Luke 6:23) alongside the non-resistance sayings §29 takes as the key, and that 1 Corinthians 1:20 ff. states in the first person a valuation that ranks the weak and foolish above the wise and mighty. The verses are cited correctly and can be checked. What §45 cannot establish is which stratum is older, because the only criterion on the page for calling a saying accretion is the type-hypothesis of §§29–33, and that is what the history is supposed to confirm.

**What §42 does not establish.** Paul's motive. "The genius of hatred", "this hate-obsessed false-coiner", "what he himself did not believe was believed by the idiots among whom he cast his teaching": the text reads one verse (1 Corinthians 15:14) and infers from the doctrine's later use, priestly power over herds, to its author's intention. That inference is the third great error's own shape, a cause supplied after the effect, and no letter of Paul is read for the motive. *Daybreak* §68 gives the fuller hypothesis (inability to keep the Law, faith as escape and revenge), and it is a hypothesis about a conversion, not a proof.

**The step that fails.** The move from "the mechanism of the *Genealogy* fits the shape of early Christianity as the *Antichrist* tells it" to "the *Antichrist* proves that this is how early Christianity happened". A mechanism that fits a story the mechanism was used to write has fitted itself. What would repair the step is what Preface §7 demanded and §28 refused: the dating of the Gospels relative to Paul's letters, the sorting of strata by a philologist's criteria, a reading of the letters for what Paul says he believed. The claim as stated is false; the weaker claim, that the *Antichrist* offers a coherent hypothesis about how a practice became a doctrine, whose parts (two strata in the Gospels, a doctrine of retribution grounded on resurrection) are checkable, is true and is what Kaufmann's chapter defends.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Reconstruct §§29–33's redeemer type as an explicit argument from the Doctrine of Types, type-fact to doctrine, in numbered premises. Then derive, from the Doctrine's own terms, what evidence would confirm or disconfirm a type-hypothesis about a historical person known only through texts written by others; and state whether the Gospels, on Nietzsche's own reading of them at §§31 and 44 as falsified by the first community, can be that evidence.

<Solution>
**The argument.** *P1.* A person's values and doctrine are causally explained by his psycho-physical constitution together with his circumstances (the Doctrine of Types; BGE 231). *P2.* A constitution with a morbid oversensitivity to contact feels every resistance as unbearable and knows pleasure only in not resisting (§§29–30, "two physiological realities"). *P3.* Such a constitution, "translated into its ultimate logic", yields exactly the practice of non-resistance, no enmity, no guilt, no distance, and blessedness as a present state (§§29–30, 33). *P4.* That practice is recoverable from the Gospels once the traits that contradict it are set aside as the first community's accretions (§31). *C.* The redeemer had that constitution, and his teaching was that practice.

**What would confirm or disconfirm it.** The Doctrine's claim is causal, so its evidence for a person is of two kinds. (i) Independent evidence of the type-fact: for a living subject, physiology and behaviour outside the doctrine, which is what Knobe and Leiter's heritability and attitude–behaviour studies are for a population; for a historical person, testimony about conduct not selected by the doctrine, and testimony from witnesses who did not share the hypothesis. (ii) Evidence that the doctrine is *his*: texts whose stratum can be dated by criteria independent of what the hypothesis predicts (language, transmission, cross-attestation), so that "original" and "accretion" are sorted before the type is read off. The hypothesis would be disconfirmed if the sayings most securely attributable on independent grounds included judgement and retribution, or if the constitution of P2 could be shown, from other cases, to yield different doctrines (which §30's Epicurus and §§20–23's Buddhism already suggest).

**Whether the Gospels can be that evidence.** Not on Nietzsche's own account of them. §31 says the type "has been preserved to us only in a very distorted form", coarsened by the disciples and enriched for "propaganda"; §44 says the Gospels are "evidence of the already irresistible corruption within the first community". A source declared corrupt throughout can serve as evidence only if a criterion says which parts are corrupt, and the only criterion §§29–31 use is the type itself. So the Gospels, on that reading, supply neither (i) nor (ii): every trait that fits confirms and every trait that does not is filed under distortion. The one route by which they could be evidence, source criticism that dates strata on independent grounds, is the one §28 sets aside as "learned idling". The argument is therefore a type-hypothesis with the record removed by the author's own hand, which is the grade the scorecard gives it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

A comparison of two codes by their treatment of one topic, with the second code's treatment of its outcastes left out by the very author who had just documented it, establishes nothing about the value of either; it establishes that the verdict of §2 was reached first and the quotations chosen after.

§61: "Cesare Borgia as Pope…. Christianity would thereby have been abolished!" State what the *Genealogy*'s method, a real history of how a value came to be, can say about a counterfactual of this kind, and what §61 is doing instead. Then say whether any part of the section survives as an argument, and name it.

<Solution>
**What the method can say.** Nothing. A genealogy, as Preface §7 and II:12–13 define it, is a documented account of what a practice or value was, how it was reinterpreted, and by whom; its objects are "the actually ascertainable, what actually existed". A counterfactual is by construction not ascertainable and did not exist; there is no document of what a Borgia papacy would have done, and no mechanism in the treatises that outputs "would have been abolished" from any input. The most a genealogy could say about Cesare Borgia is what he was and what valuation he expressed, which is what the ressentiment lesson found Kaufmann saying (an ideogram for unsublimated passion) and what BGE 197 says (not to be called unhealthy).

**What §61 is doing.** Staging the verdict of §2 as history. "That would have been a victory of the only sort I desire today" says what the author values; "Christianity would thereby have been abolished" dresses that valuation as an outcome. The section's own verbs give it away: "I see in my mind's eye a possibility", "I behold a spectacle". It is a value-verdict with a scene attached, and Tanner's objection is the historian's: a religion that survived what Christianity survived is not shown to be abolishable by one pope's character.

**What survives.** Two things, both smaller than the section. First, the historical thesis that the Renaissance was a revaluation "in the very seat of Christianity", an attempt to "set the noble values on the throne": that is Burckhardt's picture of the Renaissance and Nietzsche's use of it, and it is a claim about what happened that could be argued from records. Second, the reading of Luther as a priestly type, "all the vindictive instincts of a failed priest in him", who "restored the Church: he attacked it": that is I:7's psychology applied once more, on the model of *Daybreak* §68's Paul, and it is at least a hypothesis about a documented life with letters and sermons to check it against. Neither survives as more than a hypothesis; the counterfactual survives as nothing.

<Exercise>
The "type" of §§29–31 is a constitution with one dominant sensitivity, an oversensitivity to contact, from which "two physiological realities" and then a whole practice are derived. Write what a psychology would have to say for "a type explains a doctrine" to be a *causal* claim: what the units of a constitution are, how one of them comes to dominate, and how a dominant one issues in a valuation. Then find the sentence in BGE 12 that reopens the soul as such an order, and say what it commits the next module to.

<Solution>
**What a psychology would have to say.** Three things. (i) *Units.* A constitution is not one fact but many: for §30's "extreme capacity for suffering and irritation" to produce both "instinctive hatred of reality" and "instinctive exclusion of all enmity", there must be several dispositions that the one sensitivity acts on, and they must be the kind of thing that can be ranked, since the sensitivity is said to override the "need for resistance" that would otherwise be present. Nietzsche's word for the units is *drives* (*Triebe*) and *affects*. (ii) *Dominance.* "Translate such a physiological habitus into its ultimate logic" (§29) presupposes that one drive can command the others so that their expression takes its form; a psychology has to say what command among drives is, whether it is a fact about strength alone or an order with a structure. (iii) *Valuation.* The step from a dominant drive to "resist not evil" as a morality, "the incapacity for resistance here becomes morality" (§29), requires that a drive's mode of discharge be experienced and stated as a value; a psychology has to say how a felt incapacity becomes a "thou shalt not", which is the same step the doer lesson found in *Twilight*'s "his virtue is the consequence of his happiness". That is (i) stated as a hypothesis: the soul is a society, its citizens are drives and affects, and a society is an order.

**What it commits the next module to.** Saying what the order is. If the society has only a physics, the strongest drive wins and (ii) is a fact about force, which is the reading on which a type explains a doctrine as a tomato seed explains a tomato; if the society has a government, a rank-order that is itself normative, then (iii) is a fact about that order and the naturalist reading has to say how an order of drives can be a "space of reasons". That is the epiphenomenalism dispute between Leiter and Clark and Dudrick on BGE 12 and 19, and <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the next lesson</Ref> begins there: the type this lesson watched being applied to one man with no record is, in Module 5, given its units, its politics and the evidence Knobe and Leiter bring for it.
</Solution>

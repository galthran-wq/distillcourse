The Third Treatise asks what ascetic ideals mean and answers that the same ideal is a different thing in an artist, a philosopher and a priest — and that the philosopher's version, presented first as healthy, turns out to be the priest's in disguise. This lesson reconstructs the first ten sections of the treatise as three arguments and one piece of construction: the type-relativity of an ideal's meaning (§1), the artist's borrowed ideal (§§2–5), the philosopher's ideal as the optimum of his type (§§6–8), and the reversal in §§9–10 that puts the philosopher inside the priest's form; the construction is what Maudemarie Clark calls a beginning "calculated to mislead".

The course's argument arrives here with two treatises reconstructed and one hole named twice. <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">The First Treatise</Ref> explained the inversion of values by ressentiment and could not explain its victory; <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the Second</Ref> produced a moralized guilt, self-cruelty read as deserved, and closed on II:24's admission that nobody administers it. The Third Treatise is where the administrator appears, but it does not begin with him. It begins with a question about meaning and a list of six answers, and the working hypothesis from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>, that values are explained by the type that needs them, is what turns the list into an argument. What this lesson adds to the spine is the treatise's first result: "the ascetic ideal" names a structure with no content of its own, whose meaning is what it does for its bearer, and the bearer the treatise pretends to exempt, the philosopher, is by §10 wearing the priest's form. <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">The priest lesson</Ref> takes §§11–22 and <Ref lesson="perspectivism">the perspectivism lesson</Ref> takes §12, which sits inside them.

The route: §1 as an outline with its conclusion at the end; Wagner and *Parsifal* as the artist's case, with the one sentence of Nietzsche's own history that the case needs; §6's three definitions of the beautiful and Heidegger's charge that the Kant under attack is Schopenhauer's; §§7–8's unmarried philosophers and the optimum; §§9–10's caterpillar; and Clark's reading of the whole as a trap, with what it does to a reader who stops at §8.

## §1: one ideal, six meanings, and the sentence the treatise is written to earn

<Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">The protocol lesson</Ref> dissected §1 as a specimen of the aphorism that builds to a formula: a question, a list of six items that serve as the treatise's table of contents, and a boxed conclusion, "it would rather will nothingness than *not* will", that §28 restates. What that dissection did not do is read the list as an argument, and it is one.

The six items are the same ideal and six different answers to "what does it mean". To artists it means "nothing, or too many things" (*Nichts oder zu Vielerlei*). To philosophers and scholars, "something like a nose and instinct for the most favourable preconditions of high spirituality". To women, one more charm, "a little *morbidezza* on fine flesh". To "the physiologically failed and disgruntled (the majority of mortals)", "an attempt to seem 'too good' for this world, a holy form of debauchery, their chief weapon in the struggle with slow pain and boredom". To priests, "the real priestly faith, their best instrument of power, also the 'supreme' licence for power". To saints, "a pretext for hibernation", "their rest in nothingness ('God')".

Read as data, the list is incoherent: the same three words, poverty, humility, chastity, are a support for the artist's confidence, a working condition for the philosopher, a weapon for the sick, and a tool for the priest, and the artist's "nothing" contradicts every other entry. Read as an argument, the incoherence is the premise.

<Remark title="GM III:1 reconstructed">
*P1.* What an ideal *means* (*bedeutet*) is what it does for the type that holds it: the question "what do ascetic ideals mean" is answered, in every one of the six cases, by naming a bearer and a use, never by naming a content.

*P2.* The ascetic ideal is held by types with opposite needs: a type that needs nothing from it (the artist), a type that needs a working condition (the philosopher), a type that needs a weapon against its own pain (the majority), a type that needs an instrument of rule (the priest).

*C.* "The ascetic ideal" names a structure, the valuation of poverty, humility and chastity, and not a content; it has no meaning of its own, only as many meanings as it has types of bearer, and a question about the ideal is a question about types.

*The premise not stated.* That the *same* practice can carry opposite uses for opposite types without ceasing to be one practice. That premise is II:12's rule, restated: a thing's present use is imposed by whatever will masters it, so use and thing are "toto caelo" distinct (<Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>). §1 applies the rule not to punishment, II:13's example, but to an ideal.
</Remark>

The reconstruction is the Doctrine of Types applied to an ideal instead of a value, and the two applications differ in one respect that matters later. Knobe and Leiter's doctrine, restated from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>, holds that type-facts explain what a person values; §1 holds that type-facts explain what one and the same valuation *is* for the person who holds it. A valuation's meaning is then no more a property of the valuation than a punishment's purpose is a property of the punishment.

### The one thing common to all six

The list has a second half, and it is not part of the list. "That the ascetic ideal has meant so much to man at all expresses the basic fact of the human will, its *horror vacui*: it needs a goal, and it would rather will nothingness than *not* will." The sentence says what the six bearers share, which is not the ideal's meaning but their need for a meaning, any meaning. The ideal is the goal a will takes when no other is available; the six types differ in what they do with the goal and agree in having had to take one.

§1 states the sentence and does not argue it. The twenty-six sections between §1 and §28 are the argument, and §28 returns to the sentence with the priest's flock as its evidence: the sick majority, whose suffering had no answer to the question "what for?", took the ideal because "any meaning is better than no meaning at all", at the price of reading all suffering "under the perspective of guilt". The formula is therefore a conclusion about the sick, and only about the sick, and the treatise's first ten sections are about the two types it does not concern. That is the first sign that the opening is not what it looks like.

<KeyFact>
Everything a reader concludes about "the ascetic ideal" from §§2–10 is a conclusion about a structure with no content of its own: what those sections establish about the artist's and the philosopher's use of it carries nothing over to the priest's, the use §§11–22 diagnose and §28's formula is about.
</KeyFact>

## §§2–5: the artist's case is a borrowed ideal

The first spoke the treatise takes up is the one whose answer was "nothing", and §2 says why the case is worth four sections: it is "an individual case in regard to which I have often enough been consulted". Richard Wagner (1813–1883), the composer whose festival theatre at Bayreuth opened in 1876, closed his work with *Parsifal* (1882), an opera in which a Grail knighthood is redeemed by a "pure fool" who resists a seduction, and whose final scene is a Eucharist. §2 asks what it means "when an artist like Richard Wagner in his old age pays homage to chastity", and answers the question about the person before it answers the question about the ideal: Wagner had always praised chastity in some sense, "but only at the very end in an *ascetic* sense", and an artist who "leaps into his opposite" has done something a psychologist has to explain.

Nietzsche's own history with the case is one sentence of before-image. In October 1873 he wrote, for Wagner's patrons' society, the *Mahnruf an die Deutschen*, an appeal to the Germans to fund the Bayreuth building, which calls Wagner "the great, brave, unbending and unstoppable fighter" and the festival "the most noble hope" of the German spirit; in 1888 he published *The Case of Wagner*. §2's "I have often enough been consulted" is written by the author of both.

### §§2–3: the work as an author's need

§2 first takes the ideal's own reading of *Parsifal* off the table. There is "no necessary antithesis between chastity and sensuality; every good marriage, every real love affair, is beyond this antithesis", and Wagner's projected comedy on *Luther's Wedding* would have praised both. Where the antithesis does exist it "need not be a tragic antithesis": Goethe and Hafiz saw in the tension "one more charm of life". The antithesis becomes tragic in one case only, when "failed swine are brought to worship chastity", and see in it "the antithesis to the failed swine". That is the case Wagner "undeniably wanted to set to music and put on the stage at the end of his life", and §2 ends on the question the treatise will answer for the priest: "what did the swine matter to him, and what do they matter to us?"

§3 tries the charitable reading and rejects it. *Parsifal* could have been meant "as a satyr play", the tragedian's farewell laugh at tragedy and at "the crude phase in the unnaturalness of the ascetic ideal"; "one would wish it were so". Taken seriously, it is "a curse on the senses and the spirit in one breath of hatred", "an apostasy and reversion to sickly Christian and obscurantist ideals", and, the sentence the argument needs, "a self-negation, a self-cancelling on the part of an artist who till then had devoted the whole strength of his will to the opposite". Ludwig Feuerbach (1804–1872), whose *Essence of Christianity* (1841) read God as a projection of human nature, had supplied Wagner's watchword of the 1840s, "healthy sensuality"; the late writings "in a hundred places" betray "a will to preach reversion, conversion, denial, Christianity, medievalism".

### §§4–5: the artist is a valet

§4 separates the artist from the work, and the separation carries the inference. The artist is "the precondition of the work, the womb, the soil, in certain cases the dung and manure on which and out of which it grows", and insight into a work's origin is "a matter for psychologists and vivisectors", never for those who enjoy it. Homer was not Achilles and Goethe was not Faust; an artist "is to all eternity separated from the 'real'", and Wagner's late attempt to "have real existence", to be the thing he had represented, is "the typical velleity of the artist".

§5 then answers the section's question for the whole type, and the answer is §1's "nothing": "in the case of an artist we are beginning to understand what they mean: *nothing at all*, or so much that it is as good as nothing at all." Artists "have at all times been the valets (*Kammerdiener*) of some morality, philosophy or religion"; "they always need a rampart, a support, an already constituted authority; artists never stand by themselves, standing alone is contrary to their deepest instincts". Wagner "took the philosopher Schopenhauer as his covering man in front, as his rampart", and would not have "had the courage for an ascetic ideal" without "Schopenhauer's authority, which dominated Europe in the seventies".

Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) published *The World as Will and Representation* in 1819; its third book makes music the direct "language of the will itself" and its fourth makes chastity the first step in the will's denial. Wagner read it in 1854, "as one knows, persuaded by a poet, Herwegh", and §5 records the effect as "a complete theoretical contradiction between his earlier and his later aesthetic faith": in *Opera and Drama* (1851) music had been "a means, a medium, a 'woman' that needed an end, a man, in order to thrive", the drama; in the writings from 1870 music was sovereign, "not a copy of the phenomenal world like the other arts but the language of the will itself", and the musician "an oracle, a priest, indeed more than a priest, a kind of mouthpiece of the 'in-itself' of things, a telephone from the beyond". "What wonder that one day he finally spoke *ascetic ideals*."

<Remark title="GM III:2–5 reconstructed">
*P1.* An artist's valuations are borrowed: the type needs "an already constituted authority" and does not stand alone (§5).

*P2.* Wagner's late homage to chastity in an ascetic sense was borrowed from Schopenhauer's philosophy, by way of Schopenhauer's doctrine of music, which first made the musician a metaphysician and then let him "speak ascetic ideals" (§5).

*P3.* A work is evidence about its author's needs and about nothing else: the artist is "the womb, the soil" of the work, and the work no more states a truth about its subject than Goethe was Faust (§4).

*C.* *Parsifal* means nothing about the value of asceticism; it means what its author needed, a rampart, and the ideal's meaning for artists is therefore "nothing at all" (§5), or the meaning of whatever authority the artist leaned on, which comes to the same.

*The premise not stated.* That the need for a rampart is a type-fact and not a biography: §5 asserts it "at all times", for artists as such, and supports it with one case. The generalization is the weak joint, and III:5 does not pretend otherwise; it turns from the case it has to "the more serious question" of the type that lent the rampart.
</Remark>

From a section that opened by asking what the ideal means, the expected next move is a verdict on *Parsifal*. The section does not deliver one. It concludes that the artist's ideal is not his own, passes the question to the philosopher whose ideal it was, and so treats the artist's case as a pointer to a type with "the courage to be himself": Schopenhauer, "a really independent spirit, a man and knight with a glance of bronze". The next four sections answer what it means when *that* type pays homage to the ideal.

## §6: three definitions of the beautiful, and whose Kant is under attack

The treatise reaches Schopenhauer through his aesthetics, and §6 is a three-way comparison, Kant, Schopenhauer and Stendhal on the one phrase "without interest", with Nietzsche's verdict at the end.

**Kant.** The *Critique of Judgment* (1790), §§2–5, defines the beautiful by the delight it gives: "the delight which determines the judgement of taste is independent of all interest" (§2), where an interest is "the delight we connect with the representation of the *existence* of an object". Taste is the capacity to judge "by means of a delight or aversion *apart from any interest*; the object of such a delight is called *beautiful*" (§5). Nietzsche quotes the formula as "that is beautiful which pleases without interest" and adds that Kant, "like all philosophers", considered art "from the standpoint of the spectator" and so "imported the spectator himself into the concept 'beautiful'"; he brackets whether Kant's honouring of "impersonality and universality" in the beautiful was "a complete mistake" and leaves it there.

**Schopenhauer.** *The World as Will and Representation* I, §38, describes aesthetic contemplation as the one state in which the will is stilled: "we are for that moment freed from the vile pressure of the will, we celebrate the Sabbath of the will's hard labour, the wheel of Ixion stands still", the passage §6 quotes. Schopenhauer took over Kant's "without interest" and read it, in Nietzsche's words, "in the most personal way, out of an experience which in his case must have been one of the most regular": aesthetic contemplation "counteracts sexual interest, like lupulin and camphor", and the escape from "the will" is its whole advantage.

**Stendhal.** Against both, §6 sets one sentence of "a real 'spectator' and 'artist'", the French novelist Stendhal (1783–1842): *la beauté n'est que la promesse du bonheur*, beauty is only a promise of happiness (*De l'amour*, 1822). "Here at any rate the one thing Kant makes prominent in the aesthetic condition is repudiated and eliminated: *le désintéressement*." For Stendhal the essential effect of the beautiful is "the excitement of the will ('the interest') by beauty".

### Nietzsche's argument: the will-less state is a will's relief

§6's argument is not that Stendhal is right and Kant wrong; it is a reinterpretation of Schopenhauer's report. "Granted that Schopenhauer was a hundred times right for himself personally, what does that contribute to insight into the nature of the beautiful?" Schopenhauer described "one effect of the beautiful, the calming of the will", and the question is whether the effect is normal. Stendhal, "an equally sensual but more happily constituted nature", reports the opposite effect; the disagreement between the two witnesses is settled by their constitutions, which is to say by type. And then the turn: Schopenhauer "is quite wrong in regarding himself as a Kantian on this point", because "the beautiful pleased *him* too by means of an interest, by means, in fact, of the strongest and most personal interest of all: that of the victim of torture who escapes from his torture". The will-less state is the relief of a will. §6 closes by cashing the result for the treatise's question: "what does it mean when a philosopher pays homage to the ascetic ideal? We get at least a first hint: he wants *to escape from a torture*."

<Remark title="GM III:6 reconstructed">
*P1.* Schopenhauer reports that the beautiful stills the will, and infers that will-lessness is the essence of the aesthetic state (WWR I §38).

*P2.* Stendhal, a spectator and artist of a different constitution, reports that the beautiful arouses the will.

*P3.* Where two witnesses of different types report opposite effects, neither report is evidence about the object; each is evidence about the type (the premise from §1, applied to testimony).

*P4.* Schopenhauer's report has a cause his type supplies: a sexuality he "treated as a personal enemy" (§7), against which the aesthetic state acted as a sedative.

*C.* The will-lessness Schopenhauer found in the beautiful was an interest, the relief of a tortured will; and a philosopher's homage to the ascetic ideal is, on first hint, a wish to escape a torture.

*The argument-form.* A reported absence of will is reinterpreted as the presence of a will with a different object. This is the same operation the First Treatise performed on the lambs' "goodness" (<Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the ressentiment lesson</Ref>): a professed freedom from a drive read as that drive's disguise. The third exercise runs the form on Nietzsche's own side of §6.
</Remark>

### Heidegger's correction: the Kant attacked is Schopenhauer's

The reader who has read §§2–5 of the third *Critique* will have noticed that Kant's "without interest" excludes something narrower than Schopenhauer's will. Heidegger's lectures of 1936–37 (*Nietzsche* I, §15, "Kant's Doctrine of the Beautiful. Its Misinterpretation by Schopenhauer and Nietzsche") make the point as a charge. Kant's clause is "preparatory and path-breaking", a statement of what can *never* be the determining ground of the judgment "this is beautiful", namely an interest, wanting the thing "for oneself as a possession"; it is not a description of the state. The positive content Kant gives is *Gunst*, favour: "favour is the only free delight" (§5), and comportment toward the beautiful is "unconstrained favouring", in which "we must let what encounters us, purely as it is in itself, come before us in its own stature and worth". That, Heidegger writes, is not indifference but "the supreme effort of our essential nature". Schopenhauer read the clause as the will "put out of commission … sheer apathetic drift"; Nietzsche, who "in his definition of the beautiful thinks and judges by way of opposition and therefore of reversal", took Schopenhauer's Kant for Kant's. Heidegger's verdict on Nietzsche's notebook complaint that "since Kant all talk of art has been smudged by the concept 'devoid of interest'": "since Kant? … through Kant, no; since the Schopenhauerian misinterpretation of Kant, by all means yes."

The claim is checkable, and the reader has the text to check it: §2's definition of interest as delight bound to the object's *existence*, and §5's "favour is the only free delight", are the two sentences the correction turns on. What the correction does to §6 is precise. The reinterpretation of Schopenhauer stands untouched, since it is about Schopenhauer's report and Schopenhauer's text does say the will is stilled. The blow aimed past Schopenhauer at "Kant's famous definition" lands on the definition as Schopenhauer read it, and the only charge §6 makes against Kant in his own person is the spectator's standpoint, a charge Heidegger reads as itself resting on the misreading, since Kant's favouring is a doing. Nietzsche's own positive term, *Rausch* (rapture, intoxication), stated in *Twilight*'s "Expeditions" §8 as the physiological precondition without which "no art results", is on Heidegger's account the opposite not of Kant's disinterest but of Schopenhauer's apathy.

<KeyFact>
§6's argument reaches Schopenhauer and stops there: on Heidegger's checkable reading of *Critique of Judgment* §§2–5, Kant's "without interest" excludes possession from the ground of the judgment and names the state "favour", so the will-lessness Nietzsche unmasks was never Kant's, and the one refutation in this stretch of the treatise in which the misreader is Nietzsche is a refutation of an inherited reading, not of the text.
</KeyFact>

Kaufmann's distinction from a different chapter states what is at stake for Nietzsche in the outcome. Schopenhauer's escape from the will is what Kaufmann calls "castratism", the extirpation of a drive; §8 will end on the alternative, sensuality "not removed by the approach of the aesthetic state, as Schopenhauer believed, but merely transfigured", which is sublimation, the operation <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref> makes central. §6's hint, "to escape a torture", is a diagnosis of the first operation by a philosopher committed to the second.

## §§7–8: the unmarried philosophers and the optimum of a type

§6 ended with a hint about one philosopher. §7 generalizes it, and the generalization is the section where the treatise states its type-premise in full. Schopenhauer's case has "much that is typical in him"; "as long as there are philosophers on earth, and wherever there have been philosophers (from India to England, to take the opposite poles of philosophical talent), there unquestionably exists a peculiar philosophers' irritation at and rancour against sensuality", and "a peculiar philosophers' prejudice and affection in favour of the whole ascetic ideal". Both "belong to the type"; a philosopher who lacks them "is never anything but a 'pseudo'".

The state of affairs "must first be interpreted", and the interpretation is the sentence the naturalist reading of the whole treatise rests on: "every animal, *la bête philosophe* included, strives instinctively for an optimum of favourable conditions under which it can let its whole strength out and reach its maximum feeling of power; with equal instinctiveness, and with a fine nose 'higher than all reason', every animal abhors every kind of disturbance and hindrance that lies or could lie across its path to the optimum (it is not its path to *happiness* I am speaking of, but its path to power, to action, to the most powerful action, and in fact in many cases its path to unhappiness)."

### The seven and the Buddha's fetter

The evidence is a list. "The philosopher abhors *marriage*, together with what might persuade him to it, marriage as a hindrance and calamity on his path to the optimum. Which great philosophers have been married? Heraclitus, Plato, Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz, Kant, Schopenhauer: they were not; more, one cannot even imagine them married. A married philosopher belongs to *comedy*, that is my rule; and the exception, Socrates, the malicious Socrates, married *ironically*, precisely to prove this rule." Every philosopher would say what the Buddha said at the news of his son's birth: "Râhula has been born to me, a fetter has been forged for me"; and every free spirit has the hour the Buddha had, "narrow and cramped is life in the house, a place of impurity; freedom is in leaving the house", after which "he left the house".

### §8: three words, "not one iota of virtue"

§8 opens with the verdict the rest of the treatise will exploit: "these philosophers are by no means uncorrupted witnesses and judges of the value of the ascetic ideal. They think of *themselves*; what is 'the saint' to them?" What they think of is what is "most indispensable to them personally: freedom from compulsion, disturbance, noise, from business, duties, cares; a clear head; the dance, spring and flight of thoughts; good air, thin, clear, free, dry, as the air on the heights"; "peace in every cellar, all the dogs neatly chained". Then the three words: "we know what the three great shining words (*Prunkworte*) of the ascetic ideal are: poverty, humility, chastity: and now look closely at the life of all the great, fruitful, inventive spirits; you will always find all three again in them to a certain degree. Not for a moment, as goes without saying, as though they were their 'virtues' (what has this kind of man to do with virtues?) but as the most essential and natural conditions of their *best* existence, their *finest* fruitfulness."

The rest of §8 runs each word through the type. Poverty is the philosopher's "he who possesses is possessed", humility his shunning of "three brilliant and noisy things, fame, princes and women", and chastity a matter in which "there is not a trace of chastity, by reason of any ascetic scruple or hatred of the flesh, any more than it is chastity for an athlete or a jockey to abstain from women; it is rather the will of the dominant instinct, at any rate, during the period of their advanced philosophic pregnancy". "There is not one iota of 'virtue' in all this." The three renunciations are the conditions of a maximum of power for a type whose power is spiritual, and what looks from outside like self-denial is, from inside, the dominant instinct's ordering of the lesser ones: "the greater power absorbs the lesser."

<Remark title="GM III:7–8 reconstructed">
*P1.* Every type strives for the optimum conditions under which it can let its whole strength out and reach its maximum feeling of power, and abhors what obstructs the path to that optimum (§7).

*P2.* The philosopher's type has its strength in spirituality, and spirituality's optimum conditions are freedom from possessions, from public standing and from sexual expenditure: the three things marriage, fame and "the things of 'today'" take (§§7–8).

*P3.* The ascetic ideal's three words, poverty, humility and chastity, name exactly those three freedoms (§8).

*C.* The philosopher's affection for the ascetic ideal is the type's affection for its own optimum: what the ideal *means* for a philosopher is the set of conditions of his maximal power, "not truth", not virtue, and not a valuation of self-denial at all.

*The premise not stated.* P1 is the will to power as a psychology: that the end every type pursues is its maximum feeling of power, and that resistance is sought for the sake of the overcoming. §7 states it as "an accepted and indisputable fact" and argues for it nowhere in the *Genealogy*; <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref> reconstructs the argument BGE 36 gives and the smaller doctrine that survives it. Bernard Reginster's analytic statement of §§7–10 is that the philosopher's asceticism is *instrumental* self-denial, deprivation as a means to a further end, which he and Schopenhauer both distinguish from ascetic self-denial proper, deprivation as the end itself; on that distinction the philosopher of §8 is not an ascetic in the sense §1's question asks about, and §9 will say so.
</Remark>

<Aside>
The premise's non-European check. Kaufmann's chapter on power against pleasure raises the objection that Hindu and Buddhist ascesis, whose goal is Nirvana or union with Brahman, is the counterexample to any account of asceticism as power-seeking, and answers it from the comparative-religion literature: *tapas*, the Sanskrit word for ascesis, means "heat", and the powers sought by it were magical, the yogi "became dangerous to the gods", who "tremble, fearing for their power". Nietzsche's own §10 tells the same story about Vishvamitra, whose thousand years of self-martyrdom end in a new heaven, not in release. The Buddha's reply to the tempter Mara's offer of dominion, in Kaufmann's telling, is not a refusal of power but a refusal of *this* power in favour of "causing the ten thousand worlds to thunder"; on that reading §7's "from India to England" is a claim about one type, and the two poles of the claim are the two poles of the same evidence.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
§8's verdict that philosophers are "by no means uncorrupted witnesses and judges of the value of the ascetic ideal" is the premise §§9–10 and §§23–27 will turn on the philosopher himself: a type that benefits from an ideal cannot testify to its value, and the type that wrote the *Genealogy* is a philosopher.
</KeyFact>

## §§9–10: the caterpillar, and the reversal the reader was not warned of

From §§7–8 the expected next move is a contrast: the philosopher's asceticism, healthy and self-affirming, against the priest's, which §1 called an instrument of power over the sick. §9 opens as if to draw it, "a certain asceticism, a hard and cheerful renunciation, is one of the most favourable conditions of the highest spirituality", and then does the opposite. "A more serious historical investigation shows the bond between the ascetic ideal and philosophy to be still much tighter and stronger. One might say it was only on the *leading-strings* of this ideal that philosophy learnt to take its first steps on earth."

### §9: the philosopher's tendencies were once crimes

The historical claim rests on the philosopher's own virtues. "His tendency to doubt, to deny, to suspend judgment (to be 'ephectic'), to analyse, search, explore, dare, to compare and equalize, his will to neutrality and objectivity, his will to everything *sine ira et studio*": has it been grasped "that for the longest time all these went counter to the first demands of morality and conscience?" A philosopher who arrived at self-consciousness "had to feel himself the incarnate *nitimur in vetitum*", the striving for the forbidden, and guard himself against his own feelings. §9 then widens the claim to the rule stated in *Daybreak* §18, which it quotes: "all good things were once bad things; every original sin has become an original virtue", marriage, gentleness, submission to law, each "introduced by force, as a force, to which men submitted only with shame". The philosopher's tendencies are one more instance, and the instance sets up §10's question: how did a type whose every instinct was a crime survive long enough to become a type?

### §10: the priest's form was the condition of existing

§10 answers with a history of the contemplative man. "Contemplation first appeared on earth in disguise, in an ambiguous form, with an evil heart and often an anxious head." The "inactive, brooding, unwarlike" instincts of the contemplative drew suspicion, and "the only means against it was to arouse a definite *fear* of oneself. And the old Brahmins, for example, knew how to do this." The oldest philosophers "found in themselves all the value judgments turned against them", and their means of believing in their own revolution was "cruelty toward themselves, inventive self-mortification". The case §10 recalls is the legend of King Vishvamitra, a king of the Sanskrit epics who won a sage's standing by austerities and who, "as the result of a thousand years of self-martyrdom, reached such a consciousness of power and such a confidence in himself that he undertook to build a new heaven". "Whoever has built a 'new heaven' anywhere found the power to do it first in his own hell."

Then the formula, "compressed into a short form": "the philosophic spirit had always to disguise and cocoon itself (*sich verkleiden und verpuppen*) in one of the previously established types of the contemplative man, as priest, sorcerer, soothsayer, as a religious man in general, in order to be *possible* at all: the ascetic ideal served the philosopher for a long time as a form in which to appear, as a precondition of existence; he had to *represent* it in order to be able to be a philosopher, he had to *believe* in it in order to be able to represent it." The philosopher's "peculiar withdrawn attitude, world-denying, hostile to life, suspicious of the senses, freed from sensuality, which has been maintained down to the most recent times and has almost thereby come to count as the philosopher's attitude *par excellence*", is "the result of the emergency conditions under which philosophy came to be and endured at all"; "for the longest time philosophy would not have been possible on earth at all without an ascetic wrap and cloak, without an ascetic *self-misunderstanding*". And, "expressed plainly and palpably", the sentence that names the hinge: "the ascetic priest has taken the repulsive and sinister form of the caterpillar (*Raupenform*), beneath which and behind which alone philosophy could live and slink about."

<Remark title="GM III:9–10 reconstructed">
*P1.* The tendencies that define the philosopher, doubt, denial, suspension, analysis, neutrality, were for the longest time contrary to morality and conscience (§9).

*P2.* A type whose instincts are contrary to morality can exist only by being feared, and the contemplative types already feared were the priest, the sorcerer and the soothsayer (§10).

*P3.* To appear as one of those types the philosopher had to represent the ascetic ideal, and to represent it he had to believe in it (§10).

*C.* The philosopher's world-denying, life-hostile, senses-suspecting attitude is not the philosopher's own but the priest's form, an ascetic self-misunderstanding that was the precondition of the type's existence; and whether the type has yet left the form is a question §10 asks and does not answer.

*The premise not stated.* That a form adopted as a precondition of existence remains a form, and does not become the type's own valuation. §10's "he had to believe in it" cuts the other way: a philosopher who believed the ideal held the priest's valuation, and P3 says every philosopher did. Which of the two the text asserts is what Clark's reading decides.
</Remark>

§10 ends on a question. "Has all that really changed? Has that many-coloured and dangerous winged creature, that 'spirit' which the caterpillar concealed, thanks to a sunnier, warmer, brighter world, really and finally thrown off its hood and escaped into the light? Is there today enough pride, daring, courage, self-confidence, will of the spirit, will to responsibility, *freedom of will*, for 'the philosopher' to be henceforth *possible* on earth?" §§23–27 answer it, and the answer is no.

## Clark's trap: a beginning calculated to mislead

Nietzsche described the construction himself, in the section of *Ecce Homo* (1888) on the *Genealogy*: the three treatises are "perhaps, in expression, intention and *art of surprise*, the uncanniest thing written so far", and "each time a beginning that is *calculated to mislead* (*ein Anfang, der irre führen soll*), cool, scientific, even ironic, deliberately foreground, deliberately holding off", then "gradually more unrest", until "a new truth" becomes visible "between thick clouds". Maudemarie Clark's chapter on the ascetic ideal (*Nietzsche on Truth and Philosophy*, 1990, ch. 6 §2, pp. 167–71) reads §§7–10 as that beginning for the Third Treatise.

### The distinction that is "largely apparent"

Clark's claim: "the distinction Nietzsche draws is largely apparent; GM III presents the philosopher as an instance of the ascetic priest." The description in §§7–8 "certainly seems designed to differentiate the philosopher's ascetic ideal from the priestly version": the philosopher's chastity, "not from any kind of ascetic scruple or hatred of the senses, just as it is not chastity when an athlete or jockey abstains from women", is "no more ascetic than the one attributed to the early Wagner", who did not pay homage to chastity "in an ascetic sense" until the end. "Here speaks prudence and self-affirmation, not morality or the devaluation of life." The reader is meant to approve it, and to conclude that Nietzsche accepts the philosopher's version while rejecting the priest's; Clark names Kaufmann as a reader who drew that conclusion and made Nietzsche "a proponent of both the ascetic ideal and of the belief in truth Nietzsche calls its latest expression".

§10 withdraws the exemption, and the withdrawal is in the phrase "ascetic self-misunderstanding". The misunderstanding, Clark argues, cannot be the belief that philosophy requires denying some desires, which is true, nor the misreading of self-restraint as self-denial, since Plato's *Phaedo*, "his great panegyric to the ascetic ideal", presents philosophy as "the highest pleasure and self-fulfillment". What the *Phaedo* does is interpret philosophy as "a turning away from life": philosophers have "been looking forward to death all their lives" (64a), since their aim needs "a mind freed from the contaminating influences of the senses". The self-misunderstanding is therefore the philosopher's acceptance of "the ascetic priest's valuation of natural human existence": that human life has value only by turning against the natural human condition. "There has been no independent philosopher's ascetic ideal." §11's opening, "*only now*, after we have caught sight of the ascetic priest, do we seriously come to grips with our problem", means on this reading that the philosopher's commitment is understood only by understanding the philosopher as a priest.

### What the trap does to a reader who stops

Two things move in the sequence below, and the belief line at the foot records each: at §10 the philosopher's box crosses into the priest's region, and at §27 the will to truth, the one reading the treatise, joins it there.

A reader who stops at §8 leaves with a distinction the text will retract and an approval the text will use. The distinction is the two ideals; the approval is of an asceticism that "affirms existence, and only its existence", and the reader has extended it to the philosopher's whole withdrawn attitude, which §10 will identify as the priest's. The third frame is the treatise's second use of the same device, and it is the reason the first matters: §§23–27 find "the ascetic ideal's latest and noblest form" in science, in "we knowers", and in the unconditional will to truth the *Genealogy* is written by (<Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the will-to-truth lesson</Ref>). What §8 invited the reader to approve as the philosopher's healthy condition was the reader's own, and §27 says whose ideal it is.

### The rival: Nehamas's nonmoral asceticism

The rival reading takes §§7–8 at face value. Alexander Nehamas (*Nietzsche: Life as Literature*, ch. 4, pp. 114–18) reads the philosopher's asceticism as "prudentially motivated self-control: one gives up a particular good in exchange for a further, greater advantage. Nothing but utility is involved: morality plays no part in the calculation." Nonmoral asceticism "glorifies one specific type of earthly life among others; it does not condemn the pleasures it avoids", and "need not be interested in legislating what is and what is not proper for others". On this reading the philosopher's ideal is the will to power expressed as a life, the priest's is the same ideal with a demand for universal acceptance added, and what Nietzsche opposes is the demand, not the ideal. Nehamas concedes the caterpillar: "if not" all the great philosophers saw their asceticism this way, "then priestly asceticism must have been more than a 'caterpillar form' of philosophy"; and he judges Nietzsche's claim that the affection "pertains to the type" to be overstated.

Clark's reply, in her footnote on Nehamas and in the body of §2, is that the reading has to explain "he had to *believe* in it", which makes the philosopher's acceptance of the priest's valuation a fact about every philosopher there has been and not an option some took. What the text decides between them is narrower than either reading. §§7–8 describe an asceticism that is not ascetic in §1's sense; §10 says that description has never yet been true of an existing philosopher; and the two are consistent if §§7–8 describe the philosopher of the future, whom BGE 44 gives the same avoidances, "honours, or money, or offices, or enthusiasms of the senses", without the priest's interpretation of them. That is the "partial truth" Clark grants the distinction: the ideal philosopher will avoid much of what the priest avoids "but will not misinterpret this avoidance ascetically", and whether such a philosopher exists is exactly the question §10 asked.

<KeyFact>
Clark's trap is a fact about the order of the text, not about its doctrine: §§7–8 describe an asceticism with "not one iota of virtue" in it, §10 says no philosopher who has existed held that asceticism rather than the priest's, and a reader who stops between the two has approved, under the philosopher's name, the ideal §§11–22 will diagnose and §§23–27 will find in the reader.
</KeyFact>

### What the trap does to the naturalist reading

The working hypothesis survives the reversal, though the next module's readers will deny that it does. A type-explanation of the philosopher's asceticism (§§7–8) is a claim about what the practice does for the type now; §§9–10 add a history of how the practice, and the valuation attached to it, were acquired. Those are the two things II:12 keeps apart, origin and present use, and the trap is the treatise applying its own rule to its own subject: the practice has the priest's origin and the philosopher's use, and the philosopher's *self-understanding* of the practice, the world-denial, is origin that has survived into use. What the naturalist reading cannot do is treat §§7–8 as Nietzsche's endorsement of an existing philosopher's ideal; on Clark's reading it is a description of conditions no existing philosopher has held without the priest's interpretation. Nehamas's reading, which the course meets in full in <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">the lesson on Nehamas</Ref>, needs the distinction to be real, because a philosophy that is "the product and expression of the will to power" and not a claim to truth is what his Nietzsche offers in place of the priest's; and Heidegger, who reads §6 as an inheritance from Schopenhauer, will read the whole physiology of the optimum as metaphysics in disguise (<Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref>).

What is established. §1 is an argument whose premise is that an ideal's meaning is its use for a type, whose evidence is six bearers with opposite needs, and whose conclusion is that "the ascetic ideal" names a structure with no content of its own; its closing formula is the treatise's conclusion about the sick majority, argued from §11 on, not a definition. §§2–5 show the artist's ideal to be borrowed and *Parsifal* to be evidence about its author's need for a rampart and about nothing else. §6 reinterprets Schopenhauer's will-less contemplation as a tortured will's relief, on a premise about types that settles between witnesses; Heidegger's correction, checkable in *Critique of Judgment* §§2–5, leaves that result standing and shows the blow past Schopenhauer at Kant to land on Schopenhauer's Kant. §§7–8 explain the philosopher's affection for the ideal as the type's affection for the optimum of its power, on the will-to-power premise the treatise does not argue. §§9–10 put the philosopher inside the priest's form and leave open whether he has left it. And the sequence from §7 to §11 is, on Clark's reading, a beginning calculated to mislead, whose second application is the treatise's verdict on the will to truth.

What stands open is the bearer §1 called the ideal's owner. The priest's ideal is "their best instrument of power", and the population it is used on is the fourth spoke, the sick majority, whom §§2–10 have not mentioned once. <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs §§11–22, where the priest appears as the physician who does not cure, and §28, where the formula of §1 is earned.

**The form.** Take a witness W who reports that the beautiful has effect E, and who infers that E is the essence of the aesthetic state. Find in W's type a drive D that E relieves. Boundary probe. §1 says the ideal means "nothing" to artists. Suppose a type for which the ascetic ideal means nothing at all. Is that a counterexample to the treatise's thesis that an ideal's meaning is its use for a type, or an instance of it? <Solution>
**What "means" means.** In §1, "means" (*bedeutet*) is answered in each case by a bearer and a use: the ideal *means* X to type T if T's holding of the ideal is explained by X's doing something for T. Meaning is use, and use is a relation between the structure and a will that masters it. That is II:12's rule: a thing's purpose is "imposed" by whatever currently masters it, and a thing "in itself" has no purpose.

**Why "nothing" is a meaning.** "Nothing" in §1 is not the absence of an answer but an answer of the same form as the other five: the artist holds the ideal, and his holding it is explained by something, namely his need for "an already constituted authority" (§5), which the ideal serves as well as any other authority would. The ideal does nothing for the artist *as* the ascetic ideal; it does something for him as a rampart, and any rampart would do. §5's gloss makes the point exact: "nothing at all, or so much that it is as good as nothing at all". So the artist's case is an instance of the thesis, and the strongest one: a bearer for whom the ideal has no content-specific use is the case in which the ideal's own content is shown to contribute nothing to its meaning.

**The genuine boundary.** The counterexample the thesis would have to face is a type that holds the ideal and for whom holding it does nothing, not even as a rampart: an ideal with a bearer and no use. II:12's rule says such a thing does not occur: whatever is held is mastered by something, and mastery imposes a use. A bearer with no use for the ideal would not hold it, or would hold it only as a residue of a master that has died, which is a use that has lapsed and an origin that has survived, the case §10 describes for the philosopher's self-misunderstanding. The thesis is therefore unfalsifiable at this boundary in one direction, since every holding is a use, and that is a cost, not an error: the Doctrine of Types applied to an ideal explains any bearer, and what it cannot do is predict, from the ideal's content, which bearers there will be.

Research-shaped. Read *Critique of Judgment* §§2–5 in any edition and adjudicate Heidegger's charge: does Kant's *ohne alles Interesse* mean indifference, the will "put out of commission", or unconstrained favouring? Report which sentence decides it, and state what §6's argument then hits.

<Solution>
**The sentences.** Two decide it. §2 defines the term: "the delight which we connect with the representation of the *existence* of an object is called interest", so that "without interest" excludes from the ground of the judgment any concern with whether the object exists, that is, any wanting to have, use or bring about the thing; it does not exclude delight, which the whole section is about. §5 names the state positively: of the three kinds of delight, in the agreeable, the good and the beautiful, "that of taste in the beautiful is alone a disinterested and *free* delight", and "*favour* (*Gunst*) is the only free delight", since neither an interest of sense nor an interest of reason "extorts approval". Kant's term for the aesthetic state is favour, a liking that is free because nothing compels it; Heidegger's "unconstrained favouring" translates that sentence.

**The verdict.** On those two sentences Kant's clause is a restriction on the *determining ground* of a judgment and not a description of a will-less state; nothing in §§2–5 says the will is stilled, and §5's "free" says the opposite of "put out of commission". Heidegger's further claims, that favouring is "the supreme effort of our essential nature" and that the beautiful is what "comes to the fore as pure object", go beyond §§2–5 and are his; the narrow charge, that Schopenhauer read a description of will-lessness into a clause about the ground of judgment, is decided by §2's definition of interest.

**What §6 hits.** §6's reinterpretation of Schopenhauer stands: WWR I §38 does say the will is stilled, and "the wheel of Ixion stands still" is a report of will-lessness that the tortured-will reading can reinterpret. §6's charge against "Kant's famous definition" and the remark that Schopenhauer "misunderstood the Kantian definition in a Kantian sense" hit, respectively, the definition as Schopenhauer glossed it and Schopenhauer; the second is one Heidegger would sign. The one charge against Kant in his own person, that he defined the beautiful from the spectator's side and "imported the spectator into the concept", is untouched by §§2–5, since those sections are about judgments and the argument for or against a creator's aesthetics is elsewhere; what the check shows is that the spectator charge cannot be supported by "without interest", because a free favouring is something the spectator does.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Sourcing. §§2–5 use *Parsifal* as evidence about Wagner. Run the records / argues / omits pass: what a late artwork records about its author's needs on §5's own premise; what Nietzsche argues from it; and what he omits, including one sentence on his own earlier valet-hood from the 1873 *Mahnruf*. Close with the use question: how does the provenance of §§2–5 change what they can be used to establish?

<Solution>
**Records.** On §5's premise that the artist is the work's "womb, soil, dung", a late work records its author's needs at the time of composition and nothing about its subject: *Parsifal* records that in the 1870s Wagner needed "an already constituted authority" and found it in Schopenhauer's philosophy, whose standing "dominated Europe in the seventies". On the same premise the work records nothing about chastity's value, the Grail, or redemption; §4 says as much when it separates the work from the artist "so completely that he cannot be taken as seriously as his work".

**Argues.** From the record Nietzsche argues (§2) that the homage to chastity was "in an ascetic sense" for the first time, hence a leap "into his opposite"; (§3) that the charitable reading, *Parsifal* as satyr play, fails, because the late writings "in a hundred places" preach conversion; (§5) that the leap was borrowed from Schopenhauer via the sovereignty of music; and (§5) that the artist's ideal therefore means "nothing", the general conclusion for the type from one case.

**Omits.** Three things. First, the source's own position on the chain: the author of §§2–5 wrote, in October 1873, the appeal to the Germans that called Wagner "the great, brave, unbending and unstoppable fighter" and Bayreuth the seal of "our noblest hopes", which is valet-hood by §5's definition, an artist's cause taken as one's own authority; §2's "I have often enough been consulted" does not say by whom the consultant was once retained. Second, the base rate: §5's "at all times" generalizes from one artist, and no second case is offered. Third, the alternative explanation §3 raises and drops, that a late work may be a farewell to a form rather than a conversion; it is dismissed by "one would wish it were so", not by evidence.

**Use.** The provenance makes §§2–5 usable to establish what Nietzsche's theory of the artist-type is, and what it makes of the case that mattered most to its author; it makes them unusable, on their own, to establish that Wagner's turn was borrowed rather than his own, since the one witness to the borrowing is a former party to the chain who does not say so. The strongest use is the one §5 itself proposes: as a pointer to the type that lent the authority, which is where the treatise goes.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Forward. §10 leaves the philosopher inside the priest's chrysalis and §1 gives the priest's version of the ideal one line: "their best instrument of power". Write, in three premises and a conclusion, what the priest's version must do for the priest's type if §1's line and §7's optimum-premise are both true, and predict on which population the instrument is used and what it does to that population's suffering.

<Solution>
*P1.* Every type strives for the optimum conditions of its maximum feeling of power (§7).

*P2.* The priest's type is, by the First Treatise's account, impotent in action and inventive in hatred (<Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the ressentiment lesson</Ref>): its power cannot be exercised as force and must be exercised as rule over those who need something from it.

*P3.* The ascetic ideal is, for the priest, an instrument of power and "the supreme licence for power" (§1): it does for the priest's type what the optimum does for the philosopher's, and its use is on others.

*C.* The priest's version of the ideal must secure a population that needs the priest, and secure it by supplying, through the ideal, what that population lacks and cannot supply itself.

**The prediction.** The population is the fourth spoke of §1, "the physiologically failed and disgruntled (the majority of mortals)", for whom the ideal was "their chief weapon in the struggle with slow pain and boredom". What they lack, by §28's formula, is a meaning for their suffering, an answer to "what for?"; what the priest's instrument must do is supply one, and the meaning that makes a sufferer need the supplier is one that locates the cause of the suffering in the sufferer. The instrument therefore turns the sufferer's search for someone to blame back on the sufferer, and the price, on §1's own words, is that the will "would rather will nothingness than not will": the population gets a goal, and the goal is its own guilt.

That prediction is §§11–15: the ascetic priest as "physician of the sick", life "wrestling with death" through an ideal that preserves a declining life, and the pivot of §15, "I suffer: someone must be to blame", answered with "quite so, my sheep, but you yourself". <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs it, and states what the redirection explains that the First Treatise's ressentiment could not: the victory.
</Solution>

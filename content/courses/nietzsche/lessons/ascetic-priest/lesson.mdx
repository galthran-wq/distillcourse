The ascetic priest, on the Third Treatise's account, is a physician who does not cure: he takes a herd of sufferers who need someone to blame and tells them the someone is themselves, and that redirection is what made the slave revolt win. This lesson reconstructs GM III:11–22 and III:28 as one mechanism in four parts: an ideal that a declining life uses against its own decline (§§11–13), a population that has to be kept from the healthy (§14), a change in the direction of *ressentiment* that the priest performs on that population (§15), and a set of remedies that all deaden and none heal, ranked by what they do to the sufferer (§§16–21); §28 is the conclusion the four parts earn, and the section that joins the three treatises into one argument.

The course's argument arrives here carrying two open debts. <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">The First Treatise</Ref> explained how the inversion of values was coined, by a priestly caste out of *ressentiment*, and could not explain its victory: a valuation the strong could crush from outside cannot be what defeated them, so the repair had to act on the strong from inside, once they suffer. <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">The Second Treatise</Ref> produced the site, a cruelty turned inward and read as deserved, and closed on II:24's admission that the attachment of that cruelty to the natural inclinations has an author it does not name. <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">The previous lesson</Ref> established that "the ascetic ideal" is a structure with no content of its own, whose meaning is its use for a type, and left the philosopher inside the priest's chrysalis with §11's announcement that "only now" does the problem become serious. What this lesson adds to the spine is the priest's mechanism, and with it the closing of the First Treatise's gap: the strong were not defeated by *ressentiment*; they were infected by its administration. §12, perspectivism, sits inside this stretch and is read on its own in <Ref lesson="perspectivism">the next lesson</Ref>; §§23–27, which turn the treatise on its author, belong to <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the will-to-truth lesson</Ref>.

The route: §§11–13 and the reversal in which an ideal hostile to life turns out to be life's device; §14's two populations and the danger the treatise names as nihilism; §15's two lines, in German and in the course's rendering, with the argument they compress; the remedies of §§16–21 as a ladder; §28 in three sentences; and the three treatises as one machine, with the naturalist reading's verdict on the stretch.

## §§11–13: the ideal is a device of life against its own decline

§11 opens by naming the bearer §§2–10 kept off the stage, and by stating what is at stake for him. "In that ideal the ascetic priest finds not only his faith, but also his will, his power, his interest. His right to existence stands and falls with that ideal." The reader who has taken the treatise's title question for a question about a doctrine is corrected in the same paragraph: the dispute is with a type that is "fighting for his life" against those who repudiate the ideal, and the type will "scarcely prove the happiest champion of his own ideal", so "we shall rather have to help him to defend himself properly against ourselves". The valuation to be defended is then stated in one sentence. Life, "together with the whole of which it is a part, 'Nature', 'the world', the whole sphere of becoming and passing away", is placed by the priest "in relation to an existence of quite another character, which it excludes and to which it is opposed, unless it deny its own self: in this case, the case of an ascetic life, life is taken as a bridge to another existence".

§11's evidence that this valuation needs an explanation is its distribution. "Such a monstrous valuation is not an exceptional case, or a curiosity recorded in human history: it is one of the most general and persistent facts that there are"; read "from a distant star", the earth would look like "the especially ascetic planet". The priest "belongs to no particular race; he thrives everywhere; he grows out of all classes", and since he does not breed the valuation by heredity, "it must be a necessity of the first order which makes this species, hostile as it is to life, always grow again and always thrive again". Then the sentence that sets the problem the next two sections solve: "Life itself must certainly have an interest in the continuance of such a type of self-contradiction." For an ascetic life is a self-contradiction: "here rules *ressentiment* without parallel, the *ressentiment* of an insatiate instinct and ambition that would be master, not over some element in life, but over life itself"; "here is an attempt made to utilise power to dam the sources of power"; a "rift that wills itself to be a rift, which enjoys itself in this very suffering, and becomes more and more certain of itself, more and more triumphant, in proportion as its own presupposition, physiological vitality, decreases".

### §13: the contradiction is apparent, and the ideal is a weapon

From §11 one would expect the diagnosis to run against the priest: an ideal that turns power against the sources of power is life's enemy, and life's interest in it is a paradox to be exposed. §13 does the opposite. "Such a self-contradiction, as apparently manifests itself among the ascetics, 'life turned against life', is, this much is absolutely obvious, from the physiological and not now from the psychological standpoint, simply nonsense. It can only be an apparent contradiction; it must be a kind of provisional expression, an explanation, a formula, an adjustment, a psychological misunderstanding of something whose real nature could not be understood for a long time." What the formula misdescribes is then stated as a fact: "the ascetic ideal springs from the protective and healing instincts of a degenerating life (*aus den Schutz- und Heil-Instinkten eines degenerirenden Lebens*), which seeks by every means in its power to maintain its position and fight for its existence; it points to a partial physiological depression and exhaustion, against which the most profound and intact life-instincts fight ceaselessly with new weapons and discoveries. The ascetic ideal is such a weapon."

The section's one-line statement of the strategy is the sentence the brief and the course take as the keeper: "its position is consequently exactly the reverse of that which the worshippers of the ideal imagine: life wrestles in it and through it with death and against death; the ascetic ideal is a device for the preservation of life" (*das Leben ringt in ihm und durch dasselbe mit dem Tode und gegen den Tod, das asketische Ideal ist ein Kunstgriff in der Erhaltung des Lebens*). *Kunstgriff* is a trick of the trade, an artifice; Samuel's "dodge" and Kaufmann's "artifice" both keep the sense that the ideal is a means and not a valuation life holds.

The rest of §13 says what the priest is on that account, and it is not what §11 called him. "The ascetic priest is the incarnate wish for an existence of another kind, an existence on another plane; he is, in fact, the highest point of this wish, its official ecstasy and passion: but it is the very power of this wish which is the fetter that binds him here; it is just that which makes him into a tool that must labour to create more favourable conditions for earthly existence, for existence on the human plane." With that power "he keeps the whole herd of failures, distortions, abortions, unfortunates, sufferers from themselves of every kind, fast to existence, while he as the herdsman goes instinctively on in front". And the verdict: "this ascetic priest, this apparent enemy of life, this denier, he actually belongs to the really great conserving and yes-creating forces of life" (*zu den ganz grossen conservirenden und Ja-schaffenden Gewalten des Lebens*).

<Remark title="GM III:11–13 reconstructed">
*P1.* The ascetic valuation, life as a bridge to another existence, is one of the most general and persistent facts of human history, and its bearer, the priest, recurs in every race and class without being bred (§11).

*P2.* A valuation that recurs without heredity and against the interest of the life it condemns must serve an interest of life; otherwise it would not recur (§11: "life itself must have an interest in the continuance of such a type of self-contradiction").

*P3.* Physiologically, a life cannot turn against life; what looks like that is a life "partially depressed and exhausted" fighting its own decline with whatever means its intact instincts can find (§13).

*P4.* A declining life is in danger of ending itself: "the physiological struggle of man with death (more precisely, with the disgust with life, with exhaustion, with the wish for the 'end')" (§13).

*P5.* The ascetic ideal gives the declining life an aim, self-denial, and a will that wills self-denial still wills (§13; stated in full at §28).

*C.* The ascetic ideal is a weapon of a declining life against its own decline, "a device for the preservation of life"; and the priest, who embodies the wish for another existence, is by that wish the type who keeps the sufferers "fast to existence", one of the "great conserving and yes-creating forces of life".

*The premise not stated.* That the aim the ideal supplies, self-denial, is *available* to a declining life when no other aim is: that a will exhausted for everything else can still will against itself. §13 asserts the availability and §28 states it as the fact about the will the whole treatise rests on, "it needs a goal, and it would rather will nothingness than not will"; neither section argues it, and the closest thing to an argument is §14's observation that the sick man's "nausea" and "self-contempt" are affects, hence still discharges of a will.
</Remark>

The reconstruction is the naturalist reading's key claim for the treatise, and the reading is here Nietzsche's in his own words. Leiter's commentary states it as the priest's ideal being the priest's own will to power: an ideal that looks life-negating and functions to preserve a declining form of life. The two type-terms the argument runs on, "declining" (*degenerirend*) and "sick" (*krank*), are Nietzsche's and are used in this lesson as his; the Doctrine of Types restated from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> is what makes them type-facts, a fixed psycho-physical constitution that explains what a person values, and §13 supplies no medicine for them and the lesson adds none. §16 says as much about the method: "I am proceeding, as you see, in this essay, from an hypothesis which, as far as such readers as I want are concerned, does not require to be proved; the hypothesis that 'sinfulness' in man is not an actual fact, but rather merely the interpretation of a fact, of a physiological discomfort, a discomfort seen through a moral religious perspective which is no longer binding upon us." The hypothesis is methodological naturalism stated by its author, and it is stated as unproved.

<KeyFact>
§13 reverses the polarity of §11 without withdrawing a word of it: the ideal is *ressentiment* against life (§11) and a device of life (§13) at once, because the life that holds it is a declining one, and a declining life's *ressentiment* against its own decline is the only form its will to persist can take. Everything the treatise says against the priest from here on is said against a conserving force, and the charge will have to be the cost of the conservation.
</KeyFact>

§12 stands between the two halves of the argument and is not part of it. It takes the ascetic's "will to contradiction and unnaturalness" and asks what it does when it philosophizes, and the answer, the will-less "subject of knowledge" against "only a perspectival seeing", is a thesis about knowledge that <Ref lesson="perspectivism">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs on its own; nothing in §§13–28 depends on it. §13 closes with man as "the sick animal", "the great experimenter with himself", whose sickness is the price of having "dared, innovated, braved more, challenged fate more than all the other animals put together", and whose nausea at himself, "as about 1348, the time of the Dance of Death", the year the Black Death crossed Europe, is discharged "with such force that it is immediately made into a new fetter": "it is subsequently the wound itself that forces him to live". That is the plateau in the figure, seen from inside.

## §14: the sick are the greatest danger to the healthy

§13 established that the sick majority is normal, "the more normal is this sickliness in man, and we cannot dispute this normality"; §14 draws the consequence for the rare healthy cases, and the consequence is a demand for separation. "The sick are the greatest danger for the healthy; it is not from the strongest that harm comes to the strong, but from the weakest" (*Die Kranken sind die grösste Gefahr für die Gesunden*). The harm is not force. "It is not for a minute the fear of man whose diminution should be wished for; for this fear forces the strong to be strong." What is to be feared is "not the great fear of, but the great nausea with, man; and equally so the great pity for man. Supposing that both these things were one day to espouse each other, then inevitably the maximum of monstrousness would immediately come into the world, the 'last will' of man, his will to nothingness, nihilism."

### What the sick do to the healthy

The mechanism of the harm is *ressentiment*, re-anchored from <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the First Treatise</Ref>, where I:10 defined it as a valuation that begins with a No to something outside and takes that No for a deed. §14 gives the affect a physiology and a habitat. "On such a soil of self-contempt, a veritable swamp soil, grows that weed, that poisonous growth"; "here teem the worms of revenge and vindictiveness"; "here is ever spun the net of the most malignant conspiracy, the conspiracy of the sufferers against the sound and the victorious". The sick "stalk about in our midst as living reproaches, as warnings to us, as though health, fitness, strength, pride, the sensation of power, were really vicious things in themselves, for which one would have some day to do penance". Their triumph, "their final, fondest, most sublime triumph of revenge", is named exactly: "when they succeed in pushing their own misery, in fact all misery, into the consciousness of the happy; so that the latter begin one day to be ashamed of their happiness". That is the victory of the slave revolt described as an event in the happy, and it is the first place in the *Genealogy* where the victory is given a mechanism rather than a date.

The demand follows. "Preventing the sick making the healthy sick, for that is what such a soddenness comes to, this ought to be our supreme object in the world; but for this it is above all essential that the healthy should remain separated from the sick, that they should even guard themselves from the look of the sick." The healthy may not nurse: "the higher must not degrade itself to be the tool of the lower, the pathos of distance must to all eternity keep their missions also separate", and "if they are to be enabled to do what only they must do, how can they possibly be free to play the doctor, the comforter, the 'Saviour' of the sick?" The section ends by naming the two plagues again: "against the great nausea with man! against the great pity for man!"

<Remark title="GM III:14 reconstructed">
*P1.* The sick are the majority and the healthy are rare, and the sick harm the healthy not by force but by affect: the *ressentiment* of the sufferers, discharged as reproach, makes the happy ashamed of their happiness.

*P2.* The two affects by which the harm crosses are nausea at man and pity for man, and their union is the will to nothingness, nihilism (§14).

*P3.* The healthy cannot be the physicians of the sick, since contact with the sick is the harm, and a higher type that serves a lower one has disowned its mission.

*C.* If the sick are to be tended at all, and §13 has shown that life tends them, the tending must be done by physicians who are themselves sick: a type that belongs to the herd and rules it (§15's opening).

*The premise not stated.* That the sick must be tended: that leaving the herd untended is not an option life takes. §13 supplies it, since the herd's suicidal collapse is the outcome the ideal exists to prevent, but §14's own demand for separation would be satisfied by abandonment, and the priest is derived only when §13's premise is added. The derivation is the treatise's: the priest is not observed and then explained; he is deduced from the sick, the healthy, and the wall.
</Remark>

### The refutation this section carries

The prevalent misreading of Nietzsche as a nihilist, a thinker who denies that anything has value, is answered in this section before the treatise is over, and the answer is the sentence in which nihilism is first named in the *Genealogy*. The misreading holds that Nietzsche's critique of morality ends in the denial of all values. §14 rejects that: nihilism, "the will to nothingness", is named as "the maximum of monstrousness", the outcome the whole analysis is written against, and the sick are dangerous *because* they breed it. The reason the misreading fails is causal, not rhetorical: on §13's account the ideal exists to prevent a will from ceasing to will, and §28 will say the ideal "closed the door to all suicidal nihilism"; a thinker who diagnoses an ideal as a defence against nihilism, and faults it for failing, is not a nihilist but the diagnostician of one. The replacement model is that "nihilism" in Nietzsche names a condition, the highest values devaluing themselves, that <Ref lesson="madman-argument">the madman lesson</Ref> introduced and that <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the nihilism lesson</Ref> splits in two; the discriminating passage is §14's "against the great pity for man", which is a warning against the affect that breeds nihilism and not an expression of it. The fifth exercise returns to the point.

## §15: the redirection

§14's conclusion was that the physician must be sick; §15 opens by taking hold of him. "If you have understood in all their depths the reasons for the impossibility of its being the business of the healthy to nurse the sick, it follows that you have grasped this further necessity, the necessity of doctors and nurses who themselves are sick. And now we have and hold with both our hands the essence of the ascetic priest." The essence is double. "He must himself be sick, he must be kith and kin to the sick and the abortions so as to understand them"; "but he must also be strong, even more master of himself than of others, impregnable in his will to power, so as to acquire the trust and the awe of the weak, so that he can be their hold, bulwark, prop, compulsion, overseer, tyrant, god." The §1 line that the ideal is the priest's "best instrument of power" is cashed here: the power is lordship over sufferers, "his kingdom", and it is exercised on a herd that would otherwise disperse.

### What he protects the herd against, and with what

The herd needs protection "against the healthy, doubtless also against the envy towards the healthy", and the priest wages "a war of guile (of 'spirit') rather than of force" with the beasts of prey, in which he "will in certain cases find it necessary to conjure up out of himself, or at any rate to represent practically, a new type of the beast of prey", polar bear, panther and fox in one. He goes "among even the other kind of beasts of prey, determined as he is to sow on their soil, wherever he can, suffering, discord, self-contradiction". That sentence is the one in §§11–22 that touches the strong directly, and the lesson returns to it when the gap is closed. Then the physician's method: "He brings with him, doubtless, salve and balsam; but before he can play the physician he must first wound; so, while he soothes the pain which the wound makes, he at the same time poisons the wound." He is "this wizard and wild-beast tamer, in whose vicinity everything healthy must needs become ill, and everything ill must needs become tame."

The herd is also protected against itself. Inside it "*ressentiment*, that most dangerous blasting-stuff and explosive, ever accumulates and accumulates", and "getting rid of this blasting-stuff in such a way that it does not blow up the herd and the herdsman, that is his real feat, his supreme utility". §15 then states the formula the treatise has been built to reach: "if you wish to comprise in the shortest formula the value of the priestly life, it would be correct to say: the priest is the changer of the direction of *ressentiment*" (*der Priester ist der Richtungs-Veränderer des Ressentiment*).

### The physiology of blame

What is redirected is defined before the redirection is shown. "Every sufferer searches instinctively for a cause of his suffering; to put it more exactly, a doer; to put it still more precisely, a guilty doer susceptible to suffering (*einen für Leid empfänglichen schuldigen Thäter*); in brief, something living, on which, either actually or in effigy, he can on any pretext vent his affects. For the venting of affects is the sufferer's greatest attempt at alleviation, that is to say, stupefaction, his involuntarily desired narcotic against pain of any kind." This, and not a "reflex movement" of self-protection like a decapitated frog's, is "the real physiological cause of *ressentiment*, revenge, and their family": "a demand for the deadening of pain through affect". The invalid's reasoning follows: "'It must be somebody's fault that I feel bad', this kind of reasoning is peculiar to all invalids, and is but the more pronounced the more ignorant they remain of the real cause of their feeling bad, the physiological cause." Sufferers "tear open the oldest wounds"; "they make evil-doers out of friends, wife, child, and everything which is nearest to them".

Then the two lines. The German is the eKGWB's; the English is the course's rendering, which keeps the sentence order of the original.

> „Ich leide: daran muss irgend Jemand schuld sein" — also denkt jedes krankhafte Schaf. Aber sein Hirt, der asketische Priester, sagt zu ihm: „Recht so, mein Schaf! irgend wer muss daran schuld sein: aber du selbst bist dieser Irgend-Wer, du selbst bist daran allein schuld, — *du selbst bist an dir allein schuld!*"

> "I suffer: someone must be to blame for it" — so thinks every sick sheep. But his shepherd, the ascetic priest, says to him: "Quite right, my sheep! someone must be to blame for it: but you yourself are this someone, you yourself alone are to blame for it, *you yourself alone are to blame for yourself!*"

Nietzsche's own verdict follows in the same sentence: "that is bold enough, false enough, but one thing is at least attained; thereby, as I have said, the course of *ressentiment* is *diverted*."

<Remark title="GM III:15 reconstructed: the pivot">
*P1.* A sufferer's affect seeks a cause of the suffering, and the cause it seeks is a guilty doer on whom the affect can be discharged, because discharge deadens the pain (§15).

*P2.* Discharged outward, the affect of the herd would strike the healthy, whom §14 requires to be protected from the sick, and would strike inside the herd, where it accumulates as an explosive that would destroy herd and herdsman together (§§14–15).

*P3.* The priest rules by holding the herd together and by protecting it from the healthy and from itself; his power is lordship over sufferers (§15).

*P4.* The sufferer's premise, that someone is to blame, cannot be removed, since it is the form the search for a narcotic takes; it can only be given a different object (§15: the priest grants the premise).

*C.* The priest supplies the one object that satisfies the premise, discharges the affect, and harms neither the healthy nor the herd: the sufferer himself. *Ressentiment* is not abolished; its direction is changed.

*The instrument.* §16 names it: "a temporary tyranny of such paradoxical and anomalous ideas as 'guilt', 'sin', 'sinfulness', 'corruption', 'damnation'", used "to turn the milder cases severely on to themselves, to give their *ressentiment* a backward direction". §20 identifies what the instrument was made from: "'Sin', for that is the name of the new priestly version of the animal 'bad conscience' (the inverted cruelty)". The bad conscience of <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">II:16</Ref>, cruelty with nowhere to go but inward, is the raw material; the priest's reinterpretation of it as deserved is the moralization <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">Leiter's third stage</Ref> needed and could not locate an author for.

*The premise not stated.* That the sufferer can accept the verdict: that "you yourself alone are to blame" is a sentence a sufferer can believe. It can be believed only of a self that could have done otherwise, and that self is the doer behind the deed of <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">I:13</Ref>. The redirection therefore presupposes morality's first presupposition, and §15 calls the verdict "false enough" for exactly that reason: it is false of the sufferer, who is what he is by type, and effective because the sufferer holds the fiction that makes it intelligible.
</Remark>

The redirection works where an argument would not because it returns something to the sufferer that no outward object could: efficacy. A sufferer who is to blame is a sufferer who can act; his suffering gains an explanation and a moral standing, and his own activity is enlisted on the side of the punishment, as self-castigation, so that the search for a guilty doer ends in a project he can carry out. The premise "someone is to blame" was a demand for an object to act on, and the priest's object is the only one the sufferer can always reach. That is what the ideal offers a distressed type: its own agency, recruited into self-punishment, at the price of the verdict.

### The gap closed

<Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">The ressentiment lesson</Ref> ended on a missing premise: for I:7's claim that the inversion "has achieved victory", the First Treatise needs a mechanism by which the strong come to hold the values that condemn them, and nothing in I:1–17 supplies one; a table of values the strong can ignore cannot have defeated them. The shape of the gap fixed the shape of any repair: the mechanism must act on the strong from inside, once they suffer. §15 is that mechanism, and the two sections around it say why it reaches the strong. II:16 put the strong inside walls and turned their cruelty inward, so that the strong became "sufferers from themselves" (§13's phrase for the herd); §15 has the priest go "among even the other kind of beasts of prey" to sow "suffering, discord, self-contradiction" on their soil; and §14 states the outcome as an event in the happy, who "begin one day to be ashamed of their happiness". A strong type that suffers from itself is a sufferer, and a sufferer searches for someone to blame; the priest's sentence is addressed to whoever is searching. The victory of the slave revolt is, on this account, neither the weight of numbers nor the persuasiveness of a new table; it is a physician's verdict accepted by patients who had been made patients first.

### The same priest on the stage

Zarathustra's speech "On Priests" (Z II) dramatizes the type, and the course's rule from <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref> is to map the speech onto the argued theses and state what it adds and drops. "Among them too there are heroes; many of them suffered too much, so they want to make others suffer": §15's physician who must first wound. "These shepherds, too, still belonged among the sheep": §15's "he must himself be sick". "Up the stairs on your knees, you sinners!": §20's sinner. "Nothing is more vengeful than their humility": §14's counterfeit of virtue. What the speech adds is a sentence the argued treatise reaches only at its end: "my blood is related to theirs, and I want to know that my blood is honored even in theirs." GM III:20 concedes that "we psychologists" are "probably none the less its victims"; §10 had put the philosopher inside the priest's chrysalis; but the claim of kinship by type, made in the first person, is what §§23–27 will argue about "we knowers" and what Zarathustra states as blood. What the speech drops is the mechanism: there is no sheep's sentence, no redirection, and no herd protected from the healthy, only "false values and words of delusion" in which the priests are "prisoners and marked men", and a pity for them, "I suffer and suffered with them", that §14 has just named as the affect to be guarded against. The sixth exercise runs the map in full.

## §§16–21: the remedies, ranked by what they do to the sufferer

§15 changed the direction of one affect; §§16–21 catalogue what the priest does with the rest, and the catalogue has a criterion, stated before the first remedy is named. The priest does not heal: a "mere affect-medication" cannot bring "any real healing of the sick in the physiological sense", and the instinct of life never "took healing as its goal and purpose" (§16). What it brought was containment, the sick made "harmless up to a certain point", the milder cases turned "severely on to themselves", and the whole organized into a body for which "the word 'Church' is the most popular name", kept apart from the comparatively healthy. §17 turns the fact into the objection the ranking runs on: the priest "combats only the actual suffering, the discomfort of the sufferer, not its cause, not the actual state of sickness", and that "must constitute our most radical objection to priestly medication". A treatment that never reaches the cause cannot be graded by whether it cures, since none does; it can be graded only by what it does to the sufferer's health while it relieves his pain, and that is the axis on which §19 divides the means into "innocent" and "guilty", and the axis of the ladder below. The same section grants the priest his genius, without irony: consolation is his art, and Christianity is "a great treasure-chamber of ingenious consolations". Every remedy on the ladder relieves; what the ranking asks of each is its price.

### The two deadening means: hypnosis and work

The first means treats the feeling of life as the disease and lowers it. The depression it fights has, on §17's own list, physiological causes, a mixing of races or classes, a climate the race cannot adapt to, fatigue, a wrong diet, blood-disease, and the remedy addresses none of them; it addresses the feeling, by removing everything that produces one: "no more wishes, no more wants; shun everything which produces affect, which produces 'blood'", no love, no hate, no revenge, no work, and for the intellect the rule that one must make oneself stupid. Nietzsche's name for the result is "hypnotism", a human approximation of hibernation, "a minimum of assimilation and metabolism in which life just manages to subsist". The relief is real, and §17 says so in the strongest terms it uses for any remedy: "in countless cases they really escaped by the help of their system of hypnotism away from deep physiological depression". The cost to health is twofold. The regime "can and does pave the way to all kinds of mental disturbances", hallucinations and ecstasies which the practitioners report as visions; and its supreme state, in the Indian religions as in Christian mysticism, is deep sleep taken for union with the divine, so that "the hypnotic sensation of nothingness, the peace of deepest sleep, anaesthesia in short" is what "passes with the sufferers and the absolutely depressed for their supreme good, their value of values". A sufferer treated this way is left at the one level of health at which nothing is felt; the depression is not removed but put beyond the reach of consciousness, and the plateau of §13's figure is here a plateau at the floor.

<Aside>
§17's apparatus. The Indian evidence for deep sleep as salvation is quoted from the Vedānta commentaries of Śaṅkara, the eighth-century systematizer of the doctrine that the self is one with Brahman, in the rendering of Paul Deussen, Nietzsche's school friend at Pforta and the translator of the Vedānta, whom §17 calls "the first real European expert of the Indian philosophy"; §17 adds that all of it "simply expresses the same criticism on life as did the clear, cold, Greekly cold, but yet suffering Epicurus". The mental disturbances are given one case, "the history of St Theresa", the sixteenth-century Spanish Carmelite whose autobiography records raptures and visions; the rule for the intellect is Pascal's, *il faut s'abêtir*. None of it enters the ranking, which needs only that the hypnotic means ends in anaesthesia and calls it the value of values.
</Aside>

The second means is commoner because it asks less: mechanical activity, "called today by the somewhat ignoble title of the 'blessing of work'" (§18). It relieves by occupancy rather than reduction, filling consciousness with action until "there is little room left for suffering; for narrow is it, this chamber of human consciousness". Its corollaries, "absolute regularity, punctilious unreasoning obedience, the chronic routine of life, the complete occupation of time", amount to "a training in 'impersonality'", and where the sufferers are slaves or prisoners the priest need not prescribe the work at all, only "juggle a little with the names" until they "see a benefit in objects which they hated". The cost to health is nil in the direction the guilty means will run, and that is why it ranks where it does: the depression is not deepened, only crowded out, and the sick are left as they were with one thing subtracted, the self that had room to suffer. The two deadening means share a mechanism: each shrinks the consciousness in which suffering registers, one by emptying it and one by filling it, and neither adds to the sickness.

### The two social means: the small joy and the herd

The third means is the first that stimulates rather than deadens, and the treatise is exact about what it stimulates. "The ordaining of a little joy", above all "the joy in producing joy (such as doing good, giving presents, alleviating, helping, exhorting, comforting, praising)", with "love your neighbour" as its rule, is "a stimulation of the strongest and most life-assertive impulse, the will to power", administered "in the most cautious doses" (§18). The evidence that it is will to power and not altruism is the feeling it delivers, "the happiness involved in the 'smallest superiority' which is the concomitant of all benefiting, helping, extolling, making oneself useful". The dose is cautious for a reason §18 gives in the same sentence: the sick who are not given it "hurt each other, and naturally in obedience to the same radical instinct". The cost to health is none, since a dose of the life-assertive impulse is the opposite of a subtraction; the cost is in the ceiling, a will to power permitted at the smallest scale on which superiority can still be felt. Neighbour-love is the sick's will to power at the smallest permitted scale, and the priest's regime keeps it there.

The fourth means organizes the third. Christianity began, on §18's account, as "co-operative unions for poverty, sickness, and burial" in the lowest stratum of Roman society, and that origin is the evidence that the small joy, made mutual, produces a community; the community gives the sufferer a new interest that takes him out of the personal part of his discontent, his aversion to himself. The herd is on this reckoning a triumph in the fight with depression, and it is the priest's creation on the herd's demand: "wherever a herd exists it is the instinct of weakness which has wished for the herd, and the cleverness of the priests which has organised it". The cost to the sufferer's health is again none; what the herd costs is charged to the other population, since by the same necessity the strong strive for isolation as the weak for union, and the born master is "disturbed and wounded to the quick by organisation". §14's danger, the sick assembled into a conspiracy against the sound, is on this account a remedy working as designed. §19 sums the four up as the "innocent" methods, innocent "according to modern standards", and the ladder's lower rungs are fixed: each relieves, and none leaves the sick sicker than it found them.

### The guilty means: the orgy of feeling, and the sinner

The fifth means inverts the mechanism of the first four. Where they lower the feeling of life or divert it, the guilty means raise it to excess: "to produce an orgy of feeling (*Ausschweifung des Gefühls*)" is the whole of the method, and the excess is the anaesthetic (§19). §20 describes the procedure as a shock that unswitches the soul, plunging it into terror, frost, ardour or rapture so that the smallness of a chronic depression is lost in the jolt, and the priest's materials are every strong affect the sufferer has, rage, fear, lust, revenge, cruelty, despair, each unleashed "under the sanction of a religious interpretation and justification". The relief is the greatest of the five, and the cost is stated in the same breath: the orgy "has subsequently to be paid for, this is self-evident, it makes the ill more ill", and on that ground alone the means is "according to modern standards a 'guilty' kind". That sentence is the ladder's top rung, the one remedy that relieves by adding to the sickness.

The orgy of feeling needs a licence, and the licence is the instrument §15 forged. Every strong affect the priest unleashes is unleashed by way of guilt, the "keynote" of all his "agonising and ecstatic music", and §20 says in its own voice where the guilt came from: it is the feeling whose origin "the previous essay" gave "as a piece of animal psychology and nothing else", the bad conscience of II:16 in its crude state, which took shape only in the hands of the priest, "real artist that he was in the feeling of guilt". The product has a name: "'Sin', for that is the name of the new priestly version of the animal 'bad conscience' (the inverted cruelty)", and §20 rates it "the greatest event in the history of the diseased soul". What sin does to the sufferer is a second cost, distinct from the excess and permanent where the excess is episodic. A sufferer who does not know why he suffers, caged and searching for reasons, is given a first hint on the cause: he is to look for it in himself, in his guiltiness, in a piece of his past, and to understand the suffering as punishment; from then on "he is in the plight of a hen round which a line has been drawn. He never gets out of the circle of lines. The sick man has been turned into 'the sinner'" (*aus dem Kranken ist „der Sünder" gemacht*). A depression has a cause the sufferer does not know; a sin has a cause he is, and a cause he is cannot be treated, only punished.

§20 records the success and §21 the bill, and the two are measured on different axes. On the axis of relief the success is complete: the old depression and fatigue were conquered outright, life became interesting again, "awake, eternally awake, sleepless, glowing, burnt away", and the demand of the priest's initiates for centuries was "More pain! More pain!". On the axis of health §21 reads the word the regime's defenders use. It grants that the treatment "reformed" man, and takes the word to mean tamed, weakened, discouraged, refined, emasculated, so that "such a system, even granted that it makes the ill 'better', under any circumstances also makes them more ill". The historical evidence for the bill is epidemiological: the St Vitus and St John dances of the Middle Ages, the witch-hysteria, and the mass death-cravings whose cry, *evviva la morte!*, ran across Europe, on which §21 calls the ideal "the real fatality in the history of the health of the European man". The evidence is cited rather than argued, and the fourth exercise grades it.

<Remark title="the ranking's criterion, reconstructed">
*P1.* None of the priest's means heals: each combats the suffering and not its cause, and the instinct of life never "took healing as its goal" (§§16–17).

*P2.* The means therefore cannot be ranked by whether they cure; they can be ranked only by what they do to the sufferer's health while they deaden his suffering (§17's "most radical objection" fixes the axis).

*P3.* Hypnotic reduction, mechanical activity, the small joy and herd organization deaden without adding to the sickness: the sick are left "as they were", harmless and organized (§§16–19).

*P4.* The orgy of feeling deadens by a discharge that "has subsequently to be paid for": it "makes the ill more ill", and the guilt that licenses it makes the sick man a sinner who can never leave the circle (§§20–21).

*C.* The innocent means rank above the guilty means, and the criterion is the priest's effect on the health of the population he tends, not on their pain; by the same criterion the most successful remedy, sin, is the most costly, because its success is measured in relief and its cost in health.

*What the ranking is not.* "Innocent" and "guilty" are in scare quotes and are "according to modern standards"; the ranking is not a moral one, and §20 insists the priest "has prescribed it in the most implicit belief in its utility and indispensability, often enough almost collapsing in the presence of the pain which he created". The physician acts in good faith. The charge is the bill.
</Remark>

### Why the physician's compassion is part of the disease: pity versus pity

The remedies presuppose a valuation, and Bernard Reginster's reading of the treatise names it. Schopenhauer, whose morality of compassion the *Genealogy*'s Preface takes as its first target, held two things: that morality is grounded in compassion, and that happiness is the absence of suffering. The priest's regime serves both, the hypnotic means aiming at "the hypnotic sensation of nothingness" as the "value of values", the small joy prescribing neighbour-love; and both rest on "the old, life-negating condemnation of suffering", which Reginster finds is the whole of what Nietzsche opposes in the ideal: not its self-denial, which Clark too calls its "saving grace", but its "valuation of human life". The claim that the priest's pity harms is not a Darwinian novelty, and the Preface (§5) says so: "it was precisely on the unworthiness of pity that the philosophers had agreed until now: I name only Plato, Spinoza, La Rochefoucauld and Kant, four spirits as different from each other as possible, but united in one respect: in the deprecation of pity". Pity is bad for the pitied, because "it includes a measure of condescension" and denies, in *The Gay Science* §338's phrase, "the personal necessity of suffering"; and for the pitier, whom the cry of the sufferer calls "aside" from his own path. BGE 225 states the two pities in one sentence: "in man creator and creature are united", and the priest's pity is "for the 'creature in man', for that which must be formed, broken, forged, torn, burned and purged", against which "our converse pity" protests. What the physician tends is the creature, and what his tending deadens is the creator; that is the sense in which the compassion is the disease.

<KeyFact>
The attack on the priest is not an attack on consolation. §17 grants that the hypnotic means brought "genuine relief" in "countless cases", §18 that the herd was "a genuine advance and triumph", and §20 that the guilty means were prescribed in good faith; a reader who takes §§16–21 for a polemic against comfort has read the relief and missed the bill, and will be unable to read §28, where the same consolation is credited with saving the will.
</KeyFact>

## §28: the conclusion earned

§22 closes the diagnosis with a page of taste against the New Testament, and §23 turns to the question of a counter-ideal that §§24–27 pursue. §28 is the treatise's last section, and it returns to the sentence §1 stated without argument. The section is short enough to reconstruct in three sentences, and the reconstruction is the lesson's result.

**First sentence.** "If you except the ascetic ideal, man, the animal man, had no meaning. His existence on earth contained no end; 'What is the purpose of man at all?' was a question without an answer." "He suffered also in other ways, he was in the main a diseased animal; but his problem was not suffering itself, but the lack of an answer to that crying question, 'To what purpose do we suffer?' Man, the bravest animal and the one most inured to suffering, does not repudiate suffering in itself: he wills it, he even seeks it out, provided that he is shown a meaning for it, a purpose of suffering. Not suffering, but the senselessness of suffering was the curse which till then lay spread over humanity" (*Die Sinnlosigkeit des Leidens, nicht das Leiden, war der Fluch*).

**Second sentence.** "And the ascetic ideal gave it a meaning! It was up till then the only meaning; but any meaning is better than no meaning; the ascetic ideal was in that connection the *faute de mieux* par excellence. In that ideal suffering found an explanation; the tremendous gap seemed filled; the door to all suicidal nihilism was closed." At a price: "The explanation, there is no doubt about it, brought in its train new suffering, deeper, more penetrating, more venomous, gnawing more brutally into life: it brought all suffering under the perspective of guilt."

**Third sentence.** "But in spite of all that, man was saved thereby, he had a meaning, and from henceforth was no more like a leaf in the wind, a shuttlecock of chance, of nonsense; he could now 'will' something, absolutely immaterial to what end, to what purpose, with what means he wished: the will itself was saved." And what the willing was: "this hate of the human, and even more of the animal, and more still of the material, this horror of the senses, of reason itself, this fear of happiness and beauty, this desire to get right away from all illusion, change, growth, death, wishing and even desiring, all this means, let us have the courage to grasp it, a will to nothingness, a will opposed to life, a repudiation of the most fundamental conditions of life, but it is and remains a will!" The closing sentence, in the German and the course's rendering:

> Und, um es noch zum Schluss zu sagen, was ich Anfangs sagte: lieber will noch der Mensch das Nichts *wollen*, als *nicht* wollen…

> And, to say at the end what I said at the beginning: man would still rather *will* nothingness than *not* will.

<Remark title="GM III:28 reconstructed">
*P1.* Man's problem was never suffering as such, which he wills and seeks when it has a purpose; it was the absence of an answer to "what for?" (§28).

*P2.* A will without an answer to "what for?" is in danger of not willing at all: suicidal nihilism, the "tremendous void" (§28; §13's "wish for the end").

*P3.* The ascetic ideal supplied an answer, the only one so far: suffering as punishment, life as a bridge (§28; §11; §20), and by supplying it "closed the door to all suicidal nihilism".

*P4.* The answer brought new suffering, since it "brought all suffering under the perspective of guilt", and the willing it directs is a will against life (§28; §§20–21).

*C.* The ideal saved the will at the price of turning it against life; man "would rather will nothingness than not will", and the two millennia of the ideal's rule are the proof that the will's need for a goal outranks its need for life.

*What the conclusion commits Nietzsche to.* Clark's chapter reads §28 with *Ecce Homo*'s gloss on the treatise: the ideal held power "because it was the only ideal so far, because it had no rival. Above all, a *counter-ideal* was lacking, until Zarathustra." If the will needs a goal and the only goal so far cost life, the critique is incomplete without a goal that gives suffering a meaning at a price other than guilt. That is the problem of the course's sixth module, and <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the nihilism lesson</Ref> takes §28's "will to nothingness" as the despair the positive doctrine answers. §28 does not supply the counter-ideal; it fixes what one would have to do.
</Remark>

### Two answers to why senseless suffering needs a meaning

Reginster's reading of the treatise supplies the comparison that shows what the priest's answer is and what it is not. Two conceptions of what it is for a good to compensate suffering must be told apart. On the first, the good is independent of the suffering and outweighs it, as a reward outweighs a labour; that is the Christian doctrine of redemption, on which "these sufferings remain regrettable in principle: even though the happy moments of my life outweigh the unhappy ones, I can coherently deplore the latter and wish that my life had been spared them altogether". On the second, the good is impossible without the suffering, which is its "essential constituent": from the standpoint of the will to power, "suffering cannot be coherently deplored, and it cannot be deplored because it is an essential constituent of the good". The priest's meaning is of the first kind, life "as a bridge to another existence" on which suffering is served as a sentence, and that is why it leaves the sufferer's valuation of life untouched: suffering stays an objection, now an objection with an explanation.

The counter-ideal has a second failure to avoid, and Reginster finds it dramatized in Zarathustra's donkey, the beast of Z IV's "Awakening" that brays *Ja* to everything and whom Zarathustra's "omni-satisfied" resemble: "Always to bray Yea-Yuh, that only the ass has learned, and whoever is of his spirit." That is "counter-adaptation", a sham affirmation that conceals suffering by being "relentlessly non-confrontational and adaptable", never frustrated because it never wanted what it did not get.

The two failures are the ends of the line the counter-ideal has to be found between: a will that wills nothingness because life is an objection, and a will that says Yes to everything because nothing is. §28 reaches exactly one point on that line, the first, and reports that it was "better than no meaning". The positive doctrine's task, stated from here, is a meaning of suffering that neither condemns it nor conceals it; the course's fourth-module verdict on the priest is therefore not that he failed but that he succeeded at the only thing that was then possible, and that the possibility has since changed.

## The three treatises joined: one machine

The three treatises were introduced by the course's spine as one argument in three installments, and this section states the joint in the form the naturalist reading gives it: a mechanism, its evidence, and the premise it leaves unstated, at each of three stages, with what each stage needed from the next.

The First Treatise supplied the inversion. *Ressentiment*, the affect of a class denied the deed, resolved the contradiction between hating the noble and admiring them under their own code by coining "evil" of them first, and the priestly caste articulated the coinage (I:7–14). What it could not supply was the victory: a table of values the strong could ignore. The Second supplied the site. Enclosed by conquest, the animal turned its cruelty inward and became a sufferer from itself (II:16–17); the creditor–debtor relation, escalated to a debt before God, gave that cruelty a currency (II:19–21); what it could not supply was the author of the reading of that cruelty as deserved, whom II:24 leaves unnamed. The Third supplies the administrator and the instrument. The priest, a type deduced from the sick, the healthy and the wall between them, takes the sufferer's demand for a guilty doer and returns the sufferer to himself (§15); the instrument is guilt, "the new priestly version of the animal bad conscience" (§20), which is the First Treatise's affect discharged on the Second Treatise's site by the Third's type. The output is the morality of <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>'s MPS, altruism, equality, pity and guilt, held by the strong as well as the weak.

The joint is Nietzsche's, not the commentator's: §20's "I have already indicated in the previous essay the origin of this feeling" is the Third Treatise citing the Second, and §14's "the conspiracy of the sufferers against the sound" is the First's affect at work in the Third's population. Reginster's caution against Leiter's reconstruction, recorded in <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the ressentiment lesson</Ref>, is that the three treatises are not three sealed mechanisms; §15 is the evidence for the caution, since its one formula uses all three: an affect from I, a site from II, and the priest's "impregnable will to power" from III. The caution does not loosen the joint; it says the joint is a single sentence.

### The naturalist reading's verdict on §§11–22 and §28

The working hypothesis from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> was that Nietzsche's method is methodological naturalism plus the Doctrine of Types, and that the test is whether a stretch of text renders as mechanism plus evidence with a nameable missing premise. For this stretch the verdict is the cleanest in the *Genealogy*, and for a reason the text states: §16's hypothesis that sinfulness is "the interpretation of a fact, of a physiological discomfort" is the naturalist reading in the author's own sentence. The mechanism is §§13–15: a declining life, a device, a redirection. The evidence is §§17–21's catalogue and epidemiology, cited from the Indian scriptures on deep sleep, from the origin of Christianity in burial societies, from the dancing manias and the witch trials, and graded by the course's sourcing standard as illustration rather than proof, since no case is argued and every case is selected by the conclusion. The missing premises are three and are named above: that a will exhausted for everything else can still will self-denial (§13), which §28 asserts; that the herd must be tended rather than abandoned (§14), which §13 supplies; and that the sufferer can accept "you yourself", which needs I:13's doer as a belief the sufferer holds. None of the three is an is–ought step; the stretch is descriptive from §11 to §28, and the valuation it carries, that health outranks relief, is the standard the note to I:17 stated for the whole book.

What the reading deflates here is smaller than what it deflated in <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the Second Treatise</Ref>. §13's "yes-creating forces of life" and §20's physician who "almost collapses in the presence of the pain he created" are read as they stand, since the reading needs the priest to be a conserving force; and the verdict it returns is a diagnosis rather than a refutation: the ascetic valuation is the best the sick can do for themselves in their condition, and it is destructive and self-defeating for them all the same, which is what the text says at every section from §13's device to §21's bill. The rival readings return with §§23–27 and with the doctrine: Nehamas will need the priest's interpretation to be one interpretation among others that a stronger one masters, and Heidegger will read the physiology of decline as metaphysics; <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">the Nehamas lesson</Ref> and <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref> take them up where they bear. <Ref lesson="antichrist-applied-genealogy">The Antichrist lesson</Ref> runs this section's machinery on one record, Jesus and Paul, and Paul's "genius of hatred" is §15 applied.

### What is established, and what stands open

What is established. The ascetic ideal is a device by which a declining life fights its own decline, "life wrestling in it and through it with death and against death", and the priest who embodies it is a conserving force (§§11–13). The sick are the majority and the danger to the healthy, and the danger is nihilism, named in §14 as the union of nausea and pity; the priest is deduced from the demand that the healthy not tend the sick (§14). The priest changes the direction of *ressentiment*: the sufferer's "someone is to blame" is granted its premise and returned its object, "you yourself", and the instrument is guilt, the priestly reinterpretation of the animal bad conscience (§§15, 20); the strong, made sufferers by II:16 and sown with self-contradiction by the priest, accept the verdict as patients, and that is the victory the First Treatise could not explain. The remedies rank by their cost to health, and the most effective, sin, costs most (§§16–21). And §28 earns §1: not suffering but its senselessness was the curse, the ideal gave the only meaning so far at the price of guilt, and man would rather will nothingness than not will, which commits the author to a counter-ideal the treatise does not supply.

What stands open is inside the stretch and after it. §12's claim that there is "only a perspectival seeing" is a thesis about knowledge that the mechanism does not use and that <Ref lesson="perspectivism">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs against the falsification thesis and against relativism. And §28's "only meaning so far" raises the question §§23–27 answer about the person who has just diagnosed it: from what ideal is the *Genealogy* itself written, and whether science, the ideal's apparent antagonist, is its latest form; <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the will-to-truth lesson</Ref> reads that answer.

<Exercise>
Write §15's pivot as an argument with two premises and a conclusion, in words. Then name, for the sufferer's sentence and for the priest's, the affect, the object the affect seeks, and the object the priest supplies; and state which of the three the priest leaves untouched.

<Solution>
*P1.* A sufferer's affect seeks a cause of the suffering, and specifically a guilty doer on whom the affect can be discharged, because the discharge deadens the pain (§15: "every sufferer searches instinctively for a cause of his suffering; more exactly, a doer; still more precisely, a guilty doer susceptible to suffering").

*P2.* Discharged on any object outside the sufferer, the affect would strike the healthy, whom §14 requires to be protected, or the herd, which it would blow up together with the herdsman (§§14–15).

*C.* The priest grants the premise and supplies the one object that discharges the affect without harm to the healthy or the herd: the sufferer himself. "Quite right, my sheep! someone must be to blame: but you yourself are this someone."

The affect is *ressentiment*, defined in I:10 as a valuation that begins with a No to something outside and here given its physiology as a demand for a narcotic through discharge. The object it seeks is a guilty doer outside the sufferer, "friends, wife, child", the healthy. The object the priest supplies is the sufferer. What the priest leaves untouched is the affect and its premise: "someone must be to blame" is affirmed word for word, "quite right", and the diversion changes the subject of the sentence and nothing else. That is why the treatise's shortest formula for the priest is *Richtungs-Veränderer*, changer of direction: the quantity is conserved and only its direction is altered, exactly as II:16's instincts were conserved and turned inward.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "The ascetic priest is the villain of the *Genealogy*; the Third Treatise is an attack on priests." Name the two sections that refute it, state what the treatise actually charges the priest with, and give the criterion by which the charge is a charge.

<Solution>
The claim fails at §13 and at §20. §13 says the priest, "this apparent enemy of life, this denier, actually belongs to the really great conserving and yes-creating forces of life": through him "life wrestles with death and against death", and his wish for another existence is "the fetter that binds him here" and makes him "a tool that must labour to create more favourable conditions for earthly existence". A villain is not a conserving force. §20 says the priest "prescribed it in the most implicit belief in its utility and indispensability, often enough almost collapsing in the presence of the pain which he created": he acts in good faith, and §17 grants that his consolations brought "genuine relief" in "countless cases". §11 had already said that the priest "will scarcely prove the happiest champion of his own ideal" and that "we shall rather have to help him to defend himself properly against ourselves".

What the treatise charges is the cost of the remedy. §17: he "combats only the actual suffering, not its cause, not the actual state of sickness"; §§19–21: the guilty means, the orgy of feeling licensed by guilt, "makes the ill more ill", and the reinterpretation of suffering as punishment turns "the sick man into 'the sinner'", who "never gets out of the circle of lines". The charge is a bill, not a motive.

The criterion by which it is a charge is health: the ranking of remedies in §§17–21 is by their effect on the sufferer's health, not on his suffering, and the treatise's ranking standard from the note to I:17, the good of the higher type against the majority, is what makes a remedy that relieves the majority and sickens it a cost. Without that standard the priest's regime is a success, which is how §20 reports it: "his kingdom had come". The claim would need §13 to be ironic, and §13's own sentence, "you understand me already", says it is not.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive, from §§14–15, why the redirection had to fall on the sufferer rather than on the priest or on the healthy. State two premises about the herd and the conclusion, then state what the derivation adds to the First Treatise's gap: an explanation of the victory that is not the weight of numbers.

<Solution>
**Premise 1 (the herd's affect).** The herd's *ressentiment*, discharged outward, would fall on the healthy: the sick are "the conspiracy of the sufferers against the sound and the victorious" (§14), and §14's whole demand is that the healthy be kept from that discharge, since it is the mechanism by which "the sick make the healthy sick".

**Premise 2 (the herd's cohesion).** The priest rules by holding the herd together, and inside the herd *ressentiment* "accumulates and accumulates" as "the most dangerous blasting-stuff"; "getting rid of this blasting-stuff in such a way that it does not blow up the herd and the herdsman, that is his real feat" (§15).

**Conclusion.** The affect cannot be discharged on the healthy (premise 1: it is what the priest exists to prevent, and a healthy type discharged on would answer with force, which the sick lose); it cannot be discharged on the priest (premise 2: he is the herdsman, and a herd that discharges on its herdsman disperses, which is the outcome §13's life-instinct exists to prevent); and it cannot be discharged inside the herd on other members (premise 2: that is the explosion). The only object left that satisfies the demand for a guilty doer, discharges the affect, and harms neither the healthy nor the herd's cohesion is the sufferer himself. The redirection is forced by elimination; "you yourself" is the last object standing.

**What it adds to the gap.** The First Treatise's victory could not be numbers, because the treatise's own standard ranks by the good of the few and I:16 shows the strong reviving whenever a Napoleon appears. The derivation gives a mechanism that does not count heads: the strong come to hold the values that condemn them not because many hold them but because, once II:16 has made the strong suffer from themselves, they are sufferers, and a sufferer searching for a guilty doer accepts the physician's verdict as a patient. The verdict is addressed to whoever is searching, and its acceptance is one sufferer at a time. Wallace's expressive reading explained why the *weak* adopted the inversion; §15 explains why the *strong* did, and the two explanations share no premise except the affect.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Boundary probe. Consider a sufferer whose suffering has an obvious external cause: a wound inflicted by a named enemy. Decide whether §15's mechanism applies to him: state what the mechanism needs, whether the priest's redirection would be possible or necessary in that case, and what the answer shows about the population §14 describes. Then grade §21's epidemiological evidence, the dancing manias and the witch trials, as evidence for the mechanism by the course's sourcing standard.

<Solution>
**What the mechanism needs.** §15 states it: the sufferer's search for a guilty doer is "the more pronounced the more ignorant they remain of the real cause of their feeling bad, the physiological cause". The mechanism runs on a suffering whose cause is not available to the affect: an internal, physiological depression that the sufferer cannot locate and so must invent an object for. §20 restates the condition: the sufferer is "not clear as to the why and the wherefore", "like an animal shut up in a cage", and consults the priest "in his desire for reasons".

**The wounded man.** His affect has its object: the named enemy, on whom it can be discharged "actually or in effigy". The premise "someone is to blame" is satisfied by the facts, and the discharge is revenge, which §15 lists in *ressentiment*'s family but which here needs no invention. The redirection is *possible*, since the priest can always add "and you deserved the wound", but it is not *necessary*: the affect has somewhere to go, nothing accumulates, and the herd is not endangered by it. The priest's art has no purchase because there is no gap in the causal story for his verdict to fill. That is also why the mechanism is not the reflex of the decapitated frog: the frog's movement has an object, the acid, and §15 insists the difference from *ressentiment* "is fundamental".

**What it shows about §14's population.** The herd is defined by the kind of suffering it has: "sufferers from themselves" (§13), whose "real cause" is physiological and unknown to them. The mechanism is not a theory of suffering in general but of suffering without an available cause, and §14's "the sick" are exactly the population for whom that is the standing condition. The wounded man is a member of the herd only if his wound is not what he suffers from; the strong who accept the priest's verdict are, on II:16's account, exactly such cases, since what they suffer from is their own enclosed cruelty and not any enemy. The boundary confirms the closure of the gap rather than threatening it: the strong become patients only when their suffering has no outward object.

**Grading §21.** By the course's sourcing standard, records / argues / omits, §21 *records* that dancing manias, witch persecutions and mass death-cravings occurred where the "penance and redemption-training" had been established; it *argues* that the training caused them ("we find, in consequence of"); it *omits* any base rate, any case where the training was established and the epidemics absent, and any competing cause, of which §17 itself had listed several, diet, disease, climate, class-mixture. The evidence therefore establishes that the phenomena are compatible with the mechanism and were selected by it; it cannot establish the mechanism, since every case is chosen by the conclusion and none is controlled. Its use is the one §17 proposes for religion generally: as the documentation that "in certain places in the world there was almost bound to prevail" a physiological depression that was handled by "moral psychology", which is a claim about the *existence* of the herd and not about the priest's causal role in its epidemics.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Rank the three treatises' contributions, I (the inversion by *ressentiment*), II (bad conscience and the debt to God), III (the priest and the redirection), as necessary, enabling and precipitating conditions of the slave revolt's victory over the strong, and defend the ranking from the sections. Then state which of the three the First Treatise's own argument lacked, and where nihilism enters the account.

<Solution>
**Necessary: II.** Without II:16's enclosure and internalization the strong do not suffer from themselves, and a strong type that does not suffer is not searching for a guilty doer; §15's verdict has no addressee. Everything the Third Treatise does presupposes a population of "sufferers from themselves" (§13), and among the strong that population exists only because of the walls. II is necessary in the strict sense: remove it and no mechanism acting on the strong from inside exists.

**Enabling: I.** *Ressentiment* is the affect the priest redirects, and the inversion is the content the verdict carries: "you yourself are to blame" is a moral verdict only under a code on which the natural inclinations are evil, which I:7–14 coined. I supplies the material and the vocabulary; it does not, by itself, reach the strong, which is the gap. It enables the victory by making a guilty doer's guilt mean what the priest needs it to mean.

**Precipitating: III.** The priest is the agent whose act turns a suffering strong type into a patient: §15's sentence, delivered, is the event. §20 dates the effect, "for a few thousand years", and §14 describes it in the happy, who "begin one day to be ashamed of their happiness". Without the priest, II's inward cruelty stays what II:23 shows it was among the Greeks, folly and not sin, and I's inversion stays the valuation of a class the strong can ignore.

The ranking can be defended against the alternative that makes I necessary: the affect is necessary for the *content* of the victory, but a suffering enclosed animal could have been given another verdict, II:23's Greek gods being the control case, so I is necessary for the victory's being *this* morality and not for the strong's being defeated by some morality. The alternative that makes III merely enabling fails on §20's own claim that "sin" was "the greatest event in the history of the diseased soul": the treatise dates the change to the priest's act.

**What the First Treatise lacked** is the necessary condition: a mechanism acting on the strong from inside. Leiter's gap, restated from the ressentiment lesson, is that I:1–17 explains the coinage and not the acceptance, and the acceptance requires a sufferer, which I cannot produce and II can.

**Where nihilism enters.** Not as the victory's cause but as its remainder. §14 names "the will to nothingness, nihilism" as what the union of nausea and pity would breed, and §28 says the ideal "closed the door to suicidal nihilism" while directing the saved will against life. The account therefore uses nihilism twice, as the danger the ideal defends against and as the form the defence takes, and neither use is an endorsement: the treatise's author names nihilism as the "maximum of monstrousness" in the one section in which the word first appears, which is the sentence the nihilist misreading cannot accommodate.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Map Zarathustra's "On Priests" (Z II) onto GM III:11–15 line by line: which sentences correspond to the priest as sick among the sick, which to the redirection, and which have no argued counterpart. Then state what the speech adds with "my blood is related to theirs" that the treatise's "we" does not say at §15, and where the treatise does say it.

<Solution>
**Sick among the sick.** "Many of them suffered too much, so they want to make others suffer" maps onto §15's physician who "must first wound" and §14's sufferers who "thirst after being hangmen". "These shepherds, too, still belonged among the sheep" is §15's "he must himself be sick, he must be kith and kin to the sick". "Nothing is more vengeful than their humility" is §14's counterfeit of virtue, the "revengeful ones disguised as judges". "Their spirit drowned in their pitying" is §14's great pity for man, named there as one of the two plagues.

**The redirection.** "Their faith commands: 'Up the stairs on your knees, you sinners!'" and "who created such caves and stairs of penitence?" correspond to §20's sinner and to the "scourge, the hairy shirt, the starving body, contrition"; "they knew no other way to love their God than to nail the human being to a cross" is the guilt of II:21 as the priest's instrument. But no line of the speech contains the sheep's sentence or the priest's answer: the speech has the *product* of the redirection, penitents on their knees, and not the operation.

**No argued counterpart.** "Oh that someone would yet redeem them from their redeemer" has none: GM III has no wish for the priest's rescue, only the demand (§14) that the healthy stay away from him. "I suffer and suffered with them; to me they are prisoners and marked men" has none, and contradicts §14, which names pity for man as the affect that breeds nihilism; the speech's sympathy is what the treatise's analysis forbids the healthy. "The spirit of these redeemers consisted of gaps; but into every gap they had plugged a delusion, their stopgap, whom they named God" corresponds to nothing in §§11–15 and to §20's sufferer "in his desire for reasons" only loosely. And the speech drops the whole apparatus: no herd protected from the healthy, no wall, no ranking of remedies, no §13 in which the priest is a conserving force.

**What "my blood is related to theirs" adds.** At §15 the treatise's "we" is the diagnostician's: "we have and hold with both our hands the essence of the ascetic priest". Zarathustra's sentence claims kinship of type between the speaker and the priest, first person, and adds a demand, "I want to know that my blood is honored even in theirs", that the diagnosis be also a self-recognition. The treatise does say it, in two places and later: §20's "probably even we ourselves are still 'too good' for our work, probably we ourselves are none the less its victims", which concedes infection and not kinship; and §§23–27, where "we knowers", the men of science and the author among them, are found to be the ideal's "latest and noblest form", which is kinship argued rather than declared. The speech states at once, as blood, what the argued treatise reaches by its last sections as a conclusion about the will to truth, and it states it with an affect, pity, that the argument has excluded; that is what the dramatization adds and what it undercuts in the same sentence.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Forward. §28 says the ascetic ideal was "the only meaning so far" (*bisher der einzige Sinn*). Write the question this raises about the person who has just diagnosed it: from what ideal is the *Genealogy* itself written? Then find §23's opening move on science as the ideal's "natural antagonist" and state, in one sentence, what would have to be true for science to be that antagonist.

<Solution>
**The question.** If every willing so far "has taken its direction from the ascetic ideal", and the ideal is what a declining life does with suffering, then the *Genealogy*'s own willing, the will to know what ascetic ideals mean, has a direction too, and §8 already said that a type which benefits from an ideal is not "an uncorrupted witness and judge" of it. The question is whether the diagnosis is written from outside the ideal, from a counter-ideal, or from inside it, from one of its forms; and §28's "so far" says no counter-ideal has yet existed, which leaves only the second.

**§23's move.** §23 asks where the ideal's opposition is: "The ascetic ideal expresses one will: where is the opposition will, in which an opposition ideal expresses itself?" It then names the candidate everyone would name, modern science, "the natural antagonist of the ascetic ideal", and answers at once that science is nothing of the kind: "it is not the opposite of the ascetic ideal but rather its latest and noblest form", because science, where it is a will and not merely a method, "rests on a faith", the faith in the value of truth, and "that faith is still the ascetic ideal itself".

**What would have to be true.** For science to be the ideal's antagonist, its will to truth would have to be a valuation science had grounded rather than inherited: a reason for wanting truth at any price that did not itself presuppose that truth is worth more than life. §24 says that no such grounding exists, "the will to truth requires a critique", and §27 that Christianity's own truthfulness is what destroyed Christian dogma and must now destroy Christian morality by asking what its will to truth means. The lesson after next, <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the will-to-truth lesson</Ref>, reconstructs §§23–27 with GS 344 and BGE 1–4 as the argument that the antagonist is the ideal's latest form, and states what the argument does to the *Genealogy*'s own method: the diagnostician of §§11–22 is writing from the ideal he diagnosed, and the treatise says so.
</Solution>

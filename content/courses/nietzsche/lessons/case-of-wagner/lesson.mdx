Nietzsche dedicated *The Birth of Tragedy* to Richard Wagner as the man who would bring tragedy back, and sixteen years later wrote *The Case of Wagner* to prove that the same music was a symptom of the disease he had named decadence.

The reversal concerns no stranger. Wagner (1813–1883), the composer of *Tristan and Isolde* and the four-opera cycle *The Ring of the Nibelung*, was the great friendship of Nietzsche's twenties, and *The Birth of Tragedy* (BT) was written in his orbit. In 1888, five years after Wagner's death, Nietzsche published a short, mocking pamphlet that calls him "a disease". The obvious explanations are personal: the listener's taste changed; Nietzsche, the pagan, could not forgive Wagner's turn to Christianity in his last opera, *Parsifal*; or he came to hate what Wagner's festival at Bayreuth, a small town in northern Bavaria, had become. If one of them is the whole story, the pamphlet is a document about the end of a friendship, and its talk of decadence is a grudge in medical dress.

Nietzsche claims the opposite. The pamphlet's preface says that a philosopher "is not free to pass him by", because "Wagner is modernity in concentrated form". That is the claim that a body of music can be read as evidence about a whole culture: the culture whose highest values, on <Ref lesson="nihilism" id="decadence-the-weakness-behind-passive-nihilism">the account of the previous lesson</Ref>, devalue themselves, and whose declining life Nietzsche calls *decadence*. Two things have to be settled to assess it. Is there a way of judging an artwork as a symptom that is more than a judgment of taste? And does *The Case of Wagner* (CW) actually use it, section by section? The previous lesson ended on the question that supplies the method, asked of every work of art: "is it hunger or superabundance that has here become creative?"

## 1871 and 1888: one music, two verdicts

What exactly did Nietzsche believe about Wagner when he wrote BT, and what did he later stop believing?

### The hope of 1871

BT opens with a foreword addressed to Wagner, dated "Basel, end of the year 1871":

<Quote source="BT, Foreword to Richard Wagner, trans. W. A. Haussmann">
These earnest ones may be informed that I am convinced that art is the highest task and the properly metaphysical activity of this life, as it is understood by the man, to whom, as my sublime protagonist on this path, I would now dedicate this essay.
</Quote>

The hope was specific. BT had argued that Greek tragedy was a Dionysian chorus seeing an Apollonian vision, and that Socrates' optimism killed it (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-chorus-and-the-vision">the chorus and the vision</Ref>). Its last sections look for a rebirth in modern Germany and find it in music:

<Quote source="BT 19, trans. W. A. Haussmann">
Out of the Dionysian root of the German spirit a power has arisen which has nothing in common with the primitive conditions of Socratic culture … namely, *German music* as we have to understand it, especially in its vast solar orbit from Bach to Beethoven, from Beethoven to Wagner. … And when did we require these highest of all teachers more than at present, when we experience *a re-birth of tragedy*?
</Quote>

On BT's theory, music copies the will itself rather than any appearance of it (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="intoxication-the-individual-dissolved">intoxication: the individual dissolved</Ref>). So Wagner's music was the Dionysian power speaking, and his music drama, music joined to a myth and a hero who make it bearable, was tragedy come back.

### Three explanations that fail

Each of the three explanations meets a text that contradicts it, and every one of those texts is Nietzsche's own account, written in 1888 in his autobiography *Ecce Homo* (EH) and in *Nietzsche contra Wagner* (NcW), a selection of his earlier passages on Wagner. Testimony that late, from an interested party, cannot settle what actually caused a break that happened in the 1870s. What it does settle is the criterion he applied in 1888: whatever the cause, the verdict he published is not argued from taste, from *Parsifal*'s religion or from Bayreuth.

**Taste.** In EH, written in the autumn of 1888, the same season the pamphlet appeared, Nietzsche writes of *Tristan* as no former admirer who had lost the taste would:

<Quote source="EH, 'Why I Am So Clever' 6, trans. W. Kaufmann">
But to this day I am still looking for a work that equals the dangerous fascination and the gruesome and sweet infinity of *Tristan*—and look in all the arts in vain. … I think I know better than anyone else of what tremendous things Wagner is capable … and given the way I am, strong enough to turn even what is most questionable and dangerous to my advantage and thus to become stronger, I call Wagner the great benefactor of my life.
</Quote>

**Parsifal.** The legend has the pagan break with Wagner because Wagner turned Christian in *Parsifal* (1882), in which a chaste "pure fool" heals the wounded king of the knights of the Grail. Nietzsche's dating rules it out as his reason. He put the break six years before the premiere: "By the summer of 1876, during the time of the first Festspiele, I said farewell to Wagner in my heart" (NcW, "How I Broke Away from Wagner" 1). The text of *Parsifal* reached him in 1878, at the very time he sent his own *Human, All Too Human* to Bayreuth: "This crossing of the two books—I felt as if I heard an ominous sound—as if two swords had crossed" (EH, on *Human, All Too Human* 5). On his account *Parsifal* confirmed a break already made; NcW calls it "the proof".

**Bayreuth.** The festival did repel him, and he says why. "Wagner had been translated into German! The Wagnerian had become master over Wagner.— German art. The German master. German beer" (EH, on *Human, All Too Human* 2). What he "never forgave" was that Wagner "became *reichsdeutsch*" (EH, "Why I Am So Clever" 5): a patriot of the German Empire founded under Prussia in 1871. And NcW adds that once in Germany Wagner "had condescended step by step to everything I despise—even to anti-Semitism". Yet *Ecce Homo* refuses to call any of this the cause:

<Quote source="EH, on Human, All Too Human 3, trans. W. Kaufmann">
What reached a decision in me at that time was not a break with Wagner: I noted a total aberration of my instincts of which particular blunders, whether Wagner or the professorship at Basel, were mere symptoms. … It was then, too, that I first guessed how an activity chosen in defiance of one's instincts, a so-called "vocation" for which one does not have the least vocation, is related to the need for deadening the feeling of desolation and hunger by means of a narcotic art—for example, Wagnerian art.
</Quote>

On this account the exhausted listener was Nietzsche himself: a young professor of classical philology at Basel, in a post he had no vocation for, using Wagner's music as a tired man uses a drug. The break, so described, came when he stopped needing the drug and saw what he had been taking it for.

**What the texts say instead.** On his account the music did not change, and neither did its power over him. What changed was what he took it to be evidence of: in 1871 a Dionysian power, by the 1880s a remedy for a particular kind of need, his own included. *The Gay Science* (GS) says so in a section of 1887, looking back on BT:

<Quote source="GS 370, trans. W. Kaufmann">
In the same way, I reinterpreted German music for myself as if it signified a Dionysian power of the German soul … You see, what I failed to recognize at that time both in philosophical pessimism and in German music was what is really their distinctive character …
</Quote>

<KeyFact>
A verdict about what a work is used for does not depend on whether its critic enjoys it. That is why the verdict of 1888 can condemn a work its author still calls unequalled, and why anyone can check it against the works without sharing his taste.
</KeyFact>

## Hunger or superabundance: a test for art

What is the new question, and why should it be more than a new taste? GS 370 starts from the premise BT shared with Schopenhauer, that art answers to suffering (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-wisdom-of-silenus">the wisdom of Silenus</Ref>), and then splits the sufferers in two:

<Quote source="GS 370, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Every art, every philosophy may be viewed as a remedy and an aid in the service of growing and struggling life; they always presuppose suffering and sufferers. But there are two kinds of sufferers: first, those who suffer from the *over-fullness of life*—they want a Dionysian art and likewise a tragic view of life, a tragic insight; and then those who suffer from the *impoverishment of life* and seek rest, stillness, calm seas, redemption from themselves through art and knowledge, or intoxication, convulsions, anaesthesia, and madness. … He that is richest in the fullness of life, the Dionysian god and man, cannot only afford the sight of the terrible and questionable but even the terrible deed and any luxury of destruction, decomposition, and negation. … Conversely, those who suffer most and are poorest in life would need above all mildness, peacefulness, and goodness in thought as well as deed—if possible, also a god who would be truly a god for the sick, a healer and savior; also logic, the conceptual understandability of existence—for logic calms and gives confidence—in short, a certain warm narrowness that keeps away fear and encloses one in optimistic horizons.
</Quote>

### Two kinds of suffering

BT asked how art makes suffering bearable. GS 370 asks which suffering a work answers, and the answer can come from either end of strength. The person who suffers from over-fullness has more force than outlets: his trouble is tension, and he wants an art that shows the terrible, because he can stand it and it gives his strength something to meet. The person who suffers from impoverishment lacks force: his trouble is the burden of living at all, and he wants an art that lifts the burden off. Both suffer, and both turn to art. What they need from it is opposite.

### Reading a work backwards

GS 370 then names its method:

<Quote source="GS 370, trans. W. Kaufmann">
… my eye grew ever sharper for that most difficult and captious form of *backward inference* in which the most mistakes are made: the backward inference from the work to the maker, from the deed to the doer, from the ideal to those who need it, from every way of thinking and valuing to the commanding *need* behind it. Regarding all aesthetic values I now avail myself of this main distinction: I ask in every instance, "is it hunger or superabundance that has here become creative?"
</Quote>

A medicine cabinet shows how the inference runs. A shelf of sleeping pills, painkillers and sedatives says something about whoever stocked it that a shelf of climbing ropes and running shoes does not. Each object is made for a need, and the need can be read off the object without interviewing its owner. The backward inference treats an artwork the same way. Ask what the work does to its audience, and what state the audience must be in for that to be welcome. A work that offers rest, release from oneself, intoxication or a saviour is made for someone who needs those things, and that need is the "hunger" that has become creative in it.

The obvious way to sort art would be by what it wants, permanence or change, and GS 370 finds each desire ambiguous. Destruction can come from "an overflowing energy that is pregnant with future", or from "the hatred of the ill-constituted, disinherited, and underprivileged", the anarchists of <Ref lesson="nihilism" id="the-two-named-responses">the previous lesson</Ref>. The wish to make things last can come from "gratitude and love", as in Rubens, the Flemish painter of crowded, exuberant seventeenth-century canvases, the Persian poet Hafiz or Goethe, or from "the tyrannic will of one who suffers deeply", who takes revenge on all things "by forcing his own image, the image of his torture, on them". That last case gets a name, the "distinctive character" the 1887 section said he had missed in German music:

<Quote source="GS 370, trans. W. Kaufmann">
This last version is *romantic pessimism* in its most expressive form, whether it be Schopenhauer's philosophy of will or Wagner's music: romantic pessimism, the last great event in the fate of our culture.
</Quote>

<Remark title="Romantic pessimism">
*Romantic pessimism* is art or philosophy made from hunger: a deep suffering that needs rest, redemption from itself or intoxication, and imposes the image of its suffering on everything as a law. Its opposite, which GS 370 calls *Dionysian pessimism*, comes from superabundance and can afford to look at the terrible without needing to be saved from it.
</Remark>

The word *Dionysian* has changed sides. In BT it named the intoxication in which the individual dissolves, and Wagner's music was its modern voice. In GS 370 it names the strength that can afford the terrible, and intoxication, "convulsions, anaesthesia", appears on the other list, among the needs of the impoverished. <Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="what-goes-the-metaphysics-and-the-comfort">BT's 1886 preface</Ref> had already dropped the hopes for German music as romanticism; GS 370 says what the word means once Wagner no longer owns it.

## The pamphlet read in order

What does the test find when it is run on Wagner? The pamphlet is short enough to read whole: *The Case of Wagner: A Musician's Problem*, subtitled "A Letter from Turin, May 1888", published in Leipzig in the autumn of 1888 and written for German readers, most of whom revered Wagner. Its motto, *ridendo dicere severum*, means "to say what is serious through laughter". It has a preface, twelve numbered sections, two postscripts and an epilogue, and nearly every page is mockery with an argument inside it.

### Preface: the diagnostician is a patient

The preface begins by saying who is writing:

<Quote source="CW, Preface, trans. T. Common">
<Original lang="de">
Womit also hat er seinen härtesten Strauß zu bestehn? Mit dem, worin gerade er das Kind seiner Zeit ist. Wohlan! Ich bin so gut wie Wagner das Kind dieser Zeit, will sagen ein décadent: nur daß ich das begriff, nur daß ich mich dagegen wehrte. Der Philosoph in mir wehrte sich dagegen.
</Original>

With what then does the philosopher have the greatest fight? With all that in him which makes him the child of his time. Very well then! I am just as much a child of my age as Wagner—*i.e.*, I am a decadent. The only difference is that I recognised the fact, that I struggled against it. The philosopher in me struggled against it.
</Quote>

Three things are fixed before Wagner is examined. First, the diagnosis includes its author: not "you are sick and I am well" but "we had the same illness, and I saw it". Second, the subject is larger than music: "My greatest preoccupation hitherto has been the problem of *decadence* … 'Good and evil' form only a playful subdivision of this problem. … Morality *denies* life." Third, the reason for choosing Wagner: "Through Wagner modernity speaks her most intimate language … one has almost calculated the whole of the value of modernity once one is clear concerning what is good and evil in Wagner."

<Quote source="CW, Preface, trans. T. Common">
The greatest event of my life took the form of a *recovery*. Wagner belongs only to my diseases.
</Quote>

### Sections 1–2: *Carmen* as the control case

The pamphlet's first move is a comparison. Georges Bizet's *Carmen* (1875), an opera about a Spanish soldier who deserts for a gypsy woman and kills her when she leaves him, is held up against Wagner:

<Quote source="CW 1, trans. T. Common">
"All that is good is easy, everything divine runs with light feet": this is the first principle of my æsthetics. … In short: this music assumes that the listener is intelligent even as a musician,—thereby it is the opposite of Wagner … Bizet makes me productive. Everything that is good makes me productive. I have gratitude for nothing else, nor have I any other touchstone for testing what is good.
</Quote>

The touchstone is GS 370's test applied to the listener: a work made from abundance asks for intelligence and gives energy. Bizet is a foil, not a rival in rank (the preface admits "it is not malice alone which makes me praise Bizet"). *Carmen* is the control case: fatal passion and a killing that need nothing from the audience except attention. Its love is "the *mortal hatred* between the sexes" (CW 2), love as a fate and a war, not as a rescue.

### Section 3: "someone always wants to be redeemed"

Against that control, section 3 names what every Wagner opera turns on:

<Quote source="CW 3, trans. T. Common">
<Original lang="de">
Wagner hat über nichts so tief wie über die Erlösung nachgedacht: seine Oper ist die Oper der Erlösung. Irgendwer will bei ihm immer erlöst sein: bald ein Männlein, bald ein Fräulein – dies ist sein Problem.
</Original>

Wagner pondered over nothing so deeply as over salvation: his opera is the opera of salvation. Someone always wants to be saved in his operas, now it is a youth; anon it is a maid, this is *his problem*.
</Quote>

Then comes a list of seven cases, each a joke about one plot. *Erlösung*, rendered here "salvation", is the Christian word for redemption.

<Figure
  src="./figures/redemption-catalogue.svg"
  alt="A table of seven rows with columns the work, the one saved, the saviour, and CW 3's words. The Flying Dutchman: the Dutchman, the sea's 'eternal Jew'; Senta, faithful unto death; 'he marries, that is to say, he concerns us no longer'.   caption="Section 3's catalogue: seven plots, one shape."

The catalogue is comic, and its claim is serious: the dramatic engine of the works is a need to be saved. The section then turns the joke on the sexes:

<Quote source="CW 3, trans. T. Common">
The "Flying Dutchman" preaches the sublime doctrine that woman can moor the most erratic soul, or to put it into Wagnerian terms "save" him. Here we venture to ask a question. Supposing that this were actually true, would it therefore be desirable?—What becomes of the "eternal Jew" whom a woman adores and *enchains*? He simply ceases from being eternal, he marries, that is to say, he concerns us no longer.—Transferred into the realm of reality, the danger for the artist and for the genius—and these are of course the "eternal Jews"—resides in woman: *adoring* women are their ruin. … In many cases of woman's love, and perhaps precisely in the most famous ones, the love is no more than a refined form of *parasitism* …
</Quote>

The "eternal Jew" is the Wandering Jew of Christian legend, condemned to roam the earth until the Last Judgement; Wagner's Dutchman, a captain condemned to sail the seas until a woman's faithful love releases him, is a sea-going version of him. Nietzsche makes the wanderer the type of the artist or genius, and the opera's "salvation" his domestication. "Woman" plays two roles in the argument: the redeemer of the plots and the audience they are written for. The claim about women is meant as stated, one instance of a view he held throughout his mature books.

After a last joke, that Wagner would have "saved" Goethe too, section 3 names the value all these rescues serve:

<Quote source="CW 3, trans. T. Common">
In short: *Parsifal*. … The philosopher writes thereto an epilogue: *Holiness*—the only remaining higher value still seen by the mob or by woman, the horizon of the ideal for all those who are naturally short-sighted.
</Quote>

**What the section shows, read with the module.** First, the plots answer to the need GS 370 assigned to the impoverished: "a god for the sick, a healer and savior". Second, the saviours are a girl, a knight, a fool, a woman's prayer. The God who guaranteed redemption is absent from most of the works, and the hunger for redemption is intact. That is the shadow of God from <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="the-shadow-and-the-starlight">the shadow and the starlight</Ref>: the need survives the belief that answered it, and the opera house satisfies it without the belief. Third, section 3 notices what *Lohengrin* asks of its audience. The knight saves Elsa on condition she never asks his name, so "'Lohengrin' contains a solemn ban upon all investigation and questioning. In this way Wagner stood for the Christian concept, 'Thou must and shalt *believe*'." The culture that lost God lost him to <Ref lesson="nihilism" id="two-thousand-years-of-training-in-truthfulness">truthfulness</Ref>, the habit of asking what grounds a belief; the redemption opera asks its audience to stop asking.

### Section 4: the *Ring* runs aground on Schopenhauer

Section 4 tells one more redemption story, in which "it is Wagner himself who is saved". Its subject is the *Ring*, four operas drawn from Norse and German myth. Wotan, ruler of the gods, governs by contracts carved on his spear, and his order is paid for with stolen gold. His grandson Siegfried, a hero raised without fear, shatters the spear and wins Brünnhilde as his bride. Siegfried is betrayed and murdered, Brünnhilde rides into his funeral pyre, and the fire consumes the gods.

Wagner wrote the text of the cycle between 1848 and 1852 as a revolutionary: in 1849 he took part in one of the uprisings in the German states and fled into exile. In 1854, with only the first opera set to music, he read Schopenhauer. In section 4's words, he had "believed in the *Revolution* as only a Frenchman could have believed in it", France being the country of 1789, and asked where the evil in the world came from:

<Quote source="CW 4, trans. T. Common">
From "old contracts": he replied, as all revolutionary ideologists have done. In plain English: from customs, laws, morals, institutions … This Siegfried does. … For a long while Wagner's ship sailed happily along this course. … The ship dashed on to a reef; Wagner had run aground. The reef was Schopenhauer's philosophy … and he translated the "Ring" into Schopenhauerian language. Everything goes wrong, everything goes to wrack and ruin, the new world is just as bad as the old one:—Nonentity, the Indian Circe beckons … Brunnhilda … must first versify the fourth book of "The World as Will and Idea." *Wagner was saved.* … It was the *philosopher of decadence* who allowed the *artist of decadence* to find himself.
</Quote>

The fourth book of Schopenhauer's main work ends in the denial of the will, the cure in which the wise man stops wanting (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-verdict-and-the-way-out">the verdict, and the way out</Ref>); "the Indian Circe" is the nothingness of Buddhist release. The ending did not change: the gods still burn. What changed is what the burning means. In the revolutionary reading, Siegfried and Brünnhilde are "the dawn of the golden age, the twilight of the Gods of old morality"; in the Schopenhauerian one, the fire is a world giving itself up. A Yes to a new world was rewritten into a No to every world: the weary No of passive nihilism, "its most celebrated form Buddhism" (notebook 9[35]), which BT had already met as "a Buddhistic negation of the will" (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="each-alone-fails">each alone fails</Ref>). Section 4 shows a single work taking the turn.

### Section 5: decadence at work

Section 5 drops the jokes and states the diagnosis:

<Quote source="CW 5, trans. T. Common">
*The artist of decadence.* That is the word. And here I begin to be serious. … Is Wagner a man at all? Is he not rather a disease? Everything he touches he contaminates. *He has made music sick.* … For the mere fact that no one guards against him is in itself already a sign of decadence. Instinct is weakened, what ought to be eschewed now attracts. … To recognise what is harmful as harmful, to be able to deny oneself what is harmful, is a sign of youth, of vitality. That which is harmful lures the exhausted: cabbage lures the vegetarian. Illness itself can be a stimulus to life but one must be healthy enough for such a stimulus! Wagner increases exhaustion—*therefore* he attracts the weak and exhausted to him.
</Quote>

This is the previous lesson's definition of decadence, an organism that "prefers what is disadvantageous for it" (A 6), applied to an audience (<Ref lesson="nihilism" id="decadence-the-weakness-behind-passive-nihilism">decadence</Ref>). The argument runs from the listeners: an art that weakens, and that the weak crave, shows by who is drawn to it what it feeds, as the sick choose the régime that keeps them sick. The next sentence sets a boundary. "Illness itself can be a stimulus to life", but only for someone "healthy enough": the same art that sinks the exhausted can strengthen the strong. That is EH's "strong enough to turn even what is most questionable and dangerous to my advantage", and it is why the diagnosis and the gratitude do not contradict each other.

Section 5 then uses the medical vocabulary of the 1880s, in which French and German doctors were classifying hysteria, neurosis and "degeneration":

<Quote source="CW 5, trans. T. Common">
*Wagner est une névrose.* … Wagner is the *modern artist par excellence*, the Cagliostro of modernity. All that the world most needs to-day, is combined in the most seductive manner in his art, the three great stimulants of exhausted people: *brutality*, *artificiality* and *innocence* (idiocy). … Only with morbid music can money be made to-day; our big theatres live on Wagner.
</Quote>

Cagliostro was an eighteenth-century impostor who passed as a healer and magician. The clinical words are symptom-readings, not diagnoses Nietzsche was trained to make: they name observable features of the works and their effects (convulsive emotion, overstimulation, heroes and heroines who belong in "a hospital ward"), and they borrow medicine's authority to do it.

The previous lesson's account of passive nihilism predicted this: when the spirit weakens, "everything which revives, heals, soothes, benumbs comes to the fore in a variety of disguises: religious, or moral or political or aesthetic" (notebook 9[35]). Section 5 is the aesthetic disguise, examined in one artist.

### Sections 6–7: the style of decadence

Is decadence only in the plots and the audience, or in how the music is built? Section 6 imagines Wagner's success turned into a teacher giving advice to young composers: overwhelm the audience, elevate it, make it wonder vaguely. "Above all, no thoughts! … The *colour of the melody is* all-important here, *the melody itself* is of no importance. … *First principle*: melody is immoral. *Proof*: 'Palestrina'. *Application*: 'Parsifal.'" Palestrina, the sixteenth-century master of church music, stands for the holy music without tunes; the rules put effect before structure. Section 7 then states the general form:

<Quote source="CW 7, trans. T. Common">
<Original lang="de">
Womit kennzeichnet sich jede literarische décadence? Damit, daß das Leben nicht mehr im Ganzen wohnt. Das Wort wird souverän und springt aus dem Satz hinaus, der Satz greift über und verdunkelt den Sinn der Seite, die Seite gewinnt Leben auf Unkosten des Ganzen – das Ganze ist kein Ganzes mehr. Aber das ist das Gleichnis für jeden Stil der décadence: jedesmal Anarchie der Atome, Disgregation des Willens, »Freiheit des Individuums«, moralisch geredet – zu einer politischen Theorie erweitert »gleiche Rechte für alle«.
</Original>

How is *decadence* in *literature* characterised? By the fact that in it life no longer animates the whole. Words become predominant and leap right out of the sentence to which they belong, the sentences themselves trespass beyond their bounds, and obscure the sense of the whole page, and the page in its turn gains in vigour at the cost of the whole,—the whole is no longer a whole. But this is the formula for every decadent style: there is always anarchy among the atoms, disaggregation of the will,—in moral terms: "freedom of the individual,"—extended into a political theory "*equal* rights for all." … The whole no longer lives at all: it is composed, reckoned up, artificial, a fictitious thing.
</Quote>

Take a paragraph by a writer in love with his own phrases. Each sentence is built around one dazzling word, each sentence is quotable, and the paragraph goes nowhere: you remember the phrases and not the point. That is the structure section 7 describes, and it applies it to Wagner's scores, praising and damning in one observation:

<Quote source="CW 7, trans. T. Common">
If we wish to admire him, we should observe him at work here: how he separates and distinguishes, how he arrives at small unities, and how he galvanises them, accentuates them, and brings them into pre-eminence. But in this way he exhausts his strength … Once more let it be said that Wagner is really only worthy of admiration and love by virtue of his inventiveness in small things, in his elaboration of details,—here one is quite justified in proclaiming him a master of the first rank, as our greatest musical *miniaturist* who compresses an infinity of meaning and sweetness into the smallest space.
</Quote>

The miniaturist and the decadent are the same finding. The life of the music is in its fragments, "a host of short fragments of from five to fifteen bars each", and the large forms do not grow out of them: Wagner, section 7 says, "disguised his inability to create organic forms, under the cloak of a principle", his "dramatic style", in which the stage action, not the music's own development, decides what comes next. The formula then climbs two levels: a morality of the "freedom of the individual" releases each person from service to anything larger, and a politics of "equal rights for all" makes a society of equal parts with no order of rank. Whether that political claim holds is argued in <Ref lesson="herd-morality-in-modern-dress">herd morality in modern dress</Ref>; here it is only said to have the same form as a style.

<KeyFact>
Decadence in CW 7 is a structural fact, not a mood: the part overruns the whole. It can be checked in a page of prose or a score without knowing anything about the author's health.
</KeyFact>

### Sections 8–12: the actor

If Wagner is a miniaturist, why does his art overwhelm crowds? Sections 8 to 12 answer: the dominant instinct in him was the actor's.

<Quote source="CW 8, trans. T. Common">
Wagner never calculates as a musician with a musician's conscience, all he strains after is effect, nothing more than effect. … A man is an actor when he is ahead of mankind in his possession of this one view, that everything which has to strike people as true, must not be true. This rule was formulated by Talma: it contains the whole psychology of the actor, it also contains—and this we need not doubt—all his morality. Wagner's music is never true.

—But it is supposed to be so: and thus everything is as it should be.
</Quote>

François-Joseph Talma was the great French tragic actor of Napoleon's time. His rule says that what convinces on a stage is calculated for the audience, not true to the thing shown. Section 9 finds it in Wagner's plots, built backward from the overwhelming scene: "he begins with the third act".

The line after "never true" carries the condition. A calculated effect works only on people who take it for the real thing: once a listener sees the third act being prepared, it stops overwhelming him. So the actor needs an audience that does not judge for itself, and a section of GS from 1887 says the theatre produces one:

<Quote source="GS 368, trans. W. Kaufmann">
There one is common people, audience, herd, female, pharisee, voting cattle, democrat, neighbor, fellow man; there even the most personal conscience is vanquished by the leveling magic of the great number.
</Quote>

In a crowd the single listener's judgment gives way to the crowd's response, and an illusion that each would see through alone passes unseen. This is where the actor's illusion parts from the one BT defended. The Apollonian dream is illusion known to be illusion: the dreamer says "It is a dream! I will dream on!" (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="dream-the-beautiful-individual">dream: the beautiful individual</Ref>). The actor's illusion works only so long as it is not known to be one, which is why it needs the herd.

Section 11 names the herd Wagner's stage found: "*Germans!*… The definition of a German: an obedient man with long legs", whose Empire rose with Wagner as "a proof of one and the same thing—obedience and long legs". The politics is taken up in <Ref lesson="nietzsche-and-politics">Nietzsche and politics</Ref>.

Section 12 keeps BT's distinction between the two illusions and turns it into a demand, the last lines of the letter:

<Quote source="CW 12, trans. T. Common">
*That the stage should not become master of the arts.*

*That the actor should not become the corrupter of the genuine.*

*That music should not become an art of lying.*
</Quote>

### The postscripts: "Salvation from the Saviour"

The first postscript repeats one sentence as a refrain, "One pays dearly for having been a follower of Wagner", and begins with the Germans' early resistance to him: "People guarded themselves against him as against an illness,—not with arguments—it is impossible to refute an illness". That parenthesis is the method of <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="discrediting-how-a-belief-dies-without-being-refuted">discrediting</Ref> applied to a taste: one does not refute it, one explains what makes it attractive without its being good. Then an anecdote from Wagner's funeral in 1883:

<Quote source="CW, Postscript, trans. T. Common">
The first Wagner Society, the one in Munich, laid a wreath on his grave with this inscription, which immediately became famous: "Salvation to the Saviour!" Everybody admired the lofty inspiration which had dictated this inscription … Many also, however (it was singular enough), made this slight alteration in it: "Salvation *from* the Saviour"—People began to breathe again—
</Quote>

The society was quoting the last words of *Parsifal*, sung as the curtain falls: *Erlösung dem Erlöser*, redemption to the Redeemer.

The postscript's centre is *Parsifal*:

<Quote source="CW, Postscript, trans. T. Common">
He flatters every nihilistic (Buddhistic) instinct and togs it out in music; he flatters every form of Christianity, every religious expression of decadence. He that hath ears to hear let him hear: everything that has ever grown out of the soil of impoverished life, the whole counterfeit coinage of the transcendental and of a Beyond found its most sublime advocate in Wagner's art … In the art of seduction "Parsifal" will for ever maintain its rank as a stroke of genius.… I admire this work. I would fain have composed it myself. … The subtlety with which beauty and disease are united here, reaches such a height, that it casts so to speak a shadow upon all Wagner's earlier achievements: it seems too bright, too healthy.
</Quote>

*Parsifal* ends with the wounded king healed by the holy spear, the chaste fool raised to guard the Grail, and the knights' communion on Good Friday. So *Parsifal* holds the whole diagnosis at once. The "counterfeit coinage of the transcendental and of a Beyond" is the previous lesson's second premise, values that measure this world by another (<Ref lesson="nihilism" id="the-hidden-premise-values-that-measured-this-world-by-another">the hidden premise</Ref>); the sacrament staged for an audience that has given up the faith is the shadow of God; the "nihilistic (Buddhistic) instinct" is passive nihilism's wish for rest. The praise is not irony. The diagnosis needs the work to be a masterpiece of seduction, because a weak work would explain nothing about why a culture loves it.

<Figure src="./figures/parsifal-grail-temple.jpg" alt="Painted stage design of a vast domed sanctuary: rows of marble columns with gilded capitals recede under dark, gold-patterned vaults; through a central arch a lit upper gallery rings the dome, and on the floor below sits a low circular platform with steps at its centre, the place of the knights' communion." caption="The Grail temple as the Russian painter Paul von Joukowsky designed it for the first Parsifal, Bayreuth 1882: the setting of the work in which Nietzsche found beauty and disease united, the Christian sacrament done in gold for an audience that no longer believed in it." />

The Second Postscript refuses to make Wagner the cause of anything, and pairs him with Bernini, the seventeenth-century sculptor of marble in theatrical motion:

<Quote source="CW, Second Postscript, trans. T. Common">
Things are generally bad. Decay is universal. Disease lies at the very root of things. If Wagner's name represents the ruin of music, just as Bernini's stands for the ruin of sculpture, he is not on that account its cause. All he did was to accelerate the fall … He possessed the ingenuousness of decadence: this constituted his superiority. He believed in it. He did not halt before any of its logical consequences. The others hesitated—that is their distinction. They have no other.
</Quote>

It runs GS 370's test on Johannes Brahms too, then promoted as Wagner's opposite ("he thirsts after abundance"), and ends on the age as a whole. Wagner is chosen not as the worst case but as the most complete one.

### The Epilogue: the standard stated

Only at the end does the pamphlet state the standard it has been using:

<Quote source="CW, Epilogue, trans. T. Common">
According to the measure of energy of every age, there is also a standard that determines which virtues shall be allowed and which forbidden. The age either has the virtues of *ascending* life, in which case it resists the virtues of degeneration with all its deepest instincts. Or it is in itself an age of degeneration, in which case it requires the virtues of declining life … Æsthetic is inextricably bound up with these biological principles: there is decadent æsthetic, and *classical* æsthetic,—"beauty in itself" is just as much a chimera as any other kind of idealism. Within the narrow sphere of the so-called moral values, no greater antithesis could be found than that of *master-morality* and the morality of *Christian* valuations: the latter having grown out of a thoroughly morbid soil … the master-morality ("Roman," "pagan," "classical," "Renaissance"), on the other hand, being the symbolic speech of well-constitutedness, of *ascending* life, and of the Will to Power as a vital principle. Master-morality *affirms* just as instinctively as Christian morality *denies* ("God," "Beyond," "self-denial,"—all of them negations). … "World" is a Christian term of abuse.
</Quote>

Ascending against declining life is the standard of the revaluation the previous lesson ended on (<Ref lesson="nihilism" id="a-new-standard-ascending-life">a new standard</Ref>), here applied to art. *Master-morality* is Nietzsche's name for the valuation of the strong, who call themselves good first; the pamphlet uses the term without arguing for it, which <Ref lesson="master-morality">master morality</Ref> does. The next sentences seem to undercut the whole pamphlet:

<Quote source="CW, Epilogue, trans. T. Common">
These antithetical forms in the optics of values, are *both* necessary: they are different points of view which cannot be circumvented either with arguments or counter-arguments. One cannot refute Christianity: it is impossible to refute a diseased eyesight. … The concepts "true" and "untrue" do not seem to me to have any sense in optics. That, alone, which has to be guarded against is the falsity, the instinctive duplicity which *would fain* regard this antithesis as no antithesis at all: just as Wagner did … To cast side-long glances at master-morality, at *noble* morality (—Icelandic saga is perhaps the greatest documentary evidence of these values), and at the same time to have the opposite teaching, the "gospel of the lowly," the doctrine of the *need* of salvation, on one's lips!
</Quote>

So the final charge is not that Wagner is a decadent. The Preface said the author is one too. The charge is falsity. The *Ring* takes its gods and its Siegfried from the Icelandic sagas, the record of a noble and affirming morality, and Wagner set them to a gospel of redemption. The Christian, by contrast, is honest:

<Quote source="CW, Epilogue, trans. T. Common, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Das Bedürfnis nach Erlösung, der Inbegriff aller christlichen Bedürfnisse hat mit solchen Hanswursten nichts zu tun: es ist die ehrlichste Ausdrucksform der décadence, es ist das überzeugteste, schmerzhafteste Ja-sagen zu ihr in sublimen Symbolen und Praktiken. Der Christ will von sich loskommen.
</Original>

The need of salvation, the quintessence of all Christian needs, has nothing in common with such clowns; it is the most honest expression of decadence, the most convinced and most painful affirmation of it, in sublime symbols and practices. The Christian wishes *to be rid* of himself.
</Quote>

The last paragraph widens the charge to everyone: modern man "says yea and nay in one breath. … we are *false*." It closes: "this essay, as you observe, was inspired by gratitude."

## The strongest objection: a diagnosis of the doctor's old friend

A careful critic has two charges against the method, one from logic and one from the pamphlet itself. The first: judging a work by the health of its maker is the genetic fallacy that <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="discrediting-is-a-genetic-fallacy">the death of God</Ref> already met, inferring a thing's worth from how it arose, and here the origin is a composer's nervous system, which nobody can inspect. Nietzsche himself wrote in 1887, in *On the Genealogy of Morals* (GM), that "one does best to separate an artist from his work, not taking him as seriously as his work" (GM III 4). The second: the Epilogue says the two "optics of values", the standpoints of master-morality and of Christian morality, are "both necessary", that they cannot be met with arguments, and that "true" and "untrue" have no sense in optics. If so, the condemnation of Wagner has no ground left to stand on.

### The first charge: a fallacy about origins

**The evidence is in the works.** GS 370's inference runs "from the ideal to those who need it", and nearly everything CW offers as evidence can be checked without Wagner's medical history: most of the seven plots turning on someone being saved (section 3), a cycle rewritten from revolt to resignation (4), what the audience is drawn by (5), small unities galvanised in a whole that does not grow (7), scenes built backward from their effect (9). The talk of nerves is an addition the argument does not need. And GM III 4, read on, does not forbid the inquiry; it assigns it: "Insight into the origin of a work concerns the physiologists and vivisectionists of the spirit; never the aesthetic man, the artist!" CW is written by the vivisectionist, who calls Wagner the modern soul's "most *instructive* case". The aesthetic man in Nietzsche goes on finding *Tristan* unequalled.

**It discredits; it does not refute.** CW does call the music false: "Wagner's music is never true" (CW 8), and it must not become "an art of lying" (CW 12). But the falsity is the actor's, not a false statement's. A proposition is false when the world is not as it says, and showing that is refutation. Music states nothing that the world could fail to match. What is false in Wagner, on CW's account, is an effect passing for what it is not: a scene built backward from its climax passing for drama, a stimulant for the exhausted passing for depth. That falsity is not exposed by counter-evidence but by explaining the effect, and an explained effect stops working.

That is the method of <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="discrediting-how-a-belief-dies-without-being-refuted">discrediting</Ref>, and it meets the genetic-fallacy charge as it did when the charge was put against <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="discrediting-is-a-genetic-fallacy">the death of God</Ref>. The fallacy infers that a thing is false or bad from where it came from. CW's target is not the music's worth taken alone but a conviction of its listeners: that being overwhelmed by it is evidence of its depth. Discrediting that conviction takes two steps. First, the conviction has no independent support: being overwhelmed is all the evidence the Wagnerite offers. Second, the overwhelming is fully explained without the depth, by stimulants that work on any exhausted listener (CW 5) and by effects calculated in advance (CW 8–9). The listener's rapture then proves nothing about the music, and the beauty is left standing.

### Same origin, opposite verdicts

The genetic fallacy would condemn Wagner for his origin, a decadent constitution. The Preface gives the author the same origin and the opposite verdict: both are decadents, and the difference is "that I recognised the fact, that I struggled against it". If the origin decided, the two would stand or fall together. Since they do not, what CW judges is what each does with the condition, which is a judgment about the work, not its source.

### The second charge: optics

What is an *optics of values*? In the Epilogue's sense it is the angle from which a kind of life sees what is valuable: declining life sees through the Christian valuations, ascending life through the noble ones, and neither can be argued out of what it sees, any more than "a diseased eyesight" can be refuted. Grant that, and the pamphlet's two charges against Wagner fare differently.

**The charge of falsity survives.** It does not say that one optic is right. It says that Wagner holds two at once while passing for one, the saga heroes set to the gospel of redemption. Checking that needs no place outside both optics, only a comparison of the work with itself. The Epilogue applies it evenly: the Christian, who holds one optic consistently, is acquitted as "the most honest expression of decadence".

**The charge of decadence does not.** Most of the pamphlet says something else: that Wagner's art serves declining life, that he "has made music sick". That is not a finding about consistency. It ranks one optic above the other, and on the Epilogue's own terms the ranking is made from inside one of them. Its own image shows this: an eyesight is "diseased" only by the measure of a healthy one, and the measure is ascending life's. "Decadent" and "classical" aesthetic are what ascending life calls the two; the declining optic, for which holiness is still "the only remaining higher value" (CW 3), sees in the same works its best art. Either the verdict is a report from one optic, which the other cannot be argued out of, or it claims that ascending life is the better standard, and the "both necessary" line has just removed the ground for that claim.

### The gap

So the reply saves the charge of falsity and leaves the verdict of decadence resting on one premise: that ascending and declining life is a standard by which one optic can be ranked above the other, and that a style can be read as the sign of one or the other. That is exactly what the "optics" passage puts in doubt, and the pamphlet asserts it, in the Epilogue and in NcW ("aesthetics is nothing but a kind of applied physiology"), without arguing it. CW's evidence shows that Wagner's art serves the need for rest and redemption. That serving this need marks declining life depends on a psychology in which a person is many drives that can turn against each other.

<Conclusion>
You can now say what changed between 1871 and 1888: on Nietzsche's own account, not the music or its hold on him but the question put to it, "is it hunger or superabundance that has here become creative?", and you can run that test through *The Case of Wagner* in its own order and say which part of the diagnosis each part of it puts to work.

The case also shows what the diagnosis becomes when it meets an art. It was built for beliefs, and music asserts nothing, so in Wagner it reads what a work supplies instead of what it claims, and discredits instead of refuting: the shadow of God becomes a need met for an evening without the belief, decadence and passive nihilism become features of an audience, a score and one rewritten ending. Two things depart from that picture: the diagnostician is a former patient who still loves the drug, and the standard the verdict of decadence needs, ascending against declining life, reaches past anything the module has argued.

You can state the strongest objection in its two charges and say what survives it: the charge of falsity, which compares a work with itself, and not, without a further premise, the charge of decadence. That premise is the next problem. The diagnosis keeps saying that instincts weaken one another and that a will can "disaggregate", which presupposes a person made of more than one will; <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">the self as a society of drives</Ref> supplies that picture.
</Conclusion>

For each entry, say whether "someone wants to be saved" describes the plot, is a stretch, or is a joke on something else.

<Solution>
**Holds.** *Tannhäuser*: a sinner redeemed by a pure woman's intercession; "innocence has a preference for saving interesting sinners" is a fair summary. *The Flying Dutchman*: the whole plot is the search for a redeeming woman. *Parsifal*: Kundry longs for release and is released by the chaste fool; the opera's last words are about redemption. *Lohengrin*: Elsa is rescued, and the condition "never ask" is the ban on questioning section 3 picks out. *Ring*: reading the end of Wotan's compromised order as his "salvation" is an interpretation, but the ending is a release from a corrupted order.

**A stretch.** *Tristan and Isolde*: Isolde is not saved by Tristan in any ordinary sense; the lovers seek release in death together, and "married women also like to be saved by a knight" fits only if the love-death counts as rescue. CW 3 seems to know this: its second joke about *Tristan* changes the subject to King Marke, "the perfect husband" who "can ask only one question".

**A joke on something else.** *The Mastersingers*: Eva is not in need of redemption; the comedy turns on a contest. The point of "a knight who also happens to be a Wagnerite" is that Walther's new song, which breaks the guild's rules and wins, stands for Wagner's music winning against its critics.

**Verdict.** Five of seven entries hold, one fits loosely, one satirises a different target. The comic form exaggerates the catalogue's regularity; its main claim survives the check.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A boundary probe for GS 370. A composer, deaf and ill, writes in his last years string quartets of great austerity, without consolation in their texts (they have none) and without any offer of rest. Another artist, healthy and prosperous, writes sentimental works whose every ending promises release from the burdens of life. (a) Which is "hunger" and which "superabundance" on GS 370's test? (b) What does this show about the relation between the artist's suffering and the classification? (c) Which sentence of CW 5 states the same boundary?

<Solution>
**(a)** The test asks what the work supplies and what state its audience must be in to want it. The austere quartets offer no rest, redemption or anaesthesia; they demand concentration and can be borne only by a listener with strength to spare. They fall on the side of superabundance, though their maker suffered greatly. The sentimental works supply exactly what GS 370 lists for the impoverished, "rest, stillness, calm seas, redemption from themselves"; they fall on the side of hunger, though their maker suffered little.

**(b)** The classification does not measure how much the artist suffered. Every art presupposes "suffering and sufferers"; what separates the two kinds is what the work does with the suffering, shows the terrible or relieves its audience of life. A healthy person can make hunger-art for the hungry; a sick one can make art of superabundance. That is why the backward inference runs "from the ideal to those who need it", not from the biography.

**(c)** "Illness itself can be a stimulus to life: but one must be healthy enough for such a stimulus!" (CW 5). Illness is a fact about a person; what decides is what the person's strength makes of it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The Epilogue condemns Wagner not for decadence but for "falsity, the instinctive duplicity" of casting glances at noble morality while preaching "the doctrine of the *need* of salvation". Compare this with the incomplete nihilism of <Ref lesson="nihilism" id="the-third-response-incomplete-nihilism">the previous lesson</Ref>. Is Wagner, on CW's account, an incomplete nihilist in art? Derive the answer, and say how the Christian of the Epilogue differs from both.

<Solution>
**Incomplete nihilism.** Notebook 10[42]: "Attempts to escape nihilism without revaluating our values so far: they produce the opposite, make the problem more acute." Its plainest form keeps the morality and drops its ground, as George Eliot did.

**Wagner on CW's account.** His works keep the Christian need, redemption, chastity, pity ("Wagner had the virtue of *décadents*, pity", CW 7), and the beyond, while dropping, in most of them, the God who grounded it (the Epilogue: "If Wagner were a Christian, then Liszt was perhaps a Father of the Church!"). That is the structure of incomplete nihilism: the old values without their ground, offered as a way out of the emptiness. It "makes the problem more acute" as 10[42] predicts: the need for redemption is fed and refined while nothing can answer it. The duplicity adds a second evasion: it avoids admitting even which values it holds.

**The Christian.** The believer of the Epilogue keeps both the values and their ground. His need of salvation is "the most honest expression of decadence", a consistent No to the self ("The Christian wishes *to be rid* of himself"). He is not a nihilist in the previous lesson's sense, since his world behind the world is still standing for him; on Nietzsche's standard he is decadent, but not false.

**Result.** Yes: CW's Wagner is the incomplete nihilist of art, keeping the shadow of God and selling it as an experience. The ranking that follows from the Epilogue is honest decadence (the believer), then the "ingenuousness of decadence" that at least draws its consequences (Wagner, Second Postscript), then the Epilogue's "Christian gentleman", whose "innocence in contradiction" it calls modern "*par excellence*".
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
CW 5 says that in decadence "instinct is weakened, what ought to be eschewed now attracts", and CW 7 calls the decadent style a "disaggregation of the will". Take both literally. What must be true of a person for an instinct to be weakened by another, for something harmful to attract him, and for his will to come apart? Sketch the picture of the self these sentences need, and contrast it with Plato's soul in which reason rules the passions.

<Solution>
**What the sentences presuppose.** For a person to be drawn by what harms him, there must be in him more than one thing that wants: something that is drawn and something whose interest it harms. For an instinct to be "weakened", it must have a strength that can be greater or less relative to others. For a will to "disaggregate", it must be an aggregate in the first place: a combination of parts that, in health, pull in one direction and, in decadence, pull apart, like the words and sentences of CW 7's page.

**The picture.** The self is a plurality of drives, each with its own direction and strength, and "the will" is the order among them when one direction governs. Health is a well-ordered plurality; decadence is one whose order has broken down, so that what serves one part (the craving for stimulation, for rest) harms the whole.

**Against Plato.** Plato's soul has a ruler, reason, distinct in kind from the passions and able to judge them from outside. In CW's picture what governs is itself a drive or a coalition of drives, and a "weakened instinct" can be corrected only by a different order among them, not by an outside judge: the premise <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="reading-a-section-as-an-argument-bge-68">BGE 68</Ref> already presupposed, no judge in the mind that has no stake.

**Where the course takes it.** *Beyond Good and Evil* states it outright, the soul as "a social structure of the drives and affects" (BGE 12) and willing as a commanding among many "under-wills" (BGE 19). That is the subject of <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">the self as a society of drives</Ref>, which supplies the psychology the CW diagnosis assumes.
</Solution>

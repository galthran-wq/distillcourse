Most of what we do, think and feel goes on without our being conscious of it; if so, why does consciousness exist at all, and what can it show us about ourselves? The question comes straight out of <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">the self as a society of drives</Ref>, which described a person as many drives in an order of rank, with the intellect as "the blind instrument of another drive" (D 109) and only "the last scenes of reconciliation and the final accounting" of their contests reaching awareness (GS 333). Consciousness does not feel like a late reporter of contests held elsewhere. It feels like the whole self: the place where I weigh reasons and decide, and the one thing I see without distortion.

That feeling has a long pedigree. Plato's reason is the part of the soul that sees; Christianity placed the conscience inside, where God sees the heart and the believer examines himself before confession; and René Descartes, the seventeenth-century French philosopher, made "I think" the first certainty and declared the mind easier to know than any body. On that picture, looking inward is the best evidence a person has about himself, and a psychology of drives working out of sight is contradicted by the plainest testimony there is.

So two questions need answers. What is consciousness for, if the deciding is done below it? And what can it show us of ourselves, given what it is for? The obvious answer to the first is that consciousness exists for knowing: so that we see the world and ourselves as they are and deliberate in that light. That answer only works if knowing, deliberating and acting need consciousness. *The Gay Science* (GS) section 354, written in 1887 for the book's added fifth part, begins by denying that they do.

## Life without the mirror: what consciousness is not needed for

The question has force only once one sees how much of mental life goes on without consciousness, and GS 354 opens by showing it:

<Quote source="GS 354, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The problem of consciousness (more precisely, of becoming conscious of something) confronts us only when we begin to comprehend how we could dispense with it … For we could think, feel, will, and remember, and we could also "act" in every sense of that word, and yet none of all this would have to "enter our consciousness" (as one says metaphorically). The whole of life would be possible without, as it were, seeing itself in a mirror. Even now, for that matter, by far the greatest portion of our life actually takes place without this mirror effect; and this is true even of our thinking, feeling, and willing life, however offensive this may sound to older philosophers. For what purpose, then, any consciousness at all when it is in the main superfluous?
</Quote>

### What "consciousness" means here

The parenthesis fixes the topic: not being awake, not sensing, but *becoming conscious of something*, a state of mine presented to me as mine. The image is a mirror. A man can be angry without that; he becomes conscious of his anger when the anger appears to him as an object, "I am angry", which he could note, consider, or report. Everything GS 354 goes on to say concerns this doubling.

### A case: the man who fell at eleven

*Daybreak* (D, 1881) section 119 gives a case from Nietzsche's own life:

<Quote source="D 119, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
One day recently at eleven o'clock in the morning a man suddenly collapsed right in front of me as if struck by lightning, and all the women in the vicinity screamed aloud; I myself raised him to his feet and attended to him until he had recovered his speech — during this time not a muscle of my face moved and I felt nothing, neither fear nor sympathy, but I did what needed doing and went coolly on my way. Suppose someone had told me the day before that tomorrow at eleven o'clock in the morning a man would fall down beside me in this fashion — I would have suffered every kind of anticipatory torment, would have spent a sleepless night, and at the decisive moment instead of helping the man would perhaps have done what he did. For in the meantime all possible drives would have had time to imagine the experience and to comment on it.
</Quote>

Everything the situation required got done: the man was seen, judged to need help, lifted and tended. None of it came with a conscious state of fear or sympathy, and the section adds the reverse case: with a day's warning, the conscious rehearsal would have made the action worse. Skilled action does not wait for the mirror, and sometimes the mirror gets in its way.

### Late and unfinished

An earlier section of the same book, GS 11 (1882), had already drawn the conclusion about consciousness's place:

<Quote source="GS 11, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Consciousness is the last and latest development of the organic and hence also what is most unfinished and unstrong. Consciousness gives rise to countless errors that lead an animal or man to perish sooner than necessary … If the conserving association of the instincts were not so very much more powerful, and if it did not serve on the whole as a regulator, humanity would have to perish of its misjudgments and its fantasies with open eyes …
</Quote>

GS 11 goes on to name the inherited view's mistake: "One thinks that it constitutes the kernel of man; what is abiding, eternal, ultimate, and most original in him." Organisms lived, fed, fled and cooperated long before anything like reflective consciousness appeared; whatever it is, it arrived last, on top of a life that was already working.

## Why consciousness exists: the need to be understood

If thinking and acting do not need the mirror, something else must have. GS 354 offers what it calls "the perhaps extravagant surmise", in two moves.

### A proportion, then a surmise

The first move is a proportion: "the subtlety and strength of consciousness always were proportionate to a man's (or animal's) capacity for communication, and … this capacity in turn were proportionate to the need for communication." Where people were forced "for a long time to communicate and to understand each other quickly and subtly", the capacity accumulated across generations. The second move turns the proportion into an origin:

<Quote source="GS 354, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Supposing that this observation is correct, I may now proceed to the surmise that consciousness has developed only under the pressure of the need for communication; that from the start it was needed and useful only between human beings (particularly between those who commanded and those who obeyed); and that it also developed only in proportion to the degree of this utility. Consciousness is really only a net of communication between human beings; it is only as such that it had to develop; a solitary human being who lived like a beast of prey would not have needed it. … As the most endangered animal, he needed help and protection, he needed his peers, he had to learn to express his distress and to make himself understood; and for all of this he needed "consciousness" first of all, he needed to "know" himself what distressed him, he needed to "know" how he felt, he needed to "know" what he thought.
</Quote>

The steps run as follows. A weak animal survives only in a group. A group helps only a member whose need it understands: the one who is hungry, hurt or afraid must signal which, and a group that acts together must pass orders and reports between "those who commanded and those who obeyed". To say what distresses you, you must first pick the distress out and fix it as something that can be said. That picking-out is what "knowing" one's own state means in the quotation, and it is what GS 354 calls becoming conscious. The solitary predator, which never has to report anything, has no use for it.

<Remark title="Herd">
*Herd* (German *Herde*, in the 1887 spelling *Heerde*) is the animal group seen as a unit of survival: members who live, move and are protected together, and who must coordinate to do so. GS 354 uses "social" and "herd" side by side for the human group seen this way, as a biological fact about a weak animal.
</Remark>

### Words are the form consciousness takes

The surmise needs one more claim, and GS 354 states it next:

<Quote source="GS 354, trans. W. Kaufmann">
For, to say it once more: Man, like every living being, thinks continually without knowing it; the thinking that rises to consciousness is only the smallest part of all this — the most superficial and worst part — for only this conscious thinking takes the form of words, which is to say signs of communication, and this fact uncovers the origin of consciousness. In brief, the development of language and the development of consciousness (not of reason but merely of the way reason enters consciousness) go hand in hand.
</Quote>

Two details keep this from saying more than it does. Reason is not tied to language: thinking, inferring and judging go on "continually" without consciousness, and what grew with language is only "the way reason enters consciousness". And language here is wider than speech: "not only language serves as a bridge between human beings but also a mien, a pressure, a gesture." Any sign that carries a state from one animal to another counts.

## What a shared sign can carry: the average

A tool made for one job can often do others, so the origin of consciousness in communication need not limit what it shows. A word, it seems, can name anything, including what only I feel. The reply turns on what makes a sign work at all, and *Beyond Good and Evil* (BGE, 1886) section 268 spells it out:

<Quote source="BGE 268, trans. H. Zimmern">
Words are vocal symbols for ideas; ideas, however, are more or less definite mental symbols for frequently returning and concurring sensations, for groups of sensations. It is not sufficient to use the same words in order to understand one another: we must also employ the same words for the same kind of internal experiences, we must in the end have experiences in common. … In all souls a like number of frequently recurring experiences have gained the upper hand over those occurring more rarely: about these matters people understand one another rapidly and always more rapidly — the history of language is the history of a process of abbreviation; on the basis of this quick comprehension people always unite closer and closer. The greater the danger, the greater is the need of agreeing quickly and readily about what is necessary; not to misunderstand one another in danger — that is what cannot at all be dispensed with in intercourse.
</Quote>

### Why a sign carries only what is shared

When I tell you "I am afraid", the word works only if you connect it with an inner experience of your own of the same kind. So a word can transmit only what recurs often enough in many people to have been named, and what speaker and hearer both have. A state I alone have either has no word, or is given the word of the nearest common state, which the hearer fills with his own experience of that common state. Danger adds a second pressure, speed: the signs that survive are short and quick to grasp, "a process of abbreviation".

### Words exist for the extremes

*Daybreak* had found the same limit five years before BGE, from the side of the person describing himself:

<Quote source="D 115, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
… words really exist only for superlative degrees of these processes and drives; and where words are lacking, we are accustomed to abandon exact observation because exact thinking there becomes painful … Anger, hatred, love, pity, desire, knowledge, joy, pain — all are names for extreme states: the milder, middle degrees, not to speak of the lower degrees which are continually in play, elude us, and yet it is they which weave the web of our character and our destiny.
</Quote>

The section draws the consequence for self-knowledge at once: "We are none of us that which we appear to be in accordance with the states for which alone we have consciousness and words, and consequently praise and blame." A person forms his opinion of himself from the outbursts, "on the basis of data in which the exceptions outweigh the rule", and that opinion, "the so-called 'ego'", then becomes "a fellow worker in the construction of our character and our destiny".

### A translation, worked

The limit is easiest to see on a single state, taken apart and then said.

<Example title="A mixed feeling put into words">
**The situation.** You and a friend applied for the same post. You got it; he did not. That evening someone asks how you feel about him.

**What is there to report.** Taken apart as honestly as you can manage, the state has several parts at once, in proportions particular to this evening: sympathy (his disappointment hurts you), relief (it was not you), satisfaction (you won), unease (he may resent you), and a small fear (next time the verdict may go the other way).

**What the shared vocabulary offers.** Each part has a common word, but the state is not the parts: it is this blend, in these proportions, and no word names that. A sentence must pick. "I feel bad for him" picks the one part that has a short common word and that the hearer will recognise and approve. "I'm relieved it was him" and "I'm glad I won" are words that exist, and that praise and blame have made almost unsayable among peers.

**What was lost, and why no better word would save it.** The proportions are lost because a word that named them would have to be shared, and nobody else has had this blend. The approved part is kept because the vocabulary was shaped by many speakers over a long time, and it carries their valuations with it, D 115's "praise and blame". The sentence is not a lie. It is the herd's nearest sign for a state that was yours alone.

**The check.** The person asked, hearing "I feel bad for him", understands at once, which is what the sentence was for. And if you think "I feel bad for him" to yourself, the same translation has taken place, with yourself as the hearer.
</Example>

### GS 354's conclusion: the average

GS 354 draws the general conclusion. Here the original matters, for two words:

<Quote source="GS 354, trans. W. Kaufmann">
<Original lang="de">
Mein Gedanke ist, wie man sieht: daß das Bewußtsein nicht eigentlich zur Individual-Existenz des Menschen gehört, vielmehr zu dem, was an ihm Gemeinschafts- und Herden-Natur ist; daß es, wie daraus folgt, auch nur in bezug auf Gemeinschafts- und Herden-Nützlichkeit fein entwickelt ist, und daß folglich jeder von uns, beim besten Willen, sich selbst so individuell wie möglich zu verstehen, »sich selbst zu kennen«, doch immer nur gerade das Nicht-Individuelle an sich zum Bewußtsein bringen wird, sein »Durchschnittliches«, – daß unser Gedanke selbst fortwährend durch den Charakter des Bewußtseins – durch den in ihm gebietenden »Genius der Gattung« – gleichsam majorisiert und in die Herden-Perspektive zurück-übersetzt wird. Unsre Handlungen sind im Grunde allesamt auf eine unvergleichliche Weise persönlich, einzig, unbegrenzt-individuell, es ist kein Zweifel; aber sobald wir sie ins Bewußtsein übersetzen, scheinen sie es nicht mehr …
</Original>

My idea is, as you see, that consciousness does not really belong to man's individual existence but rather to his social or herd nature; that, as follows from this, it has developed subtlety only insofar as this is required by social or herd utility. Consequently, given the best will in the world to understand ourselves as individually as possible, "to know ourselves," each of us will always succeed in becoming conscious only of what is not individual but "average." Our thoughts themselves are continually governed by the character of consciousness — by the "genius of the species" that commands it — and translated back into the perspective of the herd. Fundamentally, all our actions are altogether incomparably personal, unique, and infinitely individual; there is no doubt of that. But as soon as we translate them into consciousness they no longer seem to be.
</Quote>

*Durchschnittlich*, "average", is a word of frequency: what most members have. That is why it does not contradict D 115: the peaks are what everyone has felt and has a word for, so in this sense they are the average too, and what drops out in both sections is the low, continuous, particular degree. "Governed" renders *majorisiert*, "outvoted": the thought is overruled by a majority, which is exactly the worked example, where the sentence chosen was the one the most speakers could share. The "genius of the species" is a phrase from <Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy">Schopenhauer</Ref>, who used it for the species' will steering individuals in love toward its own ends; GS 354 turns it into the species' interest commanding what a thought may become.

### "Phenomenalism and perspectivism as I understand them"

The section then names what it has found:

<Quote source="GS 354, trans. W. Kaufmann">
This is the essence of phenomenalism and perspectivism as I understand them: Owing to the nature of animal consciousness, the world of which we can become conscious is only a surface- and sign-world, a world that is made common and meaner; whatever becomes conscious becomes by the same token shallow, thin, relatively stupid, general, sign, herd signal; all becoming conscious involves a great and thorough corruption, falsification, reduction to superficialities, and generalization.
</Quote>

"Made common and meaner" renders *verallgemeinerte, vergemeinerte*, "generalised, made *gemein*": the German *gemein* means both "common to many" and "low, ignoble", and the pair of words slides from the first sense to the second. *Phenomenalism*, as the sentence uses it, is the claim that what consciousness gives is a surface, appearances; *perspectivism* is the claim that the surface is seen from one angle, the herd's. That is all the word means in GS 354. The fuller doctrine that there is "only a perspective seeing" belongs to <Ref lesson="perspectivism">perspectivism</Ref>.

## The nearest is the farthest: what introspection reports

If consciousness renders a person in the herd's signs, the inner world, which the tradition held to be the best known of all, should be where a person is most systematically misled about himself.

### A commentary on a felt text

D 119, the section of the <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="what-a-drive-is-the-market-place-laughter">market-place laughter</Ref>, contains a theory of dreams that makes the point on a case where no one doubts that interpretation is going on:

<Quote source="D 119, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
Why was the dream of yesterday full of tenderness and tears, that of the day before yesterday humorous and exuberant, an earlier dream adventurous and involved in a continuous gloomy searching? Why do I in this dream enjoy indescribable beauties of music, why do I in another soar and fly with the joy of an eagle up to distant mountain peaks? These inventions, which give scope and discharge to our drives to tenderness or humorousness or adventurousness or to our desire for music and mountains … are interpretations of nervous stimuli we receive while we are asleep, very free, very arbitrary interpretations of the motions of the blood and intestines, of the pressure of the arm and the bedclothes, of the sounds made by church bells, weathercocks, night-revellers and other things of the kind. That this text, which is in general much the same on one night as on another, is commented on in such varying ways … the explanation of this is that today's prompter of the reasoning faculty was different from yesterday's — a different drive wanted to gratify itself …
</Quote>

The sleeper does not hear bells and then interpret them; he hears music. The commentary is all that reaches him, and it arrives with the conviction of a perception. D 119 then removes the boundary between sleep and waking:

<Quote source="D 119, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
… do I have to add that when we are awake our drives likewise do nothing but interpret nervous stimuli and, according to their requirements, posit their 'causes'? that there is no essential difference between waking and dreaming? … that our moral judgments and evaluations too are only images and fantasies based on a physiological process unknown to us, a kind of acquired language for designating certain nervous stimuli? that all our so-called consciousness is a more or less fantastic commentary on an unknown, perhaps unknowable, but felt text?
</Quote>

The *text* is what goes on in the body and among the drives: felt, never read. The *commentary* is what consciousness presents: a story written by whichever drive is at its height, and, by GS 354, written in the herd's signs, "a kind of acquired language". Six years separate the two sections. D 119 says who writes the commentary; GS 354 says in what language it must be written.

### The familiar is not the known

The method that seems safest fares worst. GS 355, the section after 354, asks what people mean by "knowing" something, and answers from a phrase heard in the street, "he knew me right away": to know is to reduce something strange to something familiar.

<Quote source="GS 355, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Isn't our need for knowledge precisely this need for the familiar, the will to uncover under everything strange, unusual, and questionable something that no longer disturbs us? Is it not the instinct of fear that bids us to know? … Even the most cautious among them suppose that what is familiar is at least more easily knowable than what is strange, and that, for example, sound method demands that we start from the "inner world," from the "facts of consciousness," because this world is more familiar to us. Error of errors! What is familiar is what we are used to; and what we are used to is most difficult to "know" — that is, to see as a problem; that is, to see as strange, as distant, as "outside us."
</Quote>

The inner world is the most familiar thing a person has, so by this account it is the hardest to see as a problem. Descartes's starting point, the mind as the best-known thing, is the kind of method GS 355 calls the "error of errors"; the natural sciences, the section ends, owe their certainty to having chosen "what is strange" for their object.

### "Everybody is farthest away from himself"

GS 335, a section of 1882, puts the result as a maxim:

<Quote source="GS 335, trans. W. Kaufmann">
How many people know how to observe something? Of the few who do, how many observe themselves? "Everybody is farthest away — from himself"; all who try the reins know this to their chagrin, and the maxim "know thyself!" addressed to human beings by a god, is almost malicious.
</Quote>

The German line is *Jeder ist sich selbst der Fernste*, "each is to himself the farthest". *Der Fernste* is the opposite of *der Nächste*, "the nearest", the word the German Bible uses for "the neighbour" one is commanded to love; the saying inverts a proverb, "each is nearest to himself". "Try the reins" is the biblical phrase for God searching the inner parts of a man. The god of Delphi, whose temple bore "know thyself", asked for what is hardest.

GS 335 then shows the failure on a case. A man explains what makes an action moral: he judges "this is right", infers "therefore it must be done", and does it. "You are speaking of three actions instead of one", comes the reply: the judging, the inferring and the doing. The man treated only the last as an action, with the first as the given that makes it moral. But "when you judge 'this is right,' that is an action, too", and an action can be done "in a moral and in an immoral manner", so the verdict needs an explanation of its own. The man's answer, that his conscience told him so, only moves the question back a step, and the section ends it there: the judgment "this is right" itself "has a pre-history in your instincts, likes, dislikes, experiences". The man reports the last scene, in the vocabulary his community uses for moral action, and takes it for the whole.

The self-report has an author as well as a language. In BGE 19, the governing drive says "I" and takes the credit for what the under-souls did (<Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="who-says-i-the-commonwealth-and-its-governing-class">*L'effet c'est moi*</Ref>). GS 354 adds that it says it in the herd's words. D 119's drive only wrote the story; BGE 19's also signs the deed as its own, so the report names the wrong agent as well as simplifying the act.

<KeyFact>
Introspection is not randomly unreliable; it errs systematically. It shows a person as more average than he is (GS 354), drawn from the peaks that everyone has words for (D 115), and as one reasoning self where many drives were at work (GS 335, BGE 19).
</KeyFact>

## The herd in the individual: BGE 268 and GS 116

GS 354 is about what one person can know of himself. BGE 268 runs the same mechanism across a population and many generations, and its conclusion concerns rank. The section's question is *Was ist zuletzt die Gemeinheit?*, "What, after all, is commonness?", with *gemein* again in both its senses, and the second half of the section answers it:

<Quote source="BGE 268, trans. H. Zimmern">
<Original lang="de">
Welche Gruppen von Empfindungen innerhalb einer Seele am schnellsten wach werden, das Wort ergreifen, den Befehl geben, das entscheidet über die gesammte Rangordnung ihrer Werthe, das bestimmt zuletzt ihre Gütertafel. Die Werthschätzungen eines Menschen verrathen etwas vom Aufbau seiner Seele, und worin sie ihre Lebensbedingungen, ihre eigentliche Noth sieht. Gesetzt nun, dass die Noth von jeher nur solche Menschen einander angenähert hat, welche mit ähnlichen Zeichen ähnliche Bedürfnisse, ähnliche Erlebnisse andeuten konnten, so ergiebt sich im Ganzen, dass die leichte Mittheilbarkeit der Noth, dass heisst im letzten Grunde das Erleben von nur durchschnittlichen und gemeinen Erlebnissen, unter allen Gewalten, welche über den Menschen bisher verfügt haben, die gewaltigste gewesen sein muss. Die ähnlicheren, die gewöhnlicheren Menschen waren und sind immer im Vortheile, die Ausgesuchteren, Feineren, Seltsameren, schwerer Verständlichen bleiben leicht allein, unterliegen, bei ihrer Vereinzelung, den Unfällen und pflanzen sich selten fort. Man muss ungeheure Gegenkräfte anrufen, um diesen natürlichen, allzunatürlichen progressus in simile, die Fortbildung des Menschen in's Ähnliche, Gewöhnliche, Durchschnittliche, Heerdenhafte — in's Gemeine! — zu kreuzen.
</Original>

Whichever groups of sensations within a soul awaken most readily, begin to speak, and give the word of command — these decide as to the general order of rank of its values, and determine ultimately its list of desirable things. A man's estimates of value betray something of the structure of his soul, and wherein it sees its conditions of life, its intrinsic needs. Supposing now that necessity has from all time drawn together only such men as could express similar requirements and similar experiences by similar symbols, it results on the whole that the easy communicability of need, which implies ultimately the undergoing only of average and common experiences, must have been the most potent of all the forces which have hitherto operated upon mankind. The more similar, the more ordinary people, have always had and are still having the advantage; the more select, more refined, more unique, and difficultly comprehensible, are liable to stand alone; they succumb to accidents in their isolation, and seldom propagate themselves. One must appeal to immense opposing forces, in order to thwart this natural, all-too-natural *progressus in simile*, the evolution of man to the similar, the ordinary, the average, the gregarious — to the ignoble!
</Quote>

### The argument, step by step

The middle sentences tie values to drives: the groups of sensations that "give the word of command" in a soul decide its "order of rank of its values", so a person's values show the structure of his drives, the order of rank among drives which, on <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="unity-is-an-achievement-heap-tyranny-commonwealth">the society-of-drives picture</Ref>, is what a person is. The rest is a selection argument. (1) Need drew together those who could signal their needs to one another. (2) Signalling succeeds only for experiences the signallers share: the average and common ones. (3) So those whose inner life is mostly average are understood, helped, and bound closer to others, while those whose experience is rare are misunderstood and left alone. (4) Alone, they meet accidents unhelped "and seldom propagate themselves". (5) Over generations, the type drifts toward the similar: *progressus in simile*, "progress toward the like".

The conclusion is meant at full strength. The "more select, more refined, more unique" are the higher type, and the common is the "ignoble"; the section's own title asks what commonness is, and answers that it is the product of the most powerful force that has acted on mankind. Nothing in the section treats the ordinary as an equal alternative to the rare. It asks for "immense opposing forces" to be called up against the drift, which is a demand that the rare be protected and bred against the average. What those forces could be, and what society they would need, belongs to <Ref lesson="order-of-rank">the order of rank</Ref>.

### Morality as herd instinct

GS 116 (1882) had already given the herd's valuations a name:

<Quote source="GS 116, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Wherever we encounter a morality, we also encounter valuations and an order of rank of human impulses and actions. These valuations and orders of rank are always expressions of the needs of a community and herd: whatever benefits it most — and second most, and third most — that is also considered the first standard for the value of all individuals. Morality trains the individual to be a function of the herd and to ascribe value to himself only as a function. … Morality is herd instinct in the individual.
</Quote>

<Remark title="Herd instinct">
The *herd instinct* is the drive in each member to conform, to obey, and to value what keeps the group together: an individual's valuations ranked by what benefits the community. "Morality is herd instinct in the individual" (GS 116) means that what a person feels as his own conscience is the group's needs, trained into him as a drive among his drives.
</Remark>

The link to consciousness is direct. The words in which a person thinks about what he ought to do are the herd's words, and they carry the herd's ranking of what matters: the worked example's "I feel bad for him" was chosen because the hearer would approve it. So the conscious voice that judges "this is right" in GS 335 speaks with the herd instinct's accent, whatever drive is using it. How that instinct came to rule a whole civilisation's values is the subject of <Ref lesson="herd-morality-in-modern-dress">herd morality in modern dress</Ref>.

## The argument in seven steps

Laid out with the sections each step rests on, the case has seven steps, and they are not equally strong.

<Figure
  src="./figures/argument-chain.svg"
  alt="Seven boxes in a column joined by arrows: 1 most thinking, feeling, willing and acting goes on without becoming conscious (GS 354, GS 333, D 119); 2 so the existence of consciousness needs an explanation of its own (GS 354); 3, dashed, the most endangered animal needed help, and had to say what it needed (GS 354), annotated 'a conjecture about prehistory, offered without evidence'; 4 saying needs signs that mean the same thing to speaker and hearer (BGE 268, GS 354), annotated in blue 'checkable today: try it on any word'; 5, amber, a state becomes conscious as it takes the form of such signs (GS 354), annotated 'asserted, not argued: the premise steps 6 and 7 cannot do without'; 6 so consciousness renders inner life in the shared vocabulary: what is common passes, the individual not (GS 354, D 115, BGE 268), annotated in blue 'checkable today: words exist for the extremes'; 7, bold, so introspection shows us as more average, simpler and more unified than we are (D 119, GS 335, GS 355)."
  caption="The argument reconstructed: its prehistory is a conjecture and its link between consciousness and signs is asserted; the steps about signs can be tested now."
/>

Step 1 is an observation, and D 119's man at eleven is an instance of it; step 2 follows. Step 3 is a conjecture about prehistory, GS 354's own "extravagant surmise". Steps 4 and 6 are claims about how signs work, which D 115 and BGE 268 check. Step 7 follows from 6 for anyone whose self-knowledge comes through conscious reflection.

The joint everything turns on is step 5. That becoming conscious of a state *is* putting it into communicable form, GS 354 states ("only this conscious thinking takes the form of words … and this fact uncovers the origin of consciousness") but does not argue. Steps 4 and 6 are about language; without step 5 they would say only that our *reports* average us away, and leave open a consciousness that sees the individual state and then loses it in the telling. Step 5 closes that gap by making consciousness and the telling the same act.

## The strongest objection: a story no one can check

A serious critic attacks steps 3 and 5 together. The account of the endangered animal is an evolutionary story with no evidence behind it, of a kind that can be told for any trait. And it seems to be refuted by what everyone sees: a dog in pain, an infant crying, are conscious, and neither has words. If consciousness exists without language, step 5 is false, and the conclusion about the average falls with it.

### What can be checked without the prehistory

The reply concedes the first charge and moves the weight off it: nothing in the conclusion depends on step 3 being true. Steps 4 and 6 hold of signs now, whatever their origin, and they are enough to show that every conscious *self-description* averages its subject away. D 115's vocabulary of extremes, BGE 268's "experiences in common", and GS 355's account of knowing as reducing to the familiar are claims about present practice that a reader can test on his own sentences, as the worked example did. The prehistory is offered as the explanation of these facts, and if it were dropped, the facts would remain to be explained by something else.

### The narrow reading

The second part of the reply returns to GS 354's opening parenthesis: the topic is "becoming conscious of something", the mirror. The dog's pain is a state the dog is in; whether the dog has its pain before it as *its pain*, picked out and fixed, is a different question, and that second thing is what GS 354 ties to signs. Nietzsche does not deny the first. GS 357, three sections later, praises Leibniz, the German philosopher (1646–1716) who held that the mind has countless perceptions too slight to be noticed:

<Quote source="GS 357, trans. W. Kaufmann">
… Leibniz's incomparable insight … that consciousness is merely an *accidens* of experience and not its necessary and essential attribute; that, in other words, what we call consciousness constitutes only one state of our spiritual and psychic world (perhaps a pathological state) and not by any means the whole of it.
</Quote>

On this reading the animal's pain belongs to the large psychic world that is not consciousness in GS 354's sense. And GS 354 itself does not deny animals consciousness of the narrow kind: its proportion holds for "a man's (or animal's) capacity for communication", so a social animal that signals alarm, submission or hunger to its group should have more of it than a solitary hunter.

### The gap

The narrow reading saves the claim by making it partly a definition. GS 354 decides that only the mirrored, sign-ready state counts as "consciousness", and does not argue that the dog's or the infant's felt pain is not what the word names; a critic who means by consciousness simply what it is like to be in pain finds his topic set aside, not refuted.

<Aside title="What later psychology found">
Experiments of the 1970s set out four identical pairs of stockings and asked shoppers to choose the best. Most chose the pair on the right; asked why, they cited quality, and none mentioned position, which had decided. People reported not the influence that moved them but the reason their culture would give for such a choice. Results of this kind, and the much older observation that most mental processing never reaches awareness, are what GS 354 would predict: a self-report that is a plausible commentary in shared terms. How far such findings bear on Nietzsche's psychology is taken up in <Ref lesson="drive-psychology-and-experiment">drive psychology and experiment</Ref>.
</Aside>

## Does consciousness do anything?

If consciousness is a surface that reports in borrowed words, one may conclude that it does nothing: that it watches the drives' work and narrates it. Some of Nietzsche's texts go that far, others do not, and this is where readers of him genuinely divide.

### Two readings

The strong reading takes its warrant from *Twilight of the Idols* (TI, 1888), in the chapter on the four great errors:

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 3, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The "inner world" is full of phantoms and will-o'-the-wisps: the will is one of them. The will no longer moves anything, hence does not explain anything either — it merely accompanies events; it can also be absent.
</Quote>

On this reading consciousness accompanies action as a spectator. The weaker reading points to texts in which consciousness is an organ with effects, weak and fallible but not idle. The *Genealogy of Morals* (GM, 1887) describes early humans enclosed in society, cut off from their old guides, "their regulating, unconscious and infallible drives", and so "reduced to their 'consciousness,' their weakest and most fallible organ!" (GM II 16). An organ that can fail is one that does something. GS 11 sets humanity "the task of incorporating knowledge and making it instinctive", which only makes sense if conscious knowledge can change the instincts. A notebook of April–June 1885 gives consciousness a positive job:

<Quote source="Notebook 34, April–June 1885, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge, p. 9">
Just as there are many things a general doesn't want to know, and must not know if he is to keep hold of his overall view, so in our conscious mind there must be above all a drive to exclude, to chase away, a selecting drive — which allows only certain facts to be presented to it. Consciousness is the hand with which the organism reaches out furthest: it must be a firm hand.
</Quote>

### What the surface shows does not depend on whether it acts

GS 354 sits between the two. It calls consciousness "in the main superfluous", which says unnecessary for most of life, not for all of it; and the need it explains, communication, is a real job with real effects on what the group does. The argument about the average needs only that: whatever consciousness does, what it *shows* is the communicable surface. Whether it also moves anything is a further question, which <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer behind the deed</Ref> and <Ref lesson="four-great-errors">the four great errors</Ref> take up.

One reading the texts rule out is the popular image of Nietzsche as an enemy of reason who wants consciousness silenced. GS 11's task is more knowledge, incorporated, and GS 354 calls consciousness unfinished, still "in the process" of developing, not worthless. The danger the texts do name is narrower. *Ecce Homo* (EH, written 1888) names it in the sentence just before its passage on the organizing "idea" that grows "deep down" and trains the other capacities before the task is known (<Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="the-commonwealth-strong-drives-in-an-order-of-rank">the commonwealth</Ref>): "The whole surface of consciousness — consciousness is a surface — must be kept clear of all great imperatives … So many dangers that the instinct comes too soon to 'understand itself.'" The danger is that the surface, speaking in the common words, names a task before the drives below have grown into it.

<Conclusion>
You can now say why, on Nietzsche's account, consciousness exists at all: thinking, feeling and acting go on without it, and what called it into being was the need of a weak, social animal to make its states understood, so that becoming conscious of a state is putting it into shared signs (GS 354). You can say why that makes consciousness show the average: a sign works only for what speakers share (BGE 268), words exist for the extremes (D 115), and so introspection writes "a more or less fantastic commentary on an unknown … but felt text" (D 119) in the herd's vocabulary, which is why "everybody is farthest away from himself" (GS 335). You can lay out the argument in seven steps, separate its conjecture (the prehistory) from its assertion (consciousness is sign-form) and from the steps a reader can test, and say what BGE 268 concludes when the same mechanism runs across generations: a drift toward the common that only "immense opposing forces" could check.

The shared language has a grammar as well as a vocabulary, and the grammar puts a subject in front of every verb. "I am afraid we are not rid of God because we still have faith in grammar", TI says ('"Reason" in Philosophy' 5). <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">No doer behind the deed</Ref> takes up what that grammar makes us believe about ourselves: that behind every act stands a doer who could have done otherwise, and who can therefore be blamed.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
From memory: what is the "I", according to BGE 19 as <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">the self as a society of drives</Ref> read it? Then combine that answer with GS 354: in what language does the "I" announce a decision, and what does the combination predict about how a person will describe one of his own decisions afterwards? Test the prediction on Byron's "I refuse to be the slave of any appetite" from D 109.

<Solution>
**BGE 19.** In an act of will, one part commands and others obey, and the commanding part uses "the synthetic term 'I'" for the whole, taking the credit for what the obeying "under-souls" did: *L'effet c'est moi*. The "I" is a title held by whichever drive or coalition governs.

**Combined with GS 354.** The governing drive announces itself through consciousness, and consciousness takes the form of shared signs. So the decision is reported in the community's vocabulary, which names the common and approved motives and has no words for the particular drive and its proportions.

**Prediction.** A person describing his decision will (a) present it as made by one reasoning self, (b) name a motive that has a common, respectable word, and (c) omit the drive that actually governed if that drive has no such word or would be blamed.

**Test.** Byron's sentence fits all three. It is in the first person, as a single self's resolution. It names a respectable motive, self-command, freedom from appetite. And D 109's analysis finds a different governing drive behind it, pride, which "feels the domination of his whole bearing … by a single affect as an affront". The sentence is pride's decision, spoken in the vocabulary of rational self-mastery that the community praises.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Do the worked translation again on a new case. You meet a former partner, now visibly happy with someone else, and afterwards a friend asks how it felt. List the parts of the state as honestly as you can, write the sentence you would most likely say, and say for each dropped part whether it was dropped because it has no common word or because its word would be blamed. Then say why no richer vocabulary would close the gap.

<Solution>
**Parts of the state (one honest version).** Gladness that she is happy; a sting of loss; wounded pride that the new partner seems to suit her better; relief at no longer being responsible for her happiness; a flicker of wishing it would not last; curiosity about the new person; the odd sense of a former intimacy now become a stranger's.

**The likely sentence.** "I'm happy for her", or "It was a bit strange, but fine."

**Why each part is dropped.** *Gladness* is kept: it has a common word and is approved. *The sting of loss* has a word but is usually cut down to "a bit strange", since dwelling on it would be pitied or judged. *Wounded pride* and *wishing it would not last* have words that would be blamed ("jealous", "spiteful"), so they are dropped for that reason. *Relief* and *curiosity* have words but would be taken badly in this context. *The former intimacy now a stranger's*, and above all *the proportions of the whole*, have no common word at all: no one else has had this blend about this person.

**Why a richer vocabulary would not close the gap.** A new word for the blend would work only if the hearer could connect it with an experience of his own of the same kind (BGE 268). Either he has had something like it, in which case the word names the common core and still drops what was particular to this evening, or he has not, in which case the word transmits nothing. The limit lies in what makes a sign a sign, not in the size of the dictionary: BGE 268's "we must in the end have experiences in common", the same reason the worked example gave for why no better word would save the proportions.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "Nietzsche thinks consciousness does nothing; it is a useless by-product." State what GS 354 and GS 11 actually claim, which other text the claim could appeal to, and what premise the claim would need to add.

<Solution>
**What GS 354 claims.** Consciousness is "in the main superfluous": most thinking, feeling, willing and acting goes on without it. That is a claim about most of life, not all of it. The same section gives consciousness a function: it developed because it was "needed and useful … between human beings", as "a net of communication". A thing that was useful and was developed "in proportion to the degree of this utility" is not a useless by-product.

**What GS 11 claims.** Consciousness is "the last and latest development of the organic and hence also what is most unfinished and unstrong", and it "gives rise to countless errors". An organ that causes errors has effects. GS 11 also sets the task of "incorporating knowledge and making it instinctive", which presupposes that conscious knowledge can come to shape the instincts. GM II 16 calls it an "organ", "weakest and most fallible": weak, not idle.

**Where the claim could look for support.** TI, "The Four Great Errors" 3: "The will no longer moves anything … it merely accompanies events." That text supports a strong reading on which conscious willing is an accompaniment.

**What it would have to add.** To get from GS 354 to "consciousness does nothing", the claim must add that the communicative job itself has no effect on action, or extend TI's sentence about the will to consciousness as a whole. Neither is in GS 354. The correct statement is that GS 354 makes consciousness superfluous for most of life, useful for communication, and in every case a surface that shows the average; whether it moves anything is a further question on which the texts divide.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A boundary probe. An octopus lives alone, never signals to others of its kind except in mating, and yet solves new problems flexibly: it opens jars, escapes tanks, and learns by watching. Suppose it is conscious. Which step of the seven-step argument does that put under pressure, and does the narrow reading of GS 354, with GS 357, save the argument? What would the octopus have to lack for the argument to come through intact?

<Solution>
**Under pressure.** Step 5, that a state becomes conscious as it takes the form of shared signs, and with it the proportion in GS 354 ("the subtlety and strength of consciousness always were proportionate to a man's (or animal's) capacity for communication"). An animal with almost no communication but rich flexible behaviour, if conscious, looks like consciousness without the need that is supposed to have produced it. Step 3 is not touched: it is a story about humans.

**What the problem-solving shows.** Less than it seems. GS 354 says thinking, problem-solving and acting can all go on without consciousness ("we could think, feel, will, and remember, and we could also 'act'"). Flexible problem-solving is therefore no evidence of consciousness in GS 354's sense. The objection needs the octopus to be conscious in the narrow sense.

**The narrow reading.** GS 354's topic is "becoming conscious of something", a state presented to oneself as one's own. GS 357 grants that consciousness is "only one state" of the psychic world. If the octopus's consciousness is felt states without that mirroring, it belongs to the large unmirrored psychic world, and the argument is untouched.

**What it would have to lack.** The octopus must lack the doubling: the capacity to have its own states before it as objects that could be reported. If it had that, without any history of communication, step 5 would be false as a general claim, and GS 354 would have to retreat to the weaker claim that, in humans, reflective consciousness and language grew together. The probe shows where the argument's definitional element lies: it survives the octopus exactly as far as "conscious" is restricted to mirrored, sign-ready states, and that restriction is stipulated, not argued.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Corroboration. Set D 115 ("The so-called 'ego'", in *Daybreak*, 1881, period 2) beside GS 354 (written for *The Gay Science* Book V, 1887, period 3). First, from the dates alone, predict which of the two will contain an explanation of *why* language fails the inner life. Then state (a) what the two sections agree on, (b) what GS 354 adds that D 115 lacks, and (c) what D 115 contains that GS 354 does not, and say what the comparison can be used to establish about the development of the theory.

<Solution>
**Prediction.** The later text is more likely to carry the explanation: period 2's sections are observations and experiments in psychology, and period 3's long sections argue for theses.

**(a) Agreement.** Both hold that language misrepresents the inner life, and that we misread ourselves through it. D 115: words "exist only for superlative degrees", and "we are none of us that which we appear to be in accordance with the states for which alone we have consciousness and words". GS 354: "each of us will always succeed in becoming conscious only of what is not individual but 'average'". Both tie consciousness to words: D 115 speaks of "the states for which alone we have consciousness and words".

**(b) What GS 354 adds.** An explanation: consciousness and language developed together "under the pressure of the need for communication", so the misrepresentation follows from what consciousness is for. It adds the herd ("social or herd nature", "genius of the species"), and it adds the generalisation from feelings to the whole conscious world ("a surface- and sign-world").

**(c) What D 115 has that GS 354 lacks.** The particular mechanism of extremes (words for peaks, none for the "milder, middle degrees"), and the consequence that the resulting self-image, "the so-called 'ego'", becomes "a fellow worker in the construction of our character and our destiny". GS 354 speaks of the average, D 115 of the extreme, but these are one distortion seen from two sides: the peaks are what everyone shares a word for, the common in GS 354's sense.

**What it establishes.** Both are published books, so both are Nietzsche's considered views of their dates. The comparison shows that the observation (language misdescribes us) came by 1881 and the explanation (consciousness is a herd organ) by 1887: the theory grew from the observation, not the reverse. It cannot be used to claim that D 115 already held the herd thesis, and it does not show that the explanation is right; it shows that the explanation was built to account for a fact Nietzsche had noticed six years earlier.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design a study that could tell GS 354's hypothesis apart from a rival: that consciousness developed for solitary planning, letting an animal rehearse actions before trying them. Say what each hypothesis predicts that the other does not, describe one observation or experiment for each prediction, and say what result would count against GS 354.

<Solution>
**Different predictions.** GS 354 predicts that what becomes conscious tracks what is *communicable*: states with shared names, at their extremes, in the community's categories. The planning rival predicts that what becomes conscious tracks what is *useful for rehearsal*: states relevant to choosing an action, whether or not anyone could name them. Across species, GS 354 predicts that the reflective capacities track social signalling ("a man's (or animal's) capacity for communication"); the rival predicts that they track the complexity of the problems an animal solves alone.

**Design 1: within people.** Teach two groups different vocabularies for a family of similar feelings (one group gets several fine-grained terms, the other one coarse term), then induce the feelings and ask both groups to report and discriminate them. GS 354 predicts that the fine-vocabulary group not only *reports* but *notices* more distinctions, since becoming conscious is taking sign form. The planning rival predicts that noticing tracks relevance to a decision the subject must make, not vocabulary.

**Design 2: across species.** Compare closely related species that differ in social signalling but face similar solitary problems, using tests of self-directed attention that do not depend on language. GS 354 predicts the more communicative species shows more; the rival predicts no difference, or a difference tracking problem complexity.

**What would count against GS 354.** A finding that people discriminate and report fine states they have no words for as well as states they can name, or that a solitary species with no signalling shows the mirrored self-consciousness that a closely related social species lacks. Either would put step 5 under pressure. The structural steps (4 and 6) would survive, since they concern what signs carry; step 7 would then hold only for self-*reports*, not for consciousness itself.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Rewrite "the lightning flashes" and "I think" without a grammatical subject, keeping what each sentence reports. BGE 17 offers one model: "a thought comes when 'it' wishes, and not when 'I' wish". Then say what each original sentence seems to claim that its rewrite does not, and where that extra claim came from.

<Solution>
**Rewrites.** "There is a flash" (or simply "flash", as one says "rain"). "Thinking is going on", or "there is a thought", or in BGE 17's form, "it thinks", where "it" is as empty as the "it" of "it is raining".

**What the originals add.** "The lightning flashes" separates a thing, the lightning, from something the thing does, the flashing, as if the lightning could exist without flashing and chose to flash. "I think" says not only that thinking is happening but that there is a subject, the I, which does it and could stop. In both, the subject is an extra entity behind the event.

**Where it came from.** Not from the event: what is observed in each case is a flash and a thought. It came from the grammar of the shared language, which requires every verb to have a subject. BGE 17 says the inference runs "according to the usual grammatical formula: 'To think is an activity; every activity requires an agency that is active; consequently'". This is a further consequence of GS 354's thesis: consciousness takes the form of the shared language, so it takes over that language's grammar as well as its vocabulary, and grammar supplies a doer. <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">No doer behind the deed</Ref> builds Nietzsche's critique of free will and blame on this point (GM I 13: "the doer is merely a fiction added to the deed").
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Nietzsche's philosophy is twelve books of numbered sections written between 1872 and 1888, and an argument reconstructed from them is only as good as the rule that says which sections may be cited for which claim. This lesson supplies the rules: the corpus in order with the genre of each book, the numbered section as the unit of citation, a five-step protocol for turning a section into premises and a conclusion, the rule that ranks published books above notebooks as evidence, and the working hypothesis the course will test against the texts.

The course's first move, in <Ref lesson="madman-argument">the lesson on the death of God</Ref>, stated the problem Nietzsche's books answer: the foundation of European morality is gone and the edifice still stands. The reconstruction there was done by hand, and two of its decisions were left unjustified: that Section 343, written five years after the parable, may be read into Section 125, and that Twilight of the Idols may be cited for the premise both sections lack. What stands open is the method. This lesson and <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the next</Ref> supply it; this one states the rules, the next runs them on the one "book" that breaks them.

The route: the corpus as a dated list in three genres; the section as a unit that builds to a formula; the protocol, run to completion on the madman and afresh on a three-sentence aphorism; the evidence rule, with the three analytic readers who state it; and the hypothesis, with the three places it can fail.

## The corpus: twelve books in sixteen years, three genres

A reconstruction cites a section by book and number, so the first thing to fix is the list of books and their dates. The count of twelve holds on one convention: the four *Untimely Meditations* (1873–76) are counted as one book, the two volumes of *Human, All Too Human* (1878–80) as one, and two small compilations of 1888 (*Nietzsche contra Wagner*, the *Dionysus Dithyrambs*) are not counted. On that convention the list runs from *The Birth of Tragedy*, published in January 1872 when its author was a 27-year-old professor of classical philology at Basel, to *Ecce Homo*, finished in November 1888 in Turin. Sixteen years, twelve books, and from 1878 on roughly one a year.

Two books have a second date. *The Gay Science* was published in 1882 as four books of numbered sections; the 1887 second edition added a preface and a fifth book, written after *Zarathustra* and *Beyond Good and Evil*. The madman reconstruction depended on this: its first premise came from Section 343, which opens Book V, so a reader with the 1882 edition has the parable and no premise about morality.

*Thus Spoke Zarathustra* appeared in three parts in 1883 and 1884; a fourth part was printed at Nietzsche's own expense in 1885 in forty copies for friends, and reached the public only in 1892. The timeline marks both additions, because the order of composition is the first check the protocol makes: a later section may explain an earlier one, and the reverse needs an argument.

### Genre fixes what a section can be cited for

The twelve books fall into three genres, and the genre decides what a section can be evidence for. A *sustained treatise* argues in sequence across sections: *The Birth of Tragedy*, the *Meditations*, the three essays of the *Genealogy* and *The Antichrist* are of this kind, and a section of them may be cited as a step in an argument that the neighbouring sections continue. An *aphorism collection* argues inside its sections: *Human, All Too Human*, *Daybreak*, *The Gay Science*, *Beyond Good and Evil* and *Twilight of the Idols* are of this kind, and a section of them is cited whole, since the next section usually changes the subject.

A *dramatization* argues through a character. *Zarathustra* is the only book of this kind, and a speech of Zarathustra is testimony from a character, which the course maps onto the theses argued elsewhere and never cites as a premise in Nietzsche's own voice.

The partition is Nietzsche's own retrospect as much as a reader's. In *Ecce Homo* he claims "the most manifold art of style that any one man has ever had at his disposal" (EH III 4), and the sequence of books reads as a sequence of stylistic experiments: essay blended with polemic, then three books of aphorisms, then *Zarathustra* as an experiment in dithyrambs, then *Beyond Good and Evil*, whose aphorisms are long and anchored in their context, then the *Genealogy*'s three sustained inquiries. The genre lane of the timeline carries those labels.

<KeyFact>
The two most-quoted titles in Nietzsche's name, *Zarathustra* and *The Will to Power*, are the two the protocol cannot cite for a premise: the first is a character's speech, and the second, as <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the next lesson</Ref> shows, is not a book he wrote.
</KeyFact>

### The corpus ends in January 1889

The endpoint is a fact of the corpus, and it is what makes one rung of the evidence ladder below a category at all. Nietzsche's last dated letters are from Turin in the first week of January 1889, the week he collapsed in the street; he wrote nothing for publication afterwards. Three books finished in 1888 stood at different distances from print that week. *Twilight of the Idols*, whose proofs he had corrected in the autumn, appeared under an 1889 imprint and counts as published. *The Antichrist* and *Ecce Homo* were finished and prepared for the printer but not yet in press, and they reached print in 1895 and 1908 from the Nietzsche Archive, under his sister.

That gap between finishing and printing is the ladder's third rung, the *authorized manuscript*: a text that is his and a printed book that is not, which grounds a claim with the editing flagged. A corpus that had closed a year later would have no such rung; this one has it because the author stopped in the interval, and the exercise on *Ecce Homo* at the end of the lesson runs the evidence rule to that limit.

## The unit: a section that builds to a formula

With the books dated and sorted, the unit inside them has to be fixed, and here the natural expectation is wrong. From a book of numbered paragraphs called aphorisms one expects detached sayings, each complete in a sentence, and a reader who expects that quotes the memorable sentence and stops. The sections do not behave that way. Not every short section is an aphorism: many multi-sentence sections build up to one, which enters as a proper part of the section, its culmination or its summative conclusion. The compressed sentence is the conclusion; the section is the argument for it.

Nietzsche states the compression as policy. In Section 381 of *The Gay Science* he writes that he treats deep problems "as I do a cold bath: quickly in, quickly out" (*schnell hinein, schnell hinaus*), and that a book is not refuted by someone's finding it unintelligible, since "perhaps that was part of its author's intention: he did not want to be understood by 'just anyone'". *Beyond Good and Evil* 30 says the same of the audience: "our highest insights must, and should, sound like follies, in some cases like crimes, when they reach without permission the ears of those who are not made and predestined for them." Both sections are describing the same fact about the form: the premises are left for the reader to supply, on purpose.

### GM III:1: an outline and a conclusion

The *Genealogy*'s Preface (§8) offers the reader a specimen: "an aphorism is placed before this [third] treatise, and the treatise itself is its commentary." The sentence has misled readers into taking the epigraph from *Zarathustra* for the aphorism; Maudemarie Clark, John Wilcox and Christopher Janaway showed in 1997 that the aphorism is the first section of the treatise itself. Read sentence by sentence, Section 1 of the Third Treatise has the shape the protocol needs.

<Figure
  src="./figures/gm-iii-1.svg"
  alt="GM III:1 laid out as nine numbered lines: the question, then six list items (artists, philosophers, women, the physiologically failed, priests, saints) bracketed as the outline, each mapped to the treatise sections that take it up, then the closing formula boxed in amber as the conclusion and mapped to section 28, then the coda asking whether the reader has understood"
  caption="GM III:1 dissected: six list items that outline the treatise, one boxed sentence that is its conclusion, and a coda saying the sentence has been read but not yet deciphered."

The first sentence is a question, "what do ascetic ideals mean?" Then a list: to artists, "nothing, or too many things"; to philosophers and scholars, an instinct for the conditions of high spirituality; to women, one more charm; to the physiologically failed, "the majority of mortals", a way to seem "too good" for this world; to priests, "their best instrument of power"; to saints, a pretext for hibernation. Then the sentence everything before it was for:

> Dass aber überhaupt das asketische Ideal dem Menschen so viel bedeutet hat, darin drückt sich die Grundthatsache des menschlichen Willens aus, sein *horror vacui*: er braucht ein Ziel, — und eher will er noch das Nichts wollen, als *nicht* wollen.
>
> That the ascetic ideal has meant so much to man at all expresses the basic fact of the human will, its *horror vacui*: it needs a goal, and it would rather will nothingness than *not* will.

The list is the treatise's table of contents. Sections 2–5 take the artist (Wagner), 6–10 the philosopher (Schopenhauer), 11–22 the priest and the sick majority he ministers to; women and saints are never returned to, and Sections 23–27 add an item the list did not contain, modern science as the ideal's latest form. Section 28, the last, returns to the boxed sentence and restates it. The section is therefore an argument in the exact sense the course uses: the list is the evidence, the formula is the conclusion, and the twenty-seven sections between are the premises written out. The coda makes the same point about reading: "Do you understand me? Have I been understood? 'Not at all, sir!' So let us start from the beginning."

The shape generalizes: in any section that builds to a formula, the claim a reconstruction cites is at one end, the first sentence or the last, and the middle is the evidence for it; quoting the formula alone is therefore the commonest shape of a misquotation of Nietzsche.
</KeyFact>

### The rival account of the form

There is an older reading on which the form is the absence of argument. Crane Brinton's 1941 verdict, quoted by Alexander Nehamas as "characteristic of many", was that Nietzsche's work "contains a great variety of ideas, sometimes mutually contradictory, difficult if not impossible to reduce to a 'system', made still more bewilderingly varied by the aphoristic form". Kaufmann answered that each aphorism is an experiment and Nietzsche "not a system-thinker but a problem-thinker", citing Nietzsche's own line from *Twilight*, "the will to a system is a lack of integrity" (TI I 26).

Nehamas's own position, which the course meets again in <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">the lesson on Nehamas</Ref>, is that the aphorism is only one style among many and that the variety of styles is itself the doctrine: every view is a perspective, and a writer who used one style would be claiming the view from nowhere. All three agree on the fact that matters here, that the sections are not detached sayings; they differ on what the form is for. The protocol needs only the fact.

## The protocol: five steps on a numbered section

The madman reconstruction did three things by hand: it fixed a conclusion, tied premises to sentences, and named a premise the text lacked. The protocol makes those three steps explicit and adds two that the last lesson skipped.

<Remark title="the reconstruction protocol">
Given a numbered section, cited by siglum:

1. **Locate the claim.** State in one sentence what the section concludes, and name the sentence of the section that carries it, usually the last or the first.
2. **List the stated premises.** Each premise is a sentence in English tied to a sentence of the German; a premise without a sentence is not stated.
3. **Name the missing premise.** State the step validity needs that the text does not supply, and classify it: an is–ought step, a *type-fact* (a claim about the kind of person or organism involved), the free-will presupposition, or a historical assertion.
4. **Check a second passage.** Find the same claim in another book or another year, and say whether it supplies the missing premise, supplies a reason for it, or leaves the gap.
5. **State the strongest opposite reading**, and the sentence of the section that tells against it.

No notation: premises are sentences, and the German anchor is what makes them checkable.
</Remark>

The classification in step 3 is the course's working list of the premises Nietzsche most often leaves out, and the reader has met one of the three: the madman's gap was a historical assertion (no other foundation is available). The other two recur from the First Treatise on. The free-will presupposition is the assumption that the strong could have chosen to be weak, which <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">GM I:13</Ref> attacks; a type-fact is a claim about a person's fixed constitution, which the hypothesis at the end of this lesson makes central.

### Steps 4 and 5 on the madman

Step 4 asks for the same claim in another passage and a verdict on the gap. The gap was the premise that no other foundation, Kantian, utilitarian or humanitarian, can carry the same values. Section 357 of *The Gay Science*, in Book V, names what killed the belief: "Christian morality itself, the ever more strictly taken concept of truthfulness, the confessor's subtlety of the Christian conscience, translated and sublimated into scientific conscience".

That is a reason bearing on the gap. If the belief was killed by a virtue the morality itself bred, then a secular refounding that keeps truthfulness as a value keeps the thing that did the killing, and the refounding is not stable. It is not the premise: nothing in 357 says that Kant's derivation fails, only that it will inherit the same solvent. The verdict is recorded as "a reason, not the premise", and the argument for the premise is deferred to <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the Third Treatise's last sections</Ref>.

Step 5 asks for the strongest opposite reading. For the madman it is the reading that the speech argues for atheism, and it is strong because the sentence "God is dead" is what everyone quotes. The sentence of the section that tells against it is the second one: the madman "provoked great laughter" because "many of those who did not believe in God" (*welche nicht an Gott glaubten*) were standing there. An argument's conclusion is what the audience is meant to accept and does not yet hold; this audience holds it before the speech begins. The reading is struck, and the striking is recorded with the sentence, so that a reader who disagrees knows what to answer.

### A fresh run: BGE 68

The madman is a parable of forty lines. The protocol has to work on a section the reader can hold in one glance, and Section 68 of *Beyond Good and Evil*, in the part titled "Epigrams and Interludes", is three sentences:

> „Das habe ich gethan“ sagt mein Gedächtniss. Das kann ich nicht gethan haben — sagt mein Stolz und bleibt unerbittlich. Endlich — giebt das Gedächtniss nach.
>
> "I did that," says my memory. "I cannot have done that," says my pride, and stays implacable. In the end, memory yields.

  caption="BGE 68 dissected the way GM III:1 was: two premises anchored to two sentences, a conclusion in the third, and the premise the section lacks supplied from GS 354 a year later."

<Example title="the protocol on BGE 68">
**Step 1, the claim.** What a person remembers having done is edited by what the person can bear to have done. Carried by the third sentence, *Endlich — giebt das Gedächtniss nach*; the dash before the verb is the pause before the result.

**Step 2, the stated premises.**
*P1.* Memory and pride can make incompatible reports about the same deed. Anchor: sentences 1 and 2, the two speakers and the two verdicts.
*P2.* When they conflict, pride does not give way (*bleibt unerbittlich*). Anchor: sentence 2.
*C.* Memory gives way. Anchor: sentence 3.

**Step 3, the missing premise.** C follows from P1 and P2 only if one of the two must yield, and only if what yields is the content of memory rather than the person's willingness to say it. The section presents the outcome as a change in what is remembered, not as a lie told to others. The missing premise is therefore that a drive can revise the content of a memory without the person's consent or knowledge. It is a type-fact in the protocol's sense, a claim about how a mind of this kind is built, and a second one rides with it: the *mein* is the generic first person of the maxim, so the case is offered as a law and not as a confession.

**Step 4, a second passage.** Section 354 of *The Gay Science* (1887) argues that consciousness is a late surface: "the greater part of this life takes place without this mirroring", and what becomes conscious is "translated back into the herd perspective". That supplies the first missing premise in a general form. If conscious contents are a thin, late and edited report of a process that runs without them, then a drive that operates below the report can alter what the report says, and memory is one such report. The verdict is "supplies the premise, generally": 354 does not name pride, but it licenses the mechanism.

**Step 5, the opposite reading.** The strongest rival is that the section is a *moraliste*'s joke about vanity in the manner of La Rochefoucauld, a moral observation and not a psychological thesis. Against it is the second sentence's *bleibt unerbittlich* and the third's *Endlich*: the section narrates a contest over time with a winner, which is a mechanism, and the same part of the book states the general form of the thesis later on, "moralities are merely a sign language of the affects" (BGE 187). The joke is the surface; the mechanism is stated in the neighbouring sections.
</Example>

The result is a two-line preview of the psychology the course reaches in <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the lesson on the soul as a society of drives</Ref>: two drives contend, the stronger sets the record, and the "I" that remembers is the outcome rather than the referee. The reconstruction did not put that there; the section did, in three sentences, with the premise that makes it work left for GS 354 to supply.

<KeyFact>
Step 3 is the one step the text can never do for the reader: the missing premise is by definition not in the section, and the three usual classes, an is–ought step, a type-fact, the free-will presupposition, are what the rest of the course hunts section by section.
</KeyFact>

## The evidence rule: published works ground, notebooks corroborate

Step 4 sends the reader to a second passage, and the corpus has more than the twelve books to send them to. Nietzsche kept notebooks throughout, and the critical edition prints their contents as *Nachlass* (literary remains) in writing order, 176 groups of fragments from 1869 to 1889; it also prints early essays he finished and chose not to publish, and every letter. A second passage from a notebook is easy to find on any topic, and the question is what it may do.

  src="./figures/evidence-ladder.svg"

  caption="The evidence ladder: the critical edition's six categories, and what each may do in a reconstruction."
/>

The rule the course adopts is Maudemarie Clark's, stated in the first chapter of *Nietzsche on Truth and Philosophy* (1990). The published writings, "the books he published or prepared for publication, as in the cases of The Antichrist and Ecce Homo", are "far superior sources of his philosophy", and the notebooks get "a very secondary status", used "only when it provides the foundation for an interpretation against which I am arguing or when it helps to support an interpretation of a specific passage in the published writings for which the latter also provide evidence".

Clark gives two reasons beyond the obvious one that Nietzsche approved the books and not the notes. The first is about checks: a published passage sits in a book that provides "many more checks on the accuracy of interpretations", while the notes are "rearrangeable", and Clark quotes Rudolf Kuenzli's verdict on what readers have done with them: "the labyrinthians have found their labyrinth, the systematizers their system." The second is philosophical: "where Nietzsche's notes suggest a position different from that suggested by the published writings, it is usually a philosophically weaker one."

### Three analytic readers, one ordering

Clark is not alone, and the rule's strength varies by reader. Three statements of it sit side by side.

| Reader | The rule, in the reader's sentence | Permissiveness |
|---|---|---|
| Kaufmann, *Nietzsche* (1950), Ch. 2 | "It seems wholly preposterous to ignore the works which a philosopher has published, to claim that he did not really mean what he said in them, and to prefer to them the scattered scribblings which he penned on his walks." Three classes of remains: finished books held back only by the collapse, the Basel lecture notes, and the fragments. | Notes are "background material"; the finished-but-unpublished books count as books. |
| Clark, *Nietzsche on Truth and Philosophy* (1990), Ch. 1 §5 | Notes are used "only when [they help] to support an interpretation of a specific passage in the published writings for which the latter also provide evidence". | Strictest: a note never grounds. |
| Reginster, *The Affirmation of Life* (2006), Introduction §4 | A "qualified priority principle": notebook views are accepted "unless these views jibe with views explicitly discussed in the published works", where to jibe is to be "duplicated, explicitly anticipated, summarized, implied or implicated by them, or otherwise plausibly grow out of them". | More permissive: a note that grows out of a published view may state it more fully. |

All three put the published works first; they differ on what a note may add once a published anchor exists. Reginster's reason for the looser version is that the late notebooks are the workshop of a project the published books announce and never finish, and that the notes are written in a plainer style, without the "deliberate form of esoterism" of BGE 30.

The course takes Clark's version, for a reason internal to the course: its question is whether the claims hold as arguments, and an argument whose premise exists only in a note has a premise its author never asserted. Where a note states a published view more fully, the course quotes the note and says so; where it states a view the books do not, the note dates a thought and grounds nothing.

<KeyFact>
A *Will to Power* number is cited in this course only when a published passage backs it; where none does, the fragment dates a draft and grounds nothing, and the reconstruction records the gap rather than filling it from the notebook.
</KeyFact>

The other reading the ladder rules out is Heidegger's. His Nietzsche lectures of 1936–40 take the opposite ordering: the published books are the foreground, and "Nietzsche's philosophy proper" is in the notes of the 1880s, cited from the compiled *Will to Power* by fragment number and from the works by volume and page of the old *Grossoktav* edition (a citation such as "XV, 495" is that edition, volume fifteen, page 495). The course meets that convention in <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref> and has to be able to decode it; the ladder says what weight to give what it decodes.

Every citation in the course is a *siglum*, an abbreviation of the kind the Colli–Montinari critical edition uses, and the digital edition at nietzschesource.org gives each siglum a stable address. For a published work the form is book, part, section: `GM-I-13` is the thirteenth section of the *Genealogy*'s first treatise, `JGB-36` the thirty-sixth section of *Jenseits von Gut und Böse* (the German abbreviations are the edition's; the course writes BGE 36 in prose and JGB-36 as the address), `FW-125` the madman.

A letter is `BVN-1889,1245`, the year and the running number; that one is the second sheet of the letter pair in the corpus section. The prefix of a siglum therefore names the category of the edition the text sits in, which is the rung of the ladder it occupies, before the reader has opened it.

## The working hypothesis: methodological naturalism and the Doctrine of Types

The protocol and the evidence rule say how to read; they do not say what to expect. The course's tone field fixes a working hypothesis, named as such and tested rather than assumed, and this section states it in the terms its author uses so that the tests later in the course have something exact to hit.

The hypothesis is Brian Leiter's, from *Nietzsche on Morality* (2002; second edition 2015), and it has two parts. The first is a claim about method. *Methodological naturalism* is the view that "philosophical inquiry … should be continuous with empirical inquiry in the sciences": continuous in results, so that a philosophical account does not contradict what the sciences of the day establish, and continuous in method, so that the account explains by causes and types the way a physiologist or a historian does. It is not the claim that everything is physics. Leiter's reading of Nietzsche is that the explanations of moral phenomena in the *Genealogy* are of this kind: a value is explained by the type of person who needed it and the circumstances that let that need express itself.

### The Doctrine of Types: a granite that bounds without fixing

The second part is a claim about what such explanations bottom out in. Joshua Knobe and Leiter state it in their 2007 paper on Nietzschean moral psychology:

> Nietzsche accepts what we may call a "Doctrine of Types" (Leiter 2002: 8), according to which, *each person has a fixed psycho-physical constitution, which defines him as a particular type of person.* These "type-facts", for Nietzsche, are either physiological facts about the person, or facts about the person's unconscious drives or affects.

The doctrine is stated with a limit, and the limit is what the course will need. Knobe and Leiter continue: "it is important to recognize that Nietzsche's view is not that type-facts determine everything … Type-facts radically circumscribe possible developmental trajectories, but the environment (for Nietzsche, especially the moral environment) is quite significant in determining the outcomes. Think of some seeds from a tomato plant. No amount of environmental input will yield an apple tree from those seeds, yet the 'environment' … will affect which of the trajectories possible for a tomato plant, wilting, flourishing, or any of the stages in between, will be realized." Type-facts bound a life without fixing it; Leiter's name for that position is *causal essentialism*, against a classical fatalism on which nothing one does matters.

The published anchor for the doctrine, the one the evidence rule requires, is *Beyond Good and Evil* 231: "at the bottom of us, right 'down there', there is of course something unteachable, a granite of spiritual fate (*ein Granit von geistigem Fatum*), of predetermined decision and answer to predetermined, selected questions." Learning, the same section says, "transforms us, it does what all nourishment does", which is the environment's share; the granite is the type-fact. The doctrine and its limit are both in the one section.

### Three predictions the course will test

A working hypothesis that could not fail would be an assumption, so the three places it can fail are named now.

<Figure
  src="./figures/hypothesis-tests.svg"
  alt="An amber box at the top stating the working hypothesis in two clauses, methodological naturalism and the Doctrine of Types; three arrows down to three blue boxes: the sovereign individual of GM II:2, which must be a natural artefact; value creation in GS 301, which must be explicable without a faculty of legislation; will to power and eternal recurrence in BGE 36 and GS 341, which must survive as psychology; each tagged with the module that tests it"
  caption="The hypothesis and its three test sites: what the naturalist reading predicts at each, and where the course checks the prediction."
/>

First, the *sovereign individual* of GM II:2, the "ripest fruit" of the long prehistoric discipline that made human beings able to keep promises, whom Nietzsche calls autonomous and supra-ethical. The hypothesis predicts that this figure is a natural artefact of that discipline, a type bred by it, and not an ideal of autonomy the text is recommending; if the section can only be read as an ideal, the naturalist reading has to deflate a sentence it cannot explain. <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">The Second Treatise's first lesson</Ref> runs the test.

Second, *value creation*. GS 301 says that whatever has value in the present world has it not in itself but because "it was once given a value, bestowed", and that "we" did the bestowing. The hypothesis predicts that this bestowing is something a type does, explicable as what a certain constitution values in certain circumstances, without a faculty of legislation that stands outside the causal order; if creating a value turns out to require such a faculty, naturalism has a normativity problem. <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">The value-creation lesson</Ref> runs the test.

Third, the two thoughts Nietzsche called his deepest. BGE 36 argues that the world seen from inside "would be 'will to power' and nothing besides", and GS 341 puts eternal recurrence into a demon's mouth as a question. The hypothesis predicts that both survive as psychology, a claim about what drives do and a test of whether a life can be affirmed whole, and not as cosmology, claims about what the universe is made of and how time runs; if the doctrines are cosmological, naturalism has to call its author's two central thoughts errors. <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">The will-to-power lesson</Ref> and <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">the recurrence lesson</Ref> run the test, and <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">Heidegger</Ref> is the reader who says the prediction is false.

<KeyFact>
The hypothesis is falsifiable at three named sections, and the course records at each whether the naturalist reading renders the section as mechanism plus evidence or has to deflate it; <Ref lesson="naturalist-reading-verdict">the audit lesson</Ref> sums that record, and nothing before it may treat the hypothesis as settled.
</KeyFact>

What is established: the corpus is twelve dated books in three genres, ending in the first week of January 1889, and the genre fixes what a section can be cited for; the unit of citation is the numbered section, which builds to a formula, so that the premise is at an end and the middle is the evidence; the five-step protocol turns a section into premises and a conclusion and names the premise it lacks; the published works ground and the notebooks corroborate, on Clark's version of a rule Kaufmann and Reginster also state; and the working hypothesis is methodological naturalism with the Doctrine of Types, testable at three named sections.

What is not yet established is why the evidence rule has to be as strict as Clark makes it. The most-cited "book" of Nietzsche's, *The Will to Power*, is a case of the rule's failure mode, and <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the next lesson</Ref> takes it apart with the dated fragments the critical edition prints in his own order.

<Exercise>
Run all five steps of the protocol on *Beyond Good and Evil* 78, one sentence:

> Wer sich selbst verachtet, achtet sich doch immer noch dabei als Verächter.
>
> Whoever despises himself still respects himself, in the act, as the one who despises.

State the claim, the stated premises, the missing premise with its class, a second passage that bears on it, and the strongest opposite reading with the word of the sentence that tells against it. Allow fifteen minutes before opening the solution.

<Solution>
**Step 1, the claim.** Self-contempt is not the absence of self-regard; it preserves self-regard in the one who does the despising. Carried by the whole sentence, whose structure is concessive: *doch immer noch*, "still, all the same".

**Step 2, the stated premises.**
*P1.* To despise oneself is to perform an act of judging. Anchor: *verachtet*, a transitive verb with a subject.
*P2.* The judge in an act of judging is a distinct role from what is judged, and the sentence assigns it a rank: the despiser is "respected" (*achtet*). Anchor: *als Verächter*, "as the despiser".
*C.* Self-contempt leaves the self-respect of the despiser intact.

**Step 3, the missing premise.** C follows only if the self that despises and the self that is despised can be told apart within one person, so that a rank can obtain between them. The sentence presupposes a divided self and does not argue for it. The class is a type-fact: a claim about how a person is constituted, namely as more than one contending party. (It is not the free-will presupposition, and no is–ought step is involved: the sentence describes and does not prescribe.)

**Step 4, a second passage.** BGE 19 (1886, the same book): willing is "something complicated, something that is a unity only as a word", and in every act of will there is "a commanding thought" and "the affect of command", with the person "at the same time the commanding and the obeying party". That supplies the missing premise in general form: a person is a plurality in which one part can stand over another, so the despiser and the despised can be two parts of one person. Verdict: supplies the premise. A second candidate, GM III:11–13 (1887), extends it to the ascetic: "life against life" in the self-denier is, "physiologically considered", a declining life's device for preserving itself; that is C applied to the priest, and it is where <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the ascetic-priest lesson</Ref> will need this sentence.

**Step 5, the opposite reading.** The strongest rival is that the sentence is a consolation: self-contempt is not so bad, since some self-respect survives it. Against it is *Verächter*. The respect preserved is for the one who despises, that is, for the act of despising itself; the sentence says that self-contempt is a form of self-assertion, not a mitigation of it, and the neighbouring epigrams (BGE 68 on pride editing memory, BGE 187 on moralities as sign language of the affects) place it in a series about what an affect does under another name. The reading that finds comfort in the sentence has read *achtet* and skipped *als Verächter*.

The reconstruction lands on the doorstep of the Third Treatise: an ideal of self-denial that is, in the one who holds it, a way of exercising power is the thesis of GM III:11–13, and this one-sentence section is the psychology it rests on.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "Because Nietzsche wrote in aphorisms, he did not argue; reconstruction imposes a structure he rejected." Use GM III:1 and the Preface's §8, and say what the sentence "the will to a system is a lack of integrity" (TI I 26) does and does not license.

<Solution>
**Where the claim fails.** It runs together two things the texts keep apart: a system and an argument. GM Preface §8 says that an aphorism "has not been 'deciphered' just because it has been read off; rather its interpretation must now begin, and for that an art of interpretation is needed", and that the Third Treatise is "a specimen" of that art, "its commentary". A commentary that runs to twenty-eight sections and returns at the end to the sentence it started from is an argument in the plain sense: the sentence is the conclusion, the sections are its support. GM III:1 shows the structure inside a single section: a question, a list of six cases, and a formula the cases are evidence for. So the aphoristic form is, on Nietzsche's own account of it, a compressed argument whose premises the reader is instructed to write out. Reconstruction does what §8 asks; it does not impose a structure the text lacks, it makes explicit a structure the text says is there and calls "rumination" (*das Wiederkäuen*).

**What TI I 26 licenses.** "I mistrust all systematizers and avoid them. The will to a system is a lack of integrity." The sentence rejects a *system*: a set of conclusions derived from premises that are never themselves questioned, which is Kaufmann's gloss and the obvious sense of *Rechtschaffenheit*, honesty about one's premises. It licenses the reader to refuse to derive Nietzsche's views from a single master premise, and to expect that a premise found in one book may be questioned in another. It does not license the inference that a section has no premises; a writer who mistrusts systems for hiding their premises has more reason, not less, to want his own premises found.

**The residue.** What the claim gets right is that the structure is the reader's: the reconstruction is a hypothesis about the section, checkable against the German, and a different reconstruction is always possible. That is why the protocol ends with step 5, the opposite reading, rather than with a conclusion.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take the published-works rule to its limit. *Ecce Homo* was finished in November 1888 and prepared for print, but it was published in 1908 by the Nietzsche Archive under Nietzsche's sister, who had earlier suppressed at least one passage. Decide, with reasons, which rung of the evidence ladder it occupies, and then say what that does to a claim resting on *Ecce Homo* alone: the dating of the thought of eternal recurrence to August 1881 at Sils-Maria, which EH's chapter on *Zarathustra* gives ("6000 feet beyond man and time").

<Solution>
**The rung.** The third: an authorized manuscript. Clark's formulation includes "the books he published or prepared for publication, as in the cases of The Antichrist and Ecce Homo", and Kaufmann's first class of remains is the works "Nietzsche completed but did not publish because he collapsed while still negotiating with publishers", which "may be treated exactly like Nietzsche's other books". Two facts pull it below the top rung. Nietzsche did not see proofs, so the text as printed is the Archive's; and the Archive had an interest, as <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the next lesson</Ref> shows with the suppressed passage on his mother and sister. The critical edition's category is the right one: authorized (the manuscript is his and was sent to the printer) but not published (the printed book is not).

**What that does to the claim.** A claim resting on *Ecce Homo* alone carries a flag: its sentence may be Nietzsche's, and its printed context is not guaranteed to be. The Sils-Maria dating is the test case, and it passes, for a reason the ladder predicts. EH says the thought "belongs to August 1881: it was jotted on a sheet with the inscription '6000 feet beyond man and time'". The Nachlass corroborates the date: fragment NF-1881,11[141], headed "Die Wiederkunft des Gleichen. Entwurf" ("The recurrence of the same. Draft"), closes with the line "Anfang August 1881 in Sils-Maria, 6000 Fuss über dem Meere und viel höher über allen menschlichen Dingen!" That is the notebook doing the one thing the rule permits it to do, dating a thought; the philosophical content of the doctrine is then taken from GS 341, a published work, in <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">the recurrence lesson</Ref>. Where a claim rested on EH alone and no rung below corroborated it, the flag would stay on the claim.

<Exercise>
The Doctrine of Types is stated as "type-facts bound a life without fixing it". Write out what the tomato-plant analogy commits Nietzsche to and what it does not: a range, a ceiling, no unique outcome on one side; on the other, determinism of every act. Then find the clause of BGE 231 that the analogy paraphrases, and the clause of the same section that states the environment's share.

<Solution>
**What the analogy commits to.** (i) A *range*: the seeds yield a tomato plant in some state between wilting and flourishing, so a type admits many outcomes. (ii) A *ceiling*: no environment yields an apple tree, so the range has a boundary that no input moves; there are lives a given constitution cannot have. (iii) *No unique outcome*: which point in the range is reached depends on water, sun and pests, so the environment is a genuine cause and the type does not by itself pick the outcome. Knobe and Leiter draw the moral-psychological consequence: type-facts "play a powerful (but not exclusive) role in determining one's behavior and values", and a person's "crucial conscious choices and values are themselves explicable in terms of these type-facts", the *explicable* meaning that the choices are outcomes within the range, not that they are inputs from outside it.

**What it does not commit to.** Determinism of every act. The analogy says nothing about which leaf grows on which day; it fixes the kind and the range, not the token. Leiter's label, causal essentialism, is chosen against classical fatalism, the view that the outcome is fixed whatever one does. On the tomato view what one does matters, within the ceiling. A reconstruction that attributes to Nietzsche the claim "the type determines each action" has added a premise the doctrine explicitly denies.

**BGE 231.** The clause the analogy paraphrases: "there is of course something unteachable, a granite of spiritual fate, of predetermined decision and answer to predetermined, selected questions" (*ein Granit von geistigem Fatum, von vorherbestimmter Entscheidung und Antwort auf vorherbestimmte ausgelesene Fragen*), which is the ceiling: on the cardinal problems one can only "learn to the end" (*auslernen*) what is already fixed, not relearn (*umlernen*). The environment's share is the section's opening clause: "Learning transforms us, it does what all nourishment does, which also does not merely 'preserve'" (*Das Lernen verwandelt uns, es thut Das, was alle Ernährung thut*), which is the water and sun. Both clauses are in one published section, so the doctrine and its limit rest on the top rung.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The most quoted sentence of *The Will to Power* is the last sentence of its last fragment, number 1067: "This world is the will to power, and nothing besides!" Apply step 4 of the protocol: find the published passage closest to it, quote the corresponding clause in German, and record its grammatical mood. Then state the verdict the evidence rule gives.

<Solution>
**The published passage.** *Beyond Good and Evil* 36 (1886), the section that argues from the reality of drives to will to power as "the" causality. Its last sentence: "Die Welt von innen gesehen, die Welt auf ihren 'intelligiblen Charakter' hin bestimmt und bezeichnet — sie *wäre* eben 'Wille zur Macht' und nichts ausserdem." ("The world seen from inside, the world determined and designated by its 'intelligible character', would be 'will to power' and nothing besides.")

**The mood.** Subjunctive: *wäre*, "would be", and the whole section is built on *gesetzt, dass*, "suppose that", repeated three times, the last introducing the conclusion: "supposing, finally, that one succeeded in explaining our entire drive-life as the development and ramification of one basic form of will". BGE 36 states a hypothesis, argues that "the conscience of method" obliges one to try it, and gives its conclusion in the conditional. WP 1067 states the same content in the indicative, *ist*, "is", with an exclamation mark.

**The verdict.** Under the evidence rule the published passage backs the fragment's content and does not back its mood. A reconstruction may cite BGE 36 for the claim that the world is to be *tried* as will to power, and may cite WP 1067 as a notebook draft that states the claim assertorically; it may not cite WP 1067 to convert BGE 36's "suppose" into an "is", since the only text in which Nietzsche asserts it is one he did not publish. The difference between the two moods, and what turns on it, is the subject of <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the next lesson</Ref>, which shows from the dated fragments that the "book" the sentence closes was assembled after his collapse; the philosophical weight of the conditional is then the business of <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref>.
</Solution>

When Nietzsche's madman announces that God is dead, his listeners are atheists, and they laugh; his point is that they have not understood what their unbelief costs them.

The laughter looks like the right response. If "God is dead" meant "there is no God", the madman would be bringing unbelievers news they already had, and a man who runs into a market place with a lantern to tell atheists that God does not exist is a comic figure. So the sentence has to mean something else, and the parable says what kind of thing: a cost. Something the atheists in the market still have, and still rely on, has already been taken from them by their own unbelief, and they have not noticed.

What they still rely on is their values, and the question the madman raises is what holds those values up once the belief behind them has gone. Nietzsche had to ask it of himself. *The Birth of Tragedy* had justified existence through a suffering world-artist, and by 1886 he dismissed that artist-god as "arbitrary, idle, fantastic" (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="what-goes-the-metaphysics-and-the-comfort">what the 1886 preface dropped</Ref>). It was the last world behind the world he had believed in, and without it nothing he valued had a ground he could name. The obvious answer is that nothing has to: kindness is good whether or not God exists, and the unbelievers in the market are presumably decent people. That answer is the laughter, and the parable was written against it.

## The parable: GS 125 in four movements

What does the madman actually tell the laughing atheists, and where in it is the cost? The parable answers in four movements, each replying to the one before. It is section 125 of *The Gay Science* (GS), published in 1882, in the second of the three periods of Nietzsche's work.

### The lantern at noon, and the laughter

<Quote source="GS 125, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Have you not heard of that madman who lit a lantern in the bright morning hours, ran to the market place, and cried incessantly: "I seek God! I seek God!" As many of those who did not believe in God were standing around just then, he provoked much laughter. Has he got lost? asked one. Did he lose his way like a child? asked another. Or is he hiding? Is he afraid of us? Has he gone on a voyage? emigrated? Thus they yelled and laughed.
</Quote>

A lantern in daylight is a search for something that ought to be in plain view. The jokes show what God is to the crowd: a missing person, a lost child, an emigrant. For them his absence is a small fact about the inventory of the world, one item fewer, and nothing else in the world is changed by it.

### The indictment: "we have killed him"

<Quote source="GS 125, trans. W. Kaufmann">
<Original lang="de">
»Wohin ist Gott?« rief er, »ich will es euch sagen! Wir haben ihn getötet – ihr und ich! […] Gott ist tot! Gott bleibt tot! Und wir haben ihn getötet!«
</Original>

"Whither is God?" he cried; "I will tell you. We have killed him — you and I. All of us are his murderers. But how did we do this? How could we drink up the sea? Who gave us the sponge to wipe away the entire horizon? What were we doing when we unchained this earth from its sun? Whither is it moving now? Whither are we moving? Away from all suns? Are we not plunging continually? Backward, sideward, forward, in all directions? Is there still any up or down? Are we not straying as through an infinite nothing? Do we not feel the breath of empty space? Has it not become colder? Is not night continually closing in on us? Do we not need to light lanterns in the morning? … Gods, too, decompose. God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him."
</Quote>

The madman answers the crowd's joke with a correction in two parts. First, God did not wander off: he was killed, and the killers are the people in the market, "you and I". Second, the killing changed everything, and the images say what God had been for. A horizon is the line that bounds what you see and lets you place everything in it. A sun is what the earth turns around and what gives it light and warmth. "Up or down" is the most basic ranking there is. Each image is a standard of orientation, and each has gone. The madman's claim is that God had been the fixed point by which a whole culture measured what is higher and lower, better and worse; with him gone the measuring has nothing to measure from, and that is the "infinite nothing".

"God remains dead" closes a door the Christian reader expects to stay open. The God of the Gospels died and rose on the third day. This death is final.

### The demand: "must we ourselves not become gods?"

<Quote source="GS 125, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Is not the greatness of this deed too great for us? Must we ourselves not become gods simply to appear worthy of it? There has never been a greater deed; and whoever is born after us — for the sake of this deed he will belong to a higher history than all history hitherto.
</Quote>

The horror turns into a demand. If the standard by which values were measured has been destroyed, the destroyers either live without values or take over the work the standard did. The parable only asks the question; <Ref lesson="creating-values">creating values</Ref> follows Nietzsche's answer to it.

### Too early: the light of the stars

<Quote source="GS 125, trans. W. Kaufmann">
At last he threw his lantern on the ground, and it broke into pieces and went out. "I have come too early," he said then; "my time is not yet. This tremendous event is still on its way, still wandering; it has not yet reached the ears of men. Lightning and thunder require time; the light of the stars requires time; deeds, though done, still require time to be seen and heard. This deed is still more distant from them than the most distant stars — and yet they have done it themselves."
</Quote>

The ending resolves the puzzle of the opening. The crowd's unbelief is the deed, and it is done. What has not arrived is its *news*: the understanding of what follows from it. The madman ends the day by forcing his way into churches and singing a requiem for God, asking "What after all are these churches now if they are not the tombs and sepulchers of God?"

## "God is dead" is a claim about a belief

The parable leaves the sentence itself unexplained. Taken literally, "God is dead" says that a being who once lived has died, which neither a believer nor an atheist can accept: a God who exists does not die, and one who never existed could not have. Nietzsche glossed the phrase himself five years later, in the fifth book he added to GS in 1887:

<Quote source="GS 343, trans. W. Kaufmann">
<Original lang="de">
Das größte neuere Ereignis – daß »Gott tot ist«, daß der Glaube an den christlichen Gott unglaubwürdig geworden ist – beginnt bereits seine ersten Schatten über Europa zu werfen.
</Original>

The greatest recent event — that "God is dead," that the belief in the Christian god has become unbelievable — is already beginning to cast its first shadows over Europe.
</Quote>

The second clause explains the first. What has died is a *belief*, and the event is a change in what the belief can be for the people who held it. The German word *unglaubwürdig* means "not worthy of belief", the word used of a witness whose testimony can no longer be credited. It does not mean "proved false". A witness can become untrustworthy without anyone having shown that what he said was untrue. So the *death of God* names the event that the belief in the Christian God has become unbelievable for educated Europe, with whatever consequences that has for everything resting on the belief.

### The run around GS 125

The gloss says what died; it does not say how a belief can die while the people who held it notice nothing, or what killed it. A numbered section is read in its run, never alone (<Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="the-procedure">the reading procedure</Ref>), and the sections around GS 125 supply exactly these pieces. The phrase "God is dead" first appears seventeen sections earlier, opening Book Three:

<Quote source="GS 108, trans. W. Kaufmann">
After Buddha was dead, his shadow was still shown for centuries in a cave — a tremendous, gruesome shadow. God is dead; but given the way of men, there may still be caves for thousands of years in which his shadow will be shown. — And we — we still have to vanquish his shadow, too.
</Quote>

Each section of the run adds one piece the parable takes for granted:

- **108, the shadow.** A belief can be dead and its effects still shown for centuries. This is how the crowd can live on as before.
- **109–110, beliefs that last by usefulness.** Section 109 finds "shadows of God" outside the churches too, in the habit of seeing purposes in nature and laws with a lawgiver behind them, where there are "only necessities". Section 110 says why such beliefs last: they were useful, not true. A belief held for its usefulness can lose its standing without being refuted.
- **124, the open sea.** A ship that has burned its bridges finds itself on an infinite ocean with no land to go back to. The image is dread here; five years later GS 343 turns it into its opposite.
- **153, the gods slain "for the sake of morality".** A playwright first ties "the knot of morality" into his tragedy, then has "slain all gods in the fourth act, for the sake of morality", and asks what the fifth act will be. The killer, on this picture, is not an enemy of morality but morality itself: something the moral tradition taught turned against the God who stood behind it. Which value that was is the question of <Ref lesson="nihilism">nihilism</Ref>.

### Not a proof that God does not exist

The sentence has a long career as a slogan, quoted alone as if it were Nietzsche's proof that there is no God, or his boast about it. The texts rule that out, in three ways. Section 343 glosses the phrase as a statement about a belief, as above. The madman speaks with horror, not triumph. And Nietzsche kept his own atheism apart from the event. In his autobiography *Ecce Homo* (EH, written 1888) he says of it:

<Quote source="EH, 'Why I Am So Clever' 1, trans. W. Kaufmann">
I do not by any means know atheism as a result; even less as an event: it is a matter of course with me, from instinct. I am too inquisitive, too questionable, too exuberant to stand for any gross answer. God is a gross answer, an indelicacy against us thinkers — at bottom merely a gross prohibition for us: you shall not think!
</Quote>

The passage separates two things. His own unbelief, he says, was never an event at all: not reached by argument, not lived through as a crisis. "God is dead" names an event in the history of a culture, something that happened to Europe over generations. A sentence about a culture's beliefs is not a proof about God, and it would be the same sentence whether its author were an atheist, a believer or undecided.

## Discrediting: how a belief dies without being refuted

If the belief was not refuted, how did it die? A belief usually stops being held when someone shows it is false. Atheists had tried this for centuries, against the proofs of God's existence that philosophers had offered, and the result was a standoff: each refutation of a proof invited a better proof. *Daybreak* (D), published in 1881, a year before the madman, names a different method:

<Quote source="D 95, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
Historical refutation as the definitive refutation. — In former times, one sought to prove that there is no God — today one indicates how the belief that there is a God could arise and how this belief acquired its weight and importance: a counter-proof that there is no God thereby becomes superfluous. — When in former times one had refuted the "proofs of the existence of God" put forward, there always remained the doubt whether better proofs might not be adduced than those just refuted: in those days atheists did not know how to make a clean sweep.
</Quote>

### How an explanation can replace a proof

Read D 95's word "superfluous" closely. It does not say the explanation proves there is no God. It says the counter-proof is no longer needed, and a simpler case shows why.

A child believes there are ghosts in her room and asks you to look. You find nothing. That does not prove there are no ghosts: they might have left, or be invisible. Then you learn that she watched a horror film that evening. Now you have an explanation of her belief that never uses its being true: anyone who had watched that film might believe it, ghosts or no ghosts. You do not go on searching the room. Nothing has been disproved, but you no longer have any reason to take the belief seriously, because the only thing that made it look worth checking has been explained another way.

The method has two parts, and both are needed. The search turned up nothing, so the belief had no independent support. And the explanation accounts for the belief, and for how strongly it is held, without its being true. Either part alone fails: an explanation of a well-supported belief changes nothing, and a failed search alone leaves the question open. For the belief in God, the failed search is the long record of proofs that did not hold; the explanation is what D 95 calls for, an account of how the belief arose and why it weighed so much.

<Remark title="Discrediting a belief">
To *discredit* a belief is to undermine it without refuting it: when no evidence supports it, and its origin and the weight it carries are explained by causes that have nothing to do with its being true, there is no reason left to hold it, though nothing has shown it false.
</Remark>

### Weight without truth

D 95 promises to explain two things: how the belief arose, and how it "acquired its weight and importance". The second matters most, because a belief held for two thousand years by millions seems to need its truth to explain its hold. GS 110 answers that a belief's hold has a different source:

<Quote source="GS 110, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Thus the strength of knowledge does not depend on its degree of truth but on its age, on the degree to which it has been incorporated, on its character as a condition of life.
</Quote>

A belief that comforts, that holds a community together, and that has shaped habits for centuries will be held tightly whether or not it is true. Its strength is evidence of its usefulness and its age, not of its truth. Nietzsche's own explanations of where the belief in God came from point to the needs of the suffering, the power of priests and the fear of chance (<Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the ascetic priest</Ref>). Looking back in 1888 at his first free-spirit book, he described what this kind of explanation does to an ideal: "the ideal is not refuted — it freezes to death" (EH, on *Human, All Too Human* 1).

That is how the madman can say "we have killed him" to people who never refuted anything. The killers are not individual atheists with arguments. They are a culture that learned to explain its own religion: historians who read the Bible as a set of documents with authors and dates, scholars who compared it with other religions, psychologists who asked what needs faith served. Each explanation that never needed God's existence made the belief a little less credible. They are GS 153's playwright, slaying the gods for the sake of one of the culture's own values.

## What collapses with the belief: the whole of European morality

Grant that the belief is dead. The crowd's reply still stands: people can stop believing in God and go on being honest, kind and fair, so why should anything else fall? GS 343 says what the madman meant by the cost:

<Quote source="GS 343, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Much less may one suppose that many people know as yet what this event really means — and how much must collapse now that this faith has been undermined because it was built upon this faith, propped up by it, grown into it; for example, the whole of our European morality.
</Quote>

### The argument in five steps

Put as an argument, in the procedure of <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="the-procedure">the first lesson</Ref>, the claim runs:

1. For centuries Europe held its values because God stood behind them: kindness was commanded, suffering would be redeemed, truth was divine.
2. The belief in that God has become unbelievable for the educated: discredited, not refuted.
3. When the ground of a value goes, the value loses its standing, whether or not anyone notices.
4. So European morality has lost its foundation.
5. Most people keep the morality and drop the God.

Step 3 does not reach step 4 by itself. A value might have had several supports, so that losing one leaves it standing on the others. The argument needs a premise it does not state in GS: **these values had no other ground**.

Step 4 also leaves a second question open: whether losing these values is a loss at all. If they had been bad for the people who held them, their collapse would be a relief, and the madman's horror would be misplaced. So the argument needs a second premise as well, a reason why the collapse is a catastrophe and not a liberation; that premise is the subject of <Ref lesson="nihilism">nihilism</Ref>.

### The case of George Eliot

Nietzsche states half of the missing premise in *Twilight of the Idols* (TI), written in 1888, in a short section about George Eliot, the English novelist (1819–1880; her real name was Mary Ann Evans). In her twenties she lost her Christian faith, and she translated into English two of the German books that explained Christianity historically, a critical life of Jesus and a study of religion as a projection of human needs. Her novels are among the most morally serious of the century.

<Quote source="TI, 'Skirmishes of an Untimely Man' 5, trans. W. Kaufmann">
G. Eliot. They are rid of the Christian God and now believe all the more firmly that they must cling to Christian morality. That is an English consistency; we do not wish to hold it against little moralistic females à la Eliot. In England one must rehabilitate oneself after every little emancipation from theology by showing in a veritably awe-inspiring manner what a moral fanatic one is. That is the penance they pay there.

We others hold otherwise. When one gives up the Christian faith, one pulls the right to Christian morality out from under one's feet. This morality is by no means self-evident: this point has to be exhibited again and again, despite the English flatheads. Christianity is a system, a whole view of things thought out together. By breaking one main concept out of it, the faith in God, one breaks the whole: nothing necessary remains in one's hands. Christianity presupposes that man does not know, cannot know, what is good for him, what evil: he believes in God, who alone knows it. Christian morality is a command; its origin is transcendent; it is beyond all criticism, all right to criticism; it has truth only if God is the truth — it stands and falls with faith in God.

When the English actually believe that they know "intuitively" what is good and evil, when they therefore suppose that they no longer require Christianity as the guarantee of morality, we merely witness the effects of the dominion of the Christian value judgment and an expression of the strength and depth of this dominion: such that the origin of English morality has been forgotten, such that the very conditional character of its right to existence is no longer felt. For the English, morality is not yet a problem.
</Quote>

*Moral-Weiblein*, "little moralistic females", is built on *Weiblein*, the diminutive of *Weib*, a blunt word for woman: "little female". The contempt is aimed at a woman's moral zeal, and it is meant; the next sentence mocks the need to show "what a moral fanatic one is" after every step away from theology. Inside the insult is an argument, and it is a charge of inconsistency: "That is an English consistency" is irony. She dropped the premise and kept the conclusion.

The section supplies the first half of the missing premise. Christian morality came as a *command* whose source was beyond human judgment, and the system gave a reason why it had to: human beings "cannot know" what is good for them, so they must be told. Remove the one who tells, and the command has no author. The "intuitions" that are supposed to replace him turn out to recommend exactly what the command said, and that coincidence is evidence that they are its after-effects, not an independent source: the origin "has been forgotten".

### The shadow and the starlight

Step 5 is why nobody in the market feels the loss: how can a morality keep working once its ground is gone? Values held for centuries are built into habits, institutions, the way people praise and blame, even the grammar of moral talk. A habit does not stop when its reason is forgotten; it runs on what it was given, and so does a morality. People go on praising humility and blaming pride without asking what made humility a virtue, and as long as nobody asks, nothing in their daily life tells them the answer has gone. GS 108's shadow, still shown in a cave for centuries after Buddha's death, is this survival.

## The two strongest objections

The argument has two weak points that a careful critic would press: step 3 with its unstated premise, and step 2, the claim that explaining a belief is enough to finish it.

### Values never needed God

The first objection is older than Christianity. In Plato's *Euthyphro*, a short dialogue set as Socrates goes to his trial in 399 BC, Socrates asks a priest whether the gods love what is holy because it is holy, or whether it is holy because the gods love it. Put to Christian morality, the question is whether God commands love of neighbour because it is good, or whether it is good because he commands it. If the first, God is a witness to a good that holds without him, and his death changes nothing about it. Eliot is the living proof: she lost God and lost nothing of her morality, and so did the English utilitarians, who grounded right and wrong in the greatest happiness of the greatest number.

<Figure
  src="./figures/euthyphro.svg"
  alt="A fork from 'God commands love of neighbour': left in blue, commanded because good, God is a witness to a good that holds without him, so if God dies nothing changes (the objection's horn); right in amber, good because commanded, God is the source and the value holds only through him, so if God dies the value falls (GS 343's conclusion). A dashed box under the left horn: TI Skirmishes 5 attacks here, which good would anyone see without the tradition? 'not self-evident'"
  caption="The Euthyphro fork: the objection takes the left branch, and Skirmishes 5 asks whether that branch is really open."
/>

Nietzsche's reply, from Skirmishes 5, does not choose the other horn. It asks which goods are supposed to hold without God. The Christian content is specific: the equal worth of every soul, love of one's enemies, the meek inheriting the earth, humility as a virtue, suffering as a path to redemption, sin and guilt. "This morality is by no means self-evident": the Greek heroes of Homer, the ancient nobilities and much of the rest of the world did not find these things good by intuition, and many found them contemptible. The objection says the goods would be seen without the command; the only people who do see them so are people raised inside the tradition that commanded them. So the left horn of the fork assumes what it needs to show.

The reply has a gap, and it is plain. It works against the specific Christian content and reaches no further. GS 343 speaks of what was "built upon this faith", Skirmishes 5 of a morality that "stands and falls with faith in God", and neither touches minimal claims nobody ever derived from God, such as that torturing a child for amusement is wrong. Whether Nietzsche himself holds such prohibitions is a separate question, which this argument does not answer (<Ref lesson="cruelty-and-culture">cruelty and culture</Ref>). And the stronger half of the premise, that the Christian values have no other ground, is still a promissory note in 1888; the genealogy has to pay it, by showing where these values did come from (<Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave revolt in morality</Ref>).

<Aside title="The same argument from inside the Church">
In 1958 a Catholic philosopher at Oxford, writing with no reference to Nietzsche, argued that the moral "ought", in the sense of an obligation binding on everyone, is a survival from a "law conception of ethics" which makes sense only if there is a divine lawgiver. Without belief in one, she wrote, it is "as if the notion 'criminal' were to remain when criminal law and criminal courts had been abolished and forgotten", and she proposed giving the notion up. A believer and the author of *The Antichrist* agreed on the structure: the morality stands or falls with the faith. They disagreed only about whether the faith should stand.
</Aside>

### Discrediting is a genetic fallacy

The second objection uses a rule the course has already applied to Nietzsche himself: where an argument came from does not show whether it holds (<Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="illness-dates-the-turns-it-does-not-settle-the-arguments">illness dates the turns</Ref>). The *genetic fallacy* is inferring that a belief is false from how it arose. If a sick man's philosophy is not refuted by his sickness, the belief in God is not refuted by the needs it served.

D 95 does not commit the fallacy, because it never infers falsity. It says a counter-proof becomes "superfluous", which is a claim about reasons, not about truth. The difference is between an argument and a belief held without one. An argument carries its own support: its premises and steps can be checked whatever their origin, so the illness of its author is beside the point. The belief in God, after the proofs had failed, was held on upbringing, authority and need. Once those are explained, the explanation is not an extra objection added to the belief's support; it accounts for the whole of it. The child's ghosts are not shown to be unreal by the horror film; they are shown to have nothing else behind them.

So the reply holds on one condition, and the condition is real: the explanation has to be good. A thin or speculative story about where faith comes from discredits nothing. Nietzsche's confidence that the story could be told well rested on the historical and psychological work of his century.

## Dread and dawn

One question is left: how the event should be met, and the texts give two answers. The madman meets it with dread: the earth plunging away from its sun, night closing in, no up or down. GS 124, the section just before him, warns the ship that has left the land that there will be hours "when you will realize that it is infinite and that there is nothing more awesome than infinity", and that there is "no longer any 'land'" to be homesick for.

GS 343, written five years later in the voice of "we philosophers and 'free spirits'", agrees that a long collapse is coming, a "monstrous logic of terror", and then reports that the first consequences for people like him are the opposite of gloomy:

<Quote source="GS 343, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Indeed, we philosophers and "free spirits" feel, when we hear the news that "the old god is dead," as if a new dawn shone on us; our heart overflows with gratitude, amazement, premonitions, expectation. At long last the horizon appears free to us again, even if it should not be bright; at long last our ships may venture out again, venture out to face any danger; all the daring of the lover of knowledge is permitted again; the sea, our sea, lies open again; perhaps there has never yet been such an "open sea."
</Quote>

The images of GS 125 come back with their sign reversed. The horizon the madman mourned as wiped away is "free" again; the sea he said had been drunk up is "our sea", open. Both attitudes are Nietzsche's, and they are not a contradiction. For the many who need a fixed point, the loss is disorientation, and the dread is accurate about what is coming for them. For the few who can live without one, the same loss is permission: no prohibition on thinking, no horizon drawn in advance, the chance to set a course of one's own. Reading him as a prophet of doom keeps only the madman; reading him as a cheerful atheist keeps only the dawn. The texts hold both, and the madman's demand says which response a person gets. With the fixed standard gone, there are two ways to go on: live without any ranking of higher and lower, or take over the work the standard did and rank for oneself. For someone who can only do the first, the loss is the infinite nothing. Only for someone who takes over the standard's work does it become permission, and that is what "must we ourselves not become gods?" asks of the killers. Whether anyone can do that work, and how, is the subject of <Ref lesson="creating-values">creating values</Ref>.

<Conclusion>
You can now read "God is dead" as an argument rather than a slogan. It reports that the belief in the Christian God has become unbelievable for educated Europe: not refuted, but discredited, because its origin and its hold can be explained without its being true, so that a counter-proof is superfluous. From there it infers that "the whole of our European morality" has lost its foundation, through a premise the argument needs and Skirmishes 5 half states: Christian morality is a command with a transcendent source, and it has no other ground. Most people keep the morality and drop the God, the shadow in the cave and the light of a dead star, which is why the atheists in the market laugh. You can also state the two strongest objections, the Euthyphro fork and the genetic fallacy, and Nietzsche's replies, with the gap that remains: the argument reaches the specific Christian content, and its decisive premise is paid for only by the genealogy.

One thing the lesson left as "we": who, or what, did the killing. The next lesson, <Ref lesson="nihilism">nihilism</Ref>, finds the killer among the values themselves, and shows why that makes the loss worse than losing a belief.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
GS 108 and GS 125 belong to the 1882 edition of *The Gay Science*; GS 343 opens the fifth book, added in 1887. Its first paragraph in full:

<Quote source="GS 343, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The meaning of our cheerfulness. — The greatest recent event — that "God is dead," that the belief in the Christian god has become unbelievable — is already beginning to cast its first shadows over Europe. For the few at least, whose eyes — the suspicion in whose eyes is strong and subtle enough for this spectacle, some sun seems to have set and some ancient and profound trust has been turned into doubt; to them our old world must appear daily more like evening, more mistrustful, stranger, "older." But in the main one may say: The event itself is far too great, too distant, too remote from the multitude's capacity for comprehension even for the tidings of it to be thought of as having arrived as yet. Much less may one suppose that many people know as yet what this event really means — and how much must collapse now that this faith has been undermined because it was built upon this faith, propped up by it, grown into it; for example, the whole of our European morality. This long plenitude and sequence of breakdown, destruction, ruin, and cataclysm that is now impending — who could guess enough of it today to be compelled to play the teacher and advance proclaimer of this monstrous logic of terror, the prophet of a gloom and an eclipse of the sun whose like has probably never yet occurred on earth?
</Quote>

(a) Place each of the three sections in Nietzsche's periods and say what that means for its weight as evidence of his mature view. (b) Reconstruct this paragraph as claim, steps and unstated premise. (c) Say what the five years add to the 1882 sections and what they keep.

<Solution>
**(a) Dating.** GS 108 and 125 are period 2, the free-spirit books of 1878–82; GS 343 is period 3, the mature works. All three are published sections and carry full weight for their dates (<Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="ranking-the-texts-and-citing-them">ranking the texts</Ref>). Because GS 343 is later, it can be used to read the earlier two, and it says so itself: it refers back to the event they announced and glosses it.

**(b) Reconstruction.** Claim: the belief in the Christian God has become unbelievable, and this will bring down what was built on it, including the whole of European morality. Steps given: the event has happened ("the greatest recent event"); only "the few" see it yet, as a sun that has set; for the multitude its news has not yet arrived ("too great, too distant"); what must collapse is what was "built upon this faith, propped up by it, grown into it"; the example is morality. Unstated premise: European morality has no support besides the faith, so that when the faith goes the morality cannot stand on anything else.

**(c) What five years add and keep.** They add the gloss, "the belief in the Christian god has become unbelievable", which turns a parable into a claim about a belief, and they add the name of what collapses, "the whole of our European morality", which GS 125 only implies through images of horizon, sun and up and down. They keep the shadow (the event "is already beginning to cast its first shadows over Europe") and the lag between deed and news. And they add a second attitude, the philosophers' dawn, beside the madman's dread.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "In *The Gay Science* Nietzsche proved that God does not exist, and 'God is dead' is his triumphant conclusion." Use at least three texts from the lesson, and say what kind of claim GS 125 makes instead.

<Solution>
**Not a proof.** GS 343 glosses the phrase as "the belief in the Christian god has become unbelievable", a statement about the standing of a belief in a culture, not about God. D 95 explicitly says the new method makes a counter-proof "superfluous": it does not offer one. A sentence that dispenses with disproof is not a disproof.

**Not his conclusion.** EH, 'Why I Am So Clever' 1 says his own atheism was never a "result", "even less an event": it was "a matter of course … from instinct". So "God is dead" cannot be the conclusion of his reasoning about God; it names an event in Europe's history, which he separates from his own unbelief.

**Not triumphant.** The madman speaks with horror (the earth unchained from its sun, an infinite nothing, night closing in), and his listeners, the ones who laugh, are the atheists. GS 343 does report a philosophers' "dawn", but beside a "monstrous logic of terror" for the culture.

**What GS 125 claims instead.** It is a diagnosis addressed to unbelievers: their unbelief is a deed ("we have killed him"), its consequences for the values that rested on the belief have not yet reached them ("I have come too early"), and those consequences are the loss of every standard of orientation the belief supplied.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Before using TI Skirmishes 5 as evidence, settle the three things to fix about any passage (<Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="the-procedure">the reading procedure</Ref>): its date, what kind of text it is, and its argument. It is a section of *Twilight of the Idols*, written in 1888 for German readers, about an English novelist. Then take this reply from a present-day agnostic: "I don't believe in God, but I hold that all human beings are equal in dignity. I don't need God for that." Reconstruct the answer Skirmishes 5 gives, and name the premise of that answer which the agnostic could most reasonably deny.

<Solution>
**Date.** 1888, period 3: the mature works, so it can stand for his settled view.

**Kind of text.** A published book (it appeared in January 1889), so full weight. Its audience is German and its target an English type; the national contrast ("the English flatheads") is polemic, and the argument has to be separated from it. The section names Eliot as a clear case of a type, so it is evidence of his view of the type, not a biography of her.

**Argument.** Reconstructed below, as the answer to the agnostic.

**The answer.** (1) "All human beings are equal in dignity" is not a neutral intuition: it is Christian content, the equal worth of every soul before God, and most cultures in history did not hold it. (2) Christian morality was a command with a transcendent origin, given to creatures who "cannot know" what is good for them. (3) Remove the faith, and "nothing necessary remains in one's hands": the command has no author. (4) The agnostic's confidence that he knows it "intuitively" is the after-effect of the command's long dominion, whose origin "has been forgotten". So the agnostic keeps the conclusion of an argument whose premise he has given up.

**The deniable premise.** That the value of equal dignity has no ground except the Christian one. The agnostic can grant that the value came to Europe through Christianity and still argue that it has another support now, for instance in an argument from what all human beings share, such as the capacity to reason or to suffer. Nietzsche's reply is owed to the genealogy, which tries to show that such secular arguments were built after the fact to support a value already held for other reasons. Until that is shown, the premise is the agnostic's best point of attack.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A boundary probe. Take a value that no one in Europe ever derived from God: "Do not torture children for amusement." Does the death of God, as GS 343 and Skirmishes 5 argue it, touch this value? State the scope of the argument exactly, and quote the clause that fixes it.

<Solution>
**No, not as argued.** The argument runs from the discrediting of a belief to the collapse of what rested on that belief. Its reach is fixed by step 3 and its unstated premise: a value falls only if its ground was the faith and nothing else. A prohibition held by nearly every culture, religious or not, and supported by reasons that never mention God (the child's pain, the cruelty of the act), does not rest on the Christian God, so his death leaves it where it was.

**The clause that fixes the scope.** "Christian morality is a command; its origin is transcendent; … it stands and falls with faith in God." The subject is *Christian* morality, the specific content that came as a command from beyond, and GS 343 speaks of what was "built upon this faith, propped up by it, grown into it". The argument reaches whatever answers that description and nothing else.

**What follows.** The dispute is therefore about which values answer the description. The argument reaches pity, equality of souls, humility, the love of the weak and guilt; it does not reach the minimal prohibitions. Whether Nietzsche endorses those prohibitions is a separate question, which this argument leaves open (<Ref lesson="cruelty-and-culture">cruelty and culture</Ref>). Whether those specific values have a ground besides the faith is exactly what the lesson called the argument's gap, and why its decisive premise needs the genealogy.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two English renderings of the same sentences of GS 125:

<Quote source="GS 125, trans. T. Common, P. V. Cohn and M. D. Petre, 1910">
What did we do when we loosened this earth from its sun? … Do we not smell the divine putrefaction? — for even Gods putrefy! God is dead! God remains dead! And we have killed him!
</Quote>

<Quote source="GS 125, trans. W. Kaufmann, 1974">
What were we doing when we unchained this earth from its sun? … Do we smell nothing as yet of the divine decomposition? Gods, too, decompose. God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him.
</Quote>

The German reads: "Was taten wir, als wir diese Erde von ihrer Sonne losketteten? … Riechen wir noch nichts von der göttlichen Verwesung? – auch Götter verwesen! Gott ist tot! Gott bleibt tot! Und wir haben ihn getötet!"

The words that carry the differences: *losketteten* is from *losketten*, built on *Kette*, "chain", with *los-*, "off, free"; *riechen* is "smell"; *noch nichts* is "nothing yet" (*noch* = "still, yet"); *Verwesung* is the decay of a dead body, *verwesen* the verb; *bleibt* is "remains". The German ends its sentences with exclamation marks.

Find the differences that matter, decide which rendering is closer at each, and say whether any of them changes the argument the lesson reconstructed.

<Solution>
**"Loosened" against "unchained".** *Losketten* is built on *Kette*, chain: to unchain. "Unchained" is closer. The image matters a little: a chain is a bond that holds a thing in place, and the madman's point is that the earth had been held in orbit around a centre and is now free and falling. "Loosened" keeps the sense of release but drops the bond.

**"Do we not smell" against "do we smell nothing as yet".** *Riechen wir noch nichts* contains *noch*, "yet". Kaufmann keeps it; the 1910 version drops it. This one carries argument: "as yet" is the lag between the deed and its news, the same lag as "I have come too early". The 1910 version turns a question about delay into a plain rhetorical question.

**"Putrefy" and "decompose".** *Verwesen* means to decay, as a corpse does. Both are accurate; "putrefy" is coarser, and neither changes the claim.

**"God remains dead" in both.** *Bleibt* is "remains", and both keep it, rightly: it says there is no resurrection, and that the shadow of GS 108 is not a return of God but an after-effect of his death. The exclamation marks of the German, kept in 1910 and turned to full stops in 1974, change the tone, not the claim.

**Verdict.** None of the differences changes the five-step argument, but "as yet" is the one a reader would most miss, because it carries the parable's central claim that the deed is done and its consequences have not arrived.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The madman says that the people in the market killed God themselves, and the lesson said the killing was done by a culture that learned to explain its own religion historically and psychologically. Ask why a Christian culture would do that to its own faith. Which virtue, taught by Christianity itself and practised with growing strictness for centuries, could end by turning against the belief in God? Give a reason for your answer from what the lesson says about how the belief died.

<Solution>
**Truthfulness.** Christianity taught that lying is a sin, that God sees the heart, and that the conscience must examine itself honestly, in confession and before God. Practised for centuries, that discipline became the scholar's and the scientist's intellectual honesty: the refusal to believe what one has no reason to believe, and to call providence what can be explained by causes.

**Why it fits.** The belief died by discrediting: historians, philologists and psychologists explained its origin and its hold without its being true. That work requires exactly the virtue of following the evidence against one's wishes. So the killers did not attack Christianity from outside; they applied its own demand for truthfulness to its own central belief. The faith was destroyed by a value it had trained.

This is the doorstep of <Ref lesson="nihilism">nihilism</Ref>. GS 357 answers the question "what really triumphed over the Christian god" with "Christian morality itself, the concept of truthfulness that was understood ever more rigorously", and GM III 27 draws the next inference: Christianity as a dogma was "destroyed by its own morality", and the same truthfulness must now turn on Christianity as a morality. That is the sense in which, for Nietzsche, the highest values devalue themselves.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Guilt did not begin as a moral category: the German Schuld means debt, and the Second Treatise's argument is that the whole apparatus of guilt descends from a creditor's right to hurt a debtor who cannot pay. This lesson reconstructs that descent as the treatise states it, in two runs of sections: GM II:4–8 (1887), where the creditor–debtor relation is described and the pleasure that settles an unpaid debt is named, and II:19–22, where the same relation is reinterpreted between the living and their ancestors, their gods, and the Christian God, until the debt has no sum that could close it. It grades the philological premise the chain starts from, reads the negative result of II:14–15 that forces the chain to take the route it takes, and locates the one step at which owing becomes being guilty, with the premise that step borrows and the treatise never argues.

The course's argument arrives here with a question the previous lesson left open on purpose. <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">The promising-animal lesson</Ref> established that II:1–3 describe a bred capacity, the memory of the will, and end on an individual who calls his consciousness of that capacity his conscience; it ended on II:4's first sentence, which calls the consciousness of guilt "that *other* sombre thing" and says its origin lies elsewhere than in the breeding just described. Behind that stands <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref>'s result, that morality's first presupposition, an agent who could have acted otherwise, is a fiction added to the deed (GM I:13), and <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>'s rule, that a thing's origin and its present use lie *toto caelo* apart (II:12). What this lesson adds to the spine is the Second Treatise's second origin story stated as a chain: debt, compensation, cruelty, a creditor who grows into a god, and a debt paid at last by the creditor himself. The chain reads as mechanism plus evidence, graded, from II:4 to II:21; the step at which it turns moral does not, because the premise it needs is the one I:13 removed, and the treatise names the step without arguing it. That location is the lesson's result and the next-but-one lesson's test case.

The route: II:4's premise and its own hedge that retribution is a late idea; II:5–6's equivalence, with the pledge, the creditor's pleasure and the festival; II:8's measuring animal and its identity with the promiser; II:9–15's community as creditor and the negative result on punishment; II:19–21's escalation as a chain of substitutions; II:21–22's moralization, the missing premise, and the grid that makes the treatise's two origin stories two axes; then a closing statement of what is established.

## II:4: guilt descends from debt, and "he could have acted otherwise" is a late idea

From a section that opens by asking how the consciousness of guilt came into the world, and that follows three sections on breeding, one expects the answer to continue the breeding story. II:4 does the opposite: it turns on "our genealogists of morals" for the second time, denies them any "historical instinct", and answers with a word. "Have these previous genealogists of morals let themselves dream even from afar that, for example, *that moral master-concept 'Schuld' took its descent from the very material concept 'Schulden'* (*jener moralische Hauptbegriff 'Schuld' seine Herkunft aus dem sehr materiellen Begriff 'Schulden' genommen hat*)?" The German is kept in the line because <Ref lesson="key-terms-in-translation">the translation chapter</Ref> showed that every consistent English rendering changes the premise's grade: "ought" from "owe" makes it a pun, "guilt" from "debt" makes it a bare assertion, and only the German shows what it is, one word with two senses, offered as evidence for a claim about which sense came first.

### The premise graded: an identity and a conjecture

The sentence carries two claims of different evidential grade, and the course's rule from <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the etymological argument of I:4–5</Ref> applies to both. That *Schuld* means guilt and *Schulden* means debts is a fact about German, checkable in a dictionary, and the two are one lexeme: *Schuld* is the noun of *sollen*, to owe, to be obliged. That the moral sense *descended* from the material one is a claim about history, which the word-fact supports and does not prove: words shift sense for reasons that have nothing to do with the institutions they name, and an identity of two senses is compatible with two concepts that share a root and no history. The Preface's demand for "what is documented, what can actually be ascertained" (Preface §7) therefore falls on the sections that follow, and II:4 knows it: it announces the descent and offers, in its own text, only a dating and a location.

The dating is the section's second claim, and it is the one this lesson's last section depends on. Punishment "as a retaliation (*Vergeltung*) developed entirely apart from any presupposition about freedom or unfreedom of the will", to such a degree that "a high level of humanization" was needed before the animal man began to draw "those much more primitive distinctions 'intentional', 'negligent', 'accidental', 'accountable' and their opposites" and apply them to the measuring-out of punishment. The idea that "the criminal deserves punishment *because he could have acted otherwise*", now "so cheap and apparently so natural, so unavoidable" that it has had to serve as the explanation of how the sense of justice came into the world, "is in fact an extremely late, even refined form of human judging and inferring; whoever places it at the beginnings lays coarse fingers on the psychology of older humanity". The claim is a dating: the retributive idea, desert grounded in an ability to have done otherwise, came late, and came from somewhere other than the debt relation the section is about to name.

### What punishment was for most of history

The location follows from the dating. "Through the longest period of human history punishment was not at all inflicted *because* one held the wrongdoer responsible for his deed, thus not under the presupposition that only the guilty is to be punished: rather, as parents still punish their children today, out of anger at an injury suffered, which is discharged on the injurer." The anger has a bound: it is "held within limits and modified by the idea that every injury has somewhere its *equivalent* and can really be paid off, even if through a *pain* of the injurer". And that idea has a source. "Whence has this ancient, deep-rooted, perhaps now ineradicable idea drawn its power, the idea of an equivalence of injury and pain? I have already betrayed it: in the contractual relation between *creditor* and *debtor*, which is as old as the existence of 'legal subjects' at all, and which in turn points back to the basic forms of buying, selling, barter, trade and traffic."

<Remark title="II:4 reconstructed">
**P1.** One German noun, *Schuld*, carries the moral sense (guilt) and, as *Schulden*, the material sense (debts). Status: a fact about the language. Anchor: "*jener moralische Hauptbegriff 'Schuld' ... 'Schulden'*".

**P2.** Punishment as retaliation developed independently of any hypothesis about the freedom of the will; the distinctions that make an agent accountable (intentional, negligent, accidental) required "a high level of humanization" and were applied to punishment late. Anchor: "*vollkommen abseits von jeder Voraussetzung über Freiheit oder Unfreiheit des Willens*".

**P3.** For most of human history punishment was anger at an injury, discharged on its author, and bounded by the idea that every injury has an equivalent that can be paid, even in the injurer's pain. Anchor: "*aus Zorn über einen erlittenen Schaden*"; "*jeder Schaden irgend worin sein Äquivalent habe*".

**P4.** The idea of an equivalence between injury and pain has its source in the contractual relation of creditor and debtor, as old as legal subjects, and older than that in exchange. Anchor: "*in dem Vertragsverhältniss zwischen Gläubiger und Schuldner*".

**C.** The moral concept of guilt took its descent from the material concept of debt; the retributive idea, "he could have acted otherwise", is a late reinterpretation and not the origin of punishment.

**The missing premise.** P1 gives an identity and P2–P4 give a dating and a location; nothing in the section supplies the *mechanism* by which a relation between a creditor and a debtor came to carry a moral sense. The descent is asserted, and the section defers the account to what follows: II:5–6 for how a debt was settled in pain, II:8 for how the relation became a canon of justice, II:19–22 for how the creditor grew. The premise the conclusion needs, that the mechanism about to be described is one of *descent* and not of two practices that share a vocabulary, is what the rest of the treatise is offered to show.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That the identity is a homonymy with a shared root and separate histories, as English "bank" has two senses and one etymon, so that the descent claim is a genetic inference from a word. The reading is answered by the section's own procedure: the word is offered as a sign, and the evidence proper is institutional (the contract, the pledge, the law codes of II:5), so the descent claim stands or falls with that record and not with the word. II:12's rule then applies to the record: what the reader will be shown is a use of the debt relation, and the descent claim is the claim that this use came first.
</Remark>

The chain's moral step will therefore have to import that premise from somewhere else.

## II:5–6: the compensation for an unpaid debt is a feeling in the creditor, not a transfer of goods

II:4 named the relation; II:5 describes it, and its first sentences place the promising animal inside it. Calling these contracts to mind, the section says, arouses "suspicion and resistance" toward the older humanity that created them, and it says why: "Precisely here *promises are made*; precisely here it is a matter of *making a memory* for the one who promises; precisely here, one may suspect, will be a site (*Fundstätte*) of hard, cruel, painful things." The memory of the will of II:1 and the mnemonics of pain of II:3, both reconstructed in <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">the previous lesson</Ref>, are here given their first institution: a debtor is a promiser, and the creditor is the one who makes his memory.

### The pledge and the creditor's right

The debtor, "in order to inspire trust in his promise of repayment, in order to give a guarantee for the seriousness and sanctity of his promise, in order to impress repayment on his own conscience as a duty, an obligation", pledges by contract "for the case that he does not pay, something he otherwise still 'possesses', over which he still has power, for example his body or his wife or his freedom or even his life (or, under certain religious presuppositions, even his salvation, his soul's welfare, and at last even his peace in the grave: so in Egypt, where the debtor's corpse found no rest from the creditor even in the grave)".

The creditor's right on default is stated without softening. "Above all, the creditor could inflict on the debtor's body every kind of indignity and torture, for example cut off as much of it as seemed proportionate to the size of the debt: and from this point of view there were early and everywhere exact, in part horrifyingly minute, legally valid valuations (*Abschätzungen*) of the individual limbs and parts of the body." Nietzsche counts it "already a progress, a proof of a freer, more grandly calculating, more *Roman* conception of law" that Rome's Twelve Tables decreed it immaterial how much or how little the creditors cut in such a case: *si plus minusve secuerunt, ne fraude esto*, if they have cut more or less, let it be no fraud. The clause is the section's one document, and what it documents is decisive for the reconstruction: a law that makes the *amount* cut irrelevant is not measuring the cut to the debt. The right is a right to cut, licensed as such. This is the point at which II:5 adds nothing to II:3's catalogue of punishments; what it adds is the *right*, and the logic of the right is the section's subject.

### The logic of the equivalence: a Wohlgefühl in place of goods

"Let us make clear to ourselves the logic of this whole form of settlement: it is strange enough." From the word "compensation" one expects a transfer, and the section says that is exactly what the equivalence is not. "The equivalence is given by this, that in place of an advantage directly making good the injury (thus in place of a settlement in money, land, possessions of any kind) the creditor is granted a kind of *pleasurable feeling* (*Wohlgefühl*) as repayment and settlement: the pleasurable feeling of being permitted to vent his power on one who is powerless without a second thought, the voluptuous pleasure '*de faire le mal pour le plaisir de le faire*', the enjoyment of doing violence."

The pleasure has a scale, and the scale is the section's evidence for what kind of pleasure it is. It "is valued the higher the lower and baser the creditor stands in the order of society, and can easily appear to him as a most delicious morsel, indeed as a foretaste of higher rank. By means of the 'punishment' of the debtor the creditor *participates in a right of masters* (*Herren-Rechte*): at last he too comes for once to the elevating feeling of being permitted to despise and maltreat a being as 'beneath him', or at least, where the actual power of punishment, its execution, has already passed to the 'authorities', of *seeing* it despised and maltreated. The settlement thus consists in a directive to and right to cruelty (*in einem Anweis und Anrecht auf Grausamkeit*)." A pleasure that grows as the creditor's rank falls is not a pleasure in the debtor's pain as such; it is a pleasure in a change of the creditor's standing, and the text names the change: participation, for once, in a right that belongs to masters. II:10 gives the same scale from the other end: the powerful community treats offences as less serious, "the creditor has always become more humane as he has grown richer", and mercy is "the privilege of the most powerful". The section supplies both ends of one curve, and the curve is the reason the course's working hypothesis reads the pleasure as an increase in the feeling of power rather than as pleasure at pain; <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref> states the general doctrine.

<Aside>
Bernard Reginster reads II:5 as an argument against a rival psychology. Schopenhauer explained cruelty as a hedonist must: the cruel man alleviates his own pain indirectly, by comparison with a greater pain in another. That explains why he would want to *see* suffering. It does not explain why he would want to *make* it, at a cost to himself, when seeing it would do; and II:6 insists on the difference, "seeing-suffer feels good, making-suffer even more so". Reginster's point is that the two observations II:5 and II:10 supply, the pleasure larger in the low-ranked creditor and absent in the powerful one, are what a hedonist cannot predict and what the will-to-power reading predicts directly: the pleasure is the increase of a feeling of power, larger for the one who had less. That is why the passage does duty in the later lesson as psychological evidence and not only as history.
</Aside>

### II:6: the cradle, the knot, and the festival

II:6 draws the conclusion II:4 promised and marks its grade. "It is in this sphere, in the law of obligations (*Obligationen-Recht*), that the moral concept-world of 'guilt', 'conscience', 'duty', 'sacredness of duty' has its birthplace: its beginning, like the beginning of everything great on earth, has been thoroughly and long drenched in blood." The world has never quite lost "a certain smell of blood and torture", the parenthesis adds, "not even in old Kant: the categorical imperative smells of cruelty". And here "that uncanny and perhaps now inextricable knotting-together of the ideas 'guilt and suffering' was first knotted". Then the question is put again, in the form the equivalence requires: "to what extent can suffering be a settlement of 'debts' (*Schulden*)? To the extent that *making*-suffer felt good in the highest degree, to the extent that the injured party exchanged for the loss, including the displeasure at the loss, an extraordinary counter-pleasure: making-suffer, *an actual festival* (*ein eigentliches Fest*)". The next sentence fixes the status: "*Dies vermuthungsweise gesprochen*", this is said by way of conjecture, "for it is hard to see to the bottom of such underground things, apart from its being painful"; and whoever "clumsily throws in the concept of 'revenge'" has explained nothing, since revenge "leads back to the same problem: how can making-suffer be a satisfaction?".

The datum the section offers for the conjecture is a change in readers. "It is not so long ago that one could not imagine princely weddings and popular festivals of the grandest style without executions, torturings or perhaps an auto-da-fé, likewise no noble household without beings on whom one could vent one's malice and cruel teasing without a second thought (one may recall, say, Don Quixote at the court of the Duchess: we read the whole *Don Quixote* today with a bitter taste on the tongue, almost with a torture, and would be very foreign, very obscure to its author and his contemporaries, who read it with the best conscience in the world as the most cheerful of books, they nearly laughed themselves to death over it)." The example is chosen because it is checkable: the same text, two audiences, one of which laughs and one of which winces, and the difference is the datum. What it documents is a shift in sensibility toward the sight of a person made a butt, and that is evidence, in the Preface's sense, for the claim that the sight was once a pleasure taken without shame; it is not evidence for the contract anthropology, which stays at the grade Nietzsche gave it. The section's maxim follows: "*Seeing*-suffer feels good, *making*-suffer even more so: that is a hard proposition, but an old, powerful, human-all-too-human main proposition ... Without cruelty no festival: so teaches the oldest, longest history of man, and in punishment too there is so much that is *festive*!"

<Remark title="II:5–6 reconstructed">
**P1.** Contractual debt relations, with a pledge and a right on default, are older than any moral relation; they are where promises are made and memories made for the promiser. Anchor: "*Hier gerade wird versprochen*".

**P2.** The debtor pledged what he still had power over, and on default the creditor held a right over his body, fixed in law down to the limb; the Twelve Tables made the amount cut immaterial. Anchor: "*si plus minusve secuerunt, ne fraude esto*".

**P3.** The equivalence that settled an unpaid debt was not a transfer of goods but a feeling in the creditor: the pleasure of venting power on the powerless, greater the lower the creditor's rank, a participation in a right of masters. Anchor: "*eine Art Wohlgefühl als Rückzahlung*"; "*Anweis und Anrecht auf Grausamkeit*".

**P4.** Making-suffer was a festive pleasure of older humanity, taken without shame; evidence: executions at weddings and festivals, the household butt, and the changed reading of *Don Quixote*. Anchor: "*Ohne Grausamkeit kein Fest*".

**C.** "Guilt", "conscience", "duty" and "the sacredness of duty" have their birthplace in the law of obligations, in an economy in which pain paid for debt, before any idea that the sufferer deserved it.

**The missing premise.** P3 states that the creditor was *granted* the pleasure, and P2 that the contract gave him the *right*; the section does not say how a pleasure in inflicting became a right to inflict. The two are different kinds of thing, a psychological fact and a legal one, and the conclusion needs them joined: the contract is what turns a wish into an entitlement, so that the cruelty is not only enjoyed but *permitted*. The section supplies the joint by juxtaposition and not by argument, and the last section of this lesson names it (with Janaway) as *legitimation*, the axis along which the debt story contributes to guilt.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That the pledge and the cut were security and deterrence, a way of making default costly, and the pleasure a by-product. The reading is answered from the section's own document: a clause that makes the amount cut irrelevant is not calibrating a deterrent, and II:12's rule, restated in <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>, puts deterrence among the later meanings of a procedure that predates them. What survives of the reading is a grade: P4 rests on a conjecture Nietzsche labels as such, and the datum he offers for it is a change in taste, which shows that the pleasure was once unashamed and not that it was the equivalence's original content.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The equivalence of II:5 is not between an injury and a pain; it is between an injury and the creditor's pleasure in inflicting pain. That is why the chain's currency can later be paid by suffering the creditor does not inflict (the ancestors' sacrifices of II:19), by suffering the creditor undergoes himself (the God of II:21), and by suffering the debtor inflicts on himself (the man of II:22): what pays is a feeling on the inflicting side, and the inflicter and the sufferer can be the same being.
</KeyFact>

## II:8: the measuring animal, and the debtor as the promiser of II:1

II:7 is a detour, on suffering and the gods as its spectators; II:8 resumes "the train of our inquiry" and generalizes what II:5 described. "The feeling of *Schuld*, of personal obligation, has had its origin, as we saw, in the oldest and most primitive relation between persons that there is, in the relation between buyer and seller, creditor and debtor: here person first stepped up against person, here person first *measured* himself against person. No degree of civilization has been found so low that something of this relation is not already noticeable in it." The relation is then made the origin of thinking. "Making prices, measuring values, thinking out equivalents, exchanging: this preoccupied the very first thinking of man to such a degree that in a certain sense it *is* thinking: here the oldest kind of shrewdness was bred (*herangezüchtet*), here likewise the first beginning of human pride, of his feeling of precedence over other animals, may be supposed." The verb is II:1's breeding verb, and the placement is deliberate: the sagacity that II:1 listed among the promise's presuppositions, to reckon and calculate, is here bred in exchange.

The section's etymology is offered at the same grade as I:4's. "Perhaps our word '*Mensch*' (*manas*) still expresses something of this self-feeling: man designated himself as the being that measures values, that values and measures, as the 'assessing animal as such' (*das abschätzende Thier an sich*)." The "perhaps" is Nietzsche's, and the course's grading from <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the First Treatise's etymological argument</Ref> applies unchanged: the derivation is a sign the argument can do without, and the claim it decorates, that measuring is the oldest thinking, rests on the ethnographic generalization that precedes it.

The rest of the section runs the relation outward and generalizes it into a canon. "Buying and selling, together with their psychological accessories, are older even than the beginnings of any social forms of organization and association: it is rather out of the most rudimentary form of *personal* right that the germinating feeling of exchange, contract, debt, right, obligation, settlement first *transferred itself* to the coarsest and most initial social complexes (in their relation to similar complexes), together with the habit of comparing power with power, of measuring, of calculating." From there "the great generalization" followed: "'every thing has its price; *everything* can be paid off', the oldest and most naive moral canon of *justice*, the beginning of all 'good-naturedness', all 'fairness', all 'good will', all 'objectivity' on earth. Justice at this first stage is the good will among those of roughly equal power to come to terms with one another, to 'reach an understanding' again by a settlement, and, with regard to the less powerful, to *compel* them to a settlement among themselves."

The bearing on the lesson's question is a matter of direction. The relation is private before it is social: the community of II:9 will *inherit* the creditor's standing from the person, and the ancestors of II:19 will inherit it from the community. And the bearing on the previous lesson is an identity. The animal that can promise (II:1) had to be able to reckon and had to be calculable; the animal that measures (II:8) prices its own future when it pledges. The debtor of II:5 is the promiser of II:1 under contract, and the memory the creditor has a right to make in him is the memory of the will, made by the means II:3 catalogued.

II:8 sets the direction the whole chain runs in: the creditor–debtor relation is older than society, so its later bearers, the community, the ancestors, the god, acquire the creditor's standing by transfer from the person and never the reverse. A reading on which guilt is first a social feeling and then privatized runs II:8 backwards.

## II:9–15: the community as creditor, and the negative result that punishment does not produce guilt

II:8's generalization gives the community its role, and II:9–11 run the creditor–debtor relation at that scale; the chain uses two of their results, and then II:14–15 state a result that a reader of ten sections on punishment does not expect, and that decides which route the chain must take.

### II:9–11: the community inherits the creditor's standing, and justice is not born of ressentiment

"Measured always by the standard of prehistory," the community stands to its members "in that important and fundamental relation, of the creditor to his debtors": the member enjoys protection and peace "because he has pledged and bound himself to the community", and the criminal is "a breaker, a breaker of contract and word toward the whole", whom the community's anger "puts back into the wild and outlawed condition from which he was hitherto protected". The relation is II:5's with the creditor substituted, and II:10 runs II:5's scale to its other end. As the community's power grows, "the offences of the individual are no longer taken so seriously", penal law moves toward *compositio* and "the will to treat every offence as in some sense *payable*, and so to isolate the criminal and his deed from one another"; "the 'creditor' has always become more humane to the extent that he has grown richer", until a society conscious of its power "lets its wrongdoers go *unpunished*" and the justice that began with "everything is payable" ends "by *cancelling itself*": mercy (*Gnade*), "the privilege of the most powerful". The chain takes two things from the two sections. The community holds the creditor's standing by transfer from the person, which is the substitution II:19 repeats with the ancestors; and the creditor's pleasure in a settlement paid in suffering falls as his power rises, which is the second half of the curve II:5 began and the reason the course reads that pleasure as a change in the creditor's standing rather than as pleasure at pain.

II:11 closes a route. Eugen Dühring, the Berlin philosopher whose *Der Werth des Lebens* (1865) placed the home of justice "on the ground of reactive feeling", as if justice were "a further development of the feeling of having been injured", would make the chain's creditor a man of *ressentiment*, the affect <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref> defined as a No said to something outside oneself by one denied the deed, and guilt a product of that affect. Nietzsche answers with "a blunt reversal": "the last ground conquered by the spirit of justice is the ground of reactive feeling", since the reactive man must "assess his object falsely and with prejudice" and "the active, aggressive, encroaching man is still a hundred steps closer to justice than the reactive one"; and, historically, law is "the *struggle against* the reactive feelings, the war waged on them by active and aggressive powers", a stronger power ending "the senseless raging of *ressentiment*" by taking its object out of revenge's hands, fixing equivalents for injuries and, above all, instituting law, after which "'right' and 'wrong' exist only from the institution of law onward (and *not*, as Dühring would have it, from the act of injury onward)". What this settles for the chain is that its creditor is an active party and law an active power's instrument: the route from ressentiment through justice to guilt is closed. The section's last sentence, that one can guess "who has the invention of the 'bad conscience' on his conscience: the man of *ressentiment*!", sits oddly beside II:16, where the bad conscience is the caged animal's own work; <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the reconstruction lesson</Ref> records the tension among the remainders.

### II:14–15: what punishment does, and what it does not do

From a treatise that has spent ten sections on punishment, one expects punishment to be the place where guilt was manufactured. II:14 removes exactly that. Having listed in II:13 the meanings punishment has carried (the eleven of <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>), the section subtracts "one supposed utility, which in the popular consciousness counts as its most essential" and which "provides the strongest support for the faith in punishment, which today totters for several reasons. Punishment is supposed to have the value of *awakening the feeling of guilt in the guilty one*; one seeks in it the true *instrumentum* of that reaction of the soul called 'bad conscience', 'pang of conscience'." That, Nietzsche says, "lays violent hands on reality and psychology even for today, and how much more for the longest history of man, his prehistory".

The evidence is of two kinds. First, present observation: "genuine pangs of conscience are something extremely rare precisely among criminals and convicts; prisons and penitentiaries are not the breeding-grounds where this species of gnawing worm prefers to thrive", on which "all conscientious observers agree", often against their own wishes. "On the whole, punishment hardens and cools (*härtet und kältet die Strafe ab*); it concentrates; it sharpens the feeling of alienation; it strengthens the power of resistance." Second, an inference about prehistory: "it is precisely through punishment that the development of the feeling of guilt has been *most* held back, at least with regard to the victims on whom the punishing power vented itself", because the criminal sees "exactly the same kind of actions practised in the service of justice and then called good", so that his deed cannot present itself to him as reprehensible in itself. For the longest time, "the consciousness of the judges, the punishers themselves" contained nothing of having to do with a "guilty one", "but with an instigator of harm, an irresponsible piece of fate", and the punished man "had no other 'inner pain' than at the sudden arrival of something unforeseen, a terrible natural event, a falling, crushing boulder against which there is no fighting".

II:15 gives the state a name from philosophy. Spinoza, who had "relegated good and evil to the human imagination", asked himself what remained for him of the *morsus conscientiae*, the bite of conscience, and answered: "the opposite of *gaudium*, a sadness accompanied by the idea of a past thing that turned out contrary to all expectation" (*Ethics* III, Prop. 18, Schol. 1–2). "Evil-doers overtaken by punishment have for millennia felt *no differently* about their 'transgression' than Spinoza: 'here something has unexpectedly gone wrong', *not* 'I ought not to have done this'." What punishment produces is "a sharpening of prudence, a lengthening of memory, a will to proceed more cautiously, more mistrustfully, more secretly in future"; it "tames man, but does not make him 'better'".

The bearing on the chain is a constraint, and it is the reason the treatise's route looks the way it does. The chain so far runs debtor, creditor, compensation, punishment, and the feeling of guilt is a feeling of *deserving* the suffering; II:14 says that feeling is not produced in the punished, and II:15 says what is produced instead, a Spinozist regret at a miscalculation. So if guilt descends from debt, the descent cannot run through the punished debtor as such. It must run through a party who is not simply punished, or through a debtor who has become, in some way the sections so far have not described, his own creditor. That is the route II:19–22 take.

## II:19–21: the creditor grows until only he can pay

Between II:15 and II:19 stand the three sections of Nietzsche's "own hypothesis" on the origin of bad conscience, the caged animal whose instincts turn inward, which <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs; II:19 opens by calling that bad conscience "an illness, but an illness as pregnancy is an illness", and then goes back "once more to an earlier standpoint", the debt relation, to find "the conditions under which this illness reached its most terrible and most sublime peak". The verb it uses for what happened to the relation is II:12's: "The private-law relation of the debtor to his creditor ... has once more, and in a historically most remarkable and questionable way, been *interpreted into* (*hineininterpretirt*) a relation in which it is perhaps most incomprehensible to us modern men: namely into the relation of *those now living to their ancestors*." A new bearer has taken over the relation and imposed its meaning, which is the rule of <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref> applied to the treatise's own subject.

### II:19: the tribe owes its founders, and the fear grows with the tribe

"Within the original clan community (we are speaking of primeval times) the living generation always acknowledges toward the earlier, and especially toward the earliest, clan-founding one, a *juristic* obligation (and by no means a merely sentimental bond: the latter might even be denied, not without reason, for the longest duration of the human race)." The conviction is "that the clan exists *only* through the sacrifices and achievements of the ancestors, and that one has to *pay them back* with sacrifices and achievements: one thus acknowledges a *debt* (*Schuld*), which grows continually, since these ancestors, in their continued existence as powerful spirits, do not cease to grant the clan new advantages and advances from their power. For nothing, perhaps? But there is no 'for nothing' for those crude and 'poor-souled' ages." The repayments are listed: "sacrifices (at first as nourishment, in the crudest sense), festivals, chapels, marks of honour, above all *obedience*, for all customs are, as works of the ancestors, also their statutes and commands"; this last clause ties the morality of custom of II:2 to the debt, since obeying tradition as such is here repaying the dead. "Does one ever give them enough? This suspicion remains and grows: from time to time it extorts a great wholesale redemption, something monstrous by way of repayment to the 'creditor' (the notorious sacrifice of the first-born, for example; blood, human blood in any case)."

From a debt owed to the dead one would expect the fear of them to shrink as the tribe grows strong enough not to need them. II:19 states the opposite and marks it: "The fear of the ancestor and of his power, the consciousness of debts toward him, increases according to this kind of logic necessarily in exact proportion as the power of the clan itself increases, as the clan itself stands ever more victorious, independent, honoured, feared. *Nicht etwa umgekehrt!* Not the other way round! Every step toward the atrophy of the clan, all miserable accidents, all signs of degeneration, of approaching dissolution diminish rather the fear of the founder's spirit." The reasoning is the "logic" of an unpayable debt: what the tribe has, it owes; the more it has, the more it owes. Carried to its end, "the ancestors of the *most powerful* clans must finally have grown, through the fantasy of growing fear, into the monstrous, and have been pushed back into the darkness of a divine uncanniness: the ancestor is at last necessarily transfigured into a *god*. Perhaps this is even the origin of the gods, an origin thus out of *fear*!" The section adds, and the reconstruction should keep it, that this holds for prehistory and not for "the middle period, in which the noble clans form", which "gave back with interest to their originators, the ancestors (heroes, gods), all the qualities that had meanwhile become manifest in themselves, the noble qualities"; the ennobling of the gods is "by no means their 'sanctification'", and it is set aside.

### II:20: the debt is inherited, and the maximal god brings the maximum of guilt-feeling

"The consciousness of having debts to the deity has, as history teaches, by no means come to an end with the decline of the blood-kinship form of 'community'." Just as humanity inherited "good and bad" from the clan nobility, it inherited, "with the clan and tribal deities, also the pressure of still unpaid debts and the desire to redeem them". The carriers are "those broad slave and serf populations which adapted themselves to the god-cult of their masters, whether by compulsion or by submissiveness and *mimicry*"; from them the inheritance "overflows in all directions". And the debt grows with the god: "the feeling of debt toward the deity has not ceased to grow for several millennia, and always in the same proportion as the concept of god and the feeling for god have grown on earth", empire tracking deity ("the advance to universal empires is always also the advance to universal deities; despotism, with its overpowering of the independent nobility, always also prepares the way for some monotheism"). Then the ceiling: "The rise of the Christian god, as the *maximal god* attained so far, has therefore also brought the maximum of guilt-feeling (*Schuldgefühl*) on earth into appearance." The section ends on the treatise's own atheism as a prediction: with "the irresistible decline of faith in the Christian god" one might infer "a considerable decline of the human consciousness of guilt", and the prospect that "the complete and definitive victory of atheism might release humanity from this whole feeling of having debts toward its beginning, its *causa prima*. Atheism and a kind of second innocence belong together."

### II:21: the direction reverses, and the creditor pays himself

II:21 opens by admitting what II:19–20 left out. "So much, provisionally, in brief and in the rough, on the connection of the concepts '*Schuld*', 'duty' with religious presuppositions: I have so far deliberately left aside the actual *moralization* of these concepts (their being pushed back into conscience, more precisely the entanglement of the *bad* conscience with the concept of god), and at the end of the previous section even spoke as though this moralization did not exist, consequently as though those concepts must now necessarily come to an end once their presupposition has fallen, the faith in our 'creditor', in God. *The state of the facts departs from this in a terrible way.*" With the moralization of guilt and duty, "with their being pushed back into the *bad* conscience, there is quite properly given the attempt to *reverse* the direction of the development just described, at least to bring its movement to a standstill". The prospect of a final redemption is to close pessimistically; "the gaze is to rebound disconsolately from an iron impossibility"; and the concepts "guilt" and "duty" are "to turn *backwards*, against whom? There can be no doubt: first against the 'debtor', in whom the bad conscience now settles, eats in, spreads and grows polyp-like in every breadth and depth, until at last, with the unredeemability of the debt, the unredeemability of the penance, the thought of its unpayability ('eternal punishment') is conceived; finally, however, even against the 'creditor'", whether the *causa prima* of man, "the ancestor, who is now burdened with a curse ('Adam', 'original sin', 'unfreedom of the will')", or nature, "into which the evil principle is now placed ('devilization of nature')", or existence as such, "left over as worthless in itself (the nihilistic turning away from it, the desire for nothingness or for its 'opposite', Buddhism and the like)".

Then the ceiling of the chain, in one sentence. "Until all at once we stand before the paradoxical and horrifying expedient in which a tortured humanity found a temporary relief, that *stroke of genius of Christianity*: God himself sacrificing himself for the debt of man, God himself paying himself back out of himself, God as the only one who can redeem from man what has become unredeemable for man himself, *the creditor sacrificing himself for his debtor*, out of love (is one to believe it?), out of love for his debtor!" The equivalence of II:5 is still the frame: an unpayable debt is settled in suffering, and the suffering is the creditor's own. This is the escalation's last substitution, and it is the point <Ref lesson="antichrist-applied-genealogy">the Antichrist lesson</Ref> takes up when it runs the machinery on Paul.

<Remark title="II:19–21 reconstructed as a chain of substitutions">
**P1.** The private-law relation of debtor and creditor was reinterpreted (*hineininterpretirt*) as the relation of the living to their ancestors, as a juristic and not a sentimental obligation, repaid in sacrifice, festivals and obedience. Anchor: "*eine juristische Verpflichtung*".

**P2.** The debt to the ancestors grows in proportion to the clan's power, because what the clan has it owes; the fear of the founder rises as the clan rises and falls as it decays. Anchor: "*Nicht etwa umgekehrt!*".

**P3.** Carried to its end, the founder of the most powerful clan is "transfigured into a god"; the gods have an origin in fear. Anchor: "*der Ahnherr wird zuletzt nothwendig in einen Gott transfigurirt*".

**P4.** Gods are inherited together with their debts, by conquered and servile populations first, and grow with empires; the Christian god is the maximum attained, and brings the maximum of guilt-feeling. Anchor: "*Maximal-Gottes ... Maximum des Schuldgefühls*".

**C1.** The feeling of *Schuld* toward a god is the debt relation of II:4–8 with the creditor substituted step by step, and its magnitude tracks the creditor's.

**P5 (II:21).** The moralization of the concepts, their being "pushed back into the *bad* conscience", reversed the development's direction: instead of lapsing with faith in the creditor, the debt became unredeemable, turned against the debtor (eternal punishment) and against the creditor (original sin, the devilization of nature, the worthlessness of existence).

**C2.** Christianity's expedient settles the unredeemable debt from the creditor's side, God sacrificing himself for man: the equivalence of II:5 with the creditor as the sufferer.

**The missing premise.** Between C1 and P5 stands the sentence "the state of the facts departs from this in a terrible way", and no argument. II:20 predicted that the debt would lapse with the creditor; II:21 reports that it did not and names what happened instead, *Moralisirung*, but the section says only where the concepts went (back into the bad conscience) and not by what mechanism a lapsed creditor became an eternal one. The premise the reversal needs is an agent with an interest in the debt's *not* lapsing, and II:22 names one: the will to self-torture of the caged animal, which had "seized the religious presupposition" for its own use. That agent is II:16's, and the lesson's last section states what its intervention requires.

**Evidential status.** The ancestor cult and its inflation into a god are conjectural anthropology of the 1880s, and the treatise offers for them the "logic" of an unpayable debt and no document; the course grades them as Nietzsche grades the retributive idea in II:4, as a hypothesis with a location in the record. The two claims that carry the chain's weight are of a different kind: that the concept of god and the feeling of guilt have grown together (a claim about the history of religion with a record) and that the Christian doctrine of atonement has the form of a creditor paying a debtor's debt (a claim about a text, checkable against it).

**The strongest opposite reading.** That the ancestor debt and the God debt are two stories and not one chain, joined by a pun on *Schuld*. The reading is answered by II:19's own verb, *hineininterpretirt*: the section claims a reinterpretation of one relation, which is exactly what II:12's rule says a genealogy consists of, and the chain's links are each a new bearer of the same relation. What the reading rightly presses is that the *first* link, from a private to an ancestral creditor, is the one with no evidence beyond the logic of fear.
</Remark>

## II:21–22: where owing becomes being guilty, and the premise the step borrows

II:21 named the moralization and II:22 describes its result; between them lies the step this lesson exists to locate, from *owing* to *being guilty*, and the two sections give the step a location, an agent and a description, and no argument.

### The moralization named, and the word that fixes the moral sense

The location is II:21's parenthesis: "*die eigentliche Moralisirung dieser Begriffe (die Zurückschiebung derselben in's Gewissen, noch bestimmter, die Verwicklung des schlechten Gewissens mit dem Gottesbegriffe)*", the actual moralization of these concepts, their being pushed back into conscience, more precisely the entanglement of the bad conscience with the concept of god. Christopher Janaway's judgment on the passage is the one the course adopts: Nietzsche's glosses on moralization "are brief and confusing, so that it is much easier to describe the end-result of this process than the process itself", and the one thing the glosses fix is that the maximal Christian god is a presupposition of it. The word *Schuld* does not, by itself, mark where the moral sense begins, since it can mean debt in every sentence of II:19–21, and translators divide on it: Clark and Swensen render *Schuldgefühl* in II:20 as "feelings of guilt", and Mathias Risse has argued for "feelings of indebtedness" on the ground that guilt, for Nietzsche, does not predate the Christian god. What fixes the moral sense is II:22's vocabulary around the word: the man of bad conscience wills "to find himself *guilty* and *reprehensible* (*schuldig und verwerflich*) to the point of unatonability (*Unsühnbarkeit*)". *Verwerflich* is a judgment of desert and not of account, and *Sühne* is atonement, which is what a guilt receives where a debt receives payment. That sentence, and not any earlier occurrence of *Schuld*, is where the word has unambiguously changed sense.

### II:22: the will to self-torture seizes the religious presupposition

II:22 recapitulates the treatise in one sentence and shows the step's agent. "One will already have guessed what actually happened with all this and under all this: that *will to self-torture* (*Wille zur Selbstpeinigung*), that suppressed cruelty of the animal-man made inward, scared back into himself, of the one locked up in the 'state' for the purpose of taming, who invented the bad conscience in order to hurt himself after the more natural outlet of this wish-to-hurt was blocked: this man of the bad conscience has *seized upon the religious presupposition* in order to drive his self-torment to its most gruesome hardness and sharpness. A debt toward God (*eine Schuld gegen Gott*): this thought becomes his instrument of torture." The first half of the sentence is II:16 compressed, and <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs it; what this lesson takes from it is the direction of use. The religious presupposition, the maximal creditor of II:20, is *seized*: the escalation supplies the instrument, and the man of bad conscience supplies the hand.

What follows is the instrument in use. He "grasps in 'God' the ultimate opposites he can find to his actual and irredeemable animal instincts, he reinterprets these animal instincts themselves as *Schuld* toward God (as enmity, rebellion, revolt against the 'Lord', the 'Father', the ancestor and beginning of the world), he stretches himself on the contradiction 'God' and 'Devil', he throws every No he says to himself, to nature, naturalness, the factuality of his being, out of himself as a Yes, as existing, bodily, real, as God, as the holiness of God, as God the judge, as God the executioner, as the beyond, as eternity, as torment without end, as hell, as the immeasurability of punishment and guilt." Nietzsche's name for the state is "*a kind of madness of the will* in psychic cruelty that has absolutely no equal: the will of man to find himself guilty and reprehensible to the point of unatonability, his will to think himself punished without the punishment ever being able to become equivalent to the guilt". The equivalence of II:4–5 is here made to fail *by design*: a debt whose punishment can never balance it is a debt that can be paid forever, which is what an instrument of self-torture requires.

### The missing premise: the debtor as a doer behind the deed

The step from owing to being guilty has a form, and the form shows what it needs. A debtor who owes and cannot pay is, on II:14–15, an unlucky calculator: "something has unexpectedly gone wrong". A debtor who is *guilty* is one who is held to have been able to pay and to have chosen not to, and who holds himself so: the suffering he undergoes is *deserved*, and desert requires a chooser. That is the retributive idea of II:4, "he could have acted otherwise", which II:4 dated as late and placed outside the debt relation; and it is the agent <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref> found I:13 dismantling, the substratum "free to express strength or not", added to the deed by grammar and needed by the weak. *Twilight*'s "Four Great Errors" §7, quoted in that lesson, says in one clause why it was adopted: "men were thought of as 'free' so that they could become guilty (*um schuldig werden zu können*)", the doctrine of will "invented essentially for the purpose of punishment, that is, of *finding guilty*", by "the priests at the head of the ancient communities" who wanted "to create for themselves a right to ordain punishments, or to create for God a right to do so".

The Second Treatise does not argue the premise. II:4 dates it, II:21 names the step that uses it, II:22 describes the state that results, and no section between them says how a debtor came to regard himself as one who could have paid. The result is a missing premise of the clearest kind the course meets: a premise supplied by another treatise, and supplied there as a *falsehood*. I:13 argued that the doer behind the deed is a fiction; the Second Treatise's account of guilt needs the debtor to believe exactly that fiction about himself. Brian Leiter's reconstruction, which <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the reconstruction lesson</Ref> tests section by section, makes this its third stage: guilt proper is bad conscience moralized, and the moralization "requires the free-will presupposition to make the sufferer culpable". The course's verdict here is narrower than Leiter's and is what the reconstruction lesson will have to accommodate: the treatise contains the step's *location* (II:21's parenthesis), its *agent* (II:22's will to self-torture) and its *instrument* (II:20's maximal creditor), and contains no argument for the step, because the premise it needs is one the book has already declared a lie.

### Two origins, two axes: Janaway's grid

The lesson's chain and the next lesson's mechanism are, on the surface, two explanations of one thing, and Janaway states the difficulty exactly. II:8 says the feeling of *Schuld* "had its origin in the relationship between buyer and seller, creditor and debtor"; II:16 says the origin of bad conscience is hostility and cruelty "turning back against the possessors of such instincts". The two are "not only distinct but of different kinds, the one invoking a psychological process supposed to occur in each individual ..., the other a cultural regularization of exchange between individuals", and, as II:14 showed, neither on its own reaches the feeling of guilt: the debt relation "operates independently of any feeling of guilt by those party to it", and internalized cruelty could have taken the form of fear, jealousy or shame rather than guilt.

  caption="Janaway's resolution: the debt chain supplies legitimation and internalization supplies direction; guilt is the cell in which cruelty is both turned inward and read as rightful punishment, and neither axis alone reaches it."
/>

His resolution is to read the two as axes and not as rivals. Internalization changes the *object* of cruelty, from others to the self; the debt relation changes its *status*, from a wish to a right: "through his 'punishment' of the debtor the creditor participates in a right of lords", so that the cruelty is not only enjoyed but *permitted*. Cross the two and there are four cells: simple cruelty to others (the festival), punishment of others (the creditor, the community), simple cruelty to self (the caged animal of II:16), and punishment of self, cruelty turned inward *and* interpreted as rightful, which is guilt. On this reading the chain of II:4–8 contributes exactly one thing to the origin of guilt, the legitimating interpretation, and II:19–21 contribute the creditor before whom the interpretation can become permanent; the direction of the cruelty is the other lesson's business. The grid also answers the question a reader of II:22 has to ask, why a being would punish *itself*: the answer is *Beyond Good and Evil* §78, "whoever despises himself still respects himself as a despiser". Being despised is painful whoever does it; but one who identifies with the despiser, "to some extent split off from oneself as its object", stands in a positive relation to himself. The self-punisher is gratified as the inflicter, not as the sufferer, and the pleasure of II:5, the creditor's and not the debtor's, is the one that survives the turn inward.

<Remark title="the moral step reconstructed, with its missing premise">
**P1 (II:4–8).** A debt is settled, on default, by the creditor's pleasure in inflicting suffering, and the contract makes that pleasure a right.

**P2 (II:19–21).** The creditor has been substituted step by step until he is a god the debt to whom cannot be paid; with the moralization, the debt turned against the debtor as unredeemable and against the creditor as the source of a curse.

**P3 (II:16, deferred to the next lesson).** The debtor's own cruelty has turned inward, and he has an instinct to hurt himself with no outward object.

**P4 (borrowed, unargued).** The debtor takes himself to have been able to pay and to have refused: a doer behind the deed, "free to express strength or not" (I:13), thought free "so that he could become guilty" (*Twilight* VI:7).

**C.** The debtor inflicts suffering on himself as *deserved*, before a creditor who guarantees that it is always deserved: guilt, "the will of man to find himself guilty and reprehensible to the point of unatonability" (II:22).

**Status of P4.** Dated late and placed outside the debt relation by II:4; argued false by I:13 and BGE 21; explained as an invention for punishing by *Twilight* VI:7; named as the third stage by Leiter; nowhere argued in II:4–22. It enters the Second Treatise as what the man of bad conscience *believes*, and the treatise's account of guilt is an account of what that belief does once an instinct for self-cruelty has an interest in holding it.

**What the grading yields.** The chain from II:4 to II:21 is a history of a relation and its bearers, mechanism plus evidence, at the grades assigned above; the step at C is not a link in that history but the arrival, in the debtor's self-interpretation, of a premise from elsewhere. The naturalist reading holds for the chain and has to say, in the reconstruction lesson, whether "the debtor believes P4" is itself a mechanism-plus-evidence claim or a place where the psychology of types is being asked to explain how a falsehood came to be held by everyone.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The missing premise is the same premise twice. Morality's first presupposition (I:13, an agent who could have done otherwise) and the moral step of guilt (II:21, a debtor who could have paid) are one claim, and the Second Treatise's genealogy of guilt therefore depends on the First Treatise's result instead of adding to it: where the First showed the doer to be a fiction, the Second shows what the fiction is for.
</KeyFact>

## ★ II:7: suffering needs a spectator, and free will was invented for the show

II:7 stands between the equivalence and the measuring animal as a parenthesis Nietzsche marks as such, and it contains a second etiology of the free-will idea that the core path does not need and the doer lesson's reader will want. The section refuses "to help our pessimists to new water for their creaking mills of disgust with life": when humanity was not yet ashamed of its cruelty, "life on earth was more cheerful than now", and the pessimist's sickness is a "swamp-flower" of "the diseased tenderness and moralization by which the animal 'man' finally learns to be ashamed of all his instincts". The claim that matters is about what offends: "what actually arouses indignation against suffering is not suffering in itself, but the senselessness of suffering", and neither the Christian, "who interpreted a whole hidden salvation-machinery into suffering", nor the naive ancient, "who knew how to find a meaning in suffering only from the standpoint of the spectator or the inflicter", lacked one. To get "hidden, undiscovered, unwitnessed suffering out of the world", the ancients "were almost compelled to invent gods and intermediate beings", something "that never misses an interesting painful spectacle": "'every evil is justified whose sight edifies a god', so ran the primitive logic of feeling".

The consequence for free will is stated as a question and is an inversion of the retributive account: "Must not that philosophical invention, so daring and so fateful, then quite new to Europe, of 'free will', of man's absolute spontaneity in good and evil, have been made above all to create a right to the idea that the interest of the gods in man, in human virtue, could never be exhausted? On this stage of earth there should never be a lack of really new things, really unheard-of tensions, entanglements, catastrophes: a completely deterministic world would have been foreseeable for the gods and consequently soon tiresome." This is a use-story for free will beside *Twilight*'s: invented for the spectacle, not for the punishment. By II:12's rule both are uses and neither is an origin, and the sentence the reader should keep is the one that makes suffering *meaningful* by giving it a witness: III:28's "man was saved, he had a meaning" will run the same operation with the ascetic priest as the witness and the sufferer himself as the one who is to blame.

## What is established, and what the next lesson takes up

What is established, one result per section. II:4 offers one German word with two senses as evidence for a descent of the moral sense from the material one; the identity is a fact, the descent a conjecture with a record to find, and the section dates the retributive idea, "he could have acted otherwise", as late and outside the debt relation. II:5–6 supply the record's first entries: a pledge on the body, a legal right to cut fixed down to the limb and made indifferent to amount by the Twelve Tables, and an equivalence in which the unpaid debt is balanced not by goods but by the creditor's pleasure in inflicting suffering, scaled to his rank, and once festive; the whole is labelled conjecture by its author, with the changed reading of *Don Quixote* as the datum for the festival. II:8 makes the measuring animal the promising animal under contract, and sets the chain's direction from the private relation outward. II:9–11 give the community the creditor's standing, mercy to the strong, and deny justice a birth in ressentiment. II:14–15 state the negative result: punishment yields prudence and hardness, never the feeling of deserving, so guilt cannot descend through the punished debtor. II:19–21 run the escalation, one variable growing until the creditor is a god to whom the debt is unpayable, and the creditor pays it himself; II:20's prediction that the debt lapses with the creditor is refuted by II:21's "the facts depart from this". II:21–22 locate the moralization, name its agent as the will to self-torture, and describe the state; the step from owing to being guilty needs a debtor who could have paid and refused, which is I:13's doer, and the treatise names the step without arguing the premise. Janaway's grid makes the debt chain one axis, legitimation, and the next lesson's internalization the other; guilt is the cell where both hold.

What is not established is the other axis. II:22's agent, the animal that "invented the bad conscience in order to hurt itself after the more natural outlet was blocked", is described in II:16–18 by a mechanism this lesson has only quoted: instincts that cannot discharge outward turn inward once walls and peace enclose the animal, and the walls were built by conquest. <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs that mechanism and evaluates the state-by-conquest premise it rests on; <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the reconstruction lesson</Ref> then assembles Leiter's three stages from the two lessons, tests whether the twenty-five sections fit them without remainder, and returns to the sovereign individual with the two readings the promising-animal lesson left undecided.

Closed book. Write the escalation of II:4–21 as four lines. On each line name the debtor, the creditor, what is owed, what is paid, and the section that states it; then state in one sentence which of the five items is the only one that changes from line to line.

<Solution>
**Line 1 (II:4–6).** Debtor: a person under contract. Creditor: another person, roughly his equal. Owed: a fixed sum, pledged on body, wife, freedom or life. Paid: the sum, or on default the creditor's pleasure in inflicting suffering, "a warrant for and title to cruelty".

**Line 2 (II:19).** Debtor: the living generation of the clan. Creditor: the ancestors, above all the founder, "continuing as powerful spirits". Owed: the clan's existence, as "a juristic obligation". Paid: sacrifices, festivals, chapels, obedience to custom, and from time to time a "wholesale redemption", the first-born.

**Line 3 (II:19–20).** Debtor: the tribe, victorious and feared, and then its conquered and servile heirs. Creditor: the founder "transfigured into a god", inherited with the debt and grown with empire. Owed: a debt that increases with the tribe's power, "not the other way round". Paid: cult, and never enough; the debt is inherited unsettled.

**Line 4 (II:20–21).** Debtor: humanity, for which the debt has become "unredeemable". Creditor: the Christian God, "the maximal god attained so far", before whom the "maximum of guilt-feeling" appears. Owed: everything. Paid: by the creditor himself, "God sacrificing himself for the debt of man, God paying himself back out of himself".

**The variable.** The creditor. The debtor is always the one who owes and does not pay; what is owed grows only because the creditor does; the currency of payment is II:5's throughout, suffering on some party's side; and the sections change. Only the creditor's identity is substituted at every step, which is why the escalation is a chain of substitutions with one variable and not four stories.

<Exercise>
"Nietzsche shows that guilt is nothing but debt, so guilt is an illusion." Break the claim from II:4's own sentence and from the rule of II:12 (the genealogy lesson): state what the descent claim licenses, what the identity claim would need that II:4 does not give, and why a value's origin in debt could not by itself show the value to be an illusion.

<Solution>
**The descent is not an identity.** II:4 says the moral master-concept *Schuld* "took its descent (*Herkunft*) from the very material concept *Schulden*". A descent is a claim about history, that one sense came out of the other, and it is compatible with the two senses now differing in every respect that matters: a debt is paid, a guilt is atoned; a debt lapses with the creditor, II:21's guilt did not. "Nothing but" asserts an identity, that guilt is (still) debt, and II:4 does not assert it; II:22's *verwerflich* and *Unsühnbarkeit* show a sense the material concept never had. What the descent licenses is a *history*: a reconstruction of the bearers and reinterpretations by which one concept became the other, which is what II:5–22 provide. What the identity would need is an argument that no reinterpretation added anything, and the treatise argues the opposite, that the moralization added the doer and the unpayable creditor.

**Origin does not refute.** II:12's rule, that the origin of a thing and its present use lie *toto caelo* apart, cuts both ways. It forbids inferring the origin from the use, which is the genealogists' error; it equally forbids inferring the present worth from the origin, which is the genetic fallacy the genealogy lesson set aside. A concept that began in the law of obligations and now names a moral relation is, by the rule, a concept whose present meaning was imposed by a later master, and nothing about the first master's meaning settles whether the later one is illusory. If "guilt is an illusion" is to be argued, it needs a premise about what guilt now *presupposes*, and the treatise supplies one: the doer of I:13, held by the debtor about himself. The illusion charge, where Nietzsche makes it, rests on that presupposition being false, not on the concept's descent from debt. The claim as stated collapses a history into a definition, and II:13's principle applies: "definable is only that which has no history".
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Dühring's thesis, which II:11 attacks, is that justice is "a further development of the feeling of having been injured". Using II:11 and the definition of *ressentiment* in <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref> (I:10), state (a) what the thesis would make of the chain's creditor and of the guilt that descends from him; (b) the two claims with which II:11 refutes it, one about the affects and one about the history of law, and the test from I:10 that decides whether II:5's creditor is a man of ressentiment; and (c) whether the section's last sentence, that the man of ressentiment "has the invention of the 'bad conscience' on his conscience", can be held together with the refutation, and on what reading.

<Solution>
**(a) What the thesis would make of the chain.** If justice grows out of the feeling of having been injured, the settlement of II:5 is an act of revenge, and the creditor who is paid in his own *Wohlgefühl* is a man of *ressentiment* in I:10's sense: one to whom "the real reaction, that of the deed, is denied" and who compensates himself "with an imaginary revenge". Guilt, descending from that settlement, would then be the product of the affect the First Treatise made the origin of "evil", and the Second Treatise's origin story would repeat the First's with a new vocabulary: a reactive valuation, a No said first to an outside. The creditor of II:19–21, the god, would be that affect's projection, and the escalation a history of ressentiment rather than of a contract.

**(b) The two claims, and the test.** *On the affects.* I:10 said that the revenge of the impotent falsifies its enemy "in effigy"; II:11 draws the consequence for justice: the reactive man "must assess his object falsely and with prejudice", so "the active, aggressive, encroaching man is still a hundred steps closer to justice than the reactive one", and Dühring's thesis is met with "a blunt reversal", "the last ground conquered by the spirit of justice is the ground of reactive feeling". Being just, the section adds, "is always a positive attitude": justice in the just man is the mastery of the reactive affect, not its development. *On the history of law.* Law is "the struggle against the reactive feelings, the war waged on them by active and aggressive powers": a stronger power takes the object of ressentiment "out of the hands of revenge", sets equivalents for injuries "to which ressentiment is from now on directed once and for all", and institutes law, after which an offence is treated as a "crime against the law, rebellion against the supreme power itself" and no longer as the injured party's injury; "right" and "wrong" therefore exist "only from the institution of law onward" and not, as Dühring holds, "from the act of injury onward". *The test.* I:10 admits nobles to the affect and excludes any subject who can strike back: ressentiment in a noble "consummates and exhausts itself in an immediate reaction, and therefore does not poison", and the affect is ressentiment proper only where the deed is denied and the feeling kept. II:5's creditor, whatever his rank, holds a right to the deed; his anger at the injury is "discharged on the injurer" (II:4), on the body, by the cut. By I:10's own criterion the affect is exhausted in the reaction, and the creditor is not a man of ressentiment. That, and not the creditor's social standing, is what makes the chain's first link an active party; the low-ranked creditor of II:5 is low in rank and high in power over this debtor, which is exactly why the settlement is worth more to him.

**(c) The last sentence.** It can be held together with the refutation on one reading and not on another. II:11 has just said that the aggressive man has always had "the freer eye, the better conscience on his side", and the sentence is the converse: the *worse* conscience belongs to the one who cannot discharge. Read as a claim about the *interpretation* that makes a conscience "bad", the sentence is consistent with the chain: the state, cruelty with no outward object, is II:16's caged animal's, and what the man of ressentiment invents is the reading of that state as a fault, the No that I:10 defines turned, for once, on the self. That turning is what the slave-revolt lesson found the First Treatise unable to explain and handed to III:15's priest, who "changes the direction of ressentiment" from "someone must be to blame" to "you yourself are to blame"; on this reading II:11's sentence is a promissory note the Third Treatise pays. Read as a claim about the *origin of the state*, the sentence contradicts II:16, whose hypothesis makes the bad conscience the work of instincts turned inward by enclosure and says nothing of reactive feeling; and II:11 cannot have it both ways, since a state produced by ressentiment would be produced by the affect the section has just excluded from justice and from law. The lesson's reading keeps the first and records the second as a remainder, which is where <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the reconstruction lesson</Ref> takes it up.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Boundary probe. Stipulate a society with full debt relations, contracts and default, but in which every default is settled by a fine paid in goods and no creditor is ever permitted to hurt a debtor. On II:5–6's account, would the chain reach "guilt" in that society? State what the equivalence premise contributes that a fine cannot, and whether the escalation of II:19–21 could still moralize without it.

<Solution>
**What the fine lacks.** II:5 defines the equivalence by what it is *not*: "in place of a settlement in money, land, possessions of any kind", the creditor is granted a feeling. A fine is precisely a settlement in goods; it makes the creditor whole and leaves him no title to anything on the debtor's person. Three things follow. The debtor's body is never pledged, so II:5's "here promises are made, here a memory is made for the promiser" loses its instrument; the creditor never "participates in a right of masters", so the pleasure that scales with his low rank is never granted; and the knot of II:6, "guilt and suffering", is never tied, because no suffering enters the settlement. The stipulated society has debts and has default and has *Schulden*; it has no economy in which suffering pays.

**Whether the chain reaches guilt.** Not by II:5–6's route. The moral step of II:21–22 requires a debtor who interprets suffering he undergoes as *rightful* payment, and that interpretation is borrowed from the creditor's side of an equivalence in which suffering is what pays. In the fine society there is no such creditor to borrow it from: the only rightful payment is goods, and a debtor who could not pay would be, on II:15's model, an unlucky calculator and nothing more. The internalized cruelty of II:16, if it arose, would have no debt-shaped interpretation available to legitimate it, and Janaway's cell (d) would be empty; the society could produce cell (b), simple self-cruelty, and might produce shame, but not a suffering read as deserved payment.

**Whether II:19–21 could moralize without it.** The escalation substitutes the creditor and inherits the currency; it does not create one. A tribe that owed its ancestors and paid in goods would owe them more as it grew, on II:19's logic, and its founder could still be "transfigured into a god"; but the debt to that god would be a debt in tribute, and the ceiling of II:21, the creditor "paying himself back out of himself" in *suffering*, would have no meaning, since the currency the God-debt is settled in is II:5's and not a fine's. So the escalation could produce a god and a growing tribute, and could not produce a debt that suffering settles, which is the debt guilt is. The equivalence premise is therefore not one link among others; it is the currency the whole chain is denominated in, and without it the chain's last two frames cannot be drawn.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Locate, in II:21–22, the sentence at which *Schuld* unambiguously means guilt rather than debt, naming the neighbouring word that fixes the sense. Then write the premise about the debtor's ability to have paid that the transition requires, and say which treatise supplies it, in which section, and with what verdict.

<Solution>
**The sentence.** Not any occurrence of *Schuld* alone, since in II:19–21 each can be read as "debt" (Risse reads *Schuldgefühl* in II:20 as "feeling of indebtedness" for that reason). The sense is fixed in II:22: "*der Wille des Menschen, sich schuldig und verwerflich zu finden bis zur Unsühnbarkeit*", the will of man to find himself guilty and reprehensible to the point of unatonability. Two neighbouring words do the fixing. *Verwerflich*, reprehensible, is a judgment of desert, which no debt receives; and *Unsühnbarkeit* is built on *Sühne*, atonement, which is what a guilt requires where a debt requires payment. The same sentence continues with "his will to think himself *punished* without the punishment ever being able to become *equivalent* to the guilt": the equivalence of II:4–5 is named and declared unattainable, and a debt that can never be balanced by any payment has ceased to be a debt.

**The premise.** To be guilty rather than merely owing, the debtor must be held, and hold himself, to have been *able* to pay and to have *chosen* not to: the suffering is deserved because the default was his act. In the course's words from I:13, he must be a doer behind the deed, a substratum "free to express strength or not", so that the failure to pay is an exercise of that freedom.

**Where it comes from, and its verdict.** The First Treatise, I:13, where it is stated as morality's first presupposition and rejected: "there is no 'being' behind doing", the doer is "merely added to the deed", and the demand that strength should not express itself as strength is the demand of the weak, who "need the belief in a neutral 'subject' with free choice" for their revaluation. BGE 21 adds that the *causa sui* is "the best self-contradiction devised so far", and *Twilight* VI:7 explains the belief's adoption as an invention for punishing, "men were thought of as 'free' so that they could become guilty". The Second Treatise's own II:4 dates the idea as "extremely late" and places it outside the debt relation. So the premise arrives in II:21–22 as a belief the debtor holds about himself, supplied by the First Treatise with the verdict *false*, and argued nowhere in the Second.
</Solution>

Research-shaped. Read *The Antichrist* §§25–26 (the course's copy is in the research cache) and compare its five-stage account of how Israel's God was "denaturalized" with II:19–21's escalation, as two accounts of the same step, a national god becoming the moral creditor. <Solution>
**Metadata.** GM II:19–21, 1887, a treatise on the origin of bad conscience, addressed to readers of *Beyond Good and Evil*; the passage is explicitly "provisional, in brief and in the rough" and its anthropology is prehistoric. *The Antichrist* §§25–26, written 1888, a polemic against Christianity, and the passage is about a documented people, Israel from the monarchy to the priestly redaction of its scriptures; it announces "five stages" of a process and names the priesthood as its agent.

**Records.** II:19–21 records no document: its evidence is the "logic" of an unpayable debt, the general history of religion (universal empires, universal gods; monotheism after despotism), and the form of the Christian doctrine of atonement, "the creditor sacrificing himself for his debtor". A §§25–26 records a text: the Hebrew Bible is cited as containing "the documentation" of a falsification, and the stages are keyed to it, a natural Yahweh of a nation "in power and with a good conscience about it", the loss of that power ("anarchy within, the Assyrian from without"), the retention of the God at the price of altering him into "a God who demands", the priestly reinterpretation of "all good fortune as reward, all misfortune as punishment for disobedience, for 'sin'", and the rewriting of the national past as "a salvation-mechanism of guilt toward Yahweh and punishment, piety and reward", with the "sacred book" discovered to fix the will of God. A §25's driver is the opposite: the god is altered when the nation *fails*, "the old God could no longer do what he formerly could", and the priest reinterprets misfortune as punishment to keep him. In the *Genealogy* the maximal creditor is the product of victory; in the *Antichrist* the moral creditor is the product of defeat, salvaged by a priestly class with an interest.

**Omits, and disagrees.** II:19–21 omits an agent for the moralization and admits it ("the state of the facts departs from this"); A §§25–26 supplies one, the priest, and gives him a motive, "the conditions for preserving the power of the priest". II:19–21 makes the God-debt grow with power, "not the other way round"; A §25 makes the moral God a response to powerlessness. The two are reconcilable only if the growth of the creditor (II:19–20) and the moralization of the debt (II:21) are two processes with two drivers, which is exactly the seam II:21 marks, and which the *Antichrist* fills with the Third Treatise's priest rather than the Second Treatise's caged animal. The verdict of the pass: the *Antichrist*'s account is the one with a record and an agent, the *Genealogy*'s the one with a logic and a gap, and <Ref lesson="antichrist-applied-genealogy">the Antichrist lesson</Ref> tests whether the record bears the weight the polemic puts on it.

Forward. II:22 calls guilt before God "the will to self-torture". Write what must already exist in the animal for a debt to be turned into an instrument of self-torture rather than into the prudence of II:15, and then find the first sentence of II:16 that states it. State what that sentence claims and what it presupposes about instincts.

<Solution>
**What must already exist.** A debt is turned into self-torture only by a being that *wants* to hurt itself: II:22 says the man of bad conscience "seized upon the religious presupposition in order to drive his self-torment to its most gruesome hardness", so the wish to torment comes first and the debt is its instrument. For that, the animal must possess (i) an instinct for cruelty, the will to make suffer that II:5–6 documented in the creditor and II:6 called "an old, powerful, human-all-too-human main proposition"; (ii) a loss of that instinct's outward object, since an animal that can still vent cruelty on others has, on II:14–15, no occasion to become its own creditor; and (iii) a redirection of the instinct onto itself rather than its extinction. Without (i) there is no inflicter; without (ii) the inflicter has other work; without (iii) the blocked instinct simply fades and the animal becomes II:15's prudent one. Guilt is (i) plus (ii) plus (iii), with II:5's interpretation "this is rightfully mine to inflict" added by the debt relation.

**The sentence.** II:16, in its hypothesis on the origin of bad conscience: "*Alle Instinkte, welche sich nicht nach Aussen entladen, wenden sich nach Innen*", all instincts that do not discharge themselves outwardly turn inward, "this is what I call the *internalization* (*Verinnerlichung*) of man: with it there first grows in man what he later calls his 'soul'". The section frames it as the change the animal underwent "when it found itself finally enclosed within the walls of society and of peace", and names the instincts: "hostility, cruelty, pleasure in persecution, in attack, in change, in destruction: all of that turning against the possessors of such instincts: *that* is the origin of the 'bad conscience'".

**What it claims and presupposes.** The sentence claims a conservation: an instinct denied outward discharge does not lapse but discharges inward, so that the caged animal's cruelty acquires the animal itself as object. It presupposes that instincts are quantities of force that must discharge somewhere (BGE 13: "a living thing wants above all to *discharge* its strength"), that the enclosure was sudden and external ("walls", "peace", a "state" imposed rather than contracted), and that the redirected cruelty is still cruelty, gratifying to the inflicter, which is why BGE 78's despiser can respect himself. Those presuppositions, the conservation of instinctual force and the state as conquest, are what <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs and evaluates: the mechanism this exercise has had to assume is that lesson's subject, and the "will to self-torture" of II:22 is that mechanism's product with II:20's creditor added.
</Solution>

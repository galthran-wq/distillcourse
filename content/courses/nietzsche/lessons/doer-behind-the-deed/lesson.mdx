The lambs in GM I:13 blame the birds of prey for being birds of prey, and Nietzsche's charge is that morality does the same thing at scale: it invents a neutral doer behind every deed so that strength can be blamed for not being weakness. This lesson reconstructs that charge as an argument from four texts written in three consecutive years, GM I:13 (1887), *Beyond Good and Evil* 16–21 (1886) and the "Four Great Errors" chapter of *Twilight of the Idols* (written in the summer of 1888), and states exactly which presupposition of morality the argument removes and which it leaves standing.

The course's argument stands one premise short of the First Treatise's conclusion. <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">The previous lesson</Ref> showed that every row of I:14's workshop, and the charge "evil" itself, runs on one presupposition: that an incapacity is a choice, so that the weak could have taken revenge and the strong could have refrained. Its forward exercise had the reader write that premise out in the lambs' own terms, that a bird of prey could have chosen not to be one. What stands open is whether the premise is true, and I:13 is the one section of the treatise that is not history but argument: it says the premise is false because the chooser it needs is a piece of grammar. This lesson reconstructs that argument, follows it into the *Beyond Good and Evil* sections that run it on the thinker and on the will, reads *Twilight*'s catalogue of the mechanisms that produce the fiction, and draws the boundary of what has been removed. The sovereign individual who "may promise" is <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">the next lesson</Ref>'s test of that boundary; the positive psychology that BGE 19 previews is <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drives lesson</Ref>'s.

The route: I:13 reconstructed from the lightning and its flash; BGE 16–17 as the same argument run on "I think"; BGE 19's anatomy of one act of will, kept as the replacement; BGE 21's verdict that free will and un-free will are one mythology from two sides, with the determinist misreading refuted; the Four Great Errors as a mechanism catalogue, with one twentieth-century test of its direction; and the boundary of what is removed, with the Kantian reader's objection met, before a closing section states what is established.

## I:13: the doer is added to the deed

From an argument against free will one expects a thesis about causes, that every act has a prior cause and so could not have been otherwise. I:13 offers no such thesis. Its claim is about a habit of language, and the example is meteorological. Strength, the section says, is nothing but its expressions: "a quantum of force is just such a quantum of drive, will, effect; more, it is nothing other than this driving, willing, effecting itself", and it "can appear otherwise only under the seduction of language (and the fundamental errors of reason petrified in it), which understands and misunderstands all effecting as conditioned by an effecting something, by a 'subject'". Then the analogy. "Just as the common people separate the lightning from its flash and take the latter as a doing, as the effect of a subject called lightning, so popular morality separates strength from the expressions of strength, as if behind the strong man there were an indifferent substratum that was *free* to express strength or not."

The section then states the denial in the words the course's concept carries. "But there is no such substratum; there is no 'being' behind doing, effecting, becoming; 'the doer' is merely added on to the deed (*hinzugedichtet*): the deed is everything." The German verb matters: *dichten* is to compose, as a poet composes, and *hinzudichten* is to add by invention. And the diagnosis of what grammar does is exact: "the common people basically double the deed when they have the lightning flash: that is a doing-doing (*Thun-Thun*); it posits the same happening once as cause and then once more as its effect." A sentence with a subject and a verb reports one event twice, as the thing that acts and as the act.

### The lambs' judgment and what it needs

The section opened with the lambs, and the analogy is there to show what their judgment presupposes. "That the lambs bear a grudge against the great birds of prey is not strange: only there is no reason to blame the great birds of prey for taking little lambs." The lambs' ideal, that whoever is least like a bird of prey is good, is faultless as an ideal; the birds "will look a little mockingly" and say "we even love them: nothing is tastier than a tender lamb". What is not faultless is the step from ideal to blame. "To demand of strength that it *not* express itself as strength, that it not be a will to overpower, a will to throw down, a will to become master, a thirst for enemies and resistances and triumphs, is just as absurd as to demand of weakness that it express itself as strength." The demand is absurd, on the section's account, for the same reason that demanding of the lightning that it not flash is absurd: there is nothing there to receive the demand except the flashing.

The section's last movement names who needs the substratum and why. The "suppressed, secretly smouldering affects of revenge and hatred exploit this belief for their own ends and in fact maintain no belief more ardently than the belief that *the strong man is free* to be weak and the bird of prey to be a lamb: thereby they gain the right to make the bird of prey *accountable* for being a bird of prey." Read "cold and without prejudice", the whole of slave morality's self-praise "means nothing more than: 'we weak ones are simply weak; it is good if we do nothing for which we are not strong enough'", and the prudence of the weak has "clothed itself in the pomp of a renunciatory, quiet, patient virtue, just as if the weakness of the weak, that is, his essence, his effecting, his whole singular, unavoidable, undetachable reality, were a voluntary achievement, something willed, chosen, a deed, a merit." Then: "this kind of man *needs* the belief in the neutral, freely choosing 'subject' from an instinct of self-preservation, self-affirmation, in which every lie tends to sanctify itself." The subject, "or, to speak more popularly, the soul", "has perhaps been the best article of faith on earth so far because it made possible for the majority of mortals, the weak and oppressed of every kind, that sublime self-deception of interpreting weakness itself as freedom, their being-this-way as merit."

<Remark title="I:13 reconstructed">
**P1.** Language separates every happening into a doer and a doing (the lightning and its flash), and the popular mind takes the separation for a fact about the world. Anchor: "the seduction of language"; "the common people separate the lightning from its flash".

**P2.** The separation is a doubling: there is no lightning apart from the flash, and no strength apart from its expressions; "a quantum of force" is "nothing other than this driving, willing, effecting itself". Anchor: "*Thun-Thun*"; "the deed is everything".

**P3.** The lambs' judgment, "the bird of prey is evil", is a charge and not a description only if a bird of prey could have refrained from preying, that is, only if behind the bird there is a substratum "free to express strength or not". Anchor: "to demand of strength that it not express itself as strength"; "free to be weak".

**P4.** By P2 there is no such substratum: "the doer is merely added on to the deed".

**C.** The lambs' charge rests on a fiction added to the deed; the morality of *ressentiment* holds the fiction because it needs it ("the strong man is free to be weak, therefore the bird of prey is accountable for being a bird of prey").

**The missing premise.** P2 is asserted from the lightning case and generalized to persons in one clause ("so popular morality separates strength from the expressions of strength"). Nothing in I:13 argues that a person is to his deeds as lightning is to its flash; the analogy shows what the substratum picture *is*, not that it is false of humans. The premise that fills the gap is a type-fact in the protocol's sense from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>: a claim about what a human agent is made of, and BGE 12, 17 and 19 are where the course finds it stated.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That I:13 is a rhetorical flourish and Nietzsche elsewhere relies on doers, choosers and the will. The reading has texts on its side, above all GM II:2's sovereign individual, and the lesson's last section states what the argument must not be taken to deny for those texts to stand.
</Remark>

### What the argument proves and what it does not

The section proves less than the sentence "the deed is everything" sounds. It does not say that no one acts; the birds of prey act, and I:13 describes them acting. It does not say that acts have no causes, or that they have. It says that the picture on which an act is issued by a neutral something that could equally have issued a different act is an artefact of the way sentences are built, and that morality's charge of "evil" is a use of that artefact. That is a narrow result, and its narrowness is what makes the rest of the lesson necessary: to move from "grammar supplies a doer" to "there is none" the argument needs an account of what is there instead, and I:13 gives only "a quantum of force". The two sections of *Beyond Good and Evil* that follow give the account for thinking and for willing.

One clause of I:13 fixes the argument's reach. Scientists "do no better when they say 'force moves, force causes'", and "our whole science still stands, despite all its coolness, its freedom from affect, under the seduction of language and has not got rid of the changelings foisted on it, the 'subjects' (the atom, for example, is such a changeling, as is the Kantian 'thing-in-itself')". The argument is not aimed at morality alone; it is aimed at a habit that produces subjects wherever a verb needs one, and the starred section at the end of the lesson reads that clause with BGE 12 and 17.

<KeyFact>
I:13 removes a chooser, not a cause: it says the neutral substratum "free to express strength or not" is composed by grammar, and says nothing about whether acts are determined. A reader who files the section as "Nietzsche denies free will, therefore everything is caused" has imported the second half, and BGE 21 will reject it in the same breath as the first.
</KeyFact>

## BGE 16–17: the same argument run on the thinker

I:13's missing premise is that a person is to his deeds as lightning is to its flash, and *Beyond Good and Evil*, published the year before, had already run the argument on the one case where the doer seemed given from inside: the "I" of "I think". BGE 16 opens on "harmless self-observers who believe in the existence of 'immediate certainties,' such as 'I think,' or the 'I will' that was Schopenhauer's superstition", Schopenhauer being the philosopher whose *World as Will and Representation* (1818) had made the will the one thing known from within rather than from outside. "Immediate certainty", the section says, "like 'absolute knowledge' and the 'thing in itself', contains a *contradictio in adjecto*", a contradiction in the adjective, and "we should free ourselves from the seduction of words", the phrase I:13 will reuse.

Then the dissection. "When I dissect the process expressed in the proposition 'I think,' I get a whole set of bold claims that are difficult, perhaps impossible, to establish, – for instance, that *I* am the one who is thinking, that there must be something that is thinking in the first place, that thinking is an activity and the effect of a being who is considered the cause, that there is an 'I,' and finally, that it has already been determined what is meant by thinking, – that I *know* what thinking is. Because if I had not already made up my mind what thinking is, how could I tell whether what had just happened was not perhaps 'willing' or 'feeling'?" The proposition presupposes "that I compare my present state with other states that I have seen in myself, in order to determine what it is", and a state identified by a retrospective comparison "has absolutely no 'immediate certainty' for me".

BGE 17 supplies the positive fact and the diagnosis. "A thought comes when 'it' wants, and not when 'I' want. It is, therefore, a falsification of the facts to say that the subject 'I' is the condition of the predicate 'think.' *It* thinks: but to say the 'it' is just that famous old 'I' – well that is just an assumption or opinion, to put it mildly, and by no means an 'immediate certainty.' In fact, there is already too much packed into the 'it thinks': even the 'it' contains an interpretation of the process, and does not belong to the process itself. People are following grammatical habits here in drawing conclusions, reasoning that 'thinking is an activity, behind every activity something is active, therefore –.'" The section closes on the same parallel I:13 draws: "the older atomism looked behind every 'force' that produces effects for that little lump of matter in which the force resides", and "perhaps one day even logicians will get used to making do without this little 'it' (into which the honest old I has disappeared)".

<Remark title="BGE 16–17 reconstructed">
**P1.** "I think" is taken as an immediate certainty, a datum with no interpretation in it. Anchor: BGE 16, "harmless self-observers".

**P2.** The proposition contains at least four claims: an I exists and is the one thinking; the I is the cause of the thinking; thinking is one kind of activity, told from willing and feeling; and what thinking is has already been settled. Each is an inference, none a datum. Anchor: BGE 16, "a whole set of bold claims".

**P3.** The observable fact is that "a thought comes when 'it' wants", not when I want. Anchor: BGE 17.

**P4.** The inference from "thinking happens" to "something thinks" follows the grammatical schema "behind every activity something is active", the schema of I:13's lightning. Anchor: BGE 17, "grammatical habits".

**C.** The thinking subject is added to the thinking as the lightning is added to the flash; even "it thinks" says too much.

**The missing premise.** P3 is offered as "a tiny little fact", and it is an introspective report: that thoughts arrive unbidden is true of many thoughts and does not show that none is produced. The argument needs the premise that no case of thinking is observed *as* the effect of an I, and BGE 16's point about retrospective comparison is the nearest the text comes to arguing it.
</Remark>

### Hume as resource, not source

The reader who knows Hume will recognise the report of BGE 17. In the *Treatise* (Book I, Part IV), Hume looks for the self and finds only perceptions: whenever he enters most intimately into what he calls himself, he stumbles on some particular perception and never catches himself without one. BGE 16–17 agrees on the finding, that no I is observed, and differs on two points that carry weight later. Hume keeps the bundle and asks what binds it; Nietzsche says the "it" that would even name the bundle is already an interpretation, so that the question of binding is the grammar asking again. And Hume's argument is epistemic, about what introspection can find, while Nietzsche's is genealogical: the I is not merely unobserved, it is *produced*, by the schema "behind every activity something is active", and a produced belief can be asked what it is for. I:13's answer to that question, that the weak need it, is what the Hume comparison cannot supply.

<Aside>
BGE 18, one sentence long, is the section between the dissection and the will, and it says why the theory of free will survives its refutations: "that a theory is refutable is, frankly, not the least of its charms: this is precisely how it attracts the more refined intellects. The theory of 'free will,' which has been refuted a hundred times, appears to owe its endurance to this charm alone – somebody will always come along and feel strong enough to refute it." The sentence is a joke with a thesis in it: refutation is itself an exercise of the strength the theory flatters, so refuting it re-enacts it. The lesson's refutation of the determinist misreading, below, is written with that sentence in view.
</Aside>

## BGE 19: willing is not simple, and the felt freedom is a fact about command

If the "I" of "I think" is grammar, the "I" of "I will" should go the same way, and BGE 19 sends it there; but it does more, because it is the one section in the group that says what is there instead. "Philosophers tend to talk about the will as if it were the most familiar thing in the world", and Schopenhauer "would have us believe that the will is the only thing that is really familiar". Against this: "willing strikes me as, above all, something *complicated*, something unified only in a word – and this single word contains the popular prejudice". The section then takes the word apart.

  src="./figures/act-of-will.svg"
  alt="A parts list under the heading one act of will, BGE 19: three columns, feelings (the state away from which, the state towards which, the muscles beginning to move), a commanding thought, and the affect of command, quoted as I am free, it must obey. Beneath, an amber box labelled the commander, with the label freedom of the will equals the commander's feeling of superiority, and three arrows down to three blue boxes labelled under-wills, with the French L'effet c'est moi beside the arrows and the gloss the commander takes credit for what the under-wills do."
  caption="BGE 19's anatomy: feelings, a commanding thought, and the affect of command; 'freedom of the will' names the commander's side of a relation."

Three ingredients. First, "a plurality of feelings, namely: the feeling of the state *away from which*, the feeling of the state *towards which*, and the feeling of this 'away from' and 'towards' themselves", accompanied by "a feeling of the muscles that comes into play through a sort of habit as soon as we 'will,' even without our putting 'arms and legs' into motion". Second, "in every act of will there is a commandeering thought, – and we really should not believe this thought can be divorced from the 'willing,' as if some will would then be left over!" Third, and "above all", "the will is fundamentally an *affect*: and specifically the affect of the command. What is called 'freedom of the will' is essentially the affect of superiority with respect to something that must obey: 'I am free, "it" must obey' – this consciousness lies in every will."

### The error attached to willing

Having named the parts, the section names the mistake, and it is not the mistake a reader expects. "We are, under the circumstances, both the one who commands and the one who obeys", and "we are in the habit of ignoring and deceiving ourselves about this duality by means of the synthetic concept of the 'I.'" As a result, "the one who wills believes, in good faith, that *willing suffices for action*. Since it is almost always the case that there is will only where the effect of command, and therefore obedience, and therefore action, may be expected, the appearance translates into the feeling, as if there were a *necessity of effect*." The error is not that willing causes nothing; the sentence takes for granted that the command produces "obedience, and therefore action". The error is sufficiency: the commander credits the outcome to the command alone, "and consequently enjoys an increase in the feeling of power that accompanies all success". Clark and Dudrick make the distinction with the gloss on causation given by J. L. Mackie, the Australian-born philosopher whose 1965 analysis of causes as conditions is the standard one: "A caused P" ordinarily claims A was necessary and sufficient *in the circumstances*: faulty brakes cause the accident without sufficing for it, since the speed and the wet road were needed too, and willing causes action without sufficing for it, since the drives commanded must be disposed to obey. They also fix which acts the phenomenology fits: not every voluntary movement, but the case of overcoming a resistance, the exercise of willpower, which is the case in which a thought does take the form of a command and an affect of command is felt. *Ecce Homo*'s remark that Nietzsche knows "nothing of willing from experience" is intelligible on that reading and on no other.

The section's last image supplies the model of the self that I:13 left blank. The one who wills "takes his feeling of pleasure as the commander, and adds to it the feelings of pleasure from the successful instruments that carry out the task, as well as from the useful 'under-wills' or under-souls – our body is, after all, only a society constructed out of many souls –. *L'effet c'est moi*: what happens here is what happens in every well-constructed and happy community: the ruling class identifies itself with the successes of the community." The French is Louis XIV's *l'état c'est moi* with the state replaced by the effect: the ruling drives take the credit for what the commanded drives do, as a pharaoh says he built the pyramids. "All willing is simply a matter of commanding and obeying, on the groundwork, as I have said, of a society constructed out of many 'souls'."

<KeyFact>
BGE 19 is the replacement, not only the critique: what "I will" reports is a relation of command among drives, and the feeling of freedom is a real feeling, the commander's, misread as a fact about a substratum. The argument of this lesson therefore keeps two things I:13 did not mention, a difference between drives that command and drives that obey, and the feeling of freedom as a datum; <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drives lesson</Ref> asks whether the society has a government or only a physics.
</KeyFact>

## BGE 21: causa sui and un-free will are one mythology from two sides

From a denial of free will one expects the affirmation of its contrary, and BGE 21 is the section that refuses. Its first target is the metaphysical version of freedom. "The *causa sui* is the best self-contradiction that has ever been conceived, a type of logical rape and abomination", and "the longing for 'freedom of the will' in the superlative metaphysical sense (which, unfortunately, still rules in the heads of the half-educated), the longing to bear the entire and ultimate responsibility for your actions yourself and to relieve God, world, ancestors, chance, and society of the burden – all this means nothing less than being that very *causa sui* and, with a courage greater than Münchhausen's, pulling yourself by the hair from the swamp of nothingness up into existence." Baron Münchhausen, in the tall tales printed under his name in the 1780s, pulls himself and his horse out of a bog by his own pigtail; a cause of itself would have to do the same.

The second target arrives in the next sentence. "Suppose someone sees through the boorish naiveté of this famous concept of 'free will' and manages to get it out of his mind; I would then ask him to carry his 'enlightenment' a step further and to rid his mind of the *reversal* of this misconceived concept of 'free will': I mean the 'un-free will,' which is basically an abuse of cause and effect." The abuse is to "objectify 'cause' and 'effect' like the natural scientists do", "in accordance with the dominant mechanistic stupidity which would have the cause push and shove until it 'effects' something"; cause and effect should be used "only as pure *concepts*, which is to say as conventional fictions for the purpose of description and communication, not explanation. In the 'in-itself' there is nothing like 'causal association,' 'necessity,' or 'psychological un-freedom.'" What remains is one sentence: "The 'un-free will' is mythology; in real life it is only a matter of *strong* and *weak* wills."

<Remark title="BGE 21 reconstructed">
**P1.** Freedom of the will "in the superlative metaphysical sense" is the demand to be the whole and ultimate cause of one's actions, owing nothing to world, ancestors, chance or society. Anchor: "to relieve God, world, ancestors, chance, and society of the burden".

**P2.** A whole and ultimate cause of oneself is a *causa sui*, and a *causa sui* is self-contradictory. Anchor: "the best self-contradiction that has ever been conceived".

**P3.** The "un-free will" takes cause and effect to be things in the world that compel, whereas they are concepts we use to describe and communicate, not features of the "in-itself". Anchor: "conventional fictions for the purpose of description".

**P4.** A will that is "un-free" would be a will compelled by such things; by P3 there are none. Anchor: "in the 'in-itself' there is nothing like 'causal association,' 'necessity,' or 'psychological un-freedom.'"

**C.** Neither "free will" nor "un-free will" describes anything; the difference that is real is a difference between strong and weak wills.

**The missing premise.** P3 is a thesis in the philosophy of science stated in a clause, and it is stronger than the argument needs: to reject "un-free will" it suffices that nothing in the description of an act as caused adds a compulsion to it, which is Hume's point about necessity, not a denial that causal descriptions are true. The course carries only the weaker form. What "strong and weak wills" are, and how the difference is measured, is not said here; BGE 19 gave the model (a commonwealth in which command is obeyed, or is not) and the Doctrine of Types gives the metaphysics.
</Remark>

### The refutation: Nietzsche is not a determinist

The claim to be refuted is common enough to be the default reading of any text that denies free will, and it goes: Nietzsche denies free will; whoever denies free will holds that every act is fixed by prior causes; therefore Nietzsche is a determinist, and "no one is responsible" means that everyone is compelled. The claim is false, and BGE 21 is the text that falsifies it, because the section rejects "un-free will" in the sentence after it rejects *causa sui* and calls both "mythology". The reason the claim fails is that it takes the denial of free will to be a claim about causes, when in I:13 and BGE 17 it is a claim about grammar: the chooser is an artefact of the subject-verb sentence, and a compelled chooser is the same artefact with the arrow reversed, "an abuse of cause and effect". The replacement model is the one BGE 19 and 21 give together: there are wills of different strengths, that is, commonwealths of drives in which command is more or less obeyed, and "freedom" is the commander's feeling of a well-obeyed command, which is real as a feeling and states nothing about a substratum. The discriminating example is the sentence of *Twilight* that sounds most deterministic, "no one is accountable for existing at all, or for being constituted as he is", read with its stated ground: not that one's acts are compelled, but that "no one *gives* a human being his qualities", so that there is no giver to hold to account, which is a claim about what is there, not about what pushes it. The second exercise below returns to the claim after the *Twilight* material has been read.

### Kant as the target by name

BGE 21 does not name Kant; *Twilight*'s eighth section does, rejecting the idea that a human being gives himself his qualities and adding that "the nonsensical idea here last rejected was propounded, as 'intelligible freedom', by Kant, and perhaps also by Plato before him". The reader who knows the *Critique of Pure Reason* will recognise what "freedom in the superlative metaphysical sense" describes: Kant's intelligible character, the agent considered as a thing in itself, outside time, who is the ground of his empirical character and so "could have" acted otherwise although every empirical act is determined. That agent bears "the entire and ultimate responsibility" for his actions and owes them to nothing in the world, which is exactly P1. Kant's solution to the free-will problem is, on BGE 21's account, the *causa sui* under another name, and I:13's parenthesis had already placed "the Kantian 'thing-in-itself'" among the changelings grammar leaves behind. The is–ought step Kant's ethics needs is not this lesson's subject; the presupposition it needs is, and the last section states what the Kantian has left to say.

## Twilight's Four Great Errors: a catalogue of the mechanisms

*Twilight of the Idols* was written in August and September 1888 and published in January 1889, a year after the *Genealogy*, and the chapter "The Four Great Errors" is Nietzsche's own compression of the argument the three earlier texts spread across four sections. From a restatement one expects new premises. It adds none. What it adds is a list of the mechanisms by which the fiction is produced, each with a case, and the fourth is the one that connects the fiction to punishment. "There is no more dangerous error than that of mistaking the consequence for the cause: I call it reason's intrinsic form of corruption." The example is a real book. Luigi Cornaro, a Venetian nobleman, published the first of his *Discorsi della vita sobria* in Padua in 1558, in old age, recommending the sparse diet on which he said he had recovered his health, and the book was reprinted in English for three centuries; Nietzsche says "even now many thousands of copies are printed in England every year", and that "hardly any book (the Bible rightly excepted) has done so much harm, has shortened so many lives". The reversal: "the worthy Italian saw in his diet the cause of his long life: while the prerequisite of long life, an extraordinarily slow metabolism, a small consumption, was the cause of his meagre diet. He was not free to eat much or little as he chose, his frugality was not an act of 'free will': he became ill when he ate more." The physiology is Nietzsche's; the structure is the point. Two regularities, a thin diet and a long life, are read as cause and effect when a third fact, the constitution, produces both. Knobe and Leiter name the pattern *Cornarism*: given two correlated effects of one deep cause, one is mistaken for the cause of the other, and the deep cause, the type-fact, is not seen at all.

Section 2 generalises the case to every morality. "The most general formula at the basis of every religion and morality is: 'Do this and this, refrain from this and this – and you will be happy! Otherwise.…'" The reversal: "a well-constituted human being, a 'happy one', *must* perform certain actions and instinctively shrinks from other actions, he transports the order of which he is the physiological representative into his relations with other human beings and with things. In a formula: his virtue is the consequence of his happiness." A morality that prescribes conduct for the sake of a good life is Cornaro prescribing his diet for everyone: it takes for the cause of flourishing what is one of flourishing's expressions, and it does so without regard to the difference between constitutions that made the diet fatal to "a scholar of our day, with his rapid consumption of nervous energy".

### Errors two and three: false causality and imaginary causes

The second error is BGE 19's error restated as a claim about evidence, and the restatement moves the argument from what willing feels like to what willing has ever shown. §3 asks where the belief that we know what a cause is came from, and answers: from the "inner facts", will, spirit and ego, "none of which has up till now been shown to be factual". The will was the one place a cause seemed to be caught in the act, and the model was then projected outward, so that every event became an action and every action the effect of a will, until "an agent ('subject') foisted itself upon every event". The inference runs in two lines. The will, the ego and the motive are believed in as inner causes observed at work; none is observed *as* a cause, since the will "merely accompanies events, it can also be absent", and the motive is a surface phenomenon of consciousness that conceals rather than exposes the *antecedentia* of the act. Therefore "the will did it" reports an accompaniment as a cause, and every agent projected outward on the will's model inherits the report's standing. What this adds to BGE 19 is a verdict on the evidence: the doer-picture is not merely unproven but unprovable from inside, because the only evidence ever offered for it was introspective, and introspection reports what accompanies an act, never what produces it.

The third error supplies the psychology of how a cause gets invented, and its case is a dream. A sleeper hears a distant cannon-shot. The sensation persists "as a kind of resonance" until the cause-creating drive admits it to the foreground with a story attached, "a cause is subsequently foisted", often a whole little novel with the dreamer as its chief character; the story is experienced first, "with a hundred details which pass like lightning", and the shot follows. The order of composition and the order of experience run opposite ways: the sensation comes first and is explained last, and the explanation is lived as if it had come first.

The two-line form: a state is felt and arrives without a cause; the cause-creating drive supplies one, composed after the state and experienced before it. Therefore what is felt as the reason for a state is the state's product and carries no information about the state's actual antecedents. §4 says the waking case is the same: bodily states, restraint, pressure, tension, "explosion in the play and counter-play of our organs", excite the drive, and "we want to have a reason for feeling as we do". §5 says which reasons get chosen, and the selection rule is the argument's sharpest part: the drive is "excited by the feeling of fear", "any explanation is better than none", and so the cause selected is not the true one but the familiar and alleviating one, the banker's business, the Christian's sin, the girl's love. §6 then files morality and religion under the heading. Sin, guilt and punishment are causes foisted on physiological discomfort; grace, good conscience and the Christian virtues are causes foisted on physiological well-being; both are "translations of pleasurable and unpleasurable feelings into a false dialect". Errors two and three are therefore two halves of one result. The doer is not observed (two), and the reason he is said to have acted on is composed after the act (three); a doctrine of free will that survives both cannot be resting on evidence, which is why §7 has to give it a motive instead.

### The direction, tested a century later

The third error makes a prediction about direction: the state comes first and the reason is composed after, so a person's professed reasons should be poor predictors of what they do and good records of what they have done. Nietzsche could not test it. Two results from twentieth-century social psychology did, and Knobe and Leiter read them as the prediction confirmed. Richard LaPiere, a Stanford sociologist, travelled the United States in the early 1930s with a young Chinese couple and recorded their reception at some 250 hotels and restaurants: they were refused service once. He then wrote to every establishment asking whether it would accept Chinese guests, and of those that answered, more than nine in ten said it would not. The professed principle and the act came apart in almost every case, and in the direction the chapter predicts: the attitude was not the cause of the conduct. James Fendrich's 1967 study supplies the retroactive half. Subjects who filled in an attitude questionnaire about Black Americans and were then invited to an NAACP meeting showed no correlation between the answers and attendance; subjects who first decided whether to attend and *then* filled in the questionnaire showed a substantial one. The decision was made, and the attitude composed to fit it: the cannon-shot in a laboratory.

<Aside>
What the two studies establish and what they do not. Both are twentieth-century results from a discipline Nietzsche did not have, and the frame in which Knobe and Leiter use them is the naturalist's: a psychology that predicts that professed reasons follow conduct has a testable consequence, and the consequence held. The studies do not show that reasons never cause conduct; later work found conditions under which attitudes and behaviour do correlate (a subject looking at herself in a mirror is one), and a review of eighty-eight studies put the attitude–behaviour correlation at about fourteen per cent of variance on average. What the studies rule out is the picture on which the professed reason is the transparent cause, which is the picture the third error names and the second presupposition of MPS, transparency, states.
</Aside>

### Error four: free will invented for punishment

The fourth error is the one the lesson exists for, and the chapter states its origin as a motive. "The error of free will. – We no longer have any sympathy today with the concept of 'free will': we know only too well what it is – the most infamous of all the arts of the theologian for making mankind 'accountable' in his sense of the word, that is to say for making mankind dependent on him.… Everywhere accountability is sought, it is usually the instinct for punishing and judging which seeks it." Then the sentence that ties this lesson to the Second Treatise: "the doctrine of will has been invented essentially for the purpose of punishment, that is of finding guilty", and, in the German that carries the purpose clause, *die Menschen wurden "frei" gedacht, um gerichtet, um gestraft werden zu können, um schuldig werden zu können*: men were thought of as "free" so that they could be judged, so that they could be punished, so that they could become guilty. "Consequently, every action had to be thought of as willed, the origin of every action as lying in the consciousness", which the chapter calls "the most fundamental falsification *in psychologicis*" made into "the very principle of psychology". "Christianity is a hangman's metaphysics."

Section 8 draws the consequence, and it is a consequence about what is *not* there rather than about what compels. "That no one gives a human being his qualities: not God, not society, not his parents or ancestors, not he himself. No one is accountable for existing at all, or for being constituted as he is, or for living in the circumstances and surroundings in which he lives. The fatality of his nature cannot be disentangled from the fatality of all that which has been and will be." "One is necessary, one is a piece of fate, one belongs to the whole, one is in the whole – there exists nothing which could judge, measure, compare, condemn our being, for that would be to judge, measure, compare, condemn the whole." "That no one is any longer made accountable", "this alone is the great liberation – thus alone is the innocence of becoming (*die Unschuld des Werdens*) restored."

<Remark title="the four errors as two-line inferences">
**1. Consequence for cause.** A and B are regularly correlated and share a deep cause C; morality reads A as the cause of B. Therefore the precept "do A to obtain B" mistakes an expression of the type for its cause. (Cornaro; "his virtue is the consequence of his happiness".)

**2. False causality.** The will, the ego and the motive are taken as observed inner causes; none is observed as a cause, each accompanies the act. Therefore "the will did it" reports an accompaniment as a cause. (BGE 19's error restated; "the will no longer moves anything".)

**3. Imaginary causes.** A state is felt; the cause-creating drive, driven by fear, supplies a familiar and soothing cause, composed after the state and experienced before it. Therefore the causes morality and religion cite for well-being and discomfort are retrofitted. (The cannon-shot; sin and grace.)

**4. Free will.** The instinct to punish needs the punished to be accountable; accountability needs a doer who could have done otherwise. Therefore the doctrine of free will is invented, "for the purpose of punishment". (§7; the lambs of I:13, who "gain the right" to hold the bird of prey accountable.)

Errors 1–3 explain how a doer gets composed; error 4 says what the composition is used for. Only error 4 states a historical claim, and the sixth exercise asks what evidence the *Genealogy* gives for it.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
"Men were thought of as 'free' so that they could become guilty" is a genealogical claim in the sense of <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>: it assigns the concept an origin in a use, the will to punish. On the course's own rule from GM II:12 that assignment cannot refute the concept; it removes the concept's claim to be a datum of introspection, which BGE 16 and *Twilight* §3 had already denied on other grounds. The Second Treatise's account of guilt in <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the guilt lesson</Ref> is where the origin is argued rather than asserted.
</KeyFact>

## What the argument removes, and what it leaves standing

The four texts now stand as one argument, and its result has a boundary that the texts draw and that a summary would erase. Recall the target from <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>: morality in the pejorative sense has a descriptive component of three presuppositions, that agents have free will, that the self is transparent to introspection, and that persons are essentially similar, and a normative component of altruism, equality and pity. The argument of this lesson is addressed to the first presupposition and removes it. Without a substratum "free to express strength or not", the strong could not have chosen weakness, the weak did not choose their weakness, and the workshop's five renamings of the previous lesson revert to descriptions: "not being able" stays "not being able", and "evil" says "they are strong". That is the whole of the argument's negative result, and it is the result the First Treatise needed to turn *ressentiment*'s judgment from a charge back into a report.

### Three things the argument keeps

The remainder is not nothing, and each item has a text. First, a difference of type: BGE 21's "strong and weak wills" is a real difference among persons, and BGE 19 says what it consists in, a commonwealth of drives in which command is obeyed against a commonwealth in which it is not. That difference is what the noble valuation of <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the master-and-slave lesson</Ref> tracked and what I:13's "quantum of force" measures. Second, the feeling of freedom as a datum: BGE 19 does not say the feeling is illusory, it says what it is a feeling *of*, the commander's superiority over what obeys, and a psychology that explains a feeling has not abolished it. Third, and only announced here, responsibility as a bred capacity: GM II:2 will describe an individual "entitled to make promises", whose "long unbreakable will" gives him "his standard of value", and it calls the long history that produced him "the history of how responsibility originated". A responsibility with a history is not a *causa sui*; whether it is anything more than a type-fact with a proud name is <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">the next lesson</Ref>'s question and the naturalist reading's first strain.

The Doctrine of Types from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>, that each person has a fixed psycho-physical constitution which with circumstances explains his values, is exactly this remainder stated as a hypothesis: type-facts are what "strong and weak wills" name, the tomato seed that no environment makes an apple tree is Cornaro's metabolism generalised, and the environment's share is why the doctrine is not the "un-free will" BGE 21 rejects.

### The Kantian reader's objection, and the answer from the drives

The reader who holds Kant's ethics has a reply that does not contest a word of the reconstruction, and the course states it at strength before answering it. Christine Korsgaard, the Harvard Kantian whose *Sources of Normativity* (1996) is the modern statement, argues that the truth of determinism is irrelevant to the practical standpoint: a subject who knows every move is fixed by "the system" must still decide what to do, and must decide *as if* free, because deciding is what the standpoint of deliberation is. On that view the third-person facts about how a decision is produced, which is what BGE 19 and the Four Great Errors supply, cannot touch the first-person question "what ought I to do?", and Nietzsche's psychology, however sound, is beside the point. The answer the course adopts is Mathias Risse's, a Harvard philosopher whose 2007 essay appears in the same volume as Knobe and Leiter's; the table sets the two positions against each other row by row.

| The Kantian reply (Korsgaard) | The Nietzschean answer (Risse) |
|---|---|
| From the deliberative standpoint I must regard myself as free; the truth of determinism does not change how I deliberate. | Granted for determinism: it leaves the decision process as it is, and my answers to "why did you act?" unchanged. |
| Third-person facts about the mechanism of decision are therefore irrelevant to practical reason. | Not these facts. BGE 19 and GS 333 say the process is a contest of drives in which one prevails, not a standpoint detached from all desire; if true, the agent's account of *why* she acted is systematically false. |
| Introspection confirms the standpoint of "deliberative detachment": I experience myself as standing back from my desires. | Detachment from *some* desires at a time is real; detachment from *all* at once is the "I" BGE 17 removed, and the feeling of it is the commander's affect of BGE 19: real as a feeling, evidence of nothing. |
| So the mechanism can be conceded and the ethics kept. | So the mechanism, if correct, is a fact about the standpoint itself, and an ethics built on the standpoint's self-description is built on the error the fourth chapter names. |

Risse's answer is that Korsgaard's argument works for determinism and not for the drive psychology, because the two bear on different things: determinism leaves the *description* of deciding untouched, and BGE 19 changes it. Whether the answer is decisive depends on the psychology being true, which Risse says has not been shown; what the exchange establishes is that the presupposition this lesson removes cannot be reinstated by the practical standpoint alone, since the standpoint's own report is what the argument has put in question.

<KeyFact>
The condition under which the boundary fails. The removal holds only as long as nothing Nietzsche himself affirms needs the chooser it strikes, and GM II:2 is the text that can break it. If the sovereign individual's "freedom" turns out to require that he could have withheld his promise on the same constitution and in the same circumstances, then he is the substratum "free to express strength or not" under a new name, and this lesson's argument removes him together with the lambs' bird of prey. The boundary survives only if II:2's "free" can be read as the name of a kind of commonwealth of drives, one in which a command given today is still obeyed at the promised date; that reading is what the next lesson has to show the text can bear, or report that it cannot.
</KeyFact>

## ★ The subject and the atom: one changeling in two sciences

I:13's parenthesis, that the atom and the Kantian thing-in-itself are "changelings" of the same kind as the subject, and BGE 17's remark that "the older atomism looked behind every 'force' that produces effects for that little lump of matter in which the force resides", say that the critique of the doer is not aimed at morality but at a habit that morality shares with physics. BGE 12 had said so first. It reports that "materialistic atomism" has been "refuted" and that "the Pole Boscovich", the eighteenth-century Jesuit physicist whose *Theoria* (1758) replaced extended atoms with point centres of force, taught "us to renounce the belief in the last bit of earth that 'stood still'"; and it asks for the same war "on the atomistic need" in its other form, "*soul atomism*", "the belief that the soul is something indestructible, eternal, indivisible, that it is a monad, an *atomon*". The habit, in both sciences, is to demand a thing behind every effecting, and the argument of this lesson is that habit's critique applied to the moral case.

Two consequences follow, and one is a cost. The first is a reading rule Kaufmann states: when Nietzsche speaks of "mistaking effects for causes" he is speaking "with the vulgar", in the language of things and their external relations, since a philosophy that has renounced substrata cannot strictly speak of a cause pushing an effect at all; Kaufmann's proposal is that "aspect" would have served better than "consequence" in *Twilight* §2, and that the polemical antitheses obscure a position that deprecates neither consciousness nor the body. The second is the cost. *Twilight* §3 adds "and even your atom, messieurs mechanists and physicists, how much error, how much rudimentary psychology, still remains in your atom!", and I:13 says "our whole science" has not got rid of the subject. A naturalist reading explains moral phenomena by methods continuous with the sciences; a text that says the sciences' own objects are changelings of grammar puts the method's standing in question, and that question, whether the critique of the subject leaves any science to be continuous with, is the one <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">the fable lesson</Ref> takes up in *Twilight*'s "'Reason' in Philosophy" and <Ref lesson="alleged-biologism">the biologism lesson</Ref> turns against the reading itself.

## What is established, and what the next lesson tests

What is established: the argument of I:13 is not a thesis about causation but about grammar, that a subject-verb sentence reports one event twice and that popular morality takes the doubling for a chooser behind the deed; BGE 16–17 run the argument on "I think", finding at least four unproven claims in two words and a grammatical schema, "behind every activity something is active", as their source; BGE 19 supplies the replacement, one act of will as feelings, a commanding thought and the affect of command, with "freedom of the will" as the commander's real feeling and "willing suffices for action" as the error, so that a difference among drives and the feeling of freedom survive the critique; BGE 21 rejects the metaphysical free will as a *causa sui* and the "un-free will" as the same mythology reversed, leaving "strong and weak wills"; *Twilight*'s Four Great Errors catalogue the mechanisms by which a doer is composed, consequence for cause, false causality, imaginary causes and the invention of freedom for punishment, with one twentieth-century test of the third error's direction; and the boundary is drawn: MPS's first descriptive presupposition, that the strong could have chosen weakness, is removed, transparency is damaged and deferred, and type-difference, the feeling of freedom and a bred responsibility remain, which is the Doctrine of Types.

What is not yet established is whether the remainder is stable. GM II:2 describes a sovereign individual who "may promise", who is "autonomous", who has "the right to say yes to himself", and whose freedom the text calls a "consciousness of power". On this lesson's verdict such an individual is a doer behind a deed unless "freedom" is redescribed as a fact about the kind of commonwealth his drives form. <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs GM II:1–3, states the two readings of that individual, Leiter's deflation and Anderson's autonomy, and asks which one the text of II:2 can bear.

I:13 says the weak believe "the strong man is free to be weak" more ardently than they believe anything. Write the premise about the weak's *interest* that connects this belief to *ressentiment* as <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the previous lesson</Ref> defined it, and locate the premise's statement in the last sentences of I:13 and in the fifth row of I:14's workshop.

<Solution>
**The premise.** *The weak need the strong to be accountable, because only an accountable enemy can be the object of a merited hatred, and only a chosen weakness can be a merit.* Two interests are folded into it. The first is *ressentiment*'s: the previous lesson defined the affect as a No to an outside that is denied the deed and compensates with "an imaginary revenge" taking the form of a judgment; a judgment "you are evil" is a revenge only if the judged could have been otherwise, so the affect requires the belief as the condition of its own discharge. The second is self-affirmation: the weak's "good" is derived by negation from the enemy's "evil", and the derived "good" is a merit only if weakness was chosen.

**Where I:13 states it.** The last sentences: the affects of revenge and hatred "exploit this belief for their own ends", and "thereby they gain the right to make the bird of prey accountable for being a bird of prey"; "this kind of man *needs* the belief in the neutral, freely choosing 'subject' from an instinct of self-preservation, self-affirmation"; and the subject "has perhaps been the best article of faith on earth so far because it made possible for the majority of mortals, the weak and oppressed of every kind, that sublime self-deception of interpreting weakness itself as freedom, their being-this-way as merit". "Needs", "self-preservation" and "self-affirmation" are the interest; "the right to make accountable" is the first fold and "weakness itself as freedom" the second.

**Where I:14 states it.** The fifth row: "not being able to take revenge (*Sich-nicht-rächen-Können*) is called not wanting to take revenge (*Sich-nicht-rächen-Wollen*), perhaps even forgiveness". The verb change from *können* to *wollen* is the premise applied to the weak's own case: it converts an incapacity into a choice and so into a merit, and it is the same conversion, run on the enemy, that makes "they are strong" into "they are evil". The workshop's interlocutor hears it as a lie ("weakness is to be lied into a merit"), and I:13's "every lie tends to sanctify itself" says why the liar does not know it: the belief is produced by the need.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
*Twilight* §7 asserts that "the doctrine of will has been invented essentially for the purpose of punishment". GM II:4 contains the corresponding claim about the idea that "the wrong-doer deserves punishment because he could have acted otherwise". Read the two passages side by side using the sourcing questions of <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>: say which carries a historical argument and which only an assertion, and report what evidence GM II:4 offers.

<Solution>
**Twilight §7.** The passage asserts an origin in a motive: "everywhere accountability is sought, it is usually the instinct for punishing and judging which seeks it"; "the doctrine of will has been invented essentially for the purpose of punishment"; "the psychology of will has as its precondition the desire of its authors, the priests at the head of the ancient communities, to create for themselves a right to ordain punishments". It names an agent (priests), a motive (the right to punish) and a product (the doctrine of free will), and offers no date, no document and no intermediate stage. It is a genealogical hypothesis stated as a result, in a book written to compress; the section itself calls what it gives "only the psychology of making men accountable".

**GM II:4.** The passage makes a different and weaker claim, and argues it. It says that "the wrong-doer deserves punishment because he could have acted otherwise" is "an exceedingly late, and even refined form of human judgment", and that "throughout the longest period of human history punishment was never based on the responsibility of the evil-doer for his action, and was consequently not based on the hypothesis that only the guilty should be punished". Its evidence is comparative and conceptual: that "a high degree of civilisation was always first necessary" before the distinctions "intentional", "negligent", "accidental", "responsible" could be drawn and applied; that early punishment was inflicted "out of anger at an injury", as parents still punish children, an anger "kept in bounds" by the idea that "every injury has somewhere its equivalent"; and, in the sections that follow, the creditor–debtor relation as the older institution in which that equivalence was fixed, with the legal material of II:5–6 as the record.

**The verdict.** GM II:4 carries the historical argument: it establishes, from the lateness of the distinctions punishment now uses and from the survival of an older, non-moral punishment beside them, that the free-will idea is not the origin of punishment but a later interpretation laid over it, which is the rule of II:12 (origin and use lie apart) applied to punishment. It does *not* establish *Twilight* §7's stronger claim, that free will was invented *for* punishment; a late idea may have been adopted by punishers without having been made by them. §7 is an assertion; II:4 is an argument for a weaker thesis that §7 presupposes. What the two together license is exactly what the lesson's KeyFact said: the free-will idea is not a datum of introspection but an interpretation with a history, and the history is the Second Treatise's to give, in <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref> and <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the reconstruction of guilt</Ref>.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
GM II:2 describes "the sovereign individual", "the autonomous, supra-ethical individual", "the man of the personal, long, and independent will, competent to promise", who has "a genuine consciousness of power and freedom" and finds in his "long unbreakable will" his "standard of value". Using I:13's verdict, write the objection that this individual is a doer behind a deed; then read II:2's phrase about "the long history of how responsibility originated" and say what would have to be true of "freedom" for the two passages to be consistent.

<Solution>
**The objection.** I:13 says there is no "being" behind doing and that "the doer is merely added on to the deed"; a subject "free to express strength or not" is a fiction of grammar exploited by the weak. II:2's individual is presented as the owner of a will that he holds as a possession ("the owner of a long unbreakable will"), as free ("this man who has grown to freedom", "this lord of the free will"), as autonomous, and as the bearer of a "consciousness of power and freedom". An owner of a will is a subject behind a willing; a free lord of the will is the substratum I:13 struck; and a "consciousness of freedom" is, by BGE 19, the commander's affect mistaken for a fact. On I:13's verdict, II:2 describes the doer behind the deed in praising terms and calls him the ripest fruit of the tree.

**The phrase that resists the objection.** II:2 says this individual is what "society and its morality of custom finally bring to light", the end of a "colossal process" of breeding in which "man was made genuinely calculable"; his responsibility has a "long history" of "origination". A freedom with a history of production is not a *causa sui*: it owes itself to "ancestors, chance, and society", which is exactly what BGE 21's metaphysical freedom refused to owe. So II:2's "free" cannot mean what BGE 21 rejects without the treatise contradicting itself across two sections.

**What "freedom" would have to be.** For the two passages to be consistent, II:2's "freedom" must name a *type-fact* in the sense of the Doctrine of Types: a bred constitution in which the commonwealth of drives is so ordered that a command given today is still obeyed at the promised date, that is, the "strong will" of BGE 21 and the "well-constructed and happy commonwealth" of BGE 19, called "free" because that is what the feeling of such a constitution is from the commander's side. On that reading "autonomous" means self-governed as a well-ordered commonwealth is, not uncaused; "his standard of value" is the noble self-affirmation of I:2, now grounded in reliability; and "responsibility" is a capacity, to answer for a future, rather than an accountability for being of a kind. The reader is now on <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">the next lesson</Ref>'s doorstep: that reconciliation is Leiter's deflationary reading of the sovereign individual, and the lesson states Anderson's rival reading, on which II:2's "autonomy" is an ideal that a type-fact cannot exhaust, and asks which the text's own sentences can bear.
</Solution>

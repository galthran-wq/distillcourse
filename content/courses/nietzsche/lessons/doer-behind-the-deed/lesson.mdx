A court asks whether a man who broke another man's jaw could have held back, and if he could, says he deserves to suffer; what does that verdict assume stands behind the blow, and is anything there? Two men quarrel in a tavern; one insults the other, and the other strikes him and breaks his jaw. The court does not treat the blow the way it would treat a falling roof tile. A tile that falls on a head is removed, not blamed. The striker is blamed because the court pictures a person standing behind the blow: a self that was there before the anger, watched it rise, and chose to let it through.

The same shape appears in a sentence about the weather. <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">Consciousness and language</Ref> ended on the grammar of the shared language in which consciousness is written: it puts a subject in front of every verb, and the last exercise there found that "the lightning flashes" says more than "there is a flash". What it adds is a thing behind the event, one that could exist without flashing. The court's striker stands to his blow as the lightning stands to its flash, with one addition: he is pictured as able to have not struck.

The two previous lessons have made that self hard to find. <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">The self as a society of drives</Ref> found no ruler above the drives, only drives in an order of rank, with the word "I" as the title of whichever one governs. <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">Consciousness and language</Ref> found that what reaches awareness is a surface, written in the herd's words. The obvious move is to keep blame and relocate it: the court blames the governing coalition of drives, whatever it is, instead of a soul. Whether that works depends on what blame needs. So the questions are two. What exactly does the court's verdict presuppose about the striker? And can a person made of drives supply it?

## What blame presupposes: "he could have acted differently"

The court's verdict seems as old as justice itself: a wrongdoer is punished because he did wrong when he could have done right. The *Genealogy of Morals* (GM, 1887) begins its answer by denying that the idea is old.

<Quote source="GM II 4, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
… punishment, as requital, evolved quite independently of any presupposition concerning freedom or nonfreedom of the will … The idea, now so obvious, apparently so natural, even unavoidable, that had to serve as the explanation of how the sense of justice ever appeared on earth — "the criminal deserves punishment because he could have acted differently" — is in fact an extremely late and subtle form of human judgment and inference … Throughout the greater part of human history punishment was not imposed because one held the wrongdoer responsible for his deed, thus not on the presupposition that only the guilty one should be punished: rather, as parents still punish their children, from anger at some harm or injury, vented on the one who caused it — but this anger is held in check and modified by the idea that every injury has its equivalent and can actually be paid back, even if only through the pain of the culprit.
</Quote>

### Two things a punishment can be

The section separates two practices that the word "punishment" covers. The old one needs only that harm was done and that someone did it: anger is vented on the cause, and the idea that pain can pay for injury sets a measure on it. The striker's jaw-breaking would be answered by a payment in pain whether or not he could have held back, the way a parent smacks a child who broke a vase without asking about the child's inner freedom. Where that idea of payment came from belongs to <Ref lesson="debt-and-punishment">debt and punishment</Ref>.

The newer practice adds a condition, and with the condition a new kind of judgment. It punishes only the one who could have acted otherwise, and it punishes him *because* he could have. Call this *retributive blame*: the judgment that a person deserves to suffer for a deed because the deed was up to him in a way that makes the suffering his due. It is this judgment, not punishment as such, that Nietzsche attacks.

## The lightning and its flash: a doer added to the deed

The first argument starts from the court's picture itself and asks where it comes from. The answer comes in GM I 13, in the middle of the essay's account of how the weak invented "evil". The section opens with a fable about the animals Nietzsche used throughout the essay for two types of valuation: birds of prey, who take what they want, and lambs, who are taken.

<Quote source="GM I 13, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
That lambs dislike great birds of prey does not seem strange: only it gives no ground for reproaching these birds of prey for bearing off little lambs. And if the lambs say among themselves: "these birds of prey are evil; and whoever is least like a bird of prey, but rather its opposite, a lamb — would he not be good?" there is no reason to find fault with this institution of an ideal, except perhaps that the birds of prey might view it a little ironically and say: "we don't dislike them at all, these good little lambs; we even love them: nothing is more tasty than a tender lamb."

To demand of strength that it should not express itself as strength, that it should not be a desire to overcome, a desire to throw down, a desire to become master, a thirst for enemies and resistances and triumphs, is just as absurd as to demand of weakness that it should express itself as strength. A quantum of force is equivalent to a quantum of drive, will, effect — more, it is nothing other than precisely this very driving, willing, effecting, and only owing to the seduction of language (and of the fundamental errors of reason that are petrified in it) which conceives and misconceives all effects as conditioned by something that causes effects, by a "subject," can it appear otherwise. For just as the popular mind separates the lightning from its flash and takes the latter for an action, for the operation of a subject called lightning, so popular morality also separates strength from expressions of strength, as if there were a neutral substratum behind the strong man, which was free to express strength or not to do so. But there is no such substratum; there is no "being" behind doing, effecting, becoming; "the doer" is merely a fiction added to the deed — the deed is everything.
</Quote>

The lambs and birds of prey are not peoples. They stand for two ways of valuing, the one that takes what it wants and the one that suffers it and names the taker evil; how the second came to rule Europe is <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave revolt</Ref>. Here the fable matters for one thing the lambs' judgment needs: that the bird of prey could have refrained.

### What the sentence about lightning adds

The sentences right after the excerpt name what that addition does to the event:

<Quote source="GM I 13, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
The popular mind in fact doubles the deed; when it sees the lightning flash, it is the deed of a deed: it posits the same event first as cause and then a second time as its effect.
</Quote>

One discharge of light is counted twice: once as the lightning, the cause, and once more as the flash, its effect. Nothing new has been observed; one event has been given two names and a relation between them.

The German words make the parallel visible. The word translated "the doer" is *der Thäter*, which is not a term of grammar: it is the ordinary German word, and the legal one, for the perpetrator of a crime, the man in the dock. So the word GM I 13 uses for the thing grammar adds to the deed is already the court's word. The phrase translated "a fiction added to the deed" is *hinzugedichtet*, "made up and added on", as a poet adds lines.

### The argument in five steps

Set out as an argument, the lightning passage runs like this.

1. The language requires a subject for every verb.
2. So it presents every event as a thing doing something: the lightning flashes.
3. Applied to a person, the same form splits him into a doer and his deeds: a self that strikes, and the blow it struck.
4. Nothing but grammar supports the split; the person is the drives in action, as the flash is the lightning.
5. So the free subject behind the deed is a fiction: "the deed is everything".

Steps 1 to 3 describe the language, and nobody disputes them. Step 4 carries the weight, in two ways. It carries the moral conclusion: if the striker *is* his anger in action, there is no neutral self standing behind the anger that "was free to express strength or not", and nobody left over to whom "you could have held back" is addressed. And it is the step that needs evidence, because it claims that grammar drives the belief in a doer, rather than only expressing a belief that has other grounds.

### "It thinks": the same form inside the mind

*Beyond Good and Evil* (BGE, 1886) looks for that evidence in the one place the subject seems certain: the "I think" that René Descartes made the first certainty. Descartes, the seventeenth-century French philosopher, was the voice of trust in looking inward that <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">consciousness and language</Ref> argued against.

<Quote source="BGE 17, trans. H. Zimmern">
<Original lang="de">
… dass ein Gedanke kommt, wenn "er" will, und nicht wenn "ich" will; so dass es eine Fälschung des Thatbestandes ist, zu sagen: das Subjekt "ich" ist die Bedingung des Prädikats "denke". Es denkt: aber dass dies "es" gerade jenes alte berühmte "Ich" sei, ist, milde geredet, nur eine Annahme, eine Behauptung, vor Allem keine "unmittelbare Gewissheit". Zuletzt ist schon mit diesem "es denkt" zu viel gethan: schon dies "es" enthält eine Auslegung des Vorgangs und gehört nicht zum Vorgange selbst. Man schliesst hier nach der grammatischen Gewohnheit "Denken ist eine Thätigkeit, zu jeder Thätigkeit gehört Einer, der thätig ist, folglich –".
</Original>

… a thought comes when "it" wishes, and not when "I" wish; so that it is a perversion of the facts of the case to say that the subject "I" is the condition of the predicate "think." One thinks; but that this "one" is precisely the famous old "ego," is, to put it mildly, only a supposition, an assertion, and assuredly not an "immediate certainty." After all, one has even gone too far with this "one thinks" — even the "one" contains an interpretation of the process, and does not belong to the process itself. One infers here according to the usual grammatical formula — "To think is an activity; every activity requires an agency that is active; consequently" …
</Quote>

The observation is one anyone can check: thoughts arrive. You do not decide to have the idea that solves a problem; it occurs to you, often when you have stopped trying. The inference to an "I" that produced it runs on the grammatical rule, not on anything observed. The translation's "one thinks" renders *es denkt*, "it thinks", with the empty "it" of "it is raining", and BGE 17 says even that much subject is too much. BGE 16 makes the same point against "I will", which Schopenhauer, the philosopher of the <Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-world-from-inside-one-blind-will">one blind will</Ref>, had treated as the one thing known directly.

### Different grammar, different philosophy

BGE 20 turns the point into a claim about whole languages, and the claim is testable in principle:

<Quote source="BGE 20, trans. H. Zimmern">
… where there is affinity of language, owing to the common philosophy of grammar — I mean owing to the unconscious domination and guidance of similar grammatical functions — it cannot but be that everything is prepared at the outset for a similar development and succession of philosophical systems, just as the way seems barred against certain other possibilities of world-interpretation. It is highly probable that philosophers within the domain of the Ural-Altaic languages (where the conception of the subject is least developed) look otherwise "into the world," and will be found on paths of thought different from those of the Indo-Germans and Mussulmans, the spell of certain grammatical functions is ultimately also the spell of physiological valuations and racial conditions.
</Quote>

The "Ural-Altaic languages" were a family, as philologists of the 1880s grouped them, that included Finnish, Hungarian, Turkish and Mongolian, and which Nietzsche took to build sentences around a much weaker notion of the subject than Greek, Latin or German. The prediction is that thinkers there would not have built philosophies around a substance-self. The last clause goes further, and is meant: grammar's spell is itself the expression of "physiological valuations and racial conditions" (*Rasse-Bedingungen*), the inherited constitution of peoples as nineteenth-century writers understood descent. On this view a people's grammar records how its drives value, and the subject in Indo-European grammar is a valuation that hardened into syntax.

The prediction has not held up. Linguists have since dropped "Ural-Altaic" as a family, and Finnish and Turkish sentences have grammatical subjects, with verbs that agree with them, as plainly as German ones do.

<Remark title="The doer–deed fiction">
The *doer–deed fiction* is the belief that behind every action stands an agent distinct from it, which exists before the action, could have done otherwise, and so can be held answerable for what it did. GM I 13 traces it to grammar's subject–predicate form, which splits one event into a thing and its act; "the doer is merely a fiction added to the deed".
</Remark>

### Why the weak need the fiction

GM I 13 does not stop at the error. It asks who profits from it, and its answer is the lambs:

<Quote source="GM I 13, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
… no wonder if the submerged, darkly glowering emotions of vengefulness and hatred exploit this belief for their own ends and in fact maintain no belief more ardently than the belief that the strong man is free to be weak and the bird of prey to be a lamb — for thus they gain the right to make the bird of prey accountable for being a bird of prey. … this, listened to calmly and without previous bias, really amounts to no more than: "we weak ones are, after all, weak; it would be good if we did nothing for which we are not strong enough"; but this dry matter of fact, this prudence of the lowest order which even insects possess (posing as dead, when in great danger, so as not to do "too much"), has, thanks to the counterfeit and self-deception of impotence, clad itself in the ostentatious garb of the virtue of quiet, calm resignation, just as if the weakness of the weak — that is to say, their essence, their effects, their sole ineluctable, irremovable reality — were a voluntary achievement, willed, chosen, a deed, a meritorious act. This type of man needs to believe in a neutral independent "subject," prompted by an instinct for self-preservation and self-affirmation in which every lie is sanctified. The subject (or, to use a more popular expression, the soul) has perhaps been believed in hitherto more firmly than anything else on earth because it makes possible to the majority of mortals, the weak and oppressed of every kind, the sublime self-deception that interprets weakness as freedom, and their being thus-and-thus as a merit.
</Quote>

The fiction does two jobs at once, and both need it. Turned outward, it makes the strong blameable: if a free subject stands behind the bird of prey, the bird *could* have been a lamb, so its predation is guilt. Turned inward, it makes weakness a merit: if the lamb could have struck and chose not to, its not striking is virtue, "a voluntary achievement, willed, chosen". Without the free subject, the lamb's restraint is only what lambs do, and the bird's predation only what birds do. The argument needs this second half to explain why so thin a fiction has been believed "more firmly than anything else on earth": its strength is not its evidence but its use.

<KeyFact>
BGE 17 shows the inference to an "I" running on grammar in one case; BGE 20 only predicts that grammar steers whole philosophies, and the prediction failed. Neither proves step 4, that nothing *but* grammar supports the split between the striker and his blow. If something else did, say, a felt self that observes and chooses, grammar would only be naming it. That rival support is taken up below, with willing and with deliberation.
</KeyFact>

## Free will in the superlative sense: being one's own cause

Suppose grammar were not the problem and there really were a self behind the striker's blow. The court would still need that self to be of a particular kind, and the second argument shows the kind is impossible. It is the opening of BGE 21.

<Quote source="BGE 21, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Die causa sui ist der beste Selbst-Widerspruch, der bisher ausgedacht worden ist, eine Art logischer Nothzucht und Unnatur: aber der ausschweifende Stolz des Menschen hat es dahin gebracht, sich tief und schrecklich gerade mit diesem Unsinn zu verstricken. Das Verlangen nach "Freiheit des Willens", in jenem metaphysischen Superlativ-Verstande, wie er leider noch immer in den Köpfen der Halb-Unterrichteten herrscht, das Verlangen, die ganze und letzte Verantwortlichkeit für seine Handlungen selbst zu tragen und Gott, Welt, Vorfahren, Zufall, Gesellschaft davon zu entlasten, ist nämlich nichts Geringeres, als eben jene causa sui zu sein und, mit einer mehr als Münchhausen'schen Verwegenheit, sich selbst aus dem Sumpf des Nichts an den Haaren in's Dasein zu ziehn.
</Original>

The *causa sui* is the best self-contradiction that has yet been conceived, a kind of logical rape and unnaturalness; but the extravagant pride of man has managed to entangle itself profoundly and frightfully with this very folly. The desire for "freedom of will" in the superlative, metaphysical sense, such as still holds sway, unfortunately, in the minds of the half-educated, the desire to bear the entire and ultimate responsibility for one's actions oneself, and to absolve God, the world, ancestors, chance, and society therefrom, involves nothing less than to be precisely this *causa sui*, and, with more than Münchhausen daring, to pull oneself up into existence by the hair, out of the swamp of nothingness.
</Quote>

*Causa sui* is Latin for "cause of itself", the term philosophers had used for God, the one being that owes its existence to nothing else. The 1907 English translation softened *logische Nothzucht* to "logical violation"; *Nothzucht* is the legal word for rape, and the revision restores it. Baron Münchhausen is the boaster of the eighteenth-century German tall tales who, stuck in a swamp with his horse, pulled both out by his own pigtail.

### Running the regress on the striker

Why *ultimate*? Restraining the striker needs only that the blow came from him; the court says more, that he *deserves* to suffer. Another man, insulted the same way, held back, and the striker's suffering pays for the difference between them. If that difference was dealt to him, by birth, upbringing or luck, he pays for what was not his doing. So desert needs the difference to be his own doing all the way down: the "entire and ultimate" responsibility that absolves "God, the world, ancestors, chance, and society". This is the premise compatibilism, met below, denies.

The court says he could have held back. Why didn't he? Because he is a man in whom insult turns to violence faster than restraint can act: hot-tempered, proud, used to settling things with his fists. The blow came from that character, so he must be responsible not only for the blow but for the character it came from. Is he? He may have shaped it, by habits he chose. But he chose those habits with the character he had then, so the question repeats: was he responsible for *that* character? At each step back, the self that did the shaping was itself already shaped, and the chain ends in what he never chose at all: his parents, his body, his upbringing, the town he grew up in. To be ultimately responsible he would have had to make himself from nothing, pulling himself up by the hair.

In steps:

1. Ultimate responsibility for an act requires responsibility for the character it came from.
2. That requires having made one's character.
3. Making it requires a prior self, which needs a prior character, and so on.
4. The regress ends only in what was not chosen, or in a self that caused itself, and nothing causes itself.
5. So ultimate responsibility is impossible.

<Remark title="Causa sui">
Free will in BGE 21's "superlative, metaphysical sense" requires the agent to be *causa sui* with respect to his character, and BGE 21 calls the concept a self-contradiction: to cause anything, a thing must already exist, so it cannot bring about its own existence.
</Remark>

### The four-stage retreat

Nietzsche had run the same regress, in historical form, eight years earlier. *Human, All Too Human* (HH, 1878) section 39, titled "The Fable of Intelligible Freedom", tells the history of responsibility as a retreat:

<Quote source="HH I 39, trans. H. Zimmern">
… man is made responsible for his operations, then for his actions, then for his motives, and finally for his nature. Eventually it is discovered that even this nature cannot be responsible, inasmuch as it is an absolutely necessary consequence concreted out of the elements and influences of past and present things, — that man, therefore, cannot be made responsible for anything, neither for his nature, nor his motives, nor his actions, nor his effects.
</Quote>

The section's target is Schopenhauer's way of stopping the regress at the last stage. Because we feel guilty for *being* what we are, not only for what we did, he concluded that our character itself must be the free act of a self outside time, an "intelligible freedom": any choice made in time has earlier causes and restarts the regress, so only a timeless act could be its own first cause. HH 39 refuses the inference: the guilt proves only that people feel it, not that it is warranted. "It is only because man believes himself to be free, not because he is free, that he experiences remorse and pricks of conscience." Schopenhauer's timeless self-choosing self is the *causa sui* in its most respectable dress.

<KeyFact>
The causa sui argument removes *ultimate* responsibility, the kind deserved suffering needs. It says nothing against a weaker kind, which asks only that the act came from the agent's own character and not from outside pressure; whether that kind can carry blame is the strongest objection below.
</KeyFact>

## "Unfree will" is the same mistake run backwards

A reader who accepts the regress will draw the obvious conclusion: then everything I do is determined by causes I never chose, and I am a puppet of my drives, my upbringing and the laws of nature. BGE 21's second half is written against exactly this conclusion.

<Quote source="BGE 21, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
If any one should find out in this manner the crass stupidity of the celebrated conception of "free will" and put it out of his head altogether, I beg of him to carry his "enlightenment" a step further, and also put out of his head the contrary of this monstrous conception of "free will": I mean "unfree will," which is tantamount to a misuse of cause and effect. One should not wrongly reify "cause" and "effect," as the natural philosophers do (and whoever like them naturalize in thinking at present), according to the prevailing mechanical doltishness which makes the cause press and push until it "effects" its end; one should use "cause" and "effect" only as pure concepts, that is to say, as conventional fictions for the purpose of designation and mutual understanding — not for explanation. … It is we alone who have devised cause, sequence, reciprocity, relativity, constraint, number, law, freedom, motive, and purpose; and when we interpret and intermix this symbol-world, as "being-in-itself," with things, we act once more as we have always acted — mythologically. The "unfree will" is mythology; in real life it is only a question of strong and weak wills.
</Quote>

### The puppet picture is the free-will picture with the strings reversed

The popular image of Nietzsche is a hard determinist, someone who holds that every human act is fixed by prior causes as a billiard ball's path is fixed by the cue. There is evidence for the image. In July 1881 he wrote to his friend Franz Overbeck, a theologian in Basel, that he had found in the seventeenth-century Dutch philosopher Spinoza a "precursor", and that Spinoza "denies the freedom of the will" among five doctrines they shared. *Daybreak* (D, 1881) section 130 ends by imagining "those iron hands of necessity which shake the dice-box of chance", and our purposes as throws of those dice. But D 130 says "perhaps" four times, and BGE 21, five years later, rejects the picture outright.

The reason is that the puppet picture keeps the very structure that made free will a fiction. "Unfree will" pictures a self that wants to act one way and is pushed by causes outside it to act another: a doer behind the deed, now a prisoner instead of a sovereign. The cause that "press[es] and push[es]" is the lightning behind the flash again, a thing separate from its effect and acting on it.

This does not undo the regress, which needs much less than a shoving cause: only that the character had antecedents, parents, body, upbringing, that the striker did not make. That survives the rejection.

Remove the doer and there is no one left to be pushed. The striker is not a self that his anger compelled; he is, at that moment, the anger striking.

### Two ways to want the puzzle

BGE 21 ends with a diagnosis. Who cares so much about the question of free will, and why?

<Quote source="BGE 21, trans. H. Zimmern">
… in general, if I have observed correctly, the "non-freedom of the will" is regarded as a problem from two entirely opposite standpoints, but always in a profoundly personal manner: some will not give up their "responsibility," their belief in themselves, the personal right to their merits, at any price (the vain races belong to this class); others on the contrary, do not wish to be answerable for anything, or blamed for anything, and owing to an inward self-contempt, seek to get out of the business, no matter how. The latter, when they write books, are in the habit at present of taking the side of criminals; a sort of socialistic sympathy is their favourite disguise. And as a matter of fact, the fatalism of the weak-willed embellishes itself surprisingly when it can pose as "la religion de la souffrance humaine"; that is its "good taste."
</Quote>

The diagnosis applies the method of the death of God's <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="discrediting-how-a-belief-dies-without-being-refuted">discrediting</Ref>, an explanation of why a belief is held that makes no use of its truth, to both sides of the debate. The defender of free will wants his merits to be his own; the "vain races" (*die eitlen Rassen*) are, in the period's sense of "race" already met in BGE 20, peoples marked by an inherited trait, here vanity, the need to be credited, and the section names none. The defender of determinism wants his failures not to be. "*La religion de la souffrance humaine*", French for "the religion of human suffering", was a phrase of the day for compassion made into a creed, in which the criminal is first of all a sufferer. Nietzsche's contempt for it is meant: the determinist who pleads for the criminal is, on this diagnosis, pleading for himself.

## What willing is: a command, an obedience, and the pleasure of the commander

BGE 21 drops free and unfree will and keeps "strong and weak wills". What can make a will strong, if not its freedom? The answer starts from what an act of will is. The court imagines the striker's will as one simple thing, a decision that either holds the arm back or does not. BGE 19 takes that simple thing apart, and finds the feeling of freedom inside it. Part of BGE 19 is already familiar from <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="who-says-i-the-commonwealth-and-its-governing-class">the commonwealth of drives</Ref>: in every act of will one part commands and others obey, and the commanding part says "I" for the whole and takes the credit, *L'effet c'est moi*. The section's first half, which that lesson left aside, lists what an act of will contains:

<Quote source="BGE 19, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Wollen scheint mir vor Allem etwas Complicirtes, Etwas, das nur als Wort eine Einheit ist, – und eben im Einen Worte steckt das Volks-Vorurtheil … in jedem Wollen ist erstens eine Mehrheit von Gefühlen, nämlich das Gefühl des Zustandes, von dem weg, das Gefühl des Zustandes, zu dem hin, das Gefühl von diesem "weg" und "hin" selbst, dann noch ein begleitendes Muskelgefühl … so zweitens auch noch Denken: in jedem Willensakte giebt es einen commandirenden Gedanken … Drittens ist der Wille nicht nur ein Complex von Fühlen und Denken, sondern vor Allem noch ein Affekt: und zwar jener Affekt des Commando's. Das, was "Freiheit des Willens" genannt wird, ist wesentlich der Überlegenheits-Affekt in Hinsicht auf Den, der gehorchen muss: "ich bin frei, ›er‹ muss gehorchen" …
</Original>

Willing seems to me to be above all something complicated, something that is a unity only as a word — and it is precisely in the one word that popular prejudice lurks … in all willing there is firstly a plurality of sensations, namely, the sensation of the condition "away from which we go," the sensation of the condition "towards which we go," the sensation of this "from" and "towards" itself, and then besides, an accompanying muscular sensation … in the second place, thinking is also to be recognized; in every act of the will there is a ruling thought; — and let us not imagine it possible to sever this thought from the "willing," as if the will would then remain over! In the third place, the will is not only a complex of sensation and thinking, but it is above all an affect, and in fact the affect of the command. That which is termed "freedom of the will" is essentially the affect of superiority in respect to him who must obey: "I am free, 'he' must obey" — this consciousness is inherent in every will …
</Quote>

### Three ingredients and one mistake

The list has three items. *Sensations*: a felt state one is moving away from, a felt state one is moving towards, the feeling of the movement itself, and a muscular readiness that starts "by force of habit" even before the limbs move. A *commanding thought*: "this and nothing else is necessary now", which cannot be subtracted to leave a pure will behind. And an *affect*, a felt surge, which is the heart of it: the feeling of being the one who commands. The 1907 translation's "emotion" for *Affekt* is revised to "affect": the felt surge of a drive on an occasion, as <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="four-marks-of-a-drive">the society of drives</Ref> defined it.

What people call "freedom of the will" is the third item: the commander's feeling of superiority over what must obey. Then comes the mistake. Because one usually wills only where obedience can be expected, the willer comes to believe "that willing *suffices* for action", and credits the success to the will itself: the commanding part feels the triumph over resistance as its own doing, though the obeying parts did the work. The feeling of freedom is real; what it is a feeling *of* is command, not self-causation.

This is half of what step 4 of the grammar argument needed. The strongest rival support for a doer was a felt self that chooses, and BGE 19 explains that feeling without one: it is what any drive feels when it commands and expects to be obeyed.

### A case worked: putting down the fork

<Example title="Willing against resistance, taken apart">
**The situation.** You have eaten one slice of chocolate cake and are reaching for a second. You tell yourself: put down the fork and step away from the cake. You do.

**The sensations.** A state you are moving away from (the cloying fullness, the picture of yourself after the third slice), a state you are moving towards (the lightness of having stopped, the look of yourself in the mirror next week), the tug of the movement between them, and the hand already beginning to set the fork down before you have finished the sentence.

**The commanding thought.** "Not this one. Now." It singles out one course and excludes the rest; take it away and nothing like a will is left over.

**The affect of command.** The feeling of being in charge: "I am free; the craving must obey." This is what the experience of freedom consists in.

**What did the work.** Something obeyed. On the society-of-drives picture it was another drive with its own interest: vanity about the mirror, fear of a doctor's verdict, the pleasure of keeping a resolution. The craving did not yield to a self with no wants; it lost to a rival that was stronger tonight. You know this from the other evenings, when the same sentence is said, the same affect of command is felt, and the fork goes back to the plate for a second slice. The command was given both times; obedience came once.

**Where the credit goes.** On the good evening you say "I resisted", and enjoy the triumph over resistance as your own doing. On the bad evening you say "my will was weak". In both, the one word "will" covers a contest among drives whose outcome the commander announced but did not produce.
</Example>

### Strong and weak will without a free will

The worked case shows what BGE 21's "strong and weak wills" can mean once the doer is gone. A strong will is not a will freer than others. It is a person in whom the commanding drive is regularly obeyed: the drives are ordered under one governing coalition, so the command and the result coincide. A weak will is a person in whom they come apart, because the drives are not so ordered. A notebook of spring 1888, unpublished, says this outright, and goes further than BGE 21:

<Quote source="Notebook 14, spring 1888, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge, p. 265">
Weakness of the will: this is a metaphor which can be misleading. For there is no will, and hence neither a strong will nor a weak one. Multiplicity and disaggregation of the impulses, lack of system among them, results as 'weak will'; their coordination under the dominance of a single one results as 'strong will' — in the first case it is oscillation and the lack of a centre of gravity; in the second precision and clarity of direction.
</Quote>

The note is a draft, and its first sentence contradicts the book's way of speaking: BGE 21 keeps "strong and weak wills" as real. What it adds is the reading of the book's phrase. Strength of will is a fact about the order of the drives, the same order that <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="unity-is-an-achievement-heap-tyranny-commonwealth">the commonwealth</Ref> made a person's unity. And the note's "disaggregation of the impulses" makes weak will a form of decadence: "disaggregation of the will" is how <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner" id="sections-67-the-style-of-decadence">the style of decadence</Ref> described a whole whose parts no longer pull in one direction.

## The strongest objection: my reasons are my own

A serious critic now replies that the arguments so far, even if all sound, attack a target nobody needs. Responsibility does not require a self-made self. It requires only that the act came from the agent's own character and reasons, and not from a gun at his head or a seizure. The striker was not forced; he struck because he was angry and judged the insult worth a blow. And deliberation is real: people weigh reasons, and the weighing changes what they do. That is enough to hold him answerable, and the doer can stay a figure of speech for "the person acting from his own reasons". The position has a name, *compatibilism*: responsibility is compatible with every act having causes, so long as the causes run through the agent's own character and reasons.

The reply from Nietzsche's side goes after the weighing, because the objection rests on it.

### What deliberation observes

D 129, titled "Alleged conflict of motives", describes what happens when we deliberate, and finds that what we observe is not the contest that decides:

<Quote source="D 129, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
… before an act there step into our reflective consciousness one after another the consequences of various acts all of which we believe we can perform, and we compare these consequences. We believe we have resolved upon an act when we have decided that its consequences will be more favourable than those of any other … But at the moment when we finally do act, our action is often enough determined by a different species of motives than the species here under discussion, those involved in our 'picture of the consequences'. What here comes into play is the way we habitually expend our energy; or some slight instigation from a person whom we fear or honour or love; or our indolence, which prefers to do what lies closest to hand; or an excitation of our imagination brought about at the decisive moment by some immediate, very trivial event; quite incalculable physical influences come into play; caprice and waywardness come into play; some emotion or other happens quite by chance to leap forth: in short, there come into play motives in part unknown to us, in part known very ill, which we can never take account of beforehand. Probably a struggle takes place between these as well, a battling to and fro, a rising and falling of the scales — and this would be the actual 'conflict of motives': — something quite invisible to us of which we would be quite unconscious. I have calculated the consequences and the outcomes and in doing so have set one very essential motive in the battle-line — but I have not set up this battle-line itself, nor can I even see it: the struggle itself is hidden from me, and likewise the victory as victory; for, though I certainly learn what I finally do, I do not learn which motive has therewith actually proved victorious.
</Quote>

The section grants the critic his weighing. Deliberation happens, and its result, "a picture of the consequences", becomes one motive in the fight. What it denies is that the weighing *is* the fight. The decisive contest runs among habits, fears, loves, fatigue and accidents of the moment, and it is "quite invisible". The deliberator learns what he did, not which motive won. This is <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">the conscious surface</Ref> applied to choice: what reaches awareness is the comparison of consequences, in words, and the comparison is taken for the cause.

### A match versus a ton of powder

*The Gay Science* (GS) section 360, from the fifth book of 1887, draws the distinction the critic's "my own reasons" runs together:

<Quote source="GS 360, trans. W. Kaufmann">
I have learned to distinguish the cause of acting from the cause of acting in a particular way, in a particular direction, with a particular goal. The first kind of cause is a quantum of dammed-up energy that is waiting to be used up somehow, for something, while the second kind is, compared to this energy, something quite insignificant, for the most part a little accident in accordance with which this quantum "discharges" itself in one particular way — a match versus a ton of powder. Among these little accidents and "matches" I include so-called "purposes" as well as the even much more so-called "vocations" … The usual view is different: People are accustomed to consider the goal (purposes, vocations, etc.) as the driving force, in keeping with a very ancient error; but it is merely the directing force — one has mistaken the helmsman for the steam.
</Quote>

Apply it to the tavern. The striker's reason, "he insulted me, and an insult must be answered", is real, and it did something: it pointed the anger at this man, now, with a fist. It is the helmsman. What made the blow happen at all was the anger, built up over the evening and over a temperament, waiting for an outlet. That is the steam. The critic's "he acted from his own reasons" credits the helmsman with the voyage.

### Intention as a symptom

BGE 32 puts the consequence for moral judgment in one sentence. For most of history, it says, an act was judged by its consequences; then, in what it calls the moral period, by its intention; and now "the suspicion arises that the decisive value of an action lies precisely in that which is *not intentional*". The intention, BGE 32 concludes, "is only a sign or symptom, which first requires an explanation". Retributive blame judges the intention as the act's origin. On D 129 and GS 360, the intention is one visible part of a process whose decisive parts are not visible, and blaming it is reading the symptom as the disease.

### The pale criminal

*Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (Z, 1883–85), the parable-book in which Nietzsche's prophet preaches, gives the critique a human face in the chapter "On the Pale Criminal" (Z I 6). Zarathustra addresses the judges of a murderer:

<Quote source="Z I 6, 'On the Pale Criminal', trans. W. Kaufmann">
But thought is one thing, the deed is another, and the image of the deed still another: the wheel of causality does not roll between them.

An image made this pale man pale. He was equal to his deed when he did it; but he could not bear its image after it was done. Now he always saw himself as the doer of one deed. Madness I call this: the exception now became the essence for him. A chalk streak stops a hen; the stroke that he himself struck stopped his poor reason: madness after the deed I call this. … Thus speaks the red judge, "Why did this criminal murder? He wanted to rob." But I say unto you: his soul wanted blood, not robbery; he thirsted after the bliss of the knife. His poor reason, however, did not comprehend this madness and persuaded him: "What matters blood?" it asked; "don't you want at least to commit a robbery with it?"
</Quote>

The red judge, the judge in his red robes, and the criminal each make one of the errors traced above. The judge reads the criminal's motive off the reason the criminal gave, robbery, as the critic reads the striker's off "an insult must be answered"; Zarathustra says the reason was the helmsman, steering a thirst for blood toward something reason could accept. And the criminal himself, after the deed, makes himself "the doer of one deed": he takes the image of the act for his essence, the doer–deed fiction turned on himself until it stops his reason "as a chalk streak stops a hen", like the old trick of freezing a hen by holding its beak to a chalk line. The image is the conviction that he *is* a murderer, a self that did this and could have not, and it is the image, not the deed, that makes him pale.

### The gap

The reply wins against one form of the objection and leaves another standing. It shows that the critic's "own reasons" do less than he thinks: they direct the act without causing it, and they are not where the decision is made. So the reasons cannot carry retributive blame, which needs the act to have been up to a deliberating self. But Nietzsche argues against the moralist's "could have done otherwise" and retributive blame, never against a responsibility that asks only that the act came from the agent's own character, whoever and whatever that character is. BGE 21's "strong will" is such a notion, and he leaves it undeveloped. What he holds against it is the regress's premise: it can ground restraint, not deserved suffering, because the character it points to was dealt.

D 129 also finishes what BGE 19 began for step 4. BGE 19 explained the felt self that commands; D 129 shows that the felt self that weighs and chooses does not see the decision it seems to make. Together they leave the belief in a doer with no evidence that needs a doer to explain it: everything the self feels, it would feel as a society of drives. That is a strong case for step 4, not a proof. It shows the doer is not needed, not that there is none.

## Does the conscious will cause anything?

The reply leaves a question it does not settle, and the texts divide over it. If the conscious purpose is the helmsman, does it at least steer? <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language" id="does-consciousness-do-anything">Consciousness and language</Ref> met the same fork for consciousness as a whole; here it arrives for the will.

On one reading, conscious willing is an accompaniment: the sense of deciding is a story told alongside a process that would run the same without it. GS 360's last sentences lean that way ("And not even always the helmsman … a direction, to be sure, but — no helmsman at all?"), the spring 1888 notebook says "there is no will", and *Twilight of the Idols* (TI, 1888) will say that the will "no longer moves anything … it merely accompanies events" (<Ref lesson="four-great-errors">the four great errors</Ref>). On the other reading, the conscious will is real and effective, as the order of a commanding drive rather than the choice of a free subject: BGE 19's command is a command, and obedience follows it often enough to be expected; GS 360's helmsman, on the ordinary day, does direct the ship; and BGE 21 keeps the strong will.

The critique of blame does not wait for the fork to be settled. On the first branch, nobody deliberates their way into a deed at all. On the second, the deliberating is a drive commanding, and the drive that commanded was itself made by what the striker never chose. Either way the court's "could have held back" names a self that is not there. *Beyond Good and Evil* sits on the second branch; the late *Twilight* moves toward the first.

## What falls and what stands

The tavern verdict bundles a punishment, a claim of desert, the guilt the striker is expected to feel, the excuse he might offer, and a judgment of his character. Which of them needed the self behind the blow?

### What falls

On the regress's premise, that deserved suffering needs a difference the sufferer made himself, retributive blame falls with the self behind the blow. Guilt, as the feeling that one *deserves* to suffer for being what one is, loses the same premise. TI's maxims draw the rule of conduct: "Not to perpetrate cowardice against one's own acts! Not to leave them in the lurch afterward! The bite of conscience is indecent" (TI, "Maxims and Arrows" 10). The excuse falls with the accusation: the striker cannot plead that his anger compelled him, because there was no him for it to compel.

*Daybreak* drew the practical conclusion for criminals early, in the free-spirit years:

<Quote source="D 13, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
Towards the re-education of the human race. — Men of application and goodwill assist in this one work: to take the concept of punishment which has overrun the whole world and root it out! There exists no more noxious weed! … Indeed, they have gone so far in their madness as to demand that we feel our very existence to be a punishment — it is as though the education of the human race had hitherto been directed by the fantasies of jailers and hangmen!
</Quote>

D 202 proposes to treat the criminal "as a mental patient", with "the prudence and goodwill of a physician", to "wipe pangs of conscience from his soul as a matter of cleanliness", and to say "every 'guilty person' is a sick person". Then it admits: "No, the hour for that has not yet arrived."

That programme sits uneasily with BGE 21's contempt, five years later, for writers who take "the side of criminals" out of "socialistic sympathy". BGE 21 does not withdraw the denial of responsibility. It attaches a suspicion to it: the denial can be the self-contempt of someone who wants to answer for nothing, dressed as compassion.

<Aside title="Freedom kept as a word">
Nietzsche keeps the word "freedom" in a sense that has nothing to do with being one's own cause. GS 347 (1887) imagines "such a pleasure and power of self-determination, such a freedom of the will that the spirit would take leave of all faith and every wish for certainty, being practiced in maintaining himself on insubstantial ropes and possibilities and dancing even near abysses". The *Genealogy*'s "sovereign individual", "master of a free will", is a being with a "protracted and unbreakable will" who can promise because he knows he is strong enough to keep his word (GM II 2); what that figure is worth to Nietzsche is a question for <Ref lesson="debt-and-punishment">debt and punishment</Ref>. In both, "free" names strength and coordination of will. Reading it as a self that chooses its character and earns praise for it puts back what BGE 21 took away.
</Aside>

### What stands

Strong and weak will stand, as degrees of coordination among drives. Character stands: that the striker is a proud man in whom insult turns to violence is a fact about the order of his drives, which others can know, predict, fear or admire. Rank stands: persons differ in strength, and saying so describes rather than reproaches. And a community can still restrain a man who breaks jaws, as it restrains a flood, without claiming that he deserves to suffer.

Praise is the harder case, and the regress applies to it exactly as to blame. HH 107 drew the conclusion: whoever understands this "may no longer either praise or blame", and "can admire strength, beauty, abundance, in themselves; but must find no merit therein". Where that leaves admiration, and what Nietzsche later called the "innocence of becoming", becoming freed from the categories of guilt and purpose, is the subject of <Ref lesson="four-great-errors">the four great errors</Ref>.

<Conclusion>
You can now say what the court's "he could have acted differently" presupposes: not punishment, which is older than the idea, but a self behind the blow that is ultimately responsible for it. Two arguments remove that self. Grammar splits every event into a thing and its act and so adds a doer to the deed, a fiction the weak need to make the strong blameable and their weakness a merit; BGE 19 and D 129 back its key step by explaining the felt self that commands and chooses without any doer, which makes a strong case, not a proof. Ultimate responsibility requires being *causa sui*, which nothing can be, and "unfree will" is the same doer made a prisoner. Against compatibilism, reasons turn out to be the helmsman, not the steam; responsibility from one's own character is never refuted, only denied the power to make suffering deserved. On that desert premise, retributive blame and guilt's premise fall; strong and weak will, character and rank stand.

That leaves the steam itself. If no doer moves the drives, something does: GS 360 calls it "a quantum of dammed-up energy that is waiting to be used up somehow", and GM I 13 called strength "a desire to overcome … a thirst for enemies and resistances and triumphs". <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">Will to power against survival and the will to live</Ref> takes up what that energy is and what it wants.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
In two or three sentences, using what <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">consciousness and language</Ref> established about GS 354: why is a creature whose consciousness takes the form of a subject–predicate language especially prone to the doer–deed fiction? Then say what BGE 17 and BGE 20 add to that explanation.

<Solution>
**From GS 354.** Becoming conscious of a state is putting it into the form of shared signs, so whatever reaches awareness arrives already in the language's form. A language that requires a subject for every verb therefore presents every conscious event, a thought, a decision, a blow, as something a subject does; the doer is built into the form in which the event becomes conscious at all, before any judgment is made about it.

**What BGE 17 adds.** A check on the one case where the subject seems directly observed: "a thought comes when 'it' wishes, and not when 'I' wish". What is observed is a thought arriving; the "I" that thinks it is inferred "according to the usual grammatical formula", so the inference runs on grammar, not on observation.

**What BGE 20 adds.** A comparative prediction: languages in which "the conception of the subject is least developed" should lead their philosophers to "look otherwise 'into the world'". If grammar drives the belief, the belief should vary with the grammar, which makes the explanation testable rather than merely suggestive. Tested, it fails as stated: the Ural-Altaic grouping has been dropped, and Finnish and Turkish have grammatical subjects. So the case for step 4 rests on BGE 17 and on the analyses of willing (BGE 19) and deliberation (D 129), not on BGE 20.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "Nietzsche denies free will, so he holds that we are puppets of our drives and nobody can do anything." Use BGE 21 to say which half of the free/unfree pair he also rejects, why, and what replaces both.

<Solution>
**What the claim gets right.** BGE 21 does reject free will "in the superlative, metaphysical sense": the ultimate responsibility it promises requires being *causa sui*, and that is "the best self-contradiction that has yet been conceived".

**Where it fails.** The claim moves from "no free will" to "unfree will", and BGE 21 rejects that move in so many words: whoever drops free will should "also put out of his head the contrary of this monstrous conception … I mean 'unfree will'". The reason is structural. A puppet is a self that wants one thing and is pulled by strings to do another, so "unfree will" keeps the doer behind the deed and makes it a prisoner instead of a sovereign. It also treats cause and effect as things that "press and push", which BGE 21 calls reifying "conventional fictions … not for explanation". "The 'unfree will' is mythology."

**What replaces both.** "In real life it is only a question of strong and weak wills." A strong will is a person whose drives are ordered under a governing coalition, so that command and result coincide; a weak will, one whose drives are disordered (BGE 19; the spring 1888 note's "coordination under the dominance of a single one"). People do things, and some do them with "precision and clarity of direction"; what is denied is a self behind the drives that either chooses freely or is coerced. The puppet image fails because there is no one behind the drives to be pulled.

**Bonus diagnosis.** BGE 21 would also say something about the person who likes the puppet conclusion: the "fatalism of the weak-willed" wants not to be "answerable for anything".
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the causa sui argument on praise instead of blame. A composer writes a great symphony. Does the argument remove the right to admire him? Lay the regress out in steps, say what HH 107 concludes, and say what survives the argument.

<Solution>
**The regress on praise.** (1) Ultimate credit for the symphony requires ultimate credit for the gifts and character it came from: the ear, the memory for structures, the stubbornness through years of failed drafts. (2) That requires having made those gifts and that character. (3) Whatever discipline he chose to train them, he chose with the gifts and character he already had, so the question repeats. (4) The chain ends in what he never chose: a body, a family that had a piano, a teacher, a century with orchestras in it. (5) So ultimate credit, the kind that says he *deserves* honour because the work was up to a self-made self, is impossible, for exactly the reason ultimate blame is.

**What HH 107 concludes.** Whoever understands this "may no longer either praise or blame, for it is absurd to praise and blame nature and necessity". But the same sentence goes on: "He can admire strength, beauty, abundance, in themselves; but must find no merit therein", as one loves "a fine work of art, but does not praise it, because it can do nothing for itself".

**What survives.** Admiration of the composer as a strong, rich, well-ordered type, and of the symphony as an expression of that strength, survives, because it does not need desert: we admire a mountain or a racehorse without thinking they made themselves. Ranking survives too: he is a higher type than a hack, and saying so describes the order of his drives. What goes is *merit* in the moral sense, the claim that he deserves reward because he could have failed and chose not to. The symmetry with blame is the point: the argument cannot keep the composer's merit and drop the striker's guilt, and Nietzsche does not try to. What he puts in place of praise is taken up in <Ref lesson="four-great-errors">the four great errors</Ref>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A counter-argument task. The lamb says: "The bird of prey is evil, because it could have refrained." Using GM I 13: (a) what does the judgment presuppose, and what does it do for the lamb? (b) Name two facts the judgment fails to account for. (c) State what would have to be true for the judgment to survive.

<Solution>
**(a) Presupposition and use.** It presupposes a "neutral substratum behind the strong man, which was free to express strength or not to do so": a doer behind the bird's predation that could have been a lamb. It does two things for the lamb. Outwardly, it makes the bird accountable "for being a bird of prey", so the lamb gains a right to condemn what it cannot resist. Inwardly, it makes the lamb's own not-attacking "a voluntary achievement, willed, chosen", so weakness becomes virtue: "their being thus-and-thus as a merit".

**(b) Two facts it misses.** First, the predation is not a choice laid on top of the bird but what the bird is: "a quantum of force is equivalent to a quantum of drive, will, effect"; to ask strength not to express itself "is just as absurd as to demand of weakness that it should express itself as strength". Second, the lamb's restraint is not restraint: it does not refrain from striking, it cannot strike, so the merit it claims belongs to a choice it never had. GM I 13 reduces the lamb's morality to "we weak ones are, after all, weak; it would be good if we did nothing for which we are not strong enough", the prudence "which even insects possess" when they play dead.

**(c) What would have to be true.** There would have to be, in the bird, a self distinct from its drives that could have overruled them, and that self would have to be ultimately responsible for which drives it has (otherwise its overruling or not overruling is just one more fact about its make-up). That requires a doer that grammar did not invent and a *causa sui* that logic permits. Both are what the first two arguments deny. The judgment survives only on the metaphysics that BGE 21 calls "a kind of logical rape". (Why the lamb's judgment came to rule a civilisation is <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave revolt</Ref>.)
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A boundary probe. A woman spends an hour weighing whether to take a job abroad: she lists consequences, consults friends, sleeps on it, and decides to go. Which part of the case against the doer (the grammar argument, the causa sui argument, or BGE 19's analysis of willing) has the hardest time with this case, and why? Then say what each branch of the fork over conscious willing says the hour was, using D 129 and GS 360.

<Solution>
**Which argument struggles.** BGE 19's analysis of willing has the hardest time on its own. BGE 19 describes a single act of will, a command and an obedience; an hour of reasoning looks less like a command and more like the work of a deliberating self, which is just what the compatibilist objection appeals to. The grammar argument is untouched (it is about the form of description) and so is causa sui (even a perfect deliberator did not make the character she deliberates with), but neither shows that the hour did nothing. That is the job D 129 and GS 360 take on.

**What D 129 says the hour was.** A real activity with a real product: "a picture of the consequences", which becomes "one very essential motive in the battle-line". But not the battle: the decision was settled among motives she did not list (habit, a friend she admires, restlessness, a trivial event that morning), "something quite invisible", and "I do not learn which motive has therewith actually proved victorious". She learns that she is going.

**On the first branch (accompaniment).** The hour was the surface narrating a contest decided below it: GS 360's purpose as a "beautifying pretext … after the event", a ship that "has a direction, to be sure, but — no helmsman at all". The same decision would have come without the weighing.

**On the second branch (effective drive-order).** The hour was a commanding drive (say, ambition, with the habit of careful planning as its instrument) doing its work: directing the energy, the helmsman steering the steam. It made a difference, but as one drive commanding others, not as a free subject choosing.

**What the probe shows.** On neither branch does the hour make her the ultimate source of the decision, so retributive praise or blame of it still lacks its premise. What the case does show is where the fork matters: whether her reasoning changed the outcome. The texts do not settle that, and the critique of blame does not need them to.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
GS 360 distinguishes "the cause of acting" from "the cause of acting in a particular way", and calls the first "a quantum of dammed-up energy that is waiting to be used up somehow, for something". If no doer moves the drives, what is this energy, and what does it want? Use GM I 13's description of strength to say what kind of thing it is (survival? pleasure? something else?), and say why "waiting to be used up somehow" is not the same as "wanting to stay alive".

<Solution>
**What the energy is.** Not a property of a self behind the drives, since there is none, but the drives themselves as forces: GM I 13's "quantum of force … equivalent to a quantum of drive, will, effect … nothing other than precisely this very driving, willing, effecting". GS 360 adds that it builds up ("dammed-up") and must discharge somehow; the purpose only decides where.

**What it wants, on GM I 13.** Strength is "a desire to overcome, a desire to throw down, a desire to become master, a thirst for enemies and resistances and triumphs". That is a want for resistance to overcome, not for comfort: an energy that seeks enemies is not trying to avoid danger.

**Why it is not survival.** An energy "waiting to be used up somehow" is spent whether or not spending it keeps the organism alive; a creature that only wanted to survive would hoard its strength and avoid "enemies and resistances". The tavern striker's anger, discharged at the risk of prison and a broken hand, is the plainest case: it served no survival interest and was spent anyway. Nor is it pleasure: a thirst for triumphs wants the obstacle, and pleasure is what comes with overcoming it.

**Where this leads.** The principle Nietzsche puts here is BGE 13's: "A living thing seeks above all to discharge its strength — life itself is will to power; self-preservation is only one of the indirect and most frequent results thereof." <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">Will to power against survival and the will to live</Ref> develops this energy as will to power, the desire to overcome resistance, and tests it against Darwin's self-preservation and Schopenhauer's will to live.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

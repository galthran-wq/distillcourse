Nietzsche made empirical claims about human beings: that our conscious reasons are often stories told after the fact, and that character is set more by constitution than by upbringing; a century of experimental psychology has tested claims of exactly this shape. The module argued for these claims from texts. <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="what-deliberation-observes">D 129</Ref> said that when we deliberate "I do not learn which motive has therewith actually proved victorious"; <Ref lesson="four-great-errors" id="the-moral-formula-turned-round">the Four Great Errors</Ref> said that Cornaro's slender diet, like a good man's virtue, followed from what his body already was. Those are not claims a reader can settle by reading more carefully. They say how people work, and whether people work that way is a question of fact.

Nietzsche asked for exactly that test. "We, we others who thirst after reason, are determined to scrutinize our experiences as severely as a scientific experiment, hour after hour, day after day. We ourselves wish to be our experiments and guinea pigs" (GS 319). He wrote it in 1882, before any laboratory of psychology could run the experiments, and the experiments came later: on why people say they chose what they chose, on whether their professed principles predict what they do, on how alike twins and adopted children turn out.

The obvious way to use them is to look up a finding that sounds like Nietzsche and declare him a prophet. That move fails twice. It never states what he claimed in a form that could have come out false, so any finding would do. And it never asks whether the thing the experiment measured, a personality score, a stated attitude, a share of variation across thousands of people, is the thing he was talking about, a drive in one person. Each claim has to be put in a form the world could refute before any finding is allowed to count for or against it.

## Five claims that could have been false

A claim tests nothing until something could count against it. "Our reasons are not what they seem" survives any finding; "people sincerely give reasons for choices that the reasons did not cause" is refuted by a careful study in which they never do.

### The experimental stance

Nietzsche did not treat his psychology as revelation. *Daybreak* (D, 1881) ends its account of the "moral interregnum", the time after the old moral feelings have lost their authority and before new ones exist, with a programme: "the best we can do in this interregnum is to be as far as possible our own *reges* [kings] and found little experimental states. We are experiments: let us also want to be them!" (D 453). *The Gay Science* (GS, 1882) lists the research a science of morals would need, "a history of love, of avarice, of envy, of conscience, of pious respect for tradition, or of cruelty", the "moral effects of different foods", "men's experiences of living together, in monasteries, for example", and says it "would require whole generations, and generations of scholars who would collaborate systematically" (GS 7). The man often filed as an irrationalist, an image <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language" id="what-the-surface-shows-does-not-depend-on-whether-it-acts">consciousness and language</Ref> already set aside, ends a section on self-knowledge: "Therefore: long live physics!" (GS 335).

### The claims, one sentence each

Read with that stance, the module's psychology contains five claims of fact.

1. **Reasons after the fact.** The reasons a person sincerely gives for what he did are often not what caused it (D 119, D 129; TI, "The Four Great Errors" 4).
2. **Principles that do not steer.** The moral principles a person professes are a poor guide to what he will do (GS 335; D 129).
3. **Constitution.** Character is largely set by an inborn constitution (BGE 231; GM Preface 2; EH, "Why I Am So Clever" 2).
4. **Upbringing shapes the form.** Upbringing and custom shape the form a drive takes, not its strength or kind (D 38; HH 107).
5. **A life as an experiment.** These claims can be tried on oneself, by living deliberately and watching what one actually does (GS 319, 324; D 453).

The fifth is a stance, not a finding, and no study tests it. The other four can be tested, and each has been, by research that never had Nietzsche in mind. That is what makes the comparison worth something: nobody designed these experiments to vindicate him.

## Claim 1: reasons supplied after the choice

The first claim is the module's most distinctive. D 129 said that the deliberator sees the comparison of consequences but not the contest of motives that decides; *Twilight*'s dream of the cannon shot, read in <Ref lesson="four-great-errors">the Four Great Errors</Ref>, said that the story is produced after the sensation and felt as coming first. The prediction that follows is specific: people will give sincere, detailed reasons for choices those reasons did not produce, and the reasons will sound no different from real ones.

### The stockings, and the swapped faces

<Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language" id="the-gap">Consciousness and language</Ref> already met one test of this: shoppers asked to pick the best of four identical pairs of stockings mostly took the pair on the right, then cited its quality and never its position. A sharper test came in 2005. Participants were shown two photographs of women's faces, side by side, and asked which they found more attractive. The experimenter then handed them the chosen card and asked them to explain their choice. On a few trials, by a card-magician's sleight of hand, they were handed the face they had *rejected*.

At least three swaps in four went unnoticed, even counting the people who said afterwards that something had seemed odd. The people who missed the swap then explained a choice they had not made. Some praised features that only the rejected face had, such as its earrings. When the researchers compared these explanations with the ones given on ordinary trials, they found them about as detailed, as confident and as emotional. The phenomenon now goes by the name *choice blindness*.

<Remark title="Confabulation">
*Confabulation* is giving sincere reasons for a choice, an act or an attitude that were not its causes. The person is not lying: he believes his reasons.
</Remark>

### A narrator with nothing to go on

The most striking cases come from patients whose two brain hemispheres had been surgically disconnected to control severe epilepsy. The wiring is crossed: each hemisphere receives what lies on the opposite side of the point the eyes are fixed on, and moves the opposite hand. Language sits mostly in the left hemisphere, so in such a patient an image flashed to the left of the fixation point reaches only the right hemisphere, and no speech. One patient had a chicken's claw flashed to the left hemisphere alone, the speaking one, and a snow scene to the right hemisphere alone, and was asked to pick matching cards. His right hand, run by the left hemisphere, picked a chicken; his left hand, run by the right, picked a snow shovel. Asked why, the speaking hemisphere, which had never seen the snow, answered at once: the chicken claw goes with the chicken, and you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed. It had no access to the cause and produced a reason anyway, with no hesitation.

<Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language" id="a-commentary-on-a-felt-text">D 119</Ref>, the dream theory in which one night's drive reads the same nervous stimuli as tenderness and another's as flight, called waking consciousness "a more or less fantastic commentary on an unknown, perhaps unknowable, but felt text"; *Twilight* added that the commentary is produced afterwards and experienced as coming first. The split-brain patient's answer has exactly that structure, and it was given without the slightest sense of guessing.

<KeyFact>
The sincerity of a reason is no evidence that it caused anything: a reason made up for a choice never made, or for a cause never seen, passes every test the speaker himself could apply to it. That is D 129's claim of 1881, now with experiments behind it.
</KeyFact>

### What the studies do not show

The experiments show that reasons *can* be produced without being causes, under conditions arranged to separate the two. They do not show that stated reasons never cause anything, and they leave open how often everyday reasons are confabulated. That boundary matches the fork in the module: <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="does-the-conscious-will-cause-anything">the doer behind the deed</Ref> left open whether conscious willing steers at all, and nothing here settles it. GS 44 adds a turn the experiments bear out in their own way: "it may be even more essential to know the fictitious and fanciful motives to which men ascribed their conduct. For their inner happiness and misery has come to men depending on their faith in this or that motive." A confabulated reason is false as a cause and real as a belief, and the belief has effects of its own.

## Claim 2: principles that do not steer conduct

The second claim moves from single choices to standing principles. If what decides an act runs below consciousness, a person's professed principles, what he says he values and would do, should be a weak guide to what he does.

### Two hundred and fifty doors

In the early 1930s a sociologist drove some ten thousand miles around the United States with a young Chinese couple, at a time when prejudice against Chinese people was open and common. Over two years they stopped at about 250 hotels, auto camps and restaurants. They were turned away once. Six months after each visit he mailed every establishment a question: "Will you accept members of the Chinese race as guests in your establishment?" About half wrote back.

### How weak, measured

The trip was one case, and a sociologist's careful notes are not an experiment. It started a literature that is. By the 1960s a review of dozens of studies found little evidence that attitudes predicted behaviour at all, and the next generation of researchers set out to find when they do. Each of their studies recorded, for a group of people, what each said about something (voting, blood donation, a minority) and what each then did about it, and asked how closely the two went together across the people. The answer is reported as *variance explained*: the share of the differences between people in what they did that goes along with the differences in what they said. A review of 88 such studies in 1995 put the average at about 14 percent. That does not mean people act on their attitudes one time in seven. It means that ranking people by their stated attitude accounts for about a seventh of the spread in their conduct, and the other six-sevenths goes with something else. Other experiments found the arrow often running the other way: people who are induced to do something come to hold the attitude that fits it.

Fourteen percent is not nothing, and here Nietzsche's own text is closer to the record than the summary "principles do nothing" would be. GS 335, the section that ends "long live physics", says that "our opinions, valuations, and tables of what is good certainly belong among the most powerful levers in the involved mechanism of our actions, but that in any particular case the law of their mechanism is indemonstrable". The claim is not that principles do nothing. It is that they are one lever among many, and that no one can tell from inside which lever moved a given act. The attitude studies support that claim in that form.

## Claims 3 and 4: constitution and upbringing

Is character set by what a person is born with or by how he is brought up, and if upbringing shapes something, what does it shape?

### The granite

<Quote source="BGE 231, trans. H. Zimmern">
<Original lang="de">
Das Lernen verwandelt uns, es thut Das, was alle Ernährung thut, die auch nicht bloss "erhält" -: wie der Physiologe weiss. Aber im Grunde von uns, ganz "da unten", giebt es freilich etwas Unbelehrbares, einen Granit von geistigem Fatum, von vorherbestimmter Entscheidung und Antwort auf vorherbestimmte ausgelesene Fragen. Bei jedem kardinalen Probleme redet ein unwandelbares "das bin ich" …
</Original>

Learning alters us, it does what all nourishment does that does not merely "conserve" — as the physiologist knows. But at the bottom of our souls, quite "down below," there is certainly something unteachable, a granite of spiritual fate, of predetermined decision and answer to predetermined, chosen questions. In each cardinal problem there speaks an unchangeable "I am this"; a thinker cannot learn anew about man and woman, for instance, but can only learn fully — he can only follow to the end what is "fixed" about them in himself. Occasionally we find certain solutions of problems which make strong beliefs for us; perhaps they are henceforth called "convictions." Later on — one sees in them only footsteps to self-knowledge, guide-posts to the problem which we ourselves *are* — or more correctly to the great stupidity which we embody, our spiritual fate, the *unteachable* in us, quite "down below." — In view of this liberal compliment which I have just paid myself, permission will perhaps be more readily allowed me to utter some truths about "woman as she is," provided that it is known at the outset how literally they are merely — *my* truths.
</Quote>

The section makes two claims, and the first is easy to miss. "Learning alters us": what a person takes in changes him, as food does. Below that changeable layer lies "something unteachable", and the section says what it consists of: "decision and answer" on "cardinal problems", the few questions on which a person's convictions turn out to be footprints of what he already is. *Fatum* is Latin for fate. The last sentence uses the doctrine on its author. The "truths about 'woman as she is'" that follow, in sections 232 to 239, are announced as his granite, convictions he could only follow to the end, and so as "*my* truths", not demonstrated ones; they are read whole in <Ref lesson="herd-morality-in-modern-dress">herd morality in modern dress</Ref>.

The *Genealogy* (GM, 1887) states the same claim for a philosopher's whole thought: "our ideas, our values, our yeas and nays, our ifs and buts, grow out of us with the necessity with which a tree bears fruit — related and each with an affinity to each, and evidence of one will, one health, one soil, one sun" (GM Preface 2). And *Ecce Homo* (EH, written 1888) puts it in its bluntest physiological form: "Nobody is free to live everywhere", because "the tempo of the metabolism is strictly proportionate to the mobility or lameness of the spirit's feet; the 'spirit' itself is after all merely an aspect of this metabolism" (EH, "Why I Am So Clever" 2).

<Remark title="Type-facts">
A *type-fact* is a fact about a person's constitution, his physiology or the standing make-up of his drives, that is fixed early, changes little, and sets the range of what he can believe, value and do. "Type" (*Typus*) in these passages means an individual kind of person, as Cornaro's slow metabolism made him a kind. Nietzsche never collects the texts into a doctrine or uses the term; the systematic form is a modern reconstruction, and the passages above are what carry it.
</Remark>

### Twins and adopted children

How could anyone tell whether character comes from constitution or from upbringing? Parents pass on both their genes and their home, so the resemblance of a child to its parents cannot separate them. Two natural experiments can.

The first compares two kinds of twins. Identical twins come from one fertilized egg and share all their genes; fraternal twins come from two and share about half, like ordinary siblings. Both kinds grow up in the same home at the same time. If identical twins resemble each other in a trait much more than fraternal twins do, the extra resemblance tracks the extra shared genes. The second design follows adopted children: they share genes with the parents who did not raise them and a home with the parents who did, so one can ask which they come to resemble.

The results are the most replicated in the study of personality. Roughly two-fifths to a half of the variation between people in the major personality traits (how outgoing, how anxious, how agreeable, how conscientious they are) goes with differences in their genes; twin studies put it near a half, adoption studies somewhat lower. A survey published in 2015 pooled fifty years of twin research, some 14.5 million twin pairs measured on almost eighteen thousand traits, and found an average of 49 percent across all of them. The share that goes with the family a child grows up in, the parents' style, the house, the values preached at the table, is far smaller: for personality, usually under a tenth and often close to nothing. Three children adopted into one kind, loving home turn out, in personality, hardly more alike than three strangers.


These shares are variance explained, the measure of [the attitude studies](#how-weak-measured), here read for personality: of the differences between people, how much goes with their differences in genes, and how much with their differences in homes.

<KeyFact>
When children resemble their parents in temperament, the resemblance runs mostly through the genes they share, not the home they share. That supports claim 3 for a population.
</KeyFact>

### One disposition, many forms

Claim 4 says more than that upbringing matters little. It says that what custom shapes is the form a drive takes. D 38, read in <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="what-a-drive-is-the-market-place-laughter">the society of drives</Ref>, has the drive that becomes "the painful feeling of cowardice" under one custom and "the pleasant feeling of humility" under the Christian one: same drive, two moral shapes.

A finding from cultural psychology has that shape. It needs two facts first. People differ, stably, in how badly they need a firm answer and how poorly they bear ambiguity; psychologists call it the *need for closure*. And cultures differ in where they look for the cause of an act: American habit treats the individual as the author of what he does, so an act is read as a sign of his character, while Chinese habit more readily treats the group as an agent, so the same act is read as a sign of the group he belongs to.

In experiments run in the United States and in Hong Kong around 2000, participants read an account of an ambiguous social event, an act done by someone who belonged to a group, and rated how far it came from that person's own dispositions and how far from the group's. Their need for closure was measured, and in a follow-up raised by putting them under time pressure. People high in that need leaned harder on their own culture's habit: the Americans attributed the act more to the individual's character, the Hong Kong Chinese more to the group's, and neither shifted toward the other's habit. The disposition was the same in both places, a craving for a firm answer, and the culture supplied the answer it fixed on. Map it onto D 38: the need for closure is the drive, the same in kind on both sides of the Pacific, and the culture's habitual explanation is the shape it takes. That is D 38's pattern, and it is also the "causal instinct" of <Ref lesson="four-great-errors">the Four Great Errors</Ref>, reaching for the explanation that has relieved it most often.

## What the numbers cannot say

The heritability findings look like a vindication of the granite, and the obvious conclusion is that the twin studies proved Nietzsche right about fixed character. Three things block that conclusion, and one of them cuts against him.

### A population statistic, not a share of a person

A heritability of 50 percent does not mean that half of a given person's character came from his genes and half from elsewhere. It means that in a particular population, in the range of environments it actually lived in, about half of the differences between people went along with differences in their genes. Change the population or the range of environments and the number changes. For the same reason heritability says nothing about whether a trait can be changed: height is highly heritable, and average height rose across Europe through the twentieth century as children ate better. Claim 3 concerns individuals, "the unteachable in us", and the statistic reaches individuals only if each person is typical of the group, which the statistic cannot show.

### The situation

The sharper counterweight comes from social psychology. In 1973 seminary students in Princeton were sent across campus to give a short talk; some had been told they were late, some that they had time. In a doorway on the way lay a man slumped and coughing. Of the students who had time, about six in ten stopped to help; of those told they were late, one in ten. Half had been asked to talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan, and that made no reliable difference. Whether a man stopped for a stranger in distress depended less on his principles than on whether he was in a hurry.

Being in a hurry, measured on the same scale as the attitude figure, explains about 14 percent of the differences in who helped: the original report gave no such share, and it was computed later by correlating which condition each student was in, hurried or not, with whether he helped. Character, measured act by act, does worse. In large studies of children's honesty in the 1920s, some eleven thousand children were given test after test in which they could cheat, lie or steal unobserved, and whether a child cheated on one test predicted whether he would cheat on another at a level explaining about 5 percent of the variation. Situations explain a real share of what people do, and fixed types, by themselves, cannot.

Nietzsche allowed some of this. "Our circumstances do not only conceal and reveal it to us, no! they magnify and diminish it. One should regard oneself as a variable quantity" (D 326). But the passages that carry the type-claim, BGE 231, GM Preface 2, EH's metabolism, give circumstance no such weight, and nothing in them would have predicted the hurried seminarians.

### No method for telling a type-fact from a habit

The last limit is in the texts themselves. A person's settled ways might be granite or might be habit laid down by years of practice, and nothing in what Nietzsche wrote says how to tell which. He knew he was not arguing the point. The *Genealogy*, explaining guilt by physiological depression, says: "I proceed on a presupposition that I do not first have to demonstrate to readers of the kind I need", and continues that "psychological pain" is "more probably" a matter of the belly than of the "psyche" (GM III 16). The claim is stated as a presupposition and carried by examples like Cornaro, chosen because they fit.

It is not an idle presupposition. *Twilight* rests a judgment of worth on it: "Every individual may be scrutinized to see whether he represents the ascending or the descending line of life. Having made that decision, one has a canon for the worth of his self-interest"; the descending one "has small worth, and the minimum of decency requires that he take away as little as possible from those who have turned out well. He is merely their parasite" (TI, "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man" 33). That sentence needs type-facts that can be read off an individual. The heritability numbers, being about populations, cannot supply them; <Ref lesson="skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man">the Skirmishes</Ref> reads the passage in its chapter.

## The strongest objection: these findings test something else

A serious critic now says the whole comparison is a category mistake. Nietzsche's "drives" are not the traits psychologists measure: a drive interprets, wants and competes, while "extraversion" is a score on a questionnaire. A heritability estimate is a population statistic, and his claims are about the constitution of individuals. And the situationist results show that conduct bends to circumstance in a way a doctrine of fixed types cannot allow. So the evidence neither confirms nor refutes him; it is about something else.

### The reply, texts first: Nietzsche allowed plasticity

The objection assumes that the type-claim says everything about a person is fixed, so that any changeability refutes it. The texts say otherwise. BGE 231 itself begins "Learning alters us", and puts the granite only beneath that, in the answers to "cardinal problems". <Ref lesson="sublimation" id="the-trellis">Sublimation</Ref> read D 560, where one can "dispose of one's drives like a gardener" and the "unchangeability of character" is a philosophers' prejudice. D 534 prescribes change "in the smallest doses but unremittingly over long periods of time", until the new valuation "has laid down a new nature in us"; D 462 cures a habit of cursing one's surroundings by "countless little exercises in the opposite direction". And GS 290, on giving style to one's character, describes the work and its limit in the same sentence:

<Quote source="GS 290, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Here a large mass of second nature has been added; there a piece of original nature has been removed — both times through long practice and daily work at it. Here the ugly that could not be removed is concealed; there it has been reinterpreted and made sublime.
</Quote>

"The ugly that could not be removed" is the granite; everything else in the sentence is change. So the type-claim, read through Nietzsche's own texts, is a claim about a floor: a few deep decisions that do not move, and above them drives that can be trained, redirected and given style. A floor of that kind allows conduct to bend above it, so the objection's third point, that circumstance moves what people do, does not refute the claim in this form.

### What heritability cannot decide

If the texts claim a floor, can the twin numbers confirm it? They cannot, and the reason is the one [the population-statistic section](#a-population-statistic-not-a-share-of-a-person) gave for changeability, applied to both halves of the figure.

Take the half of the differences that does not go with genes. The number says only that it is not genetic, and the home accounts for little of it. A difference laid down in the womb, by the chance course of one brain's development, or by an illness or a shock that marked one child and not the sibling counts as non-genetic, and it may never move again. A world in which every trait were fixed at birth could therefore show a heritability of a half. Now take the genetic half. It need not be fixed either: height is highly heritable, and it rose across Europe as children ate better. A world in which every trait stayed open to training could also show a heritability of a half. Total fixity, a floor and full plasticity all fit the same number, so the number cannot choose among them.

What could bear on it is evidence of another shape: evidence about what in a person's conduct moves and what holds. Two pieces of it are already in this chapter, both on the side of movement. Single acts bend to the situation, as the hurried seminarians showed, and the form a disposition takes follows the culture, as the need for closure did in the United States and in Hong Kong.

### The structure of a life, not the single act

The second part of the reply concerns the situationist results. Act by act, character predicts little: one honesty test, about 5 percent of another. But the same honesty studies, when they summed a child's behaviour over a battery of tests and compared it with another battery of different tests, found the two agreeing at a level explaining roughly a quarter to a third of the variation, five or more times what one test could. Each battery was still only a handful of afternoons. Studies in the 1970s that recorded the same people's conduct day after day for weeks found the pattern continue: the more occasions averaged, the more closely one stretch of a person's conduct predicted another. Character shows over a run of conduct, not in one act. The type-claim was never about single acts: BGE 231 speaks of "each cardinal problem", and Cornaro's metabolism explained his diet over decades, not each meal. The hurried seminarian is a fact about one afternoon. A doctrine of types survives it in the form Nietzsche gave it, as a claim about the shape of a life.

Set beside the evidence of movement, this rules out two extremes. If conduct were fixed act by act, one honesty test would predict the next nearly as well as a battery does. If nothing in a person were stable, a battery would predict another battery hardly better than one test predicts another. What the record shows is single acts that move with circumstance above a pattern across many acts that holds: the shape of a floor.

What it does not show is where the stable part came from. The honesty batteries measured children at one stretch of their childhood; nothing in them says whether the pattern was there at birth or laid down by years of habit, or whether long practice of the kind D 534 prescribes could shift it. That is exactly the question on which, [as the texts left it](#no-method-for-telling-a-type-fact-from-a-habit), Nietzsche offered no method, and the record does not answer it for him. The floor reading fits the evidence; it is not established by it.

### What the reply leaves open

The reply carries an unstated premise and leaves one point standing. The premise is that his drives and the psychologists' traits are the same kind of thing, or at least that traits are the outward measure of drives. That is plausible, since a drive that interprets and competes would show in stable differences of conduct, but it is assumed, and no experiment has measured a drive in his sense. The point that stands is the situation. Nietzsche allowed that circumstances magnify and diminish a person's power; he did not allow that a trivial circumstance, being late, would decide whether a man of principle helps a stranger. On that, the record says he was wrong.

<Conclusion>
You can now state the module's psychology as claims the world could refute, and say what the experimental record did to each. The negative half is where he was furthest ahead, stating in 1881 what experiments began to show in the 1970s. Reasons supplied after the fact: confirmed by choice blindness, by split-brain narration and by the stockings, in exactly D 129's form, where a confabulated reason sounds like a real one and its sincerity is no evidence that it was the cause. Professed principles: a weak lever, about a seventh of the variation in conduct, as GS 335 allows when it calls valuations powerful levers whose working in any single case cannot be shown. Constitution and upbringing: about half the differences in personality go with genes and very little with the home, which supports claim 3 for populations, not for any one person. His own texts, BGE 231, D 560, D 534 and GS 290, read the type-claim as a floor beneath trainable drives, not as fixity at birth; heritability cannot tell those two apart, but single acts that bend to circumstance and a pattern across many acts that holds fit the floor. Against him: situations bend single acts more than he allowed, and he gave no method for telling a type-fact from a habit, though *Twilight* builds judgments of individual worth on type-facts.

If a person's values grow out of his type as fruit from a tree, then a history of values is a history of the types that made them and needed them. <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">Genealogy as a method</Ref> turns that thought into a way of reading the history of morals.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "Twin studies have shown that Nietzsche was right: character is fixed at birth." Say what a heritability estimate measures and name the two overreaches in the claim. Then state the reading of the type-claim that Nietzsche's own texts give, with the passages, and say which evidence bears on that reading and which does not.

<Solution>
**What heritability measures.** The share of the differences between people in a population, in the range of environments they lived in, that goes along with differences in their genes. For major personality traits it is about two-fifths to a half.

**Overreach 1: from population to person.** A heritability of 50 percent does not say that half of any one person's character came from genes, or that his character is fixed. It is a fact about variation in a group, and reaches an individual only on the premise that he is typical, which the statistic cannot show.

**Overreach 2: from heritable to unchangeable.** Heritability says nothing about whether a trait can change. A highly heritable trait can shift with the environment (height rose across Europe with better nutrition), and heritability itself changes when the range of environments changes. And about half of the variation is not genetic at all; situations decide much of single acts.

**The texts' reading.** A floor: a few deep, stable features beneath drives that can be trained. BGE 231 begins "Learning alters us" and puts the "granite" only in "decision and answer" on "cardinal problems"; D 560 calls the "unchangeability of character" a philosophers' prejudice and lets one "dispose of one's drives like a gardener"; GS 290 adds second nature and removes original nature "through long practice", and conceals only "the ugly that could not be removed". "Fixed at birth" is the careless reader's version, and the texts reject it.

**Which evidence bears on it.** Not heritability: a figure of a half fits total fixity (the non-genetic half can be laid down in the womb or by early accident and never move) as well as it fits a floor or a character open to training throughout. What bears on the floor is evidence about what moves and what holds: single acts bend to the situation (the hurried seminarians), the form of a disposition follows the culture (need for closure), and a battery of acts predicts another battery five or more times better than one act predicts another. That pattern fits a floor; it does not show whether the stable part was there at birth or laid down by habit.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A research-shaped task. Design a study that would distinguish two explanations of why a person holds the values he holds: (A) values follow type, they grow out of a constitution present before any moral teaching (GM Preface 2); (B) values follow habituation, they are trained in by upbringing and practice. Say what you would measure, when, and in whom; what result would favour each explanation; and what GS 7 asked for in 1882 that your design supplies.

<Solution>
**The design.** A long-term study of adopted children, ideally including some twins adopted into different families. Measure, in early childhood and before much moral teaching has taken hold, temperamental differences that can be observed: how fearful, how irritable, how persistent, how sociable each child is. Record the values taught in each adoptive home (religious or secular, strict or permissive, the principles the parents state). In adulthood, measure the person's values two ways: what he professes, and what he does when it costs him something, in situations designed to test it.

**What favours (A).** Adult values, especially as shown in costly conduct, are predicted by early temperament and by the birth parents' values more than by the adoptive home; identical twins raised apart converge in their values; and where the adoptive home leaves a mark, it shows in the vocabulary of the professed values rather than in the costly conduct (a fearful child becomes a cautious Catholic in one home and a cautious secular liberal in another).

**What favours (B).** Adult values track the adoptive home's teaching, in conduct as well as in words, and early temperament adds little; twins raised apart diverge with their homes.

**The likely mixed result, and why the design still decides something.** Values in the narrow, culture-bound sense (which religion, which party) will track the home and the surrounding culture; the deeper dispositions behind them (caution, combativeness, need for a firm answer) will track temperament. Nietzsche's claim is about the second level, the form-versus-substance claim of D 38, so the design tests the right thing only if it measures both levels separately.

**What GS 7 asked for.** A study of "how differently men's instincts have grown, and might yet grow, depending on different moral climates", requiring "generations of scholars who would collaborate systematically". The adoption design is a way of watching one constitution grow in different moral climates, which is what GS 7 wanted and could not have.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A boundary probe. Describe how one and the same trait could have a heritability near 80 percent in one population and near zero in another. Then say what the case shows about reading type-facts off heritability estimates, and which passages of Nietzsche's already allow what the case shows.

<Solution>
**The construction.** Heritability is the share of the differences between people that goes with their genes, relative to the other differences present. In a population where every child gets ample food, schooling and stimulation, the environment varies little, so most remaining differences in, say, measured intelligence follow genetic differences: heritability is high. In a population where some children are badly fed, rarely schooled and seldom spoken to, environmental differences are large and swamp the genetic ones: heritability is low, though the genes are the same kind. Nothing about the genes changed between the two cases; the range of environments did.

**The real case.** A study of seven-year-old American twins, published in 2003, found exactly this for IQ: in the poorest families about 60 percent of the variation went with the shared family environment and the genes added almost nothing, while in affluent families the pattern was nearly reversed. Later studies replicated the pattern in the United States and not everywhere else, which is itself a sign that the number belongs to a population in its circumstances.

**What it shows.** A heritability estimate is not a measure of how much of a trait is "in" a person's constitution. It cannot tell a type-fact from a habit, because the same constitution yields a high number in one setting and a low one in another. Reading "granite" off a high heritability confuses a property of a population's circumstances with a property of the stone.

**Passages that allow it.** D 326: "Our circumstances do not only conceal and reveal it to us, no! they magnify and diminish it. One should regard oneself as a variable quantity whose capacity for achievement can under favourable circumstances perhaps equal the highest ever known." And D 560's gardener: the same plants grow differently under different hands. GS 7 asks how instincts "might yet grow, depending on different moral climates". These texts put the constitution's expression at the mercy of its climate, which is what the case demonstrates. What they do not supply is the method for separating the two, which the case shows is needed.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A sourcing drill. A newspaper headline reads: "Half of who you are is in your genes, says study of 14 million twins." The study behind it is a 2015 meta-analysis (a statistical pooling of earlier studies) that combined 2,748 published twin studies from fifty years, covering about 14.5 million twin pairs measured on 17,804 traits, from height and blood pressure to depression and personality; across all traits the average heritability was 49 percent. Twin studies assume that identical and fraternal twins are treated equally alike by their environment. (a) State in one sentence, for a newcomer, what the 49 percent means. (b) Say what kind of evidence the study is and what its sample covers. (c) Say what the study cannot establish about a single person, and what the twin-method assumption, if false, would do to the number. (d) Rewrite the headline so that it is true.

<Solution>
**(a) What it means.** Averaged over thousands of measured traits, about half of the differences between people in each trait went along with differences in their genes, in the populations and environments the original studies happened to sample.

**(b) What kind of evidence.** A meta-analysis: it collects no new data but pools the results of earlier twin studies. Its sample is large but not a random sample of humanity; twin registries are concentrated in a few wealthy countries (much of the data comes from Europe, North America and Australia), so the environments it covers are a narrow range. Its traits are a mixture of physical, medical and psychological measures, so "49 percent" averages over very different things; personality traits on their own come out somewhat lower in other surveys.

**(c) What it cannot establish.** Nothing about how much of any one person's character came from genes, and nothing about whether a trait can be changed: heritability describes variation in a group under given conditions. If identical twins are in fact treated more alike than fraternal twins (dressed alike, mistaken for each other, placed in the same classes), part of their extra resemblance is environmental, and the method counts it as genetic, so the estimate would be too high.

**(d) A true headline.** "In wealthy countries, about half of the differences between people across many traits go with differences in their genes, a pooling of fifty years of twin studies finds." Less exciting, and the "who you are" has gone, because the study is about differences between people, not about the make-up of a person.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
BGE 187 says: "one can always ask: What does such an assertion indicate about him who makes it? There are systems of morals which are meant to justify their author in the eyes of other people; other systems of morals are meant to tranquilize him, and make him self-satisfied; with other systems he wants to crucify and humble himself, with others he wishes to take revenge, with others to conceal himself, with others to glorify himself … In short, systems of morals are only a sign-language of the emotions." Take one value you hold (for example, that everyone deserves equal consideration, or that one should never give up). Say what kind of person would most need that value, and what it would do for him in BGE 187's terms. Then say what would have to be added to turn this one diagnosis into a history of a whole morality.

<Solution>
**A worked answer (possible answers vary).** Take "one should never give up". The person who most needs it is one whose drives do not keep him at a task by themselves, a man of strong ambition and unsteady persistence, or one who has failed and must not let the failure count. In BGE 187's terms the value works to "tranquilize him", by turning repeated failure into a test of virtue, and perhaps to "glorify himself", by making endurance itself a merit. A person whose drives held him at his work without effort would hardly need the precept; for him, on <Ref lesson="four-great-errors">the Four Great Errors</Ref>' account, "all that is good is instinct, and hence easy". Take instead "everyone deserves equal consideration": the person who most needs it is one who would come off worse under any ranking, and it would serve to "justify" him and perhaps to "take revenge" on those who would rank him lower. Whether that diagnosis is right is exactly what has to be shown, not assumed.

**What would have to be added.** One diagnosis reads a value as a sign of the person who holds it. A history needs three things more. First, the people who made the value, not only those who now hold it: which type first needed it, under what conditions. Second, how the value spread and won: which types took it over, and what made it serve them. Third, what it has done since to the people who live by it, which types it has strengthened and which it has weakened, since a value's meaning can change as different wills take it over.

**Where this goes.** That is the programme of <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">genealogy as a method</Ref>: GM Preface 3 asks "under what conditions did man devise these value judgments good and evil? and what value do they themselves possess?", and GS 345 says the value of a morality must be judged by its effects, not its origin. If values grow out of types, as GM Preface 2 says, a history of values is a history of which types made them and which types they made.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

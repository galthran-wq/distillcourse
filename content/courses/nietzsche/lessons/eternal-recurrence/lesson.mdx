The demon of The Gay Science §341 does not say the universe repeats; he asks whether you could bear it if it did, and the doctrine's force in the published works is the question, not the cosmology. This lesson reads GS 341 clause by clause for the three constraints it places on a Yes, follows the thought through its three stagings in *Zarathustra*, sets out the cosmological, practical and literary readings by what each needs the text to say, and shows the test applied to its own teacher: Zarathustra fails it, and the place where he fails fixes what affirmation is.

The course's positive doctrine arrived at <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the previous lesson</Ref> with a stance and no test: GS 276's *amor fati* says "some day I want to be only a Yes-sayer" and gives no way to tell a Yes to a life from a Yes to a year of it, and the revaluation of suffering derived there from the three conditions of <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref> covers a held desire's resistance and not the pain that arrives from outside. Two rules fixed earlier also bear on everything below: the published-works rule of <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>, that a notebook corroborates and never grounds, and the dramatization rule of <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref>, that a speech in *Zarathustra* is cited for what it stages. This lesson supplies the test, and grades the readings of it by those two rules. What is affirmed, the content of the Yes, is the next lesson's.

The route: GS 341 in full and dissected into recurrence, sameness and eternity, with what each closes; the mood of the published statements, GS 341, BGE 56 and GS 342; the stagings, riddle, crisis and hymn, with the dwarf's line returned to the dwarf and the animals' song to the animals; the cosmological reading with its evidence, a dated notebook, a letter, the finite-force argument, Simmel's counterexample and Heidegger's placement; the practical reading reconstructed, with Soll's objection, Clark's marriage analogy and Reginster's distinction between wishing a moment's eternity and wishing its recurrence; Zarathustra failing the test in "On Redemption"; what the naturalist reading can take from all this and what it cannot; and, starred, the thought's afterlife in four books.

## GS 341: three constraints on a Yes

The section is the fourth from the end of Book IV of *The Gay Science* (1882), and it is the one place in the books Nietzsche published where the thought is stated at length. Everything the readings disagree about is in its sentences, so it is quoted whole.

<Quote source="GS 341, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Das grösste Schwergewicht. — Wie, wenn dir eines Tages oder Nachts, ein Dämon in deine einsamste Einsamkeit nachschliche und dir sagte: „Dieses Leben, wie du es jetzt lebst und gelebt hast, wirst du noch einmal und noch unzählige Male leben müssen; und es wird nichts Neues daran sein, sondern jeder Schmerz und jede Lust und jeder Gedanke und Seufzer und alles unsäglich Kleine und Grosse deines Lebens muss dir wiederkommen, und Alles in der selben Reihe und Folge — und ebenso diese Spinne und dieses Mondlicht zwischen den Bäumen, und ebenso dieser Augenblick und ich selber. Die ewige Sanduhr des Daseins wird immer wieder umgedreht — und du mit ihr, Stäubchen vom Staube!“ — Würdest du dich nicht niederwerfen und mit den Zähnen knirschen und den Dämon verfluchen, der so redete? Oder hast du einmal einen ungeheuren Augenblick erlebt, wo du ihm antworten würdest: „du bist ein Gott und nie hörte ich Göttlicheres!“ Wenn jener Gedanke über dich Gewalt bekäme, er würde dich, wie du bist, verwandeln und vielleicht zermalmen; die Frage bei Allem und Jedem „willst du diess noch einmal und noch unzählige Male?“ würde als das grösste Schwergewicht auf deinem Handeln liegen! Oder wie müsstest du dir selber und dem Leben gut werden, um nach Nichts mehr zu verlangen, als nach dieser letzten ewigen Bestätigung und Besiegelung?
</Original>

The greatest weight. — What if, some day or night, a demon were to steal after you into your loneliest loneliness and say to you: "This life, as you now live it and have lived it, you will have to live once more and innumerable times more; and there will be nothing new in it, but every pain and every joy and every thought and sigh and everything unspeakably small and great in your life must come to you again, and all in the same series and sequence — and likewise this spider and this moonlight between the trees, and likewise this moment and I myself. The eternal hourglass of existence is turned over again and again — and you with it, speck of dust!" — Would you not throw yourself down and gnash your teeth and curse the demon who spoke thus? Or have you once experienced a tremendous moment when you would have answered him: "You are a god, and never have I heard anything more divine!" If that thought gained power over you, it would transform you as you are, and perhaps crush you; the question in each and every thing, "do you want this once more and innumerable times more?", would lie upon your actions as the greatest weight! Or how well disposed would you have to become to yourself and to life, to long for nothing more than this ultimate eternal confirmation and seal?
</Quote>

### Recurrence, sameness, eternity: what each closes

The demon's speech places three separate constraints on the life it describes, and each does a job the others cannot. The analysis is the one R. Lanier Anderson gives in the [Stanford Encyclopedia entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nietzsche/#EterRecuSame), and it answers the objection that a thought experiment which is only imagined can make no difference to anything.

<Figure
  src="./figures/gs341-dissected.svg"
  alt="A three-row table. Each row names a constraint in amber, the demon's clause that states it, and, in a dashed box struck through in amber, the escape it blocks. Row one, recurrence: this life as you now live it and have lived it, once more and innumerable times more; blocks hope for a better next time, the past placed ahead again so that the whole is judged. Row two, sameness: nothing new, every pain and every joy, all in the same series and sequence; blocks selective affirmation, a Yes bought by leaving out what one would change. Row three, eternity: the eternal hourglass turned over again and again, you with it, speck of dust; blocks a finite horizon, bearing it once or twice and being done, with Kundera's Agnes named. A footer states that the mood throughout is conditional and that the demon asserts nothing about the world."
  caption="Three constraints, three escapes closed: the past put ahead, selection forbidden, and no last time."
/>

**Recurrence** puts the whole life, past included, in front of the person asked. Practical attention runs forward, because the future is what can still be acted on; a judgment on the value of a life *overall* has no such asymmetry, and a reader who exploits the asymmetry ("at least I never have to go through that again") is not judging the life but evading part of it. Placing the life ahead once more makes self-concern, which only ever runs forward, do the work of an evaluation of the whole.

**Sameness** forbids editing. "Nothing new in it", "every pain", "in the same series and sequence": the life to be wanted again is this one, not this one improved, and the Yes cannot be bought by leaving out what one would rather change. The last lesson's closing exercise found that GS 276's one permitted negation, looking away, is exactly what a test must forbid; "the same series and sequence" is the clause that forbids it.

**Eternity** is the constraint whose work is hardest to state, and Reginster's chapter 5 states it. A finite number of repetitions leaves a horizon inside which endurance is possible: a life one would be willing to go through twice can still be a life one is glad will then be over. Milan Kundera's *Immortality* (1990) gives the case, and the SEP entry uses it: his character Agnes, asked the demon's question about her marriage, would relive it once or twice and not forever. "Innumerable times" removes the point at which the life is over and done with, so that only a Yes to the life *as such*, and not to bearing it, can answer.

### The mood: what the published works assert

From a thought its author called, in *Ecce Homo*, "the fundamental conception" of *Zarathustra* and "the highest formula of affirmation that is at all attainable", one expects an assertion. The published statements contain none. GS 341 is conditional and interrogative from its first word to its last: *wie, wenn*, "what if"; "would you not"; "or have you once experienced"; "if that thought gained power over you"; "how well disposed would you have to become". The section describes a situation, two possible reactions to it, and the kind of person the second reaction would require. It does not say the demon is right.

BGE 56 (1886) is the other published statement, and it is a portrait of a type. Whoever has "thought pessimism through to its depths" and looked "beyond good and evil" at "the most world-negating of all possible ways of thinking" will have "opened his eyes to the inverse ideal: to the ideal of the most high-spirited, vital, world-affirming individual, who has learned not just to accept and go along with what was and what is, but who wants it again *just as it was and is* through all eternity, insatiably shouting *da capo*". The section names an ideal and the person who fits it; the recurrence enters as what that person wants, not as what the world does. And GS 342, "Incipit tragoedia", the section that follows the demon, is the first section of *Zarathustra*'s Prologue printed a year early: it hands the thought to a character, and the next section of this lesson follows it there.

<Remark title="a translation note: Wiederkommen, Wiederkunft, das Gleiche">
GS 341 does not use the doctrine's title. Its verb is *wiederkommen*, "come again" ("must come to you again"), and its quantifier is *noch einmal und noch unzählige Male*, "once more and innumerable times more". The phrase *die ewige Wiederkunft des Gleichen* is the notebook's (11[141], below) and, in the books, the animals' (Z III 13, "the teacher of the eternal recurrence") and *Twilight*'s ("I, the teacher of the eternal recurrence", "What I Owe the Ancients" §5). *Wiederkunft* is rendered "recurrence" by Kaufmann and Del Caro and "return" by Hollingdale and by Krell's Heidegger; nothing in the argument turns on the choice. *Des Gleichen* does carry weight: *gleich* is "same", "like" and "equal", and "of the same" is the sense the sameness constraint needs, since a return of the *like* would leave room for the improved next time the constraint exists to exclude.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The published works assert a conditional and a type: GS 341 asks what a person would have to be to answer the demon "you are a god", and BGE 56 names that person. The one indicative statement of a world that repeats in any book Nietzsche published is in the mouths of Zarathustra's animals, and he calls it a hurdy-gurdy song.
</KeyFact>

## The three stagings: riddle, crisis, hymn

GS 342 handed the thought to Zarathustra, and *Zarathustra* stages it three times: as a riddle in Part III (written in January 1884), as a crisis eleven speeches later in the same part, and as a hymn at the part's end and again in Part IV (1885). The dramatization rule reads each staging for what it adds, drops or undercuts, and the first thing it does here is return two famous sentences to their speakers.

### The gateway "Moment": the dwarf's circle, and a question

"On the Vision and the Riddle" (Z III 2) is told by Zarathustra to sailors, "to you, the riddle-drunk", as "the vision of the loneliest one". He climbs a mountain path with the spirit of gravity on his shoulders, "half dwarf, half mole", and at a gateway sets him down.

<Quote source="Z III 2, 'On the Vision and the Riddle' §2, trans. Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
„Siehe diesen Thorweg! Zwerg! sprach ich weiter: der hat zwei Gesichter. Zwei Wege kommen hier zusammen: die gieng noch Niemand zu Ende. Diese lange Gasse zurück: die währt eine Ewigkeit. Und jene lange Gasse hinaus — das ist eine andre Ewigkeit. Sie widersprechen sich, diese Wege; sie stossen sich gerade vor den Kopf: — und hier, an diesem Thorwege, ist es, wo sie zusammen kommen. Der Name des Thorwegs steht oben geschrieben: „Augenblick“. Aber wer Einen von ihnen weiter gienge — und immer weiter und immer ferner: glaubst du, Zwerg, dass diese Wege sich ewig widersprechen?“ — „Alles Gerade lügt, murmelte verächtlich der Zwerg. Alle Wahrheit ist krumm, die Zeit selber ist ein Kreis.“ „Du Geist der Schwere! sprach ich zürnend, mache dir es nicht zu leicht!
</Original>

"See this gateway, dwarf!" I continued. "It has two faces. Two paths come together here; no one has yet walked them to the end. This long lane back: it lasts an eternity. And that long lane outward – that is another eternity. They contradict each other, these paths; they blatantly offend each other – and here at this gateway is where they come together. The name of the gateway is inscribed at the top: 'Moment.' But whoever were to walk one of them further – and ever further and ever on: do you believe, dwarf, that these paths contradict each other eternally?" – "All that is straight lies," murmured the dwarf contemptuously. "All truth is crooked, time itself is a circle." "You spirit of gravity!" I said, angrily. "Do not make it too easy on yourself!"
</Quote>

"Time itself is a circle" is the sentence most often quoted as Nietzsche's doctrine, and in the text it is the dwarf's, spoken "contemptuously", and rejected on the spot: "do not make it too easy". Nehamas's chapter on the recurrence (1985, ch. 5) makes the assignment and draws the consequence: Zarathustra tells the story "in order to ward him off", and the dwarf is "as unconditionally frightened by this idea as Zarathustra's animals are later unquestioningly glad about it". What Zarathustra then says in his own voice is a chain of questions, delivered "softer and softer, for I was afraid of my own thought": "must not whatever can happen already have happened … and this slow spider that creeps in the moonlight, and this moonlight itself, and I and you in the gateway whispering together … must not all of us have been here before?" The spider and the moonlight are GS 341's, and so is the mood: the demon's "what if" has become "must not", still a question.

Heidegger's lectures of 1937 read the same lines and reach the same textual result from the opposite philosophical direction. The dwarf, on his reading, "keeps to the outside": he sees the two lanes meeting somewhere in infinity and recurrence as "a sort of parade passing through the gateway", which is why nothing about it troubles him. The lanes "collide" only for "one who does not remain a spectator but who is himself the Moment", acting toward the future and "accepting and affirming the past, by no means letting it drop". Two readers who agree on nothing else agree that the circle is not the doctrine, and that the difference between the dwarf and Zarathustra is a difference in where one stands.

The vision then breaks: a dog howls, and Zarathustra finds a young shepherd on the ground "with a thick black snake hanging from his mouth", which has bitten into his throat. "Bite down! Bite off the head!" The shepherd bites, spits the head "far away", and stands up "no longer shepherd, no longer human – a transformed, illuminated, laughing being", with "a laughter that was no human laughter". The speech ends by handing the listeners the riddle: "who is the shepherd into whose throat the snake crawled this way?" The riddle is not solved in its own chapter, and the lesson does not solve it beyond what the second staging supplies.

### The Convalescent: the crisis, and the catechism mocked

"The Convalescent" (Z III 13) opens with Zarathustra summoning "my most abysmal thought" out of his depths, and collapsing "like a dead man" the moment it answers; he lies for seven days without eating, and when he sits up his animals, the eagle and the snake, speak. Their speech is the one passage in the published books that states the recurrence as a fact about the world.

<Quote source="Z III 13, 'The Convalescent' §2, trans. Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
Alles geht, Alles kommt zurück; ewig rollt das Rad des Seins. Alles stirbt, Alles blüht wieder auf, ewig läuft das Jahr des Seins. Alles bricht, Alles wird neu gefügt; ewig baut sich das gleiche Haus des Seins. Alles scheidet, Alles grüsst sich wieder; ewig bleibt sich treu der Ring des Seins. In jedem Nu beginnt das Sein; um jedes Hier rollt sich die Kugel Dort. Die Mitte ist überall. Krumm ist der Pfad der Ewigkeit.“ — — Oh ihr Schalks-Narren und Drehorgeln! antwortete Zarathustra und lächelte wieder, wie gut wisst ihr, was sich in sieben Tagen erfüllen musste: — und wie jenes Unthier mir in den Schlund kroch und mich würgte! Aber ich biss ihm den Kopf ab und spie ihn weg von mir. Und ihr, — ihr machtet schon ein Leier-Lied daraus?
</Original>

"Everything goes, everything comes back; the wheel of being rolls eternally. Everything dies, everything blossoms again, the year of being runs eternally. Everything breaks, everything is joined anew; the same house of being builds itself eternally. Everything parts, everything greets itself again; the ring of being remains loyal to itself eternally. In every Instant being begins; around every Here rolls the ball There. The middle is everywhere. Crooked is the path of eternity." – "Oh you foolish rascals and barrel organs!" answered Zarathustra, smiling again. "How well you know what had to come true in seven days – and how that monster crawled into my throat and choked me! But I bit off its head and spat it away from me. And you – you have already made a hurdy-gurdy song of it?"
</Quote>

Three things are settled here. The snake of the riddle is named: "that monster crawled into my throat", and what it was is given a few lines on, "my great surfeit of human beings", the sadness that "yawned" the sentence "eternally he returns, the human of whom you are weary, the small human being". The nausea of the crisis has a specific object, the recurrence of the small human being, which GS 341 had left open. And the cosmological restatement is the animals', greeted with "barrel organs" (*Drehorgeln*) and "a hurdy-gurdy song" (*ein Leier-Lied*): a tune ground out by a machine. The animals then deliver a second catechism, "we know what you teach: that all things recur eternally and we ourselves along with them", with the "great year of becoming" turning "like an hourglass", and name him "the teacher of the eternal recurrence"; Zarathustra "did not hear that they were silent", and the chapter ends with him conversing with his soul. The text places the doctrine's one indicative statement in the mouths of two animals and has its teacher mock it twice and then not answer it.

### The hymn: "all joy wants eternity"

The third staging is not an argument. "The Other Dance Song" (Z III 15) ends on twelve strokes of a bell, "for all joy wants eternity – wants deep, wants deep eternity!", and "The Seven Seals" (Z III 16), subtitled "the Yes and Amen Song", repeats seven times "how then could I not lust for eternity and for the nuptial ring of rings – the ring of recurrence!". Part IV's "Sleepwalker Song" (Z IV 19) adds the clause that binds the hymn to the sameness constraint: "have you ever said Yes to one joy? Oh my friends, then you also said Yes to all pain. All things are enchained, entwined, enamored … if you ever wanted one time two times … then you wanted everything back". A Yes to one thing is, on the hymn's own logic, a Yes to the whole it is knotted into; the hymn states what answering the demon "you are a god" sounds like, and nothing about whether he is right.

The rule from <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref> asks of a speech what it adds, drops and undercuts relative to the argued works.

**Adds.** A content for the nausea: GS 341 says "every pain and every joy" and names none; "The Convalescent" names the pain that is hardest to want again, the recurrence of "the small human being", and Part IV will give the small human being a face in the higher men. It also adds a picture of what the thought does to the person it seizes: seven days as if dead, then the need to sing.

**Drops.** GS 341's second person. The demon speaks to "you"; *Zarathustra* has the thought seize a character, and the reader watches. What the section asked the reader to answer, the book answers on stage, which is why the hymn can be read as a report of a Yes without being an argument for one.

**Undercuts.** Two readings. "Time itself is a circle" is a line Zarathustra calls "too easy" and leaves crouching on a stone, so a reading that takes it for the doctrine has to read the drama against its own stage directions. The animals' "wheel of being" is called a hurdy-gurdy song by the only character entitled to teach the doctrine, so a reading that takes their catechism for the teaching has the same problem one chapter later. The book's frame is an instruction, and the instruction is not to read the cosmology as the doctrine.
</Example>

<KeyFact>
Every indicative statement of recurrence in *Zarathustra* belongs to a character the drama marks as having got it wrong, the dwarf who is dismissed and the animals who are mocked; what Zarathustra says in his own voice is a question at the gate and a Yes in the hymn, which is GS 341's grammar exactly.
</KeyFact>

## The cosmological reading, and its evidence

From a thought its author called fundamental, one expects an argument in the books. There is none; the argument exists, dated and in two versions, and it is in the notebooks. The cosmological reading is the reading that grounds the doctrine there, and it is a serious one, with Löwith (1935), Heidegger (1937) and, in the last two decades, Paul Loeb behind it. The audit states it at strength before grading it.

### The dated origin: a pocket notebook and a letter

The thought's first written record is fragment 141 of the eleventh notebook of 1881, printed in the critical edition as [NF-1881,11[141]](http://www.nietzschesource.org/#eKGWB/NF-1881,11[141]). It is an outline with a title and a date at the end.

<Original lang="de">
Die Wiederkunft des Gleichen. Entwurf. 1. Die Einverleibung der Grundirrthümer. 2. Die Einverleibung der Leidenschaften. 3. Die Einverleibung des Wissens und des verzichtenden Wissens. (Passion of knowledge.) 4. The innocent one. The individual as experiment. The easing of life, lowering, weakening — transition. 5. The new weight: the eternal recurrence of the same. {/* img sils-maria-nietzsche-haus: the Nietzsche-Haus in Sils-Maria (Engadin), the summer house of 1881–1888 by the lake, a modern photograph of the museum building; the reader must see the house and the valley where 11[141] was written; caption must state 'modern photograph of the museum building' and give the fragment's date, August 1881; candidate humanall-sils-maria-haus — Wikimedia Commons File:Sils_Nietzsche_Haus.JPG, photographer 'Paebi', CC BY-SA 4.0, https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Sils_Nietzsche_Haus.JPG; placement float-right, width m */}

Two things in the fragment bear on the readings. Its title phrase is already "the eternal recurrence of the same", and its fifth heading calls the thought "the new weight" (*das neue Schwergewicht*), which GS 341 will call "the greatest weight" a year later: the notebook and the section share a name for what the thought *does*, and the name is a weight on action, not a fact about physics. And the fragment's own use for the thought is "the strongest means of incorporating it": a practice, taught in order to be lived, before any proof.

The dating is corroborated by a letter. On 14 August 1881 Nietzsche wrote from Sils-Maria to Heinrich Köselitz, the composer he called Peter Gast and the friend who later co-edited the compilation <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the compilation lesson</Ref> dissolved: "On my horizon thoughts have risen the like of which I have never yet seen — of that I want to let nothing be heard, and keep myself in an unshakeable calm. I shall probably have to live a few years more!" The letter names no thought; it records a date, an intensity ("tears of jubilation"), and a decision to stay silent. *Ecce Homo* (1888) supplies the scene the letter withholds: the thought "belongs in the August of the year 1881: it is thrown onto a sheet with the inscription '6000 feet beyond man and time'", by "a mighty pyramidal block of stone not far from Surlei" on the lake of Silvaplana, the next lake down the valley from Sils. The three documents agree on the month and the place. The fragment is the only one of them that contains the thought.

### The finite-force argument, and Simmel's wheels

Seven fragments later the same notebook contains the argument the cosmological reading rests on, and it is worth quoting in full because the published works never restate it.

<Quote source="NF-1881,11[148], eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Die Welt der Kräfte erleidet keine Verminderung: denn sonst wäre sie in der unendlichen Zeit schwach geworden und zu Grunde gegangen. Die Welt der Kräfte erleidet keinen Stillstand: denn sonst wäre er erreicht worden, und die Uhr des Daseins stünde still. Die Welt der Kräfte kommt also nie in ein Gleichgewicht, sie hat nie einen Augenblick der Ruhe, ihre Kraft und ihre Bewegung sind gleich groß für jede Zeit. Welchen Zustand diese Welt auch nur erreichen kann, sie muß ihn erreicht haben und nicht einmal, sondern unzählige Male. So diesen Augenblick: er war schon einmal da und viele Male und wird ebenso wiederkehren, alle Kräfte genau so vertheilt, wie jetzt […] Mensch! Dein ganzes Leben wird wie eine Sanduhr immer wieder umgedreht werden und immer wieder auslaufen […] Und dann findest du jeden Schmerz und jede Lust und jeden Freund und Feind und jede Hoffnung und jeden Irrthum und jeden Grashalm und jeden Sonnenblick wieder, den ganzen Zusammenhang aller Dinge.
</Original>

The world of forces suffers no diminution: for otherwise in infinite time it would have grown weak and perished. The world of forces suffers no standstill: for otherwise it would have been reached, and the clock of existence would stand still. The world of forces therefore never comes into an equilibrium, it never has a moment of rest, its force and its motion are equally great at every time. Whatever state this world can reach at all, it must have reached, and not once but innumerable times. So this moment: it was already here once and many times and will likewise return, all forces distributed exactly as now […] Man! Your whole life will, like an hourglass, be turned over again and again and run out again and again […] And then you find every pain and every pleasure and every friend and enemy and every hope and every error and every blade of grass and every glance of sun again, the whole coherence of all things.
</Quote>

<Remark title="the finite-force argument reconstructed (NF-1881,11[148]; NF-1888,14[188])">
*P1.* The world is a finite quantity of force, and force is neither created nor lost.

*P2.* An infinite time has already elapsed.

*P3.* If a final state, an equilibrium or a standstill, were possible for this world, it would have been reached in infinite time; it has not been reached; so no final state is possible.

*P4 (unstated).* A finite quantity of force admits only a finite number of distinct states.

*C.* In infinite time with finitely many states and no terminal one, every reachable state has been reached, and "not once but innumerable times": this moment, with every force "distributed exactly as now", has been and will be again.

*The 1888 version.* Fragment 14[188] of the spring of 1888 repeats P3 as "our only certainty": if the world "could reach a state of equilibrium, or had any goal at all that included duration, unchangeability, the once-for-all … that state would have to have been reached. But it has not been reached: from which follows …". The ellipsis is Nietzsche's. The 1888 note uses the argument against "mechanism" and the heat-death that "Thomson" (Lord Kelvin) had drawn from it; it is the argument's second and last statement, and it is a notebook's.
</Remark>

The argument breaks at P4, and the break was published in 1907. Georg Simmel, in *Schopenhauer und Nietzsche*, gave the counterexample Kaufmann calls "very elegant": three wheels of equal size on one axle, one point marked on the rim of each and the three marks in a line, the second wheel turning twice as fast as the first and the third at $1/\pi$ times its speed. The first two marks realign after every turn of the first wheel; the third mark is at $n/\pi$ of a turn after $n$ turns, and $n/\pi$ is never a whole number, because $\pi$ is irrational. Three parts, finite space, infinite time, and the initial configuration never recurs. Finitely many *parts* do not give finitely many *states*, and nothing in P1 to P3 supplies P4.

Clark adds a second objection that matters more for this course. Every premise but one is a priori: P1 and P4 are claims about the nature of force, P3 about the nature of time and possibility; the only empirical premise is that the world has not ended. A philosopher who, on Clark's reading of the mature works, rejects substantive a priori truths cannot ground his central doctrine on three of them. Kaufmann's report that Nietzsche called the recurrence "the most scientific of all possible hypotheses" (WP 55, a notebook of 1887) does not change the argument's character; it records what its author hoped for it.

### Heidegger's placement: not a proof, a projection

The strongest defender of the cosmological reading does not defend the proof. Heidegger's lectures of the summer of 1937, *The Eternal Recurrence of the Same*, make the doctrine "Nietzsche's fundamental metaphysical position" and then argue that treating the notebook arguments as physics, whether to refute them or to vindicate them, mistakes what they are. The argument is "by no means a proof that could have its force in the cogency and conclusiveness of its deductive steps"; what it proffers is "the unfolding of a projection, by no means its computation and its grounding". The premises are "positings that are co-posited" in a projection of beings as a whole onto "Being as eternally recurrent"; the "force" in them "is not what physics means by it". A proof that could settle it would be beside the point in the way a proof of God's existence is: "a god who must permit his existence to be proved in the first place is ultimately a very ungodly god". Heidegger also supplies the correct name for the manuscript: the fragments the editions string together as a "proof" lie "in altogether disparate places" in "the manuscript bearing the catalogue number M III 1", the notebook photographed above.

On this reading the thought is a *Glaube*, a "belief", but of a kind that cannot be held at a distance from what it is about: to think the thought "is not to drive a vehicle through it", and the indifference "between 'bicycle' and 'the Kaiserstuhl'", the hills one rides out to, "does not obtain between the thinking of the thought of return and what is actually thought and experienced there". Heidegger and the practical readers therefore agree that the physics is irrelevant, and disagree on what is left: for him a metaphysics of beings as a whole, for them a test. Loeb's recent defence (SEP §6.3) takes the other fork, and holds that the thought is literally about the world's temporal structure; his challenge to the practical readers, that they owe an account of why the three constraints make a difference if nothing recurs, is the one the first section of this lesson answered.

<KeyFact>
The cosmological reading needs the notebooks to ground the doctrine, since the published works contain no argument for a repeating world and mock the one character who states one; under the course's evidence rule a notebook corroborates and never grounds, so the reading is not refuted here but graded: as a cosmology the doctrine is Nachlass, and the reading owes an account of why its author withheld the proof from every book.
</KeyFact>

## The practical reading: the test reconstructed

The alternative is to read GS 341 as what it says, a question about a person, and the alternative has to explain why a question that is only imagined could be "the greatest weight". The reading was begun by Ivan Soll (1973), given its form by Nehamas (1985), Clark (1990) and Reginster (2006), and reaches from Soll's own objection to Reginster's account of what the eternity constraint is for.

### An ideal, not an imperative

The oldest form of the practical reading turns the demon into Kant: act only so that you could will the act to recur eternally. Kaufmann rejects the assimilation on two grounds that stand. Kant's categorical imperative appeals to a contradiction in universalized maxims and takes no interest in how one *feels* about the consequences; the demon appeals to nothing else. And the recurrence would deter from an action, if at all, only through "affective response to the consequences", which is a description of a disposition and not a rule. Clark's version is the one the course adopts: the doctrine is "an ideal for human beings: to become the kind of person who, in the situation described, would consider the demon's message divine". Christopher Janaway makes the grammatical point that supports it. GS 341 "proceeds by questions and conditionals" and contains no "ought"; it is like asking "how great a specimen of physical prowess would you have to become to succeed in winning the London Marathon ten times in a row?", a question that "simply says what a certain kind of greatness would consist in" and enjoins nothing.

### Soll's objection, and the marriage question

Soll's objection is the sharpest thing said against the reading, and Clark's reply is the reading's hinge. A recurrence of one's life "should actually be a matter of complete indifference": the recurring life must be qualitatively identical in every cycle, so no memory links it to this one, no consciousness continues, nothing "carries over from my death to the birth of my double in the later cycle". A clear conception of that removes any reason to gnash one's teeth, and with it any reason for the test to sort anyone. Clark grants the premise and keeps the test.

The reply is an analogy. Couples ask each other a question with the same defect: "if you had it to do all over, would you marry me again?" Answering it requires "a contradictory or unrealistic model: you, knowing what you do now, going through an experience identical to one in which you knew much less", and no one thinks the defect stops the question from testing what the two people feel about the marriage; "refusal to adopt it would show evasion, not intellectual honesty". The demon's situation is built the same way. He comes in "your loneliest loneliness", where critical powers are at their lowest, and the reaction GS 341 asks about is the reaction of someone who "accepted the message uncritically as the truth". To play the game is to imagine the recurrence as continuous with this life, adding its joy and its suffering to this one's, which is a misconstrual of recurrence and the right model for the test, because what the test measures is the present attitude toward this, non-recurring, life. Passing is being "a person who would respond joyfully to the demon's proclamation of eternal recurrence if she accepted it as the truth, interpreted it in the uncritical or preanalytic manner", and responded honestly.

### Reginster: two roles, and two wishes named "eternity"

Reginster separates two jobs the concept of recurrence can do. In its *theoretical* role it names a property of the life to be affirmed (the life will in fact recur, so affirming it means affirming that); in its *practical* role it says something about the attitude of affirmation itself, and the life's actual properties are beside the point. His own analogy is a lover's: to urge someone to live the next moment so that they could wish it would never end is not to inform them that it will never end, but to describe the attitude one wants them to be able to take. The practical reading takes the recurrence in the second role, and Reginster's contribution is what the *eternity* in the demon's speech then means.

Two wishes carry the word. The lover's "let this never end" is a wish for permanence: one state, held, time stopped. It expresses a perfection "that would not be altered or corrupted by permanence", and it is the shape of every ideal Nietzsche opposes, the "being" of Plato and of the Christian eternal life, which *Twilight* diagnoses as the philosophers' hatred of "death, change, age". The demon's "once more and innumerable times more" is a wish for the opposite object. To want a moment *again* is to acknowledge that it ends, and to want its recurrence rather than its permanence is to want it as something that passes: it "suggests that there are perfections to which impermanence might actually be essential", perfections "such that permanence would actually undermine them". What is affirmed under the recurrence is therefore becoming as such, "a temporally extended process that involves change", and a life can pass the demon's test only if it has already revalued becoming, which is what <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the previous lesson</Ref>'s derivation did for suffering: on the three conditions, a satisfaction is the overcoming of a resistance, so a final state in which nothing remains to overcome is not the goal but its loss. The eternity constraint is not a device for making the test harder; it is the clause that names the object of the Yes, and rules out the most natural way of misidentifying it.

<Remark title="the practical reading reconstructed (Clark 1990, ch. 8 §§1 and 3; Reginster 2006, ch. 5 §III)">
*P1.* Affirmation of life, the stance GS 276 wishes for, is a valuing of this life as a whole and as an end (<Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the previous lesson</Ref>, P2 of "affirmation as the answer to despair").

*P2.* A Yes that selects, that looks away from part of the life or values the life for a state beyond it, is not a Yes to the whole as an end; a test of affirmation must therefore make the whole life, with nothing subtracted and no arrival, the object of a single attitude.

*P3.* GS 341's three constraints construct exactly that object: recurrence puts the whole ahead, sameness forbids subtraction, eternity forbids an arrival or a last time.

*C.* The willingness to answer the demon "you are a god", taken uncritically and honestly, is the criterion of affirmation; and since the criterion is an attitude to this life, the truth of the recurrence is irrelevant to the role the thought plays.

*The premise it does not state.* That there is a non-selective Yes to be had at all: that becoming and the suffering in it can be the object of joy rather than endurance. GS 341 asks "how well disposed would you have to become", and the disposedness is the substantive revaluation the previous lesson derived and left with an open fault line at pain from outside. The demon's test demands the Yes on that gap; the test does not close it.
</Remark>

## Zarathustra fails the test: "On Redemption" and life as a means

The practical reading's strongest textual result is that the book's own teacher does not pass, and that the reason he does not pass is the reading's definition of affirmation. The evidence is two speeches of Part II (summer 1883), read by Clark in the fourth section of her chapter, and the nausea of "The Convalescent".

<Quote source="Z II 20, 'On Redemption', trans. Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
Die Vergangnen zu erlösen und alles „Es war“ umzuschaffen in ein „So wollte ich es!“ — das hiesse mir erst Erlösung! […] „Es war“: also heisst des Willens Zähneknirschen und einsamste Trübsal. Ohnmächtig gegen Das, was gethan ist — ist er allem Vergangenen ein böser Zuschauer. Nicht zurück kann der Wille wollen; dass er die Zeit nicht brechen kann und der Zeit Begierde, — das ist des Willens einsamste Trübsal. […] Dass die Zeit nicht zurückläuft, das ist sein Ingrimm; „Das, was war“ — so heisst der Stein, den er nicht wälzen kann. Und so wälzt er Steine aus Ingrimm und Unmuth und übt Rache an dem, was nicht gleich ihm Grimm und Unmuth fühlt.
</Original>

To redeem those who are the past and to recreate all "it was" into "thus I willed it!" – only that would I call redemption! […] "It was": thus is called the will's gnashing of teeth and loneliest misery. Impotent against that which has been – it is an angry spectator of everything past. The will cannot will backward; that it cannot break time and time's greed – that is the will's loneliest misery. […] That time does not run backward, that is its wrath. "That which was" – thus the stone is called, which it cannot roll aside. And so it rolls stones around out of wrath and annoyance, and wreaks revenge on that which does not feel wrath and annoyance as it does.
</Quote>

The speech names the mechanism of despair the previous lesson previewed. The will "cannot will backward"; "it was" is a stone it cannot move; and its response is "revenge", which "punishment" then names "with a lying word", so that "all willing itself and all living is supposed to be – punishment". <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">The Second Treatise's</Ref> guilt is this revenge given a theology. The redemption the speech asks for, "all 'it was'" recreated "into 'thus I willed it!'", is the demon's second reaction in a different grammar: to want the past again is to have willed it. The speech ends with the question whether the will "has already become its own redeemer", and Zarathustra breaks off "like one who is terrified".

### The diagnosis: a life valued as a means

Clark's reading starts from what Zarathustra says he could not do without. "The now and the past on earth – alas, my friends – that is what is most unbearable to me. And I would not know how to live if I were not also a seer of that which must come." What must come is the overman, and the human being is, from the Prologue on, "a bridge and not an end". That is the structure of the ascetic ideal from <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref>, in which life had value only as the means to its own overcoming; Zarathustra's ideal keeps the structure and changes the destination. Two chapters earlier, "The Tomb Song" (Z II 11) shows what the structure is compensating for: "you murdered the visions and dearest wonders of my youth", "what is all murder of human beings compared to what you have done to me?", the loss of a first, unreflective affirmation ("all beings shall be divine to me"), endured, the song says, only through his will, which for Zarathustra is his creating, and what he has created is the overman.

Then the thought seizes him. Imagined as the demon requires, "going on and on", the recurrence means that "whatever he achieves will come undone, and he will need to redo it": the small human being returns, the overman is never established, and a life that had value as a means to that arrival has, with the arrival removed, no value at all. That is the content of the nausea in "The Convalescent", "the small human being recurs eternally … that was my surfeit of all existence", and it is why the crisis has that object and no other. Soll had asked why recurrence should trouble anyone, since each cycle could contain the same progress; Clark's answer is that it should not trouble anyone who values the process, and it must trouble anyone who values only the end. The recurrence "does not preclude" affirmation; "it does preclude one way of doing so": valuing life "only as a means to something beyond the process itself – heaven, nirvana, the rock sitting on top of the mountain … or any other kind of utopia". What is left as the only alternative to despair is "finding intrinsic value in life itself, that is, valuing the process of living as an end", and the recurrence, uncritically construed, is Nietzsche's formula for what that is: "to be joyfully willing to engage in the same activities again and again, even if one had no hope of the goal being finally achieved".

Clark and Nehamas disagree about who "the small human being" is, and the disagreement is a small worked case of the dramatization rule. Nehamas identifies him with the Prologue's "last human being", who "invented happiness" and blinks. Clark objects that the text introduces him elsewhere: "the small human being, especially the poet – how eagerly he puts his accusations against life into words! Hear him, but do not fail to hear the lust that is in all his accusing!" The small human being of "The Convalescent" is the accuser of life, who affirms his own life by condemning life, which is the type of <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave revolt</Ref>'s priest and of the tarantulas; and he is the same as the "enemies" of "The Tomb Song", who deprived Zarathustra of his first affirmation by the same accusing. On Clark's identification the nausea, the revenge and the overman are one structure: the accusers cost Zarathustra his Yes, the overman is his revenge on them, and the recurrence forecloses the revenge by bringing them back. The identification is textual, and the course records it as the stronger of the two.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Affirmation, on the practical reading, requires valuing this life as an end and not as a bridge; Zarathustra values it as a bridge to the overman, cannot will the recurrence of what the bridge was to leave behind, and fails the test he is to teach: the book applies its own criterion to its own hero, and the criterion is the one GS 341's eternity constraint states.
</KeyFact>

## What the naturalist reading needs, and cannot use

The working hypothesis of the course reads Nietzsche's claims as a causal psychology of types, and the audit of this lesson asks what that psychology can do with the recurrence.

### What it can hold: the test as a practice on the drives

It can hold the practical reading whole, and Janaway shows it can do more. The self of <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drive lesson</Ref> is a "society of drives", BGE 12's "subjective multiplicity", and the greatness the course reaches in the next lesson has three internal conditions on that society: drives individually strong, as many as possible, and "bound into a unity" in their conflict, BGE 212's "the ability to be just as multiple as whole, just as wide as full". The affirmative attitude the demon tests is, on the most economical reading, a *symptom* of a set of drives that satisfies those conditions: a person whose drives are unified is one who can say Yes to the whole of what they have done. Janaway argues for a second link "with the causality running the other way round": attitudes of self-affirmation or self-negation "might in addition cause alterations to our drives and their relations to one another in such a way as to move them nearer to a state" satisfying the conditions. Someone striving to disown a strong drive is one drive "complaining about another" (*Daybreak* 109); someone who wills the recurrence of the life that drive shaped has stopped disowning it, and the drives are, by that much, more of a unity. The notebook fragment's own instruction, "we teach the teaching — it is the strongest means of incorporating it", is the same causal claim in 1881: the thought is a practice, and the practice changes the one who practises it.

That is the naturalist's version of the doctrine: a test of total self-affirmation, restricted to the few capable of it, continuous with the drive psychology and describable without a single sentence about the world, with the recurrence as a practice that may produce the integration it tests for. It needs GS 341, BGE 56, BGE 212 and *Daybreak* 109, all published, and it needs the will-to-power lesson's is–ought step, since "greatness" is being defined.

### What it cannot use: the cosmology, on two grounds

The naturalist reading cannot use the cosmological doctrine, and not only because it is Nachlass. If the world recurred, and one knew it, the demon's "would you" would be idle: the test asks for an attitude one is free to have or lack toward a life that is, for all one knows, single, and a proof of recurrence would convert the question into information about one's future. Clark's argument against Magnus applies: a doctrine whose force came from the *truth* of recurrence "is made dependent on cosmological argumentation", and the argumentation is in the notebooks and fails. The practical reading is not merely compatible with the absence of a cosmology; it needs the absence.

The literary reading is stated here in one paragraph and evaluated in <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">the life-as-literature lesson</Ref>. Nehamas reads the gateway speech's "all things knotted together" and "what returns … is my own self" (Z III 1) as the claim that a life is a whole in the way a well-made work is: every element interconnected, so that to want any part different is to want to be someone else. His test case is a novel. "Could Anna Karenina not have fallen in love with Vronsky? … Could she not have been Oblonsky's sister?" For a literary character such questions are "very difficult, if not impossible, to answer", and Nehamas holds that Nietzsche assimilates the ideal person to the ideal character: a life affirmed is one from which nothing is subtractable. Nehamas himself names the objection, that "reality is capable of supporting counterfactuals" that literature cannot, and Module 7 decides whether the reading survives it. Heidegger's placement of the doctrine as the unity of Nietzsche's one metaphysical thought is likewise carried, unanswered, to <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref>.

## ★ The literary afterlife

The thought had readers who were not philosophers, and Krell's analysis in the second Heidegger volume collects four. Two precede Nietzsche's writing of it and two follow: Melville's Queequeg (*Moby-Dick*, 1851) has his coffin built, recovers, and keeps it as a sea-chest; Upton Sinclair's novella *The Overman* (1907) has an islander people for whom "life is their task, and life only"; Rilke's "Das Karussell" (1906) turns a carousel through its riders "and now and then a white elephant"; and the "Ithaca" episode of *Ulysses* (1922) ends Bloom's day in the cosmological catechism the animals sing.

## What is established, and what the higher-type lesson takes up

What is established. GS 341 states the thought as a conditional and a question, and its three constraints, recurrence, sameness and eternity, each close an escape: the past placed ahead so the whole is judged, subtraction forbidden, and no last time, the constraint Kundera's Agnes shows the work of. BGE 56 names the type who would answer the demon "you are a god", and GS 342 hands the thought to Zarathustra, where it is staged as riddle, crisis and hymn: the circle is the dwarf's and dismissed, the "wheel of being" is the animals' and mocked, Zarathustra's own sentences are questions and a Yes, and the crisis gives the nausea its object, the recurrence of the small human being. The cosmological reading rests on a dated notebook, 11[141] of August 1881, corroborated by the Köselitz letter and by *Ecce Homo*, and on the finite-force argument of 11[148] and 14[188], which needs a finite number of states it does not have, as Simmel's three wheels show; Heidegger, its strongest defender, denies that the argument is a proof at all and reads it as the unfolding of a metaphysical projection. Under the evidence rule the doctrine as cosmology is Nachlass. The practical reading uses only published text: the doctrine is an ideal, not an imperative; Soll's objection is answered by building the uncritical model into the test, as the marriage question does; and Reginster's distinction between wishing a moment's eternity and wishing its recurrence shows the eternity constraint naming the object of the Yes, becoming itself, so that the test presupposes the revaluation of suffering and does not supply it. Zarathustra fails the test because he values life as a bridge to the overman, and "On Redemption" and "The Tomb Song" show why: affirmation requires valuing this life as an end. The naturalist reading holds the practical doctrine and, with Janaway, reads the willing of the recurrence as a practice that may integrate the drives it tests; it cannot use the cosmology, on the evidence rule and because a true recurrence would make the demon's question idle.

What stands open is the content. The demon asks "how well disposed would you have to become to yourself and to life", and BGE 56 names the person who would answer him, "the most high-spirited, vital, world-affirming individual"; neither says what such a person is made of, which drives in what order, or what an aristocratic society, a spiritualized passion and a character "given style" have to do with it. <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs that type from BGE 257–262, *Twilight*'s "Morality as Anti-Nature" and GS 290, and states the normative component of the critique of morality it grounds.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Closed book. State the three constraints GS 341 places on the life the demon describes, quote for each the clause of the demon's speech that carries it, and name the escape each one blocks. Then say which of the three is the hardest to explain on the practical reading, and give the case that shows its work.

<Solution>
**Recurrence.** "This life, as you now live it and have lived it, you will have to live once more" (*noch einmal … leben müssen*): the past is placed ahead, so that the future-directed self-concern that practical reasoning runs on is made to judge the life as a whole rather than the part that can still be changed. It blocks the escape of a better next time and the evasion "at least I never have to go through that again".

**Sameness.** "There will be nothing new in it, but every pain and every joy … all in the same series and sequence" (*in der selben Reihe und Folge*): the life to be wanted is this one unedited. It blocks selective affirmation, a Yes bought by looking away from what one would change, which GS 276's "looking away shall be my only negation" had still permitted.

**Eternity.** "Once more and innumerable times more"; "the eternal hourglass of existence is turned over again and again" (*immer wieder umgedreht*): there is no point at which the life is over and done with. It blocks the escape of endurance, bearing the life a bounded number of times.

**The hardest.** Eternity, as Reginster and the SEP entry both record. Recurrence and sameness make sense as devices for an honest whole-life evaluation; a finite number of repetitions would already do that. The case is Kundera's Agnes in *Immortality*: she would relive her marriage once or twice and not forever, so her life passes a finite test and fails the demon's, and what the difference shows is that a Yes to bearing something is not a Yes to it. The constraint's positive content is Reginster's: to want a moment *again*, endlessly, is to want it as something that passes, so the eternity of the demon is a wish for becoming and not for permanence.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "Nietzsche believed, and tried to prove, that the universe literally repeats itself; the eternal recurrence is his cosmology." Locate the proof and date it; state what the published works say instead, with the sentence in each that the claim cannot accommodate; then state what a defender of the cosmological reading may still say, and why that is a reading to be audited rather than a refutation of the practical one.

<Solution>
**Where the proof lives.** In the notebooks, twice: NF-1881,11[148] (August 1881, the notebook M III 1) and NF-1888,14[188] (spring 1888). Both run the finite-force argument: no diminution, no standstill, therefore no equilibrium, therefore every reachable state reached "innumerable times". Neither was published, and the published-works rule of <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> lets a notebook corroborate a published claim and never ground one.

**What the published works say instead.** GS 341 is conditional throughout: "what if … a demon", "would you not", "how well disposed would you have to become"; there is no indicative sentence about the world for the claim to cite. BGE 56 describes a *type*, "the most high-spirited, vital, world-affirming individual" who "wants it again just as it was and is through all eternity", which is a person's wish and not a physics. "The Convalescent" contains the one indicative statement of a repeating world in the books, "everything goes, everything comes back; the wheel of being rolls eternally", and puts it in the mouths of the animals, whom Zarathustra answers with "barrel organs" and "a hurdy-gurdy song"; "On the Vision and the Riddle" puts "time itself is a circle" in the dwarf's mouth and has Zarathustra reject it as "too easy". A reading on which the cosmology is the doctrine has to read the books' two statements of it against the books' own stage directions.

**Why the proof fails on its own terms.** Simmel's three wheels (1907): finitely many parts in infinite time need not repeat a configuration, since the states are not finite; the argument's missing premise, a finite number of states, is not supplied by finite force. Clark adds that every premise but one is a priori, which the mature Nietzsche's view of truth cannot license.

**What a defender may still say.** That the notebook proof is real, dated and repeated seven years apart; that Nietzsche called the thought "the most scientific of all possible hypotheses" (WP 55); and that Heidegger, without defending the proof as physics, reads the doctrine as Nietzsche's fundamental metaphysical position, "the unfolding of a projection" of beings as a whole, so that its being in the notebooks does not make it less than the doctrine. Loeb's recent work holds the literal reading against the practical consensus.

**Why that is an audit, not a refutation.** The defender's claim rests on the Nachlass as ground, which the course's evidence rule does not permit, and it owes an account of why the author withheld the proof from every book and mocked its one staged statement; those are debts to be recorded and weighed, in <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref>, not errors of fact. The practical reading, for its part, needs only what is published, and its claim, that the doctrine's *force* is independent of its truth, is untouched by anything the defender cites: a notebook proof, even a sound one, would not change what GS 341 asks.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive, from the three constraints, why removing "eternity" and asking for a single exact repetition would let a life pass the test that fails the full version. Use Kundera's Agnes as the instance. Then state what the derivation shows the eternity constraint is *for*, and connect it to Reginster's distinction between two wishes.

<Solution>
**The single-repetition test.** Keep recurrence and sameness: the whole life, unedited, once more. A person who can bear the life, who judges it on the whole worth having had, passes: to go through it once more is a finite cost, and the judgment "worth it, on balance" suffices. Agnes, in *Immortality*, would relive her marriage once or twice; her Yes is a Yes to having had it, and to bearing it again within a horizon.

**Why the full test fails her.** Add eternity: "innumerable times", the hourglass turned "again and again". There is no longer a point after which the life is over and done with, so "worth it, on balance" has nothing to be on balance *against*; endurance presupposes an end to endure until, and the constraint removes the end. What can still answer the demon is a Yes to the life as such, not to bearing it; Agnes has the first Yes and not the second, and the full test sorts them where the finite test cannot.

**What the constraint is for.** Not a raising of the bar by repetition, but the removal of a category of answer: conditional affirmation, the Christian's Yes to earthly life "as a test of faith" to be redeemed later, and the reader's Yes to a life that will at least stop. With no "over and done with", the only thing left to affirm is the process, and the only attitude that can affirm a process without end is one that wants it *as* a process.

**Reginster's two wishes.** The lover's "let this never end" wishes eternity as permanence: one perfect state, held, time stopped, which is the ideal of "being" Nietzsche opposes. The demon's "once more and innumerable times" wishes eternity as recurrence: the moment ended and begun again, which is coherent only for a perfection "to which impermanence might actually be essential". Agnes's "once or twice" is a third thing, a bounded return, and it is the attitude of someone who has not revalued becoming and has only priced it. The eternity constraint is the clause that names becoming as the object of the Yes; a life that still wants a final state cannot pass it, which is why the test presupposes the revaluation of suffering derived in <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the previous lesson</Ref> and does not replace it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Boundary probe. Stipulate a person who affirms every moment of their life, regrets nothing in it, and values the whole of it as a means to a future state, a completed work, a redeemed humanity, an overman. On Clark's reading, do they pass the demon's test? State the premise about "life as an end" the reading needs, the passages that supply it ("On Redemption", "The Tomb Song"), and what the nausea of "The Convalescent" shows about the case.

<Solution>
**They fail.** The demon's life recurs "innumerable times" with "nothing new in it": the future state is never reached, or is reached and undone and to be reached again. A person whose Yes to each moment is a Yes to its contribution toward that state has, once the state is removed from the horizon, nothing left to say Yes to; their affirmation of every moment was conditional on an arrival, and the eternity constraint deletes the arrival. Regretting nothing is not enough: the test asks for the whole to be wanted again as what it is, and this person wants it again as what it leads to.

**The premise.** That affirmation is valuing this life *as an end*, "the process of living as an end and not merely as a means" (Clark). Without it the case passes, since a life valued as a means can be wanted again as a means. The premise is P1 of the practical reconstruction and P2 of the previous lesson's "affirmation as the answer to despair"; its ground is GM III:28, where the ascetic ideal saved the will precisely by valuing life as a means to its own negation, and Clark's point is that the recurrence, uncritically imagined, makes that strategy impossible and leaves valuing the process as the only alternative to despair.

**The passages.** "On Redemption" (Z II 20): Zarathustra "would not know how to live" if he were not "a seer of that which must come"; the human being is "a bridge and not an end" (Prologue 4); redemption would be "all 'it was'" recreated "into 'thus I willed it'", which he asks for and has not got, breaking off "terrified". "The Tomb Song" (Z II 11): the "visions and dearest wonders of my youth" murdered by his enemies, "all beings shall be divine to me" lost, endured only through his will, which is to say through what he has created, the overman. The two speeches together show a life whose value is borrowed from a future and whose past is a stone.

**What the nausea shows.** The stipulated person is Zarathustra, and "The Convalescent" is the test run on him. The thought seizes him and the object of his nausea is exact: "the small human being recurs eternally", the thing the overman was to overcome, back again. The nausea is not at the recurrence in general but at the removal of the arrival, which is what the case predicts. Soll's alternative, that each cycle could contain the same progress and so console, does not apply, because Zarathustra does not imagine the recurrence realistically; imagined as the demon requires, "whatever he achieves will come undone". The boundary the case marks is the line between a Yes to the process and a Yes to its result, and the reading's whole content is that only the first is affirmation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Research-shaped. Take three documents for the doctrine's origin: NF-1881,11[141], the letter to Köselitz of 14 August 1881 (BVN-1881,136), and the postcard to his mother of 24 August 1881 (BVN-1881,142). For each give the metadata (kind of document, addressee, date, place), then run records / argues / omits. Then state what *Ecce Homo* III "Zarathustra" §1 (1888) adds to the three, and what the postcard to his mother deflates in it.

<Solution>
**11[141].** A notebook entry in the pocket notebook M III 1, addressed to no one, self-dated "beginning of August 1881 in Sils-Maria, 6000 feet above the sea". *Records:* a title, "The recurrence of the same", a five-part outline in which the thought is the fifth item, "the new weight", and an instruction, "we teach the teaching — it is the strongest means of incorporating it". *Argues:* nothing; there is no argument in the fragment, and the finite-force argument is seven fragments on (11[148]). *Omits:* any statement of the thought's content beyond its name, and any claim about the world. What it establishes: the date, the place, the name, and that the thought's first recorded use was as a practice.

**BVN-1881,136.** A letter, to Heinrich Köselitz in Venice, Sils-Maria, 14 August 1881. *Records:* "on my horizon thoughts have risen the like of which I have never yet seen"; a resolve to "let nothing be heard" of them; "tears of jubilation" on his walks; the fear of being "one of the machines that can explode". *Argues:* nothing. *Omits:* the thought itself, by stated decision. What it establishes: that something happened in early August that its author ranked above everything before it, corroborating the fragment's date without corroborating its content, since the "thoughts" are unnamed.

**BVN-1881,142.** A postcard, to Franziska Nietzsche in Naumburg, Sils-Maria, 24 August 1881. *Records:* "here up above, 6000 feet higher than Genoa, where the snowmelt drags on until June, and in July and August snow falls", a room at 8 degrees Réaumur, "cutting winds", thunderstorms he finds "torturous", a diet, a request for gloves and a notebook. *Argues:* nothing. *Omits:* any thought at all. What it establishes: that "6000 feet" was, in the same month, the writer's ordinary way of naming his altitude to his family, and that the summer was cold, wet and ill.

**What *Ecce Homo* adds.** A scene and a signature, seven years later: the thought "belongs in the August of the year 1881", was "thrown onto a sheet" inscribed "6000 feet beyond man and time", and came to him by "a mighty pyramidal block of stone not far from Surlei", walking by the lake of Silvaplana. It adds the stone, the lake, the day and the phrase "beyond man and time", and it calls the thought "the highest formula of affirmation that is at all attainable". Everything it adds beyond the fragment is retrospective, and the fragment's own inscription reads "6000 feet above the sea and much higher above all human things", not "beyond man and time".

**What the postcard deflates.** The altitude. In *Ecce Homo* "6000 feet" is a figure for standing beyond humanity; on the postcard it is the reason for cold hands and a request for gloves. The three 1881 documents together support the date, the place, the name and the elation; they do not support the scene, and the scene is the part most often retold. The course's rule for *Ecce Homo* applies: a self-description of 1888 is evidence for how its author wanted the thought remembered, and the notebook is evidence for what he wrote.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Dramatization. Map "The Convalescent" (Z III 13) onto GS 341 clause by clause: the seven days' collapse, the nausea, the animals' first song, Zarathustra's "hurdy-gurdy" rebuke, the animals' second catechism with "teacher of the eternal recurrence", and his silence. For each give the counterpart in GS 341 or "none". Then state what the staging adds that GS 341 leaves open, and which reading it undercuts.

<Solution>
**The map.** The collapse "like a dead man" for seven days: GS 341's "if that thought gained power over you, it would transform you as you are, and perhaps crush you" (*verwandeln und vielleicht zermalmen*), the crushing staged. The nausea, "gnashed and lashed day and night": the first reaction, "would you not throw yourself down and gnash your teeth" (*mit den Zähnen knirschen*), with the verb carried over. The animals' first song, "everything goes, everything comes back; the wheel of being rolls eternally": none; GS 341 contains no statement about the world, only the demon's "what if". The rebuke, "barrel organs", "a hurdy-gurdy song": none as text; as function, it is the section's conditional mood enforced against a character who has turned the question into an assertion. The second catechism, "we know what you teach … the great year of becoming … like an hourglass": the hourglass is GS 341's (*die ewige Sanduhr des Daseins*), so the image maps and the "we know what you teach" does not, since GS 341 teaches nothing and asks. "The teacher of the eternal recurrence": none; the section has a demon and a "you", no teacher. The silence, "he did not hear that they were silent": none, and it is the staging's last word on the catechism.

**What the staging adds.** The content of the nausea. GS 341 says "every pain and every joy" and does not say which pain makes the Yes hardest; "The Convalescent" says: "the small human being recurs eternally", the accuser of life, whom the overman was to overcome. That is a content the argued works never supply, and Clark's reading of the failure depends on it. The staging also adds a *sequel* to the crisis, the need to sing, which is the third staging's cue and GS 341's "you are a god" given a form.

**What it undercuts.** Any reading that takes the animals' song as the doctrine: the book has its one indicative cosmology sung by animals, called a hurdy-gurdy song by the only character entitled to teach, and answered with silence, so a reader who cites Z III 13 for the cosmological reading is citing the passage against its stage directions. Under the dramatization rule the animals' catechism is filed as what the drama stages, a doctrine at second hand, and the silence as the text's instruction on how much to make of it.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Forward. GS 341 asks "how well disposed would you have to become to yourself and to life" to long for the demon's confirmation: it names an attitude and not a content. Write, in three or four sentences, what a person would have to be like, in terms of the drives of <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drive lesson</Ref> and the three conditions of <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref>, to answer the demon "you are a god". Then find BGE 56's one-sentence portrait of that person and quote its two operative phrases.

<Solution>
**The person.** A society of drives that are individually strong, many, and in conflict, bound into a unity that does not disown any of them: someone in whom no drive is "complaining about another" (*Daybreak* 109) to the point of wishing its history away, since to wish one drive's work undone is to fail the sameness constraint. Their satisfactions are of the resistance kind, A §2's "feeling that power increases, that a resistance is overcome", so that the suffering in the life is ingredient and not cost, and no final state is wanted, since a final state would end the overcoming; that is what lets the eternity constraint, the wish for becoming, be met rather than merely borne. And they value the process as an end and not as a bridge, which is the condition Zarathustra fails. Janaway's version is the sum: strength, multiplicity and organic unity of the drives, with the affirmative attitude both a symptom of that unity and a practice that produces more of it.

**BGE 56.** The section names "the ideal of the most high-spirited, vital, world-affirming individual, who has learned not just to accept and go along with what was and what is, but who wants it again just as it was and is through all eternity, insatiably shouting *da capo*". The two operative phrases: "wants it again *just as it was and is*", which is the sameness constraint as a wish, and "insatiably shouting *da capo*", "from the top", which is the eternity constraint as a wish, and a wish for the performance to be played again rather than for it to stop. The section adds one more clause, that the wish is directed "not just to himself but to the whole play and performance … to the one who needs precisely this performance – and makes it necessary": the Yes is to the whole, and to oneself as its condition.

**The connection.** BGE 56's sentence describes a type and says nothing about how such a person comes to exist, which drives in what order, or what social conditions produce them. <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs that type from BGE 257–262, *Twilight*'s "Morality as Anti-Nature" and GS 290: the pathos of distance, the spiritualization of the passions, and giving style to one's character are the content the demon's attitude and BGE 56's portrait leave open.
</Solution>

In eight numbered sections of Twilight of the Idols Nietzsche turns his psychology of drives, consciousness and the will on morality and religion, and finds that both rest on four mistakes about cause and effect. The chapter is called "The Four Great Errors", and it sits in *Twilight of the Idols* (TI, written in the summer of 1888, published in January 1889) right after "Morality as Anti-Nature", whose fifth section <Ref lesson="sublimation" id="no-standpoint-outside-life">Sublimation</Ref> read: a condemnation of life is a symptom of the kind of life that pronounces it.

The charge is wider than it looks. A moral sentence seems to make no causal claim at all. "Be temperate", "love your neighbour", "you are guilty" are commands and verdicts, and a command cannot confuse a cause with an effect the way a bad medical theory can. Yet the first section says of the error of confusing cause and effect that "every single sentence which religion and morality formulate contains it". If that is right, morality is not only a set of commands. It carries a hidden theory of how human beings work: what makes a person happy, what makes him feel guilty, what stands behind his acts. And a theory of that kind can be tested against a psychology.

The module has built one. A person is <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">a society of drives</Ref> in an order of rank; consciousness is <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">a surface written in the herd's words</Ref>; the free doer behind the deed is <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">a fiction</Ref>; every drive wants <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">to overcome resistance</Ref>; a drive can be <Ref lesson="sublimation">spiritualized</Ref> instead of cut out. Reading the chapter well means two things at once. For each of the four errors, find which of these results exposes it, and check that the result really does. Then mark the places where the chapter claims more than the module established.

## The template: Cornaro's diet (sections 1 and 2)

The chapter opens with a book about eating.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 1, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The error of confusing cause and effect. There is no more dangerous error than that of mistaking the effect for the cause: I call it the real corruption of reason. Yet this error belongs among the most ancient and recent habits of mankind: it is even hallowed among us and goes by the name of "religion" or "morality." Every single sentence which religion and morality formulate contains it; priests and legislators of moral codes are the originators of this corruption of reason.

I give an example. Everybody knows the book of the famous Cornaro in which he recommends his slender diet as a recipe for a long and happy life — a virtuous one too. Few books have been read so much; even now thousands of copies are sold in England every year. I do not doubt that scarcely any book (except the Bible, as is meet) has done as much harm, has shortened as many lives, as this well-intentioned curiosum. The reason: the mistaking of the effect for the cause. The worthy Italian thought his diet was the cause of his long life, whereas the precondition for a long life, the extraordinary slowness of his metabolism, the consumption of so little, was the cause of his slender diet. He was not free to eat little or much; his frugality was not a matter of "free will": he became sick when he ate more. But whoever is no carp not only does well to eat properly, but needs to. A scholar in our time, with his rapid consumption of nervous energy, would simply destroy himself with Cornaro's diet. *Crede experto.*
</Quote>

### What the worthy Italian got backwards

Alvise Cornaro was a Venetian nobleman who lived in Padua and died in 1566, at an age he put in the nineties. Near forty, his health broken, he took his doctors' advice and cut his food to about twelve ounces a day; his *Discourses on the Sober Life* (the first part printed in 1558) told the world that this ration had given him a long, healthy and virtuous old age. Translated as a guide to long life, it was still a steady seller in Nietzsche's England.

Cornaro saw two facts that went together, a slender diet and a long life, and read the first as the cause of the second. Nietzsche keeps both facts and adds a third that explains them: a slow metabolism. A body that burns little needs little, so it eats little; a body that burns little wears out slowly, so it lives long. On this account the diet does not cause the long life at all. Both are effects of one constitution, and the proof Cornaro offered, his own old age, is equally well explained without his recipe.

That is why the book is dangerous and not only wrong. Prescribed to everyone, the recipe hands a slow body's diet to fast bodies. The *carp*, a fish proverbial in Nietzsche's day for living a century on almost nothing, can live on Cornaro's ration; the scholar who spends nervous energy at a great rate cannot. *Crede experto*, Latin for "believe one who has tried": the chronically ill author speaks from his own trials of diet.

One clause looks ahead. "He was not free to eat little or much": Cornaro's frugality, which he took for his virtue, was what his body demanded. The first example of the error already carries the fourth error, free will, inside it.

### The moral formula turned round

Section 2 shows why a diet book belongs in a chapter about morality. Cornaro's recipe has the shape of every moral recipe.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 2, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The most general formula on which every religion and morality is founded is: "Do this and that, refrain from this and that — then you will be happy! Otherwise …" Every morality, every religion, is this imperative; I call it the great original sin of reason, the immortal unreason. In my mouth, this formula is changed into its opposite — first example of my "revaluation of all values": a well-turned-out human being, a "happy one," must perform certain actions and shrinks instinctively from other actions; he carries the order, which he represents physiologically, into his relations with other human beings and things. In a formula: his virtue is the effect of his happiness. A long life, many descendants — this is not the wages of virtue; rather virtue itself is that slowing down of the metabolism which leads, among other things, also to a long life, many descendants — in short, to Cornarism.
</Quote>

This answers the opening's puzzle about commands. A moral imperative comes with a promise attached, "then you will be happy", and the promise is a causal claim: these acts produce that condition. Nietzsche keeps the correlation the promise rests on. Virtuous people often are happy, as frugal Cornaro was long-lived. He reverses the arrow. Both the conduct and the well-being flow from what the person already is.

"A well-turned-out human being" renders *ein wohlgeratner Mensch*, the opposite of the *Missratenen*, the ill-constituted, whom <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology" id="the-definition-in-the-last-books">will to power</Ref> met in *The Antichrist*'s definition of the good. So "happy" here is not a mood. It is A 2's happiness, "the feeling that power is growing", in a person built to have it. "The order, which he represents physiologically" is the order of rank among his drives that <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="the-commonwealth-strong-drives-in-an-order-of-rank">the commonwealth of drives</Ref> made a person's unity; "physiologically" is the 1888 word for what a person is below his opinions. A person so ordered "must" act in certain ways, as Cornaro had to eat little. And the canon of "shalt" and "shalt not" that such a person follows is what "Anti-Nature" 4 called a <Ref lesson="sublimation" id="healthy-and-anti-natural-morality">healthy morality</Ref>: rules in the service of a drive, the athlete's training rules, which express his strength and do not produce it.

<KeyFact>
Section 2 calls the reversal the "first example" of the revaluation of all values: the facts stay, the arrow turns. Which arrow is right is an empirical question, and the two readings predict differently. If virtue causes happiness, imposing the conduct on anyone should make him happier; if both flow from the type, the conduct imposed on the wrong type should fail or harm, as Cornaro's diet would ruin the scholar.
</KeyFact>

### Peoples, a pale young man, a party

The section goes on to apply the reversal three more times, and the applications are where its harder claims sit.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 2, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The church and morality say: "A generation, a people, are destroyed by license and luxury." My recovered reason says: when a people approaches destruction, when it degenerates physiologically, then license and luxury follow from this (namely, the craving for ever stronger and more frequent stimulation, as every exhausted nature knows it). This young man turns pale early and wilts; his friends say: that is due to this or that disease. I say: that he became diseased, that he did not resist the disease, was already the effect of an impoverished life or hereditary exhaustion. The newspaper reader says: this party destroys itself by making such a mistake. My higher politics says: a party which makes such mistakes has reached its end; it has lost its sureness of instinct. Every mistake in every sense is the effect of the degeneration of instinct, of the disintegration of the will: one could almost define what is bad in this way. All that is good is instinct — and hence easy, necessary, free. Laboriousness is an objection; the god is typically different from the hero. (In my language: *light feet* are the first attribute of divinity.)
</Quote>

The first of the three you have already rewritten, in the last exercise of <Ref lesson="sublimation">sublimation</Ref>: luxury as the craving of an exhausted nature for stronger stimuli, the decline as cause. The other two have the same form. The young man's disease is real, but that he caught it and could not throw it off says something about his constitution before the disease: "an impoverished life or hereditary exhaustion". The party's blunder did not ruin it; that it blundered shows it was finished.

The words carry their 1888 sense, and the sense is meant. To *degenerate* (*entarten*) is to lose the strength of one's kind; "the disintegration of the will" is the <Ref lesson="nihilism" id="decadence-the-weakness-behind-passive-nihilism">decadence</Ref> whose formula was "to have to fight the instincts", and "Anti-Nature" 2 had already found in the degenerate those who cannot <Ref lesson="sublimation" id="moderation-and-the-inability-not-to-respond">impose moderation</Ref> on a drive. An *instinct* is a drive acting without the detour through consciousness that <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">consciousness and language</Ref> described. So "all that is good is instinct, and hence easy, necessary, free" says that what a well-ordered person does well, he does without deliberating, as a skilled pianist does not decide each finger. Effort, the hero's labour against his own reluctance, is a sign that the drives are not in order. "Light feet" is the phrase with which <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner" id="sections-12-carmen-as-the-control-case">*The Case of Wagner*</Ref> praised *Carmen*: "everything divine runs with light feet".

Here the chapter already reaches past the module. The psychology of drives was a psychology of persons: a person's unity is an order among his drives, and a person declines when that order falls apart. Section 2 extends it without comment to peoples and to parties. Whether a people has an order of drives, or a "sureness of instinct", in any sense beyond metaphor is asserted and not derived.

The section also makes an assumption about persons that the module did not test: that a person's constitution fixes his conduct, as Cornaro's metabolism fixed his diet. That is an empirical claim about types, and the starred lesson <Ref lesson="drive-psychology-and-experiment">Nietzsche's psychology and the experimental record</Ref> sets it beside what twin studies and experiments on self-explanation have found.

## The second error: where our idea of a cause came from (section 3)

The first error took for granted that we know what a cause is and only misplace it. Section 3 asks where the idea came from in the first place, and its answer turns the module's analysis of the will into an account of how the whole world came to be seen as made of agents.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 3, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The error of a false causality. People have believed at all times that they knew what a cause is; but whence did we take our knowledge — or more precisely, our faith that we had such knowledge? From the realm of the famous "inner facts," of which not a single one has so far proved to be factual. We believed ourselves to be causal in the act of willing: we thought that here at least we caught causality in the act. Nor did one doubt that all the antecedents of an act, its causes, were to be sought in consciousness and would be found there once sought — as "motives": else one would not have been free and responsible for it. Finally, who would have denied that a thought is caused? that the ego causes the thought?

Of these three "inward facts" which seem to guarantee causality, the first and most persuasive is that of the will as cause. The conception of a consciousness ("spirit") as a cause, and later also that of the ego as cause (the "subject"), are only afterbirths: first the causality of the will was firmly accepted as given, as empirical.
</Quote>

### Three inner facts, three module results

The *inner facts* are the three things introspection seems to show directly: that my willing moves my arm, that my motives are there in consciousness to be found, and that "I" think my thoughts. Each was taken apart earlier in the module, and section 3 compresses those results into three sentences.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 3, trans. W. Kaufmann">
<Original lang="de">
Der Wille bewegt nichts mehr, erklärt folglich auch nichts mehr – er begleitet bloß Vorgänge, er kann auch fehlen.
</Original>

Meanwhile we have thought better of it. Today we no longer believe a word of all this. The "inner world" is full of phantoms and will-o'-the-wisps: the will is one of them. The will no longer moves anything, hence does not explain anything either — it merely accompanies events; it can also be absent. The so-called motive: another error. Merely a surface phenomenon of consciousness, something alongside the deed that is more likely to cover up the antecedents of the deeds than to represent them. And as for the ego! That has become a fable, a fiction, a play on words: it has altogether ceased to think, feel, or will!
</Quote>

*The will.* <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="three-ingredients-and-one-mistake">BGE 19</Ref> found that an act of will is a complex of sensations, a commanding thought and the affect of command, and that the willer mistakes the obeying drives' work for the success of his willing. *The motive.* <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="what-deliberation-observes">D 129</Ref> found that deliberation sees a comparison of consequences while the contest that decides is "quite invisible"; in <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="a-match-versus-a-ton-of-powder">GS 360</Ref>'s image the motive one can name is the helmsman, not the steam, and here it becomes "a surface phenomenon of consciousness", the surface <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">consciousness and language</Ref> described. *The ego.* The "I" was, in <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="who-says-i-the-commonwealth-and-its-governing-class">the commonwealth of drives</Ref>, the title held by whichever drive governs, and in <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="the-lightning-and-its-flash-a-doer-added-to-the-deed">GM I 13</Ref> the doer "added to the deed" by grammar. Section 3's "fable, a fiction, a play on words" is that conclusion in its sharpest form.

### From the ego to God

Then the section draws the consequence the module had not drawn: if our idea of cause came from these inner facts, every cause we see outside ourselves inherits their error.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 3, trans. W. Kaufmann">
What follows from this? There are no mental causes at all. The whole of the allegedly empirical evidence for that has gone to the devil. That is what follows! And what a fine abuse we had perpetrated with this "empirical evidence"; we created the world on this basis as a world of causes, a world of will, a world of spirits. The most ancient and enduring psychology was at work here and did not do anything else: all that happened was considered a doing, all doing the effect of a will; the world became to it a multiplicity of doers; a doer (a "subject") was slipped under all that happened. It was out of himself that man projected his three "inner facts" — that in which he believed most firmly, the will, the spirit, the ego. He even took the concept of being from the concept of the ego; he posited "things" as "being," in his image, in accordance with his concept of the ego as a cause. Small wonder that later he always found in things only that which he had put into them. The thing itself, to say it once more, the concept of thing is a mere reflex of the faith in the ego as cause. And even your atom, my dear mechanists and physicists — how much error, how much rudimentary psychology is still residual in your atom! Not to mention the "thing-in-itself," the *horrendum pudendum* of the metaphysicians! The error of the spirit as cause mistaken for reality! And made the very measure of reality! And called God!
</Quote>

The steps are these. A person believed he caught causation in the act when he willed and his arm moved. He then read every event on that model: the storm was someone's anger, the plague someone's punishment, "a doer slipped under all that happened". As the gods retreated, the doer stayed in thinner form: a "thing" is something that stays the same under its changing effects, as the ego seemed to stay the same under its acts, and the atom is the physicist's last such thing, the lump that <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">Boscovich</Ref> had already dissolved into points of force. The "thing-in-itself", the thing as it is apart from all its appearances, gets the Latin tag *horrendum pudendum*, "something horrible and shameful". At the top of the series stands a spirit that causes the world by willing it: God.

<Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">Consciousness and language</Ref> ended on a sentence of the same book: "I am afraid we are not rid of God because we still have faith in grammar" (TI, "'Reason' in Philosophy" 5). Section 3 gives the psychology behind that faith: the subject of the sentence is the ego projected, and God is the last subject. <Ref lesson="reason-in-philosophy">Reason in philosophy</Ref> reads that chapter whole.

### "No mental causes at all": stronger than the module

One sentence of section 3 goes further than any earlier lesson went. "The will no longer moves anything … it merely accompanies events; it can also be absent", and "there are no mental causes at all". <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="does-the-conscious-will-cause-anything">The doer behind the deed</Ref> ended on a fork over exactly this. On one branch, conscious willing is an accompaniment, a story told alongside a process that would run the same without it. On the other, it is real and effective, as the command of a governing drive rather than the choice of a free subject: BGE 19's command is often obeyed, and <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="strong-and-weak-will-without-a-free-will">BGE 21</Ref> keeps "strong and weak wills". *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886) sat on the second branch. Section 3, two years later, puts *Twilight* squarely on the first.

There is a gentler reading. On it, section 3 denies only that a mind made of some other stuff than the body pushes the body around, and leaves untouched the idea that conscious thought, as one bodily process among others, makes a difference. The words do not support it. "It can also be absent" says that the will is not needed where it appears, which is more than a rejection of mind–body dualism; and "no mental causes at all" has no qualifier. The strong reading is the one the text states.

<KeyFact>
The chapter needs much less than it says. Its argument against free will needs only that no will stands outside the drives as the self-made cause free will requires; the module showed that. "There are no mental causes at all" goes beyond that and beyond BGE 19 and 21, and section 3 asserts it ("we have thought better of it") without an argument. Nietzsche never reconciled the two positions in print.
</KeyFact>

## The third error: how a cause gets invented (sections 4 to 6)

Section 3 said what we wrongly take for causes. Sections 4 to 6 explain how a wrong cause gets supplied at all, and why the mind accepts it.

### The cannon shot: a cause slipped in afterwards

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 4, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The error of imaginary causes. To begin with dreams: ex post facto, a cause is slipped under a particular sensation (for example, one following a far-off cannon shot) — often a whole little novel in which the dreamer turns up as the protagonist. The sensation endures meanwhile in a kind of resonance: it waits, as it were, until the causal instinct permits it to step into the foreground — now no longer as a chance occurrence, but as "meaning." The cannon shot appears in a causal mode, in an apparent reversal of time. What is really later, the motivation, is experienced first — often with a hundred details which pass like lightning — and the shot follows. What has happened? The representations which were produced by a certain state have been misunderstood as its causes.

In fact, we do the same thing when awake. Most of our general feelings — every kind of inhibition, pressure, tension, and explosion in the play and counterplay of our organs, and particularly the state of the *nervus sympathicus* — excite our causal instinct: we want to have a reason for feeling this way or that — for feeling bad or for feeling good. We are never satisfied merely to state the fact that we feel this way or that: we admit this fact only — become conscious of it only — when we have furnished some kind of motivation. Memory, which swings into action in such cases, unknown to us, brings up earlier states of the same kind, together with the causal interpretations associated with them — not their real causes. The faith, to be sure, that such representations, such accompanying conscious processes, are the causes, is also brought forth by memory. Thus originates a habitual acceptance of a particular causal interpretation, which, as a matter of fact, inhibits any investigation into the real cause — even precludes it.
</Quote>

The dream comes first because in it the order of events can be checked. A gun fires far away. The sleeper dreams a story, a quarrel, a duel, an execution, which ends in the shot, and wakes with the story as the shot's cause. But the sound came first: it was the shot that called up the story. The story was produced to fit the sensation and then experienced as leading up to it, "an apparent reversal of time".

<Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language" id="a-commentary-on-a-felt-text">Consciousness and language</Ref> read *Daybreak*'s dream theory, in which the same bodily stimuli get a different story each night depending on which drive is hungry, and its extension to waking life: "a more or less fantastic commentary on an unknown, perhaps unknowable, but felt text". Section 4 adds two things to that account. The first is the order in time: the commentary comes after the text and is felt as coming before it. The second is memory. A feeling arrives, and memory supplies not its real cause but the explanation that was given last time, so the old interpretation repeats itself and "precludes" any search for the real cause.

A notebook of spring 1888, unpublished, has the draft, with an example that makes the second point plain:

<Quote source="Notebook 15, spring 1888, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge, pp. 270–271">
In the phenomenalism of the 'inner world' we invert the chronology of cause and effect. The fundamental fact of 'inner experience' is that the cause is imagined after the effect has taken place … e.g., 'I am unwell' — a judgement like this presupposes a great and late-attained neutrality on the part of the observer: the naive man always says 'This or that makes me unwell' — his being unwell only becomes clear to him once he sees a reason for being unwell …
</Quote>

You wake with a heavy, anxious feeling, the residue of a bad night. Before you have noticed the feeling as such, it has a reason: the meeting at ten, the remark you made yesterday. The reason enters consciousness together with the feeling, so it seems to have caused it. To say only "I feel bad" and leave the cause open is, on the note's account, a late and difficult achievement.

### Any explanation is better than none

Why should the mind supply a false cause at all, rather than wait for the true one? Section 5 answers with the drives.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 5, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The psychological explanation of this. To derive something unknown from something familiar relieves, comforts, and satisfies, besides giving a feeling of power. With the unknown, one is confronted with danger, discomfort, and care; the first instinct is to abolish these painful states. First principle: any explanation is better than none. Since at bottom it is merely a matter of wishing to be rid of oppressive representations, one is not too particular about the means of getting rid of them: the first representation that explains the unknown as familiar feels so good that one "considers it true." The proof of pleasure ("of strength") as a criterion of truth.

The causal instinct is thus conditional upon, and excited by, the feeling of fear. The "why?" shall, if at all possible, not give the cause for its own sake so much as for a particular kind of cause — a cause that is comforting, liberating, and relieving. That it is something already familiar, experienced, and inscribed in the memory, which is posited as a cause, that is the first consequence of this need. That which is new and strange and has not been experienced before, is excluded as a cause. Thus one searches not only for some kind of explanation to serve as a cause, but for a particularly selected and preferred kind of explanation — that which has most quickly and most frequently abolished the feeling of the strange, new, and hitherto unexperienced: the most habitual explanations. Consequence: one kind of positing of causes predominates more and more, is concentrated into a system, and finally emerges as dominant, that is, as simply precluding other causes and explanations. The banker immediately thinks of "business," the Christian of "sin," and the girl of her love.
</Quote>

The argument runs in five steps:

1. Something unexplained is felt as a danger, and the fear rouses a drive to explain it, the *causal instinct*.
2. What the drive wants is relief, so the first explanation that turns the strange into the familiar satisfies it, true or not.
3. The relief, together with the feeling of having mastered the unknown, is taken as the mark of truth: "the proof of pleasure ('of strength') as a criterion of truth".
4. The explanation that has brought relief most often is reached for first next time, so one kind of explanation wins more and more cases.
5. It hardens into a system that excludes every other: business for the banker, sin for the Christian, love for the girl.

Two module results meet here. Step 1 is <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language" id="the-familiar-is-not-the-known">GS 355</Ref>, read in that lesson: what people call knowing is reducing the strange to the familiar, driven by "the instinct of fear", and the familiar is precisely what is hardest to know. Step 3 is <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology" id="power-means-resistance-overcome">will to power</Ref>: the unknown is a resistance, the explanation overcomes it, and A 2's "feeling that power is growing" comes with the overcoming whether or not the explanation is true. That is why a comforting explanation can feel more certain than a correct but disquieting one.

### Morality and religion as a false dialect

Section 6 applies the mechanism to the explanations morality and religion give of how we feel.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 6, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The whole realm of morality and religion belongs under this concept of imaginary causes. The "explanation" of disagreeable general feelings. They are produced by beings that are hostile to us (evil spirits: the most famous case — the misunderstanding of the hysterical as witches). They are produced by acts which cannot be approved (the feeling of "sin," of "sinfulness," is slipped under a physiological discomfort; one always finds reasons for being dissatisfied with oneself). They are produced as punishments, as payment for something we should not have done, for what we should not have been (impudently generalized by Schopenhauer into a principle in which morality appears as what it really is — as the very poisoner and slanderer of life: "Every great pain, whether physical or spiritual, declares what we deserve; for it could not come to us if we did not deserve it." *World as Will and Representation* II, 666). They are produced as effects of ill-considered actions that turn out badly. (Here the affects, the senses, are posited as causes, as "guilty"; and physiological calamities are interpreted with the help of other calamities as "deserved.")

The "explanation" of agreeable general feelings. They are produced by trust in God. They are produced by the consciousness of good deeds (the so-called "good conscience" — a physiological state which at times looks so much like good digestion that it is hard to tell them apart). They are produced by the successful termination of some enterprise (a naive fallacy: the successful termination of some enterprise does not by any means give a hypochondriac or a Pascal agreeable general feelings). They are produced by faith, charity, and hope — the Christian virtues.

In truth, all these supposed explanations are resultant states and, as it were, translations of pleasurable or unpleasurable feelings into a false dialect: one is in a state of hope because the basic physiological feeling is once again strong and rich; one trusts in God because the feeling of fullness and strength gives a sense of rest. Morality and religion belong altogether to the psychology of error: in every single case, cause and effect are confused; or truth is confused with the effects of believing something to be true; or a state of consciousness is confused with its causes.
</Quote>

The *general feelings* (*Allgemeingefühle*) are the background states of the whole body: heaviness or lightness, unease or wellbeing, without a particular place. The section lists the explanations religion and morality give of them and reads every one as a story supplied after the fact. The hard cases are meant as stated. The women burned as witches in the trials of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries were, on this account, people whose bodily disorders were explained by a familiar cause, the devil; "hysterical" is the period's medical word, and the *Genealogy* adds that "the 'witches' themselves did not doubt" their guilt, "and yet there was no guilt" (GM III 16). The good conscience, the believer's calm after a good deed, is a physiological state close to good digestion. Blaise Pascal, the seventeenth-century French mathematician who became a severe Christian ascetic, could finish any enterprise and still not feel well, because his feeling did not come from his enterprises.

<Example title="One low mood, explained twice">
**The state.** For three weeks after a long fever a woman feels heavy, joyless and irritable. The cause is the body's slow recovery.

**The moral explanation.** The feeling looks for a reason, and memory supplies the familiar ones (section 4). She has been short with her mother; she skipped church; she has been thinking only of herself. The feeling now has a cause, sin, and comes with a remedy, repentance. The explanation relieves (section 5): an unexplained weight has become a debt that can be paid. "One always finds reasons for being dissatisfied with oneself."

**The recovery.** In the fourth week strength returns. She feels light, hopeful, kindly. That too gets a reason: she has been forgiven; God's grace is at work; her good resolutions are paying off. "One is in a state of hope because the basic physiological feeling is once again strong and rich."

**What section 6 says happened.** The physiological state came first both times. The guilt and the grace were "translations of pleasurable or unpleasurable feelings into a false dialect", and the translation was taken for the cause of what it translated.
</Example>

The word "dialect" places the section inside the module. A dialect is a shared way of speaking, and <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">consciousness and language</Ref> showed that a state becomes conscious only by taking the form of shared signs. Sin, guilt, punishment, grace and good conscience are the shared vocabulary in which a Christian culture lets its members become conscious of what they feel; section 4's "we admit this fact only, become conscious of it only, when we have furnished some kind of motivation" is the same claim. "False" is section 6's addition: this dialect does not only flatten the feeling, as every shared vocabulary does, it assigns it a wrong cause. And the cause it assigns is one that calls for judgment.

The *Genealogy* draws the consequence that matters most for what comes later. "'Someone or other must be to blame for my feeling ill' — this kind of reasoning is common to all the sick, and is indeed held the more firmly the more the real cause of their feeling ill, the physiological cause, remains hidden" (GM III 15). The priest, on that account, tells the sufferer who is to blame: he himself. How that turns the sufferer's pain into guilt is <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the ascetic priest</Ref>.

<KeyFact>
Section 6 ends by naming three confusions, and they are the chapter's errors in small: cause taken for effect (the first error), "truth … confused with the effects of believing something to be true" (the proof of pleasure, section 5), and "a state of consciousness … confused with its causes" (the second and third errors). Morality and religion, on this account, commit all of them in their account of how we feel.
</KeyFact>

## The fourth error: free will, invented for punishment (section 7)

The first three errors describe ways a cause gets misread. The fourth asks why one misreading, the free will behind the act, was held onto so tightly.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 7, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The error of free will. Today we no longer have any pity for the concept of "free will": we know only too well what it really is — the foulest of all theologians' artifices, aimed at making mankind "responsible" in their sense, that is, dependent upon them. Here I simply supply the psychology of all making responsible.

Wherever responsibilities are sought, it is usually the instinct of wanting to judge and punish which is at work. Becoming has been deprived of its innocence when any being-such-and-such is traced back to will, to purposes, to acts of responsibility: the doctrine of the will has been invented essentially for the purpose of punishment, that is, because one wanted to impute guilt. The entire old psychology, the psychology of will, was conditioned by the fact that its originators, the priests at the head of ancient communities, wanted to create for themselves the right to punish — or wanted to create this right for God. Men were considered "free" so that they might be judged and punished — so that they might become guilty: consequently, every act had to be considered as willed, and the origin of every act had to be considered as lying within the consciousness (and thus the most fundamental counterfeit *in psychologicis* was made the principle of psychology itself).

Today, as we have entered into the reverse movement and we immoralists are trying with all our strength to take the concept of guilt and the concept of punishment out of the world again, and to cleanse psychology, history, nature, and social institutions and sanctions of them, there is in our eyes no more radical opposition than that of the theologians, who continue with the concept of a "moral world-order" to infect the innocence of becoming by means of "punishment" and "guilt." Christianity is a metaphysics of the hangman.
</Quote>

### The psychology of making responsible

<Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">The doer behind the deed</Ref> showed what retributive blame needs: a self behind the act, ultimately responsible for it, which the *causa sui* argument shows cannot exist. Section 7 asks the next question. If the doctrine has no support, why has it been believed so firmly? Its answer is the use the doctrine serves. Whoever wants to punish needs someone who deserves it, and only a person who could have done otherwise deserves it. So the wish to punish goes looking for freedom, and finds it everywhere: "every act had to be considered as willed".

Who wanted the right to punish? "The priests at the head of ancient communities", for themselves "or … for God". The *Genealogy*'s lambs needed the fiction for a different purpose, to make the bird of prey blameable and their own weakness a merit (GM I 13, as read in <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="why-the-weak-need-the-fiction">the doer behind the deed</Ref>), and *Daybreak* had given free will yet another parentage: the doctrine "has human pride and feeling of power for its father and mother" (D 128, 1881). The three accounts do not compete. Each names a drive that profits from the belief, and a belief that serves several drives at once has every reason to last.

### Old punishment, late freedom

Section 7 seems to contradict a text the module already read. <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="two-things-a-punishment-can-be">GM II 4</Ref> says that "punishment, as requital, evolved quite independently of any presupposition concerning freedom or nonfreedom of the will", and that "he could have acted differently" is "an extremely late and subtle form of human judgment". How can free will be invented for punishment if punishment is older than free will?

The texts allow one reconciliation, and it is the natural one. Punishing is old: anger vented on the one who did harm, measured by the idea that pain can pay for injury. What was invented was not punishment but its justification as *desert*, the claim that the punished man has earned his suffering because the act was up to him. That is <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="two-things-a-punishment-can-be">retributive blame</Ref>, and on GM II 4 it is late. Section 7's "invented essentially for the purpose of punishment" then means: invented so that punishment could claim to be just. The priests' contribution was the right to punish, not the practice.

### The hangman and the innocence of becoming

"Christianity is a metaphysics of the hangman" is the chapter's most quoted sentence, and its meaning is in the clause before it. A *moral world-order* is the belief that the world itself is arranged by a judge, so that suffering is punishment and fortune reward; it is Schopenhauer's "every great pain declares what we deserve" made into a cosmology. A metaphysics of the hangman is a picture of the whole of reality built around the executioner's job.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 7, trans. W. Kaufmann">
<Original lang="de">
… gibt es in unsern Augen keine radikalere Gegnerschaft als die der Theologen, welche fortfahren, mit dem Begriff der »sittlichen Weltordnung« die Unschuld des Werdens durch »Strafe« und »Schuld« zu durchseuchen. Das Christentum ist eine Metaphysik des Henkers…
</Original>

… there is in our eyes no more radical opposition than that of the theologians, who continue with the concept of a "moral world-order" to infect the innocence of becoming by means of "punishment" and "guilt." Christianity is a metaphysics of the hangman.
</Quote>

The sentence cuts against the popular image of the "immoralist" as someone who wants more harshness. Its aim, stated in the same breath, is "to take the concept of guilt and the concept of punishment out of the world again". The immoralist wants less punishing, and above all no punishing that claims to be deserved.

<Remark title="The innocence of becoming">
The *innocence of becoming* (*Unschuld des Werdens*) is the condition of events, human acts included, when they are not traced back to a will, a purpose or a responsible agent: becoming freed from the categories of guilt and purpose. It is not moral innocence, the condition of someone who did nothing wrong; it is the absence of any standpoint from which "wrong" in the sense of deserved guilt could be assigned. Section 7 says the doctrine of the will took it away; section 8 says how it is "restored".
</Remark>

## Nothing besides the whole (section 8)

The four errors have been named. The last section asks what doctrine is left when they are removed.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 8, trans. W. Kaufmann">
What alone can be our doctrine? That no one gives man his qualities — neither God, nor society, nor his parents and ancestors, nor he himself. (The nonsense of the last idea was taught as "intelligible freedom" by Kant — perhaps by Plato already.) No one is responsible for man's being there at all, for his being such-and-such, or for his being in these circumstances or in this environment. The fatality of his essence is not to be disentangled from the fatality of all that has been and will be. Man is not the effect of some special purpose, of a will, and end; nor is he the object of an attempt to attain an "ideal of humanity" or an "ideal of happiness" or an "ideal of morality." It is absurd to wish to devolve one's essence on some end or other. We have invented the concept of "end": in reality there is no end.

One is necessary, one is a piece of fatefulness, one belongs to the whole, one is in the whole; there is nothing which could judge, measure, compare, or sentence our being, for that would mean judging, measuring, comparing, or sentencing the whole. But there is nothing besides the whole. That nobody is held responsible any longer, that the mode of being may not be traced back to a *causa prima*, that the world does not form a unity either as a sensorium or as "spirit" — that alone is the great liberation; with this alone is the innocence of becoming restored. The concept of "God" was until now the greatest objection to existence. We deny God, we deny the responsibility in God: only thereby do we redeem the world.
</Quote>

### No one gives man his qualities

The first sentence lists four candidates for the giver of a person's qualities and rejects each. God goes because a person is not the effect of anyone's purpose. Society and parents go too, and this is the surprise: they plainly *cause* much of what a person is. What section 8 denies them is not causal influence but *responsibility*, the standing of an agent who made the person on purpose and answers for the result. Parents did not choose the child they got, any more than the child did. The last candidate, "he himself", is the <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="running-the-regress-on-the-striker">*causa sui*</Ref> of BGE 21; the "intelligible freedom" attributed to Immanuel Kant, the eighteenth-century German philosopher, is the timeless self-choosing self that Schopenhauer took from him and that HH 39 refused in <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="the-four-stage-retreat">the four-stage retreat</Ref>.

"The fatality of his essence" does not mean a life foretold. It means that what a person is belongs to the same web of necessity as everything else that has been and will be, one strand among the rest, with no point at which a responsible author could be found. Nor does it mean that a person is a purpose's failure or success: "we have invented the concept of 'end'".

"No one is responsible" cancels more than guilt. If no one gives a person his qualities, no one earns them either, so merit goes with guilt: Cornaro's frugality, which he took for his virtue, was no more to his credit than the scholar's appetite is to his blame. Section 2 already said as much in its own terms: if "his virtue is the effect of his happiness", virtue is what a well-turned-out type does, not a prize he won. What survives is the admiration that <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="what-stands">the doer behind the deed</Ref> found HH 107 leaving standing: one "can admire strength, beauty, abundance, in themselves; but must find no merit therein". The well-turned-out human being of section 2 can be admired as a fine type is admired, the way one admires a racehorse, and ranked above an exhausted one. What can no longer be said is that he deserves his happiness.

### Why the whole cannot be judged

The second paragraph runs as an argument:

1. A person is one part of the whole and inseparable from it.
2. To judge, measure or sentence the part is therefore to judge the whole.
3. To judge the whole, something would have to stand outside it.
4. Nothing stands outside it.
5. So nothing can judge, measure or sentence our being.

Step 4 is where God was. A creator outside the world, who made it for a purpose and judges it by that purpose, is exactly the outside standpoint the argument denies. That is why "the concept of 'God' was until now the greatest objection to existence": a world measured against its author's intention can fall short, and on <Ref lesson="nihilism" id="the-hidden-premise-values-that-measured-this-world-by-another">the account of nihilism</Ref> the highest values did just that, measuring this world by an invented one and finding it wanting. The argument is also "Anti-Nature" 5's, now applied to existence rather than to moralities: no one has <Ref lesson="sublimation" id="no-standpoint-outside-life">a standpoint outside life</Ref> from which to judge its value.

<Quote source="TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 8, trans. W. Kaufmann">
<Original lang="de">
Der Begriff »Gott« war bisher der größte Einwand gegen das Dasein... Wir leugnen Gott, wir leugnen die Verantwortlichkeit in Gott: damit erst erlösen wir die Welt.
</Original>

The concept of "God" was until now the greatest objection to existence. We deny God, we deny the responsibility in God: only thereby do we redeem the world.
</Quote>

*Erlösen*, "to redeem", is the word of Christian redemption, *Erlösung*, the word <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner" id="the-postscripts-salvation-from-the-saviour">*The Case of Wagner*</Ref> mocked in "Salvation from the Saviour". Here it is turned round. The world is redeemed not by a saviour who pays its debt but by the discovery that it owes none, because no one is responsible for it, God included.

### Where section 8 reaches past the module

Everything up to "no one is responsible" follows from the module: the *causa sui* argument, the drive-self, the doer added to the deed. The innocence of becoming follows too, since it is the same sentence seen from the side of events: if no one is responsible, no event can be traced back to a guilty will. The last sentence says more. "Only thereby do we redeem the world" treats a world that cannot be condemned as a world that has been affirmed, and that step needs a premise the chapter never states: that the verdict of guilt was the only thing standing between a person and a yes to the world.

The premise is not obvious. Take away the judge and the world's suffering stays where it was; it is only that no one deserved it. A person can grant that no one is to blame for his illness, his losses or his century, and still find the whole not worth wanting. A world beyond judgment could just as well be a world one merely puts up with, or turns away from without condemning. The module gave no grounds for choosing the yes, and the chapter supplies none. Turning the innocence of becoming into a yes to the whole is the work of <Ref lesson="amor-fati">amor fati</Ref> and <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">eternal recurrence</Ref>.

## The strongest objection: a chapter that saws off its own branch

A serious critic now turns the chapter on itself, with two charges.

The first: the chapter is self-undermining. Section 3 says there are no mental causes at all. Sections 5 to 7 then explain beliefs by fear, relief, the feeling of power, the wish to punish and the priests' wish for authority, every one of them a mental cause. Either section 3 is false or sections 5 to 7 explain nothing.

The second: the chapter commits the fallacy it has been warned about since <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="discrediting-is-a-genetic-fallacy">the death of God</Ref>. That the doctrine of free will was invented so that men could be punished says something about its inventors, and nothing about whether men are free. A true belief can have a disreputable origin.

### The causes that remain

The reply to the first charge starts from what section 3 denies. Its targets are three: the will as the cause of the act, the conscious motive as its cause, and the ego as the cause of thought. It does not deny that a person's states cause anything. The causes the later sections name are of another kind: "general feelings", "the play and counterplay of our organs", the "causal instinct" excited by fear, the "instinct of wanting to judge and punish". These are drives and bodily states, the steam of GS 360 (<Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="a-match-versus-a-ton-of-powder">a match versus a ton of powder</Ref>), and the module never doubted that drives cause. Fear, in section 5, is the felt face of a state of the organism; what section 3 denies is that the feeling, the representation in consciousness, is what does the causing. On that reading the chapter is consistent: the causes of belief are drives, and what consciousness reports of them is a surface. The reading does not need section 3's strongest sentence, which stays the overreach marked under section 3.

### Discrediting, not refuting

The reply to the second charge comes from Nietzsche's own rule, stated in *The Gay Science* a year before *Twilight*:

<Quote source="GS 345, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The mistake made by the more refined among them is that they uncover and criticize the perhaps foolish opinions of a people about their morality, or of humanity about all human morality — opinions about its origin, religious sanction, the superstition of free will, and things of that sort — and then suppose that they have criticized the morality itself. But the value of a command "thou shalt" is still fundamentally different from and independent of such opinions about it and the weeds of error that may have overgrown it … Even if a morality has grown out of an error, the realization of this fact would not as much as touch the problem of its value.
</Quote>

Nietzsche names "the superstition of free will" himself, and says that exposing it does not touch a morality's value. Applied to truth instead of value, the rule says that an account of where the doctrine of free will came from cannot show that it is false.

Section 7's first sentence looks as if it tries to. "We know only too well what it really is — the foulest of all theologians' artifices": the doctrine's origin, an artifice of theologians, is given as what it *is*, and read alone that is the fallacy.

But the sentence begins "Today we no longer have any pity for the concept", and the "today" points back. Section 3 had already said "Meanwhile we have thought better of it. Today we no longer believe a word of all this", and had taken apart the will, the motive and the ego on which the doctrine rests. Section 7 opens by treating that case as closed and then states its own task: "Here I simply supply the psychology of all making responsible." The refutation is in section 3, and behind it BGE 21's *causa sui* argument; section 7 does the other half of what <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="discrediting-how-a-belief-dies-without-being-refuted">the death of God</Ref> called a *discrediting*: once a belief has lost its support, an explanation of why it is held anyway, which makes no use of its truth. A belief that has no evidence and is held as firmly as anything on earth needs such an explanation, and "invented for the purpose of punishment" supplies it.

Say what section 7 does show, which part of the chapter and of the module carries the case against free will, and why Nietzsche himself would reject the inference the claim attributes to him.

<Solution>
**What the claim gets wrong.** It makes section 7 an argument from origin to falsity: the doctrine came from the wish to punish, so it is false. That inference is the genetic fallacy, and Nietzsche's own rule rejects it. GS 345 says that criticizing "opinions about its origin, religious sanction, the superstition of free will, and things of that sort" does not criticize a morality itself: "Even if a morality has grown out of an error, the realization of this fact would not as much as touch the problem of its value." The same holds for truth: a belief's disreputable origin does not make it false.

**What carries the case.** Section 3 and the module's arguments: the will is a complex of sensation, commanding thought and the affect of command (BGE 19); the decisive contest of motives is invisible (D 129); the doer is a fiction added to the deed (GM I 13); and ultimate responsibility would require being *causa sui*, "the best self-contradiction that has yet been conceived" (BGE 21). These show that the free will retributive blame needs has no support and could not exist.

**What section 7 shows.** Why a belief without support has been held so firmly: "the instinct of wanting to judge and punish" needed it, since only a free agent can deserve punishment. That is a *discrediting*, in the sense of <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="discrediting-how-a-belief-dies-without-being-refuted">the death of God</Ref>: an explanation of a belief and of its weight that makes no use of its truth. It completes the refutation; it does not replace it. Read alone, section 7 would establish only that free will is a convenient belief, which is compatible with its being true.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run section 2's reversal on a modern recommendation: "People who exercise regularly are happier and live longer, so exercise and you will be happy." (a) State the moral version of the claim, in section 2's formula. (b) State the type-first explanation, naming the common cause. (c) Design the evidence that would decide between the two arrows, and say what each reading predicts.

<Solution>
**(a) The formula.** "Do this (exercise), refrain from that (sitting still), then you will be happy and long-lived!" The correlation between exercise and wellbeing is read as cause and effect.

**(b) The type-first reading.** A person with abundant energy, sound health and well-ordered drives exercises because he must: energy wants spending, and rest is a burden to him. The same constitution makes him cheerful and long-lived. Exercise and happiness are two effects of one cause, the "order which he represents physiologically", as Cornaro's diet and long life were two effects of his metabolism. The exhausted person, told to exercise, is being handed someone else's régime.

**(c) The deciding evidence.** The two readings agree on the correlation and differ on interventions. If exercise causes the wellbeing, assigning exercise at random to people who would not have chosen it should raise their wellbeing, on average, to the level of those who chose it. If the type causes both, the assigned exercisers should gain little or nothing, and some, the low-energy and the ill, should be harmed or drop out; and a measure of energy or health taken *before* the exercise should predict both later exercise and later happiness. A randomized trial with a measure of baseline constitution decides between them. A mixed result is possible and informative: exercise helps somewhat, and the type explains much of the correlation. Section 2 asserts the pure type-first reading for every moral recipe; this design is how one would test it for one.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A sourcing drill. Set "The Four Great Errors" 7 beside GM II 4. First the metadata: give the date and kind of each text, and say what each is trying to explain. Then state the apparent contradiction between them, and write a two- or three-sentence judgment that reconciles them or says why they cannot be reconciled. Bring in D 128 (1881) if it helps.

<Solution>
**Metadata.** GM II 4: *On the Genealogy of Morals*, second essay, 1887, a published book, part of a historical account of guilt, bad conscience and punishment; it explains where the *practice* of punishment came from. TI "Errors" 7: *Twilight of the Idols*, written summer 1888, published January 1889, a short polemical chapter; it explains why the *doctrine* of free will was held.

**The apparent contradiction.** GM II 4: punishment "evolved quite independently of any presupposition concerning freedom or nonfreedom of the will"; "he could have acted differently" is "an extremely late and subtle form of human judgment". "Errors" 7: "the doctrine of the will has been invented essentially for the purpose of punishment". If punishment came first and went on without free will, it seems it did not need free will, so free will cannot have been invented for it.

**The judgment.** They are reconciled once one separates punishing from punishing as deserved. Punishment is old and needed no freedom: anger vented on the one who caused harm, measured by an equivalence of injury and pain. What the doctrine of the will supplied, late, was the claim that the punished man *deserves* his pain because the act was up to him, which gave punishers, priests first, a right to punish and not merely a practice; so "Errors" 7's "invented … for the purpose of punishment" means "invented so that punishment could be just", which GM II 4's "late and subtle" dating supports. D 128 does not contradict either: it names other parents of the same doctrine, "human pride and feeling of power", and a belief can be fed by several drives at once.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A boundary probe. In a notebook of 1885 Nietzsche wrote: "How long have I been concerned in my own mind to prove the perfect innocence of becoming! And what strange paths I've taken in this quest! … Was it not to give myself a feeling of complete irresponsibility — to place myself outside all praise and blame, independent of all past and present, in order to run after my own goal in my own way?" Apply section 5 of the chapter to Nietzsche's own doctrine. Does the confession discredit the innocence of becoming? What would it take for the doctrine to survive it?

<Solution>
**Section 5 applied.** Section 5 says that the explanation which relieves is the one "considered true": "the proof of pleasure ('of strength') as a criterion of truth". The notebook confesses that the innocence of becoming was a wanted conclusion, sought by "strange paths" because it would give its author "a feeling of complete irresponsibility". By section 5's test, a doctrine its holder wanted for the relief it brings is suspect: his conviction that it is true could be the relief speaking.

**Does that discredit it?** Only half. A discrediting has two parts: the belief has no independent support, and its being held is explained without its truth. The confession supplies the second part, a motive; it does not supply the first. By Nietzsche's own rule (GS 345), the origin of a belief "would not as much as touch" its value, and does not settle its truth either.

**What it would take to survive.** Independent support, arguments that would hold for someone who had no wish to be irresponsible. The chapter offers them: the *causa sui* argument (no one gives man his qualities, "nor he himself"), the analysis of the will in section 3 and BGE 19, and the argument that nothing stands outside the whole. If those hold, the doctrine survives its author's motive, as a true theorem survives a mathematician who wanted it true. What the probe shows is the boundary of section 5: it is a reason to check a convenient belief's support, not a proof that the belief is false, and it applies to Nietzsche's doctrines as much as to the priests'.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
GS 345 says that nobody has yet examined "the value of that most famous of all medicines which is called morality", and that "the first step would be — for once to question it". Section 7 has told you where the doctrine of free will came from. Say what more you would need to know to judge the *value* of a morality built on guilt and punishment, and why an account of its origin alone cannot give it. Then say what kind of history could.

<Solution>
**What the origin gives.** That the doctrine of the will served "the instinct of wanting to judge and punish", and the priests' wish for a right to punish. That tells us whose interest it served when it arose. It does not tell us what the morality does now to those who live by it, and GS 345 puts the value there: a medicine is judged by its effect on the patient, "completely independent of whether he thinks about medicine scientifically or the way old women do".

**What more is needed.** Its effects: what a morality of guilt and punishment makes of the people who hold it, whether it strengthens or weakens them, which types it favours and which it ruins, and what has become of human beings under it compared with what became of them under other valuations. The standard behind the question is the one the course has met since <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner">the case of Wagner</Ref>: whether a valuation belongs to ascending or to declining life.

**What kind of history could.** A history that follows a value not only back to its origin but forward through its uses: how the meaning of punishment changed as different wills took it over, what the practice did to those who suffered and those who imposed it, and what kind of human being it bred. Such a history bears on value because it reports effects, not because it reports a shameful birth. That is the method of <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">genealogy as a method</Ref>, which answers the charge that any history of morals commits the genetic fallacy.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

The Genealogy's Preface asks for the value of moral values to be called into question by a real history of their origin, and its Second Treatise (§§12–13) states the rule that makes such a history possible: what a thing is now used for says nothing about where it came from. This lesson establishes what kind of argument the three treatises are: a history with documentary evidence, run under a rule that separates a thing's origin from its present use, whose critical force is to strip a value of its claim to be given rather than to prove its precepts false, aimed at a target bounded so that the author's own values fall outside it.

The course's first module fixed the problem and the method. <Ref lesson="madman-argument">The madman lesson</Ref> reconstructed the problem: the foundation European morality rested on is gone and the edifice still stands. <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">The protocol lesson</Ref> supplied the corpus in order, the five-step reconstruction, the rule that published works ground and notebooks corroborate, and the working hypothesis of methodological naturalism; <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the compiled-book lesson</Ref> showed what the rule protects against. What stands open is the book the method was built for. This lesson reads the *Genealogy*'s own statement of what it is doing, in the Preface and in the two sections of the Second Treatise that state its method, and settles what such a history can and cannot prove before <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the next lesson</Ref> begins the First Treatise's history.

The route: the Preface's question and the question it replaced; the targets and the demand for a documented history; the rule of II:12 and the eleven meanings of punishment in II:13; the genetic-fallacy objection, conceded and answered; and the target, morality in the pejorative sense, with the scope problem its boundary creates.

## The Preface's question: not where evil comes from, but what the judgments are worth

The Preface, signed "Sils-Maria, Upper Engadine, July 1887" (a village in the Swiss Alps where Nietzsche spent his summers), opens with a claim about its readers: "we are unknown to ourselves, we knowers", because "we have never sought ourselves" (§1). The eight sections that follow say what seeking would consist of, and §3 fixes the question by recording the one it replaced.

The replaced question was a boy's. At thirteen Nietzsche wrote an essay on the origin of evil and "gave God the honour and made him the father of evil". The question asks for a cause, and the answer names one. §3 then records the change: "some historical and philological training, together with an innate fastidiousness in psychological questions, soon transformed my problem into another one":

> unter welchen Bedingungen erfand sich der Mensch jene Werthurtheile gut und böse? *und welchen Werth haben sie selbst?*
>
> under what conditions did man invent those value judgments good and evil? *and what value do they themselves have?*

  src="./figures/two-questions.svg"

  caption="Preface §3: the question about a cause is replaced by a question about conditions of invention and a second question about worth; the second is the book's."
/>

The new question has two parts, and the second is the new thing. The first part still asks for an origin, but of an invention rather than of a substance: the judgments "good" and "evil" were made by people under conditions, and the conditions can be stated. The second part asks what the judgments are worth, and §3 immediately says by what standard: "Have they so far hindered or furthered human flourishing (*Gedeihen*)? Are they a sign of distress, of impoverishment, of the degeneration of life? Or, conversely, do they reveal the fullness, the force, the will of life, its courage, its confidence, its future?" The standard is a fact about the life that holds the values, which is what makes the second question one a naturalist can pursue with the same evidence as the first.

### The question dated: a notebook of 1885

The question is older than the book. A notebook fragment of 1885, `NF-1885,2[190]`, asks it in nearly the same words: "was sind unsere Werthschätzungen und moralischen Gütertafeln selber werth?" ("what are our valuations and moral tables of goods themselves worth?"). On the evidence ladder of <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> the fragment sits on the fifth rung: it dates the question to two years before the Preface and grounds nothing, and the published §3 is the sentence a reconstruction cites.

<KeyFact>
The Preface's standard for the value of a value, whether it furthers or hinders the flourishing of the life that holds it, is itself a claim about facts; the is–ought step the naturalist reading must supply is hidden in the word *Gedeihen*, and every later test of the hypothesis in this course is a test of whether that word can be cashed as a fact about types.
</KeyFact>

## The targets and the demand: a documented history against a hypothesis

The Preface names an opponent before it states a method, and the method is stated as what the opponent lacks. The opponent is a book and its author. Paul Rée, a philosopher four years Nietzsche's junior and his companion in the winter of 1876–77 at Sorrento, on the Bay of Naples, where *Human, All Too Human* was begun, published *Der Ursprung der moralischen Empfindungen* (*The Origin of the Moral Sensations*) in 1877. §4 calls it "a clear, clean and clever, also precocious little book", the first in which "an inverted and perverse kind of genealogical hypothesis, its properly *English* kind" had confronted Nietzsche distinctly, and says of it: "perhaps I have never read anything to which I said No, sentence by sentence, conclusion by conclusion, as I did to this book".

The sentence that follows is the method's verdict on that No. Nietzsche says he referred to Rée's propositions in his own books "not by refuting them (what have I to do with refutations!) but, as befits a positive spirit, by putting in place of the improbable the more probable, and in some cases in place of one error another". A hypothesis is not refuted; a more probable one is set beside it. That is the relation the whole book will bear to the English account.

### The English hypothesis and its two missing things

What the English account says is stated in one sentence at the opening of the First Treatise (I:2), quoted as the historians' "decree": originally, unegoistic actions were praised and called good "from the standpoint of those to whom they were shown, that is, to whom they were *useful*; later the origin of this praise was *forgotten*, and unegoistic actions, because they were habitually praised as good, were simply *felt* as good". Four moves, which I:2 lists: utility, forgetting, habit, error. Rée, §4 adds, sees in the altruistic mode of valuation "the moral mode of valuation *as such*" (*an sich*).

§7 states what the account lacks. Nietzsche had wanted "to turn such a sharp and impartial eye in a better direction, the direction of the *real history of morality*, and to warn him in time against such English hypothesis-mongering into the blue (*ins Blaue*)". The colour that matters to a genealogist of morality is not blue but grey: "*das Graue*, I mean the documentary (*das Urkundliche*), what is actually ascertainable, what actually existed, in short the whole long hieroglyphic script of the human moral past, so hard to decipher". This script "was unknown to Dr Rée; but he had read Darwin".

<Remark title="the Preface's argument against hypothesis-history">
**P1.** An account of the origin of a moral valuation is a history, and a history must rest on documentary evidence: what "actually existed" and is "actually ascertainable". Anchor: §7, *das Urkundliche*.

**P2.** The English account supplies a mechanism (usefulness, forgetting, habit, error) and no documents. Anchor: §7, "this script was unknown to Dr Rée"; I:2, the decree.

**C1.** The English account is not refuted; it is unfounded, and a documented account displaces it as the more probable. Anchor: §4, "not by refuting them".

**C2.** The English account cannot pose the second question at all, because it takes the value of "good" as given. Anchor: §6, "the value of these 'values' was taken as given, as factual, as beyond all questioning".

**The missing premise.** What counts as a document for a history of a value: etymology, law, cult practice, the physiology of the people who held it. The Preface asserts this and does not argue it; the First Treatise's §§4–5 are where the assertion is cashed, and <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the next lesson</Ref> grades that evidence.

**The strongest opposite reading.** Rée's mechanism might be corroborated by documents he did not cite. The Preface's charge is that he did not look, and §7's "hieroglyphic script" concedes that the looking is hard; nothing in the Preface says that nothing would be found.
</Remark>

The two conclusions differ in kind. C1 is about evidence and can be reversed by evidence. C2 is about a question that cannot be asked from inside the English account: if "good" simply means "useful to the recipient", asking what the judgment "good" is worth becomes asking whether usefulness is useful. §6 states the demand that C2 makes room for: "we need a *critique* of moral values, *the value of these values is itself first to be called into question*, and for that a knowledge of the conditions and circumstances is needed out of which they grew, under which they developed and shifted". The knowledge is the history; the critique is what the history is for.

§5 names what the demand is aimed at. The value at issue "was in particular the value of the *unegoistic*, the instincts of pity, self-denial, self-sacrifice, which Schopenhauer had gilded, deified and made otherworldly for so long that they finally remained for him as 'values in themselves'". In these instincts Nietzsche "saw the great danger to mankind", the morality of pity as "the uncanniest symptom of our European culture", its detour "to a new Buddhism? to a European Buddhism? to nihilism?", the condition <Ref lesson="madman-argument">the madman lesson</Ref> defined as the highest values devaluing themselves.

### Rée against Nietzsche on "good", in one line

The contrast the First Treatise opens with can be stated now as a preview. On the English account the word "good" was first used by the *recipients* of useful acts, and came to be felt as intrinsic once its use was forgotten. On Nietzsche's account (I:2) the word was first used by "the good themselves, that is, the noble, the powerful, the higher-placed and high-minded", of themselves, "in contrast to everything low"; the recipients of usefulness had nothing to do with it. Both are claims about who first said the word, and the second is offered as documented (I:4–5) where the first is not. Which document, and how much it carries, is the next lesson's question.

### How the book asks to be read

§8 ends the Preface with an instruction. The aphoristic form is difficult "because today this form is not taken seriously enough": an aphorism "properly minted and cast is not 'deciphered' by being read; its *interpretation* (*Auslegung*) has then first to begin". The Third Treatise is offered as a model: "an aphorism is placed before this treatise, and the treatise itself is its commentary". <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">The protocol lesson</Ref> showed that the aphorism is III:1, not the *Zarathustra* epigraph. The last word of the Preface names the faculty the reading requires, "for which one must be almost a cow and in any case not a 'modern man': *das Wiederkäuen*", rumination, chewing the cud. The five-step protocol is what rumination amounts to when written down: locate the claim, tie the premises to sentences, name what is missing, find the second passage, state the opposite reading.

## The rule from the Second Treatise: origin and use lie "toto caelo" apart

The First Treatise's history presupposes a rule it never states, and the Second Treatise states it in two sections that stand in the middle of a different argument. Reading II:12–13 before I:1 is this course's decision, a convention of seminars rather than the book's order, and the reason is the one just given: I:2's claim that the noble's self-naming and not the recipients' use is the origin of "good" is an application of the rule, and the rule has to be on the table before the application can be judged.

II:12 opens by separating two problems that "fall apart, or ought to: unfortunately one usually throws them together": the origin (*Ursprung*) and the purpose (*Zweck*) of punishment. The "previous genealogists of morals" have proceeded naively: "they find some 'purpose' in punishment, for example revenge or deterrence, then innocently set this purpose at the beginning, as the *causa fiendi* of punishment, and are done." Against this Nietzsche states what he calls the most important proposition "for every kind of history", won with effort and to be won again:

> dass nämlich die Ursache der Entstehung eines Dings und dessen schliessliche Nützlichkeit, dessen thatsächliche Verwendung und Einordnung in ein System von Zwecken *toto coelo* auseinander liegen
>
> that the cause of the coming-into-being of a thing and its eventual usefulness, its actual employment and placement in a system of purposes, lie *toto caelo* apart

*Toto caelo* is Latin, "by the whole heaven": as far apart as two things can be. Nietzsche writes it in the older spelling *coelo*, and had used the same phrase in Preface §4 for the distance between the morality of custom and altruistic valuation. The sentence continues with the mechanism that produces the distance: "something existing, having somehow come about, is again and again interpreted to new views by a power superior to it, newly taken possession of, transformed and redirected to a new use"; all happening in the organic world "is an overpowering, a becoming-master, and all overpowering and becoming-master is a new-interpreting, an adjusting, in which the previous 'meaning' and 'purpose' must necessarily be obscured or wholly extinguished".

### The eye, the hand, and punishment

The belief the rule replaces is a general one, and its generality is what makes the rule general. "From of old one had believed one grasped in the demonstrable purpose, in the usefulness of a thing, a form, an institution, also its ground of origin: the eye as made for seeing, the hand as made for grasping. So one also imagined punishment as invented for punishing." The teleological inference runs from present function to origin, and the rule denies it for organs, legal institutions, social customs, political usages, forms in art and in religious cult alike, since "however well one has grasped the usefulness of any physiological organ … one has thereby grasped nothing yet about its coming-into-being".

What replaces the inference is a claim about what a purpose is. "All purposes, all utilities are only *signs* that a will to power has become master over something less powerful and has stamped on it, out of itself, the meaning of a function; and the whole history of a 'thing', an organ, a custom can in this way be a continuous sign-chain (*Zeichen-Kette*) of ever new interpretations and adjustments, whose causes need not themselves be connected among one another, but on the contrary in some cases merely follow and replace one another by chance."

<Remark title="the rule of II:12, reconstructed">
**P1.** Whatever exists is repeatedly taken over and reinterpreted to a new use by a power superior to it. Anchor: "immer wieder von einer ihm überlegenen Macht auf neue Ansichten ausgelegt".

**P2.** Each takeover imposes a new meaning and obscures or extinguishes the previous one. Anchor: "der bisherige 'Sinn' und 'Zweck' nothwendig verdunkelt oder ganz ausgelöscht werden muss".

**C1.** The present use of a thing is the last imposition, not the cause of the thing's coming-into-being; the two lie *toto caelo* apart.

**C2.** The history of a thing is the sequence of impositions, and the causes of successive impositions may be unrelated: a sign-chain, not a development toward a goal (*progressus*).

**The missing premise, and where it is stated.** The reconstruction needs an agent for every takeover, and the section supplies one: a will to power. It is stated as a theory "of a power-will playing itself out in all happening", set "against the ruling instinct and taste of the age", which prefers Herbert Spencer's "adaptation", a "second-rank activity". Read at that scale the premise is cosmological, a claim about all happening, and step 3 of the protocol classifies it as a type-fact about everything. Read at the scale the rule is used at, a practice is given its use by whoever currently controls it, the premise is a claim about agents and institutions and nothing more. The course uses only the second, weaker form from here on, and <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref> decides whether the first form can be held at all.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Nietzsche calls the rule "this main point of historical method", and it is the premise every later lesson uses without restating it: the noble's coinage and not the recipients' use is the origin of "good" (I:2), debt precedes guilt (II:4), and the ascetic ideal means a different thing to each type that holds it (III:1). Its consequence for method is that any account of a value from its present function, utilitarian or evolutionary, is not a history at all.
</KeyFact>

## Punishment: one procedure, eleven meanings, none the origin

II:13 returns to punishment and applies the rule, and the application is the lesson's worked case because it supplies the one thing II:12's principle lacks, a list.

### Durable and fluid: the procedure is older than its use

The section first distinguishes two things in punishment: "on the one hand the relatively *durable* element, the custom, the act, the 'drama', a certain strict sequence of procedures; on the other the *fluid* element, the meaning, the purpose, the expectation attached to the carrying-out of such procedures". Then it assumes, "*per analogiam*, according to the main point of historical method just developed, that the procedure itself is something older, earlier than its use for punishment, that the latter was first *put into*, *interpreted into* the procedure, which had long existed but was customary in another sense".

### Undefinable: a concept with a history is a synthesis

The present concept, on II:13's description, is such a synthesis. "In a very late state of culture (for example in present-day Europe) the concept 'punishment' in fact no longer presents *one* meaning at all, but a whole synthesis of 'meanings'": the history of its exploitation for the most various purposes has "crystallized into a kind of unity which is hard to dissolve, hard to analyse and, what must be stressed, wholly *undefinable*". The parenthesis that follows states a principle the whole course will meet again: "today it is impossible to say definitely *why* one punishes: all concepts in which a whole process is semiotically condensed elude definition; definable is only that which has no history."

### The schema: eleven meanings, unranked

The list is offered "to give at least an idea of how uncertain, how supplementary, how accidental 'the meaning' of punishment is, and how one and the same procedure can be used, interpreted, adjusted to fundamentally different intentions". Nietzsche calls it a schema "that presented itself to me on the basis of a relatively small and accidental material": a sample, not a census. The eleven are not ranked, and none is claimed for the origin. In an earlier stage, the section adds, the synthesis is looser, the elements shift their weight case by case, and one of them, "perhaps the purpose of deterrence", can seem to cancel all the rest; the seeming is exactly what the naive genealogist takes for the *causa fiendi*.

<Example title="the rule applied to Rée's thesis on punishment">
Preface §4 records Rée's claim: for punishment "the terroristic purpose" (*der terroristische Zweck*), deterrence, is "essential and original". The rule is run on it in four steps.

**Step 1, locate the claim.** Deterrence is the origin of punishment. In II:13's terms, it is one of the eleven present meanings, the one that in some eras "seems to cancel the rest".

**Step 2, apply II:12.** A present meaning is the sign of the last mastering of the procedure, not of its coming-into-being; the two lie *toto caelo* apart. So the fact that punishment now deters, however well established, is no evidence about how the procedure arose.

**Step 3, apply II:13.** The procedure is older than its use as punishment at all, so it is older than any purpose of punishment, deterrence included. A purpose cannot be the origin of something that predates its use for that purpose.

**Step 4, state what Rée's claim now needs.** Independent evidence that the earliest form of the procedure was performed with the deterrent expectation attached: a document, in §7's sense, and not the present function. Rée offered the present function. The claim is therefore not shown false; it is shown to rest on evidence that cannot support it, which is C1 of the Preface's argument applied to one thesis.

**The displacement.** The more probable account, in §4's phrase, is offered in II:4–6: the oldest document Nietzsche finds is the contract between creditor and debtor, in which inflicted pain is *compensation* for an unpaid debt and no deterrence is intended; <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt-and-guilt lesson</Ref> reconstructs that account and grades its documents.
</Example>

## What a genealogy cannot prove: the genetic fallacy and the argument the Preface licenses

A history built under the rule establishes how a value arose, who imposed each of its meanings, and what it did for them. It does not establish whether the value's precepts are true or false, and the Preface's second question, what the values are worth, is not answered by any fact about where they came from. Both halves of that sentence are Nietzsche's, and the second is the one his readers most often deny him.

The reading to be rejected runs: Nietzsche argues that because morality arose from ressentiment, morality is false. The inference from a belief's origin to its truth-value is the *genetic fallacy*, and it is invalid: a proposition's truth is independent of the route by which anyone came to hold it. If the *Genealogy* were that inference, the book would be a long fallacy.

It is not, and the evidence is a published section written the same year as the Preface. §345 of *The Gay Science*, in the Book V of 1887, is titled "Morality as a problem", and it separates three things: a history of the origin of moral feelings, a critique of them, and a history of ethical systems. The history of origin is "something different from a critique of them", and its analogy for the independence is a medicine, whose value to the patient does not depend on what the patient believes about medicine:

> Aber der Werth einer Vorschrift „du sollst" ist noch gründlich verschieden und unabhängig von solcherlei Meinungen über dieselbe und von dem Unkraut des Irrthums, mit dem sie vielleicht überwachsen ist: so gewiss der Werth eines Medikaments für den Kranken noch vollkommen unabhängig davon ist, ob der Kranke wissenschaftlich oder wie ein altes Weib über Medizin denkt. Eine Moral könnte selbst aus einem Irrthum gewachsen sein: auch mit dieser Einsicht wäre das Problem ihres Werthes noch nicht einmal berührt.
>
> But the value of a precept "thou shalt" is still fundamentally different from and independent of such opinions about it and of the weeds of error with which it is perhaps overgrown: as surely as the value of a medicine for the sick person is wholly independent of whether the patient thinks about medicine scientifically or like an old woman. A morality could even have grown *out of* an error: with this insight the problem of its value would not even be touched.

The published sentence has a notebook twin two years older. `NF-1885,2[189]`, the fragment immediately before the one that first asks the value question, reads: "the question of the origin (*Herkunft*) of our valuations and tables of goods does not in the least coincide with their critique, as is so often believed: even though the insight into some *pudenda origo* certainly brings with it, for the feeling, a diminution in value of the thing that so arose and prepares a critical mood and attitude toward it". The fragment corroborates and dates; GS 345 grounds. Its second clause matters as much as its first: a shameful origin (*pudenda origo*) does diminish the *feeling* of a value's worth and prepares a critical mood. The logical inference is denied and the psychological effect is recorded, in one sentence, in 1885.

### The replacement: force on standing, not on truth

What a genealogy does, on Brian Leiter's account, is rhetorical rather than deductive: it removes a value's claim to be natural, universal, or given, shifts the burden of justification onto those who hold it, and makes the reader suspicious of what had been beyond question. The account is exact about what is lost and what is not. Nothing about the truth of "help the suffering" changes when its history is told; what changes is that the precept can no longer present itself as what morality *is*, and its defenders now owe an argument they did not owe before.

<Example title="the argument the Preface licenses">
**P1.** Morality presents its values as universal and non-contingent: as given. Anchor: BGE 186, where every philosopher who "grounded" morality took "morality itself as given (*gegeben*)"; Preface §6, "the value of these 'values' was taken as given".

**P2.** The real history of those values shows them contingent, specific to a type of person, and born of that type's need. Anchor: the three treatises, which is to say the claim the rest of the course tests section by section.

**C.** Morality's claim to *that kind* of authority, universal and non-contingent, is unfounded.

**What C is not.** C is not "morality's precepts are false" and not "the opposite precepts are true". It is a claim about standing: the value has been moved from given to made, and its defenders now owe the argument they took themselves to have.

**The invalid neighbour.** "Morality began in ressentiment; therefore its precepts are false" shares P2's evidence and reaches a different conclusion by an inference GS 345 concedes is invalid. The two arguments are distinguished by their conclusions, not by their evidence, which is why a reader who has only seen the evidence cannot tell which one is being made.
</Example>

The grammar is the one <Ref lesson="madman-argument">the madman lesson</Ref> took from Reginster: a belief can be refuted, suspended, or discredited, and Section 343 discredited the belief in God without refuting it. A genealogy does to a value what the death of God did to that belief. The discriminating case is a utilitarian who accepts the whole history, agrees that "good" was coined by nobles and inverted by slaves, and answers: altruism still maximizes welfare, whatever its route into the language. The genealogy has no reply to him at the level of truth. It has a reply at the level of standing: he can no longer say, with Rée, that altruistic valuation is moral valuation *an sich*, and he now has to argue for the value he took as given, against a history that says a particular type needed it. Whether that argument can be won is a separate question, and it is the question the second half of the course takes up.

### Three readers, one rule

Three analytic readers answer the genetic-fallacy objection in three ways, and each answer commits its holder to a reading of II:12.

| Reader | What genealogy does | What II:12's *toto caelo* is | What the reading needs later |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leiter, *Nietzsche on Morality* (2015), ch. 5 | Rhetorical force: strips the value's claim to be given, shifts the burden; not a deductive refutation | The methodological principle that a present use is not an origin, which is what blocks the fallacy and makes the history non-teleological | Only the psychology of types the hypothesis already assumes |
| Reginster, *The Affirmation of Life* (2006), ch. 4 §V | Not a critique at all, but the *empirical premise* of one: the history exposes the physiological type of a code's creators (strength or weakness), and a critique from the ethics of power then uses that premise | The rule that lets the history read a value as a symptom of the type that imposed it, rather than of the use it now serves | The standard of power as a defended value, which is <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">Module 6's</Ref> burden |
| Nehamas, *Life as Literature* (1985), ch. 4 | Interpretation modelled on reading a text: exposing a value's contingent origin impugns its claim to objectivity, so the fallacy objection does not arise as a question about a fixed thing | Read literally: a thing *is* its sign-chain, "the sum of its effects", so there is no origin apart from the interpretations | <Ref lesson="perspectivism">Perspectivism</Ref> read as the thesis that there is no fact apart from interpretations |

The three do not agree with one another. Reginster's note on the chapter says outright that Nehamas's version, on which contingent origins impugn objectivity, "is guilty of the genetic fallacy", and that his own earlier attempt to find a "new form of critique" in the book was mistaken: the genealogy, he now holds, "does not articulate a new form of critique". Leiter's "rhetorical" and Reginster's "empirical premise" are compatible: the first says what the history does to a reader, the second what it contributes to an argument. Nehamas's reading is the one <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">the closing module</Ref> states at its strongest, and it is the only one of the three that takes II:12's "sign-chain" as a claim about what a thing is rather than about how its history should be written.

Walter Kaufmann gives a fourth account of what the Preface's demand amounts to, older than the three above and about the revaluation rather than the genealogy. On his reading the "revaluation of all values" is not a new table of values but "a courageous becoming conscious" (the phrase is from a notebook, WM 1007, corroborated for Kaufmann by *Ecce Homo* IV 1's "act of supreme self-examination"): "the diagnosis itself is the revaluation", an internal criticism that uncovers "how many lies were concealed under the most honoured type of their contemporary morality" (BGE 212). ## The target: morality in the pejorative sense, and the scope problem

The licensed argument's P1 speaks of "morality", and the word has to be bounded, because Nietzsche has values of his own and an argument against all valuation would indict them. Preface §6 says that for one who learns to ask the question of pity "the belief in morality, in *all* morality, totters"; §103 of *Daybreak* says "I deny morality as I deny alchemy, that is, I deny its premises", and in the same section that "many actions called immoral ought to be avoided and resisted, and many called moral ought to be done and encouraged, but for other reasons than hitherto"; BGE 202 says that "higher moralities are, or ought to be, possible". The critic of "all morality" recommends actions and holds "higher moralities" possible, so the target of the critique is narrower than the word.

Simon Robertson names the resulting task the *scope problem*: characterize the target so that the object of critique is separated from the positive ideal, and so that the ideal is left "immune to whatever objections inform the critique". Four accounts have tried, each inheriting the previous one's target and revising one condition.

  src="./figures/scope-family-tree.svg"

  caption="The scope problem's family tree, Robertson's pp. 84–89: each account keeps its predecessor's target and revises one condition; the last is the boundary the course uses."

### MPS: Leiter's two components

The account the course inherits its vocabulary from is Leiter's. *Morality in the pejorative sense*, MPS, is his name for whatever normative system Nietzsche's critique is aimed at, characterized by the features such systems share rather than by their historical instances. On Leiter's characterization, a morality is MPS if it

> (1) presupposes three particular descriptive claims about the nature of human agents pertaining to free will [necessary for moral responsibility], the transparency of the self [due to which agents can be evaluated on the basis of their motives], and the essential similarity of all people [whereby 'one moral code is appropriate for all'], and/or (2) embraces norms that harm the 'highest men' while benefiting the 'lowest'.

The first is the *descriptive component*, attacked as false; the second the *normative component*, attacked as harmful, and its content is a list: altruism and pity (*Mitleid*), the alleviation of suffering, equality and impartial justice, "English" happiness and comfort (BGE 228), the extirpation of aggressive instincts. Leiter's emphasis is on the normative component: the main objection is that morality thwarts the flourishing of higher individuals, and "a morality could be an MPS even if it did not involve a commitment to an untenable descriptive account of agency".

### Robertson's revision: authority and jurisdiction

Robertson keeps both of Leiter's components and argues that they leave the harm unexplained: if a morality could be MPS without the descriptive commitments, nothing in the account says why its norms would have the harmful effects. His supplement adds to the descriptive presuppositions a commitment to objectivity in three forms, and singles out two: "(c) morality is normatively authoritative, in that compliance with it is categorically required, and (d) morality is universal in jurisdiction". The thesis he pushes is that "the combination of (Ic) and (Id) is necessary for an outlook falling amongst Nietzsche's critical targets, with the conjunction of these and (II)", the content, "being both necessary and sufficient". The explanation the revision buys is stated in one sentence: "it is by presenting itself as normatively authoritative and universal that morality holds in place the specific values it advances". The higher type whose flourishing morality thwarts "ought not comply with it", and "that would not be possible if morality were normatively authoritative. So he needs to deny that it is."

The revision draws the boundary where the licensed argument needs it. P1 of that argument was that morality presents its values as given, universal and non-contingent; Robertson's (Ic) and (Id) are that presentation stated as conditions. A value that makes no claim to categorical, universal authority, an ideal held by one type for itself, is not caught by an argument against that claim, and Nietzsche's own ideal, on Robertson's reading "a quasi-aesthetic individualist perfectionism" whose "overarching imperative" is "to perfect oneself by 'becoming what one is'" (GS 270, 290, 335), is of that kind.

BGE 186 gives the boundary its textual form. The section contrasts the "science of morals" philosophers have attempted, "they wanted morality to be *grounded*, and every philosopher so far has thought that he has provided a ground for morality", with the "modest" descriptive project of "collecting material, formulating concepts … a *typology* of morals". The grounding project, Nietzsche says, "was only an erudite form of good faith in the dominant morality, a new way of expressing it", and it took "morality itself" as "given". A typology of morals is a history of made things; a grounding of morality is the defence of a given one. The *Genealogy* is the first project, and its target is anything that presents itself as the second.

### The authority puzzle, stated and handed on

The boundary creates a debt. Janaway and Robertson state it in their introduction as the *authority puzzle*: "if Nietzsche denies the kinds of objectivity upon which morality's claims to authority rest, he may thereby deprive his own positive ideal of a legitimate claim to objectivity and authority; in which case, the evaluative and normative claims constitutive of his own positive outlook would be no more justified than, or superior to, those he rejects". Denying (Ic) for morality is easy; keeping any authority for a perfectionist ideal after the denial is the problem, and it is the second of the three tests <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> set for the naturalist hypothesis. This lesson states the puzzle and does not answer it; <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the value-creation lesson</Ref> reconstructs the answers on offer.

<KeyFact>
MPS's descriptive component is the protocol's list of missing premises seen from the target's side: the free-will presupposition, transparency (a type-fact about minds), and essential similarity (a type-fact about types) are the three things a reconstruction of a section is told to hunt for at step 3, because they are the three things the critique says morality assumes and does not state.
</KeyFact>

What is established: the *Genealogy* asks two questions, under what conditions the judgments good and evil were invented and what they are worth, and the second is answered by no fact about origin; its opponent is a hypothesis-history with a mechanism and no documents, which the book displaces rather than refutes; its rule, stated in II:12 as the main point of historical method, is that a thing's origin and its present use lie *toto caelo* apart because use is imposed by whatever will currently masters the thing, and II:13's eleven meanings of punishment are the rule's evidence; the inference from origin to falsity is conceded invalid in GS 345 and in a notebook of 1885, and the argument the book licenses moves a value from given to made without moving a precept from true to false; and the target, MPS, is bounded by its two components and by Robertson's line, whatever claims categorical and universal authority, so that the author's own values fall outside it.

What is not yet established is the history itself. The Preface's C1 says the English account is unfounded and a documented one is more probable; the documents have not been produced. <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">The next lesson</Ref> reads the First Treatise's opening sections, where the utilitarian genealogy is displaced, the etymological evidence is laid out, and the two modes of valuation are stated with the asymmetry that carries the charge.

<Exercise>
Closed book. State the two questions of Preface §3, the one Nietzsche asked at thirteen and the one that replaced it, and give the two-part form of the second. Then name the word in §3 that marks the change from a question about a cause to a question about an invention, and the standard §3 gives for answering the second part.

<Solution>
**The boy's question:** where does evil come from? (Answered at thirteen: God is "the father of evil".) **The book's question:** "under what conditions did man invent those value judgments good and evil? and what value do they themselves have?" The first part asks for conditions of invention, the second for worth.

**The word:** *erfand*, "invented" (*erfand sich der Mensch jene Werthurtheile*). A cause is found; a judgment is made. The verb moves the origin of "good" and "evil" from the world into human activity under conditions, which is what makes a history of it possible.

**The standard:** whether the judgments have "hindered or furthered human flourishing (*Gedeihen*)", whether they are "a sign of distress, impoverishment, degeneration of life" or of "its fullness, force, will". The standard is a fact about the life that holds the values, which is what the naturalist reading needs and what the is–ought step has to be found in.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
"Nietzsche's genealogy commits the genetic fallacy: it argues that because morality began in ressentiment, morality is false." Break the claim. Name the sentence of GS 345 that concedes the invalidity of the inference, state the rung of the evidence ladder that `NF-1885,2[189]` occupies and what it may therefore do for the reconstruction, and write out the argument form the Preface actually licenses, with its conclusion stated so that it cannot be mistaken for "morality is false".

<Solution>
**The concession.** GS 345: "Eine Moral könnte selbst aus einem Irrthum gewachsen sein: auch mit dieser Einsicht wäre das Problem ihres Werthes noch nicht einmal berührt", "a morality could even have grown out of an error: with this insight the problem of its value would not even be touched". The same section separates "a history of the origin of these feelings and valuations" from "a critique of them". A published section of 1887 therefore states that origin does not decide value, which is the invalidity the objection accuses the book of ignoring.

**The fragment.** `NF-1885,2[189]` is a posthumous fragment, the fifth rung: it corroborates and dates, and never grounds. It shows the concession in Nietzsche's hand two years before the Preface, "the question of the *Herkunft* of our valuations does not in the least coincide with their critique, as is so often believed", and it adds the psychological clause, that a *pudenda origo* diminishes the feeling of value and prepares a critical mood. Used correctly it dates the thought and corroborates GS 345; it is not the sentence the reconstruction cites.

**The licensed form.** P1: morality presents its values as universal and non-contingent, as given (BGE 186; Preface §6). P2: the real history of those values shows them contingent, type-specific, and born of a particular need (the treatises). C: morality's claim to *that kind* of authority is unfounded. The conclusion is about standing, not truth: the value is moved from given to made, and its defenders now owe an argument. The objection's version keeps P2 and replaces C with "its precepts are false", which does not follow from P1 and P2 and is the step GS 345 disowns. The two arguments differ in their conclusion and share their evidence, so the way to tell them apart in any passage is to ask what the passage concludes, not what it cites.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
II:13 lists eleven meanings of punishment and does not say which is the present one. Derive from II:12's principle why *no* present meaning could be the origin of the procedure even in principle, and state the premise the derivation needs. Then run step 3 of the protocol on that premise: classify it, and say in which of its two readings (a claim about all happening, or a claim about agents and institutions) the derivation still goes through.

<Solution>
**The derivation.** By II:12, every purpose or utility a thing now has is "only a sign that a will to power has become master over something less powerful and stamped on it the meaning of a function". A meaning is therefore an imposition on something that already existed to be imposed on; the thing precedes its meaning by the very relation "imposed on". II:13 states the consequence for punishment: the procedure "is something older, earlier than its use for punishment", and the use was "put into, interpreted into" it. So any present meaning, being an imposition, presupposes a prior existence of the procedure, and cannot be the cause of the procedure's coming-into-being. This holds "even in principle" because it follows from what a meaning *is* on II:12's account and not from any fact about which meaning is current.

**The premise the derivation needs.** That meanings are imposed by a will and are not intrinsic to the thing: the "will to power" clause of II:12. Without it, a thing could carry its meaning from its origin, as the eye is said to carry seeing, and the teleological inference would be open again.

**Step 3 on the premise.** It is a type-fact at whatever scale it is read. Read as "a power-will playing itself out in all happening", it is a claim about everything and therefore cosmological, and a naturalist has to treat it as a hypothesis about the world rather than a result. Read as "a practice is given its use by whoever currently controls it", it is a claim about agents and institutions, of the kind a historian makes without metaphysics. The derivation goes through on the second reading alone: it needs only that punishment's meanings were put into the procedure by someone, not that all happening is will to power. That is why the course carries the rule forward in the weak form and defers the strong one to the will-to-power lesson.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Consider a stipulated case in which a practice's present use is identical to its origin: a tool made for one job and still used only for it. State whether II:12's rule fails in that case or applies vacuously, say exactly what the rule claims about the general case, and state what observation would falsify it.

<Solution>
**Vacuous, not failed.** II:12 does not claim that origin and use always differ in content; it claims that they are two distinct questions whose answers "lie *toto caelo* apart", so that knowing the use "grasps nothing yet" about the origin. In the stipulated case the two answers happen to coincide, but the coincidence is learned from the history of the tool (it was made for this and never taken over), not from its present use. The rule's point, that the present use is not evidence for the origin, holds in the case exactly as in the case of punishment: the tool's present use would be the same if it had been made for something else and later redirected, so the present use cannot distinguish the two histories.

**What the rule claims in general.** (P1) that whatever exists is liable to takeover and reinterpretation by a superior power; (P2) that every takeover imposes a new meaning and obscures the old; (C) that the present use is therefore the sign of the last takeover and not evidence of the origin. It is a claim about the *evidential* relation between use and origin, plus an empirical claim about how often takeovers happen in the organic and social world (II:12 says "again and again").

**What would falsify it.** The evidential claim would be falsified by a class of cases in which present use reliably predicted origin, that is, in which the teleological inference from function to genesis was found to be a good one across organs, institutions and customs; II:12 denies this and names the eye and the hand as the paradigm errors. The empirical claim would be falsified by a record in which institutions typically kept one meaning from origin to present. II:13's eleven meanings for one procedure are offered as evidence against the second, "on the basis of a relatively small and accidental material", which is to say as a sample and not a proof.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take Preface §4 as a source about Paul Rée and run the sourcing pass on it. Metadata: author, date, audience, purpose. Then: what does §4 *record* about Rée's method that can be used as evidence; what does it *argue*; what does it *omit* that another source (the Preface's own §2, and the chapter of Janaway's study on Rée) would supply; and how does its provenance change what it can be used to establish?

<Solution>
**Metadata.** Author: Nietzsche, the book's former companion and, in *Human, All Too Human*, its interlocutor. Date: July 1887, ten years after the book. Audience: readers of Nietzsche's previous books, whom §8 presupposes. Purpose: to introduce three treatises on the same subject as an alternative, "not by refuting" but by "putting the more probable in place of the improbable".

**Records.** That the book was published in 1877 with the title *Der Ursprung der moralischen Empfindungen*; that its method is a genealogical hypothesis of "the English kind"; that it identifies altruistic valuation with moral valuation as such; that it holds the deterrent purpose of punishment to be essential and original; that it draws on Darwin (§7). These are checkable against the book and are the usable evidence.

**Argues.** That the method is "inverted and perverse"; that Nietzsche said No to it "sentence by sentence"; that it is hypothesis-mongering "into the blue" with no documents; that Nietzsche had already, in his own earlier books, set the more probable beside it. The verdicts are the author's and are what the source cannot establish about the book.

**Omits.** The friendship and the collaboration: §2 records the Sorrento winter of 1876–77 without naming Rée as its companion, and §4 presents him only as the author of the book; the common ground, that both men treat morality as something with a natural history rather than a supernatural one, which is what made Rée's book the "first" statement Nietzsche could position his own against; and the specific arguments of Rée's chapters, which §4 summarizes in one clause. Janaway's chapter on Nietzsche and Rée is the place those are laid out.

**Use, not bias.** §4 is a hostile witness writing an introduction to his own competing account. It establishes what Nietzsche took Rée's method to be and what he set against it, which is exactly what this lesson needs from it; it establishes nothing about whether Rée's mechanism is right, and its silence on the common ground means the reader who has only §4 will overestimate the distance between the two books. The provenance makes §4 a first-rate source for Nietzsche's method and a second-rate source for Rée's.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Samuel's 1913 translation renders II:12's *die Ursache der Entstehung eines Dings* as "the origin of the existence of a thing" and *Zweck* as "end". Compare the German with Samuel and with any modern translation you can reach (Clark and Swensen, Kaufmann and Hollingdale, or Diethe), and decide which English pair, origin/purpose, descent/aim, or source/end, best preserves the *toto caelo* contrast. State why the choice matters for the rule.

<Solution>
**The German.** The rule contrasts *die Ursache der Entstehung* ("the cause of the coming-into-being") with *Nützlichkeit*, *Verwendung* and *Einordnung in ein System von Zwecken* ("usefulness, employment, placement in a system of purposes"). The first term names a causal event in the past; the second names a present function. The section's opening pairs *Ursprung* with *Zweck*.

**Samuel.** "The origin of the existence of a thing" for *Ursache der Entstehung* loses the word *cause* and doubles "origin" and "existence"; "end" for *Zweck* is accurate but in 1913 English carries "final cause", which imports the teleology the section denies. Samuel also renders *Zeichen-Kette* as "sign-chain", which is exact.

**Modern renderings.** Kaufmann and Hollingdale (1967) have "the cause of the origin of a thing and its eventual utility, its actual employment and place in a system of purposes", which keeps *cause* and reads "purposes" for *Zwecken*; this is the rendering Nehamas quotes. Clark and Swensen (1998) and Diethe (1994) likewise keep "cause" and use "purpose" or "purposes". Whatever edition is to hand, the check is whether *Ursache* survives.

**The verdict.** "Origin/purpose" preserves the contrast best, provided "origin" is read with its cause: the rule opposes a causal event to a present function, and "descent/aim" softens the first term into a lineage while "source/end" hardens the second into a final cause. The choice matters because the rule is *anti-teleological*: its claim is that a present purpose is not a cause of coming-into-being. A translation that renders *Zweck* as "end" and drops *Ursache* moves the sentence toward the very picture, function as final cause, that it exists to reject, and a reader reconstructing the rule from that English would find the contrast weaker than the German states it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Preface §4 and GM I:2 report that the English derive "good" from the usefulness of unegoistic actions to those who received them. Write out the premise about *who first used the word "good"* that this derivation presupposes, and state what evidence would be needed to establish it. Then say what the rule of II:12 implies about the derivation's use of the word's present sense.

<Solution>
**The presupposed premise.** That the word "good" was first applied by the *recipients* of unegoistic actions, to those actions, from the standpoint of their own benefit. The derivation cannot start otherwise: "useful to those to whom they were shown" locates the first speakers among the beneficiaries. A second premise rides with it: that "good" at its first use meant "useful to me", so that the later sense, intrinsic goodness, is a forgetting of that meaning.

**The evidence needed.** A document in §7's sense showing the earliest use of the word: its etymology, and the social position of those who used it first. The derivation offers none; it offers the present association of "good" with "altruistic" and reasons backwards from it.

**What II:12 implies.** The derivation infers the origin of "good" from its present use, which is the inference the rule forbids: the present sense, "good" as unegoistic, is by II:12 the sign of the last will to impose a meaning on the word, not evidence about who coined it. So the English derivation is a hypothesis-history in the exact sense of the Preface, and its first premise is open.

**The doorstep.** GM I:2 denies precisely the premise about who spoke first: "the judgment 'good' did *not* originate among those to whom goodness was shown"; it was "the good themselves, that is, the noble, the powerful", who "felt and posited themselves and their actions as good". The etymological evidence I:4–5 offers for that claim, and the grade it earns, are the first business of <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the next lesson</Ref>.
</Solution>

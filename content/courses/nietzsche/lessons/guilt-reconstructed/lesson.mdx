Leiter's commentary compresses the Second Treatise into three stages — a bred memory of the will, cruelty turned inward, and that cruelty moralized by the free-will lie — and the test of the naturalist reading is whether the twenty-five sections fit those stages without remainder. This lesson is the module's application, in the genre the course uses for one: a published scholarly result, the three-stage reconstruction of GM II in chapter 7 of Brian Leiter's *Nietzsche on Morality* (2002; 2nd ed. 2015), is restated as an explicit argument with a section beside each premise and then checked against the primary text section by section. The check produces a scorecard: sections a stage fits, sections a stage must deflate, sections a stage leaves unused, and sections that are not a stage at all. The scorecard is the lesson's result, and its format is what the course's final audit reuses.

The course's argument arrives here with the Second Treatise in three pieces and one dispute. <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">The promising-animal lesson</Ref> reconstructed II:1–3 as a bred memory of the will and left II:2's sovereign individual between two readings, Leiter's natural artefact and R. Lanier Anderson's achieved self; <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref> ran the chain of II:4–8 and 19–22 from *Schulden* to a debt before God and located the step at which owing becomes being guilty (II:21) as a step that borrows the doer of GM I:13 without arguing for it; <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">the internalization lesson</Ref> reconstructed II:16–18 as a mechanism with two products, self-cruelty and the ideals, and closed by naming three remainders for this lesson to carry: II:2's fruit, II:11's man of ressentiment who "has the invention of the bad conscience on his conscience", and II:19's pregnancy. What stands open is the order in which the three pieces combine and whether a psychology of types can hold all twenty-five sections in that order. What this lesson adds to the spine is the verdict for the naturalist reading on this treatise: it holds, at the price of one paragraph read as rhetoric and one product carried unused, and the sovereign individual is where the price is paid. The Third Treatise's opening lesson, <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">on the ascetic ideal across types</Ref>, takes up what the treatise's own last sections say the moralized guilt still lacks.

The route: the reconstruction stated as numbered premises with citations; the check, stage by stage, with the sections each stage lights and leaves; II:2 adjudicated sentence by sentence between Leiter and Anderson; the remainders, II:18's pregnancy and II:11's man of ressentiment; Christopher Janaway's grid as the rival reconstruction, with Clark and Dudrick's as a third column; the treatise's own hand-off in II:21–25; and the scorecard with the verdict.

## The reconstruction stated: three stages, twenty of twenty-five sections, one borrowed premise

A reconstruction is an argument with its sources attached, and the course's protocol from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> requires that each premise carry its section. Leiter's reconstruction is stated below in the course's own words, since the book is cited at chapter level. The three stages, the deflation of the sovereign individual and the claim that Christianity maximizes indebtedness are the reading Leiter restates in his 2011 paper on II:2, the reading Bernard Reginster's 2003 review of the first edition names as the book's Second-Essay mechanism, "internalized cruelty", and the account of the difference between bad conscience and guilt-consciousness that Christopher Janaway's 2007 chapter cites as one of three on record; the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on Nietzsche places the book's treatment of guilt at pp. 178–95 of the second edition.

<Remark title="Leiter's three stages (Nietzsche on Morality, ch. 7), as an argument">
**Stage 1, conscience (II:1–3).** *P1.* An animal that can promise must first have been made calculable, and the morality of custom, enforced by the social straitjacket over the whole prehistoric period, made it so (II:2, citing *Daybreak* §9). *P2.* The means was pain: "only what does not cease to hurt stays in the memory", and the penal catalogue is the record of the technique (II:3). *C1.* The memory of the will, the pre-moral conscience, is a bred capacity with a history of cruelty behind it (II:1).

**Stage 2, bad conscience (II:16–18).** *P3.* Man was enclosed, suddenly and by force, within "the walls of society and of peace", and the bulwarks of the enclosure, punishments above all, blocked the outward discharge of his instincts of hostility and cruelty (II:16–17). *P4.* An instinct that cannot discharge outward discharges inward (II:16, the hypothesis). *C2.* Cruelty acquired the animal itself as its object; the inner world gained "depth, breadth, height"; this self-directed cruelty is the bad conscience, at great cost and as the birth of psychological interiority (II:16, II:18).

**Stage 3, guilt (II:19–22, with I:13).** *P5.* The private-law relation of debtor and creditor was reinterpreted as the relation of the living to their ancestors, then to gods, until the Christian God, "the maximal god attained so far", brought "the maximum of guilt-feeling" (II:19–20). *P6.* The man of bad conscience seized the religious presupposition as an instrument: a debt toward God that no punishment can balance (II:21–22). *P7 (borrowed).* The sufferer takes himself to have been able to do otherwise: a doer behind the deed, free to express strength or not (I:13; *Twilight* VI:7). *C3.* Guilt proper is bad conscience moralized: self-cruelty read as punishment the sufferer deserves, before a creditor who guarantees that it is always deserved. Its maximum is reached in Christianity, whose creator myth makes the debt unpayable and the debtor culpable (II:21).

**What the reconstruction is for.** Leiter's commentary on the First Treatise leaves a gap it names: ressentiment explains the inversion of values and not its victory, since nothing in the First Treatise says why the strong would ever accept a valuation that condemns them. Stages 2 and 3 are the first half of the answer: the strong, once enclosed, have a cruelty with nowhere to go, and a reading of that cruelty as deserved is available to them. The second half is the Third Treatise's priest.

**The premise's status.** P7 is the only premise from outside the treatise, and the First Treatise argued it false. It enters stage 3 as a belief the debtor holds, not as a fact the reconstruction asserts, which is what <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref> found in II:21–22 before the reconstruction was named.
</Remark>

Two features of the shape decide what the test can find. The stages are ordered by what a self-directed force is *read as*: pain read as a memory (stage 1), cruelty with the self as its object (stage 2), that cruelty read as deserved punishment (stage 3). Each stage therefore keeps the previous one's product and adds an interpretation to it, which is II:12's rule from <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>, that a thing's use is imposed by whatever will currently masters it, applied to the cruelty itself. And the ordering is tighter than the treatise's own: the sections run 1–3, 4–15, 16–18, 19–25, and the reconstruction reads them 1–3, 16–18, then 4–8 folded into 19–22, so the twelve sections on debt, community, justice and punishment are moved, and the test has to say where they went.

<KeyFact>
The reconstruction has one premise from outside the Second Treatise, it stands at the third stage, and it is the premise the First Treatise argued false: the Second Treatise's account of guilt does not add a mechanism to I:13's result but shows what the fiction is for. Everything the naturalist reading can claim for GM II as mechanism-plus-evidence stops at the point where the debtor comes to believe he could have paid.
</KeyFact>

### Stage 1 fits II:1 and II:3, and uses II:2 for its process only

The first stage is a paraphrase of the promising-animal lesson's result with one subtraction. II:1's two faculties, forgetting as a positive inhibition and the memory of the will as its bred counter-faculty, are the stage's object; II:3's mnemonics of pain, "one burns something in so that it stays in the memory", are its mechanism; and II:2 supplies the agent, the morality of custom, whose labour "really made" man calculable. All three are used as <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">that lesson</Ref> reconstructed them, at the grade it assigned: the mnemonic use is documented as a technique and not established as the origin of punishment, by II:12's own rule.

What the stage does not use is the second paragraph of II:2. The sovereign individual, "liberated again from the morality of custom", "this master of the free will, this sovereign", who names his dominating instinct conscience, is the fruit of the process the stage describes, and the stage stops at the process. The subtraction is deliberate: Leiter's 2011 paper on the passage calls the figure "a fortuitous natural artifact (a bit of 'fate'), not an autonomous achievement for which anyone could be responsible", and on that reading the paragraph adds a proud name to a bred regularity and no further mechanism. The check records the fact without yet judging it: stage 1 is II:1–3 minus the treatise's only occurrence of the sovereign individual.

### Stage 2 fits II:16–17, and uses II:18 for its mechanism only

The second stage is II:16's hypothesis with II:17's presuppositions attached, exactly as <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">the internalization lesson</Ref> graded them: instincts are conserved and discharge inward when the outward direction is blocked; the enclosure was sudden, imposed and complete; the conquest by a pack is one supplier of those three properties and is separable from the mechanism. Leiter's version keeps the ambivalence II:16 insists on, that the inward turn is "a self-inflicted wound that is nonetheless the birth of psychological interiority", and the stage's phrase for the product, "an inner psychic depth", is II:16's "depth, breadth, height".

What the stage does not use is what II:18 credits the same force with. II:18 identifies the inward force with the state-building force, "in my language: the will to power", and the stage uses that identification; the same section then calls the bad conscience "the true womb of ideal and imaginative events", credits it with "an abundance of new, strange beauty and affirmation, and perhaps beauty itself", and derives from it "the value of the unegoistic". A three-stage history of a sickness has a place for bad conscience as guilt's material and no place for it as the ground of the ideals, and II:19's first sentence, "a sickness as pregnancy is a sickness", names both. The stage cites II:18 for its mechanism and leaves its yield.

### Stage 3 fits II:19–22, absorbs II:4–11, and needs II:14–15 as a premise it does not name

The third stage is where the reconstruction has moved the most text, and the check has to account for three groups of sections. II:19–22 are the stage's own: the debt reinterpreted upward until the creditor is the Christian God (II:19–20), the moralization named in II:21's parenthesis as "the entanglement of the bad conscience with the concept of god (*die Verwicklung des schlechten Gewissens mit dem Gottesbegriffe*)", and II:22's man of bad conscience who "has seized upon the religious presupposition (*hat sich der religiösen Voraussetzung bemächtigt*)" so that "a debt toward God" becomes "his instrument of torture". The stage's ceiling is II:20's sentence: "the rise of the Christian god, as the maximal god attained so far, has therefore also brought the maximum of guilt-feeling on earth into appearance."

II:4–8 enter the stage as material. The stage's guilt is a debt read as deserved, and the debt is the creditor–debtor relation of II:4–5 with its equivalence of injury and the creditor's pleasure, escalated in II:19–21; without II:5's "warrant for and title to cruelty" there is no rightful inflicter for the self to become. II:9–11 enter as the route: the community holds the creditor's standing by transfer from the person (II:9), and that transfer is the first link of the chain the ancestors and the god continue. Neither group is a fourth stage, and the debt lesson's chain of substitutions is what the reconstruction folds into its third.

II:14–15 are the group the reconstruction uses without naming, and the check makes the use explicit. Janaway records that Leiter calls the material on punishment "somewhat tangential" to the treatise's argument, and for II:9–13 the description is defensible. For II:14–15 it is not. Those sections establish that punishment "hardens and cools", produces prudence and a Spinozist regret, "here something has unexpectedly gone wrong", and never the feeling of deserving; "it was not on this soil that grew the 'bad conscience'". That is a negative premise the third stage cannot do without: if punishment produced guilt, guilt would descend through the punished debtor as such and stage 2 would be idle. Because it does not, guilt has to arrive by a debtor who is his own inflicter, which is stage 2's product, and by a reading of the infliction as deserved, which is stage 3's. The reconstruction's route is forced by II:14–15, and a version of it that calls those sections tangential has mislabelled one of its own premises.

<Remark title="the reconstruction's unnamed premise">
**P8 (II:14–15).** Punishment does not produce the feeling of guilt in the punished; it produces hardness, prudence and a longer memory.

**What it does in the argument.** It closes the direct route from stage 1 to stage 3. A bred memory of "I will nots" (stage 1) plus a creditor's punishment (II:4–8) would yield a calculating debtor and never a guilty one, so the moralization of stage 3 has to act on a cruelty that is already self-directed, and stage 2 is a necessary link rather than an interlude. Without P8 the three stages could be two.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Twenty of the twenty-five sections fit a stage once II:4–11 are read as stage 3's material and route and II:14–15 as its unnamed premise; the five that do not are II:12–13, which state the method the reconstruction runs on, II:24–25, which prophesy, and the one paragraph of II:2 the first stage stops before. The remainders are therefore not a scattered residue but two named passages and one product, and the naturalist reading is tested on exactly those.
</KeyFact>

## The sovereign individual adjudicated: II:2 sentence by sentence

The promising-animal lesson sorted II:2's paragraph into two vocabularies, the artefact vocabulary of a process that yields a type and the achievement vocabulary of a standing earned and held, and stated the two readings that divide them without deciding. The reconstruction forces the decision, since stage 1 keeps the first column and drops the second, and the check above records that drop as the first remainder. The adjudication runs on the paragraph's sentences, asking of each what Leiter must say, what Anderson must say, and whether the text itself decides.

### What each reading must say about the other's sentences

Leiter's reading takes the first column literally and the second as honorifics: "free", "autonomous", "sovereign", "privilege" and "deserves" keep the approval they carry in the morality under attack and acquire a new content, a bred reliability, which is Charles Stevenson's persuasive definition applied to the language of freedom. On that reading the sentence it must stretch is "liberated again from the morality of custom (*das von der Sittlichkeit der Sitte wieder losgekommene*)", which can only mean that the type no longer needs the straitjacket that made it; the promising-animal lesson's image was a spring that stays compressed after the clamp is removed.

Anderson's reading takes the second column literally: the sovereign individual is "a possible type defined by the capacity of a whole self to assume a diachronically stable attitude of commitment, which persists through the alterations of the individual drives", and individuality here "is not merely a thin, descriptive property possessed automatically by every single human being; on the contrary, it is a rare and high achievement". On that reading the sentences it must supply are "the ripest fruit" of a process society ran and "has become an instinct": the process gave the material and the self did the integrating, and "instinct" is how the achievement feels once complete. The paragraph says neither of those things, and the reading adds them.

The tally in the figure is the check's result and not a verdict. Two sentences are literal only for Leiter, one is literal only for Anderson, and three are readable either way: *errungen*, won by struggle, is one verb against a paragraph of breeding vocabulary and cannot carry the case alone; "privilege" and "deserves" cohere with a ranking among types as well as with a relabelling; and "this master of the free will" is decided by what "free" means, which is I:13's question and not II:2's.

### The two replies, and the weight of a paragraph that occurs once

Each side has answered the other, and the answers are on the record. Leiter's 2014 review of the volume in which Anderson's chapter appeared asks whether the emergent self, "a numerically distinct psychological object over and above its constituent drives", answers Nietzsche's actual target, the eliminativist materialism of the 1850s, or begs the question against a reading on which drives and their ranking explain everything the self is credited with. Anderson had raised the same worry himself against a transcendental reading of the self, calling it "potentially question-begging against the Leiter-style naturalist", and his own argument is built to avoid it: the self is the drives' mutual recruitment, not a thing posited above them.

His counter is the case of weakness of will. A person who acts against her own considered assessment is intelligible only if the assessment belongs to something other than the drive that won, and "criticism of weakness of will and related forms of evaluative inconsistency are central to Nietzsche's core philosophical stances", so that "eliminative naturalism about the self lacks the resources to make sense of these complaints". His 2022 paper restates the reading for II:2 through Bernard Williams's distinction between two senses of responsibility, one tied to voluntary action and one to an ambitious conception of responsible agency, and reads the sovereign individual's "privilege of responsibility" as the second: autonomy as the overcoming of psychological weakness by unifying the self, which needs no chooser.

Leiter's strongest point is not a reading of any sentence but a count. His 2011 paper opens on it: most readers of the *Genealogy* "would, one suspects, be astonished to discover the prominence recent scholarship has given to the enigmatic figure of the 'sovereign individual,' who appears just once, in section 2 of the second essay, and never appears again, in the *Genealogy* or indeed anywhere else in the corpus". Janaway, who does not deflate the passage, agrees on the consequence: the treatise from II:4 onward can be followed "without trespassing further into an elucidation of the sovereign individual", and nothing after II:3 says the man of bad conscience is one. The weight the passage is asked to carry is therefore out of proportion to its footprint, and a reading that makes II:2 the key to Nietzsche on freedom has to explain why the key is used once.

### The verdict, as fact

The paragraph underdetermines the dispute. Three of its sentences are decided only by what "free" means, and that is fixed outside it, by I:13's argument that the substratum "free to express strength or not" is a fiction added to the deed and by BGE 21's verdict that there are "only strong and weak wills". Given those results, which <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref> established as the course's, the deflation is the cheaper reading: it reads II:2 with the premises the book has already argued, it adds no faculty, and it costs one paragraph, which it must call rhetoric.

The achievement reading costs more: it must supply a mechanism of self-integration the paragraph does not describe, read "instinct" against its sense, and carry a self that the drive-reductive reading says it has not earned. What it buys is not nothing, and the course records the purchase: an account of broken promises and weak wills as gaps between a whole and its parts, which the deflation has to explain as one drive defeating another.

That purchase is not needed here. Stage 3's debtor, who believes he could have paid, is the opposite of a sovereign individual; the one type in the treatise who vouches for himself and despises the whippets is the one type who does not feel guilt, and the reconstruction of guilt runs without him.

<KeyFact>
Deflating II:2 costs the reconstruction of guilt nothing and costs the course one paragraph, and the paragraph does not stay deflated: <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref> and <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the value-creation lesson</Ref> need a self that can call something good, and the question whether a bred type can do that is the question II:2 poses in a hymn. The verdict here is local: on the Second Treatise, the naturalist reading holds by reading the hymn as a type's self-report.
</KeyFact>

## The remainders: II:18's pregnancy and II:11's man of ressentiment

Two passages the check marked are not disputes between readings but places where the text says more than three stages of a sickness can carry, and the course's rule is to record what a reading loses if it drops them and whether anything in the reading forces the drop.

### The pregnancy: what the naturalist reading loses if it keeps only the sickness

II:18 credits the inward-turned force with "negative ideals", with "an abundance of new, strange beauty and affirmation", and with "the value of the unegoistic", and II:19 fixes the treatise's verdict in one simile: the bad conscience is "a sickness, as pregnancy is a sickness (*eine Krankheit, wie die Schwangerschaft eine Krankheit ist*)". The three stages use bad conscience once, as the material that the religious presupposition is seized to torture, and a reader who takes the stages for the treatise leaves with a mechanism that produces guilt and nothing else. The internalization lesson stated the loss for the course: the revaluation of suffering in <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the nihilism lesson</Ref> and the sublimation of <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref> presuppose that the "artist's cruelty" of II:18 is productive, and a Second Treatise flattened to a sickness has no place for either.

Nothing in the three stages forces the flattening. The stages are a history of how one product was read; they are silent on the second product rather than opposed to it, and a naturalist who keeps II:18's yield has to add no faculty and no premise, only the observation, which Leiter's own account of the higher type makes elsewhere, that suffering is causally linked to creation. Reginster's review of the first edition presses exactly this link as under-argued, and the pressure is fair: whether suffering causes creativity or the creative merely also suffer is a question the *Genealogy* does not settle. But that is a question about the second product's evidence, not about whether the reconstruction can carry it. The pregnancy is a remainder, a claim the stages leave on the table, and not a refutation.

### The man of ressentiment: II:11 against II:17

The second remainder is a tension inside the text, and the reconstruction inherits it rather than creating it. II:11 ends its attack on Eugen Dühring, the Berlin philosopher whose thesis that justice grows from the feeling of having been injured <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref> saw the section refute, with a guess about authorship: "one can already guess who has the invention of the 'bad conscience' on his conscience: the man of *ressentiment*! (*wer überhaupt die Erfindung des "schlechten Gewissens" auf dem Gewissen hat, — der Mensch des Ressentiment!*)". II:16 makes the caged animal the "inventor" of the bad conscience, and II:17 requires that the enclosure was met with "no struggle and not even a *ressentiment*". Read as claims about one event, the sentences contradict: the bad conscience was invented either by the affect the <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">slave-revolt lesson</Ref> defined, a No said to something outside oneself, or by an animal that had no one to say No to.

The three stages have a way of reading II:11 that removes the contradiction, and it is worth stating because it costs something. If "bad conscience" in II:11 means the moralized form, stage 3's guilt, then the man of ressentiment invented not the inward turn but its reading as deserved, and the sentence is a preview of the Third Treatise's priest, who tells the sufferer "you yourself are to blame". Janaway notes that Nietzsche uses "bad conscience" for both the raw internalization and the developed guilt-consciousness, and GM III:20 confirms the double use by calling internalization "the feeling of guilt in its raw state". The cost is that II:11 is then not evidence for stage 2 at all, and the man of ressentiment enters stage 3 as an agent the three stages have no box for. Reginster's third criticism of the book is that "three essays, three mechanisms" is too tidy and that the mechanisms interpenetrate; II:11 is the sentence in which the First Treatise's affect appears inside the Second's history, and the reconstruction accommodates it only by reading it forward into the Third.

<Remark title="the two remainders, graded">
**II:18's products.** Left unused by the stages; not contradicted by them; needed later by the course. Grade: *a remainder the reading can keep at no cost*.

**II:11's man of ressentiment.** Contradicts II:16–17 if "bad conscience" means the same thing in both; consistent if II:11 means the moralized form. Grade: *a tension the reading resolves by assigning the sentence to stage 3 and importing an agent the three stages do not name*; the agent is the Third Treatise's business.
</Remark>

## Two reconstructions, one section list: Leiter's stages against Janaway's grid

The grid is not a sequence, and placing the three stages on it shows where the two reconstructions agree and where the disagreement is a real one.

### Where the stages sit on the grid

Stage 2 is cell (b), "simple cruelty to self": the caged animal of II:16, the cell Janaway's schema predicts and the text describes as "the bad conscience in its beginning". Stage 3 is cell (d), "punishment of self, conceived as rightful recipient of suffering": guilt. Stage 1 has no cell. The grid begins with a cruelty that is already the animal's and asks only where it is directed and how it is read; the bred memory and the calculable animal of II:1–3 are what the grid presupposes, the condition under which there is a self to be enclosed and a debtor to be held to account. The two reconstructions therefore agree on the treatise's middle and end and differ on whether its beginning is a stage or a precondition, and that is a small difference: Janaway sets II:1–3 aside, Leiter counts them, and neither uses the sovereign individual.

### Whether I:13 is still needed without a moralization stage

The grid has no separate "moralization by the free-will lie". Legitimation, the column the debt chain supplies, does the work: to read one's self-cruelty as *rightful* punishment is already to read oneself as one who deserves it. Janaway is explicit about what that reading contains: "I cannot feel guilty unless I believe that there is something I have done which I truly ought not to have done, that I have violated an obligation that I conceive myself genuinely and rightly to be under", and the order of the story is that "the need to inflict cruelty on oneself comes first, the incentive to conceive oneself as a legitimate recipient of cruelty comes second as a way of giving meaning to self-cruelty, and the invention of reasons why one deserves cruelty comes last".

"Genuinely and rightly under" an obligation one has violated is desert, and desert of *punishment* is the retributive idea II:4 dated late, "he could have acted otherwise"; the belief that fills cell (d) is the doer of I:13 under another description. So the answer to the brief's question is that I:13 is still needed, and that the two reconstructions place it differently: Leiter names it as the premise of a stage, Janaway carries it inside the word "rightful" without naming it. The course's verdict from the debt lesson stands for both: the treatise contains the step's location, agent and instrument, and no argument for the belief, because the belief is one the book has already declared a lie.

What Janaway calls "moralization" is then something further down the track: not the transformation of indebtedness into guilt-feeling, which he and Aaron Ridley argue predates any belief in God, against Mathias Risse's reading on which no one feels guilt before Christianity, but "the elevation of feeling guilty into a virtue", its incorporation into what the morally good person is, by means of the metaphysical picture in which the animal self stands in perpetual transgression against an infinite creditor. That is II:21's "entanglement of the bad conscience with the concept of god" read as a claim about *value* rather than about *feeling*, and it is a genuine disagreement with the three stages about what the word names: for Leiter the moralization is the arrival of desert, for Janaway it is the arrival of the judgment that deserving it is good. The scorecard records the disagreement; the text of II:21, whose glosses on the word Janaway calls "brief and confusing", does not decide it.

### A third column: Clark and Dudrick's genealogy of the person

Maudemarie Clark and David Dudrick's commentary on *Beyond Good and Evil* reads GM II as a genealogy of the political order among the drives, the soul of BGE 12, and their account of II:19–22 gives stage 3 a product the other two reconstructions do not. On their reading the debt to ancestors is the aggressive drives venting on other drives, the sacrifice of the first-born on the drive to nurture, and the escalation to a God who is "the most extreme opposite" of the animal instincts is the point at which "the concepts of guilt and debt are moralized in a way that allows for categorical commitments": only now can a human being act "not because doing so will satisfy some antecedent desire but because they take doing so to be good".

Moralization is on this reading not a lie grafted on but the birth of the person, the being that can act from values, and the sovereign individual is an achieved order among the drives rather than an artefact or an achievement of a self. The reading is one column of the audit and not a verdict: it supplies stage 3 with a positive product as II:18 supplies stage 2 with one, and it reads "sovereign" politically, as the promising-animal lesson's aside anticipated; what it owes is the account of how rank among drives becomes authority, which <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drive-self lesson</Ref> puts to BGE 19.

<KeyFact>
Three reconstructions, one dependence. Leiter's stages need I:13 as a named premise, Janaway's grid needs it inside "rightful", and Clark and Dudrick's genealogy of the person needs it as the content of the first categorical commitment; the Second Treatise's account of guilt cannot be stated by any of them without a debtor who believes he could have paid, and that belief is the fiction the First Treatise dismantled.
</KeyFact>

## II:21–25: the maximal debt, and where the treatise points

The reconstruction ends at II:22, and the last thing it claims, that Christianity maximizes indebtedness through the creator myth, is the treatise's own claim in II:20–21: the maximal god brings the maximum of guilt-feeling, and the debt, instead of lapsing with faith in the creditor, is "pushed back into the *bad* conscience (*mit ihrer Zurückschiebung in's schlechte Gewissen*)", turned against the debtor as unredeemable and against the creditor as the source of a curse, until "God himself sacrificing himself for the debt of man" settles it from the creditor's side. The debt lesson read that chain and its self-refuting prediction of a "second innocence"; the check adds only that the reconstruction's ceiling and the treatise's coincide, and that the coincidence is the reconstruction's strongest fit.

### II:23: the Greeks are stage 2 without stage 3

The three sections after II:22 are not a stage, and the internalization lesson read them at length; what the check records is what they do to the three stages. II:23 is a control case: the Greeks "made use of their gods precisely in order to keep the 'bad conscience' at bay", explained an outrage by one of their own with "folly, not sin", and so had stage 2's product without stage 3's. Leiter's contrast between Greek shame, which projects blame outward onto the gods, and Christian guilt, which turns it inward on a norm one supposedly could have met, is this section restated, and it is the reconstruction's evidence that the third stage is a stage and not a restatement of the second: a people with the bad conscience and no guilt is a state a two-stage account has no cell for.

### II:24–25: a prophecy, not a stage

II:24–25 are a prophecy: "a reversed attempt would be possible in itself, but who is strong enough for it?", a type "strengthened by wars and victories" with "the great health", "this Antichrist and Antinihilist, this victor over God and nothingness: *he must come one day*", and then silence in favour of Zarathustra. The course's rule from the protocol lesson is that the dramatized register is cited for what it stages and not for doctrine, and II:25 is the argued register handing the task over and saying so.

What the prophecy states, in the treatise's own words, is the limit of what three stages explain. A debt read as deserved is an instrument of torture in the hands of the man of bad conscience; the sections say nothing about who put the instrument in his hands, who keeps it there, or what the keeping is for. "Man has for too long regarded his natural inclinations with an 'evil eye'": the wedding of the inward cruelty to the *natural* inclinations is an attachment, not the mechanism, and an attachment has an author. The Second Treatise ends by naming the reversal and the type who could make it, and leaves the author of the present attachment to the Third Treatise, which opens on the question what ascetic ideals mean.

### What the application used, and where the text departs from the reconstruction

The module's results enter the scorecard as follows. From <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">II:1–3</Ref>: the memory of the will as a bred counter-faculty, the morality of custom as its agent, pain as its technique, graded as a documented technique and not an origin; used whole by stage 1. From <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">II:4–8 and 19–22</Ref>: the equivalence of injury and the creditor's pleasure, the chain of substitutions with the creditor as the one variable, and the moral step located in II:21 with its borrowed premise; used by stage 3 as material, chain and conclusion. From <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">II:16–18</Ref>: the conservation of instincts, the three properties of the enclosure separated from the conquest that supplies them, and the identification of the inward force with the will to power; used by stage 2. From <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">I:13</Ref>: the doer added to the deed; used as stage 3's premise, as a belief.

The reconstruction is clean and the text is not, and the departures are the scorecard's off-diagonal cells. II:2's fruit carries achievement vocabulary the deflation explains as a type's self-report, at the cost of one paragraph. II:18–19 give bad conscience a second product the stages do not use, and the course carries it forward. II:11 places the First Treatise's affect inside the Second's history, and the stages read the sentence forward into the Third. II:14–15 are a premise the reconstruction runs on and does not name. II:23–25 are a control case and a prophecy, and neither is a stage. Against those five departures stand twenty sections that fit, and the fit is tightest exactly where the treatise is most explicit: II:16's hypothesis, II:21's parenthesis, II:22's recapitulation, II:20's maximal god.

### The verdict, and what the next lesson takes up

What is established. Leiter's three stages, stated as an argument with a section beside each premise, fit twenty of the Second Treatise's twenty-five sections once II:4–11 are read as stage 3's material and route and II:14–15 as its unnamed negative premise. The reconstruction's one premise from outside the treatise is I:13's doer, entering at the third stage as a belief the debtor holds, and every rival reconstruction in the course's literature needs the same belief under a different name. II:2's sovereign individual is underdetermined by its own sentences, decided by I:13 in favour of the deflation, and unneeded by the reconstruction of guilt, whose debtor is the type the sovereign individual is not; the deflation costs one paragraph and is the naturalist reading's first paid price. II:18's second product and II:11's man of ressentiment are remainders the reading can carry, the first at no cost and the second by reading the sentence into the Third Treatise. II:23 is the control case that shows stage 3 is a stage, and II:24–25 name what the stages do not explain: who administers the moralized guilt, and to what end.

The naturalist reading therefore holds for the Second Treatise, as a psychology of types that renders II:1–3, II:16–18 and II:19–22 as mechanism plus evidence, at the price the spine named in advance: the sovereign individual read as a bred type with a proud name. What it has not explained is the victory. A cruelty read as deserved is an instrument; the Second Treatise leaves it in the sufferer's own hands and says, in II:24, that its attachment to the natural inclinations has lasted "too long". <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">The next lesson</Ref> opens the Third Treatise on its first question, what ascetic ideals mean, and finds that the same ideal is a different thing in an artist, a philosopher and a priest; <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref> then names the administrator.

Reconstruct Anderson's reading of II:2 as an explicit argument with three premises, one about the paragraph's vocabulary, one about what a self built from drives is, and one about what "sovereign" ranks. <Solution>
**Anderson's argument.**

*P1 (vocabulary).* II:2 describes its individual in the language of achievement and standing: "what has here at last been achieved (*errungen*) and become flesh in him", "the extraordinary privilege of responsibility", "he 'deserves' all three", "his measure of value". Achievement vocabulary reports an achievement unless there is reason to read it otherwise.

*P2 (the emergent self).* A self built out of drives and affects is not identical to its strongest drive: drives and affects recruit one another, so they are functionally bound in one repository, and the whole can be at odds with one of its parts; that is what weakness of will and a broken promise are. II:1's "memory of the will", which persists while "a world of new strange things, circumstances, even acts of will" is interposed, names a whole that holds a commitment through changes of the drives.

*C.* The sovereign individual is an ideal of self-legislation, and II:2 presents it as one.

The dispute therefore turns on the one premise the paragraph cannot settle, which is why the lesson's verdict is underdetermination, broken by I:13 in favour of the deflation.

**The German.** *Moralisirung* is a noun of process formed on *moralisiren*, and its object is *dieser Begriffe*, "these concepts", Schuld and Pflicht: what is moralized is a pair of concepts, not a feeling. *Zurückschiebung* is "a pushing back", and the direction is *in's Gewissen* in the parenthesis and *in's schlechte Gewissen* in the next sentence: the concepts are pushed back *into* something that is already there. <Solution>
**What is lacking.** *An administrator.* The mechanism produces a sufferer who reads his cruelty as deserved, and says nothing about who directed the reading onto the natural inclinations in particular, who maintains it, and in whose interest; II:24's "man has for too long regarded his natural inclinations with an 'evil eye'" describes an attachment that has lasted and names no one who made it last. *An end.* The mechanism explains why the sufferer wants an instrument of torture, since his cruelty must discharge somewhere, but not what the torture is *for* beyond its own discharge, and so not why a whole population would adopt a valuation that condemns its strongest members; the First Treatise's gap, why the strong went along, is narrowed by the Second and not closed.

**Why the man of bad conscience cannot supply them.** II:22 makes him the *subject* of the seizing and describes the state as "a kind of madness of the will"; a madness does not administer itself, and a will that "throws every No he says to himself out of himself as a Yes, as God" is the patient of the reading, not its author. II:23 shows that the reading is not forced by the mechanism, since the Greeks had the bad conscience and used their gods to keep it at bay; so something other than the mechanism chose the Christian reading over the Greek one. And II:24 says the reversal needs a *different* type, strengthened by wars and victories: the type that would redirect the attachment is not the type that suffers it, which implies that the type that first directed it was not the sufferer either.

**The connection.** The administrator and the end are the Third Treatise's subject. Its priest is "a physician who does not cure", who takes a herd of sufferers who need someone to blame and tells them, in III:15, that the someone is themselves; the redirection of ressentiment onto the sufferer is what turns an instrument of self-torture into a regime, and the ideal it serves is what gives the torture a meaning, III:28's "rather will nothingness than not will". <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">The next lesson</Ref> opens that treatise, and <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref> reconstructs the administration; the two sentences you have written are the two things the Third Treatise adds to the Second.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Janaway's grid has four cells and Leiter's story has three stages. Derive which cell each stage occupies, identify the cell no stage names, and state from II:16–17 whether Nietzsche describes that cell's state or skips from the enclosure straight to guilt.

<Solution>
**The mapping.** The grid's axes are internalization (the object of cruelty: others or the self) and legitimation (its status: a wish or a right), giving (a) simple cruelty to others, (b) simple cruelty to self, (c) punishment of others, (d) punishment of self. Stage 2, cruelty turned inward without any reading of it as deserved, is cell (b). Stage 3, cruelty turned inward and read as rightful punishment, is cell (d). Stage 1 occupies no cell: it describes the breeding of a calculable animal, the precondition under which there is a self to enclose and a debtor to hold, and the grid begins after it. Of the remaining cells, (a) is II:6–7's festival and (c) is II:5's creditor and II:9's community; both are material for stage 3 and neither is a stage.

**The cell no stage names.** All four cells are occupied by *some* section, but the stages name only (b) and (d), and the reader who expects the stages to be the grid will look for a stage at (a) or (c) and find the debt chain instead. The more instructive gap is inside cell (b) itself: the grid predicts a state of *uninterpreted* self-cruelty, cruelty with the self for object and no reading attached, and the question is whether the text describes such a state or moves at once from the enclosure to guilt.

**What II:16–17 say.** They describe it, in three phrases. II:16: the enclosed animal "impatiently tore himself apart, persecuted, gnawed at, disturbed and mistreated himself", "rubbing itself raw on the bars of its cage", and this prisoner "became the inventor of the 'bad conscience'"; there is no creditor, no God and no desert in the description. II:17's last sentence gives the state a name and a status: "this instinct of freedom pushed back, stepped back, imprisoned in the interior and at last able to discharge and vent itself only on itself: that, only that, is the bad conscience *in its beginning* (*in seinem Anfange*)". "In its beginning" is the grid's cell (b) marked as a beginning, and Janaway's list of what self-cruelty "could have taken the form" of, fear, jealousy, shame, anger at one's own impotence, is the range of readings the cell admits before legitimation selects one. GM III:20, which calls internalization "the feeling of guilt in its raw state", confirms that Nietzsche treats the raw state as distinct from the developed one. The text therefore does not skip; the grid predicts a pre-moral self-cruelty and II:16–17 describe exactly that, which is why stage 2 is a stage.
</Solution>

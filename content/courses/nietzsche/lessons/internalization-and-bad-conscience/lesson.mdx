Bad conscience, on the Second Treatise's account, is not guilt about anything: it is the cruelty of an animal whose instincts had nowhere left to go once walls and peace enclosed it, and the walls were built by conquest, not contract. This lesson reconstructs that account from the three sections that state it, GM II:16–18 (1887), as a mechanism with named premises: II:16's hypothesis that an instinct denied an outward object does not lapse but turns inward, II:17's presupposition that the enclosure which blocked it was sudden and violent, and II:18's claim that the force at work inside is the same force that builds states outside, so that bad conscience is active and productive as well as sick. It grades the state-by-conquest premise, states once and bounds the parallel with Freud, and reads II:23–25, where the treatise names what it would take to turn the mechanism round and hands the naming to Zarathustra.

The course's argument arrives here with one axis of a grid still empty. <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">The debt lesson</Ref> ran the chain of II:4–8 and 19–22 from *Schulden* to a debt before God, located the step at which owing becomes being guilty (II:21), found that the step borrows I:13's doer without arguing for it, and adopted Christopher Janaway's resolution of the treatise's two origin stories: the debt chain changes the *status* of cruelty, from a wish to a right, and something else changes its *object*, from others to the self. That something else was quoted and deferred, and its one-sentence statement is II:16's: "all instincts that do not discharge themselves outwardly turn inward." Behind it stand <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">the promising-animal lesson</Ref>'s regime, the morality of custom that made man calculable by pain, and II:14–15's negative result, that punishment produces prudence and never the feeling of deserving, so that whatever turned cruelty on the self was not the creditor's cut. What this lesson adds to the spine is the second axis: the mechanism of internalization reconstructed and its premises graded, with the ambivalence II:18 insists on kept intact. <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">The reconstruction lesson</Ref> then assembles both axes and II:1–3 into Leiter's three stages and tests them.

The route: II:16's hypothesis and the water animal, with the two products the conclusion names; II:17's two presuppositions and the conquest that supplies them, graded; II:18's "same active force" and the pregnancy line that opens II:19; the convergence of three inputs on II:22's guilt before God; and the prophecy of II:24–25.

## II:16: an instinct denied an outward object does not lapse; it turns inward

II:14 removed punishment as the manufacturer of guilt and II:15 said what punishment makes instead, and II:16 begins by saying that a hypothesis of Nietzsche's own is now unavoidable. He marks its status twice in one sentence: "my own hypothesis (*meiner eignen Hypothese*) on the origin of the 'bad conscience'", to be given "a first, provisional expression", one that "is not easy to make heard and wants to be long considered, watched over and slept on." The section is labelled by its author as a hypothesis and not as a documented history, which is the Preface's lower grade in <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>'s scale, and the lesson holds it to that grade throughout.

### The illness, and the change of element

The hypothesis is stated as a diagnosis: "I take the bad conscience to be the deep sickness (*Erkrankung*) into which man had to fall under the pressure of the most fundamental of all the changes he ever experienced, that change when he found himself finally enclosed within the ban of society and of peace (*in den Bann der Gesellschaft und des Friedens eingeschlossen*)." The image that carries the change is zoological. It must have gone with these men "as it went with the water animals when they were forced either to become land animals or to perish": creatures "happily adapted to the wilderness, to war, to roaming, to adventure" found that "with one stroke (*mit Einem Male*) all their instincts were devalued and 'disconnected' (*entwerthet und 'ausgehängt'*)." They now had "to walk on their feet and 'carry themselves' where before they had been carried by the water", and for the simplest tasks "they no longer had their old guides, the regulating, unconsciously and surely leading drives"; they were "reduced to thinking, inferring, calculating, combining causes and effects, these unfortunates, reduced to their 'consciousness', their poorest and most error-prone organ (*ihr ärmlichstes und fehlgreifendstes Organ*)."

Step through the three frames and name, at each, what has changed and what has not: in the second frame the element changes and the drives do not; in the third a guide is added and nothing is yet turned inward. Then locate in the passage the clause that keeps the drives alive through the change, "those old instincts had not with one stroke ceased to make their demands (*ihre Forderungen zu stellen*)": without it the sequence would end at the third frame with an animal that has lost its instincts, and the mechanism that follows would have nothing to run on.
</Task>

The passage contains two changes, and only the second is the hypothesis. The first is a change of guide: the drives that "led unconsciously to safety" are replaced by consciousness, and Maudemarie Clark and David Dudrick read this as the point at which an inner world of deliberation appears at all, the world II:16 calls "originally thin, as if stretched between two skins (*dünn wie zwischen zwei Häute eingespannt*)". That world is thin because deliberation is all it contains. The second change gives it volume, and II:16 states it in one sentence.

### The sentence: conservation of the instincts

"Only it was difficult and rarely possible to comply with them (*ihnen zu Willen zu sein*): in the main they had to seek new and, as it were, subterranean satisfactions."

> Alle Instinkte, welche sich nicht nach Aussen entladen, wenden sich nach Innen — dies ist das, was ich die *Verinnerlichung* des Menschen nenne: damit wächst erst das an den Menschen heran, was man später seine "Seele" nennt.
>
> All instincts that do not discharge themselves outwardly turn inward: this is what I call the *internalization* of man. With it there first grows onto man what he later calls his "soul".

Three things in the sentence fix the mechanism. The verb is *entladen*, discharge, the same word BGE 13 uses for what "a living thing wants above all": to discharge its strength. The subject is *alle Instinkte*, without exception, so the sentence is a conservation claim: an instinct is a quantity that must discharge somewhere, and blocking one direction selects the other. And the product is named at once and named twice. The first name is the *soul*: "The whole inner world, originally thin as if stretched between two skins, has spread out and up, has gained depth, breadth, height, in the measure that man's discharge outward was inhibited (*gehemmt*)." The second name comes a few lines later, after the mechanism is repeated with its agent supplied. "Those terrible bulwarks (*Bollwerke*) with which the state organization protected itself against the old instincts of freedom, punishments above all belong to these bulwarks, brought it about that all those instincts of the wild, free, roaming man turned themselves backward, against man himself. Hostility, cruelty, pleasure in persecution, in attack, in change, in destruction: all of that turning against the possessors of such instincts: *that* is the origin of the 'bad conscience'."

The list of instincts is the list from I:11 that <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref> read: the noble "toward the outside" is a beast of prey who returns from "murder, arson, rape and torture" with a light heart. II:16 takes that outward face as given, walls it in, and asks where its force goes. The answer is not "nowhere". The prisoner "who, for lack of external enemies and resistances, wedged into an oppressive narrowness and regularity of custom (*Enge und Regelmässigkeit der Sitte*), impatiently tore himself apart, persecuted, gnawed at, disturbed and mistreated himself, this animal that one wants to 'tame', rubbing itself raw on the bars of its cage", this "fool, this longing and desperate prisoner, became the inventor (*Erfinder*) of the 'bad conscience'." The verb is active. Bad conscience is not something done to the animal by its captors; it is what the animal did with itself when the captors left it nothing else to do it to. And the cage is named: *Sitte*, custom, the same word as *Sittlichkeit der Sitte* in II:2, so the regime that bred the memory of the will in <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">the promising-animal lesson</Ref> is the regime that blocked the instincts here.

<Remark title="II:16 reconstructed">
**P1 (asserted; I:11 as the cross-reference).** Man once had the instincts of a wild animal, hostility, cruelty, pleasure in persecution, attack, change and destruction, and they discharged outward, on enemies, prey and the foreign.

**P2 (asserted; developed in II:17).** Man was enclosed, all at once, within "the walls of society and of peace", and the enclosure's bulwarks, punishments above all, blocked every outward discharge.

**P3 (the hypothesis; BGE 13 as the published principle).** An instinct is a quantity of force that must discharge: what cannot discharge outward discharges inward, on its possessor.

**C.** With the enclosure, the instincts of P1 turned against the animal itself. The inner world thereby gained "depth, breadth, height": the *soul*. The instincts of hostility and cruelty thereby acquired the animal as object: the *bad conscience*. Both products are one event described twice.

**Status.** Labelled a hypothesis by its author. P1 is the noble of I:11 with the community removed. P2 is II:17's business. P3 is the whole weight of the section and is not argued in it: the water-animal image is an analogy for the change of element, not evidence that blocked instincts persist rather than fade. The nearest published support is BGE 13's principle that life "wants above all to discharge its strength", and the principle's own standing is <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref>'s question. What II:14–15 contribute is negative: prisons produce hardness and not remorse, so whatever turned cruelty inward, it was not the cut.
</Remark>

The section's last paragraph fixes the scale of the claim, and it is not the scale of a pathology report. The animal that "took sides against itself" brought "something so new, deep, unheard-of, enigmatic, contradictory and *full of future*" into the world "that the aspect of the earth was essentially changed": "it needed divine spectators to appreciate the spectacle that began there", and since then man "awakens an interest, a tension, a hope, almost a certainty, as if with him something were announcing itself, something preparing, as if man were not a goal but only a way, an episode, a bridge, a great promise (*ein grosses Versprechen*)." The last word is the Second Treatise's first word, the promise of II:1, and the sentence is the first statement of the ambivalence that II:18 makes explicit: the sickness is the condition of everything the treatise is willing to call a future.

<KeyFact>
II:16's conclusion names two products of one event, the soul and the bad conscience, and no reading can keep one and drop the other: a naturalism that explains interiority by the inward turn has thereby explained self-cruelty, and a critique that condemns self-cruelty has thereby condemned the only interiority the section knows.
</KeyFact>

## II:17: the enclosure had to be sudden, and Nietzsche makes it a conquest

P2 said the enclosure came all at once, and II:16's image already required it: the water animal does not adapt, it is thrown onto land. II:17 states what the hypothesis presupposes about that event, in Nietzsche's own numbering, and the two presuppositions are the section's whole content. Everything else in it is the picture that supplies them.

### Two presuppositions, in Nietzsche's numbering

"To the presuppositions (*Voraussetzung*) of this hypothesis on the origin of the bad conscience belongs, *first*, that that change was not gradual, not voluntary (*keine allmähliche, keine freiwillige*), and did not present itself as an organic growing-into new conditions, but as a break, a leap, a compulsion, an ineluctable fate (*ein Bruch, ein Sprung, ein Zwang, ein unabweisbares Verhängniss*), against which there was no struggle and not even a *ressentiment*." The last clause is a boundary drawn against the First Treatise: the affect <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref> defined needs a target it can name and hate, and the enclosure, on this presupposition, arrived like weather. Whatever the caged animal does with its cruelty, it is not revenge on a conqueror.

"*Second*, that the fitting of a hitherto unrestrained and unformed population into a fixed form, as it began with an act of violence, was carried through to its end with nothing but acts of violence; that the oldest 'state' accordingly appeared as a terrible tyranny, as a crushing and inconsiderate machinery, and kept on working until such a raw material of people and half-animals was finally not only thoroughly kneaded and pliable but also *formed*." The second presupposition adds two properties to the first. The enclosure is *complete*, since the machinery works "until" the material is formed, with nothing outside it; and it is *imposed*, since the material does not shape itself.

### The picture that supplies them: a pack, and no contract

"I used the word 'state': it goes without saying who is meant by it, some pack of blond beasts of prey (*irgend ein Rudel blonder Raubthiere*), a conqueror- and master-race which, organized for war and with the power to organize, without hesitation lays its terrible paws on a population perhaps vastly superior in number but still formless, still roaming." The phrase is the one <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref>'s starred section read in I:11, where the adjective belongs to the lion and the list of nobilities that follows it runs from Rome to Japan; here the conquerors are "some pack" (*irgend ein Rudel*), unnamed, and Heidegger, in the fourth volume of his *Nietzsche*, takes the phrase as "the password and countersign" for the end of metaphysics in *brutalitas*, an image with no nation in it. What the pack does is the point: "in this way the 'state' begins on earth: I think that sentimental enthusiasm (*Schwärmerei*) is done with, which had it begin with a 'contract'." Whoever "can command, whoever is by nature 'master', whoever appears violent in deed and bearing, what has he to do with contracts!" Such beings "come like fate, without ground, reason, consideration, pretext; they are there as lightning is there, too terrible, too sudden, too convincing, too 'other' even to be hated."

The work of the conquerors is described in the vocabulary II:18 will reuse. "Their work is an instinctive creating of forms, impressing of forms (*Formen-schaffen, Formen-aufdrücken*); they are the most involuntary, most unconscious artists there are: in short, something new stands there where they appear, a ruling structure that *lives* (*ein Herrschafts-Gebilde, das lebt*), in which parts and functions are delimited and made relative to each other, in which nothing at all finds a place that has not first had a 'meaning' with respect to the whole put into it." They "do not know what guilt, what responsibility, what consideration is, these born organizers"; in them rules "that terrible artist-egoism that looks like bronze and knows itself justified in its 'work' for all eternity, as the mother is in her child." And the section closes by placing the bad conscience precisely with respect to them: "It is not in *them* that the 'bad conscience' grew, that is clear from the start, but it would not have grown without them, this ugly growth; it would be missing if an enormous quantum of freedom had not been driven out of the world, or at least out of visibility, under the pressure of their hammer-blows, their artist-violence, and made as it were latent. This instinct of freedom made latent by force, this instinct of freedom pushed back, stepped back, imprisoned in the interior and at last able to discharge and vent itself only on itself: that, only that, is the bad conscience in its beginning."

<Remark title="II:17 reconstructed">
**P1 (Nietzsche's first presupposition).** The enclosure was not gradual and not voluntary: a break and a compulsion, met by no struggle and no ressentiment.

**P2 (Nietzsche's second presupposition).** The forming of the population began and ended in violence; the oldest state was a tyranny that worked until the material was formed.

**P3 (the picture).** The oldest state was a pack of conquerors, "organized for war", laying hold of a formless and more numerous population; the contract account is sentimental enthusiasm.

**C.** The conquerors' instinct of freedom, discharged outward, built "a ruling structure that lives"; the conquered population's instinct of freedom, made latent by force, could discharge only on itself, and that is the bad conscience in its beginning.

**What the argument uses.** P1 and P2 are stated as what II:16 *presupposes*, and each names a property of the enclosure: sudden, imposed, complete. P3 is a picture that has all three properties and is offered as the way the state "begins on earth", with no record, no date and no people named, against a rival picture dismissed in one word.
</Remark>

### The premise graded: three properties, one supplier

The presuppositions are stated as presuppositions, and Nietzsche gives no evidence for them; the grading question is what the mechanism takes from them and whether it takes it from the conquest or from the properties the conquest happens to have. Consider each property in turn. *Suddenness* matters because P3 of II:16 is a conservation claim about instincts that "had not ceased to make their demands": an enclosure that closed over centuries would give the instincts time to find or breed new outward outlets, the hunt, the feud, the war of the community against its neighbours, and there would be no surplus to turn inward. Clark and Dudrick note the asymmetry inside II:17 itself: the conquerors had "a long period" in which "new patterns for discharging aggressive instincts could be developed and made instinctive", and the conquered did not, which is why the mechanism runs in the conquered and not in the conquerors. *Completeness* matters because a cage with one gap is not a cage: the bulwarks of II:16 are punishments, and a population that could still discharge cruelty on outsiders, or on its own weakest members, would discharge it there. *Imposition* matters for the clause about ressentiment: a population that had chosen its enclosure could resent the choice, and the caged animal's turn inward would be one more case of the First Treatise's affect instead of a new mechanism.

The grade follows. The mechanism depends on the properties and not on their supplier: any enclosure that is sudden, complete and imposed runs it, and a conquest by an organized minority is one event that has all three at once, which is why Nietzsche reaches for it. It is not the only such event, and the section does not argue that it is; it asserts it and dismisses the alternative as *Schwärmerei*. So the state-by-conquest premise is graded *asserted, unargued, and separable*: the hypothesis of II:16 stands or falls with the three properties, and the historical claim that the first states were founded by conquest is a thesis with a literature of its own that the argument does not need and the course does not enter. Two further things are separable with it. The plausibility of the mechanism does not depend on the conquerors being "blond": the adjective is I:11's lion and the pack is "some pack", and a reader who removes the word removes nothing from P1–P3. And it does not depend on the conquerors being a *race* in any sense that outlives the section: what II:17 needs of them is a type, beings who "can command" and are "by nature master", which is the Doctrine of Types from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> applied to a founding minority, a claim about constitution and not about descent.

<Aside>
The thesis that the earliest states arose by conquest rather than by agreement has a history independent of Nietzsche. Franz Oppenheimer's *Der Staat* (1908) made conquest by a pastoral minority of an agricultural majority the origin of every state, and Robert Carneiro's "A Theory of the Origin of the State" (*Science*, 1970) argued that states form where warfare occurs in areas of circumscribed agricultural land, so that the defeated cannot flee and must submit. Both are theories of *coercion* rather than consent and both are contested; neither is evidence for II:17, which cites nothing, and the course's grade of the premise does not change if either is right. What the reader can check is narrower: whether any documented early state shows an enclosure sudden and complete enough to block a population's outward discharge, which is the property the mechanism needs and the property the literature does not usually ask about.
</Aside>

### The same premise in two other registers

The premise is not confined to II:17, and its other statements show what Nietzsche took it to be an instance of. *Beyond Good and Evil* 257, published the year before, gives the founding of aristocratic society in the same words and refuses the same illusion: "one cannot entertain humanitarian illusions about how an aristocratic society originates; the truth is harsh. Let us not be deceived about how every higher culture on earth has begun! Men whose nature was still natural, barbarians in every terrible sense of the word, predatory people who still possessed an unbroken strength of will and lust for power threw themselves on weaker, more civilized, more peaceful races." BGE 259 then states the principle of which the conquest is a case: "life itself is essentially a process of appropriating, injuring, overpowering the alien and the weaker, oppressing, being harsh, imposing your own form, incorporating", and "exploitation" is not a defect of primitive societies but "belongs to the essence of being alive as a fundamental organic function". II:17's pack is BGE 259's principle with a date attached to it, and the grade of the principle is <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref>'s question.

The dramatized register says something that looks opposite. Zarathustra's speech "On the New Idol" (*Zarathustra* I, 1883) calls the state "the coldest of all cold monsters", one that "lies coldly" when it says "I, the state, am the people", and distinguishes "the ones who created the peoples", who "hung a faith and a love over them", from "the ones who set traps for the many and call them 'state'", who are "annihilators". The target is the modern state that claims to be the people, and the speech's founders of peoples are II:17's artists of form under another name; the two texts agree that a people is made by a minority that imposes a form, and disagree only about the institution that later claims the credit. The course records the speech as a corroboration in the register that cannot be cited for doctrine, and the point is small: II:17's contempt for the contract is not a contempt for the people the contract was invented to flatter.

<KeyFact>
The grade is separable in one direction only. It protects the mechanism from the conquest: if no state was ever founded by a pack, II:16 loses nothing. It does not protect the mechanism from the properties, and the property that cannot be weakened without cost is suddenness. An enclosure that took long enough to be lived through could be resented and named, the first presupposition's clause "not even a *ressentiment*" would fail, and the caged animal's turn inward would have a rival explanation in the First Treatise's affect; II:11's man of ressentiment, who "has the invention of the bad conscience on his conscience", would then be an alternative account rather than a slip, which is the remainder <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the reconstruction lesson</Ref> has to carry.
</KeyFact>

## II:18: the same force builds states outward and ideals inward

II:17 ended by identifying the material of bad conscience as an "instinct of freedom" driven inward, and II:18 opens with a warning against the inference a reader of two sections on a sickness has been set up to draw. "Let one beware of thinking little of this whole phenomenon merely because it is from the start ugly and painful." The section's work is to say what the phenomenon is *besides* ugly, and it does so by identifying the force in it.

### One force, two materials

"At bottom it is the same active force (*dieselbe aktive Kraft*) that is at work more grandly in those artists of violence and organizers and builds states, which here, inwardly, smaller, pettier, in the direction backward, in the 'labyrinth of the breast', to speak with Goethe, creates for itself the bad conscience and builds negative ideals: that very instinct of freedom (in my language: the will to power (*der Wille zur Macht*)): only that the material on which the form-giving and violating nature of this force vents itself is here man himself, his whole old animal self, and *not*, as in that greater and more conspicuous phenomenon, the other man, other men." This is the sentence that makes II:16–18 one argument. The conquerors of II:17 and the caged animal are not two agents but one force with two materials, and the phrase "in my language: the will to power" is the treatise's only gloss on the term, made in passing, in a parenthesis, and applied to a force that builds. The same identification was made for all "overpowering and becoming-master" in II:12, where <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref> found the origin-and-use rule; here the rule's engine is turned on the animal's own drives.

What the force does to its inward material is described in the vocabulary of the workshop. "This secret self-violation (*Selbst-Vergewaltigung*), this artist's cruelty, this pleasure in giving oneself, as a heavy, resisting, suffering material, a form, in burning in a will, a critique, a contradiction, a contempt, a No; this uncanny and horribly pleasurable labour of a soul willingly at odds with itself, which makes itself suffer out of pleasure in making-suffer." The verb *einbrennen*, burn in, is II:3's verb for making a memory, and the list of what is burned in, a will, a critique, a No, is the content of a conscience. Bad conscience is on this description not a feeling that overtakes the animal but a *form* the animal imposes on itself, and the pleasure in it is the inflicter's, which is the pleasure <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref> found in BGE 78's despiser who "still respects himself as a despiser". Bernard Reginster supplies the analytic ground: cruelty toward oneself is will to power in the formal sense, the overcoming of a resistance, where the resistance overcome is one's own will, and "the ascetic enjoys his self-inflicted suffering not as suffering but as a victory over himself." The pleasure of the caged animal is the pleasure of the conqueror with the population replaced by the self.

### The products: negative ideals, beauty, and the value of the unegoistic

The section then lists what the inward force has made, and the list is not a list of symptoms. "This whole active (*aktivische*) 'bad conscience' has at last, as the true womb of ideal and imaginative events, also brought to light an abundance of new, strange beauty and affirmation, and perhaps beauty itself." The argument for the last clause is a contradiction made conscious: "What would be 'beautiful' if the contradiction had not first come to consciousness of itself, if the ugly had not first said to itself: 'I am ugly'?" And the section closes by returning the result to the treatise's subject, morality: after this "hint", it will be "less of a riddle" how "an ideal, a beauty can be intimated in contradictory concepts like *selflessness*, *self-denial*, *self-sacrifice*", and one thing is known from now on, "of what kind the pleasure is that the selfless, the self-denying, the self-sacrificing man feels from the start: this pleasure belongs to cruelty." The last sentence is the section's formula: "so much provisionally on the descent (*Herkunft*) of the 'unegoistic' as a moral value and on the marking-out of the ground from which this value has grown: only the bad conscience, only the will to self-mistreatment (*Wille zur Selbstmisshandlung*), provides the presupposition for the value of the unegoistic."

That formula is the naturalist reading's form exactly. A value, the unegoistic, is explained by a type, the animal whose cruelty has the self for its only object, and by a circumstance, the enclosure; the explanation names a mechanism and a pleasure and cites no further faculty. What the reading must also keep, on pain of misreading the section it is explaining, is the other half of the list: the same mechanism is credited with ideals, imagination and beauty, and the treatise says so before it says anything about guilt.

### II:19's first sentence: the ambivalence in one line

The next section opens with the line that fixes the treatise's verdict, and the lesson locates it precisely because translations move it: in the eKGWB it is the first sentence of II:19, not the last of II:18. "It is a sickness, the bad conscience, that is beyond doubt, but a sickness as pregnancy is a sickness (*eine Krankheit, wie die Schwangerschaft eine Krankheit ist*)." Walter Kaufmann read the simile as the treatise's conclusion that bad conscience is "a necessary evil", and the reading is right about the direction and too weak about the content: a pregnancy is not an evil that is tolerated but a condition that produces, and II:18 has just named the products. The naturalist reading has here a mechanism with two outputs, self-torture and the whole inventory of ideals, and it must report both, because a reading that keeps the sickness and drops the pregnancy cannot explain why the treatise that diagnosed the sickness also calls its bearer "a great promise".

### The misreading, refuted from the sections

Bad conscience in II:16–18 is cruelty turned inward and given form, active, productive, and prior to any moral concept. The misreading is that "bad conscience is guilt: society made us feel guilty", and it is common enough to open a published set of teaching notes: Tad Beckman's comments on the *Genealogy* for a Harvey Mudd College course (1995) state the Second Essay's objective as "the discovery of the origins of bad conscience, or guilt", and carry the pair through the notes as one thing. The sections reject it, and the rejection is a matter of inventory. II:16–18 contain no creditor, no debt, no God and no doer: the words *Schuld*, *Gläubiger* and *Gott* do not occur in the mechanism, and the conquerors "do not know what guilt is". What the sections describe is a self-directed cruelty that burns in "a will, a critique, a No", and what they credit it with is beauty and the ideals. Guilt enters in II:21–22, when the man of bad conscience "seizes upon the religious presupposition", and it enters with the borrowed premise the debt lesson located, that the sufferer could have done otherwise. The replacement model is Janaway's: internalization gives "simple cruelty to self", and cruelty to self "could have taken the form" of fear, jealousy, shame or anger at one's own impotence, none of which is guilt; guilt is the cell of the grid where the cruelty is also read as *rightful*, and that reading is supplied by the debt chain, not by the cage.

The discriminating example is in the treatise itself, in the control case II:23 supplies among the closing sections: the Greeks, on Nietzsche's account, *had* the bad conscience, since they needed their gods as a device to keep it at bay, and did not have guilt, since the gods took the guilt on themselves. A reader who equates the two cannot read that section.

### The Freud parallel, once and bounded

Sigmund Freud's *Civilization and Its Discontents* (1930), chapter VII, describes the superego as aggressiveness that, denied outward expression by civilization, is "introjected, internalized", sent back to the ego and turned against it as conscience, and Freud wrote in 1925 that he had long avoided reading Nietzsche, "whose guesses and intuitions often agree in the most astonishing way with the laborious findings of psychoanalysis", so as not to be prejudiced in his own work. The parallel is a later, independent formulation of a similar mechanism, and it confirms nothing: two hypotheses that agree are two hypotheses, and the course does not use Freud as a source for what Nietzsche argued or as evidence that he argued it well. The agreement is good for one thing, placing II:16's mechanism in a family of internalization hypotheses, and the point at which the two part, whether guilt is the direct product of the inward turn or a later reading of it, is exactly where the *Genealogy* needs the debt chain.

<KeyFact>
II:18 makes bad conscience a mechanism with two products, self-cruelty and the ideals, and the pregnancy line of II:19 is the treatise's own insistence that both are kept. The course carries the second product forward: <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref>'s claim that suffering can be a condition of creation rests on the "artist's cruelty" being productive here, and a reading that flattens II:18 into "society made us sick" loses that lesson before it begins.
</KeyFact>

## The site the escalation needed: three inputs converge on II:22

The debt lesson closed on II:22's sentence and deferred half of it, and the half is now reconstructed. "That *will to self-torture*, that suppressed cruelty of the animal-man made inward, scared back into himself, of the one locked up in the 'state' for the purpose of taming, who invented the bad conscience in order to hurt himself after the more natural outlet of this wish-to-hurt was blocked: this man of the bad conscience has *seized upon the religious presupposition* in order to drive his self-torment to its most gruesome hardness and sharpness." Every clause of the first half is II:16–17 compressed: "locked up in the 'state'" is II:17's enclosure, "invented the bad conscience in order to hurt himself" is II:16's *Erfinder*, and "after the more natural outlet was blocked" is P3. The sentence's grammar gives the order of the treatise's inputs: the man of bad conscience is the subject, the religious presupposition is what he seizes, and the seizing is the moralization. Bad conscience is there before the God it takes up as an instrument.

## II:23–25: the treatise's own forward hook

The mechanism is complete at II:22, and the three sections that close the treatise do not add to it. They say what the mechanism's direction leaves open, and they say it as a prophecy the course records without grading.

### The Greeks: the gods as a device against bad conscience

II:23 is a control case. "That the conception of gods in itself need not lead to this deterioration of the imagination", the self-crucifixion of II:22, "is shown by every glance at the Greek gods, these mirrorings of noble and self-mastering men, in which the *animal* in man felt itself deified and did not tear itself apart, did not rage against itself." The Greeks "made use of their gods for the longest time precisely in order to keep the 'bad conscience' at bay (*sich das 'schlechte Gewissen' vom Leibe zu halten*), in order to be permitted to remain glad of their freedom of soul", and the use is documented from Homer: Zeus on Aegisthus, in the *Odyssey*'s first book, complains that mortals blame the gods for evils they make for themselves "through folly (*Unverstand*)", and the noble Greek, faced with an outrage by one of his own, concluded "a god must have deluded him". Folly, not sin: the gods "served as causes of evil" and "took upon themselves not the punishment but, what is nobler, the guilt". The bearing on the mechanism is that the inward force is one thing and the interpretation put on it is another: a people with the bad conscience and a religion that takes the blame off them did not arrive at II:22, and so the arrival there was not forced by the mechanism alone.

### II:24: the reversed attempt, and who could make it

II:24 states the problem the treatise leaves and the shape of a solution. "Man has for too long regarded his natural inclinations with an 'evil eye', so that they have at last become wedded (*verschwistert*) in him to the 'bad conscience'." The wedding is an attachment, not the mechanism: the force turned inward is fixed, and what it is turned *against* was chosen by an interpreter, who in the Third Treatise will have a name. "A reversed attempt would be possible in itself, but who is strong enough for it?, namely to wed to the bad conscience the *unnatural* inclinations, all those aspirations to the beyond, contrary to sense, to instinct, to nature, to the animal, in short the ideals so far, which are all ideals hostile to life, ideals that slander the world." What the attempt would need is stated as a type: "spirits strengthened by wars and victories, for whom conquest, adventure, danger, even pain have become a need", habituated "to sharp high air, to winter wanderings, to ice and mountains in every sense", possessed of "the great health (*die grosse Gesundheit*)".

The section ends in the future tense. ### What the hook is, and what it is not

The three sections are read for what they establish about the mechanism and not for their doctrine. They establish that the direction of the force is fixed and its attachment is not, which is II:12's rule applied to the bad conscience itself: a will superior to it may impose a new use. They name the attachment that has held, the natural inclinations, and the agent who made it is the Third Treatise's business, the ascetic priest of III:15 who tells the sufferer "you yourself are to blame", which is the direction change <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref> reconstructs. And they name a reversal as a possibility, a type as its condition, and a character from another book as its bearer. The last is a prophecy and is recorded as one. The course's rule from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> is that the dramatized register is cited for what it stages and not for doctrine, and II:25 is the argued register handing a task to the dramatized one and saying so; what Zarathustra does with it is Module 6's subject, and no premise of this lesson's reconstruction depends on it.

<KeyFact>
II:24 rules out the reading its own Greeks invite. The Greek device keeps bad conscience at bay; the reversed attempt does not return to it. It keeps the inward cruelty and changes what the cruelty is wedded to, so the man of the future is neither the beast of prey before the walls nor the Greek with his gods but the animal of II:16 with its cruelty pointed at the life-denying ideals. What the treatise proposes to overcome is an attachment and not the mechanism, and a reading of Nietzsche's ideal as a return to the pre-internalized animal fails at this section; <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref> depends on the same point, since sublimation presupposes the inward turn it redirects.
</KeyFact>

## ★ The thin inner world: two changes in II:16, and a soul with a structure

The core path treats II:16's two products, the soul and the bad conscience, as one event described twice, and that is what the section says. What it leaves undescribed is what the soul so produced is *like*, and Clark and Dudrick's reading of II:16–18, in their commentary on *Beyond Good and Evil*, gives it a structure that <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drive-self lesson</Ref> will need.

Their first observation is that II:16 contains two changes and that the first is prior. The water animal on land is "reduced to thinking, inferring, calculating": the regulating drives that had left "no room for uncertainty about what to do" are disconnected, and where two drives conflict the animal must now deliberate. That is a first-person point of view, and it is the inner world the section calls "thin, as if stretched between two skins": deliberation is all it holds. The second change, the turning inward of the blocked instincts, is what gives it "depth, breadth, height", and a being with only the first change has a perspective but not, on their reading, a soul in the sense that makes it a person.

Their second observation follows from II:18's identification of the force. If the inward force is the *same* form-giving force that made "a ruling structure that lives" out of a formless population, then what it makes out of the animal's drives is a ruling structure among *them*: a commanding drive and obeying drives, which is BGE 12's definition of the soul as "a social structure of the drives and affects (*Gesellschaftsbau der Triebe und Affekte*)". The blocked aggressive instincts, on this reading, are "vented upon 'another' population, the nomad's other drives", and the venting is what orders them. The analogy they use to make a political order among drives intelligible is the primatologist Frans de Waal's: chimpanzees and wolves form dominance hierarchies without any member taking itself to have a political standing, so a drive can have a rank without being a homunculus that knows it. The conquerors of II:17 "had no idea they were bringing about a political order among the drives" of the people they formed, which is what makes them, in Nietzsche's phrase, "the most involuntary, most unconscious artists there are".

What the reading buys is a soul with an inside, so that II:16's "depth, breadth, height" name a structure and not only a direction, and the drive-self lesson can take the *Genealogy*'s account of interiority as continuous with BGE 12 and 19. What it costs is a step the sections do not take: II:16–18 say that the force turned inward *forms* the animal and say nothing about the drives forming a hierarchy among themselves, so the political order is inferred from BGE and read back. The core path does not need the inference, and the reconstruction lesson does not test it.

## What is established, and what the next lesson takes up

What is established, one result per section. II:16 states, as a hypothesis its author labels one, that instincts are conserved: what cannot discharge outward discharges inward, and the enclosure of the animal in society and peace therefore produced, in one event, the soul as an interior with volume and the bad conscience as cruelty with the self for its object; the water animal carries the change of element and the change of guide, and the conservation premise is what the section does not argue, with BGE 13 as its published principle and II:14–15 as its negative support. II:17 states two presuppositions, that the enclosure was sudden and imposed and that the forming was violent and complete, and supplies all three properties with one picture, a pack of conquerors laying hold of a formless population, against a contract dismissed as sentimental enthusiasm; the premise is graded asserted, unargued and separable, since the mechanism consumes the properties and not their supplier, and the conquerors' blondness and race are not premises. II:18 identifies the force in the bad conscience with the force that builds states, "in my language: the will to power", turned on the animal's own self as material, and credits it with negative ideals, beauty and the value of the unegoistic, so that II:19's pregnancy line names a mechanism with two products that any reading must keep. II:22's will to self-torture is II:16–17 compressed and is the subject that seizes the religious presupposition, so bad conscience precedes guilt before God; three inputs converge there, and the last step still borrows I:13's doer. II:23–25 show, with the Greeks, that the inward turn does not fix its own object, and prophesy a reversal for a type and a figure that the argued register does not supply.

What is not established is the order in which the inputs combine and whether the sections fit that order without remainder. Leiter compresses the treatise into three stages, a bred memory of the will, cruelty turned inward, and that cruelty moralized by the free-will lie, and assigns II:1–3, II:16–18 and II:19–22 with I:13 to them in turn. <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">The reconstruction lesson</Ref> states that argument with the section each premise cites, tests each stage against the sections it claims, and records the remainders: II:2's sovereign individual, which the promising-animal lesson left between two readings; II:11's man of ressentiment who "has the invention of the bad conscience on his conscience" against II:17's enclosure "without even a ressentiment"; and II:18's pregnancy, which a three-stage history of a sickness has to carry as a product and not as a symptom.

(iii) The blocked aggression is turned back on the self: Freud's superego takes over the aggressiveness "the ego would have liked to satisfy upon other, extraneous individuals" and directs it against the ego; Nietzsche's instincts "turn against the possessors of such instincts". (iv) The result is an inner agency that punishes: a conscience. (v) The result is ambivalent: civilization's achievements are paid for in it.

**Claims Nietzsche makes and Freud does not.** The enclosure was sudden and imposed by conquest (II:17); the inward force is the *same* force that builds states, the will to power, and is form-giving and artist-like (II:18); its products include beauty, the ideals and the value of the unegoistic; the pleasure in self-cruelty is the inflicter's pleasure in overcoming resistance; and the moralization into guilt is a later, separable step that needs a creditor and the free-will lie.

**Claims Freud makes and Nietzsche does not.** The internalized aggression is lodged in a distinct agency, the superego, structurally described; the mechanism runs in each child through the introjection of parental authority and is reinforced by the fear of losing love; the sense of guilt is the *direct* product of the internalization, not a later interpretation of it; and the theory is tied to a clinical method that claims to observe it.

**Why the agreement is not evidence.** Corroboration between sources establishes a fact when the sources are independent *observations* of it. These are two hypotheses, and what they share is a structure, not an observation: neither cites a record of the enclosure, and both infer the inward turn from the present state of conscience plus a conservation assumption about drives. Two theorists reaching the same inference from the same kind of premise shows that the inference is natural, not that it is true; and where they differ, on whether guilt is the direct product or a later reading, the difference is exactly the point at which the *Genealogy*'s argument needs the debt chain and the borrowed premise. The agreement is good for one thing, locating II:16's mechanism as one member of a family of internalization hypotheses, and for nothing the course's grading needs.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Rank the three inputs to the guilt before God of II:22, the custom-bred memory of II:1–3, the creditor's debt of II:4–8 and 19–21, and the internalized cruelty of II:16–18, as necessary, enabling or precipitating, and defend the ranking from the sections. Then say which input the treatise's own last sentence on the subject, II:22's, treats as the agent.

<Solution>
**Necessary: internalized cruelty.** Without an inflicter who wants to hurt and has only himself as object, no debt becomes an instrument of self-torture; II:14–15 showed that a debtor who is merely punished becomes prudent, not guilty, and II:22 makes the will to self-torture the subject that "seized upon the religious presupposition". Remove it and the God-debt is a tribute, not a guilt.

**Necessary as well, but as a condition of the others: the memory of the will.** A debtor who could not hold a debt across time could neither contract (II:8 makes the debtor the promiser) nor carry a debt to ancestors or to a god; and the cage of II:16 is the "regularity of custom", so the regime that bred the memory is the enclosure that turned the instincts. It is necessary, but its necessity is inherited by both of the other inputs rather than acting on the product directly.

**Enabling: the creditor's debt and its escalation.** The debt chain supplies the interpretation "rightfully inflicted" and, through II:19–21, a creditor before whom that interpretation can never be discharged. Janaway's grid shows why it is enabling and not precipitating: internalization alone gives self-cruelty that could have taken the form of fear, jealousy or shame; the debt relation is what makes the self-cruelty a *punishment*, and II:21's moralization is the moment it does so. It enables the specific form, guilt, without itself doing the seizing.

**Precipitating: none of the three; the borrowed premise.** What turns a self-torturer with a creditor into one who deserves it is the debtor's belief that he could have paid and refused, I:13's doer, which none of the three inputs supplies and II:4 dates as late. The precipitating condition of the guilt before God is a falsehood imported from the First Treatise, which is the debt lesson's result restated as a ranking.

**The agent in II:22.** Internalized cruelty. The sentence's grammatical subject is "this man of the bad conscience", the "suppressed cruelty of the animal-man made inward"; the religious presupposition is the object he seizes and the debt to God is "his instrument of torture". The treatise's own ordering puts the inflicter first and the creditor in his hand.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Forward. The three inputs converge on II:22, and the text never states the order in which they combine. Write them as three numbered stages in the order the mechanism requires, stating for each which of the others must already exist, and mark the one transition that needs a premise from the First Treatise.

<Solution>
**Stage 1: conscience as a bred memory (II:1–3).** The morality of custom burns in a memory of the will, an animal that can hold a prohibition and a promise across time. Requires nothing prior in the treatise; it is the treatise's starting condition and II:2's "ripest fruit" stands at its end.

**Stage 2: bad conscience as internalized cruelty (II:16–18).** The same regime, "the narrowness and regularity of custom" with punishments as its bulwarks, blocks outward discharge, and the instincts turn inward. Requires Stage 1 in two ways: the enclosure that blocks is the regime that burned in, and the "No" the inward force burns into the self (II:18) has the prohibition of Stage 1 as its content. Does not yet require a creditor or a god: the Greeks of II:23 are at this stage.

**Stage 3: guilt as bad conscience moralized (II:19–22, with I:13).** The debt relation of II:4–8, escalated until the creditor is a god, is seized by the man of Stage 2 as an instrument, and his self-cruelty is read as deserved. Requires Stage 1, since the debtor is the promiser (II:8), and Stage 2, since II:22's subject is the will to self-torture that does the seizing. The transition from Stage 2 to Stage 3 is the one that needs a premise from outside: "deserved" requires a debtor who could have paid and chose not to, the doer behind the deed that I:13 argued is a fiction and *Twilight* VI:7 says was invented "so that men could become guilty". No section of the Second Treatise supplies it.

**The doorstep.** These three stages, conscience, bad conscience, and guilt moralized by the free-will lie, with the borrowed premise at the third transition, are Brian Leiter's three-stage reconstruction of the Second Treatise, and <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the reconstruction lesson</Ref> tests whether II:1–25 fit them without remainder, beginning with the sentence in II:2 that calls the fruit of Stage 1 "sovereign" and "free".
</Solution>

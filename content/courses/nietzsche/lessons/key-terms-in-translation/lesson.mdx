A translator's choice of one English word for schlecht decides whether the First Treatise's asymmetry survives on the page; this chapter runs that check on six terms the course's arguments depend on. Each term is taken through the German text of record, the translations the course has on disk, and the reconstructed premise the term sits in, and the result is recorded as a verdict on the premise rather than on the translator: survives, collapses, or shifts to a different claim.

The First Treatise module has reached its conclusion one premise at a time, and every premise was tied to a German word in a parenthesis. <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">The master-and-slave lesson</Ref> stated the minimal pair as "good" (*gut*) first against "evil" (*böse*) first, with "bad" (*schlecht*) as the noble's afterthought; <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref> kept *Ressentiment* in the French Nietzsche wrote it in; <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref> read *Twilight*'s purpose clause, that men were thought free "so that they could become guilty" (*schuldig*), with the German beside it. Those parentheses were one-sentence checks. This chapter is the fuller treatment they pointed to: a stated procedure, run on the terms the course's reconstructions cannot do without, with the verdict each rendering yields. It is a starred chapter and nothing on the core path depends on it; the core path resumes at <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref>, and later lessons' translation exercises cite this one.

The route: the check stated as a four-column procedure and run on a two-word particle in *Ecce Homo*; *schlecht* and *böse* against the asymmetry; *Schuld* against the Second Treatise's philological premise; *Mitleid* against the Preface's target; *Wille zur Macht* against the domination reading; *Übermensch* against the Prologue's imagery; and *Rausch* with two coined compounds, where the question is whether a translator footnotes or domesticates.

## The check as a procedure: a translation is a reading

A translation of a numbered section is a reading of it that has been fixed in print, and the reconstruction protocol of <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>, which locates a claim, lists its premises and names the missing one, is run on that reading and not on the German unless the reader supplies the German. The consequence is mechanical. If a premise depends on a word, and the word has more than one English rendering in use, then the reconstruction has as many versions as there are renderings, and the versions can differ in validity. The check that follows is the discipline of finding out whether they do.

<Figure
  src="./figures/translation-check.svg"
  alt="A four-column card: the German term with its sentence and section; the candidate renderings in use; the premise of a course reconstruction the term sits in; the verdict on that premise under each rendering, with schlecht filled in as the worked row: 'bad' survives, 'evil' collapses the asymmetry"
  caption="The check as a card: one row per term, and the question is always which premise survives which word."

Four columns. First, the German, with the sentence and the section it occurs in, from the critical edition (eKGWB), so that the term is a token in a sentence and not a dictionary entry. Second, every rendering in use across the translations the reader can reach, each attributed. Third, the premise of a course reconstruction the term carries, cited by lesson and by the premise's number. Fourth, the verdict: the premise restated under each rendering, and whether it still says what the German says. The third column is what makes the exercise an argument rather than a glossary. A term with nothing in the third column has no row, however interesting its history, and a term with three premises in the third column has three rows.

David Farrell Krell, the translator of Heidegger's four Nietzsche volumes, opens his glossary with the rule the check applies: "Translation should not and cannot be one-to-one substitution. If it is done that way it may be *wortwörtlich* but can never be *wortgetreu*; although literal, it will not be faithful." The check adds one thing to that rule: faithfulness is measured against a premise, not against a feeling for the language, and a rendering is unfaithful exactly when a reconstruction that is valid in the German is invalid in the English or supports a conclusion the German does not.

### Two words dropped: 'eher noch' and Cesare Borgia

The smallest possible case shows the procedure's reach. In *Ecce Homo* (written in the autumn of 1888, published only in 1908), Nietzsche complains that readers of *Beyond Good and Evil* took his "higher man" for an idealistic type and adds: "*Wem ich ins Ohr flüsterte, er solle sich eher noch nach einem Cesare Borgia als nach einem Parsifal umsehn, der traute seinen Ohren nicht*" (EH III §1). Cesare Borgia, the Renaissance condottiere and papal son whose murders Machiavelli recorded, is set against Parsifal, the chaste hero of Wagner's last opera (1882). The two words *eher noch*, "even rather", are the whole of the sentence's logic: they make the comparison a ranking of two options neither of which is being praised.

<Figure
  src="./figures/eher-noch.svg"
  alt="Three rows: the German sentence from Ecce Homo III 1 with eher noch in bold, licensing a comparison of two cases neither of which is an ideal; the rendering with the particle dropped, look for a Cesare Borgia rather than a Parsifal, licensing admiration; Kaufmann's rendering with even kept, licensing a ranking in which even the worse case is preferred to Parsifal; a footer stating that no rendering licenses the claim that Borgia is the higher type"
  caption="Two words dropped move the sentence from a ranking to an endorsement; Kaufmann's 'even' puts them back."
/>

Walter Kaufmann's chapter on sublimation makes the case in one paragraph (ch. 7 §III). "It is, for example, a common misconception that Nietzsche admired Cesare Borgia and glorified him." The sentence from *Ecce Homo* had been read as "look for a Cesare Borgia rather than a Parsifal"; "translators and interpreters have not always minded the *eher noch*", and with it restored, "one should look 'even for a Cesare Borgia rather than for a Parsifal'", which "leaves no doubt that Nietzsche considered Cesare Borgia far from admirable but preferred even him to the Parsifal ideal". The third column here is the misreadings outcome: the proto-fascist reading needs Borgia to be the ideal, and the German sentence does not supply him. The fourth column records that the truncated rendering supplies him and the full one does not, and that neither supplies the claim in *Twilight*'s "Expeditions" §37, where Nietzsche reports, in his critics' voice, that Borgia "was certainly not to be set up as a 'higher man', as a kind of superman [*als eine Art Übermensch*], in the way I set him up", and then argues that the modern reader's failure to do evil is inability rather than virtue. A reader working from the two-word cut has a Nietzsche who admires a murderer; a reader working from the German has a Nietzsche who ranks strong impulses unsublimated above no impulses at all, which is the thesis of <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref>.

<Aside>
A term the check clears. BGE 12 declares war on the atomistic need for a soul-substance with the phrase "*einen schonungslosen Krieg aufs Messer*", "a merciless war to the knife". Three renderings are in use: Kaufmann's "a war to the death"; Norman's, which breaks the noun phrase open, "declare war — a ruthless fight to the finish — on the 'atomistic need'"; and Hollingdale's literal "war to the knife". Clark and Dudrick's footnote says the first two were chosen "presumably because they don't think the image provoked by a literal translation makes sense", and reports Norman's as "a ruthless war to the finish", one word off the printed page. The third column for this term is empty: no premise of the doer argument or the drives argument depends on the knife, and the reconstruction of BGE 12 in <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref> reads identically under all three. The check's verdict is "harmless", and recording that verdict is part of the method: the point is not that every word matters but that the ones that do can be identified. The misquotation is the method's other rule in small: the second column is filled from the translations themselves, never from a commentator's report of them.
</Aside>

## schlecht and böse: two German contrasts, one English 'good'

The First Treatise's title is two pairs, "*Gut und Böse*", "*Gut und Schlecht*", and the whole of its argument lives in the fact that German has one word on the left and two on the right. From the English titles one would expect the pairs to be safe, since English also has two words, "bad" and "evil"; the danger is not that English lacks the distinction but that "evil" is the stronger and more available word for every contrary of "good", so that a translator reaching for emphasis at *schlecht* has the collapse available at every sentence.

<Figure
  src="./figures/two-contrasts.svg"
  alt="The German gut at left with two colored arrows to schlecht, labelled noble contempt from I:2 and I:10, and to böse, labelled ressentiment's hatred from I:10; to the right the English targets bad and evil with solid mapping lines, and a dashed amber line from schlecht to evil labelled the collapse; a footer quotes I:11's sentence that needs two English words"
  caption="One German 'good', two German contraries, and the one substitution that would leave English with a single contrary term."
/>

The third column for this row is the asymmetry reconstruction of <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the master-and-slave lesson</Ref>: P1, that noble valuation coins "good" of itself and derives "bad" (*schlecht*) of the others as a contrast term; P2, that slave valuation coins "evil" (*böse*) of the others first. The premises are about the *order* of coining, and the order is stated in the German by the difference between two words. I:10 gives the slave side: "*er hat 'den bösen Feind' concipirt, 'den Bösen', und zwar als Grundbegriff*". I:11 gives the noble side and then the comparison: "*Dies 'schlecht' vornehmen Ursprungs und jenes 'böse' aus dem Braukessel des ungesättigten Hasses ... wie verschieden stehen die beiden scheinbar demselben Begriff 'gut' entgegengestellten Worte 'schlecht' und 'böse' da! Aber es ist nicht derselbe Begriff 'gut'.*"

### Samuel across I:4, I:10 and I:11: the pair holds

Horace Samuel's 1913 translation is the one English text of the *Genealogy* the course has in full, and it is a data point rather than a standard: its "aristocrat" for *der Vornehme* and its "resentment" for *Ressentiment* date it. On this row it holds without exception. I:4 leaves the term in German and glosses it: "the German word '*schlecht*' itself: this word is identical with '*schlicht*'", which "simply denoted the plebeian man in contrast to the aristocratic man", after the sentence in which "'vulgar,' 'plebeian,' 'low,' are made to change finally into 'bad'". I:10 has the noble's "negative conception, 'low,' 'vulgar,' 'bad,'" as "merely a pale late-born foil", and the slave's "'the evil enemy,' the 'evil one,' and indeed that is the root idea". I:11 keeps both terms in the sentence that compares them: "This 'bad' of aristocratic origin and that 'evil' out of the cauldron of unsatisfied hatred ... these two words 'bad' and 'evil,' how great a difference do they mark, in spite of the fact that they have an identical contrary in the idea 'good.' But the idea 'good' is not the same". And the I:17 note survives: "*Beyond Good and Evil*—at any rate that is not the same as 'Beyond Good and Bad.'" In these three sections and the note, "evil" never stands for *schlecht* and "bad" never stands for *böse*.

### The collapse, made visible

The counterfactual is worth drawing, because it shows what kind of damage a bad rendering does. Substitute "evil" for *schlecht* throughout the three sections and redraw the minimal pair.

<Figure
  src="./figures/minimal-pair-collapsed.svg"
  alt="The minimal pair from the master-and-slave lesson redrawn: the noble panel's derived cell now reads they, the evil, with schlecht in parentheses and the word evil highlighted in amber; the slave panel's first cell reads they, the evil, with böse; footers note that the cells now carry the same word, that I:11 would read these two words evil and evil, how differently they stand, and that the order survives in the German but has no surface on this page"

The German argument is not refuted by the substitution; it is made unstatable. P1 and P2 both say the others are called "evil", one as a derived term and one as a first term, and the sentence of I:11 that exists to mark the difference becomes "these two words 'evil' and 'evil', how differently they stand there". A reader of that page cannot recover the asymmetry from the text, and the treatise's title becomes one pair. That is the fourth column's most severe verdict, "collapses", and it is worth distinguishing from the verdict "shifts": a collapsed premise is one the English cannot express, while a shifted premise is one the English expresses as a different claim. The *Schuld* row below is a shift.

<KeyFact>
The asymmetry is a claim about two different contrary terms, and it needs two English words as surface, not as ornament: a translation that keeps "bad" and "evil" apart in I:4, I:10, I:11 and the I:17 note carries the First Treatise's argument, and one that merges them anywhere in those places carries a treatise with no title and a minimal pair with one cell.
</KeyFact>

## Schuld: one word, and a premise that is evidence rather than proof

The Second Treatise's philological premise sits in a word that English is forced to split, and here the check returns "shifts" in both directions. II:4 asks whether the genealogists of morals ever dreamed "*dass zum Beispiel jener moralische Hauptbegriff 'Schuld' seine Herkunft aus dem sehr materiellen Begriff 'Schulden' genommen hat*": that the moral master-concept *Schuld* took its descent from the very material concept *Schulden*. In German *Schuld* is one noun meaning both guilt and debt, and *Schulden*, its plural, is debts; the sentence asserts a descent between two senses of a single word. In English the reader must be told this, because "guilt" and "debt" are unrelated words, and every rendering either tells it or hides it.

<Figure
  src="./figures/schuld-branches.svg"
  alt="The German Schuld and Schulden as one amber node at the top, with two blue branches to guilt throughout and debt throughout or ought and owe; beneath each branch a note stating what the II:4 premise becomes: guilt descends from guilt, the identity vanished and the premise a bare assertion; ought descends from owe, an English pun standing in for a German fact; a green box at the bottom gives the rendering that keeps the German in the line, the identity visible as one fact offered as evidence for a conjecture"
  caption="One German word, two English words, and three ways the II:4 premise reads depending on which is chosen and whether the German is kept."
/>

The third column is the premise <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref> reconstructs and <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref> taught the course to grade: an etymological identity offered as evidence for a historical conjecture, that the practice of holding a person answerable descends from the creditor's right against a debtor. The premise's actual form matters. The identity of the two senses is a fact about German; the descent of the moral sense from the material one is a conjecture about history, which the identity supports and does not prove, and for which II:5's law codes and II:8's "buying, selling, exchange" are the evidence proper. This is the same grading the course applied to *schlecht*/*schlicht* in I:4, where the word-history was found to hold and the inference from word-history to value-history was found to need a further premise about who used the word and of whom.

### Three renderings and what each makes the premise say

Samuel's choice is instructive because he chose twice. His title for the treatise is "'Guilt,' 'Bad Conscience,' and the Like", and in II:4 he writes that "the cardinal moral idea of 'ought' originates from the very material idea of 'owe'", with a footnote that the German word "means both debt and guilt. Cp. the English 'owe' and 'ought,' by which I occasionally render the double meaning." The footnote is honest and the line is misleading in a precise way: "ought" and "owe" are related words in English, so the sentence reads as a pun that happens to work in both languages, and the reader takes the premise to be wordplay. A rendering with "guilt" throughout makes the opposite error. "The moral concept guilt descends from the material concept debt" is a sentence with no identity in it, and the reader takes the premise for a bare historical assertion, unsupported in the sentence that makes it, which is exactly the charge the Preface laid against Rée's hypothesis-history. The rendering that keeps the premise's form is the one that keeps the German in the line: "the moral concept 'guilt' (*Schuld*) took its descent from the very material concept 'debts' (*Schulden*)". Read that way, the sentence reports one fact, that a single word carries both senses, and offers it as the evidence it is.

<Remark title="II:4's premise under three renderings">
**The German.** One word, two senses; the moral sense is claimed to descend from the material one. Status: an identity used as evidence for a conjecture.

**"Ought" from "owe" (Samuel).** Two related English words; the sentence reads as a pun that the translator has matched. Status shifts to wordplay, and the reader discounts it.

**"Guilt" from "debt" (a consistent rendering).** Two unrelated English words; the sentence reads as an unsupported historical assertion. Status shifts to a bare claim, and the reader either accepts it on authority or rejects it as a genetic fallacy.

**"Guilt (*Schuld*)" from "debts (*Schulden*)".** The identity is visible and the conjecture is separate from it. Status preserved: the identity is evidence, the descent is the conjecture, and the grading of <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref> applies to the second and not the first.
</Remark>

The row has a second entry. *Twilight*'s "Four Great Errors" §7, quoted in <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref>, says men were thought free "*um schuldig werden zu können*", and *schuldig* is the adjective of the same noun: guilty, and also indebted. The purpose clause therefore connects the free-will fiction to the Second Treatise's debt in one word, and an English reader who knows only "guilty" has the connection removed. The fourth exercise below derives what the premise proves under each reading.

<KeyFact>
*Schuld* fails in the opposite direction from *schlecht*. The *schlecht* collapse removes a distinction English has two words for, and a translator repairs it by choosing consistently; the *Schuld* shift adds a distinction German has no words for, and consistency is what does the damage, since every consistent English choice fixes the premise at one of the two wrong grades. The rule that follows binds two later quotations: II:4's sentence in <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref> and *Twilight*'s *um schuldig werden zu können* carry the German in the line, or they carry a premise of a different evidential grade from Nietzsche's.
</KeyFact>

## Mitleid: 'pity' names a different state from 'compassion'

The Preface names the value whose value it will question, and the word is *Mitleid*. §5 says the issue was "*der Werth des 'Unegoistischen', der Mitleids-, Selbstverleugnungs-, Selbstopferungs-Instinkte*", which Schopenhauer had deified, and that Nietzsche came to see "*die immer mehr um sich greifende Mitleids-Moral*" as the uncanniest symptom of European culture. Samuel has "the instincts of pity, self-denial, and self-sacrifice" and "the morality of pity"; Hollingdale's *Antichrist* §7 opens "Christianity is called the religion of pity" for "*die Religion des Mitleidens*"; Norman's BGE 260 lists "pity" among the qualities slave morality honours; Del Caro's Zarathustra has a speech titled "On the Pitying" and, in the same book, "Let your compassion be a guessing, so that you might first know whether your friend wants compassion" in "On the Friend". Reginster's essay on the target writes "compassion" throughout: "Schopenhauer's 'morality of compassion' is a target of explicit substantive criticism throughout Nietzsche's works". Two English words are in use for one German one, and the split runs through the middle of a single translation.

The German word is *mit-leiden*, to suffer with, and Nietzsche puns on the compound twice in *Beyond Good and Evil*, each time sending Norman to a footnote. BGE 222 says of the man of "modern ideas": "*Er leidet: und seine Eitelkeit will, dass er nur 'mit leidet'*", which Norman renders "He suffers: and his vanity would have it that he only pities", with the note "In German: *mit leidet* (literally: 'suffers with'). Here as elsewhere, Nietzsche is playing on the similarities between the terms *leiden* (to suffer) and *Mitleid* (pity)." BGE 293 asks "*Aber was liegt am Mitleiden Derer, welche leiden!*", Norman's "But what good is the pity of the sufferer!", with the note "Nietzsche is again playing on the similarity between *Mitleiden* (pity) and *leiden* (to suffer)." Both notes state the fact the text hides, and both, inside the same parenthesis, gloss *Mitleid* as "pity": the apparatus records the compound and the rendering discards it, so that a reader who does not stop at the superscript meets "suffers" and "pities" as two unrelated verbs where the German has one verb twice. "Compassion", from the Latin *com-pati*, is the same compound in another language and keeps the suffering-with; "pity" is a different state, one felt from above toward a sufferer one is not suffering with, and it carries condescension the German does not. That is the reason the choice is not one of tone. The third column here is what Nietzsche's objection to *Mitleid* is an objection *to*, and the two renderings make it an objection to two different things.

### Which state the dispute is about

Reginster's reconstruction of the Schopenhauer dispute is the check's third column in detail. Schopenhauer's *Mitleid* is defined by identification: for an action to be done "simply and solely for the sake of another", "this entire difference between me and everyone else, which is the very basis of my egoism, is eliminated, to a certain extent at least" (*On the Basis of Morality* §16). His argument for why egoism is the default is an argument from directness: "everyone is given to himself directly, but the rest are given to him only indirectly through their representation in his head; and the directness asserts its right" (§14). Compassion, so defined, is the state in which that directness is overridden and the self's difference from the sufferer dissolves. Reginster reads Nietzsche's second line of objection, in *Daybreak* 133 and 148 and in Preface §5, as aimed at exactly that structure: not at the sufferer being helped, but at the self-devaluation the state requires, and he argues that Nietzsche's later view is that altruism does not need this selflessness and may be incompatible with it.

Under "compassion" that objection is statable and Schopenhauer is visible as its target. Under "pity" it is not, because pity is not a self-dissolving state; a person who pities stands apart from the sufferer, and the objection reads instead as a complaint about condescension, about looking down. The course has already seen why that reading is wrong: I:10's account of noble contempt, which "looks away" and does not falsify its object, makes looking down the noble's own affect, and Nietzsche's charge against *Mitleid* is not that it looks down. A §7's first objection, that "one loses force when one pities" and that suffering "becomes contagious", survives either word, since it is about the state's effect; the objection about selflessness survives only "compassion".

<Remark title="the Mitleid row">
**German.** *Mitleid*, *Mitleiden*: suffering-with; Schopenhauer's technical term for the state in which the difference between self and other is eliminated.

**Renderings.** "Pity" (Samuel, Hollingdale, Norman, Del Caro in "On the Pitying" and Part Four's "last sin"); "compassion" (Del Caro in "On the Friend"; Reginster; the Schopenhauer literature).

**Premise.** The genealogy lesson's C1 target: the value of the "unegoistic" instincts, and Reginster's two objections, that *Mitleid* devalues suffering and that it requires self-devaluation.

**Verdict.** The effect objection (A §7) survives both; the selflessness objection survives "compassion" and shifts, under "pity", to a charge of condescension that the text does not make and I:10 contradicts.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The verdict is scoped by sentence, not by translation. Where Nietzsche's sentence is about what the state does to the one who has it, A §7's loss of force and contagion, BGE 293's "pity of the sufferer", "pity" is exact and the pun on *leiden* is the only loss. Where the sentence states the Schopenhauer dispute, Preface §5's *Mitleids-Instinkte* and *Daybreak* 133, "pity" replaces the objection to self-dissolution with an objection to looking down, which I:10 assigns to the noble and does not criticize. A course quotation from the second class of sentences carries "compassion" or the German; one from the first may keep its translator's "pity".
</KeyFact>

## Wille zur Macht: the rendering is settled and the reading is not

"Will to power" is the one rendering with no rival: Norman, Hollingdale, Kaufmann and Krell all have it, and the check's second column is a single entry. The third and fourth columns are nevertheless full, because each English word imports a sense the German sentence blocks. From a settled translation one would expect a settled reading; the phrase's history is the opposite, and the reason is in the two nouns.

"Will" names a faculty in English, the thing that does the willing. BGE 19, reconstructed in <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref>, denies that there is such a thing: "*Wollen scheint mir vor Allem etwas Complicirtes, Etwas, das nur als Wort eine Einheit ist*", "willing strikes me as, above all, something complicated, something unified only in a word" (Norman), and the section's remainder is a parts list, feelings, a commanding thought and the affect of command. A "will to power" read with the English faculty in it is a will that BGE 19 has already dissolved. "Power" in English is, by default, power over someone, and *Macht* covers that sense; but the German sentence that states the doctrine in BGE 13 uses a different noun for what is discharged. "*Vor Allem will etwas Lebendiges seine Kraft auslassen — Leben selbst ist Wille zur Macht —: die Selbsterhaltung ist nur eine der indirekten und häufigsten Folgen davon*": above all a living thing wants to let out its *strength* (*Kraft*); life itself is will to power; self-preservation is one of the indirect consequences of this. Norman keeps the two nouns apart, "discharge its strength" and "will to power"; a reader who hears "power" as domination has to explain why the doctrine's own illustration is a discharge, and why self-preservation, which domination would serve, is demoted to a consequence.

### The refutation: 'will to power' is not 'desire to dominate'

The reading to be refuted is the one the English phrase produces unaided: will to power is the desire to dominate others, the translation is exact, and so the reading is exact. It is false, and the sentence that falsifies it is BGE 259, where Nietzsche states the doctrine in its harshest vocabulary and flags the vocabulary in the same breath. "*Leben selbst ist wesentlich Aneignung, Verletzung, Überwältigung des Fremden und Schwächeren, Unterdrückung, Härte, Aufzwängung eigner Formen, Einverleibung und mindestens, mildestens, Ausbeutung, — aber wozu sollte man immer gerade solche Worte gebrauchen, denen von Alters her eine verleumderische Absicht eingeprägt ist?*" Norman: "life itself is essentially a process of appropriating, injuring, overpowering the alien and the weaker, oppressing, being harsh, imposing your own form, incorporating, and at least, the very least, exploiting, — but what is the point of always using words that have been stamped with slanderous intentions from time immemorial?" The list is a list of what a living body does to what is outside it, and "exploitation", the section adds, "belongs to the essence of being alive as a fundamental organic function". The reason the domination reading fails is that domination is a relation between agents and the doctrine is stated of organic function: a body that grows, incorporates and imposes its form is doing what BGE 13 calls discharging strength, and the aristocratic body of BGE 259 does it "not out of any morality or immorality, but because it is alive". The replacement model is Reginster's, stated in <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref>: a first-order end, resistance to it, and the overcoming of the resistance, of which domination over persons is one instance among many and not the definition. The discriminating case is BGE 13 itself: on the domination reading, self-preservation is what power is for; on the discharge reading, self-preservation is a by-product, which is what the sentence says.

Heidegger's rendering is the row's last entry, and it moves the other way. His four volumes read *Wille zur Macht* as the answer to what beings are, and in the later volumes compress it to *Wille zum Willen*, "will to will"; Krell keeps "will to power" in the text and lists it without comment in the glossary. The reading is not a translation choice but an interpretation of what "will" names, and <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref> states it at strength; the check records here only that the same English phrase carries the psychological reading, the domination misreading and the metaphysical reading, and that nothing in the two words decides between them.

<KeyFact>
The English "will to power" is exact and underdetermined: "will" imports a faculty BGE 19 dissolves and "power" imports a relation between agents that BGE 13's *Kraft auslassen* and BGE 259's "organic function" do not have, so the check's verdict on this row is "shifts", and the shift is toward a reading the German sentences deny in their own words.
</KeyFact>

## Übermensch: the prefix decides between 'over' and 'super'

The word has four renderings in use and the choice among them is the one translation decision in this chapter that a later lesson's refutation depends on. "Superman" is the oldest English form: Thomas Common's Zarathustra (1896, revised for the 1909 Levy edition) used it, Samuel's I:16 has "Napoleon, that synthesis of Monster and Superman", Hollingdale's *Twilight* §37 has "a kind of superman", and Shaw's play *Man and Superman* (1903) had given the word its associations before any of them. "Overman" is Kaufmann's (1954) and Del Caro's (2006); "overhuman" is Graham Parkes's (2005); "beyond-man" appears in commentary. The third column is <Ref lesson="overman-staged">the overman lesson</Ref>'s refutation of the breeding-programme misreading, on which the overman is a superior kind of human to be produced; the fourth column turns on a prefix.

<Figure
  src="./figures/ueber-prefix.svg"
  alt="Two panels. Left, über as over or across: a rope drawn between two posts labelled Thier and Übermensch over a shaded abyss, a walker on the rope labelled Übergang and Hinübergehende, a dashed downward arrow labelled Untergang into the abyss, with the Prologue's phrases about a bridge and not a purpose and arrows of longing for the other shore beneath, and the note that every motion is horizontal. Right, super as above: a block labelled superman standing on a block labelled man with an upward arrow labelled higher, and the note that there is no rope, no crossing, no going under."
  caption="The Prologue's images run across and under; 'super-' runs up. Only one prefix can carry the rope."
/>

Nietzsche did not coin the word: *Übermensch* had been in German use for two centuries before him, so whatever "superman" brings with it is Shaw's and not his. Kaufmann's objection to the English word is that "Nietzsche's conception depends on the associations of the word *über*", and the prefix is where the ambiguity sits. German *über* means both "above" and "across", *über dir*, above you, and *über die Brücke*, across the bridge; English "over" carries both senses, over one's head and over the river; "super-" carries only the first. A rendering is therefore tested by which sense the text selects, and Nietzsche's uses point both ways. The anchor Kaufmann himself cites is vertical: the third *Untimely Meditation*'s "true self" that lies "not deeply concealed within you but immeasurably high over you (*über dir*)". Zarathustra's Prologue, where the word is announced and where the course's refutation reads it, selects the other sense in every image it builds, and it names the vertical sense in order to reject it: §3's *überirdische Hoffnungen*, hopes above the earth, are what "the poisoners" preach, and the overman is set against them as *der Sinn der Erde*. Del Caro's translator's note gives the reason the check can test against the text: "overman" is preferred to "superhuman" because "it preserves the word play Nietzsche intends with his constant references to going under and going over", while "the comic book associations called to mind by 'superman' and super-heroes generally tend to reflect negatively, and frivolously, on the term".

### The Prologue's four images, read in the German

Prologue §3 announces the word, "*Ich lehre euch den Übermenschen. Der Mensch ist Etwas, das überwunden werden soll*", and gives it a location, "*Der Übermensch ist der Sinn der Erde*", the meaning of the earth. §4 then builds four images on the prefix, and every one of them is horizontal. "*Der Mensch ist ein Seil, geknüpft zwischen Thier und Übermensch, — ein Seil über einem Abgrunde*": a rope fastened between animal and overman, a rope over an abyss. "*Was gross ist am Menschen, das ist, dass er eine Brücke und kein Zweck ist: was geliebt werden kann am Menschen, das ist, dass er ein Übergang und ein Untergang ist*": a bridge and not a purpose, a going-over and a going-under. "*Ich liebe Die, welche nicht zu leben wissen, es sei denn als Untergehende, denn es sind die Hinübergehenden*": those who go under are those who cross over. And the great despisers are "*Pfeile der Sehnsucht nach dem andern Ufer*", arrows of longing for the other shore. Del Caro's rendering keeps all four, "a rope over an abyss", "a bridge and not a purpose", "a crossing over and a going under", "arrows of longing for the other shore", and his note on the first page of the Prologue records the pun the English is built to carry: *untergehen*, the sun's going down, "is a 'going over' or transition, *übergehen*".

The check's fourth column follows from the images. "Over" and "across" are what a rope, a bridge, a crossing and a far shore are made of; "super" is above, and a superior kind standing on the human is a pedestal, not a bridge. The breeding-programme misreading needs the pedestal, a type to be produced and ranked; the Prologue's overman is what the human is a transition toward, and the human is loved for being a *Übergang*, which a pedestal cannot say. The one use of the word in the argued books points the same way: I:16's Napoleon is "*diese Synthesis von Unmensch und Übermensch*", a synthesis of inhuman and overhuman, the word attached in its only *Genealogy* appearance to a man the same sentence calls a monster, which is a problem posed and not an ideal named. "Superman" can carry Samuel's sentence; it cannot carry the rope.

### Herr as 'ruler': the same decision threaded through Part Four

Del Caro's second note carries the decision at the point where the overman and the word *Herr* meet. "On the Higher Man" (Part Four) has "God died: now we want – the overman to live" two lines above "How shall human being be overcome?", and the section's "only now the higher man becomes – ruler!" carries Del Caro's footnote: "Neither 'lord' nor 'master' fits here for *Herr*, 'ruler.' See 'On the Three Evils' where Nietzsche defends *Herrschsucht*, 'lust to rule,' a noun based on *herrschen*, 'to rule,' which in turn is based on *Herr*, ruler. Nietzsche's motif for TSZ Part 4 is 'who shall be ruler of the earth.' The earth can neither be 'lorded' nor 'mastered,' but according to Nietzsche, it shall be ruled." The note does what the check asks of a translator: it names the candidates, states the choice, and ties it to a second passage in which the same root does its work. Whether "ruler" is right is a question the overman lesson can put to the text; what the note makes possible is that the question can be put at all.

<KeyFact>
The prefix decides the sense, not the doctrine. Selecting "across" removes the pedestal: a rendering whose prefix means only "above" cannot state the transition the four images describe, so the reader of "superman" has no text in which to find them. It does not by itself refute the breeding reading, which can be restated horizontally as a crossing to be engineered; that refutation needs Z I "On the New Idol", BGE 251 and A §4's "lucky accident", and <Ref lesson="overman-staged">the overman lesson</Ref> supplies them. The row's verdict is therefore "collapses" for the Prologue's premise and "harmless" for I:16 and *Twilight* §37, which "superman" carries.
</KeyFact>

## Rausch, Versucher, Herrschsucht: footnote or domesticate

The last row is three words, and what they share is that no English word covers them, so that the translator's choice is between a footnote that makes the choice visible and a domesticated rendering that hides it. The check's verdict on the choice depends on whether a premise sits in the word; for one of the three it does, and it is a premise the course reaches only in its last module.

*Rausch* is the physiological precondition of art in *Twilight*'s "Expeditions" §8: "*dazu ist eine physiologische Vorbedingung unumgänglich: der Rausch. Der Rausch muss erst die Erregbarkeit der ganzen Maschine gesteigert haben*", and "*das Wesentliche am Rausch ist das Gefühl der Kraftsteigerung und Fülle*". Hollingdale renders it "intoxication" throughout: "a certain physiological precondition is indispensable: intoxication. Intoxication must first have heightened the excitability of the entire machine", and "the essence of intoxication is the feeling of plenitude and increased energy". Krell, translating Heidegger's §14 on the same passage, renders it "rapture", and his footnote states the policy: "*Rausch* is commonly rendered as 'frenzy' in translations of Nietzsche's writings, but 'rapture,' from the past participle of *rapere*, to seize, seems in some respects a better alternative. No single English word—rapture, frenzy, ecstasy, transport, intoxication, delirium—can capture all the senses of *Rausch*." He adds that "in modern German *Rausch* most often refers to drunken frenzy or narcotic intoxication", and that he chose "rapture" "because of its complex erotic and religious background". The same sentence of Nietzsche's, in Krell's Heidegger, reads "rapture must first have augmented the excitability of the entire machine".

The premise is Heidegger's, and it is the one <Ref lesson="alleged-biologism">the biologism lesson</Ref> tests: that Nietzsche's physiology of art is not physiology. §14 sets the two readings side by side. "On the one hand, art is to be the countermovement to nihilism ... On the other hand, art is at the same time to be properly grasped by way of physiology and with its means", and "if art is just a matter of physiology, then the essence and reality of art dissolve into nervous states, into processes in the nerve cells". "Intoxication" makes the nerve-state reading the natural one, since the English word names a chemical condition, and Nietzsche's list in §8, which subordinates "the influence of narcotics" to sexual excitement, the feast, the contest, cruelty and the will, becomes a list of ways of being drunk. "Rapture" makes the state a seizure of the whole person and keeps Heidegger's claim, that *Rausch* is a way of being rather than a nerve-state, available in the English. The sixth exercise asks which reading each word makes easy and which Heidegger's argument needs; the check's verdict here is "shifts", with the shift running toward the reading the translator's own philosophy prefers, which is the case in which a footnote is the only honest instrument.

### Del Caro's Versucher: the choice laid out as it was made

<Figure
  src="./figures/del-caro-versucher-footnote.png"
  alt="A printed page of the Del Caro Zarathustra: the opening of 'On the Vision and the Riddle', with a small superscript 1 after the word researchers in the first line; the speech runs down the page in the text size, and a footnote in smaller type fills the last eight lines at the foot, its German terms in italic"
  caption="Del Caro's page 124. In the text the superscript after 'researchers' is the only sign that a choice was made; the note that argues it fills eight lines of small type at the foot, longer than the sentence it serves."
/>

"On the Vision and the Riddle" (Part Three) opens with an address, "*Euch, den kühnen Suchern, Versuchern*", and the second noun has three senses that one English word cannot hold. Del Caro's footnote sets them out: "When the prefix *ver-* is added to *suchen*, to seek or to search, the verb is modified to mean try, attempt, but also tempt, so that the noun *Versucher* means both one who attempts and one who tempts. The noun *der Versuch*, meanwhile, means both attempt and experiment. Nietzsche frequently alludes to his favorite deity, Dionysus, as the *Versucher-Gott*, i.e. as the tempter god, attempter god (experimenter). I render this wordplay as 'searcher' and 'researcher' to preserve the wordplay, but wherever this particular combination occurs in TSZ or elsewhere, one should suspect Nietzsche is exploring the relationship between searching, attempting (experimenting, researching) and tempting." The rendering "bold searchers, researchers" keeps the sound of the pun and loses two of its three senses; the footnote returns them, and returns with them the reason the address stands at the head of the speech that stages eternal recurrence, whose reader is being tempted, tested and made a subject of an experiment at once. A translation that renders the phrase without a note leaves no trace that a choice was made, and a reader of that translation cannot know that the recurrence is introduced as a temptation.

The third word is the one Del Caro's *Herr* note already carried. *Herrschsucht* is *herrschen*, to rule, with the suffix *-sucht*, which names a pathological craving (*Trunksucht* is alcoholism, *Eifersucht* jealousy), and Del Caro's "lust to rule" in "On the Three Evils", where it is weighed beside sex and selfishness as the three "best-cursed things in the world", keeps both the ruling and the pathology. A domesticated "ambition" would keep neither, and the speech's argument, that the cursed thing has been slandered, needs the curse to be audible in the word.

<Remark title="the apparatus as argument">
Del Caro's two notes and Krell's share a form: the German term is named, the candidates are listed, the choice is stated, and the reader is told what the choice loses. That form is the check's four columns filled in by the translator instead of by the reader, and a translation that carries it can be audited; one that does not must be audited from the German. Samuel's *Schuld* footnote is the same form in 1913. The course's rule for a quotation it depends on is therefore: quote the translation the course uses, and where the row's third column is non-empty, put the German in the line.
</Remark>

## What the check establishes

What is established: a translation is a reading fixed in print, and the check runs a reconstruction under each rendering of a load-bearing term and records whether the premise survives, collapses or shifts; the two-word particle *eher noch* shows the smallest unit at which the check can turn a ranking into an endorsement; *schlecht* and *böse* are two contraries of one "good", Samuel keeps them apart in I:4, I:10, I:11 and the I:17 note, and a rendering that merged them would leave the asymmetry intact in the German and unstatable in the English; *Schuld* is one word for guilt and debt, and every English rendering shifts II:4's premise, to a pun or to an assertion, unless the German is kept in the line, so that the identity reads as the evidence it is and the descent as the conjecture it is; *Mitleid* is suffering-with, "compassion" names Schopenhauer's self-dissolving state and "pity" names a different one, and only the first carries the selflessness objection Preface §5 makes; "will to power" is settled and underdetermined, and BGE 13, 19 and 259 in the German deny both the faculty and the domination the English words import; *Übermensch* turns on a prefix, the Prologue's rope, bridge, crossing and far shore are horizontal, and only "over" can carry them; and *Rausch*, *Versucher* and *Herrschsucht* are words no English word covers, where the translator's footnote is the check's four columns filled in advance.

Nothing on the core path depends on this chapter. The First Treatise's argument was reconstructed in <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the master-and-slave lesson</Ref>, <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref> and <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref> with the German in the parentheses, and the module's application, <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref>, maps a speech of Zarathustra onto that argument in Del Caro's English. The check is available to any later lesson whose exercises ask what a translator's word does to a proof, and the rows for *Schuld*, *Wille zur Macht* and *Übermensch* are where <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref>, <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref> and <Ref lesson="overman-staged">the overman lesson</Ref> will find them.

II:4's premise is an etymological identity. Derive what the premise would prove if *Schuld* had two unrelated senses that happened to share a spelling (as English "bank" does), and what it does prove given one root; say what further evidence the second case needs and where the Second Treatise offers it; and then state which of the three renderings in the lesson hides the conjecture's status, and how.

**Two unrelated senses.** If "guilt" and "debt" were homonyms in German, the identity would prove nothing: a shared spelling is no evidence of a shared history, and "the moral concept *Schuld* descends from the material concept *Schulden*" would be a claim about two words that happen to coincide, on a par with deriving a riverbank from a savings bank. The premise's whole force depends on the two senses being one word by descent.

**One root.** Given one root, the identity proves that German speakers at some time used one word for owing and for being answerable, so that the moral sense and the material sense were not distinguished in the vocabulary. It does not prove which sense came first, nor that the practice of holding a person answerable *descends* from the creditor's right, since a word can carry two senses that arose together or in the other order. What it establishes is that the descent conjecture is consistent with the record of the language and that the burden of showing the two senses were always separate falls on the other side, which is exactly how the master-and-slave lesson graded I:4's *schlecht*/*schlicht*: the word-history holds, and the inference from word-history to value-history needs a premise about usage.

"Ought" from "owe" (Samuel's line) makes the identity look like an English pun, so the reader files the premise as wordplay and never asks what evidence the conjecture has. <Exercise>
*Ressentiment* is a term with no translation problem in the modern editions: Nietzsche wrote it in French in a German text and the later translators keep it (Samuel's 1913 "resentment" is the exception). Explain why keeping the foreign word is itself a translation decision, what the French word signals that "resentment" does not, and whether the asymmetry argument of the First Treatise would change if "resentment" were used throughout.

<Solution>
**Why it is a decision.** A translator who keeps *Ressentiment* is choosing to reproduce Nietzsche's own choice of a foreign word over the German alternatives, and the choice has content. <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">The slave-revolt lesson</Ref> recorded the alternatives: German has *Groll* (a grudge) and *Rachsucht* (vengefulness), and Nietzsche used neither. Keeping the French preserves the fact that the term was marked as foreign in the original; translating it as "resentment" domesticates a word Nietzsche refused to domesticate, and the reader of Samuel cannot tell that anything was refused.

**What the French signals.** Three things. The prefix: *re-sentiment* is a feeling felt again, and I:10's definition turns on not forgetting and on waiting, on an affect that is kept rather than discharged; English "resentment" has the same etymology but not the same audible prefix. The register: the French moralists (La Rochefoucauld, whom Preface §5 names among the philosophers who despised pity) are the tradition of psychological observation Nietzsche placed himself in, and the word imports that register. The condition: I:10 defines the term by impotence, "beings to whom the real reaction, that of the deed, is denied"; English "resentment" is an ordinary emotion anyone may feel and get over, and the technical sense, an affect that cannot discharge and so poisons, is carried by the foreign word's strangeness.

**Would the asymmetry change?** No, in its logic; yes, in its readability. The asymmetry is a claim about which term is coined first, stated in I:10 and I:11, and it does not depend on the name of the condition that produces the slave's valuation; P1 to P3 of the master-and-slave lesson's reconstruction survive under "resentment". What changes is the boundary of the concept. I:10 says nobles feel the affect and discharge it (Mirabeau, who forgot insults), so that *ressentiment* in the treatise's sense is a condition and not a class; under "resentment", an emotion any reader has felt and discharged, the boundary between the noble's discharged affect and the slave's retained one is harder to hold, and the reading on which "resentment" is simply what oppressed people feel, a class fact, becomes the natural one. The word is not a premise of the asymmetry; it is the name of the mechanism, and the mechanism's definition by impotence is what the foreign word keeps in view.

<Solution>
**The German.** "*Der Rausch muss erst die Erregbarkeit der ganzen Maschine gesteigert haben: eher kommt es zu keiner Kunst*", followed by a list in which sexual excitement is "the oldest and most original form", and then "*Das Wesentliche am Rausch ist das Gefühl der Kraftsteigerung und Fülle*". The word in ordinary modern German is drunkenness or a narcotic high, as Krell's note concedes; Nietzsche's list places narcotics late and subordinate, after feasting, contest, cruelty, spring and the will.

**"Intoxication" (Hollingdale).** Makes the nerve-state reading easy: art's precondition is a chemical condition of the "machine", and the physiology of art is a physiology in the ordinary sense, of excitability and its causes. On this reading Nietzsche's list is a list of ways of being drunk and the "feeling of plenitude and increased energy" is a symptom. The reading is consistent with the German's ordinary sense and with the naturalist hypothesis, on which a physiology of art is what it says.

**"Rapture" (Krell).** Makes the seizure reading easy: the person is taken hold of as a whole (*rapere*, to seize), and the physiology is of a state of the whole living being rather than of its nerves. Krell's note says the choice was made for the word's "erotic and religious background", which fits the German's first item and its festival items, and Heidegger's §14 needs it: his claim is that "if art is just a matter of physiology, then the essence and reality of art dissolve into nervous states", and that Nietzsche's physiology must be read otherwise, as art's being "the countermovement to nihilism". Under "intoxication" that claim has to argue against the translation; under "rapture" it is already half made.

**The verdict.** "Shifts", and the shift runs toward the reading of the philosopher being translated. That is not a fault in Krell, whose footnote lays the choice out and names the alternatives, and it is not a fault in Hollingdale, whose word is the ordinary sense. It is the case the check exists for: a term with no covering English word, where each rendering is a reading, and where the course's own question, whether Nietzsche's physiology of art is biology or something Heidegger says biology cannot be, cannot be settled by either translation but only by the German list and by the argument of <Ref lesson="alleged-biologism">the biologism lesson</Ref>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Read Zarathustra's Prologue §§3–4 in Del Caro and list every image built on "over" or "across": the rope, the bridge, the going-under, the going-over, the crossing, the other shore, and any others you find. For each, write the German word from the critical edition. Then decide whether "superman" could carry the list, image by image, and state what the decision implies for a reading on which the overman is a superior kind of human to be bred.

<Solution>
**The list, with the German.** "The overman is the meaning of the earth" (*der Sinn der Erde*: a location, not a height). "A rope fastened between animal and overman – a rope over an abyss" (*ein Seil, geknüpft zwischen Thier und Übermensch, — ein Seil über einem Abgrunde*). "A dangerous crossing, a dangerous on-the-way, a dangerous looking back" (*ein gefährliches Hinüber, ein gefährliches Auf-dem-Wege, ein gefährliches Zurückblicken*). "A bridge and not a purpose" (*eine Brücke und kein Zweck*). "A crossing over and a going under" (*ein Übergang und ein Untergang*). "Those who do not know how to live unless by going under, for they are the ones who cross over" (*Untergehende ... Hinübergehende*). "Arrows of longing for the other shore" (*Pfeile der Sehnsucht nach dem andern Ufer*). "He strides as spirit over the bridge" (*über die Brücke*). Every image is a horizontal transit with a fall beneath it.

**Can "superman" carry them?** The rope: no; a rope is between two points at one level, and "super" places the second point above. The bridge: no, for the same reason. The going-over and going-under: no; *Übergang* and *Untergang* are a pair, over and under, and "super" has no under. The crossing and the other shore: no; a shore is across, not up. The meaning of the earth: no; "super-" is what the Prologue's "extraterrestrial hopes" (*überirdische Hoffnungen*) are made of, and §3 calls their preachers poisoners. Not one image survives the prefix. Kaufmann's remark that "the allegories Zarathustra uses in his first speech have led most readers astray" is a remark about readers of "superman": under that word the rope and the abyss read as a hero above a drop, and under "overman" they read as a transition.

**The doorstep.** A superior kind of human to be bred is a pedestal reading: a type that stands above the present one and can be produced. The Prologue's overman is what the human is a bridge toward, and the human is loved for being a *Übergang*, a transition, which no product is. The choice of "overman" is not decoration; it is the reading on which <Ref lesson="overman-staged">the overman lesson</Ref> refutes the breeding-programme misreading from the text, with Z I "On the New Idol", BGE 251 and A §4's "lucky accident" as its further evidence. The reader who has just found that no image survives "super-" has done the linguistic half of that refutation.
</Solution>

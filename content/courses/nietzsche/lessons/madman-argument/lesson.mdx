The sentence 'God is dead' in The Gay Science §125 is not a claim about whether God exists; it reports that a belief which once founded European values has stopped being believed, and concludes that the values must follow. This lesson turns the passage that carries the sentence, a parable of a madman in a marketplace, into an argument: four numbered premises, one conclusion, and one premise the text needs and does not state.

This is the first move of the course's argument, and nothing precedes it. The course claims that Nietzsche's books, read as premises and conclusions in words, hold together as one extended argument about the origin, authority and cost of European morality, and that a naturalist reading makes them hold best. Before any of that can be tested, the problem those books answer has to be stated, and Nietzsche states it in this passage: the foundation is gone, the edifice is standing, and its inhabitants have not noticed. What stands open at the end is the method, since a reconstruction done once by hand has to become a protocol that can be run on any numbered section, which <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the next lesson</Ref> supplies.

The route: the passage read as five moves, the reconstruction in standard form, the delay premise and the word it introduces, the misreading the text rules out, and the premise the argument is missing.

## The passage: a marketplace that already disbelieves

A parable can be reconstructed only after it has been read whole, and Section 125 is short enough to read whole; it sits in Book III of *The Gay Science* (*Die fröhliche Wissenschaft*) and is the passage everyone quotes. The book was published in 1882 as four books of numbered sections; a preface and a fifth book were added to the second edition of 1887. The critical edition prints the section under the siglum [FW-125](http://www.nietzschesource.org/#eKGWB/FW-125), and the German quoted below is that text; translations in this lesson are the course's own unless a translator is named.

The section's title is *Der tolle Mensch*. The English convention since Walter Kaufmann's translation is "the madman"; *toll* is nearer to "crazed" or "raving" than to a clinical word, and the course keeps "madman" as the fixed label. The opening sentence sets the scene and, with it, the first premise:

> Habt ihr nicht von jenem tollen Menschen gehört, der am hellen Vormittage eine Laterne anzündete, auf den Markt lief und unaufhörlich schrie: „Ich suche Gott! Ich suche Gott!“ — Da dort gerade Viele von Denen zusammen standen, welche nicht an Gott glaubten, so erregte er ein grosses Gelächter.
>
> Have you not heard of that madman who lit a lantern in the bright morning, ran to the market and cried without cease: "I seek God! I seek God!" Since many of those who did not believe in God happened to be standing together there, he provoked great laughter.

The listeners do not believe in God. That is stated before the madman says anything of substance, and it fixes what the speech can and cannot be arguing. A speech that tried to persuade this crowd that God does not exist would be persuading them of what they already hold; the laughter confirms it. The crowd's jokes ("is he lost?", "is he hiding?", "has he gone on a voyage, emigrated?") treat God's absence as settled and the search as absurd. The madman's opening question, *Wohin ist Gott?*, "Where has God gone?", is answered by him, not by them:

> Wir haben ihn getödtet, — ihr und ich! Wir Alle sind seine Mörder!
>
> We have killed him: you and I. We are all his murderers.

### The questions are about consequence

What follows is a run of questions, and every one of them is about what follows from the killing, none about whether it happened. "How were we able to drink up the sea? Who gave us the sponge to wipe away the whole horizon? What did we do when we unchained this earth from its sun?" The sea, the horizon and the sun are things that everything else is oriented by; the questions say that removing the belief removed an orientation. The run ends in the same key: the holiest and mightiest thing the world has yet possessed "has bled to death under our knives", and then, *Mit welchem Wasser könnten wir uns reinigen?*, "with what water could we clean ourselves?"

The crowd falls silent. The madman smashes the lantern, and the section's last speech is about time:

> „Ich komme zu früh, sagte er dann, ich bin noch nicht an der Zeit. Diess ungeheure Ereigniss ist noch unterwegs und wandert, — es ist noch nicht bis zu den Ohren der Menschen gedrungen. Blitz und Donner brauchen Zeit, das Licht der Gestirne braucht Zeit, Thaten brauchen Zeit, auch nachdem sie gethan sind, um gesehen und gehört zu werden.“
>
> "I come too early," he said then, "my time is not yet. This tremendous event is still under way, still travelling; it has not yet reached the ears of men. Lightning and thunder need time, the light of the stars needs time, deeds need time even after they are done, to be seen and heard."

A coda reports that the madman entered several churches that day and sang his *Requiem aeternam deo*, "eternal rest to God": the opening words of the Catholic mass for the dead, *Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine*, with God put in the place of the deceased. Led out, he answered only: "What are these churches now, if not the tombs and sepulchres of God?"

The listeners' unbelief is a premise of the speech, not its conclusion, and the staging is not incidental: only an audience whose belief is gone while its values are intact can serve, later in the argument, as a symptom of the delay rather than a counterexample to the collapse.

<Aside>
Kaufmann reads the madman as Nietzsche "prophetically envisaging himself", and tests two analogies for the role. The prophet Hosea experienced his own marriage as a symbol of God's relation to Israel, so that a private fate became a public prophecy; Kaufmann grants the parallel in form (a private agony read as a coming general one) and withdraws it in content, since the Hebrew prophets spoke as mouthpieces of a God who promised, while the madman speaks doom without promise. The figure that fits the content is Cassandra, "prophetess of doom without promise and nemesis without love". The analogy is proposed, tested against what the text says, and kept only in the respect that survives.
</Aside>

## The reconstruction: four premises and a conclusion about morality

The section is a parable, and a parable is not an argument until someone writes the premises out. The course's standing method is the one applied here.

<Remark title="argument reconstruction">
To *reconstruct* a passage is to restate it as numbered premises and a conclusion in plain words, each premise tied to a sentence of the text, so that three things can be checked: whether the conclusion follows from the premises (validity), whether the premises are true (soundness), and which premise the inference needs that the passage does not state. The reconstruction is the reader's; the text supplies only the anchors.
</Remark>

The conclusion is the first thing to fix, since it decides which sentences count as premises. The listeners already grant that there is no God, so the conclusion is not that. The questions of consequence point at what the conclusion is about: what happens to the things that were oriented by the belief. Section 125 names those things only in images (sea, horizon, sun). The sentence that names them plainly is in Section 343, the opening of Book V, written for the 1887 edition:

> … was Alles, nachdem dieser Glaube untergraben ist, nunmehr einfallen muss, weil es auf ihm gebaut, an ihn gelehnt, in ihn hineingewachsen war: zum Beispiel unsre ganze europäische Moral.
>
> … everything that must now collapse, once this belief is undermined, because it was built on it, leaned against it, had grown into it: for example our whole European morality.

That sentence supplies the first premise and names the conclusion's subject. With it the reconstruction runs as follows.

<Example title="GS 125 in standard form">
**P1 (the dependence).** European values, the moral ones above all, rested on belief in the Christian God. *Anchor:* GS 343, "built on it, leaned against it, had grown into it: for example our whole European morality". GS 125 carries the same premise only as image: the sea, the horizon, the sun.

**P2 (the collapse).** That belief has collapsed among the educated of the present. *Anchor:* GS 125, "we have killed him: you and I", spoken to a crowd that "did not believe in God"; GS 343, the belief "has become unbelievable" (*unglaubwürdig geworden*).

**P3 (the structural rule).** What rests on a foundation falls when the foundation is removed. *Anchor:* GS 125's questions ("what did we do when we unchained this earth from its sun? Where is it moving now?"); GS 343, "must now collapse … because it was built on it".

**P4 (the delay).** The fall arrives after a delay: the consequences of the collapse are not yet visible to those it has happened to. *Anchor:* GS 125, "I come too early … the light of the stars needs time"; GS 108, the shadow shown in the cave.

**C.** European morality must fall, and its heirs do not yet know it. *Anchor:* GS 343, "this long plenitude and sequence of demolition, destruction, downfall, overturning that now stands before us".

**Validity.** From P1, P2 and P3 the first half of C follows: the values rested on the belief, the belief is gone, so the values must go. P4 adds the second half, that the heirs do not yet know it, and explains the crowd's laughter as a symptom rather than a refutation. One step is not covered: P3 says that what rests on a foundation falls when the foundation is removed, but says nothing about whether another foundation can be put in its place. That premise is examined in the last section.

**Soundness, in outline.** P2 is an empirical claim about educated Europe in the 1880s; Nietzsche's evidence for it is the marketplace itself, and GS 357 calls the decline of belief "a pan-European event". P1 is a historical claim about the authority of the values, not their content: the claim is that their claim on us was underwritten by God, whatever their origin. P3 enters the text only as images (the sea, the horizon, the sun), and as a premise it holds only if nothing else can serve as a foundation, which is the premise the text does not state.
</Example>

## The delay premise: light from a dead star

P4 is the premise the parable spends its last speech on, and it is the premise that makes the argument addressable to its audience at all. Without it, the crowd's laughter would be evidence against the conclusion: educated people have stopped believing and their values are visibly intact, so the values did not depend on the belief. With it, the laughter is what the argument predicts. The consequences of removing a foundation take time to arrive, and the people standing in the interval see nothing wrong.

The parable's image for the interval is astronomical: *das Licht der Gestirne braucht Zeit*. Light leaving a star reaches an observer long after it left, so a star can be seen for a period after it has ceased to exist. The belief is the star, the values are the light still arriving, and the observer in the marketplace sees a sky full of light and concludes that nothing has happened. Martin Heidegger, in his 1940 Freiburg lectures on European nihilism, glosses the same image: the process "resembles the process in which the light of a star that has been extinguished for millennia still gleams, but in its gleaming nonetheless remains a mere 'appearance'". The gloss matters because Heidegger reads Nietzsche as a metaphysician, against the naturalist reading this course tests, and still reads P4 exactly as the reconstruction does: whatever "God is dead" means, it is an event with a lag.

### Section 108 states P4 first

The sentence "God is dead" does not first appear in Section 125. It appears seventeen sections earlier, at the head of Book III, in a section titled *Neue Kämpfe*, "New struggles":

> Nachdem Buddha todt war, zeigte man noch Jahrhunderte lang seinen Schatten in einer Höhle, — einen ungeheuren schauerlichen Schatten. Gott ist todt: aber so wie die Art der Menschen ist, wird es vielleicht noch Jahrtausende lang Höhlen geben, in denen man seinen Schatten zeigt. — Und wir — wir müssen auch noch seinen Schatten besiegen!
>
> After Buddha was dead, his shadow was still shown for centuries in a cave: a tremendous, dreadful shadow. God is dead: but given the way of men, there may for millennia yet be caves in which his shadow is shown. And we: we must still defeat his shadow too.

Section 108 is P4 stated as a historical parallel before the parable dramatizes it: a belief can be over and its shadow can be exhibited for a very long time afterwards. Section 343 then gives the delay its present tense: the event "is already beginning to cast its first shadows over Europe", and "the event itself is far too great, too remote, too far outside the grasp of the many for even the news of it to count as having arrived". The three statements agree on the structure and differ only in tense: Section 108 says the shadow will persist, Section 125 says the news has not arrived, Section 343 says the first shadows are visible to a few.

### The word for the interval's end

Section 343 calls what stands before Europe "this long plenitude and sequence of demolition, destruction, downfall, overturning". Nietzsche has a single word for the state the sequence ends in. *Nihilism* names the condition in which the highest values have lost their foundation and the question "why?" receives no answer: values still professed, nothing left to hold them up. The word's first use in The Gay Science is in Section 346, three sections after 343, where the coming generations are offered an either/or, "abolish your reverences or yourselves", and both branches are called nihilism. The word is the course's subject in <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the lesson on nihilism and affirmation</Ref>, where it splits into two conditions; here it is only the name for what C describes once the delay has run out.

<Aside>
The word is older than Nietzsche's use of it. Its first philosophical use is Friedrich Jacobi's open letter to Fichte of 1799, which called Fichte's idealism nihilism; its currency comes from Turgenev's *Fathers and Sons* (1862), where the nihilist Bazarov holds that nothing counts as real except what the senses deliver and nothing counts as authoritative that rests on tradition alone. Nietzsche's "European nihilism" takes the word from that Russian usage and changes its scope: not a position some people hold but a process the whole culture is in.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
P4 cuts both ways: it turns intact values into a predicted symptom, and it could absorb any intact values whatever as "not yet". What stops the premise from immunizing the argument is that Sections 108 and 343 give the delay a scale and a first sign, centuries to millennia, and "first shadows" already visible to a few.
</KeyFact>

## Discredit, not refute: what the misreading cannot accommodate

The three sections make one claim, and the course's reading of it stands against a reading that is at least as widespread: that "God is dead" is Nietzsche's announcement of atheism, the parable an argument that God does not exist. Section 343's own definition of the event decides between the two readings.

### What Section 343 says the death is

Section 343 opens by defining the phrase in an apposition: *das grösste neuere Ereigniss, — dass „Gott todt ist“, dass der Glaube an den christlichen Gott unglaubwürdig geworden ist*, "the greatest recent event, that 'God is dead', that belief in the Christian God has become unbelievable". The subject of the second clause is the belief, and the predicate is *unglaubwürdig*, unworthy of belief. Neither clause says that the belief has been shown false. Kaufmann's formulation of the point is the standard one: Nietzsche "is not saying, as it were: you have been told that there is a God, but verily I say unto you, There is no God. What he does say is 'God is dead'", and the sentence is "a declaration of what he takes to be a historical cultural fact", a diagnosis of a civilization rather than a metaphysical thesis.

Bernard Reginster's 2006 study of nihilism in Nietzsche sharpens the grammar. A belief can be *refuted*, shown false; it can be *suspended*, its truth and falsehood both left open; and it can be *discredited*, left unrefuted while the possibility of its truth is no longer taken seriously. His illustration is a child who reports ghosts in her room. Looking and finding none does not refute the report, since ghosts could have left or be invisible; learning that she had watched a horror film that evening does not refute it either, but it explains the belief without appeal to ghosts, and after that the possibility is not worth pursuing. Nietzsche's own statement of the method is §95 of *Daybreak* (*Morgenröthe*, 1881), the book of aphorisms on the prejudices of morality that precedes The Gay Science; the section is titled "Historical refutation as the definitive refutation": formerly one tried to prove that there is no God; today one shows how the belief arose and acquired its weight, "and a counter-proof that there is no God thereby becomes superfluous".

Run the parable through the distinction. The madman never argues that God does not exist; he says the belief has been killed and asks what the killing costs. Section 343 says the belief has become *unglaubwürdig*. Both are statements about a belief's standing, not about God, and the argument's conclusion inherits that: C is about the standing of European morality, whose foundation has been discredited, not about whether its precepts are true.

### The reading and the sentences it has no place for

The misreading, then: "God is dead" is an argument for atheism. It is **rejected** because three sentences of the section it claims to read have no place in it. The listeners "did not believe in God" before the madman spoke, so the reading's conclusion is the speech's premise. Every question the madman asks is about consequence, "with what water could we clean ourselves?", and an argument for a conclusion the audience already holds would have no reason to spend itself on consequences. And the madman's one act after the speech is a requiem sung in churches, a rite for a death, addressed to buildings he calls tombs; a proof needs no rite.

The replacement is the reading the reconstruction already embodies: a diagnosis of what follows, for the values that depended on it, from a belief's loss of credit. Kaufmann adds the methodological half. Nietzsche's atheism is "a corollary of his basic commitment to question all premises and to reject them unless they are for some reason inescapable", against George Allen Morgan's *What Nietzsche Means* (1941), an American study written a decade before Kaufmann's, which held that atheism is "beyond question the major premise" of the philosophy. If atheism were the premise, the argument would beg its question against a believer; since it is a corollary, the argument's real premise is P2, a claim about what educated Europeans in fact believe, and a believer can accept P2 as a description of their neighbours while rejecting it as a description of themselves.

### The tone is not a lament

The same section fixes the claim's tone, and the tone is evidence about the claim. The free spirits, Section 343 says, feel at the news "as if lit by a new dawn"; the sea "lies open again", and "perhaps there has never been such an open sea". Reginster draws the inference the course adopts: since the death of God can be received with cheerfulness, nihilistic despair does not follow from it alone, and any reading on which the parable is a lament has added a premise the section denies. Which premise that is, and what it costs, is the business of <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the nihilism lesson</Ref>.

<KeyFact>
Because the belief is discredited rather than refuted, C inherits the same limit: it is a claim about the standing of European morality, not the truth of its precepts, and every genealogy in the course has that shape, since a history of how a value arose can remove its authority but not prove it false (<Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref> states the limit).
</KeyFact>

## ★ What "revaluation" was planned to be

Section 125 states a problem, and the reader who has met only this passage can take the problem for the programme. Nietzsche's own plan for the programme survives in a note of autumn 1888: the projected *Revaluation of All Values* was to have four books: *The Antichrist*, an attempt at a critique of Christianity; *The Free Spirit*, a critique of philosophy as a nihilistic movement; *The Immoralist*, a critique of "the most fatal kind of ignorance, morality"; and *Dionysus*, a philosophy of eternal recurrence. Only the first was written, and it is the book this course reads in <Ref lesson="antichrist-applied-genealogy">the lesson on The Antichrist</Ref>.

## The missing premise: why the edifice cannot be refounded

The reconstruction has one gap, between P3 and C. P3 says that what rests on a foundation falls when the foundation is removed. Between P3 and C sits an assumption: that no other foundation can be put under the same values. The assumption is not obvious. The reader who knows Kant's categorical imperative knows one proposal for exactly such a refounding, a moral law binding on all rational beings with no theological premise; utilitarianism is a second; an appeal to shared humanity a third. If any of them succeeds, the values survive the belief, and the edifice stands on new footings. Neither Section 125 nor Section 343 mentions the possibility.

<Figure
  src="./figures/refounding-candidates.svg"
  alt="An edifice labeled European morality, the precepts, stands above a dashed, withdrawn foundation block labeled belief in the Christian God; three dashed candidate footings sit below it, Kant's moral law, utility, and shared humanity, and an amber bar across them reads the missing premise, none of these carries the same precepts, asserted in Twilight IX:5 and argued in the Genealogy"
  caption="The inference from P3 to C needs a premise the parable never states: that none of the three secular footings can carry the same precepts; Twilight IX:5 asserts it, and the argument for it is the Genealogy."
/>

Nietzsche does mention it, in *Twilight of the Idols*, written in 1888, in the fifth section of the chapter "Expeditions of an Untimely Man". The occasion is George Eliot and the English moralists who "have got rid of the Christian God, and now feel obliged to cling all the more firmly to Christian morality". In R. J. Hollingdale's translation:

> When one gives up Christian belief one thereby deprives oneself of the right to Christian morality. For the latter is absolutely not self-evident … Christianity is a system, a consistently thought out and complete view of things. If one breaks out of it a fundamental idea, the belief in God, one thereby breaks the whole thing to pieces: one has nothing of any consequence left in one's hands.

That is the missing premise, asserted. The section adds a sub-argument: Christian morality "is a command: its origin is transcendental; it is beyond all criticism … it possesses truth only if God is truth; it stands or falls with the belief in God". The sub-argument establishes that Christian morality *as a command from God* cannot survive God; it does not establish that the same precepts cannot be re-derived from a Kantian or utilitarian base, which is what the English were attempting. The passage closes the gap by declaring it closed.

<Remark title="where the missing premise is argued">
The argument for the missing premise is not in The Gay Science or in Twilight. It has two parts elsewhere. That the precepts themselves are products of a particular history and a particular human type, so that they carry no authority once their claim to be given is removed, is the Genealogy's business, beginning in <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the lesson on genealogy as method</Ref>. That the secular refoundings themselves inherit the ascetic faith they were meant to replace is the Third Treatise's business, in <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the lesson on the will to truth</Ref>. Until those arguments are reconstructed, the step from P3 to C is an assertion, and the reconstruction records it as one.
</Remark>

A second thing the argument does not state is what killed the belief. P2 reports the collapse and assigns agency ("you and I") without a mechanism, and the parable's images (drinking the sea, wiping away the horizon) describe the size of the deed, not its cause. Section 357, also in Book V, names the cause in one sentence, and the closing exercise on what killed the belief reads it; the mechanism it names is the thesis of the Genealogy's last section, GM III:27, and is reconstructed in <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the will-to-truth lesson</Ref>.

<KeyFact>
The step from "the foundation is gone" to "the edifice must fall" needs the premise that no other foundation is available; Twilight IX:5 asserts it against the English, and the argument for it is the Genealogy.
</KeyFact>

What is established: "God is dead" is a claim about a belief's standing, not about God; reconstructed from Sections 108, 125 and 343 it is an argument with four premises and a conclusion about the standing of European morality; its delay premise is what makes the audience's intact values a symptom rather than a counterexample; and it lacks the premise that would rule out a secular refounding. What is not established is a method. The reconstruction was done by hand on one famous passage with two others to help, and the rules by which those two were chosen, over the hundreds of other numbered sections, were not stated. <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">The next lesson</Ref> states them: the corpus in order, the aphorism as a unit, a five-step protocol, and the rule that published works ground a claim and notebooks only corroborate it.

Break this claim: "The madman's speech in Section 125 is an argument that God does not exist." Name the two sentences of the section that presuppose the audience's atheism, and state in one sentence what the speech argues instead.

<Solution>
**The two sentences.** First, the second sentence of the section: the madman provoked laughter because "many of those who did not believe in God" were standing there (*welche nicht an Gott glaubten*). The listeners' unbelief is narrated as a fact before the speech begins. Second, the charge itself: "We have killed him: you and I. We are all his murderers." The *ihr* includes the listeners among the agents of the belief's collapse; one does not argue people into a conclusion while charging them with having brought it about.

**Where the claim fails.** An argument's conclusion is what the audience is meant to accept at the end and did not hold at the start. Here the audience holds the alleged conclusion at the start, so the speech cannot be an argument for it. The claim mistakes a premise (P2) for the conclusion.

**What the speech argues instead.** That the collapse of belief the listeners share has consequences for everything that rested on it, above all morality, which they have not yet felt because those consequences arrive after a delay (P1, P3, P4, hence C). The requiem in the churches confirms the reading: the madman's response to the death is a rite for it, not a demonstration of it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The reconstruction's step from "the foundation is gone" (P2 with P3) to "the edifice must fall" (C) assumes that no other foundation is available. Write, in one sentence, a Kantian premise that would block the inference. Then find the one sentence of Twilight, "Expeditions of an Untimely Man" §5, in which Nietzsche denies that premise, and say whether the denial is argued or asserted.

<Solution>
**A blocking premise.** "The moral precepts that Christianity taught as God's commands can be derived, without any theological premise, from the requirement that a rational agent act only on maxims it could will as universal law; so their authority survives the loss of belief in their divine origin." With this premise added, P1 becomes false as a claim about the values' *authority* (they rested on God historically but do not depend on him logically), and C no longer follows.

**The sentence.** "When one gives up Christian belief one thereby deprives oneself of the right to Christian morality." The sentences that follow, "Christianity is a system … If one breaks out of it a fundamental idea, the belief in God, one thereby breaks the whole thing to pieces", restate the same denial.

**Argued or asserted.** Asserted, with a sub-argument that does not reach the premise. The sub-argument is that Christian morality "is a command: its origin is transcendental … it possesses truth only if God is truth". That shows Christian morality *as a divine command* cannot survive God; it does not show that the same precepts cannot be re-derived from a Kantian base, which is precisely what the blocking premise proposes. Nietzsche's reply to the Kantian derivation lies elsewhere, in the claim that the derivation only relabels an inherited valuation (GS 193, quoted by Kaufmann: "Kant wanted to prove, in a way that would dumbfound the common man, that the common man was right"), and that claim is itself a genealogical one whose reconstruction begins in <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Suppose P4 were false: the consequences of the belief's collapse arrived at once, with the collapse. List the sentences of Section 125 that become unnecessary, state whether the conclusion about morality still follows, and say what P4 is doing in the argument.

<Solution>
**Unnecessary sentences.** The whole last speech ("I come too early … lightning and thunder need time, the light of the stars needs time, deeds need time … this deed is still further from them than the furthest stars"), the smashed lantern that precedes it, and the lantern lit in daylight that opens the section, whose point is that the madman is searching for something in a light no one else needs yet. The requiem coda would also lose its point, since a requiem sung to people who already see the corpse is not a provocation.

**Does C still follow.** The first half does: P1, P2 and P3 give "European morality must fall" without any premise about timing. The second half, "and its heirs do not yet know it", does not, and worse, the setting now contradicts the conclusion. If consequences arrive at once, then a marketplace of educated unbelievers with intact values is a case where the belief collapsed and the values did not: a counterexample to the conjunction of P1 and P3. Without P4, the parable would be evidence against its own conclusion.

**What P4 does.** It reconciles the argument with its only visible evidence. The listeners' intact values are, under P4, exactly what the argument predicts during the interval, and their laughter is the symptom of not yet having heard. P4 turns the audience from a counterexample into a confirmation, and it is why the parable is staged in a marketplace of unbelievers rather than of believers.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Compare three renderings of the charge. The German: *Wir haben ihn getödtet, — ihr und ich!* Thomas Common (1910): "*We have killed him*,—you and I!" Walter Kaufmann (1974): "We have killed him—you and I." State whether any rendering changes who the accusation reaches, and say what the dash is doing in all three.

<Solution>
**Who is reached.** No rendering changes it. In all three the subject is "we", and the appositive after the dash unpacks the "we" into "you and I": the listeners (*ihr*, second person plural, the crowd of unbelievers) and the speaker. The accusation reaches everyone in the marketplace, the mockers included. A translation that read "*you* have killed him" or "*I* have killed him" would change the argument, since P2 as a deed with agents would then have only one class of agent; none of the three does that.

**What the dash does.** In the German the comma-plus-dash (*, —*) is Nietzsche's habitual pause mark, and here it separates the deed from the assignment of responsibility: first the fact (we have killed him), then, after a beat, the roster of who "we" is. Both translators keep the dash and drop the comma, which preserves the beat. The dash is what makes "we" include the listeners: without it, "we have killed him, you and I" reads as a single breath in which "you and I" could be taken as the speaker's rhetorical flourish; with it, "you and I" lands as a separate accusation.

**What does differ.** Common italicizes the verb clause ("*We have killed him*") and keeps the exclamation mark, which moves the stress onto the killing as an event; Kaufmann removes the exclamation and lets the dash carry the stress, which moves it onto the roster. Common also renders the second *tolle Mensch* in the section as "the insane man", a clinical word the German does not use (*toll* is "crazed", "raving"), where Kaufmann keeps "madman" throughout. Neither choice moves a premise: a rendering can shift emphasis without shifting who the argument reaches, and checking that requires the German.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Reconstruct Section 108, the Buddha's shadow, as an argument of its own with two premises and an implicit conclusion, and show that it is P4 of the Section 125 reconstruction stated seventeen sections earlier.

<Solution>
**The reconstruction.**

*P1 (the parallel).* After the Buddha's death, his shadow was still shown in a cave for centuries: a belief can be over and its exhibited image can persist long after.

*P2 (the case).* God is dead, and "given the way of men" his shadow will likewise be shown in caves for a very long time, perhaps millennia.

*C (implicit).* The consequences of the death of God will not be visible for a long time after the death itself; those who live in the interval will go on seeing the shadow and taking it for the thing.

The section then adds an imperative that is not part of the argument: "and we, we must still defeat his shadow too", which presupposes C and sets a task.

**Identity with P4.** P4 of the Section 125 reconstruction reads: the fall arrives after a delay, and the consequences of the collapse are not yet visible to those it has happened to. Section 108's C is that sentence with the delay given a scale (centuries for the Buddha, millennia for God) and a mechanism (the shadow exhibited by people who need it). Section 125 restates the same premise with an astronomical image and a present-tense speaker ("I come too early"); Section 343 gives it the present tense of a process already begun ("its first shadows"). Section 108 is therefore not a separate thesis but the argument's timing premise, placed at the head of the book in which the parable appears.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The argument as reconstructed does not say what killed the belief: P2 reports the collapse and names agents, not a cause. Section 357 of The Gay Science contains the sentence: *Man sieht, was eigentlich über den christlichen Gott gesiegt hat: die christliche Moralität selbst, der immer strenger genommene Begriff der Wahrhaftigkeit, die Beichtväter-Feinheit des christlichen Gewissens, übersetzt und sublimirt zum wissenschaftlichen Gewissen, zur intellektuellen Sauberkeit um jeden Preis.* Translate it, and write in one premise the cause it names. Then say what that premise does to P1.

<Solution>
**Translation.** "One sees what actually triumphed over the Christian God: Christian morality itself, the ever more strictly taken concept of truthfulness, the confessor's subtlety of the Christian conscience, translated and sublimated into scientific conscience, into intellectual cleanliness at any price."

**The premise.** *P2a (the cause).* Belief in the Christian God was destroyed by a virtue that Christianity itself bred, truthfulness, once that virtue was applied to the belief's own credentials: Christian conscience, trained to confess everything, turned into scientific conscience and forbade itself the belief in God as a lie.

**What it does to P1.** It makes the dependence run in both directions. P1 says the values rested on the belief; P2a says the belief was killed by one of the values. The edifice did not lose its foundation to an outside force; one of its own storeys, the will to truth, removed the foundation from under the rest. That is why the missing premise of the last section cannot be supplied by a secular refounding that keeps truthfulness as a value: any such refounding inherits the storey that did the killing.

**Where this goes.** The premise is the thesis of the Genealogy's last section, GM III:27, which states that Christian truthfulness, "translated into scientific conscience", is the mechanism by which "all great things bring about their own destruction", and which then turns the same mechanism on Nietzsche's own will to truth. <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">The will-to-truth lesson</Ref> reconstructs that argument; this exercise has written its first premise.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

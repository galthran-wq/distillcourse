The First Treatise claims that the word 'good' was coined twice — once by nobles naming themselves, once by their subjects naming the nobles 'evil' and themselves 'good' by subtraction — and that the second coinage is the morality Europe now has. This lesson reconstructs the treatise's opening sections, I:1–10, as three arguments: the displacement of the utilitarian account of who first said "good", the etymological evidence offered in its place and the grade that evidence earns, and the two modes of valuation with the one asymmetry between them that carries the whole charge against the second.

The course's argument stands here. <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">The genealogy lesson</Ref> established what kind of argument the treatises are: a history with documents, run under the rule of II:12 that a thing's present use is no evidence of its origin, whose critical force moves a value from given to made without moving a precept from true to false, and whose target, morality in the pejorative sense, is bounded so that the author's own values fall outside it. Its forward exercise left the reader holding the premise the English derivation cannot do without, that the recipients of useful acts were the first to say "good". What stands open is the history itself: the Preface's C1 said a documented account is more probable than the English hypothesis, and no document had yet been produced. This lesson produces the documents the First Treatise offers, grades them, and isolates the premise the rest of the treatise depends on, so that <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the next lesson</Ref> can supply the mechanism, the priestly caste and *ressentiment*, by which the second coinage displaced the first.

The route: I:1–3 and the three objections to the English derivation; I:2's first coinage and the pathos of distance; I:4–5's etymologies, reconstructed as an argument and graded row by row; I:10–11's asymmetry, stated as a minimal pair; BGE 260's typology with BGE 257 and *Human, All Too Human* §45 as its second passages; and the relativist misreading, refuted by the text's own ranking.

## The utilitarian genealogy: three objections, one of which the treatise builds on

The account the First Treatise displaces was previewed at the end of the genealogy lesson; I:2 states it in full, in quotation marks, as a "decree". Man "originally praised and called 'good' unegoistic actions from the standpoint of those to whom they were shown, that is, those to whom they were useful; later this origin of the praise was forgotten, and unegoistic actions, simply because as a matter of habit they had always been praised as good, were also *felt* as good, as though they were something good in themselves". Nietzsche's inventory of the derivation follows at once: "we have 'utility', 'forgetting', 'habit', and finally 'error', all as the basis of a valuation on which the higher man has hitherto prided himself as on a kind of privilege of man as such."

The derivation has four steps, and I:1–3 attack three of them. The objections are of different kinds, and only one of them is the ground on which the treatise then builds.

### (a) The wrong speaker: "the good themselves"

The first objection is about who spoke. "The judgment 'good' did *not* originate among those to whom goodness was shown. Much rather has it been the good themselves, that is, the noble, the powerful, the higher-placed and high-minded, who felt and posited themselves and their doing as good, that is, of the first rank, in contrast to everything low, low-minded, common and plebeian" (I:2, *die Vornehmen, Mächtigen, Höhergestellten und Hochgesinnten*). This is a rival historical claim, and it is the claim the rest of the treatise is a history of. Everything that follows in I:4–11 either documents it (the etymologies) or elaborates it (the two modes).

### (b) Forgetting is impossible where the use is daily

The second objection, I:3, is internal to the derivation: "quite apart from the fact that this hypothesis about the origin of the value judgment 'good' cannot be upheld historically, it suffers from an inherent psychological contradiction." Step 3 requires the usefulness of unegoistic actions to have been forgotten. But "in what conceivable way is this forgetting possible? Has the utility of such actions perhaps ceased at some point? The contrary is the case: this utility has rather been an everyday experience at all times", and what is experienced every day "must necessarily become impressed on consciousness with ever-increasing distinctness" rather than fade. The derivation needs a forgetting that its own first step rules out.

I:3 then concedes a point to a rival version of the utilitarian account. Herbert Spencer, the English philosopher whose *Data of Ethics* (1879) identified "good" with "useful", holds that in the judgments "good" and "bad" mankind "is simply summarizing and investing with a sanction its *unforgotten* and unforgettable experiences concerning the useful-purposive and the harmful-non-purposive". This version has no forgetting to explain, so objection (b) does not touch it; Nietzsche calls it "much more reasonable" and "coherent and psychologically tenable", and adds in the same sentence "it is not the truer for that". What still defeats it is objection (a): Spencer, like Rée, the former companion whose 1877 *Origin of the Moral Sensations* the Preface names as the derivation's source (<Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref> read §4 and §7 on him), has the recipients of usefulness coining the word.

### (c) The present use read back as the origin

The third objection is the rule from the Second Treatise. Step 4 of the derivation is the present sense of "good", the unegoistic act felt as good in itself, and the derivation reasons backwards from that sense to a first use among beneficiaries. <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">The genealogy lesson</Ref> reconstructed II:12's rule: a thing's present use is the sign of the last will to impose a meaning on it, not evidence about its coming-into-being. I:2 applies the rule without naming it: "it is because of this origin that the word 'good' is far from having any *necessary* connection with 'unegoistic' actions, as the superstition of those genealogists of morals has it. On the contrary, it is only with a *decline* of aristocratic value judgments that this whole antithesis 'egoistic' 'unegoistic' presses more and more on the human conscience", and "it is, to use my own language, the *herd instinct* that finally comes to a word (and to words) in it". The present association of "good" with "unegoistic" is on this account late, and it is the herd's imposition, which is exactly what II:12 says a present use is.

<Remark title="I:1–3 reconstructed">
**P1.** On the English derivation, the word "good" was first applied by the *beneficiaries* of unegoistic acts, its origin forgotten, and the act then felt as good in itself. Anchor: I:2, the decree.

**P2.** A usefulness that is re-experienced daily cannot be forgotten. Anchor: I:3, "in what conceivable way is this forgetting possible".

**P3.** A word's present use is not evidence of its origin. Anchor: II:12, imported.

**P4.** The word "good" was in fact first applied by the noble to themselves, "in contrast to everything low". Anchor: I:2; evidence promised for I:4–5.

**C.** The English derivation is unfounded (P1 fails at step 3 by P2, and its only evidence, the present sense, is inadmissible by P3), and a rival account with a different first speaker is available (P4).

**The missing premise.** P4 is asserted in I:2 and argued only in I:4–5; until those sections are graded, C's second clause is a claim with a promissory note attached. The reconstruction's validity does not depend on the etymologies; its soundness does.

**The strongest opposite reading.** Spencer's version survives P2, and I:3 says so. Against Spencer the argument has only P3 and P4, that is, only the rule and the rival history: the utilitarian account is not refuted from within, it is displaced by a competitor with a document.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Of the three objections, only (a) is a historical claim, and only (a) survives against Spencer; the First Treatise's case against the utilitarian genealogy reduces to the identity of the first speaker, which is why I:4–5's evidence about that speaker bears the weight of the whole opening.
</KeyFact>

## The first coinage: pathos of distance and the right to give names (I:2)

If the first speakers were the noble, the treatise owes an account of what they were doing when they spoke, and I:2 gives it in a single sentence about a feeling. From a history of the word "good" one would expect the first use to be a judgment on some act or person; I:2 makes it a self-description of a class, before any act was judged at all.

<Task>
Step through the three frames and write down, for each, what exists at that step: a feeling, then a word for oneself, then a word for the others. Then locate in I:2 the sentence that makes the feeling precede the word ("out of this pathos of distance they first took the right to create values"); the order of the frames is the order of that sentence.
</Task>

The feeling is the *pathos of distance* (*Pathos der Distanz*): "the chronic and dominant collective and fundamental feeling of a higher ruling kind in relation to a lower kind, to an 'under' (*ein Unten*)", which I:2 names as "the origin of the antithesis 'good' and 'bad'". Out of it "they first took the right to create values, to coin the names of values". The parenthesis that follows generalizes the point to language: "the masters' right of giving names goes so far that one should allow oneself to grasp the origin of language itself as an expression of the power of the rulers: they say 'this is that and that', they seal every thing and event with a sound and thereby take possession of it, as it were." The remark is offered as a permissible extension and not as a premise; the argument needs only that *this* word was coined by *these* speakers.

Two features of the coinage matter for what follows. First, utility is excluded by the temperature of the act: "what had they to do with utility! The standpoint of utility is as alien and inappropriate as possible in relation to such a hot outpouring of the highest rank-ordering, rank-distinguishing value judgments." Calculation of usefulness presupposes "a low degree of warmth", and the coinage is not a calculation. Second, the contrary term comes afterwards and comes from contempt, not from injury. I:10 supplies the wording: the noble mode of valuation "seeks its opposite only in order to say Yes to itself still more gratefully, still more jubilantly", and its negative concept, "'low', 'common', 'bad'", is "only a late-born pale contrast-image (*ein nachgebornes blasses Contrastbild*) in relation to its positive fundamental concept, saturated through and through with life and passion: 'we noble ones, we good ones, we beautiful ones, we happy ones!'".

BGE 260 states the same order as a rule about moral language in general: "moral expressions everywhere were first applied to people and then, only later and derivatively, to actions (which is why it is a tremendous mistake when historians of morality take their point of departure from questions such as 'why do acts of pity get praised?')." The question in the parenthesis is the English psychologists' question. On the treatise's account it cannot be the first question, because a word for persons of a rank exists before any act is praised with it.

<KeyFact>
The contrary term the first coinage produces is *schlecht*, "bad", and never *böse*, "evil": contempt at a distance names the common, and nobody in I:2 coins a word for an enemy. That boundary is what keeps the two coinages apart once both sides call themselves "good", and it is the one variable the minimal pair of I:10–11 turns on.
</KeyFact>

## The etymological argument: reconstructed and graded (I:4–5)

I:2 promised a document for the claim that the noble spoke first, and I:4 opens with what put Nietzsche "on the right track": "the question of what the designations of 'good' coined in the various languages actually mean etymologically". The sections that follow are the treatise's only evidence for P4 in the sense the Preface demanded, a record rather than a mechanism, and they are 19th-century philology, some of it wrong. The course's task is to reconstruct the argument they make and then grade each etymology separately, so that what survives can be told from what does not.

### The argument as stated

I:4 reports a finding: "they all lead back to the *same conceptual transformation*, that everywhere 'noble', 'aristocratic' in the social sense is the fundamental concept out of which 'good' in the sense of 'spiritually noble', 'aristocratic', of 'spiritually high-natured', 'spiritually privileged' developed with necessity: a development that always runs parallel with that other one which finally makes 'common', 'plebeian', 'low' pass over into the concept 'bad'." I:5 adds that the nobles name themselves "in perhaps the most frequent cases simply after their superiority in power" (the mighty, the masters, the commanders) or after its visible sign (the rich, the possessors), but also "after a *typical character trait*: and this is the case that concerns us here". The Greek nobility, whose "mouthpiece is the Megarian poet Theognis" (an aristocratic elegist of the 6th century BC whose verses complain of the rise of the common people), called themselves "the truthful".

<Remark title="I:4–5 reconstructed">
**P1.** In several unrelated languages the word for "good" descends from a word for noble rank or for a trait the nobility claimed, and the word for "bad" from a word for the common. Anchor: I:4 (*schlecht*/*schlicht*), I:5 (*bonus*, *esthlos*, *kakos*, *deilos*, *malus*, *fin*, *gut*).

**P2 (unstated).** The history of a value word tracks the history of the valuation: if the word for "good" was once the word for "noble", then "good" was once predicated of the noble by the noble.

**C.** The value words were rank words first; "good" was coined by the noble of themselves, which is P4 of the previous argument.

**The missing premise.** P2. A word can change its meaning without its new users inheriting the old users' standpoint; the inference from word-history to value-history needs the assumption that the transformation was carried by the same class, which I:4 asserts ("with necessity") and does not argue. The I:17 note, which asks for prize essays on "what pointers linguistics, especially etymological research, gives for the history of the development of moral concepts", treats the connection as a research programme rather than a result.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That the parallel word-histories are a coincidence of a general kind: high-status terms drift toward general approval and low-status terms toward disapproval in many languages for reasons of politeness and prestige, without any class having coined the value in its own favour. On this reading P1 can be fully true and C false.
</Remark>

<Task>
Before reading the grades, cover the right-hand column and mark for each row whether the claim is about the *form* of the word (that one descends from the other) or about its *meaning* (what the older word denoted). The one etymology I:4 calls "the most eloquent example" is the one that holds. *Schlecht*, "bad", "is identical with *schlicht*", "plain", "compare *schlechtweg*, *schlechterdings*", and "originally designated the plain, the common man, still without a suspecting side-glance, simply in contrast to the noble". Modern lexicography agrees on the identity: Old High German *sleht* meant "even, smooth, plain, simple", the pejorative sense grew out of "simple" in contrast to "costly, excellent", and the old sense survived in the doublet *schlicht*. The one correction is the date. I:4 puts the shift "around the time of the Thirty Years' War, thus late enough" (1618–48, the war that devastated the German lands); the dictionaries put its beginning in the 15th century, a century or two earlier. The correction weakens nothing in P1, and it removes the rhetorical point I:4 attached to the date, that the shift was recent enough to be visible.

The Latin case holds in form and fails in gloss. *Bonus* does descend from Old Latin *duonus* and earlier *duenos*, attested on the Duenos inscription (a 6th-century BC vase); I:5 says so and says it as a hypothesis, "provided that I am right in deriving *bonus* from an older *duonus*". The gloss, *bonus* as "the warrior", "the man of discord, of splitting-in-two (*duo*)", rests on a comparison with *bellum* from *duellum* and has no support: the root proposed for *duenos* means "to fit together" or "to give", not to divide. The Greek case is the reverse. That *deilos* means "cowardly, wretched" is uncontroversial, and I:5's observation that in *kakos* and *deilos* "cowardice is underlined" is a claim about usage that Greek texts bear out; but the etymology of *esthlos* as "one who *is*, who has reality" is one old proposal among several with no agreed successor, and *kakos* and *agathos* have no accepted etymology at all. I:5 itself calls *agathos* "ambiguous in several ways" and offers the coward-reading only as "a hint in which direction to look".

### The rows that fail, and the excursus they belong to

The two rejected rows are the two Nietzsche hedged. *Malus* "which I place side by side with *melas*", the dark-coloured, is a comparison that dictionaries once entertained and have abandoned: *malus* belongs with Oscan *mallom* and Old Irish *mell*, "destruction", not with "black". The German *gut* as "the godly" and as the name of the Goths is offered as a question, "should it not mean...?", and closed with "the grounds for this supposition do not belong here"; the modern derivation is from a root meaning "to fit, to suit", and the ethnonym is unrelated. The Gaelic *fin* ("in the name Fin-Gal") does mean "fair, white", but the name Nietzsche cites, *Fionnghall*, is the medieval Irish term for the Norse settlers, "fair foreigners", not a native word of the nobility, so the row holds as lexicography and fails as evidence for the claim it was cited for.

That claim is the one to flag and leave. I:5 reads *malus* and *fin* as marks of "the pre-Aryan inhabitants" set against "the blond, namely Aryan conquering race", and adds a parenthesis on skull shapes and on whether modern democracy is the subject race regaining the upper hand. This is speculation exceeding the philology on every row it touches, and it is the germ of the "blond beast" of I:11, whose weaponization and its refutation from I:11 and BGE 251 belong to <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the next lesson</Ref>. Here it is enough to record that the argument of I:4–5 needs none of it: strip every sentence about complexion from I:5 and P1 loses two contested rows and no green one.

### What the grade means for the argument

The argument's weight does not rest on any single etymology, and the text says so in its own hedges: "it seems to me", "provided that I am right", "perhaps a hint", "do not belong here". P1 claims a pattern across languages, and one secure instance (*schlecht*/*schlicht*) plus partial instances in Latin and Greek is enough to establish that rank words *became* value words in several places. What the grade cannot repair is P2. That the word for "good" was once the word for "noble" shows that the valuation "noble = good" existed and was worded; it does not show that the noble coined it, rather than, say, that their dependants flattered them with it, or that a prestige term drifted. I:4–5 therefore document P4 in a weak form, that a noble-favouring valuation is older than the altruistic one, and leave the strong form, that the noble were its authors, to the psychological argument of I:10, where the treatise turns from philology to the structure of the two valuations.

<Aside>
I:4's digression on Buckle. The section blames the lateness of its discovery on "the democratic prejudice within the modern world in regard to all questions of origin" and names Henry Thomas Buckle, the English historian whose *History of Civilization in England* (1857–61) sought statistical laws of progress, as "the notorious case" in which "the plebeianism of the modern spirit, which is of English origin", erupted "like a muddy volcano". The digression bears on nothing in the argument; it dates the polemic (Buckle was the standard target of German conservative historiography in the 1860s) and identifies "English" in the treatise as a term of abuse for a method, not a nationality, which is how I:1's "English psychologists" is also to be read, since Rée was German.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
The two kinds of evidence do not cover for each other. The etymologies carry the treatise's only *dated* claim, that a noble-favouring valuation is older than the altruistic one, which I:7's "two millennia" needs; I:10's psychology carries authorship and cannot carry a date, because a structural asymmetry between two valuations says nothing about which was worded first. Strike the philology and the priority claim loses its record; strike the psychology and the authorship claim loses its argument.
</KeyFact>

## The asymmetry: 'good' first against 'evil' first (I:10–11)

The two coinages of the opening statement are now on the table, and I:10 states the difference between them as a difference of *order*. The section is best known for its first sentence, which names the mechanism the next lesson reconstructs: "the slave revolt in morality begins when *ressentiment* itself becomes creative and gives birth to values: the *ressentiment* of beings to whom the real reaction, that of the deed, is denied, and who compensate themselves with an imaginary revenge." This lesson takes only what the sentence's continuation says about the structure of the valuation, because that structure is the premise everything else in the treatise, including the mechanism, is built to explain.

### Two directions of the valuing glance

"While every noble morality grows out of a triumphant Yes-saying to itself, slave morality from the outset says No to an 'outside', to an 'other', to a 'not-self': and *this* No is its creative deed." The direction is then generalized: "this reversal of the value-positing glance, this *necessary* direction outward instead of back to oneself, belongs precisely to *ressentiment*: in order to arise, slave morality always needs first an opposing and external world; it needs, physiologically speaking, external stimuli in order to act at all, its action is fundamentally a reaction." And the noble case is stated as the exact contrary: "the noble mode of valuation acts and grows spontaneously".

  src="./figures/minimal-pair.svg"
  alt="Two panels side by side: the noble valuation in amber, with a filled first cell reading we the good, a Yes, and a dashed derived cell below it reading they the bad, contempt; the slave valuation in blue, with a filled first cell reading they the evil, a No, and a dashed derived cell reading we the good, by negation; a footer notes the same English word good on both sides and I:11's sentence that it is not the same concept"

I:10's last sentence gives the slave side of the pair its two cells in order. The man of *ressentiment* "has conceived 'the evil enemy', '*the evil one*', and indeed as the fundamental concept, from which he then thinks up, as an after-image and counterpart, a 'good one', himself!" I:11 opens by giving the noble side its two cells in the contrary order: "exactly the reverse, then, of the case of the noble man, who conceives the fundamental concept 'good' in advance and spontaneously, namely out of himself, and only from there creates for himself an idea of 'bad'!" The two contrary terms are then set against each other: "this 'bad' of noble origin and that 'evil' out of the cauldron of unsatisfied hatred, the first an after-creation, a side-issue, a complementary colour (*Complementärfarbe*), the second by contrast the original, the beginning, the real deed in the conception of a slave morality". Leiter's commentary on the First Essay (*Nietzsche on Morality*, ch. 6) reads the two sections as exactly this contrast, self-affirmation first with a derivative contempt against negation first with a derivative self-praise, and the course adopts his framing as the *minimal pair*: two valuations that differ in one variable, which term is coined first and of whom.

### What is charged: the order, not the content

The pair makes precise what the treatise is and is not criticizing. It is not criticizing the content of slave morality's "good": humility, patience, pity, industriousness are named in BGE 260 without any claim that they are vices. The charge is that a valuation which begins with a No to something outside itself depends for its existence on that something: it "needs, in order to arise, an opposing and external world", and it cannot say what it honours without first having something to hate. A noble valuation can be stated with the others absent; a slave valuation cannot.

<Remark title="the asymmetry, reconstructed">
**P1.** Noble valuation coins "good" of itself first, spontaneously, and derives "bad" (*schlecht*) of the others as a contrast term. Anchor: I:2, I:10 ("a late-born pale contrast-image"), I:11.

**P2.** Slave valuation coins "evil" (*böse*) of the others first, as its fundamental concept, and derives "good" of itself by negation. Anchor: I:10 ("as the fundamental concept, from which he then thinks up ... a 'good one', himself"), I:11 ("the original, the beginning").

**P3.** A valuation that begins with a No to an external object requires that object in order to exist at all. Anchor: I:10, "needs ... first an opposing and external world"; "its action is fundamentally a reaction".

**C.** Slave morality is reactive and dependent: its "good" is defined by what it is not, and it has no content of its own that survives the removal of its enemy. Noble morality is active and self-standing.

**The missing premise.** That reactive origin is a defect. C is a description; the treatise treats it as a charge, and the step from "reactive" to "worse" needs a standard by which self-standing valuations rank above dependent ones. I:10 gestures at it ("physiologically speaking"), the I:17 note names it (a physiological ranking of value-tables), and the present lesson's last section states what the text says about it; the standard's own defence is <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref>'s question.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That every valuation is contrastive, so that P1's "spontaneous" coinage is a myth: the noble's "good" is intelligible only against a "bad" it already presupposes, and the asymmetry collapses into two symmetrical contrasts. Against this, I:10's evidence is the *temperature* of the two contrary terms, contempt being "too careless, too light, too much looking-away" to make a caricature of its object, while hatred draws "the evil enemy" in detail; the reading is answered by a claim about affects, and the answer is only as good as the psychology that backs it.
</Remark>

### The same word, not the same concept

I:11 blocks the objection that "good" means one thing and that the two moralities merely disagree about who has it. "How differently the two words 'bad' and 'evil' stand there, seemingly set against the same concept 'good'! But it is *not* the same concept 'good'." Asked "who is actually 'evil' in the sense of the morality of *ressentiment*", the section answers "in all strictness: precisely the 'good one' of the other morality, precisely the noble, the powerful, the ruling one, only recoloured, only reinterpreted, only reseen through the poison-eye of *ressentiment*." The word "good" is the one constant across the pair and the one thing that changes meaning; the two contrary terms, which differ in English as well as in German, are what keep the two concepts apart on the page. A translation that renders *schlecht* and *böse* by one English word erases the pair, and with it the treatise's title; the check of how the course's translations handle the two words across I:4, I:10 and I:11 is the set piece of <Ref lesson="key-terms-in-translation">the translation chapter</Ref>, and one exercise below runs it for these three sections.

<KeyFact>
The whole charge of the First Treatise against slave morality is carried by one variable of the minimal pair, whether "good" or the contrary term is coined first: the content of what is called good is not the target, the reactive order is, and every later section either explains how that order came about or asks what is wrong with it.
</KeyFact>

## The typology in one aphorism: BGE 260, with BGE 257 and HA 45 as second passages

The protocol's fourth step, in <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>'s wording, is to find the same claim in another book or another year and say whether it supplies the missing premise, supplies a reason for it, or leaves the gap; for the two modes the second passage is not in the *Genealogy*. BGE 260, published a year before it in 1886, states the typology in one aphorism; BGE 257 states the condition the noble mode requires; and *Human, All Too Human* I §45, from 1878, states the same two codes nine years earlier under the title "Twofold prehistory of good and evil". The three passages corroborate the typology and, in one respect, contradict the *Genealogy*.

### BGE 260: two types, and both in one soul

The aphorism reports a finding from "wandering through the many subtle and crude moralities that have been dominant or that still dominate over the face of the earth": "certain traits regularly recurring together", from which "two basic types became apparent to me and a fundamental distinction leapt out. There is a *master morality* and a *slave morality*". The next clause is the one the misreadings drop: "I will immediately add that in all higher and more mixed cultures, attempts to negotiate between these moralities also appear, although more frequently the two are confused and there are mutual misunderstandings. In fact, you sometimes find them sharply juxtaposed, inside the same person even, within a single soul." The types are types of valuation, not of person, and the same soul can hold both.

The master side of BGE 260 restates I:2 and I:10 in the vocabulary of value-creation. "The noble type of person feels that he determines value, he does not need anyone's approval, he judges that 'what is harmful to me is harmful in itself', he knows that he is the one who gives honor to things in the first place, he creates values." Its "good" and "bad" are "noble" and "despicable", and the aphorism marks in parenthesis that "the contrast between 'good' and 'evil' has a different lineage". The despised are "people who were cowardly, apprehensive, and petty, people who thought narrowly in terms of utility", "grovelers", "begging flatterers and, above all, liars: it is a basic belief of aristocrats that base peoples are liars. 'We who are truthful', that is what the nobility of ancient Greece called themselves", which is I:5's Theognis without the etymology.

The one piece of evidence for master morality in Nietzsche's own hand is a quotation. "'Wotan has put a hard heart in my breast', reads a line from an old Scandinavian saga: this rightly comes from the soul of a proud Viking. This sort of a man is even proud of not being made for pity: which is why the hero of the saga adds, by way of warning, 'If your heart is not hard when you are young, it will never be hard.'" The passage is offered as a sample of noble self-description rather than as an argument, and it is the aphorism's only document, which puts BGE 260 on the same evidential footing as I:4–5: a typology illustrated, not established.

The slave side is introduced by a conditional. "What if people who were violated, oppressed, suffering, unfree, exhausted, and unsure of themselves were to moralize: what type of moral valuations would they have? A pessimistic suspicion of the whole condition of humanity would probably find expression." Then the order of the pair, in the aphorism's own terms: "The slave's gaze resents the virtues of the powerful", and only "conversely" are the "qualities that serve to alleviate existence for suffering people ... pulled out and flooded with light: pity, the obliging, helpful hand, the warm heart, patience, industriousness, humility, and friendliness". Two sentences later comes the clause that ties the aphorism back to I:1–3: "Slave morality is essentially a morality of utility. Here we have the point of origin for that famous opposition between 'good' and 'evil'." The English psychologists' derivation, on this reading, was an accurate description of the *second* coinage, mistaken for a description of the first; their "utility" is the slave's utility, and the Preface's §4 complaint that Rée took "the moral manner of valuation as such" to be the altruistic one is the same point from the target's side.

### BGE 257: the political premise, flagged

The pathos of distance returns in BGE 257 as a precondition rather than an origin, and with a premise attached. "Every enhancement so far in the type 'man' has been the work of an aristocratic society, and that is how it will be, again and again, since this sort of society believes in a long ladder of rank order and value distinctions between men, and in some sense needs slavery." The argument runs: without "the pathos of distance as it grows out of the ingrained differences between stations", "that other, more mysterious pathos could not have grown at all, that demand for new expansions of distance within the soul itself", that is, "the enhancement of the type 'man'". The sociological pathos is claimed as the causal precondition of the psychological one, and the aphorism adds that "the noble caste always started out as the barbarian caste".

The premise this carries is political: that an order of rank between persons is necessary for an order of rank within a soul. It is not needed by anything in the First Treatise, whose asymmetry is stated between two modes of valuation and holds whatever the political arrangement that produced them. It is needed by the positive doctrine, where the higher type is defined by the pathos of distance, and <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref> is where the premise is examined and where its strongest reading, Hurka's, is stated. This lesson records it and does not use it.

### HA 45: the same typology, one verdict reversed

The second-passage rule has so far been applied to negative results: a fragment that corroborates a concession, a section that dates a thought. Here it is applied to a positive one, and it returns a difference. *Human, All Too Human* I §45 (1878) already has both codes. "The notion of good and bad has a twofold historical origin: namely, first, in the spirit of ruling races and castes. Whoever has power to requite good with good and evil with evil and actually brings requital ... acquires the name of being good; whoever is powerless and cannot requite is called bad." "Good and bad is, for a considerable period, tantamount to noble and servile, master and slave." Then, "second, in the spirit of the subjugated, the powerless. Here every other man is, to the individual, hostile, inconsiderate, greedy, inhuman, avaricious, be he noble or servile; bad is the characteristic term for man, for every living being, indeed, that is recognized at all, even for a god."

  src="./figures/ha45-vs-gm.svg"

  caption="The same typology nine years apart: 1887 adds a mechanism and reverses the verdict on which code Europe inherited."

Three things differ. HA 45 defines the noble "good" by the power to requite, gratitude and revenge included, where I:2 defines it by the pathos of distance; the 1878 criterion is relational and the 1887 one is a feeling of rank. HA 45 has no mechanism for the second code and no asymmetry: the powerless simply find everyone bad, and there is no first coinage of "evil" from which "good" is derived. And HA 45 ends with a verdict the *Genealogy* reverses: "Our existing morality has developed upon the foundation laid by ruling races and castes." I:7 says the opposite, that the slave revolt "has behind it a history of two millennia, and which today has only moved out of our sight because it has achieved victory". The 1878 distinction is, in Kaufmann's reading, "clearly a sociological one, not racial or biological", which is also how the 1887 text has to be read if I:5's excursus is set aside.

The protocol's verdict on the second passage is therefore mixed. HA 45 corroborates the *typology*: two codes, "good" as the ruling class's name for itself, a separate valuation among the powerless. It does not corroborate the *asymmetry*, which is new in 1887, and it contradicts the *descent* of present morality. A reconstruction that cites HA 45 for the two modes is on firm ground; one that cites it for the claim that Europe's morality is the slaves' is citing a passage that says the reverse, and must argue that Nietzsche changed his mind, which the I:7 and BGE 195 texts of 1886–87 show he did.

<KeyFact>
The two modes of valuation are in the corpus from 1878 and the asymmetry between them is not: the minimal pair, the priestly caste and *ressentiment* are 1886–87 additions, and the same additions reverse the 1878 verdict on which code Europe's morality descends from.
</KeyFact>

## The relativist misreading refuted: the text ranks, and says by what

Nietzsche ranks the two modes, and the text says by what standard; this is the correct claim, and the misreading it displaces is the one that a reader of BGE 260's "both in one soul" and of the *Genealogy*'s refusal to refute morality by its origin is most likely to form. The misreading, stated once: Nietzsche describes two moralities as an anthropologist describes two kinships, takes no side, and "beyond good and evil" means the suspension of all valuation. The course rejects it, on three sentences.

<Figure
  src="./figures/ranking-evidence.svg"
  alt="A struck-through box at the top reading the claim that Nietzsche only describes two moralities and takes no side; below it three amber boxes, each with a quotation and a green line naming what it ranks by: GM I:10 on spontaneous action against reaction, ranking by origin; the GM I:17 note on a physiological elucidation and the good of the majority against the minority, ranking by value for a stronger type; BGE 260's suspicion, utility and the words good and stupid coming closer, ranking by its own vocabulary"
  caption="Three passages the relativist reading cannot accommodate, and the standard each ranks by."
/>

The first sentence is I:10's own vocabulary: "acts and grows spontaneously" against "its action is fundamentally a reaction", "triumphant Yes-saying" against a No that "needs external stimuli in order to act at all". These are not the terms of a neutral description; "spontaneous" and "reactive" are ranked by the sentence that pairs them, and the minimal pair of the previous section is a ranked pair. The second is the note at the end of I:17, which asks that "all tables of values, all the 'thou shalts' known to history or ethnology, need primarily a physiological elucidation and interpretation", and states the standard's shape: "the question 'what is this or that table of values and morality *worth*?' will be asked from the most varied standpoints; in particular the question 'valuable *for what*?' can never be analysed too finely." The example given is the treatise's own ranking: what has value "for the greatest possible durability of a race" would "by no means have the same value if it were a question of forming a stronger type", and "the good of the majority and the good of the minority are opposed standpoints: to hold the former as in itself the higher we leave to the naïveté of English biologists". The third is BGE 260's description of slave morality, which uses "resents", "suspicion", "distrustful", "essentially a morality of utility" and closes with the observation that where it rules "language shows a tendency for the words 'good' and 'stupid' to come closer together": the description ranks in every clause.

The reason the misreading fails is that it confuses two things the course has kept apart since <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>. The *Genealogy* does not refute slave morality by its origin, and GS 345 says a morality "could even have grown out of an error" without its value being touched; that is the genetic-fallacy point, and it is a point about what a history can prove. It is not a point about whether the historian has a standard. The treatise's standard is stated in the I:17 note and in the Preface's §3, whether a value-table "hindered or furthered human flourishing", is a sign "of distress, impoverishment, degeneration of life" or "of its fullness, force, will"; and it is applied throughout in the vocabulary of health, activity and self-affirmation. The replacement model is: two modes of valuation, ranked by a criterion external to both, that criterion being a fact about the life that holds the values. Whether the criterion can bear the weight, and whether "life" or "power" can be a standard without begging the question, is the question <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref> reconstructs; here the point is only that the criterion is stated and used.

The discriminating example is in the text's own footnote-sentence. I:17 closes: "what I mean by that dangerous motto inscribed on the body of my last book: *Beyond Good and Evil*. At any rate that is not the same as 'Beyond Good and Bad'." A reader who takes the motto as the suspension of all valuation cannot parse the sentence; a reader who takes it as the rejection of one pair, *gut*/*böse*, in favour of a standard that keeps the other, *gut*/*schlecht*, in some form, can. The relativist reading has no way to distinguish the two pairs; the sentence exists to distinguish them.

<Aside>
The standard, stated analytically. Hurka's essay "Nietzsche: Perfectionist" (in Leiter and Sinhababu, *Nietzsche and Morality*, 2007) reads the I:17 note's "the good of the majority and the good of the minority are opposed standpoints", with BGE 258 and a notebook fragment, as a formal aggregative principle: society should maximize the excellence of its best members, with the rest weighted at zero, "the opposite of John Rawls's famous maximin principle", which he calls "maximax". This is the antiegalitarianism of I:17 turned into a decision rule, and it is the strongest analytic statement of what the ranking standard would have to be if it were a principle of distribution. The course states it in <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref> and tests it there; it is recorded here as the reading that takes the note most literally.
</Aside>

What is established: the utilitarian derivation of "good" fails at its third step and offers as evidence only the present sense that II:12 rules inadmissible, and its one substantive rival is a claim about the first speaker; I:2's first coinage is a self-description out of the pathos of distance, with "bad" as a contrast term coined afterwards from contempt; I:4–5's etymologies establish that rank words became value words in several languages, with *schlecht* from *schlicht* as the secure case, and do not establish who carried the transformation, so the authorship claim rests on the psychology of I:10; that psychology yields the minimal pair, "good" of oneself first against "evil" of the other first, and the charge the treatise carries is the reactive order and not the content; BGE 260 states the typology with both modes possible in one soul, BGE 257 attaches to the pathos of distance a political premise the treatise does not need, and HA 45 shows the typology in 1878 without the asymmetry and with the verdict on descent reversed; and the text ranks the two modes by a stated standard, so that "beyond good and evil" is the rejection of one pair and not of valuation.

What is not yet established is how the second coinage displaced the first. The asymmetry says that slave valuation needs an enemy in order to exist; it does not say why a class should have valued that way, or how its valuation came to be Europe's. I:10's first sentence names the condition, *ressentiment*, and I:6–7 name the class through which it worked, the priestly caste; <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs that mechanism from I:6–16 and BGE 195, and states what *ressentiment* alone cannot explain.

**What falls.** P1 of the I:4–5 reconstruction ("in several languages the word for 'good' descends from a word for noble rank") and with it C ("the value words were rank words first"). **The doorstep.** I:10 names the condition the premise describes: *ressentiment*, "the *ressentiment* of beings to whom the real reaction ... is denied". The premise the reader has just written is the definition of *ressentiment* as a valuation, and the agent BGE 195 names, together with I:6–7's priestly caste, is the mechanism by which it became Europe's morality. <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs both, and asks what *ressentiment* explains and what it does not: it explains the inversion, and it does not by itself explain why the inversion won.

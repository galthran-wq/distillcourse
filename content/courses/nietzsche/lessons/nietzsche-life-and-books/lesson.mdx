Between 1872 and 1888 Nietzsche wrote about fifteen books, and almost none of them is a treatise: they are numbered sections, a line to a few pages each, and the notebooks his sister turned into his 'main work' were notes he had abandoned.

That makes a practical problem for anyone who wants to know what he thought. Suppose the question is what he held about the world as a whole. A famous answer sits at the very end of a thick volume called *The Will to Power*: "This world is the will to power, and nothing besides!", using his name for a drive to grow and overcome resistance that <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">will to power as psychology</Ref> explains. The volume has his name on the spine. But he never published it, never finished it, and dropped the plan for it in 1888; the sentence is a notebook entry from the summer of 1885, and its place as the book's last word was chosen by editors after he could no longer object. In a book he did publish a year later, the same thought appears as a hypothesis, hedged with "supposing" and "granted".

The obvious method, collecting every sentence printed under his name and treating each as a doctrine, fails three ways at once. His views changed over sixteen years, so two sentences can contradict each other because they were written a decade apart. Some of the most quoted sentences come from notes he never released, or released in a different form. And a numbered section rarely states its argument: it gives a claim, an image or a joke, and leaves the reader to work out what supports it. Before any passage can be used, three things have to be settled about it: when it was written, what kind of text it is, and what argument it is making.

## Professor at 24, wanderer at 34, silent at 44

The first of the three, the date, needs a map of the life, because his views changed in phases. Readers divide his working life into three *periods*, numbered 1 to 3 in what follows: **1869–1876**, when as a young professor he wrote as Wagner's ally; **1878–1882**, when as a self-declared "free spirit" he wrote cool, experimental, psychological sections on morality and religion; and **1883–1888**, from *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* to the long, argued works of his last working year. The years 1876–77 are the turn between the first two.

Friedrich Nietzsche was born in 1844, the son of a Lutheran pastor who died when the boy was four, and grew up with his mother and his sister Elisabeth in Naumburg, a cathedral town near Leipzig. He studied classical philology, the scholarly study of ancient Greek and Latin texts, at Leipzig.

Two encounters of his student years decided what his first period would be about. In 1865 he picked up in a Leipzig second-hand bookshop the main work of Arthur Schopenhauer, the philosopher who taught that existence is suffering and that the wise man stops wanting. In 1868 he met Richard Wagner, the composer, whose music he already revered. Schopenhauer gave him the problem of his first book and Wagner the living artist in whom its last sections hoped to see Greek tragedy reborn (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy">Schopenhauer's pessimism and The Birth of Tragedy</Ref>).

### Period 1, Wagner's ally (1869–1876)

In 1869 the University of Basel, in Switzerland, offered him a chair in classical philology. He was twenty-four and had not finished his doctorate; Leipzig awarded it without examination. His teacher's letter of recommendation shows why Basel took the risk:

<Quote source="F. Ritschl, letter to Basel, 1869; trans. W. Kaufmann, The Portable Nietzsche, pp. 7–8">
However many young talents I have seen develop under my eyes for thirty-nine years now, never yet have I known a young man … who was so mature as early and as young as this Nietzsche. … He is now twenty-four years old: strong, vigorous, healthy, courageous physically and morally. … He will simply be able to do anything he wants to do.
</Quote>

"Healthy" did not last. In 1870, during the war between the German states led by Prussia and France, he volunteered as a medical orderly, nursed wounded men with dysentery and diphtheria in a freight car for three days, caught both diseases and came back to Basel with his health broken. From then on migraines, vomiting and failing eyesight were the background of everything he wrote. For the next seven years he taught a heavy load, spent his free days with Wagner at Tribschen, his house on the lake near Lucerne, and wrote as Wagner's ally: *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872) and four *Untimely Meditations* (1873–76).

### Period 2, the free spirit (1878–1882)

In 1876 Wagner opened his festival theatre at Bayreuth, a small town in northern Bavaria, and Nietzsche, who attended, found a cult around a man he had thought of as a lonely genius. He took a year's leave, wintered at Sorrento on the Bay of Naples, and began a different kind of book. *Human, All Too Human* (1878) carries the subtitle "A Book for Free Spirits", and in his own later gloss a free spirit is "a spirit that has become free, that has again taken possession of itself". What he had got free of, by his account, was "idealism": the habit of seeing ideal things, the genius, the saint, the hero, where there is only what is "human, all too human", which is how he glossed the title. He applied the word to his own life as well: serving Wagner as a genius and doing philology as a duty were, in his autobiography *Ecce Homo* (1888), what idealism had led him into, at the cost of asking what his own nature needed. In 1879, at thirty-four, he resigned his chair because of illness and received a small pension.

The decade that followed is the one people picture. He had no job, no house and no family of his own. He moved with the seasons between boarding-house rooms in Genoa and Nice in winter and the Alpine village of Sils Maria, in the Upper Engadine valley of south-east Switzerland, in summer. The books of these years, *Daybreak* (1881) and the first edition of *The Gay Science* (1882), are collections of numbered sections on morality, religion, art and knowledge, written in a cool, experimental, psychological register.

### Period 3, from Zarathustra to Turin (1883–1888)

In the winter of 1882–83, at Rapallo on the coast east of Genoa, he wrote in about ten days the first part of *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, a narrative, not a treatise, in which a prophet named Zarathustra, after the ancient Persian prophet Zoroaster, comes down from the mountains and preaches in parables to the people he meets; the four parts appeared from 1883 to 1885, the fourth in a private printing of forty copies, seven of which he gave away. Then came the mature books, where the arguments are worked out at length: *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886), *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887), and a new fifth book for *The Gay Science* (1887). In 1888 he wrote five more: *The Case of Wagner*, *Twilight of the Idols*, *The Antichrist*, *Ecce Homo* and *Nietzsche contra Wagner*.

In the first days of January 1889, in Turin, the capital of Piedmont in north-west Italy, he broke down. He sent short letters to friends signed "Dionysus" or "The Crucified". The one to Jacob Burckhardt, his former colleague at Basel, begins:

<Quote source="Letter to J. Burckhardt, 6 January 1889; trans. W. Kaufmann, The Portable Nietzsche, p. 685">
In the end I would much rather be a Basel professor than God; but I have not dared push my private egoism so far as to desist for its sake from the creation of the world.
</Quote>

Burckhardt took the letter to Franz Overbeck, Nietzsche's closest friend from Basel, who went to Turin and brought him back. He lived eleven more years, first in his mother's care in Naumburg and, after her death in 1897, in his sister's in Weimar, and wrote nothing. He died there on 25 August 1900. The cause of the collapse was long given as the brain disease of late syphilis; that was never proven, and other diagnoses have been proposed.

### Three periods, drawn by himself

The division into three periods is a convention of commentators, and a convention imposed from outside could distort the work it sorts. This one has a better pedigree: Nietzsche drew it. In 1886 and 1887 he wrote new prefaces for BT, HH, D and GS, each reading the earlier book from his later position; in BT's new preface he disowns its Schopenhauerian vocabulary and its hopes for Wagner while keeping its question. In EH he reviews every book in order as a step on one road. And the Genealogy's preface dates its own ideas back to the Sorrento winter of 1876–77 and the first free-spirit book. The periods are therefore his own map of his changes of mind, and they work as a map, not as a wall: some ideas run through all three.

What the map is for shows in a single word. In BT, written in period 1, "Dionysian", after Dionysus, the Greek god of wine and ecstasy, names an intoxication in which the individual dissolves into the whole; in the books of 1888 the same word names a strong, affirmative attitude to life that keeps its passions and masters them. Quoting the word without the date mixes two different claims.

<KeyFact>
A term he used in more than one period means what the published books *of that period* make it mean. Check it there, never against a note, which can be a draft he later dropped.
</KeyFact>

### Illness dates the turns; it does not settle the arguments

The biography raises a temptation: to explain the philosophy away by the life. A sick, lonely man, the thought runs, would of course praise strength and despise pity. The objection to this is plain. Where an argument came from does not show whether it holds, any more than a theorem proved in a fever is false for that reason. Biography tells you *when* he changed his mind and *under what conditions*; whether the new view is right has to be judged on its arguments.

Nietzsche goes further and claims the illness as an instrument. In EH, looking back on 1879, the year he called his lowest, he writes:

<Quote source="EH I 1, trans. W. Kaufmann">
In the midst of the torments that go with an uninterrupted three-day migraine, accompanied by laborious vomiting of phlegm, I possessed a dialectician's clarity par excellence. … Looking from the perspective of the sick toward healthier concepts and values and, conversely, looking again from the fullness and self-assurance of a rich life down into the secret work of the instinct of decadence — in this I have had the longest training. … Now I know how, have the know-how, to reverse perspectives.
</Quote>

His claim is that alternating between sickness and recovery trained him to see how values look from each side, and that this double view is what a critique of values needs. Whether the claim holds is a question about EH as a whole (<Ref lesson="ecce-homo">Ecce Homo</Ref>); here it serves as his own statement that the illness is part of the method, not a cause standing behind it.

## Why numbered sections: a system cannot question its own premises

The second thing to settle about a passage is what kind of text it is, and the first kind to understand is the one he used most. Philosophers before him had mostly written treatises: an argument laid out from premises to conclusions, chapter by chapter, as in Spinoza's *Ethics* (1677), built like a geometry book from definitions and axioms. Nietzsche from 1878 on wrote numbered sections, and the reader needs to know whether that was a choice with a reason or a limitation to work around.

### The obvious explanation, and what it misses

The obvious explanation is the illness. A man who could read for only short stretches, who composed while walking and wrote in a pocket notebook, would produce short pieces. That is part of the truth: he did work that way, and said so. But it does not explain why the long, connected books of 1886 and 1887, written under the same illness, are still built from numbered sections.

He gives a philosophical reason in TI, among the one-line maxims of the opening chapter:

<Quote source="TI Maxims 26, trans. W. Kaufmann">
I mistrust all systematizers and I avoid them. The will to a system is a lack of integrity.
</Quote>

The maxim is itself an example of the form: it states a verdict and leaves the reason out. The reason can be rebuilt from other sections, in two steps.

**A system's premises are placed beyond question.** A system starts from first premises and derives everything else from them. Whatever it proves, it cannot prove its own starting points: it lays them down as foundations, and its method consists in building on them, not in asking whether they hold. A treatise built like the *Ethics* prints its axioms on its first page, and it prints them there so that nothing after them has to argue for them.

**The author's values sit inside those premises.** In BGE 6 he says what the foundations of a philosophy usually contain. Every great philosophy so far has been "the confession of its originator, and a species of involuntary and unconscious auto-biography", and "the moral (or immoral) purpose in every philosophy has constituted the true vital germ out of which the entire plant has always grown". The section ends by saying what the confession reveals: the philosopher's morality "furnishes a decided and decisive testimony as to who he is, that is to say, in what order the deepest impulses of his nature stand to each other". The German word for these impulses, *Triebe*, is usually translated *drives*.

Put the two steps together. If a system's foundations carry its author's values, then those values sit in the one place the system's method exempts from inspection. To build a system is to agree not to look there. That is the "lack of integrity".

### What a numbered section asks of its reader

The obvious objection runs the other way. A system at least states its premises; a numbered section states a claim and leaves the premise that supports it unwritten. Hiding a premise looks less honest than stating one.

The answer is that the two forms do different things with the premise, and what matters is not whether it is written down but whether it is open to question. A system writes its premise down as a foundation, the point where argument stops. A numbered section claims no foundation at all. Its premise has to be found by the reader, and the only way to find it is to ask what would have to be true for the claim to hold; whoever has asked that has already put the premise up for questioning. This does not make the section's claim true. It changes what the reader does with the premise, from building on it to testing it.

In Nietzsche's usage an *aphorism* is any numbered section, from a one-line maxim to an argument of several pages; the word does not mean a fragment, because the sections are arranged into books with chapters, and BGE and GM are far more continuous than they look. He describes the reading the form demands in the preface to GM, signed at Sils Maria in July 1887:

<Quote source="GM Preface 8, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
An aphorism, properly stamped and molded, has not been "deciphered" when it has simply been read; rather, one has then to begin its exegesis, for which is required an art of exegesis. I have offered in the third essay of the present book an example of what I regard as "exegesis" in such a case — an aphorism is prefixed to this essay, the essay itself is a commentary on it. … one has almost to be a cow and in any case not a "modern man": rumination.
</Quote>

Two things in this are instructions. The section is to be *deciphered*, so its meaning is not on its surface. And he supplies a worked example of the deciphering: the third essay of GM, roughly seventy pages, unpacks one aphorism. Which one is disputed: the line from *Zarathustra* printed at its head, or the essay's own first section. On either reading, a short section, on his own account, can carry the content of an essay.

Speed of writing is not shallowness either. In GS 381, from the 1887 fifth book, he says he approaches deep problems "like cold baths: quickly into them and quickly out again", and rejects the idea that a matter "remains ununderstood and unfathomed merely because it has been touched only in flight". The quick touch is in the writing; the slow work is assigned to the reader.

## Reading a section as an argument: BGE 68

The third thing to settle about a passage is the argument it makes, and the best place to learn how to extract one is a section so short that nothing can hide. BGE 68 has three sentences:

<Quote source="BGE 68, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
<Original lang="de">
"Das habe ich gethan" sagt mein Gedächtniss. Das kann ich nicht gethan haben, sagt mein Stolz und bleibt unerbittlich. Endlich — giebt das Gedächtniss nach.
</Original>

"I did that," says my memory. "I cannot have done that," says my pride, and remains inexorable. Eventually — the memory yields.
</Quote>

Read once, it is a witticism about vanity: people misremember the things they are ashamed of. That reading is true and small, and it is the weak one. The strong reading asks what general claim the scene is an instance of and what has to be true for the scene to go the way it does.

<Task>
Before frame 4, write down in one sentence what would have to be in the mind for the memory to win instead. Then check your sentence against frame 4.
</Task>

<Example title="BGE 68 as claim, steps and unstated premise">
**Step 1: two reports disagree.** Two sources of belief about the same past act give opposite answers. Memory says I did it; pride says I cannot have.

**Step 2: they are not doing the same job.** Memory reports what happened. Pride has a different concern: what I can bear to have been. Only the first is aimed at the facts. "Remains inexorable" marks the second as an interest, not a judgment: it does not weigh anything, it refuses.

**Step 3: the interested one wins.** "Eventually — the memory yields." The word *eventually* and the dash before the verdict say that this is not a decision made on evidence at a moment but a pressure kept up over time, under which the report gives way.

**The claim.** Generalised, the scene says: what I believe about my own past is settled by a contest between motives, and the motive with the stronger interest wins, whether or not it has the evidence.

**The step the text leaves out.** The section shows the contest and the verdict and nothing in between. It does not say how pride wins. That gap is where the reader has to supply something.

**The unstated premise.** For the story to end this way, there must be no third party in me standing above memory and pride, with no stake of its own, who would weigh the reports and side with the one that has the evidence. If such a judge existed, the memory would win, since it has the facts. The aphorism therefore presupposes a picture of the mind as a set of interested forces with no neutral referee among them.
</Example>

The premise is the philosophical content of the section. One famous picture of the mind has exactly the referee BGE 68 leaves out: Plato's soul, in which reason, a part with no appetite of its own, is meant to rule the passions. BGE 68 does not argue against that picture; it describes a case where the picture's referee does not show up, and invites the reader to ask whether it ever does.

<KeyFact>
BGE 68 presupposes that the mind has no neutral judge; it does not prove it. The argument for that premise, that a person is a society of drives with no member standing above the rest, has to be assembled from other sections (BGE 12 and 19 among them), and it is the subject of <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">the self as a society of drives</Ref>.
</KeyFact>

### The procedure

The steps used on BGE 68 make a procedure that works on any section.

1. **State the claim** as a general thesis in your own words, not as a paraphrase of the scene.
2. **List the steps** the text actually gives.
3. **Find the gap**: the place where an image, a joke or a dash stands where a step should be.
4. **Name the unstated premise** without which the steps do not reach the claim.
5. **Check it against other sections.** A section is read in its run and against the rest of the books, never alone; GS 125, the madman who announces that God is dead, is read inside the run of sections around it in <Ref lesson="death-of-god">the death of God</Ref>.

Two cautions keep the procedure honest. Build the strong reading, the one that makes the section a real claim, but take nothing into it that the words do not support; "inexorable" and "eventually" carry the reading of BGE 68, and a reading that ignored them would be invention. And where a passage admits two readings, say which one you are building and why. In the preface to D he asks for exactly this: to read "slowly, deeply, looking cautiously before and aft, with reservations, with doors left open".

## Books first, notebooks second: the book he never wrote

The kind of text matters as much as the date, because alongside the books there is a much larger mass of writing he did not publish. The German word for it is *Nachlass*, what someone leaves behind: for an author, everything unprinted at his death. Nietzsche's runs to thousands of pages, and the question is what weight a sentence from it carries.

### Notes as a quarry

All his working life he carried notebooks and wrote in them on walks; most of the books were assembled from such notes, rewritten. So a note is often an early draft of a section that exists in a book in a better form. After *Zarathustra* the relation changed. In July 1885 he wrote to Overbeck that his philosophy "is no longer communicable, at least not in print", and from then on the notebooks held far more than the books: hypotheses tried and dropped, plans, arguments he never released.

### The Will to Power: a plan abandoned in September 1888

From 1885 the notebooks carry plans for a large work, announced in GM III 27 as "The Will to Power: Attempt at a Revaluation of All Values". Early in 1888 he drew up a list of 374 notes for it, sorted into four books. He was not satisfied ("All in all, it was a torment", he wrote in February), and in early September 1888 he gave the plan up. Some of the material went into TI; *The Antichrist* was to be the first of four books of a work now called "Revaluation of All Values". He never wrote a book called *The Will to Power*.

### What the sister did

Elisabeth Nietzsche, two years younger than her brother, married Bernhard Förster, one of the leaders of the German antisemitic movement, in 1885. Nietzsche despised that movement, and he opposed the marriage in letters to her and to their mother. The couple left to found a German colony, Nueva Germania, in Paraguay. After Förster's suicide in 1889 and the colony's failure, she returned to Germany, found her brother famous, and took control of his papers. She founded a Nietzsche Archive, first in Naumburg and then in Weimar, and from there shaped how he was read for decades.

Three of her editorial decisions matter for reading him:

- **The compilation.** In 1901 she and a younger friend of her brother's published 483 numbered notes as *The Will to Power*; in 1906 a remodelled edition had 1,067. They arranged them under a four-line plan of 1886–87 that he had replaced with later ones, left out roughly a quarter of the texts on his own 1888 list, split and cut others, and mixed in notes he had already reworked into published books without saying which. The 1885 note quoted at the start, "This world is the will to power, and nothing besides!", became section 1067 and closed the book.
- **The delays.** EH, which repudiates much of what was being said in his name, was held back until 1908; *The Antichrist* appeared in 1895 labelled as Book One of *The Will to Power*.
- **The letters.** In 1956 an editor of his works showed that at least thirty-two letters she had printed as written to her were her own compositions, assembled in part from letters he had written to others.

### Ranking the texts, and citing them

The complete critical edition of his works and notebooks, prepared from the 1960s on, distinguishes five kinds of text: books he published; private prints (Z IV); manuscripts finished for print but not printed before the collapse (A, EH, NcW); early writings he finished and kept back, such as the essay on truth and lie of 1873; and notebook fragments. For deciding what he held they rank in that order, by how far he carried each text toward release. At the top is a text he finished, revised, signed and released; at the bottom, a note that shows what he was thinking on the day he wrote it, which he may later have rejected, revised or kept.

<KeyFact>
The line falls at *finished*, not at *printed in his lifetime*: Z IV, A, EH and NcW rank with the books, although A, EH and NcW appeared only after the collapse. A notebook entry stays a draft whenever it was written, and its number in *The Will to Power* records only where his editors put it.
</KeyFact>

The citation form follows from the ranking. A book is cited by abbreviation and section; a note by its notebook number, the fragment number within it, its date and the word "unpublished", because the numbers of the critical edition follow the order in which he wrote.

One influential reading takes the rule further: notes are background only, and nothing in them changes the picture the books give. The first half is the ranking above; the second is too strong. The notebooks contain at least one argument no book contains, an attempted physical proof that everything recurs, which <Ref lesson="recurrence-as-cosmology">recurrence as cosmology</Ref> examines and finds wanting. Such a note can serve as scaffolding for reading the books, provided it is labelled unpublished and treated neither as his settled view nor as a secret doctrine the books suppressed.

TI was in press from his manuscript in late 1888 and appeared in January 1889, days after the collapse, so it is a published book in every sense but the date. EH was revised "on the gold scales from the first to the last word", as he wrote on 8 December 1888, and the publisher found the manuscript clean and printable. WP has no manuscript of his at all: it exists only as an editorial arrangement of separate notes.
</Aside>

## Can numbered sections add up to one argument?

The strongest objection to reading him this way is that there may be no one argument to reconstruct. A writer who refused systems and wrote in sections cannot, the objection runs, be rebuilt as one connected argument; any coherent Nietzsche is the reader's invention, and what survives is fragments.

Nietzsche's reply comes in four parts, each from a published text. First, the Genealogy's preface claims a unity of origin for his ideas:

<Quote source="GM Preface 2, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
… our ideas, our values, our yeas and nays, our ifs and buts, grow out of us with the necessity with which a tree bears fruit — related and each with an affinity to each, and evidence of one will, one health, one soil, one sun.
</Quote>

Second, the form invites reconstruction: a section is a compressed argument to be "deciphered", and GM III shows one being unpacked. Third, the unity he claims is of the kind BGE 6 describes when it says a philosopher's morality testifies to "in what order the deepest impulses of his nature stand to each other": the unity of a valuer whose drives stand in one order of rank, which shows up across many sections without being a list of axioms. Fourth, what he rejects in a system is a premise placed beyond question, not a premise written down. A reconstruction writes his premises down, but as claims to be tested against the texts and against the facts, never as axioms that end the inquiry; built that way, it does not do what TI Maxims 26 condemns.

There is a gap the reply does not close. The unity he claims is the unity of a person and a task, not of a set of propositions, and two published passages can still conflict. Where they do, a reader who builds an argument from him has to say which passage he builds on and why, usually the later and more argued one, and cannot pretend the conflict away.

<Conclusion>
You can now place any passage before using it. Its date puts it in one of three periods: the Wagnerian professor of 1869–76, the free spirit of 1878–82, or the mature works of 1883–88, from Zarathustra to the collapse in January 1889. Its kind ranks it: published book, finished manuscript, or notebook entry cited by number, date and "unpublished", with *The Will to Power* recognised as his editors' arrangement. And you can read a numbered section as an argument: the claim, the steps the text gives, the gap where an image stands in for a step, and the premise that fills it, as BGE 68 turned out to presuppose a mind with no neutral judge in it.

The next lesson takes the first period at its word: Schopenhauer's case that a life of wanting is not worth living, and the young professor's answer to it in *The Birth of Tragedy*.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Place each passage in its period and say, in a clause each, what the date tells you about the weight it can bear as evidence of his mature view: BT 5 ("only as an aesthetic phenomenon is existence and the world eternally justified"); GS 125 (the madman who announces the death of God); Z Prologue 2 (Zarathustra meets an old hermit who has not heard that God is dead); GM I 10 (the slave revolt in morality); TI "How the True World Finally Became a Fable".

<Solution>
- **BT 5: period 1 (1872).** Written as Schopenhauer's disciple and Wagner's ally. It counts as his view in 1872; the 1886 preface keeps BT's question and disowns its metaphysical vocabulary, so it has to be checked against that preface before it is used as his later position.
- **GS 125: period 2 (1882, first edition, §§1–342).** A free-spirit section, written before Zarathustra and five years before Book V's fuller treatment of the same event in GS 343. It is published and carries full weight for 1882; read it in its run of sections and alongside GS 343.

- **Z Prologue 2: start of period 3 (1883).** Published, but spoken by a character in a narrative, the prophet Zarathustra: the line is illustration, and its claim has to be confirmed in the argued books.
- **GM I 10: period 3 (1887).** A mature, published, continuously argued essay: the strongest kind of evidence there is.
- **TI "Fable": period 3 (written 1888, published January 1889).** A finished book he saw through the press in his last working year; full weight, and among the latest statements of his view.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Do for BGE 146 what the chapter did for BGE 68: state the claim, list the steps the text gives, locate the gap, and name the unstated premise.

<Quote source="BGE 146, trans. H. Zimmern">
He who fights with monsters should be careful lest he thereby become a monster. And if thou gaze long into an abyss, the abyss will also gaze into thee.
</Quote>

<Solution>
**Claim.** Sustained opposition to something changes the opponent in the direction of what he opposes; long attention to a thing makes it part of the one who attends.

**Steps given.** (1) A person fights monsters. (2) Warning: he may become one. (3) A second case: a person gazes long into an abyss. (4) The abyss gazes back. The second sentence restates the first in a new image, with "long" adding duration as the condition.

**The gap.** Neither sentence says *how* the fighter becomes a monster. The image of the abyss gazing back stands where the mechanism should be: it shows the reversal of who acts on whom without explaining it.

**Unstated premise.** The self is not insulated from what it does and attends to: a person's habits of action and attention reshape the person. If there were a fixed core self that merely uses its methods and looks at its objects without being altered by them, the warning would be empty. The premise belongs to the same picture as BGE 68's, a self made of drives and habits with no untouched centre, which is why the two sections can be read together.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "The notebooks show what Nietzsche really thought. He wrote them for himself, without the masks he wore for the public, so where a note and a book disagree, the note is his real view."

<Solution>
**The claim's best evidence.** It has a strong witness: Nietzsche himself. In July 1885 he wrote to Overbeck that his philosophy "is no longer communicable, at least not in print", and from then on the notebooks held far more than the books. Read as the claim reads it, the letter says that what he really thought went into the notes because print could not carry it.

**What the letter says, and what it does not.** It is a complaint about a medium: his philosophy resisted being put into print. It does not say that a finished doctrine existed in the notes and was being kept out of the books. Three facts show which reading fits.

1. **The late notes were written for print.** After the letter he published more, not less: BGE in 1886, GM in 1887, and five books written in 1888. Many of the notes of 1885–88 were material for a book he meant to publish, announced in GM III 27 as *The Will to Power*. They are drafts toward print, not a private alternative to it.
2. **He dropped the plan they served.** In early September 1888 he gave it up, took some finished texts from it into TI and *The Antichrist*, and left the rest. Notes written for an abandoned plan are a record of attempts, including attempts he rejected, not of conclusions.
3. **The test case went into print whole.** The 1885 note "This world is the will to power, and nothing besides!" is the thought the claim would expect him to hold back. It appears in BGE 36 (1886), conclusion included: the world seen from within "would simply be 'Will to Power,' and nothing else". What the book adds is the argument that leads there and the conditions it depends on ("supposing", "granted"). Nothing was masked; a bare assertion became an argued hypothesis.

To survive, the claim would need a case where a note states a view and the published version of the same thought conceals it rather than arguing for it. BGE 36, its own best example, shows the reverse. Where a note and a book disagree, the later, revised, signed text is the better evidence of his view.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A notebook entry contradicts a section of a book he published a year later. Which counts as his view? Does your answer change if the note was written a year *after* the book? Is every text printed after 1889 demoted the same way?

<Solution>
**Note earlier than the book.** The book counts. He wrote the note, then a year later published a contrary section: the natural reading is that he considered the thought and rejected or revised it. The note is evidence of a stage he passed through.

**Note later than the book.** The book still stands as his public view, because it is the only one of the two he released, but the note now has a different value: it is evidence that he was reconsidering. The honest report is "published view X; an unpublished note of the following year moves toward Y", not "his view was Y". Whether the move was completed can only be settled by a later book.

**The limit of the rule.** The rule is "finished and released first", not "printed in his lifetime first". A, EH and NcW were finished for print in late 1888 and unprinted only because of the collapse and his sister's decisions; they rank with the books. So if an unprinted text of this kind contradicts an earlier book, the later finished manuscript is the better evidence, exactly as a later book would be. The demotion applies to notebook fragments, not to every text published after 1889.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The sentence "This world is the will to power, and nothing besides!" is cited in some books as "WP 1067" and in others as "notebook 38[12], June–July 1885, unpublished". Say what each citation tells a reader about the sentence, which one survives a change of edition, and what the compilation's placing the note last did to how it was read.

<Solution>
**WP 1067** says only that the note is section 1067 of the 1906 compilation. It gives no date, no indication that the text is unpublished, and no link to his own plans: the number was assigned by the editors in 1906 and could not exist in the 1901 edition, which had only 483 sections. A different arrangement of the compilation would change it again.

**38[12], June–July 1885, unpublished** says that the text is the twelfth fragment in the thirty-eighth manuscript of the critical edition's chronological numbering, written in the summer of 1885, and never released by him. That number is tied to the order of his own manuscripts, so it is the same in every modern edition and translation of the notebooks.

**The effect of the placement.** Section 1067 is the final section of the compilation, so a reader of *The Will to Power* met it as the last word of his "main work", the conclusion to which everything else led. In fact it is a note written a year before BGE, from a notebook, in a phase when the plan for the book was still changing, and the published version of the thought, BGE 36, presents it conditionally. The compilation turned an unpublished, exploratory note into a final doctrine. The thought was never "discarded"; the book that framed it as a conclusion was never his.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Describe precisely the judge whose absence BGE 68 presupposes: what properties would it need for the memory to win? Then name what plays that role in Plato's picture of the soul, and say what would follow for self-knowledge if no part of a person has those properties.

<Solution>
**The judge.** It would need (1) access to the competing reports, memory's and pride's; (2) no stake of its own in which answer is true, so that its verdict follows the evidence and not an interest; and (3) authority over the other parts, so that its verdict actually settles the belief.

**In Plato.** Reason: the part of the soul that knows, wants only the truth and the good of the whole, and is meant to rule the spirited part and the appetites.

**If no such part exists.** Every verdict about myself is issued by some interested part, and what I believe about my own past and motives reflects which interest is strongest, not which report is accurate. Self-knowledge then becomes something to achieve against my own interests, never something I have by looking inward. This is the thesis argued in <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">the self as a society of drives</Ref>: from BGE 12 and 19, a person is a commonwealth of drives in an order of rank, and none of them is a disinterested ruler standing above the rest.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

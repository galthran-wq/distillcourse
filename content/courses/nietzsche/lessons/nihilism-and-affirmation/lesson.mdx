Nihilism, in Nietzsche, is two problems that need two different answers: the disorientation that follows when values lose their foundation, and the despair that follows when the highest values turn out to be unrealizable in this world — and only the second is what the ascetic ideal was protecting against. This lesson bounds the word by its published uses, reconstructs each problem as a two-premise argument with Bernard Reginster's 2006 analysis, locates despair in GM III:28 and in a speech of *Zarathustra*, shows why the metaethical answers to disorientation leave despair untouched, and reads GS 276 as the shape of the answer that does touch it: affirmation, with a revaluation of suffering as its cost.

The course's argument arrives here with a debt and a doorstep. The critique is done: <Ref lesson="madman-argument">the first lesson</Ref> reconstructed "God is dead" as a collapse of foundations with a delay, <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref> found in III:28 that the ascetic ideal saved the will from "suicidal nihilism" at the price of guilt and that the author thereby owes a counter-ideal, and <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the will-to-truth lesson</Ref> found that the counter-ideal cannot be science, since science creates no values. The doorstep is Life's last word in <Ref lesson="self-overcoming-staged">the previous lesson</Ref>: a will that "must always overcome itself", and seven speeches later a soothsayer answering that "everything is empty, everything is the same, everything was". What stands open is the positive doctrine, and it cannot be stated until its problem is, because "nihilism" in Nietzsche names two problems that pull in opposite directions. This lesson separates them, assigns each its answer, and hands the test of the second answer to <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">the recurrence lesson</Ref>.

## The word, bounded: five published sites and one notebook definition

The definition of nihilism that the secondary literature quotes is not in a book Nietzsche published. "What does nihilism mean? That the uppermost values devaluate themselves. The aim is lacking; 'why?' finds no answer" is a notebook entry of autumn 1887 (NF-1887,9[35], printed as WP 2), and Heidegger's 1940 lectures on European nihilism open on it as the word's essence. Under the rule fixed in <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> and enforced in <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the compilation lesson</Ref>, a notebook corroborates and never grounds, so the first task is to see what the published works say the word means, and then to check the notebook against them.

### The five sites

The word occurs a dozen or so times in the books of 1887 and 1888, and its sense is fixed at five load-bearing places; in each the noun attached to it is nothingness as something *willed*, not values as something lost.

<Figure
  src="./figures/word-bounded.svg"

  caption="Five published sites and one notebook: the books attach the word to a will and to nothingness; the notebook's clause about values devaluing themselves has no published counterpart."

GS 346, written for the 1887 edition three sections after the section that defines the death of God, sets an either/or before "the coming generations": *entweder schafft eure Verehrungen ab oder — euch selbst!*, "either abolish your reverences or yourselves". "The latter would be nihilism; but would not the former also be nihilism? This is our question mark." The section is the one published place where the word is attached to *both* things, the abolition of values and the abolition of oneself, and it attaches the second without hesitation and the first only as a question.

The *Genealogy*'s Preface §5 uses the word first, of the morality of pity, "the great danger to mankind", the will "turning against life", and asks whether Europe's growing preference for pity is its "detour to a new Buddhism? to a European Buddhism? to nihilism?" GM III:14, from <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref>, names the danger to the healthy: should the great nausea at man and the great pity for man "one day mate", what comes into the world is "the 'last will' of man, his will to nothingness, nihilism" (*sein Wille zum Nichts, der Nihilismus*). III:28 uses the word once, of what the ascetic ideal prevented: "the door was closed to all suicidal nihilism" (*die Thür schloss sich vor allem selbstmörderischen Nihilismus zu*), and its subject in the surrounding sentences is a will with no aim, *der Wille für Mensch und Erde fehlte*, behind every human fate the refrain "in vain!". *The Antichrist* §7 (1888) says of pity that "through pity life is denied, made more worthy of denial: pity is the *practice* of nihilism" (*Mitleiden ist die Praxis des Nihilismus*), and that pity "persuades to nothingness"; "one does not say 'nothing': one says 'beyond' instead, or 'God', or 'the true life'". *Twilight*'s "Morality as Anti-Nature" §5 gives the same content without the word: morality as Schopenhauer formulated it, "denial of the will to life", is "the *décadence* instinct itself, making an imperative of itself: it says 'perish!'" In every case the word or its content is attached to a will and to what the will is turned toward.

### What the notebook adds, and what it does not

Set the notebook definition beside these. Its second clause, "the aim is lacking; 'why?' finds no answer", has a published counterpart in III:28 almost word for word: "his existence on earth contained no goal; 'why man at all?' was a question without an answer" (*„wozu Mensch überhaupt?“ — war eine Frage ohne Antwort*). Its first clause, "the uppermost values devaluate themselves", has none. No published sentence says that nihilism *is* the devaluation of values; the nearest, GS 346, asks whether abolishing one's reverences would be nihilism and leaves the question standing. So the published uses fix the word to a will and its object, nothingness, and to a goal that is missing; the devaluation of values enters only in the notebooks, and the literature that defines nihilism by that clause is defining it by the Nachlass.

This is not a verbal point. The two clauses of the notebook name two different conditions, and the next section shows that they are logically independent, that the published works attach the word to the second, and that Nietzsche's answers are answers to different things.

## Reginster's split: two conditions of a goal's power to inspire

A goal gives a life meaning, on the assumption that runs through everything Nietzsche wrote on the subject and is stated in GS 1, only if it inspires the agent to go on: "man has to believe, to know, from time to time why he exists". Reginster's analysis begins by asking what a goal needs in order to do that, and finds two conditions, from which the split follows.

### A concrete case: two ways a goal stops inspiring

Take a person whose highest goal is to write a novel, and whose other pursuits, friendships and a profession, take their point from that one. There are two distinct ways the goal can stop carrying the life. First, the person comes to believe that the goal has no value: not that novels are worse than they thought, but that the standard by which they had ranked it, say the worth of art, has no standing, so that nothing ranks anything. Second, the person keeps the valuation intact and comes to believe that the goal is unattainable, and unattainable not by an accident of talent or leisure, which would make only *this* life a failure and leave the person wanting to live as someone else, but by the nature of the world, so that no change of circumstance would help.

The first failure is a failure of the goal's **value**; the second is a failure of its **realizability**. Reginster's terms for the two nihilisms follow: *disorientation*, when the values by which goals could be ranked have lost their standing; *despair*, when the highest values are held and the world cannot realize them.

The two are not two descriptions of one state. They blame opposite things. Disorientation says there is nothing wrong with the world and something wrong with our values: the values were taken for facts and are not. Despair says there is nothing wrong with our values and something wrong with the world: the values are held, and the world will not have them. Reginster's formula for the second is the notebook sentence that best fits the published uses: "a nihilist is a man who judges of the world as it is that it ought not to be, and of the world as it ought to be that it does not exist" (NF-1887,9[60], WP 585).

### Disorientation reconstructed

<Remark title="nihilism as disorientation">
*P1 (normative objectivism).* A value has authority over an agent only if it has objective standing, independent of the agent's perspective, needs and affects.

*P2 (anti-realism).* There are no objective values: what has value "does not have it in itself, by its nature; nature is always valueless: rather a value was once given to it, bestowed, and we were the givers and bestowers" (GS 301, *wir waren diese Gebenden und Schenkenden*); the "true world" that was to hold them has been abolished (<Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">the fable lesson</Ref>).

*C.* No value has authority; nothing ranks any goal; "'why?' finds no answer" in the sense that no answer could bind.

*Validity.* Immediate. *Soundness.* P2 is Nietzsche's, argued in the books. P1 is not: it is the nihilist's, and the metaethical answers reconstructed two sections below are its denial. A reader who holds P2 and drops P1 is not disoriented, which is why GS 343 can report the death of God as "a new dawn". The distress the argument names is not indifference but loss, and Reginster grounds the loss in the natural need of GS 1: a being that has to know why it exists is frustrated, not merely informed, when no answer binds.
</Remark>

### Despair reconstructed

<Remark title="nihilism as despair">
*P1 (the highest values are life-negating).* The values that have governed Europe, the Christian-moral ones above all, are values whose realization requires conditions this world does not supply: a world without suffering, contradiction, becoming and death. They are values "from the standpoint of which this life deserves to be repudiated" (Reginster, ch. 1 §II.2), and they were realizable, if at all, only in another world, which is what "God" and "the beyond" named.

*P2 (there is no other world).* "God is dead": the other world is discredited, and with it the only site in which such values could be realized.

*C.* The highest values cannot be realized anywhere; the world as it is "ought not to be", and the world as it ought to be "does not exist" (WP 585).

*Validity.* Immediate. *The premise the madman's argument lacked.* <Ref lesson="madman-argument">The first lesson</Ref> recorded that GS 343's cheerfulness shows despair does not follow from the death of God alone; P1 is the premise that has to be added, and Reginster's contribution is to name it. Nietzsche's own word for the values in P1 is "moral"; the *Genealogy*'s three treatises are the argument that they have the origin and the function P1 says.
</Remark>

### What makes a value "highest"

Despair needs its values to be the highest ones, and Reginster gives the adjective two features, since the loss of a merely favourite goal produces lowered expectations, not despair. A highest value is, first, one whose realization is *expected* rather than aspired to, so that its absence is not the absence of a good but a positive evil; someone who aspires to friendship and has none has a reason to find life empty, someone who expects it and has none has a reason to call the condition intolerable. Second, it is a value whose realization *conditions the value of every other good*, so that no success with lesser goods redeems its failure: the intellectual work that took its point from the friendships goes pointless with them.

Right: the notebook sentence WP 585 dissected, a nihilist judges of the world as it is that it ought not to be, bracketed to feature one, the expected good is absent and its absence condemns, and of the world as it ought to be that it does not exist, bracketed to premise two of despair, no other world; a footer reads that both features are needed for the inference from unrealizable to ought not to be."
  caption="The two features of a highest value, and WP 585's two clauses laid over them: the first clause is the expectation disappointed, the second is the other world gone."

WP 585's sentence, read against these features, is the inference from unrealizable to condemned, and it goes through only with both: "the world as it is ought not to be" is the disappointed *expectation*, since a merely absent good would license indifference and not a verdict; "the world as it ought to be does not exist" is the loss of the site in which a value that *conditions everything else* could have been realized.

### Why disorientation must be settled first

The two problems are independent, and one of them has a precedence. A value with no standing cannot be despaired of: there is no reason to grieve that the world will not realize a value one no longer takes to bind. So the disorientation branch, if it is resolved by *keeping* the nihilist's premise that values need objective standing, dissolves despair along with everything else; and if it is resolved by *dropping* that premise, so that values bind without objective standing, then the life-negating values bind again, and despair is back.

Left, a dashed empty box labelled a value with no standing, with the note that it cannot be unrealizable, since nothing is held that the world could fail; a crossed arrow to a struck-through label despair. Right, a solid amber box labelled a value that binds, with an arrow to a globe-shaped outline labelled this world, crossed, and a dashed outline labelled the other world, marked none: the label despair beneath. A footer states that despair presupposes a value that stands, so disorientation has to be answered before despair can register as a problem, and that answering it by restoring standing to values restores the despair the ascetic ideal held off."
  caption="Despair needs a value that stands: an empty box cannot be unrealizable, so the disorientation branch has to be settled before the despair branch is even a problem."

That precedence is what the opening statement asserts when it says that only despair is what the ascetic ideal was protecting against. III:28's ideal did not give values objective standing; it gave a suffering will a goal it could hold and a world, the beyond, in which the goal was realized. What it held off was a will with a value and no world.

<KeyFact>
Disorientation blames the values and despair blames the world, and the order is fixed: a value with no standing cannot be despaired of, so any answer that gives values back their standing gives despair back its object, which is why Nietzsche's answer to despair cannot be a theory of what values are.
</KeyFact>

## Despair in the texts: III:28 reread, and "The Soothsayer"

The split is Reginster's; the test is whether the published statements of nihilism fall on the despair branch by their own sentences. III:28 and the speech of *Zarathustra* Part II (summer 1883) that the previous lesson left on the doorstep are the two statements to run it on.

### III:28: a will with a value and no world

<Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">The priest lesson</Ref> reconstructed §28's three sentences; the sentences bear rereading with the split in hand.

<Quote source="GM III:28, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Sein Dasein auf Erden enthielt kein Ziel; „wozu Mensch überhaupt?“ — war eine Frage ohne Antwort; der Wille für Mensch und Erde fehlte; hinter jedem grossen Menschen-Schicksale klang als Refrain ein noch grösseres „Umsonst!“ […] Der Mensch, das tapferste und leidgewohnteste Thier, verneint an sich nicht das Leiden: er will es, er sucht es selbst auf, vorausgesetzt, dass man ihm einen Sinn dafür aufzeigt, ein Dazu des Leidens. Die Sinnlosigkeit des Leidens, nicht das Leiden, war der Fluch, der bisher über der Menschheit ausgebreitet lag, — und das asketische Ideal bot ihr einen Sinn! […] In ihm war das Leiden ausgelegt; die ungeheure Leere schien ausgefüllt; die Thür schloss sich vor allem selbstmörderischen Nihilismus zu.
</Original>

His existence on earth contained no goal; "why man at all?" was a question without an answer; the will for man and earth was lacking; behind every great human destiny there sounded as refrain a still greater "In vain!" […] Man, the bravest animal and the one most accustomed to suffering, does not deny suffering as such: he wills it, he even seeks it out, provided one shows him a meaning for it, a *what-for* of suffering. The senselessness of suffering, not suffering, was the curse that lay spread over humanity until now, and the ascetic ideal offered it a meaning! […] In it suffering was interpreted; the tremendous void seemed filled; the door was closed to all suicidal nihilism.
</Quote>

Every clause is on the despair branch. The problem is not that suffering has no value but that it has no *what-for*: a goal is missing, "in vain" is the refrain, and what is wanted is a meaning that would make the suffering count toward something. The ideal's solution was to supply the goal and a world in which it is reached, "suffering as punishment, life as a bridge", from §§11 and 20; that is a life-negating value with a site for its realization. And what the ideal's collapse returns is not indifference but the "tremendous void" (*die ungeheure Leere*), a will that still needs an aim and has none: a value held, a world that will not have it.

<Remark title="III:28 classified">
By P1 of despair, the ideal's goal is life-negating: its realization lies beyond this life. By P2, the ideal's God is denied (III:27). The section's own conclusion, that man "would rather will nothingness than not will", is despair's C with the will's response written in: the value is kept, the world is condemned, and the will takes nothingness for an aim rather than have none. The disorientation branch does not appear: no sentence of §28 says that the ideal's values lacked standing. The section's whole charge is that they had standing, worked, and cost too much.
</Remark>

### The Soothsayer: despair felt, in three clauses

"The Soothsayer" (Z II 19) is the speech the previous lesson set on the doorstep. Zarathustra hears a soothsayer, and the speech is the sentence with which nihilism enters the book.

<Quote source="Z II 19, 'The Soothsayer', trans. Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
„— und ich sahe eine grosse Traurigkeit über die Menschen kommen. Die Besten wurden ihrer Werke müde.

Eine Lehre ergieng, ein Glauben lief neben ihr: „Alles ist leer, Alles ist gleich, Alles war!“ […]

Wohl haben wir geerntet: aber warum wurden alle Früchte uns faul und braun? […]

Umsonst war alle Arbeit, Gift ist unser Wein geworden, böser Blick sengte unsre Felder und Herzen gelb. […]

Wahrlich, zum Sterben wurden wir schon zu müde; nun wachen wir noch und leben fort — in Grabkammern!“ —
</Original>

"– and I saw a great sadness descend over humanity. The best became weary of their works.

A doctrine circulated, a belief accompanied it: 'Everything is empty, everything is the same, everything was!' […]

We harvested well, but why did all our fruits turn foul and brown? […]

All work was for naught, our wine has become poison, the evil eye seared yellow our fields and hearts. […]

Indeed, we have already become too weary to die; now we continue to wake and we live on – in burial chambers!" –
</Quote>

Then the narrative: the prophecy "went straight to his heart and transformed him", Zarathustra "became like those of whom the soothsayer had spoken", took no food for three days, and dreamed that he was "guardian of graves" on "the mountain fortress of death", until a black coffin burst open and "spewed forth thousandfold laughter". Del Caro's note records that the dream was one Nietzsche had and described to a friend; the map records it as the felt form of the doctrine and nothing more.

<Example title="the soothsayer's doctrine classified by its three clauses">
The doctrine has three clauses, and the split sorts them.

**"Everything was" (*Alles war*).** The achievements are past and nothing carries forward: the harvest came in and the fruit rotted, "all work was for naught" (*umsonst*, III:28's refrain). This is despair's C: goals held, and the world, here time, will not keep what was achieved. The clause says nothing about the standing of the values by which the work was worth doing.

**"Everything is empty" (*Alles ist leer*).** The wells dried, the sea withdrew, "the depths do not want to devour". The image is of a world that will not receive what is wanted of it, not of values that fail to bind: despair again.

**"Everything is the same" (*Alles ist gleich*).** This clause is ambiguous, and Reginster records the ambiguity: read as "all efforts come to the same failure" it is despair; read as "nothing ranks above anything" it is evaluative indifference, the disorientation branch. The speech does not decide it. What decides it is the sentence after the doctrine: the best became weary *of their works*, which is a relation to goals still held, and "too weary to die" is a will with a value it cannot realize and not a will with no values. Two clauses are despair by their sentences, the third is despair by its context.
</Example>

Three speeches later, in "On Redemption", the book supplies despair's mechanism in one sentence, and the course previews it here in one sentence only: "'It was': thus is called the will's gnashing of teeth and loneliest misery" (*„Es war“: also heisst des Willens Zähneknirschen und einsamste Trübsal*), the will "impotent against that which has been", and its revenge on time is what the speech calls the spirit of revenge. A will diagnosed this way fixes what an answer to it cannot be. Its values' standing is not what it lost, so nothing done to standing reaches it; and a world is what the ascetic ideal gave it, at the price III:28 charges, so the answer cannot be another world. The next two sections are the two constraints applied: the metaethical answers work only on standing, and GS 276 keeps this world and changes the value.

## Disorientation's answers, and why they leave despair standing

From a philosopher who denies objective values one expects the metaethics to be the whole of the positive doctrine: show that values never needed standing, and "why?" gets its answer back. The strategy is in the published texts, in two forms, and it cannot be the whole, for the reason the precedence established above supplies.

### Two ways to dissolve disorientation

The disorientation argument has two premises, and each can be denied.

**Subjectivism** denies P1, normative objectivism. Values are "interpretations" from the standpoint of our "affects" and "passions"; their authority does not depend on their being facts, and GS 301's "we were the givers and bestowers" is read not as a debunking but as a description of where authority comes from. Harold Langsam's version has Nietzsche argue in two steps: normative objectivism is itself a value judgment, so on the nihilist's own anti-realism it has no objective standing either; and since no objective fact can then be appealed to, the nihilist has to be *won over* to the value of created values, which is what *Zarathustra*'s pictures of creation are for. The strategy's cost is on its face: it turns demonstration into seduction, and it owes an account of why a desire is a reason. Reginster reads Nietzsche's hint, that the passions carry "a quantum of reason", as the promissory note.

**Fictionalism** keeps P1 and denies that P2 disorients. Values would need objective standing to bind, and there is none; so one engages in make-believe in objective values, a "useful fiction" lived as if true. Nadeem Hussain's reconstruction, which <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the value-creation lesson</Ref> evaluates, gives Nietzsche a cognitivist semantics and an anti-realist metaphysics and lets the practice of valuing continue on a pretence its practitioners see through.

Both dissolve disorientation, because both remove the inference from "not objective" to "does not bind". Each is a reading of the same published sentence; the course does not decide between them here, since the question of what value creation is belongs to the later lesson.

### The limit: a value without standing can still be unrealizable

<Task>
Step through the three frames and, at each, name the premise of the disorientation argument that the strategy removes: at the first, P1; at the second, the inference from P2 to C, by adding a practice; at the third, nothing on the right, and state why: neither strategy touches P1 of despair, that the highest values are life-negating, or P2, that there is no other world. Then check the third frame against III:28: the ideal's values did their work for two millennia as an interpretation, which is what a fictionalist would call them, and the treatise's charge against them is not that they were fictions.

<Remark title="the limit of metaethics reconstructed (Reginster, ch. 2 §V.2)">
*P1.* Subjectivism holds that the authority of a value does not depend on its objective standing.

*P2.* The life-negating values of despair's P1 are values. So, by P1, their lack of objective standing does not devalue them either.

*P3.* A value that binds and whose realization requires another world is, with that world gone, unrealizable.

*C.* Subjectivism leaves despair exactly where it was: the highest values bind and cannot be realized.

*The fictionalist case.* Fictionalism makes every morality a game of make-believe, and on that account one game is as good as another for the function Nietzsche calls a morality's "value", giving a life direction. The old Christian morality did that for a long time, and III:28 says so. So the fictional character of its values cannot be what is wrong with them, and the charge that they are harmful and must be replaced needs a ground the fiction does not supply.

*Bearing.* Overcoming despair therefore requires a revaluation that is "no longer metaethical but substantive": it must engage the content of the life-negating values and replace them. The standard of that revaluation is the will to power (ch. 3), which <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref> reconstructed as psychology, and its result is the ideal the next section reads.
</Remark>

The consequence for the course's question is direct. A naturalist who has explained where values come from can answer disorientation by a theory of valuing; the theory is what <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the value-creation lesson</Ref> tests. But nothing in that theory says which values to hold, and despair is a fact about which values are held. The positive doctrine has to say what is worth valuing in a world that has only itself, and that is not metaethics.

## Affirmation: GS 276 as the shape of the answer to despair

From an answer to despair one expects a demonstration that the highest values are realizable after all, some replacement for the other world in which they could be reached. The published text does the opposite: it keeps this world, necessity included, and changes the values. The section that states the change is the one that opens Book IV of *The Gay Science*, written for the new year of 1882 and published that year, and it states it without using the word "yes" until its last sentence.

<Quote source="GS 276, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Zum neuen Jahre. — Noch lebe ich, noch denke ich: ich muss noch leben, denn ich muss noch denken. Sum, ergo cogito: cogito, ergo sum. Heute erlaubt sich Jedermann seinen Wunsch und liebsten Gedanken auszusprechen: nun, so will auch ich sagen, was ich mir heute von mir selber wünschte und welcher Gedanke mir dieses Jahr zuerst über das Herz lief, — welcher Gedanke mir Grund, Bürgschaft und Süssigkeit alles weiteren Lebens sein soll! Ich will immer mehr lernen, das Nothwendige an den Dingen als das Schöne sehen: — so werde ich Einer von Denen sein, welche die Dinge schön machen. Amor fati: das sei von nun an meine Liebe! Ich will keinen Krieg gegen das Hässliche führen. Ich will nicht anklagen, ich will nicht einmal die Ankläger anklagen. Wegsehen sei meine einzige Verneinung! Und, Alles in Allem und Grossen: ich will irgendwann einmal nur noch ein Ja-sagender sein!
</Original>

For the new year. — I still live, I still think: I must still live, for I must still think. *Sum, ergo cogito: cogito, ergo sum.* Today everyone permits himself to state his wish and his dearest thought: well, so I too want to say what I wished of myself today and which thought first ran across my heart this year, which thought shall be the ground, warrant and sweetness of all my further life! I want to learn more and more to see what is necessary in things as what is beautiful: so I shall be one of those who make things beautiful. *Amor fati*: let that be my love from now on! I want to wage no war against the ugly. I want not to accuse, I want not even to accuse the accusers. Looking away shall be my only negation! And, all in all and on the whole: some day I want to be only a Yes-sayer!
</Quote>

### The section dissected: four steps to the last sentence

The section is a resolution in four clauses, and each clause withdraws one thing the despairing will does.

<Figure
  src="./figures/gs276-dissected.svg"
  alt="Four boxes in a column, each with its German phrase, its rendering and the move it withdraws from despair, joined by arrows down to a fifth box in amber. One: das Nothwendige als das Schöne sehen, to see the necessary in things as the beautiful; what it withdraws, the judgment that what cannot be otherwise ought to be otherwise. Two: keinen Krieg gegen das Hässliche, no war against the ugly; what it withdraws, the project of removing what the highest values condemned. Three: nicht einmal die Ankläger anklagen, not even accuse the accusers; what it withdraws, ressentiment against those who still condemn. Four: Wegsehen sei meine einzige Verneinung, looking away my only negation; what it keeps, one negation, and what it withdraws, the accusation. Fifth, amber: nur noch ein Ja-sagender, only a Yes-sayer, the first and only yes, stated as some day. A side note reads that Amor fati is Latin, love of fate, and that the section is dated the new year 1882, Genoa."
  caption="GS 276 in four withdrawals and one word: each clause removes something the despairing will does, and the Yes arrives last, as a wish for some day."
/>

The first clause reverses despair's verdict at its root. Despair judges that what is necessary in the world, suffering, becoming, loss, ought not to be; the resolution is to "see the necessary in things as the beautiful", which is not to deny the necessity but to value it. The second withdraws the war: the life-negating values are values in whose name the ugly, meaning what they condemn, is to be fought, and the resolution declines the fight. The third withdraws *ressentiment* in the form <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave revolt</Ref> gave it: the accusation that finds someone to blame, extended even to the accusers. The fourth keeps exactly one negation, looking away, and it is a negation without a verdict. Then the word: "some day", "only a Yes-sayer".

The section's own name for the stance is Latin and stays so: *amor fati*, love of fate. Two sections later the book tells the reader what fate to love, and the sections are read in later lessons: GS 283's "live dangerously" (*gefährlich leben!*), "build your cities on Vesuvius", and GS 290's "one thing is needful", to "give style" to one's character, which <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref> reads as the content of the stance. The programme of Book IV is affirmation stated first as a wish and then as a set of practices.

### The Dionysian: the same stance named in 1888

Six years later, in the last section of *Twilight*'s "What I Owe the Ancients", Nietzsche names the stance and says what it is not.

<Quote source="TI, 'What I Owe the Ancients' §5, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Das Jasagen zum Leben selbst noch in seinen fremdesten und härtesten Problemen; der Wille zum Leben, im Opfer seiner höchsten Typen der eignen Unerschöpflichkeit frohwerdend — das nannte ich dionysisch […] Nicht um von Schrecken und Mitleiden loszukommen, nicht um sich von einem gefährlichen Affekt durch dessen vehemente Entladung zu reinigen — so verstand es Aristoteles —: sondern um, über Schrecken und Mitleid hinaus, die ewige Lust des Werdens selbst zu sein, — jene Lust, die auch noch die Lust am Vernichten in sich schliesst…
</Original>

The saying-Yes to life even in its strangest and hardest problems; the will to life, rejoicing in its own inexhaustibility in the sacrifice of its highest types: that is what I called Dionysian […] Not in order to get rid of terror and pity, not in order to purge oneself of a dangerous affect by its vehement discharge, which is how Aristotle understood it: but in order, beyond terror and pity, to *be* the eternal joy of becoming itself, the joy that includes even the joy in destruction…
</Quote>

The sentence adds to GS 276 the object of the Yes: "even in its strangest and hardest problems", becoming and destruction included. And it separates the stance from its nearest neighbour. Aristotle's tragedy discharges terror and pity; the Dionysian is "beyond" them, and pity is named because, on III:14 and *The Antichrist* §7, pity is the affect that breeds the will to nothingness. A Yes that still needed to be purged of pity would be a Yes to a world with the pitiable removed, which is the other world again.

### The reconstruction, and what it leaves open

<Remark title="affirmation as the answer to despair">
*P1 (despair).* Despair is the judgment that this life cannot realize what is valued, made from the standpoint of values that require another world.

*P2 (affirmation).* Affirmation values this life, its necessity included, as an end: "to see the necessary in things as the beautiful", "the saying-Yes to life even in its strangest and hardest problems".

*C.* Affirmation dissolves despair by changing what is valued, not by realizing the old values: the world "as it ought to be" is no longer a world other than this one, so the judgment that this one "ought not to be" has lost its standard.

*Validity.* The conclusion follows, and it follows without any claim that the old values were false or groundless: a person who affirms need not have refuted anything, which is why the answer to despair is independent of the answer to disorientation.

*Two sham forms excluded.* *Ecce Homo* II 10 states the formula, "that one wants nothing to be different, not forward, not backward, not in all eternity", and separates it from two attitudes: "not merely *bear* what is necessary, still less conceal it, but *love* it". Reginster names them resignation, which accepts the necessary while deploring it, and concealment, which pretends the necessary away, and reads *Zarathustra*'s "omni-satisfied" donkey, "Yea-Yuh" to everything, as the second. <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">The priest lesson</Ref> drew the same two failure modes on a line; both keep the old condemnation of suffering, one openly and one by hiding it.

*What is not yet given.* Two things. First, a test: GS 276 says "some day" and offers no way to tell whether one has become a Yes-sayer, and whether a Yes to a moment is a Yes to the life the moment belongs to; that is GS 341's demon, sixty-five sections later, and <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">the recurrence lesson</Ref>. Second, a content: P2 says this life is valued as an end and does not say what in it is valued or by whom; the higher type of <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the next module's third lesson</Ref> is the answer the argued works give.
</Remark>

## The revaluation of suffering: an ingredient, not a cost

P2 of affirmation, taken seriously, has a price, and the price is stated in a sentence of Reginster's that reads as a provocation until it is derived: "the lack of suffering implies the lack of true happiness". The derivation runs on the psychological doctrine of will to power, and it is the point at which the module's psychology becomes the positive doctrine's ground.

### The derivation from the three conditions

<Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">The will-to-power lesson</Ref> fixed the doctrine that survives analysis: the will to power is satisfied only by (i) a determinate first-order end, (ii) resistance to it, and (iii) success in overcoming the resistance, with A §2's definition as anchor, "what is happiness? the feeling that power increases, that a resistance is overcome". One further premise is Schopenhauer's, and Nietzsche keeps it: suffering is the will's hindrance by an obstacle, the felt form of a desire not yet satisfied.

<Example title="from the three conditions to the inversion (Reginster, ch. 4 §IV, ch. 6 §I.1)">
**Step 1.** Happiness, on A §2, is the overcoming of resistance in the pursuit of a determinate end: conditions (i) to (iii) met.

**Step 2.** Condition (ii) is a necessary condition: no resistance, no overcoming, no happiness in this sense. A game against an opponent who cannot play yields none, which the will-to-power lesson worked.

**Step 3.** Resistance to a desire's satisfaction is, by Schopenhauer's definition, suffering: the desire is held and not yet satisfied, and that state is felt as displeasure.

**Step 4.** So suffering, in that sense, is a necessary condition of happiness; and since the overcoming is *of* the resistance, the suffering is not a stage on the way to the happiness but part of what the activity consists in: an ingredient, "not a complement or precondition, but a constituent".

**Step 5.** Therefore a life without suffering is a life without happiness of this kind. GS 338 states the conclusion in the published register: happiness and unhappiness "are two siblings and twins that grow up together or, as with you, *stay small* together" (*mit einander gross wachsen oder … mit einander — klein bleiben*). Reginster's inversion, "the lack of suffering implies the lack of true happiness", is Step 5 with "true" marking the A §2 sense against contentment.

**What the derivation assumes.** That happiness *is* the resistance kind. A §2 defines it so, and the definition is the is–ought step the will-to-power lesson marked; the derivation shows what follows from it, not that it is true. And Step 3 uses "suffering" in Schopenhauer's technical sense, the frustration of a desire, which is narrower than pain: the closing subsection returns to the gap.
</Example>

The derivation changes the meaning of III:28. The ascetic ideal's error was not, on this reading, to give suffering a meaning; man "does not deny suffering as such", he wills it "provided one shows him a meaning for it". The error was the meaning it gave: guilt, a sentence passed on the sufferer, which makes the suffering count toward a goal in another world. The counter-ideal gives suffering a meaning inside this one: resistance overcome. Both are interpretations of the same fact; they differ in where the what-for is located.

<Figure
  src="./figures/suffering-two-exits.svg"
  alt="The loop of the will-to-power lesson redrawn: a determinate end, resistance to it now relabelled suffering in amber, the resistance overcome, satisfaction, the resistance gone, and a dashed return arrow labelled a new end or a greater resistance. Two exits leave the suffering node. The upper exit, blue, is labelled the ascetic ideal, GM III:28: suffering interpreted as guilt, a what-for located in another world, the door to suicidal nihilism closed at the price of life. The lower exit, amber, is labelled the counter-ideal: suffering interpreted as resistance overcome, a what-for located in this world, the ingredient of the new happiness. A footer states that both exits give suffering a meaning and that they differ in where the meaning is, and that on the lower exit the lack of suffering implies the lack of true happiness."
  caption="One loop, two meanings for the same node: the ascetic ideal reads the resistance as guilt owed elsewhere, the counter-ideal reads it as what the activity overcomes here."
/>

The *Genealogy* already contains the counter-ideal's reading of suffering in one place, and it is the place where the bad conscience is born. GM II:18 calls the self-violation of the caged animal "this artist's cruelty" (*diese Künstler-Grausamkeit*), "this pleasure in giving oneself, as a heavy, resisting, suffering material, a form": the material resists, the form is the overcoming, and the pleasure is in the activity. <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">The internalization lesson</Ref> read the section for its mechanism; reread from here it is the three conditions applied to oneself, with the suffering counted as material and not as sentence.

### What Nietzsche is committed to, and where the ally comes from

The revaluation commits Nietzsche to a claim about ideals. A life without suffering is not the ideal, and every ideal that defines happiness as suffering's absence, Schopenhauer's satisfaction of all desires, the "English happiness" of comfort, the Buddhist deep sleep, the last human being of the *Zarathustra* Prologue who "invented happiness" and "left the regions where it was hard to live", is on the wrong side of the line. Two pictures of the will's condition stand against each other here. Schopenhauer's pendulum swings between pain, while a desire is unsatisfied, and boredom, once it is; there is no third position, and heaven, all desires satisfied once and for all, sits at the boredom end. Goethe's Faust asks the devil for the opposite, "a game that none may win who play", desires "perpetually rekindled", and Nietzsche defines the Dionysian by that request: "the soul that has, but wants to want and will" (EH III, on *Zarathustra*, §6). Affirmation replaces the pendulum with the bargain.

<Aside>
The same verdict on a painless ideal was reached in 1884 by a writer who had not read Nietzsche. William James, in "The Dilemma of Determinism" (1884), asks why "the painting of any paradise or utopia, in heaven or on earth" awakens "yearnings for escape", and answers that "the white-robed, harp-playing heaven of our sabbath-schools" and Spencer's "tea-table elysium" are "lubberlands, pure and simple", pictures "of light upon light" that are "vacuous and expressionless, neither to be enjoyed nor understood", so that *tedium vitae* "is the only sentiment they awake in our breast"; "to our crepuscular natures, born for the conflict", if a race of "unexampled insipidity" is the whole fruit of the victory, "better lose than win the battle". James's insipid creatures are the last human being of the Prologue, who has "one's tiny pleasure for the day and one's tiny pleasure for the night". A notebook entry of 1888 states the mechanism the essay only feels: displeasure "acting as an ingredient of joy", "this play of resistance and victory" as "the strongest stimulus" of the feeling of power (WP 699, Nachlass). The constituent reading above is the analytic form of the same point, and James is the evidence that the point is not a Nietzschean idiosyncrasy.
</Aside>

The commitment extends to pity, and this is where the module's thread from III:14 is tied off. If suffering is an ingredient of the good, then an affect whose object is the removal of suffering as such is an affect against the good, and *The Antichrist* §7's "pity is the practice of nihilism" is not hyperbole but the revaluation's corollary. The Preface to the *Genealogy* (§5) had already called the morality of pity "the great danger to mankind", the point at which "the will turning against life" announces itself. Reginster's reading of Nietzsche's own "kind of compassion" completes the corollary: its object is "precious capabilities squandered", someone "halted at something less than he might have become", which can be aroused by comfort as easily as by pain. The compassion that survives the revaluation is a response to missed overcoming, and it is compatible with wanting a friend's suffering "higher and worse than ever" (BGE 225).

### The open fault line

The revaluation does not cover everything it is asked to, and Reginster says so. Step 3 used "suffering" for the frustration of a held desire. Pain in the plain sense, illness, bereavement, the suffering that does not come from a desire's resistance but arrives from outside, is not resistance in that sense; Reginster handles it separately, as a stimulant that spawns desires and so gives the will something to do, and concedes that the extension is weaker than the derivation. He also concedes the stringency: "ordinary human beings will not welcome all resistance, or any pain, as an opportunity for the pursuit of power", and "much unexpected pain and suffering might actually undermine whatever prospect for greatness I might have had". Meade McCloughan's review presses this as the reading's fault line: not all suffering fits the challenge-and-resistance model, and much of it could not be affirmed in the way the reading needs. The course records the objection as open. What the revaluation establishes is narrower than its slogan: that the presence of suffering in a life is not as such an objection to the life, and that the ideal of a life without it is a life without the good. What it does not establish is that any particular suffering is affirmable.

<KeyFact>
The derivation's scope is the recurrence test's problem. It covers suffering as a held desire's resistance; the demon of GS 341 asks for a Yes to "every pain and every joy", the pain from outside included, so the test the next lesson runs demands exactly the claim this section has not established, and a reader who fails that test may be failing on this gap and not on the recurrence.
</KeyFact>

## The nihilist misreading, refuted from the texts

The correct claim first. Nietzsche diagnoses nihilism as the consequence of the ascetic ideal's collapse, names it the danger in the one section of the *Genealogy* where the word first appears, and proposes affirmation against it; every published use of the word is on the side of the accusation.

The misreading, stated once: *Nietzsche is a nihilist; his philosophy ends in "nothing matters"*. It is rejected because three published sentences have no place in it. III:14 calls the will to nothingness "the maximum of monstrousness" (*etwas vom Unheimlichsten*), the thing above all to be feared. III:28's whole analysis is of how the ascetic ideal "closed the door to all suicidal nihilism", and the treatise's charge is that the door was closed at too high a price, not that it should have been left open. And *The Antichrist* §7 uses "nihilism" as the name of what is wrong with his opponents' virtue: a nihilist does not call pity nihilism.

### Why the misreading fails: it collapses the two branches

The causal explanation is the split. Nietzsche holds P2 of the disorientation argument: there are no objective values, "we were the givers and bestowers". A reader who also holds P1, that a value binds only if it is objective, infers C, that nothing binds, and attributes the inference to the author of P2. But P1 is the premise Nietzsche rejects, and GS 346 is the place he says so: the "either/or" makes abolishing one's reverences a question and abolishing oneself the certainty, and the book's answer to the question, given by Book IV in 1882 before Book V posed it, is that abandoning the reverences leaves one a Yes-sayer, not a nihilist. The misreading takes the anti-realist premise for a verdict, and it can do so only by ignoring that the despair branch, where the verdict lives, is the one Nietzsche attacks.

The replacement model is the two-branch figure: on the left, a premise Nietzsche holds and a conclusion he blocks; on the right, a condition he diagnoses and an answer he gives.

### The discriminating example: characters Zarathustra overcomes

What a defender of the misreading can still cite is a set of sentences, and each is disposed of by a rule the course already has. "Everything is empty, everything is the same, everything was" is the soothsayer's, and the dramatization rule of <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref> reads a character's line for what it stages: Zarathustra "became like" the soothsayer, dreamed the graveyard, woke, and answered "I will yet show him a sea in which he can drown"; in Part IV the soothsayer returns as "the proclaimer of the great weariness" who taught "all is the same, nothing is worth it, the world is without meaning", and is a guest at the table, not its host. "Nothing is true, everything is permitted" is said in Part IV by the Shadow, and cited in GM III:24 as the Assassins' formula, a measuring rod for how far the free spirits have *not* gone. And "everything is false" is a notebook line; the evidence rule of the compilation lesson leaves it as corroboration of nothing the books say.

<KeyFact>
Every published sentence in which Nietzsche uses the word "nihilism" is an accusation, and the sentences a nihilist reading needs are a character's, a notebook's, or a premise of the disorientation argument that is nihilism only with the objectivist premise Nietzsche denies.
</KeyFact>

## ★ The notebooks' typology: pessimism to classical nihilism

The notebooks of 1887 and 1888 contain a taxonomy of nihilisms that the published works never state, and Heidegger's lectures of 1940 organize the fourth volume of his *Nietzsche* around it. The course keeps it in a starred section for two reasons: it is Nachlass throughout, and the split of this lesson cuts across it.

<Figure
  src="./figures/typology.svg"
  alt="A ladder of five rungs rising left to right, all drawn in the muted colour and marked Nachlass. A footer states that the published works state none of these grades and that the split of this lesson cuts across the ladder."

### The rungs

The two lowest rungs are conditions the published works describe in other words. *Pessimism*, "the world does not have the value we believed" (WP 32), is what GS 346 calls the world being "worth less", and the notebook calls it a "preliminary form" (WP 9). *Incomplete nihilism* deposes the highest values and sets "new ideals" "in the old place", the transcendent one; the notebooks' own example is exact, "in place of dogmatic Christianity it posits Wagnerian music" (WP 1021).

The upper rungs are the notebooks' alone. *Extreme nihilism* says there is no truth in itself, and at this rung the notebooks distinguish two moods, *passive*, "the weary nihilism that no longer attacks", and *active*, "a violent force of destruction" (WP 22 to 23, from the same notebook as WP 2). Above both stands the rung Heidegger has Nietzsche claim for himself, *classical* nihilism, "the ideal of supreme powerfulness" (WP 14), which clears the ground for new values.

### Where the split cuts the ladder

Two remarks bear on the lesson. First, Reginster observes that the passive/active distinction presupposes despair: resignation to a hopelessly evil world and its destruction as hopelessly evil are both attitudes of a will that still holds the values by which the world is evil, whereas a disoriented will has no values to resign or destroy for. The notebooks' most detailed taxonomy is therefore a taxonomy of the despair branch. Second, incomplete nihilism is the grade on which the course's <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">will-to-truth lesson</Ref> already stands: science set in the old place of the ideal, "the ideal's latest and noblest form", is what III:23 to 25 describe, and the notebook's Wagnerian music is the same move with a different tenant. The ladder is one reading's arrangement of the notebooks; <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref> states that reading at strength.

## What is established, and what the recurrence test takes up

What is established. The published works attach "nihilism" to a will and to nothingness, GS 346, GM III:14 and III:28, *The Antichrist* §7, and the definition by "the highest values devalue themselves" is a notebook's, with a published counterpart for its second clause only. Reginster's split separates two conditions of a goal's power to inspire, its value and its realizability, into two nihilisms: disorientation, values without standing, reconstructed from normative objectivism and anti-realism, and despair, life-negating highest values with no world to realize them in, reconstructed from the premise the madman's argument lacked. The two blame opposite things, and despair presupposes a value that stands, so disorientation is settled first and any settlement that restores values' authority restores despair's object. III:28 and the soothsayer fall on the despair branch by their sentences. Subjectivism and fictionalism dissolve disorientation and leave despair standing, so the positive doctrine cannot be metaethics alone. GS 276 answers despair by changing what is valued, in four withdrawals and a last-sentence Yes, and *Twilight* names the stance Dionysian; the answer is independent of the disorientation branch and leaves a test and a content unsupplied. The answer's price is the revaluation of suffering, derived from A §2 and the three conditions: suffering as a constituent of happiness, the counter-ideal's meaning for it "resistance overcome" against the ideal's "guilt", pity its practical opposite, with the extension to pain from outside recorded as open. And the nihilist misreading fails because it takes the disorientation branch's premise for the despair branch's verdict.

What stands open is the test. GS 276 says "some day" and gives no way to tell whether one has become a Yes-sayer, and nothing in it makes the whole of a life, rather than a moment or a year, the object of the Yes. Sixty-five sections later a demon poses the question that does. <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs GS 341 and its three stagings in *Zarathustra* as that test, and evaluates the cosmological, practical and literary readings of it by what each needs the text to say.

GM III:28: the ascetic ideal "closed the door to all suicidal nihilism"; the section's charge against the ideal is the price, guilt, not the closing, so the author is on the side of the door being shut. *The Antichrist* §7: "pity is the practice of nihilism", "pity persuades to nothingness"; the word is the name of what is wrong with the morality he attacks, and a thinker who held that nothing matters would not have a charge to make.

**Where the claim fails.** It takes a premise for a conclusion. Nietzsche holds that values have no objective standing (GS 301); "nothing matters" follows only with the further premise that a value binds only if it is objective, and that premise, normative objectivism, is the nihilist's, which Nietzsche rejects and GS 346 puts as a question that Book IV had already answered. The claim also collapses the two branches: the verdict "nothing matters" belongs to the despair branch as its felt form, and that branch is the one Nietzsche diagnoses and answers.

**What a defender can cite.** (a) "Everything is empty, everything is the same, everything was." It is the soothsayer's, a character's; the dramatization rule cites a speech for what it stages, and the book stages Zarathustra becoming like the soothsayer, dreaming the graveyard, and answering with "a sea in which he can drown"; in Part IV the soothsayer is a guest and "proclaimer of the great weariness". The line is the disease staged, with the physician present. (b) "Everything is false", and the notebook definition by the devaluation of values. Both are Nachlass; the published-works rule lets a notebook corroborate a published claim and never ground one, and no published sentence calls nihilism the devaluation of values or endorses "everything is false". (c) GS 301 itself. It is P2 of the disorientation argument, which Nietzsche holds, and it is nihilism only with P1, which he denies; the split is what shows that a premise of the left branch is not a verdict on either.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Derive "the lack of suffering implies the lack of true happiness" from the three conditions of the will to power and A §2's definition of happiness, stating each step and where BGE 13's anti-preservation premise enters. Then name the premise the derivation needs about what happiness is, and state the counterexample McCloughan's review raises against the reading, saying what the derivation does and does not establish once the counterexample is granted.

<Solution>
**The derivation.**

*Step 1 (A §2).* Happiness is "the feeling that power increases, that a resistance is overcome": the will to power satisfied, which requires (i) a determinate end, (ii) resistance to it, (iii) the resistance overcome.

*Step 2 (BGE 13).* A living thing "wants above all to discharge its strength"; self-preservation is a by-product. This is where the anti-preservation premise enters: the being is not seeking a state of rest in which nothing more is wanted, so the pendulum's boredom end is not its goal, and condition (i) is always renewed. Without BGE 13 one could hold A §2's definition and still take the ideal to be a final satisfaction after which the will stops; BGE 13 rules that out.

*Step 3.* Condition (ii) is necessary: no resistance, no overcoming, no increase of power. A won game cannot be won again for the same satisfaction.

*Step 4 (Schopenhauer's definition, kept).* Suffering is the felt form of a desire held and not yet satisfied: the will's hindrance by an obstacle. So resistance in condition (ii), from the inside, is suffering.

*Step 5.* Since the activity that is happiness is the overcoming *of* the resistance, the suffering is not a stage before the happiness but part of what the activity consists in: a constituent, not a precondition.

*C.* A life with no suffering in this sense meets neither (ii) nor (iii), and has no happiness in A §2's sense; hence the inversion, with "true" marking the A §2 sense against contentment. GS 338's twins, happiness and unhappiness that "grow up together or … stay small together", is the published statement.

**The premise the derivation needs.** That happiness *is* the resistance kind, A §2's definition taken as the account of the good. The definition is asserted, not argued, and the will-to-power lesson marked it as the is–ought step. Grant it, and the inversion follows; deny it, and the derivation shows only what one kind of satisfaction requires.

**The counterexample.** Not all suffering is a held desire's resistance. Illness, bereavement, the suffering that arrives from outside and spawns no project, is pain in the plain sense, and it is not a constituent of any overcoming; McCloughan's review presses that much suffering could not be affirmed in the way the reading needs, and Reginster concedes that "much unexpected pain and suffering might actually undermine whatever prospect for greatness I might have had". Reginster's extension to pain, as a stimulant that spawns desires and so gives the will something to overcome, is weaker than the derivation and he says so.

**What survives.** The derivation establishes that the presence of suffering in a life is not as such an objection to it, and that an ideal of a life without suffering is an ideal of a life without the good in A §2's sense; the last human being who "invented happiness" is on the wrong side. It does not establish that every suffering is affirmable, and affirmation as P2 of the previous reconstruction, which values this life "with its necessity included", needs that stronger claim for the sufferings that are necessary and not resistance. The gap is the reading's open fault line, and the course records it as open.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Boundary probe. Stipulate a person whose highest value, in Reginster's two-feature sense, is fully realized in this world: what they expected, they have, and it conditions the rest of their goods, which prosper. State whether, on the split, this person can be a nihilist, which branch could still apply and on what premises, and say what the case shows about the independence of the two problems and about the order between them.

<Solution>
**Despair cannot apply.** Despair is the conviction that the highest values cannot be realized in this world; the stipulation makes them realized. Neither feature of a highest value is disappointed: the expected good is present, and the conditioned goods prosper. The soothsayer's "everything was" has no purchase, since what was harvested has not rotted.

**Disorientation can.** Nothing in the stipulation says the person's values have objective standing, and nothing says the person has dropped normative objectivism. Suppose both P1 and P2 of the disorientation argument hold for them: they believe a value binds only if it is a fact, and they come to believe that no value is a fact. Then their realized highest value loses its authority, and with it its power to make the life meaningful, even while it is fully realized: the friendships are there and no longer count as anything. That is GS 346's first horn, abolishing one's reverences, felt as loss because of the need GS 1 names.

**What the case shows.** The two problems are independent in both directions: the earlier figure showed that a value with no standing cannot be despaired of (no disorientation-free despair), and this case shows that a value fully realized can be disoriented (no realization-proof disorientation). The order is not symmetric, though. A disoriented person with a realized value has nothing to grieve *about the world*, and their remedy is metaethical: drop P1, and the value binds again, realized. A despairing person's remedy is not metaethical, since restoring the value's authority restores the despair. So the case confirms the precedence: disorientation is settled first, and settling it in the nihilist's favour, by keeping P1, dissolves despair only by dissolving every value, which is why Nietzsche does not settle it that way.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Corroboration. Take GS 346 ("our question mark", 1887) and GM III:28 (1887) as two published statements about nihilism. For each: give the metadata (book, date, the voice and audience); state what it records, what it argues, and what it omits; classify it as disorientation or despair by its sentences, quoting the deciding phrase; and then report whether either states the notebook definition (NF-1887,9[35], WP 2: "the uppermost values devaluate themselves; the aim is lacking; 'why?' finds no answer") in so many words, and what the nearest published sentence is.

<Solution>
**GS 346.** Book V of *The Gay Science*, written for the second edition of 1887, in the author's first person plural ("we godless ones") addressed to "my curious gentlemen" who do not yet understand. It *records* a discovery: "that the world is not worth what we believed, that is about the surest thing our mistrust has finally got hold of", and the refusal to say it is worth *less*, since inventing values that "outrank the value of the actual world" is now seen as "an extravagant aberration of human vanity". It *argues*, from that refusal, a suspicion: that in laughing at "man and world" the godless have taken "a step further in contempt for man", and so stand before an either/or. It *omits* any answer to its own question mark, and it omits the mechanism of the mistrust, which GS 357 and III:27 supply. **Classification:** both branches, distinguished by mood. "Abolish yourselves" is the despair horn, stated as certain: "the latter would be nihilism". "Abolish your reverences" is the disorientation horn, stated as a question: "but would not the former also be nihilism?" The section is the one published text that names the disorientation branch, and it names it interrogatively.

**GM III:28.** The last section of the Third Treatise, 1887, in the author's voice, summing up for the reader the treatise has just moved inside the ideal. It *records* the fact the ideal answered: "man does not deny suffering as such … provided one shows him a meaning for it". It *argues* that the ideal's meaning, guilt, saved the will at the price of a will to nothingness, and that "man would rather will nothingness than not will". It *omits* the counter-ideal, which *Ecce Homo* says "was lacking, until Zarathustra". **Classification:** despair, by every sentence: "his existence on earth contained no goal", "in vain", "the tremendous void", "suicidal nihilism"; the values' standing is never in question, and the section's charge is that they worked.

**The notebook definition.** Neither section states it in so many words, and no published text does. The second clause of WP 2 has a near-verbatim published counterpart in III:28: "'why man at all?' was a question without an answer" (*eine Frage ohne Antwort*), and "his existence on earth contained no goal" (*enthielt kein Ziel*) for "the aim is lacking". The first clause, "the uppermost values devaluate themselves", has none: the nearest is GS 346's question whether abolishing one's reverences would be nihilism, which is not an assertion. **Verdict.** The published works state nihilism in the despair form, a missing goal and an unanswered what-for, and the devaluation form is a notebook's; a reading that defines nihilism by the notebook's first clause has, under the published-works rule, corroboration for a claim the books do not make.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Map "The Soothsayer" (Z II 19) onto GM III:28 line by line: "everything is empty", "everything is the same", "everything was", "all work was for naught", "the best became weary of their works", "too weary to die", and the graveyard dream with the laughing coffin. For each give the counterpart in §28 or "none". Then state what the speech adds that §28 lacks, and whether the argued works have a counterpart for the counter-image, the coffin's "thousandfold laughter".

<Solution>
**The map.** "Everything was": §28's refrain "in vain!" behind every human destiny, the achievements not carrying forward. "Everything is empty": "the tremendous void" (*die ungeheure Leere*), "the will for man and earth was lacking". "Everything is the same": no single counterpart; the clause is ambiguous between despair and indifference, and §28's "any meaning is better than no meaning" is the nearest, since it treats the sameness of all meanings as the void's own form. "All work was for naught": "in vain!" again, and "his existence on earth contained no goal". "The best became weary of their works": §28's "he suffered from the problem of his meaning", a relation to works still held. "Too weary to die, we live on in burial chambers": "the door was closed to all suicidal nihilism", and "man would rather will nothingness than not will", a will that keeps going with nothingness for an aim. The graveyard dream: none as image; its content, "I had renounced all life", a "guardian of graves" among "symbols of conquest", is the will to nothingness of §28 in the first person. The laughing coffin: none.

**What the speech adds.** An emotional register: §28 describes a condition, the speech has it transform a character, three days without food, speech lost; the reader is shown despair as an illness that takes a physician. A first person: Zarathustra "became like those of whom the soothsayer had spoken", so the diagnostician is inside the diagnosis, as the tarantulas' bite and "On the Hinterworldly" already did. And the counter-image: the coffin that bursts with "thousandfold laughter … of children, angels, owls, fools and butterflies", which the disciple reads as Zarathustra himself, "the coffin full of colorful sarcasms", and which Zarathustra declines to confirm, shaking his head at the interpreter. The counter-image is unexplained in the speech.

**Whether the argued works have it.** Not as an image and not as an argument. §28 ends on the commitment to a counter-ideal and supplies none; *Ecce Homo* says the counter-ideal "was lacking, until Zarathustra". The nearest argued statement of the counter-image's content is GS 276's "some day only a Yes-sayer" and *Twilight*'s "the eternal joy of becoming itself", which say what the laughter would be a laughter *at*, without the laughter. The dramatization rule files the coffin under adds: the book stages the answer to despair as an image before the argued works state it as a stance, and a staged image is cited for what it stages, not as the stance's argument.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Forward. GS 276 says "some day I want to be only a Yes-sayer" and gives no way to tell whether one has become one. Write, in two or three sentences, what a test of affirmation would need: what it must make the object of the Yes, and why a Yes to a moment or a year does not suffice. Then find the section of *The Gay Science* sixty-five sections later whose demon poses that test, state its question in one sentence, and say what it adds to GS 276.

<Solution>
**What a test needs.** The Yes of GS 276 is to "the necessary in things", and the necessary is not a moment but the whole in which each moment is bound to every other; a test has to make the *whole life*, with everything in it in its order, the object of a single Yes, so that a Yes to any part is a Yes to the parts it required. A Yes to a moment can be bought by forgetting what it cost, and a Yes to a year by looking away from the rest; "looking away shall be my only negation" is exactly the move a test must close off, since a life affirmed by looking away from half of it is a life affirmed by concealment, the sham form *Ecce Homo* II 10 excludes. The test must therefore forbid selection: same life, every pain and every joy, in the same sequence, and ask whether the Yes survives.

**The section.** GS 341, "Das grösste Schwergewicht", the greatest weight: a demon steals into your loneliest loneliness and says that "this life, as you now live it and have lived it, you will have to live once more and innumerable times more; and there will be nothing new in it, but every pain and every joy and every thought and sigh and everything unspeakably small and great in your life must come to you again, and all in the same series and sequence"; then the question, whether you would curse the demon or answer "you are a god and never have I heard anything more divine", and the last sentence: "how well disposed would you have to become to yourself and to life to *long for nothing more* than this ultimate eternal confirmation and seal?"

**What it adds.** Everything a test needs and GS 276 lacks: the whole life as the object, sameness and sequence to forbid selection, eternity to forbid the thought that once was enough, and a question in the conditional rather than a wish, so that the answer is something a reader can fail. It also adds the form Janaway reads it in: not an imperative but a description of "what a certain kind of greatness would consist in", by question and conditional, like asking what a runner would have to be to win a marathon ten times running. <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">The next lesson</Ref> is that test, reconstructed from GS 341 and its three stagings in *Zarathustra*, and the reading on which its force is the question and not the cosmology.
</Solution>

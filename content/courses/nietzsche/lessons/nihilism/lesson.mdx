When a culture stops believing in God, why should the values that belief held up turn against the people who still hold them, instead of standing on their own or quietly lapsing?

<Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="the-argument-in-five-steps">The death of God</Ref> ended in exactly this situation. Its argument ran from a discredited belief to the conclusion that "the whole of our European morality" had lost its foundation, and the atheists in the market laughed, sure that nothing much followed. Their expectation is the obvious one. A value whose support has gone can stand on its own: kindness seemed good before anyone traced it to God and can seem good after. Or it can lapse quietly, the way a rule of etiquette lapses once nobody remembers what it was for, and people feel freer. Either way the people who held the values lose a reason, not their footing.

Nietzsche expected neither. In 1887 he forecast despair and destruction for the next two centuries of Europe. He called the process *nihilism*, a word that usually describes a person: someone who believes in nothing, holds no principles, shrugs at good and evil. He used it for something happening to a whole culture, and he dated it: it is "standing at the gate", he wrote in an unpublished note of 1885–86, the "uncanniest of guests". Before the forecast can make sense, two questions the death-of-God argument left open need answers. Who killed God? The madman said "we", and never said with what. And what in the values themselves could turn their loss into an attack on the people who hold them?

## The killer was a Christian virtue

Who, then, did the killing? The madman's "you and I" points at the people in the market, and <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="weight-without-truth">the earlier lesson</Ref> identified them as a culture that learned to explain its own religion: historians, comparative scholars, psychologists. That answer names the agents and leaves the motive open. Why would a Christian culture do this to its own faith?

### Two thousand years of training in truthfulness

In 1887 Nietzsche added a fifth book to *The Gay Science* (GS), and in it he answered:

<Quote source="GS 357, trans. W. Kaufmann">
<Original lang="de">
Man sieht, was eigentlich über den christlichen Gott gesiegt hat: die christliche Moralität selbst, der immer strenger genommene Begriff der Wahrhaftigkeit, die Beichtväter-Feinheit des christlichen Gewissens, übersetzt und sublimiert zum wissenschaftlichen Gewissen, zur intellektuellen Sauberkeit um jeden Preis.
</Original>

You see what it was that really triumphed over the Christian god: Christian morality itself, the concept of truthfulness that was understood ever more rigorously, the father confessor's refinement of the Christian conscience, translated and sublimated into a scientific conscience, into intellectual cleanliness at any price. Looking at nature as if it were proof of the goodness and governance of a god; interpreting history in honor of some divine reason, as a continual testimony of a moral world order and ultimate moral purposes; interpreting one's own experiences as pious people have long enough interpreted theirs, as if everything were providential, a hint, designed and ordained for the sake of the salvation of the soul — that is all over now, that has man's conscience against it, that is considered indecent and dishonest by every more refined conscience — mendaciousness, feminism, weakness, and cowardice.
</Quote>

*Wahrhaftigkeit*, truthfulness, is a moral demand, not a theory of knowledge: the duty not to lie, to others or to oneself. Christianity taught it with unusual force. Lying is a sin; God sees the heart, so there is no hiding from him; confession requires a person to examine his own conscience for the motives he would rather not see. Kept up for centuries, that discipline became a habit of mind, and the habit outgrew the confessional. A conscience trained to refuse comfortable lies about itself learned to refuse them about nature and history too. The same section calls atheism "the most fateful act of two thousand years of discipline for truth that in the end forbids itself the lie in faith in God". ("Feminism" in this list renders *Feminismus*, which here means effeminacy: a womanish giving way to what one would like to believe, ranked with weakness and cowardice as what a conscience trained to truthfulness despises.)

So the madman's killers acted from a Christian motive. Their tools were history and psychology, but what made them use the tools against their own faith was the conscience that faith had trained.

### From the dogma to the morality

GS 357 explains the death of God. Later in 1887, at the end of *On the Genealogy of Morals* (GM), Nietzsche drew the next inference:

<Quote source="GM III 27, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
All great things bring about their own destruction through an act of self-overcoming: thus the law of life will have it. … In this way Christianity as a dogma was destroyed by its own morality; in the same way Christianity as morality must now perish, too: we stand on the threshold of this event. After Christian truthfulness has drawn one inference after another, it must end by drawing its most striking inference, its inference against itself; this will happen, however, when it poses the question "what is the meaning of all will to truth?"
</Quote>

The *dogma* is what Christianity says is the case: that God exists, that the world has a moral order, that the soul lives on. The *morality* is what it says one should do and value: love of neighbour, humility, pity, chastity, the confession of guilt. Truthfulness destroyed the first. It has not finished, because the morality still makes claims of its own (that pity is good, that the meek are blessed, that every soul has equal worth), and truthfulness will ask of each of them what it asked of the dogma: what grounds it, and what need it serves. Last of all it will ask that question about itself.

This explains a fact the death-of-God argument only recorded. In step 5 of that argument most people keep the morality and drop the God (<Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="the-shadow-and-the-starlight">the shadow and the starlight</Ref>). The shadow cannot last indefinitely, because the virtue that killed the God is part of the morality that survives him, and it is still at work.

<KeyFact>
One would expect a faith to be destroyed by its enemies. On Nietzsche's account Christianity was destroyed by its most faithful practitioners, with a virtue it had taught them, so the more seriously a person holds its values, the harder truthfulness turns against them. That is what "the highest values devalue themselves" means.
</KeyFact>

### The note from Lenzer Heide: a need that has become a need for the untrue

Nietzsche worked out what this does to the people who live through it in a long note dated "Lenzer Heide, 10 June 1887". Lenzerheide is a mountain village in eastern Switzerland, on the road from Chur toward the high valley where he spent his summers. The note is titled "European Nihilism", runs to fifteen numbered paragraphs, and was never published. By the ranking of <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="ranking-the-texts-and-citing-them">the first lesson</Ref> it is a draft, so it can elaborate what the published books say, never replace it; its first two paragraphs restate GS 357 and GM III 27, written the same year.

  caption="The Lenzer Heide note in five groups. The amber groups describe the crisis; the grey one concerns eternal recurrence, the thought of existence repeating itself endlessly, without a goal."

The first paragraph lists what the "Christian moral hypothesis" gave people:

<Quote source="Notebook 5[71] §1, Lenzer Heide, 10 June 1887, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge">
1. It endowed man with an absolute value, in contrast to his smallness and contingency in the flux of becoming and passing away.
2. It served the advocates of God by conceding to the world, despite suffering and evil, the character of perfection, including that 'freedom' — evil seemed full of meaning.
3. It posited that man knows about absolute values, thus giving him adequate knowledge precisely of what is most important.

It shielded man from despising himself as man, from taking sides against life, from despairing of knowledge: it was a means of preservation — in sum, morality was the great antidote to practical and theoretical nihilism.
</Quote>

The last sentence matters most. Its two kinds of nihilism come from its own list: practical nihilism is despising oneself and taking sides against life, theoretical nihilism is despairing of knowledge. Christianity was itself a cure, and what it cured was a nihilism older than itself, taking the word for now in its plainest sense: the threat that a small creature in a world of change and suffering would find nothing worth living for. It is the threat Silenus voiced in <Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-wisdom-of-silenus">the wisdom of Silenus</Ref>, best never to be born. The Greeks of *The Birth of Tragedy* answered it with art; Europe answered it with God. The second paragraph says what happens when the cure's own ingredient dissolves it:

<Quote source="Notebook 5[71] §2, Lenzer Heide, 10 June 1887, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge">
But among the forces that morality cultivated was truthfulness: this, in the end, turns against morality, discovers its teleology, the partiality of its viewpoint — and now the insight into this long-ingrained mendacity, which one despairs of ever shedding from oneself, is what acts as a stimulus: a stimulus to nihilism. We notice needs in ourselves, planted there by the long-held moral interpretation, which now appear to us as needs for the untrue; on the other hand, it is these needs on which depends the value for whose sake we endure living. This antagonism — not esteeming what we know and no longer being permitted to esteem what we would like to pretend to ourselves — results in a process of dissolution.
</Quote>

Take a believer who has learned to bear a long illness as a trial sent for the good of his soul. The interpretation gave him a need: that his suffering mean something. Truthfulness now tells him the interpretation was invented, which leaves the need in place and forbids the only thing that satisfied it. He cannot go back, because his conscience will not let him believe what he now thinks false. He cannot go forward, because the need does not go away when its object does. That bind, multiplied across a culture, is the "process of dissolution".

## Nihilism is a condition, not an opinion

The process now needs its name, and the name invites a misreading. In a notebook of autumn 1887 Nietzsche defined it in three lines:

<Quote source="Notebook 9[35], autumn 1887, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge">
Nihilism: the goal is lacking; an answer to the 'Why?' is lacking. What does nihilism mean? That the highest values are devaluated.
</Quote>

### What "highest values" are

A culture's *highest values* are the ones that give the others their point. Work, family, learning and kindness each had value in Christian Europe, but the answer to "why do any of it?" ran up to something above them: the salvation of the soul, the absolute worth of every person before God, the truth that was God's. The Lenzer Heide list is a list of exactly these. When they lose their standing, the lower goods are not refuted one by one; they lose their "why", the way a career loses its point for someone who has stopped believing in the firm it serves. That is what "the goal is lacking" means.

<Remark title="Nihilism">
*Nihilism*, in Nietzsche's sense, is the condition of a culture whose highest values have been devalued: they have lost their standing, or they are still held and judged to be unrealizable in the only world there is, so that the question "why?" has no answer.
</Remark>

### Refuting the slogan: "the nihilist believes that nothing matters"

The definition makes nihilism something that happens to people who still care. The popular sense says the opposite: a nihilist is a person who holds that nothing has value. That sense came to Nietzsche's readers from Russia. Ivan Turgenev's novel *Fathers and Sons* (1862) made "nihilist" the name of a young man who accepts no principle on authority, and by the 1880s the word meant the Russian revolutionaries who assassinated Tsar Alexander II with a bomb in 1881.

<Figure src="./figures/russian-nihilists-1881.jpg" float="right" width="m" alt="A 19th-century wood engraving of a snowy embankment lined with bare trees. A huge white burst of smoke and flying debris fills the centre and right of the picture, with people silhouetted against it, arms flung up. To the left, a closed carriage drawn by two horses stands by a wall while mounted escorts rear and scatter and bodies lie in the snow. A long ornamental iron railing runs across the foreground along the canal." caption="St Petersburg, 13 March 1881: the second bomb explodes on the Catherine Canal embankment beside the Tsar's carriage. This is what newspaper readers of the 1880s pictured when they read 'nihilist'." />

Nietzsche read those Russians differently from their admirers and their enemies. What he saw in "nihilism à la Petersburg" was "the belief in unbelief even to the point of martyrdom" (GS 347), and he grouped it with patriotism and petty aesthetic creeds as symptoms of "the need for a faith, a support, backbone, something to fall back on". People who die for a cause have not stopped valuing.

The slogan fails at one step. If nothing mattered to the nihilist, losing the highest values would cost him nothing, and the fitting response would be indifference: there is nothing to mourn in the loss of what was never worth anything. Nietzsche's nihilists are never indifferent. In the fourth part of *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (Z), the prophet's shadow, who has followed him into every forbidden place, reports the slogan and then grieves:

<Quote source="Z IV 9, 'The Shadow', trans. W. Kaufmann">
'Nothing is true, all is permitted': thus I spoke to myself. Into the coldest waters I plunged, with head and heart. … Alas, where has all that is good gone from me — and all shame, and all faith in those who are good? … Do I have a goal any more? A haven toward which my sail is set? … O eternal everywhere, O eternal nowhere, O eternal — in vain!
</Quote>

"Nothing is true, all is permitted" is the shadow's line, spoken in despair, and Nietzsche gives it to a character, not to himself. The lament that follows is the voice of someone whose values are still in force: he wants a goal, a haven, the good he lost. A note of the winter of 1887–88 names the state exactly: the nihilist "cannot endure this world which one yet does not want to deny" (notebook 11[99], November 1887–March 1888, unpublished). The nihilist is defined by what he still demands.

## Why the loss is a catastrophe: the measure outlives its world

That answers the death-of-God lesson's second question in outline: the loss hurts because the values still bind. It does not yet say why the binding values turn against the world instead of simply going slack. Two different things can happen to a person whose highest values lose their ground.

### Two ways to lose a value: disorientation and despair

The first is the one the madman describes. The standard by which higher and lower were measured has gone, and with it the ranking: "Is there still any up or down? Are we not straying as through an infinite nothing?" (GS 125). Call this *disorientation*: the values no longer bind, and there is nothing left to rank by.

The second is worse. The Lenzer Heide note describes it:

<Quote source="Notebook 5[71] §§4–5, Lenzer Heide, 10 June 1887, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge">
However, an extreme position is replaced not by a moderated one but by a new extreme one, its converse. Thus belief in the absolute immorality of nature, in the absence of purpose and meaning, becomes the psychologically necessary affect once belief in God and an essentially moral order can no longer be sustained. … One interpretation has perished; but because it was regarded as *the* interpretation, there now seems to be no meaning at all in existence, everything seems to be in vain.

It remains to be shown that this 'In vain!' constitutes the character of our present nihilism. … Continuing with an 'In vain', without aim and purpose, is the most paralysing thought, especially when one realises one's being fooled and yet has no power to prevent oneself being fooled.
</Quote>

Here the values still bind. The person still measures the world by them, finds that the world contains none of what they promised, and concludes that existence is meaningless. Call this *despair*: the values are still held, and judged unrealizable. "In vain!" is not the cry of someone without standards. It is the cry of someone whose standard is intact and whose world has failed it.

Nietzsche states the choice in a published section of 1887, speaking for people who might call themselves "godless, or unbelievers, or perhaps immoralists" and who know that "the world in which we live is ungodly, immoral, 'inhuman'":

<Quote source="GS 346, trans. W. Kaufmann">
<Original lang="de">
»entweder schafft eure Verehrungen ab oder – euch selbst!« Das letztere wäre der Nihilismus; aber wäre nicht auch das erstere – der Nihilismus? – Dies ist unser Fragezeichen.
</Original>

… the terrifying Either/Or: "Either abolish your reverences or — yourselves!" The latter would be nihilism; but would not the former also be — nihilism? — This is our question mark.
</Quote>

Abolishing yourselves, keeping the reverences and condemning the life that cannot meet them, is despair carried to its end. Abolishing the reverences, giving up the values that "perhaps made it possible for us to endure life", is disorientation. Nietzsche calls both nihilism and does not choose, because both horns share an assumption: that the old reverences are the only possible measure. His question mark leaves room for a third possibility, judging by a new one.

### The hidden premise: values that measured this world by another

Why should a culture fall into despair instead of mere disorientation? The obvious answer is that the values were good, and losing good things hurts. Nietzsche cannot give that answer, because he thinks these values were bad for the people who held them. And an ordinary bad value is a relief to lose. Take a rule that forbids meat on Fridays on God's authority: when the authority goes, the rule has nothing behind it, it lapses, and people eat what they like. That is the expectation <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="the-argument-in-five-steps">the argument in five steps</Ref> leaves behind, and it points away from despair. So the question becomes what kind of bad value survives the loss of its ground and goes on hurting.

Nietzsche's word for the kind he means is *hostile to life*, and it has a definite sense. A value is hostile to life when it asks of this world what only another world could give (a life without suffering, an end that the whole of history is heading for, something that never passes away) and so ranks this world by how far it falls short. A note of the following winter states the demand and what became of it:

<Quote source="Notebook 11[99], 'Critique of nihilism', November 1887–March 1888, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge">
In short: the categories 'purpose', 'unity', 'being', by means of which we put a value into the world, we now extract again — and now the world looks valueless … Result: belief in the categories of reason is the cause of nihilism — we have measured the value of the world against categories that refer to a purely invented world.
</Quote>

Purpose, unity and being are what the world was required to have: a goal it is heading for, a whole in which every part has its place, something lasting beneath the change. The world of experience shows none of them, so they were located in an invented world beyond it, and this world was valued by how far it shared in them. The Christian values are cases of that demand. The soul was ranked above the body, the eternal above what passes, a hereafter without suffering above a life full of it, the selfless deed above the self that does it.

Now compare such a value with the Friday rule when the ground goes. The Friday rule was a command, and God was its source: remove the source and nothing is left to obey. A life-hostile value is not a command but a standard, a picture of what a world worth having would contain, and God was not its source but the place where the standard was met. Truthfulness removes the belief that the standard is met anywhere. It does not remove the standard, because the standard was never derived from that belief. The order ran the other way, as 11[99] says: the demand for purpose, unity and being came first, and the invented world was posited to meet it. And the needs the moral interpretation planted stay planted, as the believer with the long illness showed under Lenzer Heide §2. So the measure survives its world, the only world left is measured by the yardstick of one that does not exist, and it fails, necessarily. The failure reads as a verdict on existence. That is why the collapse brings no relief.

This is the second premise the death-of-God argument lacked; without it, the death of God leaves the values merely ungrounded, a loss of footing that can be survived. Nietzsche put the point in the *Genealogy*: the reigning ideal had laid a "curse" upon reality, and nihilism, "the great nausea, the will to nothingness", was what "was bound to grow out of it" (GM II 24).

The case had already happened once, in Schopenhauer. GS 357 goes on to say that as soon as the Christian interpretation is rejected, "Schopenhauer's question immediately comes to us in a terrifying way: *Has existence any meaning at all?*" Schopenhauer was, Nietzsche says, Germany's first "admitted and inexorable atheist", and yet his answer remained "stuck in precisely those Christian-ascetic moral perspectives in which one had renounced faith along with the faith in God". <Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-verdict-and-the-way-out">The verdict, and the way out</Ref> found that his verdict on life needed a standard the pendulum never stated. Here is where that standard came from: a life without pain, and salvation by the denial of the will, are the Christian ideal of the saint with the God taken out. Schopenhauer is the despairing face of nihilism in a single mind. His *pessimism* was a verdict on life; nihilism is the condition of a culture in which that verdict comes to seem forced.

<KeyFact>
Premise 2 carries the whole account, and nothing so far shows it true. That the highest values were life-negating is a claim about what they were made for and by whom, and Nietzsche's evidence for it is his genealogy of the ascetic ideal (<Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the ascetic priest</Ref>).
</KeyFact>

### The antidote was itself a will to nothingness

The last step explains why the values devalue *themselves* in a second sense. The *Genealogy* ends by saying what the Christian-ascetic ideal had done for human beings:

<Quote source="GM III 28, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
Man, the bravest of animals and the one most accustomed to suffering, does not repudiate suffering as such; he desires it, he even seeks it out, provided he is shown a meaning for it, a purpose of suffering. The meaninglessness of suffering, not suffering itself, was the curse that lay over mankind so far — and the ascetic ideal offered man meaning! … the door was closed to any kind of suicidal nihilism. … We can no longer conceal from ourselves what is expressed by all that willing which has taken its direction from the ascetic ideal: this hatred of the human, and even more of the animal, and more still of the material, this horror of the senses, … this longing to get away from all appearance, change, becoming, death, wishing, from longing itself — all this means … a will to nothingness, an aversion to life, a rebellion against the most fundamental presuppositions of life; but it is and remains a will! … man would rather will nothingness than not will.
</Quote>

The antidote worked by giving suffering a meaning, and the meaning was hostile to life: it taught people to want the eternal against the passing, the spirit against the body, the beyond against the here. So the cure for the first nihilism was a nihilism in disguise. In *The Antichrist* (A, 1888) Nietzsche names the disguise: one "does not say 'nothingness' but 'beyond' or 'God,' or 'true life'" (A 7). The collapse does not create the life-negation; it uncovers it.

## The strongest objection: lower the bar

A secular humanist has a reply ready, and it is the best objection to the whole account. Grant that the values have lost their ground. It does not follow that they condemn anything. One can keep justice, kindness and truthfulness as ideals one *aspires* to without *expecting* the world to realize them. An unfulfilled aspiration leaves a life less good than it might have been, not worthless: someone who hopes for deep friendships and does not find them has a reason to regret, not a reason to prefer not to exist. Only a disappointed expectation turns the absence of a good into an evil. So moderate the values, hold them as aspirations, and no "In vain!" follows.

### The reply: whose values, and what they were for

Nietzsche's texts answer in three steps. The first is in Lenzer Heide §2, already quoted: the needs the moral interpretation planted are needs "on which depends the value for whose sake we endure living". A need is not a policy and cannot be moderated by deciding to hold it more loosely. And §4 adds a claim about how such needs behave when their object is taken away: "an extreme position is replaced not by a moderated one but by a new extreme one, its converse". The objection describes the outcome a calm philosopher would choose; Nietzsche predicts what a culture will actually do.

The second step is GM III 28: what human beings could not bear was meaningless suffering. An aspiration to kindness gives the suffering of a cancer ward no meaning. The objection leaves the old wound open, and the ascetic ideal had been the only thing that closed it.

The third step is the hardest, and the note gives it at full length. It asks *whose* values these were:

<Quote source="Notebook 5[71] §§9–12, Lenzer Heide, 10 June 1887, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge">
9. Now, morality protected life from despair and from the plunge into nothingness for those men and classes who were violated and oppressed by men: for powerlessness against men, not powerlessness against nature, is what engenders the most desperate bitterness against existence. Morality treated the despots, the men of violence, the 'masters' in general, as the enemies against whom the common man must be protected, i.e., first of all encouraged, strengthened. Consequently, morality taught the deepest hatred and contempt for what is the rulers' fundamental trait: their will to power. To abolish this morality, to deny it, corrode it: that would be to imbue the most hated drive with an opposite feeling and evaluation. If the suffering, oppressed man lost his belief in being entitled to despise the will to power, he would enter the phase of hopeless desperation. This would be the case if the masters' trait were essential to life, if it turned out that even the 'will to morality' only disguised the 'will to power', that even this hating and despising is a power-will. The man oppressed would realise that he stands on the same ground as the oppressors and that he has no privilege, no higher rank than them.

10. Rather the reverse! There is nothing to life that has value except the degree of power — assuming, precisely, that life itself is the will to power. For those who have come off badly, morality provided protection from nihilism by conferring on each an infinite value, a metaphysical value, and positioning him within an order that does not coincide with the worldly order of rank and power: it taught submission, meekness, etc. If belief in this morality fell into ruin, those who come off badly would lose their consolation — and would be ruined too.

11. This ruin takes the form of — ruining oneself, as an instinctive selection of what must destroy. Symptoms of this self-destruction of those who have come off badly: self-vivisection, poisoning, intoxication, Romanticism, above all the instinctive compulsion to act in ways that make mortal enemies of the powerful (breeding one's own executioners, so to speak); the will to destruction as the will of a still deeper instinct, the instinct for self-destruction, the will into nothingness.

12. Nihilism as a symptom of the badly off having lost all consolation: that they destroy in order to be destroyed, that once separated from morality they no longer have any reason to 'submit' — that they settle on the ground of the opposite principle and themselves want to have power by forcing the powerful to become their executioners. This is the European form of Buddhism, doing No after all existence has lost its 'meaning'.
</Quote>

The words need their meanings. "Will to power" is Nietzsche's name for the drive to grow and overcome resistance, which <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">will to power as psychology</Ref> explains; here it is enough that the "masters" are the type in whom that drive is undisguised, not a social class with a programme. "Those who have come off badly" (*die Schlechtweggekommenen*) are, the note says two paragraphs later, "above all physiologically" badly off, "no longer politically": the sickly, the weak, the failures of every class. "The European form of Buddhism" is his standing name for a weary No to existence, not for the Asian religion.

Read so, the passage makes three claims, and none of them is softened by context. Christian morality was, among other things, a consolation for the powerless: it gave each of them an infinite worth in an order above the worldly order of rank, and it licensed them to despise the strength they lacked. Life itself is will to power, and nothing in life has value except its degree. And if the consolation fails, the people it consoled will not moderate their values; they will turn on themselves and on the powerful, destroying "in order to be destroyed". The passage shows what the objection overlooks. The values were not held as optional ideals by people who could afford to lower the bar. They were held by those who needed them most, as a protection against despair, and those are exactly the people who cannot moderate them.

<KeyFact>
Nietzsche grants the objection for a few. Asked who will prove strongest in the crisis, the note answers: "The most moderate, those who have no need of extreme articles of faith, who … can think of man with a considerable moderation of his value and not therefore become small and weak" (§15). Lowering the bar is a sign of health in the exception, not a remedy a culture can adopt by argument.
</KeyFact>

The reply has a gap, and it is plain. Every step of it is a psychological claim: that needs cannot be moderated, that extremes flip into their converse, that the consoled will destroy. Nietzsche asserts these in an unpublished note without arguing them there. Their support has to come from his psychology of drives (<Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">the self as a society of drives</Ref>), and the claim that the consoled values were the values of the weak from his account of where those values came from (<Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave revolt in morality</Ref>).

## Passive, incomplete, active: three ways to live in it

A culture in this condition does not all respond alike, and Nietzsche sorted the responses in the same autumn 1887 note that defined the word. The distinction exists only in that note; the published books use its pieces without the labels.

### The two named responses

<Quote source="Notebook 9[35], autumn 1887, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge">
It is ambiguous: (A) Nihilism as a sign of the increased power of the spirit: as active nihilism. … the force of the spirit may have grown so much that the goals it has had so far ('convictions', articles of faith) are no longer appropriate … It achieves its maximum of relative force as a violent force of destruction: as active nihilism. The opposite would be the weary nihilism that no longer attacks: its most celebrated form Buddhism: as passivist nihilism. … (B) Nihilism as a decline and retreat of the spirit's power: passive nihilism: as a sign of weakness: the force of the spirit may be wearied, exhausted, so that the goals and values that have prevailed so far are no longer appropriate and are no longer believed — that the synthesis of values and goals (on which every strong culture rests) dissolves, so that the individual values wage war on each other: disintegration — that everything which revives, heals, soothes, benumbs comes to the fore in a variety of disguises: religious, or moral or political or aesthetic, etc.
</Quote>

The note uses two nearly identical words. "Passivist" appears under (A), as the name for active nihilism's opposite: the weary kind that no longer attacks. "Passive" appears under (B), as the name for the weakness that produces such weariness; below, "passive" covers both. The two named responses share a diagnosis, the old goals no longer fit, and differ in why. In *passive nihilism* the spirit has weakened below its goals. The values come apart and fight each other, and what fills the gap is whatever numbs: new religions, political creeds that promise rest, art used as an anaesthetic. Zarathustra hears its voice in a soothsayer who preaches "All is empty, all is the same, all has been!" (Z II 19). In *active nihilism* the spirit has grown past its goals, and it does not wait for what has lost its standing to fall. A note of that winter (unpublished, November 1887–March 1888) is blunt about what that means: "Nihilism is the state of strong spirits and wills: and for these it's not possible to stop at the No 'of judgement' — the No of the deed springs from their nature. An-nihil-ation by the judgement is seconded by annihilation by the hand." Zarathustra speaks in this voice himself: "what is falling, we should still push" (Z III 12, 20).

A gentler reading, popular since the middle of the twentieth century, takes active nihilism to be intellectual criticism only: pushing what falls means exposing a dead value for what it is. The notes do not bear the restriction out: "a violent force of destruction" and "annihilation by the hand" mean what they say. The published books do add a test that tells two kinds of destruction apart. Destruction can come from "an overflowing energy that is pregnant with future", or from "the hatred of the ill-constituted, disinherited, and underprivileged, who destroy, must destroy, because what exists, indeed all existence, all being, outrages and provokes them. To understand this feeling, consider our anarchists closely" (GS 370). The second kind is the self-destruction of Lenzer Heide §§11–12, wearing the mask of strength.

### The third response: incomplete nihilism

The 9[35] labels leave out the most common case, the one the death-of-God lesson already met: the atheists in the market, and George Eliot in Nietzsche's portrait of her, who keep the values and drop their ground (<Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="the-case-of-george-eliot">the case of George Eliot</Ref>). Nietzsche named it in another note of the same autumn:

<Quote source="Notebook 10[42], autumn 1887, unpublished, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
Main proposition. How complete nihilism is the necessary consequence of the ideals entertained hitherto. — Incomplete nihilism; its forms: we live in the midst of it. — Attempts to escape nihilism without revaluating our values so far: they produce the opposite, make the problem more acute.
</Quote>

*Incomplete nihilism* is the condition of people who have arrived in nihilism and try to get out without touching the values that produced it. Keeping the morality without its God is the plainest form, and "we live in the midst of it" places it: it is the ordinary state of the educated in 1887, not a rare case.

The note says such attempts make the problem worse, which is a stronger claim than that they put it off. It does not give the steps; they follow from what the chapter has already shown.

1. Keeping the morality keeps truthfulness, which is part of it. So the attempt does not stop the work that killed God: truthfulness goes on to ask of pity, humility and the equal worth of souls what grounds them (GM III 27). The end is not avoided.
2. Keeping the morality also keeps its measure, and with it premise 2: this world is still ranked by what only another world could supply. While the values are held as if nothing had happened, their demand on this world goes unchallenged.
3. In the meantime the needs the values planted are fed, not weaned. People go on staking on them "the value for whose sake we endure living" (Lenzer Heide §2), and no other way of valuing is learned, because the old one still seems to work.
4. When truthfulness finally reaches the morality, it finds that measure fully in force, the needs at full strength, and nothing prepared to replace it. The collapse comes later and takes more with it.

That is what showing God's shadow on the cave wall costs (<Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="the-shadow-and-the-starlight">the shadow and the starlight</Ref>): the condemnation of this world is postponed, and every year of postponement adds to what it will condemn. *Complete* nihilism, by contrast, is seeing that nihilism follows necessarily from the old ideals, and drawing that consequence to the end, which is the only position from which the ideals themselves can be revalued.

### Decadence: the weakness behind passive nihilism

Passive nihilism has a cause, and Nietzsche names it with a word French critics of the 1880s had made current for over-refined, declining art: *decadence*. *The Antichrist* defines it without the art:

<Quote source="A 6, trans. W. Kaufmann">
I call an animal, a species, or an individual corrupt when it loses its instincts, when it chooses, when it prefers, what is disadvantageous for it. … Life itself is to my mind the instinct for growth, for durability, for an accumulation of forces, for power: where the will to power is lacking there is decline. It is my contention that all the supreme values of mankind lack this will — that the values which are symptomatic of decline, nihilistic values, are lording it under the holiest names.
</Quote>

*Twilight of the Idols* (TI, 1888) puts it in one line: "To have to fight the instincts — that is the formula of decadence: as long as life is ascending, happiness equals instinct" (TI, "The Problem of Socrates" 11). A decadent organism, person or culture works against its own conditions of thriving and calls that virtue. On this account nihilism is not the cause of the decline but its last consequence, "just the logic of decadence", as a note of spring 1888 says. What instincts are, and how they can work against each other inside one person, is the subject of <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">the self as a society of drives</Ref>; Nietzsche's first full diagnosis of decadence in a living artist is <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner">the case of Wagner</Ref>.

<Remark title="Decadence">
*Decadence* is Nietzsche's name for declining life: instincts that work against themselves, so that an organism, a person or a culture prefers what harms it and honours that preference with its highest names. Passive nihilism is how it looks once the old names have lost their credit.
</Remark>

### Is Nietzsche a nihilist?

His own texts press the question. Zarathustra, his spokesman, speaks with the voice of active nihilism: "what is falling, we should still push". And in a note of the winter of 1887–88, drafted as the preface to the large work he was then planning, Nietzsche describes himself in these terms:

<Quote source="Notebook 11[411], November 1887–March 1888, unpublished, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
… the first perfect nihilist of Europe who, however, has even now lived through the whole of nihilism, to the end, leaving it behind, outside himself.
</Quote>

Five forms are on the table: two states, disorientation and despair, and three responses, passive, incomplete and active nihilism. Three of the five are easy to rule out. Incomplete nihilism is the refusal to revalue the old values, and revaluing them is Nietzsche's whole proposal. Despair and passive nihilism both rest on what 9[35] calls the pathological part of nihilism: "the tremendous generalisation, the inference that there is no meaning at all". Despair draws that inference outright ("In vain!"); passive nihilism lives with it and numbs the pain of it. Nietzsche accepts the premise and refuses the inference. He agrees that there is no world behind the world, and in GS 346 he refuses to conclude that this one is worth less: "We are far from claiming that the world is worth less; indeed it would seem laughable to us today if man were to insist on inventing values that were supposed to excel the value of the actual world."

Active nihilism is the hard case, because it does not draw that inference either. It is strength, a spirit that has outgrown its goals, and Zarathustra speaks for it. But the same note says what that strength lacks. Active nihilism is "on the other hand a sign that one's strength is insufficient to productively posit for oneself a new goal, a 'Why?', a belief", and the whole condition is "a pathological intermediate state … whether because the productive forces are not yet strong enough or because decadence is still hesitating". Active nihilism is strong enough to destroy the old goals and not yet strong enough to set new ones. Its content is a No. If it stopped there, it would leave a culture with no measure at all, which is the fifth form, disorientation: the other horn of GS 346, and also nihilism.

So for Nietzsche active nihilism is a stage and a tool, not a place to stop. Its No clears away the old measure so that a new one can be set, and the new one is what he is after. Zarathustra says as much in the same speech: he pushes what is falling as "a prelude of better players", and he speaks surrounded by "broken old tablets and new tablets half covered with writing" (Z III 12, 1 and 20). The self-description in 11[411] fits this exactly. "Perfect" means complete, the opposite of the incomplete nihilism of 10[42]: he has drawn the consequence of the old ideals to the end, where incomplete nihilism flinches from it. And he has done so in order to come out the other side, "leaving it behind, outside himself".

That leaves one sense in which he is a nihilist without qualification: he denies that there is a true world behind this one, and so does the man who cries "In vain!". A note from the same autumn notebook says what decides between them:

<Quote source="Notebook 9[41], autumn 1887, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge">
… the measure of force is how far we can admit to ourselves illusoriness, the necessity of lies, without perishing. To this extent nihilism, as the denial of a true world, of a being, might be a divine way of thinking.
</Quote>

"Illusoriness, the necessity of lies" is what truthfulness finds at the end of its work: there is no true world to measure by, and the world we live in is one we have simplified in order to live in it. Nietzsche and the despairing nihilist both admit this. The note's test is what the admission does to the one who makes it. The despairing nihilist still measures by the world he has just denied, so for him the admission turns into "In vain!".

<KeyFact>
The sickness is not the belief that there is no true world; it is holding that belief while still measuring by the true world. In someone strong enough to stop measuring by it, the same belief is "a divine way of thinking". That is the sense in which "the first perfect nihilist of Europe" has left nihilism "behind, outside himself" without giving up anything he denied.
</KeyFact>

## After nihilism: revaluation, not restoration

If the condition is real, what can be done about it? The two-premise figure shows the two ways out. The first is to restore the ground, to bring God or some substitute world behind the world back. Nietzsche thinks it closed, because the truthfulness that killed God would have to be unlearned, and he reads the attempts to reopen it as symptoms: "Faith is always coveted most and needed most urgently where will is lacking" (GS 347). The second is to change the measure.

### A new standard: ascending life

A *revaluation of values* does not restore the old values or invent new ones from nothing. It judges every value by a different standard: not whether it is anchored in another world, but whether it serves ascending life or declining life, growth or exhaustion. By that standard the verdict "In vain!" loses its force, because it was pronounced by the old measure. *Twilight* shows the reversal on the case of man himself. A philosopher who judges human beings by their ideals, by what they wish to be, finds those ideals empty: "If a philosopher could be a nihilist, he would be one because he finds nothing behind all the ideals of man." His error is the one this chapter has traced, judging what is real by what is merely wished for, and the same section corrects it: "What justifies man is his reality — it will eternally justify him" (TI, "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man" 32).

"Revaluation of All Values" was the subtitle of the large work announced in GM III 27, and then the title of the four-book project of which *The Antichrist* was to be the first book (<Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="the-will-to-power-a-plan-abandoned-in-september-1888">the plan abandoned in 1888</Ref>). The *Genealogy* describes the figure who could carry it out as the opposite of the nihilist: one who will redeem reality "from the curse that the hitherto reigning ideal has laid upon it", and from "the will to nothingness, nihilism"; "this Antichrist and antinihilist; this victor over God and nothingness" (GM II 24).

<Remark title="Revaluation of all values">
The *revaluation of all values* is Nietzsche's response to nihilism: to judge every inherited value by the standard of ascending life, keeping or replacing it according to whether it serves growth or decline, instead of restoring the world behind the world that once guaranteed it.
</Remark>

### Too early, and the hardest test

Nietzsche did not think the revaluation could be done quickly. GM III 27 ends: "this is the great spectacle in a hundred acts reserved for the next two centuries in Europe — the most terrible, most questionable, and perhaps also the most hopeful of all spectacles." The madman's "I have come too early" is the same estimate in a parable: the deed is done, its consequences are generations away.

The Lenzer Heide note also names the test any revaluation must pass. Its sixth paragraph imagines "existence as it is, without meaning or goal, but inevitably recurring, without any finale into nothingness: 'eternal recurrence'", and calls it "the most extreme form of nihilism: nothingness ('meaninglessness') eternally!" For those who come off badly the thought would be "a curse" (§14). The note ends by asking how the strongest, the most moderate, would take it: "What would such a man think of eternal recurrence?" (§15). It leaves the question open. A life that could want its own endless repetition, with no goal outside it, would have stopped measuring itself by another world; <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">eternal recurrence</Ref> takes the question up.

<Conclusion>
You can now say what Nietzsche means by nihilism and why he thinks Europe's highest values devalue themselves. Christianity trained truthfulness as a duty for two thousand years; truthfulness, turned on the faith, discredited God (GS 357), and must now turn on the morality (GM III 27). Nihilism is the condition that follows: "the goal is lacking; an answer to the 'Why?' is lacking". It is not the opinion that nothing matters, since the nihilist still holds the values he has lost. It takes two forms, disorientation, where the measure goes, and despair, where the measure stays and condemns the only world left: "In vain!" Despair follows only through a second premise, that the highest values were life-negating, measuring this world by an invented one, and Nietzsche's evidence for that premise is his genealogy of the ascetic ideal. You can state the strongest objection, hold the values as aspirations and lower the bar, and Nietzsche's reply: the values were the consolation of those who most needed them, who cannot moderate them, while the few who can are the healthiest. And you can tell passive nihilism (decline, decadence, what numbs) from active (strength that destroys what is falling but cannot yet set a new goal), and both from incomplete nihilism (escapes that keep the old values and make the problem worse), and say why Nietzsche, "the first perfect nihilist of Europe", is none of them: he accepts that there is no world behind the world, refuses the inference that nothing has value, and uses active nihilism's No as a stage on the way to a new measure. What divides him from the despairing nihilist is not a belief but the strength to hold the same one without the old measure. You can also say why incomplete nihilism makes the crisis worse rather than later: it keeps the measure and feeds the needs while truthfulness goes on removing their ground. What he proposes instead is a revaluation by the standard of ascending life.

The next lesson, <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner">the case of Wagner</Ref>, applies the diagnosis to the artist Nietzsche had once hailed as tragedy reborn, and asks of his music the question GS 370 asks of all art: is it hunger or superabundance that has become creative?
</Conclusion>

GS 370 asks of every work of art and philosophy: "is it hunger or superabundance that has here become creative?" Take a work you know that offers its audience redemption: a passion setting, a novel that ends in renunciation, a film whose hero finds peace by letting go of the world. Apply the question. Name the feature of the work that decides the answer, and say what the answer would tell you about the culture that loves the work.

<Solution>
**Possible answers** differ with the work; the method is what matters.

**The deciding feature.** Ask what the work needs its audience to be. A work made from hunger needs an exhausted audience: it offers rest, release, an end to wanting, redemption from oneself, and it works only on someone who feels life as a burden to be laid down. GS 370 lists what such sufferers seek: "rest, stillness, calm seas, redemption from themselves through art and knowledge, or intoxication, convulsions, anaesthesia". A work made from superabundance can show the terrible without offering a way out of life; it gives strength and asks for it, and it does not need its audience to be tired.

**Applied.** A film whose hero finds peace by renouncing the world, and whose climax is the release itself, is on the hunger side if what the audience takes home is relief at the thought of not having to want anything any more. A tragedy that shows a hero destroyed and leaves the audience more willing to live is on the other side.

**What it says about the culture.** On the 9[35] account, a culture that loves redemption-art in large numbers is showing passive nihilism: what "revives, heals, soothes, benumbs comes to the fore" in aesthetic disguise (notebook 9[35]). The work is a symptom read backwards, from the ideal to those who need it.

This is the test <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner">the case of Wagner</Ref> applies at full length, to the composer of whom Nietzsche wrote that "someone always wants to be redeemed in his operas", and to *Parsifal* above all.
</Solution>

GM III:12 rejects the "pure, will-less, painless, timeless subject of knowledge" and replaces it with a claim about eyes: there is only a perspectival seeing, and objectivity is the ability to bring more eyes to bear, not the absence of any. This lesson reconstructs the section as an argument with a target, the ascetic ideal's epistemology, and a result, a redefinition of "objectivity" with the word in scare quotes; it then separates that result from two theses it is routinely confused with, the early claim that all truths are illusions and the claim that every view is as good as any other, and names the one problem the section leaves standing.

The course's argument arrives at §12 from inside the priest's stretch of the treatise. <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">The first ten sections</Ref> established that the ascetic ideal is a structure whose meaning is its use for a type, and that the philosopher's version of it has so far always been the priest's in disguise; <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">§§11–22 and §28</Ref> reconstructed the priest's mechanism, the redirection of "someone is to blame" onto the sufferer, and closed by noting that §12's claim about knowledge is a thesis the mechanism does not use. What this lesson adds to the spine is the reading on which that thesis belongs where it sits: the will-less knower is what the ascetic ideal looks like in epistemology, §12 argues it away as a contradiction rather than a lie, and the perspectivism it puts in its place, read as Maudemarie Clark reads it, is a rejection of foundations and of the thing-in-itself, not a denial that anything is true. <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">The next lesson</Ref> takes §§23–27, where the same critique reaches the *Genealogy*'s own will to truth.

The route: §12's place in the priest's philosophy; the argument against the eye with no direction, reconstructed; "objectivity" redefined as command of one's for and against; the falsification thesis of 1873 and Clark's reading of why the mature Nietzsche does not hold it; the relativist misreading, refuted from the section's own comparative; BGE 22's self-application and the self-refutation worry it invites; three construals of the section; and *Zarathustra*'s "On Immaculate Perception" as the same critique with a different charge.

## §12 in its place: the ascetic priest philosophizes

### The priest brought to philosophize: Vedanta and Kant's "intelligible character"

§11 ended on the priest's self-contradiction, "life against life", and §12 opens by asking what such a will does when it is "brought to philosophize". The answer is a diagnosis before it is an epistemology. An incarnate will to contradiction "will look for error precisely where the real life-instinct posits truth most unconditionally".

After the manner of the ascetics of the Vedanta philosophy, a school of Indian thought for which the manifold world is illusion, it "degrades corporeality to an illusion, likewise pain, multiplicity, the whole conceptual opposition 'subject' and 'object'", and its triumph culminates in reason's decree against itself, "there is a realm of truth and being, but reason is excluded from it". Kant's "intelligible character of things", a constitution of things of which the intellect grasps exactly this much, that it is wholly incomprehensible to the intellect, keeps "something of this lascivious ascetic duality" in it.

### The target located: the will-less subject is the priest's knower

The section's target, when it arrives, is therefore already located. The "pure, will-less, painless, timeless subject of knowledge" is the knower the ascetic priest's philosophy needs: a subject that has cut off its own conditions, the body, the affects, time, exactly as the priest's ideal cuts off life's conditions in order to preserve a declining life (<Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref>, §§11–13).

The two named ancestors are Kant's pure reason and Schopenhauer's will-less knower, the "pure, will-less subject of knowledge" of *The World as Will and Representation* I §34, whose aesthetic version §6 already read as a tortured will's relief (<Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">the previous lesson</Ref>).

From an epistemological passage one expects a theory of knowledge. §12 gives a type-diagnosis first, and only then an argument, and the order is the treatise's method applied to its own subject: the ideal of will-less knowing is explained by what it does for a type before it is refuted as a concept.

### The hinge: the priest's reversals as training

There is a sentence of gratitude between the diagnosis and the refutation, and it is where the section turns positive. "Let us, precisely as knowers, not be ungrateful toward such resolute reversals of the accustomed perspectives and valuations, with which the spirit has all too long raged against itself": to "see differently for once, to want to see differently, is no small discipline and preparation of the intellect for its eventual 'objectivity'". The priest's reversals are useless as doctrine and useful as training. That sentence is the hinge, and the rest of the section says what the training is for.

<KeyFact>
Because §12 treats will-less knowing as a type's need before it treats it as a failed concept, the treatise has committed itself to asking the same question of its own knowing, what ideal it serves; that is the question §§23–27 put to the *Genealogy* itself, and the reflexive turn <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs is possible only because the theory of knowledge sits here, inside the diagnosis of the priest, and not in a treatise of its own.
</KeyFact>

## The eye turned in no direction: the argument against the will-less subject

### The refutation, with the German beside it

The refutation is two sentences long, and the course reads it with the German beside it because seven of its phrases stand in scare quotes and the marks do two different jobs. Four, in the first sentence, name the concepts the sentence rejects, the "pure, will-less, painless, timeless subject of knowledge", "pure reason", "absolute spirituality", "knowledge in itself"; three, in the second, name the words it keeps and redefines, „Erkennen", „Begriff", „Objektivität".

<Quote source="GM III:12, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Hüten wir uns nämlich, meine Herrn Philosophen, von nun an besser vor der gefährlichen alten Begriffs-Fabelei, welche ein „reines, willenloses, schmerzloses, zeitloses Subjekt der Erkenntniss“ angesetzt hat, hüten wir uns vor den Fangarmen solcher contradiktorischen Begriffe wie „reine Vernunft“, „absolute Geistigkeit“, „Erkenntniss an sich“: — hier wird immer ein Auge zu denken verlangt, das gar nicht gedacht werden kann, ein Auge, das durchaus keine Richtung haben soll, bei dem die aktiven und interpretirenden Kräfte unterbunden sein sollen, fehlen sollen, durch die doch Sehen erst ein Etwas-Sehen wird, hier wird also immer ein Widersinn und Unbegriff von Auge verlangt. Es giebt nur ein perspektivisches Sehen, nur ein perspektivisches „Erkennen“; und je mehr Affekte wir über eine Sache zu Worte kommen lassen, je mehr Augen, verschiedne Augen wir uns für dieselbe Sache einzusetzen wissen, um so vollständiger wird unser „Begriff“ dieser Sache, unsre „Objektivität“ sein.
</Original>

Let us, my philosophers, from now on guard ourselves better against the dangerous old conceptual fable that posited a "pure, will-less, painless, timeless subject of knowledge"; let us guard against the tentacles of such contradictory concepts as "pure reason", "absolute spirituality", "knowledge in itself": here an eye is always demanded that cannot be thought at all, an eye that is to have no direction whatever, in which the active and interpreting forces, through which alone seeing first becomes seeing-*something*, are to be tied off, are to be absent; what is demanded here is therefore always an absurdity and a non-concept of an eye. There is only a perspectival seeing, only a perspectival "knowing"; and the more affects we allow to speak about a matter, the more eyes, different eyes, we know how to bring to bear on the same matter, the more complete will our "concept" of this matter, our "objectivity", be.
</Quote>

### The analogy: seeing is always seeing-something

The argument is stated as a claim about eyes and meant as a claim about knowing, and the analogy has to be unpacked before the premises can be checked. Seeing is perspectival in a literal sense: a distance and an angle always obtain between an eye and what it sees, and the object's look depends on them. An eye "with no direction whatever" would be an eye with no spatial relation to its object, which is to say not in space, which is to say not an eye. That much is trivial.

The section's work is done by the clause about the "active and interpreting forces": seeing becomes seeing-*something*, a thing picked out against a ground, only because the eye is directed and selective. The forces the will-less subject is supposed to lack are therefore the forces without which there is nothing to call seeing at all.

### Clark's cognitive analogue: there is no neutral corner

Clark's unpacking supplies the cognitive analogue. A perspective in knowing is whatever on the knower's side affects the intellectual "look" of an object: the corpus of beliefs one already holds, the cognitive capacities one has, the interests and, in §12's own word, the *affects* that direct attention. A non-perspectival knowing would then be knowledge grounded in beliefs no rational being could reject, a neutral corner from which everything else is judged, and the section's "absurdity and non-concept" says there is no such corner.

The affects are not the noise on the signal; they are the direction without which there is no signal. That is the premise the priest's epistemology denies and the premise the rest of the treatise has been using, since every type-diagnosis in it reads a valuation as an affect's work.

<Remark title="GM III:12 reconstructed: against the will-less subject">
*P1.* Seeing is always a seeing-*something*, and what makes it so are the active, interpreting, directed forces of the eye: remove them and nothing is left that is seeing (§12: "through which alone seeing first becomes seeing-something").

*P2.* Knowing stands to its object as seeing stands to its object: it is done by an embodied knower whose affects direct and select, and a "knowledge in itself" would be a knowing with those forces "tied off" (§12's analogy, stated as a premise).

*P3.* An eye with no direction is a contradiction, "a non-concept of an eye"; by P2, a knower with no affect is the same contradiction in the other domain.

*C.* "Pure reason", "absolute spirituality", "knowledge in itself" and the "pure, will-less, painless, timeless subject of knowledge" name nothing; there is only a perspectival seeing and a perspectival "knowing".

*The premise not stated.* P2 is asserted by the metaphor and argued nowhere in §12; Clark's reading supplies it as anti-foundationalism, the denial of self-justifying beliefs, and her grounds for attributing that to Nietzsche are in *Beyond Good and Evil* (BGE 16's dissection of "I think" as a bundle of assumptions) and in the history of the "true world" that <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">the fable lesson</Ref> reconstructs. Without P2 the section refutes only a bad theory of vision.
</Remark>

### Absurdity, not disguise: how §12's charge differs from §6's

The argument's form is worth marking, because it differs from §6's. §6 unmasked Schopenhauer's will-less contemplation as a will's disguise, a report of absence reinterpreted as a presence with another object; the charge there was dishonesty about a drive. §12 does not say the will-less knower is a disguised will. It says the concept is a *Widersinn*, an absurdity, and that charge needs no fact about anyone's motives, only the premise that knowing is directed.

The section closes by turning the concept's own ideal against it: "to eliminate the will altogether, to unhinge the affects one and all, supposing we could: would that not be to castrate the intellect?" The verb is Nietzsche's own, *castriren*, and it names the operation <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">the previous lesson</Ref> set against *sublimation*, the redirection of a drive onto a new object with its force kept: castration is the drive extirpated instead of redirected. Here it is the will-less subject's own program, named as a mutilation.

<Aside>
The same point in another register. Heidegger's 1936 lectures on will to power as art (*Nietzsche* I, §25) give Nietzsche's image for affect-conditioned perception from the notebooks: a lizard "hears the slightest rustling in the grass but does not hear a pistol shot fired quite close by", and the notebook formula is that "the perspectival is the basic condition of all life". Heidegger reads this not as epistemology but as ontology, a claim about what a living being is, with each drive in the organism holding its own perspective. The lizard illustrates P1 exactly: its hearing is a hearing-*something* because it is tuned, and an untuned ear would hear nothing. What Heidegger adds, that the tuning is what a living thing *is*, is the third construal of §12 the lesson takes up below, and it is a notebook thesis, not §12's.
</Aside>

## "Objectivity" in scare quotes: the command of one's for and against

The refutation would be a purely negative result if §12 stopped at "there is only a perspectival seeing". It does not stop there; the sentence continues into a comparative, "the more eyes, the more complete", and the sentence before the refutation had already redefined the word the comparative ends on.

<Quote source="GM III:12, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
dergestalt einmal anders sehn, anders-sehn-wollen ist keine kleine Zucht und Vorbereitung des Intellekts zu seiner einstmaligen „Objektivität“, — letztere nicht als „interesselose Anschauung“ verstanden (als welche ein Unbegriff und Widersinn ist), sondern als das Vermögen, sein Für und Wider in der Gewalt zu haben und aus- und einzuhängen: so dass man sich gerade die Verschiedenheit der Perspektiven und der Affekt-Interpretationen für die Erkenntniss nutzbar zu machen weiss.
</Original>

to see differently for once in this way, to *want* to see differently, is no small discipline and preparation of the intellect for its eventual "objectivity", the latter understood not as "disinterested contemplation" (which is a non-concept and an absurdity), but as the capacity to have one's for and against *in one's power* and to hang them out and in: so that one knows how to make precisely the difference of perspectives and affect-interpretations useful for knowledge.
</Quote>

### The marks replace the referent: a property of a view becomes a virtue of a type

The quotation marks are the point. "Objectivity" without them names what the will-less subject was supposed to have, a view of the matter with nothing of the viewer in it; §12 has just called that a non-concept. "Objectivity" with them names a capacity of a knower: *das Vermögen, sein Für und Wider in der Gewalt zu haben*, to have one's for and against in one's power, to hang an affect out and another in, and to use the *difference* between the resulting views. The word is kept and its referent replaced.

What was a property of a view becomes a virtue of a type, and the treatise's own vocabulary says so: the capacity is a *Zucht*, a discipline, and a strong type is one whose drives are commanded rather than obeyed (<Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drive-self lesson</Ref> reconstructs that psychology from BGE 12 and 19).

### „Begriff": a concept that admits degrees

The second redefined word gets the same treatment in the sentence that follows. Unquoted, a *Begriff* is a grasp (*begreifen*, to grasp) of what a thing is, and it is had or not had: a knower has the concept of a triangle or lacks it. §12 puts the word in marks and then does to it what the unquoted word cannot bear, gives it a comparative: our „Begriff" of a matter is *vollständiger*, more complete, the more eyes are brought to bear on it.

A concept that comes in degrees is an assembly of perspectives, not a grasp of the matter in itself, and it is complete only in the limit that adds every eye. The marks on „Begriff" and „Objektivität" are therefore one operation done twice: each word is kept for its ranking role and re-referred, from a relation to the thing in itself, which the section has just called a non-concept, to a relation among a knower's perspectives. GS 354's account of concepts as herd-signs, read below, keeps this assembly picture and drops the inference that assembling falsifies.

### Addition, never subtraction: what the comparative does

Two consequences follow, and the treatise uses both. The first is that §12 *ranks*: a concept assembled from more perspectives is "more complete" than one assembled from fewer, and a knower who can command his for and against ranks above one who cannot. The second is that the redefined objectivity is reached by addition, never by subtraction.

The will-less subject was the limit of subtracting affects; §12's objectivity is the limit of commanding them, and the two limits point in opposite directions. That is why the section can call the priest's reversals a "discipline and preparation": each reversal is one more perspective a knower has learned to hang in and out, and the ascetic's error was to mistake the exercise for the destination.

### BGE 6 from the other end: every philosophy is a confession

BGE 6 states the same doctrine from the other end. "Every great philosophy so far" has been "the self-confession of its author and a kind of involuntary and unremarked memoir", because the "drive for knowledge" is not the father of philosophy; another drive has used knowledge as a tool, and "every drive is tyrannical, and as such it attempts to philosophize". The section is usually quoted as a debunking.

Read with §12 it is the same claim: every philosophy is perspectival because an affect directed it, and the difference between philosophies is not that some are affect-free but which affect commanded and whether the author commanded it. BGE 6 also supplies the case §12 needs for its ranking, the scholar in whom "there might really be something like a drive for knowledge, some small independent clockwork" ticking apart from the rest of his drives: a knower with *one* eye, exactly the impoverished first frame.

<KeyFact>
"Objectivity" in §12 is a capacity of a knower and not a property of a view, reached by adding commanded affects and never by subtracting them; the section therefore ranks knowers by breadth of command and puts the will-less subject at the bottom of the ranking rather than outside it, so that the ascetic ideal's knower is not exempt from §12's standard but its worst case.
</KeyFact>

## What §12 is not (a): the falsification thesis of 1873

The correct claim first: §12 says that more perspectives see more, and it nowhere says that any perspective sees falsely. The misconception it is most often read as, stated once: *perspectivism is the thesis that all human knowledge falsifies, that "truths are illusions", so that Nietzsche denied that anything we believe is true.* The course rejects this reading of the mature works, and the rejection has a date attached, because Nietzsche did hold the thesis once.

### The position Nietzsche abandoned: *On Truth and Lie*

"On Truth and Lie in an Extra-Moral Sense" is an essay of 1873 that Nietzsche did not publish, which by the course's <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">published-works rule</Ref> makes it evidence for a position he held and not for one he asserted; it dates the falsification thesis, and it is where the thesis is argued rather than assumed. It opens with a fable, "in some remote corner of the universe, poured out and glittering in innumerable solar systems, there was once a star on which clever animals invented knowing", and moves to language.

A word is "the copy of a nerve stimulus in sounds". To infer from the stimulus to a cause outside us is already "a false and unjustified application of the principle of sufficient reason"; language-makers designate "only the relations of things to men" and use "the boldest metaphors" to do it: "a nerve stimulus, first transferred into an image: first metaphor. The image again copied into a sound: second metaphor. And each time a complete leaping-over of the sphere, right into the middle of a wholly different and new one."

The physicist Ernst Chladni (1756–1827) had shown that a bowed plate strewn with sand throws the sand into standing patterns; the essay's image for our relation to things is a man born deaf who studies those figures and "will swear that now he must know what people call sound". "As the sound appears as a sand figure, so the enigmatic X of the thing-in-itself appears first as a nerve stimulus, then as an image, finally as a sound."

The essay's conclusion follows from the chain, and it is the sentence the "new Nietzsche" of the 1970s made his signature.

<Quote source="WL (1873), eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Was ist also Wahrheit? Ein bewegliches Heer von Metaphern, Metonymien, Anthropomorphismen kurz eine Summe von menschlichen Relationen, die, poetisch und rhetorisch gesteigert, übertragen, geschmückt wurden, und die nach langem Gebrauche einem Volke fest, canonisch und verbindlich dünken: die Wahrheiten sind Illusionen, von denen man vergessen hat, dass sie welche sind, Metaphern, die abgenutzt und sinnlich kraftlos geworden sind, Münzen, die ihr Bild verloren haben und nun als Metall, nicht mehr als Münzen in Betracht kommen.
</Original>

What, then, is truth? A mobile army of metaphors, metonyms, anthropomorphisms, in short a sum of human relations which were poetically and rhetorically heightened, transferred, adorned, and which after long use seem to a people fixed, canonical and binding: truths are illusions of which one has forgotten that they are such, metaphors that have been worn out and drained of sensuous force, coins that have lost their image and now count as metal, no longer as coins.
</Quote>

Clark's chapter on the essay (*Nietzsche on Truth and Philosophy*, ch. 3) reads the argument for what it rests on, and it rests on two inherited doctrines: a representational theory of perception, on which what we perceive is a construction from sensation and never the object, and the metaphysical correspondence theory of truth, on which a belief is true only if it matches the thing-in-itself. Given both, "truths are illusions" follows: perception hands us metaphors of an X we never reach, and truth was defined as reaching it.

The essay's own words put the thing-in-itself in the definition, "the pure truth apart from consequences" (*die reine folgenlose Wahrheit*), and then declare it "quite incomprehensible to the creator of language and not in the least worth striving for". The denial of truth is a *disappointed correspondence theory*: it needs the metaphysical standard in order to say that everything falls short of it.

### Why the mature works cannot hold it

The step Clark's reading turns on is that the later Nietzsche rejects the thing-in-itself as a contradiction, not as unknowable. The history of the "true world" in *Twilight of the Idols* ends by abolishing it and the "apparent world" with it, so that there is no longer a "mere appearance" for anything to be; <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">the fable lesson</Ref> reconstructs that argument stage by stage and is the ground the present reading stands on.

Once there is no X, the chain of *Truth and Lie* loses its left-hand box, and with it the only reason for calling its links illusions. A perspective can then fall short of nothing. In Clark's formulation, "perspectivism excludes only something contradictory: as creative power is not limited by the inability to make a square triangle, cognitive power is not limited by the inability to have nonperspectival knowledge". What remains of truth is the minimal notion on which a belief is true if things are as it says, and §12 is compatible with a great many beliefs being true in that sense.

The positive doctrine this leaves is anti-foundationalism, and Clark's image for it (ch. 2 §3) is the boat Otto Neurath and Quine used against Descartes: a belief system that must be rebuilt at sea, plank by plank, with the other planks holding it up, because "we cannot even inquire into the truth or reliability of a belief except by using other beliefs". The foundationalist is the one who thinks the boat can be repaired only in dry dock, on beliefs that justify themselves.

§12's "eye turned in no direction" is that dry dock, and the section's "absurdity" is the claim that there is no such place to haul the boat out. Justification is contextual, every belief is revisable, and none of that entails that any belief is false: "the fact that our beliefs could be false does not entail that they are false".

### The two later passages that look like the old thesis

Two sections of *The Gay Science* are quoted for the falsification thesis, and the course reads both for what they are about. GS 354 (Book V, 1887) explains consciousness as a late product of the need to communicate, a "connecting net between man and man", so that "the world of which we can become conscious is only a surface- and sign-world, a generalized, a vulgarized world", and calls this "the real phenomenalism and perspectivism as *I* understand it". The section does say that becoming conscious involves "falsification" (*Fälschung*), and Clark grants that the word is a residue of the representational model.

But its subject is consciousness, the part of thinking that happens in words, against the thinking that "goes on continually" without them; it is a thesis about what is *lost in translation into signs*, not about knowledge as such, and it explicitly disclaims the framework the falsification thesis needs: "it is not the opposition of 'thing in itself' and appearance that concerns me here: we do not 'know' nearly enough to be entitled even to that distinction." A passage that refuses the thing-in-itself cannot be the 1873 thesis, which was built on it.

GS 374, "Our new 'infinite'", is the mature statement in the section's own idiom.

<Quote source="GS 374, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Wir können nicht um unsre Ecke sehn: es ist eine hoffnungslose Neugierde, wissen zu wollen, was es noch für andre Arten Intellekt und Perspektive geben könnte … Die Welt ist uns vielmehr noch einmal „unendlich“ geworden: insofern wir die Möglichkeit nicht abweisen können, dass sie unendliche Interpretationen in sich schliesst.
</Original>

We cannot see around our own corner: it is a hopeless curiosity to want to know what other kinds of intellect and perspective there might be … The world has rather become "infinite" for us once more: insofar as we cannot reject the possibility that it includes infinite interpretations.
</Quote>

The corner is §12's direction, and the sentence says what a directed knower cannot do: certify that his perspective is the only kind. It does not say that what he sees from his corner is false, and the section's own next move is to refuse to "deify" the unknown, since "too many ungodly possibilities of interpretation" are counted into it, "too much devilry, stupidity, foolishness of interpretation". Interpretations can be stupid: the passage that opens the world to infinite interpretations ranks them in the same breath.

<KeyFact>
The falsification thesis needs a thing-in-itself to fall short of, and *Truth and Lie* (1873, unpublished) supplies one by definition; the mature works reject that concept as a contradiction, so §12's perspectivism can deny foundations and the view from nowhere while leaving the truth of ordinary beliefs untouched, and GS 354's "falsification" is a claim about what consciousness loses in signs, stated in a section that disowns the thing-in-itself outright.
</KeyFact>

### The discriminating example: one cathedral, two stations

The discriminating example is Simon Blackburn's: "a view of St Paul's cathedral may be from front or back, near or far, pavement level or roof level, through a wide-angle lens or a telephoto. But no such view is erroneous *per se*, or erroneous just because of the possibility of shifting our point of view." A view is partial, and the remedy for partiality is the one §12 prescribes, "walk on, change the perspective, conjoin and synthesize it with other views".

Perspective-talk entails error only if a further premise is smuggled in, that a view which could have been otherwise is thereby false, and that premise is the 1873 one in a new coat.

<Aside>
The leaf, and why the mature Nietzsche does not need it. *Truth and Lie* has a second argument for the illusion thesis that does not go through the thing-in-itself, and Clark reconstructs it in its strongest form (ch. 3 §2, an interpretation she credits to Sidney Morgenbesser). Every concept is formed by "equating the unequal": "just as it is certain that one leaf is never wholly the same as another, so it is certain that the concept 'leaf' is formed by arbitrarily dropping these individual differences". A literal statement, "that chair is red", therefore has the structure of a metaphor, an identity asserted where there is only similarity, and differs from a metaphor only in that we have forgotten the difference. Clark's refutation is that the argument is inconsistent nominalism. If only individuals are real, then "the same word" is itself only a similarity between different sounds and inscriptions; and if "x is red" says only that x is relevantly similar to the other things called red, it asserts no false identity and there is nothing to call an illusion. The third exercise derives the inconsistency in full. The mature works do not need the argument because they have dropped its conclusion, and GS 354's account of concepts as herd-signs keeps the observation (concepts generalize) without the inference (therefore they falsify).
</Aside>

## What §12 is not (b): relativism, refuted from the comparative

### The comparative ranks: *um so vollständiger*

The reader met the relativist misreading once before, on valuations: <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the master-morality lesson</Ref> refuted the claim that the *Genealogy* describes two moralities and takes no side, from three sentences in which the text ranks and says by what. On knowledge the misreading recurs in the form "every view is as good as any other", and it is refuted the same way, from the text's own comparatives.

§12's is *um so vollständiger*, "the more complete": a comparative ranks, and it ranks by breadth of command over perspectives on "the same matter" (*dieselbe Sache*). GS 374 ranks interpretations as stupid or otherwise in the sentence that opens the world to them. BGE 6 ranks philosophies as confessions, with the scholar's clockwork drive at the bottom. A doctrine that could not tell a fuller concept from a thinner one could not state any of these sentences.

### Clark and Nehamas agree, and the agreement is evidence

The two readings the course sets against each other in its last module agree here, and the agreement is evidence. Alexander Nehamas, whose Nietzsche is otherwise as far from Clark's as the course reaches, writes that it "does not follow" from perspectivism "that any interpretation is as good as any other", citing GS 357's "we must reject the Christian interpretation", and that "perspectivism does not result in the relativism that holds that any view is as good as any other; it holds that one's own views are the best for oneself without implying that they need be good for anyone else".

Both inferences, he adds, are instances of what Nietzsche calls nihilism: the assumption that "if some single standard is not good for everyone and for all time, then no standard is good for anyone at any time". That is the relativist's step named as the ascetic ideal's own reflex, all-or-nothing, and it is a reading of the misreading that Clark and Nehamas share.

### Railton's cave: two matters are not two perspectives

Peter Railton's cave separates what perspectivism does permit from what it does not. A rat and a bat inhabit one cave; the rat represents it by scent and touch, the bat by echo, and "neither representation would present that world in ways adequate to the needs of the other". Asked which is truer, Nietzsche's early answer applies: "the insect or the bird perceives an entirely different world from the one that man does, and the question which of these perceptions of the world is the correct one is quite meaningless" (WL).

Railton's point is that this is a pluralism about *representational systems*, each capable of truth, and that within any system representations can be "more encompassing, more discriminating, more generalizable, and more reliable", which is a ranking. Two knowers with different sense organs and different needs are not two perspectives on the same matter competing for completeness; they are two matters. §12's comparative is about the first case, and relativism needs it to be about the second.

<KeyFact>
The relativist's inference, from "no single standard for everyone" to "no standard for anyone", is the all-or-nothing reflex the treatise has already diagnosed as the ascetic ideal's own; refuting relativism about knowledge is therefore not a side-result but the treatise's method turned on a misreading of it, and it holds on both of the course's rival readings, Clark's and Nehamas's.
</KeyFact>

## BGE 22: the self-application, and the worry it leaves standing

### The physicists' "bad philology": law as interpretation

§12 is a doctrine about all knowing, so it applies to itself, and Nietzsche applied it in his own hand a year earlier. BGE 22 addresses the physicists: the "conformity of nature to law" they are proud of "exists only through your interpretation and bad 'philology'"; it is "not a matter of fact, not a 'text', but only a naively humanitarian correction and distortion of meaning" that accommodates "the democratic instincts of the modern soul", the reflex "everywhere equality before the law". BGE 14 had said the same of physics as a whole, "only an interpretation and arrangement of the world, not an explanation".

Then §22 supplies the rival eye: someone "with the opposite intention and art of interpretation" could read from "the same nature and with reference to the same appearances" the "tyrannically ruthless and inexorable enforcement of power-claims", and would end by asserting what the physicist asserts, a necessary and calculable course, "not because laws rule in it, but because laws are absolutely lacking, and every power draws its ultimate consequence at every moment". The last sentence turns on the author.

<Quote source="BGE 22, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Gesetzt, dass auch dies nur Interpretation ist — und ihr werdet eifrig genug sein, dies einzuwenden? — nun, um so besser. —
</Original>

Supposing that this too is only interpretation — and you will be eager enough to raise this objection? — well, so much the better. —
</Quote>

### "So much the better": what the reply disarms and what it leaves

"So much the better" is §12 applied: the will-to-power reading of nature is one more eye on the same appearances, offered as such, and the physicist's objection concedes the thesis it was meant to defeat, since to call the reading an interpretation is to grant that the physicist's is one too. What the sentence does not do is say what "so much the better" buys. The reflexive objection, stated by Arthur Danto in 1965 and answered by everyone since, runs on a short tree.

<Figure
  src="./figures/self-refutation-tree.svg"
  alt="A small proof tree. At the top, the premise P: every view is an interpretation. An arrow down to: so P is itself an interpretation.   caption="The self-refutation charge as a tree: the step from 'possibly false' to 'false' is where it breaks, and both readings say so, for different reasons."

### The tree, and the step that breaks

The tree: (P) every view is an interpretation; so P is an interpretation; so P is possibly false, which is to say some views may not be interpretations; *so* some views are not interpretations, and P is false. The invalid step is the last.

Clark's answer is that anti-foundationalism generates no paradox at all, because it says of every belief that it *may* be false and of none that it *is*, so P applied to itself yields only that P is revisable; "perspectivism is, of course, a perspectival truth, but this does not imply that any competing claim is also true". Nehamas's answer, from the chapter the course reads in Module 7, is that the inference "equates the fact that P is an interpretation and therefore possibly false with the fact that it is actually false", and that P can be refuted only by producing a view that is actually not an interpretation, which nobody has.

The two answers agree on the invalid step and disagree on what P claims: for Clark it claims that no belief is self-justifying, for Nehamas that every view involves selection the viewer cannot see while viewing, and Clark's charge against the second is that it smuggles the falsification thesis back in.

The course states the tree here and settles nothing; <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">the lesson on Nehamas</Ref> takes the problem in full, where the status of Nietzsche's own claims is the whole question, and <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the next lesson</Ref> meets its nearer form, the *Genealogy*'s critique of the will to truth written by a will to truth.

## Three construals of §12, and the naturalist reading's pick

### Epistemological, psychological, ontological: what each needs §12 to say

The section has been read as three different kinds of claim, and what each construal needs the passage to say is the test that sorts them.

<Figure
  src="./figures/three-construals.svg"
  alt="Three columns. Epistemological, Clark and Leiter: the claim is about justification, no self-justifying beliefs, no view from nowhere; what section 12 must say, that the eye with no direction is the foundationalist's neutral corner; what it costs, the affects become one perspective-factor among beliefs and interests. Psychological, Gemes: the claim is about drives and affects and how they relate, a doctrine within psychology; what section 12 must say, that knowing is an affect's work and objectivity the mastery of affects by a strong type; what it costs, the section's claim about knowledge in itself becomes a symptom-reading. Ontological, Heidegger, Deleuze, Richardson: the claim is about what living things are, perspectives as will-to-power centres; what section 12 must say, that the perspectival is the basic condition of all life; what it costs, the textual ground is the notebooks, not section 12. A highlighted band across the first two columns reads: the naturalist reading's pick, epistemological plus psychological, affects as type-facts."
  caption="Three construals sorted by what each needs §12 to say; the working hypothesis takes the first two together and leaves the third to the notebooks."
/>

The *epistemological* construal (Clark, and Brian Leiter after her) takes the claim to be about justification: there are no self-justifying beliefs and no neutral corner, and "objectivity" names the widest command of the perspectives that justification actually runs through. It needs §12's eye with no direction to be the foundationalist's standpoint, which the sentence about "knowledge in itself" supports.

The *psychological* construal (Ken Gemes) takes epistemological readings to be misguided and the doctrine to be one *within* psychology, about how drives and affects relate and how a strong type commands them; it needs the section's "more affects we allow to speak" to be its centre, which it is, and it pays by turning the sentence about "knowledge in itself" into a symptom rather than a thesis.

The *ontological* construal (Heidegger, and in the analytic literature John Richardson's will-to-power centres) takes perspectives to be constituents of living things, which is what the lizard's rustle and the notebook formula assert; its cost is that §12 says nothing about lizards, and the course's <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">evidence rules</Ref> keep a notebook formula from grounding a reading of a published section.

### The naturalist reading's pick: affects as type-facts

The working hypothesis takes the first two together, and the reason is the Doctrine of Types. On the naturalist reading the affects that direct a perspective are type-facts, the fixed psycho-physical constitution <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> introduced as what defines a person as a type, so the epistemological claim (justification is perspectival) and the psychological claim (perspectives are affects at work) are one claim seen from two sides, and "objectivity" as command of one's for and against is a capacity that some types have and most do not.

What the hypothesis cannot do is treat the third construal as §12's content, and the closing module's <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">Heidegger lesson</Ref> is where that refusal is tested, since Heidegger's whole reading depends on taking the notebook formula as the doctrine and §12 as its surface.

<Aside>
The neo-Kantian background, in one paragraph. The vocabulary of "perspective" and "optics" that §12 uses was not Nietzsche's invention. Friedrich Albert Lange's *History of Materialism* (1866), which Nietzsche read in 1866 and praised, and Afrikan Spir's *Denken und Wirklichkeit* (1873) had naturalized Kant: the structures Kant made a priori conditions of experience (space, time, causality) became contingent features of human psychology, and Gustav Teichmüller's *Die wirkliche und die scheinbare Welt* (1882) had already called such features "perspectives". Nietzsche's step was to tie the perspectives back to affects, which is why the psychological construal has a textual foothold the neo-Kantian sources lack. Clark's "neo-Kantian" label for her own reading refers to this lineage and to nothing in Kant's own doctrine of the thing-in-itself, which the reading rejects.
</Aside>

## "On Immaculate Perception": the same critique with a different charge

### The moon as monk: the speech's figure

*Thus Spoke Zarathustra* II (1883) contains a speech that stages the critique of will-less knowing four years before §12 argued it, and the course's rule for the book, restated from <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref>, is to map the speech onto the theses argued elsewhere and state what it adds, drops or undercuts.

The speech is "Von der unbefleckten Erkenntniss", "On Immaculate Perception", and its figure is the moon. Rising "broad and pregnant" on the horizon, the moon "lied to me with its pregnancy"; it is "lecherous and jealous, this monk in the moon, lecherous for the earth and for all the joys of lovers", creeping "catlike" over the rooftops. "This parable I give to you sentimental hypocrites, you 'pure perceivers'! I call you lechers!" The pure perceiver's creed is then quoted back at him.

<Quote source="Z II, 'On Immaculate Perception', eKGWB; translation after Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
„Das wäre mir das Höchste — also redet euer verlogner Geist zu sich — auf das Leben ohne Begierde zu schaun … Und das heisse mir aller Dinge unbefleckte Erkenntniss, dass ich von den Dingen Nichts will: ausser dass ich vor ihnen da liegen darf wie ein Spiegel mit hundert Augen.“ … Wo ist Unschuld? Wo der Wille zur Zeugung ist.
</Original>

"For me what is highest," thus speaks your lying spirit to itself, "would be to look upon life without desire … And to me the immaculate perception of all things would be that I desire nothing from things, except that I might lie there before them like a mirror with a hundred eyes." … Where is innocence? Where there is will to beget.
</Quote>

### The mapping: a mirror with a hundred eyes and no direction

The mapping is exact in one place and instructive where it fails. The "mirror with a hundred eyes" is §12's will-less subject drawn as a picture: many eyes, no direction, nothing wanted from the things seen. §12's many eyes are the opposite, each with an affect, and the section's comparative counts affects, not eyes; the speech has the pure perceiver claim the multiplicity while refusing the one thing that makes an eye an eye.

"To look upon life without desire" is §6's "disinterested contemplation", the Kant-through-Schopenhauer formula <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">the previous lesson</Ref> read as a tortured will's relief. And "where is innocence? where there is will to beget" is the positive doctrine both sections reach, that the directed, wanting, begetting eye is the honest one.

### The added charge: hypocrisy needs §6's form, absurdity needs only P1

What the speech adds is a charge §12 does not make. The moon is a hypocrite: it wants the earth and pretends to want only to look, and the pure perceivers are "lechers" whose "desire lacks innocence, and now therefore you slander all desiring". That is an accusation about motives, and it is §6's argument-form, a reported absence of will read as a will's disguise, applied to knowers instead of aesthetes. §12 argues something weaker and safer: not that the will-less knower is lying, but that the concept is a *Widersinn*, so there is nothing there to lie about.

The argued register can carry the second charge with no evidence about anyone; it can carry the first only with a type-diagnosis attached, which is what §12's opening paragraph on the priest's philosophy supplies for the priest and for nobody else. The dramatization therefore over-claims exactly where a dramatization can: it makes every pure perceiver a moon, and the treatise reserves that verdict for the type it has diagnosed.

### What is established, and what stands open

What is established. §12 sits inside the priest's stretch because its target is the ascetic ideal's epistemology, the will-less subject who has cut off the intellect's conditions as the priest cuts off life's. Against it the section argues that seeing is always seeing-*something* through directed, interpreting forces, that a knower without affect is the same contradiction as an eye without direction, and that "objectivity", in scare quotes, is a capacity, the command of one's for and against, reached by adding perspectives and never by subtracting them, so that the section ranks knowers and puts the will-less one at the bottom.

The perspectivism this yields is not the falsification thesis of 1873, which needed a thing-in-itself to fall short of and which the mature works cannot state, and it is not relativism, which the section's own comparative and GS 374's "stupidity of interpretation" refute; GS 354's "falsification" is a claim about consciousness stated in a section that disowns the thing-in-itself.

BGE 22 applies the doctrine to Nietzsche's own reading of nature, and the self-refutation tree that invites breaks at the step from "possibly false" to "false", on both the working reading and its rival. Of three construals the naturalist reading takes the epistemological and the psychological together, affects as type-facts, and leaves the ontological one to the notebooks. And *Zarathustra*'s moon stages the picture exactly while charging hypocrisy where §12 charges absurdity.

What stands open is the section's own application to the treatise that contains it. §12 ranks knowers by breadth of command and calls the priest's reversals a discipline; §§23–27 will ask what ideal the *Genealogy*'s own knowing serves, find the unconditional will to truth there, and declare that it "requires a critique". <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs that argument, with GS 344 as its second passage; the ground §12's reading stands on, the rejection of the thing-in-itself, is <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">the fable lesson</Ref>'s, and the reflexive problem left standing above is <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">Module 7's</Ref>.

<Exercise>
Clark refutes *Truth and Lie*'s leaf argument as inconsistent nominalism. Reconstruct the argument in the strongest form she gives it (the Morgenbesser version), then derive the inconsistency: state what must be true of the word "leaf" for the argument to say that the concept "discards differences", and show that the same premise, applied to the word, removes the conclusion. Finally say why the mature Nietzsche does not need the argument.

<Solution>
**The argument, strongest form.** (1) Every concept is formed "by equating the unequal": no leaf is wholly the same as another, so the concept "leaf" is made by dropping the differences between leaves. (2) A metaphor ("Henry is an ostrich") is false because it asserts an identity where there is only a similarity. (3) A literal statement ("that chair is red") has the same structure: it asserts that this chair shares an identical property with the other things called red, where there is only similarity. (4) The only difference between literal and metaphorical usage is that in the literal case we have forgotten the difference. (5) Therefore literal truths are "illusions we have forgotten are illusions".

**The inconsistency.** The argument's premise is nominalism: only individuals are real, and only words are universal. For premise (1) to say that "leaf" discards differences, "leaf" has to be one word applied to many unequal leaves, that is, a universal. But on nominalism the word is not exempt: different utterances and inscriptions of "leaf" are themselves only similar sounds and marks, never identical, so "the same word" is itself an equation of the unequal. If Nietzsche is a consistent nominalist he must read "x is red" as saying that x is *relevantly similar* to the other things called red, not that it shares an identical essence with them. But then "x is red" asserts no identity, and premise (3) is false: the statement is not a metaphor and there is nothing in it to be an illusion. The argument needs realism about the word to convict the concept, and nominalism about the concept to convict the statement, and it cannot have both.

**Why the mature works do not need it.** The argument was one of two supports for "truths are illusions", and the conclusion is what the later Nietzsche gave up when he gave up the thing-in-itself: with no "original essences" for a word to fail to correspond to, there is no falsity in a concept's generalizing. GS 354 keeps the observation, concepts are herd-signs that generalize and flatten, and drops the inference, since it disowns the thing-in-itself in the same section; §12 keeps only the perspectival premise, and its comparative, "more complete", is a comparison between concepts that generalize, not a verdict that generalizing falsifies.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take §12's rule to its limit. Suppose a knower who could command *every* perspective on a matter, hanging each affect in and out at will. Is that knower the will-less subject returned by another route? State the difference §12 draws between the two limits, and say what the limit case shows about why "objectivity" is in scare quotes.

<Solution>
No. The two limits are reached by opposite operations and describe different knowers. The will-less subject is the limit of *subtraction*: affects removed one by one until none directs the eye, and §12's verdict on that limit is that nothing is left that sees ("would that not be to castrate the intellect?"). The knower who commands every perspective is the limit of *addition under command*: every affect available, each still directed, each still an affect, with the knower choosing which speaks. At every moment such a knower sees from *some* corner; what distinguishes him is that he can change corners, not that he has none. GS 374's sentence holds of him too: he cannot see around his corner, he can only walk to another. The all-commanding knower therefore has, at any instant, exactly the structure of the first frame of the sequence, one directed eye, plus the capacity that the will-less subject lacks by definition, a for-and-against to command.

The limit case shows why the scare quotes are needed. Unquoted "objectivity" names a property a *view* would have if nothing of the viewer were in it; that property has no limit case among knowers, because the sequence of adding commanded affects never converges on it, it converges on a knower with all the affects. Quoted "objectivity" names the capacity that sequence does converge on. The word is kept for its normative role, it still says which knower ranks higher, and its referent is replaced so that the ranking can be earned by a type. A reader who drops the quotation marks reads §12 as promising the view from nowhere by another route; the marks are there to say that the route leads somewhere else.

Its answer is the need to communicate, and its perspectivism, "as I understand it", is the claim that what enters consciousness enters as signs, and so as generalized, "herd" content: "the world of which we can become conscious is only a surface- and sign-world". §12 is about knowing, *Erkennen*, and about the concept of a knower; its perspectivism is that knowing is directed by affects and that "objectivity" is command of them.

**Agreement.** Both deny a knower with nothing of the knower in the knowing: 354 by making consciousness a social organ shaped by the herd's needs, 12 by making the eye without direction a non-concept. Both keep a comparative: 354's "the largest part of our life goes on without this mirroring", 12's "more complete". Neither mentions the other's mechanism; 354 does not use affects, 12 does not use signs.

**Support or undercut.** 354 looks like an undercut, since it says becoming conscious involves "falsification" (*Fälschung*). The sentence that decides it is: "It is not the opposition of 'thing in itself' and appearance that concerns me here: we do not 'know' nearly enough to be entitled even to that distinction." The falsification thesis of 1873 was the claim that our representations fail to correspond to the thing-in-itself; a passage that refuses to draw the distinction cannot be making that claim, so its "falsification" can only mean what the passage says it means, loss of individuality in translation into shared signs, "flat, thin, relatively stupid, general". That is compatible with anti-foundationalism and supports it: it locates the perspective on the side of the knower's constitution and refuses the standpoint from which the loss would count as error. The word in §12 that the same reading turns on is the scare-quoted *„Erkennen"*: the quotation marks mark that "knowing" no longer names the will-less subject's activity, and they do for that word what §12 does for "objectivity" outright.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Map "On Immaculate Perception" onto §§6 and 12: the moon, the mirror with a hundred eyes, "to look upon life without desire", "where is innocence? where there is will to beget". Then state what the speech charges that §12 does not, which argument-form that charge requires, and which of the two charges the argued register can support without a fact about anyone's motives.

<Solution>
**The mapping.** The moon, "lecherous for the earth" while pretending to want only to look, is the pure perceiver as a type: a will disguised as will-lessness, which is §6's Schopenhauer. The "mirror with a hundred eyes" that "desires nothing from things" is §12's will-less subject drawn as a picture, and the picture is instructive because it has *many* eyes and no direction: the speech lets the pure perceiver claim the multiplicity §12 prizes while refusing the affect that §12 says makes an eye an eye. "To look upon life without desire" is §6's *interesselose Anschauung*, the Kant-through-Schopenhauer formula, which §12 also names and calls a non-concept. "Where is innocence? where there is will to beget" is the positive doctrine of both sections: the directed, wanting eye is the honest one (§6's Stendhal, §12's commanded affects).

**The added charge.** The speech charges *hypocrisy*: "you sentimental hypocrites, you lechers! Your desire lacks innocence, and now therefore you slander all desiring." §12 charges *absurdity*: the will-less subject is "a non-concept and an absurdity", which is a claim about a concept, not about anyone's honesty. The hypocrisy charge requires §6's argument-form, the one <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">the previous lesson</Ref> drew as a template: a reported absence of will (E) is read as the satisfaction in disguise of a drive (D) found in the reporter's type. That form needs a fact about the type, the D, and the treatise supplies one for Schopenhauer (a sexuality "treated as a personal enemy") and for the ascetic priest (§12's opening), and for no one else.

**Which the register supports.** The absurdity charge, with no fact about motives: it follows from the premise that seeing is seeing-*something*, and holds of the concept whoever holds it. The hypocrisy charge holds only where a type-diagnosis has been done, so Zarathustra's extension of it to every "pure perceiver" is the dramatization over-claiming, which is what the course's reading rule for the book (<Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref>) predicts a speech will do: it stages the verdict the argued works reserve for a diagnosed type as if it held of a whole class.

<Exercise>
BGE 22 ends: "Supposing that this too is only interpretation, and you will be eager enough to raise this objection? Well, so much the better." Write out the objection the sentence invites, as an argument, and Nietzsche's one-sentence reply as an argument; then say what §12's redefinition of "objectivity" would need to add for the reply to succeed rather than merely to disarm.

<Solution>
**The objection.** (1) You say the physicists' "conformity of nature to law" is interpretation, not text. (2) Your rival reading of nature as will to power is, by your own doctrine, also interpretation. (3) An interpretation has no claim on anyone who does not share its perspective. (4) So your reading has no more claim than the physicists', and your critique of them establishes nothing.

**The reply.** (1) The physicists' objection grants premise (2), that will to power is an interpretation, only by granting that "conformity to law" is one too, since the objection's only ground is the general doctrine that every reading of nature is a reading. (2) That was the thesis under dispute; conceding it is conceding the point. (3) So the objection, if raised, strengthens the position it attacks: "so much the better". The reply is valid and it *disarms*: it shows that the physicist cannot both call the will-to-power reading a mere interpretation and keep "conformity to law" as text.

**What it does not do.** It does not say why the will-to-power reading should be preferred, and premise (3) of the objection is untouched: if both are interpretations, which ranks higher? §12's redefinition is what would have to be added. On it, "objectivity" is command of one's for and against and a concept is more complete the more perspectives it is assembled from; so the reply would succeed if the will-to-power reading could be shown to be *one more eye* on the same appearances, one the physicist's democratic instinct had hung out, so that the concept of nature including it ranks above the concept excluding it. BGE 22's own words invite this: the rival interpreter reads "the same nature, with reference to the same appearances". The addition is a ranking of perspectives on one matter, and it has to be earned from the matter, not from the doctrine that everything is a perspective. That is exactly the question <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the next lesson</Ref> raises about the *Genealogy* itself, whose knowing is one more perspective by its own account and whose ideal, §§23–27 will say, is the will to truth; and the reflexive form of premise (3), whether a doctrine that ranks perspectives can rank its own, is the self-refutation problem <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">Module 7</Ref> settles or fails to.
</Solution>

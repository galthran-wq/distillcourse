The Second Treatise opens with a breeding problem — how nature made an animal able to bind its own future — and answers with pain: memory was burned in, custom did the burning, and the 'ripest fruit' is an individual who calls the result his conscience. This lesson reconstructs the three sections that carry that answer, GM II:1–3 (1887), with the 1881 aphorism of *Daybreak* they cite for their central term, grades the one body of evidence they offer by the treatise's own rule, and states the readings of the sovereign individual between which the passage has to decide.

The course's argument arrives here with one result and one open question. <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">The previous lesson</Ref> removed morality's first presupposition, the chooser "free to express strength or not", and found that three things survive the removal: a difference between strong and weak wills (BGE 21), the feeling of freedom as a real feeling on the commander's side of a relation among drives (BGE 19), and, only announced, "responsibility as a bred capacity". Its closing test was that the boundary holds only if GM II:2's "free" individual can be read as the name of a kind of commonwealth of drives rather than as the substratum I:13 struck. That is this lesson's question, and the Second Treatise's own order supplies the material: II:1 says what a promise requires, II:2 says what produced an animal that can give one, II:3 says how, and the three together end on the word "conscience". What this lesson adds to the spine is the naturalist reading's first strain: the sections read cleanly as mechanism plus evidence until II:2, where the vocabulary of achievement and right sits on top of the vocabulary of breeding, and two readings offer to say which vocabulary is literal: the deflation of Brian Leiter, whose naturalism is the course's working hypothesis, and the autonomy reading of R. Lanier Anderson, the Stanford philosopher whose chapter "What Is a Nietzschean Self?" (2012) is the course's text for it. The lesson states both with the sentences each must explain and does not decide; <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the reconstruction lesson</Ref> decides, after <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref> has read what II:4 begins.

The route: II:1's two faculties, forgetting and the memory of the will, with the reconstruction; II:2's morality of custom, sourced to *Daybreak* §9, and its fruit; II:3's mnemotechnics with the catalogue graded; the two vocabularies of II:2 and the two readings that divide them; the strain against I:13 with the two reconciling routes; and a closing statement of what is established.

## II:1: forgetting is a faculty, and the promise is the one case in which it is suspended

From a treatise on guilt one expects an opening about wrongdoing. II:1 opens on a capacity, and states it as a problem in animal husbandry. "To breed an animal that *may* promise (*ein Thier heranzüchten, das versprechen darf*): is this not precisely the paradoxical task that nature has set itself with regard to man? is it not the real problem *of* man?" The modal verb is *darf*, not *kann*: the animal is to be one that is permitted to promise, entitled to, and the whole section is an account of what such an entitlement presupposes. That the problem "has been solved to a high degree" is said to be astonishing to anyone who has properly weighed "the force working against it, that of *forgetfulness*".

The counter-force is the section's first claim and the one a reader is least prepared for. Forgetfulness "is no mere *vis inertiae*, as the superficial believe; it is rather an active, in the strictest sense positive faculty of inhibition (*Hemmungsvermögen*)", and what it inhibits is the entry into consciousness of everything "lived, experienced, taken in", which is processed "in the state of digestion (one might call it *Einverseelung*, in-souling)" as invisibly as bodily nutrition, "the so-called *Einverleibung*, incorporation". The two coinages are built on one pattern, *ein-ver-Leib* and *ein-ver-Seele*, body and soul, so that the analogy is stated in the words themselves. What the faculty does is "shut the doors and windows of consciousness for a time", keep it "undisturbed by the noise and struggle with which our underworld of serviceable organs works for and against one another", and make "a little *tabula rasa* of consciousness, so that there is room again for what is new, above all for the nobler functions and functionaries, for governing, foreseeing, predetermining (for our organism is arranged oligarchically)". It is "a doorkeeper (*Thürwärterin*), as it were, a maintainer of psychic order, of rest, of etiquette", and without it "there could be no happiness, no cheerfulness, no hope, no pride, no *present*". The man in whom the apparatus fails "is to be compared to a dyspeptic (and not only compared): he cannot get 'finished' with anything".

The second faculty is bred against the first. "Precisely this necessarily forgetful animal, in which forgetting represents a force, a form of *robust health*, has now bred in itself a counter-faculty, a memory, with whose help forgetfulness is suspended (*ausgehängt*, taken off its hinges) for certain cases: namely for the cases where a promise is to be made." What is bred is not retention. It is "by no means merely a passive being-unable-to-get-rid-of the impression once scratched in, not merely the indigestion from a word once pledged that one cannot get finished with, but an active *not-wanting*-to-get-rid-of, a willing on and on of what was once willed, a real *memory of the will*". The consequence is stated as a chain: "between the original 'I will', 'I will do' and the actual discharge of the will, its *act*, a world of new strange things, circumstances, even acts of will may without hesitation be interposed, without this long chain of the will breaking".

### What the chain presupposes

The section's last sentence turns from the faculty to its conditions, and this is where the treatise's history begins. "But what does all this presuppose! How much must man first have learned, in order to dispose over the future in advance to this degree: to separate the necessary from the accidental in what happens, to think causally, to see the distant as present and anticipate it, to fix with certainty what is end and what is means to it, in general to be able to reckon, to calculate; and how must man himself have first become *calculable*, *regular*, *necessary* for this, even to himself and his own representation of himself, in order at last to be able, as one who promises does, to vouch (*gut sagen*) for himself as future!" The list runs from cognitive equipment to a fact about the animal itself: it cannot promise unless it has first been made the kind of thing whose future behaviour can be predicted, by others and by itself. *Gut sagen für* is the language of surety, standing security for a debt, and the phrase is the first sign that the section on promising is the first section of a treatise on debt.

<Remark title="II:1 reconstructed">
**P1.** A promise binds a present act of will to a future act across an interval in which other events and other acts of will intervene; it requires that the first willing persist through them ("this long chain of the will"). Anchor: "between the original 'I will' ... and the actual discharge of the will".

**P2.** The animal's default is active forgetting, a healthy inhibition that clears consciousness of what has been taken in; on that default nothing willed persists. Anchor: "*Hemmungsvermögen*"; "a form of robust health".

**P3.** Persisting through the interval is not retention (a scratched-in impression) but a selective, willed holding, "an active not-wanting-to-get-rid-of", and the animal that has it acquired it as a "counter-faculty" (*Gegenvermögen*) bred against P2. Anchor: "*angezüchtet*"; "a real memory of the will".

**P4.** Such a holding is possible only for an animal that can calculate and that is itself calculable, regular and necessary, to itself included. Anchor: "how must man himself have first become calculable".

**C.** The capacity to promise is a bred achievement with a history, and its precondition is a prior process that made the animal calculable; whatever made man calculable is the origin of the right to promise.

**The missing premise.** P3 asserts that the memory of the will was *bred* (*angezüchtet*) and names no breeder and no mechanism; the section is a description of the faculty and a list of its conditions, and the causal story is deferred to II:2 (the agent: the morality of custom) and II:3 (the means: pain). P4 is a claim about what promising requires, and it is stronger than the requirement of ordinary reliability: an animal could keep its word by habit without "seeing the distant as present". The premise the treatise needs is that the historical process it is about to describe produced calculability *of the required kind*, and that is what II:2 asserts and II:3 is offered to show.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That II:1 is phenomenology and not history: a description of what a promise is, from inside, which happens to use the vocabulary of breeding as a figure. The reading fits the first two-thirds of the section and fails at the last sentence, which asks what the faculty presupposes and answers with a state the animal had to be *made* into; II:2's first sentence then names that making "the long history of the descent of responsibility".
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The memory of the will is not a good memory. It is a suspension of forgetting for one item, a promise, and II:1 makes its precondition a fact about the animal rather than about the item: the promiser must himself be calculable. A history of promising is therefore a history of how the animal was made predictable, and that is a history of society acting on the individual before it is a history of anything the individual does.
</KeyFact>

## II:2: the morality of custom made man calculable, and its fruit stands outside it

II:1 ended on a precondition; II:2 opens by naming the process that met it, and its first sentence classifies the whole passage: "Precisely this is the long history of the *descent* (*Herkunft*) of responsibility." The task of breeding an animal that may promise "includes, as its condition and preparation, the nearer task of first making man to a certain degree *necessary*, *uniform*, like among like, regular and consequently *calculable*." The agent of that nearer task is named with a citation. "The tremendous labour of what I have called the 'morality of custom' (*Sittlichkeit der Sitte*; compare *Daybreak* pp. 7, 13, 16), the real labour of man on himself in the longest period of the human race, his whole *prehistoric* labour, has its meaning here, its great justification, however much hardness, tyranny, dullness and idiocy also dwell in it: with the help of the morality of custom and the social straitjacket (*Zwangsjacke*) man was *really made calculable*."

### Daybreak §9: the definition II:2 cites

The page numbers are to the first edition of *Morgenröthe* (1881), the aphorism collection published six years before the *Genealogy*, and Samuel's 1913 translation resolves them as §§9, 14 and 16. §9 is titled *Begriff der Sittlichkeit der Sitte*, "concept of the morality of custom", and it defines the term in a sentence Nietzsche flags as one that will sound coarse: "*Morality* (*Sittlichkeit*) *is nothing other* (and above all nothing *more*!) *than obedience to customs* (*Sitten*), of whatever kind these may be; customs, however, are the traditional way (*die herkömmliche Art*) of acting and evaluating." The definition is restricted at once: "In matters where no tradition (*Herkommen*) commands, there is no morality; and the less life is determined by tradition, the smaller the circle of morality becomes." And it is a definition of two words that English separates. German *Sitte* is custom, and *Sittlichkeit* is the ordinary word for morality built on it, so that *Sittlichkeit der Sitte* says in three words what English needs a sentence for: the morality that consists in custom, the moral standing that obedience to custom confers.

What §9 says about the individual is the sentence II:2 will reverse. "The free man is *unsittlich*, because in everything he wants to depend on himself and not on a tradition: in all original conditions of mankind 'evil' (*böse*) means as much as 'individual', 'free', 'arbitrary', 'unaccustomed', 'unforeseen', '*incalculable*' (*unberechenbar*)." An action done "not because tradition commands it but from other motives, for instance for the sake of individual advantage, indeed even from the very motives that once founded the tradition", counts as immoral "and is felt so even by its doer". Tradition is "a higher authority that one obeys not because it commands what is useful to us but because it *commands*", and the feeling before it is a fear "of a higher intellect that commands, of an incomprehensible indeterminate power, of something more than personal", a fear §9 calls superstition. Originally "everything was custom, and whoever wanted to raise himself above it had to become lawgiver and medicine man and a kind of demigod: that is, he had to *make* customs, a terrible, life-endangering thing". The aphorism ends on the cost: "Under the rule of the morality of custom, originality of every kind has acquired a bad conscience."

Two things follow for II:2. First, "calculable" in the 1887 text is the antonym of "incalculable" in the 1881 text: the labour that made man calculable is, by §9's definition, the labour of making him no longer "free" in the sense that custom calls evil. Second, §9 already contains the exclusion II:2 states in a parenthesis, "(for 'autonomous' and 'moral' (*sittlich*) exclude each other)": if morality is obedience to tradition, then whatever gives itself its own law is by definition outside morality. II:2 does not change the 1881 definition; it applies it to the end of the process the definition described, and the fifth exercise asks what the application adds.

### The straitjacket and the fruit

The passage then moves from the process to its end, and the seam is the treatise's own. "If on the other hand we place ourselves at the end of the tremendous process, there where the tree at last ripens its fruits, where society and its morality of custom at last bring to light what they were only the *means* to: then we find as the ripest fruit on its tree the *sovereign individual*, like only to himself, liberated again from the morality of custom (*das von der Sittlichkeit der Sitte wieder losgekommene*), the autonomous, supra-moral (*übersittliche*) individual (for 'autonomous' and 'moral' exclude each other), in short the human being of his own independent long will, who *may promise*." From a history of morality one expects the product to be moral. The product is *übersittlich*, over or beyond *Sitte*, and the exclusion in the parenthesis says why: what custom bred is something custom cannot contain.

The rest of the paragraph describes this individual from inside and from outside. Inside: "a proud consciousness, twitching in every muscle, of what has here at last been achieved (*errungen*) and become flesh (*leibhaft geworden*) in him, a real consciousness of power and freedom, a feeling of the completion of man in general". Outside: "this one who has become free (*Freigewordne*), who really *may* promise, this master of the *free* will, this sovereign", who cannot fail to know "what superiority (*Überlegenheit*) he thereby has over everything that may not promise and vouch for itself, how much trust, how much fear, how much reverence he awakens (he 'deserves' all three), and how, with this mastery over himself, mastery over circumstances, over nature and over all creatures of shorter will and less reliability is necessarily given into his hand". Then the valuation: "the 'free' man, the possessor of a long unbreakable will, has in this possession also his *measure of value* (*Werthmaass*): looking out from himself toward the others, he honours or he despises". He honours "those who promise like a sovereign, heavily, rarely, slowly", who give their word "as something one can rely on, because he knows himself strong enough to uphold it even against accidents, even 'against fate'"; and he holds his kick ready "for the scrawny whippets who promise without being permitted to (*ohne es zu dürfen*), and his rod for the liar who breaks his word already in the moment he has it in his mouth".

The paragraph's last sentence is the one II:3 picks up. "The proud knowledge of the extraordinary *privilege* of *responsibility*, the consciousness of this rare freedom, this power over himself and over fate, has sunk down in him to his lowest depth and has become an instinct, a dominating instinct: what will he call it, this dominating instinct, supposing he needs a word for it? But there is no doubt: this sovereign man calls it his *conscience*."

<Remark title="II:2 reconstructed">
**P1.** Promising requires a calculable animal (II:1's C).

**P2.** Man was made calculable by the morality of custom, defined (D 9) as obedience to tradition as such, enforced by the "social straitjacket" over the whole prehistoric period; this labour is the "descent of responsibility". Anchor: "*wirklich berechenbar gemacht*".

**P3.** The morality of custom was only a means; at the end of the process its product is an individual who is no longer under it, who gives himself his own law (autonomous, *übersittlich*), and who therefore may promise. Anchor: "*wozu sie nur das Mittel war*"; "*wieder losgekommene*".

**P4.** That individual's consciousness of his power and freedom has become his dominating instinct, and he names it "conscience". Anchor: "*zum dominirenden Instinkt*".

**C.** Conscience, in its highest form, is the name a bred, self-reliable individual gives to the felt possession of his own long will; it descends from the morality of custom and stands outside it.

**The missing premise.** P3 states that the process produced something outside itself and offers the tree and its fruit as the whole of the argument. Nothing in the passage says *how* obedience to custom as such produces an individual who obeys only himself; the mechanism by which uniformity yields sovereignty is not given, and the two readings below divide over whether one is needed. P3 also says the individual is "like only to himself" (*nur sich selbst gleiche*) after a process whose whole aim was to make men "like among like", and the passage does not say whether the fruit is rare by design or by accident.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That the paragraph is ironic: an individual "bred" by a straitjacket and proud of it is being mocked, and "sovereign", "deserves" and "privilege" are in scare quotes. The German puts quotation marks around "free" once (*der "freie" Mensch*) and around "deserves" once (*er "verdient"*), and around nothing else; the reader who takes the whole paragraph as irony has to explain why the marks are where they are and not everywhere, and why II:3 opens by treating the conscience just described as a real, late, ripe fruit with "a long history" behind it.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
II:2 is one paragraph, and it is the only passage in the published works that describes an individual as sovereign, autonomous and master of a free will in one breath. Every reading of Nietzsche on freedom passes through it, which is why the interpretive weight the paragraph is asked to carry is itself a finding: Christopher Janaway, in a 2007 essay on guilt in the Second Treatise, observes that the treatise from II:4 onward can be followed without settling what the sovereign individual is, and that nothing after II:3 says the man of bad conscience is one.
</KeyFact>

## II:3: pain as mnemonic, and the catalogue graded by the treatise's own rule

II:2 ended on "conscience", and II:3 opens by repeating the word with a question mark and turning it into history. "His conscience? It can be guessed in advance that the concept 'conscience', which we meet here in its highest, almost strange form, already has a long history and transformation of form behind it. To be permitted to vouch for oneself, and with pride, hence also to be permitted to say Yes to oneself: that is, as was said, a ripe fruit, but also a *late* fruit; how long this fruit had to hang harsh and sour on the tree!" Then the problem restated as a question about technique: "'How does one make a memory for the human animal? How does one impress something on this partly dull, partly scattered momentary understanding, this incarnate forgetfulness, so that it stays present?'" The answer is the section's thesis. "This age-old problem was, as one can imagine, not solved with exactly tender answers and means; perhaps there is nothing more terrible and uncanny in the whole prehistory of man than his *mnemotechnics*. 'One burns something in so that it stays in the memory: only what does not cease to *hurt* stays in the memory': that is a main proposition of the oldest (unfortunately also the longest) psychology on earth."

### What the catalogue documents

The evidence comes in two lists. The first is general: "it never went without blood, tortures, sacrifices when man held it necessary to make himself a memory; the most gruesome sacrifices and pledges (among them the sacrifice of the first-born), the most repulsive mutilations (for instance castrations), the cruellest ritual forms of all religious cults (and all religions are at the deepest level systems of cruelties): all that has its *origin* (*Ursprung*) in that instinct which guessed in pain the most powerful aid to mnemonics." Asceticism is filed under the same heading: "a few ideas are to be made inextinguishable, omnipresent, unforgettable, 'fixed', for the purpose of hypnotizing the whole nervous and intellectual system by these 'fixed ideas', and the ascetic procedures and forms of life are means to release those ideas from competition with all other ideas". A general law is then proposed: "the worse mankind's memory was, the more terrible the aspect of its customs; the severity of penal laws in particular gives a measure of how much trouble it had in winning victory over forgetfulness and keeping a few primitive requirements of social life present to these slaves of the moment's affect and desire".

The second list is the catalogue, introduced with the Germans as the case: a people that does not regard itself as especially cruel, "but one need only look at our old penal ordinances (*Strafordnungen*) to find out how much trouble it takes on earth to breed a 'people of thinkers'". The old German punishments: "stoning (the legend already lets the millstone fall on the head of the guilty), breaking on the wheel (the most characteristic invention and speciality of the German genius in the realm of punishment!), impaling, having the criminal torn or trampled by horses ('quartering'), boiling the criminal in oil or wine (still in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries), the popular flaying ('cutting into strips'), cutting the flesh out of the breast; also smearing the evildoer with honey and leaving him to the flies under a burning sun." And the yield: "With the help of such images and procedures one at last retains five, six 'I will nots' in the memory, in regard to which one has given one's promise in order to live under the advantages of society, and really! with the help of this kind of memory one at last came 'to reason'!" The section closes on the price: "how much blood and horror lies at the bottom of all 'good things'!"

### The rule of II:12 turned on II:3

The catalogue is the treatise's evidence for the mechanism, and the course grades evidence by the rule the treatise itself states. Recall from <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref> the proposition of II:12 that "the cause of the coming-into-being of a thing and its eventual usefulness, its actual employment and placement in a system of purposes, lie *toto caelo* apart", and II:13's list of eleven meanings that have been put into the one procedure of punishment, none of them its origin. One of the eleven is "punishment as the making of a memory, whether for him who suffers the punishment, so-called 'improvement', or for the witnesses of the execution". The mnemonic use is on Nietzsche's own list of uses.

Run the rule on II:3. What the old penal ordinances document is that punishment in the German lands was carried out with these procedures at these dates; that is a claim about the *durable element* of II:13, the act, and it is checkable against the ordinances, which exist. What II:3 adds is a claim about purpose: the procedures were the means by which "five, six 'I will nots'" were burned into a forgetful animal "so that" it could promise. That is a claim about the *fluid element*, the meaning attached to the act, and II:12 says that a present or past meaning of a procedure is the sign of the will that then mastered it, not of the procedure's coming-into-being. The sentence "all that has its *origin* in that instinct which guessed in pain the most powerful aid to mnemonics" is the exact inference II:12 refuses when it is drawn by "previous genealogists of morals": a purpose found in the practice, "innocently set at the beginning as the *causa fiendi*".

<Example title="grading II:3 by the four steps used on Rée">
**Step 1, locate the claim.** Sacrifice, mutilation, ritual cruelty and penal cruelty originate in the mnemonic instinct: pain was used to make memory, and memory was made so that man could promise.

**Step 2, apply II:12.** That punishment *served* to fix prohibitions in memory, if true, is a fact about a use imposed on the procedure by whoever administered it, and the eleven-item list of II:13 files it as one such use. A use cannot be read back as the *causa fiendi* of the procedure; the ordinances show the act and its severity, not the intention behind its first performance.

**Step 3, apply II:13.** The procedure is older than any of its uses, the mnemonic one included, and in an early stage the meanings shift their weight; "making a memory" can dominate in one era as deterrence does in another without being the origin of either.

**Step 4, state what II:3 now needs.** Independent evidence that the earliest penal and ritual cruelty was performed *with the mnemonic expectation attached*: a document that says the pain is inflicted so that the rule is remembered. II:3 offers the severity of the ordinances and the proverb about what hurts; the proverb is testimony that the mnemonic use was *known*, and the severity is compatible with every one of the eleven meanings.

**The verdict.** II:3 establishes that pain was a documented mnemonic technique, that it was recognized as one ("a main proposition of the oldest psychology"), and that the cost of a bred memory was paid in cruelty. It does not establish that memory-making was the origin of punishment, sacrifice or asceticism, and by the treatise's own rule it could not. The third exercise asks for the strongest version of the inference that survives the rule and for the evidence that would distinguish "punishment used to burn in memory" from "punishment that happened to".
</Example>

<KeyFact>
II:3 is the reader's first case of the method turned on its author: the catalogue is evidence that punishment was cruel, the mnemonic function is one of the eleven uses II:13 itself lists, and II:12 forbids reading a use as an origin. What survives is smaller than the section claims and enough for II:1: that a memory of the will was made at the cost of pain is documented as a technique, and the treatise's mechanism needs only the technique, not the origin.
</KeyFact>

## Two vocabularies in one paragraph: the readings of the sovereign individual

The grading of II:3 leaves II:1 and II:3 standing as mechanism plus evidence, which is what the naturalist reading of <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> predicts a section of the *Genealogy* will turn out to be. II:2 is where the prediction meets its first difficulty, and the difficulty is visible before any reading is applied: the paragraph runs two vocabularies side by side. One is the vocabulary of a process that produces a type. The other is the vocabulary of a standing that is earned and held.

The two readings below agree on two boundaries. The sovereign individual is not the overman of *Zarathustra*: the word *Übermensch* does not occur in the *Genealogy*, and II:2's individual is the product of custom, not of any overcoming of it in the book's sense. And he is not a causa sui: whatever "free" means in II:2, the paragraph says in its first sentence that he has a *Herkunft*, a descent, and BGE 21 had rejected the freedom that owes nothing to "ancestors, chance, and society" as a self-contradiction. The dispute is about what is left.

### Leiter: the sovereign individual as a natural artefact

The deflationary reading is Brian Leiter's, whose naturalist hypothesis <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> fixed as the course's; he states it for II:2 in a 2011 paper, "Who Is the 'Sovereign Individual'?", which calls the figure "a fortuitous natural artifact". The reading takes the left column literally. The sovereign individual is what the breeding process yields at its end, "the ripest fruit on its tree": a description of a type that has emerged, not an ideal that anyone is enjoined to become.

Each phrase of the right column is then mapped onto a text the course has already read. His "freedom" is a fact about his constitution, the "long unbreakable will" that BGE 21 calls a strong will and that the Doctrine of Types treats as a type-fact. His "consciousness of power and freedom" is the commander's affect of BGE 19, real as a feeling and evidence of nothing beyond the constitution that has it. His "measure of value", honouring the reliable and despising the whippets, is the noble self-affirmation of GM I:2, in which the type looks out from itself and names what resembles it good.

### The honorifics as a persuasive definition

What the reading does with the honorifics themselves, Leiter's paper names: Nietzsche "engages in what Charles Stevenson (1938) would have called a 'persuasive definition' of the language of 'freedom' and 'free will', radically revising the content of those concepts, but in a way that aims to capitalize on their positive emotive valence". Stevenson, the American philosopher whose 1938 paper in *Mind* introduced the term, meant by it the move of keeping a word's emotive charge while changing its descriptive content, so that the approval attached to "free" or "just" transfers to whatever the redefiner now applies the word to.

On this reading that is what II:2 does with "free", "autonomous", "sovereign", "privilege" and "deserves": the words keep the honour they carry in the morality Nietzsche is attacking, and their content becomes a bred reliability. The reading is continuous with I:13 by construction. A type that keeps its word because it is that type is a bird of prey that preys; calling it "free" adds no substratum, and the sovereign individual is the strong will of BGE 21 wearing the language of the free will it replaces.

The sentence the reading must explain is "liberated again from the morality of custom". On the deflation it can only mean that the type no longer needs the straitjacket that made it, a spring that stays compressed after the clamp is removed, and "autonomous" means self-regulating in the sense in which a thermostat is.

### Anderson: an achieved self, and autonomy as a norm

The rival reading is R. Lanier Anderson's, the Stanford philosopher whose *Stanford Encyclopedia* entry on Nietzsche the course has cited since its first lesson; the text for it is his 2012 chapter "What Is a Nietzschean Self?", which argues the reading from II:1 before it reaches II:2, and his 2022 reply to Leiter restates it for II:2 directly. It takes the right column literally, and it starts from what II:1 says the capacity *is*. The memory of the will is "an active not-wanting-to-get-rid-of" that persists while "a world of new strange things, circumstances, even acts of will" is interposed. Anderson reads that as the description of "a whole self" that "assume[s] a diachronically stable attitude of commitment, which persists through the alterations of the individual drives and controls action even in the face of their vicissitudes", so that "what characterizes the type is precisely that there is a difference between the self as a whole and the variable drives".

The argument from the chain is short. If the self were simply the dominant drive, as the deflationary reading of BGE 12 and 19 has it, then a drive that later became dominant would carry the "self" with it, and the chain would break by definition. II:1's chain does not break, and something has to be what holds it.

### The emergent self: neither the ruling drive nor a self behind the drives

That something is not the Kantian standing-back self. Anderson's chapter is written against two positions at once: the eliminativism on which "what speaks for 'the self' is nothing but the strongest or dominant drive", and a transcendentalism on which Nietzsche is committed to "a buried transcendental dimension", a self that stands back from all the drives. His third way is an *emergent* self, "a functional grouping of drives and affects" that is a psychological object in its own right because it is the structure within which drives recruit one another.

<Figure
  src="./figures/emergent-self.svg"
  alt="Two panels under the question who holds the I will do of II:1 across the interval. Left, in blue, the self as the dominant drive: four drive circles, the largest labelled drive a equals the self, with an arrow down to a box reading I will do; a note reads that if b later outgrows a, the self is b, and a's I will do has no owner, so the chain of the will breaks by definition.   caption="What holds the promise: on the deflation, whichever drive rules, so that a change of ruler breaks the chain; on Anderson's reading, the grouping itself, which can be at odds with one of its members, and that gap is what a broken promise is."
/>

What distinguishes the emergent self from a mastering drive is that it can suffer "a 'gap' between its own activity and that of some constituent(s)". The evidence Anderson offers is weakness of will: a person who acts against her own considered assessment is intelligible only if the assessment belongs to something other than the drive that won, and Nietzsche's criticism of weakness of will is "central to [his] core philosophical stances". A broken promise is the same gap in II:1's terms, a commitment held by the whole and overridden by a part.

On this basis autonomy is a norm and selfhood "a task or achievement". The sovereign individual is the case in which the emergent self has attained "a certain kind of unity, or greater integration, among the drives and affects" by "regulating control over them that is exercised by and through the attitudes proper to the emerging self", and that is "a recognizable form of autonomy. The self here follows values and laws it gives to itself." Individuality in II:2 "is not merely a thin, descriptive property possessed automatically by every single human being; on the contrary, it is a rare and high achievement".

The sentence Anderson must explain is "the ripest fruit" of a process that "society and its morality of custom" ran, together with "has become an instinct". An achievement is something its subject does, and the paragraph's grammar has society doing it; an instinct is the opposite of a law one gives oneself. Anderson's answer is that the process supplied the material and the self did the integrating, and that the sovereign individual is rare exactly because the material rarely integrates. The paragraph does not say this, and the reading supplies it.

The distinction the two readings turn on is stated most cleanly by Ken Gemes, in a 2006 paper on free will and the sovereign individual, in a form the course will reuse: the free will Nietzsche denies is *deserts* free will, the freedom that would make one accountable in the sense punishment needs, and the free will he affirms in II:2 is *agency* free will, the unity that makes a set of drives one agent at all.

## The strain: II:2 against I:13, and the two routes through BGE 19 and 21

The two readings are readings of one paragraph; the course's question is what the paragraph does to the argument of <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the previous lesson</Ref>. I:13 said there is no "being" behind doing and that the substratum "free to express strength or not" is composed by grammar and needed by the weak. II:2 says "this master of the free will, this sovereign" has "the consciousness of this rare freedom, this power over himself and over fate". If "free" in II:2 means what I:13 denies, the treatise contradicts itself across two sections written in the same summer, and the naturalist reading, which reads I:13 as a result, inherits the contradiction. If it does not, one of two things is true, and they are the two routes.

### Route A: "free" names a type

The first route runs from BGE 21 through BGE 19 and is the deflation carried through. BGE 21 leaves "only a matter of strong and weak wills"; a "long unbreakable will" is a strong will; and BGE 19 says what a strong will is from inside: "the affect of superiority with respect to something that must obey: 'I am free, "it" must obey'". II:2 uses the same noun. The sovereign "cannot fail to know what superiority (*Überlegenheit*) he thereby has", and BGE 19's phrase is *der Überlegenheits-Affekt*, the affect of superiority. On this route the sovereign individual's "consciousness of power and freedom" *is* BGE 19's affect, and the paragraph's proud language is the commander's report, *l'effet c'est moi*, of what the commonwealth of his drives reliably does. Nothing in it needs a chooser.

The route's cost is the right column of the vocabulary figure: "liberated", "privilege", "deserves" and "his measure of value" are the type's self-description, and on the Doctrine of Types a self-description is an expression of the type, true as a report of how the type feels and not a claim the course has to underwrite. That is a stable position. It is also a deflation, and it reads a paragraph Nietzsche wrote in the register of a hymn as if the register were decoration.

### Route B: "free" names an achievement

The second route reads I:13 narrowly and BGE 19 differently. I:13's target is the substratum that "could have" done otherwise, the chooser the weak need in order to blame; that is Gemes's deserts free will, and BGE 21's causa sui is its metaphysical form. II:2's individual is not blamed and blames no one; he vouches. What he has is agency, the unity that lets a plurality of drives count as one will across time, and I:13 says nothing against unity.

BGE 19 on this route is read for the word "commander": the section says "we are, under the circumstances, both the one who commands and the one who obeys", and a commander who is also the obeyer is not one drive among the rest but the whole in one of its relations; Anderson's emergent self is a name for that whole, and the "gap" between it and a recalcitrant drive is what a broken promise would be. The route's cost is the left column: "bred", "ripest fruit", "instinct". It has to say that the process is the material and the self is what the material became, and that "instinct" describes how the achievement feels once it is complete, which is the point at which the reading is supplying sentences the paragraph does not contain.

<Aside>
Nehamas's version of Route B is the one <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">the Nehamas lesson</Ref> will state in full, and it is worth one paragraph here because it starts from the same texts. He takes BGE 21's "strong and weak wills" with a notebook fragment on weakness of will as "the multitude and disgregation of impulses and the lack of any systematic order among them", against "their coordination under a single predominant impulse", and reads "freedom of the will so construed" as "not the absence of causal determination but a harmony among all of a person's preference schemes". The sovereign individual is then the limiting case of a unity that is achieved over time and never final, and *Twilight*'s "attained 'freedom of the will'" is its name. What distinguishes this from Anderson is what unifies: for Nehamas a single taste, an aesthetic organization that an audience can recognize; for Anderson a regulating control the self exercises. What distinguishes both from Leiter is that the unity is done, not bred.
</Aside>

### What the routes share, and what decides between them

Both routes pass through BGE 19, and they diverge at one word in it. If the commander is a drive, or a coalition, that rules because it is obeyed, then "free" in II:2 reports a rank, the type-fact of Route A. If the commander is the whole in its ruling relation, something that can be at odds with one of its own parts and still be itself, then "free" reports the whole's hold on itself, the achievement of Route B. BGE 19 does not say which, and <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drives lesson</Ref> is where the course asks the section whether its society has a government or only a physics, the question on which the political order of drives in the aside above also turns. Two things are settled here without waiting for it. First, the metaphysical freedom BGE 21 rejects is not in II:2 on either route: the sovereign individual has a *Herkunft*, and both readings keep it. Second, whichever route is taken, the sovereign individual is what the previous lesson's KeyFact allowed him to be, a commonwealth in which "a command given today is still obeyed at the promised date"; the routes differ on whether the commonwealth's holding together is a fact about its parts or a thing the commonwealth does.

<KeyFact>
The strain is not a contradiction. Both routes make II:2 consistent with I:13, at a price each pays in the other's column of the vocabulary figure, and the naturalist reading survives on Route A by deflating the paragraph's honorifics. What the strain shows is where the working hypothesis becomes a decision rather than a reading: a psychology of types can carry the sovereign individual only as a type with a proud name, and whether the *Genealogy* needs him to be more than that is what the treatise's account of guilt, which turns on whether the free-will lie is a lie about a chooser or about a self, will decide.
</KeyFact>

## What is established, and what the next lesson takes up

What is established, one result per section. II:1 makes forgetting an active faculty of inhibition. It makes the memory of the will a bred counter-faculty that suspends forgetting for the one case of a promise. It makes the promiser's own calculability the precondition of the capacity. II:2 names the process that met the precondition: the morality of custom, defined in *Daybreak* §9 as obedience to tradition as such, under which the free individual is the evil one. At the end of that process it places a sovereign individual "liberated again" from it, autonomous and *übersittlich*, whose consciousness of his own long will has become the instinct he calls conscience. II:3 states the mechanism, pain as mnemonic, and offers the German penal catalogue and the cruelties of sacrifice, mutilation and cult as its evidence. The treatise's own rule of II:12 grades that evidence as documentation of cruelty and of a known technique, not of an origin. II:2 runs two vocabularies, of a bred type and of an earned standing. Two readings divide them: Leiter's natural artefact under a persuasive definition, and Anderson's achieved emergent self. The paragraph is consistent with I:13 on two routes through BGE 19 and 21, the first reading "free" as a type-fact and the second as an achievement. The routes diverge at whether BGE 19's commander is a ruling drive or a whole that holds.

What is not established is which route the *Genealogy* takes, and the treatise's next move changes the subject in a way that will bear on the answer. II:4 opens: "But how then did that other 'sombre thing', the consciousness of guilt, the whole 'bad conscience', come into the world?" and calls it *other*: the conscience of II:2, a Yes said to oneself, is not the bad conscience the treatise's title names, and the origin of the second is not the breeding that produced the first. II:4 answers with a word, the German *Schuld*, which means guilt and means debt, and with an institution older than any morality. <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs that chain, from the creditor's right to hurt a debtor who cannot pay to a debt before God, and locates the step at which owing becomes being guilty; <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the reconstruction lesson</Ref> then puts the sovereign individual back on the table and tests which of this lesson's readings the whole treatise can bear.

**C.** The capacity to promise is a counter-faculty bred against the animal's healthy default, and it presupposes a prior process that made the animal calculable; the right to promise is an achievement with a history.

**What is left to supply.** The agent and the means of that prior process: II:2 names the agent (the morality of custom and the social straitjacket) and II:3 the means (pain as mnemonic). II:1 asserts "bred" (*angezüchtet*) and gives no breeder.

<Solution>
**The break.** Two sentences of II:2 are fatal to the Kantian reading. The sovereign individual is "the ripest fruit" of a process that "society and its morality of custom" ran, and the paragraph opens by calling itself "the long history of the *descent* of responsibility": a Kantian autonomous will has no descent, since its freedom belongs to it as a rational being and not as the product of a straitjacket. And his freedom "has sunk down in him to his lowest depth and has become an instinct, a dominating instinct": an instinct is the opposite of a rational will's self-legislation. I:13 then removes the "could have willed otherwise": the substratum "free to express strength or not" is composed by grammar and needed by the weak, and BGE 21 calls the freedom that owes nothing to "ancestors, chance, and society" a causa sui and a self-contradiction. The Kantian agent is a doer behind a deed by construction, and II:2's individual is presented as the deed's outcome.

**What Anderson must claim.** That "autonomous" in II:2 means neither a rational faculty standing outside the drives nor a will that could have done otherwise, but an emergent self that has attained a self-generated unity among the drives it has: "a more unified, more harmoniously ordered, more internally disciplined and effective 'social structure'", following "values and laws it gives to itself" in the sense that the regulating control is exercised through the self's own attitudes. That is autonomy as a *norm* attained by some and not others, not a faculty possessed by all rational beings; Anderson is explicit that Nietzsche "denies that autonomy comes built in as standard equipment along with humanity or rationality as such". On that reading the charge misses, because the Kantian content (rationality, universality, the could-have-done-otherwise) is not what "autonomous" carries.

<Exercise>
The lesson graded II:3's catalogue by the rule of II:12. Derive the strongest version of Nietzsche's inference that survives the rule, stated as premises and a conclusion, and then design the evidence that would distinguish "punishment was used to burn in memory" from "punishment happened to burn in memory": what document, or what pattern in the ordinances, would decide between them?

<Solution>
**What survives.** Drop the origin claim and keep the technique claim.

*P1.* Pain is a mnemonic: what continues to hurt is retained (the proverb II:3 quotes as "a main proposition of the oldest psychology").

*P2.* Early penal and ritual practice inflicted severe, public, memorable pain, and the severity is documented (the ordinances; the catalogue).

*P3.* The mnemonic use of such pain was *known* to those who inflicted it: II:13 lists "punishment as the making of a memory ... for the witnesses of the execution" among the meanings punishment has carried, and the proverb is testimony that the connection was proverbial.

*C.* Pain was among the means by which prohibitions were fixed in memory, and a bred memory of the will was in part paid for in cruelty. This is a claim about a *use* of punishment, one of II:13's eleven, and it is what II:1's mechanism actually needs: a technique that made a forgetful animal retain "five, six 'I will nots'". What is dropped is "all that has its *origin* in that instinct", which reads the use back as the *causa fiendi* and is the inference II:12 forbids.

**The distinguishing evidence.** "Used to" and "happened to" differ in whether the mnemonic effect was *intended* by whoever imposed the meaning, and intention leaves traces in two places. First, in the procedure's *design*: a punishment used as a mnemonic is calibrated to witnesses (public, spectacular, ritualized, performed at the place of the offence or on market days) and to durability of the mark (branding, mutilation that stays visible), whereas a punishment that is only compensation or only removal is calibrated to the injured party or to the offender alone; the ordinances record where, before whom and with what accompanying proclamation a sentence was executed, and a pattern of witness-directed staging would support "used to". Second, in *statements of purpose*: a sentencing formula, a law's preamble or a chronicle that says the penalty is inflicted "as an example", "so that others may be warned", "in memory of" the crime, states the mnemonic expectation attached, which is II:13's "fluid element" caught in a document; a corpus in which such formulas appear only late, or only for some offences, would date and delimit the use. What no evidence of this kind can show is that the *procedure* began for that purpose, since II:13 says the procedure predates all its uses; the strongest surviving claim is dated and use-shaped, and that is the shape II:12 says a genealogical claim has to have.

<Exercise>
Boundary probe. Stipulate an animal that never forgets: every impression it has ever taken in stays present. On II:1's account, could such an animal promise? State what the passage says the memory of the will is, why perfect retention would not yield it, and what that shows about the premise "forgetting is active and healthy".

<Solution>
**What the memory of the will is.** II:1 says it is "by no means merely a passive being-unable-to-get-rid-of the impression once scratched in ... but an active *not-wanting*-to-get-rid-of, a willing on and on of what was once willed". It is selective (it holds "certain cases: namely the cases where a promise is to be made"), it is a holding *of a will* and not of an impression, and it operates by suspending forgetting for that case only (*ausgehängt*).

**Why perfect retention fails.** The stipulated animal retains everything, so it retains the promise; but retention is exactly what II:1 says the memory of the will is *not*. Three things go wrong. First, the animal has no present: II:1 says there can be "no happiness, no cheerfulness, no hope, no pride, no *present*" without forgetting, and its picture of the never-forgetting man is the dyspeptic who "cannot get finished with anything"; an animal with no present has no standpoint from which a future act is *its* act. Second, retention is indiscriminate, so the promise persists on the same footing as every intervening impression, whereas the chain of the will requires that the promise *outrank* "a world of new strange things, circumstances, even acts of will" interposed between it and the act; the animal remembers that it promised and remembers with equal force every reason it has since acquired not to keep it. Third, and decisively, the memory of the will is a willing, "a willing on and on of what was once willed", and a retained impression is not a willing: the perfectly retentive animal has the *indigestion* II:1 explicitly distinguishes from the capacity, "the indigestion from a word once pledged that one cannot get finished with". So the animal could recall its promise and could not promise.

**What this shows about the premise.** Active forgetting is not a premise about an obstacle the capacity must overcome; it is a premise about the *shape* of the capacity. The memory of the will is defined as a selective exception to forgetting, and without a background of forgetting there is nothing for the exception to be an exception to: the doorkeeper is what makes holding one item an act rather than a condition. The premise is therefore load-bearing in a way a reader can miss: remove it, and II:1's distinction between a bred memory of the will and a bad digestion collapses, and with it the claim that promising is an achievement rather than a defect.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Research-shaped. Open *Daybreak* §9 in the critical edition (the course's copy is in the research cache; the eKGWB siglum is M-9) and run the sourcing pass of <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref> on it: what does it *record*, what does it *argue*, and what does it *omit* that II:2 adds? Then report whether II:2 changes the 1881 definition or applies it.

<Solution>
**Metadata first.** *Morgenröthe*, 1881, Book I, §9, an aphorism collection published six years before the *Genealogy*; the audience is the reader of a book of "thoughts on moral prejudices" (its subtitle), and the aphorism's purpose is announced in its first sentences: to state a "basic insight into the origin of morality" that "sounds coarse" and seems to "slander morality".

**Records.** A definition: morality is "nothing other, and above all nothing more, than obedience to customs", customs being "the traditional way of acting and evaluating"; a scope condition: no morality where no tradition commands; a lexical claim: in all original conditions "evil" means "individual, free, arbitrary, unaccustomed, unforeseen, incalculable"; a description of the feeling before tradition as fear of "a higher intellect that commands", called superstition; an enumeration of what originally fell under custom (education, health, marriage, medicine, agriculture, war, speech and silence, dealings with one another and with the gods); a ranking of "the most moral" as the one who sacrifices most to custom; a claim that under custom "originality of every kind has acquired a bad conscience". The definition and the lexical claim are the usable evidence; the enumeration is checkable against ethnographic and philological sources, which the aphorism does not cite.

**Argues.** That the free individual is *unsittlich* because he depends on himself rather than on tradition, "and is felt so even by its doer"; that the Socratic moralists of self-mastery for the individual's own advantage are the exception and "are, in the deepest sense, evil" from custom's standpoint; that the community feels an individual's guilt as its own and bears his punishment as its own. These are the author's verdicts on what the definition implies, and they are what the source cannot establish on its own.

**Omits, and II:2 adds.** §9 has no *end* to the process and no *product*: it describes the rule of custom as a standing condition and the free individual as its permanent outcast, "the evil and dangerous one" who "felt himself so". II:2 adds the tree and the fruit: the morality of custom was "only the means", it had an end, and at the end stands an individual who is free in §9's sense (self-dependent, outside tradition) and sovereign rather than evil. §9 also omits the promise: its free individual is the incalculable one, and calculability appears only as what custom demands; II:2 makes calculability the *precondition* of a new kind of freedom. And §9 omits the mechanism of pain, which II:3 supplies.

**Changes or applies?** Applies. The definition is unaltered (II:2 cites it and repeats its exclusion, "autonomous and *sittlich* exclude each other", which is a consequence of "morality is obedience"). What II:2 changes is the *evaluation* of the free individual, from custom's "evil" to the genealogist's "sovereign", and the change is a change of standpoint, from inside the rule of custom to its end, not a change of definition. The 1881 aphorism already says that the one who rises above custom "had to make customs" and become "a kind of demigod"; II:2's sovereign individual, who gives himself his own law, is that sentence read as a promise kept.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Translation comparison. Take the four German words the reconstruction of II:2 turns on, *souverain*, *autonom*, *übersittlich* and *Gewissen*, and compare Samuel's renderings ("sovereign", "autonomous", "super-moral", "conscience") with the German, and with Clark and Swensen's "supramoral" and the Cambridge "supra-ethical" for the third. State which rendering of *übersittlich* keeps the link to *Daybreak* §9's *Sitte* visible, and why Leiter's reading needs it visible.

<Solution>
**The four words.** *Souverain* and *autonom* are loanwords in the German as in the English, and Samuel's "sovereign" and "autonomous" carry them over intact; both are political words, one from the vocabulary of rulers, one from Kant's, and the German reader hears both as foreign in the same way the English reader does. *Gewissen* is the ordinary German for conscience and is rendered by the ordinary English; nothing is lost, and II:3's point that the word has "a long history and transformation of form" applies to both. The load falls on *übersittlich*.

**Übersittlich.** The word is *über* plus the adjective of *Sitte*, custom, and *sittlich* is the ordinary adjective for "moral" precisely because German morality-words are built on *Sitte*, as English "moral" is built on Latin *mos*, custom, without the connection being audible. In II:2 the word occurs beside "the morality of custom" (*Sittlichkeit der Sitte*) and inside the parenthesis "'autonomous' and '*sittlich*' exclude each other", so that the German reader sees three forms of one root in one sentence. Samuel's "super-moral" and Clark and Swensen's "supramoral" render *sittlich* as "moral" and so make the word a rank above morality; the Cambridge "supra-ethical" does the same with "ethical". None of the three shows that *übersittlich* means beyond *custom*; a rendering that did would be "supra-customary", or would gloss the term as Anderson's quotation of Clark and Swensen partly does by translating the neighbouring phrase as "liberated again from morality *and custom*". So the answer to "which keeps the link visible" is: none of the published renderings, and the Cambridge one least of all, since "ethical" has no customary sense in English; the link has to be restored by a gloss.

**Why Leiter needs it.** On the deflationary reading the sovereign individual is a type that the morality of custom bred and that no longer needs it, and "autonomous" means self-regulating rather than self-legislating. That reading is easy to state if *übersittlich* means "beyond custom": the fruit has outgrown the straitjacket, and §9's definition, morality is obedience to tradition, makes "autonomous excludes *sittlich*" a tautology, not a moral thesis. It is hard to state if *übersittlich* means "supra-moral" or "supra-ethical": then the paragraph is placing its individual above morality as such, which is the language of an ideal, and the persuasive-definition reading has to explain away one more honorific. Anderson's reading gains from the English and Leiter's from the German, which is a reason the course reads the German, and a reason <Ref lesson="key-terms-in-translation">the translation chapter</Ref> treats *Sitte* and *Schuld* as words no single English term renders.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Forward. II:2 ends with the sovereign individual naming his dominating instinct "conscience". II:4 opens by asking how "that other 'sombre thing', the consciousness of guilt, the whole 'bad conscience'" came into the world, and says its origin lies elsewhere. Write the two-word German clue II:4 gives for that other origin, and predict from it what *relation* between persons the treatise will name as the origin of guilt.

<Solution>
**The clue.** *Schuld* and *Schulden*: II:4 asks whether the genealogists of morals have ever suspected "that the main moral concept '*Schuld*' (guilt) has its descent from the very material concept '*Schulden*' (debts)". One German word does the work of two English ones, and II:4's whole opening is the claim that the moral sense of the word is the later one.

**The prediction.** If guilt descends from debt, the relation the treatise will name is the one in which debts exist: the relation between a *creditor* and a *debtor*. II:4 says so in its last sentence, that the idea of an equivalence between injury and pain "has its origin in the contractual relationship between creditor and debtor, which is as old as the existence of legal subjects at all and in turn points back to the basic forms of buying, selling, barter and trade", and II:5 opens on the debtor who pledges "his body, his wife, his freedom or even his life" as security for repayment, giving the creditor the right to inflict pain in place of the unpaid sum. That relation, and the chain of substitutions by which a debt to a creditor becomes a debt to ancestors, to gods and to God, is what <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs; the connection to this lesson is that the debtor who pledges is the promising animal of II:1 under a contract, and the memory the creditor has a right to make in him is the memory of the will, made by the means II:3 described.
</Solution>

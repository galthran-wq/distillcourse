Twilight of the Idols compresses the history of Western metaphysics into six sentences, from Plato's attainable true world to 'INCIPIT ZARATHUSTRA', and the argument in the compression is that once the true world is abolished there is no 'merely apparent' world left to be dismissed as false. This lesson reads the chapter that precedes the six sentences, "'Reason' in Philosophy", as the account of how a true world gets built and the four theses it ends on; reconstructs the six stages as a ladder on which one predicate moves and one pair stays; states the sixth stage as an argument with a premise about the word "apparent"; sets Maudemarie Clark's reading of that stage beside Martin Heidegger's; and takes from Clark the developmental fact the module needs, that the sixth stage is where Nietzsche stopped denying truth, and why it took him a decade.

The module has so far put a psychology under the genealogy: <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drives lesson</Ref> found a soul that is a society of drives with command as its relation, and <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the previous lesson</Ref> drew the line between will to power as a claim about what drives seek, which the treatises use, and will to power as a claim about what the world is, which BGE 36 argues for in the conditional and BGE 22 calls "only an interpretation". Two questions stood open at that line. The cosmological reading needs a "world seen from inside", an "intelligible character" of things, and both phrases are Kant's names for a world behind the one that appears; whether Nietzsche still had such a world for the doctrine to be true of is the first. The second is older: <Ref lesson="perspectivism">the perspectivism lesson</Ref> rested its reading of GM III:12 on the claim that the mature Nietzsche rejects the thing-in-itself, so that a perspective falls short of nothing, and it named this lesson as the ground that claim stands on. The six sentences answer both. Read as an argument they remove the true world and, with it, the only sense in which this world could be "apparent"; on Clark's reading that removal is the point at which the early thesis that all truths are illusions lost its premise. <Ref lesson="self-overcoming-staged">The next lesson</Ref> reads the *Zarathustra* speech in which the same history is told as one sufferer's biography.

The route: "'Reason' in Philosophy" §§1–5 as the genesis of a true world, with §5's "we still believe in grammar" as the doer fiction at the scale of metaphysics; §6's four theses sorted by what each is about; the six stages quoted in German and in Hollingdale's English and walked as a ladder; stage six reconstructed, the inversion reading refuted, and Clark's gloss set beside Heidegger's; Clark's account of stages four and five as Nietzsche's own books, with BGE 15 as the hinge; and the three consequences the module takes from a world with no behind.

## 'Reason' in Philosophy §§1–5: how a true world gets built

The chapter before the fable is where the thing stage six abolishes gets built. "'Reason' in Philosophy" is six sections of *Twilight of the Idols*, written in August and September 1888, and it gives the true world a genesis before the fable gives it a history; <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref> read the "Four Great Errors", two chapters later in the same book, as the catalogue of mechanisms by which a doer is composed, and this chapter is the same catalogue at the scale of worlds. It carries its subject in scare quotes: *Die „Vernunft" in der Philosophie*. The marks matter to the reading, and Clark's account of them is given below. First the chapter's own order, because it is the order of the *Genealogy*'s method and not of a treatise on knowledge.

### Egyptianism: the diagnosis comes before the epistemology

From a chapter on reason in philosophy one expects a theory of knowledge. §1 gives a type-diagnosis: "You ask me about the idiosyncrasies of philosophers?" The first idiosyncrasy is "their lack of historical sense, their hatred of even the idea of becoming, their Egyptianism", the word coined from the practice of embalming. Philosophers "think they are doing a thing honour when they dehistoricize it, *sub specie aeterni*, when they make a mummy of it"; everything they have handled "for millennia has been conceptual mummies", and "death, change, age, as well as procreation and growth, are for them objections, refutations even". The formula is two sentences long: "What is, does not become; what becomes, is not."

The section then narrates how a hatred becomes a doctrine. The philosophers believe "even to the point of despair, in that which is", cannot get hold of it, and look for the reason it is being withheld: "It must be an illusion, a deception which prevents us from perceiving that which is: where is the deceiver to be found?" The answer is the senses, "which are so immoral as well", and the moral follows: "escape from sense-deception, from becoming, from history, from falsehood". The senses are convicted not by an argument but by a need, the need of a type that cannot bear that things pass. That is the order the treatise used on the ascetic priest in <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref> and on the will-less knower in <Ref lesson="perspectivism">III:12</Ref>: the ideal is explained by what it does for a type before it is examined as a concept.

### The senses do not lie: what "reason" adds to their evidence

§2 states the counter-thesis in a form that fixes the chapter's vocabulary. Heraclitus, the Ephesian of about 500 BC for whom everything flows, is set apart "with high reverence", and then corrected: he too "was unjust to the senses", rejecting their evidence because it showed things "as if they possessed duration and unity", where the Eleatics, Parmenides's school, rejected it because it showed plurality and change. Both were wrong about the witness.

<Quote source="TI III:2, trans. Hollingdale">
<Original lang="de">
Dieselben lügen weder in der Art, wie die Eleaten es glauben, noch wie er es glaubte, — sie lügen überhaupt nicht. Was wir aus ihrem Zeugniss machen, das legt erst die Lüge hinein, zum Beispiel die Lüge der Einheit, die Lüge der Dinglichkeit, der Substanz, der Dauer… Die „Vernunft" ist die Ursache, dass wir das Zeugniss der Sinne fälschen. Sofern die Sinne das Werden, das Vergehn, den Wechsel zeigen, lügen sie nicht… Aber damit wird Heraklit ewig Recht behalten, dass das Sein eine leere Fiktion ist. Die „scheinbare" Welt ist die einzige: die „wahre Welt" ist nur hinzugelogen…
</Original>

The senses lie neither in the way the Eleatics believe nor as he believed: they do not lie at all. It is what we make of their evidence that first introduces a lie into it, for example the lie of unity, the lie of materiality, of substance, of duration. "Reason" is the cause of our falsification of the evidence of the senses. In so far as the senses show becoming, passing away, change, they do not lie. But Heraclitus will always be right in this, that being is an empty fiction. The "apparent" world is the only one: the "real" world has only been lyingly added.
</Quote>

The verb of the last sentence is *hinzulügen*, to add by lying, and it is built like the *hinzudichten*, to add by composing, of GM I:13's doer "added on to the deed": the true world stands to the world the senses show as the lightning stands to its flash, a second item made from the first by a habit of speech. §3 then says where knowledge is: "We possess scientific knowledge today to precisely the extent that we have decided to accept the evidence of the senses", and the rest is "abortion and not-yet-science: which is to say metaphysics, theology, psychology, epistemology", with logic and mathematics set aside as sign-systems in which "reality does not appear at all, not even as a problem".

The scare quotes on "reason" carry Clark's reading of the whole passage, and the reading decides whether the chapter contains the falsification thesis or its opposite. If "reason" named the faculty by which anyone counts two chairs, then "the lie of unity, the lie of substance" would say that every concept falsifies, which is the 1873 position. Clark's argument (1990, ch. 4 §2) is that the marks make "reason" the philosophers' interpretation of that faculty, a non-natural power of a priori knowledge that can overrule the senses, and that the "lies" are its products: not the ordinary enduring thing but the metaphysical substrate that does not change, not counting but "being". The evidence is §3, which treats logic and mathematics as formal and empty rather than as false, and §1's own account of the motive: the categories were not a mistake of observation but a weapon against becoming. Falsification in this chapter is done by a faculty that does not exist, and is therefore not inevitable.

### The last vapour placed first: God as *ens realissimum*

§4 names the second idiosyncrasy, "mistaking the last for the first". The "highest concepts", which are "the most general, the emptiest concepts, the last fumes of evaporating reality", are placed "at the beginning as the beginning", because the philosophers' reverence forbids that "the higher" should "grow out of the lower". So everything of the first rank "must be *causa sui*": "that which is, the unconditioned, the good, the true, the perfect", none of which can have become, and since supreme concepts cannot be incompatible with one another they collapse into one: "thus they acquired their stupendous concept 'God'. The last, thinnest, emptiest is placed as the first, as cause in itself, as *ens realissimum*", the most real being. The true world of the fable's first three stages is this concept's address.

BGE 2 had given the same genesis a name two years earlier, "the faith in opposite values". The metaphysicians' question is "how could anything originate out of its opposite? Truth from error, for instance?", and their answer is that "things of the highest value must have another, separate origin of their own", in "the lap of being, the everlasting, the hidden God, the 'thing-in-itself'". The section adds the doubt §4 leaves out, "whether opposites even exist", and the course's <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">method lesson</Ref> is the answer in practice: every treatise derives a high value from its apparent opposite by a real history. Clark's reading of BGE 2 (ch. 6 §3) makes the connection explicit: a true world is required exactly by a valuation that forbids the good to have a low origin, which is why the fable's history is a history of valuations as much as of doctrines.

### The seduction of grammar: the doer fiction at the scale of metaphysics

§5 supplies the mechanism, and it is the one the reader has. "Today we see ourselves as it were entangled in error, necessitated to error, to precisely the extent that our prejudice in favour of reason compels us to posit unity, identity, duration, substance, cause, materiality, being"; the advocate of the error is not the eye, as with the sun's apparent motion, but "our language". Language "belongs in its origin to the age of the most rudimentary form of psychology", and "the basic presuppositions of the metaphysics of language, which is to say, of reason", are then listed: "it is this which sees everywhere deed and doer; this which believes in will as cause in general; this which believes in the 'ego', in the ego as being, in the ego as substance, and which projects its belief in the ego-substance on to all things: only thus does it create the concept 'thing'". Then the derivation: "it is only from the conception 'ego' that there follows, derivatively, the concept 'being'".

<Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">The doer lesson</Ref> reconstructed I:13's charge that a subject-verb sentence reports one event twice, as the thing that acts and as the act, so that "the doer is merely added on to the deed". §5 is that charge run upward. The ego, taken as a substance because "I" is the subject of every sentence, is projected onto things; things become substances; substances need causes and the will is taken as "something which produces an effect", "a faculty", which "today we know is merely a word"; and at the top of the projection stands the being that is the cause of itself. The Eleatics' error of being had "the most direct power of persuasion" because "every word, every sentence we utter speaks in its favour", and even their opponents were caught: "Democritus, among others, when he invented his atom", which I:13 had already listed with "the Kantian 'thing-in-itself'" among grammar's "changelings". The section closes on the sentence the chapter is remembered by.

<Quote source="TI III:5, trans. Hollingdale">
<Original lang="de">
Die „Vernunft" in der Sprache: oh was für eine alte betrügerische Weibsperson! Ich fürchte, wir werden Gott nicht los, weil wir noch an die Grammatik glauben…
</Original>

"Reason" in language: oh what a deceitful old woman! I fear we are not getting rid of God because we still believe in grammar.
</Quote>

The sentence is the delay premise of <Ref lesson="madman-argument">the madman's argument</Ref> given a mechanism. GS 108 said that God's shadow would be shown in caves for millennia after his death; §5 says where the cave is. Grammar is the habit that keeps supplying a doer for every deed and a being for every becoming, and the last doer, the *ens realissimum*, is re-derived every time a sentence is spoken. That is why the fable that follows ends not with an abolition but with a second abolition: to remove the true world and keep the grammar that generated it is to keep its shadow.

<Remark title="'Reason' §§1–5 reconstructed: the genesis of a true world">
**P1.** Philosophers of a certain type hate becoming; change, death and growth are for them objections. Anchor: §1, "Egyptianism", "what becomes, is not".

**P2.** The senses show becoming and nothing else; they "do not lie". Anchor: §2.

**P3.** A type that hates becoming and cannot find being convicts the witness: the senses are declared deceivers, and "reason", a faculty of non-sensory access, is posited to reach what they hide. Anchor: §1, "where is the deceiver to be found?"; §2, "'reason' is the cause of our falsification".

**P4.** What "reason" delivers is supplied by grammar: the ego as substance, projected onto things as being, cause, thing, and the will as a faculty. Anchor: §5, "the metaphysics of language".

**P5.** The emptiest of these concepts, being that cannot have become, is placed first as *causa sui* and *ens realissimum*. Anchor: §4.

**C.** The "true world" is a doer added to the deed of the senses: a being lyingly added (*hinzugelogen*) to becoming, by the same habit that adds a lightning to its flash.

**The missing premise.** P3 is a type-diagnosis, and the chapter asserts it. Nothing in §§1–5 argues that the philosophers' preference for being came from a hatred of becoming rather than from the observation §5 also records, that the categories are used with a "subjective certainty" no experience seems to warrant; the section says that from that certainty "in India as in Greece they committed the same blunder" of inferring a higher origin "instead of a very much lower one, which would have been the truth", and the lower origin is asserted, not shown. The naturalist reading takes the missing premise from GS 354 and the Doctrine of Types: the categories are inherited herd-signs, and a type's valuations are what its constitution needs. §6's fourth thesis restates P3 as a claim about physiology, and the is–ought question it raises is flagged there.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
"'Reason' in Philosophy" contains no thesis that human knowledge falsifies: the "lies" of §2 are products of a faculty in scare quotes that the chapter denies exists, and §3 locates knowledge exactly where the senses are accepted. What the chapter argues is a genesis: the true world is grammar's doer at its largest scale, which is why removing God without removing the grammar leaves his shadow standing.
</KeyFact>

## §6: four theses, two about the world and two about who needs another

§6 compresses the chapter into four numbered propositions, "so fundamental and new an insight", and says why: "I shall thereby make it easier to understand, I shall thereby challenge contradiction." The course reads them in the same spirit, as the chapter's own reconstruction, and sorts them by what each is about, since the four are not of one kind.

<Quote source="TI III:6, trans. Hollingdale">
<Original lang="de">
Erster Satz. Die Gründe, darauf hin „diese" Welt als scheinbar bezeichnet worden ist, begründen vielmehr deren Realität, — eine andre Art Realität ist absolut unnachweisbar.

Zweiter Satz. Die Kennzeichen, welche man dem „wahren Sein" der Dinge gegeben hat, sind die Kennzeichen des Nicht-Seins, des Nichts, — man hat die „wahre Welt" aus dem Widerspruch zur wirklichen Welt aufgebaut: eine scheinbare Welt in der That, insofern sie bloss eine moralisch-optische Täuschung ist.

Dritter Satz. Von einer „andren" Welt als dieser zu fabeln hat gar keinen Sinn, vorausgesetzt, dass nicht ein Instinkt der Verleumdung, Verkleinerung, Verdächtigung des Lebens in uns mächtig ist: im letzteren Falle rächen wir uns am Leben mit der Phantasmagorie eines „anderen", eines „besseren" Lebens.

Vierter Satz. Die Welt scheiden in eine „wahre" und eine „scheinbare", sei es in der Art des Christenthums, sei es in der Art Kant's (eines hinterlistigen Christen zu guterletzt) ist nur eine Suggestion der décadence, — ein Symptom niedergehenden Lebens…
</Original>

First proposition. The grounds upon which "this" world has been designated as apparent establish rather its reality: another kind of reality is absolutely undemonstrable.

Second proposition. The characteristics which have been assigned to the "real being" of things are the characteristics of non-being, of nothingness: the "real world" has been constructed out of the contradiction to the actual world: an apparent world indeed, in so far as it is no more than a moral-optical illusion.

Third proposition. To talk about "another" world than this is quite pointless, provided that an instinct for slandering, disparaging and accusing life is not strong within us: in the latter case we revenge ourselves on life by means of the phantasmagoria of "another", a "better" life.

Fourth proposition. To divide the world into a "real" and an "apparent" world, whether in the manner of Christianity or in the manner of Kant (which is, after all, that of a cunning Christian) is only a suggestion of décadence, a symptom of declining life.
</Quote>

**The first thesis** turns the philosophers' argument around. The grounds on which this world was called apparent were change, becoming and plurality, the senses' evidence; those are, by §2, exactly what a real world shows, and "another kind of reality" is "absolutely undemonstrable" (*unnachweisbar*). Clark reads the last clause precisely: it does not say the empirical world is the only world, it says it is the only demonstrable one, and the demonstration in question is empirical, since §3 has just denied that there is any other kind. The thesis is the rejection of a priori knowledge stated as a thesis about worlds.

**The second thesis** says what the true world is made of. Its "characteristics" are the ones §4 listed, the unconditioned, the unchanging, the *causa sui*, and each is a negation of something the actual world has: it was "constructed out of the contradiction to the actual world", and is therefore itself the apparent world "in so far as it is no more than a moral-optical illusion". The compound adjective is the whole chapter: an optical illusion because it is a picture of nothing, moral because the need that painted it was a valuation.

**The third thesis** changes subject, from what there is to who needs the other world. Fabling (*fabeln*) about it "has no sense at all" unless "an instinct of slander, detraction and suspicion against life" is strong in us; then the other world is revenge, "a phantasmagoria of 'another', a 'better' life". That is GM III's charge stated for metaphysics: the ascetic ideal, which <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">the Third Treatise</Ref> read as a declining life's way of holding itself together, has a doctrine of two worlds as its epistemology, and III:12's will-less knower was already its philosopher. Clark's reading (ch. 6 §3) makes the thesis a diagnosis rather than a refutation: because the true world rests on a contradictory concept it can have no cognitive basis, so its persistence needs a non-cognitive explanation, and the explanation is the one the treatise gave.

**The fourth thesis** is the third stated as physiology: the division itself, "whether in the manner of Christianity or in the manner of Kant", is "a symptom of declining life". The course flags the step. "Symptom of declining life" is a type-fact in the sense <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> gave the term, and the thesis moves from "false" (theses 1–2) to "sick" (thesis 4) without stating the premise that would connect them; that is the is–ought seam the course has marked at A §2 and will take up where the positive doctrine has to say what makes a life ascending. The thesis also carries the exception the next section needs: "that the artist places a higher value on appearance than on reality constitutes no objection to this proposition. For 'appearance' here signifies reality once more, only selected, strengthened, corrected." The artist's *Schein* is not a second world but a treatment of the one there is.

<Aside>
The theses as a history of value-foundations. Bernard Reginster's reconstruction of Nietzsche's anti-objectivism (2006, ch. 2 §II) reads the first three stages of the fable as three sources of objective value: Platonic realism, on which values are "parts of the metaphysical furnishings of the world, where they wait to be discovered"; a divine command, which is stage two's promise; and Kantian rationalism, on which values bind because pure practical reason binds every rational agent. Reginster's Nietzsche rejects the first by explanatory economy, since moral facts explain nothing that a psychology of needs does not, and the second by Schopenhauer's objection that "absolute obligation is certainly a *contradictio in adjecto*", since a commanding voice "cannot possibly be imagined except as threatening or promising". What the two rejections leave is not a false belief but a predicament: values whose authority was supposed to come from a true world have no authority, and "'why?' finds no answer". That is the disorientation <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the nihilism lesson</Ref> separates from despair, and the fable's stages four and five are its first symptoms: a true world that "obligates" nothing (stage four) and that is "not even a duty any longer" (stage five). The fable is a history of an error about truth and, by the same sentences, a history of where value was supposed to come from.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
The fourth thesis' "symptom of declining life" is a type-fact asserted, not derived: the chapter passes from "false" (theses 1–2) to "sick" (thesis 4) without the premise that would connect them. Nothing in the fable depends on that step; its six stages read the same with theses 3 and 4 deleted.
</KeyFact>

## The six stages: the predicate moves and the pair stays

The fable is theses 1 and 2 told as a history: the one-page chapter that follows §6, with its own title and subtitle, takes the true world that the second thesis called a construction out of negations and walks it from its first occupant to its abolition, and the sixth sentence draws the first thesis' consequence for the world that was left. The title needs a translation note before anything else. The German is *Wie die „wahre Welt" endlich zur Fabel wurde*, and *wahr* is "true"; Hollingdale renders it "Real World" throughout and marks the choice in a footnote (*die Wahrheit* = the truth, corresponding to *wahre Welt* = real world), Kaufmann keeps "True World", and the course's concept carries the German. The translation exercise at the end runs the two renderings against the argument; the body quotes Hollingdale and writes "true world" in its own prose.

### The text, in two languages

<Quote source="TI IV, 'How the Real World at last Became a Myth: History of an Error', trans. Hollingdale">
<Original lang="de">
1\. Die wahre Welt erreichbar für den Weisen, den Frommen, den Tugendhaften, — er lebt in ihr, er ist sie. (Älteste Form der Idee, relativ klug, simpel, überzeugend. Umschreibung des Satzes „ich, Plato, bin die Wahrheit".)

1\. The real world, attainable to the wise, the pious, the virtuous man: he dwells in it, he is it. (Oldest form of the idea, relatively sensible, simple, convincing. Transcription of the proposition "I, Plato, am the truth.")

2\. The real world, unattainable for the moment, but promised to the wise, the pious, the virtuous man ("to the sinner who repents"). (Progress of the idea: it grows more refined, more enticing, more incomprehensible: it becomes a woman, it becomes Christian.)

3\. The real world, unattainable, undemonstrable, cannot be promised, but even when merely thought of a consolation, a duty, an imperative. (Fundamentally the same old sun, but shining through mist and scepticism; the idea grown sublime, pale, northerly, Königsbergian.)

4\. The real world: unattainable? Unattained, at any rate. And if unattained also unknown. Consequently also no consolation, no redemption, no duty: how could we have a duty towards something unknown? (The grey of dawn. First yawnings of reason. Cockcrow of positivism.)

5\. The "real world": an idea no longer of any use, not even a duty any longer; an idea grown useless, superfluous, consequently a refuted idea: let us abolish it! (Broad daylight; breakfast; return of cheerfulness and *bons sens*; Plato blushes for shame; all free spirits run riot.)

6\. We have abolished the real world: what world is left? the apparent world perhaps? But no! with the real world we have also abolished the apparent world! (Mid-day; moment of the shortest shadow; end of the longest error; zenith of mankind; INCIPIT ZARATHUSTRA.)

Each stage is one sentence about the true world followed by a parenthesis, and the parenthesis has two jobs: it names the stage's occupant, obliquely at first ("I, Plato, am the truth"; "it becomes Christian"; "Königsbergian", for the city Kant never left) and not at all after stage three, and from the third stage on it tells the time of day. The occupants of the last three stages are the reader's to identify, and the next subsection does so with Clark's help.

### Reading the ladder: what changes from stage to stage

The sentence's subject is the same in all six stages, and the count from the task is one: the lower box changes in the last frame and in no other. What moves through the first five is the predicate, and the movement is monotone. The true world is *attainable* (stage one: the philosopher "dwells in it, he is it"); then *unattainable for the moment but promised*, with a condition of entry, repentance, that stage one did not need (stage two); then *unattainable, undemonstrable, unpromisable*, and kept as a postulate of practical reason, "a consolation, a duty, an imperative" (stage three: Kant's thing-in-itself, which the first *Critique* declares unknowable and the second reinstates as what morality requires). Stage four draws the consequence stage three refused: unattained, therefore unknown, and "how could we have a duty towards something unknown?" Stage five draws the next: an idea that does nothing, not even obligate, is "superfluous, consequently a refuted idea", and the refutation is by uselessness, not by proof. The parenthetical day runs with the predicate: the "old sun" through Kant's mist, the grey dawn and "cockcrow of positivism", "broad daylight; breakfast", noon.

One typographical fact carries Clark's reading of the last three stages. Through stage four the true world is written without quotation marks; at stage five it becomes *die „wahre Welt"*, the so-called true world, and stays so. Stage four still speaks of a world that exists and is unknown, which is why it can only find it useless; stage five denies that there is one, and the marks record the change. Then the sixth stage does something the first five never do: it mentions the other member of the pair. The apparent world (*die scheinbare*) had been the unspoken contrast term of every predicate on the ladder, and the sixth sentence names it in order to remove it.

### The occupants of stages four and five: Nietzsche's own books

The first three occupants are named by their glosses; the last three are not, and the readings differ. Bernd Magnus put the positivist Auguste Comte at stage four and found Nietzsche only at stage six; Heidegger reads stage five as "the first segment of his own way in philosophy", the books from *Human, All Too Human* (1878) to *The Gay Science* (1882); John Wilcox, followed by Clark, finds Nietzsche's own development in all three. Clark's evidence is the fit. Stage four's true world is "unattained" and therefore "unknown", and *Human, All Too Human* §9 had said exactly that: "It is true that there might be a metaphysical world; the absolute possibility of it can hardly be disputed. We view all things through the human head and cannot cut this head off; though the question remains, what of the world would still be there if it had been cut off." HA 16 called the thing-in-itself "worth a Homeric laugh", "empty of meaning", and did not deny it. That is stage four without quotation marks, and Magnus's observation that the stage's tone toward positivism is "uncharacteristically gentle" is explained if the positivist in question is the author.

Stage five is *The Gay Science* and *Beyond Good and Evil* on the same test. GS 54 asks "What is 'appearance' for me now? Certainly not the opposite of some essence: what could I say about any essence except to name the attributes of its appearance! Certainly not a mask that one could place on an unknown X or remove from it!", and BGE 16 says of "immediate certainty", "absolute knowledge" and "the thing in itself" that each "contains a *contradictio in adjecto*". These are denials of the concept, not reports of its uselessness: the marks belong on the "true world", and the free spirits of the gloss are the *Gay Science*'s. What stage five has not yet done is the sixth sentence, and the course's reading of what that sentence adds is the next section's.

A reader who takes stage five for the end has read a history of the true world and missed the history of the error: the subtitle's *Irrthum* is the pair, and the apparent world enters the fable only in the sentence that strikes it.
</KeyFact>

## Stage six as an argument: the pair ends, it is not reversed

From a history that ends "we have abolished the real world" one expects the surviving world to be promoted: what was "apparent" becomes the real one, the sensuous takes the place the supersensuous held. The sixth sentence says the opposite, and says it as a question answered: "what world is left? the apparent world perhaps? But no!" The course reads the "but no" as the conclusion of a short argument whose first premise is about a word.

### The reconstruction

<Remark title="Stage six reconstructed">
**P1.** "Apparent" (*scheinbar*) is a relative term: a world is apparent as against a true one, and the tag has no content except the contrast. Anchor: TI III:6, second thesis, the "true world" was "constructed out of the contradiction to the actual world"; BGE 34, "if someone wanted to completely abolish the 'world of appearances', assuming you could do that, at least there would not be any of your 'truth' left either", which is the same relativity stated from the other side.

**P2.** The true world has been abolished (stage five): not shown to be unknown, but denied, on the ground that its concept is a contradiction (GS 54, BGE 16) and that what remains of it is "useless, superfluous".

**C.** Nothing is left for this world to be apparent as against; the tag comes off with the standard. "With the real world we have also abolished the apparent world."

**What the conclusion is not.** It is not that this world is now the true one: "true" was the other tag of the same pair, and P1 removes it by the same step. And it is not that this world is abolished: the sentence's "what world is left?" has an answer, this one, and the fourth thesis has just said that the artist's "appearance" is "reality once more". What ends is the pair.

**The missing premise.** P1 says the tag has no content but the contrast. That is true of "apparent" as the philosophers of §1 used it, where the deceiver was posited to explain why being was not perceived; it is not obviously true of every use, and Clark's reading of Nietzsche's own stage five (below) turns on a case where this world was called false against something that was not a "true world" but a hidden stratum of sensation. The argument is valid, and its first premise is exactly as strong as the claim that no other standard was ever in play.
</Remark>

### The inversion reading, refuted from the sentence

The correct claim first: stage six deletes both tags of the true/apparent pair and leaves one world that is neither. The reading the course rejects is the one Nietzsche's own phrase invites. A notebook of 1870 calls his philosophy "inverted Platonism" (*umgedrehter Platonismus*), and Heidegger, whose lectures are the reading's most careful statement and its most careful rejection, notes that Nietzsche "himself often expresses the state of affairs in that way", both "to make clear what he means in a rough and ready fashion" and "because he himself often thinks that way, although he is aiming at something else". On the inversion reading, stage six says: the sensuous is the true world and the supersensuous the apparent one, what was below is now above.

The reading fails on the sixth sentence's own words, and Heidegger's §24 says why: "If we take the inversion strictly in this sense, then the vacant niches of the 'above and below' are preserved, suffering only a change in occupancy, as it were. But as long as the 'above and below' define the formal structure of Platonism, Platonism in its essence perdures." An inversion keeps two slots and swaps their contents; the true/apparent pair is the slots. Stage six does not swap: "the apparent world can be what it is only as a counterpart of the true. If the true world collapses, so must the world of appearances." The replacement is a world with no above and no below, which is what P1 delivers.

The discriminating case is inside the chapter. If stage six were an inversion, the artist of the fourth thesis, who "places a higher value on appearance than on reality", would be its hero: appearance on top. The thesis says instead that the artist's case "constitutes no objection", because "'appearance' here signifies reality once more, only selected, strengthened, corrected". An inversion would need the artist's *Schein* to be a second, higher world; the text makes it a treatment of the one world, and the tragic artist "affirms all that is questionable and terrible in existence" rather than ranking a sensuous world over a supersensuous one. The phenomenalism exercise at the end runs the same test on that label.

Clark's objection to the rival column is textual. Heidegger's "nonsensuous world" that survives to be re-ranked slides, in his own paragraph, into "the nonsensuous world of the spirit", art and philosophy, and nothing in *Twilight*'s chapter is about those; the chapter is about whether the empirical world or another is the object of knowledge, and it says "any other kind of reality is absolutely undemonstrable". "If the nonsensuous world is the world accessible to a priori or nonsensuous knowledge, Heidegger gives us no reason to doubt that Nietzsche flatly denies its existence." Heidegger's reply, which the course only places here, is that the denial is itself a metaphysical decision, a last answer to the question what beings are, and that the answer is will to power: the reading needs Nietzsche to remain inside the question he claims to have ended, and <Ref lesson="alleged-biologism">the biologism lesson</Ref> is where that need is tested against the naturalist hypothesis.

The test the lesson runs is narrower and decidable from *Twilight* itself: whether "apparent" survives as a term after the fable. It does, in one form only. §2 wrote that "the 'apparent' world is the only one", with the adjective in scare quotes, the so-called apparent world, and §6's first thesis wrote "this" world in the same marks; after the fable the word appears in the fourth thesis' artist, redefined as "reality once more". The word is kept as the name of what others called this world, never as a description of it. That is the pattern the perspectivism lesson found on "objectivity": a term kept for its history and re-referred, the marks doing the re-referring.

## Clark's turning point: why a sixth stage had to be written

<Ref lesson="perspectivism">The perspectivism lesson</Ref> established that *On Truth and Lie* (1873) denied truth on a definition, truth as correspondence to the thing-in-itself, that the mature works reject, and that GM III:12 therefore denies foundations without denying that anything is true. What it took on credit is the history: when the definition was dropped, why the denial of truth outlived it, and what the fable has to do with either. Clark's fourth chapter is that history, and its thesis is that the fable's last two stages are Nietzsche's own account of it.

The chronology has a gap that needs explaining. The thing-in-itself is rejected as a contradiction in *The Gay Science* (1882) and *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886), yet those same books go on saying that knowledge falsifies: GS 107 speaks of "the general untruth and mendaciousness that is now given us through science", BGE 4 makes the falseness of a judgment no objection to it, BGE 24 has science keep us in "a suitably falsified world", and BGE 34 asks "why shouldn't the world that is relevant to us be a fiction?" If the denial of truth rested on the thing-in-itself, it should have gone when the concept went. It did not, and the fable's separate fifth and sixth stages, on Clark's reading, are Nietzsche recording that it did not: "that he puts the logical consequences of stage 5 in a separate stage gives strong evidence that Nietzsche later recognized his initial failure to appreciate the consequences of denying the thing-in-itself, which means that he himself went through a period in which he denied the thing-in-itself, but continued to characterize the empirical world as mere appearance or illusion."

### Stage five from inside: the hidden true world of sensations

What let a writer who had called the thing-in-itself a contradiction go on calling this world a fiction is the question, and Clark's answer (ch. 4 §4) names a second standard. GS 54, the section that denies the "unknown X", opens by discovering that "the whole primal age and past of all sentient being continues in me to invent, to love, to hate, to infer", and concludes that life is a dream that must go on being dreamed. That is the representational model of perception inherited from Schopenhauer, on which what is known is always a representation, and with the thing-in-itself gone it has one consequence: only representations exist, and what "falsifies" must falsify something inside them. The candidate, explicit in the notebooks and implicit in GS 354's "corruption, falsification, reduction to superficialities and generalization", is the "chaos of sensations", the given before the categories organize it; the a priori features Kant had made conditions of experience, unity, substance, cause, number, are on this view an inherited program the brain imposes on sensation for the species' survival, and what they impose on, the sensations, is what they misrepresent. On that picture GS 110's "erroneous articles of faith", that "there are enduring things; that there are equal things; that there are things, substances, bodies", are errors against sensation, and logic and mathematics falsify because they presuppose the substances (GS 111).

The picture is a stage-five picture: the true world denied, this world still "apparent". What it does not notice is that the chaos of sensations, equated with reality and knowable only by a theory about what precedes consciousness, is one more true world under another name, and the sentence in which Nietzsche notices is BGE 15.

<Quote source="BGE 15, trans. Norman">
<Original lang="de">
Um Physiologie mit gutem Gewissen zu treiben, muss man darauf halten, dass die Sinnesorgane nicht Erscheinungen sind im Sinne der idealistischen Philosophie: als solche könnten sie ja keine Ursachen sein! Sensualismus mindestens somit als regulative Hypothese, um nicht zu sagen als heuristisches Princip. — Wie? und Andere sagen gar, die Aussenwelt wäre das Werk unsrer Organe? Aber dann wäre ja unser Leib, als ein Stück dieser Aussenwelt, das Werk unsrer Organe! Aber dann wären ja unsre Organe selbst — das Werk unsrer Organe! Dies ist, wie mir scheint, eine gründliche reductio ad absurdum: gesetzt, dass der Begriff causa sui etwas gründlich Absurdes ist. Folglich ist die Aussenwelt nicht das Werk unsrer Organe —?
</Original>

To study physiology with a good conscience, we must insist that the sense organs are not appearances in the way idealist philosophy uses that term: as such, they certainly could not be causes! Sensualism, therefore, at least as a regulative principle, if not as a heuristic principle. What? and other people even say that the external world is the product of our organs? But then our body, as a piece of this external world, would really be the product of our organs! But then our organs themselves would really be the product of our organs! This looks to me like a thorough *reductio ad absurdum*: given that the concept of a *causa sui* is something thoroughly absurd. So does it follow that the external world is not the product of our organs?
</Quote>

The argument is short and its reach is the whole of stage five. The falsification thesis of GS and BGE rested on a physiology, "physiology and the history of animals" in GS 354's own words, which explains how sensations are organized by inherited forms; a physiology presupposes sense organs, bodies and the bodies they interact with as real things and real causes; so the theory that was to show the empirical world a fiction presupposes that part of the empirical world is not one. Either the sense organs are real, and the chaos of sensations is not the only reality, or they are constructions from sensation, and the theory has no organs to do the constructing: "our organs themselves would really be the product of our organs". The *causa sui* of the last clause is §4's *causa sui*, the mark of the true world, found inside the theory that was to abolish it. Once the argument is granted there is no empirical basis for equating reality with sensation, and the sensations, if they are to be the standard against which concepts falsify, have to be known a priori, which makes them a "true world" by the fable's own definition. Stage six is the recognition; the *Genealogy*, published the next year, opens on the sentence "for such truths do exist" (*denn es giebt solche Wahrheiten*), of the "plain, harsh, ugly, repellent, unchristian, immoral" kind, and neither it nor the four books after it calls science, logic or causality a falsification again.

<Remark title="Clark's turning point reconstructed">
**P1 (1873).** Truth is correspondence to the thing-in-itself, "the pure truth apart from consequences", and our concepts, two metaphors away from any X, do not reach it; therefore "truths are illusions". Anchor: WL; the perspectivism lesson's chain.

**P2 (1882–86).** The thing-in-itself is a contradiction: an essence is conceivable only through its appearances, so an essence independent of all appearance is "not a mask that one could place on an unknown X". Anchor: GS 54; BGE 16.

**C1.** P1's definition of truth defines nothing, and the ground for calling our truths illusions is gone with it.

**P3 (1882–86, unnoticed).** The world of representation is nevertheless a falsification of the chaos of sensations, on the authority of physiology. Anchor: GS 110–111, GS 354, BGE 4, 24.

**P4 (BGE 15).** A physiology presupposes real organs and real causes; a reality known only prior to all organization is known a priori, which is to say it is a true world. Anchor: the *reductio*.

**C2 (1887–88).** No standard remains against which the empirical world is "apparent": "such truths do exist", "the senses do not lie", and the sixth stage states C1 and C2 as one sentence.

**What "true" then means.** Clark's minimal notion: a belief is true if things are as it says, the equivalence "'snow is white' is true iff snow is white" that anyone who shares the word accepts, with no addition about independence from every possible knower. The label she gives the position is her own, *neo-Kantian*: truth is not independent of all cognitive capacities and interests, as Descartes's evil demon and divine guarantee required it to be, and it is not a matter of what works either, since that would discard the equivalence rather than explain it. The label is contested, most directly by readers who take "neo-Kantian" to import Kant's own doctrine of appearance, and the course states it as hers with the page (1990, pp. 31–34, 51–54) and uses the content, not the name.
</Remark>

### GS 354, and what the last holdover is about

One published passage after BGE says "falsification" (*Fälschung*) with a hidden standard still behind the word. *Twilight*'s §2 uses the verb too, "'reason' is the cause of our falsification of the evidence of the senses", but there the falsifying is done by the scare-quoted faculty the chapter denies exists, so it is not knowledge falsifying anything; GS 354 is the last passage in which the word still names something that knowing fails to reach, and the perspectivism lesson read it: GS 354, in the fifth book of *The Gay Science* (1887), on consciousness as a "connecting net between man and man" whose contents are "a surface- and sign-world, a generalized, a vulgarized world". Clark cites it twice, and the two citations are the two halves of her argument. In ch. 4 §4 it is evidence of the representational holdover, the sentence in which Nietzsche "certainly denied" that his falsification thesis depended on the thing-in-itself ("it is not the opposition of 'thing in itself' and appearance that concerns me here: we do not 'know' nearly enough to be entitled even to that distinction") while still leaning on the chaos of sensations to give the word "falsification" a victim. In ch. 5 it is read for its subject, which is not knowledge but consciousness, the small part of thinking that happens in communicable signs; what is "falsified" there is the individuality of an act when it is translated into herd-signs, "flat, thin, relatively stupid, general", and the section's closing sentence, "we have no organ for knowledge, for 'truth'", disowns the thing-in-itself rather than reintroducing it. The two readings are compatible: the section is about consciousness and its usefulness, its vocabulary is the old one, and it is the last place in the published works where the old vocabulary appears with a hidden standard behind it. On the naturalist reading it does the module's work: consciousness as a herd-organ is the psychology of <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">BGE 12 and 19</Ref>, on which willing and thinking go on beneath what is conscious of them, stated with the affirmation that what goes on beneath is real.

<KeyFact>
The sixth stage is GM I:1's "such truths do exist" written as history. Abolishing the apparent world is abolishing the falsification thesis, and Clark's evidence that Nietzsche wrote the stage about himself is BGE 15: the *reductio* that found a *causa sui* inside the physiology his own denial of truth had been resting on.
</KeyFact>

## What the fable does for the module

The module's question was whether the naturalist reading can keep will to power as psychology and refuse it as cosmology without doing violence to the texts. The fable settles three things that question needed, and the course states them as the module's own result.

**Interpretation is not falsification.** BGE 22 called the physicists' "conformity to law" and its own will-to-power reading of nature "only interpretation", and the previous lesson left the sentence disarming an objection without ranking anything. What the fable supplies is the status of the word. An interpretation falsifies only if there is a text-in-itself it misreads; with the true world gone there is no such text behind the appearances, and "interpretation" names a reading of this world among other readings of it, to be ranked, as III:12 ranks, by how many eyes it brings to bear. That is the ground the perspectivism lesson named and this lesson has now laid: a perspective falls short of nothing because there is nothing in itself to fall short of, and the equivalence "true iff things are as it says" is untouched by the fact that the saying is done from somewhere.

**The cosmological reading loses its object.** BGE 36 offered to read the world "on its 'intelligible character'", *auf ihren „intelligiblen Charakter" hin*, and the phrase is Kant's, the *Critique*'s name for a thing considered as it is in itself apart from its appearance; the previous lesson noted that the cosmology's vocabulary was the thing-in-itself's. After stage six there is no world behind the interpreted one for the doctrine to be the inside of. "The world seen from inside" can then be, at most, one more interpretation of the one world, which is what BGE 22 already called it, and the naturalist reading's verdict on the previous lesson, that the cosmological doctrine has the status of a hypothesis offered under a method and never an assertion, is now backed by the fable: an assertion would have needed a world the fable abolished. The rival reading takes the same sentence the other way, as the end of one metaphysics and the "highpoint" from which the next begins, with will to power as its answer to what beings are; the course has placed that reading three times now and argues it once, in <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">Module 7</Ref>.

**The drives are not appearances of anything.** BGE 36's own fourth step said it: the mechanical world was to be understood "not as illusion, a 'mere appearance', a 'representation' in Berkeley's or Schopenhauer's sense, but as something of the same rank of reality as our affects". With the pair gone that clause is no longer a supposition. The society of drives of BGE 12 and 19, the commanding affect, the type-facts the *Genealogy*'s explanations run on, are not the surface of a truer stratum, and the psychology beneath the genealogy is not beneath anything else; it is the only world the argument has, which is the naturalist reading's claim about what a philosophical psychology is for.

<Aside>
Where the fable is not a history of metaphysics. The reader with Plato and Kant will notice that the fable's first three stages are sentences, not doctrines, and the course keeps them so. Stage one is not the theory of Forms but the claim that the true world is "attainable" and that the philosopher "is it"; stage three is not the *Critique of Pure Reason* but a predicate, "unattainable, undemonstrable, unpromisable", plus the second *Critique*'s postulate. What Nietzsche needs from each is the one predicate the ladder moves, and Heidegger's commentary on the first stage, that "Plato's work is not yet Platonism" and that the true world for Plato "is not yet the object of a doctrine", is, whatever its own purpose, a reminder that the fable's occupants are names for positions on a scale of attainability. A reader who fills the stages with the doctrines will find the argument neither stronger nor weaker; the argument is stage six's, and it needs only that the ladder was climbed.
</Aside>

What is established. "'Reason' in Philosophy" gives the genesis of a true world as a type's revenge on becoming carried out by grammar, the doer fiction at the scale of being, and contains no thesis that knowledge falsifies: its "reason" is in scare quotes and its "lies" are the products of a faculty it denies. §6's four theses are two claims about what there is, one demonstrable world and a second built of negations, and two diagnoses of who needs the second, the fourth a type-fact asserted. The fable is one sentence about the true world with a predicate that moves from attainable to refuted while the apparent world keeps its tag, until the sixth stage names the tag and strikes it. Stage six is a valid argument from the relativity of "apparent" and the abolition of "true", it is a deletion and not an inversion, and Clark and Heidegger read it as a deletion while dividing on what remains. On Clark's reading the last three stages are Nietzsche's own books, the sixth was added because he had spent five years in the fifth, denying the thing-in-itself while keeping a chaos of sensations as a hidden standard, and BGE 15's *reductio* is where the standard fell; after it the published works keep ordinary truth, on the minimal notion, and drop the falsification thesis. For the module: interpretation is not error, the cosmological will to power has no world to be about, and the drives are not the surface of anything.

What stands open is the fable's last word. "INCIPIT ZARATHUSTRA" places a character at the point where the pair ends, and *Zarathustra*'s first part has a speech, "On the Hinterworldly", in which the two-world error is told as one sufferer's invention and cure: "it was suffering and incapacity that created all hinterworlds". The speech adds what the fable's six sentences do not state, a motive at the scale of a person, and it adds it as testimony. <Ref lesson="self-overcoming-staged">The next lesson</Ref> maps that speech, "On the Despisers of the Body" and "On Self-Overcoming" onto the module's reconstructed arguments, and states where the drama asserts what BGE 36 could not prove.

<Exercise>
Reproduce the six stages of the fable from memory in the form the lesson used: for each stage the occupant, the predicate the stage puts on the true world, and a one-word verdict from the set attainable, promised, postulated, unknown, abolished, both gone. Then mark the stage at which the quotation marks first appear around "true world", and state what the marks record.

<Solution>
1. Plato: the true world attainable for the wise, the pious, the virtuous, who "dwells in it, he is it"; verdict *attainable*.
2. Christianity: unattainable for now, promised to the same three, and to "the sinner who repents"; verdict *promised*.
3. Kant: unattainable, undemonstrable, unpromisable, but as a thought "a consolation, a duty, an imperative", the "Königsbergian" postulate; verdict *postulated*.
4. Positivism, and on Clark's reading *Human, All Too Human*: unattained, therefore unknown, therefore neither consoling nor obligating ("how could we have a duty towards something unknown?"); verdict *unknown*.
5. The free spirits of *The Gay Science* and *Beyond Good and Evil*: an idea useless and superfluous, "consequently a refuted idea: let us abolish it!"; verdict *abolished*.
6. Zarathustra: "with the real world we have also abolished the apparent world"; verdict *both gone*.

The marks first appear at stage five, *die „wahre Welt"*. Stage four still speaks of a world that exists and is unknown, which is why its only complaint is uselessness; stage five denies that there is such a world, so "true world" becomes the so-called true world, a name for what others believed in. The marks record the passage from agnosticism to denial, and they are the typographical evidence for Clark's placement of HA at stage four and of GS and BGE at stage five.

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "Stage six makes Nietzsche a phenomenalist. Once the true world is gone, only appearances exist, so his position is that the world is the sum of its appearances." Use the reconstruction's first premise and BGE 34, state what survives stage six and under what description, and say why the label reintroduces what the stage removed.

<Solution>
The claim fails on P1. "Appearance" in the philosophers' sense is a relative term, appearance as against a true world; the sixth stage abolishes the true world and therefore, by P1, the sense in which anything is "mere appearance". To say afterwards that "only appearances exist" is to keep the word after its contrast is gone, and the word then has no content: an appearance of nothing, to no one in particular, is not an appearance in the sense the pair gave it. BGE 34 makes the same point from the other side: abolish the "world of appearances" and "there would not be any of your 'truth' left either", because the two tags stand or fall together. Phenomenalism, the doctrine that physical objects are constructions out of sense-experience, needs the tag to have content, since it needs something (sensations, sense-data) for the objects to be constructions *of*; that is exactly the hidden stratum Clark's stage five ran on, the chaos of sensations, and BGE 15's *reductio* is Nietzsche's own rejection of it: the sense organs that phenomenalism would construct out of sensation are presupposed by the physiology that describes the sensations.

What survives stage six is "this world", the one §6's first thesis writes in scare quotes and §3 locates where the senses are accepted, under no description from the pair: not "true", not "apparent", the empirical world with its truths true on the minimal notion. The label "phenomenalist" reintroduces the pair by supplying, as the world's constituents, the very items (appearances) that were defined by contrast with the abolished term; a reader who wants a label has "the senses do not lie" and the *Genealogy*'s "such truths do exist", neither of which is a theory of what the world is made of. The fourth thesis' artist is the discriminating case: his "appearance" is "reality once more, only selected, strengthened, corrected", which is a phenomenalist's sentence only if one deletes "reality" from it.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Derive from stage six alone why *On Truth and Lie*'s thesis cannot be stated in 1888. Write the essay's premise about truth in one sentence, show which of its terms stage six removes, and state what "true" must then mean, using the minimal constraint the lesson took from Clark. Then say what the derivation does not settle, and where in the lesson it is settled.

<Solution>
**The 1873 premise.** A belief or word is true only if it corresponds to the thing-in-itself, "the pure truth apart from consequences"; since language reaches the X only through two metaphorical leaps (stimulus to image, image to sound), nothing corresponds, and "truths are illusions".

**The term removed.** Stage six abolishes the true world, and Clark's footnote to the fable is exact about what that world is at every stage but the last: "true being is actually ascribed to the thing-in-itself in all but the sixth stage". The premise's standard, correspondence to the thing-in-itself, therefore has no relatum after stage six. It is not that our words fail to correspond to it; it is that "corresponds to the thing-in-itself" is a relation with an empty place on one side, and a definition of truth in terms of it defines nothing. With the definition gone, the conclusion "truths are illusions" has no premise, since "illusion" was "failure to correspond to the X".

**What "true" must then mean.** Whatever survives of the concept when the metaphysical addition is removed, and the minimal constraint is the equivalence: "S" is true if and only if S, "snow is white" is true iff snow is white. Anyone who uses "true" or *wahr* accepts instances of this schema, so any account of truth must entail them, and an account that broke them (Danto's pragmatic reading, on which "God exists" could be true though God does not exist) would not be a theory of truth but a proposal to use the word differently. Stage six is compatible with the schema and adds nothing to it; the *Genealogy*'s "such truths do exist" is the schema applied to the treatise's own claims.

**What the derivation does not settle.** That stage six is the only route to the conclusion. A writer could abolish the thing-in-itself and still call this world false against a different standard, and that is what Clark finds Nietzsche doing between 1882 and 1886, with the chaos of sensations as the standard. The derivation from stage six removes the 1873 premise; it takes BGE 15's *reductio*, reconstructed in the section on Clark's turning point, to remove the substitute, by showing that a reality known only prior to all organization is itself a "true world" in the fable's sense. Only with both removed does "true" mean the minimal notion and nothing more.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Suppose a philosopher stops at stage five: the true world abolished as useless and contradictory, this world still called "apparent" or "a fiction". State what that position is, in the terms of Clark's chapter, and then take two of Nietzsche's own sentences, BGE 22's "only an interpretation" and §6's first thesis, and say what the stage-five philosopher is forced to read each of them as. Conclude by stating why stage six is needed for BGE 22 to be consistent.

<Solution>
**The position.** Stage five with this world still "apparent" is the position Clark attributes to *The Gay Science* and *Beyond Good and Evil*: a denial of the thing-in-itself as a contradiction combined with the falsification thesis, held together by a standard other than the true world, the chaos of sensations known by a physiology. It is unstable for the reason BGE 15 gives, but stated as a position it is: there is one world, and everything we believe about it is false.

**BGE 22.** "Only an interpretation" must then mean "only a falsification", since for the stage-five philosopher an interpretation of this world is a reading of something that the reading misrepresents. The section's last sentence, "so much the better", becomes unintelligible: a writer who holds that all interpretation falsifies has no reason to welcome the objection that his own reading is one, and the physicists' "text" that §22 says their laws misread would itself have to be a falsification, leaving nothing for "bad philology" to be bad about. The stage-five reader has to take "only an interpretation" as a confession of error, which is the falsification thesis in the doctrine's own mouth.

**§6's first thesis.** "The grounds upon which 'this' world has been designated as apparent establish rather its reality" cannot be held at all by the stage-five philosopher, since it asserts the reality of the world he still calls apparent. He is forced to read it as a slip, or to read "reality" as one more ranked appearance, which turns the thesis into "this world is apparent, and its being apparent is what makes it real", a sentence with no content.

**Why stage six is needed.** BGE 22 is consistent only if "interpretation" does not entail "false", and that requires that there be no text-in-itself behind the appearances which an interpretation could misrepresent, and no substitute for it either. Stage five removes the first but, as long as the world remains "apparent", keeps a standard against which readings fail; stage six removes the standard by removing the tag, so that interpretations of this world are readings of it, rankable by III:12's comparative and false only when things are not as they say. Without stage six "so much the better" is an admission; with it, it is the claim that the physicists' reading and Nietzsche's are two eyes on one world, which is what the sentence says.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run a corroboration pass on two statements of one charge. The first is §6's fourth thesis: dividing the world into true and apparent is "a symptom of declining life". The second is GM III:12's account of what the ascetic priest's will does when it philosophizes: it "will look for error precisely where the real life-instinct posits truth most unconditionally", degrades "corporeality to an illusion, likewise pain, multiplicity", and triumphs in reason's decree "there is a realm of truth and being, but reason is excluded from it". State what each passage is about, where they agree and where they differ in kind, and then report whether Clark's chapter on the ascetic ideal (1990, ch. 6 §3) supports reading the fable as a genealogical result and not only an epistemological one.

<Solution>
**Subjects.** The fourth thesis is about a doctrine, the two-world division in either of its two great forms, and classifies it as a symptom: a physiological claim about the type that produces it. III:12 is about a type, the ascetic priest "brought to philosophize", and describes what that type's will to contradiction does to the objects it meets: it convicts of error whatever life most unconditionally takes for real, the body, pain, plurality, and it finishes by making reason exclude itself from being.

**Agreement.** Both say that a two-world metaphysics is what a certain kind of life produces, not what an argument produces; both name the same objects as the victims (the body and becoming in TI III:1, corporeality and multiplicity in III:12); and both end at the same doctrine, "a realm of truth and being" from which the knower is shut out, which is stage three's "unattainable, undemonstrable" true world. The Vedanta ascetic and Kant's "intelligible character" are III:12's two examples, and Christianity and Kant are the fourth thesis' two.

**Difference in kind.** III:12 is a mechanism: a will to contradiction turned on its own faculties, with the pleasure it takes ("a voluptuous pleasure") as the mark of the drive at work. The fourth thesis is a verdict with the mechanism left out: "symptom of declining life" names the type without saying how the type produces the doctrine. The corroboration runs one way: III:12 supplies what the thesis asserts, and the thesis without III:12 is an is–ought step with no argument in it.

**Clark's chapter.** It supports the genealogical reading, and it locates the argument in BGE 2 rather than in either passage. Her claim is that Nietzsche counts a philosophical doctrine an expression of the ascetic ideal only "insofar as it demands self-denial in the service of an interpretation that devalues natural human life", and that BGE 2's "faith in opposite values" is his account of how metaphysics does so: the highest values are forbidden a low origin, so a "true world" is needed as their source, and the true world is what devalues this one. On that reading the fable's stages are stages of a valuation, not only of a theory of knowledge; the "moral" in the second thesis' "moral-optical illusion" is the faith in opposite values, and the fable is the *Genealogy*'s method applied to the concept of truth. Clark adds a caution the corroboration should keep: in HA the same genesis was "an innocent mistake" of observation, and only in BGE does it become a diagnosis, so the genealogical reading is of the mature text and not of the position as it was first held.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the translation comparison on *wahre Welt*. Hollingdale renders it "real world" throughout the fable and notes the choice; Kaufmann renders it "true world". State which English word keeps the contrast with *scheinbar* ("apparent") tight and which drifts toward "existing", with the sentence of the fable in which the drift matters most. Then say what the sixth stage's sentence loses under "real", and what, if anything, it gains.

<Solution>
**The contrast.** *Wahr* is "true", and the pair *wahr* / *scheinbar* is the pair true / apparent, in which "apparent" means "seeming rather than being what it seems": a seeming is a seeming-to-be-true, so the two words are relative to each other exactly as P1 requires. "Real" is the contrary of "unreal" or "imaginary", not of "apparent"; an apparent world can be perfectly real (it appears, and the appearing is real), and a real world need not be true of anything. "True" keeps the contrast tight; "real" drifts toward existence.

**Where the drift matters.** Stage five: *eine widerlegte Idee*, "a refuted idea". An idea of a *true* world can be refuted, since it makes a claim about what is true; an idea of a *real* world is refuted only by showing the world does not exist, which is not what stage five does, it shows the idea useless. Under "real" the fifth stage reads as an existence-denial by fiat, and Clark's point about the quotation marks, that stage five moves from "unknown" to "so-called", is harder to see.

**What the sixth stage loses.** Its argument. "With the true world we have also abolished the apparent world" is valid because "apparent" is defined against "true"; "with the real world we have also abolished the apparent world" invites the reply that appearances are real, and the reader is left with an existence claim (the apparent world does not exist) that the stage does not make and the fourth thesis' artist contradicts. The punchline under "real" sounds like the abolition of a second world; under "true" it is the abolition of a pair of tags.

**What it gains.** One thing, and Hollingdale's footnote names it: *die Wahrheit* in "I, Plato, am the truth" is unmistakably "the truth", so a translator who writes "real world" has to tell the reader that *wahr* is "true" in a note, which Hollingdale does; and "real" keeps the English reader from hearing "true" as "correct", as though the fable were about propositions rather than about worlds. The course's judgment is the one the method lesson on <Ref lesson="key-terms-in-translation">load-bearing terms</Ref> set: where the premise depends on a contrast, the rendering that preserves the contrast is the one the reconstruction runs on, and the other is recorded as a gloss.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
The fable ends "INCIPIT ZARATHUSTRA". Read Zarathustra's speech "On the Hinterworldly" (*Von den Hinterweltlern*, Part I) and write out the one sentence in it that states the two-world error as a sufferer's invention. State which stage of the fable the speech dramatizes, what the speech adds that the six sentences do not contain, and what the speaker says about the "thing in itself" of those who preach hinterworlds.

<Solution>
**The sentence.** "It was suffering and incapacity that created all hinterworlds, and that brief madness of happiness that only the most suffering person experiences." The speech's second statement of it is "Weariness that wants its ultimate with one great leap, with a death leap; a poor unknowing weariness that no longer even wants to will: that created all gods and hinterworlds."

**The stage.** The speech is told in the first person as a biography: "Once Zarathustra too cast his delusion beyond humans, like all hinterworldly", the world then seeming "the work of a suffering and tortured god"; then the cure, "I overcame myself, my suffering self, I carried my own ashes to the mountain"; then the ghost shrinking. That is stages one to three compressed into one life (a true world attained, promised, postulated) and stages five and six as the convalescence: the god "was of human make and madness, like all gods", "it did not come to me from beyond". The speech dramatizes the whole ladder as one climb, and the fable's own last word says so: Zarathustra begins where the sixth stage is reached.

**What it adds.** A motive at the scale of a person. The fable's six sentences move a predicate and name a time of day; they say nothing about why the first stage was ever occupied. The speech supplies "suffering and incapacity" and "the body that despaired of the body", which is §6's third and fourth theses (revenge on life; a symptom of declining life) given as testimony, with the type-diagnosis the fourth thesis asserts now spoken by a member of the type about himself. The addition has the authority a dramatization can give and no argument can: a sufferer reporting what his suffering made.

**The thing in itself.** Of the "next-to-godliness types" who preach hinterworlds the speech says "they too believe most in the body, and their own body is to them their thing in itself. But to them it is a sickly thing, and gladly would they jump out of their skin." The Kantian term is turned into a diagnosis: the preacher's *Ding an sich* is the body he cannot bear, and the hinterworld is the exit he invents. That is the fable's stage three with the fourth thesis inside it. <Ref lesson="self-overcoming-staged">The next lesson</Ref> maps the speech sentence by sentence against BGE 12, 19 and the fable, and states what the staging drops: the "suppose" that BGE 36 kept and the argument the fable made.
</Solution>

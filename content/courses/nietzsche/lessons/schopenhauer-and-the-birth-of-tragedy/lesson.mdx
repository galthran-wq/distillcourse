Schopenhauer argued that a life of wanting swings between pain and boredom and is better not lived; the young Nietzsche accepted every premise of that argument except its conclusion.

That looks like a logical mistake. If the premises of an argument are true and the conclusion follows from them, nobody can keep the premises and drop the conclusion. The only honest way to do it is to show that the conclusion does *not* follow: that somewhere between the description of life and the verdict on it, the argument uses a further step, and that this step can be replaced.

Schopenhauer's description is hard to fault, and it needs no religion: he was an atheist, and his argument uses no God and no afterlife. If it is right, the obvious replies fail. Saying that life is mostly pleasant denies the description. Saying that suffering will be repaid in heaven needs the world behind the world that Schopenhauer had already given up. In 1872 the twenty-seven-year-old professor of Greek at Basel, still Schopenhauer's disciple and Wagner's ally (<Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="a-professor-before-his-doctorate-18441876">period 1 of his life</Ref>), took a third route in *The Birth of Tragedy* (BT). He looked for a people who had seen exactly what Schopenhauer saw and had lived anyway, and he found them in the Greeks who invented tragedy.

## Schopenhauer's case: every want is a pain, and its end is boredom

Schopenhauer's argument is in his main work, *The World as Will and Representation* (1818, enlarged 1844). To see what Nietzsche accepted from it, the argument has to be rebuilt in its own steps, starting from what it says the world is.

<Figure src="./figures/schopenhauer-1859.jpg" alt="Black-and-white photograph of Arthur Schopenhauer at about seventy: white hair standing out at both sides of a high bald forehead, deep-lined face, thin tight mouth, white side-whiskers, a white cravat under a dark high-collared coat, one hand resting in front of him." caption="Schopenhauer in Frankfurt in 1859, a year before his death." float="right" width="m" />

### The world from inside: one blind will

Schopenhauer starts from Kant, the philosopher who argued in 1781 that space, time and cause are forms our own minds impose on whatever we perceive. If so, we know things only as they *appear* to us, never as they are *in themselves*. Kant held that the thing in itself stays unknown.

Schopenhauer answered that there is one place where we reach it. Each of us knows one object twice: our own body, which we see from outside like any other object, and which we also know from inside, as wanting, striving, pushing. That inner side he called *will*, and he concluded that it is the inner nature of everything, the force in a falling stone and a growing plant as much as in a hungry animal. It is blind: it has no goal beyond going on. And because space and time, which divide the world into separate things, belong only to appearance, the will behind them is one. Separate individuals exist only in appearance; Schopenhauer called the principle that splits the one will into many the *principium individuationis*, the principle of individuation. That is step 1 of his argument: behind appearances there is one blind will, and we are it.

<Remark title="The will to live">
Schopenhauer's name for the blind, insatiable striving that he held to be the inner nature of everything, ourselves included. It is not a choice anyone makes: it is what each of us is.
</Remark>

This is a world behind the world, like Plato's Forms in the course prerequisites, with one difference that matters for everything that follows. Plato's true world is good and rational. Schopenhauer's is a striving that wants without end and without reason.

### The pendulum

What such a will does to a creature made of it follows in four more steps. **Step 2:** every want starts from a lack, and a lack is felt as pain; thirst is the paradigm. **Step 3:** getting what you want removes the pain and adds nothing: the drink ends the thirst, and once the thirst is gone the drink gives no pleasure at all. **Step 4:** if nothing is left to want, the result is not peace but boredom. **Step 5:** a new want arrives, because the will never stops, so life swings between the two.

<Quote source="WWR I §57, trans. E. F. J. Payne, p. 312">
Willing and striving are its whole essence, and can be fully compared to an unquenchable thirst. The basis of all willing, however, is need, lack, and hence pain, and by its very nature and origin it is therefore destined to pain. If, on the other hand, it lacks objects of willing, because it is at once deprived of them again by too easy a satisfaction, a fearful emptiness and boredom come over it … Hence its life swings like a pendulum to and fro between pain and boredom, and these two are in fact its ultimate constituents. This has been expressed very quaintly by saying that, after man had placed all pains and torments in hell, there was nothing left for heaven but boredom.
</Quote>

<Task>
Before frame 4, name one pleasure in your own day that did not end a want you had already felt. Then look at the box in frame 4: your example is aimed at step 3, and nowhere else.
</Task>

Everything rests on step 3. If some satisfactions were *positive*, a good felt in itself and not merely the end of a pain, the pendulum would have a point worth reaching. Schopenhauer denies it outright: "All satisfaction, or what is commonly called happiness, is really and essentially always negative only, and never positive" (WWR I §58). Pain announces itself; its absence is noticed only by comparison, and the comparison fades.

### The verdict, and the way out

From the pendulum Schopenhauer draws a verdict: existence is worse than non-existence. A life whose best moments are the absence of pain, and whose idle moments are boredom, would be better never begun. Since the trouble is the will itself, the cure is to stop willing. Art gives a brief release: absorbed in a painting or a piece of music, a person contemplates without wanting anything, and for that moment the pendulum stops. Compassion and the ascetic's self-denial give a lasting one, because they see through the separation of individuals to the one will suffering in all of them and refuse to go on feeding it. Tragedy, on his account, teaches exactly this lesson: it shows

<Quote source="WWR II ch. 37, as quoted in BT, Attempt at a Self-Criticism 6, trans. W. A. Haussmann">
that the world, that life, cannot satisfy us thoroughly, and consequently is *not worthy* of our attachment. In this consists the tragic spirit: it therefore leads to *resignation*.
</Quote>

This verdict is *Schopenhauer's pessimism*: a claim about the value of existence, not a gloomy mood. Now look at how it is reached. Steps 1 to 5 describe what living is like. The verdict, step 6, adds something the description does not contain: a standard for judging a life, namely what it feels like to the one who lives it, its balance of felt pain against felt pleasure. Given that standard and step 3, the balance can never come out positive, and the verdict follows. Without a standard, a description of a life, however bleak, says nothing about whether it is worth living.

<KeyFact>
The description and the verdict are joined by a premise the pendulum never states: that a life is judged by what it feels like to live it. Keeping the description and refusing the verdict is consistent only if that standard is replaced, and *The Birth of Tragedy* replaces it.
</KeyFact>

## Silenus: the Greeks knew all this and lived

If Schopenhauer is right, a people that sees clearly what existence is should turn away from it, and he pointed to the religions of India, which teach release from the cycle of desire, as the people who had. The Greeks seemed to be the opposite case. German culture since the eighteenth century had pictured them as serene and cheerful, a people of white marble and noble simplicity, and on that picture the Greeks lived well because they never looked. BT's first move is to show that they did look.

### The wisdom of Silenus

Silenus was the old companion of Dionysus, half man and half beast, a figure of the woods. The legend Nietzsche tells has Midas, king of Phrygia in Asia Minor, hunting him for a long time and finally catching him:

<Quote source="BT 3, trans. W. A. Haussmann, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Elendes Eintagsgeschlecht, des Zufalls Kinder und der Mühsal, was zwingst du mich dir zu sagen, was nicht zu hören für dich das Erspriesslichste ist? Das Allerbeste ist für dich gänzlich unerreichbar: nicht geboren zu sein, nicht zu sein, nichts zu sein. Das Zweitbeste aber ist für dich: bald zu sterben.
</Original>

Wretched race of a day, children of chance and misery, why do you compel me to tell you what it would be best for you not to hear? What is best of all is utterly beyond your reach: not to be born, not to *be*, to be *nothing*. The second best for you, however, is to die soon.
</Quote>

This is Schopenhauer's verdict in a Greek mouth, and Nietzsche calls it Greek "folk-wisdom": the people knew it. Their myths are full of it: the Titan Prometheus chained for helping men, Oedipus who kills his father and marries his mother because he was clever enough to solve a riddle, the curse on the house of Atreus that drives Orestes to kill his mother. A people with these stories had not failed to notice what existence is.

### The Olympians as a mirror

And yet the same people produced the most brilliant life anyone had lived. Nietzsche's explanation is that they made their gods for the purpose:

<Quote source="BT 3, trans. W. A. Haussmann, revised">
The Greek knew and felt the terrors and horrors of existence: to be able to live at all, he had to interpose the shining dream-birth of the Olympian world between himself and them. … To be able to live, the Greeks had, from direst necessity, to create these gods. … Thus do the gods justify the life of man, in that they themselves live it: the only satisfactory theodicy!
</Quote>

A *theodicy* is a justification of God in the face of evil: an account of why a good God allows suffering. Here the direction is reversed. The Homeric gods are not good; they are beautiful, quarrelsome, lustful, immortal versions of human beings, and they justify *human* life by living it in glory. Seen in that mirror, existence became desirable again, and Nietzsche notes how completely Silenus was turned around: for Homer's heroes the worst thing is to die soon, and the second worst to die at all. Achilles, in the underworld, would rather be a hired labourer on earth than king of the dead.



The bearing on Schopenhauer is direct. The Greeks are a counterexample to the inference from "existence is like this" to "existence should be refused": a people accepted the description and affirmed the life. What stood between the knowledge and the refusal was art.

<KeyFact>
BT reads a culture's art as the answer to a need, and infers the need from the answer: "what sufferings this people must have undergone, in order to be able to become thus beautiful!" (BT 25). The same inference, from a value to the need that produced it, becomes the method of the <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">genealogy</Ref>.
</KeyFact>

## Apollo and Dionysus: two ways art makes knowledge bearable

The Olympians solve the problem by covering it: in Nietzsche's words, the terrors were "veiled and withdrawn from sight". Tragedy cannot work that way. It puts Oedipus's blinding and Prometheus's torment in front of thousands of people, who come back every year to watch. So "art" is too coarse a name for the answer. BT needs two powers, one that makes beautiful images and one that tears them down, and it names them after two Greek gods.

<Figure

### Dream: the beautiful individual

Apollo, "the shining one", is the god of light, of sculpture and of prophecy. His state is the dream: everyone, in dreams, is a perfect maker of images, and the images are clear, shaped and complete. In a good dream we also know, faintly, that it is a dream, and Nietzsche notes that a dreamer in danger can say "It is a dream! I will dream on!" The *Apollonian* is this power to make clear forms and enjoy them as forms, knowing they are appearance. Its rule is measure: Apollo's commands are "know thyself" and "nothing too much", and his figures are *individuals*, each with its outline. Nietzsche calls Apollo the divine image of Schopenhauer's principle of individuation.

### Intoxication: the individual dissolved

Dionysus is the god of wine and ecstasy, worshipped in spring festivals with music and dancing crowds. His state is intoxication, in which the boundaries between persons fall away. Schopenhauer had called the world of separate individuals the veil of Maya, borrowing an Indian word for the world as illusion; in the *Dionysian* state "the veil of Maya has been torn" and each reveller feels himself one with all the others and with nature:

<Quote source="BT 1, trans. W. A. Haussmann, revised">
In song and in dance man exhibits himself as a member of a higher community: he has forgotten how to walk and speak, and is on the point of taking a dancing flight into the air. … Man is no longer an artist, he has become a work of art.
</Quote>

The Dionysian is not an image of anything. Its art is music, which on Schopenhauer's theory, accepted in BT, copies the will itself rather than any appearance of it, because music has no images: the other arts copy things that can be seen, while music renders only the rise, tension and release of striving, which is what the will is. (For Schopenhauer the listener still hears that striving without wanting anything himself, which is why music could also count as a release from the will.) So the Dionysian state is Schopenhauer's description *felt*: the individual is seen to be appearance, and what is real is the one will, with its contradiction and its pain.

### Each alone fails

Taken alone, each power fails, and the failures show why both are needed. Apollo alone hardens into the rigid, military art and state of Doric Sparta, an order that holds only by keeping the Dionysian out; or it produces Homer's naive beauty, which veils. Dionysus alone, among peoples without an Apollo, produced orgies of sex and cruelty. Among the Greeks, who had both, it produced something worse for a thinking person: knowledge without the will to act. When the ecstasy ends and everyday life returns, it disgusts the one who has seen through it:

<Quote source="BT 7, trans. W. A. Haussmann, revised">
In this sense the Dionysian man may be said to resemble Hamlet: both have for once seen into the true nature of things, they have *perceived*, but they are loath to act; for their action cannot change the eternal nature of things. … Knowledge kills action, action requires the veil of illusion. … he now discerns the wisdom of the sylvan god Silenus: and loathing seizes him.
</Quote>

The Dionysian man has Schopenhauer's knowledge and, with it, Schopenhauer's verdict; the next step for him, BT says, is "a Buddhistic negation of the will". What has to be explained is how a person can keep the knowledge without the verdict: see what Silenus saw, and still act and live.

## Tragedy: a Dionysian chorus seeing an Apollonian vision

The answer BT gives is a theory of what happened in the theatre of Dionysus at Athens in the fifth century BC, where the tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides were performed at the god's festival. It starts from a fact the ancient tradition reports: tragedy began as a chorus, a group of singers and dancers, and only later added actors and a plot.

### The chorus and the vision

The original chorus were satyrs, men costumed as the half-goat companions of Dionysus. On BT's reading the chorus is the Dionysian throng made into art: revellers so carried away that they see themselves transformed into the god's companions, and, so transformed, *see the god*. The scene with its actors is their vision, and what they see is their own god suffering. The tradition says that the earliest tragedy had one subject, the sufferings of Dionysus, and in the mystery cults Dionysus was the god who as a boy was torn to pieces by the Titans. BT reads that myth as Schopenhauer's metaphysics in story form: the one god broken into many pieces is the one will split into individuals, and suffering in them. Later the stage showed Prometheus or Oedipus instead, but BT holds that these are masks of the same god (BT 10). The chorus can see its god only as the dream sees anything, in a clear form, and the clear form of suffering is one individual caught in his own fate: a hero with a name and a story. Every tragic hero is the suffering Dionysus seen through Apollo. The spectators, seated in rising rings around the dancing floor, were no separate audience; they found themselves in the chorus.

So tragedy joins the two powers in a definite order. The Dionysian state comes first and is the substance: the chorus feels the truth Silenus spoke. The Apollonian vision is laid over it and gives it form: one hero, one fate, one shape the eye can hold. The chorus, as Nietzsche puts it, is a "living wall" that shuts out everyday reality, and from behind it the worst can be looked at. "Art saves him," he writes of the Greek who is in danger of the Buddhist conclusion, "and through art life saves him, for herself" (BT 7).

What the spectator takes home BT calls the *metaphysical comfort*: "that, in spite of the perpetual change of phenomena, life at bottom is indestructibly powerful and pleasurable" (BT 7). The hero is destroyed; the will that the chorus feels, and that the hero was one appearance of, goes on, and in the moment of ecstasy, BT 17 says, the spectators feel themselves one with its "indomitable desire for being and joy in existence". Schopenhauer could not have written either sentence. His will is blind and joyless, and a life made of it has nothing better than relief.

<Aside title="Tristan without words: a test of the order (BT 21)">
Nietzsche tests the order with music his first readers knew: the third act of Wagner's *Tristan and Isolde* heard as pure music, without words or stage. Nobody, he argues, could take that in directly, as the will itself speaking, without collapsing. What lets an audience bear it is the hero: the dying Tristan with his words, a single fate to attach the feeling to. "The myth protects us from the music," and the music gives the myth a depth no words could.
</Aside>

### What the hero knows: Prometheus

The Dionysian content of a tragedy is carried by its myth, and the clearest case in BT is Prometheus, the Titan who stole fire from the gods and gave it to men, and was chained to a rock where a bird of prey tore at him, in the version Aeschylus put on the stage.

<Example title="Prometheus read by the two impulses (BT 9)">
**The Dionysian content.** The myth says that the best thing human beings have, fire and with it all culture, was got by a crime against the gods, and must be paid for with suffering. In Nietzsche's words: "The best and highest that men can acquire they obtain by a crime, and must now in their turn take upon themselves its consequences." Generalised: an individual who reaches beyond the boundaries of individuation, towards what belongs to the whole, "trespasses and suffers". This is the contradiction at the heart of things, the same that Silenus voiced, now told as a story.

**The Apollonian form.** Aeschylus gives the Titan a clear shape and a clear quarrel, and sets it in a frame of justice: fate stands over gods and men alike and the conflict is weighed on its scales. Apollo is the god of boundaries and justice, and that is his contribution.

**The joined result.** Nietzsche states it as one formula: "Whatever exists is alike just and unjust, and equally justified in both." The suffering is not explained away and not condemned. The spectator sees it as necessary and as belonging to the greatest thing men have done, and leaves with the crime given dignity rather than shame.
</Example>

Nietzsche presses the point by contrasting this myth with the biblical story of the Fall, and the passage has to be read whole:

<Quote source="BT 9, trans. W. A. Haussmann, revised">
The tale of Prometheus is an original possession of the entire Aryan family of races, and documentary evidence of their capacity for the profoundly tragic; indeed, it is not improbable that this myth has the same characteristic significance for the Aryan race that the myth of the fall of man has for the Semitic, and that there is a relationship between the two myths like that of brother and sister. … The best and highest that men can acquire they obtain by a crime, and must now in their turn take upon themselves its consequences … a bitter reflection, which, by the *dignity* it confers on crime, contrasts strangely with the Semitic myth of the fall of man, in which curiosity, beguilement, seducibility, wantonness, in short, a whole series of pre-eminently feminine passions, were regarded as the origin of evil. What distinguishes the Aryan representation is the sublime view of *active sin* as the properly Promethean virtue … Accordingly crime is understood by the Aryans to be a man, sin by the Semites a woman; as also, the original crime is committed by man, the original sin by woman.
</Quote>

"Aryan" and "Semitic" were the philology of 1872's names for two families of languages, Indo-European (from Sanskrit and Greek to German) and Semitic (Hebrew, Arabic), and for the stocks of myth that came down with them. Nietzsche uses them to set two kinds of guilt against each other: a crime committed, actively and for a prize, and a sin suffered by yielding to seduction. The gender is in his German: *der Frevel*, the crime, is masculine, *die Sünde*, the sin, is feminine, and he makes the grammar into a claim, naming curiosity, seducibility and wantonness as "pre-eminently feminine passions" and the source of evil on the passive model. The point for the argument is the first contrast: the tragic myth justifies human guilt, where the moral myth only condemns it. So already in 1872 the book ranks myths by whether they let the guilty go on living, and puts that rank above the moral one. The question of guilt itself returns in <Ref lesson="guilt-before-god">guilt before God</Ref>.

## "Only as an aesthetic phenomenon": a justification for whom?

Tragedy, so far, consoles the spectator. BT claims much more: it says that tragedy *justifies existence*, and a consolation is not a justification. Something that makes suffering bearable to watch does not show that the suffering was worth having. To get from one to the other, BT adds a premise about what the world is.

### The assumption behind the answer

Nietzsche states it in BT 4, and he calls it what it is, an assumption:

<Quote source="BT 4, trans. W. A. Haussmann, revised">
The more clearly I perceive in nature those all-powerful art-impulses, and in them a fervent longing for appearance, for redemption through appearance, the more I feel myself driven to the metaphysical assumption that the truly existent primordial unity, eternally suffering and full of contradiction, needs the rapturous vision, the pleasurable appearance, for its continuous redemption.
</Quote>

Here Schopenhauer's one will reappears as the *primordial unity*, and it suffers, as on his account. But it does not need to be denied. It needs to be *looked at*: it produces the world of individuals the way a dreamer produces a dream, and is redeemed, moment by moment, in the beauty of what it makes. On this assumption art is not a human escape from the will; it is the will's own activity, and the world itself is its artwork.

<Figure src="./figures/transfiguration-raphael.jpg" float="right" width="s" alt="Raphael's Transfiguration, a tall painted panel in two halves. Above, Christ floats in a burst of white light with arms raised, flanked by two floating prophets, while three disciples lie thrown down on the mountaintop beneath him. Below, in shadow, a dense crowd: on the right a family holds up a convulsed boy with rolled-back eyes and flung-out arms; on the left the disciples gesture and point, unable to help; a kneeling woman in the foreground turns toward them." caption="Raphael, The Transfiguration (1516–20). Below, the possessed boy and the helpless crowd, BT 4's 'reflection of eternal primordial pain'; above, the vision that rises out of it." />

BT 4 gives a picture of it. In Raphael's *Transfiguration*, painted in Rome in 1516–20, the lower half shows a boy possessed, his despairing family and the helpless disciples: for Nietzsche, "the reflection of eternal primordial pain, the sole basis of the world". Out of it rises the upper half, Christ floating in light, a vision "of which those wrapt in the first appearance see nothing". The suffering below and the vision above need each other, which is the relation of Dionysus and Apollo.

### Pictures in the artist's dream

Now the sentence the whole book turns on can be read in its place:

<Quote source="BT 5, trans. W. A. Haussmann, revised">
<Original lang="de">
… wohl aber dürfen wir von uns selbst annehmen, dass wir für den wahren Schöpfer derselben schon Bilder und künstlerische Projectionen sind und in der Bedeutung von Kunstwerken unsre höchste Würde haben, denn nur als aesthetisches Phänomen ist das Dasein und die Welt ewig gerechtfertigt.
</Original>

… rather we may assume with regard to ourselves that its true author uses us as pictures and artistic projections, and that we have our highest dignity in our significance as works of art, for only as an *aesthetic phenomenon* is existence and the world eternally *justified*.
</Quote>

The sentence goes on: our awareness of this significance "hardly differs from the kind of consciousness which the soldiers painted on canvas have of the battle represented thereon". The *aesthetic justification of existence* is therefore a justification for the artist of the world, and for us only insofar as we are his pictures.

That raises a difficulty. BT 4 made the artist "eternally suffering", and a sufferer who merely looked away from his pain at pretty pictures would be the Olympian veil again, not a justification of the pain. For the suffering itself to be justified, the one who makes and watches the spectacle must *want* it, pain included. BT 24 says that he does, and gives the model: a dissonance in music. The clash hurts taken alone, the music wants it, and the listener desires it. In the same way the tragic myth shows that "even the Ugly and Discordant is an artistic game which the will, in the eternal fulness of its joy, plays with itself", and the Dionysian is "primordial joy experienced in pain itself". BT adds an image from Heraclitus, the early Greek thinker of change: the world-building power is like a child who builds sandhills and knocks them down again, for the pleasure of the game.

So BT's ground of the world is pain and primordial delight at once, which is how BT 4's "eternally suffering" and BT 7's "indestructibly powerful and pleasurable" can both be said of it. This goes beyond Schopenhauer's step 1, where the one will is blind and nothing else: it takes no pleasure in anything. BT keeps the individual's pendulum, steps 2 to 5, exactly as Schopenhauer drew it; what it changes is the will taken as a whole, which no longer only suffers. The change does not touch the premises the verdict used, which were all about what a life is like for the individual living it. It supplies what the new standard needs: a standpoint from which suffering can be wanted. And it belongs to the same assumption as BT 4, stated, never argued.

<Figure
  src="./figures/two-chains.svg"
  alt="Two columns of argument steps. Left, Schopenhauer 1818: five boxes from one blind will to life swinging from pain to boredom, then a dashed line labelled 'measured by what it feels like to live it' down to a blue verdict box, 'better not to exist: stop willing'. Right, The Birth of Tragedy 1872: one large box, 'steps 1–5 accepted (step 1 enlarged, below); Silenus says the same', an amber dashed box 'assumed, never argued: the one will suffers and delights; it needs appearance to redeem it', and a verdict box, 'existence is justified, as an aesthetic phenomenon'"
  caption="Same description of a life, different verdict: BT keeps steps 1 to 5, lets the one will delight as well as suffer, and changes what stands between the description and the verdict."

This is how BT replaces Schopenhauer's standard. Schopenhauer judged a life by what it feels like to the one who lives it, and condemned it. BT judges the world as one judges a tragedy: by what it is as a spectacle, to the one who makes and watches it. By that standard the suffering is no objection; it is part of what makes the spectacle great.

### The objection: the sufferer gets nothing

The strongest objection follows at once. A justification addressed to the god is no help to the person in pain. If my suffering is justified only because it makes a fine spectacle for the world-artist, I have been told why it is good for someone else. And tragic suffering is grand: a king's fall, a Titan's crime. Most suffering is not. A long illness or a dull, defeated life is no tragedy and makes no spectacle, and the justification does not seem to reach it.

The 1872 book has a partial reply. Art does not only justify the world from above; it also changes how the sufferer can live. The Dionysian man of BT 7, sickened by what he has seen, is saved by art "as a saving and healing enchantress", which turns the horrible into the *sublime* and the absurd into the *comic*, "representations with which it is possible to live". And the old Athenian of BT 25, asked how the Greeks could be so beautiful, answers that they had suffered enough to need it. Art serves the sufferer as well as the god.

The reply has a gap, and it is plain: in 1872 the sufferer is helped to *bear* existence, while the *justification* stays with the god. BT does not say that a human being can justify his own life. On one reading, popular since the middle of the twentieth century, the book already holds that art is the response of a strong, healthy organism to suffering, a claim about human beings. The text does not bear that out. BT 4 and 5 make the redemption the primordial unity's, and the healthy-organism language arrives only in 1886 and after.

## Socrates: the optimism that killed tragedy

If tragedy did all this, its end needs explaining. The great age was short: Aeschylus's first victories in the 480s BC, Euripides dead in 406, and nothing of the same rank after. BT's answer is that a new idea entered Athens and made tragedy impossible, and it names the idea's carrier: Socrates, the philosopher who questioned his fellow citizens in the streets and was executed in 399 BC.

### Virtue is knowledge

Euripides, the last of the three tragedians, wrote under a new rule, which BT formulates as "to be beautiful everything must be intelligible": the parallel to the Socratic "only the knowing one is virtuous". So he opened his plays with a speech explaining the whole situation, and closed them with a god lowered onto the stage to settle every loose end (the *deus ex machina*). Mystery, and with it the chorus, became an embarrassment. Behind Euripides stood Socrates:

<Quote source="BT 14, trans. W. A. Haussmann">
Let us but realise the consequences of the Socratic maxims: "Virtue is knowledge; man only sins from ignorance; he who is virtuous is happy": these three fundamental forms of optimism involve the death of tragedy.
</Quote>

If virtue is knowledge and the virtuous are happy, then suffering is a mistake that better thinking can correct, and a play must show the good rewarded and the bad punished. BT calls the belief underneath *theoretical optimism*: "the imperturbable belief that, by means of the clue of causality, thinking reaches to the deepest abysses of being, and that thinking is able not only to perceive being but even to *correct* it" (BT 15). It is a third answer to Silenus, beside resignation and art: the description is false, or at least curable.

| Answer to Silenus | Accepts the description? | What it does about it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resignation (Schopenhauer; Indian religion) | yes | stops willing |
| Theoretical optimism (Socrates, modern science) | no: knowledge can correct existence | thinks, explains, improves |
| Tragedy (BT) | yes | makes it bearable and justifies it as art |

<Aside title="Socrates as a monstrosity of reason (BT 13)">
Nietzsche saw in Socrates a reversal of the normal order of a mind. In productive people instinct creates and consciousness criticises; in Socrates "it is instinct which becomes critic; it is consciousness which becomes creator: a perfect monstrosity *per defectum*". The dying Socrates, calm because he had argued himself out of the fear of death, became the new ideal of Greek youth. *Ecce Homo* names this diagnosis, Socrates as "a typical decadent", one of the book's two lasting innovations.
</Aside>

### Where optimism ends

BT does not dismiss science. It argues that science carries optimism to its own limit. The circle of what can be known has endless points on its edge, and every serious inquirer reaches one where he "sees to his dismay how logic coils round itself at these limits and finally bites its own tail" (BT 15). At that point a "tragic perception" breaks through, which "in order even to be endured, requires art". Kant and Schopenhauer, by showing that space, time and cause reach only appearances, had in BT's view done exactly this to modern optimism: they ended its claim to know the essence of things.

What optimism does to a society BT states in a passage that has to be quoted whole. "Alexandrian culture" is its name for a culture of scholars and science, after Alexandria in Egypt, the city of the great library in the centuries after Alexander, and it means the modern world:

<Quote source="BT 18, trans. W. A. Haussmann, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Man soll es merken: die alexandrinische Cultur braucht einen Sclavenstand, um auf die Dauer existieren zu können: aber sie leugnet, in ihrer optimistischen Betrachtung des Daseins, die Nothwendigkeit eines solchen Standes und geht deshalb, wenn der Effect ihrer schönen Verführungs- und Beruhigungsworte von der "Würde des Menschen" und der "Würde der Arbeit" verbraucht ist, allmählich einer grauenvollen Vernichtung entgegen. Es giebt nichts Furchtbareres als einen barbarischen Sclavenstand, der seine Existenz als ein Unrecht zu betrachten gelernt hat und sich anschickt, nicht nur für sich, sondern für alle Generationen Rache zu nehmen.
</Original>

Let us mark this well: the Alexandrian culture requires a slave class to be able to exist permanently; but in its optimistic view of life it denies the necessity of such a class, and consequently, when the effect of its beautifully seductive and tranquillising utterances about the "dignity of man" and the "dignity of labour" is spent, it gradually drifts towards a dreadful destruction. There is nothing more terrible than a barbaric slave class, who have learned to regard their existence as an injustice, and now prepare to take vengeance, not only for themselves, but for all generations.
</Quote>

"Slave class" here denotes the mass of people whose hard labour carries a culture: whoever does the work so that a few can make art, science and philosophy. The claim has two halves. Every high culture rests on such a base: Greek culture rested on actual slaves, and the modern one on its labourers. And optimism cannot admit this, because its creed is happiness for all through knowledge. It therefore promises the labourers what it cannot give, teaches them that their condition is an injustice, and prepares their revenge. This is Nietzsche's first statement of a thesis he restates in 1886, that every elevation of humanity has needed "slavery in some sense", which <Ref lesson="order-of-rank">the order of rank</Ref> takes up at full length.

The book's remedy for modern Alexandrianism was a rebirth of tragedy out of German music, meaning Wagner's (BT 16–24). <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner">The case of Wagner</Ref> follows what became of that hope.

## 1886: the question kept, the answer rewritten

Which of this is Nietzsche's own view is settled by two later texts. In August 1886 at Sils Maria he wrote "An Attempt at Self-Criticism" for a new edition of the book, retitled *The Birth of Tragedy, or: Hellenism and Pessimism*; and in late 1888 he reviewed it again in *Ecce Homo* (EH). These are the later Nietzsche judging the earlier one, the reading rule fixed in <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="three-periods-drawn-by-himself">three periods, drawn by himself</Ref>. Both keep the book's question and rewrite its answer.

### What stays: the question

<Quote source="BT, Attempt at a Self-Criticism 1, trans. W. A. Haussmann, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Ist Pessimismus nothwendig das Zeichen des Niedergangs, Verfalls, des Missrathenseins, der ermüdeten und geschwächten Instinkte? … Giebt es einen Pessimismus der Stärke?
</Original>

Is pessimism *necessarily* the sign of decline, of decay, of failure, of exhausted and weakened instincts? … Is there a pessimism of *strength*?
</Quote>

The question splits two things Schopenhauer had joined: seeing that existence is terrible, and turning away from it. The Greeks of the tragic age, at their strongest, *wanted* the terrible in their art, and so seeing it need not be a symptom of weakness. The 1886 preface then turns the suspicion around: perhaps Socrates' optimism, and modern science with it, is the symptom of decline, "fear and evasion of pessimism". And it names the task the book attempted: "to view science through the optics of the artist, and art through the optics of life".

Section 5 of the preface says what the book was against. Art, "and *not* morality", was set up as the properly metaphysical activity of man; Christianity, which is "*only* moral", appears in the book only as a "hostile silence", because a morality of absolute standards must condemn art, and with it life, "for all life rests on appearance, art, illusion, optics, necessity of perspective and error". Here, it says, "a pessimism 'Beyond Good and Evil'" announced itself for the first time. The section ends on a question:

<Quote source="BT, Attempt at a Self-Criticism 5, trans. W. A. Haussmann, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Moral selbst, wie? sollte Moral nicht ein "Wille zur Verneinung des Lebens", ein heimlicher Instinkt der Vernichtung, ein Verfalls-, Verkleinerungs-, Verleumdungsprincip, ein Anfang vom Ende sein? Und, folglich, die Gefahr der Gefahren?
</Original>

Morality itself, what? May not morality be a "will to deny life", a secret instinct for annihilation, a principle of decay, of diminution, of slander, a beginning of the end? And, consequently, the danger of dangers?
</Quote>

The name he gave the book's counter-doctrine, he says, was *Dionysian*. The suspicion about morality returns, argued, in <Ref lesson="danger-of-dangers">the danger of dangers</Ref>.

### What goes: the metaphysics and the comfort

The same preface is merciless about the answer. The artist-god of BT 4 and 5 is "arbitrary, idle, fantastic, if you will"; what mattered was only that it already betrayed a spirit set against the moral reading of life. He regrets having expressed new valuations "in Kantian and Schopenhauerian formulae", quotes Schopenhauer's verdict that tragedy leads to resignation, and answers: "Oh, how differently Dionysus spoke to me!" He disowns the hopes he had placed in German music, which he now calls romanticism through and through. And he turns the book's comfort against it. The "metaphysical comfort" of BT 7 and 18 is the typical romantic ending, he says, collapse before the old God; instead the young pessimists "should first of all learn the art of *earthly* comfort", and "learn to *laugh*", and so "send all metaphysical comfortism to the devil, and metaphysics first of all!" (Self-Criticism 7).

<Aside title="The same disowning, in Zarathustra (1883)">
"The work of a suffering and tortured god, the world then seemed to me," Zarathustra says of a belief he once held, and calls it a "delusion beyond man", a god made of his own suffering (Z I 3, "On the Afterworldly"). Reading this as Nietzsche's verdict on BT is an interpretation, but the description fits BT 4 almost word for word.
</Aside>

In *Ecce Homo* the verdict of 1888 is short. "Hellenism and Pessimism" would have been the better title, "suggesting the first instruction about how the Greeks got over their pessimism". The tragedies prove "that the Greeks were not pessimists: Schopenhauer went wrong at this point, as he went wrong everywhere". The book's two innovations were the Dionysian and the diagnosis of Socrates as "a typical decadent". And he calls himself "the first *tragic philosopher*: that is, the most extreme opposite and antipode of a pessimistic philosopher" (EH, "The Birth of Tragedy" 1, 3). The word *Dionysian* has meanwhile changed its meaning, from the dissolving intoxication of 1872 to the affirmation of life by a strength that keeps its passions; so the word is read with its date (<Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="three-periods-drawn-by-himself">three periods, drawn by himself</Ref>), and <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner">the case of Wagner</Ref> follows the change.

### The later reply to the objection

Without the artist-god, the aesthetic justification has no one to address except us, and that is where the objection of the previous section gets its answer, years after 1872. In *The Gay Science* (1882) the formula returns with one word changed:

<Quote source="GS 107, trans. W. Kaufmann">
As an aesthetic phenomenon existence is still *bearable* for us, and art furnishes us with eyes and hands and above all the good conscience to be able to turn ourselves into such a phenomenon.
</Quote>

"Bearable", not "justified"; and the artist is now the human being, who makes his own life into something he can look at. That move, the sufferer as the artist of his own existence, belongs to the 1880s, and <Ref lesson="amor-fati">amor fati</Ref> follows it to its end.

<Conclusion>
You can now say exactly where Nietzsche parted from Schopenhauer. Schopenhauer's description, a will that swings between the pain of wanting and the boredom of having, with satisfaction only relief, becomes a verdict against life only through a standard: a life judged by what it feels like to live it. *The Birth of Tragedy* keeps the description, which Silenus had already spoken, and changes the standard. The Greeks bore the knowledge through art, first in the Olympian mirror and then in tragedy, where a Dionysian chorus sees an Apollonian vision; and existence is justified "only as an aesthetic phenomenon", a justification for the world-artist whose pictures we are, who suffers and delights in the same act, and not for us. Socrates' optimism, the belief that knowledge can correct existence, ended tragedy. In 1886 Nietzsche kept the question "is there a pessimism of strength?" and the suspicion that morality is "the danger of dangers", and threw out the artist-god, the metaphysical comfort and the hopes for Wagner.

That leaves the answer without its foundation. BT's artist-god was the last world behind the world Nietzsche believed in, and when it went, the question of what can hold values up without any such world became urgent. <Ref lesson="death-of-god">The death of God</Ref> states that question in its full form.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Three texts of Nietzsche's on BT carry dates: the Foreword to Richard Wagner (Basel, end of 1871); the Attempt at a Self-Criticism (Sils Maria, August 1886); the review in *Ecce Homo* (written late 1888, published 1908). Say which can speak for his mature view and why, and find in Self-Criticism 5, as quoted or described in the lesson, one phrase that could not have been written in 1871.

<Solution>
**Foreword, end of 1871.** Period 1, written as the book's dedication to Wagner; it states the book's view at the time ("art is the highest task and the properly metaphysical activity of this life"), not a later one.

**Self-Criticism, 1886.** Period 3, a published preface: the mature Nietzsche judging the earlier book. It can speak for his mature view of BT, and it is the text the lesson used to sort what he kept from what he dropped.

**EH, late 1888.** Also period 3. Unprinted until 1908, but a manuscript he had finished for print, so on the ranking of <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="ranking-the-texts-and-citing-them">the first lesson</Ref> it counts with the books. It is the latest of the three.

**A phrase impossible in 1871.** Section 5 calls the book's position "a pessimism 'Beyond Good and Evil'". *Beyond Good and Evil* is the title of the book he published in 1886, the year of the preface, and the phrase names a programme, against the moral interpretation of life, that the 1871 Foreword does not state: the Foreword's word is "art", not "against morality". The question whether morality is "the danger of dangers" is a second candidate, for the same reason.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
In Sophocles' *Oedipus the King*, Oedipus solves the riddle of the Sphinx, becomes king of Thebes, and discovers by his own relentless inquiry that he has killed his father and married his mother; he blinds himself. Do for this play what the lesson's Example did for Prometheus: say what the Dionysian content is, what the Apollonian form contributes, and why, on BT's account, the audience leaves neither simply depressed nor simply consoled. BT 9 reads the myth with the sentence "wisdom is a crime against nature"; use it.

<Solution>
**The Dionysian content.** The myth joins three things: the man who solves nature's riddle is the man who breaks the holiest natural law, parricide and incest. BT 9 reads the conjunction as a claim: wisdom that forces nature to give up its secrets is itself unnatural, and the one who plunges nature into an abyss must undergo the dissolution of nature in himself. "Wisdom is a crime against nature." This is Silenus's knowledge again: the deepest insight and the worst fate are one.

**The Apollonian form.** Sophocles presents this as a clear, step-by-step investigation, a legal knot that the judge unties link by link, to his own ruin. BT notes the "Hellenic delight" in this unravelling: the clarity of the plot, the precise speech of the hero, the single visible figure all give the horror a shape the eye can hold. They are, in BT's image, bright spots that heal an eye that has looked into darkness.

**Why neither depressed nor consoled.** The Dionysian content keeps the audience from comfort: nothing in the play says the world is just or the suffering a mistake. The Apollonian form keeps them from nausea: they watch one man's fate, clearly drawn, and are not swallowed by the knowledge. What they take away is BT 7's metaphysical comfort: the hero is destroyed, but the life of which he was one appearance goes on, powerful and, at bottom, joyful, with a joy that on BT 24's account contains the hero's pain the way music contains a dissonance. In BT 9's words about the later *Oedipus at Colonus*, his passive suffering becomes his highest activity.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute: "Nietzsche disagreed with Schopenhauer about how much people suffer. Schopenhauer was a pessimist because he exaggerated the misery of life; the young Nietzsche saw that the Greeks were happy." Name the step of the argument where the real disagreement lies.

<Solution>
**The claim gets BT backwards on the facts.** BT 3 insists that the Greeks "knew and felt the terrors and horrors of existence", that Silenus's verdict was their own folk-wisdom, and that they were "so singularly qualified for *sufferings*". BT 25 infers from their beauty how much they "must have undergone". So BT does not lower Schopenhauer's estimate of suffering; it confirms it, and denies that the Greeks were the cheerful, untroubled people of the eighteenth-century picture.

**Where the disagreement is.** BT accepts steps 1 to 5 of the reconstruction, the will and the pendulum; its one addition, a will that delights as well as suffers, concerns the whole, not how a life feels to the one living it. It rejects step 6, the verdict that non-existence is better. The verdict needed a standard, a life judged by what it feels like to the one who lives it, and BT replaces that standard: existence is judged as an aesthetic phenomenon.

**What BT adds to make the replacement work.** The premise of BT 4, that the "eternally suffering" primordial unity needs beautiful appearance for its redemption, together with BT 24's addition that it plays its game of suffering "in the eternal fulness of its joy". Without them, tragedy only consoles the spectator; with them, the world as a whole is justified as the artwork of an artist who wants it, pain included. The disagreement is about value, and it rests on a metaphysical assumption, not on a different count of pains.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Take BT's sentence "only as an aesthetic phenomenon is existence and the world eternally justified" at its word. Work out what it implies for an ordinary life that contains no tragedy and no art: a clerk who never goes to the theatre, whose suffering is a long illness. What would justify that life on BT's terms, and what does BT 5's own clause about "pictures and artistic projections" concede?

<Solution>
**Step 1: who does the justifying.** On BT 4–5 the justification is not a property of the clerk's experience; it is the standpoint of the primordial unity, the "true author" of the world, for whom the whole world of appearances is a vision that redeems its suffering.

**Step 2: what this implies for the clerk.** His life is justified in exactly the way every other appearance is: as part of the world-artist's spectacle. It does not matter whether he ever sees a tragedy; the clerk is himself a picture in the god's dream. So the justification reaches the ordinary life, but only from outside.

**Step 3: what the clause concedes.** BT 5 says we are "pictures and artistic projections", and that our consciousness of our significance "hardly differs from the kind of consciousness which the soldiers painted on canvas have of the battle represented thereon". The clerk is justified without knowing it and without being helped by it. His illness is a dissonance in a music he cannot hear.

**The problem.** A justification the sufferer cannot share answers the god's question (why make such a world?) and not the clerk's (why go on?). BT's partial answer, art as healing enchantress (BT 7), helps only those who have art. This is the gap the lesson named, and why Nietzsche's later formula in GS 107 moves the artist inside the life: existence becomes "bearable for us" because art gives us the means "to turn ourselves into such a phenomenon".
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A boundary probe for Schopenhauer's argument. Suppose there are pleasures that end no want felt beforehand: the smell of coffee in a kitchen you walk into, a view you did not expect. (a) Which step of the reconstruction does such a pleasure attack? (b) Does the verdict survive if the step falls? (c) Does BT need the step to fall?

<Solution>
**(a)** Step 3, the one in the box of frame 4 of the pendulum: "satisfaction is only relief", or in WWR I §58, all satisfaction is "always negative only, and never positive". An unsought pleasure that removes no prior pain is positive in exactly the sense Schopenhauer denies.

**(b)** Not in its full form. If some pleasures are positive, a life is not a pendulum between pain and boredom with nothing good at either end; the balance can, at least sometimes, come out above zero. Schopenhauer has a reply, which shows how much rests on the step: the pleasure, he could say, only brings into sharp relief a dull misery we had stopped noticing, so it too is relief. Whether that reply is more than a rescue of the theory is the question to argue. Either way, the verdict now depends on an empirical claim about pleasure, not on the structure of willing.

**(c)** No. BT accepts the description of a life, steps 2 to 5, as the Greeks did in Silenus's saying; the joy it adds to step 1 belongs to the will as a whole and leaves step 3 untouched. Its disagreement is with the standard of step 6. Even if every satisfaction were mere relief, BT's justification would stand, because it does not judge existence by the balance of pleasure and pain at all. This is the strength and the cost of its strategy: it concedes the worst about how life feels and moves the question of value elsewhere.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Schopenhauer explains boredom as having nothing to want. Consider a prisoner in a cell, bored for weeks, who wants one thing very badly: to get out. (a) Why does this case trouble Schopenhauer's explanation? (b) Propose a better account of what the bored prisoner lacks, and say what it implies about what human beings want.

<Solution>
**(a)** The prisoner has a strong, determinate want, so on Schopenhauer's account he should be in pain, at the "lack" end of the pendulum, and not bored. He is both: he wants to get out and he is bored. So boredom cannot simply be the absence of anything to want.

**(b)** What the prisoner lacks is not an object of desire but something to *do* about it: his want gives him no activity, because nothing he can do in the cell brings it closer. A person can also be bored while busy, when the task meets no resistance. The better account: boredom is the absence of resistance to work against in pursuing what one wants. What human beings want, then, is not only to have their wants satisfied, which would leave them at Schopenhauer's empty end of the pendulum, but to meet obstacles and overcome them.

That is the doorstep of Nietzsche's mature psychology. <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">Will to power as psychology</Ref> builds on exactly this: what a living being wants above all is "the feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome", and on that view Schopenhauer's step "satisfaction is only relief" was looking at the wrong thing, the end of the striving rather than the striving.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

Plato argues from the thirsty man who does not drink that reason, a part with no wants of its own, rules the soul; what happens to self-control and to a person's unity if no such ruler is ever found? The argument (*Republic* IV) starts from something everyone has seen in themselves. Something in the thirsty man bids him drink and something forbids it, and since one and the same thing cannot pull two ways at once, there are at least two things in him. The forbidding part, Plato says, comes from calculation: it is *reason*, the part that wants nothing for itself except the truth and the good of the whole, and that is why it is fit to rule anger and appetite. Christianity added a second feature to the inherited picture: the soul is one indivisible thing, which survives death and is judged. A person on this picture has a ruler that stands apart from the passions, and a unity that is given at birth, not made.

The opposite picture has already been at work, unargued. <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="reading-a-section-as-an-argument-bge-68">BGE 68</Ref>, where pride makes memory yield, only works if the mind holds no judge without a stake of its own. *The Case of Wagner* diagnosed decadence as a "disaggregation of the will" (<Ref lesson="case-of-wagner" id="sections-67-the-style-of-decadence">sections 6–7</Ref>), and <Ref lesson="nihilism" id="decadence-the-weakness-behind-passive-nihilism">decadence</Ref> was "to have to fight the instincts": both presuppose a person made of more than one will. And before anyone can ask where values like "good and evil" came from, a prior question needs an answer: who does the valuing.

Take Plato's ruler away and three problems open at once. What are the parts, if not reason, spirit and appetite? What is self-control, if nothing stands above the passions to control them? And if nothing holds the parts together from above, how unified can a person be, and why are some people a character while others are a crowd?

## Soul-atomism goes, the soul stays: BGE 12

The first thing to settle is what Nietzsche rejects, because the obvious guess is wrong. A thinker who removes the ruling soul sounds like a materialist who removes the soul altogether and keeps only the body. *Beyond Good and Evil* (BGE, 1886) section 12 says the opposite, and it says it through an analogy with physics.

### What physics had done to the atom

The analogy is to Roger Boscovich (1711–1787), a Jesuit physicist from Dubrovnik on the Adriatic coast, who in 1758 proposed that matter is not made of tiny solid lumps. It is made of points of force, without size, that attract and repel each other; what we feel as a hard, impenetrable body is a strong repulsion at short distances. Nothing the lump explained needed the lump. BGE 12 calls this "the greatest triumph over the senses", since every sense says that solid things are solid, and then turns the same move on psychology:

<Figure src="./figures/boscovich-portrait.jpg" float="right" width="s" alt="Oil portrait of a clean-shaven man in his forties in a black Jesuit habit, seated before shelves of large bound folios; his right hand rests on a brass armillary sphere and his left holds a pair of dividers. A painted Latin inscription along the bottom edge names him as Father Roger Boscovich of the Society of Jesus, professor of mathematics at the Roman College." caption="Boscovich at forty-five, two years before the theory of point-forces: a Jesuit mathematician, with a sphere and dividers in hand." />

<Quote source="BGE 12, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
<Original lang="de">
… man muss zunächst auch jener anderen und verhängnissvolleren Atomistik den Garaus machen, welche das Christenthum am besten und längsten gelehrt hat, der Seelen-Atomistik. Mit diesem Wort sei es erlaubt, jenen Glauben zu bezeichnen, der die Seele als etwas Unvertilgbares, Ewiges, Untheilbares, als eine Monade, als ein Atomon nimmt: diesen Glauben soll man aus der Wissenschaft hinausschaffen! Es ist, unter uns gesagt, ganz und gar nicht nöthig, "die Seele" selbst dabei los zu werden und auf eine der ältesten und ehrwürdigsten Hypothesen Verzicht zu leisten … Aber der Weg zu neuen Fassungen und Verfeinerungen der Seelen-Hypothese steht offen: und Begriffe wie "sterbliche Seele" und "Seele als Subjekts-Vielheit" und "Seele als Gesellschaftsbau der Triebe und Affekte" wollen fürderhin in der Wissenschaft Bürgerrecht haben.
</Original>

… one must also above all give the finishing stroke to that other and more portentous atomism which Christianity has taught best and longest, the soul-atomism. Let it be permitted to designate by this expression the belief which regards the soul as something indestructible, eternal, indivisible, as a monad, as an atomon: this belief ought to be expelled from science! Between ourselves, it is not at all necessary to get rid of "the soul" thereby, and thus renounce one of the oldest and most venerated hypotheses … But the way is open for new acceptations and refinements of the soul-hypothesis; and such conceptions as "mortal soul," and "soul as subject-multiplicity," and "soul as social structure of the drives and affects," want henceforth to have legitimate rights in science.
</Quote>

### Three replacements for one atom

What goes is *soul-atomism*: the soul as an atom in the old sense, *atomon*, "uncuttable", a thing with no parts that therefore cannot come apart, and so cannot die. That is the soul of the Christian afterlife, which must stay one and the same thing to be the one that is judged.

What stays is "the soul" as a hypothesis, in three new versions, and each reverses one property of the atom:

- **"Mortal soul"** reverses *indestructible and eternal*: the soul comes to be and passes away with the living body.
- **"Soul as subject-multiplicity"** reverses *indivisible*: what says "I" is many subjects, not one.
- **"Soul as social structure of the drives and affects"** says how the many hang together. The German word is *Gesellschaftsbau*, the build of a society: an arrangement of members with relations of rank among them, not a pile.

BGE 12 names Boscovich, not Plato, and aims at the Christian soul. Plato's three-part soul is already a soul with parts, so it differs from BGE 12's picture at exactly one point: who rules.

## What a drive is: the market-place laughter

"Drives and affects" are the members of the new soul, and the word needs content before it can carry an argument. The obvious content is biological: a drive is an urge like hunger or sex, a pressure that wants relief. Nietzsche's drives include those, but his examples show something hunger alone would never suggest: a drive reads the world.

### One event, six readers

*Daybreak* (D, 1881) section 119 runs an experiment the reader can check against memory:

<Quote source="D 119, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
Suppose we were in the market place one day and we noticed someone laughing at us as we went by: this event will signify this or that to us according to whether this or that drive happens at that moment to be at its height in us — and it will be a quite different event according to the kind of person we are. One person will absorb it like a drop of rain, another will shake it from him like an insect, another will try to pick a quarrel, another will examine his clothing to see if there is anything about it that might give rise to laughter, another will be led to reflect on the nature of laughter as such, another will be glad to have involuntarily augmented the amount of cheerfulness and sunshine in the world — and in each case a drive has gratified itself, whether it be the drive to annoyance or to combativeness or to reflection or to benevolence. This drive seized the event as its prey: why precisely this one? Because, thirsty and hungry, it was lying in wait.
</Quote>

The laughter is the same in all six cases. What differs is what the event *is* for the one it happens to: an insult, a puzzle about laughter, a stain to check for, a gift to the world's cheerfulness. On D 119's account, the drive at its height decides what the event is before anyone deliberates about it: "To experience is to invent?", the section ends.

### Four marks of a drive

D 119 and its neighbours give a drive four marks.

**It stands.** A drive is not a passing wish but a disposition that persists between occasions and waits for one. That is why the same person meets a hundred different events with combativeness: the drive "was lying in wait".

**It interprets.** It singles out what in a situation matters to it, so that the situation arrives already read. The laughter is an insult only for the combative drive.

**It feeds or starves.** D 119 says the drives are nourished by chance: "our daily experiences throw some prey in the way of now this, now that drive", so some grow and others wither, "all according to the nutriment which the moment does or does not bear with it". A person's make-up at forty is the record of which drives happened to be fed.

**It wants to rule.** BGE 6, the section whose last sentence <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="why-numbered-sections-a-system-cannot-question-its-own-premises">the first lesson</Ref> read as saying that a philosopher's morality shows "in what order the deepest impulses of his nature stand to each other", says what each of those impulses wants:

<Quote source="BGE 6, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
… whoever considers the fundamental drives of man with a view to determining how far they may have here acted as inspiring genii (or as demons and cobolds), will find that they have all practised philosophy at one time or another, and that each one of them would have been only too glad to look upon itself as the ultimate end of existence and the legitimate lord over all the other drives. For every drive is domineering, and as such attempts to philosophise.
</Quote>

"Domineering" renders *herrschsüchtig*, "addicted to ruling". A drive does not only seek its own satisfaction; it tries to make its way of reading the world the official one, and a philosophy, on this account, is one drive's reading presented as the truth about everything.

<Remark title="Drive">
A *drive* (German *Trieb*) is a standing disposition that interprets situations in its own interest, seeks to express itself, grows or withers with what it is fed, and tries to prevail over the others. An *affect* (*Affekt*) is the felt surge of such a disposition on an occasion, an anger or a fear. Drives in themselves are morally neutral: "the same drive", D 38 says, becomes "the painful feeling of cowardice" under one custom and "the pleasant feeling of humility" under another, and "in itself it has, like every drive, neither this moral character nor any moral character at all".
</Remark>

Two cautions about words. The older English translations often render *Trieb* as "instinct" or "impulse"; the quotations here are revised to "drive" wherever the German has *Trieb*. And the political words that follow, "command", "rank", "lord", "tyrant", name relations among dispositions: which reading of a situation prevails and which drive gets to act. They do not put little persons inside the person.

## Who fights a drive? Another drive

The obvious objection to the drive picture is self-control. When I resist a craving, it seems that I, the reasonable self, am doing the resisting, from a place outside the craving. That is Plato's thirsty man exactly: something forbids the drink, and it is reason. D 109 takes this case and looks at how the resisting is actually done.

### Six methods

D 109 lists "no more than six essentially different methods of combating the vehemence of a drive":

1. **Avoid** every occasion for gratifying it, until it withers.
2. **Ration** it by strict regularity, enclosing "its ebb and flood within firm time-boundaries".
3. **Indulge** it wildly until disgust gives a power over it, a method that usually ends like "the rider who rode his horse to death and broke his own neck in the process".
4. **Associate** its gratification with a painful thought until the thought of gratification is itself painful.
5. **Divert** strength into hard labour or into another drive, so that the troublesome one is starved of energy.
6. **Weaken** "his entire bodily and physical organisation", as the ascetic does, starving the whole to weaken one part.

Then comes the turn:

<Quote source="D 109, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
… that one desires to combat the vehemence of a drive at all, however, does not stand within our own power; nor does the choice of any particular method; nor does the success or failure of this method. What is clearly the case is that in this entire procedure our intellect is only the blind instrument of another drive which is a rival of the drive whose vehemence is tormenting us: whether it be the drive to restfulness, or the fear of disgrace and other evil consequences, or love. While 'we' believe we are complaining about the vehemence of a drive, at bottom it is one drive which is complaining about another; that is to say: for us to become aware that we are suffering from the vehemence of a drive presupposes the existence of another equally vehement or even more vehement drive, and that a struggle is in prospect in which our intellect is going to have to take sides.
</Quote>

### The examples, with the rival named

D 109's own examples let the move be checked case by case. In each, a method of self-control is applied, and the section names, or lets us name, the drive that chose it.

<Example title="Naming the rival drive in D 109's cases">
**The Christian and the devil (method 4).** He trains himself to associate sexual pleasure with "the proximity and mockery of the Devil", and a murder for revenge with "everlasting punishment in Hell". The method is the intellect's work: a link built by practice. The drive it serves is fear, fear of damnation, which is stronger than the lust or the vengefulness it is turned against.

**The would-be thief (method 4 again).** He thinks of "the contempt which those he most respects would feel for him". The rival here is the fear of disgrace, one of the three D 109 names.

**The suicidal man (method 4).** He sets against the desire to die "a vision of the grief and self-reproach of his friends and relations". The rival is love, the third named drive, and it keeps him "suspended in life".

**The English poet Lord Byron and Napoleon (a variant of method 4).** Their pride "rises up and feels the domination of his whole bearing and the ordering of his reason by a single affect as an affront", whence "the habit and desire to tyrannise over the drive". Byron's diary line, "I refuse to be the slave of any appetite", sounds like reason speaking. On D 109's analysis it is pride, a drive that cannot bear to be ruled by another.

**The ascetic (method 6).** He starves his sensuality and with it "his vigour and not seldom his reason as well". What chose so costly a method is not stated; D 109's list leaves "the drive to restfulness" as a candidate, a drive whose aim is to be troubled by nothing.
</Example>

BGE, five years later, compresses the whole section into one line:

<Quote source="BGE 117, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Der Wille, einen Affekt zu überwinden, ist zuletzt doch nur der Wille eines anderen oder mehrer anderer Affekte.
</Original>

The will to overcome an affect is ultimately only the will of another, or of several other, affects.
</Quote>

<KeyFact>
Plato's thirsty man is a conflict between two wants, not between a want and something above wanting: what forbids the drink is a rival drive, such as the fear of what drinking would cost. Self-control survives the drive picture; the one who masters a drive is another drive or a coalition of drives.
</KeyFact>

## No arbiter is ever found

D 109 shows that the *fighter* of a drive is a drive. Plato's reason, however, claims a further role: not only to fight but to judge, to weigh the contenders without a stake and say which is right. Two passages go after that role directly.

### Understanding as a treaty among drives

The best candidate for a judge without a stake is understanding itself: the calm state in which a thing is seen as it is. *The Gay Science* (GS, 1882) section 333 takes the motto of Spinoza, the seventeenth-century Dutch philosopher of a calm and rational life, "not to laugh, not to lament, nor to detest, but to understand", and asks what understanding is made of:

<Quote source="GS 333, trans. W. Kaufmann, revised">
Yet in the last analysis, what else is this *intelligere* than the form in which we come to feel the other three at once? One result of the different and mutually opposed drives to laugh, lament, and curse? Before knowledge is possible, each of these drives must first have presented its one-sided view of the thing or event; after this comes the fight of these one-sided views, and occasionally this results in a mean, one grows calm, one finds all three sides right, and there is a kind of justice and a contract; for by virtue of justice and a contract all these drives can maintain their existence and assert their rights against each other. Since only the last scenes of reconciliation and the final accounting at the end of this long process rise to our consciousness, we suppose that *intelligere* must be something conciliatory, just, and good — something that stands essentially opposed to the drives, while it is actually nothing but *a certain behavior of the drives toward one another*.
</Quote>

The argument has a shape worth keeping. Fairness exists, and so does the calm that looks like a judge's; GS 333 grants both. What it denies is that they come from a separate judge. They are the outcome of a fight among interested parties that ended in a settlement, and because only the settlement reaches awareness, it is mistaken for a judge who was there from the start.

### Plato's ruler, redescribed

Nietzsche applies the same analysis to the original of the judge. Socrates, whose teaching Plato wrote down, is the man <Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="socrates-the-optimism-that-killed-tragedy">*The Birth of Tragedy*</Ref> blamed for the optimism that knowledge can correct existence, and whom *Ecce Homo* later called "a typical decadent". In *Twilight of the Idols* (TI, 1888) he is the case of a man whose drives had fallen into anarchy: a face-reader once told him he harboured "all the bad vices and appetites", and he agreed. His remedy was reason:

<Quote source="TI, 'The Problem of Socrates' 9, trans. W. Kaufmann, revised">
Everywhere the instincts were in anarchy; everywhere one was within five paces of excess: *monstrum in animo* was the general danger. "The drives want to play the tyrant; one must invent a *counter-tyrant* who is stronger." When the physiognomist had revealed to Socrates who he was — a cave of bad appetites — the great master of irony let slip another word which is the key to his character. "This is true," he said, "but I mastered them all."
</Quote>

*Monstrum in animo* is the face-reader's verdict made general: "a monster in the soul" was the danger everywhere in Athens, not only in Socrates. Reason on this account does not stand above the appetites. It is enlisted against them, made into "a counter-tyrant who is stronger", and the next section adds that "when one finds it necessary to turn reason into a tyrant, as Socrates did, the danger cannot be slight that something else will play the tyrant". Plato's commander is redescribed as one more contender that won.

## Who says "I": the commonwealth and its governing class

If no member of the society stands above the others, a new difficulty arises. Willing feels like one "I" giving one order, and the order is often obeyed. Where does that unity come from? BGE 19 answers by taking an act of will apart. Its full argument against "freedom of the will" belongs to <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer behind the deed</Ref>; the part needed here is its description of what goes on inside:

<Quote source="BGE 19, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Ein Mensch, der will –, befiehlt einem Etwas in sich, das gehorcht oder von dem er glaubt, dass es gehorcht. … insofern wir im gegebenen Falle zugleich die Befehlenden *und* Gehorchenden sind … insofern wir andererseits die Gewohnheit haben, uns über diese Zweiheit vermöge des synthetischen Begriffs "ich" hinwegzusetzen, hinwegzutäuschen … Der Wollende nimmt dergestalt die Lustgefühle der ausführenden, erfolgreichen Werkzeuge, der dienstbaren "Unterwillen" oder Unter-Seelen – unser Leib ist ja nur ein Gesellschaftsbau vieler Seelen – zu seinem Lustgefühle als Befehlender hinzu. L'effet c'est moi: es begiebt sich hier, was sich in jedem gut gebauten und glücklichen Gemeinwesen begiebt, dass die regierende Klasse sich mit den Erfolgen des Gemeinwesens identificirt.
</Original>

A man who wills commands something within himself which renders obedience, or which he believes renders obedience. … we are at the same time the commanding *and* the obeying parties … we are accustomed to disregard this duality, and to deceive ourselves about it by means of the synthetic term "I" … In this way the person exercising volition adds the feelings of delight of his successful executive instruments, the useful "under-wills" or under-souls (indeed, our body is but a social structure composed of many souls) to his feelings of delight as commander. *L'effet c'est moi*: what happens here is what happens in every well-constructed and happy commonwealth, namely, that the governing class identifies itself with the successes of the commonwealth.
</Quote>

"*L'effet c'est moi*", "the effect is me", parodies the line attributed to Louis XIV of France, "*L'État c'est moi*", "I am the state". A king who says it identifies himself with a whole that many others run; the willer who says "I did that" does the same with an effect that many under-souls produced. The unity of willing is real as a fact about rule: one part commands and the rest obey. It is an illusion as a fact about who acted, and the word "I" is where the illusion lives.

### The rank changes, and so does the "I"

Nothing guarantees that the governing class is the drive one would pick. BGE 158 says who usually governs:

<Quote source="BGE 158, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Unserm stärksten Triebe, dem Tyrannen in uns, unterwirft sich nicht nur unsre Vernunft, sondern auch unser Gewissen.
</Original>

Not only our reason, but also our conscience, truckles to our strongest drive, the tyrant in us.
</Quote>

Reason and conscience, the two candidates for an impartial ruler, are here courtiers: they find reasons and verdicts for whatever rules. And the ruler can change. When pride rules, "I" is proud; when fear of disgrace has the upper hand, "I" is cautious and calls it prudence. The "I" of BGE 19 is a title held by whichever drive or coalition governs, not a fixed member of the society.

## Unity is an achievement: heap, tyranny, commonwealth

The drive picture now faces its third problem. If a person is many drives and "I" is whoever rules, the difference between someone with a character and someone without one cannot be the presence of a soul. On the old picture, everyone has one unified soul by birth; the drive picture has to say what the unity of a person consists in, and why some people have more of it than others.

The answer is that the same drives can stand in different arrangements, and the arrangement is the unity. Nietzsche's texts describe three.

### The heap: drives at war

In *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (Z), the prophet describes a murderer whose reason could not hold his drives together:

<Quote source="Z I 6, 'On the Pale Criminal', trans. W. Kaufmann">
What is this man? A ball of wild snakes, which rarely enjoy rest from each other: so they go forth singly and seek prey in the world.
</Quote>

Zarathustra speaks in images, so the line is illustration. The argued version is TI's diagnosis of the modern person:

<Quote source="TI Skirmishes 41, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Our instincts contradict, disturb, destroy each other; I have already defined what is modern as physiological self-contradiction. Rationality in education would require that under iron pressure at least one of these instinct systems be paralyzed to permit another to gain in power, to become strong, to become master. Today the individual still has to be made possible by being pruned: possible here means *whole*.
</Quote>

The heap is not a society of drives with weak members; its members may be strong. It lacks an order, so that each drive acts for itself and undoes the work of the others. This is the "disaggregation of the will" *The Case of Wagner* found in a style where the word leaps out of the sentence and the page lives at the cost of the whole.

### The tyranny: one drive starves the rest

The second arrangement is the Socratic cure: one drive, reason in his case, becomes "a counter-tyrant" and rules by suppressing the rest. D 109's sixth method is the extreme form, weakening the whole organism to weaken one drive. A tyranny is a unity, but a poor one, because it is bought by making the parts weak. Pruning is a different thing: TI Skirmishes 41's remedy for the modern heap paralyses "at least one" instinct system so that another can "become master", which leaves a hierarchy with a loser in it, not a single ruler over a starved population.

### The commonwealth: strong drives in an order of rank

The third arrangement is the one Nietzsche claims for himself, in *Ecce Homo* (EH, written 1888), explaining how a man "becomes what he is":

<Quote source="EH, 'Why I Am So Clever' 9, trans. W. Kaufmann">
Meanwhile the organizing "idea" that is destined to rule keeps growing deep down — it begins to command; slowly it leads us back from side roads and wrong roads; it prepares single qualities and fitnesses that will one day prove to be indispensable as means toward a whole — one by one, it trains all subservient capacities before giving any hint of the dominant task, "goal," "aim," or "meaning." … more capacities may have been needed than have ever dwelt together in a single individual — above all, even contrary capacities that had to be kept from disturbing, destroying one another. An order of rank among these capacities; distance; the art of separating without setting against one another; to mix nothing, to "reconcile" nothing; a tremendous variety that is nevertheless the opposite of chaos.
</Quote>

Each phrase answers the heap or the tyranny. "Contrary capacities that had to be kept from disturbing, destroying one another" is the heap's danger. "An order of rank" is its cure. "To 'reconcile' nothing" refuses the tyrant's cure, which unifies by cutting down: the contrary capacities are kept, at full strength, and kept apart. And the ruler is not installed from outside: the "organizing idea" grows "deep down", commands before it is known, and "trains all subservient capacities" for a task it has not yet announced.

Zarathustra's image for the body says the same in one line: "The body is a great reason, a plurality with one sense, a war and a peace, a herd and a shepherd" (Z I 4). The plurality stays; "one sense" is the order that makes it one.

<KeyFact>
On this picture a person is not weak for having strong, opposed drives, and not strong for having few. Strength depends on two things at once, the strength of the parts and the order among them, which is why the type Nietzsche ranks highest is "as manifold as whole" (BGE 212), a claim taken up in <Ref lesson="higher-type">the higher type</Ref>.
</KeyFact>

## The argument in six steps

Each of the three problems now has an answer: the parts are drives, the controller is a rival drive, and how unified a person is depends on the order among them. Do the answers make an argument, or only a picture? Laid out as steps, with the sections each step rests on, they look like this.

<Figure
  src="./figures/argument-chain.svg"
  alt="Six boxes in a column joined by arrows: 1 inner conflict is observed, memory against pride, a craving against the wish to be rid of it (BGE 68, D 109); 2 the side that fights the drive is itself a drive, with an aim of its own (D 109, BGE 117); 3 looking inward, one finds no motive-free arbiter, only further motives (BGE 117, GS 333); 4, in bold, so a person is a plurality of drives, each wanting to rule, with no judge above them (BGE 6, 12, D 119); 5 what says 'I' is whichever drive or coalition rules, crediting itself with the whole's success (BGE 19); 6 strong drives in a stable order make a character, without an order a heap (EH 'Clever' 9, TI Skirmishes 41). An amber dashed box points into the gap between 3 and 4: 'unstated premise: what introspection never finds is not there'; below it a dotted box: 'its support: economy; posit nothing that explains nothing further, as physics dropped the atom (BGE 12)'."

Steps 1 to 3 are observations anyone can repeat on themselves. Step 4 is the thesis. Steps 5 and 6 are what BGE 19 and the three arrangements built on it: who says "I", and what a person's unity is.

Step 4 is the premise <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="reading-a-section-as-an-argument-bge-68">BGE 68</Ref> presupposed. Steps 1 to 3 do not deliver step 4 by themselves, and this is the weak joint. From "no motive-free arbiter is ever found" it does not follow that there is none: the arbiter might work out of sight, below anything introspection can reach. The bridge from 3 to 4 is a premise no section states, that what introspection never finds is not there, and nothing proves it.

What supports that premise is economy, the rule BGE 13 calls the essence of method: "beware of superfluous teleological principles! … method … must be essentially economy of principles." Boscovich did not prove that there are no solid lumps inside his points of force; he showed that the lumps explain nothing the forces do not. The same holds for the arbiter. Every case so far, the thirsty man, pride against memory, the Christian and the devil, the calm of understanding, is explained by drives and their relations. An arbiter added on top would explain nothing further and would itself never be observed. So the six steps are an argument, not only a picture, with one premise that rests on economy rather than proof.

<Aside title="Ahead of the psychology that followed">
The picture was reached from introspective cases anyone can check, a generation before psychoanalysis described the mind as agencies in conflict below awareness, and a century before cognitive science began to describe it as many specialised processes working in parallel. Its distinctive addition is the one neither later account makes central: each part interprets the world in its own interest and wants its reading to rule.
</Aside>

## The strongest objection: who arranges the drives?

Step 6, the answer to the third problem, invites the strongest objection to the whole picture. Nietzsche speaks of drives being ordered, ranked, trained, pruned, even gardened: D 560 says that "one can dispose of one's drives like a gardener". Every one of these verbs needs a subject. A heap of drives cannot be weak-willed, because weakness of will means acting against what *I* judge best; it cannot be self-critical, or decide to change its own order. Either something besides the drives does the arranging, and then Plato's commander is back under another name, or nothing does, and then the talk of mastery, character and "becoming what one is" has no one to apply to.

### The reply, from the texts

Nietzsche's texts give the reply in four parts.

**The arranger is a drive, and no regress follows.** D 109 and BGE 117 already said who masters a drive: another drive or several. The objection assumes that whatever orders the drives must stand above all of them. It need only stand above the ones it orders, and it can itself be ordered by others, as a minister commands his department and obeys his king. Nothing in such a structure has to be outside it.

**Command and obedience happen among the parts.** BGE 19 describes willing as exactly this: some parts command, others obey, and the commanding parts say "I". Weakness of will is then a precise event: the coalition that governs, and so speaks as "I", is defeated in a given case by a drive it does not command.

**Self-criticism can be a treaty.** GS 333 showed how a judgment that weighs several sides can arise without a judge: each drive presents its one-sided view, and the fight ends "in a kind of justice and a contract". A person who criticises his own conduct is not consulting a stakeless judge; several of his drives have reached a settlement against another.

**The ruling order grows from below.** The arranger need not be a gardener standing over the beds: in the commonwealth it is EH's "organizing idea", which grows among the drives it comes to order.

### What the reply keeps: a structure, not a ruler

Pressed further, the objection has a stronger form. Even granting all four points, a person seems able to stand back from a drive, ask whether it should rule, and answer no. Two readings take this seriously, and each adds one thing to the reply.

The first reading adds a distinction between *authority* and *strength*. A ruling class rules not only because it is stronger but because the governed accept its reading as speaking for the whole, and keep accepting it on days when some faction is stronger. Read so, the drives that rank highest in a person are those his values authorise. A value is what BGE 6 says every drive attempts: a reading of the world, "this matters more than that", that has become standing and that the other drives defer to. That is still not Plato's ruler, because what receives the deference is itself a drive with interests of its own. The craving yields not to a judge but to a coalition whose estimate of what matters it has been trained to follow.

BGE 158 threatens this reading: if reason and conscience always truckle to "our strongest drive", authority is only strength under a politer name. The commonwealth answers the threat. Its order is the organizing idea described above, which the drives grew into, so it holds when one of them surges. BGE 158 and EH then describe different people: in one, rank follows strength; in the other, it follows a trained order. Authority is something a person may or may not achieve.

The second reading adds *higher-order drives*. Drives constantly recruit one another (fear borrows the intellect, pride borrows combativeness), and some of them take other drives as their object. Standing back from a craving is then the work of a drive about cravings, and the self is the whole structure, which persists while particular drives come and go. No member stands outside it; the arranging is done inside the society, by parts whose task is the order of the rest.

| | First reading | Second reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it adds | authority, distinct from strength | drives whose object is other drives |
| Who says no to the craving | a coalition holding standing values | a drive about cravings |
| What the self is | the order of authority, where achieved | the whole structure of drives recruiting one another |
| What it must answer | BGE 158: rank may only ever follow strength | nothing further; it claims less |

### The gap

The order of rank answers how unified a person is: whether his drives make a heap, a tyranny or a commonwealth. It does not answer a prior question, which neither the reply nor either reading closes: why these drives belong to one society at all, and not to two societies sharing a body. Nietzsche never asks this in so many words, and never answers it. What his notebooks show is how open the underlying hypothesis still was for him. A note of the late summer of 1885 does not reach the membership question; it is still asking whether a single subject is needed at all:

<Quote source="Notebook 40, August–September 1885, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge, p. 46">
The assumption of the single subject is perhaps unnecessary; perhaps it is just as permissible to assume a multiplicity of subjects on whose interplay and struggle our thinking and our consciousness in general is based? A kind of aristocracy of 'cells' in which mastery resides? Certainly an aristocracy of equals which together are used to ruling and know how to command?
</Quote>

The note floats plurality as a hypothesis, ends every sentence of it with a question mark, and never says what would bind the many subjects, or the "aristocracy of cells", into one; it never became a section of a book. The published texts describe the society and its orders in detail and leave the same thing unstated: what makes the members members of this society.

<Conclusion>
You can now say what Nietzsche keeps of the soul and what he removes. BGE 12 removes the soul-atom, the indivisible and immortal soul, and keeps the soul as "a social structure of the drives and affects". You can say what a drive is (a standing disposition that interprets, feeds or starves, and wants to rule), and why the one who fights a drive is another drive: D 109's six methods are each chosen by a rival, and the intellect is "the blind instrument" that carries the choice out. You can lay out the argument in six steps and name its weak joint, the move from "never found" to "not there", which economy supports and does not prove. And you can say how unified a person is on this picture: as unified as the order of rank among his drives, achieved in the commonwealth, lost in the heap, bought cheaply in the tyranny, with the "I" as the title of whichever drive governs. What the picture leaves open is membership: why these drives form one society rather than two.

The argument leaves one fact unexplained. If most of what a person does is drives at work, with the intellect as their instrument, why does consciousness feel like the whole self, the place where decisions are made? <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">Consciousness and language as herd instruments</Ref> takes up why consciousness exists at all, and why it shows us so little of what goes on below it.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
From memory: state BGE 68 as the first lesson reconstructed it, as a claim with an unstated premise. Then say which step of this chapter's six-step argument asserts that premise, what further premise carries the argument to that step, and what this chapter added in support that BGE 68 alone did not have.

<Solution>
**BGE 68.** "'I did that,' says my memory. 'I cannot have done that,' says my pride, and remains inexorable. Eventually, the memory yields." Claim: what I believe about my own past is settled by a contest of motives, and the motive with the stronger interest wins whether or not it has the evidence. Unstated premise: there is no judge in me, without a stake of its own, standing above memory and pride and siding with the evidence (<Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="reading-a-section-as-an-argument-bge-68">the first lesson</Ref>).

**Where it sits.** Step 4 asserts it: a person is a plurality of drives with no judge above them. The argument reaches step 4 from step 3 ("no motive-free arbiter is found") only through a further unstated premise, that what introspection never finds is not there.

**What this chapter added.** Three things. (1) Cases of the same structure that are not about memory: D 109's cases of self-control, where the controller turns out to be a rival drive in each case, and BGE 117's generalisation. (2) GS 333, which takes the strongest candidate for a stakeless judge, understanding, and explains its calm as a settlement among drives. (3) The reason to accept the bridge from 3 to 4: economy (BGE 13). An arbiter would explain nothing that the drives and their relations do not already explain. The introspection premise is still not proved, so neither is BGE 68's presupposition; it is now the economical hypothesis instead of a bare presupposition.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Read D 119 as a source. Its metadata: a section titled "Experience and invention" in *Daybreak*, published 1881, in the second period, a free-spirit book of numbered sections. (a) From the date alone, say which parts of the mature picture, set out in BGE (1886) and EH (1888), a section of 1881 might not yet contain; then check the quoted passage: does any drive in it command the others? (b) Say what the section records, what it argues, and what it omits. (c) What can it be used to establish about Nietzsche's mature view, and what not?

<Solution>
**(a) Date and check.** The ranked "social structure", the governing class that says "I" and unity as an order of rank are in BGE 12 and 19 and EH 'Clever' 9. A section of 1881 may have the plurality without the order. The passage bears this out: six drives each seize the event in turn, "according to whether this or that drive happens at that moment to be at its height", and none of them commands another.

**(b) Records, argues, omits.** It *records* a thought-experiment the reader can check against experience: one event, laughter in the market, met in six different ways by six different people. It *argues* that what an event is for us depends on which drive is "at its height" and seizes it as prey; that drives are fed by chance, so that some grow and others wither; and that "to experience is to invent". It *omits* any ruling drive or order of rank: the drives take turns according to chance and hunger, and nothing in the section commands the others. It also omits any account of how the drives could be ordered, which D 560's gardener at least gestures at.

**(c) Use.** As a published book of period 2, D 119 is good evidence that by 1881 Nietzsche held that drives interpret, that they are many, and that they are fed by chance: the first three marks of a drive. It cannot be used as evidence of the order of rank, the governing class, or unity as an achievement; for those the evidence is BGE 6, 12, 19 and EH 'Clever' 9. A reader who quoted D 119 for "the self is a commonwealth of drives" would be attributing a later thesis to an earlier text.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "If the self is a society of drives, nobody can be self-controlled. Self-control means the self controlling its desires; if there is no self above the desires, there is nothing to do the controlling, and Nietzsche must deny that anyone ever resists a temptation."

<Solution>
**What the claim gets right.** On Nietzsche's picture there is no self above the desires. BGE 12 removes the soul-atom, and D 109 and BGE 117 say that whatever fights a drive is another drive.

**Where it goes wrong.** It runs together two things BGE 12 and its sequels keep apart: the soul-atom, a controller outside the drives, which is removed; and an order of rank among the drives, which is kept and is the whole subject of BGE 19 and EH 'Clever' 9. Self-control needs a controller, not an outside one. D 109 describes self-control in detail, six methods of it, and treats it as real: cravings are in fact avoided, rationed, tied to painful thoughts, starved. What it changes is the description of the controller: "the blind instrument of another drive which is a rival of the drive whose vehemence is tormenting us".

**The replacement.** A person is self-controlled when the drives that govern, the coalition that says "I", reliably prevail over those they do not authorise; weakness of will is a case where a drive outside the governing coalition wins. On this account self-control comes in degrees and can be gained or lost, which is what experience shows. The extreme of it, achieved without weakening the controlled drives, is EH's "order of rank among these capacities".

**The claim's hidden premise.** It assumes that only something outside the desires could control them. That is Plato's premise, and the chapter's argument is that nothing observed requires it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A boundary probe. Imagine a creature with exactly one drive, say hunger, and nothing else. Could it feel inner conflict, suffer weakness of will, or feel regret? What does your answer show about the work that plurality does in Nietzsche's psychology?

<Solution>
**Inner conflict.** No. Conflict is one drive against another (D 109: "for us to become aware that we are suffering from the vehemence of a drive presupposes the existence of another"). With one drive there is nothing for it to conflict with; the creature can meet obstacles outside itself but cannot be divided.

**Weakness of will.** No. Weakness of will is the governing coalition losing to a drive it does not command. With one drive, the governor and the governed coincide, so the governing drive can never be defeated from inside.

**Regret.** No, in any sense beyond frustration. Regret is a later drive, or the same governing coalition in a different state, condemning what an earlier one did, as pride condemns what memory reports in BGE 68. A single drive has no standpoint from which to condemn its own satisfaction.

**What this shows.** Every phenomenon the old picture explained with a soul above the passions, conflict, self-control, failure of self-control, self-judgment, the drive picture explains with plurality and order. Take the plurality away and these phenomena do not get a simpler explanation; they disappear. So plurality is not an extra assumption added to make the theory interesting. It is what the observations of step 1 require, and a creature with one drive would be not a simpler person but no person at all: it would have no rank to achieve, and so no unity to achieve either.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design the observation that would favour a motive-free arbiter over the drive picture. Say what the arbiter predicts that the drive picture does not, and then explain why introspection cannot supply the observation.

<Solution>
**What the arbiter predicts.** An arbiter without a stake, siding with the better reasons, predicts that a person's verdicts track the evidence *independently of which drive is strong at the moment*. The drive picture predicts the opposite: verdicts shift with the state of the drives (D 119: the same laughter read differently "according to whether this or that drive happens at that moment to be at its height"), and the side with the stronger interest wins even against the evidence (BGE 68).

**A design.** Present the same people with the same question under conditions that strengthen different drives (hungry and fed, insulted and flattered, tired and rested) while holding the evidence fixed, and compare their verdicts. If verdicts stay put while the drives are varied, that is evidence for something that judges independently of the drives. If they move with the drives, the drive picture is favoured. A more pointed version: find cases where people reach a verdict against their strongest current interest, and test whether some other drive (fear of disgrace, pride in being fair) predicts those cases.

**Why introspection cannot supply it.** Three reasons, all from the chapter. (1) GS 333: only "the last scenes of reconciliation and the final accounting" reach consciousness, so a settlement among drives looks from the inside exactly like a judge's verdict. Introspection sees the outcome, not how it was produced. (2) BGE 19: the governing drive says "I" and takes credit for what was done, so the inside report is written by the winner. (3) D 109: the intellect that would do the introspecting is itself "the blind instrument of another drive". The observation has to compare verdicts across conditions from outside, which is the kind of test that later experimental psychology has run on such questions.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Go back to D 109 as frame 3 of the sequence shows it. In the person fighting the craving, what did the *conscious mind* actually contribute, and what did it only report? List both, then say what this suggests about consciousness itself.

<Solution>
**Contributed.** The method's execution: building the association between gratification and a painful thought, keeping to a timetable, finding the hard labour to divert strength into. D 109 calls these the intellect's "artifices". Consciousness is where the plan is worked out in words and images.

**Only reported.** Everything D 109 says "does not stand within our own power": *that* the craving is to be fought at all, *which* method is chosen, and whether it succeeds. These are decided by the rival drive and by the relative strength of the two; consciousness registers the outcome as "I have decided to give it up" and "I have managed" or "I have failed".

**What it suggests.** Consciousness, on this evidence, is not the place where decisions are made but the place where some of them are announced and carried out, in a voice ("we") that belongs to whichever drive is winning. It reports more than it does. That raises the question why there is consciousness at all, if the deciding goes on below it, and why it presents itself as the whole self. <Ref lesson="consciousness-and-language">Consciousness and language as herd instruments</Ref> answers that consciousness developed for communication with others, and so shows us only the part of ourselves that can be said.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

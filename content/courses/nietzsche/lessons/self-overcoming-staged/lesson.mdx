In 'On Self-Overcoming' Zarathustra reports a conversation with Life itself, who tells him 'I am that which must always overcome itself' — the cosmological will to power delivered as testimony from a character, exactly where Beyond Good and Evil's argument for it breaks down. This lesson is the module's application: the artefact is three speeches of *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, "On Self-Overcoming" (Part II, 1883), "On the Despisers of the Body" and "On the Hinterworldly" (both Part I, 1883), the texts through which the doctrine of will to power entered the culture as a sentence, and the theory is the module's three reconstructed results. Each speech is mapped sentence by sentence onto an argued passage or onto nothing, and the residue is filed in three columns, what the staging adds, drops and undercuts, with the departure at the centre stated as a fact about mood: the argued work says "would be", and the speech has Life say "I am".

The module has put a psychology under the genealogy. <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">The drives lesson</Ref> found in BGE 12 and 19 a soul that is a society of drives with command as its relation, and left open whether the society's rank is strength or authority. <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">The will-to-power lesson</Ref> drew the line through the doctrine: as psychology, Reginster's three conditions, an end, a resistance and its overcoming, it is what every treatise uses; as cosmology it is argued exactly once, in BGE 36, in the conditional, from a premise the same book rejects. <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">The fable lesson</Ref> removed the world behind the world that a cosmological doctrine would have had to be true of. What stands open is the book in which the doctrine is most often quoted from: "wherever I found the living, there I found will to power" is a line of *Zarathustra*, not of *Beyond Good and Evil*, and Kaufmann, Heidegger and Reginster each cite that speech for what will to power is. This lesson reads it by the rule fixed in <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref>, and the result is the module's verdict on its own doctrine's most famous statement. <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">The next lesson</Ref> opens the positive doctrine on the problem Life's last words leave: a will that must always overcome itself, and a book in which a soothsayer answers that everything is empty.

The route: the three speeches dated and the rule recalled, with the map running against the dates by three years; "On Self-Overcoming" mapped in twelve lines across its three movements, the wisest, the living and Life; the departure stated as a difference of mood and drawn as three removes between the claim and the reader; "On the Despisers of the Body" mapped onto BGE 12 and 19 with its residue, one commander, left standing; "On the Hinterworldly" mapped onto the fable with its residue, a motive, left standing; the frame, "On Poets" and the animals' hurdy-gurdy song; and the verdict in three columns with the reading that takes the speech as doctrine placed beside it.

## The artefact: three speeches of 1883, three years before the argument

The rule this lesson runs was fixed on the First Treatise's speeches, and the first thing to recall is what it treats a speech as. <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">The tarantulas lesson</Ref> made *Zarathustra*'s genre a procedure: a speech is testimony from a character, each sentence carrying a claim is set against the argued passage that states the claim, by book and section, or against "none", and what the correspondence leaves over is filed under three headings, *adds* (what the speech contains that no argued sentence does), *drops* (what the argued works contain that the speech does not), and *undercuts* (where the speech's own frame withdraws what its sentences assert). The output is a verdict on what the speech can be cited for. The argued passage is always the premise; the speech is cited for the first column with the third beside it.

The three speeches sit early in their parts. "On the Hinterworldly" and "On the Despisers of the Body" are the third and fourth speeches of Part I, written in January 1883, directly after the three metamorphoses and the teacher of sleep; "On Self-Overcoming" is the twelfth speech of Part II, written in June and July of the same year, and "On Poets", the frame passage, is the seventeenth. The sections they map onto are BGE 12, 13, 17, 19, 22 and 36 (1886), GM II:12, III:24 and III:27 (1887), and the "Reason" chapter and the fable of *Twilight* (1888). As with the tarantulas, the speeches precede by three to five years the passages that argue what they stage, and the rule survives for the reason given there: the map is a relation between texts, not a claim about composition, and the protocol's order-of-composition check makes the later text the one to cite wherever the two disagree.

What is different this time is the standing of the artefact. The tarantula speech is one dramatization of a diagnosis the treatise argues in full; "On Self-Overcoming" is the text the doctrine is quoted from. On Kaufmann's count the phrase "will to power" enters a book Nietzsche published in "On a Thousand and One Goals" (Z I 15), mapped in <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref>, is "not mentioned again until much later, and then at length", in this speech, and occurs only once more in the book.

Heidegger, whose reading stands at the far end of the course's spectrum from Kaufmann's, calls the speech "the episode in which the first sovereign insight into the essence of will to power is achieved". And Reginster, whose three conditions the previous lesson adopted as the psychological doctrine, quotes "I am that which must always overcome itself" as the sentence in which the pursuit of power takes "the form of growth, or self-overcoming". Three readers who agree on nothing else take one sentence of a character's report as the doctrine's statement, and that is why the map matters more here than for the tarantulas: the line being mapped is the one the reception treats as the premise.

<KeyFact>
Kaufmann, Heidegger and Reginster each quote Z II 12 for what will to power is; the argued works' one argument for the cosmology, BGE 36, is quoted, when it is quoted, for its "suppose".
</KeyFact>

## "On Self-Overcoming" mapped: the wisest, the living, and Life

The speech is in three movements with a coda, and the map follows them. The first movement is addressed to "you wisest ones" and is about the will to truth. The second is Zarathustra's report of what he found when he "pursued the living": obedience, command, and a will to be master in the will of the servant. The third is a quotation: "this secret life itself spoke to me", and Life speaks for thirteen sentences. The coda returns to the wisest and to good and evil. Twelve lines carry the speech's claims, and ten of them have an argued passage.

### The wisest: the will to truth as a will to power

The speech opens by renaming a drive. "'Will to truth' you call that which drives you and makes you lustful, you wisest ones? Will to thinkability of all being, that's what I call your will!" The wisest "first want to make all being thinkable", and being "shall behave and bend", "become smooth and subservient to the spirit, as its mirror and reflection"; "that is your entire will, you wisest ones, as a will to power; and even when you speak of good and evil and of valuations". Then the line that gives the movement its charge: "You still want to create the world before which you could kneel: this is your ultimate hope and intoxication."

Each sentence has an argued counterpart, and two of them are the module's. The renaming of the will to truth is GM III:24's demand, reconstructed in <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the will-to-truth lesson</Ref>, that the will to truth "requires a critique", with the will to truth read there as the ascetic ideal's kernel; and it is BGE 22's charge against the physicists, from <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref>, that "conformity to law" is not a text but a reading, a value read into nature. The bending of being into the spirit's mirror is BGE 9's Stoics, who "want to dictate and annex your morals and ideals onto nature", and the section's generalization: philosophy "always creates the world in its own image; it cannot do otherwise", it is "the most spiritual will to power". "The world before which you could kneel" is the same sentence with the motive named.

The map records one more counterpart, BGE 211's philosophers of the future as "commanders and legislators", because the speech's wisest are said to have "placed such guests into the skiff", the valuations that float on "the river of becoming", and "gave them pomp and proud names". The people's good and evil "reveals to me an ancient will to power": that is Z I 15's tablets restated, with the tablets now traced to the wisest rather than to a people, and BGE 260's noble type who "creates values".

### Clark: where self-overcoming enters the doctrine

Clark's reading of this movement fixes what the speech has that Z I 15 did not. In the first speech on the doctrine a people esteems what makes it "rule, and triumph, and shine to the envy of their neighbor", and nothing there connects power to power over oneself; in this speech the people's values are said to reflect the will to power of the wisest, and Zarathustra warns the wisest that "this will itself, the will to power" is their "danger". On Clark's account that is the point at which self-overcoming enters the doctrine at all: the wisest are priests and philosophers, the *Genealogy* will find them proponents of the ascetic ideal, and the danger is that their will to power, satisfied by self-denial, turns the activity of valuing against life. The self-overcoming of the speech's title belongs first to the wisest and is a danger before it is a law.

### The living: command and obedience, with a will to be master in the servant

The second movement reports an investigation. "I pursued the living, I walked the greatest and the smallest paths in order to know its nature", and "with a hundredfold mirror I captured even its glance, when its mouth was closed, so that its eyes could speak to me". Three things were heard. "Wherever I found the living, there too I heard the speech on obedience. All living is an obeying." Second, "the one who cannot obey himself is commanded". Third, "commanding is harder than obeying", because the commander "bears the burden of all obeyers" and, in commanding, "risks itself"; "even when it commands itself, even then it must pay for its commanding. It must become the judge and avenger and victim of its own law."

<Quote source="Z II 12, 'On Self-Overcoming', trans. Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
Wo ich Lebendiges fand, da fand ich Willen zur Macht; und noch im Willen des Dienenden fand ich den Willen, Herr zu sein.

Dass dem Stärkeren diene das Schwächere, dazu überredet es sein Wille, der über noch Schwächeres Herr sein will: dieser Lust allein mag es nicht entrathen.

Und wie das Kleinere sich dem Grösseren hingiebt, dass es Lust und Macht am Kleinsten habe: also giebt sich auch das Grösste noch hin und setzt um der Macht willen — das Leben dran.
</Original>

Wherever I found the living, there I found the will to power; and even in the will of the serving I found the will to be master.

The weaker is persuaded by its own will to serve the stronger, because it wants to be master over what is still weaker: this is the only pleasure it is incapable of renouncing.

And as the smaller gives way to the greater, in order for it to have its pleasure and power over the smallest, so too the greatest gives way, and for the sake of power it risks – life itself.
</Quote>

The argued passage for the whole movement is BGE 19, read in <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drives lesson</Ref>: willing is "a matter of commanding and obeying, on the groundwork of a society constructed out of many 'souls'", the commanded are "under-wills", and "obeys, or that he believes to obey" makes obedience a fact about the other party. The speech's "the one who cannot obey himself is commanded" is the section's commonwealth stated as a rule, and "commanding is harder than obeying" is the section's "the burden" and its "affect of command" from the commander's side.

What the speech adds to BGE 19 is a reason the under-wills obey: the weaker serves the stronger "because it wants to be master over what is still weaker", obedience as the price of a command lower down. Clark and Dudrick take this line as the answer to the question BGE 19 leaves open, why the commanded drives ever obey, and read it as the will to power explaining the coherence of the drive-society; the drives lesson recorded that BGE 19 itself gives no account of why the rank is as it is, and the speech's account is a further claim, mapped here and not smuggled back into the section.

The sentence itself, "wherever I found the living, there I found will to power", is BGE 13's "life itself is will to power" and BGE 259's "life itself is essentially" appropriation, both reconstructed in the previous lesson and both asserted there; "the will to be master" in the will of the serving is GM II:12's rule that every purpose is a sign "that a will to power has become master of something less powerful". And "the greatest gives way, and for the sake of power it risks – life itself" is BGE 13's anti-preservation premise, that "a living thing wants to discharge its strength" and preservation is a by-product, with GS 349's "frequently risks and even sacrifices self-preservation" as its published gloss.

### One line with no counterpart: the weaker steals power

One line of the movement has no argued counterpart and the map leaves it so: "along secret passages the weaker sneaks into the fortress and straight to the heart of the more powerful, and there it steals power". No section of BGE or GM says that the weak steal power from the strong by service; the nearest is the priest's redirection of III:15, which is a different mechanism. Heidegger's reading fills exactly this row, and the verdict section returns to it.

### Life: the one argument in the speech is against a rival

Then Life speaks, and the speech gives the doctrine its canonical form.

<Quote source="Z II 12, 'On Self-Overcoming', trans. Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
Und diess Geheimniss redete das Leben selber zu mir. „Siehe, sprach es, ich bin das, was sich immer selber überwinden muss.

„Freilich, ihr heisst es Wille zur Zeugung oder Trieb zum Zwecke, zum Höheren, Ferneren, Vielfacheren: aber all diess ist Eins und Ein Geheimniss. […]

„Der traf freilich die Wahrheit nicht, der das Wort nach ihr schoss vom „Willen zum Dasein": diesen Willen — giebt es nicht!

„Denn: was nicht ist, das kann nicht wollen; was aber im Dasein ist, wie könnte das noch zum Dasein wollen!

„Nur, wo Leben ist, da ist auch Wille: aber nicht Wille zum Leben, sondern — so lehre ich's dich — Wille zur Macht!

„Vieles ist dem Lebenden höher geschätzt, als Leben selber; doch aus dem Schätzen selber heraus redet — der Wille zur Macht!" —

Also lehrte mich einst das Leben: und daraus löse ich euch, ihr Weisesten, noch das Räthsel eures Herzens.
</Original>

And this secret life itself spoke to me: "Behold," it said, "I am that which must always overcome itself.

To be sure, you call it will to beget or drive to a purpose, to something higher, more distant, more manifold: but all this is one, and one secret. […]

Indeed, the one who shot at truth with the words 'will to existence' did not hit it: this will – does not exist!

For, what is not can not will; but what is in existence, how could this still will to exist!

Only where life is, is there also will; but not will to life, instead – thus I teach you – will to power!

Much is esteemed more highly by life than life itself; yet out of esteeming itself speaks – the will to power!" –

Thus life once taught me, and from this I shall yet solve the riddle of your heart, you wisest ones.
</Quote>

From a speech in which a character teaches, one expects the character's own sentences to carry the doctrine. The text has it the other way: Zarathustra does not teach the doctrine, he is taught it, "thus life once taught me" (*also lehrte mich einst das Leben*), and what he adds in his own voice is that he will use it "to solve the riddle of your heart". The doctrine's canonical sentence is a quotation inside a speech.

Two of Life's sentences map onto the argued works directly, and the mapping shows which register argues. "You call it will to beget or drive to a purpose … but all this is one" is BGE 36's seventh step, the supposition that "our entire drive-life" could be explained "as the organization and outgrowth of one basic form of will", with procreation and nutrition "a single problem"; the speech has Life assert the unity that BGE 36 supposes. And "not will to life … will to power", with the two lines before it, is BGE 13 against Spinoza's *conatus*: the drive to self-preservation is a "superfluous teleological principle" because a living thing "wants to discharge its strength" first. The speech's version is the only argument in the speech.

<Remark title="the speech's one argument, against will to existence">
**P1.** What does not exist cannot will; so a will to existence cannot be the will of what does not yet exist.

**P2.** What exists already has existence, and cannot will what it has.

**C.** "Will to existence", Schopenhauer's *Wille zum Leben* in the form the speech names, "does not exist".

Two premises and a conclusion, valid, aimed at a rival doctrine.
</Remark>

The positive doctrine is not argued at all; Life states it, "thus I teach you". That is BGE 13's shape exactly, a razor applied to the rival and the alternative installed by assertion, and the previous lesson graded the section the same way.

The last two of Life's sentences are the ones the reception carries. "I am that which must always overcome itself" is, on Reginster's analysis from the previous lesson, the self-perpetuating structure of the will to power stated by its bearer: satisfaction removes the resistance that was its own condition, so the pursuit "assumes the form of growth, or self-overcoming", where self-overcoming is "not overcoming of the self but overcoming of the overcoming itself", the movement by which a will that has won looks for a harder opponent.

"Out of esteeming itself speaks the will to power" is Z I 15's "esteeming is creating" with the doctrine named as the esteemer, the sentence A §2 turns into a definition. The coda then states, of good and evil, what Life stated of itself: "good and evil that would be everlasting, there is no such thing! They must overcome themselves out of themselves again and again", and "whoever must be a creator in good and evil, truly, he must first be an annihilator and break values". The first is GM III:27's "law of the necessary 'self-overcoming' in the essence of life" applied to valuations, four years early; the second is BGE 211 again, and Z I 15's creators. The warm-up exercise below maps the four lines the figure leaves out.

<KeyFact>
The speech contains one argument, and it is against will to life; the positive doctrine is delivered by Life and reported by Zarathustra. BGE 13 has the same shape, a razor against self-preservation and discharge installed by assertion, so the two registers differ not in what is argued but in who asserts the rest: in 1886 the author under "suppose", in 1883 a personification under "I am".
</KeyFact>

## The departure: "would be" against "I am"

The map's two amber rows are the lesson's centre, and the departure they mark can be stated as a fact about two verbs. BGE 36's last sentence, from the previous lesson, reads: *Die Welt von innen gesehen, die Welt auf ihren „intelligiblen Charakter" hin bestimmt und bezeichnet — sie wäre eben „Wille zur Macht" und nichts ausserdem*, "the world seen from inside … would be just this 'will to power' and nothing else". Life's sentence reads: *ich bin das, was sich immer selber überwinden muss*, "I am that which must always overcome itself". The content is one proposition, one basic form of will whose pursuit is its own overcoming; the mood is *wäre* in one register and *ich bin* in the other.

### Three removes between the claim and the reader

The difference is evidential, and it can be counted. An argument stands at no remove from its reader: BGE 36's seven steps are on the page, each marked by its author with its mood, and <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the previous lesson</Ref> found, with Clark, that the first step supposes the simple, known, acting will that BGE 16 to 19 had dissolved, so that the argument begs the question it poses and its author marks the fact by never leaving the subjunctive. The reader of BGE 36 holds a conditional and can see what is wrong with its antecedent.

A personification's testimony stands at one remove: "Life" is not the author and not a thing anyone can question, and "I am that which must always overcome itself" has no steps, so there is nothing in it for a reader to check. Zarathustra's report of the testimony stands at two: a character says that a personification said it, to an audience inside the book, in a book whose speaker will say five speeches later that he too is a poet.

### What the speech offers as evidence: an induction, or a value's construction

The speech does not present itself as revelation. It names its evidence as observation, "I pursued the living", "with a hundredfold mirror I captured even its glance", and invites the wisest to "check seriously" whether Zarathustra "crept into the very heart of life".

Kaufmann takes the invitation at its word: the claim that all living beings are will to power "is evidently offered in an empirical spirit", one "may criticize Nietzsche for having performed an induction that is unconvincing", and "the conclusion 'Thus life taught me' is probably intended to stress that Nietzsche's insight is based on experience". The reading is possible only if "thus life taught me" is a figure for "thus I observed", and the cost is on the page: the speech's evidence is a mirror and a glance, and an induction whose data are a glance is not one a reader can rerun.

Clark reads the same sentences the other way, as "the obviously metaphorical and anthropomorphic language" in which Zarathustra "proceeds to formulate the cosmological version of will to power", and takes the personification as the mark that the doctrine is being constructed from the standpoint of a value rather than known. Neither reader disputes what the sentence says; they dispute what kind of sentence it is, and the dramatization rule decides that question by the speaker. A character's report of a personification's speech is testimony, whatever the character says about mirrors.

### The Cretan: a residue the argued works admit and the speech does not

There is a further fact about the first movement that the speech does not notice and the argued works do. The wisest are told that their will to truth is a will to power that "creates the world before which you could kneel"; then Life is quoted creating a world, "I am that which must always overcome itself", and the speaker who quotes her will "solve the riddle of your heart" with it. Kaufmann states the difficulty as the predicament of Epimenides the Cretan, who said that all Cretans lie: if every attempt to understand the universe is prompted by the will to power, "his own conception of the will to power must be admitted by him to be a creation of his will to power", and "if his assertion is correct, it is a fiction".

The argued works carry this residue in the open. BGE 22 ends "granted, this is only an interpretation too … well then, so much the better", and BGE 9's "philosophy is this tyrannical drive itself" is said in a book whose author is philosophizing; on Clark's reading BGE 36 is Nietzsche doing in full view what BGE 9 says philosophy does, with the moods as his admission.

Kaufmann's reply on Nietzsche's behalf is Kantian: the doctrine is a fiction of the human constitution that is necessary and therefore not subjective, so that "mankind" rather than Nietzsche stands in the Cretan's position. He grants that the reply "is not to be found in Nietzsche's writings in this form", and it concedes the objection's premise, that the doctrine is a construction, which after the fable's sixth stage is no charge, since a lie needs a true world to fall short of and the module has removed it. The reply is in the argued register either way, and the speech, which has no theory of knowledge in it, cannot make it. What the speech has instead is the frame, which is the sixth section's subject.

<KeyFact>
The departure is a difference of evidential status and not of content. BGE 36 supposes what Life asserts, marks the supposition in every verb, and is followed by an admission that the result is "only an interpretation"; the speech drops the "suppose", supplies a speaker whose authority is that she is Life, and places the doctrine two removes from any reader who might check it. What it does not supply, its own frame says it cannot: "Zarathustra too is a poet".
</KeyFact>

## "On the Despisers of the Body": one commander where BGE 19 has a class

The second speech was read once already, in <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drives lesson</Ref>, as BGE 12's expulsion of the soul-atom staged three years early; the present section runs the full map and keeps the residue that reading noticed. The speech's thesis is one sentence, and the sentence contains a phrase the reader has met.

<Quote source="Z I 4, 'On the Despisers of the Body', trans. Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
Aber der Erwachte, der Wissende sagt: Leib bin ich ganz und gar, und Nichts ausserdem; und Seele ist nur ein Wort für ein Etwas am Leibe.

Der Leib ist eine grosse Vernunft, eine Vielheit mit Einem Sinne, ein Krieg und ein Frieden, eine Heerde und ein Hirt.
</Original>

But the awakened, the knowing one says: body am I through and through, and nothing besides; and soul is just a word for something on the body.

The body is a great reason, a multiplicity with one sense, a war and a peace, one herd and one shepherd.
</Quote>

*Und Nichts ausserdem*, "and nothing besides", is the phrase with which BGE 36 closes three years later, *und nichts ausserdem*, said there of the world. In 1883 the "nothing besides" is said of the body; the map records the verbal fact and draws nothing from it beyond this, that the formula of totality was the body's before it was the world's.

### Six rows that map

Six of the speech's seven load-bearing lines have an argued counterpart, and the agreement is what gives the seventh its weight: a residue against a text the speech otherwise carries whole is an addition, not a gap in the map. Two rows carry BGE 12. "Body am I through and through, and nothing besides" is the section's expulsion of the soul-atom, "the belief that the soul is something indestructible, eternal, indivisible"; "soul is just a word for something on the body" is its other half, the hypothesis kept: the "something" is still there to be named, and BGE 12 names it "mortal soul". The speech has the section's negative clause and its positive one, in that order.

Two rows carry the multiplicity. "A great reason, a multiplicity with one sense" is BGE 12's "subject-multiplicity" and "a society constructed out of drives and affects", with the speech's "war and a peace" for the society's politics; "your small reason, what you call 'spirit'", a "tool of your body", is GS 354's consciousness, "only the smallest part" of thinking, "the most superficial part, the worst part", developed as an instrument and not a seat. The ranking, body over spirit, is the argued works' ranking, drives over consciousness.

Two rows carry the deed and the command. "It does not say I, but does I" (*die sagt nicht Ich, aber thut Ich*) is BGE 17's "it thinks", the sentence that stripped "I think" of its subject, with the doer fiction of <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">GM I:13</Ref> behind both: the speech keeps the deed, drops the grammatical doer, and gives the deed to the body. And "the self says to the ego: 'Feel pain here!' And then it suffers and reflects on how it might suffer no more, and just for that purpose it is supposed to think" is BGE 19's command with its obedience: a commanding party, an executing party, and thinking as the instrument the command puts to work, the section's "successful instruments that carry out the task". Six rows, three sections and one aphorism: the speech's body is the argued works' soul.

### The second half: the despisers as a declining type

The speech's second half maps onto the Third Treatise. The despisers' self "wants to die and turns away from life" because it is "no longer capable of that which it wants most: to create beyond itself", and "for this reason you became despisers of the body". That is GM III:11–13's account, from <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref>, of the ascetic ideal as what a declining life does with its decline, "life wrestling with death", and it is the fourth thesis of *Twilight*'s "Reason" §6, "a symptom of declining life", stated of a type by a speaker who addresses the type directly. In both registers the claim is a type-fact asserted; the speech asserts it in the second person.

### The residue: a commander with a mind of its own

The seventh row does not map. <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">The drives lesson</Ref> noticed the line in passing when it read the speech for BGE 12. The speech's body has "one sense", "one shepherd", and "behind your thoughts and feelings, my brother, stands a mighty commander, an unknown wise man, he is called self" (*ein mächtiger Gebieter, ein unbekannter Weiser — der heisst Selbst*). The self "rules and is also the ruler of the ego", it "laughs at your ego", it calls itself "the leading strings of the ego and the prompter of its concepts". That is a single agent with purposes of its own, addressed by a singular pronoun, that uses the ego's thinking "for its purposes". BGE 19's commander is "the ruling class" (*die regierende Klasse*) of a "commonwealth", a class and not a person, and BGE 12's structure is a *Subjekts-Vielheit*, a multiplicity in the subject position. The argued works name many and an arrangement among them; the speech names the arrangement's head and gives it a mind.

The map does not smooth this into BGE 19, and the reason is the dispute the drives lesson left undecided. Whether the drive-society's rank is causal strength, Leiter's physics, or recognized authority, Clark and Dudrick's government, is the premise BGE 19 underdetermines, and the speech's "sage" fits the second reading and Anderson's third, a self "with the subjective capacity to inhabit attitudes of its own", better than the first: a commander who "laughs" at the ego and "prompts" its concepts is not the strongest force among forces but a party with a view.

What the speech cannot do is decide the dispute, because it is testimony, and testimony from a character cannot supply the premise an argued section lacks. It is cited under "adds": an image of the self's unity that the argued works do not assert, which <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the value-creation lesson</Ref> will need when a naturalist has to say who creates a value, and which <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">Nehamas's reading</Ref> takes as the unity of a character.

<KeyFact>
Six of the speech's seven load-bearing lines map onto BGE 12, 17 and 19 and GS 354; the seventh gives the body a single commander, "an unknown sage", where the argued works give it a ruling class whose rank is undetermined. The line is the political reading of the drive-society delivered as a character, and the map records it as an addition rather than reading it back into a section that does not say it.
</KeyFact>

## "On the Hinterworldly": the fable told as one sufferer's biography

The third speech is the one the fable's last words point at. "INCIPIT ZARATHUSTRA" closes the sixth stage, and the third speech of *Zarathustra*'s first part is a first-person history of the two-world error: "Once Zarathustra too cast his delusion beyond humans, like all hinterworldly." <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">The fable lesson</Ref>'s last exercise read the speech for its one sentence about a motive; the present section maps it. Del Caro's coinage carries the German: *Hinterweltler* are those who believe in a world behind, with the connotations of *Hinterland* and of *Hintergedanken*, secret thoughts, and the translator's note says as much.

<Quote source="Z I 3, 'On the Hinterworldly', trans. Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
Leiden war's und Unvermögen — das schuf alle Hinterwelten; und jener kurze Wahnsinn des Glücks, den nur der Leidendste erfährt.

Müdigkeit, die mit Einem Sprunge zum Letzten will, mit einem Todessprunge, eine arme unwissende Müdigkeit, die nicht einmal mehr wollen will: die schuf alle Götter und Hinterwelten.

Glaubt es mir, meine Brüder! Der Leib war's, der am Leibe verzweifelte, — der tastete mit den Fingern des bethörten Geistes an die letzten Wände.
</Original>

It was suffering and incapacity that created all hinterworlds, and that brief madness of happiness that only the most suffering person experiences.

Weariness that wants its ultimate with one great leap, with a death leap; a poor unknowing weariness that no longer even wants to will: that created all gods and hinterworlds.

Believe me, my brothers! It was the body that despaired of the body – it probed with the fingers of a befooled spirit on the walls of the ultimate.
</Quote>

### The rows that map onto the fable and the "Reason" chapter

The speech's biography compresses the ladder, and three of its sentences are three of the fable's stages. Zarathustra's former god, "a suffering and tortured god" whose work the world seemed, is a true world's occupant with the fable's first two predicates, a world attained by its creator and promised as an escape; "this god that I created was of human make and madness, like all gods", "it did not come to me from beyond", is the fifth stage's denial, the true world as a "refuted idea"; and "I overcame myself, my suffering self, I carried my own ashes to the mountain … and behold! The ghost shrank from me!" is the convalescence that stage six's noon names. Three stages of six, in a life instead of a history.

Two sentences carry the "Reason" chapter's theses. The "other world" is "a heavenly nothing" (*ein himmlisches Nichts*), which is §6's second thesis, that the marks of true being "are the characteristics of non-being, of nothingness"; and "the belly of being does not speak at all to humans, unless as a human" is BGE 9's "it always creates the world in its own image" told from inside the creating.

The last rows carry the diagnosis. The preachers of hinterworlds, who "believe most in the body, and their own body is to them their thing in itself" but "a sickly thing", are the fable's fifth stage with the Kantian term turned into a diagnosis, as the fable lesson read it; and the closing lines, "the healthy body … speaks of the meaning of the earth", are stage six's one world with the standard that the fourth thesis of §6 supplies, the healthy against the sick. On every row the speech has the fable's result and the chapter's verdict, in a first person the argued texts do not use; what it does not have is the ladder, and what the ladder does not have is the subject of the next subsection.

### The residue: a motive the fable never states

From a speech placed at the point where the fable ends, one expects the six stages retold; the speech contains none of them. What it contains is what the fable's six sentences never state, a reason the first stage was occupied: "suffering and incapacity", "the body that despaired of the body", "weariness that wants its ultimate with one great leap". The fable moves a predicate through six stages and says nothing about why anyone wanted a true world; the speech gives a motive and no stages.

The argued counterpart of the motive is in the chapter before the fable, and the fable lesson flagged it: §6's third thesis, that fabling of another world "has no sense at all" unless "an instinct for slandering, disparaging and accusing life is strong within us", in which case "we revenge ourselves on life by means of the phantasmagoria of 'another', a 'better' life", and the fourth, that the division itself is "a symptom of declining life". Both theses assert a type-fact; neither argues it, and the fable lesson marked the step from "false" to "sick" as the is–ought seam of the chapter.

On this row, therefore, the two registers stand at the same evidential level. The speech asserts the motive as a confession, the chapter asserts it as a thesis, and the course cites the chapter because a thesis in the author's voice is what the published-works rule counts, not because the chapter argues what the speech only stages.

### The second residue: a speaker inside the error

The second residue is the confession itself. "Once Zarathustra too cast his delusion beyond humans"; "I too once cast my delusion"; "from my own ash and ember it came to me, this ghost". The fable's stages have occupants, Plato, Christianity, Kant, and on Clark's reading the last three are Nietzsche's own books; but the fable has no first person, and the "Reason" chapter diagnoses the philosophers from outside. The speech puts the speaker inside the error and has him report the cure. That is the structure the tarantulas lesson found in the bite: a diagnostician subject to the diagnosis, which no argued text of Nietzsche's does with its narrator. The speech is cited under "adds" for it, with the same qualification as the bite: a character's confession is a fact about the drama, and not evidence that the two-world error has the motive he confesses.

<KeyFact>
The fable is a history without a motive and the speech is a motive without a history. The motive, suffering, has an argued counterpart in *Twilight*'s "Reason" §6, where it is a thesis about a type and is asserted; on this row the speech adds nothing the argued works lack in evidence, only the first person, and the first person is the residue.
</KeyFact>

## The frame: "On Poets" and the animals' hurdy-gurdy song

The book states its own evidential status in the speech that stands five after "On Self-Overcoming", and the speech opens by naming the thesis of "On the Despisers of the Body". "'Since I have come to know the body better,' Zarathustra said to one of his disciples, 'the spirit is only a hypothetical spirit to me; and all that is "everlasting", that too is only a parable.'" The disciple recalls that he once added "but the poets lie too much", and asks why.

<Quote source="Z II 17, 'On Poets', trans. Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
„Warum? sagte Zarathustra. Du fragst warum? Ich gehöre nicht zu Denen, welche man nach ihrem Warum fragen darf. […]

Doch was sagte dir einst Zarathustra? Dass die Dichter zuviel lügen? — Aber auch Zarathustra ist ein Dichter.

Glaubst du nun, dass er hier die Wahrheit redete? Warum glaubst du das?"

Der Jünger antwortete: „ich glaube an Zarathustra." Aber Zarathustra schüttelte den Kopf und lächelte.

Der Glaube macht mich nicht selig, sagte er, zumal nicht der Glaube an mich.

Aber gesetzt, dass Jemand allen Ernstes sagte, die Dichter lügen zuviel: so hat er Recht, — wir lügen zuviel.

Wir wissen auch zu wenig und sind schlechte Lerner: so müssen wir schon lügen.
</Original>

"Why?" said Zarathustra. "You ask why? I do not belong to those whose Why may be questioned. […]

Yet what did Zarathustra once say to you? That the poets lie too much? – But Zarathustra too is a poet.

Do you believe now that he speaks the truth here? Why do you believe that?"

The disciple answered: "I believe in Zarathustra." But Zarathustra shook his head and smiled.

"Faith does not make me blessed," he said. "Especially not faith in me.

But supposing that someone said in all earnestness that the poets lie too much: he is right – we lie too much.

We also know too little and are bad learners, thus we simply have to lie."
</Quote>

Three sentences of the passage bear on the map, one per column. "I do not belong to those whose Why may be questioned" is the speaker declining to give reasons, in the speech that follows the one in which he gave none for Life's doctrine; the sentence is the drop column stated by the dropper.

"Zarathustra too is a poet" is the undercut, and its scope is the whole book: the disciple's "I believe in Zarathustra" is the reader's position, and the reply is that faith in the speaker is not a ground.

And "we know too little and are bad learners, thus we simply have to lie" is a statement about what a poet's sentences are made of, followed by the inventory: poets believe that "nature herself is in love with them" and "creeps up to their ears to tell them secrets", which is what Life did in "On Self-Overcoming", and "all gods are poets' parable", so that "atop the clouds we set our motley bastards and then call them gods and overmen", the overman listed with the gods among the things poets make. The tarantulas lesson paired this speech with "lose me and find yourselves" from "On the Bestowing Virtue"; the pairing holds, and what the present lesson adds is that "On Poets" names the mechanism of the very speech it follows, a secret told into a poet's ear.

The device recurs at the book's highest point, and <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">the recurrence lesson</Ref> reads it in full. In "The Convalescent" (Part III, 1884) Zarathustra's animals recite the eternal recurrence to him, "everything goes, everything comes back; eternally rolls the wheel of being", and he answers: "Oh you foolish rascals and barrel organs! … you have already made a hurdy-gurdy song of it?" The doctrine Nietzsche called his deepest is stated in the book by two animals and called a barrel-organ tune by the character who is supposed to teach it. The pattern is the same as the frame's: the book delivers a doctrine and marks the delivery as a performance, in the same breath.

The rule the frame fixes is the one the course has used since <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>, now with the book's own warrant. The speeches are evidence of what Nietzsche staged; the argued works are evidence of what he argued; the course cites the second for the first's content, and the first for what the staging adds and what it undercuts. "On Poets" is not a disclaimer of doctrine, and the map does not read it as one: it is a statement of genre, made inside the genre, and its force is exactly that a sentence like "I am that which must always overcome itself" is to be cited for what it is, a poet's report of what nature said into his ear.

<KeyFact>
The frame's cost falls on one reading. A sentence delivered by the device "On Poets" names, a secret told into a poet's ear and reported without reasons, can stand as the doctrine's statement only if the frame is set aside; the dramatization rule keeps it, and the reading of the next section, which takes Life's sentence as a report of Being, has to drop it.
</KeyFact>

## The verdict: adds, drops, undercuts, and the reading that takes the speech as doctrine

The maps are complete and the rule's second step files the residue for the three speeches together. The application's result is the departure between the two registers, stated as fact about each.

**Adds.** Four things, and the first is the one the reception took. A speaker: the cosmological doctrine has, in the speech, a first person and an authority, Life saying "I am", where the argued work has an author saying "would be" under a method he names. A motive: "suffering and incapacity" as the origin of every hinterworld, which the fable does not state and the "Reason" chapter states as a thesis. An image of the self's unity: one commander, "an unknown sage", where BGE 19 has a ruling class. And a confession: the speaker inside the two-world error, reporting his cure, which the argued works never do with their narrator. Each is cited as the speech's, with the third column beside it.

**Drops.** Everything by which the argued works mark their own status. The "suppose" (*Gesetzt*) that opens BGE 36 and returns twice; the "conscience of method" of step 5, the one indicative in the section and a demand on method rather than a claim about the world; BGE 22's "only an interpretation" and "so much the better"; BGE 13's reasons for the razor, "the economy of principles", where the speech has only the razor's result; Boscovich and the refuted atom by which BGE 12 licensed the drives; the six stages, so that a reader of "On the Hinterworldly" learns that a true world was made and abandoned and nothing of how; and the parts list of BGE 19, the feelings, the thought and the affect, by which willing was dissected before it was called command. What is dropped is not the doctrine but its mood, its method and its evidence, and the three are what an argument consists of.

**Undercuts.** The frame, in five sentences the book puts in its speaker's mouth: "Zarathustra too is a poet"; "we know too little and are bad learners, thus we simply have to lie"; "thus Life once taught me", the doctrine received rather than found; the animals' "hurdy-gurdy song" at the recurrence; and "I do not belong to those whose Why may be questioned". The argued works have nothing in this column because they have no character to withdraw from; their equivalent is the mood, and the mood is in the drops column.

### Heidegger: the servant's will as a law of Being

The reading that takes the speech as doctrine is the one the course has placed at every seam of this module and argues in the next, and its sharpest instance is a close reading of one sentence, in the 1940 typescript "Nietzsche's Metaphysics". There Heidegger writes that will to power "is mentioned, but not yet singled out as a key expression, in the second part of *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*", and that the title of the episode "in which the first sovereign insight into the essence of will to power is achieved offers a clue for the correct understanding of it".

Then the sentence: "Where I found the living, there I found will to power, and even in the will of those who serve I found the will to be master." From it: "According to this, will to power is the basic character of 'life.' Nietzsche uses 'life' as another word for Being." The warrant for the last step is a notebook entry, WM 582, "'Being', we have no other way of representing this than as 'living'. How can anything dead 'be'?", which is Nachlass and, under <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the compilation lesson</Ref>'s rule, corroborates and cannot ground.

Then the reading of the servant: the will to be master "prevails even in the willing of one who serves, not insofar as he strives to free himself from his role as underling, but precisely insofar as he is underling and servant, and as such still has the object of his labor beneath him, as an object that he 'commands'. And insofar as the servant makes himself indispensable to the master as such and so obligates and orients the master to himself, the underling dominates the master. Being a servant is still a form of will to power."

What the reading does can be stated from the map. It takes the amber row, "Life itself spoke", as a report of Being, with "Life" glossed as Being by a notebook; it fills the row the map left empty, "the weaker sneaks into the fortress and steals power", with a mechanism the speech does not state and BGE 19 does not either, the servant's domination of the master through indispensability; and it reads "commanding is self-overcoming", which the speech says of the living, as the essence of will as such, "from top to bottom a command".

Each of these is a claim about what the text means, and the course does not adjudicate it here. What it records is that the reading needs the speech to be doctrine, since the argued works give it no indicative sentence to read this way, and that the dramatization rule and the reading are therefore not two interpretations of one text but two rules about which texts count. <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">The Heidegger lesson</Ref> states the reading at strength on its own apparatus, and <Ref lesson="alleged-biologism">the biologism lesson</Ref> tests its charge that "life" here cannot be biology at all.

### Clark and Dudrick: "Life" as a personified value

A third column belongs beside the two. Clark and Dudrick's reading of BGE 13, recorded in the previous lesson, takes the "life" of "life itself is will to power" as the life of beings who live by values, and their reading of the present speech follows: the urge to "create a world before which one can kneel" is the will to value, the will to truth is what puts it "into abeyance", and the will to value "is satisfied when one sees one's image reflected in the world, when the world is seen as it ought to be, whether or not it is so".

On that reading Life's speech is not a cosmological witness at all. It is the will to value speaking in its own person, saying of itself that it must always overcome itself, and the personification is the point rather than a disguise: what testifies is a value, and a value's testimony is what a value can give. The course carries the reading as the third way of taking the amber row, beside Heidegger's (Being speaks) and the dramatization rule's (a character reports), and notes what it shares with the rule: on neither is the speech an argument for anything about the world.

<Remark title="the application: results used, and the departure">
**The artefact.** Z I 3, I 4, II 12 and the frame passage II 17: the texts through which "will to power" and "body am I entirely" entered the culture as sentences.

**The module's results used.** BGE 12 and 19, the drive-self and command, for "On the Despisers of the Body"; BGE 13's razor and BGE 36's seven steps with the question-begging first step, for Life's speech; BGE 22's "only an interpretation" for what the speech drops; the fable's sixth stage and "Reason" §6's third and fourth theses for "On the Hinterworldly"; GM II:12's rule of mastery and GM III:24's demand for a critique of the will to truth, for the first movement; Reginster's three conditions for what "must always overcome itself" means as psychology.

**The departure.** The argued works offer the cosmological doctrine as an experiment in the conditional, marked as such by its author in every verb and followed by an admission; the speech has Life assert it in the first person and Zarathustra report it as heard. Testimony replaces argument; the personification supplies the authority BGE 36 lacks and drops the "suppose". "On the Despisers of the Body" adds an image, one commander, that BGE 19 does not assert; "On the Hinterworldly" adds a motive that the fable does not state and the "Reason" chapter asserts. "On Poets" is the text's own note on its evidential status.

**What the speeches can be cited for.** As a premise: nothing; every claim the course uses is cited from the right-hand column of the maps. As evidence: that in 1883 the psychological doctrine, command and obedience among the living with a will to be master in the servant, was in place, with no argument for its extension to the world and no admission that the extension was an interpretation. For what they add: the first person, the motive, the commander and the confession, each cited with "Zarathustra too is a poet" beside it.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Every reading the course carries agrees on one thing about the amber row: on the dramatization rule it is a character's report, on Clark and Dudrick's it is a value speaking, on Heidegger's it is Being speaking, and on none of the three is "I am that which must always overcome itself" an argument. The readings divide on whether a text that is not an argument can be the doctrine, and that division is Module 7's.
</KeyFact>

## What is established, and what the positive doctrine inherits

What is established. Three speeches of 1883 are mapped by the tarantulas rule onto sections of 1886 to 1888, against the dates, and the map holds because it is a relation between texts. "On Self-Overcoming" maps ten of twelve lines onto BGE 9, 13, 19, 22, 36, 211 and 259, GM II:12 and III:24, Z I 15 and GS 349; the two that do not map are Life's sentence and the fact that Life said it. The speech contains one argument, against will to life, and it has BGE 13's shape, a razor against the rival and the alternative asserted; the positive doctrine is a personification's testimony reported by a character, two removes from any reader who could check it, where BGE 36 is a conditional whose first step Clark shows to beg the question and whose author says "only an interpretation".

"On the Despisers of the Body" maps six of seven lines onto BGE 12, 17 and 19 and GS 354, and adds one commander where the treatise has a class. "On the Hinterworldly" maps onto the fable and "Reason" §6, and adds a motive the fable lacks and the chapter asserts, together with a speaker inside the error. "On Poets" names the mechanism by which the doctrine was delivered, a secret told into a poet's ear, and the book repeats the device at the recurrence.

The verdict stands in three columns: authority and the first person added; mood, method and evidence dropped; the speaker undercut. Heidegger's reading takes the speech as doctrine and fills the empty row with a metaphysics; Clark and Dudrick take Life as a value speaking; on neither is the sentence an argument.

### What stands open: a will that must always overcome itself

What stands open is what Life said last. "That I must be struggle and becoming and purpose and the contradiction of purposes"; "whatever I may create and however I may love it, soon I must oppose it and my love, thus my will wants it". A will that must always overcome itself has, on Reginster's analysis, no permanent satisfaction, and the previous lesson recorded that as the doctrine's structure.

Seven speeches after Life's, a soothsayer speaks, and Zarathustra "became like those of whom the soothsayer had spoken": "a doctrine circulated, a belief accompanied it: 'Everything is empty, everything is the same, everything was!'" (*Alles ist leer, Alles ist gleich, Alles war!*). The course has so far called that condition nihilism and named it the enemy; it has not said what a will whose satisfaction is its own dissatisfaction would need in order not to arrive at the soothsayer's sentence. <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">The next lesson</Ref> opens the positive doctrine there, and splits the word "nihilism" into the two problems, disorientation and despair, that the will to power's structure poses in different ways.

<Exercise>
"'On Self-Overcoming' is Nietzsche's proof that life is will to power." Break the claim on evidential status: name the speaker of the sentence usually quoted, the source the speaker gives for it, and the frame passage that governs it; then say which argued passage carries the same content, in what mood, and what the speech may therefore be cited for.

<Solution>
**The speaker.** The sentence "wherever I found the living, there I found will to power" is Zarathustra's, a character's; the sentence "I am that which must always overcome itself" is Life's, a personification's, quoted by Zarathustra: "this secret life itself spoke to me … thus life once taught me". Neither is a sentence in Nietzsche's voice.

**The source.** For the first, "I pursued the living" and "with a hundredfold mirror I captured even its glance": an observation named and not recorded. For the second, a secret told by Life. The speech invites a check, "check seriously to see whether I crept into the very heart of life", and offers nothing to check against.

**The frame.** "On Poets" (Z II 17), five speeches later: "Zarathustra too is a poet"; "we know too little and are bad learners, thus we simply have to lie"; and, of the poets' method, that nature "creeps up to their ears to tell them secrets". The frame names the device.

**The argued passage.** BGE 36, in the conditional: "suppose … the world seen from inside … would be just this 'will to power' and nothing else", with one asserted clause, that will to power is "my proposition" about the drives, and with the first step supposing the simple will that BGE 16 to 19 dissolved; and BGE 22's "only an interpretation". The content is the same as Life's; the mood is *wäre*.

**The step that fails.** The inference from "a character reports that Life said it" to "Nietzsche proved it" drops the speaker, the source and the frame, and it also drops the one text in which the claim is argued, which argues it in the subjunctive. A proof would have premises a reader could reject; the speech has one argument, against will to life, and the positive doctrine has none.

**What the speech may be cited for.** That in 1883 Nietzsche staged the psychological doctrine, command and obedience with a will to be master in the servant, and staged its extension to all life as testimony; for the first-person form of "must always overcome itself", which Reginster reads as the will to power's self-perpetuating structure; and for the danger to the wisest, which Clark reads as the ascetic ideal's entry into the doctrine. Not for the claim that life is will to power, which is asserted in BGE 13 and 259 and argued nowhere.

<Exercise>
Derive from BGE 19 what "even in the will of the serving I found the will to be master" would mean as psychology, using the section's under-wills and its "obeys, or that he believes to obey". Then state whether the speech's next lines, "the weaker is persuaded by its own will to serve the stronger, because it wants to be master over what is still weaker", say the same thing, and what Heidegger's reading, the servant dominating the master through indispensability, adds that neither text states.

<Solution>
**From BGE 19.** The section makes willing a relation of command and obedience among drives: the commanding party issues a command with "an inner certainty that it will be obeyed", the under-wills obey or fail to, and the commander adds the "feelings of pleasure from the successful instruments" to its own. An under-will is therefore a party in a chain: commanded from above, it is itself a commander of whatever executes its part. "The will to be master in the will of the serving" would mean, as psychology, that a drive which obeys is not thereby without command; it commands what is below it, and its obedience is the condition of its position in the chain. The section supplies the structure, a hierarchy in which every rank but the lowest commands, and does not say that obedience is motivated by the command it purchases.

**The speech's next lines.** They add the motive. "The weaker is persuaded by its own will to serve the stronger, because it wants to be master over what is still weaker: this is the only pleasure it is incapable of renouncing." That is the chain of BGE 19 with a reason for each link: obedience is accepted as the price of a command lower down. Clark and Dudrick take exactly this as the answer to the question BGE 19 does not answer, why the commanded drives obey at all, and read it as the will to power's explanation of the drive-society's coherence. The lines say more than the section, and the map records the more as the speech's: the section has a hierarchy, the speech has a hierarchy held together by a bargain.

**What Heidegger adds.** Two things neither text states. First, that the servant commands "the object of his labor", so that service is itself a mastery over a thing: BGE 19 speaks of under-wills commanding lower under-wills, the speech of the weaker mastering "what is still weaker", and neither makes the object of labour the servant's subordinate. Second, that "insofar as the servant makes himself indispensable to the master as such and so obligates and orients the master to himself, the underling dominates the master". No sentence of the speech or of BGE 19 has the servant dominating the master; the speech's nearest line is the weaker "sneaking into the fortress" to "steal power", which is theft and not indispensability, and the map left that line without an argued counterpart. Heidegger's reading fills the empty row with a mechanism drawn from his own account of command as "disposition over the possibilities … of efficacious action", and then reads the filled row as the essence of will: "willing would never be a willing to be master if the will were merely a wishing and striving, instead of being, from top to bottom, a command". The addition is a metaphysics of command; the two Nietzschean texts have a psychology of rank and, in the speech, a bargain.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Suppose "On Self-Overcoming" were rewritten in the argued register with BGE 36's conditional restored: Life does not speak, and Zarathustra says "suppose life were that which must always overcome itself; then the values of the wisest would be its instruments". State what the speech would lose as drama and what it would gain as evidence, and then say what the comparison shows about why Nietzsche staged the doctrine at all rather than only arguing it.

<Solution>
**What it would lose.** The first person, and with it the two things the adds column filed: authority and the speaker. Under "suppose", the sentence has no one to say "I am"; the doctrine becomes a hypothesis Zarathustra entertains, and "thus life once taught me" has no referent. It would also lose the reflexive structure of the speech: Life says "whatever I may create and however I may love it, soon I must oppose it", and the coda applies the same law to the wisest's values; a supposition applied to itself is a conditional about a conditional, which is not a law anyone is under. And the frame would have nothing to undercut: "Zarathustra too is a poet" withdraws an assertion, and there would be none.

**What it would gain.** Exactly what BGE 36 has: a marked mood, so that a reader knows the doctrine is offered and not held as knowledge; a place for a method, since a supposition can be accompanied by the reason for making it, which BGE 36's step 5 supplies and the speech does not; and a place for the objection, "only an interpretation", which a supposition can absorb and an indicative cannot. In evidential terms it would move from two removes to none: an argument on the page, checkable and, as Clark shows, failing at its first step, which is still more than a testimony offers.

**What the comparison shows.** The staged form does something the argued form cannot, and it is not persuasion. A will that "must always overcome itself" is, in Reginster's analysis, a structure of desire whose satisfaction regenerates its dissatisfaction; stated as a supposition about the world it is a hypothesis, stated in the first person by Life it is a demand on the one who hears it, since "even you, seeker of knowledge, are only a path and footstep of my will". The speech delivers the doctrine as something to be willed rather than only asserted, and that is the form the positive doctrine will need: <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the next lesson</Ref> finds that affirmation is not a belief that life is will to power but a stance toward a life that is struggle and becoming, and <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">the recurrence lesson</Ref> finds the same doctrine posed as a question a demon asks rather than a claim a treatise makes. Nietzsche staged the doctrine because a stance can be staged and cannot be proved; the cost, which the rewrite makes visible, is that a staged stance cannot be cited as a proved claim, and the reception cited it as one.
</Solution>

Run the course's corroboration pass on two sources for the claim that the two-world error has a motive: "On the Hinterworldly" (Z I 3, 1883) and *Twilight*'s "Reason" §6, third thesis (1888). For each give author, date, audience and purpose; state what each records, argues and omits; say which gives the motive as a confession and which as a thesis; and report which the course may cite and whether either argues the motive.

<Solution>
**Metadata.** Z I 3: Nietzsche, January 1883, a speech by the character Zarathustra addressed to "my brothers" inside a work of fiction, purpose to convert the hinterworldly to "the meaning of the earth". TI III:6, third thesis: Nietzsche, August to September 1888, one of four numbered propositions in his own voice, in a chapter that says it compresses "so fundamental and new an insight" into propositions "to make it easier to understand" and "to challenge contradiction".

**Records / argues / omits.** The speech records a biography: a god created, recognized as "of human make and madness", overcome, "the ghost shrank from me"; it argues, by the biography, that "suffering and incapacity" and "the body that despaired of the body" created all hinterworlds; it omits every stage of the history and every philosopher, and it omits the standard by which the healthy body's voice is "more honest", which it asserts. The thesis records nothing; it states that fabling of another world "has no sense at all" unless "an instinct for slandering, disparaging and accusing life is strong within us", and that then the other world is "revenge on life"; it argues nothing, since the four theses are the chapter's summary and the chapter's §§1–5 supply a genesis (Egyptianism, the senses convicted, grammar) and not a proof that the genesis is driven by an instinct against life; it omits the person, since no one in the chapter confesses to the instinct.

**Confession and thesis.** The speech gives the motive as a confession, "I too once cast my delusion beyond humans", from a convalescent who reports his cure; the thesis gives it as a claim about a type, from outside, with the "we" of "we revenge ourselves on life" a hypothetical "we" under "in the latter case".

**Which the course may cite, and whether either argues.** The course cites the thesis, because it is in the author's voice in a work he published, which is what the published-works rule counts, and because the dramatization rule forbids citing a character's confession as a premise. But the corroboration finds that the thesis does not argue the motive either: <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">the fable lesson</Ref> marked the step from "false" (theses 1 and 2) to "sick" (theses 3 and 4) as an is–ought seam with no premise stated, and the type-fact behind it is asserted. On this claim the two sources are one assertion in two registers, a confession and a thesis, and the corroboration establishes that Nietzsche held the motive from 1883 to 1888 without arguing it in either. That is a fact about the doctrine's history and not evidence for the motive.

<Exercise>
Del Caro renders *überwinden* as "overcome" and *Selbst-Überwindung* as "self-overcoming"; Kaufmann's footnote to Z I 15 grants that "overcoming" is "admittedly inadequate" and rejects "self-surpassing, self-transcendence, and self-conquest" as worse; Samuel, in GM III:27, renders *Selbstüberwindung* "self-mastery". Run the translation check: for "surmount", "conquer" and "master" as candidates, say which keeps the link to the *über-* of *Übermensch* and to III:27's "law of the necessary self-overcoming in the essence of life", and why the link matters for reading Life's sentence as the same doctrine.

<Solution>
**The German.** *Überwinden* is *über*, over, with *winden*, to wind or twist: to get over something by twisting past it, with the thing got over left behind rather than destroyed. *Selbst-Überwindung* is the reflexive noun. *Übermensch* is the same prefix on *Mensch*, and Del Caro's "overman" keeps it, as <Ref lesson="key-terms-in-translation">the translation chapter</Ref> recorded; "On Poets" puts *Götter und Übermenschen* in one breath, and the prefix is what makes the pairing audible.

**"Surmount".** Latin *super* plus *mons*: to rise above, as a summit is surmounted. It keeps "over" and loses the reflexive: one surmounts an obstacle, and "life must always surmount itself" makes the self an obstacle rather than the thing that moves. It also loses the link to *Übermensch*, since "surmount" and "overman" share no English morpheme.

**"Conquer".** Kaufmann's "self-conquest" is rejected by him, and the reason is the one the will-to-truth lesson gave against Samuel: conquest is what a will does to something else, and a conquered self is defeated, not gone over. *Überwinden* leaves the overcome thing behind as a stage; conquest keeps it as a subject. On "conquer", Life's "I must always conquer myself" reads as a war the self loses every time, which is not the structure Reginster reads out of the sentence, "overcoming of the overcoming itself", a will that outdoes its own last achievement.

**"Master".** Samuel's "self-mastery" is the worst of the three for this sentence, because mastery is a state and not a movement: a mastered self stays mastered, whereas Life "must *always*" overcome itself, which requires that each overcoming be followed by another. The will-to-truth lesson found that III:27's mechanism, a virtue turning on its own origin, does not survive "self-mastery"; the same holds here, since a will that had mastered itself would have reached the permanent satisfaction the doctrine denies.

**Why the link matters.** The course reads Life's sentence, III:27's law and the *Übermensch* as one doctrine in three places: a will whose pursuit is its own overcoming (Reginster), a valuation that draws the conclusion against itself (III:27, "submit to the law you yourself have made"), and a type defined by going over the human (Z Prologue §3). The word that holds the three together is *über-*, and "overcome", "self-overcoming" and "overman" are the only English renderings that keep it audible. On "surmount", "conquer" or "master" the three passages become three doctrines, and the map of this lesson, which runs Life's "must always overcome itself" onto III:27's "self-overcoming", would have no word to run on. Del Caro's choice is the one the reconstruction needs, and Kaufmann's footnote, which keeps "overcoming" while calling it inadequate, is the translator's version of the same judgment.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Life says: "That I must be struggle and becoming and purpose and the contradiction of purposes"; "whatever I may create and however I may love it, soon I must oppose it and my love". Seven speeches later the soothsayer's doctrine is "everything is empty, everything is the same, everything was", and Zarathustra "became like those of whom the soothsayer had spoken". Write, in numbered premises, what a will that "must always overcome itself" would have to hold in order not to arrive at the soothsayer's sentence, using Reginster's three conditions from the previous lesson; then say which premise the speech does not supply.

<Solution>
**The problem stated.** On Reginster's analysis the will to power is satisfied by three things, a determinate end, a resistance to it, and the overcoming of the resistance, and its satisfaction removes the second condition, so that it "does not allow for permanent satisfaction". Life's "I must be struggle and becoming and purpose and the contradiction of purposes" is that structure in the first person: every purpose achieved is a purpose opposed. The soothsayer's sentence is what that structure looks like from the side of the achievements: each was overcome, so each "was", and none is worth more than the next, so "everything is the same".

**The premises a will would need.**

**P1.** The value of an overcoming does not lie in the state it produces, since that state is the next resistance's material and will itself be overcome. (Otherwise every achievement is devalued by the next, which is the soothsayer's "everything was".)

**P2.** The value of an overcoming lies in the activity of overcoming, and that activity requires the resistance: resistance is a condition of the good and not its cost. (Otherwise a will that must always meet resistance is a will condemned to perpetual cost, which is "everything is empty".)

**P3.** Therefore suffering, which is the will's hindrance by an obstacle, is an ingredient of the highest good and not an objection to it; a life of struggle and becoming can be valued for the struggle.

**P4.** And the ends overcome are not thereby "the same": each was a determinate end, and the will's satisfaction was in overcoming *that* resistance, so that the history of overcomings is a history of different achievements and not of one repeated emptiness.

**C.** A will that must always overcome itself can affirm its own structure, struggle and becoming, as a good, and does not arrive at "everything is empty, everything is the same".

**What the speech does not supply.** P2 and P3. Life says that she must be struggle and that she sacrifices herself "for power", but she does not say that the struggle is what is good, only that it is what she is; the sentence "much is esteemed more highly by life than life itself" says that life esteems something above survival and does not say what, or why struggle should be esteemed rather than merely undergone. Without P2 the speech's "must" is a fate and the soothsayer's reading of it is available, which is why the book gives the soothsayer to Zarathustra and has him become like the weary. The premise the speech lacks is the one <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the next lesson</Ref> opens on under the name nihilism: Reginster's revaluation of suffering, "the lack of suffering implies the lack of true happiness", is P3 argued from P2, and the split between disorientation (values without standing) and despair (values that cannot be realized) is what separates the soothsayer's "everything was" from the "why?" that finds no answer.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

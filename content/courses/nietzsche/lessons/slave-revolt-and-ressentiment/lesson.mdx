Ressentiment is a valuation that begins with a No to something outside itself, and the First Treatise's central claim is that the morality of good and evil was created by that No, by the priestly caste, and then universalized by Christianity. This lesson reconstructs that claim as a causal story from GM I:6–16 and BGE 195: a caste that could hate but not act, an affect that turns the valuing glance outward, a relabeling that turns each incapacity into a virtue, one primary document in which the relabeled hatred is still legible, and a symbol for the conflict the relabeling started. It then marks the point at which the story stops explaining.

The course's argument stands at the inversion. <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">The previous lesson</Ref> stated the two modes of valuation as a minimal pair, "good" of oneself first against "evil" of the other first, and showed that the treatise's charge against slave morality is the reactive order and not the content of what it calls good. Its forward exercise left the reader holding a premise: those who cannot act against what harms them can still judge it, and a judgment that substitutes for a deed begins with the deed's object. What stands open is the history: which class valued that way, what condition made it value that way, and how its valuation came to be Europe's. This lesson answers the first two questions from the text and shows that the third is answered only in part. The fiction of a doer behind the deed, which the revolt's success requires, is <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the next lesson</Ref>; the mechanisms of the victory are the Second and Third Treatises', and the lesson ends by stating the premise they replace.

The route: I:6–7 and the priestly caste as the hinge between two aristocracies, with the inversion equation and its reconstruction; I:10's definition of *ressentiment* and the word's documented precedent; I:14's workshop, run as a sequence, with the presupposition every substitution needs; I:15's Tertullian read by the sourcing drill and I:16's "Rome against Judea" read for what "Judea" names; the two readings of the creative No and the gap between inversion and victory; and, starred, I:11's blond beast read as text, before a closing section states what the treatise has established and what it hands on.

## The hinge: a caste noble by rank and impotent in action (I:6–7)

From a "slave revolt" one would expect slaves as its authors. The text names a caste of nobles. I:6 opens with the case in which "the highest caste is at the same time the priestly caste", so that "the concept of political superiority always resolves itself into the concept of psychological superiority", and the first badges of class are "clean" and "unclean", meant "crudely, coarsely, externally, narrowly" at first: the clean man "is originally merely one who washes himself, who forbids himself certain foods". The section's second half describes what such a caste is like. Its habits are "averse to action, part brooding, part explosive emotion"; it has "from the outset something unhealthy" about it; the cures it invents for its condition, fasting, sexual abstinence, "flight into the wilderness", the *unio mystica*, have "proved a hundred times more dangerous than the disease". And then the ranking is doubled in one sentence: it is "on the soil of this essentially dangerous form of human existence, the priestly form, that man first became an interesting animal, that only here did the human soul in a higher sense acquire depth and become evil, and these are the two basic respects in which man has hitherto been superior to other animals".

I:7 turns the description into a mechanism. The priestly mode of valuation "branches off" from the knightly-aristocratic mode "and then develops into its antithesis", and the occasion is jealousy, "whenever the priestly caste and the warrior caste confront one another and cannot agree about the prize". The knightly values "presuppose a powerful physicality, a flourishing, rich, even overflowing health, together with what is required to preserve it: war, adventure, hunting, dancing, war games". The priestly mode "has other presuppositions: bad enough for it when it comes to war!" Then the sentence that carries the section: "The priests are, as is well known, the most evil enemies. But why? Because they are the most impotent (*die Ohnmächtigsten*). Out of impotence their hatred grows into something monstrous and uncanny, into the most spiritual and most poisonous." The "really great haters in world history have always been priests", and the "greatest example" follows at once: "the Jews, that priestly people, which in the end could obtain satisfaction from its enemies and conquerors only through a radical revaluation of their values, that is, through an act of the most spiritual revenge". The inversion is then written out as an equation and its contrary.

The Jews "dared, with a terrifying consistency, the inversion" of "good = noble = powerful = beautiful = happy = beloved of the gods", "and held it fast with the teeth of the most abysmal hatred (the hatred of impotence)": "the wretched alone are the good; the poor, powerless, lowly alone are the good; the suffering, deprived, sick, ugly are also the only pious, the only blessed, for them alone is there salvation; whereas you, you noble and powerful, you are for all eternity the evil, the cruel, the lustful, the insatiable, the godless, and you will also eternally be the wretched, the cursed and the damned!" "We know who inherited this Jewish revaluation." The section closes by citing, by page number, the aphorism the previous lesson read as the seed, BGE 195, for "the proposition that with the Jews the slave revolt in morality begins: that revolt which has a two-thousand-year history behind it and which we no longer see only because it has been victorious".

<Remark title="I:6–7 reconstructed">
**P1.** A caste can be noble by rank and yet denied the knightly outlet of action: the priestly aristocracy is "averse to action" by habit and helpless "when it comes to war". Anchor: I:6 (the habits, the unhealthiness), I:7 ("other presuppositions").

**P2.** Hatred that cannot discharge in a deed does not disappear; it "grows into something monstrous", that is, it intensifies and turns spiritual. Anchor: I:7, "out of impotence their hatred grows".

**P3.** A spiritualized hatred that cannot act can still revalue: the one satisfaction open to it is "a radical revaluation of their enemies' values". Anchor: I:7.

**C.** The first inversion of the aristocratic equation was a priestly deed, "an act of the most spiritual revenge", and not a deed of the slaves whose name it carries.

**The missing premise.** P2 is asserted, not argued: nothing in I:6–7 says why an impotent caste invents rather than resigns, and I:6's "unhealthiness" (brooding, explosive emotion) is a description of the type, not a mechanism. The mechanism is supplied by I:10's definition of the affect, which is the next section; whether the definition supplies it, or only names it, is the question the lesson's fifth section puts to Wallace's reconstruction.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That the revolt was the slaves' own and the priests are a flourish. Against it: I:7 says the method "was appropriate only to a priestly people", BGE 261 denies that slaves create values at all, and the division of labour Wallace draws, priests who articulate and masses who internalize, is the only reading on which both sentences hold.
</Remark>

Two further sections of the stretch belong to the reconstruction as evidence for its vocabulary. I:8 describes the Christian ethic of love as having grown "out of the trunk of that tree of revenge and hatred, Jewish hatred" as "its crown", and calls the crucifixion the "bait" of "a truly grand politics of revenge", language of calculation that the fifth section returns to. I:9 is an invented epilogue from "a freethinker and a democrat", who grants that "everything is visibly becoming Judaized, or Christianized, or vulgarized (what do the words matter!)". The parenthesis is the first sign in the treatise that the three names name one thing.

<KeyFact>
The revolt is dated, attributed and measured in one sentence of I:7: it begins with a priestly people, it has "two millennia" behind it, and it is invisible because it won. A victory that hides its own history cannot be documented directly, which is why the treatise's evidence for it is a mechanism shown in the abstract (I:14) and one document in which the mechanism is still legible (I:15).
</KeyFact>

## Ressentiment: the affect defined (I:10), and where the word came from

The reconstruction's P2 says impotent hatred grows and P3 says it revalues; I:10 supplies both as a definition of one affect. The previous lesson read the section's first sentence for the structure of the valuation it produces. This lesson reads it for the condition. "The slave revolt in morality begins when *ressentiment* itself becomes creative and gives birth to values: the *ressentiment* of beings to whom the real reaction, that of the deed, is denied, and who compensate themselves with an imaginary revenge." Three features are in the sentence. The deed is denied: the affect belongs to those who cannot strike. Compensation is imaginary: what replaces the deed is a representation of it. And the representation is creative: it "gives birth to values", so the imagined revenge takes the form of a judgment about the enemy. The next sentence adds the direction of the glance the previous lesson made the minimal pair turn on: slave morality "from the outset says No to an outside", and "needs, physiologically speaking, external stimuli in order to act at all; its action is fundamentally a reaction".

### A condition of the affect, not a station

The definition is written in terms of what cannot happen to the affect, not of who has it, and I:10 itself blocks the class reading in its second half. "The *ressentiment* of the noble man himself, when it appears in him, consummates and exhausts itself in an immediate reaction, and therefore does not poison; on the other hand it does not appear at all in countless cases where it is unavoidable in all the weak and impotent." The example is Mirabeau, the orator of the French Revolution's first assembly, "who had no memory for insults and vile actions done to him and who could not forgive simply because he forgot". So a noble can feel the affect and be rid of it in the discharge; what makes it *ressentiment* in the treatise's sense is that the discharge is denied and the affect stays. The same section grades the two contrary terms of the pair by their fidelity to their object: contempt "falsifies" less than hatred, because contempt contains "too much carelessness, too much taking-lightly, too much looking-away" to turn its object "into a real caricature and monster", while "the revenge of the impotent" falsifies its enemy "in effigy".

The affect also names what it does to its bearer. The man of *ressentiment* "is neither upright nor naïve nor honest and straightforward with himself. His soul squints; his spirit loves hiding places, secret paths and back doors"; "he understands how to keep silent, how not to forget, how to wait, how to be provisionally self-deprecating and humble". A race of such men "will necessarily end up cleverer than any noble race". Prudence, waiting and memory are the abilities that the imaginary revenge requires, and the treatise treats them as the affect's products rather than its causes: a caste that could act would not need them.

### The word: French in the German, and one documented precedent

Nietzsche writes *Ressentiment* in French throughout, in a German text, and never translates it; Samuel's 1913 "resentment" loses the choice. German has *Groll* (a grudge) and *Rachsucht* (vengefulness), and neither carries the prefix: *re-sentiment* is a feeling felt again, and the definition's "not forgetting" and "waiting" are what the prefix names. The one documented precedent for the concept is Heine. In *Ludwig Börne: Eine Denkschrift* (1840), Heine, the poet Nietzsche called in *Ecce Homo* one of the two "foremost artists of the German language", writes that "the little Nazarene hated the great Greek", and defines his terms as characters rather than peoples: "I say Nazarene to use neither the expression 'Jewish' nor 'Christian', although both expressions are synonymous for me and are used by me not to designate a faith but a character", as opposed to "'Hellenes', with which word I also do not designate a particular people but a bent of the spirit"; "there have been Hellenes in German ministers' families and Jews who were born in Athens". A hatred of the great by the small, defined as a character and not as a people, is the psychology of *ressentiment* in miniature.

The precedent buys three things and not a fourth. It dates the concept before 1887; it shows the distinction born as one between two characters, which is how I:10 uses it and how BGE 260's "both in one soul" requires it; and it shows the term "Nazarene" already detached from Jew and Christian as names of peoples. It does not supply the mechanism. Heine describes a hatred; the *Genealogy* derives a morality from one, and the derivation is Nietzsche's.

<KeyFact>
*Ressentiment* is defined by what cannot happen to it: an affect denied the deed, kept, and discharged in a representation of the enemy. The definition admits nobles (Mirabeau's affect is *ressentiment* until he forgets) and excludes any subject who can strike back, so a reading that makes the term a name for a class is the first misreading the section itself blocks.
</KeyFact>

## The workshop: the mechanism demonstrated (I:14)

I:10 says the No is creative; I:14 shows what it creates, one word at a time. The section is a staged dialogue. "Will anyone look a little into the secret of how *ideals are fabricated* on earth? Who has the courage?" The speaker opens "the view into this dark workshop (*Werkstätte*)" and posts an interlocutor, "Mr Rash-and-Curious" (*mein Herr Vorwitz und Wagehals*), at the opening: "your eye must first get used to this false, shimmering light. So! Enough! Now speak! What is going on down there? Say what you see, man of the most dangerous curiosity; now I am the one who listens." The interlocutor reports: "I see nothing, I hear all the more. It is a cautious, malicious, soft murmuring and whispering from every corner and nook. It seems to me that they are lying; a sugary mildness clings to every sound. Weakness is to be lied into a merit (*Die Schwäche soll zum Verdienste umgelogen werden*), there is no doubt about it." Each "Further!" from the listener produces the next substitution.

The five rows, in the text's order and with its German, are these. "The impotence that does not requite" becomes "goodness" (*Güte*); "anxious lowliness" becomes "humility" (*Demuth*); "submission to those one hates" becomes "obedience" (*Gehorsam*), "namely to one of whom they say that he commands this submission; they call him God"; "the inoffensiveness of the weak, cowardice itself, in which he is rich, his standing at the door, his unavoidable having to wait" gets "good names, such as 'patience', which is also called *the* virtue"; and "not being able to take revenge (*Sich-nicht-rächen-Können*) is called not wanting to take revenge (*Sich-nicht-rächen-Wollen*), perhaps even forgiveness ('for they know not what they do; we alone know what they do!')". Then the listener stops the interlocutor to ask about "the masterpiece of these black artists, who make white, milk and innocence out of every black": "what do they make out of revenge and hatred?" What they demand "they call not retribution but 'the triumph of justice'; what they hate is not their enemy, no, they hate 'injustice', 'godlessness'; what they believe and hope is not the hope of revenge, the intoxication of sweet revenge ('sweeter than honey', Homer already called it) but the victory of God, of the just God over the godless"; and what they have left to love on earth "are not their brothers in hatred but their 'brothers in love'". The compensation is named too: "the last judgment", "the coming of their kingdom".

### What a staged dialogue can and cannot establish

The interlocutor is invented, and an invented witness is not evidence. What I:14 establishes is the intelligibility of I:10's mechanism: that each virtue-word of the second equation has a candidate origin in a corresponding incapacity, and that the correspondence is exact, condition for name, in every row. It is a demonstration that the relabeling *can* be performed, not a record that it was. Its status in the reconstruction is therefore the status of a hypothesis stated precisely enough to test, and the test is the section that follows, where the treatise produces the one document it has.

One feature of the demonstration is easy to read past. The interlocutor twice says the whisperers "are lying", and the German verb is *umlügen*, to lie something into something else; but the fifth row's parenthesis, "for they know not what they do", is the whisperers' own sentence about their enemies, and I:13 has just said that the weak hold the belief behind the relabeling "from an instinct of self-preservation, of self-affirmation, in which every lie tends to sanctify itself". A lie one needs in order to live is not known to be a lie by the one who tells it. The relabeling is a belief produced by a wish, and the fourth section returns to that under its proper name.

### The presupposition every relabeling needs (I:13, previewed)

The fifth row is the one that shows what all five presuppose. "Not being able" becomes "not wanting" only if wanting was available, that is, only if the weak could have taken revenge and chose not to; and the same substitution run on the strong, which is what "evil" is, says that the strong could have refrained and chose not to. I:13 states the presupposition and denies it. "That the lambs bear a grudge against the great birds of prey is not strange: only there is no reason to blame the great birds of prey for taking little lambs." "To demand of strength that it *not* express itself as strength, that it not be a will to overpower, a will to throw down, a will to become master, a thirst for enemies and resistances and triumphs, is just as absurd as to demand of weakness that it express itself as strength." What the weak believe, "with more fervour than any other belief", is "that the strong are *free* to be weak, and the bird of prey free to be a lamb: with this they win for themselves the right to hold the bird of prey responsible for being a bird of prey".

That belief is the premise the whole workshop runs on, and I:13's argument against it, that "there is no 'being' behind doing", that "the doer" is "merely added on to the deed", is the business of <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the next lesson</Ref>. Here the point is only structural: without the premise that an incapacity is a choice, every product of the workshop is a description ("we are weak") and none is a merit, and "evil" is a description ("they are strong") and not a charge.

<KeyFact>
The presupposition the five substitutions share, that an incapacity is a choice, is not spent when the next lesson removes it from I:13. The Second Treatise needs it again at its last step: a debt becomes a guilt, and bad conscience becomes the feeling of guilt, only if the debtor could have done otherwise, so <Ref lesson="guilt-reconstructed">the reconstruction of guilt</Ref> stands or falls with the premise the workshop runs on. Remove it and the workshop's products are descriptions and the Second Treatise's guilt is a debt.
</KeyFact>

## The evidence and the symbol: Tertullian (I:15) and "Rome against Judea" (I:16)

I:14 is a hypothesis about words; I:15 is where the treatise produces a document, and the course's sourcing drill applies to it as to any artifact. Metadata first. Tertullian, born in Carthage around 155 and writing there around the year 200, was the first major Christian author in Latin; *De Spectaculis* is a short treatise addressed to fellow Christians, written to dissuade them from attending the public spectacles, the theatre, the circus races and the arena, on the ground that the shows are idolatry and cruelty. Its thirtieth chapter, the one Nietzsche quotes (he cites "c. 29 ss."), is the treatise's peroration.

Then read, and check the prediction against the sentence "Credo circo et utraque cavea et omni stadio gratiora".

I:15 sets the passage up with two inscriptions and a saint. "In faith in what? In love of what? In hope of what?" These weak ones, "some day they too want to be the strong, there is no doubt, some day their 'kingdom' too shall come", and to enjoy it "one needs eternal life". Dante, Nietzsche says, "made a crude mistake when, with a terrifying ingenuity, he placed over the gate of his hell the inscription 'eternal love made me too'": over the gate of the Christian paradise "the inscription 'eternal hate made me too' would have a better right to stand, granted that a truth may stand over the gate to a lie". Then the authority "not to be underestimated in such matters", Thomas Aquinas, "gentle as a lamb": *Beati in regno coelesti videbunt poenas damnatorum, ut beatitudo illis magis complaceat*, the blessed in the heavenly kingdom will see the punishments of the damned, so that their bliss may please them the more. The sentence is in the Supplement to the *Summa Theologiae* (q. 94, a. 1), assembled after Aquinas's death from his early commentary on the *Sentences*, and it answers the question whether the blessed will see the sufferings of the damned in the affirmative.

Tertullian is quoted "in a stronger key". Faith, he says, offers "much more, something much stronger" than the shows: "instead of athletes we have our martyrs; if we want blood, well, we have the blood of Christ. But what awaits us on the day of his return, of his triumph!" Then the Latin, in the German text without translation:

But there remain other spectacles, that last and perpetual day of judgment: what a breadth of spectacle then! What shall I admire, what shall I laugh at, where shall I rejoice, where exult, when I see so many and such great kings, who were announced as received into heaven, groaning in the deepest darkness with Jupiter himself and his own witnesses!
</Quote>

The list continues through the provincial governors "melting in flames fiercer than those with which they raged against the Christians", the philosophers "blushing before their disciples as they burn together", the poets, the tragic actors "more vocal in their own calamity", the charioteer "all red in his wheel of fire", the athletes "hurled not in the gymnasium but in the fire". The chapter closes: *Credo circo et utraque cavea et omni stadio gratiora*, more pleasing, I believe, than the circus, than both theatres, than any racecourse. Nietzsche adds one line: "*Per fidem*: so it stands written."

### Records, argues, omits, and what the provenance changes

**Records.** That a church father, addressing Christians and in his own voice, offered the spectacle of the damned as the compensation for the spectacles he was asking them to renounce; that the damned he lists are the whole of the noble world, kings, governors, philosophers, poets, actors, charioteers, athletes; and that the pleasure is explicitly compared to, and rated above, the arena's.

**Argues.** Nietzsche uses the passage for three claims at once: that the "kingdom of God" is the imaginary revenge of I:10 made explicit; that the "love" of I:8 carries the hatred it grew from; and that the workshop's masterpiece, hope of revenge renamed "the victory of God", is legible in an authority's own words, which is what a staged dialogue could not show.

**Omits.** The genre: a dissuasion, in the rhetorical register of a rival advertisement, aimed at people tempted by the games. The audience: Christians under intermittent persecution, addressed by one of their own, so that the "persecutors of the Lord's name" in the passage are Tertullian's actual persecutors and the revenge has a real object. The counter-evidence: the same religion produced texts of forgiveness, which the passage does not cancel. And, by the course's own rule from <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>, that a text of the year 200 shows what the ideal was *used for* two centuries after the revolt's supposed beginning, and says nothing about its origin.

**Use.** The provenance therefore fixes what the passage can establish: not that the ideal was made from hatred, which is the origin claim, but that in at least one authoritative articulation the ideal carried its hatred openly, which is exactly what I:14 predicted and what the formula "brothers in love" was supposed to hide. The passage is one specimen of a genre, the damnation diatribe of Christian letters and sermons, and the genre is the puzzle: what "vengeful outbursts" (I:16's phrase for the Apocalypse of John) are doing, in quantity, inside a religion of forgiveness is a question the utilitarian genealogy cannot ask, and the treatise's answer is that they are the ideal's material showing through its name.

### Wishful thinking: the psychology the "imaginary revenge" compresses

Sinhababu's essay "Vengeful Thinking and Moral Epistemology" reads the Tertullian passage as an exhibit of a known mechanism of belief formation. In wishful thinking, a person's desiring that something be the case causes them to believe it; here the wish is for revenge, so the process is "vengeful thinking". Tertullian's beliefs are that the governors will burn and that he will watch, and both have the content of what he wants. On this reading, I:10's "imaginary revenge" is compressed psychology: the revenge is imaginary because it is a belief, and the belief is caused by the wish. That makes the relabeling of I:14 a case of belief and not of rhetoric, which is what the previous section's remark on "they know not what they do" required. What a belief so caused loses in standing is a further question, Sinhababu's "Unreliability Argument", and it belongs to <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the value-creation lesson</Ref>, where the course asks whether the same argument reaches Nietzsche's own values.

### Rome against Judea: the symbol, and what "Judea" names

I:16 is the treatise's conclusion, and it begins by restating the conflict as unfinished. "The two opposed values 'good and bad', 'good and evil' have fought a fearful, millennia-long fight on earth; and though the second value has long been in the ascendant, there is even now no lack of places where the fight goes on undecided." The fight has moved inward: "there is today perhaps no more decisive mark of a 'higher nature' than to be divided in that sense and really still a battleground for those opposites." The symbol of the fight, "written in a script that has remained legible across all human history, is called 'Rome against Judea, Judea against Rome'". Rome "felt the Jew to be something like anti-nature itself"; what Judea felt against Rome one may read in "the Apocalypse of John, the most immoderate of all written outbursts that revenge has on its conscience", which "the Christian instinct" with "profound consistency" attributed to the disciple of love. Who has won, "for the time being"? "Before whom does one bow down in Rome itself today, as before the quintessence of all the highest values, and not only in Rome but over almost half the earth, everywhere man has become tame or wants to become tame: before three Jews, as is known, and one Jewess." "Rome is defeated."

Then the four episodes. In the Renaissance "there was a brilliant, uncanny reawakening of the classical ideal, of the noble mode of valuation of all things: Rome itself stirred like one apparently dead under the pressure of the new judaized Rome built over it, which presented the aspect of an ecumenical synagogue and was called 'the Church'". "But immediately Judea triumphed again, thanks to that thoroughly plebeian (German and English) movement of *ressentiment* called the Reformation", with "what had to follow from it, the restoration of the Church". "In an even more decisive and deeper sense Judea once again won the victory over the classical ideal with the French Revolution: the last political nobility that existed in Europe, that of the seventeenth and eighteenth French centuries, collapsed under the *ressentiment*-instincts of the populace." And in the midst of it "the most monstrous and unexpected thing happened: the ancient ideal itself stepped bodily and with unheard-of splendour before the eyes and conscience of humanity", "the terrible and delightful counter-slogan of the privilege of the few" against "the old lying slogan of *ressentiment*, the privilege of the most". The lanes are the section's own labels, and they settle what "Judea" names. The Roman Church is "judaized Rome"; the Reformation, a German and English movement, is "Judea"; the French Third Estate that broke the last nobility is "Judea"; and Napoleon, a Corsican general, is Rome's return. A name that covers Catholic Rome, Protestant Germany and revolutionary France, and excludes no one by descent, is the name of a valuation, the "good and evil" of the section's first sentence, held wherever it is held. I:9's parenthesis said as much ("Judaized, Christianized, vulgarized: what do the words matter!"), and BGE 202 supplies the modern term of the series: "the democratic movement is the heir to Christianity". That is the "universalized" of the opening statement, and it is also why the treatise ends undecided. Napoleon is "the incarnate problem of the noble ideal as such", and the word *Übermensch*, which occurs nowhere else in the *Genealogy*, is attached in its one appearance to a man the same sentence calls a monster; <Ref lesson="overman-staged">the overman lesson</Ref> returns to that sentence.

BGE 202's "the democratic movement is the heir to Christianity" extends the use to the reader's own century, which is where <Ref lesson="overman-staged">the last human being</Ref> is staged.

## What ressentiment explains and what it cannot: the gap

The story now runs from a caste to an affect to a relabeling to a document, and what it establishes is how the inversion could have been coined. From that one expects the same story to account for the victory that I:7 announces and I:16 measures in four episodes. It does not, and the treatise says so in its own last section: "the fight goes on undecided". Two gaps have to be told apart, because they are answered differently. Wallace's gap is that the default reading of the mechanism explains neither the inversion nor the victory; his repair explains the inversion. Leiter's gap is that even the repaired mechanism leaves the victory unexplained, and the Second and Third Treatises are where the *Genealogy* pays that debt.

### Two readings of the creative No (Wallace)

The default reading, which Wallace calls the *strategic interpretation*, takes I:7–8's own vocabulary at face value: "an act of the most spiritual revenge", "a truly grand politics of revenge". *Ressentiment* is hatred with an aim, to harm the masters; unable to strike, the weak "erect a new table of values as a devious way of undermining the position and advantages of the people they despise". Wallace's essay "Ressentiment, Value, and Self-Vindication" (2007) runs the reading to its end and finds three difficulties. First, a table of values is a feckless weapon: "if the powerful are truly powerful, they could be expected simply to ignore the evaluative scheme that is proposed as an alternative to their own, or perhaps to suppress attempts on the part of the weak to advocate in its defense", so it is unclear why the weak would expect it to work. Second, the same fact makes the two-thousand-year victory of I:7 mysterious, since "the truly powerful would simply brush aside the new evaluative rhetoric of the slaves". Third, and decisively, the reading is self-undermining: "values come into existence only to the extent that a new discourse of value is internalized and taken seriously, as a framework for organizing life and experience", and "to the extent the scheme is viewed strategically, as an instrument of revenge, it cannot function as a fundamental framework for preference, deliberation, and criticism". A value held as a weapon is not a value; and I:10 says *ressentiment* "gives birth to values". Moving the calculation into the unconscious does not help, because unconscious processes "tend to follow more primitive logics of association, expression, and symbolic representation", not the logic of means and ends.

<Figure
  src="./figures/strategic-vs-expressive.svg"
  alt="Left panel: three stacked boxes run the strategic reading to its end, values invented as a weapon, a weapon the powerful can ignore, so the victory is inexplicable and a value held as a weapon is not a value, with an X; right panel: two blue boxes, hatred of the masters and the old code on which the masters are the good, joined as an unstable pair, with an amber arrow down to the new code on which the masters are evil and the hatred is merited"
  caption="Wallace's two readings: the strategic reading fails on three counts; the expressive reading makes 'evil' the term that resolves a contradiction."
/>

The replacement is the *expressive interpretation*. *Ressentiment* is envy transformed under structural deprivation, "conditions in which people find themselves systematically deprived of things that they want very much to possess, without any prospects for improvement", into a personal hatred of those who have what they lack. The powerless of I:7 then hold two attitudes that cannot both be kept: the hatred, and the only code available, on which the hated masters are "paradigms of positive value" and the qualities that distinguish them "are singled out for praise and celebration". "This is a highly unstable combination of attitudes, one which is antithetical to the slaves' ability to make sense of their own deepest emotional experience." The new code resolves it. "If the masters are evil, then hatred of them becomes a response that is merited by its object, and the latent tensions in the world-view of the slaves are thereby resolved." "*Ressentiment* becomes creative and gives birth to values when the tensions that attend it lead the powerless to adopt and internalize a wholly new evaluative framework." On this reading I:11's claim that "evil" is "the original thing, the beginning, the distinctive deed" has a reason: inventing that category is what does the resolving, and "good" of oneself follows.

The strategic language of I:7–8 is then reassigned rather than discarded. The priests are an aristocracy with the masses' affect and not their limits; they "grasp the susceptibility of the masses to this dynamic, and exploit it expressly for the purpose of undermining the power and position of the warrior class". So there is calculation in the revolt, and it is the priests'; the masses in whose psyches the values take root cannot calculate without ceasing to hold them. This is the division of labour that the reconstruction of I:6–7 needed to make its conclusion consistent with BGE 261.

<Remark title="the slave revolt, reconstructed on the expressive reading">
**P1.** A class structurally deprived of what it wants, and denied the deed, develops a personal hatred of those who have it. Anchor: I:7 ("out of impotence"), I:10 ("the real reaction, that of the deed, is denied").

**P2.** Under the only available code, the hated are the good, and their traits are what is praised. Anchor: I:2, I:10 ("we noble ones, we good ones").

**P3 (unstated).** A psyche cannot stably hate what it must at the same time admire; it needs an evaluative scheme under which its dominant affect is merited. Anchor: none in the text; Wallace supplies it as a claim about "a vindicatory self-conception".

**P4.** A code on which the hated are evil resolves the tension, and "good" of oneself follows by negation. Anchor: I:10–11.

**C.** The powerless adopt, and the priests articulate, a valuation that coins "evil" of the noble first: the slave revolt.

**The missing premise.** P3 is a psychological law asserted from outside the text; its evidence is whatever I:14 and I:15 are evidence for, the exactness of the relabeling and one document in which its product is legible.

**The strongest opposite reading.** Reginster's: the site of the revolt is the priests' own psyche and they are self-deceived about their values; or the "sour grapes" reading of R. Bittner ("Ressentiment", 1994), on which the slaves, like Aesop's fox, devalue what they cannot have. Sinhababu's objection to the second is that it cannot account for Tertullian's exultation, which is vengeful and not consoling; Wallace's objection to the first is I:7's overt strategic language about the priests, which self-deception cannot accommodate.
</Remark>

### The victory: Leiter's gap, and the premise the treatise does not state

Wallace's account of the victory is that it consists in the eclipse of the masters' normative authority: "it is only necessary that more and more people come over time to internalize the new table of values", and "there is no need to suppose, initially at any rate, that the strong types should themselves accept the new values". But that presupposes that a valuation held by many defeats a valuation held by few, that authority in matters of value is counted; and that is exactly what "Rome" denies, what the pathos of distance of <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the previous lesson</Ref> denies, and what I:16's Renaissance and Napoleon show reviving whenever a strong type appears. The First Treatise's own account of the victory is the "two millennia" and the priests' cleverness, and neither is a mechanism by which the strong come to hold the values that condemn them. Leiter's commentary states the gap as the question the First Essay cannot answer alone, why the masters would ever have gone along, and locates the answers in the Second and Third: in GM II:16, bad conscience, the cruelty of the enclosed animal turned inward, which creates inside the strong a site the new ideal can occupy; and in GM III:15–20, the ascetic priest, who takes the sufferer's "someone must be to blame" and turns it round, "you yourself are to blame". Reginster's review of Leiter adds the caution that the three treatises are not three sealed mechanisms, and that *ressentiment*, internalized cruelty and the priest's will to power interpenetrate; the caution does not remove the gap, it says where the repair will be found.

  src="./figures/the-gap.svg"

  caption="The gap: the mechanism explains how the inversion was coined, not why the strong came to hold it; GM II and III supply what I:1–17 does not."

Stated as a missing premise, the gap is this. For I:7's conclusion, that the inversion "has achieved victory", the treatise needs a premise of the form: *a valuation adopted by the many becomes, in time, the valuation of the strong*. Nothing in I:1–17 supplies it, and the treatise's own ranking standard, in the note to I:17, ranks by the good of the minority against the majority and so cannot let numbers decide. The Second and Third Treatises do not supply the premise either; they replace it with mechanisms that act on the strong from inside, and whether those mechanisms are enough is a question for their lessons. What the First Treatise establishes is smaller and exact: how a valuation that begins with a No could have been coined, by whom, and out of what.

<KeyFact>
The shape of the gap fixes the shape of any repair: a valuation the strong can crush from outside cannot be what defeated them, so the mechanism must act on the strong from inside, once they suffer. That is what the Third Treatise's priest is. III:15 calls him "the one who changes the direction of *ressentiment*", turning the sufferer's "someone must be to blame" into "you yourself are to blame"; the affect I:10 defines as a No to an outside wins, on that account, only when its direction is reversed.
</KeyFact>

## ★ I:11 read as text: the blond beast

The "blond beast" of I:11 is the beast of prey that the section places "at the base of all these noble races", and the races it lists are "Roman, Arabian, Germanic, Japanese nobility, Homeric heroes, Scandinavian Vikings": the adjective belongs to the lion. That is the correct reading, and the misreading it displaces has a documented history: that I:11 praises a Germanic master race, and that the *Genealogy* therefore underwrites the racial politics that claimed it in the 1930s. The course rejects the misreading from the passage and from three other texts in Nietzsche's hand, and then states what the passage does say, which the refutation does not make harmless.

### Why the misreading fails: five texts in Nietzsche's hand

The refutation runs from the passage outward, and each item is a text Nietzsche published or signed.

1. **The list.** Six peoples, four of them not Germanic, "all alike in this need" to "return to the wilderness"; the adjective belongs to the animal the section places at the base of all six.
2. **The section's own sentence about Germans**, which the misreading has to delete: "the deep, icy mistrust that the German arouses as soon as he comes to power, again today, is still an after-effect of that inextinguishable horror with which Europe for centuries watched the raging of the blond Germanic beast (although between the old Germanic tribes and us Germans there exists scarcely a conceptual, let alone a blood, relationship)".
3. **BGE 251**, published the year before: "the anti-Jewish stupidity" is listed among "the little stupors of the German spirit"; the Jews are "without a doubt the strongest, purest, most tenacious race living in Europe today"; and "it might be practical and appropriate to throw the anti-Semitic hooligans out of the country".
4. **Two letters to Theodor Fritsch**, the Leipzig publisher of the *Antisemitische Correspondenz*, written from Nice on 23 and 29 March 1887, four months before the *Genealogy* was drafted: "I count anti-Semitism among" the "idiosyncrasies" of "the present 'German spirit'" that he cannot watch "without much impatience"; and, returning Fritsch's newsletters, "please do not send me these things again", with the closing question "what do you think I feel when the name Zarathustra is taken into the mouths of anti-Semites?" A draft letter to his sister of December 1887 (BVN-1887,968), on her husband's continued work for the movement, asks "have you understood nothing of why I am in the world?"
5. **I:16's placement of the Roman Church** on Judea's side, which makes "Judea" a valuation and not a descent, as the fourth section showed.

### The replacement model: an ideogram, and the passage that discriminates

The replacement model is Kaufmann's: the "blond beast", like Cesare Borgia, the Renaissance condottiere (1475–1507) whom BGE 197 refuses to call unhealthy and *Ecce Homo* prefers "even" to a Parsifal, is an "ideogram for the conception of unsublimated animal passion", and "Nietzsche does not glorify" either. The discriminating example is in *Twilight of the Idols*. In "The 'Improvers' of Mankind" (§2), the Church "hunted down" the Germanic "blond beasts" and put them in monasteries, so the phrase names the object of the Church's taming; and the next two sections turn on the Aryan *Law of Manu* with the sentence that "the concept of 'pure blood' is the opposite of a harmless concept". A reader who takes "blond beast" as a racial ideal cannot read those three sections in order. Heidegger, the reader least disposed to soften Nietzsche, takes the phrase in the fourth volume of his *Nietzsche* as "the password and countersign" for the completion of metaphysics in *brutalitas*, a symbol with no race in it.

<Remark title="a clipped quotation is a forged document">
The misreading's sourcing practice is on record. Richard Oehler, co-editor of the collected works at the Nietzsche-Archiv and compiler of its indices, quoted in *Friedrich Nietzsche und die deutsche Zukunft* (1935) from *Human, All Too Human* I §475 the clause "perhaps the young stock-exchange Jew is the most disgusting invention of mankind" and cut the sentences around it, which say that one owes the Jews "the noblest man (Christ), the purest sage (Spinoza), the most powerful book, and the most effective moral law of the world", that the bond with "the enlightenment of Greco-Roman antiquity" survived the Middle Ages through Jewish scholars and physicians, and, earlier in the same aphorism, that leading the Jews to slaughter as scapegoats is a spreading "literary obscenity". The clause is quoted correctly. A quotation is a document whose metadata is its context, and a clipped one is a forgery of the metadata: the records / argues / omits pass of the fourth section applies to a scholar's citation exactly as to a church father's peroration.
</Remark>

### What the passage still says

What refuting the misreading does not do is make I:11 harmless, and the lesson does not pretend that it does. The section says that the same men who among themselves are "so inventive in consideration, self-control, delicacy, loyalty, pride and friendship" are, "toward the outside, where the foreign begins, not much better than uncaged beasts of prey", who may "come away from a hideous succession of murder, arson, rape and torture with an exuberance and equanimity of soul, as if it were no more than a student prank"; and it asks "who would not a hundred times rather be afraid, if he may at the same time admire, than not be afraid and be unable to rid himself of the disgusting sight of the failed, dwarfed, stunted, poisoned?" That is praise of cruelty released outward, and it is located precisely: it is the pathos of distance of <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the previous lesson</Ref> turned toward those outside the community, the outward face of the same feeling whose inward face coined "good". Whether a higher type needs that face, or only its sublimation, is <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref>'s question, and the passage is evidence there, not here.

## What the First Treatise establishes, and the premise it hands on

What is established: the revolt was a priestly deed, coined by a caste noble by rank and impotent in action, "out of impotence" (I:6–7), and its product is the inversion of the aristocratic equation term by term; the condition is *ressentiment*, an affect defined by the denial of the deed, kept and discharged as an imaginary revenge that takes the form of a judgment, which nobles can feel and discharge and which is therefore a condition and not a class (I:10), with Heine's Nazarene as its one documented precedent; the mechanism is a relabeling, condition for name, that I:14 demonstrates in five rows and that presupposes throughout that an incapacity is a choice (I:13); the evidence is Tertullian's *De Spectaculis* 30, which by the sourcing drill establishes not the ideal's origin but that the ideal carried its hatred openly in an authoritative voice; the symbol "Rome against Judea" names a conflict of valuations whose four episodes place the Roman Church, the Reformation and the French Revolution under one name; the creative No is best read as expression, the resolution of a contradiction between hatred and the old code, and not as a weapon, which explains neither inversion nor victory; and *ressentiment* so read explains the inversion and not the victory, which is the First Treatise's missing premise and the Second and Third Treatises' business.

What is not yet established is the argument the relabeling depends on. Every row of the workshop, and the charge "evil" itself, presupposes a doer behind the deed who could have done otherwise, and I:13 says there is none. <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs that argument from I:13, BGE 16–21 and *Twilight*'s "Four Great Errors", and states which presupposition of morality it removes and which it leaves standing.

**Omits.** The genre (a dissuasion in the register of a rival advertisement, with the hyperbole that register licenses); the audience (Christians under persecution addressed by one of their own, so that the "persecutors" are real); the counter-texts (forgiveness, love of enemies) within the same tradition, which the passage does not cancel; and the date relative to the claim.

**II:12 applied.** The rule says a thing's present use is imposed by whatever will currently masters it and is no evidence of its origin. Tertullian's text is two centuries after the revolt I:7 dates to "the Jews" and their prophets, so it shows what the ideal of the last judgment was *used for* in one place and one voice around 200: as a compensation and as a revenge. It cannot show that the ideal was *made* out of hatred, which is the origin claim; on the course's own rule that inference would be the one II:12 forbids, and I:15 does not draw it in so many words.

**What Nietzsche needs it for.** Not for the origin. He needs a document in which the relabeling of I:14 has failed to hide its content, so that "brothers in love" and "the victory of God" can be read back to "revenge" in an authoritative Christian's own words. For that purpose the date is not a defect: the later the text, the stronger the claim that the hatred survived the relabeling. What the passage establishes is corroboration of the mechanism's product, not the mechanism's history, and the sourcing drill's last question, how the provenance changes what the source can be used for, has a precise answer here: it moves the passage from evidence of origin to evidence of use, and the argument of I:14–15 needs only the latter.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
★ "GM I:11 shows Nietzsche praising a Germanic master race." Break the claim with the passage's own list of peoples, with I:11's sentence about modern Germans, with BGE 251 and the Fritsch letters of March 1887, and with I:16's placement of the Church; then state what the passage does say, and why refuting the misreading does not make it harmless.

<Solution>
**The list.** I:11 places the beast of prey "at the base of all these noble races" and names "Roman, Arabian, Germanic, Japanese nobility, Homeric heroes, Scandinavian Vikings", "all alike in this need". Four of six are not Germanic; "blond" is the lion's colour, not a people's.

**The sentence about Germans.** I:11 says the mistrust "the German arouses as soon as he comes to power" is a relic of Europe's horror at "the blond Germanic beast", and adds in parenthesis that "between the old Germanic tribes and us Germans there exists scarcely a conceptual, let alone a blood, relationship". The misreading must delete the parenthesis and read the mistrust as a compliment.

**BGE 251 and the letters.** BGE 251 lists "the anti-Jewish stupidity" among the "little stupors of the German spirit", calls the Jews "the strongest, purest, most tenacious race living in Europe today", and proposes to "throw the anti-Semitic hooligans out of the country". To Fritsch, 23 March 1887: anti-Semitism is among the idiosyncrasies of the present German spirit he cannot watch "without much impatience". 29 March 1887: "please do not send me these things again", and "what do you think I feel when the name Zarathustra is taken into the mouths of anti-Semites?" Both letters precede the *Genealogy* by four months.

**The Church.** I:16 makes the Roman Church "judaized Rome" and the Reformation "Judea"; "Judea" is a valuation, so the "noble races" of I:11 cannot be a descent group set against it.

**What the passage says.** That every noble caste restrains its cruelty *inter pares* and releases it outward, "where the foreign begins", with "murder, arson, rape, torture" carried off "as if it were no more than a student prank"; and that Nietzsche would "a hundred times rather be afraid" of that "if he may at the same time admire" than face "the disgusting sight of the failed, dwarfed, stunted, poisoned". This is praise of cruelty released outward, and it is the outward face of the pathos of distance that coined "good" inward.

**Why it is not harmless.** Refuting the racial reading relocates the praise; it does not remove it. The passage still ranks a caste that releases cruelty above a humanity that has none to release, and whether a higher type needs that face or only its sublimation is a live question the course puts to the positive doctrine in <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref>. A reading that treats the passage as merely misquoted has answered the misreading and not the text.

<Exercise>
I:13 says that when the lambs call the birds of prey evil "there is nothing to object to in the setting up of this ideal", and that the birds may "say to themselves: we bear them no grudge, these good lambs, we even love them: nothing is tastier than a tender lamb". Write the premise the lambs need for their judgment to be a charge rather than a description, and the premise the birds' reply denies. Then state what the lambs' premise does to the five rows of I:14.

<Solution>
**The lambs' premise.** *A bird of prey could have refrained from being a bird of prey*: the strong "are free to be weak", so that strength is "a deed" for which its bearer is responsible. Without this, "evil" says only "they take lambs", which is a description of what a bird of prey is, and the lambs' ideal ("he who is the opposite of a bird of prey, a lamb, is he not good?") says only "we do not take lambs", which is a description of what a lamb is.

**The birds' reply denies it.** "Nothing is tastier than a tender lamb" is not a defence and needs none: it treats taking lambs as what a bird of prey does, not as something it does *instead of* something else. I:13 puts the denial in general form: "to demand of strength that it not express itself as strength ... is just as absurd as to demand of weakness that it express itself as strength", and "a quantum of force is just such a quantum of drive, will, effect; more, it is nothing other than this driving, willing, effecting itself".

**What the premise does to I:14.** It is what turns each row from a description into a merit. "Weakness" is a description; "merit" requires that the weak could have been otherwise and chose weakness. "Not being able to take revenge" is a description; "not wanting to" requires that the ability was there. The five rows are five applications of one premise, that an incapacity is a choice, and I:13 says the premise is "the belief in the neutral, free-choosing 'subject'", which the weak hold "from an instinct of self-preservation".

**The doorstep.** The premise the lambs need is the free-will presupposition, the first of the descriptive presuppositions of morality in the pejorative sense from <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy lesson</Ref>, and I:13's argument that "there is no 'being' behind doing" is <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the next lesson</Ref>'s subject: the reader has just written the premise that lesson's reconstruction removes.

<Exercise>
"The slaves invented 'evil' as a deliberate weapon against the masters." Break the claim on Wallace's three counts, then rewrite I:10's "*ressentiment* becomes creative and gives birth to values" as an expressive claim: name the two attitudes the new values reconcile and say which of the two the text supplies and which the reconstruction adds.

<Solution>
**Count one: a feckless weapon.** A table of values harms the powerful only if they cannot ignore it, and "if the powerful are truly powerful, they could be expected simply to ignore the evaluative scheme ... or perhaps to suppress attempts on the part of the weak to advocate in its defense". The weak would have to be "massively deluded" to expect it to work, so the claim attributes to them an intention it cannot make intelligible.

**Count two: the victory becomes inexplicable.** For the same reason, if the revolt were a weapon its two-thousand-year success (I:7) would consist in harm to the powerful that the powerful could have brushed aside; the claim makes the outcome it is supposed to explain mysterious.

**Count three: a value held as a weapon is not a value.** "Values come into existence only to the extent that a new discourse of value is internalized and taken seriously, as a framework for organizing life and experience"; a scheme the slaves themselves regard as an instrument of revenge "cannot function as a fundamental framework for preference, deliberation, and criticism". I:10 says *ressentiment* "gives birth to values", so the claim, if true, would make I:10 false. Placing the calculation in the unconscious does not help, since unconscious processes follow "association, expression, and symbolic representation" rather than means and ends.

**The expressive rewrite.** *Ressentiment* becomes creative when a class that hates the masters, and that under the only available code must regard the masters as the good, adopts a code under which the masters are evil, so that its hatred is merited and the contradiction between what it feels and what it values is resolved; "good" of itself follows by negation. The two attitudes: the hatred of the masters (impotent, unable to discharge) and the acceptance of the aristocratic code on which the masters are the paradigm of good.

**Which the text supplies.** The hatred: I:7 ("out of impotence their hatred grows") and I:10 ("the real reaction, that of the deed, is denied"). The old code held by the weak: I:2 and I:10 supply the code and its content ("we noble ones, we good ones"), but the claim that the weak *accepted* it, and so were caught between admiration and hatred, is not stated; it is Wallace's P3, the need for a "vindicatory self-conception", asserted from outside the text and evidenced only by the fit of I:14–15 to its prediction. The reconstruction holds only if that premise is granted, which is why the lesson lists it as missing.
</Solution>

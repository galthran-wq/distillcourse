Beyond Good and Evil does not abolish the soul; it reopens the hypothesis as 'mortal soul', 'subject-multiplicity', 'a society constructed out of drives and affects' — and the question the naturalist reading must answer is whether the society has a government or only a physics. This lesson states the psychology that *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886) puts under the *Genealogy* (1887): BGE 12 on what the soul is, BGE 19 on what willing is, BGE 23 on what psychology is for, with the Doctrine of Types given its text and its evidence, and then reads the one dispute in the secondary literature that turns on those three sections, Brian Leiter's epiphenomenalism against Maudemarie Clark and David Dudrick's normative order of the drives.

The course's argument has reached the point where every mechanism it has reconstructed rests on a claim about a kind of person. The three treatises explained a value by a type at every step, and <Ref lesson="antichrist-applied-genealogy">the *Antichrist* lesson</Ref> ended on the sharpest instance: §§29–31 explain a doctrine by "two physiological realities" with one dominant sensitivity, and the explanation is causal only if a psychology says what a constitution is made of and how one sensitivity comes to rule the rest. <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">The doer lesson</Ref> removed the chooser and kept three things, a difference between strong and weak wills, the feeling of freedom as a datum, and a bred responsibility, and named the Doctrine of Types as that remainder stated as a hypothesis. What stands open is the hypothesis's content: the units, the structure among them, and whether the structure is a fact about forces or a fact about authority. This lesson supplies the units (drives and affects), the structure (rank, command and obedience) and the text for each, and finds that the last question is the one the text leaves open, with a cost to whichever reading closes it. <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">The next lesson</Ref> takes up the principle BGE 23 says the society runs on, will to power, and the one argument in the published works that extends it from drives to the world.

The route: BGE 12 reconstructed, with Boscovich's atom as the analogy it runs on and Zarathustra's "body am I entirely" as the same claim staged; BGE 16–19 as the negative half recalled and the positive half read to its end, the commonwealth of under-wills and *L'effet c'est moi*; BGE 23 as the programme in Nietzsche's own hand; the Doctrine of Types with the tomato plant, BGE 231 and the heritability evidence Knobe and Leiter bring; the dispute, with Leiter's claim dated through its two narrowings, Clark and Dudrick's political order, Riccardi's rebuttal and an adjudication on the words of BGE 19; and what the psychology gives the *Genealogy*.

## BGE 12: the soul-atom expelled, the soul kept

From a philosopher who has just argued that the doer is a fiction of grammar one expects the next step to be the elimination of the soul. BGE 12 takes the opposite step. The section opens on physics: "materialistic atomism" is "one of the most well-refuted things in existence", and the credit goes to "that Pole, Boscovich", Roger Boscovich, a Jesuit mathematician who was no Pole but a Ragusan, born in 1711 in the republic that is now Dubrovnik, a slip the annotated editions correct, and whose *Theoria philosophiae naturalis* (1758) replaced extended atoms with point centres of force, so that "Boscovich taught us to renounce belief in the last bit of earth that did 'stand still,' the belief in 'matter,' in the 'material,' in the residual piece of earth and clump of an atom: it was the greatest triumph over the senses that the world had ever known." Then the turn to psychology: "we must go further still and declare war – a ruthless fight to the finish – on the 'atomistic need' that, like the more famous 'metaphysical need,' still leads a dangerous afterlife in regions where nobody would think to look", and the first region named is "that other and more disastrous atomism, the one Christianity has taught best and longest, the atomism of the soul", meaning "the belief that the soul is something indestructible, eternal, indivisible, that it is a monad, an *atomon*: this belief must be thrown out of science!"

<Quote source="BGE 12, trans. Norman">
<Original lang="de">
Es ist, unter uns gesagt, ganz und gar nicht nöthig, „die Seele" selbst dabei los zu werden und auf eine der ältesten und ehrwürdigsten Hypothesen Verzicht zu leisten: wie es dem Ungeschick der Naturalisten zu begegnen pflegt, welche, kaum dass sie an „die Seele" rühren, sie auch verlieren. Aber der Weg zu neuen Fassungen und Verfeinerungen der Seelen-Hypothese steht offen: und Begriffe wie „sterbliche Seele" und „Seele als Subjekts-Vielheit" und „Seele als Gesellschaftsbau der Triebe und Affekte" wollen fürderhin in der Wissenschaft Bürgerrecht haben.
</Original>

Between you and me, there is absolutely no need to give up "the soul" itself, and relinquish one of the oldest and most venerable hypotheses – as often happens with naturalists: given their clumsiness, they barely need to touch "the soul" to lose it. But the path lies open for new versions and sophistications of the soul hypothesis – and concepts like the "mortal soul" and the "soul as subject-multiplicity" and the "soul as a society constructed out of drives and affects" want henceforth to have civil rights in the realm of science.
</Quote>

Three things are said and one is not. What is expelled is a unity: an indivisible, indestructible one that does the thinking and the willing, which is I:13's doer restated at the level of metaphysics. What is kept is a hypothesis with a structure: mortal, so it can come apart; a multiplicity, so it is many and not one; a *Gesellschaftsbau*, a society or social structure, so the many stand in an arrangement. And the people who lose the soul when they touch it are named: *Naturalisten*, naturalists, whose "clumsiness" (*Ungeschick*) is to take the refutation of the atom for the refutation of the structure. What the section does not say is what the arrangement is, and that silence is where the rest of the lesson lives.

### Boscovich's step, and the step "still further"

The analogy the section runs on fixes what "society" can and cannot mean, and <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref>'s starred section already read the parenthesis of I:13 that puts the atom and the subject in one class of "changelings". Boscovich's step was to keep the phenomena that atoms explained, impact, resistance, cohesion, and to replace the thing that explained them, an extended lump, with centres from which forces act, so that nothing was left at the microlevel that could even in principle be seen or touched. Clark and Dudrick draw the point from Friedrich Lange's *History of Materialism* (1866), the book Nietzsche took the phrase "civil rights in science" from: physicists had kept the atom after the evidence for it was gone because they wanted something at the small scale that resembled the bodies at the large scale, and Boscovich's "triumph over the senses" was the refusal of that resemblance. The soul-atom is the same craving in psychology, a thing behind thinking and willing shaped like the person it is supposed to explain. To go "still further" is to expel the thing and keep the forces, which in psychology are the drives.

  caption="The master analogy of BGE 12: a thing replaced by forces in physics, a thing replaced by drives in psychology; the analogy is silent on the order among the drives."

The analogy licenses exactly one move and is silent on a second. It licenses replacing a substance by a plurality of forces. It says nothing about the relation among the forces, because in Boscovich's physics the relation is a law of attraction and repulsion and nothing else, and BGE 12 does not say that the drives' relation is of that kind. Clark and Dudrick read the "still further" as a demand for a greater triumph over the senses than Boscovich's, which they take to require an order "even further from what can be seen and touched" than force, namely an order of authority; Leiter reads the drives as forces in a causal order and the "society" as the shape that order takes. Both readings fit the analogy. The section's other clue is the target of its contempt. The naturalists who lose the soul are, on the plain reading, the mid-century German materialists Lange had catalogued, for whom the soul was an aggregate of matter; on Clark and Dudrick's reading the clause reaches any psychology that keeps only the forces, Leiter's included. Whether the second reading is licensed is the dispute of the fifth section, and BGE 12 by itself does not decide it.

<Remark title="BGE 12 reconstructed">
**P1.** Materialistic atomism, the doctrine that matter is composed of extended, indivisible particles, is refuted, and what refuted it was the replacement of a thing by forces. Anchor: "Boscovich taught us to renounce belief in ... the residual piece of earth and clump of an atom."

**P2.** The "atomistic need" that produced the particle atom also produced the soul-atom: an indivisible, indestructible one behind thinking and willing. Anchor: "that other and more disastrous atomism ... the atomism of the soul"; "a monad, an *atomon*".

**P3.** A psychology still needs its object: an internal structure that explains a person's choices is "one of the oldest and most venerable hypotheses", and giving it up together with the atom is a clumsiness rather than a result. Anchor: "there is absolutely no need to give up 'the soul' itself"; "naturalists ... barely need to touch 'the soul' to lose it".

**C.** The soul is to be kept as a structure without a unity behind it: "mortal soul", "subject-multiplicity", "a society constructed out of drives and affects".

**The missing premise.** P3 asserts that a structure is needed and C names it a society; what "society" adds to "multiplicity" is that the many stand in an arrangement, and the section does not say what arrangement. The premise that fills the gap is a claim about how the drives relate, and it is a type-fact in the protocol's sense from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>, a claim about how a mind of this kind is built. BGE 19 is the second passage in which the course hunts it.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That the section is eliminativist despite itself: "the self is nothing but the drives", with "society" a manner of speaking. Against it stand the two clauses that make the section unusual, the refusal to give up the soul and the contempt for those who lose it, and the choice of a word, *Gesellschaftsbau*, that names a structure over its members. The reading survives only by calling those clauses rhetoric, which is the price the lesson's last table records.
</Remark>

### "Body am I entirely": the same claim staged

*Thus Spoke Zarathustra*'s first part (1883) contains the claim three years before BGE 12 argues it, in the speech "On the Despisers of the Body", and the course's rule from <Ref lesson="tarantulas-staged">the tarantulas lesson</Ref> is to map a speech onto the theses argued elsewhere and record what the staging adds, drops and undercuts.

<Quote source="Z I, 'On the Despisers of the Body', trans. Del Caro">
<Original lang="de">
Aber der Erwachte, der Wissende sagt: Leib bin ich ganz und gar, und Nichts ausserdem; und Seele ist nur ein Wort für ein Etwas am Leibe.
</Original>

But the awakened, the knowing one says: body am I through and through, and nothing besides; and soul is just a word for something on the body.
</Quote>

The speech then gives the body a politics: it is "a great reason, a multiplicity with one sense, a war and a peace, one herd and one shepherd"; "your small reason, what you call 'spirit'", is "a tool of your body"; behind thoughts and feelings "stands a powerful commander, an unknown wise man – he is called self. He lives in your body, he is your body", and the self "rules and is also the ruler of the ego", saying to it "Feel pain here!" and "Feel pleasure here!" and using the ego's thinking for its own purposes. Mapped: "body am I entirely, and nothing besides" is BGE 12's expulsion of the soul-atom; "a multiplicity with one sense, one herd and one shepherd" is the society with a ruling party; "soul is just a word for something on the body" is the kept hypothesis, since the "something" is still there to be named; and "the ruler of the ego" is BGE 19's commander before BGE 19 has been written. What the staging adds is the personification of the ruling party as a "commander" and "sage" who addresses the ego, which is the political reading delivered as a character; what it drops is the whole of the argument, the atom, Boscovich, the hypothesis; and what it undercuts is its own literalness, since a self that "does not say I, but does I" is presented as a speaker with a voice, and the speech about "the poets" in the second part warns that Zarathustra too lies too much. The passage is cited for what it stages, and what it stages is that the body's order is a ruler with a mind of its own, which is exactly the point the argued text leaves open.

<KeyFact>
BGE 12 removes a unity and keeps a structure: the naturalist reading has the section's authority for drives as centres of force and for the soul as their arrangement, and the section's warning against "naturalists" who lose the soul is aimed at whoever reads the arrangement away. Whether Leiter's causal order counts as losing the soul or keeping it is the question the section poses to the reading and does not answer.
</KeyFact>

## BGE 16–19: willing as command among under-wills

The negative half of the psychology is already in hand. <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">The doer lesson</Ref> read BGE 16's dissection of "I think" into four unproven claims and BGE 17's "it thinks", in which "even the 'it' contains an interpretation of the process", and it read the first half of BGE 19, the parts list: a plurality of feelings, a commanding thought, and "above all" an affect, "the affect of the command", with "freedom of the will" as "the affect of superiority with respect to something that must obey". It read the error too, that "willing suffices for action" is a mistake of sufficiency rather than of causation, and it kept the model the section supplies, a difference between drives that command and drives that obey. What that lesson did not do is read the section's second half as the positive psychology it is, and it is in the second half that the soul of BGE 12 gets its mechanics.

### The commonwealth of under-wills

After the parts list the section turns from what willing feels like to what it is made of. "A person who wills –, commands something inside himself that obeys, or that he believes to obey." "We are, under the circumstances, both the one who commands and the one who obeys", and "as the obedient one we are familiar with the feelings of compulsion, force, pressure, resistance, and motion that generally start right after the act of willing"; the duality is hidden by "the synthetic concept of the 'I'". Then the sentence that names the units.

<Quote source="BGE 19, trans. Norman">
<Original lang="de">
Der Wollende nimmt dergestalt die Lustgefühle der ausführenden, erfolgreichen Werkzeuge, der dienstbaren „Unterwillen" oder Unter-Seelen — unser Leib ist ja nur ein Gesellschaftsbau vieler Seelen — zu seinem Lustgefühle als Befehlender hinzu. L'effet c'est moi: es begiebt sich hier, was sich in jedem gut gebauten und glücklichen Gemeinwesen begiebt, dass die regierende Klasse sich mit den Erfolgen des Gemeinwesens identificirt.
</Original>

Accordingly, the one who wills takes his feeling of pleasure as the commander, and adds to it the feelings of pleasure from the successful instruments that carry out the task, as well as from the useful "under-wills" or under-souls – our body is, after all, only a society constructed out of many souls –. *L'effet c'est moi*: what happens here is what happens in every well-constructed and happy community: the ruling class identifies itself with the successes of the community.
</Quote>

The word for the body's structure is the word of BGE 12, *Gesellschaftsbau*, and the members are called *Unter-Seelen*, under-souls, so that the will of BGE 19 is the soul of BGE 12 caught in the act. The French is the sentence attributed to Louis XIV, *l'état c'est moi*, "the state is I", with the state replaced by the effect: the ruling drives credit themselves with what the commanded drives did, as a pharaoh says he built the pyramids, in Clark and Dudrick's gloss, when he ordered others to build them. The section closes on the same vocabulary: "All willing is simply a matter of commanding and obeying, on the groundwork, as I have said, of a society constructed out of many 'souls': from which a philosopher should claim the right to understand willing itself within the framework of morality: morality understood as a doctrine of the power relations (*Herrschafts-Verhältnisse*) under which the phenomenon 'life' arises."

### The clause that makes command a relation

One clause in the passage separates the positive psychology from the negative one: the person who wills "commands something inside himself that obeys, *or that he believes to obey*" (*das gehorcht oder von dem er glaubt, dass es gehorcht*). Command and obedience are two facts. The commander's affect is present whenever a command is issued, and the section says the belief in obedience rides with it, "an inner certainty that it will be obeyed"; obedience itself is a further fact about the other party, which may not occur. That is what makes willing a relation rather than an act: the ruling drives do not do what the under-wills do, they command it, and the outcome depends on a disposition in the commanded that the commander does not control. Clark and Dudrick read the "error attached to willing" through J. L. Mackie's account of causes as conditions, which the doer lesson already used: a command causes the act as faulty brakes cause a crash, necessary and sufficient *in the circumstances*, and the circumstance the commander overlooks is that the under-wills were disposed to obey. On their reading that disposition is the whole of what "a well-constructed and happy community" names, and the section's own words support at least this much, that "the effect of the command, therefore obedience, therefore action" is a sequence the text expects and not one it denies.

Two further observations of theirs fix the scope of the phenomenology. The parts list is implausible as a description of every voluntary act, since typing a word involves no straining of attention and no affect of superiority; it fits the case of acting against a pull, temptation overcome, willpower exercised, and it is there that the thought takes the form of a command ("back to work") and the affect of command is felt. *Ecce Homo* (written 1888), in the section on why its author is so clever, says that "'willing' something, 'striving' for something, envisaging a purpose, a wish – I know nothing of this from experience", which is a strange sentence about deliberation in general and a plain one about willpower: a life in which the drives' order is settled has no occasion for commands. The restriction matters for the dispute, because it locates the affect of command in exactly the case where the ruling party and the strongest pull come apart.

<Remark title="BGE 19 reconstructed as the positive psychology">
**P1.** "Will" names several things at once: feelings of the state away from which and towards which, a commanding thought, and an affect of command. Anchor: the parts list, "something unified only in a word".

**P2.** The affect of command is one party's affect toward another that must obey, and obedience is a separate fact from command: the one who wills "commands something inside himself that obeys, or that he believes to obey". Anchor: "*I am free, 'it' must obey*"; "or that he believes to obey".

**P3.** The two parties are both "we", and the duality is hidden by "the synthetic concept of the 'I'". Anchor: "we are ... both the one who commands and the one who obeys".

**P4.** The parties are drives: the commanded are "under-wills or under-souls", the body is "a society constructed out of many souls", and the commanding party is "the ruling class" of a "commonwealth". Anchor: the quoted sentence; *L'effet c'est moi*.

**C.** Willing is a relation of command and obedience among drives; "freedom of the will" names the commander's affect plus the instruments' borrowed pleasure, real as a feeling and misread as the sufficiency of the command for the act.

**The missing premise.** What makes a drive the commander. The section says the ruling class commands and the under-wills obey and gives no account of why the rank is as it is: whether the commander is the strongest drive (rank as causal strength) or the drive the others recognize as entitled to speak for the whole (rank as authority). Both vocabularies are in the passage; the premise is a type-fact about the order, and no section of BGE states it. The last sentence's "morality as a doctrine of the power relations under which the phenomenon 'life' arises" is the nearest the text comes, and "power relations" is neutral between the two.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That the whole section is phenomenology in the service of a debunking, showing that the experience of willing "does not track an actual causal relationship" (Leiter), so that no positive theory of willing is being offered. Against it: the sentence "the effect of the command, therefore obedience, therefore action" takes the command's causal role for granted, and the section ends on what willing *is*, "simply a matter of commanding and obeying", not on what it seems to be.
</Remark>

## BGE 23: the programme, in Nietzsche's hand

### The section's three sentences

The first part of *Beyond Good and Evil* ends on a section that says what the preceding twenty-two were for, and it is the closest thing in the published works to the working hypothesis of <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> stated by its author. "All psychology so far has been stuck in moral prejudices and fears: it has not ventured into the depths. To grasp psychology as morphology and the doctrine of the development of the *will to power*, which is what I have done – nobody has ever come close to this, not even in thought." The obstacle is named as a resistance in the researcher: "A genuine physio-psychology has to contend with unconscious resistances in the heart of the researcher, it has 'the heart' against it", and the hypothesis the heart resists is stated: "suppose somebody considers even the affects of hatred, envy, greed, and power-lust as the conditioning affects of life, as elements that fundamentally and essentially need to be present in the total economy of life, and consequently need to be enhanced where life is enhanced, – this person will suffer from such a train of thought as if from sea-sickness." The section closes on the rank of the discipline: the psychologist "can at least demand in return that psychology again be recognized as queen of the sciences (*Herrin der Wissenschaften*), and that the rest of the sciences exist to serve and prepare for it. Because, from now on, psychology is again the path to the fundamental problems."

### Three claims, and the argument the section does not make

Three claims are in the section and one argument is not. The first claim is that psychology is a *Morphologie*, a study of forms, and an *Entwicklungslehre*, a doctrine of development: the soul of BGE 12 has shapes, and the shapes have histories, which is what the *Genealogy* will supply a year later. The second is that the affects morality ranks lowest, hatred, envy, greed and the lust for power, are "conditions of life" and not its corruptions, which is the descriptive claim the whole critique of morality in the pejorative sense needs, since a morality that condemns them is condemning the conditions of what it values. The third is that the sciences serve psychology and not the reverse, which reads oddly beside the methodological naturalism of the working hypothesis until the "physio-" is weighed: a *physio-psychology* studies drives, which are embodied whether they are inherited or trained, so that psychology is continuous with physiology without being a branch of it, and Clark and Dudrick and Leiter can both cite the word. What the section does not argue is its principle. "Will to power" enters here as the thing whose forms and development psychology studies, and BGE 23 gives no reason for thinking that the drives are will to power or that command and obedience among them is an instance of it; that reason, if the published works contain it, is in BGE 13 and BGE 36, and <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs both.

<Remark title="what BGE 23 states and does not argue">
Stated: psychology is the study of the forms and the development of the will to power; the affects morality condemns are conditions of life; the other sciences serve psychology. Not argued: that the drives of BGE 12 and the command of BGE 19 *are* will to power, rather than forces of some other description. The section is the working hypothesis's manifesto in Nietzsche's hand, and it is a manifesto, not a demonstration; the naturalist reading takes the programme as its own and owes the argument for the principle, which is the next lesson's test.
</Remark>

## The Doctrine of Types, with its text and its evidence

The protocol lesson stated the Doctrine of Types as the hypothesis the course tests and anchored it in BGE 231's "granite of spiritual fate", and the doer lesson found it to be exactly what survives the removal of the chooser; what neither did is give the doctrine its content as a psychology and the evidence its authors bring for it. The doctrine is Leiter's, stated in Knobe and Leiter's 2007 essay in the words the protocol lesson quoted: "each person has a fixed psycho-physical constitution, which defines him as a particular type of person", where the *type-facts* are "either physiological facts about the person, or facts about the person's unconscious drives or affects". In the vocabulary of this lesson a type-fact is a fact about the society of drives, which drives a person has and in what order, and the doctrine is the claim that such facts, with circumstances, explain the person's values and conduct. The typical Nietzschean argument, on their account, runs one level further: a person's theoretical beliefs are explained by his moral beliefs, and his moral beliefs by his type, which is BGE 6's "every great philosophy so far has been ... the personal confession of its author" read as a causal claim.

### A range, a ceiling, no unique outcome

The doctrine's limit is stated with the doctrine, and it is the part a summary loses. "Type-facts radically circumscribe possible developmental trajectories, but the environment (for Nietzsche, especially the moral environment) is quite significant in determining the outcomes. Think of some seeds from a tomato plant. No amount of environmental input will yield an apple tree from those seeds, yet the 'environment' (the amount of water, sun, pests, etc.) will affect which of the trajectories possible for a tomato plant—wilting, flourishing, or any of the stages in between—will be realized." Leiter's name for the position is *causal essentialism*: certain facts about a person are primary and bound what the life can become, as against a *classical fatalism* on which the outcome is fixed whatever one does. The published anchor is BGE 231, which states both halves in one section: "there is of course something unteachable, a granite of spiritual fate, of predetermined decision and answer to predetermined, selected questions", and, in the same breath, "learning transforms us, it does what all nourishment does".

The two-line demonstration of a type-fact at work is the section the protocol lesson ran the whole protocol on, BGE 68: "'I did that,' says my memory. 'I cannot have done that,' says my pride, and stays implacable. In the end, memory yields." Read with BGE 19, the epigram is a command and an obedience: pride is the ruling party, memory an under-will, and the content of consciousness is what the commonwealth's ruling class permits. The doctrine's explanatory pattern is the same at every scale, the Cornaro case of *Twilight* in which a diet and a long life are both expressions of a metabolism, the redeemer of *The Antichrist* whose doctrine expresses one dominant sensitivity, and the philosopher of BGE 6 whose metaphysics expresses the rank of his drives.

### The evidence Knobe and Leiter bring

The doctrine is offered as an empirical hypothesis, and Knobe and Leiter test it against two rivals, the Aristotelian view that character is formed by upbringing and the Kantian view that conscious principles govern conduct, by asking what the psychology of the twentieth century found when it measured. Their evidence for the first half of the doctrine, that a person is born with a package of traits, is the behavioural-genetic literature on twins and adoptees. A review of five studies in five countries, twenty-four thousand twins in all, put the share of the variance in extraversion explained by genetic differences at about sixty per cent and in neuroticism at about fifty; studies of childhood aggression, the trait nearest to moral psychology, found shares between about half and seventy per cent. The comparison that carries the argument is with the effects social psychology is famous for: the Festinger–Carlsmith dissonance study, the Darley–Batson bystander study and Milgram's obedience experiment each explained thirteen to fourteen per cent of the variance in behaviour, and each "led to a veritable revolution" in its field. Heredity's share is three to five times the size of the effects that made careers.

The evidence for the second half, that type-facts "structure the course of a life", is subtler, because trait measures predict single behaviours poorly, rarely more than about one-tenth of the variance, which was the finding that started the situationist controversy of the 1970s. Knobe and Leiter take the resolution from Seymour Epstein: a trait predicts a season, not a rebound. The honesty of a child measured across one battery of tests explains about five per cent of the variance in a single further test and about four-fifths of the variance in the child's honesty across another whole battery. A type-fact in Nietzsche's sense is a fact about a life, Cornaro's diet and not any one meal, and BGE 231's "wherever a cardinal problem is at stake, there speaks an unchangeable 'this is I'" is a claim at the season's scale. The third body of evidence, the attitude–behaviour studies of LaPiere and Fendrich, the doer lesson has already read as the test of *Twilight*'s third error; here it serves the negative half of the doctrine, that conscious principles are not the cause of the conduct they are said to explain.

<Aside>
What the heritability numbers do and do not establish, in Knobe and Leiter's own terms. "Heritable" means that differences between people in a trait are partly explained by differences in their genes, not that the trait is produced by genes without the environment; a heritable difference in appearance could produce a difference in treatment that produces a difference in personality, and the authors report that this route accounts for almost nothing (attractiveness explains around two per cent of the variance in dominance and none in sociability). Adoption studies yield lower heritabilities than twin studies, and imperfect trait measurement would yield higher ones with better instruments, so the point estimates are not to be leaned on; the authors lean on the order of magnitude. The shared-environment finding is the one that cuts against upbringing, and even its best case for nurture, the adoption studies of criminality, gives the biological parents' criminality three times the explanatory share of the adoptive parents'. And the whole case is at the level of generality at which "asceticism" or "need for closure" is a trait: at the level of "hair should be covered in public" culture explains the content, and the doctrine claims only that a type-fact supplies the drive that culture gives a form. A heritable trait is a type-fact in the doctrine's sense only on a further assumption the essay states rather than tests, that what is inherited is the constitution of drives BGE 12 names and not merely a disposition that a drive psychology and its rivals would describe alike.
</Aside>

## The dispute: does the society have a government or only a physics?

The three sections and the doctrine now stand together, and the question the opening posed can be stated in their terms. The naturalist reading says a person is a society of drives in a causal order, that the commander of BGE 19 is the strongest drive, and that what appears in consciousness, the affect of command included, is an effect of the order and not a cause within it. Clark and Dudrick say the order is political: a drive commands in virtue of being recognized as entitled to speak for the whole, the recognition is a normative and not a causal fact, and the will of BGE 19 is the soul of BGE 12 acting. The dispute is the one place where the working hypothesis, applied to the text that is its best evidence, meets a rival that reads the same words at their strongest.

### Leiter's claim, dated through its two narrowings

The naturalist reading's first statement of the claim was the broadest. *Nietzsche on Morality* (2002) held that consciousness is epiphenomenal: type-facts cause both a person's conduct and the conscious states that seem to explain it, and the states do no causal work. Paul Katsafanas's 2005 article on Nietzsche's theory of mind argued that the texts do not say this, that GS 354 and the sections around it distinguish conscious from unconscious thought by its articulation in signs and not by its inertness, and that Nietzsche's psychology needs conscious states to do work, since the priest's redirection and the sovereign individual's promise are impossible without them. Leiter conceded. In his 2007 article on the will and on his own blog in 2008 he called the 2002 claim "the most serious mistake" in the book and restated the position he held: "it is clear that Nietzsche thinks *some* aspects of consciousness (e.g., our conscious experience of will) are epiphenomenal: the challenge is to specify the parameters of the epiphenomenalism". The narrowed claim is about BGE 19 and nothing wider: the experience of willing, the commanding thought with its affect, "systematically misleads us as to the causation of our actions", and the action's actual cause is the type-facts. The settled form of the claim is in Leiter's 2016 review of Katsafanas's *The Nietzschean Self*, which sets the higher-order-thought account of consciousness Mattia Riccardi had proposed against Katsafanas's and states the position without the 2002 breadth: Nietzsche's view "was that what we experience as *the will* and *willing* is epiphenomenal, not that the will does not exist."

The primary text the narrowed claim can cite is GS 354, in the fifth book *The Gay Science* acquired in 1887.

<Quote source="GS 354, trans. Common">
<Original lang="de">
Bewusstsein ist eigentlich nur ein Verbindungsnetz zwischen Mensch und Mensch, — nur als solches hat es sich entwickeln müssen: der einsiedlerische und raubthierhafte Mensch hätte seiner nicht bedurft. Consciousness is properly only a connecting network between man and man,—it is only as such that it has had to develop; the recluse and wild-beast species of men would not have needed it. […] man, like every living creature, thinks unceasingly, but does not know it; the thinking which is becoming conscious of itself is only the smallest part thereof, we may say, the most superficial part, the worst part.
</Quote>

The section says that consciousness developed "under the pressure of the necessity for communication", "especially between those commanding and those obeying", that it is "superfluous" to most of what a living thing does, and that what becomes conscious is thereby made "shallow, meagre, relatively stupid". It supports the narrowed claim and not the 2002 one: a communication network is not inert, since its whole function is to have effects between people, and the section's charge is that what it carries is a coarse translation of an individual process into herd signs, which is a claim about the *content* of the conscious report and about the report's relation to what it reports. That is the shape of the BGE 19 claim, that the felt command misdescribes the commonwealth's transaction, and it is not the shape of the claim that nothing conscious ever causes anything.

### Clark and Dudrick: a rank-order that is normative

Clark and Dudrick's reading begins from BGE 6, which ends by saying that a philosopher's morality "bears decided and decisive witness to *who he is*, that is, to the order of rank the innermost drives of his nature stand in relation to one another", and which says of the drives that each "would only too gladly present itself as the ultimate goal of existence and the legitimate master of the other drives", since "every drive is desirous of ruling". Leiter reads the rank as causal strength and the sentence as saying that the order causes the morality. Clark and Dudrick read the rank as the rank of a polity: "The ruler or ruling class in a political order is able to rule not simply because it is stronger. It is stronger, in part, because it is recognized as having the authority to rule, to speak for the whole society. Likewise, we suggest, the rank order of the drives could be a political order in this sense, so that one drive has a higher rank than another not in virtue of its causal efficaciousness, its ability to win in case of conflicts, but in virtue of being recognized as having a right to win in such cases." On that reading a person's morality is not caused by the order, it *is* the order, at the level of the person; a philosophy "confesses" a morality in the way a table's observable properties express its microstructure without making the microstructure known.

The rest of the reading follows from that move. BGE 12's "ancient and venerable hypothesis" is, on their account, Plato's theory of the soul in the *Republic*, the one canonical soul that is not indivisible and so the one to refine rather than expel: Nietzsche keeps Plato's appetites as drives, turns Plato's spirit, the part that seeks victory, into a property of every drive, will to power, and eliminates Plato's reason as the soul-atom's last hiding place, so that what distinguishes a value from a desire has to be supplied by the order among the drives rather than by a separate faculty. BGE 19's will is that order in action: when a person acts against a pull, "the drive that is causally strongest at the moment is not the one that is highest in the political order", the higher drive commands, and if the command is obeyed "the drives' causal order is brought into line with their normative order through the use of political authority". A person is, on this reading, a being whose drives form a political order, "what makes one a person is precisely the capacity for values", and the will "exists only in the space of reasons", the phrase they take from Wilfrid Sellars for the domain of justification as against the domain of causes.

The objection they anticipate is that a political order of drives needs drives that are little persons, homunculi, and they answer it with primates. Frans de Waal, the Dutch primatologist whose *Chimpanzee Politics* (1982) described rank in the Arnhem colony, observes that a dominance hierarchy explains an animal's behaviour better than its strength does and that the animals need not be conscious of their standing for the explanation to hold; a drive can be a political agent in the same sense, without consciousness, if its behaviour is best interpreted by its rank, and the homunculi are harmless because commanding and obeying are simpler activities than the acting-on-values they are brought in to explain. The reading has a naturalistic genealogy to offer as well: the *Genealogy*'s Second Treatise, on their account, is the history of how a species' drives came to form a political order, the state-builders of II:17 imposing form on a population being "the same force" that, turned inward, builds the bad conscience.

### Riccardi's rebuttal: the hierarchy is a causal order too

Mattia Riccardi's review of the book for *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews* (2012) grants it the strongest challenge yet made to naturalist readings and locates its difficulty in the primates. The dominance-hierarchy comparison "might be an insightful way of seeing it", but "it doesn't help us to see how the transactions between the drives happen within something that might be called a 'space of reasons'. Rather, it seems to suggest that they don't": a rank order in a chimpanzee colony is a causal fact about who defers to whom, studied by ethology as such, and calling the drives' order political on that model makes it causal on that model. The second objection is about values. If a person's values are the viewpoint of her highest-ranked drives, then "it is hard to see how the former ones might be rationally justified independently from the latter ones": the agent assumes whatever her ruling drives present as valuable, which is a causal account of value wearing a normative label. The third is textual: Nietzsche describes the space of reasons, in the Preface to *Daybreak*, as a "logical 'Beyond'" he has no wish to preserve, and the book does not say how its Nietzsche differs from the Kant whose apparatus it borrows.

The two sides do not disagree about the primates. Both accept de Waal's finding that rank, not strength, explains what an animal does, and both accept that the animal need not represent its rank. They disagree about one premise: whether "recognized as having authority" names a causal fact about the other drives, a disposition to defer that a complete causal description would include, or a normative fact that a causal description leaves out. If the former, the right panel of the figure is the left panel with a label added, and Riccardi's charge stands; if the latter, the left panel is incomplete in the way Clark and Dudrick say a mechanistic account of the world is incomplete, true of everything it describes and silent on what it cannot. That is a question in the philosophy of mind, and the course's rule is not to import more of it than the dispute itself uses; what the course can do is ask what the text decides.

### The adjudication on BGE 19

The ledger drawn above is the instrument. BGE 19 contains a political column, command and obey, a "he" that obeys, under-wills and under-souls, a society of many souls, a commonwealth, a ruling class, relations of dominance; and a causal and affective column, the affect of command, the effect of the command, a necessity of effect, a feeling of power, instruments, erroneous conclusions. The words "reason", "justification" and "right" do not occur in the section in connection with a drive's rank; the one legitimacy word in the group of sections is BGE 6's *berechtigten Herrn*, "legitimate master", and it sits inside a clause about what each drive "would only too gladly present itself as", so that the naturalist can read it as a drive's self-presentation and the normative reader as the order's own vocabulary. The clause "obeys, or that he believes to obey" gives command a party that can fail to obey, which is what a political relation needs and what a mere causal push does not, and it is also the clause Leiter needs for the affect of command to be a datum that misleads. The section's last sentence puts willing "within the framework of morality" and defines morality as "a doctrine of the power relations under which the phenomenon 'life' arises", and "power relations" (*Herrschafts-Verhältnisse*) is a term both readings can own.

The verdict, stated as fact: BGE 19 underdetermines the dispute. The naturalist reading fits every sentence of the section at the price of calling its political vocabulary a figure of speech, which the section's author, who chose *Gesellschaftsbau* twice and *Gemeinwesen*, *regierende Klasse* and *Herrschafts-Verhältnisse* once each, gives no sign of intending. Clark and Dudrick fit every sentence at the price of adding "reasons" to a text that speaks of power, affect and effect, and of resting the addition on an analogy, the primate hierarchy, that their critic reads as conceding the point. Neither reading is refuted by the section, and the section refutes the reading neither side holds, that there is no will at all: "the effect of the command, therefore obedience, therefore action" is a causal sequence in the text's own voice.

<KeyFact>
The naturalist reading's own history is evidence about how to read the secondary literature: a claim stated in 2002 as "consciousness is epiphenomenal" was narrowed in 2007 to the experience of willing, after Katsafanas, and restated in 2016 with a second critic's account of consciousness, Riccardi's, set against the first's. A reading that corrects itself on the text is doing what the course's protocol demands, and the corrected claim is the one the lesson tests; the 2002 claim is no longer anyone's.
</KeyFact>

## ★ A third position: the emergent self

### Anderson against both readings

The dispute above has two sides because both accept that a self, if there is one, is the order of the drives; a third reading denies that the alternatives are exhausted by a causal order and a normative one. R. Lanier Anderson's essay "What is a Nietzschean Self?" (2012) sets the eliminativist reading, Leiter's and Risse's, on which "what speaks for 'the self' is nothing but the strongest or dominant drive itself", against Sebastian Gardner's transcendentalist reading, on which Nietzsche's doctrines of value creation and self-overcoming commit him despite himself to a Kantian self that stands back from every drive, and argues that BGE 12 refutes both. Against the transcendentalist: the soul that is expelled is exactly the indivisible unity a Kantian ego is. Against the eliminativist: "a social structure is something that goes beyond the individuals who participate in it", and a "*subjective* multiplicity" is "a structure with the subjective capacity to inhabit attitudes of its own, including, potentially, attitudes towards its constituent drives and affects"; the tendency to read BGE 12 eliminatively is itself, Anderson says, "a symptom of the very 'atomistic need' Nietzsche criticizes", appearing "in the guise of a latent assumption that only the psychological atoms could be truly real". The self on this reading is numerically distinct from its drives and affects in the way a state is distinct from its citizens, which GM II:17's description of the first state as "a ruling structure that lives" supports, and it is neither a further drive nor a space of reasons.

### Leiter's reply, and the verdict unchanged

Leiter's reply, in his review of the volume, is that the emergent self answers the eliminativist materialism BGE 12 names and not the drive psychology he holds, and that an order of drives with attitudes towards its members is a description the naturalist can accept as long as the attitudes are the drives' own. The exchange leaves the lesson's verdict where it was, with one refinement: the word "society" in BGE 12 is doing work on every reading, and the readings differ on whether what it adds to "multiplicity" is a causal structure, a normative one, or a new object. The course carries the first two as the live dispute and notes the third as the position that reads the section's *Gesellschaftsbau* most literally of all.

## What the psychology gives the Genealogy

### Three configurations of one society

The three treatises were reconstructed as mechanisms with a type at the bottom of each, and the course's scorecards recorded at every row that the type was asserted and not analysed. The psychology stated in this lesson supplies the analysis in the form the naturalist reading needs: a claim about a type is now a claim about a society of drives, which drives it has, which rules, and what happens to a drive whose outlet is blocked. Each treatise's mechanism is a configuration of that society.

*Ressentiment*, as <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the ressentiment lesson</Ref> reconstructed it from GM I:10, is a drive to revenge whose deed is blocked by impotence and which commands by a No instead, so that a judgment stands where an act would have stood: in BGE 19's terms, a ruling drive whose under-wills cannot execute, and whose command becomes a valuation. Bad conscience, from <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">the internalization lesson</Ref>'s II:16, is the same society enclosed: "all instincts that do not discharge themselves outwardly turn inward", and the cruelty that had commanded deeds against enemies now commands deeds against other drives, which is why the section says the soul first "grows onto" the animal there, and why Clark and Dudrick read the Second Treatise as the genealogy of the political order itself. The priest's redirection, from <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref>'s III:15, is an intervention in the society from outside: the affect that seeks "someone to blame" is intercepted before it reaches the healthy and aimed back at the sufferer, "you yourself alone are to blame for yourself", which is a new commander installed over the sufferer's drives by a sentence, and which works, the priest lesson found, only on a society that already holds the doer fiction. And *The Antichrist*'s redeemer, "two physiological realities" with one dominant sensitivity, is a society in which one drive, the incapacity for resistance, commands everything and the doctrine is its *L'effet c'est moi*.

### Units and a structure, not yet the principle

What the psychology gives the *Genealogy* is therefore units and a structure, and what it does not give is the principle. Every configuration above is a drive coming to rule or failing to, and BGE 23 names the doctrine that would say why drives seek to rule at all, "the morphology and the doctrine of the development of the will to power", without arguing it. The question of whether the rank is causal or normative returns at <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the value-creation lesson</Ref>, where a naturalist has to say how a value can be made and not merely had, and Clark and Dudrick's political order is one of the answers on the table.

### Established, and still open

What is established. BGE 12 expels the soul-atom and keeps the soul as a mortal, multiple, structured hypothesis, on the model of Boscovich's replacement of a thing by forces, and its warning is addressed to naturalists who lose the structure with the atom; Zarathustra's "body am I entirely" stages the same claim with the ruling party personified. BGE 19's second half supplies the mechanics: willing is a relation of command and obedience among drives, the body is a *Gesellschaftsbau* of under-souls, "freedom of the will" is the ruling class's identification with the commonwealth's successes, and the clause "obeys, or that he believes to obey" makes obedience a fact separate from command. BGE 23 states the programme, psychology as the morphology and development of the will to power, with the condemned affects as conditions of life, and does not argue its principle. The Doctrine of Types has its text in BGE 231 and BGE 68 and its evidence in the behavioural genetics of the last century, which supports the bound (traits fixed at birth, little shaped by upbringing) and says nothing about the structure. The dispute between a causal and a normative order is underdetermined by BGE 19: the naturalist reading pays with the political column read as figure, Clark and Dudrick with "reasons" added and an analogy that concedes the point, and the claim the naturalist reading actually holds, after two narrowings by its author, is that the experience of willing is epiphenomenal, which GS 354 supports.

What stands open is the principle. BGE 23 says the society runs on will to power and BGE 36 is the one section of the published works that argues, step by step and in the conditional mood, that the world is will to power "and nothing besides". <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">The next lesson</Ref> reconstructs that argument, locates the step Clark shows to beg the question, and states the doctrine that survives it as psychology, in Reginster's three conditions of a first-order desire, a resistance and its overcoming.

(iii) An affect: "the will is not just a complex of feeling and thinking; rather, it is fundamentally an *affect*: and specifically the affect of the command."

**"Above all."** The affect: *vor Allem noch ein Affekt*, "above all an affect, and specifically the affect of the command". The section ranks the affect over the feelings and the thought because it is the affect that "freedom of the will" names, "the affect of superiority with respect to something that must obey".

**The fourth thing.** The party that obeys. The parts list describes the commander's side; the second half says "a person who wills –, commands something inside himself that obeys, or that he believes to obey", that "we are ... both the one who commands and the one who obeys", and that the obeying parties are "under-wills or under-souls" in "a society constructed out of many souls". Without the second half willing is an experience; with it, willing is a relation with two terms.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
"Nietzsche eliminates the self: there are only drives." Break the claim using BGE 12's "no need to give up 'the soul' itself" and Zarathustra's "a great reason", state what is eliminated and what is kept, and say which of Leiter's two positions, the 2002 claim or the narrowed one, the refutation also touches.

<Solution>
**The step that fails.** The inference from "the soul-atom is expelled" to "there is no self". BGE 12 draws the opposite conclusion in the next sentence: "there is absolutely no need to give up 'the soul' itself, and relinquish one of the oldest and most venerable hypotheses – as often happens with naturalists: given their clumsiness, they barely need to touch 'the soul' to lose it." The claim under examination is the clumsiness the section names. Zarathustra says the same in the staged register: the body is "a great reason, a multiplicity with one sense", and "behind your thoughts and feelings ... stands a powerful commander ... he is called self". "Only drives" has the multiplicity and drops the "one sense", the structure that BGE 12 calls *Gesellschaftsbau*.

**Eliminated and kept.** Eliminated: the soul as "something indestructible, eternal, indivisible ... a monad, an *atomon*", the one thing behind thinking and willing, which is I:13's doer at the level of metaphysics and BGE 17's "it" that says too much. Kept: a structured plurality, "mortal", "subject-multiplicity", "a society constructed out of drives and affects", in which some drives command and others obey (BGE 19). The self is not a further item over and above the drives on the naturalist reading, and it is not nothing: it is their order, and the claim "only drives" leaves the order out.

**Which of Leiter's positions.** The 2002 claim. "There are only drives, and consciousness is epiphenomenal" is the reading BGE 12's clause about clumsy naturalists reaches, since it keeps the forces and loses the structure in which conscious states could do anything. The narrowed claim, that the *experience of willing* misdescribes the commonwealth's transaction, is untouched by the refutation: it keeps the society, keeps command and obedience as the mechanism, and denies only that the felt command is the cause of the act. That is why the lesson tests the narrowed claim and not the broad one; the broad one is refuted by BGE 12 before the dispute begins. (On Anderson's reading the refutation reaches further, to any account on which the society is not a distinct object; the lesson's starred section records that as a third position.)

<Exercise>
Derive from BGE 19 why the feeling of freedom is reliably present in willing even if it is not the cause of the act. Use two premises about the commander's position, that it issues the command and that it identifies with the outcome, and write the conclusion. Then state whether the derivation is available to Clark and Dudrick as well as to Leiter, and what each reading adds to it.

<Solution>
**P1 (the command).** In willing, one party commands another: "a person who wills –, commands something inside himself that obeys, or that he believes to obey", and "this consciousness lies in every will" that "I am free, 'it' must obey". So whenever willing occurs, the commanding party has the affect of command, which is what "freedom of the will" is "essentially".

**P2 (the identification).** The commanding party "identifies himself with the accomplished act of willing", adds "the feelings of pleasure from the successful instruments" to its own, and "attributes the success, the performance of the willing to the will itself"; and it does so because "it is almost always the case that there is will only where the effect of command, and therefore obedience, and therefore action, may be expected".

**C.** The feeling of freedom is present whenever willing occurs, because it is the commander's affect (P1), and it is reliably confirmed by the outcome, because the commander credits itself with what the under-wills did whenever they obey, which is the usual case (P2). Nothing in P1 or P2 requires that the affect be the cause of the act: P1 makes it a constituent of commanding, P2 makes the credit a product of the sequence, and the section says the sequence's regularity is what "translates into the feeling, as if there were a *necessity of effect*". So the feeling is reliable and uninformative about causation at once, which is what "*L'effet c'est moi*" says: the ruling class in "every well-constructed and happy community" identifies itself with the community's successes whether or not it produced them.

**Availability.** The derivation is available to both readings, because both accept P1 and P2 as the text states them. Leiter adds that the affect is an effect of the type-facts that also cause the act, so that the felt command "systematically misleads us as to the causation of our actions"; the derivation then shows why a misleading feeling would be reliably present. Clark and Dudrick add that the command *does* cause the act, necessary and sufficient in the circumstances (Mackie), and that the error is only the commander's forgetting of the circumstances, the under-wills' disposition to obey; the derivation then shows why a true feeling would be systematically over-credited. The derivation is neutral; the readings differ on whether the feeling's object is a cause or a correlate, which is exactly what BGE 19 leaves open.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Consider a society of drives with no rank-order: every drive of equal strength and none recognized above the others. On BGE 19 and BGE 12, could such a being will at all? State what "command" requires, and say what the case shows about whether rank is constitutive of willing on both readings or normative on Clark and Dudrick's only.

<Solution>
**What command requires.** BGE 19 makes willing "simply a matter of commanding and obeying", and a command needs a party that issues it and a party that "obeys, or that he believes to obey". In a society with no rank there is no ruling party to issue commands and no under-will to obey; a conflict between two equal drives is a conflict, not a command, and its outcome is what Clark and Dudrick call "simple indecision followed by an action". So the being cannot will in BGE 19's sense. It can act, since drives with objects still produce behaviour, but "I will" reports nothing in it, and the affect of command, which requires "the position of the commander", has no bearer. On BGE 12 the being is a multiplicity without a *Gesellschaftsbau*: it has the plurality the section keeps and lacks the structure, so it is not a soul in the section's sense either. BGE 21's "strong and weak wills" gives the same result from the other end: a will is strong or weak according to how well its commands are obeyed, and a society with no commander has no will to be either.

**Constitutive on both readings.** The case shows that rank is constitutive of willing on both readings, not on Clark and Dudrick's only. For Leiter the rank is causal strength, and a society of equal strengths has no commander because no drive wins conflicts; willing requires a strongest drive. For Clark and Dudrick the rank is authority, and a society in which no drive is recognized above the others has no commander because there is nothing to speak for the whole; willing requires a recognized drive. The two readings therefore agree that no rank means no will, and disagree only about what the rank consists in. The case also shows where they part: the boundary case Clark and Dudrick's reading needs, and Leiter's cannot describe, is a society in which the strongest drive and the recognized drive differ, which is temptation; the boundary case Leiter's reading handles and Clark and Dudrick's must explain is a society in which the recognized drive is *always* the strongest, which on their account is the "well-constructed and happy commonwealth" in which willing is never needed, and on his is the ordinary case. The equal-strength society decides nothing between them; it shows that the concept of rank is doing the same constitutive work in both.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Reconstruct Riccardi's rebuttal to Clark and Dudrick as an explicit argument with premises and a conclusion, using his review's sentences on the dominance hierarchies. Then reconstruct Clark and Dudrick's own pre-emptive use of de Waal's chimpanzees, in which the same evidence supports the opposite conclusion. State which premise the two sides actually disagree on.

<Solution>
**Riccardi.** P1. Clark and Dudrick hold that the drives' rank-order is a political order in the "space of reasons", an order of authority and not merely of causal strength. P2. To make a political order of drives intelligible without homunculi, they compare it to the dominance hierarchies that structure the social life of primates. P3. A dominance hierarchy is a paradigm of a causal order: it is studied by ethology as a fact about who defers to whom, and the animals' behaviour is explained by rank without any appeal to justification. C1. The comparison "doesn't help us to see how the transactions between the drives happen within something that might be called a 'space of reasons'. Rather, it seems to suggest that they don't." Supplementary argument: P4. On their account the agent's values are the viewpoint of her highest-ranked drives. P5. A value that simply *is* what the dominant drives present as valuable is not rationally justified independently of them. C2. The normative order is a causal order under a normative label, and the epiphenomenalism worry the book set out to dispel returns.

**Clark and Dudrick.** P1. The objection to a political order of drives is that drives would have to be persons, homunculi, and that an explanation of a person's willing by persons inside the person is no explanation. P2. De Waal's chimpanzees show that an individual's behaviour is "sometimes best explained not in terms of his brute strength relative to his fellows but in terms of his rank in the social order", and that the animals need not take themselves to form a political order for this to hold. P3. So a political order requires neither consciousness of standing nor conscious motives; it requires only that behaviour be illuminated by interpreting it in political terms. P4. Commanding and obeying are simpler activities than acting on values, so drives that command and obey are not homunculi in the vicious sense (Dennett's test). C. The drives can form a political order, without homunculi, and since to be an agent for Nietzsche is for one's activity to make sense in political terms, drives interpreted as political agents *are* political agents.

**The disputed premise.** Both sides accept de Waal's finding (rank rather than strength explains behaviour; the animals need not know their rank). They disagree on what "rank" so established *is*: Clark and Dudrick's P3 takes "best explained in political terms" to place the explanation in the space of reasons, while Riccardi's P3 takes the ethologist's political vocabulary to name a causal order, so that "best explained by rank" is a causal explanation that mentions deference. The premise in dispute is therefore whether recognition of authority, the deference that makes a drive the commander, is a causal fact about the deferring drives or a normative fact a causal description omits. Everything else in the two arguments is shared, and BGE 19, which uses both vocabularies and reduces neither, does not decide it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Translation comparison. Norman renders BGE 12's *Gesellschaftsbau der Triebe und Affekte* as "society constructed out of drives and affects" and BGE 19's *Unterwillen* as "under-wills"; Kaufmann has "social structure of the drives and affects" and "underwills"; Clark and Dudrick gloss *Gesellschaftsbau* as "political structure". Norman renders BGE 19's *„ich bin frei, „er" muss gehorchen"* as "I am free, 'it' must obey"; Kaufmann has "'he' must obey". State which renderings make the political reading easier and whether the German settles it.

<Solution>
**Gesellschaftsbau.** *Gesellschaft* is society, *Bau* is a building or structure; the compound names a society's construction, the way it is put together. Kaufmann's "social structure" keeps the *Bau* and loses the sense of a society of members; Norman's "society constructed out of" keeps both, with the members named as the material; Clark and Dudrick's "political structure" adds a word the German does not contain and defend the addition from BGE 19's *Gemeinwesen* and *regierende Klasse*, which are political. Ease for the political reading runs Kaufmann < Norman < Clark and Dudrick. The German settles that the soul is a *structure* of members and does not settle that the structure is political: *Gesellschaft* is the word for civil society as well as for a company, and *Gesellschaftsbau* on its own is as neutral as "social order" in English.

**Unterwillen / Unter-Seelen.** Norman's "under-wills" and Kaufmann's "underwills" are equivalent; both keep the *unter*, which places the wills below a commander, and both keep the equation with *Unter-Seelen*, under-souls, that ties BGE 19 to BGE 12. The prefix states the relation in every rendering: a will that is *under* another is subordinate, and subordination is rank, of either kind. Neutral.

**„er" muss gehorchen.** The German pronoun is *er*, "he", masculine and personal. Kaufmann keeps it; Norman gives "it", which reads the pronoun as referring to a thing (a body, a drive as a mechanism) rather than a party. The personal pronoun makes the political reading easier, because a "he" that must obey is a subject who could refuse, and Clark and Dudrick make exactly that point against Leiter, who also writes "it". The German settles the pronoun and not what it refers to: Nietzsche puts *er* in scare-quotes, which marks the personification as a way of speaking about "something inside himself" (*einem Etwas in sich*), a neuter noun. So the sentence personifies the obeying party and flags the personification at once, and a translation must choose what the quotation marks leave open. Verdict: the German makes the political reading available at every term and mandatory at none; the term that comes nearest to deciding is the flagged "he", and the flag is Nietzsche's.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Clark and Dudrick locate the genealogy of the political order of the drives in GM II:16–18, read with the state-builders of II:17. Using <Ref lesson="internalization-and-bad-conscience">the internalization lesson</Ref>'s reconstruction of the mechanism and its grading of the state-by-conquest premise, say what II:16–17 can and cannot establish about how a society of drives acquires a ruling party, and state whether the passage supports a causal or a normative account of the rank, or neither.

<Solution>
**What II:16–17 establishes.** II:16's mechanism is the conservation of instincts under enclosure: "all instincts that do not discharge themselves outwardly turn inward", and the enclosure is II:17's state, "a pack of blond beasts of prey" imposing form on a formless population, which the internalization lesson graded as the anti-contract premise the mechanism needs and which the text asserts rather than documents. Read with BGE 19, the passage says how a society of drives acquires an inward structure at all: drives that commanded deeds against enemies now command deeds against other drives, and the animal acquires "depth, breadth, height", a "soul". II:18 adds that "the same force" that builds states builds the bad conscience, inward and "on a smaller, pettier scale", which is the sentence Clark and Dudrick's reading rests on: the polity of drives is literally the polity of the state turned inward.

**What it cannot establish.** Two things. First, the passage explains the origin of internal command, that drives come to command drives, and not the origin of *rank*: it does not say which drive becomes the ruling party or why, since the cruelty that turns inward is the cruelty that happened to be blocked. Second, the state-builders of II:17 rule by force, "without the least idea what guilt, responsibility, consideration are"; the analogy therefore supplies the *causal* half of a political order, form imposed by strength, and the internalization lesson found that the text's own word for what they impose is "form" and "meaning", the II:17 sentence Anderson quotes on "a ruling structure that lives ... in which nothing at all finds a place that has not first had placed into it a 'meaning' with respect to the whole". A structure in which places have meanings is more than a hierarchy of strengths, and less than a space of reasons, since the meaning is imposed and not recognized.

**Causal, normative, or neither.** The passage supports a causal account of how the order arises, by conquest and enclosure, and is neutral on what the order is once it exists: Clark and Dudrick can claim it as a "naturalistically acceptable account of the existence of the political order", which is what they claim and no more, and Leiter can claim it as the internalization of a causal order of strength. The one clause that leans is II:17's "meaning with respect to the whole", which gives the imposed order a structure of roles; the one clause that leans back is the same section's insistence that the imposers know nothing of responsibility. The Second Treatise, like BGE 19, supplies both vocabularies. The internalization lesson's grade of the state-by-conquest premise carries over unchanged: whichever reading takes the genealogy of the political order from II:17 inherits a premise the text asserts and does not document.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
BGE 23 calls psychology "the morphology and the doctrine of the development of the will to power". Write what the society of drives needs "will to power" to mean for BGE 19's command and obedience to be an instance of it, using BGE 6's sentence that "every drive is desirous of ruling". Then find the one section of *Beyond Good and Evil* that extends the claim from drives to "the world", and state in a sentence what its first word tells you about its argument.

<Solution>
**What "will to power" has to mean.** For command and obedience among drives to be an instance of will to power, each drive must seek to command: BGE 6 says so, "every drive is desirous of ruling (*herrschsüchtig*): and as such it seeks to philosophize", and "each of them would be only too glad to present just itself as the ultimate goal of existence and the legitimate master of the other drives". Will to power, at the level of the drives, is then the disposition of each drive to raise its rank, to become the commander of the commonwealth rather than one of its under-wills; the order of BGE 12 is the standing result of that contest and the willing of BGE 19 is the contest at a moment. Clark and Dudrick draw the consequence that will to power is a second-order property of every drive, the Platonic "spirit" transferred to the appetites, and Zarathustra's "On Self-Overcoming" adds the answer to why under-wills obey at all: "that the weaker should serve the stronger, to that it is persuaded by its own will, which would be master over what is weaker still". On that meaning, obedience is itself a form of the drive to command.

**The extension.** BGE 36 is the section, and it argues that if nothing is "given" as real except our drives, and if the causality of the will is the only causality, then "the world viewed from inside ... would be 'will to power' and nothing else". Its first word, in the German as in Norman, is *Gesetzt*, "Suppose". The argument is a chain of conditionals whose antecedents are hypotheses the section asks the reader to grant, one of which, that the will can act on the will, is the claim BGE 19 has just analysed, and one of which, that the causality of the will is the only causality, is the conclusion's own content stated as a premise. That is the doorstep of <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the next lesson</Ref>, which reconstructs BGE 36 step by step, finds with Clark where the argument begs the question, and states the doctrine that survives as psychology.

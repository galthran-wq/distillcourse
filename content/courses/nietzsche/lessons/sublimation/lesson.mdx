A dangerous drive cannot be argued out of wanting, and the church's answer was to destroy it: "If thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out." The question is whether anything is lost when such a drive dies, and whether its force could be kept without its danger. The formula comes from the Sermon on the Mount, where Jesus says it just after the saying that a man who looks at a woman with desire has already committed adultery in his heart (Matthew 5:29). For centuries the discipline of monks and saints followed that line: fasting, celibacy, silence, the body starved so that its wants would die.

The drive cannot be argued with because, as <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">will to power</Ref> found, every drive wants to discharge its strength against resistance. Morality has offered two things to do with it. Let it out, and it does what it wants: the brawler breaks a jaw, the adulterer wrecks two families. Cut it out, and the danger is gone with the drive.

Of the two, cutting out looks like the responsible choice. If a drive is dangerous, less of it should be better, and none of it best. Nietzsche rejects that choice, and to reject it he has to say what is lost when a dangerous drive dies. His alternative, keeping a drive's force and changing what it is aimed at, raises two more questions. How can a drive keep its force while what it aims at changes, and who changes it, if there is no self above the drives? And when a crude drive has become a refined one, lust become love, has anything happened beyond the old vice putting on better clothes?

## Let it out or cut it out: the two old treatments

What exactly is wrong with cutting a dangerous passion out? The danger is real, and a man without the passion cannot be dragged down by it. The answer is in a short chapter of *Twilight of the Idols* (TI, 1888) called "Morality as Anti-Nature", whose first section sets the church's choice beside what it costs.

<Quote source="TI, 'Morality as Anti-Nature' 1, trans. W. Kaufmann">
All passions have a phase when they are merely disastrous, when they drag down their victim with the weight of stupidity — and a later, very much later phase when they wed the spirit, when they "spiritualize" themselves. Formerly, in view of the element of stupidity in passion, war was declared on passion itself, its destruction was plotted; all the old moral monsters are agreed on this: *il faut tuer les passions*. The most famous formula for this is to be found in the New Testament, in that Sermon on the Mount, where, incidentally, things are by no means looked at from a height. There it is said, for example, with particular reference to sexuality: "If thy eye offend thee, pluck it out." Fortunately, no Christian acts in accordance with this precept. Destroying the passions and cravings, merely as a preventive measure against their stupidity and the unpleasant consequences of this stupidity — today this itself strikes us as merely another acute form of stupidity. We no longer admire dentists who "pluck out" teeth so that they will not hurt any more.

To be fair, it should be admitted, however, that on the ground out of which Christianity grew, the concept of the "spiritualization of passion" could never have been formed. After all, the first church, as is well known, fought against the "intelligent" in favor of the "poor in spirit." How could one expect from it an intelligent war against passion? The church fights passion with excision in every sense: its practice, its "cure," is *castratism*. It never asks: "How can one spiritualize, beautify, deify a craving?" It has at all times laid the stress of discipline on extirpation (of sensuality, of pride, of the lust to rule, of avarice, of vengefulness). But an attack on the roots of passion means an attack on the roots of life: the practice of the church is hostile to life.
</Quote>

### A passion's stupid phase

The section does not deny that passions are dangerous. It says *when* they are: in their first phase, when a passion discharges on whatever is nearest and drags its owner down "with the weight of stupidity". The jealous man who smashes the room, the vengeful one who ruins himself to ruin his enemy, are passions in that phase. *Il faut tuer les passions*, "one must kill the passions", is the French formula of the "old moral monsters" who drew the obvious conclusion: if the passion is stupid, abolish the passion.

The church's list of what to abolish is close to the old list of deadly sins: sensuality, pride, the lust to rule, avarice, vengefulness. Its method has two names in the section. *Extirpation* (*Ausrottung*) is rooting out, as a weed is pulled with its root. *Castratism* (*Kastratismus*) takes its image from the case the Sermon on the Mount had in mind, sexuality, and names the result: a man who is no longer able to do what he was tempted to do, and is called good for it.

### Why the dentist is wrong

The dentist sentence carries the argument in one image. A tooth that aches can be treated; a dentist who pulls every aching tooth has stopped the pain and removed the tooth's use, for good. The stupidity was in the *phase*, the way the passion discharged, and extirpation cures it by removing the thing that had the phase. Set out in steps:

1. A drive is a store of strength that wants to be spent (<Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology" id="what-bge-13-says-term-by-term">BGE 13</Ref>: "a living thing wants above all to *discharge* its strength").
2. Whatever a person does, the good included, is done with the strength of some drive. *Beyond Good and Evil* (BGE, 1886) counts even "hatred, envy, covetousness, and imperiousness" among the "life-conditioning emotions", which "must be further developed if life is to be further developed" (BGE 23).
3. So destroying a drive destroys a share of the person's strength, including the strength any good use of that drive would have needed.
4. The danger lay in how the drive discharged in its first phase, not in its existing at all.
5. So extirpation removes a defect of the phase by removing the drive, and leaves the person weaker than any use of the drive would have left him.

Step 2 is the one a moralist would contest. <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives">The society of drives</Ref> found nothing in a person but drives in some order: no reason standing apart with strength of its own. If that is right, there is no other source for the strength a good act needs. That is the sense of "an attack on the roots of passion means an attack on the roots of life".

<KeyFact>
The argument stands or falls with step 2. Whoever keeps <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="platos-ruler-redescribed">Plato's picture</Ref>, a reason that rules the soul and wants only the good, can grant the other four steps and still approve the knife: for him it removes a danger and leaves the power to do good untouched. Extirpation is a loss only if the drives are all the strength a person has.
</KeyFact>

## Who reaches for the knife

If extirpation is so plainly a loss, its popularity needs explaining. The second section of the chapter explains it by asking who chooses it.

<Quote source="TI, 'Morality as Anti-Nature' 2, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The same means in the fight against a craving — castration, extirpation — is instinctively chosen by those who are too weak-willed, too degenerate, to be able to impose moderation on themselves; by those who are so constituted that they require La Trappe, to use a figure of speech, or (without any figure of speech) some kind of definitive declaration of hostility, a cleft between themselves and the passion. Radical means are indispensable only for the degenerate; the weakness of the will — or, to speak more definitely, the inability not to respond to a stimulus — is itself merely another form of degeneration. The radical hostility, the deadly hostility against sensuality, is always a symptom to reflect on: it entitles us to suppositions concerning the total state of one who is excessive in this manner.

This hostility, this hatred, by the way, reaches its climax only when such types lack even the firmness for this radical cure, for this renunciation of their "devil." One should survey the whole history of the priests and philosophers, including the artists: the most poisonous things against the senses have been said not by the impotent, nor by ascetics, but by the impossible ascetics, by those who really were in dire need of being ascetics.
</Quote>

### Moderation, and the inability not to respond

The section names what the weak-willed lack: the ability to *impose moderation* (*Maß*, measure) on a drive, to feel it and not obey it at once, to let a stimulus pass without responding. Moderation is not a fourth treatment beside discharge and extirpation. It is a capacity, and it decides which treatments a person can use. Someone who has it can let a drive out on his own terms, at a time and in a measure he chooses, which is discharge governed rather than stupid; he can also hold a drive back without killing it. Someone who lacks it, who must react to every provocation and give in to every craving, is left with the stupid discharge or with no drive at all: the only way for him not to act on a drive is to have no occasion to feel it. La Trappe is the abbey in Normandy that gave its name to the Trappists, a French monastic order known for perpetual silence, hard labour and fasting: the "cleft" between a man and his passion built in stone.

*Degenerate* (*entartet*) is the word for a type that has lost the strength of its kind. It is the same diagnosis as <Ref lesson="nihilism" id="decadence-the-weakness-behind-passive-nihilism">decadence</Ref>, whose formula was "to have to fight the instincts": not to be able to do anything else with them. That explains the second paragraph of the quotation: the bitterest attacks on the senses come from people who needed the radical cure and could not carry it through. Their hatred of sensuality measures the strength of a drive they can neither govern nor kill.

### D 109's six methods, sorted by what they do to the drive

<Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="six-methods">The society of drives</Ref> read *Daybreak*'s (D, 1881) section 109, its six methods "of combating the vehemence of a drive": avoid every occasion for it, ration it, indulge it to disgust, tie it to a painful thought, divert strength elsewhere, weaken the whole body. The point there was who chooses the method: a rival drive, using the intellect as its "blind instrument". Read against the three treatments, the list shows something else.

Rationing lets the drive out on a schedule, discharge held to a measure; avoidance, the painful association and the ascetic's starvation are extirpation by slower or faster routes; indulgence spends the drive until it disgusts. The fifth method, which "brings about a dislocation of one's quanta of strength by imposing on oneself a particularly difficult and strenuous labour", comes nearest to the third treatment, and it still is not it. The strength is moved *away*: the troublesome drive is starved so that another can eat. Nothing in it lets the drive itself be satisfied on a better object. The question the church "never asks", how to "spiritualize, beautify, deify a craving", is missing from Nietzsche's own list of 1881 too.

## The third treatment: spiritualization

The third section of "Anti-Nature" answers that question with two examples, and both turn a passion the church wanted dead into something the church's enemies are proud of.

<Quote source="TI, 'Morality as Anti-Nature' 3, trans. W. Kaufmann">
<Original lang="de">
Die Vergeistigung der Sinnlichkeit heißt Liebe: sie ist ein großer Triumph über das Christentum. Ein andrer Triumph ist unsre Vergeistigung der Feindschaft.
</Original>

The spiritualization of sensuality is called *love*: it represents a great triumph over Christianity. Another triumph is our spiritualization of *hostility*. It consists in a profound appreciation of the value of having enemies: in short, it means acting and thinking in the opposite way from that which has been the rule. The church always wanted the destruction of its enemies; we, we immoralists and Antichristians, find our advantage in this, that the church exists. In the political realm too, hostility has now become more spiritual — much more sensible, much more thoughtful, much more *considerate*. Almost every party understands how it is in the interest of its own self-preservation that the opposition should not lose all strength; the same is true of power politics. A new creation in particular — the new *Reich*, for example — needs enemies more than friends: in opposition alone does it *feel* itself necessary, in opposition alone does it *become* necessary.

Our attitude to the "internal enemy" is no different: here too we have spiritualized hostility; here too we have come to appreciate its value. The price of fruitfulness is to be rich in internal opposition; one remains young only as long as the soul does not stretch itself and desire peace. Nothing has become more alien to us than that desideratum of former times, "peace of soul," the *Christian* desideratum; there is nothing we envy less than the moralistic cow and the fat happiness of the good conscience. One has renounced the *great* life when one renounces war.
</Quote>

### Hostility, worked

*Vergeistigung*, "spiritualization", is built on *Geist*, which in German is mind, intellect and wit as much as spirit, and never the soul (*Seele*). A passion that "weds the spirit" has entered the service of intelligence. What that changes is easiest to see in the second example, followed through one case.

<Example title="One hostility, three treatments">
**The drive.** A scientist hates a rival in her field: the one whose papers answer hers, whose name comes up whenever hers does. The hostility is strong and it wants what hostility wants, to overcome the rival.

**Discharged.** She blocks his grants, spreads rumours about his data, perhaps once loses her temper in public. The drive gets its object, the rival harmed, in its crudest form. If she succeeds, the rival is gone, and with him the resistance her hostility was feeding on: the force is spent and nothing has grown. If she fails, she has ruined her own name. This is the passion's "stupid" phase.

**Extirpated.** She decides that hostility is a sin and works it out of herself: she stops reading his papers, prays or meditates for peace, and is gentle when they meet. If this succeeds, the hostility is gone, and so is the drive that made her work at night to answer him. What remains is "peace of soul": the field has lost a combatant and she has lost her spur.

**Spiritualized.** She keeps the hostility and changes what it aims at. She no longer wants the rival destroyed but *beaten*, which needs him strong: she reads him more carefully than anyone, wants his best arguments, and measures her work by whether it would survive his attack. The object has changed from the rival's ruin to victory over a rival who remains formidable; the form has changed from a blow to a contest that only intelligence and years can conduct. The force is all there, more of it than before, because the contest now runs her whole working life.
</Example>

Love is the same move made by the other passion. Sensuality in its first phase wants a body, now, and is indifferent to whose; love keeps the desire and gives it a person, over years, with everything the person is. The ideal the church set against both passions, "peace of soul", gets a diagnosis in the same section: it is often a misread bodily state, a good digestion, the first weariness of evening, "the senile weakness of our will", "laziness, persuaded by vanity to give itself moral airs". Sometimes it is the calm of mastery in the middle of work. Rarely is it the conquest of a passion.

<Remark title="Sublimation and spiritualization">
*Sublimation*, which Nietzsche also calls *spiritualization* (*Vergeistigung*), is the treatment of a drive that keeps its force and changes its object and its form: instead of discharging on the nearest object or being destroyed, the drive is held back until it takes an object only intelligence and long time can reach, and is satisfied there. It is set against two other treatments, discharge (letting the drive out on its present object) and extirpation (rooting it out).
</Remark>

## How a drive can change its aim and keep its force

The case raises the question the opening put second. Hostility that wants the rival ruined and hostility that wants him strong look like two different wants. If a drive is defined by what it is after, changing its object should mean replacing one drive by another, and the talk of one drive "keeping its force" would be a confusion.

### The two layers of a want

The answer is in the structure of will to power. The climber of <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology" id="power-means-resistance-overcome">will to power</Ref> wanted two things at once: an end, the summit, and the pursuit of that end against resistance, the north face. The second layer is what makes a drive's force separable from its present object. What the hostile scientist's drive wants at that layer is to overcome an opponent, and a rival kept formidable offers more of that than a rival destroyed. Her drive has changed its end and kept its second layer, and the second layer is where its satisfaction lies.

That is also why not every new object will do. A sublimated drive must find in its new object resistance of its own kind: hostility needs an opponent, sensuality a person. The brawler who became a trial lawyer (<Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">will to power</Ref> asked what had happened to his aggression after fifteen years without a punch) kept his drive because cross-examination gave it an opponent to break; a desk job would have starved it.

### A word from chemistry

The word Nietzsche uses for the change came from the laboratory. To *sublimate* is to heat a solid until it passes into vapour and to let the vapour condense again elsewhere, leaving its impurities behind. *Human, All Too Human* (HH, 1878), the first book of his second period, uses it in its first section for ideals: what looks like disinterested contemplation or an "unegoistical action" is among the "sublimations in which the fundamental element appears almost evaporated, and is only to be discovered by the closest observation". What the inquiry needs, the section says, is "a chemistry of the moral, religious, aesthetic ideas and sentiments".

A year later, in *Mixed Opinions and Maxims* (1879, the sequel to HH), the word has its psychological sense: Christianity, he writes, "has always been a find" for "people with sublimated sexuality" (section 95). That is the modern meaning, used a generation before psychoanalysis made the word famous.

### "Good actions are sublimated evil ones"

The thesis that follows from the chemistry is stated in HH 107, a section about what is left of praise and blame once actions are seen as necessary:

<Quote source="HH I 107, trans. H. Zimmern">
<Original lang="de">
Gute Handlungen sind sublimirte böse; böse Handlungen sind vergröberte, verdummte gute.
</Original>

All these motives, however, whatever fine names we may give them, have all grown out of the same root, in which we believe the evil poisons to be situated; between good and evil actions there is no difference of species, but at most of degree. Good actions are sublimated evil ones; evil actions are vulgarised and stupefied good ones.
</Quote>

"No difference of species, but at most of degree": good and evil acts are the same drives at different stages of refinement. Read with "Anti-Nature" 1, "stupefied" (*verdummt*) is the passion's first phase, the one that drags its victim down "with the weight of stupidity", and "sublimated" its second. The cure the church chose treated a difference of degree as a difference of kind, and so tried to destroy the root of the good acts along with the bad ones.

### The fast: how constraint produces a sublimation

How does a drive get from the first phase to the second? *Beyond Good and Evil* has the answer, in the only section of that book where the verb appears with its full sense:

<Quote source="BGE 189, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Hiermit ist auch ein Wink zur Erklärung jenes Paradoxons gegeben, warum gerade in der christlichsten Periode Europa's und überhaupt erst unter dem Druck christlicher Werthurtheile der Geschlechtstrieb sich bis zur Liebe (amour-passion) sublimirt hat.
</Original>

Many kinds of fasts are necessary; and wherever powerful drives and habits prevail, legislators have to see that intercalary days are appointed, on which such a drive is fettered, and learns to hunger anew. Viewed from a higher standpoint, whole generations and epochs, when they show themselves infected with any moral fanaticism, seem like those intercalated periods of restraint and fasting, during which a drive learns to humble and submit itself — at the same time also to *purify* and *sharpen* itself; certain philosophical sects likewise admit of a similar interpretation (for instance, the Stoa, in the midst of Hellenic culture, with the atmosphere rank and overcharged with Aphrodisiacal odours). Here also is a hint for the explanation of the paradox, why it was precisely in the most Christian period of European history, and in general only under the pressure of Christian value judgments, that the sexual drive sublimated itself into love (*amour-passion*).
</Quote>

*Intercalary* days are days inserted into a calendar, as a leap day is; the section starts from the English Sunday, a day of enforced idleness that makes the Englishman long for his working week. The Stoa is the Stoic school, which taught freedom from passion in a Greek world it found sensual to excess. *Amour-passion*, French for passionate love, is the exclusive, lifelong, exalted love of one person that European poetry celebrated from the troubadours of the twelfth century on, and that the ancient world, on this account, did not know.

The paradox is that the church's method was extirpation and its result, in this case, a sublimation. The fast explains it. A drive "fettered" for a time is not destroyed if it is strong enough to outlast the fetter. It learns to wait, to "hunger anew", and in waiting it "purifies and sharpens" itself, so that when it is released it is a different, finer thing. On this account centuries of Christian pressure on sexuality worked as a fast, and what came out was love. The previous section, BGE 188, states the general law: what has made life worth living, "virtue, art, music, dancing, reason, spirituality", has come out of "long *obedience* in the same direction", a constraint kept up for generations.

<KeyFact>
Sublimation needs two things: a strong drive and a long constraint. A constraint on a weak drive kills it, which is extirpation; a strong drive without constraint discharges, which is the stupid phase.
</KeyFact>

## Who sublimates: the gardener and the goal

A fast imposed by a church on a whole epoch needs no one inside the person to direct it. But "Anti-Nature" speaks of *our* spiritualization of hostility, as something done, and *Daybreak* says outright that a person can do it to himself.

<Quote source="D 560, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
What we are at liberty to do. — One can dispose of one's drives like a gardener and, though few know it, cultivate the shoots of anger, pity, curiosity, vanity as productively and profitably as a beautiful fruit tree on a trellis; one can do it with the good or bad taste of a gardener and, as it were, in the French or English or Dutch or Chinese fashion; one can also let nature rule and only attend to a little embellishment and tidying-up here and there; one can, finally, without paying any attention to them at all, let the plants grow up and fight their fight out among themselves — indeed, one can take delight in such a wilderness, and desire precisely this delight, though it gives one some trouble, too. All this we are at liberty to do: but how many know we are at liberty to do it? Do the majority not believe in themselves as in complete fully-developed facts? Have the great philosophers not put their seal on this prejudice with the doctrine of the unchangeability of character?
</Quote>

### The trellis

The gardener's image adds what the chemistry lacked: a way of working on a drive that is neither war on it nor surrender to it. A fruit tree trained flat against a trellis, an espalier, is the same tree, with the same sap, made to grow along lines it would never take by itself, and it bears more fruit for it. The four national styles say that there is no single right shape: a French garden is clipped geometry, an English one cultivated wildness. And the drives listed, "anger, pity, curiosity, vanity", include the ones a moralist would pull up.

The objection was already put in <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="the-strongest-objection-who-arranges-the-drives">the society of drives</Ref>: a gardener stands outside the garden, and there is no self outside the drives to do the gardening. The reply there was that the arranger is itself a drive, or a coalition of drives, ranked above the ones it orders. *Daybreak* has a sharper version of the worry in an earlier section: "Woe to the thinker who is not the gardener but only the soil of the plants that grow in him!" (D 382). Sublimation needs that difference between gardener and soil, so it needs to say what in a person plays the gardener.

### A goal commended to the passions

*Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (Z, 1883–85) puts it in the chapter "On Enjoying and Suffering the Passions":

<Quote source="Z I 5, 'On Enjoying and Suffering the Passions', trans. W. Kaufmann">
Once you suffered passions and called them evil. But now you have only your virtues left: they grew out of your passions. You commended your highest goal to the heart of these passions: then they became your virtues and passions you enjoyed.

And whether you came from the tribe of the choleric or of the voluptuous or of the fanatic or of the vengeful, in the end all your passions became virtues and all your devils, angels. Once you had wild dogs in your cellar, but in the end they turned into birds and lovely singers. Out of your poisons you brewed your balsam.
</Quote>

The book is parable, but the mechanism is stated in one clause: "you commended your highest goal to the heart of these passions". What redirects a drive is a goal, and a goal is itself the aim of some drive that has come to rule. That is the ruling "idea" of <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="the-commonwealth-strong-drives-in-an-order-of-rank">the commonwealth of drives</Ref>, which grows among the drives, begins to command, and trains the others "as means toward a whole" before its task is known. The scientist's hostility was sublimated by her ambition for her work, which gave the hostility a job.

This marks the difference from D 109. There the rival drive wanted the troublesome one weaker, and its methods starved it. Here the ruling drive wants the other strong, because it needs that strength for its own end. A note of autumn 1887 puts the rule in one line:

<Quote source="Notebook 9, autumn 1887, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge, p. 163">
Summa: to master the passions, not to weaken or exterminate them! the greater the mastering force of the will, the more freedom may be given to the passions. the "great man" is great through the free play he gives his desires and the even greater power that is capable of taking these magnificent monsters into its service.
</Quote>

The note is unpublished, and the published texts say the same thing in pieces. Its value is the proportion: the stronger the ruling drive, the stronger the passions it can afford to keep. Extirpation is the policy of a weak ruler, who can be safe only among weak subjects.

### Goethe: strong drives held in one whole

*Twilight* gives one human example of the rule at full scale. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (1749–1832) was Germany's greatest poet and also a novelist, a natural scientist and a minister of state in Weimar; he is, a later section says, "the last German for whom I feel any reverence" (TI Skirmishes 51).

<Figure src="./figures/goethe-portrait.jpg" alt="Half-length oil portrait of Goethe in old age: grey hair, a dark frock coat over a white cravat with a red pin, seated in a leather chair, holding an opened letter and looking off to the right with a steady, alert expression." caption="Goethe at 79, painted by Joseph Karl Stieler in 1828, four years before his death." float="right" width="m" />

<Quote source="TI, 'Skirmishes of an Untimely Man' 49, trans. W. Kaufmann">
He bore its strongest instincts within himself: the sensibility, the idolatry of nature, the anti-historic, the idealistic, the unreal and revolutionary (the latter being merely a form of the unreal). … he did not retire from life but put himself into the midst of it; he was not fainthearted but took as much as possible upon himself, over himself, into himself. What he wanted was totality; he fought the mutual extraneousness of reason, senses, feeling, and will …; he disciplined himself to wholeness, he *created* himself.
</Quote>

"Its" is the eighteenth century's. Where a weaker man would have cut most of his age's tendencies out to keep his peace, Goethe kept them all and gave each work inside one life: sublimation not of one drive but of many at once.

<Aside title="The alchemist's trick">
Nietzsche tried the method on himself. At Christmas 1882, after the humiliating collapse of a friendship he had hoped would become more, he wrote to Franz Overbeck, the Basel friend of <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books">his life and books</Ref>, that he was being "broken on the wheel of my own feelings": "If I do not discover the alchemists' trick of turning even this filth into gold, I am lost." Within weeks he had written the first part of *Zarathustra*. The letter shows that he meant sublimation as something a person can be forced to attempt, not that it worked.
</Aside>

## Sublimation in a culture: refined forms and their price

HH 107's thesis reaches further than single lives. If good actions are sublimated evil ones, then a culture's most admired achievements should turn out to be old drives held back and refined over generations. Nietzsche's books name several such lines.

*Cruelty*: BGE 229 calls it "my thesis" that "almost everything that we call 'higher culture' is based upon the spiritualising and intensifying of cruelty", the pleasure in another's suffering refined into the pleasure of tragedy and, turned inward, into the knower's severity with his own wishes; <Ref lesson="cruelty-and-culture">cruelty and culture</Ref> reads the passage whole. *Dissimulation*: D 248 derives honesty from the long need to appear honest among the powerful: "What is dissimulated for a long time at last becomes nature: dissimulation in the end sublimates itself, and organs and instincts are the surprising fruit of the garden of hypocrisy."

The last line turned on its source. What killed the Christian god, on GS 357's account of <Ref lesson="nihilism" id="two-thousand-years-of-training-in-truthfulness">two thousand years of training in truthfulness</Ref>, was "Christian morality itself, the concept of truthfulness", the father confessor's refinement of conscience "translated and *sublimated* into a scientific conscience". Two thousand years of training in not lying, to others and to oneself, produced a discipline that could no longer lie to itself about God. The sublimated form turned on the faith that had trained it.

Sublimation is therefore no moral guarantee. The refined form is stronger and more intelligent than the crude one, and nothing ensures that it is safer, kinder, or friendly to what produced it. The *Genealogy* (GM, 1887) says it of cruelty: if it is less visible today, perhaps it "simply requires a certain sublimation and subtilization", appearing "translated into the imaginative and psychical and adorned with such innocent names that even the tenderest and most hypocritical conscience is not suspicious of them" (GM II 7). A sublimated drive is the same drive, harder to recognize.

## The standard behind the ranking: a morality as a symptom

Nietzsche ranks spiritualization above both indulgence and castration, and the ranking needs a standard. The rest of "Anti-Nature" supplies it, and it turns out to be a way of reading every morality.

<Quote source="TI, 'Morality as Anti-Nature' 4–5, trans. W. Kaufmann">
I reduce a principle to a formula. Every naturalism in morality — that is, every healthy morality — is dominated by an instinct of life; some commandment of life is fulfilled by a determinate canon of "shalt" and "shalt not"; some inhibition and hostile element on the path of life is thus removed. *Anti-natural* morality — that is, almost every morality which has so far been taught, revered, and preached — turns, conversely, *against* the instincts of life: it is *condemnation* of these instincts, now secret, now outspoken and impudent. When it says, "God looks at the heart," it says No to both the lowest and the highest desires of life, and posits God as the *enemy of life*. The saint in whom God delights is the ideal eunuch. Life has come to an end where the "kingdom of God" begins.

Once one has comprehended the outrage of such a revolt against life as has become almost sacrosanct in Christian morality, one has, fortunately, also comprehended something else: the futility, apparentness, absurdity, and *mendaciousness* of such a revolt. A condemnation of life by the living remains in the end a mere symptom of a certain kind of life: the question whether it is justified or unjustified is not even raised thereby. One would require a position *outside* of life, and yet have to know it as well as one, as many, as all who have lived it, in order to be permitted even to touch the problem of the *value* of life: reasons enough to comprehend that this problem is for us an unapproachable problem. When we speak of values, we speak with the inspiration, with the way of looking at things, which is part of life: life itself forces us to posit values; life itself values through us when we posit values. From this it follows that even that anti-natural morality which conceives of God as the counter-concept and condemnation of life is only a value judgment of life — but of what life? of what kind of life? I have already given the answer: of declining, weakened, weary, condemned life.
</Quote>

### Healthy and anti-natural morality

A healthy morality, in section 4, is a set of commands in the service of a drive. An athlete's training rules are the plainest case: "shalt not" drink before a race, "shalt" sleep early, each rule removing an obstacle on the way to something the athlete's strongest drive wants. Such a morality constrains drives and can be very hard; BGE 189's fast is one. What it does not do is condemn them. An anti-natural morality condemns the instincts as such: "God looks at the heart" means that the wish itself is the sin, whether or not it is acted on, so the only innocent heart is one without the wish. Hence "the saint in whom God delights is the ideal eunuch".

### No standpoint outside life

Section 5 asks what such a condemnation can be worth, and answers that it cannot be a verdict on life, because no one is in a position to give one. To judge the value of life one would need to stand outside it and still know it from inside, as all who have lived it know it. Every valuation is made from inside some life, by its drives ("life itself values through us"). So a morality that condemns life is not evidence about life; it is evidence about the life that holds it, as a fever is evidence about the patient and not about the weather.

This is the standard behind the three treatments. <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology" id="the-definition-in-the-last-books">Will to power</Ref> gave ascending and declining life their content: ascending life is will to power growing, declining life is will to power failing (A 6). Extirpation is the treatment declining life prescribes for itself, a weak will that cannot moderate its drives turned into an imperative for everyone. Section 5 ends on morality as Schopenhauer formulated it, the "negation of the will to life", which is <Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-verdict-and-the-way-out">his cure for existence</Ref>: since the trouble is willing itself, stop willing. That morality, section 5 says, "is the very instinct of decadence, which makes an imperative of itself. It says: 'Perish!' It is a condemnation pronounced by the condemned."

### "A piece of fatum"

Section 6 seems to cut against the gardener. It mocks the moralist who says to a single human being, "You ought to be such and such!", because "the single human being is a piece of *fatum* from the front and from the rear", fate through and through; "to say to him, 'Change yourself!' is to demand that everything be changed, even retroactively". If no one can change himself, it is hard to see who trains the trellis.

The two passages ask different things. The moralist demands that a person become another type, a man without his passions, which means cutting out what he is. The gardener works with the plants that are there and changes how they grow. Sublimation is change inside the fate: the same drives, given new objects by a ruling drive that is itself part of the same fate. That is why it can be done, and why only someone with strong drives to begin with can do much of it.

## The strongest objection: every virtue a refined vice

A serious critic now presses on HH 107. If love is refined lust, honesty refined dissimulation, justice refined revenge and the knower's integrity refined cruelty, two things seem to follow. The account is unfalsifiable: any virtue whatever can be given a base ancestor after the fact, and nothing could show that a given virtue had none. And it is deflating: if love is lust, love has been unmasked, and the higher forms Nietzsche ranks so carefully are the old vices after all. Either way the doctrine explains nothing it did not already assume.

### Continuity, not unmasking

The first reply comes from the passage the word itself came from. HH 1 asks what if "the most beautiful colours have been obtained from base, even despised materials". The colours are still beautiful: the chemistry of a dye does not make the cloth ugly. What Nietzsche claims is the opposite of "nothing but". Love is lust that has become what lust in its first phase could not be: lasting, particular, intelligent. That a thing came from something else does not settle what it is worth, the point <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="discrediting-is-a-genetic-fallacy">the death of God</Ref> made against the genetic fallacy, and here it works in the virtues' favour.

BGE 2 states the continuity as a hypothesis, the value of good things perhaps "consists precisely in their being insidiously related, knotted, and crocheted to these evil and apparently opposed things", and the *Genealogy* as a historical rule: "All good things were formerly bad things; every original sin has turned into an original virtue" (GM III 9). The deflation charge assumes that a virtue is worth only what its origin was worth, which is the metaphysicians' assumption HH 1 begins by rejecting: that "more highly valued things" must have a "miraculous origin" of their own.

### A claim that could fail

The second reply has to meet the charge of unfalsifiability, and it can do that only with a claim that could have come out otherwise. The obvious candidate does not qualify. The theory says that a drive a discipline destroys leaves no refined form behind. But if the only evidence that a drive was strong is that a refined form appeared under a harsh discipline, as love appeared under Christianity, then the refined form is being read as proof of the strength that was supposed to explain it. No case could count against the theory read that way, which is the circle the objection describes.

A test needs the two terms measured separately: the strength of the crude drive on one side, the height of the refined form on the other. BGE 75 makes a claim of that shape: "The degree and nature of a man's sensuality extends to the highest altitudes of his spirit." The strength of a crude drive shows in a person before any refinement, in how sensual, how combative, how quick to anger he is. Read this way, BGE 75 says that this strength sets the ceiling of what the drive can become. "Anti-Nature" 3 treats hostility exactly as it treats sensuality: a crude passion whose force is kept and given a new object. If the refined form spends the crude drive's force, it can have no more than the crude drive had, so the ceiling carries over from sensuality to hostility. Strength alone does not guarantee the refined form, since without constraint the drive simply discharges. So the prediction runs one way: the greatest love should come from the strongly sensual, and the most original work in a contested field from the strongly combative, and people with weak passions should not excel at the refined forms. If the greatest lovers turned out to be the least sensual, or the most original scientists the least combative, the theory would be in trouble. The claim is comparative, and it is exposed to the record.

### The gap

Three things remain unsupplied. The mechanism shows how a drive can keep its force and change its form; it does not show that the new form is *higher*. "Higher" comes from the standard of section 5, ascending against declining life, which the chapter states and does not argue. Nietzsche knew that refinement alone certifies nothing: a note of spring 1888 says that in Plato moral judgments were torn from the conditions they grew in and, "under the semblance of sublimation, are denaturalised" (notebook 14, unpublished). Telling a real sublimation from that semblance again needs the standard.

The second is the hydraulic picture underneath the fast: a drive held back keeps its force and must spend it somewhere. That is a model taken from the physiology of energy of his day, assumed rather than shown; a drive held back might simply weaken, and the theory has no way to rule that out in advance.

The third is the measure the comparative test depends on. The theory needs a way of telling how strong a drive is that does not depend on whether the drive survived constraint, and the texts do not give one: Nietzsche judges a drive's strength case by case, from temperament and biography, and nothing in the books says how two people's sensuality would be ranked before their histories are known. Until that measure exists, the claim is testable in principle and untested in fact.

## Is sublimation what will to power really is?

One reading makes sublimation the key to the whole of Nietzsche. On it, will to power is at bottom self-overcoming, its highest and truest form the passions mastered and spiritualized; the conquerors and men of prey who appear in his books are images of unsublimated passion, cited as material, not as ideals. <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology" id="the-opposite-correction-fails-too">Will to power</Ref> already tested the general form of this reading against BGE 259. Its sublimation form can be tested against what "Anti-Nature" and its neighbours say about the unsublimated strong.

### What the reading gets right

It has real support. "Anti-Nature" calls spiritualization a "great triumph" and puts it above the church's knife. HH 107 calls the crude forms "stupefied". And in *Ecce Homo* (EH, written 1888) Nietzsche recalls telling people "that they should sooner look *even* for a Cesare Borgia than for a Parsifal" (EH, 'Why I Write Such Good Books' 1). Cesare Borgia (1475–1507), son of a pope, conquered a state for himself in central Italy by war, treachery and murder. Parsifal is the chaste "pure fool" who heals the king of the Grail knights in Wagner's last opera, the work <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner" id="the-postscripts-salvation-from-the-saviour">the case of Wagner</Ref> found flattering "every nihilistic (Buddhistic) instinct". The "even" says that Borgia is no ideal.

### What the texts add

The same texts rank something else. "Anti-Nature" 2 divides people by whether they can impose moderation on a drive, and says the radical cure is "indispensable only for the degenerate". It says nothing against the strong who neither extirpate nor spiritualize; its contempt is for the castrated. BGE 197 defends Borgia against the moralists:

<Quote source="BGE 197, trans. H. Zimmern">
The beast of prey and the man of prey (for instance, Caesar Borgia) are fundamentally misunderstood, "nature" is misunderstood, so long as one seeks a "morbidness" in the constitution of these healthiest of all tropical monsters and growths, or even an innate "hell" in them — as almost all moralists have done hitherto. Does it not seem that there is a hatred of the virgin forest and of the tropics among moralists? And that the "tropical man" must be discredited at all costs, whether as disease and deterioration of mankind, or as his own hell and self-torture? And why? In favour of the "temperate zones"? In favour of the temperate men? The "moral"? The mediocre?
</Quote>

"Healthiest of all tropical monsters" is not the language of a man who thinks Borgia a failure of sublimation. And the *Ecce Homo* sentence is a preference order: Parsifal is the castrated ideal, and even Borgia, unsublimated, ranks above it.

### Where Borgia stands in the ranking

That looks like a contradiction of the ranking so far, in which discharge was a passion's "stupid" phase. It is not one, because Borgia's drives were not in that phase. The stupid phase is a passion that drags its owner down, the jealous man smashing his own room. Borgia's violence was spent with calculation, timed and aimed at a state he meant to build, which takes the capacity "Anti-Nature" 2 calls imposing moderation: letting a provocation pass until it suits him. His drives were discharged, but under government.

So the texts support an order in two parts, not a single line. Spiritualization ranks above crude discharge. And strength, whether spiritualized or governed, ranks above extirpation. The "even" in *Ecce Homo* puts Borgia below the ideal, and the preference puts him above Parsifal.

### Against the master key

Two more facts count against making sublimation the key. BGE 189 credits the great sublimation of sexuality to "the pressure of Christian value judgments", the very morality Nietzsche attacks, not to a healthy nature. And GS 357's sublimated truthfulness produced nihilism: a conscience refined enough to destroy the faith that trained it. In the texts, then, sublimation is a mechanism ranked above extirpation and above crude discharge, not the meaning of will to power.

<Conclusion>
You can now name the three treatments of a drive and say how Nietzsche orders them: spiritualization above crude discharge, and strength, spiritualized or governed by moderation, above extirpation. Crude discharge lets a passion act in its stupid first phase; extirpation, the church's "castratism", removes the danger by removing the strength the danger used, and is chosen by those who cannot impose moderation; spiritualization keeps the force and changes the object and the form, as sensuality becomes love and hostility a need for strong enemies. You can explain how that is possible, because a drive's satisfaction lies in overcoming resistance, which a new object can supply, and what produces it: a strong drive under long constraint, as in BGE 189's fast, directed by a ruling goal that wants the drive strong, the gardener of D 560. You can answer the charge that every virtue is a vice unmasked (continuity, not "nothing but", and a comparative claim exposed to the record), and name what the account leaves unshown: why the new form is higher, the hydraulic model underneath, and a measure of a drive's strength that does not depend on whether it survived.

"Anti-Nature" 5 read a morality as the symptom of the kind of life that holds it, so that a condemnation of life tells us about the condemner. *Twilight*'s next chapter, <Ref lesson="four-great-errors">the Four Great Errors</Ref>, generalizes the move: it finds that morality and religion as such confuse cause and effect, taking the symptoms of a physiological state for the causes of it.
</Conclusion>

**The two layers.** The climber wants an end, the summit, and wants to pursue that end against resistance, the north face; take the resistance away (the cable car) and something she wanted is gone though the summit is reached. Since the satisfaction lies at the second layer, the drive satisfied in love is the drive that was satisfied in a casual encounter, at a higher degree; HH 107's "no difference of species, but at most of degree". BGE 75 says the same from the other side: "The degree and nature of a man's sensuality extends to the highest altitudes of his spirit."

Sensuality cannot be redirected onto stamp collecting or bookkeeping: those offer resistance, but not the resistance of a desired person. <Exercise>
Take envy: the drive that cannot bear another's superiority. Describe envy (a) discharged, (b) extirpated and (c) spiritualized, naming in each case the object and the form of the drive, and say what is left of its force. Then name the sentence of "Anti-Nature" 3 that the spiritualized form most resembles, and say why.

<Solution>
**(a) Discharged.** Object: the superior person's advantage. Form: pulling him down, by gossip, sabotage or ridicule, or wishing him harm. The force is spent on reducing him, and when he falls the envy's object is gone; nothing has been built.

**(b) Extirpated.** The envious man decides envy is a sin and roots it out: he stops comparing himself, cultivates contentment, tells himself that all are equal before God. If it works, the drive is gone, and with it the sting that would have made him work to equal the man he envied. What remains is the "peace of soul" of section 3, here very likely "laziness, persuaded by vanity to give itself moral airs".

**(c) Spiritualized.** Object: no longer the other's ruin but the other's level, to be equalled and passed. Form: emulation, a contest in which the superior man is wanted superior, since beating a lesser man would mean nothing. The force is all kept and given work; it now drives a long effort.

**The sentence.** "Another triumph is our spiritualization of *hostility*. It consists in a profound appreciation of the value of having enemies." Spiritualized envy needs the superior rival to remain superior, as spiritualized hostility needs the enemy to remain strong: "in opposition alone does it *become* necessary". In both, the change of aim is from destroying the other to overcoming him while he stands.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "Sublimation is Nietzsche's name for self-control by willpower: the reasonable self holds a drive down until it behaves." Say what the claim gets wrong about *who* does the controlling and about *what* is done to the drive, using D 109, BGE 117, Z I 5 and the notebook of autumn 1887.

<Solution>
**Who.** There is no reasonable self above the drives to hold anything down. D 109: the intellect is "only the blind instrument of another drive"; "at bottom it is one drive which is complaining about another". BGE 117: "The will to overcome an affect is ultimately only the will of another, or of several other, affects." What redirects a drive is, on Z I 5, a goal: "You commended your highest goal to the heart of these passions", and a goal is the aim of a ruling drive, the organizing idea of the commonwealth of drives. "Willpower" in the claim's sense, a faculty standing apart from all drives, is the <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">doer behind the deed</Ref>, a subject standing apart from its acts, which is a fiction.

**What.** Holding a drive down until it behaves is D 109's combat: rationing, avoidance, association with a painful thought. All of them aim at less of the drive. Sublimation aims at the drive kept strong and given a new object. The notebook of autumn 1887 states the difference as a rule: "to master the passions, not to weaken or exterminate them! the greater the mastering force of the will, the more freedom may be given to the passions". A ruler who holds his passions down is weak by that rule; the strong ruler lets them run and puts them to work.

**What survives in the claim.** Constraint does belong to sublimation: BGE 189's fast fetters a drive so that it "learns to hunger anew" and "purifies and sharpens" itself. But the constraint is a means to the drive's refinement, not to its defeat, and it is imposed by a drive with its own end, not by a self without drives.

<Exercise>
A boundary probe. A man has a violent temper so weakly governed that he cannot let any provocation pass without striking out; he also has no strong ambition or goal of any kind. (a) Can his temper be sublimated, on Nietzsche's account? (b) What does "Anti-Nature" 2 say he must do instead? (c) For whom, then, is sublimation the recommended treatment, and what does the case show about the claim that it ranks above extirpation?

<Solution>
**(a) No.** The lesson gave sublimation two routes, and both are closed to him. The first is the fast imposed from outside (BGE 189), which needs no gardener in the person, only a drive held back long enough to "hunger anew". But he has "the inability not to respond to a stimulus": no rule holds his temper back while a provocation is in front of him, so the only constraint that works on him is La Trappe's, the removal of every occasion. That is not a fast but extirpation; a drive never fed is not refined but starved. The second route is the gardener's (Z I 5, D 560): a ruling goal to which the drive is "commended", a drive strong enough to give the temper a better object. He has no goal at all. A weak ruler cannot afford strong subjects, and here there is no ruler.

**(b) The radical cure.** "Anti-Nature" 2: castration and extirpation are "instinctively chosen by those who are too weak-willed, too degenerate, to be able to impose moderation on themselves", who "require La Trappe … some kind of definitive declaration of hostility, a cleft between themselves and the passion". And: "Radical means are indispensable only for the degenerate." For this man the knife is a necessity, the best of the options open to him.

**(c) Who sublimation is for.** For those with strong drives and something stronger still to rule them: the notebook's "even greater power that is capable of taking these magnificent monsters into its service", Goethe who "bore its strongest instincts within himself". The ranking of spiritualization above extirpation is therefore not a recommendation to everyone. It ranks *types*: the one who can spiritualize is higher than the one who must castrate, and the existence of a man who must is, for Nietzsche, a symptom ("always a symptom to reflect on"), not an objection to the ranking. What the case shows is the boundary of the doctrine: it is advice only for the strong, and a diagnosis of the rest.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A monastery's rule forbids all anger: a monk who feels anger must confess it as a sin, and any act of anger is punished. (a) Derive Nietzsche's verdict on the rule from "Anti-Nature" 1, 2 and 4. (b) Then write the rule a spiritualizing community would put in its place, and say from "Anti-Nature" 3 and BGE 189 what that rule would require of the monks and what it would produce.

<Solution>
**(a) The verdict.** The rule condemns the drive itself, not its stupid discharge: since the felt anger must be confessed as sin, it says No to the wish, as "God looks at the heart" does (section 4). That makes it anti-natural, in section 4's sense: not a "shalt not" removing an obstacle on the path of some drive, but a condemnation of an instinct. Its method is extirpation, the dentist pulling the tooth (section 1), and so it removes the strength anger would give to anything the monks undertake: zeal, endurance, the energy to fight their own sloth. Section 2 adds the diagnosis: a community that needs such a rule is either made of men who cannot moderate their anger or ruled by those who cannot. And among the "impossible ascetics", those whose anger is too strong for the rule and too weak to be governed, it will breed the most poisonous hatred of anger in others.

**(b) A spiritualizing rule.** It would forbid anger's crude discharge, the blow, the insult, and require that anger be kept and directed: at the monk's own failings, at a hard task, at an opponent worth arguing with. "Anti-Nature" 3's model is the enemy valued: the community would want opponents, inside and outside, and train its members to fight them by thought and work rather than to have no enemies. BGE 189 supplies the method: periods of restraint, a fast imposed on the drive, long enough for it to "humble and submit itself" and "at the same time also to *purify* and *sharpen* itself". Such a rule requires monks with strong anger and strong goals; it would produce not peaceful men but formidable ones, whose anger had become severity, rigour and polemical force. Whether that is "higher" is the standard of section 5: it is the treatment ascending life gives itself.

<Solution>
**(a) The rewrite.** The verdict makes license and luxury the cause and the people's ruin the effect. Read as a symptom: a people already declining, exhausted, short of strength, craves ever stronger and more frequent stimulation, because a weary nature needs stronger stimuli to feel anything; license and luxury are the form that craving takes. So the decline is the cause, and the license and luxury are its effect, signs of a ruin already under way.

**(b) What would make it right.** That the craving for stimulation appears *after* the decline begins and not before; that strong, rising peoples can afford abundance without being ruined by it, as a healthy man can drink without becoming a drunkard; and that removing the luxury (sumptuary laws, enforced austerity) does not halt the decline. If austerity reliably saved declining peoples, the original verdict would stand.

**(c) The general error.** Mistaking the effect for the cause, the same error as the extirpating moralist's: he sees a passion doing damage and blames the passion, where the damage comes from the weakness that cannot govern it. This is the first of the errors in the next chapter of *Twilight*, which uses exactly this case: "The church and morality say: 'A generation, a people, are destroyed by license and luxury.' My recovered reason says: when a people approaches destruction, when it degenerates physiologically, then license and luxury follow from this (namely, the craving for ever stronger and more frequent stimulation, as every exhausted nature knows it)" (TI, 'The Four Great Errors' 2). The chapter opens with a diet, the slender diet of a famous Venetian who credited it with his long life, and turns it the same way; <Ref lesson="four-great-errors">the Four Great Errors</Ref> reads it whole.

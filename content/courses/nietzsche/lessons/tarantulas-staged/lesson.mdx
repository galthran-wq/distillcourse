'On the Tarantulas' says in a parable what the First Treatise says in a history — that preaching equality is revenge in disguise — and the parable ends with Zarathustra himself bitten, which the history has no way to say. This lesson is the module's application: the artefact is two speeches of *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, "On the Tarantulas" (Part II, 1883) and "On a Thousand and One Goals" (Part I, 1883), and the theory is the First Treatise as the last three lessons reconstructed it. Each sentence of the speeches is mapped onto an argued passage or onto nothing, and the residue is stated in three columns: what the staging adds, what it drops, and what it undercuts.

The module's argument is complete on the side of the diagnosis. <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">The master-and-slave lesson</Ref> stated the minimal pair, "good" of oneself first against "evil" of the other first; <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref> supplied the caste, the affect and the workshop, and marked the gap between the inversion and its victory; <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref> removed the chooser the workshop's relabelings presuppose. What stands open is the victory, and the Second Treatise takes it up. Before it does, the course fixes the rule by which it reads the one book of the corpus that is neither treatise nor aphorism, on the speech that stages this module's diagnosis; the rule returns in <Ref lesson="self-overcoming-staged">the self-overcoming lesson</Ref> and <Ref lesson="overman-staged">the overman lesson</Ref>, where the staged theses are the positive doctrine's.

The route: the reading rule, with the two passages in which the book states it about itself; "On the Tarantulas" mapped line by line, with the sentence that has a reception history read with its object; "On a Thousand and One Goals" mapped as the first coinage at the scale of a people; the verdict in three columns, adds, drops, undercuts; and a closing section that states what the map establishes and what the Second Treatise inherits from the speech as well as from the treatise.

## The reading rule: a speech is testimony from a character

<Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">The protocol lesson</Ref> divided the corpus into three genres and gave *Zarathustra* a genre of its own, dramatization: a book that argues through a character, whose speeches the course maps onto theses argued elsewhere and never cites as premises in Nietzsche's voice. That was a classification. The present lesson turns it into a procedure, and the first thing the procedure needs is a reason to treat the speaker as a character rather than as a mouthpiece. The book supplies two.

The first is in Part I, at the end of "On the Bestowing Virtue", the speech in which Zarathustra dismisses the disciples who have followed him out of the town. "I counsel you to go away from me and guard yourselves against Zarathustra! And even better: be ashamed of him! Perhaps he deceived you." Then: "You say you believe in Zarathustra? But what matters Zarathustra! You are my believers, but what matter all believers! You had not yet sought yourselves, then you found me. All believers do this; that's why all faith amounts to so little. Now I bid you lose me and find yourselves; and only when you have all denied me will I return to you." A speaker who instructs his audience to deny him has told the reader what a quotation from him is worth as a creed.

The second is in Part II, in "On Poets". A disciple reminds Zarathustra that he once said "the poets lie too much", and asks why. The reply: "Yet what did Zarathustra once say to you? That the poets lie too much? – But Zarathustra too is a poet. Do you believe now that he speaks the truth here? Why do you believe that?" The disciple answers, "I believe in Zarathustra", and Zarathustra "shook his head and smiled": "Faith does not make me blessed. Especially not faith in me." The speech goes on to say what poets do instead of knowing: "we know too little and are bad learners, thus we simply have to lie", and "all gods are poets' parable, poets' cock and bull".

### The direction of the map runs against the dates

From the word "dramatization" one would expect the argument to come first and the staging after it, as a play follows the history it stages. The dates run the other way. Part I of *Zarathustra* was written in January 1883 and Part II in June and July of the same year; *Beyond Good and Evil* followed in 1886 and the *Genealogy* in 1887. "On the Tarantulas" precedes the treatise it is read against by four years, and "On a Thousand and One Goals" precedes it by the same margin. The speeches could not have staged the treatise; the treatise, if anything, argued what the speeches had already staged.

The rule survives the reversal because it is a relation between texts and not a claim about composition. The course's question is never "what did Nietzsche mean by the tarantula" but "which argued sentence does this line correspond to, and what remains when the correspondence is exhausted". A correspondence can be read in either direction; the *residue*, what the speech contains that no argued sentence does and what the argued works contain that the speech lacks, is what the map is for. The protocol's order-of-composition check still applies: where a speech and a section disagree, the later text is the one to cite, and in every case below the later text is the treatise.

<Remark title="the dramatization rule, stated">
**Input.** A speech of Zarathustra, with its part, its number and its date; and the theses of the argued works that the course has reconstructed.

**Step 1.** Each sentence of the speech that carries a claim is set against the argued passage that states the same claim, by book and section, or against "none".

**Step 2.** The residue is recorded in three columns. *Adds*: what the speech contains that the argued works do not. *Drops*: what the argued works contain that the speech does not. *Undercuts*: where the speech's frame (its speaker, its narrative, its own warnings) withdraws what its sentences assert.

**Output.** A verdict on what the speech can be cited for: the argued passage, never the speech, is the premise; the speech is cited for what it adds, with what it undercuts stated beside it.

**The rule's own premise.** That the argued works are the standard. A reader who holds that *Zarathustra* is the doctrine and the treatises its footnotes, which is Heidegger's order of the corpus, runs the map the other way, and <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref> is where the course states that reading at strength. Here the published-works rule of <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> fixes the direction: a treatise's section is a step in an argument, a speech is testimony, and testimony is checked against argument.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The two passages in which the book warns against its own speaker, "lose me and find yourselves" and "Zarathustra too is a poet", are not asides to the doctrine but its frame: the character who says "humans are not equal" is the same character who says "perhaps he deceived you", and a citation that keeps the first sentence and drops the second has quoted half a document.
</KeyFact>

## "On the Tarantulas" mapped: eleven lines against the argued works

The rule's first step is the map, and the speech's setting is fixed before any line is mapped. Zarathustra stands at a spider's hole among "the ruins of an ancient temple"; he touches the web, the tarantula comes out, "your triangle and mark sits in black" on its back, and he addresses it. "Revenge sits in your soul: wherever you bite, there black scabs grow; your poison makes the soul whirl with revenge!" Then the parable declares itself: "So I speak to you in parables, you who cause the souls to whirl, you preachers of equality! Tarantulas you are to me and hidden avengers!" (*versteckte Rachsüchtige*). The tarantulas are then given a voice, in quotation marks, and the rest of the speech alternates between their creed and Zarathustra's diagnosis of it.

### The tarantulas' creed, line by line

The creed comes in three quoted sentences, and each has an argued counterpart. "'That the world become full of the thunderstorms of our revenge, precisely that we would regard as justice,' – thus they speak with one another." That is I:14's masterpiece from <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref>, where what the whisperers demand "they call not retribution but 'the triumph of justice'"; Zarathustra's line a paragraph earlier, that he tears the web so that "your revenge might spring forth from behind your word 'justice'", is the workshop's fifth row with the curtain named. "'We want to exact revenge and heap insult on all whose equals we are not' – thus vow the tarantula hearts." That is I:10's *ressentiment*: an affect denied the deed, kept, and discharged as a judgment on those who have what its bearer lacks, a valuation that "needs, physiologically speaking, external stimuli in order to act at all". And "'will to equality' – that itself from now on shall be the name for virtue; and against everything that has power we shall raise our clamor!" That is the workshop's operation in one sentence: an incapacity, the inability to be the equal of the powerful, receives the name of a virtue.

Zarathustra's diagnosis then restates the treatise's condition. "You preachers of equality, the tyrant's madness of impotence (*der Tyrannen-Wahnsinn der Ohnmacht*) cries thus out of you for 'equality': your secret tyrant's cravings mask themselves thus in your words of virtue!" The word is I:7's, "out of impotence (*aus der Ohnmacht*) their hatred grows into something monstrous", and the structure is I:6–7's: the man of *ressentiment* has not given up the desire to rule, he has repressed it, and the repressed lust to rule is what the revaluation serves. I:13 supplies the phrase the speech compresses, "the self-deception of impotence". The speech's "aggrieved conceit, repressed envy (*verhaltener Neid*)" is the same affect under its other name: envy transformed, under deprivation, into a hatred that the new code makes merited.

Two lines map onto *Beyond Good and Evil* rather than the *Genealogy*, and both name a target the treatise leaves unnamed. "Preachers of equality" have no historical referent in the speech, which is the parable's own discipline; BGE 202, in the argued register, supplies one: "the democratic movement is the heir to Christianity", and the socialists, anarchists and "ideologists of revolution" of the section are "united in their dogged opposition to any special claims, special rights, or privileges (which means, in the last analysis, that they are opposed to any rights: since when everyone is equal, no one will need 'rights' anymore)" and "united in the religion of pity". And the line "There are those who preach my doctrine of life, and at the same time they are preachers of equality and tarantulas" maps onto BGE 44, where "something that abuses this name" of free spirit is found "in all the countries of Europe, and in America as well": "they belong to the *levelers*, these misnamed 'free spirits' – as eloquent and prolifically scribbling slaves of the democratic taste and its 'modern ideas.'" Both times the speech says "I do not want to be mixed in with and mistaken for" them; BGE 44 says why the mistake is available.

### The judge, the pharisee, and the drive to punish

"But thus I counsel you my friends: mistrust all in whom the drive to punish is strong! Those are people of bad kind and kin; in their faces the hangman and the bloodhound are visible." The argued passage is the fourth of *Twilight*'s Four Great Errors from <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref>: "everywhere accountability is sought, it is usually the instinct for punishing and judging which seeks it", and the doctrine of free will was "invented essentially for the purpose of punishment". The speech has the drive; the chapter has the mechanism, that the drive needs a doer who could have done otherwise and so invents one. "Mistrust all those who speak much of their justice!" has a second argued passage, GM II:11, where the *Genealogy* argues against the attempt "to sanctify revenge under the name of justice" and holds that "the active, aggressive, arrogant man is still a hundred paces nearer to justice than the reactive"; that section belongs to <Ref lesson="debt-and-guilt">the debt lesson</Ref>, and the map records it as the place where the assertion becomes an argument.

"And when they call themselves 'the good and the just' (*die Guten und Gerechten*), then do not forget that all they lack to be pharisees is – power!" The "good and just" are Zarathustra's standing name for the moral majority throughout the book, and the sentence is I:13's lambs with the missing term supplied: the lambs' "let us be different from the evil, namely good", read "cold and without prejudice", "means nothing more than: 'we weak ones are simply weak'", and the speech adds what the lambs would be if they were not weak. That addition is I:14's fifth row run in reverse, from "not wanting" back to "not being able".

### The sentence with a reception history, read with its object

The correct claim first: "humans are not equal" is, in the speech, a sentence about the preachers' equality, spoken by a character, and qualified in the next line by the overman. The misreading it has to displace is the one with the longest record, that the sentence is Nietzsche's political programme, the "humans are not equal" of a party that would rank persons by force. The course rejects the misreading, and the reason it fails is in the paragraph the sentence sits in. "I do not want to be mixed in with and mistaken for these preachers of equality. For thus justice speaks to me: 'humans are not equal.' And they shouldn't become so either! What would my love for the overman be if I spoke otherwise?" The object of "not equal" is the tarantulas' "will to equality", which the speech has just diagnosed as revenge; the sentence denies the tarantulas' premise, not a legal status. Its qualifier makes the denial a claim about rank in the sense of <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the pathos of distance</Ref>, a difference of type that the overman names at its limit; and the "war and inequality" that follows is spelled out as "inventors of images and ghosts shall they become in their hostility", a contest of valuations, "good and evil, and rich and poor, and high and trifling, and all the names of values" as "weapons and clanging signs that life must overcome itself again and again".

The replacement model is the one the argued works give. The premise the political reading needs is real and the course flagged it in the master-and-slave lesson: BGE 257's claim that an order of rank between persons is a precondition of an order of rank within a soul, "and in some sense needs slavery". That is the passage a political reading of Nietzsche must answer, because it is argued, and it is examined in <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref>. The speech is not that passage; a character says the sentence, a tarantula bites him in the next paragraph, and the book's frame has already said "perhaps he deceived you". The discriminating example is the sentence's own recurrence: "On Scholars", nine speeches later in Part II, closes with the same words, "For human beings are not equal: thus speaks justice. And what I want, they would not be permitted to want!", where the object is the scholars' wish that Zarathustra want what they want. Both times the sentence asserts a difference of type against a demand for sameness; neither time does it prescribe an arrangement of persons. The second exercise below returns to the claim after the rest of the map has been read.

<KeyFact>
Nine of the speech's eleven load-bearing lines have an argued passage, and the two that do not are the lines a reader is most likely to quote: "that mankind be redeemed from revenge: that to me is the bridge to the highest hope", and the bite. Neither has a counterpart in GM I because the treatise diagnoses *ressentiment* in others and offers no hope of deliverance from it, and never touches the affect it describes; the two lines with no map are exactly the two the third section files under "adds" and "undercuts".
</KeyFact>

## The bite: the speaker inside the diagnosis

The speech's last movement is the one the treatise has no equivalent for, and its structure is a sequence: a web and a clamour, a hidden affect named, and the speaker bitten. Zarathustra has just turned from the tarantulas to the temple ruins beside their hole, where "the vault and the arch bend and break each other as they wrestle", and drawn the lesson that "struggle and inequality and war for power and supremacy are found even in beauty", and he has told his friends "let us also be enemies", "divinely let us struggle against each other". Then: "Alas! Then the tarantula bit me, my old enemy! Divinely sure and beautiful it bit me on the finger!"

### The remedy: a pillar, not an argument

The tarantula's reasoning is given: "'Punishment and justice must be' – thus it thinks. 'Not for nothing shall he sing his songs in honor of hostility here!'" The bite is the tarantula's own justice applied to the man who praised enmity, and it works: "Yes, it has avenged itself! And alas! Now it will also make my soul whirl with revenge!" The remedy is not an argument. "But so that I do not whirl, my friends, bind me fast to this pillar here! I would rather be a stylite (*Säulen-Heiliger*, a pillar-saint, the ascetic who lives on a column) than a whirlwind of revenge! Indeed, Zarathustra is no tornado or whirlwind; and if he is a dancer, nevermore a tarantella dancer!"

<Aside>
The image assumes a piece of folklore the reader may not have. In Apulia, in the heel of Italy, the bite of the local wolf spider (*Lycosa tarantula*, named after the town of Taranto) was held from the late Middle Ages to produce a frenzy, *tarantism*, curable only by hours of whirling to a fast tune; the tune and the dance are the tarantella. Athanasius Kircher, the Jesuit polymath, printed a specimen of the music, the "Antidotum Tarantulae", in his *Magnes* of 1641, and the belief survived into the twentieth century, when the anthropologist Ernesto de Martino documented its last practitioners in *La terra del rimorso* (1961). The speech's "whirl" (*drehend*) and its closing refusal to be "a tarantella dancer" are that folklore: the bitten dance, and the dance is revenge. Zarathustra's alternative, the pillar, is the one posture in which the bitten do not dance, and the book's own figure for affirmation, the dancer, is kept by the negation, "nevermore a *tarantella* dancer". Whether the pillar is affirmation or a stylite's asceticism is a question the speech leaves standing, and <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">the ascetic-ideal lesson</Ref> is where the course asks what a pillar is for.
</Aside>

### The adder's bite: discharged, not bound

The bite answers a speech of Part I, "On the Adder's Bite", where Zarathustra, bitten in the neck by an adder while asleep, thanks the snake and tells his disciples: "A small revenge is more humane than no revenge at all", and "if you have been done a great wrong, then quickly add five small ones", since "ghastly to behold is a person who suffers a wrong all by himself". The two speeches are one thesis about *ressentiment*: the small revenge discharges the affect, and the man who forgoes it keeps it. That is I:10's Mirabeau, "who had no memory for insults", whose affect "consummates and exhausts itself in an immediate reaction, and therefore does not poison". The adder's bite is discharged in the thanks and the joke; the tarantula's is not discharged at all, and has to be bound.

<KeyFact>
The argued works place one diagnostician inside the sickness he treats, and it is not their narrator. GM III:15's ascetic priest "must himself be sick" to understand the sick, and "to play the physician he must first wound"; the treatise says it of the priest, its subject, and the speech says it of the speaker. That is why the bite is filed under "adds" and against no section of GM I, and why <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the ascetic-priest lesson</Ref>, where III:15 becomes the mechanism by which the slave revolt won, meets in the priest a figure the reader has already seen bitten, in the first person, four years earlier.
</KeyFact>

## "On a Thousand and One Goals": the first coinage at the scale of a people

The tarantula speech stages the second half of the First Treatise, the *ressentiment* half; the speech of Part I that stages the first half is "On a Thousand and One Goals", written five months earlier, and the map for it runs onto I:2 and BGE 260 rather than onto I:10 and I:14. Its subject is where values come from, and its answer is a people. "Many lands Zarathustra saw and many peoples; thus he discovered many peoples' good and evil. No greater market place on earth did Zarathustra find than good and evil." "No people could live that did not first esteem; but if they want to preserve themselves, then they must not esteem as their neighbor esteems." Then the sentence that carries the speech: "A tablet of the good hangs over every people. Observe, it is the tablet of their overcomings; observe, it is the voice of their will to power."

### Four tablets, one law

The speech gives four tablets, each one sentence, each a command that was hard for the people that hung it. "'Always you shall be the first and tower above others: no one shall your jealous soul love, unless it is the friend' – this is what made the soul of a Greek tremble", the first clause being Homer's, the counsel Hippolochus gives his son Glaucus in the sixth book of the *Iliad*. "'Speak the truth and be skilled with the bow and arrow' – this seemed both dear and difficult to the people from whom my name derives", the Persians, whose education Herodotus (I.136) summarised as riding, archery and truth-telling. "'Honor father and mother and comply with their will down to the roots of one's soul' – this tablet of overcoming a different people hung over themselves and became powerful and eternal thereby", the Jews. "'Practice loyalty and for loyalty's sake risk honor and blood even on evil and dangerous things' – teaching themselves thus another people conquered themselves", the Germans.

Each tablet is a self-overcoming, and the speech gives the law that generates them: "Praiseworthy to them is whatever they consider difficult; what is indispensable and difficult, is called good". "Whatever lets them rule and triumph and shine, to the dread and envy of their neighbor, that they consider as the high, the first, the measuring, the meaning of all things." And: "once you discover a people's need and land and sky and neighbor, you guess as well the law of their overcomings". That last sentence is the Doctrine of Types from <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref> applied to a people instead of a person: a constitution (need) and circumstances (land, sky, neighbour) explain a table of values.

### "Man is the esteemer": I:2 as a parable

The speech's general claim follows the four cases. "Indeed, humans gave themselves all of their good and evil. Indeed, they did not take it, they did not find it, it did not fall to them as a voice from heaven. Humans first placed values into things, in order to preserve themselves – they first created meaning for things, a human meaning! That is why they call themselves 'human,' that is: the esteemer (*der Schätzende*). Esteeming is creating (*Schätzen ist Schaffen*): hear me, you creators! Esteeming itself is the treasure and jewel of all esteemed things. Only through esteeming is there value, and without esteeming the nut of existence would be hollow."

The map runs onto the first coinage of I:2 from <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the master-and-slave lesson</Ref>, where the noble "first took the right to create values, to coin the names of values", and "the masters' right of giving names goes so far" that "they say 'this is that and that', they seal every thing and event with a sound and thereby take possession of it". The speech's "whatever lets them rule and triumph and shine, to the dread and envy of their neighbor" is the pathos of distance between peoples rather than between castes; its "voice of their will to power" is the phrase's first appearance in a book Nietzsche published, three years before BGE 260 has the noble type "determine value" and "create values" in the argued register. And the speech's last movement, "First peoples were creators and only later individuals; indeed, the individual himself is still the youngest creation", is the thesis GM II:2 will argue when it calls the sovereign individual "the ripest fruit" of a long breeding; <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs that section.

What the speech does not contain is the whole of the treatise's second half. There is no priest, no *ressentiment*, no inversion; every tablet is a noble tablet, a people's self-affirmation, and "good and evil" is the name of every people's pair. The German is the mark of the difference: the speech says *Gutes und Böses* of every people, Greek and German alike, where the treatise of four years later reserves *böse* for the second coinage and gives the noble contrary its own word, *schlecht*. The asymmetry the master-and-slave lesson built the module on is not in the speech's vocabulary, and a reader who quotes "no greater power did Zarathustra find on earth than the works of the lovers: 'good' and 'evil' are their names" for the treatise's typology has quoted a text in which the typology's one variable does not yet exist. The starred <Ref lesson="key-terms-in-translation">translation chapter</Ref> is where the two contraries are checked across translators; here the point is that the check has nothing to find, because the 1883 text uses one word.

<KeyFact>
The speech brackets the asymmetry's date. HA 45 (1878) and "On a Thousand and One Goals" (January 1883) both have value-creation with one pair for every coiner; BGE 260 (1886) has the two contraries, "good and bad" against "good and evil", "of a different origin", and GM I:10–11 (1887) the order between them. The variable the module runs on entered the published works between 1883 and 1886, which fixes a boundary on citation: a "good and evil" quoted from these speeches is not yet the slave pair, and reading the treatise's *böse* into it dates 1887 back to 1883.
</KeyFact>

The speech's close is the only line that reaches forward past the treatise. "A thousand goals there have been until now, for there have been a thousand peoples. Only the fetters for the thousand necks are still missing, the one goal is missing. Humanity still has no goal." The tablets are peoples' tablets and the speech says no tablet hangs over the species; the Prologue had already named what would: "the overman is the meaning of the earth" (§3). That name is the empty column of the figure above, and <Ref lesson="overman-staged">the overman lesson</Ref> maps the Prologue as this lesson has mapped Part II.

## The verdict: adds, drops, undercuts

The map is complete and the rule's second step files the residue. The application's result is the departure between the two texts, stated as fact about each: what the speech contains that the treatise does not, what the treatise contains that the speech does not, and where the speech's own frame withdraws what its sentences assert.

### Adds: an affective test the treatise states and does not enact

The speech adds three things, and the first is the bite. The treatise identifies *ressentiment* by its history and its products: a caste, an affect defined by the denial of the deed, five relabelings, a document in which the relabeled hatred is legible. The speech identifies it by contact. A diagnostician who names revenge in others is bitten, feels the whirl begin, and has to be bound; the affect is recognised by what it does to the one who touches it. The argued works state that such a test exists: BGE 30's partition of readers by what nourishes and what poisons them, and I:10's own distinction between an affect that "consummates and exhausts itself in an immediate reaction, and therefore does not poison" and one that stays. They never enact it on their narrator. The second addition is the pillar and the dance, affirmation's counter-figure, which the treatise has no place for because the treatise has no speaker who dances. The third is the line with no map at all: "For that mankind be redeemed from revenge: that to me is the bridge to the highest hope and a rainbow after long thunderstorms." GM I diagnoses; it does not promise deliverance, and the speech's promise points at "On Redemption" in the same Part, where revenge is redefined as "the will's ill will toward time and time's 'it was'", the definition <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">the recurrence lesson</Ref> takes up. Heidegger builds his whole reading of the book on that one line of the tarantula speech, "that man be redeemed from revenge", and reads the redefinition metaphysically; the course reads the revenge of this speech as I:10's *ressentiment* and defers the generalisation to <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref>.

### Drops: the history, and the evidence the history carries

The speech drops the whole of the treatise's apparatus of proof, and the cost is stated as the genre's. No priestly caste: the tarantulas are "preachers", and nothing in the speech says which class preached first or why that class and no other could. No dating: I:7's "two-thousand-year history" is absent, and the one historical gesture, "they after all were formerly the best world slanderers and burners of heretics", is a hint at I:16's series, the Church and the Reformation under one name, without the series. No inversion equation, no *bonus* and *schlecht*, and above all no document: the treatise stages its own dialogue in I:14 and then produces Tertullian in I:15, so that a hypothesis about words meets a text in which the words are used; the speech produces the tarantula's confession, which is invented, and stops. A speech of Zarathustra is at the evidential level of I:14 and nowhere higher, and I:14 is a staged dialogue that demonstrates the relabeling's intelligibility and records nothing. What the speech asserts, that equality-preaching is revenge, GM I *claims to have shown*, by a caste, an affect, a mechanism and a specimen; the map's rows all run from an assertion to the section that argues it, and never the reverse.

### Undercuts: the diagnostician bitten, and the frame

The third column is the one the argued works never fill. The *Genealogy*'s narrator stands at the workshop's mouth and posts someone else at the opening ("now I am the one who listens"); I:16 grants that "a higher nature" today is "a battleground" for both valuations, but reports the battle from outside it, and the treatise's narrator reports his own relation to the problem only in the Preface (§3), as a boy of thirteen pursuing the origin of evil. The speech's narrator is bitten by the affect he diagnosed, and asks to be tied up. That is the departure the application records: the argued works claim a distance from *ressentiment* that the drama denies its own speaker, and the drama's frame, "Zarathustra too is a poet", "perhaps he deceived you", extends the denial from the bite to every sentence. A reader who wants the diagnosis has the treatise, argued; a reader who wants to know what the diagnosis costs the one who makes it has only the speech, and the speech says the cost is a bite and a rope.

<Remark title="what the speech can be cited for">
**As a premise.** Nothing. Every claim it makes that the course uses is cited from its argued passage in the map's right-hand column.

**As evidence.** That in 1883, four years before the treatise, the diagnosis was in place without the history: the affect, the relabeling, the drive to punish, and the target's self-description as "the good and the just", all present; the caste, the dating, the etymology and the document all absent. The speech dates the diagnosis, and shows that the history was added to a diagnosis already made.

**For what it adds.** The bite, cited with the frame that undercuts it: the affective test, the pillar, and the promise of deliverance, each marked as the speech's and not the treatise's.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The residue is the result: the speech asserts, four years early, what GM I would argue, adds a test the treatise only states (the bite), drops every piece of the treatise's evidence, and places its own speaker under the diagnosis, which no argued text of Nietzsche's does. The one thing it shares with the treatise without adding or dropping is the gap: neither text says how the powerless came to rule.
</KeyFact>

## What is established, and what the Second Treatise inherits

What is established: *Zarathustra* is read by a rule, a speech is testimony from a character, mapped sentence by sentence onto argued passages with the residue filed as adds, drops and undercuts, and the book states the rule about itself in "lose me and find yourselves" and "Zarathustra too is a poet"; the map's direction runs against the dates, since both speeches (1883) precede the treatise (1887) by four years, and the rule survives because it is a relation between texts, with the later text cited wherever they disagree; "On the Tarantulas" maps nine of eleven lines onto GM I:7, I:10, I:13, I:14, BGE 44, 202 and 257 and *Twilight*'s fourth error, and two lines, deliverance from revenge and the bite, onto nothing; "humans are not equal" is a denial of the tarantulas' premise, qualified by the overman, spoken by a character bitten in the next paragraph, and the argued passage a political reading must answer is BGE 257, not the speech; "On a Thousand and One Goals" stages the first coinage of I:2 at the scale of a people, in a vocabulary (*gut und böse* for every tablet) the treatise would split, with no priest and no *ressentiment*, and its missing "one goal" points at the Prologue's overman; and the verdict stands in three columns, the bite added, the history dropped, the speaker undercut.

What is not established, in the speech or in the treatise, is how the tarantulas ever won. The speech ends with Zarathustra bound to a pillar and the preachers still preaching; I:16 ends with the fight "undecided". The slave-revolt lesson stated the missing premise: for the inversion to become Europe's, the strong themselves must come to hold the values that condemn them, and nothing in GM I supplies a site in the strong where a No could lodge. <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">The next lesson</Ref> opens the Second Treatise at that site's prehistory: the breeding of an animal that may promise, the morality of custom, and the sovereign individual of II:2, who is either the naturalist reading's first strain or a type-fact with a proud name.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
Three lines of "On the Tarantulas" were left off the map. For each, give the argued passage by book and section, or "none" with a reason: (a) "What is silent in the father learns to speak in the son; and often I found the son to be the father's exposed secret"; (b) "Their jealousy even leads them along the thinkers' path; and this is the mark of their jealousy – they always go too far, such that their exhaustion must ultimately lay itself to sleep in snow"; (c) "they after all were formerly the best world slanderers and burners of heretics".

<Solution>
**(a) BGE 264.** The aphorism holds that what a person's ancestors "most liked to do and did most constantly" cannot be erased from the soul, and that the "problem of race" in this sense is a problem of inheritance of drives; the speech's line is that claim as a family scene. The map records it as a type-fact about inheritance in the Doctrine of Types' sense, asserted in both texts and argued in neither.

**(b) None in the First Treatise.** GM I says nothing about *ressentiment* among thinkers; the nearest argued passage is BGE 6, that every great philosophy so far has been "the personal confession of its author and a kind of involuntary and unconscious memoir", which supplies the general thesis (a philosophy expresses its author's drives) but not this instance (that envy is what drives some thinkers "too far"). The line is cited for nothing; the Third Treatise's account of the scholar, in <Ref lesson="will-to-truth">the will-to-truth lesson</Ref>, is the place where the course asks whether an argued instance exists.

**(c) GM I:16 and BGE 202.** The line says that today's preachers of equality are the same type as yesterday's persecutors under a different name, which is I:16's series from <Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref>: the Church, the Reformation and the Revolution as "Judea" under three names, and BGE 202's "the democratic movement is the heir to Christianity" as the series' modern term. The speech has the identity without the series; the map runs the line onto the two sections that state the series.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
"'Humans are not equal' in 'On the Tarantulas' is Nietzsche's political programme." Break the claim from the text: quote the sentence's object, its qualifier, and the frame passage that governs it; then state what BGE 257 does assert in the argued register and why that, and not the speech, is the passage a political reading must answer.

<Solution>
**The object.** The sentence answers the tarantulas' "will to equality", which the speech has diagnosed two paragraphs earlier as "the tyrant's madness of impotence" crying for equality; "humans are not equal" denies the premise of a valuation the speech has just called revenge. It is introduced by "I do not want to be mixed in with and mistaken for these preachers of equality", so its grammatical work is to separate the speaker from a type.

**The qualifier.** "And they shouldn't become so either! What would my love for the overman be if I spoke otherwise?" The inequality asserted is a difference of type with the overman as its limit, and the "war and inequality" of the next sentence is glossed as a contest of valuations, "good and evil, and rich and poor ... all the names of values: they shall be weapons and clanging signs that life must overcome itself again and again". No arrangement of persons is prescribed.

**The frame.** The sentence is spoken by a character whom the same speech has bitten and tied to a pillar before it ends, and whose book says of him "Zarathustra too is a poet" ("On Poets") and "perhaps he deceived you" ("On the Bestowing Virtue" §3). By the dramatization rule the speech is testimony and cannot be a premise; the claim cites it as one.

**The step that fails.** The inference from "a character asserts an inequality of type" to "the author prescribes an inequality of persons": it drops the object, the qualifier and the frame, which is three deletions from one paragraph.

**What must be answered instead.** BGE 257, in the argued register: "every enhancement so far in the type 'man' has been the work of an aristocratic society", which "believes in a long ladder of rank order and value distinctions between men, and in some sense needs slavery", because "the pathos of distance" between stations is the precondition of "that other, more mysterious pathos", the demand for distance "within the soul itself". That is a stated premise with a stated ground, an order of rank between persons as the causal precondition of an order of rank within a soul, and a political reading has to engage it; the master-and-slave lesson flagged it as a premise the First Treatise does not use, and <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref> is where it is examined. Quoting the tarantula speech instead of BGE 257 is choosing the text that cannot be a premise over the one that can.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
"On a Thousand and One Goals" contains no *ressentiment*. From GM I:2 and I:10, derive what the speech would have to add in order to stage the whole First Treatise rather than its first half, and write the missing paragraph's premises (numbered, in words; not its prose).

<Solution>
The speech already contains I:2's content: peoples coin their tablets out of self-affirmation ("whatever lets them rule and triumph and shine"), the contrary of each tablet is what a neighbour esteems, and the coinage is creation ("esteeming is creating"). What it lacks is I:10's second coinage, and I:10 fixes what a second coinage needs. The missing paragraph's premises:

**P1.** Within a people, not only between peoples, there is a class that cannot act on its tablet: denied "the real reaction, that of the deed" (I:10). The speech has only whole peoples as esteemers; the treatise's second coinage needs an inside.

**P2.** That class does not stop esteeming; its esteeming turns outward first, to a No against those who can act (I:10: "from the outset says No to an 'outside'"), and its own "good" is derived from the No.

**P3.** The No takes the form of a tablet: what the class cannot do receives the name of a virtue, and what the ruling class does receives the name "evil" (I:10's "the evil enemy" as fundamental concept; I:14's relabeling).

**P4.** The two tablets that result are not two neighbours' tablets but two orders of coinage, "good" first against "evil" first (the asymmetry of I:10–11), which the speech's single pair *gut und böse* cannot express; the paragraph would need the treatise's second contrary, *schlecht*, or a periphrasis for it.

**C.** A people's "good and evil" can be the work of its lovers and creators, as the speech says, or the work of those who could not love or create and coined "evil" first; and the second is what Europe's tablet is.

The derivation shows what the map showed: the speech is the first coinage's parable, and to become the whole treatise it would need a class inside a people, a direction of the glance, a relabeling, and a second word for "bad". The last of these is the one the 1883 text could not have supplied, since the *schlecht*/*böse* distinction enters with I:4–5 in 1887.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Suppose the bite were removed from "On the Tarantulas", so that the speech ended at "Divinely let us struggle against each other!" What would the speech then assert that GM I does not, and what would it lose? State whether the bite is an argument, evidence, or a diagnostic, and what each answer would mean for citing the speech.

<Solution>
**Without the bite.** The speech would end on the temple ruins: struggle and inequality "found even in beauty", "let us also be enemies". It would then assert, beyond GM I, a positive valuation of contest as such, enmity as the form of life's self-overcoming, which the treatise nowhere states in its own voice (the nearest argued passage is BGE 257's "self-overcoming of man", and that is about rank, not enmity). It would lose the speech's one sentence that the argued works cannot supply, the speaker's own infection, and with it the frame's force: a speech that ends in a divine struggle is a speech by a speaker above the struggle, which is exactly the position "Zarathustra too is a poet" denies him.

**What the bite is.** Not an argument: it has no premises and its conclusion (bind me) is a request. Not evidence: it is an event in a parable, invented, at the level of I:14's staged dialogue and not of I:15's document. A *diagnostic*: it performs the test the adds column filed, identification of the affect by contact rather than by history, which the argued works state (BGE 30; I:10's "does not poison") and never run on their own narrator.

**What follows for citation.** If it were an argument, the speech could be cited as a premise, which the rule forbids. If it were evidence, it could be cited to corroborate I:10, which would be to corroborate a treatise with a parable written four years earlier by the same author. As a diagnostic it is cited under "adds" and only there: for the claim that Nietzsche staged the diagnostician's own liability to the affect, a claim about the drama and not about the history, and always with the undercut beside it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The tarantulas' "against everything that has power we shall raise our clamor" has two candidate argued counterparts: BGE 202's "religion of pity" paragraph and BGE 44's "levelers". Compare the three texts as sources by the course's sourcing drill: which names its target, which gives a mechanism, and which one the course may cite for the claim that equality-preaching is *ressentiment*.

<Solution>
**Metadata.** The speech: Part II of *Zarathustra*, 1883, a character addressing a spider in parable; no target named. BGE 44: 1886, an aphorism in Nietzsche's voice on the misuse of the name "free spirit"; target named as "the levelers", "in all the countries of Europe, and in America as well", "scribbling slaves of the democratic taste and its 'modern ideas'". BGE 202: 1886, an aphorism in Nietzsche's voice on European morality; targets named as "the democratic movement", "the anarchist dogs", the "socialists" who "want a 'free society'", and the general description "the morality of herd animals".

**Records / argues / omits.** The speech records a creed in the tarantulas' mouths and argues, by parable, that it is revenge; it omits every name and every mechanism beyond "impotence". BGE 44 records a type (levelers who abuse the name of free spirit) and argues that they are "un-free and ridiculously superficial"; it gives no mechanism for the levelling, only its description. BGE 202 records a set of movements and argues that they are one instinct, "the instinct of the herd animal man", with a mechanism: a religion "that indulged and flattered the loftiest herd desires" produced a morality now "increasingly apparent in even political and social institutions", so that "the democratic movement is the heir to Christianity"; it omits the affect, saying "herd instinct" and "pity" where the speech says "revenge".

**Use.** For the claim that equality-preaching *is* *ressentiment*, none of the three suffices: the speech has the affect and no argument, BGE 44 has a target and no affect, BGE 202 has a target and a descent and names the affect as herd instinct and pity rather than revenge. The claim is argued only where GM I:10 (the affect defined), I:14 (the relabeling) and BGE 202 (the descent to the present) are cited together; the speech may be cited beside them for the affect's name and for what it adds, and for nothing else. The exercise's answer is the map's: three sources, three different things each can establish, and the treatise as the only one that argues.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Del Caro renders *Rache* as "revenge" and *Gleichheit* as "equality" throughout the speech. Check, by the translation chapter's four-column method or from the German given in this lesson, whether "vengefulness" for *Rachsucht* or "sameness" for *Gleichheit* would alter the map to GM I:10 and BGE 202, and state what the German *gleich* does that English "equal" does not.

<Solution>
**Rache / Rachsucht.** The speech uses both: *Rache* (revenge, the act or its wish) in "revenge sits in your soul" and "the bridge to the highest hope", and *Rachsüchtige* / *Rachsucht* (the vengeful, vengefulness as a craving, with the *-sucht* suffix the translation chapter read in *Herrschsucht*) in "hidden avengers" and "whirlwind of revenge". Del Caro's "revenge" for both flattens a distinction the map uses: I:10's *ressentiment* is the *craving* kept and not discharged, which is *Rachsucht*, while the discharged act, *Rache*, is what Mirabeau has and what "On the Adder's Bite" recommends in small doses. Rendering the two words as "revenge" and "vengefulness" would sharpen the map to I:10 (the tarantulas' *Rachsucht* is *ressentiment*; the adder's *Rache* is its discharge) and change nothing in the map to BGE 202, which names neither.

**Gleichheit.** *Gleich* means "equal" and also "same", "like", "identical": *dasselbe* and *das Gleiche* both render "the same", and the eternal recurrence is *die ewige Wiederkunft des Gleichen*, "of the same", the phrase <Ref lesson="eternal-recurrence">the recurrence lesson</Ref> reads. "Will to equality" (*Wille zur Gleichheit*) therefore carries "will to sameness" in the German, which "equality" in English, a word of legal and political register, does not; and "humans are not equal" (*die Menschen sind nicht gleich*) reads in German as "humans are not alike", a claim of type before it is a claim of status. That reading strengthens the map to BGE 202, whose herd instinct wants "the autonomous herd" and rejects "any special claims", and it strengthens the refutation in the second exercise: the sentence denies sameness of type, which is what the pathos of distance asserts, and "equality" imports the political sense the political reading needs. Del Caro's choice is not wrong, since the tarantulas' *Gleichheit* is also a political demand; but a reader working from the English alone cannot see that the word which returns in the book's deepest thought, *das Gleiche*, is the word the tarantulas want.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The tarantula speech ends with deliverance from revenge as "the bridge to the highest hope" and says nothing about how the powerless ever came to rule. Write the question the First Treatise leaves open in one sentence, and list what a mechanism answering it would need, given that the speech's tarantulas can only clamour and the treatise's strong can only ignore.

<Solution>
**The question.** How did a valuation coined by those who could not act become the valuation of those who could, so that the strong now hold the values that condemn them?

**What a mechanism would need.** (1) A site *in the strong themselves* where a No could lodge: a valuation the strong can crush from outside cannot be what defeated them, so the mechanism must act from inside, which neither the tarantulas' clamour nor the priests' cleverness does. (2) A condition under which the strong suffer, since a valuation that relabels incapacity as merit has purchase only on someone who has an incapacity to relabel. (3) An agent who can turn the sufferer's affect in a new direction, from "someone is to blame" to "I am to blame", since the No of I:10 is aimed outward and the strong are not outside themselves. (4) A concept the turn can use, a debt owed and unpayable, which is guilt.

**Where the course finds it.** Item (1) is GM II:16's bad conscience, the cruelty of an animal enclosed by society turned inward, which creates inside the strong the site the new ideal occupies; items (3) and (4) are GM III:15's ascetic priest, "the one who changes the direction of *ressentiment*", and GM II:4–22's debt. <Ref lesson="promise-and-sovereign-individual">The next lesson</Ref> begins the Second Treatise at the prehistory of that site, the breeding of an animal with a memory of its will, and the reader arrives holding the question this exercise wrote.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

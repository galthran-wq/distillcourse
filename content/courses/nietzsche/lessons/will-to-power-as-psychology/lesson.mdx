Most of what a living thing does keeps it alive, so Darwin's readers took survival to be its aim, and Schopenhauer took every want to aim at the relief of being met; why, then, does a climber take the north face when a cable car runs to the summit, and what is a living thing after if neither survival nor relief explains it? Charles Darwin is the English naturalist whose theory of evolution by natural selection (1859) made "the struggle for existence" a phrase every educated European used. Schopenhauer is the philosopher of the <Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-world-from-inside-one-blind-will">will to live</Ref>, the blind striving that, on his account, every living thing is; that will never stops wanting, and each single want aims at the relief of being met (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-pendulum">step 3 of the pendulum</Ref>). So both answers name the aim of a living thing as a *state*: for Darwin's readers, staying alive; for Schopenhauer, the end of a want. The climber's choice fits neither. The cable car is the safer way up, and it delivers the same summit with no pain at all.

The question comes straight out of the last lesson. <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">No doer behind the deed</Ref> removed the self that was supposed to stand behind the drives and move them, and was left with what does move them: GS 360's "quantum of dammed-up energy that is waiting to be used up somehow", the steam rather than the helmsman. It also found GM I 13 describing strength as "a desire to overcome, a desire to throw down, a desire to become master, a thirst for enemies and resistances and triumphs". If the drives are not moved by a self, what do they want?

The answer matters for more than psychology. Schopenhauer's verdict that existence is worse than non-existence rested on that same step 3, that getting what you want only ends a pain. And <Ref lesson="case-of-wagner" id="the-gap">*The Case of Wagner*</Ref> condemned an art by a standard, ascending life against declining life, that the pamphlet asserted without saying what "ascending" consists in. Both need an account of what a living thing is after.

Two things make the answer hard to assess. "Will to power" is the most quoted phrase Nietzsche wrote and the most misread: it sounds like a lust to rule other people, and that is how it is usually repeated. And any principle that claims to explain what every living thing wants risks explaining nothing, because any act at all can be redescribed in its terms. So the questions are what exactly the claim says, why it should be preferred to survival and to the will to live, what "power" covers, whether it can be tested, and how far it is meant to reach.

## "Life itself is will to power": the claim and its terms

The claim has one compact published statement, a section of *Beyond Good and Evil* (BGE, 1886) that <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="the-argument-in-six-steps">the society of drives</Ref> already used for its last sentence, the rule of economy.

<Quote source="BGE 13, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Die Physiologen sollten sich besinnen, den Selbsterhaltungstrieb als kardinalen Trieb eines organischen Wesens anzusetzen. Vor Allem will etwas Lebendiges seine Kraft auslassen – Leben selbst ist Wille zur Macht –: die Selbsterhaltung ist nur eine der indirekten und häufigsten Folgen davon. – Kurz, hier wie überall, Vorsicht vor überflüssigen teleologischen Principien! – wie ein solches der Selbsterhaltungstrieb ist (man dankt ihn der Inconsequenz Spinoza's –). So nämlich gebietet es die Methode, die wesentlich Principien-Sparsamkeit sein muss.
</Original>

Physiologists should think twice before positing the drive of self-preservation as the cardinal drive of an organic being. A living thing wants above all to *discharge* its strength — life itself is *will to power* —: self-preservation is only one of the indirect and most frequent *results* of this. In short, here as everywhere, let us beware of *superfluous* teleological principles! — one of which is the drive of self-preservation (we owe it to Spinoza's inconsistency). So method commands, which must be essentially economy of principles.
</Quote>

### What BGE 13 says, term by term

The section is addressed to *physiologists*, the life scientists of the 1880s, so the claim is first of all about organisms. A *cardinal* drive is the one the others are supposed to derive from, and the target is the view that this root drive is the drive to stay alive. Spinoza, the seventeenth-century Dutch philosopher met in <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="understanding-as-a-treaty-among-drives">the society of drives</Ref>, had banished purposes from nature and then made each thing's striving to persevere in its being the very essence of the thing. To Nietzsche that striving is a purpose let back in, hence "inconsistency".

"Discharge its strength" (*seine Kraft auslassen*) is the physiological vocabulary of the day: a living thing as a store of force that has to be spent, which is what GS 360's "dammed-up energy" described. Survival is then a *result*, "indirect and most frequent": an organism that spends its strength in growing, feeding and fighting usually stays alive in the process, but staying alive is not what it is doing, and when spending its strength and staying alive conflict, it does not always choose to stay alive.

The last sentence gives the reason to prefer this to self-preservation: economy. If one principle explains both why living things usually survive and why they sometimes risk and spend their lives, a second principle of self-preservation is "superfluous".

### One principle under the drives

The drives of <Ref lesson="self-as-society-of-drives" id="four-marks-of-a-drive">the society of drives</Ref> had four marks, and the fourth was that each "wants to rule": BGE 6 called every drive "domineering", wanting to be "lord over all the other drives". BGE 19 found inside every act of will "the affect of command", the feeling of superiority over what must obey, which is what people experience as freedom (<Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed" id="three-ingredients-and-one-mistake">what willing is</Ref>). BGE 13 names what those observations have in common. Each drive is a particular way of spending strength, curiosity on problems, combativeness on rivals, and what all of them want, besides their own objects, is to prevail.

### "Power" means resistance overcome

"Power" needs content before the claim can be judged. The German *Macht* covers both capacity ("it is in my power") and mastery ("the powers that be"). A case shows which side of the word carries the claim.

Go back to the climber of the opening question. She takes the north face, a day of cold, fear and exhausting work, with a real chance of dying, though the cable car on the far side would put her on the same summit safely, sooner and without pain. What she wants is the summit *by the route that resists*.

Two features of the case matter. First, she has to care about the summit. A wall climbed for no reason would be only a wall; the face is resistance because it stands between her and something she wants. Second, the resistance is part of what she wants: take it away, by putting her on the cable car, and something she was after is gone even though the summit is reached. So the want has two layers: a want for some end, and a want to pursue that end against resistance and overcome it.

The earliest form of the idea in the books is *Daybreak* (D, 1881), which speaks again and again of the *feeling of power* (*Machtgefühl*) and never yet of a "will to power":

<Quote source="D 262, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
The demon of power. — Not necessity, not desire — no, the love of power is the demon of men. Let them have everything — health, food, a place to live, entertainment — they are and remain unhappy and low-spirited: for the demon waits and waits and will be satisfied. Take everything from them and satisfy this, and they are almost happy — as happy as men and demons can be.
</Quote>

The phrase "will to power" itself first appears in print two years later, in *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (Z, 1883): "A tablet of the good hangs over every people. Behold, it is the tablet of their overcomings; behold, it is the voice of their will to power" (Z I 15). What a people calls good, on this line, is what it finds hard and has managed.

<Aside title="The cavalry legend">
Nietzsche's sister later told how the idea came to him in 1870, when, as a medical orderly in the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71, which ended in the founding of the German Empire), he watched an exhausted Prussian regiment charge past and understood that life is not a struggle for survival but a will to power. Nothing in his books or notes supports the story. His notes do not use the phrase until about ten years later, and when they first do, they reject it. The published record runs from Daybreak's "feeling of power" (1881) through the first appearance in Zarathustra (1883) to BGE 13 (1886).
</Aside>

### The definition in the last books

The plainest definition comes in *The Antichrist* (A, written 1888), whose second section is a set of definitions followed by two consequences. It is quoted whole, because the consequences belong to it:

<Quote source="A 2, trans. W. Kaufmann">
What is good? Everything that heightens the feeling of power in man, the will to power, power itself.

What is bad? Everything that is born of weakness.

What is happiness? The feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome.

Not contentedness but more power; not peace but war; not virtue but fitness (Renaissance virtue, *virtù*, virtue that is moraline-free).

The weak and the failures shall perish: first principle of *our* love of man. And they shall even be given every possible assistance.

What is more harmful than any vice? Active pity for all the failures and all the weak: Christianity.
</Quote>

The third line is the climber's case as a definition. Happiness is not a state reached but a direction of change, power *growing*, and it is defined by an obstacle: resistance being overcome. That is why the next line refuses "contentedness" and "peace": they are the states in which nothing resists any more. *Virtù* is the Italian of the Renaissance for prowess, a man's effectiveness at what he undertakes, with no moral content, and "moraline-free" is coined like "nicotine-free".

The fifth line means what it says. "The failures" renders *die Missrathenen*, those who turned out badly, the ill-constituted. The German of the next sentence, *Und man soll ihnen noch dazu helfen*, says that one should even help them to it, help them to perish; the translation's "every possible assistance" is that help. The two lines apply the first definition: if good is what heightens power, then what keeps the failing alive against the sorting that life itself does is harmful, and the last line names the practice that does it, pity, with Christianity as its religion. Why Nietzsche calls pity harmful is argued in <Ref lesson="against-pity">against pity</Ref>.

The other half of the standard is the A 6 sentence that <Ref lesson="nihilism" id="decadence-the-weakness-behind-passive-nihilism">nihilism</Ref> used to define decadence, declining life: "where the will to power is lacking there is decline." This is the content the standard of ascending and declining life was missing in *The Case of Wagner*: ascending life is will to power growing, declining life is will to power failing. It is also the assumption under the Lenzer Heide note's "nothing to life that has value except the degree of power — assuming, precisely, that life itself is the will to power". So the valuations of the late books stand on the psychology, and the psychology is what has to be tested.

<Remark title="Will to power">
*Will to power* is the drive in a living thing to discharge its strength and to grow by overcoming resistance in pursuing whatever it pursues: "the feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome" (A 2) is its satisfaction. It wants an end, and it wants to reach that end against resistance; its satisfaction lies in the *overcoming of resistance*, an activity, not a state reached afterwards. Self-preservation and pleasure are among its results, not its aims (BGE 13).
</Remark>

## Against self-preservation: overflow, not distress

The obvious rival is older and better established. Most of what animals and people do keeps them alive, and the science of Nietzsche's day had just given that fact its most powerful explanation.

### What Darwin's theory said, and what was read out of it

Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) argued that every species produces more offspring than can survive, so that its members are in a constant "struggle for existence"; individuals whose small variations help them survive and breed leave more descendants, and over many generations the species changes. Darwin took the idea of the struggle from Thomas Malthus, an English economist who had argued in 1798 that populations always grow faster than their food supply. Strictly, the theory is about which variants spread over generations, not about what an individual animal wants. The psychology Nietzsche attacks is the one his contemporaries read out of it, and found already in Spinoza: that the basic aim of every living thing is to keep itself alive.

<Figure src="./figures/darwin-portrait.jpg" float="right" width="s" alt="Black-and-white studio photograph, head and chest, of Charles Darwin as an old man: a bald domed head, heavy white eyebrows over deep-set eyes, and a full white beard falling to his chest. He wears a dark heavy coat, turns slightly to the right and looks straight at the camera. The edges fade into a white vignette." caption="Darwin in the later 1870s, twenty years after the Origin, when 'struggle for existence' had become a slogan far beyond biology." />

*The Gay Science* (GS), in the fifth book Nietzsche added in 1887, answers it head on:

<Quote source="GS 349, trans. W. Kaufmann">
The wish to preserve oneself is the symptom of a condition of distress, of a limitation of the really fundamental instinct of life which aims at the *expansion of power* and, wishing for that, frequently risks and even sacrifices self-preservation. It should be considered symptomatic when some philosophers — for example, Spinoza who was consumptive — considered the instinct of self-preservation decisive and had to see it that way; for they were individuals in conditions of distress. … in nature it is not conditions of distress that are *dominant* but overflow and squandering, even to the point of absurdity. The struggle for existence is only an *exception*, a temporary restriction of the will to life. The great and small struggle always revolves around superiority, around growth and expansion, around power — in accordance with the will to power which is the will of life.
</Quote>

"The will to life" here is not Schopenhauer's will to live, the blind wish to go on existing that *Zarathustra* will reject below as the "will to existence". It is Nietzsche's own name for the will that life has, and the last sentence says what that will is: the will to power.

### The argument in five steps

1. If the basic aim of a living thing were to stay alive, then once it was safe and fed it would economize: spend as little strength as it could and avoid every risk.
2. Living things do not do this. Nature shows "overflow and squandering": plants put out far more seed than could ever take root, animals play, display and fight beyond any need, and human beings climb mountains, fight duels and die for causes.
3. A principle that explains the economy of hard times *and* the squandering of good ones is better than one that explains the first and needs exceptions for the second; that is BGE 13's economy.
4. "Discharge strength, grow, overcome" explains both. In good times strength is spent on growth and on resistance sought out; in hard times the same drive is restricted to the narrow aim of not dying, and survival comes out as a by-product of the spending.
5. So "the struggle for existence is only an exception, a temporary restriction of the will to life", and self-preservation, far from being the root, is what will to power looks like under pressure.

GS 349's other remarks, that Spinoza was consumptive and that "English Darwinism breathes something like the musty air of English overpopulation", explain why someone might believe the rival theory; as <Ref lesson="death-of-god" id="discrediting-is-a-genetic-fallacy">the death of God</Ref> put it, an account of where a belief comes from can make it superfluous only if the belief has no independent support, and the Darwinian theory has a great deal. The argument has to stand on step 2, the empirical step.

A Darwinian has a reply ready. Overproduction is not evidence against the theory; it is the theory's premise, the reason there is a struggle at all. Seed far beyond what can take root is how a plant gets a few seedlings through; display wins mates, and play rehearses the hunts and fights of adult life. Each has an account in terms of reproduction. But the target of GS 349 is not the theory of descent; it is the psychology read out of it, that an individual living thing is basically after keeping itself alive. Against that psychology the evidence is the last item on the list: risk that buys neither survival nor offspring. The climber on the north face while the cable car runs, the duellist risking death over a word, the martyr who could recant and dies childless for a doctrine are spending life itself on something that is neither more life nor more descendants, and a creature whose basic aim was to stay alive would do none of these things.

So step 2 does not touch natural selection, and the seeds and the play prove less than GS 349 implies; what it does establish, for human beings at least, is that self-preservation is not what a living thing is basically after.

<KeyFact>
GS 349 reverses the order of explanation: the wish to stay alive is not the root of life's striving but its symptom under distress, the shape will to power takes when it is hemmed in. A psychology built on self-preservation is a psychology of restricted life generalized to all life. The same reversal is later turned on morality: the values of those who must make do are values of distress taken for the values of life (<Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave revolt</Ref>).
</KeyFact>

### Where the struggle happens, the weak win

*Twilight of the Idols* (TI, 1888) repeats the point under the heading "Anti-Darwin" and adds a prediction of its own:

<Quote source="TI, 'Skirmishes of an Untimely Man' 14, trans. W. Kaufmann">
As for the famous "struggle for existence," so far it seems to me to be asserted rather than proved. It occurs, but as an exception; the total appearance of life is not the extremity, not starvation, but rather riches, profusion, even absurd squandering — and where there is struggle, it is a struggle for *power*. One should not mistake Malthus for nature.

Assuming, however, that there is such a struggle for existence — and, indeed, it occurs — its result is unfortunately the opposite of what Darwin's school desires, and of what one might perhaps *desire* with them — namely, in favor of the strong, the privileged, the fortunate exceptions. The species do *not* grow in perfection: the weak prevail over the strong again and again, for they are the great majority — and they are also more intelligent. Darwin forgot the spirit (that is English!); the weak have more spirit. … It will be noted that by "spirit" I mean care, patience, cunning, simulation, great self-control, and everything that is mimicry (the latter includes a great deal of so-called virtue).
</Quote>

Where survival really is at stake, the advantage goes to "spirit": care, patience, cunning, the virtues of those who cannot afford a direct fight. That is will to power in a restricted form, prevailing by indirection. How the weak, prevailing in this way, came to impose their values on Europe is the slave revolt's subject.

## Against the will to live: what satisfaction is

Schopenhauer's will to live is not the Darwinian drive to stay alive; it is a blind striving with no goal beyond going on. His case against life rested on what the striving gets when it succeeds.

### The pendulum's third step, denied

<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-pendulum">The pendulum</Ref> ran in five steps to a verdict: (1) one blind will; (2) every want is a lack, felt as pain; (3) getting what you want only removes the pain and adds nothing; (4) with nothing left to want comes boredom; (5) a new want arrives, and life swings between pain and boredom; (6) so existence is worse than non-existence. In 1872 *The Birth of Tragedy* kept steps 2 to 5 and replaced the standard that turned them into the verdict. One would expect the mature Nietzsche to go on arguing at the level of standards. The will to power does something else: it denies step 3, a premise *The Birth of Tragedy* never questioned.

The ground for the denial is the case an exercise at the end of <Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy">*Schopenhauer and The Birth of Tragedy*</Ref> raised: a prisoner in a cell, bored for weeks, though he wants one thing badly, to get out. Step 4 says boredom is having nothing to want, and the prisoner has a want. What he lacks is something to *do* about it: no resistance he can work against. The reverse case is as familiar: a person absorbed in something difficult, a hard problem or a long climb, is neither in pain nor bored, though the want is unmet the whole time. Set out as an argument against step 3:

1. Schopenhauer: satisfaction is the removal of a want's pain, so life swings between pain and boredom.
2. Boredom also comes while a want is unmet but cannot be worked on (the cell), and disappears while something difficult is pursued.
3. So what is wanted is not only the end state but the activity of overcoming obstacles in pursuit of it (A 2: "resistance is overcome").
4. That activity contains displeasure, the felt resistance, and is sought anyway.
5. So the pendulum's step 3 misdescribes desire: the satisfaction was in the pursuing, and the end state is where it stops.

A notebook of spring 1888, which Nietzsche never published, states the fourth of these steps outright:

<Quote source="Notebook 14[174], spring 1888, unpublished, trans. K. Sturge, p. 263">
Man does *not* seek pleasure and does *not* avoid unpleasure: it will be clear which famous prejudice I am contradicting here. Pleasure and unpleasure are mere consequences, mere accompanying phenomena — what man wants, what every smallest part of a living organism wants, is an increment of power. Striving for this gives rise to both pleasure and unpleasure; out of that will man seeks resistance, needs something to oppose him. Unpleasure, as an inhibition of his will to power, is thus a normal fact, the normal ingredient of everything that happens in the organic world, and man does not avoid it but instead has constant need of it: every conquest, every pleasurable feeling, everything that happens presupposes a resistance overcome.
</Quote>

A neighbouring note in the same notebook puts the point about pleasure in five words: "pleasure accompanies, it doesn't set in motion" (notebook 14[121]).

### "Not will to life but will to power"

*Zarathustra* gives the answer to Schopenhauer in the chapter "On Self-Overcoming", where Life itself speaks to the prophet. The book is parable, so the passage illustrates rather than argues, but it names its opponent:

<Quote source="Z II 12, 'On Self-Overcoming', trans. W. Kaufmann">
Where I found the living, there I found will to power; and even in the will of those who serve I found the will to be master. …

And life itself confided this secret to me: "Behold," it said, "I am *that which must always overcome itself*. Indeed, you call it a will to procreate or a drive to an end, to something higher, farther, more manifold: but all this is one, and one secret. …

"Whatever I create and however much I love it — soon I must oppose it and my love; thus my will wills it. …

"Indeed, the truth was not hit by him who shot at it with the word of the 'will to existence': that will does not exist. For, what does not exist cannot will; but what is in existence, how could that still want existence? Only where there is life is there also will: not will to life but — thus I teach you — will to power."
</Quote>

The "will to life" rejected here is Schopenhauer's "will to existence", the striving to go on being, not the "will to life" of GS 349, which was will to power under another name. The argument inside the image is short. A will to existence would want what it already has; the living, being alive, cannot want aliveness as something lacking. What it can want is more: more reach, more strength, the next resistance. "I am that which must always overcome itself" names the result of the first section's definition: if satisfaction is overcoming, every success removes the resistance it consisted in, and the striving has to find the next one, including in what it made itself ("soon I must oppose it and my love").

### The paradox: every victory ends the game

The loop has a cost the picture makes visible. If satisfaction is the overcoming, then winning destroys the condition of being satisfied: the summit reached is no longer a summit to be reached. Zarathustra says it in a line of the long chapter "On Old and New Tablets": "Alas, who was not vanquished in his victory?" (Z III 12, §30).

Sport has a motto that shows both sides: "it is not the winning but the taking part that counts". From outside the game it is true: what someone choosing whether to play wants is the playing. From inside it is false: nobody really takes part who does not care about winning, because caring about winning is what playing consists in. So a player wants victory, and victory ends the thing he wanted.

The paradox looks like Schopenhauer's boredom returning: every success ends in a lack. The difference is in what the lack means. For Schopenhauer the restlessness of wanting is the indictment of life, because a satisfaction that is only relief can never add up to a good. For the will to power the restlessness is the form satisfaction takes: the good was in the overcoming, it was had while it lasted, and the response to victory is a harder aim, not a void. A 2 had already refused "contentedness".

<KeyFact>
Denying step 3 takes away the pessimist's premise; it does not prove that life is worth living. A life that finds no resistance it can work against, the prisoner's in his cell, is as bad on this account as on Schopenhauer's. The question moves from whether wanting can ever add a good to whether a given life finds resistances it can overcome.
</KeyFact>

### Four behaviours, three predictions

The two comparisons can be put side by side. Each principle predicts what a living thing will do in a given situation; where the predictions agree, the case decides nothing, and where they part, it is evidence.

Eating when hungry is predicted by all three, which is why the survival theory looks so strong: most of daily life is the "most frequent result". The north face and the martyr are cases in which strength is spent against resistance that safety and comfort would avoid, and only will to power predicts them.

The ascetic needs more care, because Schopenhauer has his own account of him. In <Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-verdict-and-the-way-out">Schopenhauer's verdict</Ref> the ascetic's self-denial was the lasting cure: a will that has seen through to its own misery and stops wanting. So the bare fact of fasting does not decide between the two. What decides is how the fast is held. A will quieted by insight should grow indifferent, wanting less as it goes, so that the fast gets easier. A will to power should enjoy the torment and look for harder fasts, more resistance to overcome. The ascetic Nietzsche describes is the second kind, with "an unspeakable happiness at the sight of torment" (D 113, read whole in the next section). *The Gay Science* had said so of the martyr already in 1882: "Even if we offer our lives, as martyrs do for their church, this is a sacrifice that is offered for our desire for power or for the purpose of preserving our feeling of power. Those who feel 'I possess Truth' — how many possessions would they not abandon in order to save this feeling!" (GS 13).

## Mastery over others, mastery over oneself: what the word covers

The definition, overcoming resistance in pursuing an end, says nothing about whose resistance. That is where the popular reading goes wrong, and where its usual correction goes wrong in the opposite direction.

### The correct statement first

Will to power is the drive to overcome resistance, and the resistance can be another person, a problem, a material, a rival drive, or oneself. Mastery over others is one form, named often and at length; mastery over oneself is another; so are knowing, interpreting and serving.

The common reading takes the phrase to mean a desire to dominate other people, as if every act were a covert grab for control over someone else. It cannot be right, because the texts give the will to power to people who rule nobody. The martyr of GS 13 dies rather than rule. The scholar of Z II 12 is told that his will to truth, "a will to the thinkability of all beings", is "a will to power": the resistance he overcomes is a world that is not yet intelligible. The servant has it too: "even in the will of those who serve I found the will to be master" (Z II 12), since by serving the stronger the weaker gains mastery "over what is weaker still". And GS 13 ranks hurting others below benefiting them: the state in which we hurt others "is a sign that we are still lacking power".

### The opposite correction fails too

An influential correction swings the other way: will to power is "really" self-overcoming, the mastery of one's own drives, and domination of others is a misunderstanding of it, or at most a crude early stage. The texts do not allow this either. Domination of others is described at length, without apology, as a form of the same principle, and the hardest of these passages is BGE 259:

<Quote source="BGE 259, trans. H. Zimmern">
To refrain mutually from injury, from violence, from exploitation, and put one's will on a par with that of others: this may result in a certain rough sense in good conduct among individuals when the necessary conditions are given (namely, the actual similarity of the individuals in amount of force and degree of worth, and their co-relation within one organization). As soon, however, as one wished to take this principle more generally, and if possible even as the *fundamental principle of society*, it would immediately disclose what it really is — namely, a Will to the *denial* of life, a principle of dissolution and decay. Here one must think profoundly to the very basis and resist all sentimental weakness: life itself is *essentially* appropriation, injury, conquest of the strange and weak, suppression, severity, obtrusion of peculiar forms, incorporation, and at the least, putting it mildest, exploitation; — but why should one for ever use precisely these words on which for ages a disparaging purpose has been stamped? Even the organization within which, as was previously supposed, the individuals treat each other as equal — it takes place in every healthy aristocracy — must itself, if it be a living and not a dying organization, do all that towards other bodies, which the individuals within it refrain from doing to each other: it will have to be the incarnated Will to Power, it will endeavour to grow, to gain ground, attract to itself and acquire ascendancy — not owing to any morality or immorality, but because it *lives*, and because life *is* precisely Will to Power. … "Exploitation" does not belong to a depraved, or imperfect and primitive society: it belongs to the *nature* of the living being as a primary organic function, it is a consequence of the intrinsic Will to Power, which is precisely the Will to Life.
</Quote>

The words are the organic words of BGE 13. "Appropriation" and "incorporation" are what a cell does with food; "exploitation" (*Ausbeutung*) is offered as a primary function of the living, and the section asks why these words should keep the "disparaging purpose" stamped on them. The equality of individuals is allowed where they really are equal in force and worth and belong to one body, and that body, a "healthy aristocracy", does to other bodies what its members do not do to each other. What that means for societies is the business of <Ref lesson="order-of-rank">the order of rank</Ref>. Domination of others is, in the books, a form of the principle stated at full strength. The closing "Will to Life" is GS 349's usage again: life's own will, which is will to power, not Schopenhauer's will to go on existing.

### One force, from the barbarian to the ascetic

*Daybreak* had already laid the forms out as a single scale, in a section on the striving for distinction, the wish to stand out before others:

<Quote source="D 113, trans. R. J. Hollingdale">
The striving for distinction is the striving for domination over the next man, though it be a very indirect domination and only felt or even dreamed. There is a long scale of degrees of this secretly desired domination, and a complete catalogue of them would be almost the same thing as a history of culture, from the earliest, still grotesque barbarism up to the grotesqueries of over-refinement and morbid idealism. The striving for distinction brings with it for the next man — to name only a few steps on the ladder: torment, then blows, then terror, then fearful astonishment, then wonderment, then envy, then admiration, then elevation, then joy, then cheerfulness, then laughter, then derision, then mockery, then ridicule, then giving blows, then imposing torment: — here at the end of the ladder stands the ascetic and martyr, who feels the highest enjoyment by himself enduring, as a consequence of his drive for distinction, precisely that which, on the first step of the ladder, his counterpart the barbarian imposes on others on whom and before whom he wants to distinguish himself. The triumph of the ascetic over himself, his glance turned inwards which beholds man split asunder into a sufferer and a spectator, and henceforth gazes out into the outer world only in order to gather as it were wood for his own pyre, this final tragedy of the drive for distinction in which there is only one character burning and consuming himself — this is a worthy conclusion and one appropriate to the commencement: in both cases an unspeakable happiness at the sight of torment!
</Quote>

The ladder refutes both readings at once. It begins with domination of the crudest kind, so domination is not a misunderstanding of the principle. And it ends with the ascetic's self-mastery, which is not a gentler alternative to cruelty but the same act turned inward, "man split asunder into a sufferer and a spectator", with "an unspeakable happiness at the sight of torment" at both ends. The section's word is still "feeling of power", not "will to power", but it is the scale the later principle is meant to explain. How cruelty turned inward became the bad conscience is taken up in <Ref lesson="bad-conscience">the bad conscience</Ref>.

### What morality calls the forms

*Daybreak* adds who names the forms good and evil: "When man possesses the feeling of power he feels and calls himself good: and it is precisely then that the others upon whom he has to discharge his power feel and call him evil!" (D 189). And the *Genealogy of Morals* (GM, 1887) extends the forms to interpretation itself. Any custom, organ or institution, it argues, has been taken over again and again by new powers that gave it new uses:

<Quote source="GM II 12, trans. W. Kaufmann and R. J. Hollingdale">
… purposes and utilities are only *signs* that a will to power has become master of something less powerful and imposed upon it the character of a function; and the entire history of a "thing," an organ, a custom can in this way be a continuous sign-chain of ever new interpretations and adaptations whose causes do not even have to be related to one another but, on the contrary, in some cases succeed and alternate with one another in a purely chance fashion. … The democratic idiosyncrasy which opposes everything that dominates and wants to dominate, the modern *misarchism* (to coin an ugly word for an ugly thing) has permeated the realm of the spirit and disguised itself in the most spiritual forms to such a degree that today it has forced its way, has acquired the right to force its way into the strictest, apparently most objective sciences; indeed, it seems to me to have already taken charge of all physiology and theory of life — to the detriment of life, as goes without saying, since it has robbed it of a fundamental concept, that of *activity*.
</Quote>

*Misarchism* is hatred of rule. The charge is that the life sciences of the day, which made "adaptation" to outer conditions the basic fact of life, did so because a taste that dislikes whatever dominates had made activity, taking over and imposing a form, unthinkable. To interpret a thing, giving it a meaning and a use, is on this account to master it. That is the use of the principle the <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">genealogy</Ref> is built on.

## The strongest objection: a principle that explains everything

A serious critic now presses the danger flagged at the start. Every action can be redescribed as a pursuit of power: the climber masters the mountain, the martyr masters his fear, the shopper masters the shop. A principle that fits every case, whatever happens, rules nothing out, and a theory that rules nothing out is not a discovery about living things but a way of talking. The critic adds a case. People buy more ice cream on hot days. If what they wanted were power, mastery over the ice cream, the weather should make no difference; what they want is the cold sweet thing, and the heat explains the wanting. So power is either false of the case or true of it only by redescription.

### What the published form predicts

The reply from Nietzsche's side starts by noticing that the published claims are not mere redescriptions. They are contrastive: each names a behaviour that will to power predicts and some rival predicts otherwise. Two have come up already. Squandering when safe (GS 349) is set against self-preservation, which predicts economy once danger is past. The ascetic who enjoys his torment and makes his fasts harder (the table of four behaviours) is set against Schopenhauer's quieted will, which predicts indifference and easier fasts. Either could have come out the other way. The definition yields two more, and each has to be set against the right rival, because the obvious one does not always discriminate.

**Boredom amid plenty.** A 2 and D 262 predict that people given everything stay unhappy: "let them have everything — health, food, a place to live, entertainment — they are and remain unhappy". Self-preservation and plain contentment predict the opposite: a living thing whose needs are safely met has reached its aim and should be at rest. That can be checked, against the leisured, the retired and the very rich. The boredom alone does not separate will to power from Schopenhauer, though: his step 4 predicts it too, "a fearful emptiness and boredom" once a want is met too easily (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-pendulum">the pendulum</Ref>). Against him the difference lies in what comes after. The pendulum predicts some new want, any want, whose satisfaction again brings only relief. Will to power predicts that the bored look for a *harder* resistance, and that while they are absorbed in it they are neither in pain nor bored though the want is still unmet, a state the pendulum has no place for.

**The end of the game.** A 2's definition predicts that a victory too easily won gives no satisfaction. GS 13 puts it as a fact about proud natures: "An easy prey is something contemptible". Against self-preservation and contentment that is again a real prediction, since for both a win without risk is the best case. Schopenhauer predicts the emptiness after the easy win as well; where he parts from will to power is the next move. On his account the winner turns to some other want; on Nietzsche's he raises the stakes and seeks a stronger opponent.

### Power needs an end

The ice cream case loses its force once the structure of the definition is in view. Will to power is not a rival object competing with ice cream for the buyer's attention; it is a want about *how* some end is pursued, and it needs an end, as the climber needed the summit. The hot day explains the end, the wish for something cold. The principle does not say that every want is a want for power. It says what the pursuit of wants is like, and its prediction lies where the ice cream case does not look: in what follows when wants are met with nothing resisting. Not the rest that self-preservation expects, and not merely the next want of the pendulum, but a search for an end that resists.

### The gap

The reply succeeds for the hedged, psychological principle and fails for the universal one. In a notebook of spring 1888 Nietzsche wrote, unpublished: "That all driving force is will to power, that there is no physical, dynamic or psychological force apart from this" (notebook 14[121]). It might seem that the books already contradict this, since A 6 speaks of decline "where the will to power is lacking" and *Twilight* of institutions that "undermine the will to power" (TI Skirmishes 38). They do not. On the reading this lesson has given, "lacking" means weaker in degree: declining life is will to power failing, and self-preservation is will to power under distress. The trouble with the universal form lies elsewhere, and it takes four steps to see:

1. The hedged form earns its content by contrast: when the pressure is off, living things spend rather than save; given everything, they look for a harder resistance. Each claim names something a rival expects instead.
2. The universal form adds that every force there is, physical and dynamic as well as psychological, is will to power, and that each weaker or restricted form of it is still will to power.
3. Then every possible observation fits: squandering is will to power strong, economy is will to power hemmed in, decline is will to power weak, and a falling stone is will to power too. Nothing that could happen is excluded.
4. Every prediction in this section came from the hedged form. What the universal form adds is a claim about forces outside living things, and no observation of a stone or a magnet comes out differently for calling its force will to power.

So the universal form is not refuted; it is untestable, a way of describing everything once the hedged form has done the work. The testable form is the hedged one.

## Psychology, biology, world: how far the claim reaches

The gap raises the question of scope directly. Will to power can be read as three claims of increasing size: (a) a psychology, about what human beings want; (b) a biology, about what all living things do; (c) a claim about the whole world, about what every force is. The published books plainly make the first two. GS 13, *Daybreak* and A 2 are psychology; BGE 23 calls the psychology Nietzsche wants "the morphology and development-doctrine of the will to power". BGE 13, BGE 259, GM II 12, GS 349 and Z II 12's "where I found the living, there I found will to power" are biology.

The third is the opening case of <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books">Nietzsche's life and books</Ref>: the notebook sentence "This world is the will to power, and nothing besides!" (summer 1885), and its published counterpart a year later, hedged.

### BGE 36: the world from inside, under "supposing"

BGE 36 is the one place in the books where the cosmic claim is argued, and the argument is built entirely of conditions. It begins: "Supposing that nothing else is 'given' as real but our world of desires and passions, that we cannot sink or rise to any other 'reality' but just that of our drives — for thinking is only a relation of these drives to one another", and asks: "are we not permitted to make the attempt" to understand the mechanical world from this "given" too? "The conscience of method" commands the attempt, it answers, since one should not assume several kinds of causality until the attempt to make do with one has been pushed to its limit. If we believe that the will acts at all, "we *must* make the attempt to posit *hypothetically* the causality of the will as the only causality"; "'will' can naturally only operate on 'will'", so "the hypothesis must be hazarded". Then the conclusion:

<Quote source="BGE 36, trans. H. Zimmern, revised">
<Original lang="de">
Gesetzt endlich, dass es gelänge, unser gesammtes Triebleben als die Ausgestaltung und Verzweigung Einer Grundform des Willens zu erklären – nämlich des Willens zur Macht, wie es mein Satz ist –; gesetzt, dass man alle organischen Funktionen auf diesen Willen zur Macht zurückführen könnte und in ihm auch die Lösung des Problems der Zeugung und Ernährung – es ist Ein Problem – fände, so hätte man damit sich das Recht verschafft, alle wirkende Kraft eindeutig zu bestimmen als: Wille zur Macht. Die Welt von innen gesehen, die Welt auf ihren "intelligiblen Charakter" hin bestimmt und bezeichnet – sie wäre eben "Wille zur Macht" und nichts ausserdem.
</Original>

Supposing, finally, that one succeeded in explaining our entire drive-life as the development and ramification of *one* basic form of will — namely, of the will to power, as my thesis has it —; supposing that one could trace all organic functions back to this will to power, and find in it also the solution of the problem of procreation and nutrition — it is one problem —, then one would have won the right to define *all* effective force unequivocally as: *will to power*. The world seen from inside, the world defined and designated according to its "intelligible character" — it would be "will to power" and nothing besides.
</Quote>

Three things are true of this passage at once, and a fair reading keeps all three. It is conditional throughout: "supposing", "supposing", "would have won the right", "it would be". It calls the will to power "my thesis" and says method *commands* the attempt, so it is not a joke or a mere thought-experiment. And its conclusion depends on two reductions nobody had carried out, of all drive-life and of all organic function, as premises still to be earned.

"Intelligible character" is the term Kant and Schopenhauer used for what a thing is in itself, as against how it appears, and "seen from inside" is Schopenhauer's route to it: the inner nature of everything, reached from the one place we know from inside, our own willing (<Ref lesson="schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy" id="the-world-from-inside-one-blind-will">step 1 of his argument</Ref>). BGE 36 puts the will to power in exactly the place where Schopenhauer had put the will to live, with its character reversed: a will that wants more instead of a will that only wants to go on. The notebook of 1885 states the same thing without a single condition, as an answer offered to the reader: "do you want a name for this world? A solution to all its riddles? … This world is the will to power — and nothing besides! And you yourselves too are this will to power — and nothing besides!" (notebook 38[12], June–July 1885, unpublished).

By the <Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="ranking-the-texts-and-citing-them">ranking of the texts</Ref>, the published book outranks the abandoned note, and the note's version is in any case the universal one that, as the gap showed, the books' own uses contradict. The psychology with its biological extension is the form in which the books argue for the principle and use it, and it is the form used from here on; the world-thesis returns when the whole argument is assembled in <Ref lesson="the-system-assembled">the system assembled</Ref>.

<Conclusion>
You can now say what "life itself is will to power" claims in the books: that a living thing wants above all to spend its strength and to grow by overcoming resistance in pursuing its ends, so that survival and pleasure are results, and happiness is "the feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome". You can give the two arguments for it: against self-preservation, that living things squander strength when safe, so the wish to survive is will to power under distress; against Schopenhauer's will to live, that satisfaction lies in overcoming, not in relief, which removes step 3 of the pendulum and with it the premise of the pessimist's verdict, though not by itself proving life worth living. You can say what the word covers, mastery over others as plainly as mastery over oneself, with neither ranked as the real one; why the published principle makes predictions its rivals do not; and where it stops being testable, in the universal form the notebooks state and BGE 36 publishes only under "supposing".

If every drive wants to discharge its strength and overcome resistance, a dangerous drive, cruelty or lust or the wish to dominate, cannot be argued out of wanting. Morality has offered two ways of dealing with it, letting it out or cutting it out. <Ref lesson="sublimation">Sublimation</Ref> takes up Nietzsche's third way: keeping the drive's force and changing what it is aimed at.
</Conclusion>

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A stamp collector completes the set he has worked on for twenty years. Within a week he feels flat and restless. (a) From memory, state steps 2 to 6 of Schopenhauer's pendulum and explain the collector's week with them. (b) Explain the same week with the will to power. (c) Name the one step on which the two explanations part, and say what each predicts the collector will do next.

<Solution>
**(a) The pendulum.** (2) A want is a lack, felt as pain: for twenty years he lacked the missing stamps. (3) Getting what you want only removes the pain and adds nothing: the last stamp ended the lack. (4) With nothing left to want comes boredom: the flat week. (5) A new want will arrive, because the will never stops. (6) So a life of wanting is worse than none: its best moment, the completed set, was only the end of a pain, and was followed at once by emptiness.

**(b) The will to power.** What he wanted was the set *acquired against resistance*: the hunt, the rare finds, the years of overcoming scarcity and competition. That activity was the satisfaction, "the feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome" (A 2), and it was had for twenty years. The last stamp removed the resistance, and with it the activity. The flat week is "who was not vanquished in his victory?" (Z III 12, §30): the victory ended the game.

**(c) Where they part.** Step 3. On Schopenhauer's account the twenty years were pain and the completion was the only gain, which was mere relief. On Nietzsche's, the twenty years were the gain. The predictions differ accordingly. Schopenhauer predicts that some new want will turn up, any want, and that its satisfaction will again bring only relief. The will to power predicts something more specific: that he will look for a *harder* project, a rarer field or a more difficult collection, not merely another want; and that if nothing difficult offers itself, he will stay restless however comfortable he is (D 262: "let them have everything … they are and remain unhappy").
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "Will to power means that everyone secretly wants to rule other people." Give one behaviour the claim cannot explain, one passage in which domination of others is indeed the form named, and then state the claim correctly.

<Solution>
**A behaviour it cannot explain.** The martyr of GS 13 dies for his church. He rules nobody and gives up everything, yet GS 13 counts his sacrifice as "offered for our desire for power or for the purpose of preserving our feeling of power". The ascetic at the top of D 113's ladder is the same case: he does to himself what the barbarian does to others. So does the scholar of Z II 12, whose "will to the thinkability of all beings" is called "a will to power": the resistance he overcomes is an unintelligible world, not a person.

**Where domination is the form.** BGE 259: "life itself is *essentially* appropriation, injury, conquest of the strange and weak, suppression, severity, … and at the least, putting it mildest, exploitation", and a healthy aristocracy "will have to be the incarnated Will to Power" towards other bodies. D 113's ladder begins with the barbarian's "torment, then blows, then terror". So the claim is not simply false; it takes one form for the whole.

**The correct statement.** Will to power is the drive to overcome resistance in pursuing some end. The resistance can be other people (BGE 259, the barbarian), oneself (the ascetic), a problem (the scholar), or a thing to be given a function (GM II 12). Mastery over others is one form, named at full strength; it is not the definition. Nor is the correction that will to power is "really" self-mastery: the books rank neither form as the real meaning, and D 113 puts both on one ladder.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A boundary probe. You play chess every evening against a program set so weak that you always win. Using A 2's definition of happiness, predict what you will feel after a month, and what you will do. Then say why this case shows a paradox built into the will to power, and how Nietzsche's texts answer it.

<Solution>
**The prediction.** A 2 defines happiness as "the feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome". A program that never resists offers nothing to overcome, so no feeling of power grows, however many games are won. After the first few wins you should feel indifference, then boredom; GS 13 says it of proud natures: "An easy prey is something contemptible". The prediction is that you raise the program's strength or stop playing. Survival and simple contentment predict neither: for both, an unbroken string of safe wins is the best case. Schopenhauer's pendulum predicts the boredom too (step 4), but not the remedy: on his account the next want can be any want, and its satisfaction again brings only relief; nothing in it says the next want must be a *stronger* opponent.

**The boundary.** The case is the limit at which the end is reached with no resistance at all, and there the will to power predicts no satisfaction, because its satisfaction is defined by resistance. That is why the definition needs both layers: wanting to win (the end) and wanting to win *against resistance*.

**The paradox.** To play at all you must want to win; but winning removes the resistance in which the satisfaction lay. "Who was not vanquished in his victory?" (Z III 12, §30). From outside the game, what you want is the playing; from inside, you must want what ends it.

**The answer in the texts.** Not to stop wanting victory, but to treat each victory as the start of a harder game: "I am that which must always overcome itself"; "whatever I create and however much I love it — soon I must oppose it" (Z II 12). The restlessness that Schopenhauer read as the indictment of life is, on this account, the form its satisfaction takes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A derivation the chapter did not carry out. Suppose a society succeeded in abolishing all suffering: no illness, no want, no danger, nothing hard left to do. (a) Starting from the definition of will to power, derive what Nietzsche must say about such a world. (b) Then check your derivation against this passage, addressed to those who measure the worth of things by pleasure and pain, and say which of its claims your derivation does not supply.

<Quote source="BGE 225, trans. H. Zimmern">
You want, if possible — and there is not a more foolish "if possible" — *to do away with suffering*; and we? — it really seems that *we* would rather have it increased and made worse than it has ever been! Well-being, as you understand it — is certainly not a goal; it seems to us an *end*; a condition which at once renders man ludicrous and contemptible — and makes his destruction *desirable*! The discipline of suffering, of *great* suffering — know ye not that it is only *this* discipline that has produced all the elevations of humanity hitherto?
</Quote>

<Solution>
**(a) The derivation.** (1) Will to power is satisfied by overcoming resistance (A 2). (2) Resistance is felt as unpleasure; "man does not avoid it but instead has constant need of it" (notebook 14[174], unpublished). (3) A world without suffering is a world without felt resistance, since every obstacle would be removed before it could be felt. (4) So such a world removes the conditions of satisfaction, not only of pain. (5) It would therefore not be the happiest possible world but one in which the feeling that power is growing has become impossible: a life of "contentedness" and "peace", which A 2 sets against "more power" and "war". Nietzsche must say that the abolition of suffering, as a goal, is a goal of declining life (A 6), and that whatever is great in human beings needs the resistance it removes.

**(b) The check.** BGE 225 agrees with the derivation and goes further. "Well-being, as you understand it — is certainly not a goal; it seems to us an *end*" is step (5): a state in which nothing resists is where striving stops, the "contentedness" of A 2, and "renders man ludicrous and contemptible" is the verdict of declining life. What the derivation could not supply is the last sentence: the historical claim that "the discipline of *great* suffering" produced "all the elevations of humanity hitherto", and with it the wish to have suffering "increased". The definition gives only that satisfaction needs resistance, not that great resistance produced the highest human types.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A sourcing drill on BGE 36. (a) Metadata first: what kind of text is it, when, and in what part of which book? Given that, what would you expect it to assert, and how? (b) List every conditional or hedge in the passage as quoted in the chapter. (c) Rewrite its conclusion as an unconditional argument, and name each premise you had to add. (d) Set your rewrite beside notebook 38[12] and say which text says which.

<Solution>
**(a) Metadata.** A published book, *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886), from the third period, in its second part, "The Free Spirit". As a finished, released text it ranks above any note (<Ref lesson="nietzsche-life-and-books" id="ranking-the-texts-and-citing-them">ranking the texts</Ref>). One would expect a claim Nietzsche was willing to stand behind in public, so if the claim is hedged, the hedge is part of what he stands behind.

**(b) The hedges.** "Supposing that nothing else is 'given' as real but our world of desires and passions"; "are we not permitted to make the attempt"; the attempt is "commanded" by "the conscience of method", which makes it obligatory to try, not established; "we *must* make the attempt to posit *hypothetically* the causality of the will as the only causality", itself conditional on believing that the will acts at all; "the hypothesis must be hazarded"; "Supposing, finally, that one succeeded in explaining …"; "supposing that one could trace …"; "one would have won the right"; "it would be 'will to power' and nothing besides". The one unhedged phrase is "as my thesis has it", which owns the thesis without asserting that the reductions have succeeded.

**(c) The rewrite.** (1) Only our drives are given as real, and thinking is a relation among them. (2) The will is causal; there is such a thing as will acting. (3) Method forbids assuming more than one kind of causality until one kind has been tried to its limit. (4) All our drive-life is a ramification of one basic form of will, will to power. (5) All organic functions, procreation and nutrition included, derive from will to power. (6) So all effective force is will to power, and the world seen from inside is will to power. The premises added by removing the hedges are (1) and (2), which BGE 36 offers as "supposing" and as a condition (if we believe that the will acts), and above all (4) and (5), which the section presents as a success nobody had achieved. Without them only (3) remains, a rule about how to inquire, which licenses trying the hypothesis, not believing it.

**(d) Beside the notebook.** Notebook 38[12] (June–July 1885, unpublished) gives the conclusion with no premises and no hedges: "This world is the will to power — and nothing besides! And you yourselves too are this will to power — and nothing besides!", offered as "a name for this world" and "a solution to all its riddles". So the unpublished note says that the world *is* will to power; the published book says that it *would be*, if two reductions succeed, and that method requires the attempt. Both are Nietzsche's words; a reader who quotes the cosmic thesis as his doctrine is quoting the note, and has to say so.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A man with a strong aggressive drive grew up brawling. In his thirties he has become a trial lawyer famous for demolishing witnesses in cross-examination, and he has not thrown a punch in fifteen years. Using the will to power, say what changed in him and what stayed the same. Is his aggression gone, satisfied, or something else?

<Solution>
**What stayed.** The drive and its force. On the will to power it is the same desire to overcome resistance by attacking, and the satisfaction is the same in kind: "the feeling that power is growing, that resistance is overcome" (A 2). A witness who holds out under questioning is resistance, and breaking his story is overcoming it. The intensity, the pleasure in the opponent's collapse, the restlessness when no case is on, are all what they were in the tavern.

**What changed.** Not the force but its object and its means. The resistance is now an argument instead of a body, the weapon is a question instead of a fist, and the setting turns the drive's discharge into something his society rewards instead of punishing. In D 113's terms he has moved up the ladder of the striving for distinction, from "giving blows" toward "derision, … mockery, … ridicule", without leaving it.

**So which is it?** Neither gone nor merely satisfied. It has not been extirpated, since the force is all there; nor simply let out, since it no longer does what it did. It has been redirected to a new object, kept at full strength and, in a sense still to be made precise, refined. That third treatment, set against both discharge and extirpation, is what Nietzsche calls the spiritualization or sublimation of a drive, the subject of <Ref lesson="sublimation">sublimation</Ref>, which also asks whether the refined form is higher than the crude one, and why.
</Solution>

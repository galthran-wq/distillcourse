Nietzsche's published works contain exactly one step-by-step argument that the world is will to power 'and nothing besides' (BGE 36), and it begins with 'suppose' — the doctrine that survives analysis is smaller, and it is a claim about what satisfaction requires. This lesson sorts the doctrine's uses in the published works into three readings by what each needs the text to say, reconstructs the one argument in seven steps with the mood of each step marked, locates the step at which Maudemarie Clark shows it to assume what it sets out to prove, and states the doctrine that survives as psychology in Bernard Reginster's three conditions: a determinate end, a resistance to it, and the overcoming of that resistance.

The course's argument reached, in <Ref lesson="soul-as-society-of-drives">the drives lesson</Ref>, a soul that is a society of drives with command as its relation, and BGE 23's programme naming will to power as the principle the society runs on, without arguing it. <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">The compilation lesson</Ref> found that the notebook's indicative, "this world is the will to power, and nothing besides" (WP 1067), has one published counterpart, BGE 36, which reaches the same content in the conditional. What stands open is the principle itself: whether "will to power" is a claim about what drives seek, a claim about what every living thing does, or a claim about what everything is, and which of these the *Genealogy*'s mechanisms, which have used "a will to power has become master" as a method since <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the method lesson</Ref>, actually require. This lesson draws that line, and finds the genealogy on the psychological side of it. <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">The next lesson</Ref> reads the passage in *Twilight of the Idols* where the two-world metaphysics that a cosmological reading would need was abolished by Nietzsche himself.

The route: the doctrine's appearances counted and sorted into three readings; BGE 13's anti-preservation premise reconstructed and graded; BGE 36 in seven steps with Clark's objection located; the psychological doctrine derived from Schopenhauer's pendulum to Reginster's three conditions, with its rivals; *The Antichrist*'s definitional use and BGE 259's biological one; and the line between what the genealogy uses and what only the cosmology would license.

## Three readings, sorted by what each needs the text to say

The phrase "will to power" is used in the published works far more often than it is explained, and the first task is to count. In *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886) Clark counts four sections of the first two parts that reflect on the doctrine at length, §§9, 22, 23 and 36, and at least eight more that name it (§§13, 44, 51, 186, 198, 211, 227, 259); the later books name it less and never again explain it. The *Genealogy* (1887) uses it as a tool: II:12's rule that every purpose is a sign "that a will to power has become master of something less powerful", II:18's "instinct for freedom (in my language: the will to power)" building bad conscience, III:15's "will to power of the very weakest", III:18's neighbour-love prescribed by the priest as a dose of "the strongest and most life-assertive impulse". *The Antichrist* (written 1888) opens by defining good through it (§2). *Twilight* (1888) turns it against Darwin (IX:14). And *Zarathustra*'s second part (1883) puts it in the mouth of "Life": "wherever I found the living, there I found the will to power".

From a phrase used in this many sections one would expect a stated doctrine. The record is the reverse: the phrase is named, used and defined, and it is argued for exactly once, in a section whose premises and conclusion are in the subjunctive. The readings of the doctrine therefore differ not on what the sentences say but on how far they reach, and each reading needs a different thing from the text.

- The **psychological** reading takes "will to power" as a claim about human motivation: what human beings seek, and what satisfies them. It needs passages where the doctrine explains a behaviour by a desire for power, and it has them (GM III:15, III:18; A §2's definition of happiness; BGE 9 on philosophy). It needs nothing said about organisms in general or about matter.
- The **biological** reading takes it as a claim about every living thing. It needs "life itself is will to power" (BGE 13) and "exploitation belongs to the essence of being alive" (BGE 259) read as physiology, and Twilight's "where there is a struggle it is a struggle for power".
- The **cosmological** reading takes it as a claim about everything: the world "is" will to power "and nothing besides". It needs an indicative sentence saying so in a work Nietzsche published. There is one candidate, BGE 36, and it says "would be" (*wäre*); *Zarathustra* has "Life" say it, in a work of fiction; the indicative sentence is WP 1067, which <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the compilation lesson</Ref> dated to a notebook of 1885 in Zarathustra's voice.

If the world is will to power then all life is and so is every human motive; the inference does not run the other way. A psychological doctrine that is true says nothing about protoplasm or about planets, and a cosmological doctrine that is unproved leaves a psychological one exactly where it was. The readings in the secondary literature sort into four families along the same axis: Heidegger, Jaspers and Richardson read the doctrine as metaphysics or a general theory of drives; Nehamas and Poellner read it as a denial that any such theory can be given; Kaufmann, Clark, Reginster and Katsafanas read it as psychology with an evaluative use; Abel and Moore read it as biology. The course's working hypothesis, methodological naturalism, belongs to the third family, and the test of this lesson is whether the text lets it stay there.

<KeyFact>
The nesting fixes where the risk lies. BGE 36's failure, when the lesson reaches it, costs the naturalist reading nothing: an argument that does not reach the world leaves the psychology it started from untouched, and the one clause the section asserts is that psychology. The exposure runs inward: a psychological doctrine that failed would take both outer readings with it, so the test that decides the module is Reginster's analysis, not Clark's objection.
</KeyFact>

## BGE 13: the anti-preservation premise

The biological reading has its clearest sentence in the shortest of the four sections, and the sentence is a premise in an argument about method.

<Quote source="BGE 13, trans. Norman">
<Original lang="de">
Die Physiologen sollten sich besinnen, den Selbsterhaltungstrieb als kardinalen Trieb eines organischen Wesens anzusetzen. Vor Allem will etwas Lebendiges seine Kraft auslassen — Leben selbst ist Wille zur Macht —: die Selbsterhaltung ist nur eine der indirekten und häufigsten Folgen davon. — Kurz, hier wie überall, Vorsicht vor überflüssigen teleologischen Principien! — wie ein solches der Selbsterhaltungstrieb ist (man dankt ihn der Inconsequenz Spinoza's —). So nämlich gebietet es die Methode, die wesentlich Principien-Sparsamkeit sein muss.
</Original>

Physiologists should think twice before positioning the drive for self-preservation as the cardinal drive of an organic being. Above all, a living thing wants to discharge its strength — life itself is will to power —: self-preservation is only one of the indirect and most frequent consequences of this. — In short, here as elsewhere, watch out for superfluous teleological principles! — such as the drive for preservation (which we owe to Spinoza's inconsistency —). This is demanded by method, which must essentially be the economy of principles.
</Quote>

The target is the *conatus*, Spinoza's principle (Ethics III, prop. 6) that each thing strives to persevere in its being, which nineteenth-century physiology had taken over as a drive to self-preservation. The section's argument is a razor: do not posit a goal for behaviour if something already posited explains the behaviour's tendency to that result.

<Remark title="BGE 13 reconstructed">
**P1.** To posit a drive is to posit a goal: to say that certain behaviours aim at a state. Anchor: "teleological principles", "the drive for self-preservation".

**P2.** A goal may be posited only where it is needed to explain why the behaviour tends to that state; a goal posited where something else already explains the tendency is a "superfluous teleological principle". Anchor: "watch out for superfluous teleological principles"; "the economy of principles".

**P3.** Something else already explains why organic behaviour tends to preserve the organism: "above all, a living thing wants to discharge its strength", and preservation is "one of the indirect and most frequent consequences" of discharge. Anchor: the sentence itself, "life itself is will to power".

**C.** The drive to self-preservation is superfluous, and a physiology that makes it cardinal has multiplied principles beyond need.

**The missing premise.** P3 is the whole content of the section and it is asserted. Nothing in BGE 13, or in the surrounding sections, which concern philosophers and psychologists rather than organisms, says why one should posit of living things a want to discharge strength, or how discharge explains the tendency to preservation. Clark and Dudrick add the sharper point: "wants to discharge its strength" is itself a teleological principle, so the razor that removes the *conatus* has been applied to one goal-posit and not to the other, and the section does not say what makes the second necessary where the first was superfluous.

**The strongest opposite reading.** That "life" here is not biological life at all. BGE 9, four sections earlier, had asked "isn't living assessing, preferring, being unfair, being limited, wanting to be different?", and Clark and Dudrick read BGE 13's "life" in that sense, the life of beings who live by values, which turns the section's claim from physiology into a claim about the soul. On that reading the biological doctrine is not in the section at all.
</Remark>

The evidence Nietzsche offers for P3 elsewhere is of one kind: cases where organisms risk what preservation would forbid. *The Gay Science* §349 calls "the wish to preserve oneself" the "symptom of a condition of distress, of a limitation of the really fundamental instinct of life which aims at the expansion of power, and, wishing for that, frequently risks and even sacrifices self-preservation". *Twilight*'s "Anti-Darwin" (IX:14) says the "struggle for life" is "rather asserted than proved", that "the general aspect of life is not hunger and distress, but rather wealth, luxury, even absurd prodigality", and that "where there is a struggle it is a struggle for power"; "one should not mistake Malthus for nature". The notebooks add the anecdote the argument is remembered by, in an entry of 1888 printed as WP 702: "the protoplasm stretches its pseudopodia in order to search for something that resists it, not from hunger but from will to power", and nourishment is "merely a derivative appearance". The entry is Nachlass and corroborates only, under <Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the published-works rule</Ref>; it is quoted here because Heidegger's lectures quote it as the doctrine's biology, and the module's last lesson will need to know where it comes from.

<Figure
  src="./figures/preservation-vs-power.svg"
  alt="A box labelled a living thing with two arrows leaving it: a thick amber arrow to discharge its strength, glossed above all, life itself is will to power, and a thin arrow to preserve itself, glossed only one of the indirect and most frequent consequences, the conatus, Spinoza's inconsistency. A dashed box below quotes the Nachlass protoplasm entry printed as WP 702, marked corroborating only, and Twilight IX:14's struggle for power."
  caption="BGE 13's reversal: discharge is what a living thing wants above all, and preservation is demoted to a frequent by-product; the pseudopodia are the notebooks' illustration, not the published argument."

Graded as evidence, P3 is an empirical claim with anecdotal support. Risk-taking against preservation is real and the published works cite it in general terms; no published passage supplies a mechanism by which discharge produces preservation as a by-product, and the razor of P2 cuts against the section's own posit as much as against Spinoza's. What the section does establish is the premise that BGE 36 will call "my proposition": that the drives' activity is discharge, and that preservation is not the basic aim. That is a claim about drives. The step from drives to organisms in general is taken by the word "life", and the opposite reading above shows that the word does not force it.

<KeyFact>
BGE 13 removes the self-preservation drive by a razor and installs the discharge of strength by assertion: the section argues that a physiology needs one principle, not two, and does not argue which one.
</KeyFact>

## BGE 36: the argument in seven steps

The one section that argues for the cosmology is the one the module has been pointing at since <Ref lesson="will-to-power-the-book">the compilation lesson</Ref> found it behind WP 1067. Its opening and its close fix the mood of everything between.

<Quote source="BGE 36, trans. Norman (opening and close)">
<Original lang="de">
Gesetzt, dass nichts Anderes als real „gegeben" ist als unsre Welt der Begierden und Leidenschaften, dass wir zu keiner anderen „Realität" hinab oder hinauf können als gerade zur Realität unsrer Triebe — denn Denken ist nur ein Verhalten dieser Triebe zu einander —: ist es nicht erlaubt, den Versuch zu machen und die Frage zu fragen, ob dies Gegeben nicht ausreicht, um aus Seines-Gleichen auch die sogenannte mechanistische (oder „materielle") Welt zu verstehen? […] Die Welt von innen gesehen, die Welt auf ihren „intelligiblen Charakter" hin bestimmt und bezeichnet — sie wäre eben „Wille zur Macht" und nichts ausserdem. —
</Original>

Assuming that our world of desires and passions is the only thing "given" as real, that we cannot get down or up to any "reality" except the reality of our drives (since thinking is only a relation between these drives) — aren't we allowed to make the attempt and pose the question as to whether something like this "given" isn't enough to render the so-called mechanistic (and thus material) world comprehensible as well? […] The world seen from inside, the world determined and described with respect to its "intelligible character" — would be just this "will to power" and nothing else. —
</Quote>

The German opens with *Gesetzt, dass*, which Norman renders "Assuming that" and Kaufmann "Suppose", and the same word opens the last movement twice more (*Gesetzt endlich*, *gesetzt, dass man*); the conclusion's verb is *wäre*, "would be". A rendering that flattened *gesetzt* to "given that" would turn the first premise into an assertion and the section into a proof, and the argument would be a different one.

<Remark title="BGE 36 in seven steps">
**Step 1** (*Gesetzt, dass*: suppose). Nothing is "given" as real except our world of desires and passions.

**Step 2** (still under the supposition). We cannot get down or up to any other "reality" than the reality of our drives; thinking is only a relation of these drives to one another.

**Step 3** (*ist es nicht erlaubt*: is it not permitted, a question). Then we may make the experiment of asking whether this "given" suffices to understand the so-called mechanistic or material world as well.

**Step 4** (*Ich meine nicht … sondern*: a gloss on step 3, ending in a question mark). Not as an illusion, a "mere appearance" or "representation" in Berkeley's or Schopenhauer's sense, but as something "of the same plane of reality as our affects", "a primitive form of the world of affect", "a kind of life of the drives" in which the organic functions are still bound together: "a pre-form of life?"

**Step 5** (*geboten*: demanded, the section's one indicative). The attempt is not only permitted; "the conscience of method demands it". Multiple kinds of causality are not to be posited until the attempt to make do with one has been pushed to its limit, "ad absurdum, if you will"; this "follows 'from the definition', as a mathematician would say".

**Step 6** (*thun wir das*: if we do; *hypothetisch*; *die Hypothese wagen*). The question is whether we believe in the causality of the will. If we do, "and this belief is really just our belief in causality itself", we must try positing the will's causality as the only causality. "Will" can act only on "will", not on "matter" or "nerves"; so we must venture the hypothesis that wherever effects are recognized, will is acting on will, and every mechanical event in which a force is active is "a force and effect of the will".

**Step 7** (*Gesetzt endlich … so hätte man … sie wäre*: suppose finally, then one would have, it would be). Suppose we succeeded in explaining our entire drive-life as the organization and outgrowth of one basic form of will, "namely, of the will to power, which is my claim" (*wie es mein Satz ist*); suppose all organic functions could be traced to it, including procreation and nutrition, "which is a single problem". Then "we will have earned the right" to designate all efficacious force as will to power, and the world seen from inside "would be just this 'will to power' and nothing else".

**What is asserted.** One clause: that will to power is "my proposition", the hypothesis about the drives that BGE 13 stated and BGE 23 made psychology's subject. Every other step is a supposition, a permission, a demand of method, or a conditional.
</Remark>

### Where the argument assumes its conclusion

The reconstruction shows the argument's shape: the world is to be understood as will because will is the only thing given (steps 1 and 2) and the only causality we know (step 6), and method forbids a second kind of thing until the first has been tried everywhere (step 5). Clark's objection (1990, ch. 7 §2) is that the two premises which carry the argument are premises the same book has already rejected.

Step 1 gives the inner world priority over the outer: we know our drives, and nothing else. Experience cannot be the basis for that, since it challenges what experience teaches about the external world, so the basis must be a priori, and the only candidate Clark can find is a Cartesian one: that "I will" is immediately certain where the material world is not. BGE 16 had denied that there are "immediate certainties", "I think" and "I will" included, and BGE 34 calls faith in them "a stupidity that reflects little honour on us". BGE 19, reconstructed in <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer lesson</Ref>, mocks the philosophers who "speak of the will as if it were the best-known thing in the world" and finds in it "something complicated, something unified only in a word". Step 1 needs the will to be exactly that best-known thing.

Step 6 needs the causality of the will, and Clark lists the published denials: GS 127 on the "thoughtless person" for whom "will alone is effective" and "the feeling of will seems sufficient", *Twilight*'s "the will no longer moves anything, hence does not explain anything either; it merely accompanies events" (VI:3), *The Antichrist*'s will as "only a resultant" (§14). The clause that makes step 6 look unavoidable, that belief in the will's causality "is really just our belief in causality itself", is on Clark's reading the point at which Nietzsche "is playing with us": BGE 21 had already asked that "cause" and "effect" be used "only as pure concepts, as conventional fictions for the purpose of designation", precisely to drop the picture of causation modelled on a will that pushes.

The step that begs the question is therefore step 1 taken with step 6. The conclusion says the world's inside is will; the premises say will is the only given and the only causality; the term the conclusion needs, a simple will that is known and that acts, is put in by the supposition and is what the book's psychology denies there is. Step 7 then generalizes from that single case to "all efficacious force", and Clark notes that the case is not knowledge on Nietzsche's own account: the "one causality we know" is the one GS 127 calls "a magically effective force". The verdict is not that the argument is invalid. Granted its premises, it reaches its conclusion. The verdict is that the premises are not granted by their author, who marks each of them "suppose", and that the only clause he does assert is the psychological one.

### The mood, and what BGE 22 says about it

Clark's reading has a second half, which explains why the argument is in the book at all, and it turns on the section that precedes the four. BGE 22 accuses the physicists of "bad philology": nature's "conformity to law" is "not a matter of fact, not a 'text', but instead only a naive humanitarian correction" that reads "the democratic instincts of the modern soul" into the same appearances; an interpreter "with an opposite intention" could read out of them "a tyrannically ruthless and pitiless execution of power claims", and would still arrive at a "necessary" and "calculable" course, "not because laws are dominant in it, but rather because laws are totally absent". The section ends: "Granted, this is only an interpretation too — and you will be eager enough to make this objection? — well then, so much the better."

The "interpretation" of BGE 22 is not the anti-foundationalism of <Ref lesson="perspectivism">the perspectivism lesson</Ref>, on which physics is interpretation because all knowledge is; Clark's evidence is that the section treats the physicists' formulas as the text and the "laws" as the reading. What is "only an interpretation" is the metaphor read into the text, and Nietzsche puts will to power on a par not with physics but with "conformity to law": both are values read into nature, the democrat's and his own. BGE 9 had said as much of the Stoics, who "pretend rapturously to read the canon of your law in nature" and in fact "want to dictate and annex your morals and ideals onto nature", and generalized it: "it always creates the world in its own image; it cannot do otherwise. Philosophy is this tyrannical drive itself, the most spiritual will to power". On Clark's reading BGE 36 is Nietzsche doing, in his own case and in full view, what BGE 9 says philosophy always does, and the moods are his way of "letting it be known", as BGE 5 demands of a philosopher who has "the courage of the conscience that admits this". The cosmological doctrine is offered as an interpretation from the standpoint of his values, and "so much the better" is the admission that he does not hold it as knowledge.

Clark and Dudrick's later reading (2012, ch. 8) accepts the conclusion and rejects part of the route. Premise 1 can be read as saying that desires and passions are known non-observationally, which Nietzsche can accept; premise 6 can be read with their normative account of BGE 19, on which the will is the political order of the drives and its causality is the influence of that order on action. But even with both premises repaired, they find the hypothesis of step 6 applied to "all mechanical events" a "nonstarter": rocks rolling down hills are not will acting on will, and the class of events the argument could seriously concern is the class of bodily movements that are actions. Read that way, BGE 36 is not about the world but about the soul, and the cosmology drops out. The two readings of Clark's differ on what the section is for and agree on what it does not do: no published section asserts that the world is will to power.

<KeyFact>
BGE 36 establishes a conditional: if will is the only given and the only causality, then the world reads as will to power. Its author supposes the antecedent, asserts only the psychology, and closes BGE 22 by calling the result "only an interpretation"; the cosmological doctrine has, in the published works, the status of a hypothesis offered under a stated method and never an assertion.
</KeyFact>

## The doctrine that survives: satisfaction requires resistance

The argument's one asserted clause is "my proposition", that our entire drive-life is the organization and outgrowth of one basic form of will. Stated of the drives and of nothing else, that is the psychological doctrine, and it is what BGE 23 had already called psychology's subject, "the doctrine of the development of the will to power". What the clause says is not yet fixed by its words. "Power" could mean domination over others, the capacity to get what one wants, or growth; "will" is, after BGE 19, a name for a society and not a faculty. Reginster's analysis (2006, ch. 3) fixes the content by deriving it from the problem Nietzsche took the doctrine to answer, and the derivation runs from Schopenhauer.

### From Schopenhauer's pendulum to the jail cell

Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860), whose *World as Will and Representation* (1818, expanded 1844) Nietzsche read at twenty-one and argued against for the rest of his life, held that willing is life's whole essence and that the basis of all willing is lack, hence pain. The premise Reginster isolates is a second observation of Schopenhauer's, in WWR I §57: when the will "lacks objects of willing, because it is at once deprived of them again by too easy a satisfaction, a fearful emptiness and boredom come over it", so that life "swings like a pendulum to and fro between pain and boredom". Nietzsche presents will to power as the replacement for Schopenhauer's will to life: "Only where life is, is there also will; but not will to life, instead, thus I teach you, will to power!" (Z II, "On Self-Overcoming").

<Example title="from the pendulum to the three conditions (Reginster 2006, 3.I.6 and 3.II.1)">
**The pendulum.** Unsatisfied desire is pain; satisfied desire leaves boredom. Boredom is not the absence of an obligation but the absence of an inclination: the bored person "has nothing to do" because nothing engages the will. Boredom is therefore "a desire in search of an object, a desire to desire again", and its existence shows that beside the first-order desires for determinate objects there is a **second-order desire**, a desire whose object is a desire. The pendulum follows: satisfying the first-order desires frustrates the second-order one, and vice versa, so both cannot be satisfied at once.

**The jail cell.** A bare desire to have desires does not explain boredom. One can be bored in a jail cell while wanting very much to get out; the desire is present and the boredom is too. What the prisoner lacks is not a desire but something to do about it: the desire to get out "has become unable to give us something to do, since there is nothing we can do to satisfy it". The second-order desire is therefore a desire not merely to have desires but to **pursue** them.

**The unchallenging pursuit.** One can also be bored while pursuing a desire, if the pursuit consists only of unchallenging activity. So the desire is more specifically a desire to confront **resistance** in the pursuit of a determinate desire. And the resistance counts only if the first-order desire is real: an obstacle to an end one does not have is no resistance at all.

**The overcoming.** One occasionally wants desires one is powerless to satisfy, but most commonly one wants not only to confront resistance but to overcome it. "Since power is what we experience in the successful overcoming of resistance, Nietzsche calls 'will to power' this desire for the overcoming of resistance in the pursuit of determinate desires."

**The published anchor.** A §2: "What is happiness? The feeling that power increases, that a resistance is overcome." The definition Reginster derives is the one Nietzsche's own definition of happiness states, and GM I:13's description of strength as "a thirst for enemies and resistances and triumphs" is the same content in the *Genealogy*.
</Example>

### The three conditions and the paradox

The result of the derivation is a definition with three parts, which Reginster states as conditions of satisfaction and which the course will carry under his name.

<Remark title="Reginster's three conditions (2006, p. 136; 2007, p. 40)">
"The will to power will not be satisfied, therefore, unless three conditions are met: there is some first-order desire for a determinate end, there is resistance to the realization of this determinate end, and there is actual success in overcoming this resistance."

**(i) A determinate first-order end.** The will to power "gets a determinate content only from its relation to some determinate desire": a recalcitrant puzzle is resistance to the desire to understand, an opposing player to the desire to win. Without an end there is no resistance and nothing to overcome.

**(ii) Resistance to it.** Not as a means that a shorter route would make dispensable, but as what is willed: the will to power "is never satisfied unless it has opponents and resistance", it is "a thirst for enemies".

**(iii) Its overcoming.** Not a standing struggle either; there is no "expansion, incorporation, growth" unless the striving eventually succeeds. Power is "the very activity of overcoming resistance".

**Second-order.** The doctrine says how an end is pursued, not which end. It is compatible with any first-order desire that meets resistance, which is its strength as psychology and its weakness as ethics, examined below.

**Self-perpetuating.** Satisfying (iii) removes (ii): the overcoming eliminates the resistance whose presence was a condition of satisfaction. "The satisfaction of the will to power implies its own dissatisfaction, in the sense that it necessarily brings it about."
</Remark>

<Example title="the game won (Reginster 2007, pp. 40–41)">
Two players sit down to a game. Condition (i): each wants to win, and the wanting is real; a player who did not care about winning would not be playing. Condition (ii): the other player's strength is the resistance; a game against an opponent who cannot play offers none, and the will to power is not engaged. Condition (iii): one player wins. At that moment the winner's will to power is satisfied: an end was held, a resistance met, the resistance overcome.

Now apply the loop. The victory has removed the resistance; the game is over; the desire to play, which was the desire for the overcoming of this resistance in pursuit of this end, has nothing left to act on. The winner cannot repeat the game for the same satisfaction: "since the resistance to doing it has been overcome already, overcoming it again would no longer count as genuine achieving". What is needed is a new opponent or a stronger one. "Alas, who was not defeated in his victory!" (Z III, "On Old and New Tablets" §30). The two paradoxes come apart here. The weak one: satisfying the will to power requires an unsatisfied first-order desire, since a satisfied desire meets no resistance. The strong one: satisfying the will to power removes a condition of its own satisfaction. Neither makes the pursuit self-defeating; the will to power is plainly satisfiable, by winning. What they show is that it "does not allow for permanent (once-and-for-all) satisfaction", and that its pursuit "necessarily assumes the form of an indefinite, perpetually renewed striving".
</Example>

The two paradoxes are the doctrine's link to the rest of Nietzsche's positive philosophy, and the course collects them here for use later. If satisfaction requires resistance, then suffering, which Schopenhauer defined as the will's hindrance by an obstacle, is an ingredient of the highest good and not its price, which <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the nihilism lesson</Ref> uses to state what affirmation affirms. And if satisfaction removes its own condition, the pursuit of power "assumes the form of growth, or self-overcoming", which is the word "Life" uses of itself in <Ref lesson="self-overcoming-staged">the module's last lesson</Ref>: "I am that which must always overcome itself".

### The rivals: capacity, mastery, and a constitutive aim

Three other analyses take "power" in the psychological doctrine differently, and each is a published position the reader should be able to name.

**Power as capacity (Clark 1990).** To keep the doctrine from being emptied by finding power everywhere, Clark defines power as "the ability to do or get what one wants", so that the will to power is "a second-order desire for the ability to satisfy one's other, or first-order, desires". This explains why every being with desires has it, since any desire "will naturally spawn a desire for the 'power' to satisfy it". Reginster's objection is that a desire for capacity can be fully satisfied, once the means are secured, so that the insatiability Nietzsche insists on becomes an accident of particular first-order desires rather than a feature of the will to power; and that the "thirst for enemies" has no place in it, since a capacity is as well satisfied by an unresisted route.

**Power as mastery (Richardson 1996).** John Richardson reads the doctrine as a claim about drives rather than persons, and power as a drive's *development*: its mastery over other drives, where mastery, unlike "tyranny", integrates the mastered drives' ends into its own and is thereby enriched by them. The drive to seduce enrolls the drive to know and the drive to please, and "mere seduction" then loses its appeal. Reginster grants that development happens and denies that it is what power is: on Richardson's account overcoming resistance is instrumental to development and would be dispensable if development came unresisted, whereas GM I:13's "thirst for enemies and resistances and triumphs" makes resistance what is willed. Development is a by-product of overcoming resistance, not its definition.

**Power as the constitutive aim of action (Katsafanas 2013).** Paul Katsafanas takes Reginster's analysis and adds a claim about agency: the overcoming of resistance is a condition of acting at all, so that anyone who acts is thereby committed to valuing power. The position, named here and not evaluated, is the psychological doctrine's route to a normative use, and <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the value-creation lesson</Ref> takes it up where the naturalist has to say how a value can be made.

Kaufmann's older reading, on which the power Nietzsche prizes is "primarily self-control", rescued the doctrine from "might makes right" at the cost of sanitizing it. Clark's objection is textual: nothing in Zarathustra's first statement of the doctrine, "On the Thousand and One Goals", where a people esteems what makes it "rule, and triumph, and shine to the envy of their neighbour", suggests that power over oneself would satisfy it, still less that self-overcoming has a privileged place. Self-overcoming enters the doctrine only when the wisest, priests and philosophers, connect their will to power to the ascetic ideal, and that is the *Genealogy*'s Third Treatise.

<Aside>
The doctrine's psychological content is older than its name. *Daybreak* §113 (1881), "The striving for excellence", reduces "practically all of human behaviour", in Kaufmann's phrase, to a single striving, and lays it out as a ladder. The striving for excellence is "the striving to overwhelm one's neighbour, even if only very indirectly or only in one's own feelings or even dreams", and a complete list of its degrees "would almost amount to a history of culture": "tortures, then blows, then terror, then anguished amazement, then wonder, then envy, then laughing, then ridicule, then derision, then scorn, then the dealing of blows, then the inflicting of tortures: here, at the end of the ladder, stands the ascetic and martyr". The barbarian who tortures his neighbour is at the bottom and the ascetic who tortures himself at the top, and "happiness, taken as the most alive feeling of power, has perhaps nowhere on earth been greater than in the souls of superstitious ascetics", the Brahmins' king Vishvamitra deriving from a thousand years of penance the strength to build a new heaven. Kaufmann reads the section as Nietzsche coming within reach of power as a measure of value and then stepping back, calling both ends of the ladder "grimaces" and placing culture somewhere in the middle. What the section already contains, in 1881, is the second-order structure: the ascetic's satisfaction is a resistance overcome, and the resistance is himself.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
On Reginster's analysis the will to power is not a desire for domination, capacity or growth but a desire for the activity of overcoming resistance in pursuit of some other end, so that the doctrine predicts a being deprived of all resistance to be not satisfied but bored, and a being that has won to be looking for a harder opponent.
</KeyFact>

## A §2 and BGE 259: the definitional use and the biological one

Two published passages use the doctrine in ways the psychological reading has to account for, and the course reads both verbatim because both are quoted, out of context, more than any others. The first is a definition.

<Quote source="A §2, trans. Hollingdale">
<Original lang="de">
Was ist gut? — Alles, was das Gefühl der Macht, den Willen zur Macht, die Macht selbst im Menschen erhöht. Was ist schlecht? — Alles, was aus der Schwäche stammt. Was ist Glück? — Das Gefühl davon, dass die Macht wächst, — dass ein Widerstand überwunden wird.
</Original>

What is good? – All that heightens the feeling of power, the will to power, power itself in man. What is bad? – All that proceeds from weakness. What is happiness? – The feeling that power increases – that a resistance is overcome.
</Quote>

Three questions, three definitions, no argument. The third fixed the content of the psychological doctrine above: happiness is a resistance overcome, which is condition (iii) with (ii) built in. The first is the doctrine's evaluative use, and it is the place where <Ref lesson="antichrist-applied-genealogy">the *Antichrist* lesson</Ref> found the is–ought slide and left it: "all that heightens the feeling of power" is what humans seek, on the psychological doctrine, and A §2 says it is what is good. The standard now has a name, the feeling of power, and the bridge from "sought" to "good" still has no defence in the text.

<Figure
  src="./figures/a2-three-definitions.svg"
  alt="A grid with A §2's three definitions as rows and Reginster's three conditions as columns. What is good, all that heightens the feeling of power, is tagged evaluative and fixes none of the conditions. What is bad, all that proceeds from weakness, is tagged evaluative and touches condition three only by negation. What is happiness, the feeling that power increases, that a resistance is overcome, is tagged descriptive and fixes conditions two and three, with condition one implied since a resistance is to something.   caption="Only the third answer has content the psychological doctrine can use; the first borrows its term and adds the word good, and the arrow between them is the step no sentence in the passage takes."
/>

The missing premise has a documented objection and a documented reply. Brian Leiter calls the bridge, in its most direct form, "Millian": Mill argued that the only proof that something is desirable is that people desire it, and an argument from "everything seeks power" to "power is good" has that form and that flaw. Leiter's textual point is that Nietzsche takes human beings to have a diversity of first-order aims, which the Millian argument cannot admit; the philosophical point is that Schopenhauer's pessimism is the standing counterexample, since the fact that all willing seeks something may be exactly what makes existence bad. Nadeem Hussain's reply (2011) is that no inference is needed if the position is an ethical naturalism: growth, strength and the overcoming of resistance are good for us because they are what we are, our basic tendencies, and we are "inescapably in their grip". What the reply leaves open is that inescapability does not by itself make a condition good: one may be condemned to it. The course names the standard here and defers the defence to <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the value-creation lesson</Ref>.

### Poellner's objection: the doctrine permits most of what Nietzsche rejects

Before the standard can be used, the psychological doctrine meets an objection from its own structure. Peter Poellner (2012) accepts Reginster's analysis and draws its consequence: a second-order desire "cannot supply a principle from which all reasons for action can be derived", since it "gets a determinate content only from its relation to some determinate desire", and can at most be "a standard which our other values need to be consistent with". The standard rules out very little.

| Value systems the will to power excludes | Value systems it permits |
|---|---|
| Schopenhauerian will-lessness: desiring nothing, "inactive repose" | Ascetic Christianity: the Puritan suspicious even of leisure, striving against the flesh |
| Any ideal of a state with all resistance removed: Schopenhauer's happiness as the satisfaction of all desires | Kantian duty: acting against inclination is resistance overcome |
| The last human being who "invented happiness" and left "the regions where it was hard to live" (Z Prologue §5) | Utilitarian reform: any project meeting resistance in pursuit of the general welfare |

The right-hand column contains most of what the *Genealogy* attacks. Ascetic self-torture is, on the doctrine, a resistance overcome, and *Daybreak* §113 said so before the doctrine had its name; the ascetic priest's remedies in <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref> were each analysed as a small feeling of power. So "what is good? all that heightens the feeling of power" needs a further premise to prefer any of the *Genealogy*'s heroes to its villains, a premise about which ends are worth overcoming resistance for, and Poellner's point is that the doctrine, being second-order, cannot supply it. Nietzsche's ideal, he adds, "is not simply an analogue of body-building": the worth of an overcoming depends on what it overcomes and what it is toward, and that is a first-order judgment. Katsafanas's constitutivism is the attempt to get more out of the standard; the higher-type lesson supplies the first-order content, pathos of distance and sublimation, that the standard on its own lacks.

### BGE 259: "exploitation" as organic function

The second passage is the one that states the doctrine in its harshest vocabulary, and the register does not soften it.

<Quote source="BGE 259, trans. Norman">
<Original lang="de">
Leben selbst ist wesentlich Aneignung, Verletzung, Überwältigung des Fremden und Schwächeren, Unterdrückung, Härte, Aufzwängung eigner Formen, Einverleibung und mindestens, mildestens, Ausbeutung, — aber wozu sollte man immer gerade solche Worte gebrauchen, denen von Alters her eine verleumderische Absicht eingeprägt ist? […] Die „Ausbeutung" gehört nicht einer verderbten oder unvollkommnen und primitiven Gesellschaft an: sie gehört in's Wesen des Lebendigen, als organische Grundfunktion, sie ist eine Folge des eigentlichen Willens zur Macht, der eben der Wille des Lebens ist. — Gesetzt, dies ist als Theorie eine Neuerung, — als Realität ist es das Ur-Faktum aller Geschichte: man sei doch so weit gegen sich ehrlich! —
</Original>

Life itself is essentially a process of appropriating, injuring, overpowering the alien and the weaker, oppressing, being harsh, imposing your own form, incorporating, and at least, the very least, exploiting, — but what is the point of always using words that have been stamped with slanderous intentions from time immemorial? […] "Exploitation" does not belong to a corrupted or imperfect, primitive society: it belongs to the essence of being alive as a fundamental organic function; it is a result of genuine will to power, which is just the will of life. — Although this is an innovation at the level of theory, — at the level of reality, it is the primal fact of all history. Let us be honest with ourselves to this extent at least! —
</Quote>

The section argues by contrast: refraining from injury and exploitation can be "good manners between individuals" of similar force within one body, and becomes "the will to negate life" the moment it is made "the fundamental principle of society", because a living body must do to other bodies what its members refrain from doing to each other. This is the biological reading in the published text, and it is asserted: "the primal fact of all history" is the section's evidence for itself, and "let us be honest" is its argument. Two things are established about it. First, the passage is where the political misreading of the doctrine, power as the licence to dominate, takes its text, and the sentence that blocks the misreading is in the passage: exploitation is "a *result* of genuine will to power", a consequence of its pursuit, which Reginster reads as the section's own distinction between what the pursuit of power brings about and what it consists of. Second, the claim is stated of "a living body" and "every healthy aristocracy" in one breath, so that the biological doctrine and the aristocratic politics are asserted together; how much of the biology the positive ideal actually needs is the question <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref> asks of BGE 257–259.

<KeyFact>
A §2 and BGE 259 are the two passages that state the doctrine without arguing it, and each is misread by being taken for what it is not: the definition for a derivation of value, the exploitation sentence for the doctrine's content. The sentence that blocks each misreading is in the passage itself: A §2's third answer defines the standard descriptively, so the first answer's "good" is added and not derived, and BGE 259 calls exploitation "a result of" will to power, so what the pursuit brings about is not what it consists of.
</KeyFact>

## The line: what the genealogy uses, and what only the cosmology would give

The module's question was whether the naturalist reading can keep the will to power it has been using since the Second Treatise, and the answer is now a matter of checking each use against the three readings. The rule the course took from <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the method lesson</Ref> is GM II:12's, and it is stated in the doctrine's terms.

<Quote source="GM II:12, trans. Samuel">
<Original lang="de">
Aber alle Zwecke, alle Nützlichkeiten sind nur Anzeichen davon, dass ein Wille zur Macht über etwas weniger Mächtiges Herr geworden ist und ihm von sich aus den Sinn einer Funktion aufgeprägt hat.
</Original>

But all ends and all utilities are only signs that a Will to Power has mastered a less powerful force, has impressed thereon out of its own self the meaning of a function.
</Quote>

What the rule needs is a will that masters a thing and imposes a function: condition (i), an end; (ii), a thing that resists; (iii), its overcoming. The sentence's "in the organic world" widens the scope, and the section's later attack on Herbert Spencer, the English philosopher whose utilitarian account of "good" <Ref lesson="master-and-slave-morality">the First Treatise had answered</Ref> and whose *Principles of Biology* (1864) defined life as the continuous adjustment of inner relations to outer ones, asserts the biological doctrine in passing: II:12 quotes that definition, "life itself as an ever more purposive inner adaptation to external circumstances", and calls "adaptation" "an activity of the second rank, a mere reactivity", because it leaves out the "spontaneous, aggressive, expansive, form-giving forces" on whose action adaptation only follows; but the rule's work in the *Genealogy*, on punishment, on the eye and the hand, on the eleven meanings of one procedure, uses only the psychology. The same holds at every point the treatises invoked the phrase. III:15's priest, who turns "someone is to blame" back on the sufferer, exercises "the will to power of the very weakest" over the healthy: a resistance overcome by a sentence. III:18's neighbour-love is "the smallest superiority", the priest's dose of the strongest drive for those who cannot take a larger one. II:18's "instinct for freedom (in my language: the will to power)" that builds bad conscience is the same force that "builds states", turned on "the whole old animal self" as its resisting material. And I:13's birds of prey, whose strength is "a will to overpower, a will to throw down, a will to become master, a thirst for enemies and resistances and triumphs", are the three conditions in the treatise's own words.

On the other side of the line stand three things the genealogy never uses. The indicative "the world is will to power and nothing besides", which exists only in the notebook and in "Life"'s speech. One causality, will acting on will, with matter as "a pre-form of life", which is BGE 36's hypothesis at steps 4 and 6. And value written into being: the reading on which "what is good?" is answered by reading off what everything is, so that A §2 would be a discovery about the world rather than a definition. Nothing in the three treatises' reconstructed arguments, as the course's scorecards recorded them, cites any of these, and each of them would need BGE 36 to assert what it supposes.

Two readers divide at the line, and the division is the course's last module. The naturalist reading, Kaufmann's, Clark's and Reginster's, keeps the left column and reads the right as Nietzsche's overreach, flagged by his own moods; Clark's account of why he overreached is that he "generalized and glorified" the drive he valued most, picturing life and the cosmos as its expression, and her textual evidence that he knew the difference is BGE 44, which says that under prolonged danger the human "life-will had to be changed into an unconditional power-will", a sentence that makes a power-will something developed and not something that comes with life. Heidegger keeps the right column. In the first volume of his lectures (1936–37) he writes that for Nietzsche "will is nothing else than will to power, and power nothing else than the essence of will", so that "will to power is will to will", and that "every willing is a willing to be more"; on his reading will as "the pervasive character of all beings" cannot be explained by "a particular being or special mode of Being", psychology included, and the psychological reading has mistaken a special case for the whole. That reading is stated at strength in <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref> and its charge against naturalism in <Ref lesson="alleged-biologism">the biologism lesson</Ref>; here it is only placed.

### Established, and still open

What is established. The doctrine has three readings nested by scope, and the published works define and use it psychologically, assert it biologically in BGE 13 and 259, and argue for it cosmologically once, in BGE 36, in the conditional. BGE 13's removal of the self-preservation drive is a razor applied to one teleological posit and not to the other, and its positive premise, discharge as what a living thing wants above all, is asserted with anecdotal support. BGE 36 in seven steps has one asserted clause, the psychological one; its first premise needs the simple, known, acting will that BGE 16 to 19 dissolved, its sixth needs the causality of the will that GS 127 and *Twilight* deny, and its conclusion is "would be": the argument assumes its conclusion in its premise, and its author says so by his moods and by BGE 22's "only an interpretation". The doctrine that survives is Reginster's: a determinate end, resistance to it, and the overcoming of the resistance, a second-order and self-perpetuating desire, anchored in A §2's definition of happiness and GM I:13's "thirst for enemies", against Clark's capacity, Richardson's mastery and Kaufmann's self-control. Its evaluative use in A §2 is a definition with a missing premise, Leiter's Millian objection and Hussain's naturalist reply on record, and Poellner's under-determination shows the standard permits most of what the *Genealogy* condemns. Every use of the doctrine in the three treatises is on the psychological side of the line.

What stands open is the other side. The cosmological reading needs a "world seen from inside", an "intelligible character" of things, and BGE 36's own vocabulary for that is Kant's. Whether Nietzsche still had a world behind the interpreted one for the doctrine to be true of is the question of <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">the next lesson</Ref>, which reads the six sentences in *Twilight* where the "true world" is abolished and the apparent world with it, and Clark's account of why, after that, "interpretation" could no longer mean "false".

<Exercise>
Using only the three conditions, derive the strong paradox: that satisfying the will to power brings about its own dissatisfaction. State which condition makes the will to power second-order. Then apply the doctrine to a being for whom no resistance is available, the last human being of *Zarathustra*'s Prologue §5, who says "we invented happiness" and has "abandoned the regions where it was hard to live", and state what the doctrine predicts about that being's satisfaction and why.

<Solution>
**The derivation.** Satisfaction requires (i) a determinate end, (ii) resistance to it, and (iii) success in overcoming the resistance. Suppose all three are met at a time t. Then at t the resistance has been overcome, that is, eliminated: (iii) entails that (ii) no longer holds at t. But (ii) is a necessary condition of satisfaction. So the state that satisfies the will to power is a state in which one of its conditions of satisfaction has just ceased to hold, and the will to power is, at t, again unsatisfied with respect to that end. Satisfaction "necessarily brings about" dissatisfaction; nothing further is assumed. The weak paradox needs only (i) and (ii): a will to power that is engaged has a first-order desire that is not satisfied, since a satisfied desire meets no resistance.

**Second-order.** Condition (i). The will to power has no content of its own; a resistance is a resistance only relative to an end one desires, so the will to power is a desire whose object includes another desire. Conditions (ii) and (iii) say what is wanted of that desire's pursuit; (i) says there must be one.

**The last human being.** On the doctrine a being with no resistance available is not in the state A §2 calls happiness, since happiness is "the feeling that power increases, that a resistance is overcome" and condition (ii) is unmet. The doctrine predicts the pendulum's other pole: not pain but boredom, the jail cell without the wish to leave. The last human being's "happiness" is Schopenhauer's, the removal of resistance, and Zarathustra's description matches the prediction, warmth sought by rubbing against a neighbour, "a bit of poison once in a while", "one has one's little pleasure for the day and one's little pleasure for the night". The being has a determinate end only in the form of small comforts and has left the regions where anything resists; by (ii) the will to power cannot be satisfied and by the pendulum the being is bored, which is what "blinking" stages. The overman of the same Prologue is the contrast the doctrine needs, and <Ref lesson="overman-staged">the overman lesson</Ref> reads the two figures against each other.
</Solution>

Suppose only the psychological doctrine is true and nothing about organisms in general or about the world. Take four reconstructed arguments the course has run, ressentiment in GM I:10, reinterpretation in GM II:12, the philosopher's asceticism in GM III:7 to 8, and the priest's redirection in GM III:15, and state for each whether it still goes through on that supposition. **I:10, ressentiment.** Goes through. The argument needs a drive to revenge that is blocked from discharge and commands by a valuation instead; that is a first-order end (revenge), a resistance (impotence), and a substitute overcoming (the judgment "you are evil"). Nothing about non-human life or the cosmos is used.

**III:7 to 8, the philosopher.** Goes through. The claim is that the ascetic ideal is, for the philosopher, the condition under which his drive to knowledge reaches "maximal power": marriage, fame and possessions are resistances that would consume the drive, and asceticism is the removal of competing ends so that one end can be pursued against the resistance that matters. This is condition (i) with a ranking of ends, and it is psychology.

**III:15, the priest.** Goes through. **II:12, reinterpretation.** Goes through as a rule about human institutions and would need more as stated. The rule's work in the *Genealogy*, on punishment, on custom, on law, uses only the psychology: a will that masters a practice and imposes a function is a human will with an end and a resistance. Count the appearances of *Wille zur Macht* in Parts One and Two of *Beyond Good and Evil* (§§1–44) yourself, using the eKGWB text at nietzschesource.org, and classify each by domain: philosophy, life, physics or the psyche. Report which appearances are in a sentence whose main verb is indicative and states the doctrine, and say what the tally shows about Clark's claim that the four sustained sections are §§9, 22, 23 and 36.

<Solution>
**What to expect.** The phrase occurs in §§9, 13, 22, 23, 36 and 44 in the first two parts. §9: philosophy is "the most spiritual will to power": the domain is philosophy, the sentence is indicative and states the doctrine of philosophy, not of the world. §13: "life itself is will to power": domain life, indicative, a premise in a razor argument. §22: an interpreter "would show the unequivocal and unconditional nature of all 'will to power'": domain physics, the verb is conditional ("could come along", "would show"), and the section ends by calling the result "only an interpretation". §23: psychology as "the morphology and the doctrine of the development of the will to power": domain the psyche, indicative, a programme statement about what psychology is. §36: domain the world, and every governing verb but one (the demand of method) is conditional or interrogative. §44: the "life-will" changed "into an unconditional power-will": domain the human type, indicative, and it says a power-will is developed under danger rather than given with life.

<Exercise>
A §2 defines the good as "all that heightens the feeling of power". Write, as a numbered argument, the inference from the psychological doctrine to that definition, with the premise it needs made explicit. Then state Leiter's objection to that premise and Hussain's reply, and say which of the two the text of A §2 and BGE 259 supports.

<Solution>
**The argument.** P1: Human beings seek, above all, the overcoming of resistance in the pursuit of their ends; the feeling of power is the feeling of that overcoming (the psychological doctrine; A §2's third definition). P2 (the missing premise): What human beings fundamentally seek is thereby good for them. C: Whatever heightens the feeling of power is good (A §2's first definition).

**Leiter's objection.** P2 has the form of Mill's proof that pleasure is desirable because it is desired: it moves from "sought" to "good" by no further step. Two things fail. Textually, Nietzsche credits human beings with many first-order aims, so "all willing seeks power" as a first-order claim is not his; the Millian premise needs a single fundamental aim. Philosophically, Schopenhauer is the counterexample Nietzsche knew best: all willing seeks satisfaction, and Schopenhauer concluded that existence is therefore bad, so the inference from "inescapably sought" to "good" is exactly what pessimism denies.

**Hussain's reply.** Read as an ethical naturalism, the position needs no inference: growth, strength and the overcoming of resistance are good for us because they are our basic tendencies and we are inescapably in their grip, and many nineteenth-century anti-supernaturalists argued the same way. The reply concedes that the diversity of first-order ends is real and locates the value in the manner of pursuit, which is where Reginster's second-order analysis puts the doctrine.

**What the text supports.** A §2 is a definition and gives neither; it asserts C. BGE 259 supports Hussain's form of the claim more than Leiter's target: its argument is that a principle contrary to life's basic function is "the will to negate life", that is, that what opposes the inescapable tendency is bad because it opposes it, which is the naturalist inference and not the Millian one. What neither passage supplies is an answer to the pessimist's reply, that being in the grip of a tendency does not make the tendency good; the gap stands in both passages, and <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the value-creation lesson</Ref> is where the course asks whether anything in Nietzsche closes it.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
BGE 36's step 4 says the mechanistic world is to be understood not as an illusion but as "a more primitive form of the world of affect", and BGE 22 says physics is "only interpretation" and answers the objection with "so much the better". For "interpretation" to mean something other than "false", state what Nietzsche must already have rejected, and find the section of *Twilight of the Idols* that stages that rejection in six numbered sentences. The solution names the lesson that reads it.

<Solution>
For "only an interpretation" not to mean "false", there must be no text behind the interpretations that they could fail to match: no world as it is in itself, against which the physicist's "conformity to law" and Nietzsche's "will to power" would both be measured and both found wanting. Step 4 says the same in its own terms: the mechanistic world is not "a deception, a 'mere appearance', a 'representation' in the sense of Berkeley and Schopenhauer", so the drives' world and the material world are on "the same plane of reality" and neither is the appearance of a truer one. What must already be rejected is the thing-in-itself, and with it the two-world picture on which one world is real and the other apparent. On BGE 22's reading of physics, if there were a true world the will-to-power interpretation would be a candidate for correspondence with it and would have to be argued true; without one, it is a reading of the same text as the physicist's, and "so much the better" is the consistent reply.

The *Twilight* section is "How the 'Real World' at last Became a Fable: History of an Error", six numbered stages from Plato's attainable true world to the abolition of the true world and, in the sixth sentence, of the apparent world with it: "with the real world we have also abolished the apparent world". That sixth stage is what makes step 4's "not an illusion" available, and <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">the next lesson</Ref> reconstructs the ladder and Clark's account of why, after it, Nietzsche stopped calling our beliefs false.
</Solution>

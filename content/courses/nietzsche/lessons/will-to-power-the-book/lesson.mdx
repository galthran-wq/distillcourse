The book titled The Will to Power, cited as Nietzsche's system by Heidegger and by the Nazi-era editors alike, was assembled after his collapse by his sister and Peter Gast from notebook fragments that the critical edition now prints in the order he wrote them. This lesson shows that from the fragments: the title as Nietzsche used it, the outlines he drafted under it and abandoned, the plan the editors chose as a skeleton, and what a premise taken from the result lacks that a premise taken from a published section has.

<Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">The previous lesson</Ref> fixed the method: a corpus of twelve dated books, the numbered section as the unit, a five-step protocol, and Clark's evidence rule, on which the published works ground a claim and the notebooks corroborate and date but never ground. It left one thing unargued, which is why the rule has to be as strict as Clark makes it. The reason is the one "book" that a century of readers cited as if it were the twelfth, and this lesson takes it apart with the edition's own sigla. What it contributes to the course's argument is a boundary: every premise the later modules reconstruct will be located in a section Nietzsche saw through the press, and where a famous sentence has no such location the reconstruction will say so. What it hands on is the *Genealogy*, which <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the next lesson</Ref> reads as the authored, sequenced argument the compilation is not.

The route: the edition as an artefact that prints the notebooks in writing order; the title *Der Wille zur Macht* traced through the notebooks from 1885 to its abandonment in 1888; the making of the 1901 and 1906 books; the protocol run on the compilation's most quoted sentence; a case of three deleted words; the rule deployed on the whole; and, starred, the political appropriation the same editorial hands enabled, answered from sentences Nietzsche printed.

## The artefact: the critical edition prints the notebooks in the order he wrote them

The evidence rule of the last lesson ranked the notebooks fifth of six categories, and the ranking presupposes that a notebook fragment can be located: that it has a date and neighbours the way a section of *Beyond Good and Evil* has a number and a part. The edition that makes that true is the one the course cites.

The *Kritische Gesamtausgabe* begun by Giorgio Colli and Mazzino Montinari in 1967, and its digital form, the eKGWB at nietzschesource.org, prints every surviving notebook entry as a numbered fragment inside a numbered notebook group, in the order of the manuscripts: `NF-1885,38[12]` is the twelfth fragment of the thirty-eighth group of 1885, and the fragment before it and after it are what Nietzsche wrote before and after. That is the whole of the arrangement. There is no thematic order in the manuscripts, no chapter titled "will to power as knowledge" and no chapter titled "society and state", because Nietzsche never made one; the *Nachlass* (the literary remains, the course's name for the notebook category from here on) is a chronology and nothing else.

### The corrections: a record of what earlier printings got wrong

The second thing the edition does is record what earlier printings got wrong. About 6,600 corrections from the commentary volumes of the print edition are marked inline: a word the editors of 1895 or 1906 misread, a comma moved, a heading printed that the manuscript does not carry. The site's documentation gives the reason in one sentence, that where "a comma was moved, or one letter of a word has been wrongly deciphered … the meaning of the entire passage changes significantly". For the reader of this lesson the corrections matter as a standing demonstration: the sentences of the *Nachlass* reached print through a sequence of readings, each of which could be wrong, and the edition shows where the earlier ones were.

## "Der Wille zur Macht": a title written many times, a book never written

With the artefact in hand, the first question is what the phrase *Der Wille zur Macht* is in the notebooks. The expectation a reader brings from the 1906 book is that it names a work in progress, drafted over years and cut short by the collapse. The notebooks show something else: a heading, written over lists of chapter titles that change every time it is written, and dropped in the last autumn Nietzsche wrote.

### 1885: a heading, an outline, and a formula on one page

The heading appears in notebook group 2 of 1885 more than a dozen times, over outlines of four lines or forty. One of the shortest is fragment 2[74]:

<Quote source="NF-1885,2[74]">
<Original lang="de">
Der Wille zur Macht. 1. Physiologie der Rangordnung. 2. Der große Mittag. 3. Zucht und Züchtung. 4. Die ewige Wiederkunft.
</Original>

The Will to Power. 1. Physiology of the order of rank. 2. The great noon. 3. Discipline and breeding. 4. The eternal recurrence.
</Quote>

Four titles, no text. Two fragments in the same group show what the heading was standing over. Fragment 2[189] separates two questions that the course will later find separated in the *Genealogy*: "the question of the origin of our valuations and tables of goods does not at all coincide with their critique, as is so often believed", however much "insight into some *pudenda origo*" (a shameful origin) lowers a thing's value for feeling. Fragment 2[190], the next entry, asks the critical question and answers it with the formula:

<Quote source="NF-1885,2[190]">
<Original lang="de">
was sind unsere Werthschätzungen und moralischen Gütertafeln selber werth? Was kommt bei ihrer Herrschaft heraus? Für wen? In Bezug worauf? — Antwort: für das Leben. Aber was ist Leben? Hier thut also eine neue bestimmtere Fassung des Begriffs „Leben" noth: meine Formel dafür lautet: Leben ist Wille zur Macht.
</Original>

what are our valuations and moral tables of goods themselves worth? What comes of their rule? For whom? In relation to what? Answer: for life. But what is life? Here a new, more definite version of the concept "life" is needed: my formula for it runs: life is will to power.
</Quote>

The question in the first sentence is the one the *Genealogy*'s Preface will ask two years later, in almost the same words: "the value of these values is itself first to be called into question". The formula in the last sentence is the one the 1906 book was named after. They stand two sentences apart on one undated page of 1885, and the page is the reason this lesson can hand off to the next: the compilation and the *Genealogy* were drafted from the same notebooks, and what Nietzsche did with the drafts differs. One set he wrote into a three-part treatise and published in 1887. The other he left as headings.

### 1887: the plan the editors would use

The plan that mattered afterwards is fragment 7[64] of the group the edition files under 1886, written in Nice and dated by Nietzsche himself:

<Quote source="NF-1886,7[64]">
<Original lang="de">
Der Wille zur Macht. Versuch einer Umwerthung aller Werthe. Erstes Buch. Der europäische Nihilismus. Zweites Buch. Kritik der höchsten Werthe. Drittes Buch. Princip einer neuen Werthsetzung. Viertes Buch. Zucht und Züchtung. entworfen den 17. März 1887, Nizza.
</Original>

The Will to Power. Attempt at a revaluation of all values. Book One. European nihilism. Book Two. Critique of the highest values. Book Three. Principle of a new valuation. Book Four. Discipline and breeding. Drafted 17 March 1887, Nice.
</Quote>

Four book titles and a subtitle; below them the fragment continues with a numbered note on nihilism and stops. Nothing in the group assigns any other fragment to any of the four books. The plan is one of many, and it is distinguished from the others by exactly two features that will matter in the next section: it is the shortest, and it is the only one that gives Book Four the title *Zucht und Züchtung*.

### 1888: four tables of contents in one notebook group

Notebook group 14 of 1888, from the spring of the last year, carries the heading over at least six outlines, and no two share a structure.

<Task>
Compare the four columns for what they have in common: the heading and nothing else. Then note which column contains the words "als Leben", and which the editors' Book Four title "Zucht und Züchtung". The first appears in two of the four; the second in none.

David Krell, the translator of Heidegger's Nietzsche lectures, catalogued the plans and titles Nietzsche projected for a major work between 1884 and 1889, leaving out the ones that merely repeat a predecessor, and counted about 140. That is more outlines than the five years contain months: not one plan interrupted by the collapse but a heading Nietzsche kept placing over new tables of contents, which is the behaviour of a writer who has not decided what the book is.

### Autumn 1888: the title dropped

The decision came in September 1888 and it was against the title. In a letter to Franz Overbeck from Sils-Maria on 14 September, Nietzsche reports "the first book of my *Revaluation of All Values*" half finished in final form, and names it: "the first book, between us, is called *the Antichrist*". By 26 November, writing to Paul Deussen from Turin, the four-book plan has become one book: "My *Revaluation of All Values*, with the main title *the Antichrist*, is finished."

The manuscript of *The Antichrist* carries two title pages. The earlier reads "Book One of the Revaluation of All Values"; on the later, "Revaluation of All Values" is crossed out and "A Curse on Christianity" written in. Krell records the same movement in the notebook plans of that autumn: the title *The Will to Power* disappears, the former subtitle takes its place, and the last plans name Book Four "Dionysos: Philosophy of Eternal Return".

<KeyFact>
The Will to Power is the title of a book Nietzsche gave up in the autumn of 1888; what he finished under the title that replaced it is *The Antichrist*, which his sister printed in 1895 as "Book One of The Will to Power", a description of a plan he had already crossed out.
</KeyFact>

## How the compilation was made: one discarded plan, 1,067 fragments by theme

The heading was abandoned in 1888; the book appeared in 1901. What happened between is a short editorial history, and the lesson states it only as far as the compilation needs it. Nietzsche collapsed in Turin in the first week of January 1889 and wrote nothing for publication afterwards; he died in August 1900. His sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, back from the German colony in Paraguay her late husband had founded, took control of the manuscripts, founded a Nietzsche-Archiv in Naumburg in 1894 and moved it to Weimar in 1896. Peter Gast, born Heinrich Köselitz, the musician who had taken Nietzsche's dictation and corrected his proofs for a decade, did the editing.

<Figure src="./figures/elisabeth-foerster-nietzsche-1894.jpg" float="left" width="380" alt="Photograph of Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche in 1894: a woman in a black mourning gown and a long dark veil, standing behind a stone balustrade with both hands resting on the ledge." caption="Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche in 1894, the year she founded the Nietzsche-Archiv at Naumburg. She chose the 1,067 fragments and the plan that ordered them." />

The first *Wille zur Macht* appeared in 1901 as volume XV of the collected works, with 483 numbered fragments. It was based, as Heidegger's account of the book records, on the plan of 17 March 1887: the four titles of fragment 7[64] became the four books, and fragments were sorted under them by theme. A second edition in 1906 kept the plan and more than doubled the count, to 1,067, the new material mixed in with the old rather than appended; that edition was reprinted in 1911 as volumes XV and XVI of the *Grossoktav* edition, and Alfred Baeumler's Kröner pocket edition of 1930 reprinted it again. Every fragment number cited as "WM" in the century's scholarship is a number in this 1906 sequence.

Kaufmann's Prologue, written in 1950 when the compilation still stood on every shelf as a book of Nietzsche's, gives the two facts about the sorting that the notebooks confirm. The plan was chosen from among some two dozen because it "listed only the titles of the four projected parts and thus gave the editor the greatest possible freedom", and it "was also the only draft which suggested *Zucht und Züchtung* as the title of Part IV".

Nietzsche's own attempt to distribute some notes among the parts of a later, more detailed plan was ignored. And no attempt was made to distinguish notes he had already worked into the books written after *Zarathustra* from notes he had not used: a fragment that became a sentence of *Beyond Good and Evil* and a fragment he never returned to sit side by side under one heading with the same standing.

<Remark title="what is editorial and what is not">
The words of every fragment in the compilation are Nietzsche's; the edition's corrections change readings, not authorship. Three things are not his: the selection of 1,067 fragments from a *Nachlass* many times larger; their order, which follows the four titles of a plan he discarded; and the title of the whole, which he dropped in the autumn of 1888. A reader who rejects the compilation's arrangement as an argument rejects the three editorial facts and none of the words.
</Remark>

### Two readers of the same facts: Heidegger's text, Clark's exclusion

Two readers who agree on every fact above draw opposite conclusions from them, and the course needs both on record. Heidegger, lecturing at Freiburg between 1936 and 1940, states in the section of his first volume titled "The Book, The Will to Power" that "what Nietzsche himself published during his creative life was always foreground", that "his philosophy proper was left behind as posthumous, unpublished work", and that the text for his course is Baeumler's Kröner reprint, cited "by the fragment number which is standard in all editions". The compilation is his primary text, by decision.

Clark, whose rule the course adopted, excludes it as ground for the reason the last lesson gave: the notes are rearrangeable, and the published books supply checks the notes cannot. The course does not adjudicate here; it records that the reading it meets in <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref> is built on this base, and that the base is what this section has described.

## What quoting it does to an argument: WP 1067 against BGE 36

The last lesson ended with an exercise that ran step 4 of the protocol on the compilation's most quoted sentence, the last sentence of its last fragment, and found the published passage closest to it. This section completes that run with the fact the exercise could not use: the fragment's date.

The fragment is `NF-1885,38[12]`, written in June or July 1885. It opens as an address, "And do you know what 'the world' is to me? Shall I show it to you in my mirror?", and runs for a page through a cosmology: a fixed quantity of force "without beginning, without end", "a sea of forces storming and flooding in themselves, eternally changing, eternally running back", blessing itself "as what must eternally return". It closes:

<Quote source="NF-1885,38[12] (WP 1067)">
<Original lang="de">
Diese Welt ist der Wille zur Macht — und nichts außerdem! Und auch ihr selber seid dieser Wille zur Macht — und nichts außerdem!
</Original>

This world is the will to power, and nothing besides! And you yourselves are this will to power, and nothing besides!
</Quote>

Two indicatives with exclamation marks, in the second person plural, in the voice of *Zarathustra*, whose fourth part Nietzsche was finishing that year. A year later he published *Beyond Good and Evil* 36, which reaches the same content by argument and states it in the subjunctive: the world "seen from inside" *would be* (*wäre*) will to power and nothing besides, at the end of a chain of three "suppose that" (*gesetzt, dass*) clauses. The notebook sentence came first and the conditional came second: the published text is the author's revision of the draft's mood, made when he chose what to print.

The 1906 editors printed the 1885 draft as fragment 1067, the last entry of Book Four, after the fragments on eternal recurrence. A reader of the compiled book therefore meets it as the book's conclusion, in the indicative, at the end of a sequence that reads as an ascent, and nothing on the page says that it predates the conditional by a year or that its author put it in a character's mouth.

<Example title="the protocol on WP 1067, with the date">
**Step 0, resolve the siglum.** WM 1067 is `NF-1885,38[12]`, dated June–July 1885, one year before BGE 36 (1886). The date comes from the edition; the compilation gives none.

**Step 1, the claim.** The world, and the reader, are will to power and nothing else. Carried by the last two sentences, indicative, exclamatory, addressed to "you".

**Step 2, the stated premises.** The fragment argues by description: a conserved quantity of force, a play of forces without an outside, eternal return of its configurations. Each is asserted, none is derived; the address form ("shall I show it to you in my mirror?") presents a vision rather than a proof.

**Step 3, the missing premise.** From "the world is a play of forces" to "the world is will to power" the fragment needs the premise that force is will, that every quantum of force is a striving. It is not stated here. BGE 36 states it as the hypothesis to be tried, that "all mechanical occurrence, insofar as a force is active in it, is will-force, will-effect".

**Step 4, the second passage.** BGE 36 supplies the argument the fragment lacks and withholds the mood the fragment has: it argues that "the conscience of method" obliges one to try the single-causality hypothesis, and concludes in the conditional. Verdict, as in the last lesson: the published passage backs the content and not the mood. What the date adds is the direction of revision. The indicative is the earlier text; the conditional is what its author did to it for print.

**Step 5, the opposite reading.** The strongest rival is that the conditional of BGE 36 is rhetorical caution and the fragment says what Nietzsche believed. Against it is the chronology just established, and the notebook context: the fragment is drafted in Zarathustra's voice, in the year of Zarathustra IV, and its cosmology of a conserved force is material Nietzsche worked into no published book. A reconstruction may cite BGE 36 for the hypothesis and the fragment as a draft that predates it; it may not cite WM 1067 to convert "would be" into "is".
</Example>

A premise drawn from the compilation has **no author-authorized location**: no section Nietzsche saw through the press asserts it, and a reconstruction that uses it has a premise its author never asserted. It has **no visible date**: WM 1067 reads as the end of a book and was written before the book's middle, so a reader cannot tell a draft from a revision or an early view from a late one. And it has **no author-chosen neighbours**: the fragments before and after it in the compilation were placed by an editor to make a sequence, and a sequence is an argument's shape. The three lacks are exactly what the eKGWB restores, which is why the course cites fragments by siglum and never by WM number.

### Two readers, one fragment: WM 617

The third lack, the editor's neighbours, has a smaller form: the editor's numbering makes a single sentence of a note quotable as the note. The fragment printed as WM 617, written in 1888, begins "Recapitulation: to stamp Becoming with the character of Being, that is the supreme will to power", and continues: "Twofold falsification, one by the senses, the other by the mind, in order to preserve a world of being, of perdurance, of equivalence, etc." Baeumler, in his 1931 book on Nietzsche as philosopher and politician, cites WM 617 as its first sentence and omits the second.

Quoted so, the note is a metaphysics: will to power imposes Being on Becoming. Quoted whole, the note says that the imposition is a falsification, and the metaphysics is what the note diagnoses. Heidegger, in the third volume, quotes the note in full and reads the second sentence; Krell's footnote there records that Heidegger elsewhere cites it as Baeumler does. The number "617" is what makes the truncation possible, since it names the note without showing it, and the truncation reverses the note's sense.

The same editorial hand filed a note of 1887 as WM 749 under the chapter "The Will to Power as Society and Individual", section "Society and State". The note begins "The princes of Europe should indeed consider carefully whether they can do without our support" and is, on Heidegger's reading, a note on truth as seduction, one of the "immoralists" who "do not even need to tell lies". The chapter title is the editors' guess from the first sentence. A reader working from the compiled book meets the note under a heading Nietzsche did not write, among notes he did not place beside it, and reads it as political before reading it at all.

## The philology of one word: what an editorial cut manufactures

The compilation's harm to an argument is a harm of arrangement. The same editors also cut, and a cut is a smaller object on which the harm can be seen whole: three words, one inference, one source. The case is Kaufmann's, made in 1950 and restated in his 1964 article on the "suppressed manuscripts".

Section 29 of *The Antichrist*, finished in September 1888 and printed by the Archiv in 1895, describes the psychological type of Jesus and rejects the word "hero" and the word "genius" for it. The sentence continues: "Speaking with the rigour of the physiologist, a quite different word would be more in place here: the word idiot" (*das Wort Idiot*). In every printing from 1895 to 1954 the last three words were absent, and nothing on the page marked the absence.

  name="idiot-case"

/>

<Task>
Step to the second frame and state what the inference needs in order to run: that the words were cut because they were unfit to print, and that unfitness to print is a sign of the writer's state. Then step to the third and name which of the two premises the source removes. It removes the first: a term borrowed from a novel and used six more times in the same book is a term, not a symptom.
</Task>

The inference was drawn in 1931. Josef Hofmiller, a respected critic, published the deleted words in the *Süddeutsche Monatshefte* to show that Nietzsche was insane when he wrote *The Antichrist*: the editors, on this reading, had hidden a sign of madness.

Kaufmann's answer is a source. Nietzsche had read Dostoevsky in 1887 and used "idiot" as a technical term for a physiological type, the one Dostoevsky drew as Prince Myshkin in *The Idiot*: an oversensitivity to every contact, a life that consists in not resisting. The word appears in five other sections of *The Antichrist* and in *Twilight*, in the same sense. The cut of 1895 removed the one occurrence where the term's target was Jesus; the critic of 1931 read the cut as a symptom; the source of 1950 shows a term. Neither the deletion nor the diagnosis was a reading of the text; both were readings of the gap.

### The three deletions, and the legend built on them

The Archiv's cuts to *The Antichrist* were three, and Kaufmann's article lists them all.

<Remark title="the Archiv's cuts to The Antichrist (Kaufmann 1964, §III)">
| Section | What was cut in 1895 | Restored | Kaufmann's reading of the motive |
|---|---|---|---|
| §29 | the three words "das Wort Idiot" | 1954 (Schlechta's edition) | the word looked like blasphemy or madness; it is Dostoevsky's term |
| §35 | the lines on the malefactor at the cross: "the words to the malefactor on the cross contain the whole evangel …" | 1954 | the quotation misassigns a Gospel line (the centurion's, Matthew 27:54) to the thief; Gast held the proofs and had authority to cut a slip |
| §38 | the word "young" before "prince" | in the Kröner pocket edition, decades before 1954 | the reference to the young Kaiser Wilhelm II was "possibly actionable" |
</Remark>

Every cut was made by editors protecting the book or themselves, and none changes a philosophical claim; Kaufmann's verdict on the whole set is that "it is misleading to speak of 'suppression'".

Yet a legend of suppressed manuscripts grew from exactly these cuts, culminating in the 1950s and 1960s in Erich Podach's editions of the works of 1888 and his charge that the Archiv had hidden the books' true state, and in the quarrel between Podach and Karl Schlechta, whose 1954–56 edition had restored the *Antichrist* cuts and had printed the *Nachlass* of the 1880s in place of the compilation. Kaufmann's article is a reply to Podach, and its point is symmetrical with the point of the "idiot" case: an editorial cut manufactures an interpretation in whichever direction the reader is already facing.

### One forgery with content

Two further cases close the section, because both concern the sentence the course is most careful with. The first is a letter. Toward the end of October 1888 Nietzsche wrote to his sister; the original was burnt, on the sister's own note, "at my mother's request, end of 1896", and what survives is her copy with omissions marked by dots. In the fourth paragraph the copy reads that the young Kaiser "pleases me more and more: his latest move is that he has taken a very sharp stand against anti-Semitism", then "Do likewise, my brave Llama!" (her nickname), then five dots.

In the published version the five dots were replaced by a sentence: "The will to power as a principle would surely make sense to him." Podach found it in the archive; Kaufmann reports it as the one documented case in which the Archiv's editing changed a philosophical claim, attaching Nietzsche's central term to the Kaiser in a sentence Nietzsche did not write.

### One page restored

The second is a page of *Ecce Homo*. Very shortly before the collapse Nietzsche sent his publisher a replacement for section 3 of the first chapter, "Why I am so wise", with instructions to substitute it. The page names, as "the deepest opposition to myself", "my mother and sister", calls kinship with them "a blasphemy against my divinity", and adds: "I confess that the deepest objection to the 'eternal recurrence', my properly abyssal thought, is always mother and sister." The Archiv printed the earlier version and the page stayed unprinted until the critical edition of 1969; Montinari's account of its history opened the first volume of *Nietzsche-Studien* in 1972.

Kaufmann's judgement of the passage is that "in his incipient insanity Nietzsche was clairvoyant when he saw the need to repudiate her publicly", and his judgement of the whole class of cases is a comparison: to discredit the late works by the writer's state is "uncomfortably like trying to discredit Van Gogh's late paintings by pointing out that he was not altogether sane when he created them". The rank of a finished work is judged from the finished work.

<KeyFact>
The direction of editorial error runs both ways: the cut of 1895 let a critic read a symptom into a term, and the forgery of the Kaiser letter put "the will to power as a principle" into a sentence Nietzsche never wrote; in both, the editor's page, not the author's, became the evidence.
</KeyFact>

## The rule deployed: the compiled book under the module's results

This lesson is the module's application, and the artefact it runs on is the edition itself, set against the compiled book. Three of the module's results are in use. The **corpus chronology** dates WM 1067 to 1885 and puts it before BGE 36, which reverses the order a reader of the compilation infers. The **published-works rule** places every fragment of the compilation on the fifth rung and the compilation itself on none, so that a WM number is never a location. And **step 4 of the protocol**, the second-passage check, is what converts a fragment into evidence: it corroborates BGE 36 and dates its draft, and it grounds nothing on its own.

The result reproduced is Kaufmann's and Montinari's, that *The Will to Power* is an editorial object. What this lesson adds to their demonstration is the route: the reader has seen the heading over its outlines, the plan chosen as a skeleton, the crossed-out title page, and one dated fragment moved to the end of a book, and can check each at a siglum.

Where the deployed practice departs from the rule is the century in between. From 1901 until the critical edition, the scholarship that shaped how Nietzsche is read grounded on the compilation, because it was the text there was. Heidegger's lectures, delivered to the compilation's fragment numbers, are the largest case and the one this course must evaluate; <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref> reads them by resolving each "WM, n" to its siglum and date before weighing the reading, and Kaufmann's 1968 translation of the compilation, which prints the approximate date of every note, is the tool the reader can use for the same conversion.

### Where the edition's own limits lie

The edition has its own limits, and the honest statement of the application names them. Montinari's readings of "Nietzsche's often all but illegible notes", Kaufmann wrote after seeing the manuscripts in Weimar, "are more reliable than those of previous editors, although in places I am led to wonder whether we can really be sure what Nietzsche meant", since Nietzsche "so often did not finish words". The notebook order is a chronology of manuscripts, established by editors from paper and ink, not a diary with dates on every page.

The fragments' content resists the arrangement it now has as much as the one it lost: Krell cites Derrida's choice of a note from the same years, "I forgot my umbrella", as "a warning to all who trespass on the *Nachlass*", and Kaufmann's own sentence on the compilation is the one to carry away: "we look into a vast studio, full of sketches, drafts, abandoned attempts, and unfinished dreams. And in the end we should be less tempted than ever to mistake a random quotation for an ultimate position."

<Aside>
The corrections in the eKGWB are themselves a case of the rule at work at the scale of a letter. The capture the lesson's first figure shows is on the first section of *The Birth of Tragedy*: the print edition had "aesthetische", the manuscript has "ästhetische", and the site marks the change with its source, the *Nachberichte* of the print edition. Nothing philosophical turns on that one. The site's documentation gives the class of cases where something does: a moved comma, a misread letter, a heading the manuscript does not carry. The compilation's chapter titles are the largest members of that class, and the edition treats them the same way, by printing what the manuscript has and nothing it does not.
</Aside>

What is established: *Der Wille zur Macht* was a heading Nietzsche wrote over some 140 outlines in five years and dropped in the autumn of 1888; the book of that title was made in 1901 and 1906 by his sister and Peter Gast from 483, then 1,067, notebook fragments sorted by theme under the four titles of a plan of March 1887 he had discarded; the fragments' words are his and their selection, order and title are not; and a premise taken from the compilation is a premise without an author-authorized location, a visible date, or author-chosen neighbours, so that the course cites fragments by siglum and grounds nothing on them. The module's method is complete: a dated corpus, a unit, a protocol, an evidence rule, a working hypothesis, and the case that shows why the rule is strict.

What the method has not yet been used on is the book it was built for. Fragment 2[190] of 1885 asked what our valuations are themselves worth; the *Genealogy*'s Preface asks the same question in 1887 and answers that it needs a real history, and the Second Treatise states the rule that makes such a history possible. <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">The next lesson</Ref> reads those sections as the authored, sequenced argument the compilation is not.

## ★ The appropriation and its refutation from the texts

The same Archiv that made the compilation made a reading of it, and the reading had a politics. This section states the reading in three claims, as its authors made them, and answers each from a sentence Nietzsche printed or wrote, cited by siglum, without speculation about what he would have thought of his readers. The refutation is textual, and its limit is stated at the end.

### The Archiv reading: three claims

The reading's editor was Alfred Baeumler, whose Kröner pocket edition of the compilation appeared in 1930 and whose *Nietzsche der Philosoph und Politiker* followed in 1931. Its first claim is that the philosophy is a politics: will to power read as a formula for the state, and eternal recurrence, which cannot be made political, dismissed as "Egyptianism", a term Baeumler borrows from Nietzsche and turns against him, and as a "subjective", "personal", "religious" experience that, in Krell's summary of the chapter, any sound politician would suppress.

The second claim is that the "Rome against Judea" of the First Treatise is a racial verdict. Richard Oehler, the Archiv's co-editor of the collected works and compiler of their index, made it by quoting from *Human, All Too Human* I 475 the clause "perhaps the young stock-exchange Jew is the most disgusting invention of mankind", and stopping.

<Aside title="how far the second claim travelled">
Heinrich Härtle's *Nietzsche und der Nationalsozialismus* of 1937 supplied six references for the thesis that Nietzsche held the Jews responsible for Christianity, democracy and Marxism. Crane Brinton, a historian, carried the same six into his *Nietzsche* of 1941, including "G IV 26", a section of *Twilight* that does not exist; Kaufmann traced the footnote from one book to the other and found no reference checked on the way.
</Aside>

The third claim is the one this lesson has already answered: that *The Will to Power* is the system, and that the published books, in Baeumler's phrase, are a series of "poses".

### The state: the coldest monster, and the abyssal thought

To the first claim, *Zarathustra* I's speech "On the New Idol" (eKGWB `Za-I-Goetzen`): "State is the name of the coldest of all cold monsters. It even lies coldly, and this lie crawls out of its mouth: 'I, the state, am the people.'" The speech is a character's, and the course's rule for *Zarathustra* is to map it onto an argued thesis, not to cite it as a premise; but the claim being answered is a claim about the whole philosophy, and a philosophy whose dramatization calls the state the coldest monster is not a philosophy of the state.

To the same claim, on recurrence, the restored page of *Ecce Homo* names it "my properly abyssal thought" (*mein eigentlich abgründlicher Gedanke*): the author's rank for the doctrine his editor called a private feeling.

### The Jews: three sections read past the stopping point

To the second claim, three published sections. *Human, All Too Human* I 475, read past Oehler's stopping point, continues that one must ask "how much one must forgive a people … when they have had the most painful history of all peoples, not without the fault of all of us, and when one owes to them the noblest man (Christ), the purest sage (Spinoza), the most powerful book, and the most effective moral law of the world".

*Beyond Good and Evil* 251 calls the anti-Jewish feeling one of the "little stupors of the German spirit", names the Jews "without a doubt the strongest, purest, most tenacious race living in Europe today", and proposes to expel "the anti-Semitic screamers" (*die antisemitischen Schreihälse*) from the country.

The "Rome against Judea" of GM I:16 is introduced by Nietzsche as "the symbol of this struggle" (*das Symbol dieses Kampfes*) between the two valuations "good and bad" and "good and evil", a struggle the section says is now fought inside a single "higher nature"; the sentence that follows records Rome's defeat in a form no racial reading can carry, that Rome now bows "before three Jews and one Jewess". A reading that makes the symbol a race has to delete the sentence that says it is a symbol.

### The system: this lesson's evidence, and two letters under the sourcing pass

To the third claim, the evidence of this lesson, and two letters. A letter is not a numbered section, and the course reads a document of that kind by a three-question pass: what it *records* (a fact it reports), what it *argues* (a claim it makes) and what it *omits* (what its author's purpose leaves out); the fourth question, what its provenance lets it be used to establish, is the evidence ladder's and is answered by the rung.

The metadata come first. Theodor Fritsch, editor of the *Antisemitische Correspondenz*, a sheet sent privately to the movement's members, had posted Nietzsche three numbers of it in March 1887 with a letter calling his judgements on the Jews "skewed" (*schief*). Nietzsche's reply from Nice on 23 March counts antisemitism among the "idiosyncrasies" of "the present 'German spirit'" he is "too foreign to" to watch "without much impatience", and closes:

<Quote source="Letter to Theodor Fritsch, 23 March 1887 (BVN-1887,819)">
<Original lang="de">
und vielleicht dient es zu Ihrer Beruhigung, wenn ich zuletzt noch sage, daß ich unter meinen Freunden keinen Juden habe. Allerdings auch keine Antisemiten.
</Original>

and perhaps it will serve to reassure you if I say finally that I have no Jew among my friends. Admittedly no anti-Semites either.
</Quote>

A second letter, of 29 March, returns the three numbers, asks not to be sent more, and ends: "And finally, what do you think I feel when the name Zarathustra is taken into the mouths of anti-Semites?" The pass on it: it records that the papers went back; it argues that the movement is using a book against its author; it omits any view of the Jews as a people, because its purpose is to end a correspondence. The fourth question is the ladder's: a letter is on the sixth rung; it dates and it cannot ground. What these two date is a refusal, in writing, to the movement's press, in the month the plan of 17 March 1887 was drafted.

### Heidegger's answer to Baeumler: on other grounds

Heidegger's refutation of Baeumler is on other grounds and is recorded here because Module 7 needs it: his 1937 lectures on eternal recurrence reject Baeumler's exclusion of the doctrine and argue that will to power and eternal recurrence are one thought, so that a reading which keeps the first and drops the second has not read Nietzsche. That is a philosophical answer to the political reading, and Krell notes that Heidegger nonetheless praises Baeumler in his first course and shares his habit of quoting WM 617 by its first sentence. The relation between the two readers is a question for <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref>.

### The limit of the refutation

The refutation is textual and it does not make Nietzsche a liberal. BGE 251 ends its praise of the Jews with the sentence that "I am already touching on my seriousness, on the 'European problem' as I understand it, on the breeding of a new caste to rule Europe" (*die Züchtung einer neuen über Europa regierenden Kaste*). BGE 257 makes the pathos of distance between castes the precondition of every enhancement of the type "man", and BGE 259 calls exploitation a function of life. Those sections stay on the table, and <Ref lesson="higher-type-and-sublimation">the higher-type lesson</Ref> reconstructs them as arguments.

What the Archiv reading cannot accommodate is a narrower set of sentences: that the state is the coldest monster, that the anti-Semites are to be expelled, that "Rome against Judea" is a symbol of valuations, and that the book it called the system was a heading over 140 abandoned outlines. A reading that has to delete those four is refuted by them; a reading of BGE 257–259 is a separate question, and the course reaches it with the same protocol.

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "Heidegger's reading of Nietzsche can be dismissed, because it rests on a forged book." State precisely what the compilation's editorial status licenses a reader to reject, and what it does not, and say what Heidegger's own account of the book in his first volume concedes.

<Solution>
**Where the claim fails.** "Forged" is false. The words of every fragment are Nietzsche's; what is editorial is the selection, the order and the title. A forgery puts words in an author's mouth, and the compilation does that nowhere (the one documented case of a sentence added, the Kaiser letter's "will to power as a principle", is a letter and not a fragment of the book). So the premise is wrong, and the dismissal built on it does not follow.

**What the status licenses one to reject.** The compilation's arrangement as an argument: any inference that depends on one fragment's following another, on a fragment's being the "conclusion" of a book, on a chapter title, or on the sequence of the four books. A reading that treats WM 1067 as the last word because it is printed last, or WM 749 as political because it is filed under "Society and State", has used an editor's decision as a premise, and that premise can be struck.

**What it does not license.** Rejecting any individual fragment as a dated thought of Nietzsche's. A fragment cited by its siglum, with its date, is evidence on the fifth rung: it corroborates a published passage and dates a draft. Where Heidegger's reading of a fragment survives the conversion from "WM, n" to `NF-year,group[k]`, the reading stands or falls on the fragment's content and on what the published works say, not on the compilation's status. The course's evaluation in <Ref lesson="heidegger-one-thought">the Heidegger lesson</Ref> is therefore fragment by fragment, with the published counterpart found or its absence recorded.

**What Heidegger concedes.** Everything factual. His section "The Book, The Will to Power" reports the 1901 and 1906 counts, the plan of 17 March 1887 as basis, the *Grossoktav* reprint of 1911 and the Baeumler reprint, and calls the first collection "quite incomplete when compared to the available handwritten material". His decision to make the compilation his text is a decision about what "Nietzsche's philosophy proper" is, stated as such, and it is that decision, not a forgery, that the published-works rule rejects.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Take WM 1067 and write the published-works reconstruction of its thesis from BGE 36 alone, preserving the section's conditional mood: state the hypothesis, the argument BGE 36 gives for trying it, and the conclusion in the form the section gives it. Then list what a reader of WM 1067 would take to be asserted that BGE 36 does not assert.

<Solution>
**The reconstruction from BGE 36.** The hypothesis: suppose (*gesetzt, dass*) nothing is "given" as real except our drives, and suppose that the mechanical world can be understood as of the same order of reality as our affects. The argument for trying it: the conscience of method demands that one not assume several kinds of causality before the attempt to make do with one has been pushed to its limit; so one must try to explain all mechanical occurrence as will-force, and the entire drive-life as the development of one basic form of will, will to power. The conclusion, in the section's mood: the world seen from inside, "determined and designated by its 'intelligible character'", *would be* (*wäre*) will to power and nothing besides. Everything in the reconstruction is a hypothesis, a methodological obligation to test it, and a conditional result.

**What WM 1067 asserts that BGE 36 does not.** (i) The indicative: "this world *is* will to power", with the reader included ("you yourselves are this will to power"). (ii) A conservation cosmology: a fixed quantity of force "which does not grow larger or smaller", "a household without expenses and losses", enclosed by "nothing" as its boundary; BGE 36 says nothing about quantity, conservation or space. (iii) Eternal recurrence as a property of that force, "eternally running back, with tremendous years of return", blessing itself "as what must eternally return"; BGE 36 does not mention recurrence. (iv) A Dionysian world "of eternal self-creating, eternal self-destroying", named "my beyond good and evil"; the published section that carries that title argues instead of naming. A reader who takes the four from WM 1067 has taken them from an 1885 draft in Zarathustra's voice that Nietzsche revised into a conditional for print; the reconstruction records them as content the published works do not assert. The philosophical weight of the conditional, and whether the psychological doctrine survives where the cosmological one is question-begging, is the business of <Ref lesson="will-to-power-as-psychology">the will-to-power lesson</Ref>.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
The last notebook group in the edition is `NF-1888,25`, written in Turin in December 1888 and the first days of January 1889, after the last published book. Its first fragment, 25[1], is headed "Die große Politik" ("the great politics") and begins "Ich bringe den Krieg" ("I bring the war"); it goes on to demand a "party of life" strong enough to "breed humanity as a whole" and to "make an end of everything degenerate and parasitic". No published book contains this programme. Under the published-works rule, what may the fragment be cited for? Give the answer for the fragment as a whole and then for one clause inside it, "never more than in this case will it become clear how little there is to the concept 'free will': one affirms what one is, one denies what one is not", and explain why "corroborate, never ground" handles both.

<Solution>
**The fragment as a whole.** It may be cited for what it is: a draft of December 1888 or January 1889, the last weeks before the collapse, showing what Nietzsche was writing then. It dates a thought. It may not be cited to ground a claim that Nietzsche's philosophy contains a programme of "great politics", because no published section asserts one; the rule's second clause, that a note "never grounds", is exactly the clause that stops it, and the rule's reason is visible in the case: a reconstruction with this fragment as a premise would have a premise its author never asserted, drafted at a date when, by the evidence of the letters of the same weeks, his judgement of what was publishable had failed. A reader who wants to argue that the late programme was Nietzsche's view has to argue it from the published works of 1888, and they do not contain it; that is the record the reconstruction keeps.

**The clause on free will.** This clause has a published counterpart, and a strong one: *Twilight of the Idols*, "The Four Great Errors", the error of free will, and BGE 21, on "un-free will" as equally mythological (the argument <Ref lesson="doer-behind-the-deed">the doer-behind-the-deed lesson</Ref> reconstructs). For that clause the fragment corroborates: it shows the published thesis still held, restated, in the last weeks, and it dates the restatement. It adds no premise the published works lack, and it is cited beside them, not instead of them.

**Why the rule handles both.** The rule is applied sentence by sentence, not fragment by fragment. A fragment is not admitted or excluded whole; each clause is checked against the published works, and what has a counterpart corroborates while what has none dates. That is what step 4 of the protocol does when it is run on a note rather than a section, and it is why the same fragment can be evidence for one claim and inadmissible for the claim printed in the same paragraph.

<Exercise>
Fragment 2[190] of 1885 asks "what are our valuations and moral tables of goods themselves worth?" and its predecessor 2[189] separates the question of a value's origin from its critique. Reconstruct 2[190]'s question as a premise of an argument whose conclusion is that the value of moral values is unknown, and then predict what a "real history" of a value would have to supply for the question to be answered rather than only asked. Say which of the two fragments' claims the answer would have to keep.

<Solution>
**The reconstruction.** *P1.* The value of a valuation is measured by what comes of its rule, for someone, in relation to something (2[190]: "What comes of their rule? For whom? In relation to what?"). *P2.* The something is life, and life needs a more definite concept; the fragment's candidate is "life is will to power". *P3.* Moral valuation is an interpretation, and an interpretation is a symptom of the physiological states and the ruling judgements of whoever interprets ("Who interprets? Our affects."). *C.* Until the conditions under which a valuation arose and the states it expresses are known, its value is not known; it has been assumed. The missing premise is the type-fact of P3: that a valuation can be read as a symptom of a constitution at all, which 2[190] asserts and does not argue.

**What a real history must supply.** For each moral value: the conditions and circumstances under which it grew and shifted, and the type of person whose states it expresses, established from the record rather than from a hypothesis about how "good" might have come to be. The *Genealogy*'s Preface states that demand in §6: "we need a critique of moral values, the value of these values is itself first to be called into question, and for that a knowledge is needed of the conditions and circumstances out of which they grew, under which they developed and shifted" (*dazu thut eine Kenntniss der Bedingungen und Umstände noth, aus denen sie gewachsen, unter denen sie sich entwickelt und verschoben haben*), and §7 names the colour of the evidence: "the grey, that is, what is documented, what is really ascertainable, what really existed".

**What the answer must keep.** The distinction of 2[189]: the history of a value's origin is not yet its critique, however much a shameful origin lowers the value for feeling. A history that answered 2[190]'s question by exhibiting an origin and calling the value refuted would have committed the inference 2[189] warns against. The published version of the warning is GM II:12–13, that origin and present use are "toto caelo" distinct, and <Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the next lesson</Ref> reads the Preface and those two sections as the statement of what a genealogy can and cannot prove. The question the reader has just reconstructed from a notebook page of 1885 is the question that lesson opens with, in the book Nietzsche wrote from the page.
</Solution>

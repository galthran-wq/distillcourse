The Third Treatise's last move is turned on its readers: science is not the ascetic ideal's opponent but its most spiritualized form, because the will to truth at any price is itself a faith — and the Genealogy is written by that will. This lesson reconstructs that move from GM III:23–27, from the section of *The Gay Science* that III:24 cites as its argument (GS 344), and from the four sections that open *Beyond Good and Evil*; it states what the reconstruction does to the treatise's own method, and it separates, with Maudemarie Clark's chapter on the ascetic ideal, what the reflexive result proves from what it is routinely taken to prove.

The course's argument arrives here with three things in hand and one debt. <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">The priest lesson</Ref> reconstructed §§11–22 and §28: the ideal is a declining life's device against its own decline, and §28 concluded that man "would rather will nothingness than not will", which committed the author to a counter-ideal the treatise does not supply. <Ref lesson="perspectivism">The perspectivism lesson</Ref> read §12's "objectivity" as the command of one's for and against, and left standing the question of what ideal the *Genealogy*'s own knowing serves. <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">The lesson on §§1–10</Ref> found Clark's trap in the treatise's opening, a beginning "calculated to mislead", and noted that §§23–27 spring it a second time.

The debt is older: <Ref lesson="madman-argument">the course's first lesson</Ref> reconstructed "God is dead" with a premise that reported the belief's collapse and named no cause, and with a gap between "the foundation is gone" and "the edifice must fall" that a secular refounding could fill. This lesson pays the debt: §27 states the cause, and §24 says why a refounding on truthfulness inherits the faith it was meant to replace. <Ref lesson="antichrist-applied-genealogy">The next lesson</Ref> runs the three treatises' machinery on Nietzsche's own historical case.

## §23: the antagonist the treatise asked for, and the sentence that withdraws it

§1 asked what ascetic ideals mean and answered with a list of bearers; §23 returns to the ideal's power and asks the question the list deferred. "The ascetic ideal expresses *one* will: where is the opposing will, in which an *opposing ideal* expresses itself?" The ideal is a "closed system of will, goal and interpretation" that "subjects itself to no power" and grants every other power its right to exist; the section's question is why nothing has ever matched it.

### The candidate: "our whole modern science"

The section names the candidate its readers would name. "But I am told it is not lacking": modern science "has not merely waged a long and successful fight against this ideal, it has already conquered this ideal in all important respects", a "genuine philosophy of reality" that "clearly believes in itself alone, clearly possesses the courage for itself and the will to itself, and has up to now survived well enough without God, the beyond, and the virtues of denial".

That is the reader's own picture, stated in the reader's own words, and from a treatise that has just spent twelve sections on the priest one expects it to be confirmed: the priest's ideal on one side, the godless sciences on the other, the antagonist found.

The next sentence reverses it. "The truth is precisely the opposite of what is asserted here: science today has absolutely no belief in itself, let alone an ideal above it, and where it still inspires passion, love, ardour, suffering at all, it is not the *opposite* of the ascetic ideal but rather *its latest and noblest form itself*" (*dessen jüngste und vornehmste Form selber*). "Does that sound strange to you?"

### Two claims in one sentence: no ideal at all, or the ideal's form

The sentence makes two claims about two populations, and the reconstruction has to keep them apart, because the treatise's verdict falls on only one of them. The first population is most of science: "brave and modest working people" who "like their little corner", whose industry proves nothing about "science as a whole having one goal, one will, one ideal, one passion for a great faith".

For these science is "a hiding-place for every kind of cowardice, disbelief, remorse, *despectio sui*, bad conscience", "the unrest of the lack of ideals, the suffering from the lack of a great love"; "science as a means of self-narcosis". The charge here is not asceticism but its absence: a scholar who works in order not to see something has no ideal.

The second population is "those rarer cases" §24 opens on, "the last idealists left among philosophers and scholars", and of them alone §23 says that their science is the ideal's form. What makes the two populations different is what §24 will name: the second still has a faith, and the faith is the ideal's core.

§24 identifies the core, §25 says the two stand "on one ground", and GS 344, which §24 cites by page as its argument, is where the identification is argued rather than declared.

<KeyFact>
§23 does not call science ascetic; it denies science an ideal of its own: as industry it is a hiding-place, as passion it is truth "at any price", the ideal's core (§24), so the antagonist §1 asked for does not exist.
</KeyFact>

## GS 344 walked step by step: the chain §24 cites

§24 argues its identification in one paragraph and then refers the reader elsewhere: "if this seems too briefly said, let him read that section of the *Gay Science* which bears the title 'To what extent we too are still pious' (S. 260 ff.), best of all the whole fifth book". The citation is by page of the 1887 edition; the section is GS 344, written for that edition a few months before the *Genealogy*, and it is one of the few places in the published works where Nietzsche writes a chain of steps and marks each step with "consequently" (*folglich*), "therefore" (*also*), "thus" (*dergestalt*).

The course reads it with the German beside it, because three of its terms carry the argument and each has a standard rendering the argument depends on: *Ueberzeugung*, conviction; *Glaube*, faith or belief; *Wille zur Wahrheit*, will to truth.

### Convictions have no rights of citizenship, and the rule is itself a conviction

<Quote source="GS 344, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
In der Wissenschaft haben die Ueberzeugungen kein Bürgerrecht, so sagt man mit gutem Grunde: erst wenn sie sich entschliessen, zur Bescheidenheit einer Hypothese, eines vorläufigen Versuchs-Standpunktes, einer regulativen Fiktion herabzusteigen, darf ihnen der Zutritt und sogar ein gewisser Werth innerhalb des Reichs der Erkenntniss zugestanden werden, — immerhin mit der Beschränkung, unter polizeiliche Aufsicht gestellt zu bleiben, unter die Polizei des Misstrauens.
</Original>

In science convictions have no rights of citizenship, so it is said, with good reason: only when they decide to descend to the modesty of a hypothesis, of a provisional experimental standpoint, of a regulative fiction, may they be granted admission and even a certain value within the realm of knowledge, though with the restriction that they remain under police supervision, under the police of mistrust.
</Quote>

The opening states a rule of science that Nietzsche endorses ("with good reason"): a belief enters knowledge only as a hypothesis, revisable in the light of evidence, and stays under "the police of mistrust". The discipline (*Zucht*) of the scientific spirit begins with permitting oneself no convictions.

The first move follows at once: "it only remains to ask whether, for this discipline to be able to begin, a conviction must not already be there, and indeed one so commanding and unconditional that it sacrifices all other convictions to itself. One sees that science too rests on a *faith*; there is no 'presuppositionless' science at all." The rule "no convictions" cannot be a hypothesis under police supervision; it is what puts the police on the street.

Its content is a valuation: "the question whether truth is needed must not only have been answered in advance, but answered to the degree that the principle, the faith, the conviction finds expression in it: 'nothing is more needful than truth, and in relation to it everything else has only a value of the second rank'."

### Not from utility: truth and untruth have both been useful

The second move asks where that unconditional valuation could have come from, and eliminates the obvious answer. The will to truth might be "the will not to let oneself be deceived", a prudence: one avoids deception because being deceived is harmful. But that is an empirical bet, and the evidence is against it: "what do you know in advance of the character of existence, to be able to decide whether the greater advantage is on the side of the unconditionally mistrustful or the unconditionally trusting?"

<Quote source="GS 344, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Eben diese Ueberzeugung könnte nicht entstanden sein, wenn Wahrheit und Unwahrheit sich beide fortwährend als nützlich bezeigten: wie es der Fall ist. Also — kann der Glaube an die Wissenschaft, der nun einmal unbestreitbar da ist, nicht aus einem solchen Nützlichkeits-Calcul seinen Ursprung genommen haben, sondern vielmehr trotzdem, dass ihm die Unnützlichkeit und Gefährlichkeit des „Willens zur Wahrheit“, der „Wahrheit um jeden Preis“ fortwährend bewiesen wird.
</Original>

This very conviction could not have arisen if truth and untruth both showed themselves continually to be useful: as is the case. Therefore the faith in science, which now undeniably exists, cannot have taken its origin from such a utility calculation, but rather in spite of the fact that the uselessness and dangerousness of the "will to truth", of "truth at any price", is continually proved to it.
</Quote>

The premise is empirical: that untruth has been useful to life, "continually", is a claim Nietzsche argues elsewhere (BGE 4, read below, and BGE 230's "translate man back into nature") and here asserts.

What follows from it is narrower than it looks. A conditional preference for truth, held because truth usually pays, is compatible with the evidence; what the evidence rules out is that an *unconditional* rule, truth at any price, was reached by weighing. "'At any price': how well we understand that, once we have offered up and slaughtered one faith after another on this altar."

### "I will not deceive, not even myself": the ground is moral

<Quote source="GS 344, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Folglich bedeutet „Wille zur Wahrheit“ nicht „ich will mich nicht täuschen lassen“, sondern — es bleibt keine Wahl — „ich will nicht täuschen, auch mich selbst nicht“: — und hiermit sind wir auf dem Boden der Moral.
</Original>

Consequently "will to truth" does not mean "I will not let myself be deceived" but, no choice remains, "I will not deceive, not even myself": and with this we stand on the ground of morality.
</Quote>

The third move is a disjunction with one horn removed. The will to truth is either prudential or moral; it cannot be prudential (second move); so it is moral, a commitment to truthfulness "regardless of whether it serves one's other interests", in Clark's paraphrase.

The section then asks the question that morality itself must face, "why do you not want to deceive?", and answers it with a suspicion rather than a reason: if life is "designed for semblance, I mean for error, deception, dissimulation, dazzlement, self-dazzlement", and the great form of life has always sided with "the most unscrupulous *polytropoi*", then a resolve never to deceive could be "a Don-Quixotry" or "something worse, a principle hostile to life and destructive". "'Will to truth': that could be a hidden will to death."

### Another world affirmed: "still pious"

The fourth move is the one III:24 quotes, and it is where the section's title is earned.

<Quote source="GS 344, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Es ist kein Zweifel, der Wahrhaftige, in jenem verwegenen und letzten Sinne, wie ihn der Glaube an die Wissenschaft voraussetzt, bejaht damit eine andre Welt als die des Lebens, der Natur und der Geschichte; und insofern er diese „andre Welt“ bejaht, wie? muss er nicht ebendamit ihr Gegenstück, diese Welt, unsre Welt — verneinen?… Doch man wird es begriffen haben, worauf ich hinaus will, nämlich dass es immer noch ein metaphysischer Glaube ist, auf dem unser Glaube an die Wissenschaft ruht, — dass auch wir Erkennenden von heute, wir Gottlosen und Antimetaphysiker, auch unser Feuer noch von dem Brande nehmen, den ein Jahrtausende alter Glaube entzündet hat, jener Christen-Glaube, der auch der Glaube Plato's war, dass Gott die Wahrheit ist, dass die Wahrheit göttlich ist…
</Original>

There is no doubt: the truthful man, in that audacious and ultimate sense which the faith in science presupposes, thereby affirms *another world* than that of life, nature and history; and insofar as he affirms this "other world", must he not by that very fact deny its counterpart, this world, *our* world? … But one will have grasped what I am driving at, namely that it is still a *metaphysical faith* on which our faith in science rests; that we knowers of today too, we godless ones and anti-metaphysicians, still take *our* fire from the blaze that a faith thousands of years old has kindled, that Christian faith which was also Plato's faith, that God is the truth, that truth is divine…
</Quote>

"We too are still pious" is therefore a conclusion, not a mood: the atheist's unconditional truthfulness is a valuation with the content of Plato's and Christianity's, and with no other content available to ground it. The section's last sentence turns the conclusion into the treatise's problem: "but what if this itself becomes ever more unbelievable, if nothing proves to be divine any more, unless it be error, blindness, the lie, if God himself proves to be our longest lie?"

<Remark title="GS 344 reconstructed">
*P1.* Science admits no convictions; a belief enters knowledge only as a revisable hypothesis "under the police of mistrust" (*die Polizei des Misstrauens*).

*P2.* That rule is itself a conviction, "commanding and unconditional", which "sacrifices all other convictions to itself": that nothing is more needful than truth. So there is no "presuppositionless" science (*keine „voraussetzungslose" Wissenschaft*).

*P3.* An unconditional valuation of truth cannot have arisen from a utility calculation, because truth and untruth have both "continually" proved useful (*wie es der Fall ist*).

*P4.* The will to truth is therefore not the prudential "I will not be deceived" but the moral "I will not deceive, not even myself" (*auch mich selbst nicht*): "we stand on the ground of morality".

*P5.* The truthful man in that unconditional sense "affirms another world than that of life, nature and history", and in affirming it denies this one.

*C.* Our faith in science rests on a metaphysical faith, "that Christian faith which was also Plato's faith, that God is the truth, that truth is divine": we knowers of today are "still pious".

*Validity.* P1 to P4 run on the disjunction "prudential or moral" and the elimination of the first horn; Clark notes that to deny P4 one would need a third kind of ground for an overriding value, and names none. P5 is the step from "moral" to "another world", and it is the only step the section carries by assertion ("there is no doubt").

*The premise not stated.* Why a moral commitment to truthfulness must affirm a world beyond nature. A reader who holds truthfulness a moral value with no metaphysics attached, the "godless anti-metaphysician" the section addresses, is exactly the counterexample P5 needs to exclude, and GS 344 excludes him by declaring him inconsistent. Clark's reading records that III:24 quotes this step but no longer leans on it: the *Genealogy* reformulates the conclusion as "faith in a metaphysical value, a value in itself of truth", and the ground it gives is not another world but the ascetic ideal itself, the valuation on which natural existence has worth only under self-denial. On that reformulation an atheist can hold truth at any price consistently; what he cannot do is hold it for a reason that is not the ideal's.
</Remark>

## §24: the demand that the will to truth be criticized

GS 344 supplies the argument; §24 applies it to the population §23 set aside and draws a demand from it. The people are named with care, because the treatise is about to include its author among them: "these solitaries and deniers of today, these unconditional ones in one thing, in the claim to intellectual cleanliness, these hard, severe, abstinent, heroic spirits, who constitute the honour of our age, all these pale atheists, anti-Christians, immoralists, nihilists, these sceptics, ephectics, hectics of the spirit", "these last idealists of knowledge, in whom alone today the intellectual conscience dwells and is incarnate".

### The free spirits who never reached the Assassins' formula

They "believe themselves as far removed as possible from the ascetic ideal, these 'free, very free spirits': and yet, that I may disclose to them what they themselves cannot see, for they stand too near themselves: *this ideal is precisely their ideal too*, they themselves represent it today and perhaps no one else". The section stakes its author's reputation on the sentence: "if I am in any respect a solver of riddles, I wish to be one with this proposition! These are far from being free spirits: *for they still believe in truth*."

The contrast case is a legend Nietzsche uses as a measuring rod. When the Crusaders met the order of Assassins, the Ismaili sect of twelfth-century Syria, "that order of free spirits par excellence", they picked up the formula reserved for its highest grade: "Nothing is true, everything is permitted." "*That* was freedom of the spirit; with that the belief in truth itself was given notice." No European free spirit has "strayed into this proposition and its labyrinthine consequences"; on the contrary, "in no respect are they more firmly bound; in the belief precisely in truth they are, as no one else is, firm and unconditional".

The description that follows is of a discipline the course's readers will recognize as the scientific virtues: "that venerable philosopher's abstinence to which such a faith obliges, that stoicism of the intellect which finally forbids itself the No as strictly as the Yes, that wish to stand still before the factual, the *factum brutum*", "that renunciation of interpretation in general". All of it, "reckoned on the large scale, expresses asceticism of virtue just as much as any denial of sensuality (it is at bottom only a *modus* of that denial)".

### The kernel: faith in a value in itself of truth

<Quote source="GM III:24, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Was aber zu ihm zwingt, jener unbedingte Wille zur Wahrheit, das ist der Glaube an das asketische Ideal selbst, wenn auch als sein unbewusster Imperativ, man täusche sich hierüber nicht, — das ist der Glaube an einen metaphysischen Werth, einen Werth an sich der Wahrheit, wie er allein in jenem Ideal verbürgt und verbrieft ist (er steht und fällt mit jenem Ideal).
</Original>

But what compels them to it, that unconditional will to truth, is the *faith in the ascetic ideal itself*, even if as its unconscious imperative, let no one deceive himself about this: it is the faith in a *metaphysical* value, a *value in itself of truth*, such as is warranted and guaranteed in that ideal alone (it stands and falls with that ideal).
</Quote>

Then the citation, "here I let my *Gay Science* speak, compare its fifth book, p. 263", and the passage quoted above; then the demand.

<Quote source="GM III:24, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Von dem Augenblick an, wo der Glaube an den Gott des asketischen Ideals verneint ist, giebt es auch ein neues Problem: das vom Werthe der Wahrheit. — Der Wille zur Wahrheit bedarf einer Kritik — bestimmen wir hiermit unsre eigene Aufgabe —, der Werth der Wahrheit ist versuchsweise einmal in Frage zu stellen…
</Original>

From the moment the faith in the God of the ascetic ideal is denied, there is also a *new problem*: that of the *value* of truth. The will to truth requires a critique, let us herewith fix our own task, the value of truth is for once to be *experimentally* called into question…
</Quote>

The sentence before the demand explains why the problem is new: "the ascetic ideal has hitherto been master over all philosophy, because truth was posited as being, as God, as the highest court itself, because truth was not *permitted* to be a problem." "Here is a gap in every philosophy": no philosophy, oldest or youngest, has asked what the will to truth is worth, because under the ideal the question could not arise.

<Remark title="GM III:24 reconstructed">
*P1.* The ascetic ideal's core is the unconditional valuation of one thing, a valuation that grants natural existence worth only under self-denial and that, in its philosophical form, posits truth "as being, as God, as the highest court" (§24; §§11–12).

*P2.* The "last idealists of knowledge" value truth unconditionally, "at any price", and that valuation cannot be prudential (GS 344, P3–P4).

*P3.* An unconditional valuation of truth that is not prudential has no ground except the ideal's own: "a value in itself of truth, such as is warranted in that ideal alone" (§24).

*C.* The unconditional will to truth is the ascetic ideal "in its strictest, most spiritual formulation", and its holders, the ideal's declared opponents, are its "most spiritualized product" (§24; §27's "kernel").

*Corollary (the demand).* Once the ideal's God is denied, its warrant for truth's value is gone with it; so the value of truth, which the ideal made unquestionable, becomes a problem, and "the will to truth requires a critique".

*The premise not stated.* P3's "no ground except the ideal's" is an exhaustion claim: prudence is excluded by GS 344, another world by the denial of God, and nothing else is canvassed. A ground for valuing truth that is neither prudential nor ascetic is exactly what Clark's reading will say Nietzsche needs (a counter-ideal that truth serves), and its absence from §24 is why the demand is for a critique and not for a verdict.
</Remark>

### §25: one ground, and the victories that made the ideal stronger

§25 refuses the antagonist outright. "No! Do not come to me with science when I look for the natural antagonist of the ascetic ideal": science "is far from standing enough on its own; it needs in every respect first a value-ideal, a value-creating power, in whose service it may believe in itself; *it is itself never value-creating*."

Its opposition is "not to the ideal itself but only to its outworks, its dress, its play of masks, its temporary hardening, lignification, dogmatization": science "sets the life in it free again by denying what is exoteric in it". "These two, science and the ascetic ideal, stand on *one* ground: namely on the same overestimation of truth (more correctly: on the same faith in the *inestimability*, the *uncriticizability* of truth), and precisely for that reason they are necessarily allies."

The victories of science are then reread. "Does anyone seriously think that the defeat of theological astronomy meant a defeat of that ideal?" Since Copernicus, the section argues, man has "rolled faster and faster away from the centre, whither? into nothingness?", and "all science, the natural as well as the unnatural, by which I mean the self-critique of knowledge, is today set on talking man out of his previous respect for himself".

Kant's "victory over theological conceptual dogmatism", the section adds, "emancipated" the transcendentalists from the theologians and showed them "the secret path" to their hearts' desires, and the agnostics "now worship the question mark itself as God". Each victory strips an outwork and leaves the core standing, "made stronger, namely more elusive, more spiritual, more insidious".

One sentence of §25 fixes what the true antagonist would look like, and it is not science: "art, in which precisely the *lie* sanctifies itself, in which the will to deception has good conscience on its side, is much more fundamentally opposed to the ascetic ideal than is science", and "Plato against Homer: that is the whole, the genuine antagonism". The sentence is a promissory note on the counter-ideal, redeemed in the positive doctrine rather than here.

<KeyFact>
§24's demand cannot be met inside the treatise: a critique of truth's value needs a standard, the ideal was the only one so far (§28), and science "is itself never value-creating" (§25), so §§23–27 establish that the test has no ground yet rather than running it.
</KeyFact>

## §27: the law of self-overcoming closes the madman's loop

§24 said what the will to truth is; §27 says what it has done and what it will do next, and the second is stated as a law.

The section opens by setting the modern curiosities of §26 aside and naming the one thing that matters for the ideal's meaning: "everywhere else where the spirit is at work today, severely, powerfully and without counterfeit, it now dispenses with the ideal altogether, the popular expression for this abstinence is 'atheism', *except for its will to truth*. But this will, this remnant of an ideal, is, if one will believe me, that ideal itself in its strictest, most spiritual formulation, esoteric through and through, stripped of all outworks, and thus not so much its remnant as its *kernel*."

<Quote source="GM III:27, eKGWB; translation the course's">
<Original lang="de">
Alle grossen Dinge gehen durch sich selbst zu Grunde, durch einen Akt der Selbstaufhebung: so will es das Gesetz des Lebens, das Gesetz der nothwendigen „Selbstüberwindung“ im Wesen des Lebens, — immer ergeht zuletzt an den Gesetzgeber selbst der Ruf: „patere legem, quam ipse tulisti.“ Dergestalt gieng das Christenthum als Dogma zu Grunde, an seiner eignen Moral; dergestalt muss nun auch das Christenthum als Moral noch zu Grunde gehn, — wir stehen an der Schwelle dieses Ereignisses. Nachdem die christliche Wahrhaftigkeit einen Schluss nach dem andern gezogen hat, zieht sie am Ende ihren stärksten Schluss, ihren Schluss gegen sich selbst; dies aber geschieht, wenn sie die Frage stellt „was bedeutet aller Wille zur Wahrheit?“…
</Original>

All great things perish through themselves, through an act of self-sublation: so the law of life wills it, the law of the necessary "self-overcoming" in the essence of life; in the end the call always goes out to the lawgiver himself: "submit to the law you yourself have made." In this way Christianity as *dogma* perished of its own morality; in this way Christianity as *morality* must now perish too: we stand at the threshold of *this* event. After Christian truthfulness has drawn one conclusion after another, it finally draws its *strongest conclusion*, its conclusion *against itself*; this happens, however, when it poses the question "*what is the meaning of all will to truth?*"…
</Quote>

### The history: what triumphed over the Christian God

"What, asked with all strictness, has actually *triumphed* over the Christian God?" The section answers by quoting GS 357, again by page ("my *Gay Science*, p. 290"): "Christian morality itself, the concept of truthfulness taken ever more strictly, the confessor's subtlety of the Christian conscience, translated and sublimated into scientific conscience, into intellectual cleanliness at any price."

Reading nature as proof of a good God, history as the testimony of a moral world order, one's own experiences as providence: "all that is now *over*, that has conscience *against* it, that counts to all finer consciences as indecent, dishonest, as lying, feminism, weakness, cowardice; with this severity, if with anything, we are good Europeans and heirs of Europe's longest and bravest self-overcoming."

The cause the madman's argument lacked is therefore stated, and its shape is the one <Ref lesson="madman-argument">that lesson's</Ref> closing exercise wrote as P2a: the belief was killed by a virtue it bred, once the virtue was applied to the belief's own credentials. §27 adds the description of the killer as the ideal's agent, "unconditional honest atheism", which "is not the antithesis of that ideal, as it appears; it is rather only one of its last phases of development, one of its closing forms and inner consistencies, it is the awe-inspiring *catastrophe* of a two-thousand-year discipline in truth, which at the end forbids itself *the lie in the belief in God*".

### The prediction: morality perishes, on a stated timetable

The law's second application is not history but forecast. "In this way Christianity as morality must now perish too: we stand at the threshold of this event." The mechanism is the same virtue, one inference further: Christian truthfulness, having drawn the conclusion against God, draws "its conclusion against itself" when it asks what all will to truth means.

"In this becoming-conscious of the will to truth, from now on, there is no doubt of it, *morality perishes*: that great spectacle in a hundred acts which is reserved for Europe's next two centuries, the most terrible, most questionable and perhaps also most hopeful of all spectacles." The section dates its own prediction: two centuries from 1887, and the treatise's readers as the audience of the first acts.

<Remark title="GM III:27 as the completion of the GS 125 argument">
*P1 (from GS 125/343).* European values rested on belief in the Christian God.

*P2 (from GS 125/343).* That belief has collapsed among the educated.

*P2a (III:27, GS 357).* It collapsed because Christianity bred a virtue, truthfulness, whose ever stricter application forbade the belief as a lie: the collapse was self-inflicted, by "an act of self-sublation".

*P3 (III:27's law).* What perishes by self-overcoming does not stop at one conclusion: the virtue that drew the inference against God draws the next inference, against the morality that bred it, when it asks what it is itself worth.

*P4 (III:24).* A secular refounding of the same values on truthfulness inherits the virtue that did the killing, and with it the ideal's core; so the refounding cannot supply a foundation the killer will spare.

*C.* Christianity as morality must perish by the same law, and the missing premise of the first lesson (no other foundation can carry the same values) is now argued for one class of candidate: any refounding that keeps the will to truth.

*What remains unargued.* P4 covers refoundings that keep truthfulness. Kant's moral law and utilitarianism are refoundings of the precepts, not of truthfulness, and III:27 says nothing against them directly; what the *Genealogy* says against them is the First and Second Treatises' history of the precepts (<Ref lesson="genealogy-as-method">the genealogy-as-method lesson</Ref>), and *Twilight*'s assertion that Christianity "is a system" from which no piece can be removed. The first lesson's gap is narrowed here, not closed.
</Remark>

<Aside>
§27's parenthesis on India is the argument's one piece of corroboration, and it is offered as such: "the same course of development in India, in complete independence, and therefore proving something; the same ideal compelling to the same conclusion; the decisive point reached five centuries before the European era, with Buddha, more exactly already with the Sankhya philosophy, then popularized by Buddha and made into a religion". The claim is that an ascetic discipline elsewhere also ended by denying its own god, so that the law is not an accident of Christianity. Nietzsche's knowledge of Sankhya came through nineteenth-century German Indology, and whether the parallel holds is a question for that field, not for the reconstruction. Its logical role is clear enough: an independent second case is what would turn "Christianity perished of its own morality" from a history into a law.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
The mechanism that killed God, truthfulness turned on the belief that bred it, does not stop at the reporter: the will to truth that writes §27 is the next thing under its law, which is what the reflexive result below turns on.
</KeyFact>

## BGE 1–4: the value of truth as a problem, and the premise the naturalist needs

§24 says the value of truth "was not permitted to be a problem"; the book Nietzsche published a year before the *Genealogy* opens by making it one, and the four sections are the treatise's argument in another register. *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886) begins with the will to truth, explains why metaphysicians never questioned it, and then states the standard by which Nietzsche proposes to question it.

### BGE 1: the Sphinx's question

"The will to truth that still seduces us into taking so many risks, this famous truthfulness that all philosophers so far have talked about with veneration: what questions this will to truth has already laid before us!" The section reverses the direction of questioning: "we ourselves are also learning from this Sphinx to pose questions. Who is it really that questions us here? What in us really wills the truth?"

Then the question the *Genealogy* will say no philosophy asked: "Granted, we will truth: *why not untruth instead?* And uncertainty? Even ignorance? The problem of the value of truth came before us, or was it we who came before the problem? Which of us is Oedipus? Which one is the Sphinx?" And, "believe it or not, it ultimately looks to us as if the problem has never been raised until now, as if we were the first to ever see it".

Oedipus answered the Sphinx's riddle and the Sphinx died; the image reverses the roles and leaves it open which party dies of the answer. That is the reflexive question of III:27 posed in advance, with the questioner included in what is questioned.

### BGE 2–3: the faith in opposite values, and the valuation behind logic

BGE 2 explains why the question was never asked, and Clark's reading of it (ch. 6 §3) is the bridge between this book and the treatise. The metaphysicians' "fundamental belief" is "the belief in oppositions of values": truth cannot come from error, selfless action from self-interest, "the pure, sun-bright gaze of wisdom from a covetous leer". Things of the highest value "must have another, separate origin of their own", "the lap of being, the everlasting, the hidden God, the 'thing-in-itself'".

On Clark's reading this is the ascetic ideal stated as an epistemology: the metaphysician needs a "true world" not to explain anything but to keep the things he values "unsullied by connection" to nature, and that is the valuation on which natural existence has worth only by what negates it. The "true world" is required by a value judgment, and <Ref lesson="real-world-became-a-fable">the fable lesson</Ref> reconstructs its abolition.

BGE 3 supplies the naturalist's premise in one sentence: "behind all logic and its autocratic posturings stand valuations or, stated more clearly, physiological requirements for the preservation of a particular type of life", for example "that the determinate is worth more than the indeterminate, appearance worth less than the 'truth'". On the course's working hypothesis this is a type-fact doing what type-facts do (<Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson's</Ref> Doctrine of Types): a valuation explained by the constitution of the one who holds it.

### BGE 4: the falsity of a judgment is not an objection

  caption="BGE 4 dissected: the claim, the standard it substitutes for truth, and the premise that standard rests on."

"We do not consider the falsity of a judgment as itself an objection to a judgment; this is perhaps where our new language will sound most foreign. The question is how far the judgment promotes and preserves life, how well it preserves, and perhaps even cultivates, the type." The falsest judgments, "which include synthetic judgments a priori", are "the most indispensable to us"; "without measuring reality against the wholly invented world of the unconditioned and self-identical, without a constant falsification of the world through numbers, people could not live". "To acknowledge untruth as a condition of life" is what puts a philosophy "beyond good and evil".

<Remark title="BGE 4 reconstructed">
*P1.* Some judgments that are false (the fictions of logic, the self-identical, the world of number) are conditions of human life: without them "people could not live".

*P2.* A judgment's worth is measured by "how far it promotes and preserves life", how well it preserves and cultivates a type.

*C.* The falsity of a judgment is not by itself an objection to it; some false judgments are indispensable, and to reject false judgments as such would be "a renunciation of life".

*The premise not stated.* P2 is the whole argument, and it is asserted. That life-promotion for a type is the standard by which judgments, and by extension values, are to be ranked is the is–ought step in its purest form: a fact about what a type needs is made the measure of what is worth holding. The naturalist reading needs P2 to make "why not rather untruth?" a question with a naturalistic answer, and BGE 3's "physiological requirements" is where it grounds it. <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">The normativity lesson</Ref> asks whether a naturalist can hold P2 without a further premise; here the course names the premise and leaves it.

*What the section does not say.* It does not say that all judgments are false. It says that falsity does not devalue, and Clark's reading marks the difference as the turning point of Nietzsche's development: once falsity is not an objection, the early thesis that "truths are illusions" loses its point, and no book after BGE restates it (<Ref lesson="perspectivism">the perspectivism lesson</Ref>). BGE 4 bounds the value of truth; it does not deny truth.
</Remark>

### BGE 230: Nietzsche's own answer to "why knowledge at all?"

The book returns to the question at its end, and answers it in the first person. After the "twinkling, tinkling, festive words", "genuine honesty, love of truth, love of wisdom, sacrifice for knowledge, the heroism of truthfulness", BGE 230 sets the task: "to translate humanity back into nature", to stand before the "eternal basic text of *homo natura*" "hardened by the discipline of science, with courageous Oedipus eyes and sealed up Odysseus ears".

"Why do we choose it, this insane task? Or to ask it differently: 'Why knowledge at all?' Everyone will be asking us this. And we who have been prodded so much, we who have asked ourselves the same question a hundred times already, we have not found and are not finding any better answers…"

The passage is the reflexive result stated by its author a year before the *Genealogy*: the naturalizing project is undertaken by a will to truth that cannot say why, and the author knows it. The Oedipus of BGE 1 reappears, with his eyes open.

## What the reflexive result proves, and what it does not

§24 fixed "our own task" and §27 asked "what meaning would our whole being have, if not this, that in us that will to truth has come to consciousness of itself as a problem?" The treatise includes its author in what it diagnoses, and the course has to state precisely what is turned on what, because this is the most misquoted move in Nietzsche.

### The treatise's own will: "we knowers"

The *Genealogy*'s first sentence is "We are unknown to ourselves, we knowers, we ourselves to ourselves" (*Wir sind uns unbekannt, wir Erkennenden*); §24 repeats the word, now in scare quotes, "we 'knowers'", of the people whose faith it is about to name; §27 says of "us" that our being's meaning is that the will to truth has become a problem in us. The reflexive charge is therefore not an objection a critic brings from outside; it is the text's own last revision of the reader's position, and it completes the trap Clark found in §§7–11.

§10 moved the philosopher inside (the first revision in <Ref lesson="ascetic-ideal-across-types">the earlier lesson's</Ref> sequence); §§23–25 moved science inside; §§24 and 27 move the reader, who had been reading a critique of the ideal as if from outside it, inside as well. What the reader approved at §8 under the philosopher's name and expected at §23 under science's was, both times, the reader's own will to truth.

### The misconception, refuted: a critique of truth's value is not a denial of truth

The correct claim first: §§23–27 argue that the *value* placed on truth, its unconditional, overriding value, rests on the ascetic ideal and therefore needs a critique; they presuppose throughout that some beliefs are true and others false.

The misconception, stated once: *Nietzsche's analysis of the will to truth shows that there is no truth, or that science does not deliver it, and so the analysis refutes itself, since it claims to be true.* Arthur Danto's 1965 book put the "faith in truth" as a belief that truth exists, and the reading has been standard among what Clark calls the radical interpreters. The text refutes it on three grounds.

<Remark title="three grounds against the radical reading">
*1. The demand would be incoherent.* "The value of truth is for once to be *experimentally* called into question" (§24): an experiment on how valuable true beliefs are can be run only by someone who holds that some beliefs are true. Clark calls this "the most decisive kind of evidence one could have" against the reading.

*2. The faith's object is named.* §24: "faith in a metaphysical value, a *value in itself* of truth"; GS 344: "nothing is more needful than truth", everything else "of the second rank". A faith that truth outranks happiness, life, love and power cannot be derived from a belief that truth exists.

*3. §27 calls a belief a lie.* Belief in God is "the lie", and the atheism that forbids it is "honest": a writer who denied that anything is true could not say so. Nietzsche accepts what the will to truth found and asks what the finding was worth.
</Remark>

The discriminating example is §27's own question. "What is the meaning of all will to truth?" asks after *meaning* and *value* (*was bedeutet*), is asked by a will to truth of itself, and can be answered truly or falsely: a genealogy of the will to truth is a truth-claim Nietzsche means. BGE 22's "so much the better", which <Ref lesson="perspectivism">the perspectivism lesson</Ref> read as a claim offered as one interpretation and still offered, is the same distinction drawn a year earlier.

### What the result proves: a counter-ideal is needed

What the reflexive result does establish is stated by the treatise's own frame and by *Ecce Homo*'s account of it. §23 asked why the ideal has had no rival; *Ecce Homo* answers, "because it was the only ideal so far, because it had no rival. Above all, a *counter-ideal* was lacking, until Zarathustra." §25 adds the reason science cannot be that rival: it "needs a value-creating power in whose service it may believe in itself; it is itself never value-creating".

Put together with §24's demand, the three sentences yield Clark's conclusion for the treatise (ch. 6 §5): the will to truth cannot be pursued out of commitment to the ascetic ideal once the ideal is seen for what it is; it cannot be abandoned by the people Nietzsche has in mind, whose "intellectual conscience" (GS 2) he calls the mark of a higher human being; so it must be pursued "for the sake of something else", a new ideal "that requires the service of a commitment to truth". "Unthinking faith in truth would be abandoned but not the commitment to truth."

The textual ground for the second step is the later work, and Clark cites it against the reading on which the will to truth overcomes itself.

<Quote source="A 50; EH Preface 3, as quoted in Clark ch. 6 §5">
At every step one has to wrestle for truth; one has had to surrender for it almost everything to which the heart, to which our love, our trust in life, cling otherwise. That requires greatness of soul: the service of truth is the hardest service.

How much truth does a spirit *endure*, how much truth does it *dare*? More and more that becomes for me the real measure of value.
</Quote>

A writer who had concluded in 1887 that the will to truth must be given up did not write those sentences in 1888. What was given up is the *faith*: the unquestioning assumption of truth's overriding value, "needed precisely because it keeps hidden from view the dependence of the commitment to truth on the ascetic ideal".

<Remark title="what the reflexive result establishes">
*Proved by the text.* (1) The *Genealogy* is written by an unconditional will to truth: §24 says so of "we knowers", §27 says so of "our whole being". (2) That will's valuation of truth has so far rested on the ascetic ideal, and the ideal's God is denied, so the valuation is unwarranted until a new ground is found. (3) Science cannot supply the ground, since it creates no values (§25). (4) Therefore the critique demands a counter-ideal for the will to truth to serve, and the treatise names none: "a counter-ideal was lacking, until Zarathustra" (EH).

*Not proved, and not claimed.* (5) That truth-claims must be abandoned: the experiment of §24 presupposes them. (6) That the *Genealogy*'s own claims are false or self-refuting: a genealogy of a valuation is itself a truth-claim, and the treatise offers its own as one. (7) That the will to truth overcomes itself: III:27 speaks of the self-overcoming of *morality* out of truthfulness, and the works of 1888 keep the service of truth as "the hardest service".

*What follows for the course.* Item (4) is the problem of the sixth module: <Ref lesson="nihilism-and-affirmation">the nihilism lesson</Ref> takes §28's "will to nothingness" and this section's "counter-ideal needed" as one demand. Items (5)–(6) are the nearer form of the self-refutation problem that <Ref lesson="life-as-literature">the Nehamas lesson</Ref> meets in full, where the status of Nietzsche's own claims is the whole question.
</Remark>

### The rivals at their strongest

Three readings of III:24–27 compete with the one just given, and each is stated here at the strength it has. Walter Kaufmann, whose 1950 book the course met in <Ref lesson="madman-argument">its first lesson</Ref>, reads §24's "we too are still pious" as Nietzsche's own creed and quotes GS 344 at length as the statement of it (ch. 12 §III): the will to truth is "Nietzsche's faith", the sections are the centre of his positive commitment, and *The Antichrist*'s epigrams on conviction, "convictions are prisons", "the courage for an attack on one's convictions", are its later form.

Clark grants Kaufmann the commitment to truth and denies him the faith: on her reading Nietzsche objects to "the whole ideal of self-denial of which the will to truth is an expression", and Kaufmann's Nietzsche remains committed to an ideal Nietzsche rejects. The two agree on what the 1888 texts show and disagree on whether "faith in truth" and "commitment to truth" can come apart.

Alexander Nehamas, whom the course reads in full in Module 7, uses the same sections for a different charge (ch. 4, p. 131).

<Quote source="Nehamas, Nietzsche: Life as Literature, ch. 4, p. 131">
An interpretation, simply by virtue of being offered, is inevitably offered in the conviction that it is true. But then, despite any assurances to the contrary, it is presented as a view which everybody must accept on account of its being true. … Even a view that denies that there is such a thing as truth must be presented as true. Asceticism, we have seen, tries to conceal that it is an interpretation. Attacking it and demonstrating that it is after all an interpretation is still done in the name of truth. And as long as this commitment is made, the dogmatism on which asceticism depends has not yet been eliminated.
</Quote>

Clark's reply is that dogmatism is not what makes the ideal ascetic; self-denial and the devaluation of life are, and a philosopher "can oppose the ascetic ideal only by commending an opposed ideal to universal attention", which Nehamas's Nietzsche refuses to do. Nehamas's stronger textual card is BGE 4's "untruth as a condition of life" and the notebook line "facts are precisely what there is not, only interpretations", and the course's evidence rules (<Ref lesson="corpus-and-protocol">the protocol lesson</Ref>) weigh the second as corroboration only.

Maudemarie Clark's own later work, with David Dudrick (*The Soul of Nietzsche's Beyond Good and Evil*, 2012), reads BGE 1–4 as the introduction of two forces rather than one. Their key is the "magnificent tension of the spirit" of BGE's preface, unpacked through the passage of Plato's *Republic* (Book IV, 439b–c) where Socrates argues that the soul has parts: "it is wrong to say of the archer that his hands at the same time push the bow away and draw it towards him. We ought to say that one hand pushes it away and the other draws it towards him."

A tense bow needs two forces. The two are the *will to truth* introduced in BGE 1, which "aims at believing only what corresponds to the way the world actually is", and what they call the *will to value* introduced in BGE 2, which "aims to represent the world in terms of what is valuable", the force behind the metaphysicians' faith in opposite values.

On this reading "the will to truth requires a critique" is not a demand that one force be disabled but a statement that neither can be: dogmatism was overcome "by the strengthening of the truth drive relative to the value drive", and the naturalism that strengthening produces, read through GS 371–374 and the second edition's preface, "is not, in fact, true" as a complete account, because it "cannot accommodate values" and so cannot say how anything is a claim at all.

Their gloss on the preface's "we no longer believe that truth remains truth when the veils are withdrawn" is that scientific explanations "lose their truth when accompanied by an insistence that everything can be explained scientifically". That is the analytic form of the reflexive charge, stated as a puzzle about naturalism rather than as scepticism, and it is the volume's direct denial that methodological naturalism is Nietzsche's considered view.

### The working hypothesis at the end of the treatise

The naturalist reading has a straightforward account of §§23–27 and one strain. The account: the sections are a causal history of a valuation. A type (the priest, then the philosopher, then the scientist) came to hold truth unconditionally valuable; the history of that valuation runs from Plato's "God is the truth" through Christian confession to scientific conscience (GS 357, §27); the mechanism is a virtue applied to its own origin; and the evidence is the history of European unbelief, corroborated by the Indian parallel. That is a mechanism, its evidence, and an unstated premise, which is the treatise's form in every installment so far.

The strain is in the demand. "Requires a critique" and "science is never value-creating" are normative sentences: they say the valuation is unwarranted and that a new warrant must be found. A psychology of types can explain why a type holds a value; it cannot, on its own resources, say which value a type ought to hold, and §25 concedes as much for science.

On the working hypothesis, then, the Third Treatise ends by demanding something the hypothesis has no way to supply, and BGE 4's standard, life-value for a type, is the premise on which it would try. Clark and Dudrick's tense bow is the rival's version of the same finding: a naturalism true as far as it goes and unable to house the value it needs. The two readings agree on where the strain is and disagree on what it shows; the disagreement goes to <Ref lesson="value-creation-and-normativity">the normativity lesson</Ref> and to the closing module's audit.

<KeyFact>
The reflexive result turns on the value of truth, not on truth: the *Genealogy* is written by a will whose valuation has lost its warrant and science cannot supply another, so the treatise ends in a demand for a counter-ideal, not in a refutation of its own claims.
</KeyFact>

What is established. §23 withdraws the antagonist the treatise's readers would have named: most science has no ideal, and the rest holds the ideal's core. GS 344, walked step by step, shows that core to be a conviction that cannot be prudential and is therefore moral, with the content of Plato's faith that truth is divine; §24 identifies the unconditional will to truth with the ascetic ideal's kernel and demands that the value of truth be criticized; §25 puts science and the ideal on one ground and denies science any value-creating power.

§27 states the law of self-overcoming, applies it to Christianity as dogma, which perished of its own truthfulness (the cause the madman's argument lacked), and predicts its application to Christianity as morality over two centuries. BGE 1–4 pose the value of truth as a problem and answer it by a standard, life-value for a type, that is asserted and is the is–ought step in its purest form. And the reflexive result, stated precisely, proves that the treatise is written by a will whose valuation needs a counter-ideal and does not prove that its claims are false.

What stands open is the counter-ideal itself, and the case. §§23–27 say what a counter-ideal would have to do and name none; Module 6 takes that demand up with §28's "will to nothingness" as the despair it answers. And the treatises' machinery, the slave revolt, the debt become guilt, the priest's redirection and now the will to truth's self-overcoming, has been reconstructed from Nietzsche's general claims and never yet watched on a single historical record. <Ref lesson="antichrist-applied-genealogy">The next lesson</Ref> reads *The Antichrist* §§24–47 as Nietzsche's own application of it to Israel, Jesus and Paul, and grades the evidence.

<Exercise>
From memory, write GS 344's chain as five premises and a conclusion, each with the German phrase that anchors it (*Polizei des Misstrauens*, *voraussetzungslose*, *nützlich*, *auch mich selbst nicht*, *eine andre Welt*, *dass Gott die Wahrheit ist*). Then state which premise GM III:24 quotes and no longer leans on, and what it puts in that premise's place.

<Solution>
*P1.* Science admits no convictions; beliefs enter only as hypotheses under "the police of mistrust" (*die Polizei des Misstrauens*).

*P2.* That rule is itself a conviction, commanding and unconditional, "nothing is more needful than truth"; so there is no presuppositionless science (*keine „voraussetzungslose" Wissenschaft*).

*P3.* The unconditional valuation cannot come from utility, because truth and untruth have both continually proved useful (*sich beide fortwährend als nützlich bezeigten*).

*P4.* So the will to truth is not "I will not be deceived" but "I will not deceive, not even myself" (*auch mich selbst nicht*): a moral ground.

*P5.* The truthful man in that sense affirms "another world than that of life, nature and history" (*eine andre Welt*) and denies this one.

*C.* Our faith in science is still a metaphysical faith, Plato's and Christianity's, "that God is the truth" (*dass Gott die Wahrheit ist*).

The premise III:24 quotes and no longer leans on is P5, the affirmation of another world. §24 quotes the whole passage, "here I let my *Gay Science* speak", but its own formulation of the conclusion is "faith in a metaphysical value, a value in itself of truth, such as is warranted and guaranteed in that ideal alone". In place of "another world" it puts the ascetic ideal: the valuation on which natural existence has worth only under self-denial. The change matters because P5 declares the godless anti-metaphysician inconsistent (he affirms a world he denies), whereas §24's version lets him be consistent and unwarranted: he can hold truth at any price without any other world, but he cannot give a ground for holding it that is not the ideal's own.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Break this claim: "Nietzsche's critique of the will to truth refutes itself. If truth has no value, then his claim that truth has no value has no value either, and there is no reason to accept it." Distinguish the two theses the claim runs together, cite the sentence of §24 and the sentence of BGE 22 that decide between them, and state what the reflexive charge does establish.

<Solution>
**The two theses.** (a) Truth has no *unconditional* value: the overriding valuation of truth rests on the ascetic ideal and is unwarranted once the ideal's God is denied. (b) No claim is true, or the *Genealogy*'s claims are not true. The objection needs (b) and Nietzsche asserts (a). From (a) nothing follows about whether the sentence stating (a) is true; it follows only that its truth does not, by itself, oblige anyone to prefer it over a useful falsehood, which is a claim about what its truth is *worth*, not about whether it obtains.

**The deciding sentences.** §24: "the value of truth is for once to be *experimentally* called into question." An experiment on the value of true beliefs presupposes that there are true beliefs to run it with; the demand is incoherent under (b) and coherent under (a). BGE 22's last sentence: "Supposing that this too is only interpretation, and you will be eager enough to raise this objection? Well, so much the better." The reply concedes that the will-to-power reading is one interpretation among others and keeps offering it; a writer who held (b) could not say "so much the better", since he would have nothing to offer. The same self-application in §24, "we 'knowers'", offers the genealogy of the will to truth as a claim made by a will to truth, and a genealogy of a valuation is a truth-claim.

**What the charge establishes.** That the *Genealogy* is written under a valuation of truth that its own analysis has left without a warrant, so that the treatise cannot say, on its own resources, why the truths it finds should be preferred to the illusions it destroys; and that science cannot supply the warrant, since it "is itself never value-creating" (§25). The conclusion is a demand for a counter-ideal for the will to truth to serve, not a demand that the will to truth stop. What it does not establish is that any sentence of the treatise is false, or that Nietzsche must stop asserting: the 1888 works keep "the service of truth" as "the hardest service" (A 50).

The step that fails in the objection is the slide from "no unconditional value" to "no value", and from "no value" to "not true". The first slide ignores that (a) leaves conditional value in place; the second confuses a claim's worth with its truth, which is exactly the confusion BGE 4 was written to separate.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
§27 states a "law of life", self-overcoming, and applies it once to history (Christianity as dogma) and once as a forecast (Christianity as morality). Write the forecast as a three-premise argument, name the premise that is a prediction rather than a report, and then say what the law predicts about the *Genealogy*'s own will to truth: is the treatise's questioning of the value of truth a refutation of the treatise or an instance of its law?

<Solution>
**The forecast.**

*P1.* Christianity bred a virtue, truthfulness, which "taken ever more strictly" drew the conclusion against Christian dogma: Christianity as dogma perished of its own morality (§27, GS 357). This is a report.

*P2.* The law: a virtue that has drawn one conclusion against its origin does not stop; "after Christian truthfulness has drawn one conclusion after another, it finally draws its strongest conclusion, its conclusion against itself", when it asks "what is the meaning of all will to truth?" This is the law stated generally and applied to the next step: the same truthfulness, turned on the morality that bred it, will find that morality's valuation unwarranted as it found God a lie.

*P3.* The turning has begun: "we stand at the threshold of this event"; the question has been posed, in the *Genealogy*, by "us".

*C.* Christianity as morality must perish, "in the next two centuries", "a spectacle in a hundred acts".

**The predictive premise.** P2's application. That truthfulness *will* draw the conclusion against morality, and that morality will not survive it, is forecast from the law plus one prior instance; P1 reports the instance, P3 reports that the question has been asked, and only P2 says what the asking will do. The course dates the forecast (1887, two centuries) and records it as a prediction.

**The treatise's own will.** The law says every great thing perishes by an act of self-sublation, "the lawgiver himself" receiving the call to submit to his own law. The *Genealogy* is a work of the will to truth that asks what the will to truth is worth; that is P2's event happening in the text, "in us that will to truth has come to consciousness of itself as a problem". So the questioning is an *instance* of the law, and the treatise says so: "what meaning would our whole being have, if not this?" It is not a refutation, for the reason the previous exercise gave: the law concerns what a valuation does to the valuations it rests on, not whether the claims made under it are true. What the law does predict about the will to truth is stated in §27 by omission: it speaks of the self-overcoming of *morality* out of truthfulness, not of the self-overcoming of truthfulness, and the 1888 works confirm that the will to truth survives the event as a commitment without the faith. An instance of the law that perished in the act would be a will to truth that stopped wanting truth; the text describes a will to truth that stops assuming its own worth.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Suppose a science that valued truth conditionally: true beliefs pursued because and insofar as they pay, false ones kept wherever they pay better. On GS 344's account, can such a science exist, and if so, is it inside or outside the ascetic ideal? Name the premise of §344 that bears on it, and say what §23 and §25 add.

<Solution>
**It can exist, and §344 does not deny it.** The premise that bears on the case is P3: an *unconditional* valuation of truth cannot have arisen from a utility calculation, because truth and untruth have both proved useful. P3 rules out a particular origin for a particular rule, truth at any price; it says nothing against a rule that tracks utility, and a rule that tracks utility is exactly what a conditional science would hold. Clark notes the textual support: GS 123 says that "without this new passion, I mean the passion to know, science would still be promoted; after all, science has grown and matured without it until now", in the service of pleasure, honour, sustenance and salvation. A pragmatic science is therefore possible, and on Nietzsche's own account it is what most science has always been.

**Outside the ideal, but not its antagonist.** Such a science would be outside the ascetic ideal, because it would have no faith: nothing in it is valued unconditionally, so §24's identification (unconditional valuation, therefore the ideal's core) does not reach it. §23 describes it: "brave and modest working people" who "like their little corner", whose industry proves nothing about "one ideal, one passion for a great faith". §23's verdict on that population is not asceticism but the absence of an ideal, "a hiding-place", "science as a means of self-narcosis". And §25 says why it cannot be the counter-ideal either: science "needs a value-creating power in whose service it may believe in itself; it is itself never value-creating". A conditional science serves whatever values it is given, and a science with a faith serves the ascetic ideal's; neither supplies the "opposing will" §23 asked for.

**What the boundary shows.** The treatise's charge is aimed at a rare population, "the last idealists of knowledge", and the argument's force is highest exactly where the reader would have placed the antagonist. A reader who takes §§23–25 as an attack on scientific practice has missed the population the sections name; a reader who takes a pragmatic science as an escape from the diagnosis has found only §23's hiding-place. The conditional case marks the ideal's boundary from outside without offering anything to stand on beyond it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the course's corroboration pass on GS 357 and GM III:27 as two statements of the mechanism that killed the Christian God. Translate the operative sentence of each, report what each names as the agent and what each adds that the other lacks, and say which of the two the reconstruction of GS 125 in the first lesson needed.

<Solution>
**GS 357 (1887, Book V).** "One sees what actually triumphed over the Christian God: Christian morality itself, the ever more strictly taken concept of truthfulness, the confessor's subtlety of the Christian conscience, translated and sublimated into scientific conscience, into intellectual cleanliness at any price." Its frame is the question "what is German?" and the section credits the victory to "the European conscience" as "the most consequential act of a two-thousand-year discipline in truth", with Schopenhauer as the first "unconditional honest atheist" among Germans and Hegel as the "delayer par excellence". The agent is *Christian morality itself*, specified as one virtue, truthfulness, and the mechanism is a translation (*übersetzt und sublimirt*) of a religious practice, confession, into a scientific one.

**GM III:27.** Quotes the same sentence by page, then generalizes it: "All great things perish through themselves, through an act of self-sublation: so the law of life wills it … In this way Christianity as dogma perished of its own morality; in this way Christianity as morality must now perish too." The agent is again Christian truthfulness, but now as an *instance of a law* about all great things, and with a second application (morality) and a timetable (two centuries) that GS 357 does not have. GS 357 adds what III:27 lacks: the sociology, "a pan-European event in which all races have their share", the named delayers and accelerators, and the immediate consequence, that once the Christian interpretation is thrown off "the Schopenhauerian question comes to us at once in a terrible way: *has existence any meaning at all?*", a question that "will need a few centuries even to be heard completely".

**Agreement and difference.** Both name the same agent and the same mechanism, and neither names a cause outside Christianity; the corroboration is strong because III:27 quotes rather than paraphrases. They differ in scope: 357 reports one event and its aftermath; 27 subsumes it under a law and forecasts the next. And they differ in what is at risk: 357 says meaning is in question; 27 says morality is.

**Which the first lesson needed.** GS 357's sentence. The reconstruction of GS 125 lacked a cause for P2, "the belief has collapsed", and the closing exercise of that lesson wrote GS 357's sentence as P2a. III:27's law was not needed to fill the gap; it is needed for the next step, the forecast that morality follows, which is the *Genealogy*'s contribution rather than *The Gay Science*'s. The corroboration pass therefore assigns the two sources different jobs: 357 grounds the history, 27 grounds the prediction, and a reconstruction that cited 27 alone for the cause would be citing a law where a report was wanted.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Apply the course's translation check to three terms that carry §27's mechanism: *Wahrhaftigkeit* (§27, GS 357), *Redlichkeit* (BGE 227, which Norman renders "genuine honesty" with a footnote, Hollingdale "honesty", Kaufmann "honesty"), and *Selbstüberwindung* / *Selbstaufhebung* (§27; Samuel renders the first "self-mastery" and the second "self-dissolution", Clark's quotation "self-overcoming" for both). For each pair of renderings, say whether the reconstructed mechanism, a virtue turning on its own origin, survives, and which rendering Clark's reading needs.

<Solution>
**Wahrhaftigkeit.** "Truthfulness" (Samuel, Clark) names a virtue of a person, a disposition not to deceive, which is what GS 344's P4 identified the will to truth as ("I will not deceive, not even myself") and what §27 says was "bred" by confession and then drew conclusions. A rendering such as "veracity" keeps the virtue; "truth" would not, because the mechanism needs a *disposition* to be applied to its own origin, and a proposition cannot be applied to anything. The check passes for "truthfulness"; Clark's reading needs it, since her whole account of the survival of the commitment to truth after the faith is gone is an account of a virtue, not of a doctrine.

**Redlichkeit.** BGE 227 calls it "our virtue, the only one we have left", which the free spirits must "perfect" and keep from becoming "our vanity, our limitation, our stupidity". Norman's "genuine honesty" (footnoted "in German: *Redlichkeit*") and Hollingdale's and Kaufmann's "honesty" agree in making it a virtue of character; the difference is that "honesty" in English covers not lying to others, while *Redlichkeit* in Nietzsche is above all not lying to oneself, the *auch mich selbst nicht* of GS 344, and Norman's "genuine" is an attempt to mark that. The mechanism survives on all three, because BGE 227's sentence, "the only one we have left", is the same claim as §27's "except for its will to truth": a single remaining virtue, inherited, that the free spirit must keep from turning into its own stupidity. Clark's reading needs the passage on either rendering; what it cannot afford is a rendering that makes *Redlichkeit* a method ("probity" in a technical sense) rather than a virtue, because the argument that the commitment survives the faith is an argument about what a person keeps.

**Selbstüberwindung and Selbstaufhebung.** §27 uses both: great things perish "through an act of *Selbstaufhebung*", by "the law of the necessary '*Selbstüberwindung*' in the essence of life". *Aufhebung* is the word of Hegel's dialectic, cancelling and preserving in one act; *Überwindung* is the *Zarathustra* word, overcoming. Samuel's "self-dissolution" keeps the cancelling and loses the preserving; his "self-mastery" for *Selbstüberwindung* is a defect, since mastery is what a will does to something else and the sentence is about what a thing does to itself. On Samuel's pair the mechanism reads as a virtue dissolving itself, which is the reading Clark denies (the will to truth does not overcome itself; morality does, out of truthfulness). Clark's "self-overcoming" for both keeps the reflexive structure and the survival of the overcomer: what perishes is the dogma, then the morality; what draws the conclusions persists to draw the next. Her reading needs *Überwindung*'s sense and *Aufhebung*'s double meaning, and Samuel's "self-mastery" is the one rendering among the six here on which the reconstructed mechanism does not survive.

This is the check <Ref lesson="key-terms-in-translation">the translation lesson</Ref> introduced: run the premise through each rendering and record which it survives. Here it survives five of six, and the failure is instructive, because it is exactly the rendering on which "Nietzsche's will to truth destroys itself" would read as the text's own claim.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
III:27 says Christianity as dogma was unmade by the truthfulness it bred. *The Antichrist* §§39–47 tells the reverse story about its making: Paul turned a redeemer's practice into a doctrine of sacrifice, resurrection and judgment. Write the two claims as the first and last premises of one history of Christianity, state what evidence each would need to be more than assertion, and mark which of the Genealogy's mechanisms each premise borrows.

<Solution>
**The history in two premises.**

*P1 (the making, A §§39–47).* A practice, the redeemer's non-resistance and "blessedness" in this life, was converted by Paul into a doctrine: guilt, sacrifice, resurrection, judgment, a beyond. The mechanism borrowed is the First Treatise's slave revolt and the Third's priestly redirection: a type with a "genius of hatred" reinterprets a life for the sake of a population of sufferers, and gives their suffering a meaning at the price of guilt (<Ref lesson="slave-revolt-and-ressentiment">the slave-revolt lesson</Ref>; <Ref lesson="ascetic-priest">the priest lesson</Ref>, §15).

*Pn (the unmaking, III:27).* The doctrine bred a virtue, truthfulness, sharpened by confession into scientific conscience, which forbade the doctrine's central belief as a lie. The mechanism borrowed is the Third Treatise's own: the ascetic ideal's core, the unconditional value of truth, turned on the ideal's outworks and finally on its God.

*The whole.* A religion made by a priest's redirection of suffering, and unmade by the discipline in truth that its own regime of confession bred. The two premises share one agent, the priestly type, doing two things two millennia apart: supplying a meaning of suffering, and breeding the conscience that will refuse the meaning.

**What each would need.** P1 needs evidence about Paul: that the letters convert a practice into a doctrine rather than transmit one, which is a philological and historical claim about the earliest Christian texts, the gospels' dating relative to the letters, and what the "practice" was before Paul; Nietzsche's evidence in *The Antichrist* is a psychology of the type and a reading of the texts, with no documentary record of the practice independent of the texts he says Paul distorted. Pn needs evidence that the decline of belief in educated Europe was produced by truthfulness rather than by prosperity, natural science's explanatory successes, or political change; GS 357 offers a sociological claim ("a pan-European event") and the Indian parallel offers corroboration for the law, but the causal weight of the virtue against rival causes is asserted.

**Where this goes.** <Ref lesson="antichrist-applied-genealogy">The next lesson</Ref> reads Nietzsche's own application of the three treatises' machinery to Israel, Jesus and Paul, names which result each step of §§24–47 uses, and grades the evidence step by step; the history written here is its outline, with the unmaking premise already argued in this lesson and the making premise still to be examined against the record.
</Solution>

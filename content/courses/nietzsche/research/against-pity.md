# Research brief: against-pity (Against pity)

status: planned
module: what-morality-does (5 of 9), lesson 23 of 45
type: core
goal: Evaluate Nietzsche's case against pity as a moral foundation, distinguishing it from a case for cruelty

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (8, "What morality does to us"):** "What this morality does to us: pity multiplies suffering and spends the strong, the whole system rests on false beliefs about the self and harms the highest human beings, and it survives the death of God in the secular dress of equality, democracy, socialism and emancipation, which Twilight's Skirmishes diagnose as the modern form of decline"
- **Inherits:** from schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy, Schopenhauer's morality of compassion and the verdict on existence it serves; from ascetic-priest, the management of the suffering and GM III 14's "great nausea and great pity" that together beget the will to nothingness; from will-to-power-as-psychology, resistance as what a living thing wants (A 2); from cruelty-and-culture, GM II 7's "tragic pity" as a name sublimated cruelty took.
- **Contributes:** the first of the module's three charges: pity (Mitleid) treats suffering as the evil to be abolished, which depresses life, multiplies suffering, disguises a feeling of power, and condemns what every elevation of man has needed. Introduces critique-of-pity, with Nietzsche's own "higher, farther-seeing pity" (BGE 225) as the alternative.
- **Hands off:** to danger-of-dangers, which assembles the full charge sheet of which pity is one item (GM Pref 5-6 already make it the "real stake"); and to skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man, where TI Skirmishes 35-37 restate the case as "altruism the formula of decadence" and "l'impressionisme morale".

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** Nietzsche is the first modern to make pity a problem rather than the foundation of morals, and the lesson shows what he saw: that the pitier is also acting on himself (D 133), that the sufferer can wield pity as a power (HH 50), that a value which treats every suffering as an evil to be abolished has taken a side in a question about what a life is for (BGE 225, GS 338), and that he placed himself among the sufferers of this vice ("my greatest dangers lie in pity", letter to Overbeck, 14 Sept 1884, Portable p.440). The claim is compelling from inside because it follows from the module-2 psychology: if a living thing wants to overcome resistance, a morality that removes resistance on principle attacks life at the root.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** A 2 (`portable-late.txt`, p.570): "The weak and the failures shall perish: first principle of our love of man. And they shall even be given every possible assistance" — both sentences, in Kaufmann's wording; A 7 (pp.572-574) entire: pity "stands opposed to the tonic emotions", "multiplies misery", "crosses the law of selection", "preserves what is ripe for destruction", "Pity is the practice of nihilism"; GM III 14 (pp.121-125, `genealogy--text.txt`): "The sick are man's greatest danger; not the evil, not the beasts of prey", the remarks on "the sick woman" and on Dühring, "It is disgraceful to be fortunate: there is too much misery!", "against great pity for man!"; GS 377 (p.339, `gayscience-s2-bk4-bk5-appendix.txt`): the "religion of pity" with its "hysterical little males and females"; BGE 225 (`bge-zimmern.txt` line 3847): "You want, if possible ... to abolish suffering; and we? it seems we would rather have it higher and worse than ever it was!"; EH I 4 (p.228, `eccehomo-full.txt`): "pity begins to smell of the mob"; Notes 1887 (Portable p.456, printed there as WP 910): "To those human beings in whom I have a stake I wish suffering, being forsaken, sickness, maltreatment, humiliation" — unpublished, 1887.
- **What the words meant:** Mitleid is rendered "pity" by Kaufmann (his Portable Intro II, p.4, gives his reasons) and "sympathy" by Zimmern throughout BGE (222, 225, 293, 44); the lesson says "pity" and notes that Hollingdale's Daybreak and Kaufmann's "compassion" are the same German word. "The law of selection" (A 7) is Nietzsche's borrowed Darwinian phrase for life's weeding of the failed, which he elsewhere denies happens (TI Skirmishes 14: "the weak prevail"; notebook 14[123?] pp.258-260); in A 7 it names what pity "crosses". "Perish" (zu Grunde gehen) and "assistance" in A 2 say what the sentence says: love of man, as he defines it, wills the failures' going under and lends it a hand; the lesson explains how this follows from A 2's definition of good as "everything that heightens the feeling of power" and connects it to A 7, where the failures preserved by pity give life "a gloomy and questionable aspect". "Decadent" (EH I 4, A 6) is the type that chooses what harms it. "Nihilism" in "pity is the practice of nihilism" is A 7's own gloss: pity "persuades to nothingness", though "one does not say 'nothingness': one says 'the beyond', or 'God'". "Tonic emotions" are those that heighten the feeling of life.
- **Objection answered from his side:** the strongest philosophical objection: most of the suffering pity answers serves no pursuit — a child's illness, a famine — and a morality that refuses to count it as evil has lost the plain sense of the word; set the choice between a world of great achievements with unrelieved suffering and a world of relieved suffering without them, and most choose the second or are torn. Reply from the texts: BGE 225's "pity against pity" — his pity is for man's diminishment, "we see how man diminishes himself, how you diminish him" (own translation in `bge.json` notes); GS 338's morality: help friends whose distress you understand, "to share not suffering but joy"; Z II 3's "hard bed, a field cot" for a suffering friend; D 134's physician who must guard against pity to help; A 7's target is pity as "the virtue", "the religion of pity", not help as such. The texts also go further than the reply (A 2; GM III 14's separation of sick from healthy; D 146's "what would forbid us to sacrifice our neighbour as well?"), and the lesson quotes them rather than trimming the claim to fit the reply. Gap sentence: the critique is strongest against the ideal of abolishing suffering (BGE 44, 225) and weakest where suffering serves no pursuit; D 134's "it increases the amount of suffering in the world" is an empirical claim he asserts and does not support.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** that the critique is "not heartlessness" but about meddling and losing one's way (Tanner Ch.5 on GS 338), that Nietzschean compassion is simply "compassion for squandered capacities" (Reginster), and Kaufmann's note to EH I 8 that "Nietzsche's critique of pity should be considered in this light" (his own humanity as self-overcoming). What the text says: GS 338 and EH I 8 support that reading; A 2, A 7's "law of selection", GM III 14's quarantine of the sick and the 1887 note say more, and the lesson gives both.
- **Facts against the popular image:** in the lesson's texts: the Overbeck letter (p.440) and EH I 8 ("My humanity does not consist in feeling with men how they are, but in enduring that I feel with them. My humanity is a constant self-overcoming") show the critic of pity as someone who suffered from it; D 133 calls the pitiless "a different kind of egoists" and calling them evil "a moral fashion". The misreading "Nietzsche says be cruel to the weak" is real (L13); Z II 3 and GS 338 refute it, and A 2 is quoted so the refutation does not overreach.

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** A 7: pity is "the practice of nihilism"; D 134: it is a weakness that increases suffering; GS 338: the religion of pity misunderstands suffering and robs the sufferer of what is his own; BGE 225: the discipline of great suffering created every elevation of man, so the will to abolish suffering is a will against elevation.
- **Argument in plain steps (three charges).** (1) Contagion: pity adds the pitier's suffering to the sufferer's; it multiplies suffering and lowers the feeling of life in both (D 134; A 7). (2) Hidden power: pity contains a feeling of superiority over the sufferer and a pleasure in it (D 133, 135-138; GS 13, 118), and the sufferer uses it to exercise "the power to inflict pain" (HH 50); a morality that calls this selfless misdescribes it. (3) Condemnation of suffering: pity treats every suffering as an evil to be removed; but suffering is an ingredient of what makes a life worth having — overcoming resistance (A 2), the discipline that "alone has created every elevation" (BGE 225), "the path to one's own heaven ... through the voluptuousness of one's own hell" (GS 338); a morality built on pity therefore condemns those activities and the people who need them, and addresses only "the creature" in man, not "the creator" (BGE 225). Conclusion: pity is a poor foundation for morality and harmful as a ruling value; its modern elevation is itself a symptom (GM Pref 5; EH I 4).
- **Unstated premise.** In charge 3: that suffering is not merely a cost of elevation but part of its substance, so that a pity-morality cannot simply exempt the creators; and that the value of a life is measured by growth and achievement, not comfort (the standard the module keeps testing). In charge 1: that suffering is a quantity that adds (D 134).
- **What Nietzsche does not claim, and what he does.** Not that one should cause suffering, nor that one should never help: GS 338 and Z II 3 give the alternative (help that respects the other's struggle; compassion for wasted capacity). He does claim A 2 and GM III 14, and the lesson quotes them as part of the position rather than as excess.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE: suffering that serves no pursuit; the two-worlds choice. Reply from BGE 225, GS 338, Z II 3, D 134's physician; the gap sentence on D 134's empirical claim and on helping the helpless.
- **Connections.** schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy (the target; GM Pref 5); ascetic-priest (GM III 14-15, 18); cruelty-and-culture (GM II 7's "tragic pity"); danger-of-dangers (pity as one norm on the charge sheet); herd-morality-in-modern-dress (BGE 202's "religion of pity"; GS 377); skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man (TI 35-37); overman-and-last-man ("we have invented happiness"); amor-fati (loving necessity, suffering included); eternal-recurrence (Z IV 2, pity as the final sin).

## Pitfalls

- Translation: Mitleid = pity/compassion/sympathy; say once which the lesson uses and why; every Zimmern "sympathy" is Mitleid.
- BGE 225 is "pity against pity" and the discipline of great suffering, not "pity bad for both parties" (that is 82, 171, 222, 269); BGE 293 contrasts the master's pity with the cult of suffering and does not state "my kind of compassion" (that is the 1885 notebook, unpublished).
- HH 50 has no "neurotics"; its claim is the sufferer's "power to inflict pain".
- "Nietzsche says be cruel to the weak" is a real misreading (L13): refute it with GS 338 and Z II 3, and quote A 2 whole so the refutation does not overreach; A 2's second sentence is Kaufmann's, not Ludovici's "botched".

## EXERCISE PLAN

Floor check: claim to refute (ex. 2), boundary probe (ex. 4), research-shaped (ex. 5), derivation (ex. 3).

1. Retrieval (E3 from schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy and ascetic-priest): state Schopenhauer's reason for making compassion the basis of morality and the ascetic priest's way of managing suffering (GM III 15, 18); then name what the religion of pity shares with the priest.
2. Claim to refute: "Nietzsche wants us to stop helping people who suffer." Use GS 338 and Z II 3 to break it and state precisely what he rejects; then quote A 2 and say what the claim cannot be shrunk to.
3. Derivation the lesson did not do: from A 2's definition of happiness and BGE 225's "discipline of great suffering", derive why a morality that abolished suffering would have to abolish the activity A 2 calls happiness; name the premise about suffering the derivation needs.
4. Boundary probe: suffering that serves no pursuit and destroys the sufferer (a child's terminal illness). Which of the three charges still apply, which fail, and what does D 134's physician say?
5. Research-shaped: read Schopenhauer's statement that compassion is the sole source of moral action (On the Basis of Morality §16, public-domain translation) and D 133 side by side; identify the premise charge 2 attacks and quote the sentence of D 133 that attacks it.
6. FORWARD (reaches danger-of-dangers): list three other values besides pity that a morality might put first (equality, selflessness, comfort) and say for each what it might cost a person capable of great work.

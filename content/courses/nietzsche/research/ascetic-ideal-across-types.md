## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **III:1 as outline and thesis.** Artists: nothing or too many things; philosophers: a nose for the conditions of high spirituality; women: a charm; the physiologically deformed: an attempt to see themselves as too good for this world; priests: their best instrument of power; saints: a pretext for hibernation. The closing formula ('would rather will nothingness than not will') stated as the treatise's conclusion, to be earned by III:28. Reconstruct: (P) an ideal's meaning is what it does for the type that holds it; (P) the ascetic ideal is held by types with opposite needs; (C) 'the ascetic ideal' names a structure, not a content — the Doctrine of Types applied.
2. **III:2–5: Wagner as the artist's case.** Parsifal's chastity; the artist who 'needs' the ideal as alibi; artists as 'valets of a morality or philosophy'; Wagner as Schopenhauer's valet. Reconstruct the inference: (P) an artist's ideal is borrowed; (P) Wagner's late turn was borrowed from Schopenhauer; (C) Parsifal 'means' nothing about asceticism's value — only about what its author needed. One paragraph on the arc from the 1873 'Mahnruf' to 1888; no biography beyond that.
3. **III:6: Kant, Schopenhauer, Stendhal — and Heidegger's correction.** Kant: 'the beautiful is what gives pleasure without interest'; Schopenhauer: aesthetic contemplation as release from the will (torture relieved); Stendhal: 'the beautiful promises happiness' — the beautiful arouses the will. Nietzsche sides with Stendhal and reads Schopenhauer's will-lessness as sexual relief in disguise. Heidegger's charge (Vol. I §15): Kant's 'without interest' means unconstrained favouring, not indifference; Schopenhauer misread it as will-suppression; Nietzsche inherited the misreading and attacked a Kant that is not in the Critique of Judgment. The reader has Kant: state the three positions and Heidegger's correction as a checkable claim (Critique of Judgment §§2–5), and note what it does to III:6's argument (it hits Schopenhauer, not Kant).
4. **III:7–8: the philosopher's asceticism as optimum.** Heraclitus, Plato, Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz, Kant, Schopenhauer unmarried; Buddha's 'a fetter has been forged for me'; poverty, humility, chastity as 'the most appropriate and natural conditions of the philosopher's best existence' — not virtues but 'the optimum conditions for the highest and boldest spirituality'. Reconstruct: the philosopher's affinity for asceticism is 'not truth' but power — a type's need.
5. **III:9–10: philosophy's chrysalis.** Philosophy had to appear as asceticism to exist at all — 'the ascetic priest provided the repulsive and gloomy caterpillar form in which alone philosophy could live'; the philosopher as 'a further development of the priestly type'. This is the hinge: what looked like the philosopher's own healthy asceticism is the priest's costume.
6. **Clark's trap.** The treatise's opening leads the reader to approve the philosopher's asceticism as self-affirming and to distinguish it from the priest's life-denial; §§9–11 reveal the philosopher as a species of priest, and §§23–27 will reveal the scientist and the philosopher's will to truth as the same ideal. 'The art of surprise' (EH on GM). State the structure as a fact about the text and what it does to a reader who stops at §8.

## EXERCISE PLAN (floor: ≤1 warm-up; ≥2 of boundary / refute / derivation / research; one FORWARD)

1. (warm-up) Map III:1's six types to their one-line meanings and state which single sentence of III:1 the whole treatise is written to earn.
2. (refute) "Nietzsche attacks asceticism as such." Break it with III:7–8 (the philosopher's asceticism as the optimum of a type) and III:1's type-relativity; state what is attacked (the ideal as a priest's instrument; the ideal taken for a value in itself) and what is not (the practice as a type's condition).
3. (derivation) III:6 argues against Schopenhauer by a reinterpretation (will-lessness as sexual relief). Derive the same argument-form applied to Nietzsche's own position — what would 'Stendhal's beautiful' be a relief from, on a type-relative account? — and state what the derivation shows about the symmetry of genealogical unmasking (a point Nehamas's and Heidegger's readings will press).
4. (boundary probe) Suppose a type for which the ascetic ideal means nothing at all (III:1's artists 'nothing whatever'). Is that a counterexample to the treatise's thesis or an instance of it? State what 'means' means in III:1 and why 'nothing' is a meaning.
5. (research-shaped) Read Critique of Judgment §§2–5 (the reader's own Kant) and adjudicate Heidegger's charge: does Kant's 'ohne alles Interesse' mean indifference or unconstrained favouring? Report which sentence decides it and what III:6's argument then hits.
6. (sourcing) III:2–5 uses Parsifal as evidence about Wagner. Run the records / argues / omits pass: what a late artwork records about its author's needs (per III:5's own premise), what Nietzsche argues from it, what he omits (his own earlier valet-hood, the 1873 Mahnruf — one sentence, from research/texts/digitalcriti-s2-pw-MD.txt).
7. (FORWARD → ascetic-priest) III:10 leaves the philosopher inside the priest's chrysalis. Write what the priest's own version of the ideal must do for the priest's type (III:1: 'the best instrument of power') and predict the population on which it is used; the solution says III:11–15's 'physician of the sick' is the next lesson.

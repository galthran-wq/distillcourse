# Research brief: ascetic-ideals-in-artists-and-philosophers (What ascetic ideals mean for artists and philosophers (Genealogy III 1-10) read whole)

status: planned
module: guilt-and-the-priest (4 of 9), lesson 22 of 45
type: synthesis (module application, L16)
goal: Assess the opening of the Genealogy's third essay with the module's results, showing how the ascetic ideal in artists and philosophers differs from the priest's and where Nietzsche's own case is implicated

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (7, "Guilt and the priest"):** "Guilt began as debt: a promising animal learned memory through pain, cruelty was the festival pleasure that higher culture refined rather than abolished, cruelty with no outlet turned inward as bad conscience, the debt to God made it unpayable, and the ascetic priest gave suffering a meaning by making the sufferer its cause"
- **Inherits:** from ascetic-priest, the ideal as life against life, the priest, the will to nothingness and III 28's frame; from sublimation, spiritualization and the claim that a drive raised keeps its force (III 8: sensuality "transfigured"); from will-to-power-as-psychology, that every animal strives for the optimum conditions of its strength (III 7); from case-of-wagner, romantic pessimism and Parsifal as the test case (GS 370; NcW); from schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy, the aesthetic justification and Schopenhauer's release from the will.
- **Contributes:** the module's application: GM III 1-10 read whole. What ascetic ideals mean before the priest: to the artist nothing, or too many things (Wagner's Parsifal, III 2-5); to the philosopher who theorizes beauty, release from a torture (Schopenhauer, III 6); to the philosopher as a type, "an optimum condition for the highest and boldest spirituality", poverty, humility and chastity as conditions and not virtues (III 7-9), under the priest's mask, his "caterpillar form" (III 10). The reader learns to tell an asceticism that serves a life from the ideal that denies it, and sees Nietzsche's own solitude as the case. Introduces philosophers-asceticism.
- **Hands off:** to against-pity (module 5): the priest's, the artist's and the philosopher's uses of suffering are distinguished; what morality does with suffering next, pity, is the next question; to will-to-truth, since III 9-10 show philosophy on the ideal's "leading-strings" and III 23-27 show science still there.

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** III 1-10 is Nietzsche's most generous page on asceticism: the same word covers the artist who borrows a morality for a late work, the pessimist who wants the will to stop hurting, and the thinker who needs silence, poverty and no family in order to think, and only the first two are the ideal of lesson 21. He saw that the virtues of the contemplative life are conditions mistaken for merits, and that philosophy could only enter the world disguised as priestly self-denial. This explains the chastity of philosophers without a theory of repression, the grip of Schopenhauer's aesthetics on a suffering man, and why a thinker's "desert" is a quiet room.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** GM III 1 (p.97), the six-fold list (genealogy--text.txt 3270-3290), including "in the case of women at best one more seductive charm, a touch of morbidezza in fair flesh, the angelic look of a plump pretty animal"; GM III 7 (p.107, 3673), "A married philosopher belongs in comedy"; GM III 8 (pp.109-110, 3750), the "desert", "fame, princes, and women", "he who possesses is possessed"; GM III 5 (pp.102-103), artists as "valets". Texts in Set pieces.
- **What the words meant:** "Ascetic" (asketisch): the common word for a life of self-denial; "the ascetic ideal": that life valued and preached (lesson 21); III 7-9's point is that the philosopher's asceticism is the first without being the second. "Optimum conditions": from III 7's biological premise, "every animal ... instinctively strives for an optimum of favorable conditions under which it can expend all its strength". "Women" in III 1: the ascetic look as an erotic ornament, one of six cases in his list, quoted as it stands. "Valets" (Kammerdiener, III 5): artists serve a morality already made. "Caterpillar form" (III 10): the priestly disguise in which the philosopher had to live. "Pregnancy" (III 8): Nietzsche's standing image for intellectual production; chastity is its guard.
- **Objection answered from his side:** Is the philosopher's asceticism really distinct from the priest's, given that Nietzsche's own life (solitude, no wife, no post, a diet) wears the priest's marks, and given III 10's admission that the philosopher needed the priest's mask? Reply: III 7-8 give the criterion, which life the practice serves: the philosopher's poverty, humility and chastity are "an optimum condition for the highest and boldest spirituality", chosen by instinct for a work, not as merit before God or a bridge to another world (III 11); III 8's chastity is "pregnancy-energy" redirected into the work, lesson 10's sublimation, not denial; EH II 3 and 8-10 describe the same conditions in Nietzsche's own case as "self-defense", the "walling oneself in" of a pregnancy, instinct rather than virtue ("I am the opposite of a heroic nature", EH II 9). Gap sentence: the criterion is which kind of life the asceticism serves, ascending or declining, and the essay applies it without arguing for the standard; the lesson says so and points to TI "Skirmishes" 33.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** Kaufmann reads the philosophers' chastity of III 8 as "a little immortality", the passage as sublimation; the text says the same of chastity and adds the hardness: "a married philosopher belongs in comedy", philosophers "shun fame, princes, and women", the family a fetter (Buddha). The test for the reader is whether Nietzsche exempts himself from the priest's diagnosis: III 10 says the philosopher lived in the priest's form, and EH II 10 says the "small things" were his real subject; the lesson holds both.
- **Facts against the popular image:** none this lesson's texts carry beyond EH II 6's "I call Wagner the great benefactor of my life", which belongs to case-of-wagner.

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** GM III 7-8 (pp.106-112): the philosopher's asceticism is "an optimum condition for the highest and boldest spirituality"; poverty, humility and chastity are conditions, not virtues; III 1 (p.97): the will "will rather will nothingness than not will" is the thesis the essay unfolds.
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) The same word is used of six kinds of people and means something different to each (III 1). (2) To the artist it means nothing of his own: Wagner's late chastity is borrowed, artists are "valets" who need a prop (III 2-5); so the artist is separated from his work, and his homage tells of his need, not of the ideal. (3) To the philosopher who theorizes beauty it means release from the will's torture: beauty "without interest" is the spectator's view, Schopenhauer's contemplation a sedative against the wheel of Ixion (III 6); the ideal is wanted for what it stops. (4) To the philosopher as a type it means conditions: every animal seeks the optimum for expending its strength; for a thinker these are quiet, poverty, independence, no family, which look like the priest's virtues and are the instincts of a work in gestation (III 7-8). (5) His chastity is not denial but sublimation: the energy goes into the work (III 8). (6) Historically the philosopher could appear only inside the priest's form, because his virtues were crimes against the morality of mores (III 9-10). (7) So only the artist's borrowed ideal and the pessimist's sedative are lesson 21's ideal; the philosopher's asceticism serves a life, and the essay has not yet shown the priest proper (III 11). (8) Nietzsche's own case fits step 4: EH II 3 and 8-10 describe his solitude, diet and reading rules as instinctive self-defence for a pregnancy.
- **Unstated premise.** That "which life it serves" can be read off a practice (ascending or declining), applied here and not argued; and (step 5) the hydraulic claim that chastity's force is conserved and redirected.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE; gap sentence on the unargued standard.
- **Connections.** ascetic-priest (III 11-28); sublimation (III 8, II 18); case-of-wagner (III 2-5; NcW; GS 370); schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy (III 6); will-to-truth (III 9-10, 23-27); becoming-who-you-are, ecce-homo (EH II 9-10); higher-type (BGE 212); against-pity (module 5).

## APPLICATION spec (L16)

- **The text read whole:** On the Genealogy of Morals, Third Essay, sections 1-10, Kaufmann & Hollingdale, Vintage 1989, printed pp.97-116; genealogy--text.txt from line 3270. Order: III 1; 2-5 (Wagner); 6 (Kant, Stendhal, Schopenhauer); 7-9 (the philosopher); 10 (the caterpillar); then III 28's last line recalled as the frame.
- **Section map:** III 1 (pp.97-98) uses ascetic-priest: the ideal as will to nothingness, stated before the priest is on stage; the six-fold list is the essay's plan. III 2-5 (pp.98-103) uses case-of-wagner: romantic pessimism (GS 370), Parsifal's chastity as Schopenhauer's resignation set to music; and sublimation's negative test, "no necessary antithesis between chastity and sensuality"; artists as valets. III 6 (pp.103-106) uses schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy: the aesthetic justification seen from the spectator's and the sufferer's side (Kant's disinterest, Schopenhauer's release) against Stendhal's artist's side; the wheel of Ixion as Schopenhauer's pessimism in one image. III 7-8 (pp.106-112) uses will-to-power-as-psychology (every animal seeks its optimum) and sublimation (chastity as pregnancy-energy, sensuality transfigured); the result is philosophers-asceticism. III 9-10 (pp.112-116) uses genealogy-as-method and ascetic-priest: "all good things were formerly bad things", the philosopher's virtues as crimes against the morality of mores (D 18); the priest as the philosopher's caterpillar form, with III 11's priest proper as what follows.
- **Where the text goes further / departs from the module's idealisation:** (a) The philosopher's asceticism does not deny life: III 7-8 describe optimum conditions, so lesson 21's "life against life" is not the whole of what the word covers; the module's idealisation is narrower than the essay. (b) Nietzsche's own solitude is the case: EH II 3, 8-10 and the Sils-Maria signature of GM Pref 8 describe the same conditions in the author, as instinct and self-defence; the essay's criterion is applied to its own author. (c) The essay has not yet shown the priest proper: III 1-10 reach him only as a mask the philosopher wore (III 10); lesson 21 read III 11-28 first, so the reader meets the priest here as the caterpillar, not the shepherd, and holds the two together.
- **Reading order and the one sentence:** III 1-10 in order with the section map at hand, then III 28's last line; the reader can now take a page of Nietzsche on asceticism and say which of three things it is about, a borrowed ideal, a sedative against suffering, or the conditions of a work, by which life the practice serves, and apply that test to the author himself.

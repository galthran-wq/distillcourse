# Research brief: ascetic-priest (The ascetic priest and the meaning of suffering)

status: planned
module: guilt-and-the-priest (4 of 9), lesson 21 of 45
type: core
goal: Explain how the ascetic ideal gives suffering a meaning by making the sufferer its cause, and why Nietzsche calls it a trick of life against itself

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (7, "Guilt and the priest"):** "Guilt began as debt: a promising animal learned memory through pain, cruelty was the festival pleasure that higher culture refined rather than abolished, cruelty with no outlet turned inward as bad conscience, the debt to God made it unpayable, and the ascetic priest gave suffering a meaning by making the sufferer its cause"
- **Inherits:** from guilt-before-god, infinite guilt as an instrument awaiting its administrator; from slave-revolt-and-ressentiment, ressentiment as hatred looking for a target (GM I 10); from cruelty-and-culture, II 7's premise that senseless suffering is the unbearable thing; from will-to-power-as-psychology, that a will needs a goal and that the weak have a will to power too (III 14).
- **Contributes:** the module's answer in its darkest form: suffering is bearable if it has a meaning, and the priest supplied the only meaning Europe had by turning the sufferer's ressentiment back on himself, "you yourself are this someone" (III 15). The priest is sick and strong, "shepherd and advocate of the sick herd"; his ideal is "life against life", only apparently a contradiction, since it is "an artifice for the preservation of life" (III 13); his remedies run from innocent to guilty (III 17-21); "man would rather will nothingness than not will" (III 28). Introduces ascetic-ideal, ascetic-priest, will-to-nothingness.
- **Hands off:** to ascetic-ideals-in-artists-and-philosophers (GM III 1-10, read whole with this result in hand); to against-pity (III 14's "great nausea and great pity"; the morality of pity as the secular physician); to will-to-truth (III 23-27); to amor-fati (a meaning for suffering without a culprit).

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** This is "the first psychology of the priest" (EH III GM, p.313), and it explains what no theology and no anticlerical polemic had: why the sick seek out the one who tells them they are to blame, why the remedy works, and why it makes them sicker. The priest is not a fraud but a physician of a kind; the ideal is life's own device for keeping declining life alive; the unconditional will to truth is its heir. One mechanism, the redirection of ressentiment, predicts the confessional, the sermon on sin, the herd's mutual aid and the modern cult of the sufferer.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** GM III 14 (pp.121-125), genealogy--text.txt 4233-4386, whole, with the sick woman (4313), Dühring and the anti-Semites (p.124) and the pathos-of-distance demand (p.125); GM III 15 (pp.125-128, 4440-4460), the shepherd; GM III 21 (pp.141-143), "improved" as "tamed, weakened, discouraged, refined, pampered, emasculated"; TI "Skirmishes of an Untimely Man" 45 (Portable pp.549-551) and A 26 (pp.596-598), the priest as "our chandala" and the parasite who "lives on sins"; EH IV 7 (p.333, eccehomo-full.txt 5001), "that parasitical type of man, that of the priest". Texts in Set pieces.
- **What the words meant:** "The sick" (die Kranken): the physiologically declining, the "failures" and "ill-constituted" (III 14), a type, not the ill in bed; "the healthy": the well-turned-out (EH I 2). "The sick woman": an instance of the type given by sex; the lesson quotes the sentence and the Bogos proverb Nietzsche cites from ethnography, and sets beside it that the whole section is about the weak's "will to power", of which she is his extreme case. "Pathos of distance" (from master-morality): the felt sense of rank; III 14 demands that it keep the healthy from nursing the sick, a demand with social teeth, and the lesson says so. "Ascetic ideal": the valuation of self-denial and another world become an ideal, the priest's instrument of power (III 1). "Life against life" (III 11, 13): Nietzsche's own phrase for the apparent contradiction III 13 dissolves. "Herd" (Herde): the organisation of the weak the priest founds (III 18). "Chandala" (TI "Improvers" 3-4; Skirmishes 45): the Indian outcaste, used for the type produced by priestly morality. "Improved" (verbessert): the church's word, glossed in III 21 as tamed.
- **Objection answered from his side:** The account is unfair to the ascetic traditions: contemplatives report joy, not self-hatred, and asceticism exists in religions without the concept of sin. Reply: III 7-8 separate the philosopher's asceticism (optimum conditions, next lesson) from the ideal; III 17 counts the Indian "redemption", deep sleep and mystic union among the innocent means, a lowering of the feeling of life that works without guilt, so asceticism without sin is a different remedy of the same physician; III 13 grants that the priest conserves life and III 17-18 that his means work. Gap sentence: the priest is a composite type assembled from Brahmin, Buddhist, Christian and Puritan material, and no single priesthood can be checked against it; its support is the psychological fit. His reply: types are the method (Pref 7's "gray" is documented psychology, not chronicle; GS 353; TI "Improvers" 2-3 reads Manu and the church as two instances), and EH III GM claims a psychology, not a history.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** III 13's "among the greatest conserving and yes-creating forces of life" read as endorsement; III 21 insists on the harm, "the true calamity in the history of European health"; both stand. Kaufmann reads the pathos-of-distance demand as cultural and anti-political; the text asks that the healthy be kept from nursing the sick and from being "poisoned" by their misery, a demand about who may do what. Nehamas's "which one?" (which kind of life wills the ideal) is a faithful gloss; the text's answer, declining life, is harder than a question.
- **Facts against the popular image:** III 14 names Dühring and "the anti-Semites" as "men of ressentiment, physiologically unfortunate and worm-eaten"; III 26 (p.158, 5746) calls the anti-Semites "latest speculators in idealism".

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** GM III 28 (p.162): "The meaninglessness of suffering, not suffering itself, was the curse that lay over mankind so far, and the ascetic ideal offered man meaning!"; III 15 (p.128): the priest "alters the direction of ressentiment".
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) Many suffer from weakness, failure and sickness no action can cure; they are "the sick", a type (III 13-14). (2) Suffering without a meaning is unbearable (II 7) and breeds ressentiment, which seeks a guilty agent to deaden the pain by an affect (III 15). (3) Aimed outward, this ressentiment is dangerous to the healthy and useless to the sufferer; the sick are "man's greatest danger" because they poison the conscience of the fortunate (III 14). (4) The priest, sick himself and therefore trusted, redirects it: you suffer because you are guilty (III 15). (5) This gives suffering a meaning, discharges the ressentiment inward, and binds the sufferers into a herd under the priest (III 15-18). (6) His means are graded: innocent (deadening, work, small pleasures of helping, herd formation) and guilty (orgies of feeling: sin, remorse, redemption) (III 17-20). (7) The cost: the sufferer feels better and becomes sicker; "improved" means tamed (III 21). (8) Yet the ideal is life's own device: a declining life protecting itself by devaluing life; the contradiction is apparent (III 13). (9) And it is a will: better to will nothingness than not to will; the ideal "saved the will" and ruled faute de mieux, no counter-ideal existing (III 28; EH III GM).
- **Unstated premise.** That no better meaning was available to those people then (otherwise the priest's solution is a bad one, not the only one); that the healthy were never his audience; and (step 1) the physiological reading of "the sick" as a type with one cause, which the ascending/descending standard later carries.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE; gap sentence on the composite type.
- **Connections.** guilt-before-god (the instrument); slave-revolt-and-ressentiment (I 10 redirected); cruelty-and-culture (II 7; D 113-114); bad-conscience (III 20); ascetic-ideals-in-artists-and-philosophers (III 1-10); against-pity (III 14); danger-of-dangers (III 14; Pref 6); herd-morality-in-modern-dress (III 18; GS 358); will-to-truth (III 23-27); case-of-wagner (NcW); amor-fati, ecce-homo (EH I 2); skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man (TI 45; the standard).

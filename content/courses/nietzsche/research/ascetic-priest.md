# Research brief: ascetic-priest (The Ascetic Priest as Physician of the Sick)

status: planned
module: third-treatise
type: core
goal: Formulate GM III:11–22 and III:28 as a mechanism — a declining life defends itself through an ideal that redirects the sufferer's search for a cause onto the sufferer — and state what the mechanism explains that the First Treatise could not.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The ascetic ideal as a type-relative structure; the philosopher unmasked as a priest's chrysalis; from Module 2 the victory gap (why the slave revolt won); from Module 3 the moralized guilt with no administrator.
- **Contributes:** The priest's mechanism reconstructed: the ideal as life's strategy against its own decline (III:11–13); the redirection of ressentiment ('you yourself are to blame', III:15); the remedies ranked by cost (III:17–21); the closing thesis (III:28: senseless suffering, the meaning the ideal supplied, 'rather will nothingness'). The three treatises joined: what I (ressentiment) and II (bad conscience) needed from III (the priest) to become European morality — the gap closed.
- **Hands off:** To perspectivism (III:12, read separately) and will-to-truth (III:23–27), which turn the treatise on its author; to nihilism-and-affirmation, which takes III:28's 'will to nothingness' as the problem the positive doctrine answers.

Spine step: "Third Treatise: the ascetic ideal is what a declining life does with suffering — the priest redirects 'someone is to blame' onto the sufferer — and modern science is its latest form because it rests on an unconditional faith in truth; the critique reaches Nietzsche's own will to truth, and The Antichrist runs the machinery on Jesus and Paul."

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **III:11–13: the ideal as strategy.** The priest's valuation: life as a bridge to another existence; the ideal 'springs from the protective and healing instincts of a degenerating life' — 'life wrestling with death and against death'; the ascetic priest as 'the incarnate wish for being otherwise' who is nonetheless 'among the greatest conserving and yes-creating forces'. Reconstruct: (P) a physiologically declining life is in danger of ending itself; (P) the ascetic ideal channels the sufferer's will into self-denial, which is still willing; (C) the ideal preserves the declining life by giving it an aim — it is life's own device, not life's opposite. This is the naturalist reading's key claim for the treatise (Leiter Ch. 8).
2. **III:14: the sick as the greatest danger to the healthy.** The herd's ressentiment; 'the sick are the greatest danger for the healthy'; the demand that the healthy be kept apart; the priest as the one who belongs to the sick and rules them. The nihilist misreading gets its first refutation here: nihilism is named as the danger ('nausea and pity' as the two things that would breed 'the will to nothingness').
3. **III:15: the redirection.** The priest as physician who must first wound; his art: to change the direction of ressentiment. The sufferer's 'I suffer: someone must be to blame' and the priest's 'quite so, my sheep — but you yourself are that someone'. Reconstruct as the two-line pivot on which the treatise turns: (P) suffering seeks a cause to discharge affect on; (P) discharging it outward would harm the herd and the priest's rule; (C) the priest redirects it onto the sufferer — guilt, the moralized bad conscience of II:22, is the instrument. The victory gap closes: the strong were not defeated by ressentiment but infected by its administration.
4. **III:16–21: the remedies, ranked.** Innocent means: hypnotic reduction of the feeling of life (III:17 — the saint's 'deep sleep', Buddhism's hygiene), mechanical activity ('the blessing of work', III:18), small pleasures ('love of one's neighbour', III:18), herd organization (III:18–19); the guilty means: the 'orgy of feeling' (III:19–20) — sin as the reinterpretation of bad conscience — the one that made the sick sicker. The cost ranking is Nietzsche's own (III:19: 'guilty'). Reconstruct the ranking's criterion: what each remedy does to the sufferer's health, not to his suffering.
5. **III:22 (in brief) and III:28: the conclusion earned.** Man's problem was not suffering but its senselessness; the ascetic ideal offered the only meaning so far; it brought guilt, fear, the 'will to nothingness' — but it saved the will: 'man would rather will nothingness than not will at all'. Reconstruct III:28 as the treatise's conclusion in three sentences, and state what it commits Nietzsche to: a counter-ideal that gives suffering a meaning without guilt — the problem of Module 6.
6. **The three treatises joined.** I: the inversion (ressentiment); II: the site (bad conscience) and the debt to God; III: the administrator (the priest) and the instrument (guilt as redirected ressentiment). One figure; the Genealogy is one argument in three installments, and the naturalist reading renders it as mechanism plus evidence at each step. The victory gap from Module 2 is now closed — say so.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- 'Physiologically declining' and 'sick' are Nietzsche's terms; the lesson uses them as his and does not gloss them with medicine. The Doctrine of Types is what makes them type-facts on the naturalist reading; say so once.
- III:15's redirection is the single most load-bearing move in the whole course; give it its own section and its own figure; do not bury it in the remedies.
- III:12 (perspectivism) sits inside this stretch; the lesson names it and defers it in one line.
- III:28 must be earned, not quoted at the top; the lesson ends with it.
- The priest is not a villain: III:13 calls him a conserving force; III:15's physician does help — at a cost. The register stays dry; the text's own ambivalence carries it.

## EXERCISE PLAN (floor: ≤1 warm-up; ≥2 of boundary / refute / derivation / research; one FORWARD)

1. (warm-up) Write III:15's pivot as a two-premise argument with its conclusion, and name the affect, the object, and the new object.
2. (refute) "Nietzsche's ascetic priest is the villain of the Genealogy; the treatise is an attack on priests." Break it with III:13's 'greatest conserving and yes-creating forces' and III:15's physician; state the actual charge (the cost of the remedy — III:19–20's 'guilty means') and the criterion by which it is a cost.
3. (derivation) Derive from III:14–15 why the redirection had to fall on the sufferer rather than on the priest or the healthy: two premises about the herd (its ressentiment would otherwise discharge on the healthy; the priest rules by keeping the herd together) and the conclusion. State what this adds to Module 2's gap (an explanation of victory that is not numbers).
4. (boundary probe) Consider a sufferer whose suffering has an obvious external cause (an injury by a named enemy). Does III:15's mechanism apply? State what the mechanism needs (a suffering whose cause is not available to affect) and whether the priest's redirection would be possible or necessary there — and what that shows about the population III:14 describes.
5. (causation ranking) Rank the three treatises' contributions (I: inversion; II: bad conscience; III: priest) as necessary / enabling / precipitating for the victory of slave morality, and defend the ranking from the sections; then state which one the First Treatise's own argument (Leiter's gap) lacked.
6. (dramatization) Map Z II 'On Priests' onto III:11–15: which lines correspond to the priest as sick among the sick, which to the redirection, and which have no argued counterpart; state what the speech adds (Zarathustra's 'my blood is related to theirs') that GM III's 'we' does not admit.
7. (FORWARD → will-to-truth) III:28 says the ascetic ideal was 'the only meaning so far'. Write the question this raises about the person who has just diagnosed it — from what ideal is the Genealogy itself written? — and find III:23's opening sentence on science as the ideal's 'natural antagonist'; the solution says III:23–27 answer that the antagonist is the ideal's latest form, and the lesson after next reads it.

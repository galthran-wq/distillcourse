# Research brief: bad-conscience (Bad conscience: cruelty turned inward)

status: planned
module: guilt-and-the-priest (4 of 9), lesson 19 of 45
type: core
goal: Analyze the claim that bad conscience is the instinct of cruelty turned back on the self when society walled the human animal in

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (7, "Guilt and the priest"):** "Guilt began as debt: a promising animal learned memory through pain, cruelty was the festival pleasure that higher culture refined rather than abolished, cruelty with no outlet turned inward as bad conscience, the debt to God made it unpayable, and the ascetic priest gave suffering a meaning by making the sufferer its cause"
- **Inherits:** from cruelty-and-culture, the pleasure in making suffer as a standing drive and BGE 229's enjoyment "even in one's own suffering"; from debt-and-punishment, that punishment does not produce guilt (GM II 14); from sublimation, that a blocked drive is redirected, not abolished (the hydraulic picture); from will-to-power-as-psychology, "the instinct for freedom (in my language: the will to power)" (II 18).
- **Contributes:** the mechanism that gives guilt its inward sting: internalization. Instincts denied outward discharge turn on their owner; this self-cruelty is the bad conscience, "the serious illness that man was bound to contract" when enclosed in society (II 16); the enclosure was imposed by conquerors, not agreed (II 17); the same force turned inward is "the active bad conscience ... the womb of all ideal and imaginative phenomena" (II 18); "an illness as pregnancy is an illness" (II 19). Bad conscience (raw self-torment) is distinguished from guilt (its moral reading). Introduces bad-conscience, internalization.
- **Hands off:** to guilt-before-god, the moralization: self-torment becomes guilt only when read as deserved punishment for a debt; the "ancestors become gods" step is II 19 (p.89), the next lesson's opening.

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** Nietzsche is the first to derive the inner life itself, "what was later called his soul", from an aggression that lost its outlet. Decades before the clinical theories of aggression turned inward, he saw that conscience is "not the voice of God in man" (EH III GM) but a drive with a new object, and that the turn that made man sick made him deep, the inventor of ideals and beauty (II 18). It explains why the inner sting precedes any belief about deserving it, why the righteous are cruelest to themselves, and why ideals of selflessness feel like a wound.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** GM II 17 (p.86), the blond beasts passage (genealogy--text.txt 2836-2858), with Kaufmann's n.1 and the German "irgendein Rudel blonder Raubtiere, eine Eroberer- und Herren-Rasse"; GM II 16 (pp.84-85), the internalization sentences; GM II 22 (p.93, 3129), the madhouse. Texts in Set pieces.
- **What the words meant:** "Blond beasts of prey" (blonde Raubtiere): the lion, as Kaufmann's n.3 to I 11 (pp.40-41) shows from Nietzsche's own list (Roman, Arabian, Germanic, Japanese nobility, Homeric heroes, Vikings), with I 11's disclaimer (p.42) that the Germanic conquerors and modern Germans share "scarcely a conceptual relationship, let alone one of blood". "Master race" (Herren-Rasse): a people formed into a commanding type by long conditions and selection, Rasse as in BGE 262 and 264; the vocabulary is hereditary and the sentence says the first states were founded by conquest; the lesson states both. "Internalization" (Verinnerlichung): a drive's change of object from the world to its owner. "Conscience" (Gewissen) is not consciousness (Kaufmann n.1 to II 1); "bad conscience" here is raw self-torment before any moral interpretation. "Artists" (II 17): form-giving power, Nietzsche's standard sense.
- **Objection answered from his side:** Why should enclosure produce self-cruelty rather than sublimation into games, war against outsiders and art, which the chapter admits existed? Reply: II 16 says the instincts turned inward only in so far as they could not discharge outward; II 7's festivals and II 9's punishment of the criminal are outlets; internalization is the residue where discharge failed, and II 18 shows the residue is productive. Gap sentence: the premise that a blocked drive keeps its force and must go somewhere is taken over from the drive psychology and not argued here. Second, one paragraph: II 17's founding by conquest is unverifiable; reply: the mechanism needs only that the enclosure was imposed and sudden ("a break, a leap, a compulsion"), and Nietzsche frames the contract story as what has been "disposed of" rather than proving conquest.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** Kaufmann reads bad conscience as pregnancy, "burning a No into one's own soul", on its way to health; Clark & Dudrick read II 16-19 as the genealogy of political order with the soul as "a ruling structure that lives". Both are in the text, which adds what they soften: a "master race" whose work was "carried to its conclusion by nothing but acts of violence" (II 17), and II 22's madhouse. Pregnancy (II 19) and madhouse (II 22) are both given.
- **Facts against the popular image:** Kaufmann's n.1 to II 17 records that Golffing's 1956 translation deleted the blond beasts and the master race; the course quotes them. I 11's sentence that the Germanic conquerors and modern Germans share no blood is in the text.

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** GM II 16 (p.84): bad conscience is "the serious illness that man was bound to contract under the stress of the most fundamental change he ever experienced ... enclosed within the walls of society and of peace"; "All instincts that do not discharge themselves outwardly turn inward."
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) Early man discharged his instincts outward: hunting, attack, cruelty (II 16). (2) Enclosure in society was sudden and imposed by conquerors, not agreed (II 17). (3) The instincts kept their force; a drive's outlet can be closed, not its demand (premise from sublimation). (4) Blocked outward, they turned on the only object left, their owner (II 16). (5) This self-directed cruelty is the bad conscience: suffering from oneself before any thought of desert. (6) The turn opened an inner space, the "soul", and the same force "active" is "the womb of all ideal and imaginative phenomena" (II 18). (7) Hence the double verdict: an illness "as pregnancy is an illness" (II 19); a madhouse once religion drives it to the limit (II 22). (8) Punishment did not cause it (II 14) and remorse presupposes belief in freedom (HH 39): bad conscience is older than guilt, whose moral reading is later (III 20; GS 250).
- **Unstated premise.** Steps 3-4: the hydraulic picture and the substitution rule (the self becomes the object rather than the drive weakening); step 2's abruptness.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE; gap sentence on the hydraulic premise.
- **Connections.** cruelty-and-culture; guilt-before-god (II 19-22); ascetic-priest (III 20; III 15); sublimation (the healthy counterpart; shared premise); self-as-society-of-drives (D 109); doer-behind-the-deed (HH 39); higher-type, becoming-who-you-are (II 18; EH II 1); drive-psychology-and-experiment (38[1]; III 16).

That is after all how the 'state' began on earth: I think that sentimentalism which would have it begin with a 'contract' has been disposed of. He who can command, he who is by nature 'master,' he who is violent in act and bearing, what has he to do with contracts! One does not reckon with such natures; they come like fate, without reason, consideration, or pretext; they appear as lightning appears, too terrible, too sudden, too convincing, too 'different' even to be hated. ## EXERCISE PLAN

Floor check: claim to refute (ex. 2), boundary probe (ex. 3), research-shaped (ex. 4), derivation (ex. 5).

1. Retrieval (E3, sublimation): state what sublimation does to a drive (keeps the force, changes the object, raises it) and which of the three fails in internalization.
2. Claim to refute: "Bad conscience is feeling guilty about something you did." Distinguish with II 14, III 20 and HH 39; name the failing step.
3. Boundary probe: a society with abundant outward outlets for aggression; what II 16-18 predicts about its members, what II 7 adds, where the prediction becomes untestable.
4. Research-shaped (sourcing drill): II 17 whole and Kaufmann's notes (I 11 n.3; II 17 n.1) as documents of 1887 and 1967; what each can establish about "blond beasts" and "master race"; what a translation that deletes them establishes about its translator.
5. Derivation: from II 18 (the active bad conscience as womb of ideals) derive why Nietzsche cannot simply want bad conscience abolished; reconcile with D 164.
6. FORWARD (E7, reaches guilt-before-god): self-torment hurts more if believed deserved and unpayable; name what must be added to bad conscience to make it so (a creditor, a debt, a doer). Solution: the next lesson shows Christianity supplying all three, `<Ref>` guilt-before-god.

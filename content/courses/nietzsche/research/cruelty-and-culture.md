# Research brief: cruelty-and-culture (Cruelty as an ingredient of culture)

status: planned
module: guilt-and-the-priest (4 of 9), lesson 18 of 45
type: core
goal: Explain Nietzsche's claim that making suffer was the oldest festival pleasure of mankind and that higher culture rests on cruelty spiritualized rather than abolished

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (7, "Guilt and the priest"):** "Guilt began as debt: a promising animal learned memory through pain, cruelty was the festival pleasure that higher culture refined rather than abolished, cruelty with no outlet turned inward as bad conscience, the debt to God made it unpayable, and the ascetic priest gave suffering a meaning by making the sufferer its cause"
- **Inherits:** from debt-and-punishment, the premise left standing: the creditor's compensation is the pleasure of making suffer (GM II 5-6); from will-to-power-as-psychology, pleasure as the feeling of power growing (GS 13; BGE 13; A 2); from sublimation, "spiritualization" (Vergeistigung, TI "Morality as Anti-Nature" 3); from master-morality, the pathos of distance (D 113).
- **Contributes:** the premise examined: making suffer was the festival pleasure, "without cruelty there is no festival" (II 6-7; D 18); what offends is senseless suffering, so the gods were invented as spectators (II 7); culture refined the pleasure instead of abolishing it, "almost everything we call 'higher culture' rests on the spiritualization and deepening of cruelty" (BGE 229); the ladder of distinction runs from the barbarian to the ascetic (D 113). Introduces festival-of-cruelty, spiritualized-cruelty.
- **Hands off:** to bad-conscience, II 18's "active bad conscience" as the same cruelty turned inward; to ascetic-priest, II 7's premise that senseless suffering is the unbearable thing (III 28); to against-pity, "tragic pity" as sublimated cruelty (II 7).

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** Nietzsche looked at what the moral psychology of his century would not: pleasure in another's pain is a standing human pleasure, once public and festive, and civilization's achievement was its translation, not its removal. One principle explains the crowd at an execution, the audience at a tragedy, the ascetic's self-torment and the scholar's pleasure in a refutation, and makes the cross and the tragic catastrophe one family.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** GM II 7 (p.68), the "Negroes" passage (genealogy--text.txt 2102-2120); GM II 6-7 (pp.65-68, 2065-2144), the festival and the gods as spectators; BGE 229 whole (bge-zimmern.txt 3993-4041; bge-german.txt 4897-4932), thesis in the course's own translation; Z III 13 §2 (pp.330-331, zarathustra-full.txt 7995-8020), illustration; BGE 225 (zimmern 3847). Texts in Set pieces.
- **What the words meant:** "Cruelty" (Grausamkeit): pleasure in making suffer, in oneself or another, as feeling of power (II 5-6; D 18 "to practise cruelty is to enjoy the highest gratification of the feeling of power"; HH 103 "the pleasure caused by the display of our power over others"), a drive neutral in itself (D 38; HH 103 "all pleasure is, in itself, neither good nor bad"). "Festival" (Fest): the community's licensed discharge of accumulated hostility (D 18). "Spiritualization" and "deepening" (Vergeistigung, Vertiefung): a drive raised with its force kept; "deified" (vergöttlicht): made a god, not made tame. "Negroes ... representatives of prehistoric man": a physiological scale of pain-sensitivity rising with cultural refinement, the "top stratum" feeling most, the hysterical bluestocking at the top; its evidence is an unnamed doctor's report, it treats Africans as a living sample of early man, and it serves to argue that early cruelty hurt less; the lesson states the claim as made and shows that II 7's argument does not need it (II 3 and D 18 attest the festival pleasure independently). "Tragic pity": the spectator's pleasure at staged suffering, counted as cruelty under an innocent name (II 7; BGE 229).
- **Objection answered from his side:** "Nietzsche recommends cruelty." Reply: BGE 229's thesis is descriptive and its demand is "one ought to learn anew about cruelty, and open one's eyes" (zimmern 4003), to see, not to do; II 7 describes an ingredient and ranks its refined forms higher, since the pleasure now "requires a certain sublimation and subtilization" (p.68); the knower's cruelty is the top rung of BGE 229-230, the ascetic the far end of D 113. What he recommends is the discipline of great suffering (BGE 225) and not falsifying the ingredient out of the account. Gap sentence: the ranking of spiritualized over raw cruelty is asserted by placement and tone, not argued from a stated standard; the standard that would do it, ascending versus descending life, is named only in TI Skirmishes 33.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** Kaufmann's monograph reads BGE 229-230 as the doctrine of sublimation, so culture is cruelty tamed. The text: "not killed ... deified"; BGE 225 wants suffering "higher and worse"; GS 266 "Those who have greatness are cruel to their virtues"; EH III BGE 2 (p.311) BGE's psychology "practiced with admitted hardness and cruelty". One reading, then the sentences.
- **Facts against the popular image:** none in this lesson's texts; the "Negroes" passage cuts the other way and is quoted whole.

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** GM II 6-7: making suffer was a pleasure and a festival, and the debt-pain equivalence rests on it; BGE 229: higher culture rests on cruelty spiritualized and deepened, "this is my thesis".
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) Pleasure is the feeling of power growing; hurting and benefiting are both ways of feeling it (GS 13; HH 103). (2) Early communities, living in fear and constraint, discharged hostility in licensed public cruelty: the festival (D 18; II 6). (3) The gods were imagined as spectators who enjoy such spectacles; suffering seen by a god is justified; voluntary suffering acquires value (II 7; D 18). (4) What men could not bear was suffering nobody saw and that meant nothing (II 7). (5) As culture refined its members the pleasure had to appear "translated into the imaginative and psychical" under innocent names (II 7). (6) The translations are the institutions of higher culture: tragedy and its pity, the sublime, the ecstasy of the cross, asceticism turned on oneself, the knower's violence against his own spirit (BGE 229-230). (7) One ladder runs from the barbarian who torments others to the ascetic who torments himself and watches (D 113), and from the festival to the "active bad conscience" that makes ideals (II 18). (8) So the wild animal was deified, not killed, and the wish to abolish suffering wishes away the condition of every elevation so far (BGE 225).
- **Unstated premise.** Step 5: a drive's force is conserved when its outlet closes; step 6: one ingredient in all the pleasures listed, argued by enumeration, not by a test.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE; gap sentence on the unargued ranking.
- **Connections.** debt-and-punishment; bad-conscience (II 18); ascetic-priest (II 7 cashed out in III 28); against-pity (tragic pity; BGE 225); sublimation, will-to-power-as-psychology (the mechanism); schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy (GT 2); skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man (TI 37; the standard); will-to-truth (BGE 230).

## Set pieces and gems

- II 6 (p.67): "To see others suffer does one good, to make others suffer even more: this is a hard saying but an ancient, mighty, human, all-too-human principle"; "Without cruelty there is no festival: thus the longest and most ancient part of human history teaches".
- II 7 (p.68), whole: "Perhaps in those days (the delicate may be comforted by this thought) pain did not hurt as much as it does now; at least that is the conclusion a doctor may arrive at who has treated Negroes (taken as representatives of prehistoric man) for severe internal inflammations that would drive even the best constituted European to distraction; in the case of Negroes they do not do so. (The curve of human susceptibility to pain seems in fact to take an extraordinary and almost sudden drop as soon as one has passed the upper ten thousand or ten million of the top stratum of culture; and for my own part, I have no doubt that the combined suffering of all the animals ever subjected to the knife for scientific ends is utterly negligible compared with one painful night of a single hysterical bluestocking.)" Then: "What really arouses indignation against suffering is not suffering as such but the senselessness of suffering"; "every evil the sight of which edifies a god is justified".
- BGE 229 (own translation; verify eKGWB JGB-229): "Almost everything we call 'higher culture' rests on the spiritualization and deepening of cruelty: this is my thesis; that 'wild animal' has not been killed at all, it lives, it flourishes, it has only been deified" (Vergeistigung und Vertiefung der Grausamkeit; vergöttlicht); the Roman in the arena, the Christian at the cross, the Spaniard at the stake and the bullfight, the Japanese at tragedy, the Paris workman homesick for revolutions, the Wagnerienne who "undergoes" Tristan; "a drop of cruelty".
- BGE 225 (own translation): "You want, if possible, and there is no madder 'if possible', to abolish suffering; and we? it seems we would rather have it higher and worse than ever it was!"
- D 18 (p.16): "Cruelty is one of the oldest festive joys of mankind"; D 113 (p.68): "man split asunder into a sufferer and a spectator".
- Z III 13 §2 (p.330): "man is the cruelest animal. At tragedies, bullfights, and crucifixions he has so far felt best on earth; and when he invented hell for himself, behold, that was his heaven on earth" (illustration; Zarathustra to his animals).
- Homer's Contest (Portable p.33): "the tiger leaped out, voluptuous cruelty in his terrible eyes" (unpublished, 1872).

1. Retrieval (E3, will-to-power-as-psychology and debt-and-punishment): define pleasure as feeling of power (GS 13; A 2) and say which step of II 5-6 it makes intelligible.

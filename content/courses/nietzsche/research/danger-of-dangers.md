# Research brief: danger-of-dangers (Morality as the danger of dangers)

status: planned
module: what-morality-does (5 of 9), lesson 24 of 45
type: core
goal: Assess Nietzsche's overall charge that morality rests on false beliefs about the self and harms the highest human beings

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (8, "What morality does to us"):** "What this morality does to us: pity multiplies suffering and spends the strong, the whole system rests on false beliefs about the self and harms the highest human beings, and it survives the death of God in the secular dress of equality, democracy, socialism and emancipation, which Twilight's Skirmishes diagnose as the modern form of decline"
- **Inherits:** from doer-behind-the-deed, the falsity of free will and the fiction of the doer; from consciousness-and-language and drive-psychology-and-experiment, the opaque self and type-facts; from against-pity, one harmful norm; from modules 3-4, the genealogies of good/evil, guilt and the ascetic ideal as the evidence the charge rests on.
- **Contributes:** the verdict of the whole critique, assembled: which morality Nietzsche denies (D 103), its false presuppositions, its harmful norms, and the mechanism of harm (internalised concepts, not law). Introduces morality-in-the-pejorative-sense and harm-to-higher-types. The second movement of the throughline ("what they do to us") stated in full.
- **Hands off:** to herd-morality-in-modern-dress, which follows this morality into its secular forms; to skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man, where TI 33 names the standard the charge uses (ascending or descending life) and 37 states "in itself, no morality has any value"; to order-of-rank, which argues the standard.

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** Nietzsche is the first to ask what a morality costs, and to answer in terms of a type of human being rather than a sum of welfare: "precisely morality would be to blame if the highest power and splendor actually possible to the type man was never in fact attained" (GM Pref 6, p.20). The lesson shows the originality of the move — morality treated as a symptom and a cause (EH III BT 2: "seeing morality itself as a symptom of decadence is an innovation and a singularity of the first rank in the history of knowledge") — and why it is compelling from inside: once free will, the transparent self and one nature for all have fallen (module 2), a morality that presupposes them is standing on nothing, and what it then does is the only question left.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** BGE 228 (`bge-zimmern.txt`, line ~3950): Zimmern "the requirement of one morality for all is really a detriment to the higher men" (the familiar "the demand of one morality for all is detrimental to the higher men" is Kaufmann's; German "die Forderung Einer Moral für Alle ist die Beeinträchtigung gerade der höheren Menschen", verify eKGWB JGB-228); BGE 221: "it is IMMORAL to say: what is right for one is proper for another"; BGE 203 (line 2963): the danger of "the degeneracy and dwarfing of man to an absolutely gregarious animal" ("herding-animal" in Zimmern), "the universal degeneracy of mankind to the man of the future"; BGE 62 (line 1901): "equality before God" produced "a dwarfed, almost ludicrous species, a herd animal, something obliging, sickly, mediocre"; EH IV 4 (p.329, `eccehomo-full.txt`): "To consider distress of all kinds as an objection, as something that must be abolished, is the niaiserie par excellence ... almost as stupid as would be the desire to abolish bad weather — say, from pity for poor people"; EH IV 7 (pp.332-333): the morality that would un-self man as "the fact, I am declining, transposed into the imperative, all of you ought to decline", and the hedge "perhaps not humanity is degenerating but only that parasitical type of man — that of the priest"; EH IV 8 (p.334): "one sides with all that is weak, sick, failure ... the principle of selection is crossed out"; GM III 14 (pp.121-125, `genealogy--text.txt`): the sick as "man's greatest danger", quarantine of the healthy; Z III 12 §20 (p.321, `zarathustra-full.txt`): "O my brothers, am I cruel? But I say: what is falling, we should still push" (verify wording in the file); D 146 (p.92): "what would forbid us to sacrifice our neighbour as well?"
- **What the words meant:** "morality" in GM Pref 6 is the morality of the unegoistic — pity, self-denial, self-sacrifice — Pref 5 has just named as "the real stake"; in D 103 it is a morality resting on premises (free agent, transparent motive, supernatural sanction); "morality in the pejorative sense" is the course's label for this target (taken from the Secondary), not Nietzsche's word, and the body names the morality in plain words. "Type man" (Typus Mensch) and "higher men" (höhere Menschen, BGE 228) are types of human being, judged by what they can do and bear, as module 2 defined type-facts. "Anti-nature" (TI Anti-Nature 4) is a morality that turns against the instincts of life rather than organising them. "The good" in EH IV 4-5 is a type, "the benevolent, the beneficent", whom Zarathustra calls "the last men" and "the beginning of the end". "Parasite" (EH IV 7; TI Skirmishes 33) is the physiological word for a life that takes from ascending life. "Selection" (EH IV 8; A 7) is life's weeding of what has turned out badly, which the morality "crosses out". "Decline"/"decadence" carries A 6's definition.
- **Objection answered from his side:** the Harm Puzzle, in its two forms: this morality nowhere forbids a gifted person to work and almost nobody practises it fully, so how can it harm the highest; and the world has too little pity and selflessness, not too much (the objection the Secondary attributes to Foot). Reply from the texts: the harm runs through belief, not law — TI "The 'Improvers' of Mankind" 2 (p.502): the "improved" Teuton in the monastery, "a 'sinner', he was stuck in a cage, imprisoned among all sorts of terrible concepts"; D 148 (pp.93-94): "When man no longer regards himself as evil he ceases to be so" (the mechanism run in reverse); EH IV 7: the imperative "all of you ought to decline" works on those who believe it; BGE 62: the long effect of "equality before God" on the type; GS 377 and Z Prologue 5: the "last men" as where the belief leads. Nietzsche's complaint is that people over-believe in selflessness and equality, not that they practise too much kindness (D 132, 148; TI Improvers 2; BGE 44). Gap sentence: the harm is asserted in the conditional ("would be to blame if ... never in fact attained", Pref 6) and illustrated, never shown by a case; the lesson says so. The deeper gap, raised by the editor of the late notebooks as the "official objection" (Bittner Intro xxx-xxxiii): a morality hostile to life that arose from life is a puzzle; Nietzsche's own answer is GM III 13 (the ascetic ideal as "an artifice for the preservation of life" of declining life), and the alternative image — a morality that fitted once and harms now, like outgrown shoes — is the editor's, reported as his.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** that the immoralism aims only at morality's absolutism, "the taste for the unconditional", not at its content (Nehamas Ch.7); that the critique is a self-overcoming of morality out of truthfulness and leaves the herd's morality to the herd (Kaufmann; notebook "the herd's morality should rule in the herd"); and that the target is a formal construct of theses (the Secondary's "MPS"). What the text says: EH IV 4 names "the good" as a type to be devalued, BGE 203 calls the herd animal the degeneration of man, Pref 6 makes the morality itself the danger; the anti-absolutism is there (BGE 202's "I am morality itself, and nothing besides is morality!"; D 164 "no morality with an exclusive monopoly of the moral") and it is not all.
- **Facts against the popular image:** "Nietzsche is against morality, so anything goes" is a real misreading (L13); D 103 refutes it in its own words: "I also deny immorality: not that countless people feel themselves to be immoral, but that there is any true reason so to feel", and many actions called immoral "ought to be avoided ... for other reasons than hitherto"; D Preface 4 (p.4) gives his own "thou shalt"; GS 380 (pp.342-343) describes the position outside morality as a "minor madness" needed to compare moralities.

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** GM Pref 6: what if morality were the danger of dangers, so that "precisely morality would be to blame if the highest power and splendor actually possible to the type man was never in fact attained?" BGE 228: "the demand of one morality for all is detrimental to the higher men" (Kaufmann's rendering; Zimmern's in Primary passages). TI Improvers 1: "there are altogether no moral facts."
- **Which morality.** Not valuing as such (D Preface 4's "thou shalt"; TI Anti-Nature 4's "healthy morality"), and not the avoidance of acts called immoral (D 103). The target is a morality that claims to be the only one (BGE 202; D 164), binds everyone alike (BGE 221, 228), rests on free will, a transparent self and one human nature, and ranks comfort, selflessness, equality and pity first (EH IV 7-8).
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) The target morality presupposes that agents are free causes of their acts (false: doer-behind-the-deed; TI Four Errors 7), that they know their motives (false: consciousness-and-language; D 116, 129), and that one morality suits everyone (false: types differ, drive-psychology-and-experiment; BGE 221, 228). (2) Its norms favour happiness over suffering, selflessness over self-love, equality over rank, pity over hardness (EH IV 7-8; BGE 44, 201-202). (3) These norms suit the many — they protect, comfort and remove what is feared (BGE 201's "we want that some day there will be nothing more to fear") — and harm those capable of great work, who need solitude, self-concern, suffering and the sense of rank (BGE 225; GS 338; EH IV 4). (4) The harm travels by belief: a gifted person who holds the morality reads self-concern as guilt, suffering as objection, rank as injustice, and spends himself (TI Improvers 2; D 148; EH IV 7). (5) Since the worth of a culture lies in its highest specimens (the standard handed to order-of-rank; UM II 9), the morality that thwarts them is the greatest danger to what humanity could be (Pref 6).
- **Unstated premise.** Step 5's standard: that the flourishing of the highest types is what matters most; without it steps 1-4 show a trade-off (comfort for many against greatness for few), not a verdict. The critique of the presuppositions (step 1) stands independently of the standard.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE: the Harm Puzzle and the "too little pity" objection; reply from TI Improvers 2, D 148, EH IV 7, BGE 62; the gap sentence (the harm asserted in the conditional, not shown by a case); the "official objection" and its two answers (GM III 13 as Nietzsche's; the shoes as the editor's).
- **Connections.** against-pity (one norm); herd-morality-in-modern-dress (the secular forms); skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man (TI 33's standard, 37's "no morality has any value in itself"); order-of-rank and higher-type (the standard argued); creating-values (what replaces the denied morality); nihilism (GM Pref 5's "new Buddhism"; GM I 12); doer-behind-the-deed and four-great-errors (the presuppositions); the-system-assembled (where the argument "holds in its critique, strains in its reply").

## Pitfalls

- "Nietzsche is against morality, so anything goes" (L13; real and common): D 103's own second sentence refutes it.
- "Morality in the pejorative sense" is an interpretive label; the body names the morality in plain words and never attributes the label to Nietzsche.
- D 103 says nothing about the alchemists' practice having effects; quote what it says.
- D 173's "danger of dangers" is the security-minded society's voice, not Nietzsche's verdict; present it as the mirror image.
- Kaufmann's chapter is "Morality as Anti-Nature"; D 103 and 174 are not in the Portable (cite the Cambridge Daybreak).
- No constructed profile of "the genius who squanders himself" (L9 bans invented scenes); the mechanism is stated in Nietzsche's phrases, or with a documented case if one is to hand.
- The standard (step 5) is argued in order-of-rank; here it is stated as his and marked as the premise the verdict needs.
- Scholars' names in References only; "the Harm Puzzle" may be used as a phrase, unattributed.

## EXERCISE PLAN

Floor check: claim to refute (ex. 2), boundary probe (ex. 4), derivation (ex. 5), research-shaped (ex. 3).

1. Retrieval (E3 from doer-behind-the-deed, consciousness-and-language, drive-psychology-and-experiment): state the three false presuppositions and the lesson each was argued in, with one text for each.
2. Claim to refute: "Since Nietzsche attacks morality, he must think murder and theft are fine." Use D 103 to state what he denies and what he leaves standing, and D Preface 4 for his own "thou shalt".
3. Research-shaped: take one rule from a modern code of conduct (a workplace wellbeing policy, a school honour code) and test it against the four rows of the pro/con table; report which rows it occupies and which it does not.
4. Boundary probe: a society with no exceptional individuals at all, ever. Does the charge of Pref 6 still have force there? What does the answer show about the standard step 5 relies on?
5. Derivation the lesson did not do: if the three presuppositions are false but the norms were harmless to higher types, what remains of the critique? Derive the answer from the structure of the argument (step 1 stands; steps 3-5 fall) and say which of Nietzsche's texts would survive.
6. FORWARD (reaches herd-morality-in-modern-dress): read D 132 and BGE 202 and name three modern political ideals that, on Nietzsche's account, carry the same norms without God. The solution notes that <Ref> herd-morality-in-modern-dress reads BGE 199-203 and GS 377 whole to test the claim.

# Research brief: debt-and-guilt (Debt, Punishment, and the Escalation to a Debt before God)

status: planned
module: second-treatise
type: core
goal: Formulate GM II:4–8 and II:19–22 as one chain — Schuld as debt, punishment as compensation, cruelty as festival, ancestors becoming gods — and locate where the chain becomes moral.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The promising animal and the sovereign individual's conscience; the reader's forward exercise on Schuld/Schulden.
- **Contributes:** The Second Treatise's second origin story reconstructed as a chain of substitutions: the philological premise (II:4) graded; the equivalence of injury and pain (II:5–6) with cruelty as compensation and as festival; the person as measurer (II:8); and the escalation (II:19–22) from individual debt to the tribe's debt to ancestors, to gods, to the Christian God as maximal debt paid by the creditor himself. The lesson locates the one step where owing becomes being-guilty and shows it needs I:13's doer — the missing premise named.
- **Hands off:** To internalization-and-bad-conscience (II:16–18 supplies the site — bad conscience — that the escalation's last step attaches guilt to); to guilt-reconstructed.

Spine step: "Second Treatise: guilt is debt moralized — a promising animal bred by the morality of custom, the creditor–debtor relation, cruelty turned inward when society caged it, escalating to a debt to God — and the sovereign individual is the naturalist reading's first strain."

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **II:4: the philological premise, graded.** 'The moral concept Schuld descends from the material concept Schulden' — one root, two senses. What it proves (a conjecture with a history to be found), what it does not (an identity of concepts); Nietzsche's own hedge that the retributive idea ('he could have acted otherwise') is 'late', and that punishment for most of history was rage at an injury, discharged on the injurer 'as on a piece of property'.
2. **II:5–6: the equivalence.** The contractual relation: a debtor pledges body, freedom, wife, life; the creditor's compensation for an unpaid debt is the right to inflict suffering — 'a genuine festival'; 'to see suffering does one good, to make suffer even more so'; the equivalence of injury and pain as the oldest form of justice. Reconstruct: (P) debt relations preceded moral ones; (P) unpaid debt was compensated by the creditor's pleasure in cruelty; (C) 'guilt' and 'punishment' descend from an economy of pain before any moral idea of desert. Don Quixote once read as comedy, now 'with a bitter taste' — the shift in sensibility as datum.
3. **II:8: the person as measurer.** Buying, selling, exchange, 'the oldest thinking'; 'everything has its price' as the first moral canon; justice as good will among roughly equal powers. The link to the sovereign individual: the animal that measures is the animal that promises.
4. **II:9–11 and II:14–15 (in brief).** The community as creditor; the criminal as breaker of contract; mercy as the strong community's luxury; what punishment does not do — it does not produce guilt in the punished (II:14: 'punishment makes hard and cold'), only prudence. This is evidence that guilt did not come from punishment — the chain needs another route.
5. **II:19–22: the escalation.** The tribe's debt to its ancestors → sacrifice → the ancestor grows into a god as the tribe grows → the god's debt cannot be repaid → the Christian God as the maximum ('the creditor sacrificing himself for the debtor'). Reconstruct as a chain of substitutions with one variable (the creditor) growing at each step. II:22: 'the will to torture oneself' — guilt before God as 'the madness of the will' — the point at which bad conscience (II:16) is needed.
6. **Where the chain moralizes — the missing premise.** Owing becomes being-guilty only when the debtor is held able to have paid and to have chosen not to; that is I:13's doer. Nietzsche's own text (II:4) calls the idea late; Leiter's third stage names it; the lesson states that II:4–22 contain no argument for the transition, only its location (II:21–22's 'moralization of the concepts Schuld and Pflicht' — 'their being pushed back into bad conscience'). The chain's moral step is borrowed from the First Treatise.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **The chain of substitutions** (the reader's figure suggestion): individual debt → tribe/ancestors → god → the Christian God — the argument is itself a chain, drawn as one.
- **The pledge list** (II:5): body, freedom, wife, life, salvation — the debtor's collateral.
- **'Punishment is a festival'** (II:6) and the Don Quixote aside as the case of shifting sensibility.
- **II:14's 'punishment makes hard and cold'** — the negative result that forces the escalation route.
- **The Schuld/Schulden sentence** in German with the course's grading note (translation chapter cross-reference).
- **Janaway's 2×2** (p. 147) as the lesson's closing figure: the debt chain (II:4–8) supplies the 'legitimated' column, the next lesson's internalization supplies the 'inward' row, and guilt is the cell where both hold — the two origin stories are two axes, not two rivals.
- **BGE 78 on the despiser** as the one-line answer to 'why would anyone punish himself?' — the reader identifies with the inflicter, not only the sufferer.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The 'catalogue of punishments' belongs to II:3 (previous lesson); II:5–6 add the pleasure in cruelty, not more punishments.
- Nietzsche's anthropology (the primitive creditor–debtor relation, the tribe's ancestor-debt) is 19th-century conjecture; the course grades it as Nietzsche himself grades the retributive idea — as hypothesis with a location in the record. Do not import modern anthropology to 'correct' it; state the evidential status.
- II:21's Christian step ('God sacrificing himself for man's debt') is the escalation's ceiling; the Antichrist runs it on Paul — forward only.
- Keep the moralization step precise: it is the reader's clearest instance of a missing premise supplied by another treatise, and the whole three-stage reconstruction (next-but-one lesson) turns on it.

## EXERCISE PLAN (floor: ≤1 warm-up; ≥2 of boundary / refute / derivation / research; one FORWARD)

1. (warm-up) Write the escalation as four lines, naming the creditor and the debtor at each step and the section that states it.
2. (refute) "Nietzsche says guilt is nothing but debt, so guilt is an illusion." Break it: II:4 says the moral concept descends from the material one, not that it is identical; state what the descent claim licenses (a history) and what the identity claim would need (an argument II:4 does not give).
3. (derivation) II:14 says punishment does not produce guilt. Derive from II:14 and II:16 together why the Second Treatise must find guilt's site elsewhere (in the punished animal's own blocked instincts), and state the premise about discharge that the derivation uses before the next lesson supplies it.
4. (boundary probe) Suppose a society with debt relations but no cruelty in compensation (fines only). On II:5–6's account, would 'guilt' arise there? State what the equivalence premise contributes that a fine cannot, and whether the chain could still moralize without it.
5. (missing premise) Locate, in II:21–22, the sentence where 'Schuld' first means 'guilt' rather than 'debt', and write the premise (about the debtor's ability to have paid) that the transition requires; then say which treatise supplies it and in which section.
6. (research-shaped, sourcing) Read Antichrist §§25–26's five-stage denaturalizing of Israel's God and compare it with II:19–21's escalation as two accounts of the same step (the national god becoming the moral creditor): which is a history with evidence, which an assertion, and where do they disagree?
7. (FORWARD → internalization-and-bad-conscience) II:22 says the guilt before God is 'the will to torture oneself'. Write what must already exist in the animal for a debt to be turned into self-torture (an instinct for cruelty with no outward object), and find II:16's first sentence on it; the solution says the next lesson is that mechanism.

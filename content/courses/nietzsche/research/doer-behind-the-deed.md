# Research brief: doer-behind-the-deed (The Doer behind the Deed: Free Will as Fiction)

status: planned
module: first-treatise
type: core
goal: Formulate the argument of GM I:13, BGE 16–21 and Twilight's 'Four Great Errors' that the free agent behind an action is a grammatical fiction, and state which presupposition of morality it removes.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The slave revolt's mechanism and its gap; the forward exercise left the reader with the lambs' needed premise — that the bird of prey could have chosen otherwise.
- **Contributes:** The one argument in the First Treatise that is not history: the doer is a fiction added to the deed. Reconstructed from I:13 (lightning and its flash), BGE 16–17 (the cogito's unproven bundle; 'a thought comes when it wants'), BGE 19 (willing as command), BGE 21 (causa sui; un-free will equally mythological), and Twilight's Four Great Errors (the Cornaro case, false causality, imaginary causes, free will invented for punishment). Result: MPS's first descriptive presupposition removed — and the remainder named: responsibility as a type-fact, strong and weak wills, which is not nothing.
- **Hands off:** To promise-and-sovereign-individual (GM II:2's 'free' individual against I:13's verdict — the first strain); to soul-as-society-of-drives (BGE 19's under-wills as the positive psychology).

Spine step: "First Treatise: two modes of valuation with an asymmetry — 'good' coined first by the noble, 'evil' first by ressentiment — the slave revolt as the inversion, and the doer-behind-the-deed fiction that morality's first presupposition needs; Zarathustra's tarantulas stage the diagnosis."

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **I:13 reconstructed.** (P) Language separates every event into a doer and a doing ('the lightning flashes'), and 'the popular mind' takes the separation for real. (P) There is no lightning apart from the flash: strength is its expressions, not a subject that could refrain. (P) The lambs' judgment ('the bird is evil') needs the separation — a bird that could choose not to prey. (C) The judgment rests on a fiction 'added to the deed'; ressentiment's morality needs the fiction ('the weak are free to be weak, therefore the strong are accountable for being strong'). State what is proved: not that no one acts, but that the neutral, choosing substrate is an artefact of grammar.
2. **BGE 16–17: the cogito dismantled.** The 'immediate certainty' of 'I think' contains at least four claims (there is an I; it is the cause; thinking is one thing; I know what thinking is) — none certain. 'A thought comes when "it" wants' — the grammatical habit of a subject for every verb, 'even the "it" is too much'. Reconstruct as the same argument as I:13 applied to the thinker.
3. **BGE 19: willing is not simple.** A plurality of feelings (away-from, toward, the muscular feeling), a commanding thought, and 'above all' the affect of command; 'freedom of the will' is the commander's feeling of superiority over the under-wills that obey; 'L'effet c'est moi' — the ruling class identifying with the commonwealth's success. This is the positive replacement: what 'I will' reports is a relation among drives, and the felt freedom is real as a feeling. Preview only; soul-as-society-of-drives takes the full text.
4. **BGE 21: both fictions.** Causa sui — pulling oneself into existence by the hair — as 'the best self-contradiction ever conceived'; but 'un-free will' is the same mythology from the other side (cause and effect as concepts for description, not as things that push); what remains is 'strong and weak wills' — a difference of type, not of freedom.
5. **Twilight's Four Great Errors as the mechanism catalogue.** (1) Consequence for cause: Cornaro's diet did not cause his long life; his slow metabolism caused both. (2) False causality: the will, the ego, the 'thing in itself' as inner facts — none observed. (3) Imaginary causes: the sleeper's dream inventing a cannon-shot narrative after the sound — and religion inventing sin and grace after the physiological fact. (4) Free will: 'men were considered free so that they might be judged and punished' — the priest's invention; 'no one is responsible' as the innocence of becoming. Reconstruct each as a two-line inference.
6. **What is removed and what is not.** Removed: MPS's descriptive presupposition that agents could have done otherwise — hence the moralized blame of GM I and (forward) the moralized guilt of GM II. Not removed: differences among types (strong/weak wills), responsibility as a bred capacity (forward: GM II:2), and the feeling of freedom as a datum. The naturalist reading's Doctrine of Types is exactly this remainder. The reader must leave with the boundary drawn.
7. **The reader's own philosophy at the seam.** Hume's bundle and Kant's noumenal freedom are in the prerequisites; one paragraph each stating where I:13/BGE 21 agree with Hume (no observed self) and where they attack Kant (causa sui as the intelligible character — BGE 21's target by name).

- **LaPiere's road trip** (Knobe & Leiter p. 101) as the modern instance of §5's 'imaginary cause': the professed principle and the act came apart 249 times out of 250 — the number carried with its denominator, and the frame that the Four Great Errors predicted the direction.
- **Korsgaard's reply and Risse's answer** (pp. 62–68) as a two-column exchange: 'from the deliberative standpoint I must regard myself as free' / 'the drive-facts about how you decided are facts about that standpoint too' — the Kantian reader's own objection, stated at strength, then met.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The argument does not prove determinism; BGE 21 rejects 'un-free will' as firmly. The lesson must state the position as 'neither' and give the type-difference remainder, or the reader will file Nietzsche as a determinist.
- I:13's 'popular mind' and 'seduction of language' are the premises; do not import a philosophy of language beyond what the text says.
- Hume is a resource, not a source: keep the comparison to two sentences and cite the Treatise only by book and part.
- The Cornaro case's medical claims are Nietzsche's; the lesson reports the structure (reversed causation), not the physiology.
- Twilight is 1888, GM 1887, BGE 1886; the lesson cites the sequence and treats Twilight as the compressed restatement.
- Do not anticipate the sovereign individual beyond one forward sentence.

## EXERCISE PLAN (floor: ≤1 warm-up; ≥2 of boundary / refute / derivation / research; one FORWARD)

1. (warm-up) Write I:13's argument in four premises and a conclusion, each premise tied to one of its sentences.
2. (refute) "Nietzsche is a hard determinist: he denies free will, so everything is caused and no one is responsible." Break it with BGE 21's sentence on 'un-free will' and Twilight §8's 'innocence of becoming' read together; state the position that survives (type-difference; responsibility as bred) and what 'no one is responsible' means in it.
3. (derivation) BGE 17 says even 'it thinks' is too much. Derive the same conclusion for 'I will' from BGE 19: show that the commanding thought is itself an occurrence among the drives, and state the premise BGE 19 needs to avoid an infinite regress of commanders.
4. (boundary probe) Take the Cornaro schema (consequence for cause) to its limit: in a case where cause and effect are simultaneous and mutually sustaining (a habit and a temperament), does the schema still apply? State what Twilight §1 actually claims (a direction of explanation) and where it stops.
5. (missing premise) I:13 says the weak believe 'the strong man is free to be weak'. Write the premise about the weak's interest (they need the strong accountable) that connects this belief to ressentiment, and locate its statement in I:13's last sentences and I:14's workshop.
6. (research-shaped, sourcing) Twilight §7 asserts that free will was invented 'for the purpose of punishment'. Find the corresponding claim in GM II (§4's 'the criminal deserves punishment because he could have acted otherwise' — Nietzsche calls this idea 'late') and state which of the two passages carries a historical argument and which only an assertion; report what evidence GM II:4 offers.
7. (FORWARD → promise-and-sovereign-individual) GM II:2 describes an individual 'entitled to make promises', 'autonomous', with 'the right to say yes to himself'. Using I:13's verdict, write the objection that this individual is a doer behind a deed, then read II:2's sentence on 'the long history of how responsibility originated' and say what would have to be true of 'freedom' for the two passages to be consistent; the solution says the next lesson is that reconciliation.

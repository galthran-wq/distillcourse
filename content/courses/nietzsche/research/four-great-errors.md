## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** in eight short sections Nietzsche reversed the causal arrow of morality (virtue as the effect of a well-constituted life), named motivated explanation as a mechanism ("any explanation is better than none"), and concluded that nobody is responsible for being as he is, a century before psychology studied the direction of causation between character and well-being or the comfort of a bad explanation over none. The lesson shows the chapter as the module's psychology doing its work and ends on what "Errors" 8 achieves: the world redeemed from judgment.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** "Errors" 2 (pp.493-494; a people that "degenerates physiologically", the young man's "hereditary exhaustion", "a party which makes such mistakes has reached its end", "All that is good is instinct, and hence easy, necessary, free"); "Errors" 6 (p.498; "the hysterical as witches"; sin "slipped under a physiological discomfort"; the good conscience like "good digestion"); "Errors" 7 (pp.499-500; free will "the foulest of all theologians' artifices"; "Men were considered 'free' so that they might be judged and punished"; "Christianity is a metaphysics of the hangman"); "Errors" 8 (pp.500-501; "no one gives man his qualities"; "there is nothing besides the whole"; "The concept of 'God' was until now the greatest objection to existence").
- **What the words meant:** "degenerate" (entartet) and "physiologically" are the 1888 vocabulary for a type whose instincts no longer cohere (lesson 10, Anti-Nature 2); "instinct" is a drive acting without the detour through consciousness (lesson 7); "inner facts" are will, spirit and ego as supposed objects of introspection (lesson 8); "innocence of becoming" (Unschuld des Werdens) is becoming freed from the categories of guilt and purpose, not moral innocence; "fatality of his essence" means one necessity among the rest, not a foretold life; "the whole" is the totality of what has been and will be, with no outside from which to judge it.
- **Objection answered from his side:** the chapter is self-undermining: it explains the causes of moral belief while denying mental causes, and it treats the origin of free will (invented to punish) as if origin settled truth. Reply, texts first: "Errors" 3 denies that will, spirit and ego are causes, not that there are causes; the causes it names are physiological states and the drive to explain (sections 4-5), the module's own position (GS 360; D 119). On origin, Nietzsche's own rule is GS 345 (p.285): a morality grown from an error is not thereby touched in its value; so "Errors" 7 is a discrediting (lesson 3's concept), not a refutation, and the refutation is section 3's and BGE 21's. Anti-Nature 5 has conceded that no position outside life exists from which to judge. The gap, in one sentence: "there are no mental causes at all" is asserted, not argued, and is stronger than the module's BGE 19 and 21, which keep command-and-obedience and the strong will as real.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** the reading on which the chapter denies only flesh/spirit dualism and leaves the efficacy of consciousness intact (Kaufmann, Ch. 9 I). The text: "The will no longer moves anything ... it can also be absent"; "There are no mental causes at all" (p.495). A rival reading takes the words literally (Leiter). The lesson quotes the words, says they are stronger than BGE 19, and names the fork (lesson 8's, now with TI on the first side).
- **Facts against the popular image:** "Christianity is a metaphysics of the hangman" stands in a section whose aim is to take "the concept of guilt and the concept of punishment out of the world again"; the "immoralist" wants less punishing, not more.

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** TI "Errors" 1-8: morality and religion rest on four mistakes about causation (effect taken for cause; will, spirit, ego taken for causes; imaginary causes; free will); removed, "the innocence of becoming" is restored and the world no longer stands under judgment.
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) A long-lived man credits his diet; his slow metabolism caused both diet and long life (1). (2) Morality's formula "do this and you will be happy" makes the same mistake: a well-constituted human being "must perform certain actions", so "his virtue is the effect of his happiness" (2). (3) Our idea of a cause came from the "inner facts" of willing; those are not facts: the will accompanies, the motive covers the antecedents, the ego is a fable (3; BGE 19, 21; GM I 13). (4) From the ego as cause we projected doers under all events, "things" as beings, and God (3; TI "Reason" 5). (5) The dreamer supplies a cause after the sensation and experiences it as first; waking life does the same with bodily feelings (4; D 119). (6) Explanation relieves fear and gives a feeling of power, so the familiar explanation wins and hardens into a system (5; GS 355). (7) Sin, punishment, grace and the good conscience are such explanations, "translations ... into a false dialect" (6; lesson 7's herd vocabulary; Anti-Nature 4-5). (8) Free will was invented so that acts could be judged and punished (7; causa sui; GM II 4). (9) So no one gives man his qualities, nothing stands outside the whole to judge it, and denying God denies the responsibility in God (8).
- **Unstated premise.** Step 3's "no mental causes at all", which BGE 19 does not grant; step 8's move from "invented to punish" to "false", which works only with step 3 (GS 345). Step 1 assumes a type's physiology fixes its conduct, the claim the starred lesson tests.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE.
- **Connections.** doer-behind-the-deed (3, 7); self-as-society-of-drives (3, 2); consciousness-and-language (4-6); will-to-power-as-psychology (5, 2); sublimation (6); genealogy-as-method (7; GS 345); debt-and-punishment, guilt-before-god (7); ascetic-priest (6; GM III 16); amor-fati, eternal-recurrence (8); reason-in-philosophy; death-of-god and nihilism (8).

## Where the claim forks (named by content)

Lesson 8's fork, now with the text on one side: whether "the will no longer moves anything" means the will is causally idle or that no free subject stands behind a willing complex that itself causes. "Errors" 3's words say the first; BGE 19 and 21, two years earlier, keep the second. The lesson states both, says TI is the later and stronger statement, and that Nietzsche never reconciled them in print.

## APPLICATION spec (L16)

- **The text read whole:** Twilight of the Idols (1888), "The Four Great Errors" 1-8, Kaufmann, The Portable Nietzsche pp.492-501; portable-late.txt lines ~1942-2290.
- **Section map:**
  - 1 (pp.492-493), Cornaro: uses will-to-power-as-psychology (a type's physiology as the economy of its strength) and sublimation (Anti-Nature 4-5, a morality as symptom). Words: "the extraordinary slowness of his metabolism ... was the cause of his slender diet."
  - 2 (pp.493-494), morality's formula reversed: uses sublimation (Anti-Nature 4, healthy morality "dominated by an instinct of life") and will-to-power-as-psychology (A 2, "not virtue but fitness"). Words: "his virtue is the effect of his happiness."
  - 3 (pp.494-495), false causality: uses doer-behind-the-deed (BGE 19, 21; GM I 13), self-as-society-of-drives (the ego "a fable"), consciousness-and-language (the motive "a surface phenomenon of consciousness"). Words: "The will no longer moves anything."
  - 4 (pp.496-497), imaginary causes: uses consciousness-and-language (D 119, the "fantastic commentary"; the representation misread as cause) and the notebooks' chronological inversion (34[54] p.4; 15 pp.270-271, unpublished). Words: "an apparent reversal of time."
  - 5 (pp.497-498), the psychology of explanation: uses will-to-power-as-psychology (the feeling of power in deriving the unknown from the familiar) and consciousness-and-language (GS 355, fear bids us know). Words: "any explanation is better than none."
  - 6 (pp.498-499), morality and religion as imaginary causes: uses sublimation (Anti-Nature 1-5), consciousness-and-language (the herd's vocabulary as "false dialect"), the drive-self (GM III 16's belly, read forward). Words: "translations of pleasurable or unpleasurable feelings into a false dialect."
  - 7 (pp.499-500), free will: uses doer-behind-the-deed (causa sui; D 128; GM II 4). Words: "invented essentially for the purpose of punishment."
  - 8 (pp.500-501), the innocence of becoming: uses doer-behind-the-deed (no one is responsible for being thus) and reaches past the module. Words: "there is nothing besides the whole."
- **Where the text goes further / departs from the module's idealisation:** (a) Section 3's "there are no mental causes at all" against the module's BGE 19 and 21, which kept command-and-obedience and strong and weak will as real. (b) Section 8 moves from psychology to a verdict on existence: the whole cannot be judged, denying God "redeems the world"; the module gave no grounds for the step from "no one is responsible" to "the world is redeemed", which is module 8's work (amor fati; Z III 4; Skirmishes 49). (c) Section 7's genealogy of free will is not an argument against its truth; by GS 345 the argument is section 3's and BGE 21's. (d) Section 2 extends the reversal to peoples, parties and the sick young man; the module's psychology was of persons, and collective "degeneration" is asserted, not derived.
- **Reading order and the one sentence that states what the reader can now do:** read 1-2 together (the template and its moral version), then 3 (lesson 8 compressed), then 4-5-6 as one movement (dream, mechanism, application), then 7 and 8 each whole. The reader can now take any moral or religious sentence, ask which of the four errors it commits and which module result exposes it, and say where Nietzsche's conclusion outruns what that result proves.

# Research brief: genealogy-as-method (Genealogy as Method: The Value of Values and Origin versus Use)

status: planned
module: first-treatise
type: core
goal: Explain what a genealogy of a value can and cannot prove, from the Preface's demand for a real history and the Second Treatise's separation of origin from present use.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The problem (a morality whose foundation is gone) and the method (protocol, evidence rule). The reader has never seen the Genealogy and knows only that it is 'the spine'.
- **Contributes:** The Preface reconstructed (the project, the targets, the demand for a real history against hypothesis-history); GM II:12–13 read out of order as the methodological rule (origin ≠ use; use is imposed by a mastering will; the catalogue of punishment's meanings); the genetic-fallacy objection stated and answered (genealogy's force is to remove a value's claim to be given, not to refute it — Leiter Ch. 5); MPS defined with its two components; and the scope problem named. After this lesson the reader knows exactly what kind of argument the three treatises are.
- **Hands off:** To master-and-slave-morality, which begins the First Treatise's history; the scope problem is picked up again in value-creation-and-normativity.

Spine step: "Genealogy as method: the value of moral values is questioned by a real history, origin and present use are 'toto caelo' distinct, and the critical force of that history is rhetorical, not deductive — the genetic-fallacy objection answered before the treatises begin."

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **Preface §§1–3: the project.** 'We are unknown to ourselves'; the question of the origin of good and evil traced to Nietzsche's youth; the shift from 'where does evil come from' to 'under what conditions did man invent the value judgments good and evil, and what value do they themselves have?' — the second question is new and is the book's.
2. **Preface §§4–7: the targets and the demand.** Rée's The Origin of the Moral Sensations (1877) as the 'English' hypothesis-history (usefulness forgotten); Schopenhauer's pity as the 'great danger'; the demand for 'the actual history of morality', documented and grey, against 'hypothesis-mongering into the blue'. Reconstruct: (P) an explanation of morality's origin must be a history with evidence; (P) the English genealogists supply none, only a plausible mechanism; (C) their account is not refuted but unfounded — and the value question cannot even be posed by it.
3. **Preface §8: how to read.** The treatises as commentaries on aphorisms; 'rumination' — the reader's instruction, which the course's protocol formalizes.
4. **II:12–13: the rule.** Origin and purpose are 'toto caelo' apart; every purpose is a sign that a will to power has mastered something and reinterpreted it; the history of a thing is a chain of reinterpretations; punishment's list of meanings (deterrence, compensation, isolation, festival, mnemonic, payment to the powerful, quarantine of decay, compromise with revenge, declaration of war…) — 'the concept punishment no longer has one meaning but a whole synthesis'. Consequence: the utilitarian account of anything by its present use is not a history at all. This is the anti-teleological principle every later lesson uses.
5. **The genetic fallacy, stated and answered.** Objection: showing a value's ignoble origin cannot show the value false — an inference from origin to validity is invalid. Nietzsche accepts this in effect (GS 345: the origin of a morality is not its value). Leiter's account of what genealogy does: it undermines the value's claim to be natural, universal, or given, and shifts the burden — rhetorical force, not deductive. Reconstruct the legitimate argument form: (P) morality claims authority as universal and non-contingent; (P) the history shows it contingent, type-specific, born of a particular need; (C) the claim to that kind of authority is unfounded — which is not the claim that its precepts are false.
6. **MPS and the scope problem.** Leiter's two components; the reason the target must be bounded (Nietzsche has values of his own — the higher type, honesty — that a total critique of 'morality' would indict); Robertson's reframing (authority and jurisdiction, not content or a psychological premise). Define both once; the course reuses them.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- Reading II:12–13 before I:1 is the course's decision (a seminar convention, not the book's order); the lesson says so in one line and states why (the rule is needed before the history).
- Do not claim Nietzsche 'commits the genetic fallacy'; the lesson must show the argument form he actually needs and that the Preface and GS 345 keep him inside it.
- Leiter is toc-only; MPS's two components are well attested across the dossier's secondary sources (Reginster's review, the SEP), so state them as Leiter's with chapter pointers, not pages.
- 'Toto caelo' is Latin in the German text; keep it and gloss it.
- Rée must be anchored at first mention (1877 book; Nietzsche's friend of the Sorrento winter 1876–77); no Salomé episode.
- Do not let the scope problem expand: define it, show why it exists, and hand it to Module 6.
- Robertson's revision of the scope condition is Leiter's own condition quoted and amended (pp. 90–98); this is the one place the 2015 book's scope claim is on disk verbatim — quote it from there.

## EXERCISE PLAN (floor: ≤1 warm-up; ≥2 of boundary / refute / derivation / research; one FORWARD)

1. (warm-up, retrieval) State the Preface's two questions (the boy's and the book's) and give the one word in §3 that marks the change.
2. (refute) "Nietzsche's genealogy commits the genetic fallacy: it argues that because morality began in ressentiment, morality is false." Break it: name the sentence in GS 345 that concedes the invalidity, and write the argument form the Preface actually licenses.
3. (derivation) II:13 lists punishment's meanings without saying which the present one is. Derive, from II:12's principle, why no such 'present meaning' could be the origin even in principle, and state the premise (a will imposes meaning) the derivation needs.
4. (boundary probe) Consider a practice whose present use is identical to its origin (a stipulated case: a tool made for one job, still used for it). Does II:12's rule fail there, or does it apply vacuously? State what the rule claims about the general case and what it would take to falsify it.
5. (research-shaped, sourcing) Read Rée's chapter title list (1877, via the Preface's description and Janaway ch. 5's summary in the Kirwin dossier) and write the records / argues / omits pass on Nietzsche's Preface §4 as a source about Rée: what it establishes about Rée's method, what it argues, what it omits (their friendship, their common ground).
6. (translation comparison) Compare Samuel's rendering of 'Herkunft' and 'Zweck' in II:12 with the German and a modern translation the reader can access; state whether 'origin/purpose', 'descent/aim' or 'source/end' best preserves the 'toto caelo' contrast, and why the choice matters for the rule.
7. (FORWARD → master-and-slave-morality) Preface §4 says the English derive 'good' from the usefulness of unegoistic actions to those who received them. Write the premise about who first used the word 'good' that this account presupposes; the solution says GM I:2 denies exactly this premise and names the lesson that reads it.

# Research brief: genealogy-as-method (Genealogy as a method)

status: planned
module: good-and-evil (3 of 9), lesson 13 of 45
type: core
goal: Explain what a genealogy of morality claims to show and why a history of a value can bear on its worth without committing the genetic fallacy

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (6, "Good and evil"):** "Values have a history: the nobles called themselves good and the rest bad; the oppressed, unable to strike back, invented evil and called their weakness good, and Christianity is that revolt's victory, which The Antichrist traces from Jesus to Paul"
- **Inherits:** from death-of-god, discrediting (a belief explained without its being true; D 95 "historical refutation as the definitive refutation"); from will-to-power-as-psychology, the will that masters and reinterprets what is weaker (BGE 13, 36) — GM II 12 turns that psychology into a rule of historical method.
- **Contributes:** the method of modules 3-5 and its limits: the new question (the value of values, not the origin of evil), the separation of a thing's origin from its present use, the "gray" standard of evidence, and the answer to the genetic-fallacy objection, which Nietzsche states against himself in GS 345 before any critic does. Introduces genealogy and origin-and-purpose.
- **Hands off:** to master-morality, the first genealogy itself: what "good" meant before it meant unselfish (GM I 2-5), and the etymological evidence the method calls "gray".

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** Nietzsche is the first to treat morality as something that happened rather than something given: he asks under what conditions human beings devised "good" and "evil" and what those judgments are worth for life (Pref 3, p.16-17), and he does it with a rule of evidence (Pref 7) and an explicit guard against his own method's abuse (GS 345; GM II 12). The lesson shows this as a discovery: a history of a value can remove its claim to be self-evident while leaving the question of its worth to be settled on stated grounds.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** GM II 12 (p.78), the sentence that "mankind in the mass sacrificed to the prosperity of a single stronger species of man — that would be an advance", with its surrounding attack on the "democratic idiosyncrasy" and Spencerian adaptation; GM I 1 (p.24), the readiness to sacrifice every desire to "plain, harsh, ugly, repellent, unchristian, immoral truth" (both `genealogy--text.txt`). No other passage in this lesson's texts is of the kind the stance names.
- **What the words meant:** "species" (Art) in II 12 is a type of human being, as in Pref 6's "the type man" (Typus Mensch), not a biological race; "the value of these values" (Pref 6) means their value for the growth or decline of that type, the standard the First Essay Note makes explicit as "value for what?" and the opposition of two viewpoints, "the welfare of the majority and the welfare of the few" (pp.55-56). "Gray" (Pref 7) is what is documented in words, laws and customs, against the "blue" of Rée's guesswork. "English psychologists" (I 1-3) means Rée and the utilitarian historians of morals (Kaufmann's note; GS 345 names the "English historians").
- **Objection answered from his side:** the genetic fallacy — a belief's origin is irrelevant to its truth or worth. Reply, texts first: GS 345 (p.285) "even if a morality has grown out of an error, the realization of this fact would not as much as touch the problem of its value"; Pref 6 (p.20) separates the historical task ("under what conditions did man devise these value judgments") from the evaluative one ("what value do they themselves possess?"); II 12 forbids reading a present purpose back into the origin. So the history does three things, none of them a refutation: it removes the value's claim to be given, it shows whose life it served, and it supplies material for a judgment by a stated standard. The gap sentence: the standard itself — ascending versus declining life, the highest power and splendor of the type man — is asserted in Pref 6 and the First Essay Note and is not derived in the Genealogy; the lesson says so in one sentence and points to where the course tests it (danger-of-dangers, skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man).
- **Humanizing reading to test:** the reading that makes genealogy a literary alternative to ontology, a family tree selected by interest with no claim to be the right history (Nehamas Ch.3-4, Secondary). The text claims more: Pref 7 demands "what is documented, what can actually be confirmed"; I 4-5 argue from etymology; the First Essay Note asks faculties of medicine to judge "every table of values" physiologically. Genealogy in the text is a factual inquiry with an evaluative aim, not a story one is free to tell otherwise.
- **Facts against the popular image:** the lesson's texts carry one: Nietzsche himself states the genetic-fallacy caveat (GS 345, 1887) and warns that "by searching out origins, one becomes a crab" (TI Maxims 24, Portable p.470). The caveat is his before it is his critics'.

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** Pref 6: the value of moral values must be called in question, which requires knowledge of the conditions under which they grew. II 12: origin and present purpose "lie worlds apart", because every existing thing is reinterpreted by whatever masters it. GS 345: this history does not touch the value question; it is the first step to posing it.
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) A value presents itself as given: self-evident, eternal, commanded. (2) Its history shows it came to be under particular conditions, made by a particular kind of human being for that kind's needs (Pref 3; I 2). (3) Where the history explains why people hold the value without its being given, the claim to be given is removed — the move death-of-god called discrediting. (4) The history also shows what the value did: whose life it served, what it cost others (Pref 6's list: symptom, mask, remedy, poison). (5) A thing's present meaning is not its origin; practices outlive their first purpose and acquire new ones from new masters (II 12-13), so neither the origin nor the present use can be read off from the other. (6) The worth of the value for us is then judged by a stated standard — does it further or hinder the type man — on the evidence steps 2-5 supply (Pref 6; First Essay Note).
- **Unstated premise.** That the value's authority rested on its being given (step 1); a value that never claimed more than usefulness is untouched by step 3. And the standard in step 6, which Nietzsche states but does not derive.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** The genetic fallacy, in two forms: against truth (Kekulé's ring reportedly came from a dream of a snake and is still true) and against worth. Reply: GS 345 concedes the point outright; Pref 6 separates the two questions; the history undercuts only values whose justification was their source (a rule "revealed by God" is undercut by showing who wrote the revelation and why); the evaluation is made by the standard of step 6, not by the pedigree. The gap: the standard is not argued here. The lesson writes that sentence plainly and does not gloat.
- **Connections.** master-morality and slave-revolt-and-ressentiment execute the method; debt-and-punishment reuses II 12-13 on punishment; will-to-power-as-psychology supplies "reinterpreted by some power superior to it"; perspectivism (GM III 12) is the method's own epistemology; will-to-truth asks whether the genealogist's will to truth is itself ascetic (the reflexive problem, GM III 24-27); danger-of-dangers and skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man test the standard.

## Where the claim forks (named by content)

Two serious readings of what the history contributes. One: genealogy is itself the critique, an argument that beliefs caused by an unreliable process, known to be so, lose their justification (an undercutting defeater). Two: genealogy is not the critique and not strictly needed for it; it identifies the type whose life a morality served, and the critique is an evaluation by a standard of power. The text supports the second as Nietzsche's self-understanding (GS 345; Pref 6; EH III GM "preliminary studies") while the first explains why step 3 has force against a value that claimed to be given. The lesson names both by content and uses the second as the frame.

## Pitfalls

- Genealogy is not "debunking by bad origins"; both the dismissal ("he commits the genetic fallacy") and the opposite misreading ("genealogy shows morality is false") are real and documented (L13), and GS 345 is the text that refutes both.
- D 44 says the opposite of GM II 12 about the direction of significance; present it as the 1881 position Nietzsche left, not as a contradiction to smooth over.
- Z I 7 is about wisdom; Pref 8's claim that GM III is its exegesis is a reading hint, not a thesis.
- The notebook "four tasks" (1[?], p.59) and 2[182-190] are unpublished scaffolding; lead with Pref 6 and GS 345.
- No "handshake as empty weapon hand" or other folk etymology as an example of the method; the lesson's examples are Nietzsche's (punishment) or documented.
- Scholars' names stay in References; "undercutting defeater" may be used as a term but not attributed in the body.

## EXERCISE PLAN

Floor check: claim to refute (ex. 2), boundary probe (ex. 4), research-shaped (ex. 5), derivation (ex. 3).

1. Retrieval (E3 from death-of-god): restate the difference between refuting and discrediting a belief, and say which of the two GM Pref 6 asks for.
2. Claim to refute: "Nietzsche shows morality came from resentful slaves and concludes it is worthless; that is the genetic fallacy." Name the step the objection omits (GS 345; the standard of Pref 6) and the step where Nietzsche is genuinely exposed (the standard is asserted, not derived).
3. Derivation the lesson did not do: from II 12's principle, derive why a present purpose can never serve as evidence of an origin, and apply it to one meaning of punishment from II 13 that a modern penal theory states as punishment's purpose.
4. Boundary probe: a genealogy that shows a value arose among healthy, powerful people for reasons they stated openly. Does the history vindicate the value? Say what the method can and cannot conclude in that case, using step 6.
5. Research-shaped: take three of II 13's meanings of punishment and find a modern legal or penological text (sentencing guidelines, a criminal code's statement of purpose) that states each as the purpose of punishment; report whether the three are compatible with one another.
6. FORWARD (reaches master-morality): look up the earliest attested sense of the English words "villain", "gentle" and "noble", and of the German schlecht. Record what each first described. The solution notes that <Ref> master-morality makes exactly this gray evidence its starting point (GM I 4-5).

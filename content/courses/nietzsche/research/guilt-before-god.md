# Research brief: guilt-before-god (Guilt before God)

status: planned
module: guilt-and-the-priest (4 of 9), lesson 20 of 45
type: core
goal: Explain how Christianity, by Nietzsche's account, moralized bad conscience into guilt before God and made the debt unpayable

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (7, "Guilt and the priest"):** "Guilt began as debt: a promising animal learned memory through pain, cruelty was the festival pleasure that higher culture refined rather than abolished, cruelty with no outlet turned inward as bad conscience, the debt to God made it unpayable, and the ascetic priest gave suffering a meaning by making the sufferer its cause"
- **Inherits:** from debt-and-punishment, the creditor-debtor relation and the community as creditor; from bad-conscience, raw self-torment awaiting an interpretation (II 22); from slave-revolt-and-ressentiment, the priestly people who "dared to invert the aristocratic value-equation" (GM I 7); from doer-behind-the-deed, that blame needs a doer who could have done otherwise; from the reader's background, the atonement.
- **Contributes:** the culmination of the Second Essay: debt to the ancestors grows with the tribe's power until the ancestor is a god (II 19); the Christian God is the maximum god and so the maximum debt (II 20); the debt is moralized, joined to bad conscience and free will, made unpayable (II 21); the "stroke of genius", the creditor who sacrifices himself for his debtor (II 21); the Greek counter-case, "foolishness, not sin!" (II 23); sinfulness "not a fact but an interpretation" (III 16). Introduces guilt.
- **Hands off:** to ascetic-priest, the instrument: guilt is what the priest administers (III 15-16); II 24-25's "redeeming man" is a forward pointer to overman-and-last-man only.

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** Nietzsche read the doctrine that God paid man's debt by dying as the last entry in a ledger that began with cattle and body-pledges, and showed that it makes sense only on the economic logic of II 4-5 which it drives to infinity; and he predicted the reader's own culture: with the death of the creditor, "a kind of second innocence" (II 20). It explains why Christian guilt is total rather than proportionate, why grace does not end it, and why atheism has felt like relief.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** GS 135 (p.187, gayscience-s1-intro-books1-3.txt from 7617), with GS 136 and 139 beside it; GM II 21-22 (pp.91-93, 3081), the stroke of genius and the madhouse; TI "The Four Great Errors" 7-8 (Portable pp.499-501); A 25-26 (Portable pp.594-598), "The priest lives on sins", where used. Texts in Set pieces.
- **What the words meant:** "Guilt" (Schuld) remains debt until moralized, which II 21 defines as pushing debt and duty back into the bad conscience; "sin" (Sünde) is the priestly name for the animal's bad conscience (III 20) and, in GS 135, an offence against God's honour (crimen laesae maiestatis divinae), which is why it needs no victim. "A Jewish feeling and a Jewish invention" states where in his genealogy the concept arose: in the priestly people of GM I 7 and A 25, whom GS 136 calls "that people of moral genius among the nations", and whose invention Christianity, "the ultimate Jewish consequence" (A 24), carried into Europe; a historical attribution of a concept, made to disparage the concept, and the lesson says so without adding a word. "God" in II 20 is "the maximum god attained so far", a quantity of creditor; "second innocence" is mankind whose creditor has died; "metaphysics of the hangman" names the apparatus (free will, guilt, punishment) read as built for punishing.
- **Objection answered from his side:** Christian theology says grace frees from guilt, so an account on which Christianity manufactures endless guilt is a caricature. Reply: II 21 says the atonement presupposes the debt it discharges and makes it infinite in the act (only an infinite creditor pays an infinite debt); the redeemed debtor owes gratitude for a death, a larger debt; D 78 (p.47): Christianity put "misfortune and guilt" on one balance where antiquity knew "pure innocent misfortune"; GS 135: an affront to honour admits no natural restitution. Gap sentence: the account fits the terrified and scrupulous conscience and the preaching of hell, less well a piety that lives grace as release; Nietzsche's answer, that release is the feeling of a debt forgiven and so of a debt, is a strong reply, not a proof. Second, one paragraph: perhaps guilt is accurate, since wronging others is wrong whatever one owes; reply from GM I 13 and TI "Errors" 7 (the doer is the fiction guilt needs), GS 250 and III 16.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** Kaufmann's GS introduction (6, pp.21-24) reads GS 135-141 as a tu quoque against Christian antisemites. The text supports this (136, 139, 140) and says what 135 says; both are reported. The stroke of genius read as mere sarcasm: the text means genius; the device solved II 7's problem at the price of making all suffering deserved.
- **Facts against the popular image:** GS 136's "moral genius" and GS 139's Greeks preferred to Paul stand in the cluster with GS 135 (Kaufmann n.24-28, pp.185-190).

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** GM II 21 (p.92): Christianity moralized guilt and duty, turned them back against the debtor and then the creditor, until the debt became irredeemable, and resolved it by the creditor's self-sacrifice; GS 135: sin is an offence against God's honour, independent of harm.
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) A tribe feels indebted to its ancestors and repays with sacrifice and obedience (II 19). (2) The stronger the tribe, the greater the ancestor and the debt, until the ancestor is a god (II 19). (3) The maximum god brings the maximum debt (II 20). (4) Joined to bad conscience and to free will, debt becomes guilt: I owe, I am responsible for owing, I deserve to suffer (II 21; TI "Errors" 7). (5) No repayment answers an infinite creditor; original sin makes the debt congenital; nature becomes the devil's (II 21). (6) Sin is an affront to honour, so no natural restitution touches it (GS 135). (7) The atonement completes the structure: God pays himself, out of love; the debtor owes gratitude for a death (II 21). (8) So guilt before God is bad conscience given a meaning, a creditor and a permanence; it solved II 7's problem by making every suffering deserved (II 22; III 16, 20). (9) The structure is contingent: the Greek gods took the guilt on themselves (II 23); when the creditor dies the feeling declines (II 20).
- **Unstated premise.** That Christian guilt continues ancestral debt rather than being a new insight into wronging others; and that free will is a fiction (doer-behind-the-deed), without which guilt might be accurate.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE; gap sentence on which pieties the account fits.
- **Connections.** debt-and-punishment; bad-conscience; ascetic-priest (III 15-16, 20); slave-revolt-and-ressentiment, jesus-and-paul (I 7; A 24-26; D 68); doer-behind-the-deed (TI "Errors" 7); death-of-god (II 20; GS 130-132); four-great-errors (TI 7-8); amor-fati (EH I 2); overman-and-last-man (II 24-25, pointer only).

## Where the claim forks (named by content)

Whether guilt survives the death of God: once the creditor is gone the feeling has no object and declines (II 20); or the structure of self-punishment finds secular creditors (society, humanity, the future) and persists, as notebook 14[?] p.243 (unpublished) says of the socialist "closest relative of the Christian" and as herd-morality-in-modern-dress develops. Both in the texts; name by content, point forward.

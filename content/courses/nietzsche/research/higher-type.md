# Research brief: higher-type (The higher type: greatness as manifold wholeness)

status: planned
module: higher-human-being (8 of 9), lesson 39 of 45
type: core
goal: Explain the marks of Nietzsche's higher type and evaluate his claim that greatness is to be as manifold as one is whole

## ARC (L10)
- **Spine step (12, "The higher human being and the order of rank"):** "Affirmation needs a type able to bear it: the overman against the comfortable last man, the higher type who is as manifold as he is whole, the order of rank in which every elevation of man has needed an aristocratic society and the many have existed for the few, and the philosopher who creates values knowing they are made; 'What Is Noble?' is Nietzsche's own last statement of the type"
- **Inherits:** from overman-and-last-man, an image with a direction and no shape; from sublimation, many strong drives redirected rather than cut out (TI "Anti-Nature" 1-3); from master-morality, the pathos of distance; from danger-of-dangers, the charge that morality harms the highest types (now the reader meets who they are); from becoming-who-you-are, unity of the drives as an achievement (GS 290, EH II 9).
- **Contributes:** the sober, described type behind the whole critique: who the higher human beings are (Goethe, Beethoven, Napoleon, Caesar, Alcibiades, Frederick II, Leonardo), what marks them, and the one formula Nietzsche gives for greatness (BGE 212, "ebenso vielfach als ganz, ebenso weit als voll"). Introduces higher-type, free-spirit, great-health. Makes the evaluative premise of modules 3-5 visible so that order-of-rank can test the social claim built on it.
- **Hands off:** to order-of-rank: this lesson describes the type; the next asks what society the type has needed (BGE 257-259, A 57, GM II 12) and whether the argument for that holds. Maximax (the measure of a culture by its peaks) is stated here only as the premise (UM II 9, UM III 6, GM Essay I Note) and argued there. To creating-values: the higher type is "value-creating" (BGE 260); what that can mean for a thinker who says nothing has value in itself is the lesson after.

## STANCE (binding)
- **Favorable:** Nietzsche's higher type is not the strong man of the popular image but the man of warring drives who holds the war under one command, and he saw what moralists before him missed: that the same wealth of opposed instincts which ruins most who have it (Augustine wanting "the Sabbath of Sabbaths", BGE 200) is the raw material of the only greatness there is (Alcibiades, Caesar, Frederick II, Leonardo). "The price of fruitfulness is to be rich in internal opposition" (TI "Anti-Nature" 3, p.488) is a psychological discovery, and GS 382's great health, a health "one does not merely have but also acquires continually ... because one gives it up again and again", is a definition of health that medicine arrived at later.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** BGE 212's greatness sentence (Zimmern line 3486 and the German, bge-german.txt line ~4350-4361): "precisely this should be called greatness: to be able to be as manifold as whole, as wide as full" (course translation of "ebenso vielfach als ganz, ebenso weit als voll sein können"; verify eKGWB JGB-212); BGE 273 (line 5673): the man who strives after great things "looks upon every one whom he encounters on his way either as a means or as a delay and obstacle, or as a temporary resting-place"; EH I 2 (pp.224-225, eccehomo-full.txt line ~828): the well-turned-out person "carved from wood that is hard, delicate, and at the same time smells good ... he is a principle of selection, he discards much"; TI Skirmishes 33 (pp.533-534): the descending type "has small worth, and the minimum of decency requires that he take away as little as possible from the well-constituted"; TI Skirmishes 45 (pp.549-551): the criminal as "the strong human being made sick", "Catiline, the form of pre-existence of every Caesar"; GS 76 (pp.130-131): "We others are the exception and the danger", with Kaufmann n.15 that Nietzsche also defends the rule; notebook no. 910 (Portable p.456; unpublished, 1887): "To those human beings in whom I have a stake I wish suffering, being forsaken, sickness, maltreatment, humiliation."
- **What the words meant:** "Higher type" (höherer Typus, "the well-turned-out", der Wohlgeratene): a kind of human being defined by the organisation of its drives, not a class, race or profession; Nietzsche's instances cross all three (a Florentine painter, a Roman general, a Hohenstaufen emperor, a Weimar poet). "Free spirit" (freier Geist): "a spirit that has become free" (EH III HAH 1, p.283), against the "free-thinkers" and "levellers" of BGE 44 who want "the universal green-pasture happiness of the herd". "Health" (EH I 2; GS 382): the capacity to assimilate, select and recover, so that a sick man can be "healthy at bottom" and a type can be sick while never ill; "what does not kill him makes him stronger" describes the healthy type's instinct of selection and comes with the qualifier "A typically morbid being cannot become healthy" (p.224): a principle of selection, not a motivational slogan. "Means, obstacle, resting-place" (BGE 273): the striver's eye for others, stated as a fact about greatness, not an excuse. "Explosive" (TI Skirmishes 44): genius as stored force discharged when the tension in the mass is too great; the great man is historically and physiologically conditioned, not self-made from nothing.
- **Objection answered from his side:** the type is a self-portrait: Nietzsche describes himself and calls it greatness (EH I 2 ends "I have just described myself"). Reply from the texts: (1) the names are not his: Goethe above all (TI Skirmishes 49: "he disciplined himself to wholeness, he created himself"), Caesar, Napoleon, Alcibiades, Frederick II, Leonardo, Beethoven, and the type is built from them before Ecce Homo applies it to its author; (2) the marks are testable against the lives (Goethe's "tolerant, not from weakness but from strength"; Napoleon as "the return to nature", Skirmishes 48); (3) Nietzsche's own list of failures is the control: BGE 269 says the ruin of higher men is the rule, and Zarathustra Part IV parades seven "higher men" who fail the test (the kings, the leech, the magician, the last pope, the ugliest man, the voluntary beggar, the shadow) and then adore an ass (Z IV 18). The gap sentence: the formula "as manifold as whole" tells the reader what greatness is made of and not how much manifoldness a given unity can carry; Nietzsche gives instances, never a measure.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** Kaufmann reads the higher type as the Goethean man of sublimated passion and treats TI Skirmishes 33's egoism as self-overcoming (Ch. 9-10); Leiter SEP sec. 2 lists five marks (solitary and "instrumental toward others", seeks burdens, healthy as resilience, life-affirming, self-reverent). What the text says: BGE 273 is exactly "means, obstacle, resting-place" and BGE 265 goes further (the noble soul's belief that "other beings must naturally be in subjection, and have to sacrifice themselves"; read whole in what-is-noble); TI Skirmishes 33 ranks a human being's self-interest by whether he represents "the ascending or the descending line of life" and asks the descending to take as little as possible; TI Skirmishes 45 puts Dostoevski's convicts and Catiline inside the type. The sublimation reading is right about Goethe and silent about Catiline; the lesson gives both.
- **Facts against the popular image:** the type's instances are artists and statesmen, never a party or a people; BGE 44 says the free spirits are the opposite of "levellers"; GS 55 and 76 say that to "become the advocate of the rule" might be the ultimate nobility and that the exception is "the danger"; A 57 (next lesson) makes gentleness to the mediocre the exceptional man's "duty".

## The reconstruction
- **Claim and where.** BGE 212: greatness today is "to be able to be as manifold as whole, as wide as full"; BGE 200 names the men of warring drives who mastered the war; EH I 2 gives the physiology (selection, slow reaction, forgetting); TI Skirmishes 49 gives the exemplar (Goethe). The evaluative premise under all of it: UM II 9 and III 6, the goal of humanity in its highest specimens.
- **Marks of the type (assembled from the texts, with anchors).** (1) Many strong drives under one command: BGE 200, 212; TI "Anti-Nature" 3; EH II 9's "organizing idea". (2) A unifying task that grows unseen: EH II 9; GS 290's single taste. (3) Health as selection and recovery: EH I 2; GS 382. (4) Solitude and distance; others as means, obstacles, resting-places: BGE 26, 212, 273; Z I 8. (5) Self-reverence: BGE 287 (read whole in what-is-noble). (6) Affirmation: BGE 56; TI Skirmishes 49; EH II 10. (7) Rarity and ruin: BGE 269, Z IV 13 §15, GS 76.
- **Argument in plain steps (for "as manifold as whole").** (1) A human being is a plurality of drives (module 2). (2) A drive is strong only in opposition to other drives; a soul with one drive is poor, however loud the drive (D 548; Janaway's stamp collector). (3) Many strong opposed drives, left to themselves, make a weak man who longs for rest (BGE 200: Augustine's "Sabbath of Sabbaths"; GS 347's need for a commander). (4) Greatness is the case in which such a man holds the war under one command without ending it: wholeness that keeps its manifoldness (BGE 212; TI "Anti-Nature" 3). (5) Hence the fruitful man is the one richest in internal opposition and strong enough to bear it, and his rarity follows: "the higher its type, the more rarely a thing succeeds" (Z IV 13 §15; BGE 269).
- **Unstated premise.** Step 4 assumes that the value of a soul is its wealth and organisation of force, not its goodness or its happiness: the perfectionist premise, with UM II 9 behind it. Nietzsche states it as a taste ("my judgment is my judgment", BGE 43) and defends it by the names.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** The self-portrait objection (above). Second objection, from the lesson's own texts: EH II 9's "no trace of struggle ... the opposite of a heroic nature" sits against the notebook that wishes suffering on his own (no. 910) and against GS 283's "live dangerously"; the lesson states this tension plainly: the type does not seek burdens, it is one on whom burdens fall and who "exploits bad accidents to his advantage" (EH I 2), so Leiter's "seeks burdens" mark is corrected to "bears and uses them". Gap sentence as above (no measure of manifoldness).
- **Connections.** sublimation (the drives are kept, not cut); becoming-who-you-are (unity as achievement, EH II 9); danger-of-dangers (the harm presupposed this type); order-of-rank (what society the type has needed); creating-values ("value-creating", BGE 260); ecce-homo (Nietzsche as the case); what-is-noble (BGE 265, 287 read whole).

## Pitfalls
- "Higher type" is not a class, race or profession: the names cross all three; say so once, with the names, and do not apologise for the elitism or soften TI Skirmishes 33 and 45.
- UM II 9: never quote Collins ("can lie ultimately only in its highest examples"); use the course translation from the German with "not at the end".
- EH II 9 says the opposite of burden-seeking; "what does not kill him" is a principle of selection with a qualifier (EH I 2), not a slogan; TI Maxims 8 (p.467) is the war-maxim form.
- BGE 273's wording is "means, obstacle, resting-place"; "instrumental toward others" is a gloss (L8a: scholars' names stay in References).
- Zarathustra's higher men are a satire and a test (Z IV); quote them as illustration and say so.
- Published/unpublished: no. 893 and 910 are notes (Portable p.456); 5[59], 9[?], 14[?] likewise; lead with BGE, EH, TI, GS.
- Maximax (the value of a culture lies in its peaks) is stated here as premise and argued in order-of-rank; do not argue it twice. Its GM locus is the Essay I Note, I 12, I 16, II 12, not I 17.

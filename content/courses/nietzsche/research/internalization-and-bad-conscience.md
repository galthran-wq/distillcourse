# Research brief: internalization-and-bad-conscience (Internalization: The Origin of Bad Conscience)

status: planned
module: second-treatise
type: core
goal: Formulate GM II:16–18's mechanism — instincts blocked from discharge turn inward once society cages the animal — and evaluate the state-by-conquest premise it rests on.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The debt chain with its moral step located and unexplained; II:14's negative result (punishment does not produce guilt); the forward exercise's question: what instinct in the animal can be turned into self-torture.
- **Contributes:** The Second Treatise's central mechanism reconstructed: instincts that cannot discharge outward turn inward (II:16); the enclosure that blocked them was the state, founded by conquest not contract (II:17); bad conscience as active, form-giving, 'an illness as pregnancy is an illness' (II:18) — the ambivalence the naturalist reading must keep; the Freud parallel stated once and bounded.
- **Hands off:** To guilt-reconstructed, which assembles conscience, bad conscience and moralization into Leiter's three stages and tests them.

Spine step: "Second Treatise: guilt is debt moralized — a promising animal bred by the morality of custom, the creditor–debtor relation, cruelty turned inward when society caged it, escalating to a debt to God — and the sovereign individual is the naturalist reading's first strain."

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **II:16 reconstructed.** (P) Man once had instincts of a wild animal — hostility, cruelty, the pleasure in persecution, attack, change, destruction — that discharged outward. (P) The enclosure in society and peace blocked outward discharge ('the walls'). (P) Instincts that cannot discharge outward turn inward — 'this is what I call the internalization of man'. (C) The soul, in the sense of an inner life 'with depth, breadth, height', and bad conscience — the animal's cruelty turned on itself — originate together. The water-animal image: a sudden change of element, the old instincts useless, the animal 'reduced to thinking, inferring, calculating' — 'their most miserable organ'.
2. **II:17: the enclosure was conquest.** 'That change was not gradual, not voluntary'; the state was 'a pack of blond beasts of prey, a conqueror and master race' imposing form on a formless population; 'the oldest state… a fearful tyranny'; the social-contract account dismissed ('sentimentalism'). Reconstruct the premise's role: the mechanism needs a sudden enclosure; conquest supplies suddenness and violence. Evaluate: the claim is asserted with no record; state what would count as evidence (the earliest states' foundation by conquest is a historical thesis with a literature the course does not enter) and that the mechanism's plausibility does not depend on the conquest being 'blond'. The second 'blond beast': the same phrase, the same refutation as in GM I:11 (a zoological, not racial, image; the conquerors are unnamed).
3. **II:18: bad conscience is active.** The same form-giving force that built states turns inward and 'creates negative ideals' — 'this secret self-violation, this artist's cruelty'; 'an illness, as pregnancy is an illness' (II:19); the 'delight' of the self-tormentor. The ambivalence: bad conscience is the origin of beauty, ideals, the soul — and of guilt. The naturalist reading must keep both: it is a mechanism with two products.
4. **The site the escalation needed.** Now the debt chain can moralize: the debt to God is attached to an instinct for cruelty that has no outward object (II:22's 'will to torture oneself' has its material). The two origin stories (promise/custom; debt/creditor) and the mechanism (internalization) are drawn together as three inputs to guilt — the three-stage structure previewed for the next lesson.
5. **The Freud parallel, once and bounded.** Repression, sublimation, the superego as internalized aggression (Civilization and Its Discontents, 1930) are later, independent formulations of a similar mechanism; Freud's own remarks on avoiding Nietzsche are on record; the parallel is confirmation of nothing, and the course does not use Freud as a source. Two sentences.
6. **II:23–25: the treatise's own forward hook.** The Greeks' gods used to keep bad conscience at bay; the reversal that would 'wed bad conscience to the unnatural inclinations'; the coming 'Antichrist and antinihilist', 'the victor over God and nothingness' — Zarathustra named as the one who 'must come one day'. State it as the treatise's prophecy, not the course's doctrine.

- **The pregnancy simile** (II:19) as the ambivalence in one line.

- **Before/after with a third panel**: outward discharge → inward discharge (the lesson's existing figure) → the political order among the drives that the inward turn creates (Clark & Dudrick 7.5) — the 'soul' of II:16 given a structure, not just a direction.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The pregnancy line's section number: verify against the German (it opens II:19 in the standard numbering; some translations place it at the end of II:18).
- Do not present the conquest premise as established history or as refuted; it is Nietzsche's assertion, needed for suddenness, and the lesson grades it.
- The Freud parallel must not exceed two sentences; Beckman's 'appropriated' speculation is not to be repeated as fact.
- 'Blond beast' recurs here; refer back to the starred refutation in slave-revolt-and-ressentiment in one clause, do not redo it.
- The active/positive side of bad conscience (II:18) is essential to Module 6's revaluation of suffering; do not flatten it into 'guilt is bad'.

## EXERCISE PLAN (floor: ≤1 warm-up; ≥2 of boundary / refute / derivation / research; one FORWARD)

1. (warm-up) Write II:16's argument in three premises and a conclusion, and state the two products the conclusion names (the soul; bad conscience).
2. (refute) "Bad conscience is guilt: Nietzsche says society made us feel guilty." Break it: II:16–18 contain no debt and no God; state what bad conscience is at this stage (self-directed cruelty, 'artist's cruelty'), what it produces besides guilt (II:18's ideals, beauty), and where guilt enters (II:21–22, with I:13's premise).
3. (derivation) II:17 needs the enclosure to be sudden. Derive why a gradual enclosure (the contract picture) would weaken the mechanism — what the instincts would do given time — and state whether the mechanism survives with 'sudden' replaced by 'complete'. Then say what that shows about how much of II:17's conquest story the argument actually needs.
4. (boundary probe) Consider an animal enclosed alone — no society, only walls. On II:16's account, does internalization occur? State which premise concerns society specifically (the prohibition on discharging against one's fellows) and whether walls alone satisfy it.
5. (research-shaped, corroboration) Compare II:16's mechanism with Freud's superego (Civilization and Its Discontents, ch. VII — one paragraph, the reader's own copy) as two sources: list the shared claims, the claims each makes that the other does not, and state why the agreement of a later, independent theory is not evidence for II:16.
6. (causation ranking, history overlay) Rank the three inputs to guilt (custom, debt, internalization) as necessary / enabling / precipitating for the 'guilt before God' of II:22, and defend the ranking from the sections.
7. (FORWARD → guilt-reconstructed) The three inputs converge but the text never states the order in which they combine. Write the three inputs as three numbered stages in the order the mechanism requires (which must exist before which), and mark the one transition that needs a premise from the First Treatise; the solution says this is Leiter's three-stage reconstruction, tested in the next lesson.

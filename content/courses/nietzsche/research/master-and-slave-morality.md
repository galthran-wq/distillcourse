## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The rule that a value's history is a history of who coined it and why (genealogy-as-method), and the demand for evidence rather than mechanism. The forward exercise left the reader holding Rée's presupposition — that 'good' was first said by the recipients of useful acts.
- **Contributes:** GM I:1–9 reconstructed: the refutation of the utilitarian origin; the etymological argument (I:4–5) reconstructed and graded as evidence; the two modes of valuation with the asymmetry — 'good' first vs. 'evil' first — isolated as the whole of the charge (Leiter's minimal pair); BGE 260 as the typology in one aphorism; pathos of distance as the noble's condition; the relativist misreading refuted by the text's own ranking.
- **Hands off:** To slave-revolt-and-ressentiment, which supplies the mechanism (the priestly caste, ressentiment's creative No) by which the second coinage displaced the first.

Spine step: "First Treatise: two modes of valuation with an asymmetry — 'good' coined first by the noble, 'evil' first by ressentiment — the slave revolt as the inversion, and the doer-behind-the-deed fiction that morality's first presupposition needs; Zarathustra's tarantulas stage the diagnosis."

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **I:1–3: the utilitarian genealogy refuted.** The English psychologists' account — unegoistic acts praised by their beneficiaries, the usefulness forgotten, 'good' now felt as intrinsic. Nietzsche's three objections reconstructed: (a) forgetting is impossible where the usefulness is daily re-experienced (I:3 — Spencer's version at least avoids this); (b) the account gets the coiner wrong: 'good' was not said by the recipients but by 'the good themselves' (I:2); (c) it mistakes the present use for the origin (the rule from genealogy-as-method).
2. **I:2: the first coinage.** The noble, powerful, high-placed felt and posited themselves and their doings as good, 'in contradistinction to all that was low' — the pathos of distance as the origin of the right to create values; 'bad' (schlecht) as a contemptuous afterthought.
3. **I:4–5: the etymological argument, reconstructed and graded.** (P) In several languages the word for 'good' descends from words for noble rank (Latin bonus ← duonus 'warrior'; Greek esthlos 'the one who is'; German gut ~ 'the godly/Gothic') and 'bad' from words for the common (schlecht ← schlicht 'plain'; malus ~ 'the dark one'; Greek kakos, deilos 'the coward'). (C) The value words were rank words first. Grade the evidence: which etymologies hold (schlecht/schlicht does; bonus/duonus is contested), what the inference needs (that word-history tracks value-history), and where I:5's 'Aryan' and 'blond' speculation exceeds the philology — flag without expanding (I:11 is the next lesson's).
4. **The asymmetry (I:10, BGE 260) as the whole charge.** Master: 'good' of oneself first, 'bad' derived. Slave: 'evil' of the other first, 'good' derived by negation. Reconstruct as Leiter's minimal pair and state precisely what is being criticized: not the content (humility, patience) but the reactive origin — a valuation that needs an enemy to exist. This is the premise the rest of the treatise depends on.
5. **BGE 260: the typology.** Master morality's 'good = noble = powerful = beautiful = happy = beloved of god'; slave morality's suspicion of the powerful and its honour for pity, patience, humility; both types in one culture and one soul; the Norse saga quotation as master-morality evidence in Nietzsche's own hand. BGE 257: an aristocratic society as the precondition of the pathos of distance — stated, with the political premise it carries flagged for higher-type-and-sublimation.
6. **The relativist misreading refuted (L13).** Claim: 'Nietzsche only describes two moralities and takes no side.' Rejection: I:10 calls slave morality 'reactive' and master morality 'active' and 'spontaneous', I:17's closing note asks for a physiological ranking of value-tables, and BGE 260's whole vocabulary ranks. Replacement: Nietzsche ranks by a criterion — health, self-affirmation, life-enhancement — that the course will examine (will-to-power-as-psychology). Discriminating example: 'Beyond Good and Evil' does not mean 'beyond good and bad' (I:17's own footnote-sentence).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- The etymologies are 19th-century philology and several are wrong; the lesson grades them rather than accepting or dismissing them wholesale. The argument's weight does not rest on any single etymology (I:5's own hedging: 'this is at least how it seems to me').
- I:5's 'Aryan conqueror race' and 'blond' remarks are in the text; handle in one sentence as speculation exceeding the evidence, and defer the blond beast (I:11) to the next lesson where its refutation belongs.
- Do not present master morality as Nietzsche's recommendation; BGE 260 says the two coexist and that Nietzsche is describing types.
- 'Schlecht' and 'böse' must both be translated as distinct words (bad / evil) or the asymmetry disappears — this is the set piece of key-terms-in-translation; here one sentence and a pointer.
- Samuel's 1913 translation renders some terms archaically ('the good, the aristocratic'); quote him where the sense holds and the German where it does not.

## EXERCISE PLAN (floor: ≤1 warm-up; ≥2 of boundary / refute / derivation / research; one FORWARD)

1. (warm-up) Write the minimal pair in your own words: for each morality, who is named first, with which word, and what is derived.
2. (refute) "Master morality is Nietzsche's ethical recommendation." Break it with BGE 260's two sentences on coexistence and the 'higher and more mixed cultures', and with I:17's request for a ranking by a criterion not yet given.
3. (derivation the lesson skipped) I:3 says Spencer's version (good = useful, remembered) is 'more reasonable' than Rée's. Reconstruct Spencer's version, show why it survives objection (a) but not objection (b), and state what that tells you about which of Nietzsche's three objections is doing the work.
4. (boundary probe) Suppose every etymology in I:4–5 were false. Which premises of the First Treatise's argument survive (the asymmetry from I:10, the typology of BGE 260) and which do not (the claim that the rank-origin is documented)? State what evidence would then be needed.
5. (translation comparison) Compare Samuel's and the German for I:4's schlecht/schlicht sentence and for I:10's 'böse'; then find one modern English rendering (Clark/Swensen or Kaufmann — verify) and say whether it keeps 'bad' and 'evil' apart consistently across I:4, I:10 and I:11. State what is lost if it does not.
6. (research-shaped) Check one etymology against a modern dictionary: schlecht/schlicht (Grimm, Kluge, or Duden). Report the modern verdict, and state whether the inference from word-history to value-history would hold even if the etymology does.

Write the premise about the slaves' condition (impotence, a No that cannot become a deed) that would explain why their 'good' had to be derived, and find its first statement in BGE 195; the solution says GM I:10 names the condition 'ressentiment' and points to the next lesson.

# Research brief: master-morality (Master morality: good and bad)

status: planned
module: good-and-evil (3 of 9), lesson 14 of 45
type: core
goal: Explain the noble mode of valuation, in which 'good' names the valuer's own strength and 'bad' is an afterthought

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (6, "Good and evil"):** "Values have a history: the nobles called themselves good and the rest bad; the oppressed, unable to strike back, invented evil and called their weakness good, and Christianity is that revolt's victory, which The Antichrist traces from Jesus to Paul"
- **Inherits:** from genealogy-as-method, the "gray" standard (words, laws, customs) and the question what "good" first meant; from will-to-power-as-psychology, the discharge of strength that BGE 260 calls "fullness, power that wants to overflow".
- **Contributes:** the first mode of valuation: self-affirming, coined by the ruling caste about itself from the pathos of distance, with "bad" a pale afterthought; the twofold prehistory already in HH 45; the priestly branch of the aristocracy (I 6-7), which is the hinge to the next lesson. Introduces master-morality and pathos-of-distance (which returns in order-of-rank and what-is-noble).
- **Hands off:** to slave-revolt-and-ressentiment: the same vocabulary in the mouths of those who could not act; and to order-of-rank, BGE 257's claim that the pathos of distance is the precondition of every elevation of the type man.

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** Nietzsche saw that the oldest moral words did not mean kind or useful: they meant the speaker's own rank, power and happiness, and the lesson shows how much that one observation overturns — the utilitarian origin story, the assumption that "good" has always been one thing, and the picture of morality as given. His evidence is the kind he demanded in Pref 7, words that can be checked, and the lesson checks them.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** GM I 5 (pp.30-31, `genealogy--text.txt`): Latin malus as "the common man as the dark-colored, above all as the black-haired man ... as the pre-Aryan occupant of the soil of Italy who was distinguished most obviously from the blond, that is Aryan, conqueror race by his color"; Gaelic fin "originally meant the blond-headed, in contradistinction to the dark, black-haired aboriginal inhabitants"; the Celts "definitely a blond race" against Virchow's maps; and the question "whether modern democracy, even more modern anarchism and especially that inclination for 'commune' ... does not signify in the main a tremendous counterattack — and that the conqueror and master race, the Aryan, is not succumbing physiologically, too?" GM I 11 (pp.40-41): the nobles as "uncaged beasts of prey", "the splendid blond beast prowling avidly in search of spoil and victory", the list "Roman, Arabian, Germanic, Japanese nobility, the Homeric heroes, the Scandinavian Vikings", "we suffer from man"; and GM I 10 (p.37), the Greek noble's "pitying contempt" (deilos) for the commoner. BGE 257 (`bge-zimmern.txt` line 5055): the noble caste "was at first the barbarian caste", "more complete human beings, which at every level also means more complete beasts" — previewed here, read whole in order-of-rank.
- **What the words meant:** "race" (Rasse) in I 5 is a people or stock told apart by colour and by who conquered whom; Nietzsche uses contemporary ethnology (Virchow's maps of hair colour) and draws from it an argument about valuation, that "good" was first the conquerors' self-description; the democracy sentence states a physiological hypothesis about Europe, which the lesson gives in his words and connects to I 11-12's "leveling of European man". "Blond beast" (I 11) is the noble caste of any people seen from outside, and his own list spans Rome, Arabia, Japan and Homer; Kaufmann n.3 (pp.40-41) reads the beast as the lion, and that is one reading; I 11 itself later says "the blond Germanic beast" of the Teutons, and II 17 (p.86) founds the first state on "some pack of blond beasts of prey, a conqueror and master race". "Master" (Herr) is the ruling caste that holds "the lordly right of giving names" (I 2, p.26). "Pathos of distance" is the felt rank from which that right flows (I 2; BGE 257). I 7's equation states what "good" bundled: "good = noble = powerful = beautiful = happy = beloved of God".
- **Objection answered from his side:** two. (a) Etymology is not argument: words for good have several roots, the genealogist selects, and bonus from duonus "warrior" is Nietzsche's own conjecture ("I believe I may venture", I 5, p.31). Reply from the texts: the claim needs only that one major origin of "good" was the rulers' self-designation (I 2), and schlecht/schlicht, esthlos, kakos/deilos and Kaufmann's note quoting Else on Greek agathos (I 5 n.2, p.29) carry that much; the thesis is about the direction of naming, which I 10 argues independently from the psychology of the noble (affirms itself first, "seeks its opposite only so as to affirm itself more gratefully"). Gap sentence: the stronger claim that this was the only origin, and the ethnology of I 5, go beyond what the words can bear, and the lesson says so. (b) Nietzsche simply sides with the masters (the Callicles question). Reply from the texts: I 6 (p.33) says man became "an interesting animal", acquired depth and became evil "on the soil of this essentially dangerous form of human existence, the priestly"; I 7 (p.33) says history would be "altogether too stupid a thing without the spirit that the impotent have introduced into it"; I 10 (pp.38-39) says the noble needs less cleverness than the "regulating unconscious instincts" and shows "a certain imprudence"; I 17 says "Beyond Good and Evil" does not mean "Beyond Good and Bad". He admires the masters (I 16, p.53: the Romans, "the strong and noble, and nobody stronger and nobler has yet existed on earth") and holds that the slave revolt made man deep; the two are stated together, not softened into one.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** that master and slave morality and the blond beast are not racial at all (Kaufmann, Nietzsche Ch.10, pp.293-298; n.3 to I 11), "blood" meaning tradition. What the text says: I 11's list is multi-ethnic and the beast is a type; I 5 does argue ethnologically, in the vocabulary of race and colour, and I 16 and III 9 return to "pre-Aryan" remarks. The lesson states both and lets the reader hold them.
- **Facts against the popular image:** the lesson's texts carry MA 45's "In Homer the Trojan and the Greek are both good": the noble "good" was not partisan, and the enemy was not "bad".

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** GM I 2: the judgment "good" did not originate with those to whom goodness was shown; "the good" themselves, the noble, powerful, high-stationed, felt and established themselves and their actions as good, in contrast to the low. I 10: the noble mode "acts and grows spontaneously, it seeks its opposite only so as to affirm itself more gratefully". BGE 260: the noble is "value-creating".
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) The oldest words for "good" in several languages meant noble, powerful, real, truthful, warlike, happy (I 4-5). (2) The oldest words for "bad" meant plain, common, low, cowardly, without blame (I 4-5, 10). (3) So the first valuation was made by the rulers about themselves; language itself is "an expression of power on the part of the rulers" (I 2). (4) Its form is affirmation first, contrast afterward: "bad" is "a pale contrasting image" (I 10). (5) Its psychological ground is the pathos of distance, the felt rank from which the right to coin values flows (I 2; BGE 257). (6) Therefore "good" did not originally mean unselfish or useful to others; the utilitarian origin is unhistorical and psychologically absurd, since utility is an everyday experience that cannot be forgotten (I 3).
- **Unstated premise.** That the oldest attested layer of a language records the oldest valuation (words can be older than their records); and that the knightly and priestly aristocracies share one root before they part (I 6-7), which is what lets the priestly branch inherit the right to name and turn it inward.
- **The priestly branch.** In a priestly aristocracy "pure/impure" becomes the rank-marker; purity turns inward and becomes dangerous and deep (I 6); priests, "the most impotent", hate spiritually (I 7). This is the hinge: the inversion will be carried by nobles who cannot win by strength.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE: etymology is selective (reply: the direction-of-naming thesis needs only part of the evidence and is argued independently in I 10; gap sentence on I 5's ethnology and on bonus/duonus); and the Callicles question (reply: I 6-7, I 10, I 16-17 together — admiration for the Romans and the claim that the priests made man deep are both his).
- **Connections.** slave-revolt-and-ressentiment (the inversion); order-of-rank (BGE 257-258, the pathos of distance as precondition of elevation); what-is-noble (BGE 287); the-nazi-appropriation (I 5 and I 11 as passages the Archive could use without forgery); creating-values ("good" as said by a valuer about himself, BGE 260's "value-creating"); higher-type (GM III 4: Homer is not Achilles).

## Pitfalls

- "Master morality" is not "Nietzsche's morality" and not a programme for return: I 6-7 and I 17 are the texts (L13; the misreading is real and documented).
- The masters are unspiritual, not stupid: I 10's "regulating unconscious instincts" and "imprudence", not "stupid and shallow"; I 16 calls the Romans the strongest and noblest.
- GM I cites no Old English; the English examples ("villain", "gentle") are the lesson's own parallels and are introduced as such.
- "Masters wrote, the poor did not" is not Nietzsche's premise; his is I 2's naming right.
- HH 45 does not say the slave code won; it says our morality grew on the soil of the ruling castes. Present the 1878 position as what GM I reverses.
- Use Zimmern for HH 45 (bad/evil); Harvey flattens both to "bad".
- Nietzsche's etymologies are his (bonus/duonus is dubious; "I believe I may venture"); the figure marks them as his.
- Scholars' names stay in References; the lion reading is "one reading of the image".

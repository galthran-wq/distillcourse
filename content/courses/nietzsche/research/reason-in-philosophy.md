# Research brief: reason-in-philosophy (Reason in philosophy: Socrates, being and grammar)

status: planned
module: truth (6 of 9), lesson 28 of 45
type: core
goal: Analyze Nietzsche's diagnosis of why philosophers believed in unchanging being, from Socrates' equation of reason with virtue to the metaphysics built into grammar

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (9, "The true world dismantled"):** "The true world goes the way of God: the history of Platonism ends with the true world a fable, and philosophy's 'reason' turns out to be hatred of becoming plus faith in grammar."
- **Inherits:** from real-world-became-a-fable, the six stages and why stage 1 was built; from doer-behind-the-deed, grammar's invented doer (GM I 13); from nihilism, decadence as instincts working against themselves; from schopenhauer-and-the-birth-of-tragedy, Socrates as the killer of tragedy (GT 12-15).
- **Contributes:** two arguments: (a) Socrates: the rule of reason over the instincts began as a sick man's remedy, so the preference for reason is a symptom before it is an insight; (b) being: the "true being" set against the senses is made of what reason and grammar add (unity, thing, substance, permanence, doer), so the senses' testimony to change is the more faithful and "God" is grammar's last shadow. Introduces being-and-becoming and grammar-metaphysics.
- **Hands off:** to perspectivism: if "being" is reason's fiction and every report of becoming is someone's, from somewhere, what is knowing?

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** Nietzsche asks what the history of philosophy never asked of itself: not whether Parmenides, Plato and Kant were right that the real does not change, but what kind of man needs that to be true, and what in his language keeps telling him so. The move from logic to physiology and from physiology to grammar is his, and it anticipates the psychology of motivated belief and the later discovery that metaphysics is often grammar misread.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:**
  - TI "Socrates" 3 (pp.474-475, portable-late.txt ll.1300-1316): "In origin, Socrates belonged to the lowest class: Socrates was plebs. We know, we can still see for ourselves, how ugly he was. But ugliness, in itself an objection, is among the Greeks almost a refutation. Was Socrates a Greek at all? Ugliness is often enough the expression of a development that has been crossed, thwarted by crossing. Or it appears as declining development. The anthropologists among the criminologists tell us that the typical criminal is ugly: monstrum in fronte, monstrum in animo. But the criminal is a decadent. Was Socrates a typical criminal?"
  - TI "Socrates" 6 (p.476, ll.1368-1370): "one chooses dialectic only when one has no other means ... The Jews were dialecticians for that reason; Reynard the Fox was one—and Socrates too?"
  - TI "Socrates" 7 (pp.476-477, ll.1372-1382): "Is the irony of Socrates an expression of revolt? Of plebeian ressentiment? Does he, as one oppressed, enjoy his own ferocity in the knife-thrusts of his syllogisms? ... As a dialectician, one holds a merciless tool in one's hand; one can become a tyrant by means of it."
- **What the words meant:** "plebs"/"plebeian" denote the non-noble type of valuation (GM I's slave mode) and "ressentiment" its creative resentment; the claim is that dialectic was the weapon of the man without other authority, a slave revolt in method. "Crossing" (Kreuzung) denotes mixed descent leaving the instincts without one direction (BGE 200); the ugliness is read as a sign of instincts in anarchy, which the cure answers. "The Jews were dialecticians" names a people who, on his reading, had only argument where others had power (GM I 7's "priestly people"); one instance of the rule in the same sentence; state both together.
- **Objection answered from his side:** genetic fallacy: a decadent can reason well. Reply: Nietzsche dismisses not the arguments but the one judgment they serve, the verdict on life as a whole, which "can, in the end, never be true: they have value only as symptoms" ("Socrates" 2, p.474), since the living are an interested party and the dead cannot judge; for the being argument he gives independent grounds ("Reason" 6, second proposition). Cost, stated plainly: his own yes to life becomes a symptom too, which he accepts ("Anti-Nature" 5; EH I 1: health as a trained vantage). Second objection: physics confirms permanence. Reply: substance behind change is the target, not law ("how much rudimentary psychology is still residual in your atom", "Four Great Errors" 3, p.495; BGE 22).
- **Humanizing reading to test:** that Nietzsche admires the man, rejects only the doctrine and the epigoni. The text has both registers; print both: "Socrates was the buffoon who got himself taken seriously" (5, p.475), "Socrates himself has merely been sick a long time" (12, p.479), and GS 340's "I admire the courage and wisdom of Socrates in everything he did, said—and did not say" (p.272) beside "Socrates suffered life! And then he still revenged himself". The admiration is for courage and the "great erotic" (8); the verdict on Socratism stands.
- **Facts against the popular image:** "The Jews were dialecticians" sits in a chapter aimed at Socrates and the Greeks, and GM III 26 attacks anti-Semites as "the latest speculators in idealism"; report both.

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** TI "Reason" 5-6: belief in being, the ego and God rests on grammar and hatred of becoming. TI "Socrates" 10-11: Socratic rationality was a decadent's remedy.
- **Argument 1, Socrates.** (1) A judgment on the value of life as a whole cannot be true or false; it is a symptom of the judge ("Socrates" 2). (2) Socrates judged life a sickness. (3) So his judgment is a symptom of instincts in anarchy, which his ugliness also shows (3-4). (4) His cure, reason made tyrant, "reason = virtue = happiness", was the response of a man and a city whose instincts could not govern themselves: "either to perish or—to be absurdly rational" (9-10). (5) Needing to fight the instincts is itself decadence; in ascending life "happiness equals instinct" (11). Dialectic was the cure's plebeian weapon (5-7).
- **Argument 2, being.** (1) The senses show change, plurality, becoming. (2) Philosophers concluded the senses deceive, since the real must be one and unchanging. (3) But unity, thinghood, substance and permanence were put in by reason, whose habits are grammar's: a subject with properties, a doer behind every deed ("Reason" 2, 5; BGE 17). (4) So "true being" is made of what reason adds; "being is an empty fiction" (Heraclitus). (5) The highest concepts are the thinnest abstractions, "the last smoke of evaporating reality", put first as causa sui (4). (6) Grammar keeps supplying subjects: the ego as substance, projected into things, then into the world's cause, God (5; "Four Great Errors" 3).
- **Unstated premises.** Argument 1: that a global verdict on life has only symptom-value; asserted, and it governs Nietzsche's own verdicts. Argument 2: "the senses do not lie" sits uneasily beside "all perception is interpretation" (BGE 192, GS 354); perspectivism resolves it.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE.
- **Connections.** doer-behind-the-deed (grammar's doer scaled to thing and God); perspectivism; will-to-truth (Greek "fanaticism" for rationality as ancestor of truth at any cost); four-great-errors (the atom); case-of-wagner, nihilism (decadence); amor-fati (innocence of becoming).

## Where the claim forks (named by content)

(a) The reconciling reading: Nietzsche admires the man, rejects Socratism and its epigoni, and sees Socratism as necessary and overcome. (b) The diagnostic reading at full strength: Socrates is "a typical decadent", the buffoon taken seriously; the admiration is for courage and seductive power, not a retraction. (b) is what the 1888 texts say; (a) is what the career says if GS 340 and GT 15's "artistic Socrates" are weighed in.

## Pitfalls

- Not a naive empiricist: "the senses do not lie" is bounded by "insofar as they show becoming"; Heraclitus "too did the senses an injustice".
- The four propositions close argument 2; do not re-teach the fable. "Egypticism" is Kaufmann's spelling; Ludovici is superseded.
- Kant: one paragraph for this lesson's purpose (BGE 11, GS 193); the thing in itself was introduced last lesson.
- L13 target (documented): "Nietzsche thought Socrates was wrong because he was ugly." Refute with "Socrates" 2: ugliness is evidence of the condition, not the argument against the doctrine.

## EXERCISE PLAN

Floor check: claim to refute (ex. 2), boundary probe (ex. 3), derivation (ex. 4).

1. Retrieval (E3 from doer-behind-the-deed): restate GM I 13's "the doer is merely a fiction added to the deed", then show how TI "Reason" 5 extends the habit from persons to things and to God.
2. Claim to refute: "Nietzsche holds that Socrates was wrong because he was ugly." State the role of the physiognomy (3-4), the premise that carries the argument (2), and what the claim leaves out.
3. Boundary probe: a living being cannot estimate the value of life. Apply it to Nietzsche's own affirmation ("Anti-Nature" 5, EH I 1): what may he say about his yes, and what not? The solution shows he accepts the cost: his yes is a symptom of health.
4. Derivation: from "the senses do not lie; reason falsifies their testimony", derive what Nietzsche must say about a stick that looks bent in water; separate the senses' report from the judgment added, and name the strain perspectivism resolves.
5. Corroboration: TI "Socrates" 12 (1888), GS 340 (1882), GT 13 (1872). Compare, date, judge: where do they agree, where diverge, and what would have to be true for GS 340's admiration to survive beside the 1888 verdict?
6. FORWARD (reaches perspectivism): if the senses report becoming faithfully but every report is someone's, from somewhere, write a definition of "objective knowledge" that needs no view from nowhere.

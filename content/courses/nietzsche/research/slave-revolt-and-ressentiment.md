# Research brief: slave-revolt-and-ressentiment (The slave revolt in morality and ressentiment)

status: planned
module: good-and-evil (3 of 9), lesson 15 of 45
type: core
goal: Analyze how ressentiment turns the noble valuation upside down, producing 'evil' first and 'good' as its negation

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (6, "Good and evil"):** "Values have a history: the nobles called themselves good and the rest bad; the oppressed, unable to strike back, invented evil and called their weakness good, and Christianity is that revolt's victory, which The Antichrist traces from Jesus to Paul"
- **Inherits:** from master-morality, the noble good/bad and the priestly branch of the aristocracy (GM I 6-7); from doer-behind-the-deed, the doer fiction, which GM I 13 shows doing the moral work (the lamb's "he could have refrained").
- **Contributes:** the central mechanism of the critique of morality: hatred that cannot act becomes creative and coins "evil" first, "good" second, as its negation (GM I 10). Introduces ressentiment, slave-morality and slave-revolt; ressentiment returns in the ascetic priest who redirects it (ascetic-priest), in pity (against-pity), in equality as the tarantulas' revenge (herd-morality-in-modern-dress) and in the physiology of EH I 6.
- **Hands off:** to jesus-and-paul, which reads The Antichrist 24-47 whole as the test of this mechanism on the texts the reader knows best; and to debt-and-punishment, since the "imaginary revenge" needs a world in which suffering repays (GM II 4-6).

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** Nietzsche is the first to describe a passion that creates values rather than merely violating them: he saw that hatred denied its outlet does not disappear but becomes a way of seeing, and that an entire moral vocabulary — evil, humility, patience, forgiveness, justice, the Last Judgment — can be read as its work (GM I 10, 13-14). The lesson shows the mechanism as a piece of psychology nobody had before him, and shows that it predicts what it should: the enemy named first, the self defined by negation, the deferred revenge in heaven.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:** GM I 7 (pp.33-34, `genealogy--text.txt`): the Jews, "that priestly people", who "dared to invert the aristocratic value-equation (good = noble = powerful = beautiful = happy = beloved of God)" and held to the inversion "with the teeth of the most abysmal hatred (the hatred of impotence)"; the inversion in one quoted voice ("the wretched alone are the good; the poor, impotent, lowly alone are the good ... and you, the powerful and noble, are on the contrary the evil"); "with the Jews there begins the slave revolt in morality: that revolt which has a history of two thousand years behind it and which we no longer see because it — has been victorious". GM I 16 (pp.52-54): "Rome against Judea, Judea against Rome"; the Jews "the priestly nation of ressentiment par excellence"; Rome's defeat — "three Jews, as is known, and one Jewess (Jesus of Nazareth, the fisherman Peter, the rug weaver Paul, and the mother of the aforementioned Jesus, named Mary)"; the French Revolution as Judea's second triumph; Napoleon. GM I 15 (pp.48-52): Tertullian's De spectaculis 30 in full as Nietzsche prints it. GM III 14 (pp.121-125): the sick as "man's greatest danger"; "It is disgraceful to be fortunate: there is too much misery!"; the remarks on "the sick woman", Dühring and the anti-Semites. BGE 195 (`bge-zimmern.txt` line 2698): the Jews, "a people 'born for slavery'" (Tacitus), who "performed the miracle of the inversion of valuations" and coined "world" as a term of abuse. D 377 (p.170): "love your enemies" invented by the Jews, "the best haters there have ever been".
- **What the words meant:** "slave" (Sklave) names a type of valuation and the condition that produces it — "natures that are denied the true reaction, that of deeds, and compensate themselves with an imaginary revenge" (I 10, p.36) — not a legal status; a legally free priest is its chief agent (I 7). "The Jews" in I 7 and I 16 denotes the priestly people of the Second Temple and the valuation it coined, which Christianity carried to victory (I 8-9; BGE 195; A 24-26 in the next lesson); Nietzsche's history is of a valuation and its carriers, and the lesson reads it so because that is what the sentences say. Ressentiment (French, left untranslated by Kaufmann) is hatred and the wish for revenge in one who cannot act on them, turned into perception and judgment: "his soul squints" (I 10, p.38). "Imaginary revenge" is the inversion itself and its sequel, the Last Judgment (I 14-15). "Rome" and "Judea" in I 16 name the two valuations and the historical bodies that carried them; the Renaissance, the Reformation, the French Revolution and Napoleon are the later rounds.
- **Objection answered from his side:** two, both philosophical. (a) The pointed genetic fallacy: even if "cruelty is evil" was born of ressentiment, it may be true, and the history is powerless against it. Reply from the texts: GS 345 and Pref 6 (genealogy-as-method) concede that the history alone decides nothing about worth; what GM I adds is the mechanism — the valuation needs the doer fiction to make strength blameworthy (I 13), and it misdescribes itself (I 14: impotence called goodness, cowardice humility, inability to revenge forgiveness) — so the history shows the values are not what they claim to be (impartial, given, loving), and the question of worth is carried to danger-of-dangers. (b) The story explains too much: any valuation of the weak can be called ressentiment, so nothing could count against it. Reply from the texts: I 10 gives marks that can fail — the valuation is reactive (needs its enemy to exist), its "good" is derivative, its author cannot forget (Mirabeau is the contrast); EH I 6 (pp.229-231) names a weakness that is not ressentiment, "Russian fatalism", ceasing to react; D 135 (p.86) names a savage honour that pities nothing. A valuation of the weak that affirms first and needs no enemy would not qualify. Gap sentence: GM I shows the slave values are reactive and self-misdescribed; by itself it does not show them harmful, and the lesson says so.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** Kaufmann's note to I 16 (n.2, p.53), that Nietzsche was no antisemite and the passages are not about Jews as such, and the GS Introduction's reading of GS 135-141 as a tu quoque against Christian antisemites. What the text says: I 7 and I 16 make claims about a priestly people and a type of valuation, in the vocabulary Nietzsche chose ("priestly nation of ressentiment par excellence"), and BGE 195 opens by calling that people "'born for slavery,' as Tacitus and the whole ancient world say of them; 'the chosen people among the nations,' as they themselves say and believe" and credits them with "the miracle of the inversion of valuations". The lesson quotes the sentences and glosses them by what they claim about valuation; the reading that they are only a polemic against antisemites is tested, not assumed.
- **Facts against the popular image:** in the lesson's own texts: GM III 14 and III 26 attack Dühring and "these latest speculators in idealism, the anti-Semites"; the Portable's January 1889 letter "I am just having all anti-Semites shot" (p.687) and BGE 251's double edge (the "anti-Semitic bawlers" to be expelled, and "Let no more Jews come in") belong to nietzsche-and-politics and are mentioned in one sentence with a <Ref>.

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** GM I 10 (p.36): "The slave revolt in morality begins when ressentiment itself becomes creative and gives birth to values: the ressentiment of natures that are denied the true reaction, that of deeds, and compensate themselves with an imaginary revenge." I 7: the inversion and its agents; BGE 195 and I 7: where it began; I 16: that it won.
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) The oppressed suffer at the hands of the strong and want revenge (I 7, I 10). (2) They cannot act on it; "the true reaction, that of deeds" is denied them. (3) Unacted hatred does not vanish; it becomes a way of perceiving — the squinting soul (I 10). (4) It needs a way to be above the oppressor without acting. (5) It achieves this by inverting the noble equation: strength, its pleasure and its bearers are "evil"; what the weak are forced to be — meek, patient, humble, poor — is "good" (I 7, I 14). (6) The inversion needs the doer fiction: the strong could have refrained, so they are to blame; the weak chose their weakness, so it is merit (I 13). (7) Revenge is deferred to a judgment and a beyond, where it is enjoyed in advance (I 14-15). (8) The inverted values win historically — Judea over Rome, through Christianity — and once victorious become invisible as a revolt (I 7, I 16).
- **The asymmetry.** Noble: "good" of self first, "bad" of others after, as a pale contrast. Slave: "evil" of the other first, "good" of self derived as its negation. The same noble warrior is "good" in one scheme and "evil" in the other (I 11); the slave scheme "is essentially a morality of utility" (BGE 260).
- **Unstated premise.** That the inversion is driven by ressentiment rather than by a correct insight that cruelty is wrong; Nietzsche supplies a causal history and marks of the reactive, and a critic can accept the history and keep the insight (the pointed genetic fallacy). Also: that a valuation can "win" by being believed by the strong as well — I 16's "battleground" within one soul is what makes the victory total.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE: the genetic fallacy pointed (reply: the mechanism shows the values are reactive and self-misdescribed; worth is settled in danger-of-dangers) and "explains too much" (reply: I 10's marks, EH I 6's Russian fatalism and D 135 show weaknesses that are not ressentiment). Gap sentence as stated.
- **Connections.** jesus-and-paul (next: A 24-47 tests the mechanism; D 68 is Paul's "perfect revenge"); ascetic-priest (III 15: the priest alters the direction of ressentiment); against-pity (GM III 14: "It is disgraceful to be fortunate"); herd-morality-in-modern-dress (BGE 46 and 195 on the French Revolution; the tarantulas); debt-and-punishment (why suffering can repay); amor-fati and EH I 6 (freedom from ressentiment as the mark of health); nietzsche-and-politics (BGE 251; the 1889 letter).

## Where the claim forks (named by content)

Two readings of how the revolt happened. The strategic reading: the weak deliberately devised the new values as a weapon. Its problems: an implausible means to the end, a mysterious success, and values held as a tactic are not values. The expressive reading: ressentiment, set against the noble scheme, produces a psychic tension that the good/evil scheme resolves by making the hatred fitting; the many believe sincerely and the priests, "the most impotent" (I 7), exploit what they half-see (III 15). The text's language — "imaginary revenge", a workshop in which "whiteness, milk, and innocence" are made of "every blackness" (I 14), TI Maxims 31's worm that "doubles up" — is the language of self-deception, and the lesson builds the expressive reading as Nietzsche's, with the priest as the one knowing agent.

## Pitfalls

- "Slave" is a type of valuation; the lesson says so at first use and reads I 7 and I 16 for what they claim about a priestly people and its valuation, quoted whole, with Kaufmann n.2 as one reading. No sentence of distancing.
- Ressentiment is not ordinary resentment: define it, keep the French spelling, and do not attribute it to BGE (the word is absent there) or to D (which has D 68 and D 71 but no term).
- Do not present the strategic reading as Nietzsche's settled view; do not present the expressive reading as exoneration.
- Z has no master/slave vocabulary ("the good and the just", "the rabble"); quote it as illustration and name the speaker.
- GM I alone does not show the slave values harmful; the lesson does not borrow that conclusion from later lessons.
- Scholars' names in References only.

## EXERCISE PLAN

Floor check: claim to refute (ex. 2), derivation (ex. 3), boundary probe (ex. 4), research-shaped (ex. 5).

1. Retrieval (E3 from doer-behind-the-deed): explain in two sentences why the lamb's verdict on the bird of prey needs the doer fiction, citing the sentence of I 13 that says so.
2. Claim to refute: "Nietzsche says the oppressed consciously plotted to invent Christianity to weaken their masters." Name the textual features that favour the expressive reading (I 14's workshop as self-deception; TI Maxims 31) and what the strategic reading cannot explain (the success; values held as tactics).
3. Derivation the lesson did not do: from the asymmetry, derive why a slave morality must always have an enemy and what happens to it when the enemy disappears; connect to BGE 260's "morality of utility".
4. Boundary probe: a weak person who accepts weakness without hatred — EH I 6's Russian fatalism, or D 135's savage who kills the unflinching enemy as an honour. Is either ressentiment? Locate the condition in I 10's definition that is missing.
5. Research-shaped: read GM I 7 and BGE 195 side by side with the Kaufmann note to I 16 (n.2) and the Tacitus phrase BGE 195 quotes; state in two sentences what the passages claim about a priestly people and what the note claims about Nietzsche, and say whether the two are compatible.
6. FORWARD (reaches jesus-and-paul): a founder preaches non-resistance and dies forgiving his killers; his followers then preach a judgment in which his killers burn. Which of the two fits I 10's mechanism, and by which marks? The solution notes that <Ref> jesus-and-paul shows Nietzsche drawing exactly this line between Jesus and Paul (A 33, 39, 42).

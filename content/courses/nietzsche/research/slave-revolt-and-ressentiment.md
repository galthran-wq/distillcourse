# Research brief: slave-revolt-and-ressentiment (Ressentiment and the Slave Revolt in Morality)

status: planned
module: first-treatise
type: core
goal: Analyze the slave revolt (GM I:7–16, BGE 195) as a causal story — priestly caste, ressentiment's creative No, the workshop of ideals — and identify what ressentiment alone cannot explain.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The two modes and the asymmetry; the reader has the premise that slave valuation is derived from a No and is holding the question of what condition makes a valuation begin with a No.
- **Contributes:** The mechanism: the priestly caste as the hinge (I:6–7), ressentiment defined (I:10), the inversion of the noble equation (I:7), the workshop of ideals (I:14) as the psychological demonstration, Tertullian and 'Rome against Judea' (I:15–16) as the historical evidence and its symbol; and the limit Leiter marks — ressentiment explains the inversion, not its victory — which is why the book has two more treatises. The blond beast (I:11) read as text and its weaponization refuted.
- **Hands off:** To doer-behind-the-deed (I:13 sits inside this stretch and is the argument the revolt's success needs); to ascetic-priest (Module 4), which supplies the victory mechanism.

Spine step: "First Treatise: two modes of valuation with an asymmetry — 'good' coined first by the noble, 'evil' first by ressentiment — the slave revolt as the inversion, and the doer-behind-the-deed fiction that morality's first presupposition needs; Zarathustra's tarantulas stage the diagnosis."

## Key points the lesson must cover

1. **I:6–7: the priestly caste as hinge.** A noble caste (priests are 'the most evil enemies' because 'the most impotent'); the priestly mode of valuation grows from the knightly one and turns against it; 'with the Jews there begins the slave revolt in morality' — the inversion of the equation stated in full ('the wretched alone are the good…'). Reconstruct: (P) a caste noble in status but impotent in action; (P) impotence breeds hatred that cannot discharge; (P) hatred that cannot act invents — it revalues; (C) the first inversion of noble values was a priestly deed.
2. **I:10: ressentiment defined.** 'The slave revolt in morality begins when ressentiment itself becomes creative and gives birth to values.' Reactive, other-directed, needing 'external stimuli to act at all'; the noble's 'bad' as an afterthought vs. the slave's 'evil' as the original deed. The word is French in the German — Nietzsche uses it because German 'Groll' lacks the sense of rumination and impotence; one sentence.
3. **I:13: the pivot passage, previewed.** The lambs and the birds of prey sit here; the lesson reads I:13 only as the premise the revolt needs (the strong 'could have' been weak) and hands the full argument to doer-behind-the-deed.
4. **I:14: the workshop.** The invented interlocutor descends into the workshop where ideals are manufactured: weakness → 'merit'; impotence → 'goodness'; cowardly baseness → 'humility'; submission → 'obedience to God'; not being able to take revenge → 'not wanting to'. Reconstruct as a relabeling table — this is the psychological demonstration of I:10's mechanism, staged as a dialogue so that the reader watches each substitution.
5. **I:15–16: the evidence and the symbol.** Dante's inscription over Hell answered with Nietzsche's over Paradise; Tertullian's De Spectaculis on the saved watching the damned — a primary source quoted at length as the case that the ideal carries the hatred it was made from. 'Rome against Judea' — the two valuations in conflict through the Renaissance, Reformation, French Revolution, Napoleon ('the synthesis of Unmensch and Übermensch'). The claim is a history of a value-conflict, not a racial one; I:16's own sentences say which side 'Judea' names (the Christian/democratic valuation, Rome's own church included).
6. **What ressentiment cannot explain.** The inversion is explained; its victory over a caste with the power to crush it is not — and I:16 admits the conflict is unfinished. Leiter: the Second and Third Treatises supply the missing mechanisms (bad conscience as a ready-made site; the priest's redirection). State the gap as a missing premise; hand it off.
7. **★ I:11 read as text (starred).** The 'blond beast' passage: nobles as beasts of prey outside their in-group; the Roman, Arabian, Germanic, Japanese nobility, Homeric heroes, Scandinavian vikings — 'blond' is the lion (the beast), and the list is multi-ethnic. The misreading refuted with the passage's own list, BGE 251, the Fritsch letter, and I:16's placement of Rome's church on Judea's side. Bounded: the passage does praise cruelty; the lesson does not soften that, it locates it (the 'pathos of distance' turned outward) and defers the evaluation to higher-type-and-sublimation.

- **The inversion equation** (I:7) typeset beside BGE 260's noble chain from the previous lesson.
- **Tertullian's passage** (I:15) — quote the Latin lines Nietzsche quotes, translate, and state what a church father's gloating establishes that Nietzsche's own assertion could not.
- **The two-lane timeline 'Rome against Judea'** (the reader's figure suggestion): Renaissance (Rome), Reformation (Judea), French Revolution (Judea), Napoleon (Rome) — with I:16's own labels.
- **The priestly caste's position** as a hinge diagram (knightly ↔ priestly ↔ slave).
- **The strategic reading's reductio** (Wallace pp. 114–115) as a three-step figure: (1) values invented as a weapon → (2) the powerful could crush or ignore a weapon → (3) the victory I:7 asserts becomes inexplicable; then the expressive replacement drawn as tension → resolution (hatred of the masters / the old code's verdict that the masters are good / the new code that dissolves the contradiction).
- **Heine's Hellene and Nazarene** (Kaufmann 12.V): the one documented precedent for 'ressentiment' as a concept — the sourcing drill on where Nietzsche's word came from, one paragraph.
- **Wishful thinking as the mechanism of I:15** (Sinhababu): Tertullian's glee re-read as belief caused by desire — the psychological premise I:10's 'imaginary revenge' compresses.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- I:7's 'the Jews' is the book's most dangerous sentence; the lesson must show that 'Judea' in I:16 names a valuation adopted by Christianity and the modern democratic movement, and that BGE 251 and the Fritsch letter are Nietzsche's own words on the racial reading. Refute from the text; do not editorialize.
- The workshop dialogue is staged — an invented interlocutor is not evidence; the lesson says the passage demonstrates a mechanism, it does not document a history.
- Tertullian's Latin is quoted at length in the German; the course quotes a few lines and cites De Spectaculis 30.
- Napoleon as 'synthesis of Unmensch and Übermensch' is one of two uses of Übermensch in GM; note it for overman-staged.
- Do not solve the victory problem here; the gap is the hand-off.
- Wallace's and Leiter's gaps are different: Leiter says ressentiment explains the inversion but not its victory; Wallace says the strategic reading explains neither, and only the expressive reading explains the inversion. State both; the lesson adopts the expressive reading and still hands the victory question to GM II–III.

## EXERCISE PLAN (floor: ≤1 warm-up; ≥2 of boundary / refute / derivation / research; one FORWARD)

1. (warm-up) Reproduce the workshop's five relabelings in a two-column table, old word and new word, with the I:14 line each comes from.
2. (refute) "The slave revolt succeeded because the slaves were more numerous." Break it: I:7–10 give no numbers and I:16 says the conflict is unresolved; state what mechanism the treatise actually offers for the inversion and why it is not a mechanism for victory (Leiter's gap).
3. (derivation) Reconstruct I:6–7's argument that the priestly caste, not the slaves, made the revolt: three premises and a conclusion, with the premise about impotence made explicit and its evidence (the priest's 'unhealthiness') graded.
4. (boundary probe) Consider a valuation that begins with a Yes to something outside itself (admiration of a hero). Is it master or slave valuation on I:10's definitions? State what the definitions actually turn on (self-affirmation first vs. negation first) and whether admiration falls outside both.
5. (sourcing) Run the records / argues / omits pass on I:15's Tertullian quotation: what does a church father's text establish about Christian valuation; what does Nietzsche argue with it; what does the quotation omit (Tertullian's own polemical audience, the genre of De Spectaculis)?
6. (refute, ★) "GM I:11 shows Nietzsche praising a Germanic master race." Break it with the passage's own list of six peoples, with I:16's placement of Rome's church on 'Judea's' side, and with BGE 251; then state what the passage does say (cruelty released outward) and why refuting the misreading does not make the passage harmless.
7. (FORWARD → doer-behind-the-deed) I:13 says the lambs 'have every right' to call the birds of prey evil — and that the birds have every right to laugh. Write the premise the lambs need (the bird could have chosen not to be a bird) and the premise the laughter denies; the solution names it the free-will presupposition and points to the next lesson.
8. (refute, Wallace) "The slaves invented 'evil' as a deliberate weapon against the masters." Break it on Wallace's three counts (a weapon the strong can ignore; instrumentally held values are not values; the victory becomes inexplicable) and rewrite I:10's 'ressentiment becomes creative' as an expressive claim: name the contradiction the new values resolve.

# Research brief: will-to-truth (The will to truth and science as the last ascetic ideal)

status: planned
module: truth (6 of 9), lesson 30 of 45
type: core
goal: Analyze Nietzsche's claim that the unconditional will to truth is the ascetic ideal's last form and evaluate what follows for his own inquiry

## ARC (L10)

- **Spine step (10, "Truth turned on itself"):** "Without a view from nowhere there is only perspectival knowing, and the unconditional will to truth turns out to be the ascetic ideal's last form; untruth, art and illusion can serve life, and the young Nietzsche's essay on truth shows which part of this he got right first and which he later abolished."
- **Inherits:** from ascetic-priest, the ascetic ideal as a value set above life (GM III 28); from perspectivism, objectivity as more eyes; from death-of-god and nihilism, truthfulness as what killed God (GS 357).
- **Contributes:** the most reflexive move in the course: the critique reaches the critic's own value. Science and modern atheism rest on a commitment they never prove, that truth is to be had at any cost; it is moral and metaphysical ("I will not deceive, not even myself"; "God is the truth, that truth is divine"), the ascetic ideal's purest surviving form; Christianity perished of it and it must now perish of itself: "the will to truth becomes conscious of itself as a problem". Introduces will-to-truth; the intellectual conscience (GS 2) as Nietzsche's own virtue; his reply to the circle, truth turned from a duty into a measure of strength (EH Pref 3).
- **Hands off:** to value-of-untruth: if the unconditional will to truth is a faith with a history, what can untruth do for life?

## STANCE (binding)

- **Favorable:** Nietzsche is the first to ask what the will to truth is worth and to notice that nobody had asked (BGE 1). He saw that "I will not be deceived" cannot ground an unconditional demand, since deception is often useful and truth often harmful, so the real demand is a moral vow; that the vow is Plato's and Christianity's faith that truth is divine, kept without the God who made it so; and that the same vow, applied rigorously, killed that God. He saw it from inside, as a man who held the vow harder than anyone, which is why the circle is his discovery and not a critic's gotcha.
- **Hard passages this lesson must quote whole:**
  - A 55 (pp.640-641, portable-late.txt): "An anti-Semite certainly is not any more decent because he lies as a matter of principle", with its frame, "is there any difference at all between a lie and a conviction?" and "wishing not to see what one does see".
  - BGE 229 (bge-zimmern.txt l.4041): "in every desire for knowledge there is a drop of cruelty"; BGE 230: "cruelty of the intellectual conscience"; "to translate man back into nature ... with fearless Oedipus-eyes, and stopped Ulysses-ears" (l.4112).
  - GM III 24 (p.150): "Nothing is true, everything is permitted", quoted whole with its frame (the Assassins, "that order of free spirits par excellence"; Kaufmann nn.7-8).
- **What the words meant:** "truth at any cost" is ascetic because it ranks a value above life's interests and demands sacrifice to it. "Cruelty" (BGE 229-230) denotes the pleasure of making suffer, here turned on one's own wish for surface and comfort; the will to truth is refined self-cruelty, as the ascetic ideal always worked (GM II 16-18). "Conviction" (A 54-55) denotes a belief held against evidence as a need; the anti-Semite is chosen because he shows a conviction doing a lie's work.
- **Objection answered from his side:** if truth's value is conditional on life, a comforting falsehood is better whenever it serves life; the position licenses propaganda. Reply: Nietzsche sets truth not below comfort but as a measure of strength: "How much truth does a spirit endure, how much truth does it dare? ... Error (faith in the ideal) is not blindness, error is cowardice" (EH Pref 3, p.218); strength "measured by how much 'truth' it can bear undiluted" (BGE 39); "how much truth can you incorporate?" (GS 110). The comfortable lie is weakness in his vocabulary (BGE 59; EH III BT 2's "the decadents need the lie"), so the objection bites on the loose formula, not his. Gap sentence: the measure tells a strong spirit to want more truth than it needs, but not why truth rather than beauty is the test of strength; the ordering is his taste, defended by example (EH), not argument.
- **Humanizing reading to test:** "Nietzsche never doubted science, only scientism", leaving his own truthfulness unquestioned. The text goes further: the value of truth "must for once be experimentally called into question" (GM III 24, p.153); the will to truth "might be a concealed will to death" (GS 344, p.282); "why not rather untruth?" is serious (BGE 1). Show the sentences the quarantine reading cannot absorb; then show that the texts keep truthfulness as a problem and a strength rather than a duty.
- **Facts against the popular image:** "Nothing is true, everything is permitted" is the Assassins' secret in GM III 24 (Kaufmann n.8, pp.150-151) and the Shadow's despair in Zarathustra ("thus I spoke to myself. Into the coldest waters I plunged", Z IV 9, p.386), not Nietzsche's creed.

## The reconstruction

- **Claim and where.** GM III 23-27 and GS 344: the unconditional will to truth is the ascetic ideal's latest form, resting on the faith that truth is divine; Christianity perished of it and it must now become conscious of itself as a problem. BGE 1 poses the problem; EH Pref 3 replies.
- **Argument in plain steps.** (1) Science looks free of the ascetic ideal. (2) Its discipline presupposes a conviction it does not prove: that nothing is needed more than truth (GS 344). (3) That conviction is not prudence, since deception is often useful and truth harmful; the only vow that can be unconditional is "I will not deceive, not even myself", a moral vow. (4) A value above life, served even against life's interests, is the structure of the ascetic ideal (GM III 11, 23). (5) Historically the vow is the Platonic-Christian faith that God is the truth, kept after that God has gone. (6) So science is the ideal's last form, and its defeat of dogma is the ideal turning on itself (GM III 27; GS 357). (7) The same law applies next to morality and to the will to truth itself (III 24, 27).
- **Unstated premise.** Step 4: that any value held unconditionally above life is ascetic in the relevant sense. A critic: unconditional loyalty to a craft is not asceticism. Nietzsche's test: can the value be questioned in the light of life's needs, or does it forbid the question? The will to truth forbade it (BGE 1).
- **The circle.** Nietzsche questions the value of truth while practising unconditional truthfulness and offering the genealogy as true; he says so (GM III 27; D Preface 4). Three ways out, by content: (a) the "comedian of the ascetic ideal", truthfulness kept knowing it is a value with a history (GM III 27); (b) truth and value as two drives kept in tension, the drawn bow (BGE Preface); (c) truth valued as a measure of strength rather than a duty (EH Pref 3; BGE 39; GS 110). The lesson judges: he removes truth's unconditional status and reinterprets it as courage; (c) is what the 1888 texts say, (a) and (b) make (c) stable.
- **Strongest objection and Nietzsche's reply.** As in STANCE.
- **Connections.** death-of-god, nihilism; ascetic-priest; perspectivism; value-of-untruth; creating-values (BGE 211); higher-type (intellectual conscience); ecce-homo (EH Pref 3, IV 3); the-system-assembled (the reflexive problem as keystone).

## Where the claim forks (named by content)

(a) Comedian: truthfulness kept with self-awareness of its genealogy. (b) Tension: the bow kept drawn between truth and value, neither slackening into dogma or "a sure nothing" (BGE 10). (c) Conditional value: truth as what a strong spirit can endure and dare. All three are built from Nietzsche's own sentences; the lesson shows which text supports which.

## EXERCISE PLAN

Floor check: claim to refute (ex. 2), boundary probe (ex. 4), research-shaped (ex. 5).

1. Retrieval (E3 from ascetic-priest and death-of-god): state the ascetic ideal's structure (GM III 11, 28) and the virtue GS 357 says killed God; say why that virtue counts as the ideal's form.
2. Claim to refute: "Nietzsche thinks science is just another religion and its results are not to be believed." Use A 59, A 13, GS 335 for method, GS 344 for what he questions.
3. Claim-evidence-reasoning: argue that "I will not be deceived" cannot ground an unconditional demand, using GS 344's evidence (both trust and mistrust are useful) and stating the warrant.
4. Boundary probe: a truth that would destroy the one who learned it and benefit no one. Should one still want to know it? Use EH Pref 3, BGE 39 and GM III 24's "experimentally" to say what his measure answers and what it leaves open.
5. Research-shaped: find one published statement of science's commitment to truth (an integrity code, a charter, an essay) and identify GS 344's vow or its absence; is the commitment grounded prudentially or morally?
6. FORWARD (reaches value-of-untruth): list three beliefs you know to be, strictly, false or simplifications and could not act without. The solution says the next lesson makes this list Nietzsche's evidence that untruth is a condition of life, with `<Ref>` to value-of-untruth.

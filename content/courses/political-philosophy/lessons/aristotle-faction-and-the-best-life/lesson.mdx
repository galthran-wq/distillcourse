Cities are overthrown by their own citizens far more often than by enemies. Aristotle collects the cases — a love quarrel in Syracuse, a bride in Delphi, a Spartan land shortage — and asks what they have in common. If a trifle can bring a regime down, what keeps one standing, and what is a city worth keeping for?

The question comes straight out of <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle">the previous chapter</Ref>. The best regime most cities can have is polity, a fusion of oligarchy and democracy held up by a middle class, and it lasts, Aristotle said, only on "the general willingness of all classes in the state to maintain the constitution". In the Greek cities of his century that willingness was lost all the time: an oligarchy became a democracy, a democracy fell to a tyrant, a tyrant was killed and the rich took over, mostly with no foreign army involved. A theory of the best regime that cannot say why regimes fall, and what keeps them, is of little use to anyone who has to govern one.

The obvious explanation is that great changes have great causes: a lost war, a famine, a ruinous debt. Plato had offered a subtler one, in which each regime is ruined by the son it raises, and Aristotle had already objected that it gave one cause per regime and a sequence the record did not follow (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-objection-from-the-record">the objection from the record</Ref>). What he needs is an account general enough to cover every regime and specific enough to tell a statesman what to watch. He builds it, as he says, from the cases.

## Revolutions spring from trifles, not about them

Aristotle's lecture notes on faction are full of stories from the histories of particular cities, most of them now lost. Read one after another, they look like a refutation of great causes.

### Four quarrels

**Syracuse**, the chief Greek city of Sicily, once had two young men among its rulers who were close friends. While one was away, the other won over the boy he loved; the first took revenge by seducing his friend's wife. "They then drew the members of the ruling class into their quarrel and so split all the people into portions", and the constitution changed.

**Delphi**, the sanctuary of Apollo's oracle on the slopes of Mount Parnassus in central Greece and a small city in its own right, came apart over a bride. On the wedding day the bridegroom took some mishap for a bad omen and went away without her. Her relatives, insulted, slipped some of the sacred treasure among his offerings while he was sacrificing and then killed him as a temple-robber. "A quarrel about a marriage was the beginning of all the troubles which followed."

**Mytilene**, the chief city of Lesbos, is the reader's own case. A rich citizen left two daughters as his heirs; another citizen wanted them for his sons, was refused, "stirred up a revolution, and instigated the Athenians" to interfere. Aristotle names the outcome: "the war with the Athenians in which Paches took their city", the revolt of 428 whose punishment the Athenian assembly first voted and then reversed (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="a-rebel-ally-and-a-decree">a rebel ally and a decree</Ref>).

**Sparta** shows that the trifle need not be personal. During the Messenian War, the seventh-century struggle in which Sparta fought to keep the subject people it had conquered to its west (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="competition-between-many-cities">Sparta's answer to land shortage</Ref>), some citizens were ruined and others grew rich. Aristotle cites a poem of that war, *Good Order* by the Spartan poet Tyrtaeus, which speaks, he says, "of certain citizens who were ruined by the war and wanted to have a redistribution of the land". Here the occasion is a shortage of land among men who had been equals.

### The trifle and the stake

Taken one by one, these stories suggest that revolutions are accidents of temper. Aristotle draws the opposite conclusion:

<Quote source="Politics V.4, 1303b17–20, 1303b31–37, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
In revolutions the occasions may be trifling, but great interests are at stake. Even trifles are most important when they concern the rulers, as was the case of old at Syracuse […]. In general, when the notables quarrel, the whole city is involved, as happened in Hestiaea after the Persian War. The occasion was the division of an inheritance; one of two brothers refused to give an account of their father's property and the treasure which he had found: so the poorer of the two quarrelled with him and enlisted in his cause the popular party, the other, who was very rich, the wealthy classes.
</Quote>

Hestiaea, a city in the north of Euboea, the long island off the east coast of Attica, shows how it works. Two brothers quarrel over an inheritance. The poorer one turns to the poor, the richer to the rich, and a family dispute becomes a war between classes. The quarrel did not create the two parties; it found them. A spark that falls on a city already divided between rich and poor, each side convinced it is wronged, sets the city alight. The same spark in a city that is not divided goes out.

Aristotle's word for civil strife of this kind is *stasis*, faction: a quarrel of parties inside one city, as opposed to war between cities, and his word for the change of regime it may bring is *revolution*.

## Two equalities, two engines of civil war

What, then, makes a city ready? Book V opens with the two partial justices of <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="equal-in-one-thing-equal-in-all">the previous chapter</Ref>, and draws from them something that chapter did not.

### Every regime is partly just

<Quote source="Politics V.1, 1301a25–39, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Democracy, for example, arises out of the notion that those who are equal in any respect are equal in all respects; because men are equally free, they claim to be absolutely equal. Oligarchy is based on the notion that those who are unequal in one respect are in all respects unequal; being unequal, that is, in property, they suppose themselves to be unequal absolutely. The democrats think that as they are equal they ought to be equal in all things; while the oligarchs, under the idea that they are unequal, claim too much, which is one form of inequality. All these forms of government have a kind of justice, but, tried by an absolute standard, they are faulty; and, therefore, both parties, whenever their share in the government does not accord with their preconceived ideas, stir up revolution.
</Quote>

The first sentences are the old diagnosis: each party takes the one respect in which it is right for all respects. The last sentence is the new step. A regime is built on one party's measure, so it rules over the other party by a measure that party rejects. A democracy therefore contains, by construction, a class that thinks itself cheated: the rich, counted as equals of men they consider their inferiors. An oligarchy contains the mirror image: the poor, free men treated as less than free. Aristotle compresses it into one line, "Inferiors revolt in order that they may be equal, and equals that they may be superior." Faction is not a breakdown of the regime's justice. It is that justice, seen from the side it slights.

### The two kinds of equality, computed

Aristotle then names the two measures exactly. "Equality is of two kinds, numerical and proportional; by the first I mean sameness or equality in number or size; by the second, equality of ratios." His illustration is arithmetic: "the excess of three over two is numerically equal to the excess of two over one; whereas four exceeds two in the same ratio in which two exceeds one, for two is the same part of four that one is of two, namely, the half." In symbols, $3 - 2 = 2 - 1$ is the first kind, equal differences; $4 : 2 = 2 : 1$ is the second, equal ratios. These are the two equalities <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="two-equalities-the-city-could-not-reconcile">Athens built into its institutions a century apart</Ref>: Cleisthenes' one share per citizen is the numerical kind, which that chapter called arithmetic; Solon's shares in proportion to worth are the proportional kind, which it called geometric.

<Example title="one city, two fair shares">
A city has 1,000 citizens. 100 are rich, each owning ten times as much land as each of the 900 poor. How should a share in deciding the city's business be divided?

**Numerically.** One citizen, one vote. The rich cast 100 votes out of 1,000, a tenth.

**Proportionally.** Shares in proportion to property. Count the poor man's land as 1 unit: the rich own $100 \times 10 = 1{,}000$ units, the poor $900 \times 1 = 900$ units. The rich get $1{,}000 / 1{,}900$, a little over half the say.

**What each party sees.** Under the numerical rule, a hundred men who own more than half the land have a tenth of the say over it, and a majority with less than half can tax them as it pleases: to the rich this is robbery by vote. Under the proportional rule, a hundred men outvote nine hundred free citizens: to the poor this is the rule of a few over men as free as they are.

**The result.** Neither rule is a mistake in arithmetic; each is a correct application of one kind of equality. Whichever the city adopts, one party has a grievance that it can state as a principle of justice, and the regime gives it no way to stop holding that grievance.
</Example>

Aristotle's remedy is already in the arithmetic: "both kinds of equality should be employed; numerical in some cases, and proportionate in others." A regime built "simply and wholly" on either one never lasts, he says, because "they are originally based on a mistake, and, as they begin badly, cannot fail to end badly". He adds two comparisons the reader can now check against the previous chapter. Democracy is safer than oligarchy, because an oligarchy has two dangers, the oligarchs falling out among themselves and the people rising against them, while in a democracy "no dissension worth mentioning arises among the people themselves". And "a government which is composed of the middle class … is the safest of the imperfect forms": the middle is the one party whose measure the mixture does not slight (<Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="the-weight-that-turns-the-scale">the weight that turns the scale</Ref>).

<KeyFact>
"Both kinds of equality should be employed" is the recipe for <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="polity-oligarchy-and-democracy-fused">polity</Ref>. A fusion that takes election and a moderate property qualification from oligarchy and open access from democracy measures some things by worth and some by head, so each class finds its own equality somewhere in the constitution and neither carries the whole grievance of the Example.
</KeyFact>

## A state of mind, a motive, an occasion: the anatomy of faction

The partial justices explain why every regime has a party ready to rise. They do not explain why it rises in one year and not another, or why a city with the same grievance for a century suddenly comes apart over a wedding. Aristotle answers with three questions: what is the *feeling*, what are the *motives*, and where do the disturbances start?

### Three questions

<Quote source="Politics V.2, 1302a22–b5, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
The universal and chief cause of this revolutionary feeling has been already mentioned; viz. the desire of equality, when men think that they are equal to others who have more than themselves; or, again, the desire of inequality and superiority, when conceiving themselves to be superior they think that they have not more but the same or less than their inferiors; pretensions which may and may not be just. […] Such is the state of mind which creates revolutions. The motives for making them are the desire of gain and honour, or the fear of dishonour and loss […]. Other causes are insolence, fear, excessive predominance, contempt, disproportionate increase in some part of the state; causes of another sort are election intrigues, carelessness, neglect about trifles, dissimilarity of elements.
</Quote>

The three answers are three different kinds of cause. The **state of mind** is standing: a man who thinks the regime gives him less than he deserves thinks so every day. The **motives** are what he stands to win or avoid: money and honour, or the loss of them. The **occasions** are what turns the first two into action at a particular moment. Note the phrase "may and may not be just": the theory does not require the grievance to be true. A slighted man rebels whether or not he was in fact slighted.

Most of the occasions are self-explanatory once a case is attached. *Insolence and greed* in the magistrates set them against each other and against the constitution that gives them their power. *Fear* moves men who have done wrong and expect punishment, or expect to be wronged and strike first: the notables of Rhodes, an island city of the south-east Aegean, overthrew the democracy because of the lawsuits brought against them. *Contempt* moves the stronger party against a government it no longer respects, as at Megara, whose democracy fell after a defeat brought on by its own disorder. *Excessive predominance*, one man too powerful for the regime, is why democracies invented ostracism, the vote of exile the reader met at Athens (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-objection-from-the-record">the law against Peisistratus's successors</Ref>), though Aristotle thinks it "much better to provide from the first that there should be no such pre-eminent individuals".

### Disproportionate growth: a foot four cubits long

<Quote source="Politics V.3, 1302b33–1303a10, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
For as a body is made up of many members, and every member ought to grow in proportion, that symmetry may be preserved; but loses its nature if the foot be four cubits long and the rest of the body two spans; and, should the abnormal increase be one of quality as well as of quantity, may even take the form of another animal: even so a state has many parts, of which some one may often grow imperceptibly; for example, the number of poor in democracies and in constitutional states.
</Quote>

A cubit is a forearm, about half a metre; a span is a hand's width with the fingers spread, about a third of that. A foot four cubits long under a body two spans high is a monster. A city is a body of parts, rich, middling and poor, and its constitution fits the proportions those parts had when it was made. Change the proportions and the constitution no longer fits, even if no law has been touched.

Aristotle's examples are all of the same shape: a war kills off one part. At Tarentum, a Spartan colony in the heel of Italy, many of the notables died in a battle with the Iapygians, the native people of the region, shortly after the Persian War, and the polity became a democracy. At Argos, Sparta's old rival in the north-eastern Peloponnese, the Spartan king Cleomenes destroyed the Argive army around 494, and the city had to admit to citizenship some of its *perioeci*, the free but voteless people of the surrounding towns. At Athens, "after frequent defeats of their infantry at the time of the Peloponnesian War, the notables were reduced in number, because the soldiers had to be taken from the roll of citizens". The heavy infantry was recruited from men with property, so every lost battle on land thinned the propertied class while the poor who rowed the fleet were spared; the reader has seen what Sicily cost (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="does-the-arrangement-judge-athens">the Sicilian expedition</Ref>).

### Dissimilarity: the ditch that breaks a regiment

The last occasion on the list is difference itself. Colonies that took in settlers of another people at their founding, Aristotle notes, "generally produced revolution": at Sybaris in southern Italy the Achaean settlers outnumbered and expelled the colonists from Troezen who had come with them. Even the ground can divide a city:

<Quote source="Politics V.3, 1303b7–14, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
at Athens, too, the inhabitants of the Piraeus are more democratic than those who live in the city. For just as in war the impediment of a ditch, though ever so small, may break a regiment, so every cause of difference, however slight, makes a breach in a city. The greatest opposition is confessedly that of virtue and vice; next comes that of wealth and poverty; and there are other antagonistic elements, greater or less, of which one is this difference of place.
</Quote>

The Piraeus, the harbour town of Athens where the *Republic* is set, was home to the sailors, traders and resident foreigners. A phalanx holds only while its line is unbroken, and a ditch a man can step across opens a gap in it. Any line along which citizens can sort themselves, of wealth, of descent, of neighbourhood, is a place where they will sort themselves when trouble comes.

### The four quarrels read again

<Example title="placing the trifles">
Each opening case can now be taken apart into the three components. Aristotle's notes record the occasion and usually the motive; the state of mind has to be read from what the story shows.

**Syracuse.** *Occasion:* a quarrel between two rulers, the most dangerous kind, because "when the notables quarrel, the whole city is involved". *Motive:* honour, in its most personal form, a man shamed by his friend. *State of mind:* not described; but a private quarrel could "split all the people into portions" only if the people were already divided along lines the two men could pull on.

**Delphi.** *Occasion:* insolence, a bridegroom's public refusal, answered by a judicial murder dressed as piety. *Motive:* dishonour: the bride's relatives were "thinking that they were insulted". *State of mind:* again not described; that the murder was "the beginning of all the troubles which followed" means there were parties ready to take it up.

**Mytilene.** *Occasion:* a rejected suit. *Motive:* gain, two heiresses and their estates. *State of mind:* the text does not say; but the rejected father was Athens's official friend in Mytilene, its *proxenus*, and he could call the Athenians in only because the city was already divided over its alliance with them.

**Sparta in the Messenian War.** *Occasion:* disproportionate growth, the rich swelling and the poor sinking: "some are very poor and others very rich, a state of society which is most often the result of war." *Motive:* gain, land. *State of mind:* here it is stated, men who had been equals and were now unequal, demanding a redistribution.

**What the cases show.** In each case the occasion is small and dated; the grievance behind it is older. The trifle decided when the city split; the partial justices decided that it would.
</Example>

Aristotle adds one condition that makes every occasion more dangerous: revolutions "break out when opposite parties, e.g. the rich and the people, are equally balanced, and there is little or no middle class; for, if either party were manifestly superior, the other would not risk an attack upon them". This is the scale of the previous chapter seen from the other side. And he names the two ways a faction wins, force and fraud. The fraud he has in mind is recent: in 411 the Four Hundred took Athens by telling the people that the Persian king would pay for the war if the democracy were replaced, and then held the city against its will (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-historians-own-two-judgments">the oligarchy of 411</Ref>).

## How each regime falls

Aristotle then runs the anatomy regime by regime, and each regime falls along the line its own partial justice draws. From the democrat's creed one would expect democracy to fall to the poor, demanding still more equality. The record Aristotle collects shows it falling, most often, to the rich it has driven together.

### Democracies: the demagogue drives the rich together

"Revolutions in democracies are generally caused by the intemperance of demagogues." A *demagogue*, a leader of the people, wins the assembly by attacking the rich: he brings them to court, proposes to divide their property, or loads them with public expenses, "and so force[s] them to combine". At Cos, an island off Asia Minor, the notables combined and overthrew the democracy; at Megara the demagogues exiled the rich to confiscate their estates, until "the exiles, becoming numerous, returned, and, engaging and defeating the people, established the oligarchy". Democracy is undone by pressing its own principle, equal shares for all, against the one party that does not accept it.

In older times the same road led to tyranny. "Of old, the demagogue was also a general, and then democracies changed into tyrannies": the popular leader who hated the rich also commanded troops, and could turn the people's trust into a guard. Peisistratus at Athens around 560 is Aristotle's first example, the man the reader saw ask the assembly for a bodyguard (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-protector-becomes-a-wolf">the protector becomes a wolf</Ref>). In Aristotle's own day, he notes, the leaders of the people are orators who know nothing of war, and "instances to the contrary are few and slight".

### Oligarchies: excluded from without, divided from within

Oligarchies have more ways to fall, because the slighted party is both outside and inside the ruling class. From outside: when the oligarchs oppress the people, "anybody is good enough to be their champion"; and when the government is very narrow, rich men who are excluded from it make the revolution, as at Massalia, the Greek colony that is now Marseille, where rules forbidding father and son, or elder and younger brother, to hold office together kept most of the rich out. From inside: oligarchs compete for the people's favour and turn demagogue; oligarchs who have spent their fortunes "want to innovate" and back a tyrant; a few hold the highest offices while the rest of the oligarchy is shut out of them, an oligarchy inside the oligarchy, as at Elis in the western Peloponnese, where a senate of ninety members held office for life and came from certain families only. In war an oligarchy cannot trust its own people with arms, hires mercenaries, and its mercenary general may make himself tyrant. And a property qualification fixed in one generation can fail silently in the next: "after a time of prosperity … the same property becomes many times as valuable, and then everybody participates in every office". Most of these routes run through a slighted party, and the oligarchy's measure makes more of them than the democrat's does: it shuts out the poor, then the rich outside the inner circle, then the ruined among its own members. That is the double danger behind Aristotle's ranking of oligarchy as the less safe of the two.

### Polities and aristocracies: the slide from a small change

On the <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="the-sixfold-table">sixfold table</Ref> aristocracy is the rule of the best for the common good. A city ruled by men good absolutely is rare, and the regimes Greeks actually called aristocracies were something else: cities that choose magistrates "both according to their wealth and according to their merit", weighing virtue, wealth and number, as Carthage did, or virtue and number, as Sparta did. An actual aristocracy is therefore a mixture like polity, with virtue added as a third ingredient, and Aristotle classes the two by which way they lean: the mixtures that "incline more to oligarchy are called aristocracies, and those which incline to democracy constitutional governments".

So both fall the same way: when the mixture is badly made, the regime moves in the direction it leans, a polity becoming a democracy and an aristocracy an oligarchy. The rich, "if the constitution gives them power, are apt to be insolent and avaricious", which is why the aristocracies, leaning their way, are the less safe of the two. But the characteristic danger of both is the one on the list that looks least like a danger, "neglect about trifles", and Aristotle tells the case in full.

Ambracia, in north-western Greece, had a small property qualification for office and let it be lowered until it was nothing, "for the Ambraciots thought that a small qualification was much the same as none at all". At Thurii, a colony founded in southern Italy in the 440s under Athenian leadership, a law said that a general could be re-elected only after an interval of five years. Some young men popular with the soldiers wanted the law abolished so that their generals could hold command continuously, knowing the people would happily keep electing them. The magistrates resisted, then gave way, "thinking that, if only this one law was changed, no further inroad would be made on the constitution. But other changes soon followed which they in vain attempted to oppose; and the state passed into the hands of the revolutionists, who established a dynastic oligarchy." A *dynastic oligarchy*, or *dynasty*, is Aristotle's name for the narrowest oligarchy: a few powerful families who hand office from father to son and rule above the law.

One more cause lies outside every regime: a powerful neighbour of the opposite kind. In the war between Athens and Sparta, "the Athenians everywhere put down the oligarchies, and the Lacedaemonians the democracies". The reader saw the pattern at Mytilene, where the commons were Athens's friends and the oligarchs its enemies.

## What destroys, reversed, preserves

A list of ways to die is not yet a theory. What makes it one is the step Aristotle takes next: "if we know the causes which destroy constitutions, we also know the causes which preserve them; for opposites produce opposites." Every cause on the list, run backwards, becomes a rule for keeping a regime. The obvious place to start would be the biggest danger. Aristotle starts with the smallest.

### Guard against small lawbreaking

<Quote source="Politics V.8, 1307b30–1308a3, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
In all well-attempered governments there is nothing which should be more jealously maintained than the spirit of obedience to law, more especially in small matters; for transgression creeps in unperceived and at last ruins the state, just as the constant recurrence of small expenses in time eats up a fortune. The expense does not take place all at once, and therefore is not observed; the mind is deceived, as in the fallacy which says that 'if each part is little, then the whole is little'. And this is true in one way, but not in another, for the whole and the all are not little, although they are made up of littles. In the first place, then, men should guard against the beginning of change, and in the second place they should not rely upon the political devices of which I have already spoken, invented only to deceive the people, for they are proved by experience to be useless.
</Quote>

The councillors of Thurii reasoned exactly as the fallacy does: one law is a small part of the constitution, so changing it is a small change. Each step was small; the sum was a different regime. The rule that follows is to treat a small breach of law as if it were a large one, because no one can tell in advance which small breach is the first of many. Aristotle concedes that this takes judgment: "No ordinary man can discern the beginning of evil, but only the true statesman."

The second rule disposes of a kind of cleverness Aristotle had catalogued in Book IV: the oligarchic devices that open the assembly and the courts to all but fine only the rich for staying away, so that the poor, who pay nothing and are paid nothing, stay home and the rich decide. They were meant to make an oligarchy look open while keeping it closed. They are, he now says, "proved by experience to be useless": a regime that preserves itself by deceiving the party it slights has not removed the slight.

### Treat the excluded as equals, and make office unprofitable

The remaining rules are the occasions of faction, reversed one by one.

- **Against contempt and exclusion:** oligarchies and aristocracies last "because the rulers are on good terms both with the unenfranchised and with the governing classes, not maltreating any who are excluded from the government, but introducing into it the leading spirits among them". They "should never wrong the ambitious in a matter of honour, or the common people in a matter of money".
- **Against predominance:** short tenures, so that no family settles into an office, and "moderate honour for a long time rather than great honour for a short time. For men are easily spoilt; not every one can bear prosperity."
- **Against carelessness:** fear, used deliberately. "The ruler who has a care of the constitution should invent terrors, and bring distant dangers near, in order that the citizens may be on their guard, and, like sentinels in a night-watch, never relax their attention."
- **Against disproportionate growth:** review the property qualification every year, or every three or five years in a large city, and raise or lower it as the city's wealth has grown or shrunk, so that the regime keeps the proportions it was made for.
- **Against insolence and greed:** "above all every state should be so administered and so regulated by law that its magistrates cannot possibly make money."

The last rule rests on an observation about the poor that the rich rarely make. "The people do not take any great offence at being kept out of the government, indeed they are rather pleased than otherwise at having leisure for their private business, but what irritates them is to think that their rulers are stealing the public money; then they are doubly annoyed; for they lose both honour and profit." If office paid nothing, the poor would leave it to the rich and tend their farms, and the rich would hold it without being envied. Aristotle prescribes the means: the public revenue handed over before the whole assembly, copies of the accounts lodged with the brotherhoods and tribes (the hereditary associations of families every citizen belonged to by birth, and the voting divisions like <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="cleisthenes-equality-one-share-each">Cleisthenes' ten tribes</Ref>), and honours voted to magistrates known to be incorruptible.

### Do not push the regime's principle to its extreme

The partial justices return here in their most practical form. A party that believes all virtue lies in its own principle will want more of it, and Aristotle answers with the body again:

<Quote source="Politics V.9, 1309b21–35, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
A nose which varies from the ideal of straightness to a hook or snub may still be of good shape and agreeable to the eye; but if the excess be very great, all symmetry is lost, and the nose at last ceases to be a nose at all on account of some excess in one direction or defect in the other […]. The same law of proportion equally holds in states. Oligarchy or democracy, although a departure from the most perfect form, may yet be a good enough government, but if any one attempts to push the principles of either to an extreme, he will begin by spoiling the government and end by having none at all.
</Quote>

A democracy is a city of rich and poor in which the poor have the upper hand; an oligarchy, the same city with the rich on top. "Neither the one nor the other can exist or continue to exist unless both rich and poor are included in it." Push democracy until the rich are ruined, or oligarchy until the poor are crushed, and what is left is not a purer democracy or oligarchy but no constitution at all. So the democrat's friend spares the rich, not only their property but their incomes, and the oligarch's friend takes care of the poor. Aristotle quotes an oath sworn by the ruling class in some oligarchies, "I will be an enemy to the people, and will devise all the harm against them which I can", and proposes the opposite oath: "I will do no wrong to the people."

<Aside>
**Democracy's surplus: the leaky cask.** Book VI applies the same rule to democracy's revenues. Demagogues like to distribute any surplus to the poor, but "such help is like water poured into a leaky cask": the poor are always receiving and always wanting more. The true friend of the people saves the surplus and gives the poor enough, in turn, to buy "a little farm", because the best material for a democracy is a population of farmers, too busy on their land to sit in the assembly every day and content to elect and audit.
</Aside>

### The greatest preservative: education for the regime

Every rule so far works on institutions. The anatomy of faction began with a state of mind, and the last rule works there:

<Quote source="Politics V.9, 1310a12–36, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
But of all the things which I have mentioned that which most contributes to the permanence of constitutions is the adaptation of education to the form of government, and yet in our own day this principle is universally neglected. The best laws, though sanctioned by every citizen of the state, will be of no avail unless the young are trained by habit and education in the spirit of the constitution, if the laws are democratical, democratically, or oligarchically, if the laws are oligarchical. […] Now, to have been educated in the spirit of the constitution is not to perform the actions in which oligarchs or democrats delight, but those by which the existence of an oligarchy or of a democracy is made possible. […] In such democracies every one lives as he pleases, or in the words of Euripides, 'according to his fancy.' But this is all wrong; men should not think it slavery to live according to the rule of the constitution; for it is their salvation.
</Quote>

The distinction in the middle of the passage is what makes it more than a call for patriotic schooling. An oligarch's son brought up "in the spirit of oligarchy" in Aristotle's sense is not taught to enjoy being rich; he is taught the habits that let an oligarchy survive, such as self-restraint, care of the poor, keeping the accounts honest. As things are, Aristotle observes, the sons of the oligarchs "live in luxury, but the sons of the poor are hardened by exercise and toil", so the oligarchy raises its own overthrowers. In a democracy, education in its spirit is not "living as one likes", which is democracy's principle pushed to the extreme; it is living by the constitution, which is what keeps the freedom.

From a theory of revolution one would expect the strongest remedy to be force: guards, exile, the arms kept in loyal hands. Aristotle's first remedy is attention to small things and his greatest is the formation of citizens. That is the thesis the reader met in Plato, that a regime lives or dies in the souls it forms (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="states-are-as-the-men-are">states are as the men are</Ref>), turned from a story about fathers and sons into a rule a legislator can follow. The remedies are not mechanical either. Whether a breach is the first of many, whether a man has grown too great, how far a democracy may go before it stops being one: each is a judgment about a particular city at a particular time, and the rules tell the statesman what to look at, not what he will see.

<KeyFact>
Every regime slights one party, and it cannot stop doing so without becoming another regime. What it can control is whether the slight is renewed: it lasts while its rulers never give the slighted party a fresh proof of the slight, and while its citizens are formed to do what keeps the constitution rather than what its ruling party enjoys.
</KeyFact>

## Why tyranny is short, and the two ways it lasts

The rules so far are for constitutions. Aristotle applies the same method to the rule of one man, and there he reaches the part of Book V that has made it notorious: advice on how a tyranny can be kept.

### King and tyrant: two origins

The two kinds of monarchy, he says, "are contrary in their very origin". A king is set up by the better classes against the people, chosen for his own or his family's virtue, and his task is to protect the rich from injustice and the people from insult. A tyrant "is chosen from the people to be their protector against the notables", and "almost all tyrants have been demagogues who gained the favour of the people by their accusation of the notables". A king aims at honour, a tyrant at pleasure; a king is guarded by citizens, a tyrant by mercenaries. Tyranny, Aristotle concludes, is "a compound of oligarchy and democracy in their most extreme forms": like an extreme oligarchy it pursues wealth and disarms the people, like an extreme democracy it makes war on the notables and destroys anyone who stands too high.

### Insult, hatred, fear, contempt: how one man falls

Monarchies fall from the same states of mind as other regimes, but when one man holds the city the grievance is personal and so is the attack. Subjects attack a monarch, Aristotle says, "out of fear or contempt, or because they have been unjustly treated", and "of injustice, the most common form is insult, another is confiscation of property". The most common cause is insult. The attempt on the Peisistratids at Athens in 514, when Harmodius and Aristogeiton killed Hipparchus, the tyrant Hippias's brother, "arose out of the public dishonour offered to the sister of Harmodius and the insult to himself". Fear moves men who expect to be accused. Contempt moves those who no longer see a ruler worth fearing: Sardanapalus, an Assyrian king of legend, was attacked after someone saw him "carding wool with his women", and heirs who inherit a tyranny tend to lose it at once, because "living in luxurious ease, they have become contemptible".

The other injustice, the taking of property, breeds the standing motive Aristotle puts beside contempt: "hatred of tyrants is inevitable". A tyrant needs money for mercenaries and great works, and he takes it from his subjects; Dionysius, tyrant of Syracuse in the early fourth century, taxed them so hard that "within five years his subjects should bring into the treasury their whole property". The last danger comes from the city's two classes. A tyrant rises as the people's champion against the notables, so the notables are his enemies from the start, plotting because they "either want to rule or to escape subjection"; and the people, whose champion he no longer needs to be once he holds power, have no reason to defend him when anyone moves. At Syracuse, Gelo made himself tyrant in 485 and was succeeded by his brother Hiero; when Hiero died, a third brother, Thrasybulus (not the tyrant of Miletus who received Periander's advice), flattered Gelo's young son into excesses so as to rule in his name. Gelo's family gathered a party against Thrasybulus to save the tyranny, and "those of the people who conspired with them seized the opportunity and drove them all out". Democracies are tyranny's natural enemies, on the principle of Hesiod's line that <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="rivalry-among-peers">"potter hates potter"</Ref>: the two are kin, "for the extreme form of democracy is tyranny".

Kingship is preserved by the opposite of tyranny's vices: by limitation. The more restricted a king's powers, the longer they last, "for then they are more moderate and not so despotic in their ways; and they are less envied by their subjects". Aristotle's example is Sparta, where the kingship was divided between two and further limited by Theopompus, a Spartan king of about 700 BC credited with creating the elected overseers, the ephors. When his wife asked whether he was not ashamed to hand on to his sons less power than he had inherited, he answered, "No indeed, for the power which I leave to them will be more lasting."

### Two recipes for a tyrant

Tyranny, Aristotle says, "is preserved in two most opposite ways". The first is the traditional one, which he attributes to Periander of Corinth, the tyrant who <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="the-man-no-law-can-measure">cut down the tallest ears of corn</Ref>, and to the Persian kings. The tyrant kills men of spirit, forbids common meals, clubs and education, and keeps people from knowing one another, "for acquaintance begets mutual confidence"; he employs spies, sets friends and classes against each other, and keeps his subjects poor with taxes, wars and great works, of which "the Pyramids of Egypt afford an example". Aristotle sums the policy up under three heads: the tyrant "sows distrust among his subjects; he takes away their power; he humbles them".

<Figure src="./figures/olympieion.jpg" alt="Fifteen tall fluted Corinthian columns, still joined at the top by fragments of their architrave, stand on a long stone platform in a grassy field; two more columns stand apart to the left, the Acropolis rises on its rock behind them, and a few visitors walking past the base of the platform reach barely above its step" caption="The Olympieion in Athens. The people walking past the platform give the scale." float="right" width={380} />

The second recipe proceeds "upon an almost opposite principle". Since one way to destroy a kingship is to make it more like a tyranny, "the salvation of a tyranny is to make it more like the rule of a king". The tyrant keeps the power to rule against his subjects' will, because without it he is no longer a tyrant; in everything else he plays the king.

<Example title="a tyranny kept by running its dangers backwards">
The rule "opposites produce opposites" can be applied to the causes of a tyrant's fall listed in the previous subsection. Take each in turn and find the second recipe's answer to it.

**Hatred of the tyrant who takes their money.** Reversed: "he should pretend a care of the public revenues", spend nothing on presents to "courtesans and strangers and artists", and "give an account of what he receives and of what he spends", so that he seems "a steward of the public rather than a tyrant". He collects taxes visibly "only for state purposes".

**Insult.** Reversed: "the tyrant should abstain from all outrage", above all towards the young of either sex, and the women of his household should show the same restraint, since "the insolence of women has ruined many tyrannies". Honours he gives himself; punishments are inflicted "by officers and courts of law".

**Contempt.** Reversed: he should be "temperate and wide awake", not "a drunken and drowsy tyrant", and whatever virtues he neglects, he should "maintain the character of a great soldier". He should also seem "particularly earnest in the service of the Gods", for men are less afraid of a ruler who has "the very Gods fighting on his side".

**The notables' plots, and the war of rich and poor.** Reversed: honour men of merit so that they would not expect more honour from a free government; never make one man great, or else make two, "that they may look sharply after one another"; and persuade rich and poor alike that his rule protects each from the other, attaching to himself whichever is stronger.

**The result.** Book V's general rule, applied to the worst regime: find what destroys it and do the opposite.
</Example>

If the tyrant follows the second recipe, "his rule will of necessity be nobler and happier, because he will rule over better men whose spirits are not crushed, over men to whom he himself is not an object of hatred, and of whom he is not afraid. His power too will be more lasting. His disposition will be virtuous, or at least half virtuous; and he will not be wicked, but half wicked only."

### The record: a century at most

Then the record. "No forms of government are so short-lived as oligarchy and tyranny." The longest tyranny Aristotle knows is that of Orthagoras and his sons at Sicyon, a city near Corinth in the north-east Peloponnese, which lasted a hundred years "because they treated their subjects with moderation, and to a great extent observed the laws". Next come the Cypselids at Corinth, seventy-three and a half years; Cypselus "never had a body-guard". Third are the Peisistratids at Athens, thirty-five years in all, broken by two exiles. The tyranny of Gelo and his brother Hiero at Syracuse, the most lasting of the rest, held for eighteen years: Gelo seven, Hiero ten, and Thrasybulus less than one.

The durations are the argument in numbers. The tyrannies that lasted longest are those that came nearest the second recipe, and none outlasted a century. A tyranny can be preserved only by becoming less of a tyranny. That is how Aristotle can teach a tyrant to survive without recommending tyranny: the advice, followed, pulls the regime towards the rule of a king, and the measure by which it is "half virtuous" is the measure by which the true forms are ranked above it. A later reader drew the opposite lesson from advice of this kind: that political science is the art of keeping power, whatever the regime. That view is the subject of <Ref lesson="machiavelli-effectual-truth" />.

## What the city is for: not conquest

A regime can now be judged by whether it lasts, and Book V shows how to make most regimes last longer. But survival cannot be the measure of a city. A well-run tyranny lasted a century at Sicyon; a city could keep its constitution for ever and still be a bad place to live. To say what a regime should be preserved *for*, Aristotle has to say what a city is for, and he opens Book VII by saying so: "He who would duly inquire about the best form of a state ought first to determine which is the most eligible life."

### The best life: virtue, with the goods it needs

Everyone agrees, he begins, that there are three kinds of goods: external goods (wealth, power, reputation), goods of the body, and goods of the soul, and that a happy man needs all three. Nobody calls happy a man "who will sacrifice his dearest friend for the sake of half-a-farthing". The disagreement is over proportions: many think a little virtue is enough and "set no limit to their desires of wealth, property, power, reputation". Aristotle answers that the order runs the other way. Men "do not acquire or preserve virtue by the help of external goods, but external goods by the help of virtue". External goods are instruments, and like every instrument they have a limit, beyond which more of them is useless or harmful; the reader met the same principle as the limit on acquisition, "the end is always the limit" (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="where-the-limit-goes">where the limit goes</Ref>). Goods of the soul have no such limit. Aristotle's image for the confusion is music: to think external goods the cause of happiness is like attributing "a brilliant performance on the lyre … to the instrument and not to the skill of the performer".

So the best life, for a man and for a city, "is the life of virtue, when virtue has external goods enough for the performance of good actions". Fortune supplies the goods; it cannot supply the virtue. "Virtue and goodness in the state are not a matter of chance but the result of knowledge and purpose."

### Might without right

If the city's end is virtuous action, then a great many cities have mistaken it. Aristotle lists them. In Sparta and Crete "the system of education and the greater part of the laws are framed with a view to war"; at Carthage, the Phoenician city in North Africa, men wore as many armlets as they had served campaigns; among the Scythians, the horse peoples north of the Black Sea, no man who had not killed an enemy might drink from the cup passed round at a certain feast; among the Iberians of Spain, obelisks round a warrior's tomb counted the men he had killed.

<Quote source="Politics VII.2, 1324b22–36, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Yet to a reflecting mind it must appear very strange that the statesman should be always considering how he can dominate and tyrannize over others, whether they will or not. How can that which is not even lawful be the business of the statesman or the legislator? Unlawful it certainly is to rule without regard to justice, for there may be might where there is no right. The other arts and sciences offer no parallel; a physician is not expected to persuade or coerce his patients, nor a pilot the passengers in his ship. Yet most men appear to think that the art of despotic government is statesmanship, and what men affirm to be unjust and inexpedient in their own case they are not ashamed of practising towards others; they demand just rule for themselves, but where other men are concerned they care nothing about it.
</Quote>

The reader has heard this position stated by those who held it. The Athenian envoys at Melos said that justice is a question only between equals in power, and that otherwise "the strong do what they can" (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-thesis">the Athenian thesis</Ref>). Aristotle does not answer that the envoys misdescribed how powers behave. His charge is inconsistency: the same men who demand just rule at home practise mastery abroad, treating as statesmanship what they would call injustice if it were done to them. And his example is the craft analogy the reader met in the argument with Thrasymachus (<Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="whom-does-a-craft-serve-the-physician-the-pilot-the-shepherd">whom does a craft serve?</Ref>): no one thinks a physician's art is to master his patients, so why think the statesman's art is to master his neighbours?

Aristotle allows one exception, and the reader can place it. Mastery is not irrational "if the one party is, and the other is not, born to serve", in which case men may rule "only those who are intended to be subjects", as one may hunt only animals that are fit to be hunted. That is the natural slave of Book I, carrying all the difficulty <Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="where-the-theory-fails-on-its-own-terms-no-mark-no-sign-no-test">the theory's own concessions</Ref> left it. With that exception set aside, a city has no business aiming at empire. A city might even live alone, "happy in isolation", well governed, with good laws, "constituted with [no] view to war or the conquest of enemies".

### The political life and the philosophic

Among those who agree that virtue is the end, a second dispute remains, and it bears directly on the isolated city. One side withdraws from politics: ruling others is either a master's rule, which is unjust, or a lawful rule over equals, which is no injustice but takes a man away from his own good. The other side holds that "he who does nothing cannot do well": happiness is virtuous activity, and the widest field for action is rule. Put the same question to a city. If happiness is activity, a city that rules no one looks idle, and the war-cities look like the active ones. Aristotle tells both sides "you are partly right and partly wrong".

The withdrawn are right that a master's rule is nothing noble: there is nothing grand "in issuing commands about necessary things" to a slave. But rule over free men is a different thing from mastery, and to rank doing nothing above acting is a mistake, "for happiness is activity". The statesmen are right about that, and wrong about what follows. If more power meant more noble actions, a man ought to take his neighbour's power, and no father, son or friend should stand in the way; that would be true only "if we assume that robbers and plunderers attain the chief good". Among equals "the honourable and the just consist in sharing alike", so the man who takes more than his share has, in the taking, already lost the virtue that would make his later actions good. Power over others does not multiply good action.

What reconciles the two is what counts as activity. Action need not be done to other people: "the thoughts and contemplations which are independent and complete in themselves" are activity in the fullest sense, so the philosopher is not idle and the statesman need not conquer to be active. The same holds of a city. One that lives alone is not inactive, because "there are many ways in which the sections of a state act upon one another": its citizens deliberate, judge, train and educate one another. Hence "the same life is best for each individual, and for states". The city "happy in isolation" is therefore no lesser city, and its good lies in what its citizens do among themselves.

<Remark title="the common good">
Book VII gives the term the previous chapter used, the common interest that true regimes serve and perversions betray (<Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="whose-good-does-the-rule-serve">whose good does the rule serve?</Ref>), its content. The *common good* is the good life of the whole citizen body: virtuous activity, with enough external goods to perform it, shared by all who belong to the city. "A city is not to be termed happy in regard to a portion of the citizens, but in regard to them all." A regime that serves the wealth of the rich serves a part; a regime that serves the conquest of neighbours serves an end that is not the city's at all.
</Remark>

## War for the sake of peace, business for the sake of leisure

The end has to be built into the city's laws, and Aristotle derives the shape of those laws from the soul. The soul has a part with reason and a part that can obey reason; the better part is the end of the other. Life divides the same way:

<Quote source="Politics VII.14, 1333a30–b3, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
The whole of life is further divided into two parts, business and leisure, war and peace, and of actions some aim at what is necessary and useful, and some at what is honourable. And the preference given to one or the other class of actions must necessarily be like the preference given to one or other part of the soul and its actions over the other; there must be war for the sake of peace, business for the sake of leisure, things useful and necessary for the sake of things honourable. […] For men must be able to engage in business and go to war, but leisure and peace are better; they must do what is necessary and indeed what is useful, but what is honourable is better.
</Quote>

*Leisure* here is not rest after work or amusement. Aristotle's word means time free of necessity, the condition in which a man can do what is worth doing for its own sake: deliberate about the city, judge, study, enjoy music. Work and war are necessary, and a city that could not do either would not survive, but they are for something.

### The Spartan critique: like unused iron

The sharpest application is to the city every Greek theorist admired. Sparta's admirers praised its lawgiver "for making conquest and war his sole aim", and its training did win it an empire. Aristotle answers from the record:

<Quote source="Politics VII.14, 1333b21–1334a10, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
But surely they are not a happy people now that their empire has passed away, nor was their legislator right. How ridiculous is the result, if, while they are continuing in the observance of his laws and no one interferes with them, they have lost the better part of life! […] Facts, as well as arguments, prove that the legislator should direct all his military and other measures to the provision of leisure and the establishment of peace. For most of these military states are safe only while they are at war, but fall when they have acquired their empire; like unused iron they lose their temper in time of peace. And for this the legislator is to blame, he never having taught them how to lead the life of peace.
</Quote>

The image is of a weapon that keeps its edge only in use. Sparta's empire over Greece, won in 404, was broken in 371 when Thebes, the chief city of Boeotia, defeated the Spartan army at Leuctra, within Aristotle's own lifetime. The Spartans had kept their laws exactly, and the laws had taught them only war. A city trained in one virtue, courage, had nothing to do with victory once it had it. Aristotle draws the general lesson in the next chapter. The virtues of peace (justice, temperance, and the philosophy that uses leisure well) are needed most by those who have the most leisure and good fortune, so that the inhabitants of the Islands of the Blest, the poets' paradise, "will need philosophy and temperance and justice, and all the more the more leisure they have".

### The objection: faction as the engine of liberty

The critique has a strong opponent. A later republican, writing about Rome, will argue that the endless quarrel between the nobles and the common people, the very faction Book V treats as a disease, was what made Rome free, because every law that protected liberty came out of it; and that a republic built only to keep peace and to stay as it is cannot defend itself, while one built to grow, as Rome was, can (<Ref lesson="machiavelli-discourses-republic" />). On that view Aristotle has the causes of liberty and decline backwards: the city that keeps its quarrels and its arms in use lasts, and the city that seeks leisure rusts.

Aristotle's text already holds part of a reply. His unused iron describes a city built for war that wins its war, not a city built for peace, and he does not ask the best city to be weak: it "must be temperate and brave, and able to endure", because "there is no leisure for slaves". Nor does he deny that quarrels can produce good laws; his whole account of preservation is a list of laws that a faction's grievance teaches. What he denies is that the quarrel itself is the end. Whether a city can keep its quarrels useful without their hardening into the state of mind of Book V is the question the Roman case puts to him.

## A city you can take in at one view: size, site and who counts

With the end fixed, the best city can be derived from it, as a craftsman derives a tool from its work. Aristotle calls these the "materials" of the legislator: the number of the citizens, the land, and the kind of men they are.

### Size: a herald's voice

"Most persons think that a state in order to be happy ought to be large." Aristotle replies that they mistake headcount for greatness. A city has a work to do, like a man, and the greatest city is the one best fitted to it. Everything natural has a size at which it can do its work: "a ship which is only a span long will not be a ship at all, nor a ship a quarter of a mile long". A city of too few cannot supply its needs. A city of too many may supply them, as a nation does, but cannot be governed as a city: "For who can be the general of such a vast multitude, or who the herald, unless he have the voice of a Stentor?" Stentor is the herald in Homer's *Iliad* whose voice was as loud as fifty men's.

The decisive reason is not the herald, though:

<Quote source="Politics VII.4, 1326b14–25, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
But if the citizens of a state are to judge and to distribute offices according to merit, then they must know each other's characters; where they do not possess this knowledge, both the election to offices and the decision of lawsuits will go wrong. When the population is very large they are manifestly settled at haphazard, which clearly ought not to be. […] Clearly then the best limit of the population of a state is the largest number which suffices for the purposes of life, and can be taken in at a single view.
</Quote>

The previous chapter gave the many a role: to elect and audit their magistrates and to sit as judges (<Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="many-contributors-one-judgment">the feast</Ref>). That role has a condition the feast argument did not state. Guests can judge a dinner because they taste it; citizens can judge a candidate only if they know him. Past a certain size they vote for names, and elections and lawsuits are "settled at haphazard". The territory follows the same rule: large enough for its people to live "temperately and liberally in the enjoyment of leisure", and small enough to be seen whole and defended. The sea is useful for trade and defence, but its crowd of foreign merchants is kept at a distance, in a harbour "outside the city, but not too far off", and the city's fleet can be rowed by men who are not citizens.

### Citizens: spirit, and the strife of brothers

The citizens should be spirited and intelligent at once, which is the climate theory the reader has seen, now used to say what kind of people the best city should be made of (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="greek-and-barbarian-the-climate-theory-as-the-missing-mark">Greek and barbarian</Ref>). Aristotle adds an observation about spirit that sends the reader back to Book V. Plato had wanted his guardians to be like a good watch-dog, gentle to those they know and fierce to strangers (<Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="spirited-and-gentle-at-once">spirited and gentle at once</Ref>). Aristotle says that spirit works the other way: it "is more stirred against our friends and acquaintances than against those who are unknown to us, when we think that we are despised by them", and he quotes the saying "Cruel is the strife of brethren." Anger is sharpest where a man expected respect and was slighted. That is the state of mind of faction, and it explains why faction, of all conflicts, is the bitterest: it is fought among men who know each other.

### Parts and conditions

Every city needs food, crafts, arms, revenue, worship and a power to decide what is just. Does it follow that the farmers and craftsmen who supply these are part of the city? Aristotle distinguishes what a thing needs from what it is made of: a house needs a builder, but the builder is not part of the house. In the best city:

- the citizens may not be craftsmen or traders, because "such a life is ignoble and inimical to virtue", nor farmers, "since leisure is necessary both for the development of virtue and the performance of political duties";
- the *parts* of the city are the warriors and the councillors, and they are the same men at different ages, because "those who carry arms can always determine the fate of the constitution": the young serve in arms, the older deliberate and judge, and the oldest serve the gods as priests;
- the land belongs to the citizens, and the farmers, craftsmen and labourers are *conditions* of the city, not parts of it. The farmers will "of necessity be slaves or barbarian Perioeci".

The reader has seen the reasons Aristotle gives for choosing these farmers: they should be "not all of the same race and not spirited", so that "there will be no danger of their making a revolution" (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="what-the-best-city-does-with-it">what the best city does with it</Ref>). Set beside Book V, the design is complete. Faction needs a party that feels slighted and is able to act on it. Inside the ring there is no such party: the citizens are equals with the same property in land, the same leisure, the same education, and they hold office in turn by age, which is the one inequality no one resents, since "no one takes offence at being governed when he is young … especially if he will enjoy the same privilege when he reaches the required age". Outside the ring are the men who might feel slighted, and they are chosen so that they cannot combine. The best city has solved the problem of Book V by drawing its boundary round the equals and putting everyone else outside it. Its leisure is bought by the exclusion of everyone who works, which is the household of Book I, master and slave, written on the scale of a city.

### An education for leisure

The means to that end is the education Book V called the greatest preservative, now designed for the best regime. It must be public and the same for all, because "the whole city has one end", and "neither must we suppose that any one of the citizens belongs to himself, for they all belong to the state". Aristotle praises the Spartans for taking education seriously and blames them, again, for what they educated for. Children learn useful things such as reading and writing, but the aim is to make men able to use leisure, which is not amusement, "for then amusement would be the end of life": "To be always seeking after the useful does not become free and exalted souls."

<Conclusion>
Why do cities fall from within? Because every regime rests on a partial justice, numerical equality or proportional, and so carries inside it a party that feels slighted: a state of mind that waits for a motive (gain or honour) and an occasion (insolence, fear, contempt, one part grown out of proportion, a small change let through) to become a revolution, which is why trifles start revolutions that are about great things. The causes run backwards are the remedies: guard against small lawbreaking, treat the excluded fairly, make office unprofitable, do not push the regime's own principle to the extreme, and above all educate the young in the spirit of the constitution. Even tyranny lasts only by playing the king, and then never more than a century. Underneath the theory of preservation lies the answer to what a city is for: not wealth and not empire, but the virtuous activity of its citizens in leisure, for which war and work are means. The best city Aristotle derives from that end is small enough for its citizens to know one another, and it removes faction by admitting only equals and excluding everyone who works.

The reader can now classify a case of civil strife by its state of mind, motive and occasion, state the remedy as its cause reversed, and say against what end Aristotle ranks a regime that survives. What remains is to test the whole module's theory on the one city every Greek thinker admired and faulted, a regime of trained equals living off a subject population, which Aristotle's iron sentence already describes: <Ref lesson="sparta-as-test-case" />.
</Conclusion>

Apply the sourcing questions from <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="solon-at-athens">the first chapter</Ref> to Aristotle's story of Syracuse (Politics V.4: the two young men "in the government", the boy, the wife, and the constitution "once changed" when they "drew the members of the ruling class into their quarrel"). Who is writing, for whom, and from what kind of evidence? What does the passage record, what does it argue, and what does it omit? Finally, what can a two-sentence story of this kind be used to establish about Syracuse, and what about Aristotle's method?

<Solution>
**Who, when, for whom.** Aristotle, in lecture notes for his school at Athens, in the 330s or 320s BC. His students needed examples of causes, not histories of cities. The story's date is "of old", so it is at least a century or two earlier than the writing; the notes do not say where it came from, but it must rest on local histories or oral tradition, nearly all now lost.

**What it records.** That at some early date the constitution of Syracuse changed after a quarrel between two members of its ruling group, which spread through the ruling class and then the people.

**What it argues.** That the occasions of revolution can be trivial when the men involved are rulers, because "when the notables quarrel, the whole city is involved"; and, from the story, that a statesman should "put an end to the quarrels of chiefs and mighty men" at the start.

**What it omits.** The names of the men, the date, the constitution before and after the change, which side won, and its source. It gives no evidence that the love quarrel was the cause rather than one incident remembered because it made a good story.

**What it can establish.** About Syracuse, little: that a tradition existed connecting an early change of regime with a personal quarrel among the rulers. It cannot establish what the regime was, nor that the quarrel caused the change. About Aristotle's method, more: he collected many such cases, sorted them by kind of cause, and used each as an illustration of a type rather than as a proven instance. The story is evidence of the theory's shape (trifles act on a ruling class already divided), not a test of it.

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "Aristotle's theory of faction says that inequality causes revolution." Name the step that fails, and state the claim that survives, with its consequence for how a regime should be preserved.

<Solution>
**The step that fails: inequality as such.** Every city Aristotle describes is unequal in some respect: the best city is unequal by age, polity by wealth, every regime between those who hold office and those who do not. If inequality caused revolution, every city would be in revolution all the time. What Aristotle names as the cause is a state of mind: "when men think that they are equal to others who have more than themselves", or superior to those who have as much. The grievance is relative to a conception of justice, and he adds that such pretensions "may and may not be just". The same distribution of property provokes faction in a democracy (the rich resent being treated as equals) and in an oligarchy (the poor resent being treated as inferiors) for opposite reasons. And an inequality the inferior accepts, such as the young being ruled by the old in a city where they will rule in turn, causes no faction at all.

**What survives.** Faction is caused by an inequality that one party judges unjust by its own partial measure of equality, together with a motive and an occasion.

**Consequence.** Since the cause is a judgment, preservation works on judgments as much as on distributions: treat the excluded fairly and bring their leaders into office, never insult the ambitious or rob the poor, make office unprofitable so exclusion costs nothing, mix the two equalities, and educate citizens in the spirit of the constitution. Equalising property is not the remedy; Aristotle warns that "if equality of property is introduced, the state must of necessity take another form".
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Classify a case the chapter did not use. In 427 civil war broke out at Corcyra, a Corinthian colony on an island off the north-west coast of Greece, between the popular party, allied with Athens, and the oligarchs, allied with Sparta. The account below was written about 400 BC by Thucydides, the exiled Athenian general whose history of the war the reader knows (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-historian">the historian</Ref>):

<Quote source="Thucydides III.82, trans. R. Crawley">
struggles being everywhere made by the popular chiefs to bring in the Athenians, and by the oligarchs to introduce the Lacedaemonians. In peace there would have been neither the pretext nor the wish to make such an invitation; but in war, with an alliance always at the command of either faction […] opportunities for bringing in the foreigner were never wanting to the revolutionary parties. The sufferings which revolution entailed upon the cities were many and terrible, such as have occurred and always will occur, as long as the nature of mankind remains the same […]. The cause of all these evils was the lust for power arising from greed and ambition […]. The leaders in the cities, each provided with the fairest professions, on the one side with the cry of political equality of the people, on the other of a moderate aristocracy, sought prizes for themselves in those public interests which they pretended to cherish […]. Meanwhile the moderate part of the citizens perished between the two, either for not joining in the quarrel, or because envy would not suffer them to escape.
</Quote>

(a) Place the case in the three columns of Aristotle's anatomy, defending each placement from the text. (b) Which of Book V's further conditions does the passage confirm? (c) Design the test that would show Aristotle's theory to be more than a way of sorting stories: what finding would count against it?

<Solution>
**(a) The three columns.** *State of mind:* each party has a conception of equality it can cry aloud, "political equality of the people" against "a moderate aristocracy": the democrat's numerical equality and the oligarch's claim to more by worth. That they were "the fairest professions" of men who "sought prizes for themselves" does not remove them; Aristotle's state of mind includes pretensions that "may and may not be just". *Motives:* "the lust for power arising from greed and ambition", which is Aristotle's gain and honour, word for word in substance. *Occasion:* the war between Athens and Sparta, which offered each party a foreign ally: Aristotle's cause "from without", "some government close at hand having an opposite interest, or at a distance, but powerful", and the Athenians and Lacedaemonians putting down oligarchies and democracies respectively. The historian makes the same distinction between standing cause and occasion: in peace there would have been "neither the pretext nor the wish", which says the war was the occasion, not the cause.

**(b) Further conditions.** The middle: "the moderate part of the citizens perished between the two". Aristotle says revolutions break out where rich and people are balanced and "there is little or no middle class"; at Corcyra the middle was destroyed by the faction itself, so the case shows the condition operating as the strife went on.

**(c) A test.** Aristotle's theory makes a state of mind necessary: no faction without a party convinced it is treated unjustly by the regime's measure. It would count against the theory to find a revolution made by a party that had no such grievance and was moved by gain alone, for example a coup by mercenaries for pay in a city whose classes were reconciled, or a city with a sharp partial-justice grievance, a strong motive and repeated occasions that nonetheless never broke into faction for reasons the theory does not name. A fair test would collect cases independently of Aristotle (from inscriptions and other historians), code the presence of each of the three components before the event, and see whether the first is present in every case of faction and absent where none occurred. Because the grievance can be inferred too easily after the fact ("they must have felt slighted"), the coding has to use evidence from before the revolution.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two boundary cases for V.2. (a) A city in which no citizen feels unequally treated: Aristotle's "equals and similars", a polity with a large middle class. Can an occasion such as a love quarrel among the magistrates, or a lost battle, start a faction there? (b) A city with no middle class, only a few very rich and many very poor, each class sure the regime robs it. Which occasion is then enough? Use your two answers to rank the three components as necessary, enabling or precipitating.

<Solution>
**(a) State of mind at zero.** A love quarrel can still split two magistrates and their friends, but there is no division along which the city can take sides: the Hestiaea pattern needs a poor brother who can enlist the poor and a rich brother who can enlist the rich. In a city of equals and similars, each party would find nobody with a grievance to enlist. A lost battle may kill many citizens, but if the dead were not one class, the proportions of the city are unchanged. Aristotle's own claim fits: where the middle class is large "there is least faction". So the occasions, on his theory, cannot by themselves make a faction.

**(b) No middle, grievance at its maximum.** Here any occasion may serve: a trifle among the notables, an insult by a magistrate, a lawsuit, a debt, even an election result. Aristotle says that when rich and people are "equally balanced, and there is little or no middle class", revolutions break out, because each side expects to win; and the previous chapter's scale says the same.

**The ranking.** The state of mind is *necessary*: without it, occasions pass without effect. The size of the middle class and the balance between rich and poor are *enabling*: they decide whether a grievance can become a war. The occasion is *precipitating*: it fixes the date. The motives sit between, since gain and honour are always present and become effective only through a grievance. This is why Aristotle can say revolutions come "from trifles" but are not "about" them.
</Solution>

"A later republican claims that the quarrel between the few and the many was the source of Rome's liberty and greatness, and that Aristotle mistook the engine of freedom for a disease." Name two facts in Book V that the claim must account for, and state what would have to be true of a city for the claim to survive them.

<Solution>

That would need offices through which the many could block the few, and the reverse, without either holding the whole city; and a city whose classes still shared an end (at Rome, war and expansion) strong enough to keep the quarrel from ending in civil war. If those conditions held, conflict could be a source of good laws; if they failed, the quarrel would become Corcyra's. Using this chapter and the previous one: (a) list which of Book V's occasions of faction the city carries; (b) say which of its parts desire the permanence of the constitution, and whether that portion is stronger than the one that desires the reverse, as <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="the-weight-that-turns-the-scale">the previous chapter's test</Ref> requires; (c) say what Book VII predicts for it once it has won the war it trained for.

Aristotle begins the Politics not with a city but with a household: a man, a woman, children, and slaves. He says each relation of ruler and ruled in it is natural, and that the city grows out of such households as a living thing grows into its finished form. Then he faces an objection he reports from his own contemporaries: that slavery exists only by law and force, and is against nature. The lesson follows his answer and the place where he admits it does not quite work.

When the *Republic* asked why there are cities at all, its answer was need: no one is self-sufficing, so people gather, divide their labour and trade, and the city is the arrangement that supplies their wants (<Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="need-makes-the-first-city-differences-of-nature-divide-its-labour">the first city</Ref>). On that account a city is something people build, as they build a ship. The *Laws* then met the view that carries this to its end, the materialist's doctrine that justice has "no existence at all in nature", differs from city to city by agreement, and that "the highest right is might" (<Ref lesson="platos-laws-second-best-city" id="the-materialists-doctrine">Laws X</Ref>, where Glaucon's bargain, Thrasymachus and the envoys at Melos were found to be parts of one picture). Call that view *conventionalism*: the city is made, its justice is agreed, and force is what the agreement rests on. Plato answered it in the *Laws* from an order of causes; Aristotle answers from another place: from how a city grows.

If conventionalism is right, the clearest evidence for it is in the household, where one human being owns another. A slave agreed to nothing. He was taken in war, or born to a captive, or bought. If any rule is force wearing the dress of law, this one is. So whoever wants to show that rule can be natural has to show it at the point where it looks least natural, and that is where Aristotle starts. The question the chapter follows is whether the ranks of ruler and ruled, master and slave included, can be read off nature, and if they can, how anyone would tell who belongs where.

Aristotle (384–322 BC), met in <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="outside-the-text-the-objection-from-care">the Republic chapter</Ref> as Plato's student and critic, was born at Stagira, a small Greek town on the north Aegean coast. After twenty years in Plato's school he tutored the young Alexander of Macedon, and from 335 he taught at his own school in Athens, the Lyceum. In Athens he was a <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-insatiable-good">metic</Ref>, a resident foreigner without citizenship. The *Politics* is his lecture course on cities, compressed and unpolished. Its passages are cited by page, column and line of the standard 1831 edition (1253a7 is page 1253, column a, line 7), numbers every translation prints in its margin.

## Begin with what grows: household, village, city

To call a city natural, Aristotle first has to say what kind of thing a city is, and he starts by rejecting his teacher's answer.

### Take the compound apart

Some people, he reports, think that a statesman, a king, a householder and a master of slaves do the same job and "differ, not in kind, but only in the number of their subjects": a big household is a small city. The view is Plato's, from the *Statesman*, a late dialogue on the art of ruling. If it is right there is one kind of rule, knowledge commanding those who lack it, and governing citizens is managing slaves at a larger scale. Aristotle calls it "a mistake; for governments differ in kind". To see how they differ he proposes a method borrowed from the study of nature:

<Quote source="Politics I.1–2, 1252a18–26, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
As in other departments of science, so in politics, the compound should always be resolved into the simple elements or least parts of the whole. […] He who thus considers things in their first growth and origin, whether a state or anything else, will obtain the clearest view of them.
</Quote>

### Two pairings nature makes

The least parts are two pairs of beings "who cannot exist without each other". The first is male and female, joined "that the race may continue", not by any plan but from the desire every living thing shares with plants "to leave behind them an image of themselves". The second is the pair the argument about slavery rests on:

<Quote source="Politics I.2, 1252a30–34, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
and of natural ruler and subject, that both may be preserved. For that which can foresee by the exercise of mind is by nature intended to be lord and master, and that which can with its body give effect to such foresight is a subject, and by nature a slave; hence master and slave have the same interest.
</Quote>

The pairing is argued from a division of capacities. One being can plan and another can carry plans out, and each survives better joined to the other. Aristotle adds at once that nature keeps the two pairings apart: she "is not niggardly, like the smith who fashions the Delphian knife for many uses", a cheap blade that does several jobs badly, but "makes each thing for a single use". A wife is not a slave.

Then a sentence about foreigners. Among *barbarians*, the Greeks' word for everyone who did not speak Greek, "no distinction is made between women and slaves, because there is no natural ruler among them: they are a community of slaves". Aristotle quotes Euripides, the Athenian tragic poet: "It is meet that Hellenes should rule over barbarians", *Hellenes* being the Greeks' name for themselves, "as if they thought that the barbarian and the slave were by nature one". Here he reports the poets' line without arguing for it.

### Household, village, city

From the two pairings the rest grows. A household is formed "for the supply of men's everyday wants", and the poor man without a slave has an ox, "for the ox is the poor man's slave". Several households joined, aiming at "something more than the supply of daily needs", make a village, typically a colony of one family, ruled by its eldest as a king. Several villages joined make a city:

<Quote source="Politics I.2, 1252b27–1253a3, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
When several villages are united in a single complete community, large enough to be nearly or quite self-sufficing, the state comes into existence, originating in the bare needs of life, and continuing in existence for the sake of a good life. And therefore, if the earlier forms of society are natural, so is the state, for it is the end of them, and the nature of a thing is its end. For what each thing is when fully developed, we call its nature, whether we are speaking of a man, a horse, or a family. […] Hence it is evident that the state is a creation of nature, and that man is by nature a political animal.
</Quote>

The key word is *end*, in Greek *telos*: what a thing is for, and so what it is when its growth is finished. From the word "natural" one would expect the earliest and least worked-on state of a thing, the household before anyone built a city on it. Aristotle means the opposite. The nature of a horse, in this sense, is the grown horse, not the foal. So the city is natural in two senses at once. It grows out of pairings nobody chose, without a founder's plan, and it is the completed form those pairings were growing towards.

That is where the *Republic*'s answer falls short on this account. Need explains how a city begins, "originating in the bare needs of life". It does not explain what a city is, since a city continues "for the sake of a good life", and supplying wants is only the first part of living well. The answer rests on an assumption Aristotle states elsewhere in the same chapter: "nature makes nothing in vain". Things have ends, and a growth can be read by where it is going.

## Speech about the just: why man is a political animal

Granting that households grow into villages and villages into cities, there is still a gap. Bees live together, and so do cranes and herds of cattle, and nobody calls a hive a city. If the city is only the largest herd, "political" adds nothing to "social". Aristotle's answer is a capacity no other animal has:

<Quote source="Politics I.2, 1253a7–18, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Now, that man is more of a political animal than bees or any other gregarious animals is evident. Nature, as we often say, makes nothing in vain, and man is the only animal whom she has endowed with the gift of speech. And whereas mere voice is but an indication of pleasure or pain, and is therefore found in other animals (for their nature attains to the perception of pleasure and pain and the intimation of them to one another, and no further), the power of speech is intended to set forth the expedient and inexpedient, and therefore likewise the just and the unjust. And it is a characteristic of man that he alone has any sense of good and evil, of just and unjust, and the like, and the association of living beings who have this sense makes a family and a state.
</Quote>

### Voice and speech

The Greek word for speech is *logos*, which also means reason, and Aristotle uses it in both senses at once. The argument runs in three steps. Voice reports a feeling: this hurts, that pleases. Speech can say that something is better or worse for us, and once it can do that it can say that something is just or unjust, because the just is what is good for a community taken together. And a community whose members share a sense of the just is, by definition, a household or a city. So the capacity that makes human beings human can only be used in a city, the way an eye can only be used where there is light.

This community already has a name. A *polis*, in <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-polis">the first chapter</Ref>, was a self-governing body of citizens who decide their public affairs in common, and a claim placed "in the middle" of it <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="what-a-claim-in-the-middle-requires">had to win over the men around it by speech</Ref>. There the arrangement was a historical outcome, what the Greek cities built after the palaces fell. Aristotle turns it into the thing human beings are for. "Political" in "political animal" means "of the polis": an animal whose nature is finished only in a community that argues about justice.

### The whole before the part

From this he draws a stranger conclusion: the city is "by nature clearly prior to the family and to the individual". He does not mean earlier in time. He means that a part is defined by its work in the whole:

<Quote source="Politics I.2, 1253a19–29, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
if the whole body be destroyed, there will be no foot or hand, except in an equivocal sense, as we might speak of a stone hand; for when destroyed the hand will be no better than that. […] The proof that the state is a creation of nature and prior to the individual is that the individual, when isolated, is not self-sufficing; and therefore he is like a part in relation to the whole. But he who is unable to live in society, or who has no need because he is sufficient for himself, must be either a beast or a god: he is no part of a state.
</Quote>

A man outside every city is not a free individual on this account but an unfinished one, Homer's "tribeless, lawless, hearthless one". Pericles said something close of Athens alone, that the citizen who takes no part in public business is <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-citizen-who-judges">"useless"</Ref>. Aristotle says it of the species. And the city is what makes the difference between the best animal and the worst:

<Quote source="Politics I.2, 1253a31–39, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
For man, when perfected, is the best of animals, but, when separated from law and justice, he is the worst of all; since armed injustice is the more dangerous, and he is equipped at birth with arms, meant to be used by intelligence and virtue, which he may use for the worst ends. […] But justice is the bond of men in states.
</Quote>

The same passage says that "he who first founded the state was the greatest of benefactors". A city can be natural and founded at once, because "natural" names what it is for, not who started it: speech is natural too, and every child still has to be taught a language.

<KeyFact>
The slavery argument inherits this test. If a relation is natural by what it is for, a master's rule is natural only if it serves an end master and slave share (the second pairing "have the same interest"), and no history of capture, birth or sale can show a slavery natural: Aristotle has to find the natural slave in what a soul is, not in what happened to him.
</KeyFact>

## A ladder of rule from soul and body down to master and slave

A city, then, is a community of speakers who argue about justice and rule one another in turn. A household is not: in it one person commands and the others obey. If both are natural, nature must produce two kinds of rule, and Aristotle has to say what they are and how to tell them apart.

### Every composite has a ruling part

He answers by making ruling and being ruled a feature of everything made of parts:

<Quote source="Politics I.5, 1254a28–b9, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
in all things which form a composite whole and which are made up of parts, whether continuous or discrete, a distinction between the ruling and the subject element comes to light. […] But then we must look for the intentions of nature in things which retain their nature, and not in things which are corrupted. And therefore we must study the man who is in the most perfect state both of body and soul, for in him we shall see the true relation of the two; although in bad or corrupted natures the body will often appear to rule over the soul, because they are in an evil and unnatural condition. At all events we may firstly observe in living creatures both a despotical and a constitutional rule; for the soul rules the body with a despotical rule, whereas the intellect rules the appetites with a constitutional and royal rule.
</Quote>

Two things in this passage matter. The first is a distinction between kinds of rule. *Despotic* rule (from *despotes*, master of slaves) is the rule of the soul over the body: the body does not argue back, it carries out. *Constitutional* rule is rule over a part that has a voice of its own and must be persuaded, as reason must manage appetite instead of simply ordering it. *Royal* rule is a king's rule over subjects of his own kin, by affection and the authority of age; reason over appetite is both, persuasion that also has the last word. The second is a rule of evidence. Nature's intention is read in the best specimens. Where the body seems to rule the soul, the case is "corrupted" and does not count against the hierarchy. The clause lets the theory set aside any case that does not fit it; it does not say how a corrupted case is recognised.

The ladder then extends. Tame animals "are better off when they are ruled by man; for then they are preserved". "The male is by nature superior, and the female inferior; and the one rules, and the other is ruled; this principle, of necessity, extends to all mankind." The sentence that follows places the natural slave, at the point "where then there is such a difference as that between soul and body, or between men and animals".

### Three rules in one household

The household holds three relations, and the end of Book I assigns each its kind of rule. The master rules the slave despotically. The father rules his children "royally", the king's rule over kin. The husband rules his wife "constitutionally", as one citizen rules another, except that citizens take turns, while between husband and wife "the inequality is permanent". The difference is located in the soul:

<Quote source="Politics I.13, 1260a9–14, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
the freeman rules over the slave after another manner from that in which the male rules over the female, or the man over the child; although the parts of the soul are present in all of them, they are present in different degrees. For the slave has no deliberative faculty at all; the woman has, but it is without authority, and the child has, but it is immature.
</Quote>

Aristotle draws the consequence for virtue against Socrates, who in the *Republic* had made women guardians on the ground that their nature differs from men's only in degree (<Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="first-wave-women-guardians">the first wave</Ref>). The virtues of man and woman "are not, as Socrates maintained, the same; the courage of a man is shown in commanding, of a woman in obeying". The wife sits on the ladder above the slave and below the husband, and the claim is stated as a fact about nature; who may be a citizen is left to <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" />.

## The natural slave: one who apprehends reason without having it

The ladder has a rung for the natural slave. What remains is to say what a natural slave is, and why the relation could be good for him and not only for his master.

### A living instrument

Aristotle begins from what a slave does in the household. A household, like every art, needs instruments, "some are living, others lifeless": the pilot of a ship has the rudder and the look-out man. "And so, in the arrangement of the family, a slave is a living possession." Then a sentence that says what the institution is for:

<Quote source="Politics I.4, 1253b33–1254a1, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
For if every instrument could accomplish its own work, obeying or anticipating the will of others, like the statues of Daedalus, or the tripods of Hephaestus, which, says the poet, 'of their own accord entered the assembly of the Gods'; if, in like manner, the shuttle would weave and the plectrum touch the lyre without a hand to guide them, chief workmen would not want servants, nor masters slaves.
</Quote>

Daedalus was the legendary craftsman whose statues were said to move, Hephaestus the smith-god whose tripods walked. The point is that the slave's place is fixed by a job that needs doing: if tools did their own work, nobody would need slaves. Aristotle then narrows the job. A shuttle is an instrument of *production*: something else, cloth, comes out of it. A garment or a bed is only used. "Life is action and not production, and therefore the slave is the minister of action." The household slave makes nothing to be sold. He helps the master live: carries, fetches, cooks, attends.

### The definition

The definition closes the passage on the ladder:

<Quote source="Politics I.5, 1254b16–24, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Where then there is such a difference as that between soul and body, or between men and animals (as in the case of those whose business is to use their body, and who can do nothing better), the lower sort are by nature slaves, and it is better for them as for all inferiors that they should be under the rule of a master. For he who can be, and therefore is, another's, and he who participates in rational principle enough to apprehend, but not to have, such a principle, is a slave by nature. Whereas the lower animals cannot even apprehend a principle; they obey their instincts.
</Quote>

"Rational principle" is the translator's word for *logos*, the speech and reason of the previous section. The definition has three parts.

1. **Capacity.** The natural slave is someone "whose business is to use their body, and who can do nothing better". He is defined by what he can do, not by how he came to be owned.
2. **Reason heard, not had.** He can follow a reason when it is given to him, understand an order and why it is given, but cannot produce one: he cannot deliberate for himself. That is what puts him above the tame animal, which "cannot even apprehend", and below the free man. In the ladder's terms, his deliberative faculty is not weak, as a child's is, but absent.
3. **Benefit.** Because he cannot plan, he is better off planned for: "it is better for them as for all inferiors that they should be under the rule of a master." The master supplies the foresight he lacks, as the soul supplies it to the body.

"He who can be, and therefore is, another's" reads at first like a circle. It means that his nature fits him to be part of another person's life, the way a hand is part of a body, and so he already is such a part before any sale makes it official.

### Friends with a common interest

Read at full strength, the theory makes mastery the household's version of soul ruling body, done for the good of both. Aristotle says so at the end of the slavery chapters:

<Quote source="Politics I.6, 1255b9–15, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
The abuse of this authority is injurious to both; for the interests of part and whole, of body and soul, are the same, and the slave is a part of the master, a living but separated part of his bodily frame. Hence, where the relation of master and slave between them is natural they are friends and have a common interest, but where it rests merely on law and force the reverse is true.
</Quote>

The theory carries its own test. If a relation of master and slave is natural, it is a kind of friendship, with an interest the two share. If it rests on force, it is "the reverse": the slave is held against his interest, and the relation is the very thing the conventionalist described. The test rests on an assumption, that a human soul can lack its ruling part entirely and still be human. If that is possible, the conventionalist loses his strongest case. Mastery can be natural, and if even mastery can be natural, rule in general need not be force.

## Slavery by law: the objection Aristotle reports and accepts

The theory has to meet the objection it was built against. Aristotle states it at the opening of the slavery discussion, in the words of its holders:

<Quote source="Politics I.3, 1253b20–23, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Others affirm that the rule of a master over slaves is contrary to nature, and that the distinction between slave and freeman exists by law only, and not by nature; and being an interference with nature is therefore unjust.
</Quote>

### What the objectors say

In I.6 he gives the objection its full force. Slavery in the Greek cities rested on "a sort of convention, the law by which whatever is taken in war is supposed to belong to the victors". And "this right many jurists impeach, as they would an orator who brought forward an unconstitutional measure: they detest the notion that, because one man has the power of doing violence and is superior in brute strength, another shall be his slave and subject." Even philosophers, he adds, disagree.

He then diagnoses the dispute. One party identifies justice with goodwill between people, and goodwill cannot exist between a master and a slave taken by force. The other identifies justice "with the mere rule of the stronger". The reader has heard the second party speak: Thrasymachus said justice is <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="thrasymachus-the-law-is-what-the-rulers-interest-says-it-is">the interest of the stronger</Ref>, and the envoys at Melos said the strong do what they can. A third group tries to stand between them, defending slavery by capture because law and custom are "a sort of justice". Aristotle shows that this middle position contradicts itself.

<Example title="the objection run step by step">
**Premise of the defenders.** What is taken in war belongs to the victor; a captive sold under that custom is a slave justly, because the custom is law and law is a kind of justice.

**Step 1.** "What if the cause of the war be unjust?" A city that attacks without right takes captives by the same custom. If the custom makes every capture just, it makes injustice just, and nobody holds that.

**Step 2.** "No one would ever say that he is a slave who is unworthy to be a slave." The defenders themselves would not call a captured nobleman a slave in the full sense.

**Step 3.** "Were this the case, men of the highest rank would be slaves and the children of slaves if they or their parents chance to have been taken captive and sold." Capture falls on whoever loses a battle, the best men included. A rule that makes slaves of whoever is unlucky picks out no kind of person at all.

**Conclusion.** Capture in war cannot by itself make anyone a slave justly. Aristotle accepts every step: slavery "by law" is a convention, and victory proves nothing about the soul of the captive.
</Example>

One case the reader has already met fits each step. At Melos, a Greek island that had wanted to stay neutral, the Athenians <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-end-of-melos">killed the grown men and sold the women and children</Ref> after a war whose justice they did not even claim. By I.6 the Melians sold in 416 are the plain case of slavery "merely by law and force".

### Aristotle's answer: "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere"

Having conceded the objection, Aristotle says it does not reach what he means. Even the defenders of slavery by capture, he points out, stop short of their own rule:

<Quote source="Politics I.6, 1255a28–32, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Wherefore Hellenes do not like to call Hellenes slaves, but confine the term to barbarians. Yet, in using this language, they really mean the natural slave of whom we spoke at first; for it must be admitted that some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere.
</Quote>

Greeks who refuse to call a captured Greek a slave are admitting that slavery depends on what a person is, not on what happened to him. So both sides of the dispute, on this reading, are really arguing about natural slavery. The legal kind is just exactly where it happens to coincide with the natural kind, and unjust where it does not. That is the theory's answer to the objection: it agrees with the objectors about the institution and disagrees with them about nature.

## Where the theory fails on its own terms: no mark, no sign, no test

From a theory that says "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" one would expect the next step to be the mark: the sign by which the natural slave is recognised, so that the legal kind can be checked against the natural one. The text gives the opposite. At each place a mark could be looked for, Aristotle himself says it is not there.

### Four places a mark could be

**The body.** Nature, he writes, "would like to distinguish between the bodies of freemen and slaves, making the one strong for servile labour, the other upright, and although useless for such services, useful for political life". Then: "But the opposite often happens, that some have the souls and others have the bodies of freemen."

**The soul.** If men differed in body as much as the statues of the gods differ from men, "all would acknowledge that the inferior class should be slaves of the superior". The same should hold of souls, "how much more just that a similar distinction should exist in the soul? but the beauty of the body is seen, whereas the beauty of the soul is not seen." The one feature that decides the question, whether a soul has its ruling part, is the one feature nobody can look at.

**Capture.** The worked example above: war takes the noble and the base alike.

**Birth.** Greeks think good birth is the mark. Aristotle quotes Helen in a play by Theodectes, a tragic poet of his own day: "Who would presume to call me servant who am on both sides sprung from the stem of the Gods?" They think that "as men and animals beget men and animals, so from good men a good man springs. But this is what nature, though she may intend it, cannot always accomplish."

### What follows from the concessions

Aristotle's own conclusion is careful: "We see then that there is some foundation for this difference of opinion, and that all are not either slaves by nature or freemen by nature, and also that there is in some cases a marked distinction between the two classes, rendering it expedient and right for the one to be slaves and the others to be masters." The theory survives as a claim that natural slaves exist. What it loses is any way to find them.

Aristotle does not draw the next three consequences. His premises do, and each can be checked against the sentences above.

1. **No procedure.** No city could sort its slaves into natural and legal ones, since body, soul, capture and birth all fail as signs.
2. **Every actual slave is a legal slave.** Each was made a slave by capture, birth or sale, the routes I.6 calls convention. Whether any one of them is a slave by nature is unknown.
3. **The master may be the slavish one.** "Some have the souls and others have the bodies of freemen" cuts both ways. A master with the servile soul and a slave with the free one is, by Aristotle's own test, a relation resting "merely on law and force", and so "the reverse" of friendship.

The claim that ancient thinkers could not conceive of the injustice of slavery fails on these chapters: Aristotle reports the case against it in its holders' words and concedes most of it (<Ref lesson="strauss-natural-right-and-history" />). Whether natural slavery gave Spain a title to rule the peoples of the Americas was argued from these same chapters in the sixteenth century (<Ref lesson="vitoria-right-to-the-americas" />).

<Aside>
**Aristotle's own household.** His will, which survives, forbids the sale of "the servants who waited upon me", frees some of them, and hands others on as gifts. It records what one man did with the slaves he owned, not what he thought natural slavery was.
</Aside>

## Greek and barbarian: the climate theory as the missing mark

If no individual soul can be read, perhaps peoples can. Aristotle does assign slavishness to whole peoples, and not only by quoting a poet. In Book III, explaining why kingships among barbarians last although their kings hold power as tyrants do, he gives the reason in his own voice:

<Quote source="Politics III.14, 1285a19–24, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
For barbarians, being more servile in character than Hellenes, and Asiatics than Europeans, do not rebel against a despotic government. Such royalties have the nature of tyrannies because the people are by nature slaves; but there is no danger of their being overthrown, for they are hereditary and legal.
</Quote>

In the book where he designs his best city, he says where the difference comes from:

<Quote source="Politics VII.7, 1327b23–33, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Those who live in a cold climate and in Europe are full of spirit, but wanting in intelligence and skill; and therefore they retain comparative freedom, but have no political organization, and are incapable of ruling over others. Whereas the natives of Asia are intelligent and inventive, but they are wanting in spirit, and therefore they are always in a state of subjection and slavery. But the Hellenic race, which is situated between them, is likewise intermediate in character, being high-spirited and also intelligent. Hence it continues free, and is the best-governed of any nation, and, if it could be formed into one state, would be able to rule the world.
</Quote>

"Europe" here means the cold lands north and west of the Greek world, "Asia" the lands east of the Aegean, the Persian empire above all. The passage sorts peoples by two qualities: *spirit*, the high temper that will not submit, and intelligence. Only a people with both can be free and rule.

III.14 calls a whole people "by nature slaves", the phrase Book I used for the individual. Add the Euripides line and I.8's "naturally just" war against "men who, though intended by nature to be governed, will not submit", and the climate theory looks like the mark I.6 could not find. Whether Aristotle meant it as one is disputed, and each answer has the text on its side.

### Two readings: a mark by peoples, or a tendency

**A mark by peoples.** On the first reading, Books III and VII supply what Book I lacked: a soul cannot be seen, but the people it belongs to can, and climate shapes whole peoples. Greek practice, which enslaved mostly barbarians, would then be natural slavery on the whole, and the cases I.6 admits, the captured Greek noble or the slavish Greek, errors at the edge of a sorting that is broadly right.

**A tendency, not a mark.** On the second reading, the claims about peoples state tendencies. Their grammar is comparative: barbarians are "more servile in character than Hellenes", Asiatics more than Europeans. VII.7 goes on to find the same spread inside Greece: "there are also similar differences in the different tribes of Hellas", some "of a one-sided nature". And I.6 had said that birth does not reliably pass a soul on (good from good "is what nature, though she may intend it, cannot always accomplish"), so a people, being many births, will hold souls of both kinds. The classification had been questioned in the very *Statesman* whose account of rule Aristotle rejected:

<Quote source="Plato, Statesman 262d–e, trans. B. Jowett">
here they cut off the Hellenes as one species, and all the other species of mankind, which are innumerable, and have no ties or common language, they include under the single name of 'barbarians,' and because they have one name they are supposed to be of one species also.
</Quote>

Both readings grant that Aristotle thought barbarians, Asians above all, more slavish by nature than Greeks, and that a people's label cannot say whether a given man has the deliberative part. They part over what an error costs: for the first, a sorting right on the whole is the most any practice gets; for the second, every man the label gets wrong is held, on I.6's terms, by "law and force".

### What the best city does with it

The best city of Book VII shows the theory in use. Its farmland is to be worked by slaves, and Aristotle says which slaves:

<Quote source="Politics VII.10, 1330a25–30, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
The very best thing of all would be that the husbandmen should be slaves taken from among men who are not all of the same race and not spirited, for if they have no spirit they will be better suited for their work, and there will be no danger of their making a revolution. The next best thing would be that they should be perioeci of foreign race, and of a like inferior nature.
</Quote>

The *perioeci*, "dwellers-around", were the free but voteless subject towns of a territory like Sparta's (<Ref lesson="sparta-as-test-case" />). "Of a like inferior nature" speaks the language of the first reading: the farmers are to be naturally inferior, and the foreign perioeci as inferior as the slaves. The reasons attached to the first choice are of another kind, a planner's: men of mixed origin cannot combine, and men without spirit will not revolt. Those reasons hold whether or not the farmers are slaves by nature.

The next sentence promises to explain "why it is expedient that liberty should be always held out to them as the reward of their services". A natural slave, on the definition, is better off ruled, so freedom would do him no good. Either a reward need only be wanted, or liberty is meant for those slaves who turn out able to use it; the second answer concedes what the second reading claims, that the city chose its slaves by people and temper and must still find out, man by man, which were slaves by nature. Who may be a citizen of the best city is the question of <Ref lesson="aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life" />.

## Getting without limit: chrematistics, the art of money-making

The household has one more part, which Aristotle listed at the start of I.3: "the so-called art of getting wealth, which, according to some, is identical with household management". The Greek name for it is *chrematistics*, from *chremata*, money or goods. If those who equate it with running a household were right, a household would exist to accumulate. The same reasoning that ranked souls by their ends now ranks ways of acquiring, and it finds one that has no end at all.

### Two uses of a shoe

<Quote source="Politics I.9, 1257a6–13, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Of everything which we possess there are two uses: both belong to the thing as such, but not in the same manner, for one is the proper, and the other the improper or secondary use of it. For example, a shoe is used for wear, and is used for exchange; both are uses of the shoe. He who gives a shoe in exchange for money or food to him who wants one, does indeed use the shoe as a shoe, but this is not its proper or primary purpose, for a shoe is not made to be an object of barter.
</Quote>

From there Aristotle tells a short history. Exchange "arises at first from what is natural, from the circumstance that some have too little, others too much", and barter, "giving and receiving wine, for example, in exchange for corn", is "not contrary to nature" because it stops when each side has what it needs. As trade reached between countries, goods became hard to carry, so men agreed on a metal, iron or silver, whose value "was at first measured simply by size and weight, but in process of time they put a stamp upon it, to save the trouble of weighing and to mark the value". Coin then made possible a new art, retail trade, which buys in order to sell dearer, and whose aim is the coin itself.

### Where the limit goes

"But how can that be wealth of which a man may have a great abundance and yet perish with hunger, like Midas in the fable, whose insatiable prayer turned everything that was set before him into gold?" Wealth in the household's sense is a set of instruments for living, and like the instruments of every art it has a bound: the amount of property "needed for a good life is not unlimited". Retail trade has none. Aristotle explains why with a comparison to medicine:

<Quote source="Politics I.9, 1257b25–30, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
As in the art of medicine there is no limit to the pursuit of health, and as in the other arts there is no limit to the pursuit of their several ends, for they aim at accomplishing their ends to the uttermost (but of the means there is a limit, for the end is always the limit), so, too, in this art of wealth-getting there is no limit of the end, which is riches of the spurious kind, and the acquisition of wealth.
</Quote>

A doctor pursues health without limit, but he uses only as many drugs as health requires; the end sets the measure of the means. When money, a means, is made the end, nothing is left to set its measure. The pursuit becomes as unlimited as the doctor's pursuit of health, and everything else becomes its means. Some men "turn every quality or art into a means of getting wealth", so that courage, generalship and medicine, whose ends are confidence, victory and health, are practised for money.

<Figure
  src="./figures/means-ends.svg"
  alt="Two panels under the heading 'the end limits the means'. Left, plain: medicine; end: health, pursued without limit; means: drugs, diet, surgery; 'as much as health needs: the end is the limit'. Right, amber: money-making; end: money, pursued without limit; means: every art, courage, generalship, medicine; 'no end beyond money to stop them: unlimited'."
  caption="In every art the end is unlimited and the means are bounded by it; money-making makes a means the end, so the bound disappears."
/>

The origin of the confusion, Aristotle says, is a mistake about the end of life: men "are intent upon living only, and not upon living well; and, as their desires are unlimited, they also desire that the means of gratifying them should be without limit". That is the household's version of the distinction the city rested on, "the bare needs of life" against "a good life". From here on, *chrematistics* means this unlimited kind, as against the bounded acquisition that belongs to running a household; Aristotle's own use of the word is looser and covers both.

### Money born of money

Retail at least moves goods. The last step does not:

<Quote source="Politics I.10, 1258b2–8, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
The most hated sort, and with the greatest reason, is usury, which makes a gain out of money itself, and not from the natural object of it. For money was intended to be used in exchange, but not to increase at interest. And this term interest, which means the birth of money from money, is applied to the breeding of money because the offspring resembles the parent. Wherefore of all modes of getting wealth this is the most unnatural.
</Quote>

The Greek word for interest, *tokos*, is the ordinary word for a child or a calf. A coin was made to be a measure between goods, and lending it at interest makes it breed as if it were a living thing. That is the sense of "against nature" here: the coin is used for an end it was not made for, and since that end is more coin, nothing in it sets a limit. The argument against usury passes into Christian law (<Ref lesson="aquinas-law-and-kingship" />), and the claim that acquisition has a natural limit is what <Ref lesson="locke-property-and-consent" /> later sets out to remove.

<Example title="Thales and the olive presses">
Aristotle tells one case of chrematistics about Thales, the man of Miletus, a Greek city on the coast of Asia Minor, who <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-continuity-reading">held that everything came from water</Ref>.

**The setting.** Thales was mocked for his poverty, as proof that philosophy was useless.

**The move.** Reading the stars in winter, he foresaw a large olive harvest. With a little money he paid deposits on every olive press in Miletus and on the nearby island of Chios, cheaply, "because no one bid against him".

**The result.** At harvest, when everyone needed presses at once, he let them out "at any rate which he pleased, and made a quantity of money".

**What Aristotle draws from it.** The trick "is of universal application, and is nothing but the creation of a monopoly", and cities use it when they need money. Dionysius, tyrant of Syracuse around 400 BC, banished a man who had cornered Sicily's iron the same way, as one who had found a way of making money "injurious to his own interests".

**The bearing.** The story shows that the unlimited art is a real skill a wise man can master; Thales, says the story, proved that "philosophers can easily be rich if they like, but that their ambition is of another sort". The art is not condemned for being difficult or stupid. It is placed below the art of living well, as a means that has to be kept to its end.
</Example>

## Some may lead free men; none may own them

### Two kinds of rule

The theory was built to support one claim: some men are slaves by nature, so mastery over them is just. Aristotle's own vocabulary takes the claim apart. The household's ladder already set *despotic* rule beside *constitutional* rule (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="three-rules-in-one-household">three rules in one household</Ref>), and he insists they differ in kind: "the rule of a master is not a constitutional rule", for "there is one rule exercised over subjects who are by nature free, another over subjects who are by nature slaves", and "constitutional rule is a government of freemen and equals" (I.7). In such a government the citizens "rule and are ruled by turns" (I.12); a regime run for its rulers' interest is "despotic, whereas a state is a community of freemen" (III.6).

With that line drawn, "some men are slaves by nature" turns out to run four claims together: that people differ in the capacity to lead, partly by birth; that the more capable should rule; that whoever actually rules is the more capable; and that some men are fit to be owned. The first three are about *political* rule, office among the free and equal. Only the fourth is about despotic rule, and the four come out differently.

### People differ in the capacity to lead, partly by birth

The first claim is one modern evidence can test. Studies of twins compare identical pairs, who share all their genes, with fraternal pairs, who share about half; where identical twins resemble each other more, the extra likeness is put down to genes. Among about two hundred pairs of adult male twins, genetic differences accounted for about 30% of the variation in whether a man had held leadership roles, and among about two hundred female pairs for 32%; the family the twins grew up in together accounted for almost none. Reviews pooling some seventy samples find that personality predicts who leads, with correlations of .31 for extraversion and .28 for conscientiousness, and .48 for the five broad traits together. Intelligence, across some hundred and fifty samples, correlates .21 to .27 with leadership, "considerably lower than previously thought".

Three limits come with the numbers. The twin studies measured holding a leadership role, as the twins reported it, not leading well. Heritability describes variation in a population (<Ref lesson="the-cognitive-elite" />): differences among people are partly genetic, which does not make any one person's capacity fixed at birth. And correlations near .3 leave most of the variation unexplained.

>
People differ in the capacity to lead, and the differences are partly inherited; what the evidence measures is holding a leadership role more than leading well.
</Verdict>

### The more capable should rule: common ground

The second claim divides nobody in this course. The *Republic* rests on it: cities "will never have rest from their evils" until philosophers rule (<Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="third-wave-philosophers-must-rule">the third wave</Ref>). Aristotle's aristocracy is rule by "the best men", and offices given by merit are the aristocratic part of his mixed regime (<Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" />); the theorists of ruling elites and the defenders of merit hold it later. The quarrel is elsewhere: over who the capable are, how they are found, and what rule over others their capacity gives them.

### Whoever rules is not, as a rule, the most capable

The third claim would make the second automatic: if rule always fell to the most capable, whoever holds it would hold it by right. The course's own authors deny it. The demand that philosophers rule presupposes that the cities as they are are not ruled by the wise. Aristotle's deviant regimes are rule by one, few or many in their own interest. After Pericles, the historian of the war writes, his successors, "each grasping at supremacy", handed affairs "to the whims of the multitude", which "produced a host of blunders"; and the man who carried the death decree against Mytilene was "the most violent man at Athens, and at that time by far the most powerful with the commons" (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="cleon-punishment-is-both-just-and-useful">Cleon</Ref>). Inheritance does no better: when the philosopher emperor Marcus Aurelius was succeeded by his son Commodus, a Roman historian of the time wrote that "our history now descends from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust".

Where office is contested and its holders can be removed, the claim fares better. Sparta's ephors, chosen each year from the whole people, were part of the mixture that kept that city stable (<Ref lesson="sparta-as-test-case" />), and a ruling group that takes in the able from below proves the hardest to overthrow (<Ref lesson="michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites" />).

<Verdict
  grade="on balance"
  decidedBy="cases from Athens, Rome and the deviant regimes, illustrations rather than a count."
  overturnedBy="closed, hereditary or co-opting rulers who kept their capacity across generations."
>
That whoever rules is the most capable fails as a general rule; it holds, so far as the cases show, where office is contested and its holders can be removed.
</Verdict>

### Mastery by nature fails

The fourth claim is the one Book I argues: that some men may be owned, that despotic rule over them is just. The case against it is the three consequences already drawn from Aristotle's concessions (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="what-follows-from-the-concessions">what follows from the concessions</Ref>): no procedure, every actual slave a legal one, and a master who may be the slavish one.

**The strongest objection.** A defender has a serious reply in two parts. First, a justification need not come with a test. "Some men are slaves by nature" can be true when nobody can say which men, as "some of these witnesses are lying" can be true when nobody knows which ones; failing to apply a claim is a problem of knowledge, not a refutation. Second, Aristotle did offer something close to a test: III.14 calls whole peoples "by nature slaves", and VII.7 says why. A sorting by peoples that is right on the whole is the most any practice could have, and Greek practice, which enslaved mostly barbarians, claimed no more.

**Why it does not save the claim.** The first part is true of a different claim. "Natural slaves exist" survives the missing test. The claim under judgment is that *mastery* over them is just, and I.6 makes the justice of mastery depend on the pair: where this master and this slave are matched by nature they are friends, and where they are not, the relation is law and force. A right that depends on the pair, and that nobody can show he holds over any given man, licenses no act of mastery.

The second part has already been weighed: a people holds souls of both kinds (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="two-readings-a-mark-by-peoples-or-a-tendency">the two readings</Ref>), and the best city chooses its farmers for reasons that hold whether or not any of them is a slave by nature (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="what-the-best-city-does-with-it">the best city</Ref>). What decides between the two readings here is not what Aristotle meant but what a justification of mastery needs, and for that the second reading is right, whichever of them he held.

  grade="established"
  decidedBy="Aristotle's own concessions in I.5–6: the failure is internal and textual, every deciding sentence is in the Politics, and no later standard of justice is needed."
  overturnedBy="a criterion, stated in advance, that separates natural slaves from free men and reduces neither to whoever was captured nor to whoever was born where."
>
That some men are by nature fit to be owned, so that mastery over them is just, fails: no mark picks out a natural slave, so the argument justifies no actual slavery.
</Verdict>

### Freedom among equals, slavery for outsiders

One defence of the Greeks remains, and it deserves its full strength. It says they were consistent: political rule among those who share a language, laws and gods, mastery over those who do not. Aristotle states the line himself. Among barbarians "there is no natural ruler"; the poets say Hellenes should rule them, "as if they thought that the barbarian and the slave were by nature one" (I.2); "Hellenes do not like to call Hellenes slaves, but confine the term to barbarians" (I.6); barbarians are "more servile in character than Hellenes" (III.14). On this view equality is a relation among members, not a property of every human being. A people owes political rule to its own and nothing comparable to outsiders who share neither its language nor its ways, and ruling them may even do them good, as I.5 says rule benefits those fit for it.

Four answers meet it. First, the line was not held. Sparta lived on the labour of Greeks it had conquered (<Ref lesson="sparta-as-test-case" id="theory-two-the-helots-the-case-natural-slavery-never-classifies">the helots</Ref>); Athens sold the women and children of Greek Melos (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-end-of-melos">the end of Melos</Ref>); and the *Republic* has to legislate against the practice, asking "Do you think it right that Hellenes should enslave Hellenic States?" and ruling that "no Hellene should be owned by them as a slave". A rule already kept would need no law.

Second, Aristotle concedes that the line does not track nature: "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" is said of persons, VII.7 finds "similar differences in the different tribes of Hellas", and a people, being many births, holds souls of both kinds. On his own terms the line is drawn by birthplace, the criterion the verdict above already excludes.

Third, the principle cuts both ways. If enslaving outsiders is no great wrong, it was none when Rome did it to the ancestors of Europeans: at one captured town in Gaul the buyers counted 53,000 people sold, and an ancient biographer gives a round million prisoners for the whole war. The English word *slave* comes, on the traditional derivation, from *Slav*, because so many Slavs were enslaved in the Middle Ages. And corsairs from the Barbary coast of North Africa took, on one historian's estimate, between one million and 1.25 million European Christians into slavery between 1530 and 1780; the figure is extrapolated from how many captives it would take to keep the slave population level, and others think it too high. A principle that licenses all of these licenses whoever is stronger. It is the thesis the Athenians put to the Melians (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-thesis">the thesis</Ref>), not a principle of freedom.

Fourth, "it does them good": I.6 itself says that where master and slave are not matched by nature the relation is "the reverse" of a common interest, and what enslavement did to the enslaved is weighed in <Ref lesson="auditing-the-case-for-colonialism" />.

  grade="established"
  decidedBy="the helots, Melos and the Republic's law against enslaving Greeks, all in the course's own primary texts."
  overturnedBy="evidence that these cases were exceptions the Greeks themselves condemned as breaches of a rule otherwise kept."
>
The historical premise of the defence fails: the Greeks did not keep the line between Greek and barbarian, and enslaved Greeks.
</Verdict>

<Verdict
  grade="strong"
  decidedBy="Aristotle's concession that the line does not track nature, and the principle's application to Gaul, the Slavs and the Barbary captives."
  overturnedBy="a ground for the line that is neither birthplace nor strength and that Aristotle's own sorting of souls does not undo."
>
As a justification of mastery, freedom among equals and slavery for outsiders fails: it reduces to whoever was born where, which Aristotle says does not track the nature his justification needs, and applied consistently it licenses the enslavement of those who hold it.
</Verdict>

<Conclusion>
You can now reconstruct the chain from household to village to city and say why Aristotle calls the city natural: it grows from pairings nobody chose and is the end they grow towards, a community of animals whose speech argues about the just. You can set out the ladder of rule, state the natural slave's definition in its three parts (bodily work, reason heard but not had, benefit from being ruled), and show where Aristotle's own text removes every mark by which a natural slave could be found. The claim that some are slaves by nature, taken apart along Aristotle's own line between political and despotic rule, comes out four ways. People differ in the capacity to lead, partly by birth, and the evidence for it is strong. That the most capable should rule is common ground. That whoever rules is the most capable fails as a general rule and holds where office is contested and removable, a finding the cases support on balance. That some men may be owned fails on Aristotle's own concessions, and that is established. The attempt to save it by drawing the line at outsiders fails too: that the Greeks did not keep the line is established, and that the principle, kept, licenses the enslavement of those who hold it is strong. The same reasoning by ends ranks acquisition: wealth is bounded by living well, and money made into an end has no bound.

The household is a monarchy, where one rules by nature. The claim that rule can be read off nature has failed at the household's lowest rung, where Aristotle argued it hardest. Among free men capacity differs but gives no title to mastery, so who rules among them, by what claim to office and how removably, the many, the rich, the good, the one best man or the law, is the question of <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" />.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Without looking back, reconstruct the chain household → village → city, and say at each step what the association is formed for. Then state the *Republic*'s starting point for the city in one sentence (<Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="need-makes-the-first-city-differences-of-nature-divide-its-labour">the first city</Ref>), and name the phrase in Politics I.2 that answers it directly.

<Solution>
**The chain.** The household, formed by the two natural pairings (male and female, natural ruler and subject), is "for the supply of men's everyday wants". The village, several households joined, typically a colony of one family under its eldest, aims at "something more than the supply of daily needs". The city, several villages joined into a community "nearly or quite self-sufficing", originates "in the bare needs of life" and continues "for the sake of a good life".

**The *Republic*'s starting point.** "A State arises out of the needs of mankind; no one is self-sufficing": people gather and divide their labour to supply their wants.

**The reply.** "The nature of a thing is its end", with "continuing in existence for the sake of a good life". Aristotle grants that need explains how the city originates, and denies that it says what the city is. Need is the beginning; the end, living well, is the nature. A city that only supplied wants would be, on his account, a village grown large.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Apply the sourcing questions from <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="solon-at-athens">the first chapter</Ref> to Politics I.6 (the jurists who "detest the notion that, because one man has the power of doing violence … another shall be his slave"; "what if the cause of the war be unjust?"; "Hellenes do not like to call Hellenes slaves"). Who wrote it, when, for whom and why; what it records, what it argues, what it omits; and what it can be used to establish about Greek opinion in the fourth century BC. Then say what the passage does to the claim that antiquity "could not conceive" of the injustice of slavery.

<Solution>
**Who, when, for whom.** Aristotle, a resident foreigner at Athens who owned slaves, in lecture notes for his school, written between the 330s and his death in 322. The audience was students of philosophy, not an assembly; the purpose was to work out what a household and a city are.

**What it records.** That in his day jurists attacked slavery by capture as they would attack an unconstitutional law; that philosophers were divided; that some identified justice with goodwill and others with the rule of the stronger; that Greeks were reluctant to call captured Greeks slaves.

**What it argues.** That slavery by capture is a convention and cannot by itself be just, since wars can be unjust and captives can be noble; that the dispute is really about natural slavery; and that natural slaves exist, though not every slave is one.

**What it omits.** Any procedure for telling a natural slave from a legal one; the slaves' own view; any figure for how many slaves were captives, born slaves or bought.

**What it can establish.** That the injustice of slavery by force was argued openly in fourth-century Greece by more than one group, since Aristotle reports it as a known position he must answer. It cannot establish how widespread the view was, or whether anyone acted on it.

**The bearing.** A writer who states the case against slavery in its holders' words, and concedes most of it, conceived of its injustice. The passage refutes the "could not conceive" claim for Aristotle at least; why the institution stood anyway it answers in part, since natural slavery gave its defenders a ground that did not depend on capture. The claim returns in <Ref lesson="strauss-natural-right-and-history" />.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "Aristotle calls usury unnatural only because it exploits the poor." Then, as the Thales example did, place each case below on his account: natural acquisition or the unlimited art, and what end, if any, sets its limit.

(a) A farmer with more wine than he can drink trades it for a neighbour's corn.
(b) A dealer buys shoes in one town to sell them dearer in the next.
(c) A man of modest means lends money at interest to a rich shipowner, who fits out a voyage with it and comes home richer.
(d) A city short of money learns before anyone else that a famine is coming, buys up the grain in its territory and sells it at its own price.

<Solution>
**The refutation.** The claim makes the fault depend on who is harmed. Aristotle's reason does not mention the borrower: "money was intended to be used in exchange, but not to increase at interest". The fault is in the use. A coin, made as a measure between goods, is made to breed, *tokos*, "the birth of money from money", and since the end of the lending is more coin, nothing sets a limit to it. That holds whether the borrower is ruined or enriched, so case (c) is the test: nobody is exploited, and the loan is still, on his account, "of all modes of getting wealth … the most unnatural".

**(a) Natural.** Barter "arises at first from what is natural, from the circumstance that some have too little, others too much". Each side wants a thing for use, and the trade stops when each has enough. The end, living, is the limit.

**(b) The unlimited art.** The dealer buys in order to sell dearer; his aim is the coin. Aristotle says retail trade "is not a natural part of the art of getting wealth; had it been so, men would have ceased to exchange when they had enough". The shoe is used for its secondary use, exchange, and nothing in the aim stops it.

**(c) The unlimited art in its extreme form.** Usury, as above. Retail at least moves goods; here the coin itself is made to increase.

**(d) The unlimited art, put to a bounded end.** In form this is Thales' move: a monopoly made by foreseeing a demand, "of universal application", and Aristotle reports that cities use it "when they are in want of money". The gain is money from a market cornered, so it belongs to the unlimited art; what can bound it is the city's purpose. If it raises what the city needs and stops, the end sets the measure, as health sets the measure of drugs; if it keeps the price high for the gain itself, nothing does. The skill is real, and placed below living well, not condemned as stupid.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Set three texts side by side. (A) Politics I.2: "he who is unable to live in society, or who has no need because he is sufficient for himself, must be either a beast or a god". (B) The account of the polis in <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-polis">the first chapter</Ref>: argument about rule as the by-product of an arrangement the Greek cities built after the palaces fell, power placed "in the middle" of a body of equals. (C) Pericles in the Funeral Oration: "we regard him who takes no part in these duties not as unambitious but as useless". Where do they agree, where do they differ, and what follows: is the political animal a fact about humans or about Greeks after about 800 BC?

<Solution>
**Agreement.** All three tie speech and the city together, and all three treat the man outside public life as less than a full man: "a beast or a god", "useless", and in B the palace subject who had no forum in which to ask who should rule.

**Difference.** A states a fact of nature, true of the species. B states a historical outcome: the same capacities existed in the palace kingdoms, but there was no arrangement in which to use them. C is a boast about one city, made to mourners in wartime.

**What follows: two defensible positions.**
- *A fact about Greeks.* The community Aristotle calls natural appeared late and in one place; speech is universal, the city of speech about the just is not. "Political animal" then generalises one arrangement into a human essence, as Pericles generalised Athens.
- *A fact about humans.* "Natural" means the end, not the usual state. That most peoples never built a polis no more refutes the claim than rare health refutes that health is natural to the body; B gives the conditions under which the end was first reached.

A good answer notices that the second position needs the rule of evidence from I.5 (read nature in the best specimens) and so inherits its weakness: it must say, independently, which cases count as the best.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
The chapter judged that mastery by nature fails, graded the verdict established, and said it would be overturned by a criterion, stated in advance, that separates natural slaves from free men. Attack it from a sentence the chapter left open. VII.10 promises to explain "why it is expedient that liberty should be always held out to them as the reward of their services". A defender reads this as the missing test: enslave by people and temper, then watch each slave, free those who show they can plan for themselves, and keep the rest, who have now shown themselves slaves by nature. Does this test meet the verdict's condition? Say why the verdict needs the condition "stated in advance" at all, and what evidence or argument would then be needed to overturn the verdict.

<Solution>
**What the attack gets right.** It is a test of individuals, not of peoples, so the objection from the two readings does not touch it. And it answers "the beauty of the soul is not seen" in a plausible way: a soul that cannot be looked at may still show itself in conduct over years. If the theory has a procedure anywhere in the *Politics*, this is the best candidate.

**Why it does not meet the condition.** The test runs after the enslavement. Until it has been run, every slave is held without anyone knowing whether he has the deliberative part, and for each one who has it, I.6 says what the relation is while it lasts: it "rests merely on law and force", the reverse of friendship. A man freed after ten years was held by force for ten years. The test can at most show, afterwards, which masteries were never just; it cannot make any mastery just while it is going on.

**Why the verdict needs "stated in advance".** Because I.6 makes justice a property of the relation between this master and this slave, not of how things turn out. A justification of mastery has to license holding a man now, and only a criterion that can be applied before the holding begins can do that. A test that only vindicates afterwards licenses the force in the meantime, which is the thing the theory was built to deny.

**Two further weaknesses.** What earns liberty is "their services", obedience well done, which a natural slave also gives; and the judge is the master, who may be the slavish one. VII.10 calls the reward "expedient": an incentive to work, not a measure of souls.

**What would overturn the verdict.** Either a criterion applicable before anyone is enslaved that reduces neither to "whoever was captured" nor to "whoever was born where", or an argument that holding a man who can deliberate, until a later test frees him, is not force. The second would have to give up I.6's test of the pair, and with it Aristotle's own account of what makes mastery just. Neither would need to deny that people differ in ability, since the verdict never did.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A defender says the Greeks were consistent: freedom among equals, slavery for outsiders, so that the fate of the Melians was a lapse and Aristotle's theory needs no mark beyond where a man was born. State the view in its best form from I.2 and I.6. Then test it against three facts and one symmetry, using what <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-end-of-melos">the chapter on Melos</Ref> recorded, and give the grade each half of the defence earns.

<Solution>
**The view at full strength.** Among barbarians "there is no natural ruler"; the poets say Hellenes should rule them, "as if they thought that the barbarian and the slave were by nature one" (I.2); Greeks "do not like to call Hellenes slaves, but confine the term to barbarians" (I.6). Equality is a relation among those who share language, laws and gods; outsiders are owed nothing comparable, and rule may even do them good (I.5).

**Three facts.** The helots were Greeks held by Sparta. At Melos in 416 Athens killed the grown men and sold the women and children of a Greek island, after a war whose justice it did not even claim, which is also the case I.6's "what if the cause of the war be unjust?" condemns. The *Republic* has to rule that "no Hellene should be owned by them as a slave", which a kept rule would not need.

**The concession.** "Some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere", and VII.7's "similar differences in the different tribes of Hellas": on Aristotle's own terms birthplace does not track the soul, so even a Greek captive is no evidence of a free soul, nor a barbarian of a slavish one.

**The symmetry.** Applied consistently, the principle licenses Rome's sales in Gaul (53,000 at one town), the enslavement of Slavs, and the Barbary captives, on one disputed estimate a million or more. It licenses whoever is stronger, which is the Athenian thesis at Melos.

**Grades.** The historical premise, that the Greeks kept the line, fails, established: the counter-cases are in the course's own primary texts. The principle, as a justification of mastery, fails, strong: it reduces to whoever was born where, which Aristotle himself says does not track nature, and it licenses the enslavement of those who hold it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The chapter judged, on balance, that whoever rules is not, as a rule, the most capable. Attack the verdict. State at full strength the view that rulers on the whole are the most capable, because those who reach power and keep it have been selected by success. Then say what evidence would overturn the verdict, and why it was graded on balance rather than strong.

<Solution>
**The view at full strength.** Competition for power is itself a test. The incompetent lose wars, elections and factional struggles; over time the able rise and the inept fall, so that a ruling group is, on average, more capable than those it rules. Ruling minorities claim exactly this superiority, and often have it.

**Where it fails.** The selection works only where power is contested and can be taken away. Where office is inherited, as from Marcus Aurelius to Commodus, nothing selects the heir; where a ruling group co-opts its successors, it selects for loyalty, and the label of capacity drifts away from the capacity (<Ref lesson="michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites" />). And winning power is one skill, not the skill of ruling well: Cleon was "by far the most powerful with the commons" because he was the most violent man at Athens.

**What would overturn the verdict.** Closed, hereditary or co-opting rulers who kept their capacity across generations.

**Why on balance.** The chapter's cases are illustrations, not a count. A systematic comparison of rulers' capacity under contested and uncontested selection would be needed to grade it strong, and could also lower it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Aristotle says that the household is a monarchy, ruled by one by nature, and that the city is "a government of freemen and equals" who rule and are ruled in turn. Take the result of the section on where the theory fails: no free man can be shown by nature to be another's natural master. State what must then decide who rules among free men, and whether it can be nature at all.

<Solution>
Among the household's members nature was supposed to decide: the one who foresees rules the one who executes. Among free men the deliberative part is present in each, so nature gives no one a title to rule the rest in the way the soul rules the body. Three things could still decide. **Rotation**, rule and be ruled in turn, which treats the citizens as natural equals. A **claim to some merit**, wealth, birth, virtue or numbers, which each party in a city advances for itself, so that the question becomes which merit counts. Or something that is not a man at all: **law**, standing above all the claimants.

Nature can still enter, but only indirectly: as the end (the city exists for living well, so the claim that serves that end best has the strongest title), not as a mark on any one man. This is the question with which <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" /> begins: who should be sovereign, the many, the rich, the good, the one best man, or the law. Aristotle's answer there, that law is reason without desire, puts reason at the top of the ladder again, with no man standing on that rung.
</Solution>

Five candidates claim the right to rule a city: the many, the rich, the well-born, the one best man, and the law, and each has a real title to offer: freedom, property, birth, virtue, impartiality. In the Greek cities of the fourth century BC these were not abstract titles, because power kept passing from the rich to the poor and back, and each side that won rewrote the laws in its own favour. Who should hold the last word? Nature does not settle it. The household, as <Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="what-follows-from-the-concessions">the previous chapter</Ref> left it, is a monarchy: one man rules wife, children and slaves, and Aristotle held that nature put him there. A city is made of free men, each of whom has the deliberative part of the soul, and the same chapter ended on the result that no free man can be shown by nature to be another's master. So nature names no ruler for a city, and the question is left to the claimants.

The claims were not seminar positions. In the generation before Aristotle was born in 384, Athens passed from democracy to an oligarchy and back twice, in 411 and 404 (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-objection-from-the-record">the record against Plato's descent</Ref>), and in each change a party took the city and called its own claim justice. That is exactly the situation Thrasymachus described: every ruling body, one man, few or many, makes laws for its own advantage and calls obedience "just" (<Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="thrasymachus-the-law-is-what-the-rulers-interest-says-it-is">the interest of the stronger</Ref>). If he is right, there is no just claim to rule, only winners. Plato's answer was the one who knows, <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="third-wave-philosophers-must-rule">the philosopher king</Ref>; but nobody can check from outside that a ruler knows, and in the *Laws* Plato himself fell back on law and on a mixture of monarchy and democracy whose own citizen <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="sparta-saved-by-mixing">could not say what kind of regime it was</Ref>.

The obvious move is to pick the claim that is just and give that party the city. Aristotle does not start there. Weighing claims to rule presupposes two things nobody in the quarrel had defined: what it is that is being claimed, and who is entitled to claim it. He begins with those.

## A city is its constitution; a citizen is one who shares in judging

The first difficulty Aristotle raises is a practical one. When a city changes its government, say from an oligarchy or a tyranny to a democracy, some citizens refuse to honour contracts made by the old regime, "on the ground that the tyrant, and not the state, contracted them". To decide whether they may, one has to know whether the city after the change is the same city as before.

### Same singers, a different chorus

The obvious test is the place and the people. Aristotle shows that neither works. A wall is no test: you could put a wall round the whole Peloponnese, and Babylon, the great city on the Euphrates in Mesopotamia, was so large that "some part of the inhabitants" learned it had been taken three days after the event. The people are no test either, since they are always dying and being born, like the water in a river that keeps its name. So he looks at what changes in a revolution and what does not:

<Quote source="Politics III.3, 1276b1–13, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
For, since the state is a partnership, and is a partnership of citizens in a constitution, when the form of the government changes, and becomes different, then it may be supposed that the state is no longer the same, just as a tragic differs from a comic chorus, although the members of both may be identical. […] And if this is true it is evident that the sameness of the state consists chiefly in the sameness of the constitution, and it may be called or not called by the same name, whether the inhabitants are the same or entirely different.
</Quote>

The same twelve men can sing a comedy on one day and a tragedy on the next; what makes it a different performance is the form they sing in. A city, likewise, is a form imposed on a population, and a revolution replaces the form. Aristotle notices what this does to the debts and declines to settle it: whether a city "ought or ought not to fulfil engagements when the form of government changes" is, he says, "quite another question".

### The citizen: one who shares in judging and deciding

If the city is a partnership of citizens in a constitution, the next question is who the partners are. Living in the place does not make a citizen, since resident foreigners and slaves live there too, and the right to sue and be sued does not, since a trade treaty can grant it to foreigners. What the citizen has that nobody else has is a share in the city's two kinds of decision. Some offices are held by one man for a fixed term. Others have no term at all: the juror (*dicast*) and the member of the assembly (*ecclesiast*) serve whenever the court or the assembly sits. Aristotle calls these "indefinite office", and rejects the objection that they are not offices: "surely it is ridiculous to say that those who have the supreme power do not govern."

<Quote source="Politics III.1, 1275b18–21, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
He who has the power to take part in the deliberative or judicial administration of any state is said by us to be a citizen of that state; and, speaking generally, a state is a body of citizens sufficing for the purposes of life.
</Quote>

Aristotle says at once that the definition "is best adapted to the citizen of a democracy". At Sparta the people's assembly tries no cases: the five elected overseers, the ephors, try suits about contracts, and the council of elders tries homicide. There the citizen in the full sense is whoever holds one of those definite offices. The definition therefore already takes a side in the quarrel it is meant to settle: in a democracy nearly every free man is a citizen in the full sense, in an oligarchy only some are.

The common definition, a citizen is one whose parents were citizens, fails for another reason. It runs backwards without end: the first citizens of any city had no citizen parents. Aristotle quotes a joke of Gorgias, the orator from Leontini in Sicily, about Larissa, a city in Thessaly in northern Greece, whose name was also that of a kind of kettle made there, and whose magistrates bore a title that also means craftsman: "Mortars are what is made by the mortar-makers, and the citizens of Larissa are those who are made by the magistrates." Citizenship is conferred by a political act, not found in the blood. When <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="cleisthenes-equality-one-share-each">Cleisthenes</Ref> rebuilt Athens after the tyrants were expelled, he enrolled in the new tribes "many metics, both strangers and slaves". Were they citizens? They shared in ruling, so they were, Aristotle answers; whether they ought to have been is a question about justice, like the question whether a man who rules unjustly is ruling. He is.

### The constitution: the arrangement of offices

With the citizen defined, Aristotle can say what a regime is:

<Quote source="Politics III.6, 1278b8–13, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
A constitution is the arrangement of magistracies in a state, especially of the highest of all. The government is everywhere sovereign in the state, and the constitution is in fact the government. For example, in democracies the people are supreme, but in oligarchies, the few; and, therefore, we say that these two forms of government also are different: and so in other cases.
</Quote>

The Greek word translated "constitution" is *politeia*, the same word Plato's *Republic* carries as its title and the same word the earlier chapters rendered "regime". It names the arrangement of offices and, with it, the way of life a city leads; this chapter uses "constitution" and "regime" for it interchangeably. In Book IV Aristotle says what is being arranged. Every constitution has three elements: a body that *deliberates*, which decides war and peace, passes laws, imposes death, exile and confiscation, elects the magistrates and audits their accounts; the *magistracies* themselves; and the *courts*. A regime is the answer to the question who holds each of the three, and above all who holds the one that has the last word.

## The good citizen is relative to the regime; the good man is not

From the word "good citizen" one would expect the good man in his public role: courageous, just, sensible, whatever the city. Aristotle's definition of the city breaks that expectation, and he draws the consequence with a ship:

<Quote source="Politics III.4, 1276b20–31, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Like the sailor, the citizen is a member of a community. Now, sailors have different functions, for one of them is a rower, another a pilot, and a third a look-out man […]; and while the precise definition of each individual's virtue applies exclusively to him, there is, at the same time, a common definition applicable to them all. For they have all of them a common object, which is safety in navigation. Similarly, one citizen differs from another, but the salvation of the community is the common business of them all. This community is the constitution; the virtue of the citizen must therefore be relative to the constitution of which he is a member.
</Quote>

If the excellence of a citizen is to keep his constitution safe, then a good citizen of an oligarchy, who upholds the rule of the rich, would be a bad citizen of a democracy, and the reverse. The good man has one excellence wherever he lives. The two coincide, Aristotle concludes, only in the best regime, and even there not in every citizen. Ruling well takes *practical wisdom*, the capacity to see in a particular situation what ought to be done for the good, and that capacity is also what makes a man good. Being ruled does not take it: the citizen who obeys needs only a true opinion about what he is told and the habit of doing it, as a flute-maker must make a sound flute but need not know how to play it. So even in the best city the virtue of the citizen who is being ruled falls short of the virtue of the good man; only the citizen who rules has both.

In a city of free equals, where offices pass in turn, each citizen is now the one and now the other, so the good citizen has to be able to do two things: rule, and be ruled. Aristotle quotes a saying, "he who has never learned to obey cannot be a good commander", and explains it with the army: a cavalry general learns his command by serving under one. Rule over free equals is learned by obeying it, which is why the citizen of such a city "should know how to govern like a freeman, and how to obey like a freeman". Whether a craftsman can be a citizen depends, again, on the regime. In an aristocracy, where office goes to virtue, he cannot, because a life of manual work leaves no room for practising it. In an oligarchy a rich craftsman can, and in an extreme democracy every labourer is one.

If each regime makes its own good citizens, then regimes cannot be ranked by asking which one produces good citizens: each produces the citizens it needs.

## Six regimes: who rules, and for whose good

Aristotle finds a standard outside every regime by asking whom a given kind of rule is for. He has already met three kinds in the household (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="three-rules-in-one-household">despotic, royal and constitutional</Ref>), and he now sorts them by beneficiary.

### Whose good does the rule serve?

A master rules his slave "primarily with a view to the interest of the master", and the slave's good only incidentally, because a dead slave is no use. Rule over wife and children is the other way round, for the good of the ruled, as medicine and gymnastics exist for the patient and the pupil; the trainer gains from his art only when he happens to train himself. Political rule among free equals is of the second kind, which is why such citizens take office in turn: each looks after the others' interest during his year, and someone else looks after his. "But nowadays," Aristotle adds, "for the sake of the advantage which is to be gained from the public revenues and from office, men want to be always in office", as if they were invalids who stayed healthy only while they held it. The conclusion:

<Quote source="Politics III.6, 1279a17–21, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
The conclusion is evident: that governments which have a regard to the common interest are constituted in accordance with strict principles of justice, and are therefore true forms; but those which regard only the interest of the rulers are all defective and perverted forms, for they are despotic, whereas a state is a community of freemen.
</Quote>

A perversion, then, is a master's rule exercised over free men: the rulers treat the city as a household treats its slaves, as an instrument of their own good. Thrasymachus's table of regimes, in which tyranny, aristocracy and democracy each legislate for themselves, is on this account an exact description of three regimes, provided his "aristocracy", the few ruling for themselves, is read as what Aristotle calls oligarchy. Aristotle's quarrel with him is that there are three others.

### The sixfold table

<Quote source="Politics III.7, 1279a27–b10, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
The true forms of government, therefore, are those in which the one, or the few, or the many, govern with a view to the common interest; but governments which rule with a view to the private interest, whether of the one, or of the few, or of the many, are perversions. […] Of the above-mentioned forms, the perversions are as follows:—of royalty, tyranny; of aristocracy, oligarchy; of constitutional government, democracy. For tyranny is a kind of monarchy which has in view the interest of the monarch only; oligarchy has in view the interest of the wealthy; democracy, of the needy: none of them the common good of all.
</Quote>

Two words in the table mean something other than what a modern reader expects. *Democracy* here is a perversion: the rule of the poor in their own interest, not government by the people in general. The good form of rule by the many has no name of its own; Aristotle gives it "the generic name", *politeia*, the word for any constitution, translated *polity* or "constitutional government". He explains why the many's good form is rare enough to lack a name: one man or a few may excel in every virtue, but a large number can at most share military virtue, and so "in a constitutional government the fighting-men have the supreme power, and those who possess arms are the citizens". Polity, from its first appearance, is the rule of the men who can arm themselves.

Plato had five regimes, set out as a descent from best to worst (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" />). Aristotle's grid has two axes and six cells, and it has a good form of rule by the many, which Plato's descent did not.

### Number is an accident: the real line is rich and poor

The number axis breaks as soon as it is tested. What if the many who rule are rich? Aristotle sets out a case:

<Quote source="Politics IV.4, 1290a33–38, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Suppose the whole population of a city to be 1300, and that of these 1000 are rich, and do not allow the remaining 300 who are poor, but free, and in all other respects their equals, a share of the government—no one will say that this is a democracy.
</Quote>

Nor would a government in which offices went to the tallest, "as is said to be the case in Ethiopia", be an oligarchy, though the tall are few. So the number of rulers is a symptom and not the thing itself. "The real difference between democracy and oligarchy is poverty and wealth. Wherever men rule by reason of their wealth, whether they be few or many, that is an oligarchy, and where the poor rule, that is a democracy." The rich happen to be few and the poor many almost everywhere, which is why the two descriptions usually coincide. Each party also has its ground for claiming the city: the poor claim it by their freedom, which all citizens share, and the rich by their wealth, which few have.

<Example title="classifying Athens on the table">
Take Athens as the Funeral Oration described it (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-constitution-in-one-paragraph">the constitution in one paragraph</Ref>): an administration that "favours the many instead of the few", the assembly open to every citizen, poverty no bar to office, and, from the middle of the fifth century, a daily allowance that let poor men sit on the council and the juries.

**Who rules.** The assembly and the courts, where the poor are the majority. On the number axis, "the many".

**Which class.** Since the poor outnumber the rich and are paid to attend, decisions follow the poor majority. On Aristotle's corrected axis, the poor.

**For whose good.** Here the table stops. Pericles says the city is run for all and judged by all. Cleon, in the same city, called its assembly an <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="cleon-punishment-is-both-just-and-useful">"audience of a rhetorician"</Ref>, and an oligarch would say the poor vote themselves the rich men's money. If Pericles is right, Athens is a polity; if the oligarch is, a democracy in Aristotle's sense.

**The result.** The table cannot place Athens without settling the very dispute the parties are having: whether the rule of the many serves the common good or only the poor. The second axis is where the quarrel lives.
</Example>

## Each party's justice is a part; the city is not a contract

To decide whether a rule serves the common good, one has to know what is owed to whom. Aristotle starts from what both parties agree on: justice is equality, though not equality for everyone.

### Equal in one thing, equal in all

<Quote source="Politics III.9, 1280a16–25, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
they agree about the equality of the things, but dispute about the equality of the persons, chiefly for the reason which I have just given,—because they are bad judges in their own affairs; and secondly, because both the parties to the argument are speaking of a limited and partial justice, but imagine themselves to be speaking of absolute justice. For the one party, if they are unequal in one respect, for example wealth, consider themselves to be unequal in all; and the other party, if they are equal in one respect, for example free birth, consider themselves to be equal in all.
</Quote>

In plain words: everyone agrees that equals should get equal shares and unequals unequal ones, and the dispute is over who counts as equal.

The reader has seen these two equalities before, built into Athenian institutions a century apart (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="two-equalities-the-city-could-not-reconcile">two equalities the city could not reconcile</Ref>). Solon's four classes gave each citizen a share of office in proportion to his harvest: the *geometric* equality, of ratios. Cleisthenes' ten tribes gave every citizen one share: the *arithmetic* equality, one man one share. Aristotle attaches a party to each. The oligarch argues geometrically, with wealth as the measure; the democrat argues arithmetically, from free birth. Each argument is valid in its own respect. The error is the step from one respect to all respects.

### What the city is for decides which part counts

Take a business first. If men had joined together "out of regard to wealth only", their shares should follow their stakes. A *mina* was a sum of silver money, and "it would not be just that he who paid one mina should have the same share of a hundred minae … as he who paid the remaining ninety-nine". In a company, the oligarch is right.

A city is not a company, and it is not an alliance either. The Etruscans of Italy and the Carthaginians of North Africa had trade agreements and promises not to wrong each other, but nobody thought them one city. Move Corinth and its neighbour Megara, two cities on the Isthmus, until their walls touched, and they would still be two. Settle ten thousand carpenters, farmers and shoemakers side by side under laws against cheating one another, and they would not be a city if exchange and mutual defence were all they shared. Aristotle names the view he is rejecting: a law that is only "a surety to one another of justice", as the sophist Lycophron put it. That is Glaucon's account of justice as <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="a-bargain-among-the-weak">a bargain among the weak</Ref>, and this is Aristotle's answer to it:

<Quote source="Politics III.9, 1280b29–1281a4, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
It is clear then that a state is not a mere society, having a common place, established for the prevention of mutual crime and for the sake of exchange. These are conditions without which a state cannot exist; but all of them together do not constitute a state, which is a community of families and aggregations of families in well-being, for the sake of a perfect and self-sufficing life. […] Our conclusion, then, is that political society exists for the sake of noble actions, and not of mere companionship.
</Quote>

The consequence for the quarrel follows in the next sentence: those who contribute most to such a partnership have a greater share in it than those who equal or exceed them in freedom, birth or wealth but are inferior in "political virtue". The end of the city judges the claims made on it. Freedom and wealth are real contributions, without which a city cannot exist; virtue is the contribution without which it cannot live well.

## Five claimants, five injustices

With the end fixed, Aristotle puts the question the parties were fighting over: "what is to be the supreme power in the state". He runs the candidates past one test, call it the tournament: give each party the supreme power in turn, and see whether it produces something everybody would call unjust. His list has "the good" where the opening had the well-born, because good birth, as he puts it, is "only ancient wealth and virtue", so the claim of birth is folded into the claim of the good, the notables.

<Quote source="Politics III.10, 1281a11–39, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Is it the multitude? Or the wealthy? Or the good? Or the one best man? Or a tyrant? Any of these alternatives seems to involve disagreeable consequences. If the poor, for example, because they are more in number, divide among themselves the property of the rich,—is not this unjust? No, by heaven (will be the reply), for the supreme authority justly willed it. But if this is not injustice, pray what is? […] But is it just then that the few and the wealthy should be the rulers? And what if they, in like manner, rob and plunder the people,—is this just? […] Then ought the good to rule and have supreme power? But in that case everybody else, being excluded from power, will be dishonoured. For the offices of a state are posts of honour; and if one set of men always hold them, the rest must be deprived of them. Then will it be well that the one best man should rule? Nay, that is still more oligarchical, for the number of those who are dishonoured is thereby increased. Some one may say that it is bad in any case for a man, subject as he is to all the accidents of human passion, to have the supreme power, rather than the law. But what if the law itself be democratical or oligarchical, how will that help us out of our difficulties? Not at all; the same consequences will follow.
</Quote>

The first two frames show the injustice of taking: a majority or a minority uses its power to take what belongs to others. The third and fourth show the injustice of exclusion. Offices are honours, and a city in which the same men always hold them treats everyone else as less than a citizen. That is the cost of the virtuous claim the previous section seemed to crown.

Aristotle then tests the claims by their own logic. A claim to office has to rest on something that contributes to the city: among flute-players equal in skill, the better-born do not get the better flutes, because birth does not make anyone play better. And every claim, pushed to its conclusion, hands the city to a single man. If wealth gives a title, the richest man should rule the rest of the rich; if birth does, the best-born should rule the nobles; if virtue does, the best man should rule the good; and if numbers do, any group stronger than the many should rule them. "None of the principles on which men claim to rule and to hold all other men in subjection to them are strictly right."

## The feast: what the many judge better than any one of them

The weakest-looking claim is the one Aristotle defends first: that of the multitude, men "of whom each individual is but an ordinary person". The objection to it is obvious, and he states it himself. Any one of them is worse than the best man. Why should a crowd of worse men decide anything?

### Many contributors, one judgment

<Quote source="Politics III.11, 1281a42–b10, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
For the many, of whom each individual is but an ordinary person, when they meet together may very likely be better than the few good, if regarded not individually but collectively, just as a feast to which many contribute is better than a dinner provided out of a single purse. For each individual among the many has a share of virtue and prudence, and when they meet together, they become in a manner one man, who has many feet, and hands, and senses; that is a figure of their mind and disposition. Hence the many are better judges than a single man of music and poetry; for some understand one part, and some another, and among them they understand the whole.
</Quote>

The argument does not claim that ordinary men are wise. It claims that their partial knowledge adds up. A rich man's dinner is limited by one cook's range; a meal to which every guest brings a dish has more in it than any one kitchen could produce. A play is judged by an audience in which one man follows the plot, another the music, a third the verse, and between them they hear the whole of it. Aristotle attaches a condition in the next breath, and it belongs to the argument: "Whether this principle can apply to every democracy, and to all bodies of men, is not clear. Or rather, by heaven, in some cases it is impossible of application; for the argument would equally hold about brutes." A multitude "utterly degraded" adds nothing up. This is a defence of popular judgment, not of democracy in the table's sense.

### The objection from expertise

The strongest objection is the expert's. Only a physician can judge whether a patient was treated well, so physicians should be judged by physicians; and only a geometer can choose a geometer, only a pilot a pilot. On that argument the many should neither elect officials nor audit them. Aristotle gives two replies. The first is the feast again: a multitude that is not degraded, though worse as individuals than the experts, is "as a body … as good or better". The second is new. Some arts are best judged by the people who use their products:

<Quote source="Politics III.11, 1282a17–23, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
for example, the knowledge of the house is not limited to the builder only; the user, or, in other words, the master, of the house will even be a better judge than the builder, just as the pilot will judge better of a rudder than the carpenter, and the guest will judge better of a feast than the cook.
</Quote>

The builder knows how to build a house; the family that lives in it knows whether it is a good one. The citizens are the users of the city's government. They cannot draft a law or command a fleet, but they live under the law and send their sons in the fleet, and they can tell whether the official they elected did well.

### What the many should be given

The two replies decide what the many get. Aristotle sees a danger on each side:

<Quote source="Politics III.11, 1281b25–31, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
There is still a danger in allowing them to share the great offices of state, for their folly will lead them into error, and their dishonesty into crime. But there is a danger also in not letting them share, for a state in which many poor men are excluded from office will necessarily be full of enemies. The only way of escape is to assign to them some deliberative and judicial functions.
</Quote>

The many sit in the assembly and the courts, elect the magistrates and call them to account at the end of their year; they do not hold the treasury or the generalship one by one. That, Aristotle notes, was Solon's arrangement, and the reader has seen it: <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="solons-equality-to-each-by-worth">Solon's lowest class</Ref> sat in the assembly and the courts while the top classes held the high offices. He adds that the power is in the body, not in the member: "the power does not reside in the dicast, or senator, or ecclesiast, but in the court, and the senate, and the assembly". Pericles had claimed the same division of labour for Athens, that its citizens <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-citizen-who-judges">"are able to judge at all events if we cannot originate"</Ref>. The same Athens also <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="a-rebel-ally-and-a-decree">voted in anger</Ref> to kill the men of Mytilene and reversed itself the next day. Aristotle's argument tells which picture holds when: the feast works where the many are not degraded and where they judge, not where they replace the laws.

## A god among men, or the law

The previous section gave the many their place. The tournament left two other claimants: the one best man and the law. Book I's ladder of rule, with reason at the top over everything below it (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="every-composite-has-a-ruling-part">every composite has a ruling part</Ref>), leads one to expect the top rung of a city to go to the man in whom reason is strongest. Aristotle starts by taking that expectation seriously.

### The man no law can measure

<Quote source="Politics III.13, 1284a3–14, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
If, however, there be some one person, or more than one, although not enough to make up the full complement of a state, whose virtue is so pre-eminent that the virtues or the political capacity of all the rest admit of no comparison with his or theirs, he or they can be no longer regarded as part of a state; for justice will not be done to the superior, if he is reckoned only as the equal of those who are so far inferior to him in virtue and in political capacity. Such an one may truly be deemed a God among men. Hence we see that legislation is necessarily concerned only with those who are equal in birth and in capacity; and that for men of pre-eminent virtue there is no law—they are themselves a law.
</Quote>

Law is written for equals. A man who has no equals cannot be made to take his turn, and to make laws for him would be ridiculous: he would answer as the lions did in a fable told by Antisthenes, a follower of Socrates, when the hares harangued the assembly of beasts demanding equal shares: where are your claws and teeth? A city of equals has two ways to deal with such a man. It can remove him. Democracies did it by <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-objection-from-the-record">ostracism</Ref>, the vote that sent a citizen into exile for ten years; the Argonauts did it when the ship Argo refused to carry Heracles because he would have been too much for the rest of the crew; and Periander, tyrant of Corinth around 600 BC, did it when Thrasybulus, tyrant of Miletus, sent a herald to ask his advice. Periander said nothing, walked through a field and cut off the tallest ears of corn until it was level, and Thrasybulus understood that he was to kill the leading men of his city. Athens did the same to whole cities: having secured its empire, it "humbled" the Samians, the Chians and the Lesbians, Mytilene's island among them, contrary to treaty.

Aristotle does not condemn the practice. A painter will not let one foot of a figure outgrow the body, and a chorus-master will not let one singer drown the choir. Where one man's power is out of proportion to a regime of equals, ostracism rests on "a kind of political justice", though cities used it for factional ends. In the best regime, where the eminence is in virtue, exile is wrong and subjection is absurd, "as if mankind should claim to rule over Zeus". There the only course left is that "all should joyfully obey such a ruler", and that he be king for life.

### Best man or best laws?

Such a man is rare, and Aristotle doubts there is one in any actual city. The practical question is the one he puts next: "whether it is more advantageous to be ruled by the best man or by the best laws". He states the case for the man first, and at its strongest. "The laws speak only in general terms, and cannot provide for circumstances", and a craft run by written rules is absurd: "In Egypt the physician is allowed to alter his treatment after the fourth day, but if sooner, he takes the risk." A rule is fixed in advance for the usual case, and the particular case is never quite the usual one. A man on the spot can see what the rule cannot.

Aristotle's reply grants the gap and denies that the man is the better way to fill it. The man has what the law lacks, judgment of the particular, but he also has what the law lacks in the other sense, desire:

<Quote source="Politics III.16, 1287a28–32, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Therefore he who bids the law rule may be deemed to bid God and Reason alone rule, but he who bids man rule adds an element of the beast; for desire is a wild beast, and passion perverts the minds of rulers, even when they are the best of men. The law is reason unaffected by desire.
</Quote>

He then takes the physician back from the other side. A doctor who departs from the book has no motive except the cure; he "does nothing contrary to rule from motives of friendship", while magistrates "do many things from spite and partiality". A patient who suspected his doctor of being bribed by his enemies would prefer the book. And doctors themselves, when they fall ill, call in another doctor, because no one judges his own case well. Where the law runs out, it does not hand the case to one man's will: it trains magistrates "to determine matters which are left undecided by it, to the best of their judgement", and lets them amend it as experience suggests. Many such judges are better than one, because a large body, like a large quantity of water, is "less easily corrupted than a little": one man can be swept by anger, a great number are unlikely to be swept "at the same moment". Even a king, Aristotle notes, cannot see everything himself; it is already "the practice of kings to make to themselves many eyes and ears and hands and feet", and they make them from their friends. But friends are equals, and a king who thinks his friends should rule beside him has conceded that his equals should rule.

### Where decrees replace the law

The rule of law also sets the limit of the feast argument. In Book IV Aristotle describes the last kind of democracy, in which "not the law, but the multitude, have the supreme power, and supersede the law by their decrees". There the people become "a monarch … many in one", the demagogue plays the part of the tyrant's flatterer, and every complaint against an official is met with "let the people be judges". His verdict:

<Quote source="Politics IV.4, 1292a31–34, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
where the laws have no authority, there is no constitution. The law ought to be supreme over all, and the magistracies should judge of particulars, and only this should be considered a constitution.
</Quote>

The Mytilene vote was such a decree, taken by an assembly sitting as judge of a single case in the heat of the moment. The many judge well, on Aristotle's account, when they judge under law: electing and auditing officials, and trying cases the law has already defined. Ruling by decree, they are one more ruler with desires.

### What is left for the best man

The answer to "best man or best laws" is therefore conditional, and Aristotle says on what. Among men who are "alike and equal" it is "neither expedient nor just that one man should be lord of all". Only where a man or a family surpasses all the rest together in virtue is kingship just, because then "the whole is naturally superior to the part" and he stands to the city as a whole to a part. So there are two best regimes. The best simply is the rule of the outstandingly virtuous, a king or a true aristocracy, if such men exist. The best for cities as they are is the rule of law among equals, with the many judging.

<Aside>
**Aristotle's own sequence of regimes.** In III.15 Aristotle sketches how constitutions followed one another in Greek history, and the order is not Plato's (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-objection-from-the-record">the objection from the record</Ref>). The first governments were kingships, because cities were small and men of outstanding virtue few, and they were made kings for their services. When many men of equal merit arose, they would no longer endure one man's pre-eminence and set up a constitution. The rulers then enriched themselves from the treasury, wealth became the path to honour, and oligarchies grew up; these passed into tyrannies, and tyrannies into democracies, as the love of gain kept shrinking the ruling class and strengthening the masses. Now that cities are large, he adds, no other form "appears to be any longer even easy to establish". The sketch explains why democracy is everywhere in his day; it does not claim, as Book VIII of the *Republic* seemed to, that every city must pass through each stage.
</Aside>

## Polity and the middle: the best regime most cities can have

The rule of law answered "the law or a man?". It did not answer "which law?". The tournament's last frame still stands: a law can be "oligarchical or democratical", and in a city of rich and poor whichever class writes the law writes it for itself. Aristotle's last answer is about the city's makeup, not its rulers.

### Four questions a trainer would ask

He reframes political science on the model of an athletic trainer. A trainer knows the best regimen absolutely, for the best body; the best for this particular body; how to bring any body to a given level, even a low one; and the regimen that suits most bodies. Political science must know four things in the same way: the best constitution absolutely, the best under given conditions, how any given constitution can be founded and kept, and the constitution "best suited to states in general". "For the best is often unattainable." The last question is new. Plato had drawn the best city, ruled by those who know, and then a second best, in which law takes the knower's place because no man can be trusted with unchecked power (<Ref lesson="platos-laws-second-best-city" id="second-best-the-pattern-kept-the-knower-removed">the second-best city</Ref>); both were cities for a founder to lay out on new ground. Aristotle asks what most cities, already divided between rich and poor, can actually have.

### Polity: oligarchy and democracy fused

His answer is the regime with the generic name:

<Quote source="Politics IV.8, 1293b33–34, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
polity or constitutional government may be described generally as a fusion of oligarchy and democracy
</Quote>

The sixfold table defined polity differently, as the many ruling for the common good, and the step between the two definitions is the city's makeup. In a city made of rich and poor, the many are mostly the poor, and their rule serves the common good, and not the poor alone, only if the rich also have a share in it. So the good rule of the many takes the form of a mixture, a regime that gives each class a share without letting either rule. And the men the table made its citizens, those who can arm themselves, are neither the very rich nor the very poor: they are the middling men who can afford a heavy infantryman's armour, and they are the ones who make the mixture possible. Aristotle lists three ways to fuse the two regimes:

1. **Combine both rules.** In the courts, oligarchies fine the rich for not serving and pay the poor nothing; democracies pay the poor and do not fine the rich. A polity does both, so that rich and poor both attend.
2. **Take the mean.** Democracies require no property qualification for the assembly, or a tiny one; oligarchies a high one. A polity sets one in between.
3. **Take one thing from each.** Choosing magistrates by lot is democratic, electing them oligarchic; having no property qualification is democratic, having one oligarchic. A polity takes election from oligarchy and the absence of a qualification from democracy.

How can one tell a good fusion from a bad one? Aristotle's test turns an old embarrassment into a criterion:

<Quote source="Politics IV.9, 1294b14–19, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
There is a true union of oligarchy and democracy when the same state may be termed either a democracy or an oligarchy; those who use both names evidently feel that the fusion is complete. Such a fusion there is also in the mean; for both extremes appear in it.
</Quote>

His example is Sparta. Some call it a democracy: rich and poor boys get the same education, all citizens eat the same food at the common tables, the rich dress as any poor man can, and the people elect the elders and share in the overseers' office. Others call it an oligarchy: all offices are filled by election and none by lot, and a few men can sentence a citizen to death or exile. When the Spartan Megillus <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="sparta-saved-by-mixing">could not say which regime his city was</Ref>, he was, on this test, describing a successful mixture. Plato had reached the same verdict from the other end: cities named after the part that rules are <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="socratess-answer-and-what-it-leaves-standing">"not polities but parties"</Ref>, so a city that cannot be named after any part is the kind that really has a polity. Aristotle adds the condition on which it lasts: a well-mixed polity rests "on the general willingness of all classes in the state to maintain the constitution", not on foreign help and not merely on a majority.

The historian of the Peloponnesian War had praised such a mixture before Aristotle named it. The regime of 411, which handed the government to <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-historians-own-two-judgments">the Five Thousand</Ref>, men who could arm themselves as hoplites, was the best government Athens had in his time, because "the fusion of the high and the low was effected with judgment". In Aristotle's terms it is the polity of armed middling men just described, with one oligarchic device left unmixed: the end of pay for office.

### Who holds the mixture up: the middle

A fusion on paper has to be held up by people. Aristotle finds them in the class the two parties leave out of account. In every city there are three groups, "one class is very rich, another very poor, and a third in a mean", and the extremes are each unfit for the partnership of rule and obedience that a city of free men requires:

<Quote source="Politics IV.11, 1295b13–28, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
those who have too much of the goods of fortune, strength, wealth, friends, and the like, are neither willing nor able to submit to authority. The evil begins at home; for when they are boys, by reason of the luxury in which they are brought up, they never learn, even at school, the habit of obedience. On the other hand, the very poor, who are in the opposite extreme, are too degraded. So that the one class cannot obey, and can only rule despotically; the other knows not how to command and must be ruled like slaves. Thus arises a city, not of freemen, but of masters and slaves, the one despising, the other envying […]. But a city ought to be composed, as far as possible, of equals and similars; and these are generally the middle classes.
</Quote>

The citizen of a free city, Book III said, must learn both to rule and to obey. The very rich learn only to command, the very poor only to obey, and a city of the two is a household of masters and slaves on a large scale: the despotic rule of Book I where the political kind should be. The middling man can do both. He is "least likely to shrink from rule, or to be over-ambitious for it", he does not covet his neighbour's goods as the poor covet the rich man's, and nobody covets his. Aristotle quotes the prayer of Phocylides, a poet of Miletus in the sixth century BC: "Many things are best in the mean; I desire to be of a middle condition in my city."

He offers three kinds of evidence. Where the middle class is large there is least faction, and large cities are less factious than small ones because they have a larger middle. Democracies outlast oligarchies because their middle class is larger and shares more in government. And "the best legislators have been of a middle condition": Solon, "as his own verses testify", who stood between the two parties at Athens <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="solon-at-athens">like a boundary stone</Ref>; Lycurgus, Sparta's legendary lawgiver, "for he was not a king"; and Charondas, who gave laws to Catana in Sicily.

Why then is polity rare? Because the middle class is "seldom numerous", and when rich and poor fight, the winner "regards political supremacy as the prize of victory" and founds its own regime. The two leading cities of Greece made it worse: Athens set up democracies wherever it could and Sparta oligarchies, each looking "only to the interest of their own form of government".

### The weight that turns the scale

Book IV.12 turns the observation into a rule for any regime: "the portion of the state which desires the permanence of the constitution ought to be stronger than that which desires the reverse." Strength has two components. Every city, Aristotle says, is made of *quality*, meaning freedom, wealth, education and good birth, and *quantity*, meaning numbers. The rich have the quality and the poor the quantity; where the numbers of the poor outweigh the advantages of the rich, democracy results, and where the advantages outweigh the numbers, oligarchy. The middle class is the weight that can be put in either pan. Where it outweighs one or both extremes, neither can win, and the rich and the poor, who "will never consent to rule in turn, because they mistrust one another", both trust it: "The arbiter is always the one trusted, and he who is in the middle is an arbiter."

<KeyFact>
Aristotle's best regime for most cities is a matter of who lives in them before it is a matter of institutions: a polity needs a middle class large enough to outweigh at least one extreme, so the devices of mixing work only where the class structure already makes a mixture possible.
</KeyFact>

### The objection: is a mixture a regime at all?

The test of the two names has a cost, and it falls on Aristotle's own definition. A constitution is the arrangement of offices, "especially of the highest of all". In a mixture, who holds the highest? If the assembly can overrule the elected officials, the poor have the last word and the city is a democracy; if the officials can overrule the assembly, the city is an oligarchy. A mixture, on this objection, does not share the last word; it only hides which part holds it. A French jurist of the sixteenth century, Jean Bodin, pressed it to its conclusion: the power to make law that no law binds cannot be divided, so a mixed state is no state.

Aristotle's text supplies part of a reply. Every constitution has three elements, deliberation, offices and courts, and a constitution is the way they are assigned; a polity is a particular assignment, the many electing and auditing, the elected governing, the courts shared, and the "willingness of all classes" to keep it is his test that it works. That meets the charge that a mixture has no structure. It does not meet the charge that a mixture has no last word: on the day the assembly and the officials refuse each other, who decides? Aristotle never says. Whether a city must have one undivided power above all its parts is argued in <Ref lesson="bodin-sovereignty" /> and decided on the record of the English civil war of the 1640s, in <Ref lesson="english-revolution-as-test" />.

<Conclusion>
Who should hold the last word? Aristotle's answer has three parts. The many judge well together, as guests judge a feast, so they get the assembly and the courts, election and audit, but not the high offices. The law rules wherever it can, because it is reason without desire, and the best man rules only if he is beyond all comparison with the rest. And the best regime most cities can have is polity, a fusion of oligarchy and democracy held up by a middle class large enough to turn the scale between rich and poor. The answer has one gap Aristotle did not close: the case in which the parts of a mixture disagree. And its stability claim, that a city lasts while every part has a share and the middle turns the scale, is a claim about the record, which can be checked against the one Greek city that kept its regime for centuries (<Ref lesson="sparta-as-test-case" />).

A well-mixed city can still fall. Aristotle's own account of polity says it lasts only while every class is willing to keep it, and the next question is what makes a class unwilling: why citizens overthrow their own constitutions far more often than enemies do, and what, beyond keeping the peace between rich and poor, a city is for. That is <Ref lesson="aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life" />.
</Conclusion>

- *Democracy* sits in democracy, rule by the poor who took power and distributed office by lot.
- *Tyranny* sits in tyranny, one man ruling for himself.
- *Timocracy* has no cell. Plato defines it by what the rulers love, honour and victory, not by their number or by whose good they serve. A few spirited men ruling for honour could be an aristocracy of a sort or an oligarchy, depending on the second question, which Plato's sequence never asks. Aristotle's own treatment of Sparta, Plato's model of timocracy, puts it among the aristocracies and the mixtures.

**Aristotle's regime with no Platonic counterpart.** Polity, the good rule of the many. Plato's descent has only one regime of the many, democracy, and it is a step down; Aristotle's grid has room for the many ruling well.

Apply the sourcing questions from <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="solon-at-athens">the first chapter</Ref> to the feast passage (Politics III.11: the feast "to which many contribute"; "one man, who has many feet, and hands, and senses"; the condition that the argument "would equally hold about brutes"). Which of the two does the feast argument support, and which does its condition concede? Finally, what can the passage be used to establish about Aristotle's view of Athenian democracy, and what can it not?

**Who, when, for whom.** Aristotle, a resident foreigner in Athens without a vote in its assembly, in lecture notes for his school, in the 330s or 320s BC. His audience was students, not voters; his purpose was to work out which claims to rule have some truth in them.

**What it records.** That assemblies and juries of ordinary men existed and decided important matters, and that the argument for their competence was current ("the principle … is one that is maintained").

**What it argues.** That a body of ordinary men may judge better than a few experts, because partial knowledge adds up, and that users judge products better than makers; so the many should elect and audit, not hold the high offices.

**What it omits.** Which multitudes qualify. The condition ("in some cases it is impossible of application") names no test for telling a fit multitude from a degraded one.

**Pericles and Cleon.** The feast argument supports Pericles: his division of labour, judging but not originating, is exactly what Aristotle assigns the many. **What it can establish.** That Aristotle thought popular judgment had a real basis and that the practice of Athenian-style courts and elections was "really defensible". It cannot establish that he approved of Athenian democracy: a few chapters earlier he classed democracy, the rule of the poor for themselves, as a perversion, and later he called decree-rule no constitution at all. The passage defends a function of the many inside a mixed order, not the regime the many ran.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "Aristotle argues that the law should always rule rather than the best man." Name the step that fails, and state the claim that survives.

<Solution>
**The step that fails: "always".** III.13 and III.17 describe a case where the law should not rule over a man: where one man or one family so surpasses all the rest together in virtue that he is "a God among men". For such men "there is no law, they are themselves a law"; to make him take turns, or to exile him, is unjust in the best regime, and "the only alternative is that he should have the supreme power". Aristotle's argument for the rule of law is made for cities of men "alike and equal", in which no such man exists.

**A second weakness.** Even in those cities, the law does not rule alone. Aristotle concedes that the law "speak[s] only in general terms" and that particular cases must be decided by men; his answer is that the law trains magistrates for the gaps and that many judges are better than one, not that the law covers everything.

**What survives.** Among equals, which Aristotle thinks is almost every actual city, law should be supreme, because it is "reason unaffected by desire", and men should decide only what the law cannot, preferably many men rather than one. The rule of law is the answer for the best practicable regime, not for the best regime simply.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Two boundary cases for IV.11–12. (a) A city with no middle class at all, only a few rich and many poor, adopts a constitution by Aristotle's third mode of mixing: elected officials, no property qualification. Using his account, say what happens and why. (b) A city in which almost everyone is of a middle condition, with no very rich and no very poor. What does Aristotle's text imply about it, and what problem of the previous section does it leave unsolved?

<Solution>
**(a) No middle.** The mixture has no one to hold it up. The rich "cannot obey" and the poor "know not how to command", so the city is "not of freemen, but of masters and slaves, the one despising, the other envying". The rich will use the elected offices to rule for themselves; the poor, with no property bar, will use their numbers in the assembly. Since "the rich and the poor will never consent to rule in turn, because they mistrust one another", and there is no arbiter trusted by both, the first serious quarrel turns the regime into "the prize of victory": the winning party sets up a democracy or an oligarchy. IV.12's rule names the failure: the part that wants the constitution is not stronger than the parts that want something else, because no part wants this constitution for itself.

**(b) All middle.** The city is composed "as far as possible, of equals and similars", which is Aristotle's description of the best material for a city. Faction from class should be at its least, since no one covets another's goods and no one despises the rest, and each citizen can rule and obey. Mixing devices would hardly be needed. (The reader of <Ref lesson="platos-laws-second-best-city" /> will recognise the *Laws*' attempt to legislate such a city with a floor and a ceiling on property.)

**What stays open.** Class balance answers "which law?", not "who decides the case the law did not foresee?". A city of equals still has to choose between its law and a man who outshines it, and still has to decide which body has the last word; the god among men and the sovereignty objection apply to it unchanged.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive ostracism from two premises the chapter gives: (1) a constitution is an arrangement of offices among citizens, and in a city of free men it rests on their being equals who rule and are ruled in turn; (2) a man may arise whom no law fits, because his power or virtue is out of all proportion to the rest. Show why a regime of equals must either remove such a man or stop being that regime. Then explain why Aristotle calls the removal "a kind of political justice" in imperfect regimes, and why the same reasoning fails in the best one.

<Solution>
**The derivation.** By (1), the regime is a partnership in which offices pass among men treated as equals, and law is "concerned only with those who are equal in birth and in capacity". By (2), there is one man who is not equal. Three courses are possible. Keep him as an equal, taking his turn: then the city treats as equal what is unequal, which on its own principle (equality for equals, inequality for unequals) is unjust to him, and in practice his power makes the rotation a fiction. Give him permanent rule: then the regime of equals has become a monarchy, so it has stopped being that regime. Or remove him: the only course that keeps the regime as it is. Ostracism, the ten-year exile by vote, is that course made into law.

**Why "political justice" in imperfect regimes.** Uses III.13's premise. The pre-eminence ordinary regimes face is in wealth, friends or influence, not in virtue. Removing a man whose weight would overturn the constitution preserves the arrangement, as a painter cuts a foot that outgrows the figure; Aristotle grants that this is just "from their special point of view" and that perverted and true forms alike do it, while noting that cities abused it "for factious purposes".

**Why it fails in the best regime.** Uses III.17's premise. There the pre-eminence is in virtue, and the best regime exists for the virtuous life. Exiling the most virtuous man would remove the very thing the city is for, and making him a subject "would be as if mankind should claim to rule over Zeus". So the third course is closed and the second is right: "all should joyfully obey such a ruler", king for life. The same premises that make ostracism just among equals make kingship just where the inequality is in virtue.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Set Thrasymachus's account of regimes (<Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="thrasymachus-the-law-is-what-the-rulers-interest-says-it-is">the interest of the stronger</Ref>: tyrannies, aristocracies and democracies each make laws "with a view to their several interests", and these laws are the justice they deliver) beside Aristotle's sixfold table. Where do the two accounts agree, where exactly do they disagree, and what evidence would settle whether Aristotle's three true forms exist anywhere? Write a judgment of a short paragraph.

<Solution>
**Agreement.** Thrasymachus's description is Aristotle's definition of the perversions, once his "aristocracies", the few ruling for themselves, are read as what Aristotle calls oligarchies: a regime in which the ruling body, one, few or many, governs for its own interest. Aristotle even agrees that such regimes are common, and that in them men want office for its profit. He also agrees that the law takes its character from the regime ("what if the law itself be democratical or oligarchical?").

**Disagreement.** Thrasymachus says this is what every regime is, and so what justice is. Aristotle says there is a second kind of rule, for the good of the ruled, as in medicine, gymnastics and a father's rule, and that rule over free men can be of that kind; so there are three regimes that are not descriptions of the stronger's interest, and justice is the common good, not whatever the rulers command.

**What would settle it.** The existence of a regime in which neither class can rule for itself: a city whose arrangement no observer can name after one part, which all classes are willing to keep. Aristotle's candidate is Sparta, called both democracy and oligarchy. The evidence needed is the record of such a city: whether its laws in fact served rich and poor alike, or quietly served one, and whether all its classes went on willing to keep it.

**Judgment.** A good paragraph says that Aristotle does not refute Thrasymachus; he confines him. The disputed point is empirical as well as conceptual: whether mixed regimes held up by a middle exist and last. Until a case is examined, the table shows that Thrasymachus's account is incomplete as a classification, not that it is false of actual cities.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Aristotle says a polity lasts through "the general willingness of all classes in the state to maintain the constitution". Take a polity built by the third mode of mixing: the poor accept elected officials because they expect their votes to choose good ones, and the rich accept the absence of a property qualification because they expect to be elected anyway. Describe what happens the first year an election goes against the expectation of one side, and say which of the two partial justices of III.9 the losing side will invoke.

<Solution>
**If the rich lose.** Suppose the poor majority elects men of their own class to the high offices. The rich expected election to favour them; now the offices are held by men they regard as their inferiors. They will argue that they pay for the city and own its land, so being shut out of office while paying is unjust. That is the oligarch's partial justice: unequal in wealth, therefore unequal in all, entitled to a larger share. Their willingness to keep the constitution weakens.

**If the poor lose.** Suppose instead the rich win every office year after year, through money, connections and reputation. The poor expected their votes to count; they now see election working as an oligarchic device. They will argue that they are as free as the rich, so office should not depend on wealth: elect by lot, or pay us to serve. That is the democrat's partial justice: equal in free birth, therefore equal in all.

**What the case shows.** Either way, the regime survives only while each side thinks the outcome is just by its own measure, and the losing side reaches for the half of justice the mixture had compromised. The state of mind of a party that thinks itself treated unequally by the constitution is where Aristotle's account of faction begins, in <Ref lesson="aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life" />: the causes of revolution, and how a polity can be kept from it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

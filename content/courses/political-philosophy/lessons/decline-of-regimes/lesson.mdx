A city ruled by the best men decays into one ruled by the honour-loving, then the money-loving, then the free, then a tyrant. Plato tells this as a sequence of fathers and sons, each generation reacting to the last. Each regime is ended by the kind of man it raises, and the question is whether that chain is a law every city must follow or a warning about what a city's honours do to its sons.

The city built in <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king">the previous chapter</Ref> stood on one condition: its rulers had to tell each child's metal, gold, silver or iron, and time every marriage so that the right natures were born and sorted. Before the three waves interrupted him, Socrates had been about to describe what happens when that condition fails: four bad regimes, and four kinds of man who answer to them. Book VIII of the *Republic* picks the plan up again.

The descent has a destination. In Book I Thrasymachus said that the man who enslaves a whole city, instead of being called a thief, is called <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="the-shepherd">"happy and blessed"</Ref>. Socrates now proposes to answer him: to set "the most just by the side of the most unjust" and decide "whether we ought to pursue injustice, as Thrasymachus advises". The road from the best city to the tyrant is the road to that comparison.

The obvious way to explain why a regime changes is to look at events. The reader has seen it done: in the history of the war between Athens and Sparta, a plague breaks the restraint of law and a failed expedition breaks the city's power (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="does-the-arrangement-judge-athens">the plague and Sicily</Ref>). Plato starts somewhere else. In his telling each regime changes because of the kind of person it raises, and the person is formed in a household, by a father the son watches and decides not to be. That makes the story vivid and hard to check at once. A history of fathers and sons cannot be laid against a chronology the way a history of wars can, and the question of what kind of claim it makes stays open to the end.

## Change begins inside the ruling class: the mistimed birth

The first change is the hardest to explain, because the best city was built so that nothing in it would want to change. Its rulers own nothing, its soldiers are kept in a camp, and every citizen has been told he is a brother of the others.

### A united ruling class cannot be moved

The tempting answer is a blow from outside: a war lost, a famine, a plague. Socrates rules it out with a principle:

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 545c–d, trans. B. Jowett">
Clearly, all political changes originate in divisions of the actual governing power; a government which is united, however small, cannot be moved.
</Quote>

A war can kill rulers; it cannot make the survivors want something different. A ruling class that agrees with itself rebuilds after a defeat. So the question becomes how the rulers of the best city could come to disagree, and the city's own design leaves only one opening. The <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="the-guardians-camp">guardians' camp</Ref> took away anything they could quarrel over. What it could not take away was the need to breed and sort the next generation.

### The Muses' joke

Socrates hands the explanation to the Muses, the goddesses of poetry, and warns that they will speak "in solemn mockery", like adults teasing children in a tragic voice.

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 546a–b, trans. B. Jowett">
A city which is thus constituted can hardly be shaken; but, seeing that everything which has a beginning has also an end, even a constitution such as yours will not last for ever, but will in time be dissolved. […] But to the knowledge of human fecundity and sterility all the wisdom and education of your rulers will not attain; the laws which regulate them will not be discovered by an intelligence which is alloyed with sense, but will escape them, and they will bring children into the world when they ought not.
</Quote>

The Muses go on to name the "number" that governs good and bad births, in a sentence of squares, cubes and harmonies that commentators have argued over since antiquity. That is the mockery: a mock-solemn number dressed up for a plain fact. Rulers are human, the season of good births cannot be known by beings who learn through their senses, and sooner or later a child is born out of season.

The rest follows in steps. The badly born children are still the best available, so they are appointed. As rulers they neglect music, then gymnastics, so the next generation is less cultivated. Their successors have "lost the guardian power of testing the metal", and the metals mix: "iron will be mingled with silver, and brass with gold, and hence there will arise dissimilarity and inequality and irregularity, which always and in all places are causes of hatred and war."

### States are as the men are

Before he follows the two factions, Socrates states the method of the whole descent. Regimes are not made "of 'oak and rock'", but of "the human natures which are in them", and Glaucon puts it in one line: "the States are as the men are; they grow out of human characters." So there are as many kinds of soul as kinds of *regime*, the Greek *politeia*: not only who holds office, but what the city honours and so what kind of citizen it rears.

That gives five pairs. The best city, which Socrates calls *aristocracy*, "the government of the best", is matched by the just man of <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="the-soul-has-three-parts">the three-part soul</Ref>, in whom reason rules spirit and appetite. Four worse pairs follow: *timocracy*, a word Socrates coins for "the government of honour", then oligarchy, democracy and tyranny. For each he describes the city first and the man second, as he did in building the city, and for each man he tells how he came to be: what his father was, and what turned the son against him.

"Aristocracy" here is Socrates's name for the city of philosopher rulers, the best in knowledge, not the rule of a landed nobility that the word usually named in Greek cities. Timocracy is its first decline, not a kind of aristocracy.

## Timocracy: the house that teaches a boy to love honour

Once the ruling class holds two kinds of men, the question is what they do with each other. The iron and brass want land, houses and gold; the gold and silver, "having the true riches in their own nature", hold to virtue and the old order.

### The compromise between gold and iron

They fight, and then they settle:

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 547b–c, trans. B. Jowett">
There was a battle between them, and at last they agreed to distribute their land and houses among individual owners; and they enslaved their friends and maintainers, whom they had formerly protected in the condition of freemen, and made of them subjects and servants; and they themselves were engaged in war and in keeping a watch against them.
</Quote>

The "friends and maintainers" are the producers, the farmers and craftsmen of the third class, whom the guardians had protected as free men; the settlement makes them serfs. It keeps the outward form of the best city: the soldiers still do no farming or trading, eat at common meals and train their bodies. What it drops is philosophy. Wise men "are no longer to be had simple and earnest", so the new rulers prefer "passionate and less complex characters, who are by nature fitted for war rather than peace", and the city lives in "everlasting wars". The love of gold the settlement was meant to satisfy does not go away; it goes underground. The rulers hoard gold "in dark places", spend in secret, and run "away like children from the law, their father".

What the city honours above all is victory and reputation: "one thing, and one thing only, is predominantly seen,—the spirit of contention and ambition." Socrates says which actual cities this is: "those of Crete and Sparta, which are generally applauded". Sparta's equal soldiers, common messes and serfs, met in <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-hoplite-phalanx">the first chapter</Ref>, are the model for the first decline.

### The timocratic son

The question for each man of the descent is how a son comes to honour something his father did not. For the timocrat the answer comes from four directions at once.

<Example title="the timocratic son, from household to soul">
**The father.** He is "the young son of a brave father, who dwells in an ill-governed city, of which he declines the honours and offices, and will not go to law". The father is a good man living in a city that no longer rewards him; he waives his rights "in order that he may escape trouble".

**The mother.** She complains "that her husband has no place in the government, of which the consequence is that she has no precedence among other women", that he does not fight for money in the courts, and tells the son "that his father is only half a man and far too easy-going".

**The servants and the street.** The old servants tell the boy to make the father's debtors pay when he grows up and "be more of a man than his father". In the city he sees the same thing: "those who do their own business in the city are called simpletons, and held in no esteem, while the busy-bodies are honoured and applauded."

**The soul.** The father waters "the rational principle in his soul", everyone else feeds "the passionate and appetitive". The boy is pulled both ways and settles in the middle: he "gives up the kingdom which is within him to the middle principle of contentiousness and passion, and becomes arrogant and ambitious". Spirit, the part between reason and appetite, takes the throne.

**The result.** He is a good listener but no speaker, rough with slaves and obedient to superiors, a lover of hunting and of command, claiming rule "because he is a soldier and has performed feats of arms". He despises money while young and loves it more as he ages, having lost what Socrates calls virtue's only lifelong guardian, "philosophy, tempered with music".
</Example>

The son is not taught a doctrine. He watches which kind of man the people around him admire, and becomes that kind. A regime, on this account, is an education whether it means to be one or not: what the city honours reaches the boy through his mother, the servants and the street, and outweighs the example of his father.

## Oligarchy: the pilot chosen by wealth

Timocracy's rulers despise money in public. Spirit sits on their throne, and the city honours victory, not wealth. So how does money come to rule such a city? Through the one desire the settlement drove underground instead of removing: the gold the rulers hoard in secret. Hidden gold cannot stay hidden: they "invent illegal modes of expenditure", each sees another grow rich and competes with him, and the citizens as a body "become lovers of money".

### Wealth becomes the qualification

The step from loving wealth to ruling by it is short, and Socrates gives the reason in one comparison: "when riches and virtue are placed together in the scales of the balance, the one always rises as the other falls." Whatever a city honours, it cultivates. Once the rich are honoured, they are made rulers, and a law fixes a sum of property below which no one may hold office, "effected by force of arms, if intimidation has not already done their work". *Oligarchy*, which <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="a-rebel-ally-and-a-decree">the Mytilene chapter</Ref> met as the rule of a few rich families, gets Socrates's sharper definition: "a government resting on a valuation of property, in which the rich have power and the poor man is deprived of it."

### Five defects, and the drone

Socrates lists the regime's defects in order of weight, and the first reuses the image of <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="the-ship-of-state">the Ship of State</Ref>: "what would happen if pilots were to be chosen according to their property, and a poor man were refused permission to steer, even though he were a better pilot?" They would wreck the ship, and the city is the hardest ship to steer.

The second is "the inevitable division: such a State is not one, but two States, the one of poor, the other of rich men; and they are living on the same spot and always conspiring against one another." The third follows from the second: the oligarchs cannot fight a war, because if they arm the poor they fear them more than the enemy, and if they do not, they are "few to fight as they are few to rule", and they will not pay taxes for mercenaries. The fourth is that the same men farm, trade and soldier at once, against the rule of <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="one-man-one-art">one man, one art</Ref>, that each man does the one work his nature fits him for.

The fifth, "perhaps the greatest of all", is new. In this city a man may sell everything he owns and still live there, "being neither trader, nor artisan, nor horseman, nor hoplite, but only a poor, helpless creature". Socrates calls such men *drones*, after the bees that eat the honey and make none. A god made the flying drones without stings; of the walking drones he made some stingless, who "in their old age end as paupers", and some with "dreadful stings", the criminals. Wherever there are paupers, "there are hidden away thieves, and cut-purses and robbers of temples". In oligarchy, "nearly everybody is a pauper who is not a ruler".

### The miser on the throne

The oligarchic man follows the pattern. His father was the ambitious timocrat, perhaps a general, whom the son watched "foundering against the State as upon a sunken reef": put on trial "under a prejudice raised by informers", and executed, exiled or stripped of his property. Fear teaches the son "to knock ambition and passion headforemost from his bosom's throne". Poor, he works and saves, and seats the money-loving part of him "on the vacant throne", "to play the great king within him, girt with tiara and chain and scimitar", the dress of the Persian king. Reason and spirit sit on the ground at its feet. Reason may think only "how lesser sums may be turned into larger ones"; spirit may admire nothing but riches.

A soul ruled by appetite sounds like a glutton's, yet this man denies himself almost everything. The puzzle goes away once appetites are sorted in two, as Socrates sorts them. *Necessary* desires are those "of which we cannot get rid, and of which the satisfaction is a benefit to us": for simple food, as much as health and strength require. *Unnecessary* desires are those "of which a man may get rid, if he takes pains from his youth upwards", and whose presence "does no good": for "more delicate food, or other luxuries". The first kind earns money, the second spends it. The appetite that rules the oligarch is the money-making kind: he satisfies only the necessary desires and keeps the rest down, which makes him "more respectable than most people". The drone is his opposite, "the slave of the unnecessary desires".

His honesty is real but borrowed: watch him "in the guardianship of an orphan", where he can cheat unseen, and the drone in him appears. He holds his bad desires down "not making them see that they are wrong, or taming them by reason, but by necessity and fear". So he "will be at war with himself; he will be two men, and not one". The city was two cities; the man is two men.

## Democracy: equality to equals and unequals alike

Oligarchy is built to protect wealth: its founding law keeps the poor out of office and the rich in it. Yet it is overthrown by poor men, so the question is where a regime of the rich gets the poor men who end it. It makes them. The oligarchs' own good is to become "as rich as possible", and the quickest way to get richer is to let other men get poorer. They refuse to restrain the young spendthrifts by law, since "they gain by their ruin": they lend to them and buy up their estates. Men of good family are reduced to beggary and stay in the city, "ready to sting and fully armed", in debt or stripped of citizenship, and "eager for revolution".

### The poor man beside the rich one

The scene that turns the poor against the regime takes place in a war, where rich and poor stand in the same line:

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 556c–e, trans. B. Jowett">
and very likely the wiry sunburnt poor man may be placed in battle at the side of a wealthy one who has never spoilt his complexion and has plenty of superfluous flesh—when he sees such an one puffing and at his wits'-end, how can he avoid drawing the conclusion that men like him are only rich because no one has the courage to despoil them? And when they meet in private will not people be saying to one another 'Our warriors are not good for much'?
</Quote>

A weak body falls ill at a touch, and so does a divided city: each party calls in allies from outside, one oligarchic and one democratic. When the poor win, they kill some of the rich and banish others, give the rest "an equal share of freedom and power", and fill offices by lot, the device Athens used to make <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="cleisthenes-equality-one-share-each">every citizen's share the same</Ref>.

### The embroidered robe

From a philosopher who has just shown the best city collapsing, one would expect the description of democracy to be an indictment. It opens as praise:

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 557b–558a, trans. B. Jowett">
In the first place, are they not free; and is not the city full of freedom and frankness—a man may say and do what he likes? […] This, then, seems likely to be the fairest of States, being like an embroidered robe which is spangled with every sort of flower. […] there being no necessity, I said, for you to govern in this State, even if you have the capacity, or to be governed, unless you like, or go to war when the rest go to war, or to be at peace when others are at peace, unless you are so disposed […] is not this a way of life which for the moment is supremely delightful?
</Quote>

Every kind of life can be found there, so a founder who wants to design a city "must go to a democracy as he would to a bazaar at which they sell them, and pick out the one that suits him". Men sentenced to death or exile "just stay where they are and walk about the world", and "nobody sees or cares". The praise is real, and so is the cost, which the same paragraph names: the city tramples "all the fine principles which we solemnly laid down", never asking what training makes a statesman, and honours "any one who professes to be the people's friend". Socrates ends with the sentence that defines the regime:

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 558c, trans. B. Jowett">
These and other kindred characteristics are proper to democracy, which is a charming form of government, full of variety and disorder, and dispensing a sort of equality to equals and unequals alike.
</Quote>

The Greek cities had argued over <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="two-equalities-the-city-could-not-reconcile">two equalities</Ref>: one share each, or shares in proportion to worth. Kallipolis chose the second. Democracy, on this account, chooses the first and extends it to everyone, the able and the unable alike. That is its justice and, by Socrates's standard, its injustice.

<KeyFact>
Democracy's defining good is freedom, and its equality is the equal share given "to equals and unequals alike": the same principle that makes it the most varied and pleasant of cities is the one that leaves nothing in it entitled to rule.
</KeyFact>

### Two portraits of one city

The reader has seen these features before, praised by a democratic leader. Pericles, in the <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-constitution-in-one-paragraph">Funeral Oration of 431</Ref>, said that Athens is governed by the many, that "we do not feel called upon to be angry with our neighbour for doing what he likes", and that this private ease does not make Athenians lawless, because awe of the magistrates and the laws holds them. Plato wrote the *Republic* about fifty years later, in a city that had lost the war and had executed his teacher. His democracy keeps every feature Pericles praised and removes the one Pericles relied on: in it nothing holds the citizens to anything. Plato names no city, but readers in Athens could not miss whose portrait it was.

## The democratic man: a soul with no ruling part

The man who answers to democracy is the oligarch's son. He grows up in a house where the unnecessary desires, the ones that spend money instead of earning it, were held down by his father's fear of poverty. The question is what happens to them in a son who never saw his father ruined and is offered every pleasure by his friends.

### The citadel taken

The son is raised "in a vulgar and miserly way", without music or poetry, and then "has tasted drones' honey", the company of men who can supply every pleasure. His father and relatives rebuke him, and his soul becomes a civil war; sometimes the old order wins for a while. But nobody taught the son why his father's restraint was right, so the restraint has nothing to defend it. The new desires find "the citadel of the young man's soul" empty, "void of all accomplishments and fair pursuits and true words", and take it. Then they rename what they have driven out and what they bring in:

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 560d–561a, trans. B. Jowett">
modesty, which they call silliness, is ignominiously thrust into exile by them, and temperance, which they nickname unmanliness, is trampled in the mire and cast forth; […] the next thing is to bring back to their house insolence and anarchy and waste and impudence in bright array having garlands on their heads, and a great company with them, hymning their praises and calling them by sweet names; insolence they term breeding, and anarchy liberty, and waste magnificence, and impudence courage.
</Quote>

### A day in his life

If he is lucky, the riot dies down as he ages. He does not become the drone. He lets some of the exiled virtues back and settles into "a sort of equilibrium", handing the government of himself to whichever pleasure "comes first and wins the turn", and then to another. Asked to rank his pleasures, he "shakes his head and says that they are all alike, and that one is as good as another".

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 561c–d, trans. B. Jowett">
Yes, I said, he lives from day to day indulging the appetite of the hour; and sometimes he is lapped in drink and strains of the flute; then he becomes a water-drinker, and tries to get thin; then he takes a turn at gymnastics; sometimes idling and neglecting everything, then once more living the life of a philosopher; often he is busy with politics, and starts to his feet and says and does whatever comes into his head; and, if he is emulous of any one who is a warrior, off he is in that direction, or of men of business, once more in that. His life has neither law nor order; and this distracted existence he terms joy and bliss and freedom; and so he goes on.
</Quote>

From the regime of freedom one would expect the freest soul. The text shows a soul ruled, in turn, by whatever desire arrives, and calling that freedom. "All pleasures are equal" is the city's "equality to equals and unequals alike" moved inside one man. In the just soul reason ruled; in the timocrat, spirit; in the oligarch, the money-loving appetite. In the democratic man the throne is not held by any part. It is taken in turns.

## Why the thirst for freedom raises a tyrant

Democracy is the most pleasant regime in the descent, and the next step goes from it to the worst. The question is how a city where nobody has to obey anybody ends with one man whom everybody must obey.

### The insatiable good

Socrates answers with the pattern oligarchy set. Oligarchy's good was wealth, and the insatiable desire of it ruined oligarchy. Democracy's good is freedom, "the glory of the State", and the insatiable desire of it ruins democracy:

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 562c–d, trans. B. Jowett">
When a democracy which is thirsting for freedom has evil cup-bearers presiding over the feast, and has drunk too deeply of the strong wine of freedom, then, unless her rulers are very amenable and give a plentiful draught, she calls them to account and punishes them, and says that they are cursed oligarchs.
</Quote>

Greeks drank wine mixed with water; to drink it unmixed was the habit of barbarians and drunkards. The cup-bearers are the politicians who serve freedom neat. A magistrate who restrains anyone is called an oligarch, and the habit spreads from the assembly into the house. The father fears his sons. The *metic*, the resident foreigner without citizenship, is the citizen's equal. The teacher flatters his pupils, the old imitate the young, the slave bought with money "is just as free as his or her purchaser". The joke ends with the animals: "the she-dogs, as the proverb says, are as good as their she-mistresses, and the horses and asses have a way of marching along with all the rights and dignities of freemen". Last, the citizens "cease to care even for the laws, written or unwritten; they will have no one over them."

Then the turn:

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 563e–564a, trans. B. Jowett">
the excessive increase of anything often causes a reaction in the opposite direction; and this is the case not only in the seasons and in vegetable and animal life, but above all in forms of government. […] The excess of liberty, whether in States or individuals, seems only to pass into excess of slavery.
</Quote>

### The protector becomes a wolf

"Seems" is a claim that needs a mechanism, and Socrates supplies one. He divides the democratic city into three classes. The drones, now freed from the property bar that kept them out of office in oligarchy, are "almost the entire ruling power": the bold ones speak, the rest "keep buzzing about the bema", the speakers' platform, and let no one answer. The orderly rich are the bees whose honey the drones take. The people, "those who work with their own hands", are "the largest and most powerful class in a democracy" when they assemble, and they assemble for a share of the honey.

The mechanism then runs in five steps, each forced by the one before:

1. The drones' leaders take estates from the rich and hand them to the people, "taking care to reserve the larger part for themselves".
2. The rich defend themselves before the assembly and are accused of "plotting against the people and being friends of oligarchy", until, driven by "the sting of the drones", they become oligarchs in fact.
3. Afraid of them, the people raise a champion and "nurse him into greatness". "This and no other is the root from which a tyrant springs; when he first appears above ground he is a protector."
4. The protector, with a crowd at his command, brings fellow citizens to court on false charges and has them killed or banished, "hinting at the abolition of debts and partition of lands". Socrates compares him to the legend told at the temple of Lycaean Zeus in Arcadia, in the mountains of the Peloponnese: whoever tasted a human victim's entrails among the sacrifice became a wolf. Having tasted his fellow citizens' blood, the protector cannot go back.
5. His enemies try to kill him, and he makes "the famous request for a body-guard […] 'Let not the people's friend,' as they say, 'be lost to them.'" The people agree at once, "all their fears are for him—they have none for themselves", and soon he stands "in the chariot of State with the reins in his hand, no longer protector, but tyrant absolute".

Plato's first readers knew the bodyguard request as history. Peisistratus, an Athenian with "the reputation of being an extreme democrat", wounded himself around 560 BC, blamed his rivals, and persuaded the assembly to give him a guard of club-bearers; with it he seized the Acropolis and made himself tyrant.

### What the tyrant must do

Once in power, the tyrant is driven by the same logic that brought him there, and each act he takes forces the next.

<Figure

Two items carry the argument. The first is the purge. The tyrant must find out "who is valiant, who is high-minded, who is wise, who is wealthy" and destroy them, "not the sort of purgation which the physicians make of the body; for they take away the worse and leave the better part, but he does the reverse". The second is the end. When the treasure runs out, the people, "from whom he has derived his being", must support him, as a father supports a grown son. If they refuse and try to drive him out, as a father drives out a riotous son, they find "that he is weak and his son strong". The people who made him are beaten by him:

<Quote source="Republic VIII, 569b–c, trans. B. Jowett">
as the saying is, the people who would escape the smoke which is the slavery of freemen, has fallen into the fire which is the tyranny of slaves. Thus liberty, getting out of all order and reason, passes into the harshest and bitterest form of slavery.
</Quote>

## The tyrant is the most enslaved man

The city has reached tyranny; the man has not yet been described, and he is the one Thrasymachus called happy. Book IX begins with him.

### The waking dream

Socrates starts from a fact about everyone. "In all of us, even in good men, there is a lawless wild-beast nature, which peers out in sleep": desires that wake when reason sleeps and stop at nothing, incest, murder, forbidden food. A healthy man lays them to sleep before he does; the democratic man keeps them for his dreams.

His son is raised in the father's easy equality and drawn by his companions into "a perfectly lawless life, which by his seducers is termed perfect liberty". When his father's moderate desires seem about to win him back, the companions "implant in him a master passion, to be lord over his idle and spendthrift lusts—a sort of monstrous winged drone". The Greek word is *eros*, usually translated "love", but meaning here the consuming desire that wants everything and will share with nothing; it has "Madness for the captain of his guard". He spends his own money, then his parents', then beats them for more; then he robs houses and temples. What he once did only in dreams he now does awake: "he is the waking reality of what we dreamed." When men like him are many and the people are infatuated, "they choose from among themselves the one who has most of the tyrant in his own soul, and him they create their tyrant."

### Fifty slaves in a wilderness

Thrasymachus judged the tyrant from outside, by what he could take. Socrates asks for a judge who has "dwelt in the same place with him" and seen him "stripped of his tragedy attire", and offers an image of the tyrant's life from inside:

<Quote source="Republic IX, 578d–579a, trans. B. Jowett">
But imagine one of these owners, the master say of some fifty slaves, together with his family and property and slaves, carried off by a god into the wilderness, where there are no freemen to help him—will he not be in an agony of fear lest he and his wife and children should be put to death by his slaves? […] The time has arrived when he will be compelled to flatter divers of his slaves, and make many promises to them of freedom and other things, much against his will—he will have to cajole his own servants.
</Quote>

A slave-owner in a city sleeps safely because "the whole city is leagued together for the protection of each individual". The tyrant has made the whole city his enemy, so he is that master in the wilderness, surrounded by neighbours who would kill him. He cannot travel or see what other free men see; he lives "in his hole like a woman hidden in the house". He is "never the friend of anybody", and he "never tastes of true freedom or friendship". So "he who is the real tyrant, whatever men may think, is the real slave", obliged to flatter the worst of men, with more wants than anyone and the means to satisfy none.

### The verdict, and what kind of answer it is

Glaucon is asked to rank the five men by happiness, "as the general umpire in theatrical contests proclaims the result", and ranks them in the order they appeared: the just man of the best city first, then the timocrat, the oligarch, the democrat, and the tyrant last. Socrates proclaims it with a phrase taken from <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="what-parents-and-poets-praise">Adeimantus's challenge</Ref>: the best and justest is the happiest, the worst the most miserable, "whether seen or unseen by gods and men".

Socrates adds two more proofs, both from pleasure. The second says each part of the soul has its own pleasure, of learning, of honour, of gain, and only the philosopher has tasted all three, so only he can judge between them. The third says most bodily pleasures are only relief from pain, so the philosopher's pleasures are the only true ones; it ends with a calculation that the king lives 729 times more pleasantly than the tyrant, which Socrates offers with a straight face and Glaucon receives as "a wonderful calculation".

So what answers Thrasymachus is a portrait, not a proof. Thrasymachus measured happiness by how much a man could take; Socrates measures it by the condition of the soul that does the taking, and shows the man who takes most as ruled by one desire he cannot satisfy and afraid of everyone around him. The portrait persuades a reader who already accepts that the soul has parts and that its order is its health, as <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="justice-is-the-souls-health">the previous chapter</Ref> argued. A reader who does not can still grant the portrait's factual core, that a ruler who has made every capable man his enemy lives in fear of them, and ask whether that makes him less happy than the honest man he despises.

## Law or warning: Persia, Athens and the mixed constitution

The descent has been told as a chain in which each link forces the next. Read that way it predicts: every best city becomes a Sparta, every Sparta an oligarchy, every oligarchy a democracy, every democracy a tyranny. The question in the opening is whether Plato meant that.

### What kind of sequence it is

The text itself gives three signals against the predictive reading. Socrates says tyranny arises from democracy "after a sort", in the same manner as democracy from oligarchy. He calls his description of timocracy a sketch, "for a sketch is enough to show the type of the most perfectly just and most perfectly unjust", and refuses "to go through all the States and all the characters of men". And the order of the descent is the order of the soul: reason rules, then spirit, then the necessary appetites, then all appetites by turns, then one lawless appetite. It is the shortest path from the most ordered soul to the least, which is what the comparison with Thrasymachus needed. Whether actual cities walk it is a different question.

### The second telling: two mother forms

Plato told the story of regime change a second time, and the second telling has a different moral. The *Laws*, his last and longest dialogue, written in the 350s BC, is a conversation among three old men: an unnamed Athenian, Cleinias of Crete and Megillus of Sparta, who plan the laws of a new colony. In Book III the Athenian looks at real history.

He first lists seven claims to rule that every city recognises: of parents over children, the noble over the ignoble, the old over the young, masters over slaves, the stronger over the weaker, the wise over the ignorant, and whoever the lot falls on. They "are naturally opposed to each other", and their conflict is "a fountain-head of seditions". No regime can satisfy all of them at once. Then he names two "mother forms":

<Quote source="Laws III, 693d–e, trans. B. Jowett">
there are two mother forms of states from which the rest may be truly said to be derived; and one of them may be called monarchy and the other democracy: the Persians have the highest form of the one, and we of the other; almost all the rest, as I was saying, are variations of these. Now, if you are to have liberty and the combination of friendship with wisdom, you must have both these forms of government in a measure; the argument emphatically declares that no city can be well governed which is not made up of both.
</Quote>

Persia, the empire to the east that invaded Greece twice, in 490 and 480 BC, decayed by an excess of rule. Under its founder Cyrus the soldiers were free men and a wise man could speak his mind to the king. But Cyrus left his sons to be raised by women and eunuchs as "favourites of fortune", "brought up unreproved"; one son killed the other and lost the kingdom. Darius, who rebuilt the empire, "was not the son of a king, and had not received a luxurious education"; his son Xerxes, the invader of 480, was raised the royal way and failed the same way. The fathers-and-sons mechanism of Book VIII is back, applied now to kings.

Athens decayed by the opposite excess. At the time of Marathon (490) and Salamis (480), the land and sea victories over Persia, "reverence was our queen and mistress", and the people "was not as now the master, but rather the willing servant of the laws". The decline began in the theatre. Audiences that once listened in silence started to judge music by their own pleasure, with shouts and applause, so that "instead of an aristocracy, an evil sort of theatrocracy has grown up", rule by the audience. From the conceit that everyone can judge music came disobedience to rulers, then to parents and elders, then to the laws, and "at the very end there is the contempt of oaths and pledges, and no regard at all for the Gods". The reader has heard Cleon tell the same assembly that it behaved like <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="cleon-punishment-is-both-just-and-useful">"the audience of a rhetorician"</Ref>. The Athenian's summary is a symmetry: "as they led their people into utter servitude, so we too led ours into all freedom."

### Sparta saved by mixing

The remedy in the *Laws* is not rule by philosophers. It is a principle of measure: "if any one gives too great a power to anything, too large a sail to a vessel, too much food to the body, too much authority to the mind, and does not observe the mean, everything is overthrown". No young man, the Athenian says, can bear unchecked power without folly. The case that proves the principle is Sparta. By the legend the Athenian tells, three kingdoms were founded together in the Peloponnese, Argos, Messene and Sparta; the kings of the first two grasped at more than the laws allowed and ruined them, and only Sparta survived:

<Quote source="Laws III, 691d–692a, trans. B. Jowett">
A God, who watched over Sparta, seeing into the future, gave you two families of kings instead of one; and thus brought you more within the limits of moderation. In the next place, some human wisdom mingled with divine power, observing that the constitution of your government was still feverish and excited, tempered your inborn strength and pride of birth with the moderation which comes of age, making the power of your twenty-eight elders equal with that of the kings in the most important matters. But your third saviour, perceiving that your government was still swelling and foaming, and desirous to impose a curb upon it, instituted the Ephors, whose power he made to resemble that of magistrates elected by lot; and by this arrangement the kingly office, being compounded of the right elements and duly moderated, was preserved, and was the means of preserving all the rest.
</Quote>

The <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-hoplite-phalanx">two kings checked by overseers</Ref> of the first chapter now become a design: one kingship split in two, a council of elders as heavy as the kings, and the *ephors*, the five overseers elected each year, who act for the many as an official chosen by lot would.

<Remark title="the mixed constitution">
A *mixed constitution* is a regime that combines a monarchic element, an aristocratic element and a popular element, so that each can check the others and none can rule alone. Its reason is the principle of measure: any power left unchecked grows to excess and ruins itself, as Persia ruined itself by too much rule and Athens by too much freedom.
</Remark>

The mixture has a strange consequence, which Megillus discovers in Book IV when the Athenian asks him to name the regime of Lacedaemon, the Spartans' own name for their state:

<Quote source="Laws IV, 712d–e, trans. B. Jowett">
I perceive that I cannot say, without more thought, what I should call the government of Lacedaemon, for it seems to me to be like a tyranny,—the power of our Ephors is marvellously tyrannical; and sometimes it appears to me to be of all cities the most democratical; and who can reasonably deny that it is an aristocracy? We have also a monarchy which is held for life, and is said by all mankind, and not by ourselves only, to be the most ancient of all monarchies; and, therefore, when asked on a sudden, I cannot precisely say which form of government the Spartan is.
</Quote>

That is the mixture working. A regime named after its ruling part is a regime with one part strong enough to rule alone, which is what the mixture prevents. In the *Republic* Sparta was the first step down from the best city. In the *Laws* it is the one city that stepped off the descent.

### The objection from the record

The strongest objection to the descent as an account of how regimes change came from Plato's own student. Aristotle, introduced in <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="outside-the-text-the-objection-from-care">the previous chapter</Ref> as the critic of the guardians' common families, collected the constitutions of many Greek cities and set Book VIII against them:

<Quote source="Aristotle, Politics V.12, 1316a17–25, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
Further, why should the perfect state change into the Spartan? For governments more often take an opposite form than one akin to them. The same remark is applicable to the other changes; he says that the Spartan constitution changes into an oligarchy, and this into a democracy, and this again into a tyranny. And yet the contrary happens quite as often; for a democracy is even more likely to change into an oligarchy than into a monarchy.
</Quote>

He presses four more points. The cause the Muses gave, that nothing lasts and births go wrong, is not peculiar to the best city; it applies to everything that comes into being. The descent never says what tyranny turns into, and in fact a tyranny often turns into another tyranny, or into oligarchy, democracy or aristocracy, with cities to show for each. Oligarchies change for many reasons, not only because their rulers love money: the rich resent sharing power equally with the poor, and men denied office and insulted rise up whether or not they have wasted their estates. And there are many kinds of oligarchy and democracy, while Book VIII speaks as if there were one of each.

The Athenians themselves had acted on the assumption that the step from protector to tyrant could be blocked. Peisistratus's sons ruled after him until 510, when <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="cleisthenes-equality-one-share-each">the city expelled its tyrant family</Ref>, and soon after the Athenians passed a law of *ostracism*, by which the assembly could vote, once a year, to send one citizen into exile for ten years. It was passed, an Athenian account says, "as a precaution against men in high office, because Pisistratus took advantage of his position as a popular leader and general to make himself tyrant".

The block held. No popular leader made himself tyrant at Athens again, and when the democracy did fall, it fell to the few. In 411, two years after the Sicilian disaster, a council of four hundred seized power and then gave way to <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-historians-own-two-judgments">the Five Thousand</Ref>, a body limited to men who could arm themselves. In 404, after the defeat, the democracy fell to <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="help-friends-harm-enemies">the Thirty</Ref>, the junta that killed Polemarchus. Athens, the city Book VIII's portrait of democracy was drawn from, made the change Aristotle calls more common, democracy to oligarchy, twice, and the change Book VIII describes, democracy to tyranny, not once after the law against it.

The record leaves the predictive reading without support. It does not touch the claim underneath it, and Aristotle kept that claim. In the same book he names the most important means of keeping any constitution: "the adaptation of education to the form of government", for "the best laws, though sanctioned by every citizen of the state, will be of no avail unless the young are trained by habit and education in the spirit of the constitution". That is the thesis of Book VIII without the staircase: a regime rears citizens in its own image, and a regime that rears the wrong citizens loses itself. Read as a psychology of regimes, a description of what each regime's honours do to the sons raised under them, the descent survives the objection. Read as a history of how cities change, it does not, and the second telling in the *Laws* suggests that Plato himself came to think the fall could be stopped by design.

<Conclusion>
You can now run Plato's descent from the best city to tyranny and say at each step what forces the next: rulers who cannot time every birth, then two kinds of men in one ruling class; a regime of honour whose hidden gold becomes the standard; a regime of wealth that makes drones and two cities; a regime of freedom that gives equality "to equals and unequals alike" and lets the drones raise a protector, who tastes blood and asks for a guard. You can also tell each regime's man from his father's household, and say why the tyrant, on this account, is the most enslaved of men, and why that answer to Thrasymachus is a portrait rather than a proof. As history the sequence fails, as Aristotle showed from the record. As an account of what each regime's honours do to the souls raised under it, it stands, and it is the reason the *Laws* recommends mixing monarchy with democracy instead of waiting for philosophers.

The mixture leaves a problem open. Megillus could not say which regime Sparta was, and whether a regime in which no part rules alone is a regime at all cannot be settled until someone says what a regime is and who counts as ruling it. Something underneath that question was never asked either. Both of Plato's tellings start from a city already standing, with households raising sons inside it, and ask only who rules it and how it decays. Why men live in cities at all, and whether the ranks inside the household, father over son, husband over wife, master over slave, are fixed by nature or only by custom and force, is where Aristotle begins: <Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" /> asks what a city is by nature before it asks who should rule one.
</Conclusion>

- *The law does not serve its makers for long.* The same oligarchs let estates be sold freely and refuse to restrain spendthrifts, because they profit by their ruin; that is what fills the city with drones and with armed, indebted men eager for revolution. Rulers who misjudge their own interest was Socrates's first reply in Book I (<Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="rulers-make-mistakes">rulers make mistakes</Ref>). There it was a single error; here the error is written into the regime's own law and ends the regime.
- *The stronger are not the stronger.* The oligarchs cannot arm the poor without fearing them, and the sunburnt poor man in the battle line concludes that the rich are rich "only because no one has the courage to despoil them". Their rule rests on an opinion of their strength, and it lasts until the many see through it.
- *The explanation is of a different kind.* Thrasymachus explains a law by the interest of whoever holds power, and needs nothing more. Socrates explains it by what the city honours and by the soul that honour forms: the law is what men with the money-loving part on the throne want, and the same want, unchecked by reason, is what ruins them.
</Solution>

Refute the claim: "Plato predicts that every democracy becomes a tyranny." Name the steps of the text and the record that break it, and state what Plato's claim is instead.

**The derivation.** The democratic father's rule is equality among desires: none may rule the others. His desires outrun his means, so he takes his parents' property and then others'. In the city, men like him, once numerous, need the same thing on a large scale: one man who will do for all of them what the master desire does in each, and they choose "the one who has most of the tyrant in his own soul".

**The check.** Book IX: the democratic man's son is drawn into "a perfectly lawless life, which by his seducers is termed perfect liberty"; his corruptors "implant in him a master passion … a sort of monstrous winged drone", with "Madness for the captain of his guard"; he becomes awake what his father was only in dreams; he robs his parents and then temples; men like him "create their tyrant".

**What the text adds.** The pattern gives "one desire takes the throne". It does not say which desire. The text names it: eros, the consuming desire, and it adds that the corruptors implant it on purpose, as a strategy to keep him, which the pattern of a son reacting to his father does not supply.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Set Book VIII's passage on how democracy turns into tyranny beside the *Laws*' account of Athens's decline (reverence "our queen and mistress" at Marathon and Salamis; the theatre audience judging music by its pleasure; then disobedience to rulers, parents, laws, oaths and gods). Where do the two agree, where do they differ about what starts the decline and what ends it, and what does the difference show about why the *Laws* recommends mixing rather than philosopher kings?

<Solution>
**Agreement.** Both describe the same city decaying through an excess of freedom. Both trace freedom's spread from public life into the house: in Book VIII the father fears the son and the teacher his pupil; in the *Laws* men escape "the control and exhortation of father, mother, elders", then of the laws. Both end with contempt for law itself.

**What starts it.** In Book VIII it starts with a class: the drones, made by oligarchy, who serve freedom neat and plunder the rich. In the *Laws* it starts with a loss of reverence: the audience stops being silent and claims to judge what it does not understand. The first is a social cause, the second a change in what the citizens fear and respect.

**What ends it.** Book VIII ends in a tyrant, by a precise mechanism. The *Laws* ends in contempt for oaths and gods and names no tyrant.

**What the difference shows.** If decline starts with a loss of reverence, the cure is to keep the citizens' respect for something above them, and to keep any one power from growing to excess; that does not need philosophers, it needs a constitution that balances the two mother forms, with the monarchic element to command reverence and the popular element to keep friendship. Book VIII's account, where only rulers with knowledge could have stopped the first mistimed birth, points to philosophers. The *Laws*' account, where the fall comes from excess in either direction, points to measure, and measure can be built into institutions run by ordinary men.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Someone claims: "Aristotle's objection refutes Book VIII." Name two facts the claim fails to account for, and state what would have to be true for it to survive.

<Solution>
**Fact 1.** Book VIII presents its order as the order that shows the types, not as a record of what cities do: tyranny comes from democracy "after a sort", the accounts are sketches enough "to show the type", and the sequence runs in the order of the soul, from reason ruling to one lawless desire ruling. An objection from the record hits a claim the text hedges.

**Fact 2.** Aristotle keeps the thesis underneath the sequence. In the same book of the *Politics* he names "the adaptation of education to the form of government" as what most contributes to the permanence of constitutions: the young must be trained "in the spirit of the constitution". That is Book VIII's claim that a regime rears citizens in its image and loses itself when it rears the wrong ones.

**What would have to be true.** That Plato meant the sequence as a prediction about the order in which actual cities change. Then Aristotle's cases (democracy to oligarchy, tyranny to tyranny, oligarchy to tyranny) would refute it. The *Laws* gives a reason to doubt even that reading of Plato's intention, since there he treats decline as avoidable by mixture. What the objection does refute is the use of the descent as history, and anyone who reads it that way.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Megillus cannot say which regime Sparta is: tyranny (the ephors), democracy, aristocracy, the oldest monarchy. Take the objection to the mixed constitution at full strength: a regime in which no part can be named the ruler either has nobody responsible for it, or is really ruled by whichever part controls the decisive office. What would you have to define first to decide whether a mixture is a regime at all? Say what each definition would make of Sparta.

<Solution>
Two things have to be defined first. **What a regime is**: is it the whole arrangement of offices, or the part that has the last word? And **who counts as ruling**: the part that holds the highest office, the part whose interest the laws serve, or the part that can block the others?

If a regime is named by the part with the last word, the objection wins: in any dispute one of Sparta's bodies decides, and Sparta is whatever that body is, perhaps an oligarchy of elders and ephors wearing the dress of a monarchy. If a regime is the whole arrangement of offices, a mixture is a regime of its own kind, and its being unnameable by any single part is the sign that it works: no part can rule alone, which is what the mixture was for.

Aristotle's definition holds both sides at once: a constitution is the arrangement of the offices of a city, above all of the one that has the last word. He also describes a mixture of oligarchy and democracy whose mark of success is that people can call it either one, Megillus's embarrassment turned into a test (<Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" />).
</Solution>

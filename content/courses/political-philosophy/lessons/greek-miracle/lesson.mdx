Around 1200 BC the Greek world was ruled from palaces, by kings with scribes and storehouses, exactly like the kingdoms of Egypt and the Near East. Four centuries later the same people lived in small cities that argued in public about who should hold power and why, and wrote the arguments down. Nothing like this happened in the far richer kingdoms next door. The lesson's task is to say what changed and why.

Before anyone can argue about who should rule, there has to be a place where the question can be put: a body of people who think they are entitled to ask it, and who expect an answer with reasons. That place is not the normal condition of government. For most of recorded history, in most of the world, a ruler announced his will and the people below him obeyed it or rebelled. Neither obedience nor rebellion is an argument.

The tempting explanation is that the Greeks were richer, or cleverer, or simply a special people. The first two fail at once. The palaces of 1200 BC were richer than anything the Greeks built for the next five hundred years, and their clerks were competent administrators who tracked every sheep and every ingot of bronze in the kingdom. Wealth and skill were both present, and produced no argument about rule. Whatever made the difference changed between 1200 and 800 BC. The place to start is what the palace was.

## A palace has no one entitled to ask who should rule

Before asking what the palace's fall removed, look at who, inside a palace kingdom, could have asked who should rule, and where.

### What the palace was

The palace civilisation of mainland Greece is called *Mycenaean*, after Mycenae, its largest citadel, in the north-east of the Peloponnese, the big peninsula that forms southern Greece. Between about 1600 and 1200 BC a handful of fortified palaces, Mycenae, Pylos on the south-west coast, Thebes in central Greece and, after its conquest, Knossos on Crete, each ruled a territory from a walled hilltop. Inside the walls stood a great hall with a throne, the houses of the king's companions, and the storerooms.

At the top of each kingdom stood a king with the title *wanax*, and his reach covered everything. The palace ordered weapons, fitted out chariots, levied rowers from the villages for the royal ships. It fixed the religious calendar, named the animals to be sacrificed and listed what offering each person owed according to rank. It measured land in units of grain, issued bronze to smiths and recorded what they returned, counted workers and slaves. There is no sign of private trade and no money: goods went into the palace and came back out by the palace's allocation.

The men around the king were not officials of a state with defined jobs. They were his servants, each carrying, wherever the king placed him, a share of a command that in principle covered everything. War, worship, land and stores met in one person.

### How we know: storeroom lists baked by a fire

Almost everything known about the wanax comes from clay tablets written in *Linear B*, a script of about ninety syllable signs plus pictures for commodities, which scribes from Crete adapted to write Greek. It was deciphered in 1952 and turned out, to general surprise, to be Greek. The tablets survive by accident. They were working inventories, written on unbaked clay to be wiped and reused, and they were fired hard only because the palaces burned down around them.

<Remark title="What a storeroom list can establish">
A Linear B tablet records quantities: so many tripods, so many sheep, so much bronze issued to a named smith. From thousands of such lists you can establish that the palace sat at the centre of an economy it measured in detail. You cannot establish how the king was obeyed, what anyone outside the palace thought of him, or whether anyone argued with him: inventories do not record that, whatever the answer was. Even the job titles are uncertain. One word that recurs on the tablets, *te-re-ta*, has been read as "priest", as "baron holding land for service", as "villager liable to tax" and as "servant". Every claim about the palace kingdom in this section is a claim about what its stores recorded.
</Remark>

### Why the question had no place

The tablets do show who stood outside the palace. Out in the villages lived a local lord, a vassal of the wanax who passed on the palace's bronze to the smiths of his district; beside him sat a council of village elders, the heads of the leading families. Ordinary villagers, if they resembled the commoners of later Greek poems, stood by while their betters spoke and showed their feelings by a murmur.

None of these people had a standing from which to ask who should rule the kingdom. The lord held his place from the king. The elders managed a village. The palace's writing ran inward, into the king's stores; nobody outside could read it, and nothing was written for them. The question "who should rule?" was not forbidden. It had no one entitled to put it and no place where an answer would count.

This was the common pattern of the age. The palace with its scribes and stores was an import: the Mycenaeans took the system, and its writing, from Crete, whose palaces resembled those of Syria and Mesopotamia closely enough to look like the work of one school. Egypt, Babylon and the Hittite kingdom in what is now Turkey were all governed by a king above a hierarchy of servants and a corps of scribes.

## The palaces fell and were not replaced

If the palace made the question meaningless, the natural expectation is that its fall changed little: a new strongman builds a new palace, as Egypt's kingdom was rebuilt after each of its own breakdowns. In Greece that did not happen.

### What vanished

Around 1200 BC, within a generation or two, the palaces burned: Pylos, Mycenae, Thebes. Later Greeks remembered an invasion from the north; historians still argue about the causes. The result is not in doubt. No palace was rebuilt. The title wanax dropped out of the political vocabulary altogether. The scribes, the inventories and the administrative apparatus disappeared, and with them writing itself: for about four centuries, until around 800 BC, no one in Greece wrote anything that has survived. Historians call this stretch the *Dark Age*: few stone buildings, almost no contact with the East, and no written records at all.

### What survived

What survived was what had never depended on the palace. The villages went on working the same land by the same methods; the palace had taken from them, not organised their farming. This is a real difference from the river kingdoms. Egypt's agriculture depended on coordinating the Nile's flood across the whole valley, and every Egyptian breakdown ended with a new pharaoh restoring the apparatus that did the coordinating. Greek farming was organised at the scale of a village and its neighbours. Once the palace was gone, nothing in daily life called for it to be rebuilt.

The local lords survived too, and so did their title. In the tablets *basileus* meant a provincial lord under the wanax. After the fall it became the word for the leading men of a district, used in the plural: several basileis to a region, none above the others. The later Greek poems show such a community on the island of Ithaca, with its basileus, its quarrelsome nobles, its assembly and, in the background, its silent common people.

### What came back changed

When writing returned around 800 BC it was a different instrument. The Greeks borrowed it from Phoenician traders, merchants from the cities of the Lebanese coast, and the Greeks themselves called the letters "Phoenician things". It was an alphabet: some two dozen signs, each standing for a sound, which a child could learn. Linear B had been a craft held by a closed group of clerks and used for the king's private accounts. The alphabet was used for public purposes: laws cut in stone, lists of magistrates, dedications in temples, poems.

<KeyFact>
For about four centuries, c. 1200 to c. 800 BC, Greece had no palace, no king over a territory and no writing; when writing came back, it was an alphabet anyone could learn, used for things meant to be read by all.
</KeyFact>

## Sovereignty divided into offices held in turn

The fall left many small powers and no centre. The usual outcome of that situation, anywhere, is a fight until one of the powers wins and becomes the new centre. Some Greek cities did produce such winners. The Greeks called them *tyrants*, men who took sole power by force, and treated them as an outrage. The normal case went another way.

### Rivalry among peers

The nobles who replaced the palace competed in everything: in war, in athletic games, in the size of their gifts, in rights to particular cults, and in speech. The Greek word for such a contest is *agon*. A contest presupposes that the contestants are comparable. The farmer-poet Hesiod, writing in Boeotia, a farming region of central Greece, around 700 BC, observed it of ordinary trades:

<Quote source="Hesiod, Works and Days 25–26, trans. H. G. Evelyn-White">
And potter is angry with potter, and craftsman with craftsman, and beggar is jealous of beggar, and minstrel of minstrel.
</Quote>

You compete with your equals, not with your master. So a class of nobles who measured themselves against one another in everything had made themselves into peers, and peers do not accept one of their number as a wanax.

### Athens takes the king apart

Athens, where the line back to the palace age seems least broken, preserved a tradition of how its kingship was dismantled. First a war leader, the *polemarch*, was set beside the king, because some kings had proved feeble in war. Then the civil command passed to a new officer, the *archon* (the word means "the one who commands"). The archons held office first for life, then for ten years, then for one year. The man still called basileus kept only the ancestral sacrifices.

Each step pulled one function out of the king and gave it to an office. Once the offices were annual, someone had to fill them every year, and the choice was made by human decision among men who counted one another as equals. Birth could not settle it among peers of similar birth. So the choice had to be argued over.

### Two royal legends

Two royal legends, one foreign and one Athenian, make the difference visible. The first is a legend of the Scythians, the horse-riding peoples north of the Black Sea, which Greek writers recorded as a foreign curiosity. In it three brothers see four golden objects fall from the sky: a plough, a yoke, a battle-axe and a cup, standing for farming, war and worship. The gold burns the two elder brothers when they reach for it and cools for the youngest, and the elders hand the whole kingship to him. Set beside it, the Athenians' own legend of their early kings runs the opposite way: when King Pandion dies, his sons divide his inheritance, Erechtheus taking the kingship and war, Butes the priesthood. The Scythian story is the old pattern, a king who holds everything. The change shows in the Athenian one, which tells of a kingship already split into parts, as Athens had split its king's functions among offices.

In the first story order comes from one person who holds all the parts. In the second, the parts exist separately and have to be fitted together. That raises a question the first story never meets: how is a single common life to be made out of separate powers that each have a claim?

## Power in the middle: the agora as a shape of equality

Offices held in turn need a place where the turns are decided, and the things that are now shared need somewhere to be kept. The Greeks answered with a spatial image, and then built it.

### What "in the middle" meant

The phrase was *es meson*, "into the middle". Prizes for a contest were set down in the middle of the assembled men; so were spoils to be shared out, and so was a dispute brought before the community. A speaker stepped into the middle to address the gathering. Something placed in the middle belongs to none of those standing round it and lies at the same distance from each of them. Anyone who wants it must make his claim in front of all of them and justify it to all of them.

The cities of the eighth and seventh centuries were laid out on this pattern. The palace citadel had been a private dwelling ringed by walls. The new town was centred on the *agora*, an open square that was a place of assembly before it was a market. In it, in the town hall, burned the common hearth of the city, which belonged to no family because it stood for all of them, as the hearth in the king's hall had once stood for the king's household. The old citadel became the place of temples, open to public worship. The walls now went round the whole community.

### The polis

The community built round such a centre is a *polis*: a self-governing body of citizens who decide their public affairs in common. "City-state" is the usual translation, and it misleads if it suggests a small country with a government. The polis was the arrangement itself, an empty centre surrounded by equals.

### A ruler who used the phrase

The phrase was a working political formula. On the island of Samos, off the coast of Asia Minor, the tyrant Polycrates died around 522 BC and left power to his secretary Maeandrius. Maeandrius summoned all the citizens, told them he had never approved of Polycrates acting as master over men who were *homoioi* to him, "like" him and no less than he, and announced:

<Quote source="Herodotus 3.142, trans. after G. C. Macaulay">
<Original lang="grc">
ἐγὼ δὲ ἐς μέσον τὴν ἀρχὴν τιθεὶς ἰσονομίην ὑμῖν προαγορεύω.
</Original>

I place the rule in the middle, and proclaim to you equality.
</Quote>

The word he used for that equality was *isonomia*: equal standing under the law and an equal share in rule among the citizens. The scene did not end well for him. In the same speech he asked to keep a gift from Polycrates' treasury and a hereditary priesthood for himself, and one of the leading citizens answered at once that he was unfit to rule and should first "give an account of the money" he had handled.

## Justice comes down to the agora: Solon's poem as a political fact

A royal order needs only to be carried out. A claim placed in the middle has to win over the men standing round it, who owe the claimant nothing and can refuse him. What does such a claim need that an order never did?

### What a claim in the middle requires

**Speech.** A king issues an order; it needs a scribe, not an orator. A question placed in the middle is settled by the men round it, by a show of hands or a shout in the assembly, so a claim wins only if it persuades them. Speech becomes the instrument of power, and the Greeks made Persuasion a goddess.

**Publicity.** What lies in the middle belongs to everyone round it, so nobody can be trusted to handle it out of sight. That is why the Samian citizen's first demand of Maeandrius was an account of the money. Things that had been the private possessions of the king or of the great families, procedures, knowledge of the law, the sacred objects of the cults, were brought out where anyone could see them, and at Athens every official, when his year ended, had to give an account of what he had done with public business.

**Likeness.** The middle is at the same distance from everyone round it only if no one stands closer by birth. If one man's voice counted for more because of who he was, the centre would in effect be his. So the men admitted to the circle had to count as interchangeable for the city's business, whatever their wealth: *homoioi*, "like" one another, the word Maeandrius used of the Samians whom Polycrates had mastered.

### Written law

Written law serves all three needs at once. In Hesiod's world, justice, *dike*, was split between two places. In heaven it was a goddess, remote and pure. On earth it was whatever the local lords pronounced when two farmers brought them a quarrel, and Hesiod, who had lost a share of his inheritance to his brother, called those judges "bribe-swallowing lords". Justice in that world was a ruling spoken by a man.

Once laws were cut in stone and set up in the agora, justice became a text. The same text bound the rich man and the poor man, could be read by anyone who had learned the alphabet, and could be quoted against the magistrate who applied it. It could also be argued with and changed by vote.

### Solon at Athens

Athens around 600 BC was close to civil war. Loans were secured on the borrower's own body. Poor families who farmed rich men's land paid a sixth of the crop, and a family that fell behind could be sold into slavery, children with it. In about 594 BC the Athenians, by agreement between the parties, made Solon, a noble of middling wealth known for his political poems, archon with power to rewrite the laws. He cancelled the debts secured on persons, brought back Athenians who had been sold abroad, forbade lending on the body, and published a code of law. Then he defended what he had done in verse, and the Athenians kept the poems.

<Quote source="Solon, fr. 36 (West), quoted in [Aristotle], Constitution of the Athenians 12, trans. F. G. Kenyon">
Thus might and right were yoked in harmony,\
Since by the force of law I won my ends\
And kept my promise. Equal laws I gave\
To evil and to good, with even hand\
Drawing straight justice for the lot of each.
</Quote>

He held both parties off. The rich wanted to keep everything; the poor wanted the land shared out equally, and he refused both. He also refused something no palace official could have refused, because none had ever been offered it: Solon could have made himself tyrant, and wrote in another poem: "Never to my soul was dear / With a tyrant's force to govern." One man could not take into his own hands what had to stay in the middle.

<Example title="Reading Solon's poem as evidence">
Who wrote it, when, for whom, and why: Solon himself, some time after his archonship of c. 594 BC, in verse meant to be recited before Athenians, to justify his laws to both parties who were attacking him. A defence written by the man under criticism will present him at his best; that is the first thing to keep in mind.

What it records: that a written code existed and applied the same rules to "evil and to good", meaning the low-born and the noble (the Greek words are social ranks, not moral grades); that the lawgiver held power briefly and gave it up; that both parties were dissatisfied.

What it argues: that might and right can be "yoked" when force is exercised through law rather than through a man's will; and that the lawgiver's achievement was to stand between the parties rather than to join one.

What it omits: what the laws actually said, and the cost of the settlement to each side. Elsewhere we learn that the rich lost the debts owed to them and the poor did not get the land redistribution they wanted. A defence passes over what its author did not want to dwell on.

What it can be used to establish: the poem cannot prove that Solon's laws were impartial; it is his own claim. It does prove something else, and the something else is the point. Around 594 BC a holder of power at Athens thought he owed the citizens an account of how he had used it, thought "the same laws for all" was the standard he would be judged by, and published his defence to be argued over. Justice had become something one could be held to account for, in public. That is what it means to say that justice came down from heaven to the agora: it did not stop being revered, but from now on it was also discussed.
</Example>

### The objection from the spider's web

The obvious objection was made at the time. A Scythian visitor in the Solon stories, Anacharsis, laughed at the whole project:

<Quote source="Plutarch, Life of Solon 5, trans. A. Stewart and G. Long">
Anacharsis, when he discovered this, laughed at Solon's undertaking, if he thought to restrain the crimes and greed of the citizens by written laws, which he said were just like spiders' webs; for, like them, they caught the weaker criminals, but were broken through by the stronger and more important.
</Quote>

Solon's reply in the same story is that men keep agreements when neither party gains by breaking them, and that he had fitted his laws to the Athenians so that keeping them paid better than breaking them. The exchange states the polis's wager and its weak point together. Written law in the middle only binds if the strong find it worth obeying.

## Five explanations and what each leaves unexplained

The road from palace to written law is a sequence of events, and a sequence says how something happened, not why it happened in Greece and not in Phoenicia or Babylon. Five explanations compete, the arrangement itself among them. Each deserves to be taken at full strength and then tested against a society that had the proposed cause without the effect.

### Human-like gods

The claim: the Greek gods were many, quarrelsome and human in their passions; they married, cheated and fought one another. Gods like that could not stand behind any ruler as the single sacred order of the world, so with nothing sacred behind rule, rule could be questioned.

The test case is Mesopotamia. Babylon also had many gods who quarrelled, married and fought one another, and Babylon had a king whose power was bound up with them: every year, at the New Year festival, he re-enacted the victory by which the chief god had made the world's order. Many quarrelsome gods sat comfortably beside a sacred king. Mesopotamia had the cause and not the effect, so the gods were not the difference. What Greece lacked was the sacred king, and he had gone with the palace; that absence belongs to the arrangement, the fifth explanation, not to religion.

### Competition between many cities

The claim: Greece after the Dark Age held hundreds of small independent cities, each a separate experiment. An arrangement that worked in one was copied by others; failures were visible. Political invention was the product of many trials.

The test cases are the Phoenician cities of the Lebanese coast, Tyre, Sidon and Byblos, and the Etruscan cities of central Italy. Both were clusters of small independent cities that traded and competed, and neither left anything we would call political theory. (The record for both is thin, which weakens the test: we have far less Phoenician writing than Greek.)

Variety can also run in opposite directions. Greek cities ran short of farmland from the eighth century on, and answered the shortage differently. Some cities sent their surplus people overseas to found new cities. Sparta, in the late eighth century, conquered the farmland of its neighbours to the west and made their people serfs. Athens met the shortage later, in Solon's sixth century, by growing olive oil for export and widening the circle of citizens with a say in politics. Many cities explain why there were many answers, not why one of the answers was argument.

### The hoplite phalanx

The claim: from about 650 BC Greek cities fought in a new way. The *hoplite* was a heavily armed foot soldier, a farmer who could afford his own shield, helmet and spear. Hoplites fought in a *phalanx*, a packed line many ranks deep in which each man's shield helped cover his neighbour. The line won only if every man kept his place. The mounted noble champion, fighting duels in front of the army, was no longer the decisive soldier. A man who stands in the line as an equal of the noble beside him will demand an equal voice when the line goes home.

The clearest picture of the new soldier's ethic comes from the battle of Plataea in 479 BC, the land battle that ended the Persian invasion of Greece. A Spartan named Aristodemus had been the only one of three hundred to come home from Thermopylae the year before, the mountain pass where the rest had died holding off the Persian army, and he carried the disgrace of it. At Plataea he fought more bravely than anyone.

<Quote source="Herodotus 9.71, trans. G. C. Macaulay">
The Spartans who were present gave it as their opinion that Aristodemos had evidently wished to be slain in consequence of the charge which lay against him, and so, being as it were in a frenzy and leaving his place in the ranks, he had displayed great deeds.
</Quote>

He was refused the prize for valour. The phalanx did not reward the man who stood out. It rewarded the man who stood beside his equals.

The test case is Sparta itself. Sparta had the most perfect phalanx in Greece and went further than any city in making its citizens equal: each full citizen received an equal plot of land, all ate at common messes, all were called the homoioi, "the equals". It also kept something no other leading city did: two hereditary kings, from two royal families, throughout its history, held in check by five overseers elected each year. And Sparta refused the rest. Its laws were never written down; they were short sayings given the authority of an oracle and obeyed without discussion. Spartans boasted of their brevity in speech. Where other cities set up Persuasion, Sparta honoured Fear, the dread that bent every citizen to obedience. The phalanx and an equality of soldiers were present, and public argument was not. Sparta is studied at length later, in <Ref lesson="sparta-as-test-case" />; here it shows that the phalanx can make citizens equal without making them argue.

### A special people

The claim in its strongest form: the change was too sudden and too complete to be explained by circumstances. On the coast of Ionia, the strip of Greek cities on the west coast of what is now Turkey, the first Greek thinkers began around 600 BC to explain the world without gods, and Greek cities began at about the same time to govern themselves by argument. In a few generations the Greeks produced political argument, history, philosophy and mathematical proof, and no neighbour produced any of them. Nineteenth-century writers called it the "Greek miracle", and around 1900 classical scholars stated it plainly: reason broke free of myth in Ionia as if the scales had fallen from a blind man's eyes, and Europe has followed that path ever since. The Greeks themselves thought they differed in kind from the "barbarians", the peoples who did not speak Greek.

The standing objection is that an explanation by innate qualities explains development by supposing that there was none: it says the Greeks were peculiar at the end because they were peculiar at the start. The record shows the opposite. The same Greek-speaking people had lived for four centuries under palace kings of the ordinary Near Eastern kind and had kept the king's accounts in the ordinary way. Spartans and Athenians, of the same stock and language, went in opposite directions under the same pressure of land shortage. An account by inborn character has to say what trait it means, how it could be detected apart from the outcome it is meant to explain, and why it slept through the palace age. Until it does, it names the fact rather than explaining it.

### The arrangement itself

The fifth explanation says that what Greece had, and its neighbours lacked, was an arrangement: no single ruler holding the whole of power, sacred or otherwise; sovereignty broken into pieces held by peers and put in the middle of a community; public writing to fix what was agreed. Run against the three test cases, it fares as follows. Mesopotamia kept its sacred king and Phoenicia's cities kept their kings, each ruling whole, and neither produced argument: no arrangement, no result, as the explanation predicts. Sparta is the awkward case, because Sparta had kings. They were split in two and checked by overseers chosen every year, so no one man held the whole, which is part of the arrangement; but they ruled for life by birth, and Sparta had no written law and no public debate. Partial arrangement, no argument. That does not refute the explanation, but it does not confirm it either. It shows only that dividing a king's power is not enough by itself, and it points to public writing and debate as the parts that matter.

It has a weakness of its own. It explains why argument about rule became possible, not why the Greeks used the possibility as hard as they did; and much of its evidence comes from one city, Athens, which wrote most about itself.

| Explanation | What it explains | Case with the cause but not the effect |
|---|---|---|
| Many quarrelsome, human-like gods | why rule lost its sacred protection | Mesopotamia: the same kind of gods, and a sacred king |
| Many competing cities | variety of political experiments | Phoenicia, Etruria: many cities, no theory |
| Hoplite phalanx | why ordinary farmers claimed equality | Sparta: perfect phalanx, no public argument |
| A special people | the suddenness and range of the change | none can be named, so the account cannot be tested |

## What the Greeks borrowed, and what they did with it

The strongest objection to any "Greek miracle" comes from the Greeks' own side: they said they had learned from Egypt and Babylon, and in much they were right. If the borrowing went deep, the break was smaller than it looks.

### The borrowings conceded

The alphabet was Phoenician. Geometry, by the Greeks' own account, came from Egypt, where officials re-measured farmland after each Nile flood to adjust the rent owed on it; the sundial, its upright pointer and the division of the day into twelve parts came from Babylon. Babylonian astronomers had kept records of the sky for centuries, and those records stand behind the story that Thales of Miletus predicted an eclipse in 585 BC.

<Quote source="Herodotus 2.109, trans. G. C. Macaulay">
I think that thus the art of geometry was found out and afterwards came into Hellas also. For as touching the sun-dial and the gnomon and the twelve divisions of the day, they were learnt by the Hellenes from the Babylonians.
</Quote>

The borrowing went beyond techniques into the stories the Greeks told about how the world began. In Hesiod's *Theogony*, his poem on the birth of the gods, Zeus defeats the Titans and the monster Typhoeus, takes the throne at the other gods' request, and then "divided their dignities amongst them". The Babylonian creation poem, the *Enuma elish*, tells the same kind of story: the god Marduk is made king of the gods, kills the sea-monster Tiamat, splits her body to make the sky, sets the stars and the calendar in place, and assigns every power its lot.

In Babylon the poem belonged to the king: it was the text recited at the New Year festival already met, the rite in which the king re-enacted the god's victory. In this picture order does not exist on its own. A sovereign makes it and a sovereign keeps it in being.

### The continuity reading

The case that the "miracle" is a translation runs like this. The first Greek thinkers of nature, at Miletus, a Greek city on the Ionian coast, asked the myth's own question, how an ordered world came out of a beginning, and kept the myth's own shape for the answer: one power from which the rest come, and which governs them.

Three of them are known, one after another in the sixth century BC. Take the first and the third. Thales held that everything came from water, and that the earth still floats on it. Anaximenes, a generation or two later, held that everything came from air, which thickens into water and earth and thins into fire, and that air still governs what it produced: the earth rides on it, and "as our soul, being air, holds us together and controls us, so breath and air surround the whole world". In each account one element is both the origin of the others and their ruler, as Marduk or Zeus was both the maker of the world's order and its king. The gods' names have gone, but one power still sits at the top (frame 2). On this reading the elements are the old gods with new names.

The continuity reading claims the second Milesian, Anaximander, as well. His account also begins like a myth of creation: an undivided original mass, out of which pairs of opposites separate, hot from cold, wet from dry, as in the myths sky and earth are split apart.

### What was not borrowed

Asked what holds the world in place, though, Anaximander, writing around 550 BC, between Thales and Anaximenes, gave an answer that neither myth nor his neighbours had given. The earth needs nothing to hold it up. It sits at the centre of a circular cosmos, at the same distance from every point of the rim, and has no more reason to move one way than another; it stays where it is "ruled by nothing". The elements are not ranked. Each in turn encroaches on the others and then pays back what it took, so that over time none dominates. A generation later, Alcmaeon, a physician of Croton in southern Italy, put the idea in political words outright: health is the *isonomia* of the body's powers, wet and dry, hot and cold, and disease is the *monarchia* of one of them, the rule of a single power over the rest.

The borrowed material was real. What the Greeks did with it was to draw a cosmos with no king in it, held together by balance among equals around an empty centre. They also published it. Anaximander wrote his account in prose, attached to no festival and no royal rite, as a proposal any reader could dispute, the way a law in the agora could be disputed.

Two questions about this remain open. One is whether it is a break with myth or myth carried one step further. The other is whether the cosmos took the shape of the polis because its authors lived in poleis. For that reading: the fit is close (an empty centre, equal powers, each paying back in turn), and Alcmaeon borrowed the political word *isonomia* outright. Against it: no Milesian says the city was his model, and a symmetrical cosmos is a geometer's idea as much as a citizen's, since a ball at the centre of a sphere has no reason to fall any one way.

## Two equalities the city could not reconcile

Solon's "equal laws" and Maeandrius's proclamation of equality used the same idea, and it would be natural to read them as one programme. They were not.

### Solon's equality: to each by worth

Solon gave the poor equal law, and refused them equal shares. In another of his poems he says:

<Quote source="Solon, fr. 5 (West), quoted in [Aristotle], Constitution of the Athenians 12, trans. F. G. Kenyon">
I gave to the mass of the people such rank as befitted their need,\
I took not away their honour, and I granted naught to their greed.
</Quote>

He divided the citizens into four classes by yearly harvest: those whose land yielded five hundred measures of grain, oil or wine; three hundred; two hundred; and less. The highest offices went to the top classes; the lowest class could sit in the assembly and the courts. Everyone was subject to one written law, and everyone's share of power was in proportion to his standing.

What is equal here is not the shares but a ratio. The man with 500 measures holds more power than the man with 200, and the claim is that he holds more in the same proportion as his harvest is larger, so that share divided by worth comes out the same for every citizen. Give the 300-measure man the same share as the 500-measure man and the ratio breaks: he now holds more power per measure of harvest than his neighbour, which on this view is as unjust as holding less. The Greeks called this equality *geometric*, after the mathematicians' geometric proportion, an equality of ratios, and the order it produces *eunomia*, "good order", each part in its place.

### Cleisthenes' equality: one share each

The other kind is *arithmetic*: one man, one share, whatever his harvest. This is isonomia in its full sense, and Athens built it into its institutions in 508/7 BC. Cleisthenes, an Athenian noble who led the people after the city expelled its tyrant family, abolished the four old tribes, which were groupings by descent. He divided Attica, the territory of Athens, into thirty districts: ten on the coast, ten inland, ten around the city. Each of ten new tribes received one district from each zone, assigned by lot.

The design answers a specific problem. For a generation Athens had been split among three regional factions, the plain, the coast and the hills, each the clientele of a great family. A tribe made of one coastal, one inland and one city third could not vote as a region, because it contained all three. Ten tribes rather than twelve was deliberate as well. The old tribes already had twelve sub-divisions, and twelve new tribes would have been built from them, leaving the old groupings intact.

Each tribe sent fifty men to a Council of five hundred, and the official year was cut into ten periods in which each tribe's fifty presided in turn. Every citizen could expect to rule and be ruled in rotation. Command and obedience stopped being two kinds of person and became two phases of one citizen's year.

### Equality among whom

Both equalities applied only to citizens: adult men of citizen families. Women, slaves and resident foreigners stood outside both, and at Sparta the equals were a few thousand men living off a much larger subject population. The polis's equality was always an equality among some, set against the inequality of everyone else. That does not make it a pretence. It does mean that the question "equal among whom?" was built into it from the start.

The two equalities were not reconciled. The aristocratic party in every Greek city held that geometric equality was justice, since giving equal shares to unequal men is itself unequal. The democratic party held that only one-man-one-share was equality and that proportional shares were privilege renamed. Both appealed to the same word, *isotes*, equality.

<KeyFact>
The polis produced two incompatible meanings of political equality, shares in proportion to worth and one share each; which of them is justice is the question Plato and Aristotle inherit.
</KeyFact>

<Conclusion>
You can now say what changed between the palace and the polis, and why it changed in Greece. The palace kingdom left nobody entitled to ask who should rule. Its collapse around 1200 BC left the pieces without a centre, peers who would not accept a new wanax, and four centuries later a public alphabet. The king's functions became annual offices, power was placed "in the middle" of a body of equals, and law became a written text that officials answered to and citizens could dispute. Argument about rule is the by-product of that arrangement. The rival explanations, from human-like gods to the phalanx, each meet a society that had the supposed cause without the result; the explanation by an innate Greek genius meets no such test, because it names no cause that could be found anywhere else.

The arrangement carried two problems forward. The first is Anacharsis's: if law is a text in the middle, someone wrote it, and it may be nothing but the interest of whoever was strong enough to write it. The stories about Solon end on a dry note, that "things turned out more as Anacharsis thought than as Solon wished". The second is the quarrel between the two equalities. And the equality in the middle was always an equality among some. <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice">The next lesson</Ref> follows Athens, the city that went furthest with it, into a long war, and asks how its equality treated those outside the circle: equal among whom? Anacharsis's problem returns in <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" />, in the mouth of a man who says it outright to Socrates.
</Conclusion>

The timeline puts the change after the collapse, which points to the collapse and its consequences rather than to an inborn trait.

<Exercise>
A clay tablet from the palace at Pylos, transcribed and translated, reads: "Two tripod-cauldrons of Cretan workmanship; one tripod-cauldron with a single foot and a single handle; one tripod-cauldron of Cretan workmanship, burnt away at the legs; one larger goblet with four handles; two larger goblets with three handles; one smaller goblet with four handles; one smaller goblet with three handles; one smaller goblet without handles." Apply the sourcing questions: who wrote it, when, for whom, for what purpose? What does it record, what does it argue, what does it omit? Finally, what can it be used to establish about how the wanax was obeyed?

<Solution>
Who, when, for whom, why: a palace scribe at Pylos, in the last period of the palace before it burned around 1200 BC, writing for the palace administration, to keep count of vessels in a storeroom. It was a working record, meant to be wiped and reused; it survives only because the fire that destroyed the palace baked it.

What it records: a count of bronze or precious vessels, with their type, origin ("Cretan workmanship") and condition ("burnt away at the legs"). It shows a palace that kept close count of its valuables, down to the damaged items.

What it argues: nothing. An inventory makes no case to anyone, and that is itself informative: the palace's writing was for internal bookkeeping, not for addressing a public.

What it omits: everything about persons other than objects. Who owned the vessels, who could use them, who ordered them counted, what anyone thought of the king.

What it can be used to establish: that the palace centralised and recorded wealth in detail, and that writing was a closed administrative tool. It cannot establish how the wanax was obeyed, whether anyone resisted him, or whether anyone ever discussed who should rule. Silence on those questions in an inventory is no evidence either way, because an inventory would be silent whatever the truth. The right conclusion is about use, not bias: this kind of source can show the shape of the palace economy and the function of writing, and nothing about political argument.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Apply the same sourcing questions to Solon's lines "Equal laws I gave / To evil and to good, with even hand / Drawing straight justice for the lot of each" and to Anacharsis's remark that written laws are spiders' webs. Then decide: is the sentence "with Solon, justice came down from heaven to the agora" a description of what happened or a claim about what it meant? Defend your answer in three sentences.

<Solution>
Solon's lines: written by the lawgiver himself after c. 594 BC, in verse for recitation to Athenians, to defend his reforms against attack from both rich and poor. They record that a written code existed and was meant to bind high and low alike, and that its author gave up power; they argue that force exercised through law ("might and right yoked") is legitimate where force exercised through a man's will is not; they omit the content of the laws and the losses each party took (the rich lost the debts owed to them, the poor were refused redistribution of land).

Anacharsis's remark: it survives in a much later biography of Solon, as part of a story told about the lawgiver and a foreign visitor, so it is evidence of how Greeks later framed the objection rather than a transcript of a conversation. It argues that written law binds only the weak; it omits any account of why the strong might find it in their interest to obey, which is exactly what Solon's reply supplies (men keep agreements when neither side gains by breaking them).

The judgment, one defensible answer: it is both, and the two parts should be kept apart. As a description it is accurate in a narrow sense: justice stopped being a ruling pronounced by a lord and became a written public standard that officials could be held to and citizens could dispute, and Solon's own poem shows him accepting that he must account for himself. As a claim it goes further, implying that justice had become fair or effective, and Anacharsis's objection, together with the later remark that "things turned out more as Anacharsis thought", shows that this part is contested.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Imagine a city with an agora, a common hearth and annual offices, in which only the members of one family may speak in the assembly; everyone else may attend and vote by acclamation. Of the three things a claim placed in the middle requires (speech as the instrument of power, publicity, likeness of citizens), which survive, and does argument about who should rule arise? Then compare the real case of Sparta: which trait did it have, which did it refuse, and what does the comparison suggest about which trait matters most for argument to appear?

<Solution>
In the imagined city publicity partly survives (business is done in the open square) and speech is still the instrument of power, but only for one family. Likeness is gone: the citizens are not interchangeable, because only some may speak. Argument about who should rule cannot arise in the full sense. The family can debate among its own members which of them holds which office, which is the aristocratic equality of peers, but the question "who should rule?" is closed for everyone else: only one family is entitled to put it. This is close to the early polis before isonomia, in which a small group shared power equally among itself.

Sparta had likeness, to an unusual degree: equal land lots, common messes, citizens called "the equals". It refused speech and publicity: its laws were unwritten oracular sayings obeyed without discussion, and it honoured Fear rather than Persuasion. And Sparta produced no political argument of its own.

Put together, the two cases suggest that likeness is necessary but not sufficient. Equality among citizens gives the question its possible askers; speech and publicity give it a place where an answer counts. Without the first, the question has too few askers (the imagined city); without the second, it has askers and no forum (Sparta). Of the two missing pieces in Sparta, written public law looks decisive, because it is what turns a decision into a text that can be disputed afterwards.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "The Greeks invented political argument because they had no gods telling them what to do." Name two facts the claim fails to account for, say why it fails, and state what would have to be true for a version of it to survive.

<Solution>
The correct account first: what Greece lacked after 1200 BC was not gods but a sacred king, a ruler whose power was bound to the divine order and renewed in ritual.

The claim fails on two facts. First, the Greeks had plenty of gods who did tell them what to do: every city was full of altars and temples, every office opened with sacrifices and oaths, and cities consulted oracles before deciding war. Religion did not leave Greek public life. Second, Mesopotamia had many quarrelling gods too, and it had a king whose rule was bound up with them: the creation poem was recited at the New Year while the king re-enacted the god's victory. Polytheism was common to both, so it cannot be the difference.

Why it fails: it mislocates the variable. What mattered was whether anyone could claim to embody the divine order in his person and so stand above question. In Babylon the king could; in post-palace Greece nobody could, because the divine king had vanished with the palace, and the basileus who kept the sacrifices at Athens was one annual officer among others.

What would have to be true for a version to survive: it would have to be restated as "political argument arose where no ruler could claim to speak for the gods' order", and then the absence of a sacred king would need to be shown to be independent of the palace's fall. It is not independent: it is a consequence of the collapse, which is why it belongs inside the structural explanation rather than beside it.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Rank the five explanations of the Greek change (the arrangement of power in the middle, human-like gods, competition between many cities, the hoplite phalanx, a special people) as necessary, enabling or precipitating causes of the appearance of argument about who should rule. Defend your ranking in a paragraph, and say what evidence would decide between your ranking and a rival one.

<Solution>
Possible answers; two defensible rankings are given.

Ranking A. Necessary: the polis structure (no sacred king, sovereignty broken among peers and placed in the middle), because every case of political argument we have occurs where it exists, and the test cases show the other causes present without the result when it is absent. Enabling: competition between many cities (it multiplies experiments and lets a successful arrangement spread). The human-like gods drop out as a cause: Mesopotamia had the same kind of gods beside a sacred king, and what they seemed to explain, rule without sacred protection, is the missing sacred king, which belongs to the structure. Precipitating: the hoplite phalanx, which from about 650 BC put armed farmers in a position to demand a share, and so pushed the aristocratic equality of peers towards isonomia. The special-people account is left unranked, because it cannot be tested against any case.

Ranking B. Necessary: the hoplite phalanx, on the ground that without armed commoners nobody below the nobles would have had the power to force the question open, and the equality of peers would have stayed a club. Enabling: the polis structure. Precipitating: the sixth-century crises of debt and faction (Solon's Athens), which forced written law.

What would decide between them: cities where the phalanx came early but the structure was weak, or the reverse. Sparta counts against Ranking B, since it had the phalanx at its most developed and produced no argument. Ranking A would be weakened by a polis with a fully developed agora, annual offices and written law that never debated rule, or by evidence that the phalanx preceded the annual offices and public law everywhere it appeared. A careful dating of the first written laws against the first hoplite equipment, city by city, is the evidence a historian would collect.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Design the comparison that would test the explanation "political theory arose from competition between many small cities". Name a second region with many independent cities, say what you would look for in its record, and say what finding would falsify the explanation and what finding would rescue it.

<Solution>
Cases: the Phoenician cities of the Lebanese coast (Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, Arwad) from the tenth to the sixth centuries BC; the Etruscan cities of central Italy in the seventh to fifth centuries BC. Both were clusters of small, independent, trading cities in contact with Greeks.

What to look for, item by item: public inscriptions of laws set up in a central square; annual or rotating magistracies replacing a king; a council or assembly with recorded decisions; a word or formula for placing power or property "in the middle"; officials required to give accounts; any surviving text arguing about who should rule.

Falsifying finding: the cities had the many-city condition, with intense competition and copying of institutions, but kept kings or closed councils, wrote no laws for public display and left no argument about rule. On the evidence we have, Phoenicia and Etruria look like this: Phoenician cities kept their kings, and the Etruscan cities, though some seem to have replaced kings with elected magistrates, left no political literature we know of.

Rescuing finding, and the caveat a careful answer must state: the record for both regions is thin. Phoenician literature survives almost only in inscriptions and in Greek and Hebrew reports; Etruscan is barely readable beyond short texts. Absence of political argument in a record this small is weak evidence. The explanation would be rescued by inscriptions showing rotating offices and public law in Phoenician or Etruscan cities, which would show the competition at work and blame the silence on the loss of texts. The test is therefore honest only if it is run on institutions (which leave inscriptions) rather than on theory (which leaves books).
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Anacharsis said written laws are spiders' webs: they catch the weak and are torn by the strong. Write the strongest one-paragraph version of his position for a city in which the law is written and public, so that his point can no longer be that the law is hidden. What does his position become?

<Solution>
One strong version: "In this city the law is in plain view, and that is the trick. Who wrote it? The men strong enough to sit on the commission, or to make the assembly vote as they wished. They wrote rules that protect what they already have, called the rules 'justice', and taught everyone else that obeying them is right. When a poor man breaks the law he is punished; when the powerful find a law inconvenient they change it by decree, or ignore it and pay the fine. The law in the middle is not neutral ground between the strong and the weak; it is the strong man's interest written down and given a goddess's name. Publicity does not cure this; it only makes the arrangement look fair."

What his position becomes: no longer a complaint that law is weak, but a theory of what law is, namely the interest of whoever rules, dressed as a common standard. That is the thesis Thrasymachus puts to Socrates, "justice is the advantage of the stronger", which <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" /> takes up: the polis placed justice in the middle, and this is the first serious argument that what sits there is only power.
</Solution>

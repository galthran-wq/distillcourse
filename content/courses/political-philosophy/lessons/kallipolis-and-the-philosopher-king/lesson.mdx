To find out what justice is in a person, Socrates proposes to build a whole city from scratch and watch justice appear in it. The city needs farmers, soldiers and rulers; the question is who belongs in which class, who decides, and what the citizens must be told to keep the arrangement standing. Each answer rests on a claim about human nature, and the city stands only if every one of those claims is true.

The proposal answers the demand Glaucon and Adeimantus left on the table at the end of <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="reading-justice-in-large-letters">their challenge</Ref>: show what justice does inside the person who has it, "whether seen or unseen by gods and men". The parts of a soul cannot be watched. The parts of a city can, so Socrates offers to read justice first in these large letters and then look for the same pattern in the small ones.

The obvious way to read justice off a city would be to take a good one that exists and describe it, and the obvious candidate is Athens, the city its leading citizen called <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="what-holds-the-city-together">"the school of Hellas"</Ref>. Thrasymachus has already blocked that road. He took what Athens's envoys told the Melians, that the strong do what they can, and <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="thrasymachus-the-law-is-what-the-rulers-interest-says-it-is">carried it inside every city</Ref>: in any actual city, what is called justice is whatever serves the people in power, so describing an actual city's justice describes its rulers. Socrates needs a city in which every part is there for a reason anyone can check. So he admits nothing into it unless something forces it in: "let us begin and create in idea a State; and yet the true creator is necessity." Every institution of the resulting city is the answer to a problem the step before it created, and every answer takes something for granted about what human beings are like. Later in the dialogue Socrates once calls this city *Kallipolis*, "the beautiful city"; most of the time it is simply "our city", the city built in speech.

## Need makes the first city; differences of nature divide its labour

The construction begins with the simplest question a founder can ask, why people live in cities at all, and Socrates's answer is need.

  src="./figures/acropolis-over-the-city.jpg"
  alt="Photograph of the Acropolis of Athens seen from Philopappos Hill: the walled limestone rock with the Parthenon on its summit, the Odeion of Herodes Atticus and pine woods on its slope, modern houses at its foot, and the city spreading behind it to the peak of Lycabettus."
  caption="Athens from the Hill of the Muses: the fortified rock with its temples rising above the houses spread around it. Socrates builds his city from need, without a site; the city every one of his listeners knew had grown around a citadel like this one."

<Quote source="Republic II, 369b–c, trans. B. Jowett">
A State, I said, arises, as I conceive, out of the needs of mankind; no one is self-sufficing, but all of us have many wants. […] Then, as we have many wants, and many persons are needed to supply them, one takes a helper for one purpose and another for another; and when these partners and helpers are gathered together in one habitation the body of inhabitants is termed a State.
</Quote>

### One man, one art

The first needs are food, shelter and clothing, so the smallest city is "four or five men": a farmer, a builder, a weaver, a shoemaker. Socrates then asks how they should divide the work. The farmer could grow food for all four, working four times as long at farming; or he could grow a quarter as much for himself alone and spend the other three quarters of his time building his own house, weaving his own coat and making his own shoes.

Adeimantus chooses the first, and Socrates gives two reasons. The first is about people: "we are not all alike; there are diversities of natures among us which are adapted to different occupations." The second is about the work: a job "is spoilt when not done at the right time", because "business is not disposed to wait until the doer of the business is at leisure". Seed has to go in when the season comes, whatever else the farmer was making that week. So "all things are produced more plentifully and easily and of a better quality when one man does one thing which is natural to him and does it at the right time".

From that principle the city grows by itself. The farmer cannot make his own plough if it is to be any good, so smiths and carpenters join. Ploughs need oxen and weavers need fleeces, so herdsmen join. No site produces everything, so someone has to fetch what is missing from other cities, and he must carry something they want in exchange, so the farmers and craftsmen must produce a surplus, and merchants and sailors join. Inside the city people now need to swap goods, so there is a market and "a money-token for purposes of exchange". A farmer who brings his corn to market when nobody is buying should not have to sit there idle, so shopkeepers join to buy from him and sell later. Last come hired labourers, men who sell their strength for wages.

### Where justice is not yet

Socrates asks where justice is in this city. Adeimantus guesses that it lies "in the dealings of these citizens with one another", and Socrates lets the guess stand and describes the life instead. The citizens eat barley cakes and wheat loaves on mats of reeds, drink their own wine with garlands on their heads, sing to the gods, and keep their families small, "having an eye to poverty or war". Nothing in this city calls for a government, because nobody's work gets in anybody else's way.

**What the step assumes.** People differ by nature in ways that fit them for different work, and the differences are real enough to sort them by. The division of labour is not a convenience the citizens choose; it follows from need plus those differences.

From the most admired city in Greece one would expect the opposite rule. Pericles praised the Athenian who works at his own trade and still judges public business, and called the man who takes no part in it <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-citizen-who-judges">"useless"</Ref>. One man, one art, carried to its end, will take public business out of the shoemaker's hands for the same reason it keeps the farmer from making his own plough.

## Luxury makes war, war makes an army, and the army is the danger

A city in which nobody needs ruling cannot show who should rule. What gives the city a government is a complaint about the food. Glaucon breaks in: the simple city has no relishes on its table, and when Socrates adds salt, olives and cheese, Glaucon is still not satisfied: "if you were providing for a city of pigs, how else would you feed the beasts?" People who are to be comfortable lie on couches, dine off tables and eat "sauces and sweets in the modern style".

From Socrates's own words one would expect him to refuse. He calls the simple city "the true and healthy constitution of the State", and Glaucon's "a State at fever-heat". He builds the fevered city anyway, on the ground that "in such a State we shall be more likely to see how justice and injustice originate". The city the rest of the *Republic* describes is not the healthy one. It is the fevered one, purged.

### From couches to war

The couches bring the rest of luxury with them: perfumes, incense, painters and embroiderers, gold and ivory, actors and poets, tutors, barbers and cooks, and many more physicians, because people living this way fall ill more often. All of them have to be fed, and "the country which was enough to support the original inhabitants will be too small now". The city needs "a slice of our neighbours' land", and the neighbours, if they too "give themselves up to the unlimited accumulation of wealth", need a slice of ours. "And so we shall go to war, Glaucon." Socrates adds that the causes of war are the same as the causes of "almost all the evils in States, private as well as public": wanting more than the land can give.

### War is a craft

Glaucon asks why the citizens cannot defend themselves. Socrates answers with the principle the city was built on. War is a craft, as much as shoemaking, and the shoemaker was forbidden to farm so that the shoes would be good. Nobody becomes a good dice player by taking the game up in his spare time, and nobody becomes a soldier by picking up a shield the day the enemy arrives. So the fevered city needs a body of full-time soldiers, chosen for fitness and trained for nothing else. Socrates calls them *guardians*.

This is a sharp break with how Greek cities actually fought. Since the seventh century their armies had been <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-hoplite-phalanx">the hoplite phalanx</Ref>, farmers and craftsmen who owned their armour and stood in the line when the city called them. One man, one art abolishes the citizen-soldier and creates a professional military class. It also denies Athens's boast about itself: Pericles had claimed that Athenians, who live as they please, match the drilled Spartans with courage <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="what-holds-the-city-together">"not of art but of nature"</Ref>. If war is a craft, courage without art is an amateur's, and no city can stake its survival on it.

### The guard is the problem

Now the city contains something the healthy city never had: a body of trained, armed men living among farmers and shoemakers who cannot fight. The guardians are the strongest thing in the city. Nothing yet stops them from taking what they like from the people they guard, and Socrates names the danger exactly:

<Quote source="Republic III, 416a, trans. B. Jowett">
To keep watch-dogs, who, from want of discipline or hunger, or some evil habit or other, would turn upon the sheep and worry them, and behave not like dogs but wolves, would be a foul and monstrous thing in a shepherd?
</Quote>

Every care must be taken, he concludes, that the soldiers, "being stronger than our citizens, may not grow to be too much for them and become savage tyrants instead of friends and allies".

This is Thrasymachus's problem coming back inside Socrates's own city. Whoever holds the strength can write the rules for his own advantage, and the fevered city has just created a class that holds the strength.

Everything the city does next, its education, its censorship, its founding myth, its ban on gold and private houses for the guardians, is a device for making the armed class safe for the unarmed one.
</KeyFact>

## A dog that knows friend from foe: the guardian's nature and education

If the guardians are the danger, the first question is what kind of person to choose for the job.

### Spirited and gentle at once

A guardian has to be quick, strong and brave, and bravery needs what the Greeks called *thumos*, translated "spirit": the anger that makes a creature fearless and impossible to beat. But spirited natures "are apt to be savage with one another, and with everybody else". A guardian must be "dangerous to their enemies, and gentle to their friends", and the two qualities look like opposites. Socrates concludes for a moment that a good guardian is impossible.

The way out is a dog. A well-bred dog is gentle to people it knows and fierce to strangers, even though the stranger has done it no harm and the acquaintance no good. So the dog sorts friend from foe by one test, knowing or not knowing:

<Quote source="Republic II, 376a–b, trans. B. Jowett">
And surely this instinct of the dog is very charming;—your dog is a true philosopher. […] Why, because he distinguishes the face of a friend and of an enemy only by the criterion of knowing and not knowing. And must not an animal be a lover of learning who determines what he likes and dislikes by the test of knowledge and ignorance?
</Quote>

*Philosopher* in Greek means "lover of wisdom". The joke has a serious edge: the guardian's gentleness is to come from what he knows, so a love of learning is part of his nature from the start. The word that will end the construction enters it here.

### Stories come before reasons

The right nature is not enough; it has to be raised. Greek education had two parts, gymnastics for the body and what the Greeks called music, which covered poetry and stories as well as song. Socrates begins with stories, because children hear them before they can do anything else:

<Quote source="Republic II, 378d–e, trans. B. Jowett">
For a young person cannot judge what is allegorical and what is literal; anything that he receives into his mind at that age is likely to become indelible and unalterable; and therefore it is most important that the tales which the young first hear should be models of virtuous thoughts.
</Quote>

The consequence is censorship. Mothers and nurses are to tell only approved tales, and most of the poems Greeks actually raised their children on are struck out. Out goes the story the poet Hesiod told of the gods' beginnings, in which the god Cronus mutilates his father and is overthrown by his own son, because a boy should not learn that the greatest of the gods punished his father. Out go the wars among the gods in Homer's epics, the poems every Greek schoolboy learned by heart, because guardians must think quarrelling among themselves the basest thing there is. Out go heroes wailing over death, because soldiers must not fear it. Socrates is careful about who does what: "You and I, Adeimantus, at this moment are not poets, but founders of a State". The founders do not write the stories; they set the patterns the poets must follow. The first pattern is that a god is good, causes only good, never changes his shape and never lies.

<Aside title="What else the founders regulate">
The censorship does not stop at plots. Poets may not speak in the voices of bad characters at length, since a guardian who imitates many things practises being many things, against one man, one art. Musical modes that sound like lament or drunkenness are banned; the two that sound like a brave man in battle and a temperate man in peace are kept. Even medicine is reined in: a physician should cure the curable and not spend years nursing a chronic invalid through what Socrates calls "a lingering death", since in a well-ordered city everyone has a job to do and no time to spend being ill. Each rule applies the same test: does it make the guard brave, gentle and single-minded?
</Aside>

**What the step assumes.** Character is formed before it can judge what forms it. A guardian's loyalty cannot be argued into him at twenty if the stories of his childhood taught him the opposite, so it has to be bred into him before he can argue at all.

### The guard is tested and divided

Even well-raised guardians differ. Socrates proposes to watch them from childhood and test them three ways: by labours in which they might forget or be talked out of the conviction that the city's interest is the rule of their lives, by pains that might force them out of it, and by pleasures and terrors that might charm them out of it, "like those who take colts amid noise and tumult to see if they are of a timid nature". Those who come through at every age are made rulers.

That splits the guard in two. The word *guardian* in the full sense now belongs to the tested few who rule. The young fighters "whom we before called guardians may be more properly designated *auxiliaries*", helpers who enforce the rulers' decisions. With the farmers, craftsmen and traders of the first city, whom the text calls producers, the city now has its three classes: rulers, auxiliaries, producers.

## The metals in the soul: what the rulers tell the ruled

The three classes exist on paper. The difficulty is getting the people in them to accept their places. The producers did not choose to be producers. The auxiliaries are stronger than the rulers. Even the rulers will be tempted to treat the city as theirs. An argument will not hold such an order together across generations, because most people will never follow the argument, and those who do follow it may not like where it puts them.

### Two kinds of lie

Socrates has already prepared a tool. In the discussion of stories he separated two kinds of falsehood. The *true lie* is ignorance in the soul about the most important things, and nobody wants it; no one wishes to be deceived in the highest part of himself. The *lie in words* is only a copy of that, and it is sometimes useful: against enemies; with a friend who, "in a fit of madness", is about to do harm, as when you refuse the madman his sword in <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="the-returned-sword">Cephalus's case</Ref>; and in stories about the distant past, where nobody knows the truth anyway. Then he restricts who may use it:

<Quote source="Republic III, 389b–c, trans. B. Jowett">
Then if any one at all is to have the privilege of lying, the rulers of the State should be the persons; and they, in their dealings either with enemies or with their own citizens, may be allowed to lie for the public good. But nobody else should meddle with anything of the kind; and although the rulers have this privilege, for a private man to lie to them in return is to be deemed a more heinous fault than for the patient or the pupil of a gymnasium not to speak the truth about his own bodily illnesses to the physician or to the trainer.
</Quote>

The lie is medicine. Like any drug it is dangerous, so only the physicians, here the rulers, may prescribe it, and the patients must tell their physicians the truth.

### The tale of the metals

Now Socrates asks for "just one royal lie which may deceive the rulers, if that be possible, and at any rate the rest of the city". He hesitates so long that Glaucon tells him to speak and fear not.

<Quote source="Republic III, 414d–415d, trans. B. Jowett">
They are to be told that their youth was a dream, and the education and training which they received from us, an appearance only; in reality during all that time they were being formed and fed in the womb of the earth, where they themselves and their arms and appurtenances were manufactured; when they were completed, the earth, their mother, sent them up; and so, their country being their mother and also their nurse, they are bound to advise for her good, and to defend her against attacks, and her citizens they are to regard as children of the earth and their own brothers. […] Citizens, we shall say to them in our tale, you are brothers, yet God has framed you differently. Some of you have the power of command, and in the composition of these he has mingled gold, wherefore also they have the greatest honour; others he has made of silver, to be auxiliaries; others again who are to be husbandmen and craftsmen he has composed of brass and iron; and the species will generally be preserved in the children. But as all are of the same original stock, a golden parent will sometimes have a silver son, or a silver parent a golden son. […] For an oracle says that when a man of brass or iron guards the State, it will be destroyed. Such is the tale; is there any possibility of making our citizens believe in it?
</Quote>

Glaucon answers: "Not in the present generation; […] but their sons may be made to believe in the tale, and their sons' sons, and posterity after them."

The tale has two halves, and each does a separate job. The first half makes the land the citizens' mother, so that defending it is defending one's mother and every fellow citizen is a brother. The second half makes the class order a fact about what each person is made of, placed there by a god. It also gives the rulers their first duty: to watch what metal each child carries and move it to the class it fits, without pity, "for if the son of a golden or silver parent has an admixture of brass and iron, then nature orders a transposition of ranks". The Greek phrase usually translated "noble lie" is *gennaion pseudos*. *Gennaion* means "well-born" and so "noble", and also "grand" or "high-minded"; the translation used here renders it "royal".

**What the step assumes.** The many cannot be governed by the truth about why they are governed. The city's whole theory says the differences between natures are real; the tale claims no more than the theory does. What it adds is a cause (a god mixed the metals) and a feeling (we are brothers), because a true account of why some rule and others obey would not be believed, or would not be loved. The city holds the order natural and concedes that nature alone will not be believed; it never says how rulers who know the truth would sort gold from silver once the story is in the citizens' heads and not in theirs.

### The guardians' camp

The tale tells the auxiliaries they are brothers of the people they guard. Socrates does not rely on the tale alone. He also removes what the guard could prey on the city for. The guardians have no property beyond necessities, no house or storeroom anyone may not enter, a fixed yearly ration paid by the citizens, and common meals, "like soldiers in a camp". They may not touch gold or silver, and the tale gives the reason: "the diviner metal is within them". Then he says what happens if the rule is broken:

<Quote source="Republic III, 417a–b, trans. B. Jowett">
But should they ever acquire homes or lands or moneys of their own, they will become housekeepers and husbandmen instead of guardians, enemies and tyrants instead of allies of the other citizens; hating and being hated, plotting and being plotted against, they will pass their whole life in much greater terror of internal than of external enemies, and the hour of ruin, both to themselves and to the rest of the State, will be at hand.
</Quote>

The watch-dog problem has its answer in two parts. Education makes the guards want to protect the sheep, and the camp leaves them nothing to gain by eating them.

## Justice is each part doing its own: first in the city, then in the soul

The city is finished in outline. Socrates now has to find in it the thing it was built to show. He assumes that a rightly ordered city has the four virtues the Greeks commonly listed: wisdom, courage, temperance (self-control) and justice. If three of them can be located, justice will be whatever is left.

### Three virtues located, the fourth left over

**Wisdom.** The city has many kinds of knowledge, carpentry, smithing, farming, but none of them makes the city wise; carpentry makes it good at carpentry. The knowledge that "advises, not about any particular thing in the State, but about the whole" belongs to the rulers. They are the smallest class, so the whole city is wise through its smallest part.

**Courage.** A city is called brave or cowardly according to its soldiers. Their courage is the preservation, through pleasure, pain, desire and fear, of the opinion the law taught them about what is and is not to be feared. Socrates compares it to dyeing wool sea-purple: dyers prepare the white wool with care first, so that the colour takes fast and no washing can remove it. The guardians' education was the preparation, and the law's opinion is the dye. Pleasure, he adds, is a stronger solvent than any soap.

**Temperance.** Self-mastery means the better part of something ruling the worse. The city is temperate when the many desires of the many are held down by the wisdom of the few, and when rulers and ruled agree about who should rule. Unlike wisdom and courage, temperance sits in no single class. It runs through all of them "like a harmony".

**Justice.** What is left over has been in view from the beginning, "tumbling out at our feet": the principle on which the first city was founded, that each person should do the one thing his nature fits him for. Justice is each class doing its own work and not meddling in another's. Socrates tests it on two cases. A carpenter and a cobbler who swap trades do the city little harm. But when a man whom nature made a trader, "having his heart lifted up by wealth or strength or the number of his followers", pushes into the class of soldiers, or a soldier into the class of rulers, "this meddling of one with another is the ruin of the State". That is the greatest injustice a citizen can do.

The answer takes a side in a quarrel <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="two-equalities-the-city-could-not-reconcile">the Greek cities had never settled</Ref>, between equality as equal shares for everyone and equality as shares proportioned to worth. Kallipolis gives each person the place his nature fits, the second kind, and it measures worth by nature and education, not by property.

### The soul has three parts

Socrates now has to show the same pattern in a single person, and for that the soul must have parts. He starts from a principle: the same thing cannot do opposite things at the same time, with the same part of itself, toward the same object. When an archer draws a bow, we do not say that his hands push and pull the bow at the same time; one hand pushes and the other pulls. Where opposite motions occur at once, there are two things doing them.

Now take a man who is thirsty and refuses to drink, say because he knows the water will make him ill. Thirst, taken by itself, is a pull toward drink and nothing else. Something in him pulls toward the cup and something holds him back, so by the principle they are two different things. The part that calculates and forbids is *reason*; the part that "loves and hungers and thirsts" is *appetite*.

Is anger a third part, or a kind of appetite? Socrates tells a story:

<Quote source="Republic IV, 439e–440a, trans. B. Jowett">
The story is, that Leontius, the son of Aglaion, coming up one day from the Piraeus, under the north wall on the outside, observed some dead bodies lying on the ground at the place of execution. He felt a desire to see them, and also a dread and abhorrence of them; for a time he struggled and covered his eyes, but at length the desire got the better of him; and forcing them open, he ran up to the dead bodies, saying, Look, ye wretches, take your fill of the fair sight.
</Quote>

Leontius is angry at his own eyes, so anger here is fighting against a desire. It cannot be the same thing as the desire it fights, so it is not appetite. It is not reason either, since children and animals have plenty of it long before they reason. It is the third part, *spirit*, the same *thumos* the guardians were chosen for. In a conflict between reason and appetite it takes reason's side, and Socrates names its role in the city's terms: it obeys "the voice of the shepherd, that is, reason, bidding his dog bark no more." Glaucon draws the parallel himself: the auxiliaries were to be dogs, and to hear the voice of the rulers, who are their shepherds.

### Justice is the soul's health

With three parts in place the virtues transfer one by one. A person is wise when reason, which knows what is good for each part and for the whole, rules; brave when spirit holds to reason's commands through pleasure and pain; temperate when all three agree that reason should rule. And a person is just when each part does its own work, with reason and spirit together keeping watch over appetite, "which in each of us is the largest part of the soul and by nature most insatiable of gain". Injustice is a part rising up against the whole, "a rebellious subject against a true prince".

Then the comparison that answers Glaucon:

<Quote source="Republic IV, 444c–d, trans. B. Jowett">
Why, I said, they are like disease and health; being in the soul just what disease and health are in the body. […] And is not the creation of justice the institution of a natural order and government of one by another in the parts of the soul, and the creation of injustice the production of a state of things at variance with the natural order?
</Quote>

Glaucon's challenge was to place justice among the goods wanted for themselves and not only for their results. Health is the standard example of such a good: wanted for itself, and for what it lets you do. If justice is the soul's health, the ring of Gyges cannot touch it. Invisibility takes away the rewards of seeming just; it cannot take away the condition of a soul in which appetite obeys. The unjust ring-wearer gets his goods at the price of living with a soul at war with itself. Socrates adds that the division of labour in the first city was only "a shadow of justice". The real thing concerns "not the outward man, but the inward".

**What the step assumes.** The three classes of the city mirror three parts of the soul, and in both the ruling part is the smallest. The text says it in so many words: "the meaner desires of the many are held down by the virtuous desires and wisdom of the few".

<KeyFact>
If justice is the rule of reason in the soul, and only the smallest class has reason strong enough to rule, then most citizens of Kallipolis can be just only by obeying the reason of others: their justice is the order they live in, not one they hold.
</KeyFact>

## Three waves: women, children, philosophers

With justice found in the city and in the soul, Socrates turns at the start of Book V to its opposite, the forms of injustice: four worse cities, and the four kinds of man that match them. Before he can begin, the others stop him. Earlier he had said in passing that the guardians would hold wives and children in common, "as friends have all things in common", and they want to know what that means. He agrees, and warns them that each proposal he now has to defend is a wave that may drown him in laughter.

### First wave: women guardians

The obvious objection uses the city's own principle against it. Men and women differ by nature, and one man, one art says different natures should do different work; so women cannot be guardians. Socrates replies by asking which differences count. Bald men and hairy men differ by nature too, and it would be a joke to forbid hairy men to be cobblers because bald men are. The principle only ever meant differences that bear on the work. That women bear children and men beget them bears on nothing a guardian does. Socrates holds that women are on the whole the weaker in every pursuit, but that the natures fit for guarding, healing or ruling are found in both sexes. So women with guardian natures get the guardians' education, exercise with them, and go to war with them, with the lighter tasks.

### Second wave: no private families

The second wave is larger: "the wives of our guardians are to be common, and their children are to be common, and no parent is to know his own child, nor any child his parent." The reason is the city's greatest good. Nothing harms a city more than division, and division mostly starts with disagreement over the words "mine" and "not mine". The best-ordered city is the one where the most people say "mine" of the same things, and where the whole city feels a citizen's good or bad fortune as a body feels a hurt finger. Private families give each guardian a household of his own to prefer to the city, so they are abolished.

The arrangements are stated flatly in the text. The rulers will breed the guardians as Glaucon breeds his hunting dogs, from the best and from those in their prime. Marriages happen at festivals, paired by lot, but the lots are rigged: "We shall have to invent some ingenious kind of lots which the less worthy may draw on each occasion of our bringing them together, and then they will accuse their own ill-luck and not the rulers." Socrates says outright that the rulers "will find a considerable dose of falsehood and deceit necessary for the good of their subjects". Children are taken at birth to nurses in a separate quarter, and mothers are brought to suckle with care "that no mother recognises her own child". The children of the inferior, and any born deformed, "will be put away in some mysterious, unknown place". Every child born seven to ten months after a festival calls every man married at it father.

**What the step assumes.** Attachment to one's own is the root of faction, and it can be taken from the family and given to the city whole.

### Third wave: philosophers must rule

Glaucon now asks whether any of this is possible. Socrates first answers that it need not be shown possible to be useful: a painter who paints a perfectly beautiful man is no worse a painter because no such man has ever lived, and the city is a pattern of the same kind. Then he names the smallest change that would bring an actual city close to it:

<Quote source="Republic V, 473c–d, trans. B. Jowett">
Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy, and political greatness and wisdom meet in one, and those commoner natures who pursue either to the exclusion of the other are compelled to stand aside, cities will never have rest from their evils,—nor the human race, as I believe,—and then only will this our State have a possibility of life and behold the light of day.
</Quote>

Glaucon warns him that respectable men will tear off their coats and run at him with whatever weapon is to hand. So Socrates has to say who the philosopher is. He is not anyone with a taste for ideas. He loves the whole of wisdom, as a lover of wine loves every wine; the man who loves sights and sounds loves beautiful things and cannot see what beauty itself is. The difference is in what each is looking at. Beauty itself is one and the same wherever it is found; it is never beautiful in one respect and ugly in another, so a judgment about it can be fixed and true for good. That is what Socrates calls *knowledge*. A beautiful body, a beautiful custom or a beautiful speech is beautiful in one respect and ugly in another: a double is also a half, a large thing also small beside something larger. A judgment about such a thing can be right in one light and wrong in the next, and Socrates calls it *opinion*. A city that must decide what is just or good has to judge cases that look different from different sides, and only a judgment fixed on the just itself and the good itself can say which side is right. So the rulers have to know what the good is. Correct opinion, of the sort the auxiliaries were dyed with, is enough to obey a ruling but not enough to make one.

### The Ship of State

Adeimantus objects with what everyone can see: the people who spend their lives in philosophy turn out either odd or useless to their cities. Socrates grants the fact and explains it with an image.

<Quote source="Republic VI, 488a–e, trans. B. Jowett">
Imagine then a fleet or a ship in which there is a captain who is taller and stronger than any of the crew, but he is a little deaf and has a similar infirmity in sight, and his knowledge of navigation is not much better. The sailors are quarrelling with one another about the steering—every one is of opinion that he has a right to steer, though he has never learned the art of navigation and cannot tell who taught him or when he learned, and will further assert that it cannot be taught, and they are ready to cut in pieces any one who says the contrary. […] and having first chained up the noble captain's senses with drink or some narcotic drug, they mutiny and take possession of the ship and make free with the stores […] but that the true pilot must pay attention to the year and seasons and sky and stars and winds, and whatever else belongs to his art, if he intends to be really qualified for the command of a ship, […] the possibility of this union of authority with the steerer's art has never seriously entered into their thoughts. […] Will he not be called by them a prater, a star-gazer, a good-for-nothing?
</Quote>

The captain, big and strong but half-deaf and short-sighted, is the people of a democracy, who own the ship. The sailors are the politicians, competing for his favour and dulling his judgment while they help themselves to the stores. The man watching the stars is the philosopher, and he is useless only because nobody on board will let him steer.

The image names no event, but Athens's assembly had supplied its material. In 427 it voted to kill the men of a rebel city and <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="a-rebel-ally-and-a-decree">reversed the vote the next day</Ref>; in 415 <Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="two-juxtapositions">"all alike fell in love" with the expedition to Sicily</Ref> that destroyed its fleet, while the few who doubted it kept quiet for fear of seeming unpatriotic.

The image is the craft argument of Book I turned into a political proposal. Against Thrasymachus, Socrates had argued that ruling in the strict sense is a craft like <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="whom-does-a-craft-serve-the-physician-the-pilot-the-shepherd">the pilot's</Ref>, which serves those it rules. The sailors deny the premise: steering "cannot be taught", so anyone has as good a claim to the helm as anyone else. If they are right, the democratic assembly is the fair way to settle it. If there is a knowledge of steering, the ship belongs in the hands of whoever has it, whatever the crew thinks. The whole construction now rests on one question: is there something to know about ruling, and can anyone know it?

## Two objections: the unhappy guardians, and what all call "mine"

That question waits for the Cave. First, a question about the city just completed, which was built for unity: one class to each task, no family a guardian could prefer to the city, the whole feeling a citizen's hurt as a body feels a finger's. Is a city ruled for its unity good for the people who live in it?

### Inside the text: the guardians are not happy

<Example title="the painted statue">
**The objection.** At the start of Book IV Adeimantus puts it on behalf of an imagined critic. The city belongs to the guardians, yet they get nothing from it. Other rulers own land and fine houses, entertain guests and keep gold; these men are "no better than mercenaries who are quartered in the city and are always mounting guard", and are not even paid beyond their food. Socrates adds to the charge himself: they cannot travel for pleasure or keep a mistress.

**The reply, step 1.** The founders' aim "was not the disproportionate happiness of any one class, but the greatest happiness of the whole". They built a well-ordered city in order to find justice in it, and a city built for one class's happiness would not be well-ordered.

**Step 2, the statue.** A painter colouring a statue is asked why he did not paint the eyes, the most beautiful part, purple. He answers that eyes painted purple would no longer look like eyes; he gives each part its due colour so that the whole is beautiful.

**Step 3.** The same holds for classes. The founders could dress the farmers in royal robes and let the potters lounge on couches by the fire and work when they liked. Then the farmer would not be a farmer, nor the potter a potter, and if guardians become guardians "only seeming and not real", they "turn the State upside down". Each class gets "the proportion of happiness which nature assigns to them".

**Does the reply meet the objection?** It grants the premise, that the guardians lack what most people call happiness, and changes the question: the founders are not making a class happy but a city. Socrates adds that the guardians "may very likely be the happiest of men" even so, but he does not show it here. That promise is kept only later in the dialogue, by arguing that the philosopher's pleasures are the truest. So the reply holds if a city can be happy in some sense distinct from the happiness of the people in it. That is exactly the assumption the outside objection attacks.
</Example>

### Outside the text: the objection from care

The fullest objection came from Plato's own student. Aristotle (384–322 BC) studied in Plato's school at Athens for twenty years and later wrote his own book on cities, the *Politics*, which opens its survey of model constitutions with the *Republic*. His point is about what people actually look after:

<Quote source="Aristotle, Politics II.3, 1261b32–38, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
For that which is common to the greatest number has the least care bestowed upon it. Every one thinks chiefly of his own, hardly at all of the common interest; and only when he is himself concerned as an individual. For besides other considerations, everybody is more inclined to neglect the duty which he expects another to fulfil; as in families many attendants are often less useful than a few.
</Quote>

Apply it to the second wave. Each guardian will have a thousand sons, none of them his in particular, so each son will be "neglected by all alike". When every man calls every boy "mine", the word means less, not more: "love will be watery", as a little sweet wine in a great deal of water stops tasting of wine. Of the two things that make people care for something, "that a thing is your own and that it is your only one", the common family destroys both. Socrates's argument and Aristotle's share a form: each starts from what people attach to and asks what follows. They differ on one fact about human beings. Socrates thinks attachment can be moved from one's own to the whole. Aristotle thinks attachment dissolves when it is spread that thin.

<Figure
  src="./figures/all-say-mine.svg"
  alt="Two panels. Left: many adults, each joined to the same child by a faint dotted line, labelled 'every man says mine'. Right: one adult joined to one child by a single solid highlighted line, labelled 'one says mine'."
  caption="What the second wave counts as unity, the objection counts as dilution: a thousand thin ties instead of one that holds."

He presses the point to the whole city. A city is a plurality of different kinds of people; push it toward unity and it becomes a household, then a single man, and stops being a city at all. Two further charges follow from that. First, the *Republic* says almost nothing about how the producers, the great majority, are to live, own property or be educated. If they keep private families and property while the guardians give theirs up, Kallipolis is "two states in one": a garrison of guardians quartered on a city of farmers, with nothing in common to bind them, each hostile to the other. Unity was the city's aim, and the arrangement built for it splits the city in two.

Second, the same souls always rule. The god mixes gold into some souls from birth and not into others, so office never passes from one citizen to another as it does where equals take turns. The re-sorting by metal does not change this: it moves a child to the class his metal fits, and from then on the golden rule for life and everyone else never rules at all. Even among ordinary citizens, men shut out of office for good have a grievance; here those men include the silver class, trained soldiers bred for spirit and armed. A permanent ruling class over armed men who can never share its office is a standing cause of faction, the very thing the city was built to prevent.

Last, the founders take happiness away from the guardians and say the whole city is happy. But "the whole cannot be happy unless most, or all, or some of its parts enjoy happiness". Happiness is not like being an even number, which a sum can be while every part of it is odd. "And if the guardians are not happy, who are? Surely not the artisans, or the common people."

### The pattern reply meets one charge, not the objection from care

The text has a reply to one charge, that such a city cannot be built: it never claimed it could. The city is a pattern, like the painter's perfect man, and at the end of Book IX Socrates says that whether it "exists, or ever will exist in fact, is no matter", since "in heaven […] there is laid up a pattern of it" by which a man may set his own soul in order. In the dialogue's own terms the best city is possible but improbable: it waits on the chance that a philosopher is born to a king, or a king turns philosopher.

Even as a pattern, the city treats unity as the highest good of a city, and the objection from care says unity pushed that far is the destruction of a city, not its perfection. If the objection holds, the pattern carries the wrong standard. The two sides do not stand on equal footing. The dialogue asserts that attachment can be moved from one's own to the whole and offers no evidence that it can. The objection rests on things anyone can check: a duty everyone shares is left for someone else to do, and a household with many servants is served worse than one with a few. Plato's text leaves the objection unanswered.

## Out of the cave and back down: why the one who knows must be compelled

The Ship ended on whether there is something to know about ruling, and left two questions with it. What does the true pilot know that the sailors do not? And if he knows it, why would he want to come down from the stars and take the helm?

### What the ruler knows

Socrates says the highest thing to be learned is the *good*, the standard by which anything just or useful is good at all, and that without knowing it a ruler knows nothing worth having. He will not say what the good is. He offers an image instead. The sun does two things for sight: its light lets the eye see and lets things be seen, and its warmth makes living things grow. The good does the same for the mind: it makes things knowable and the mind able to know them, and it gives the things known their very being. Then he tells a story of how a person gets from where we are to there.

### The Cave

<Quote source="Republic VII, 514a–b, trans. B. Jowett">
Behold! human beings living in a underground den, which has a mouth open towards the light and reaching all along the den; here they have been from their childhood, and have their legs and necks chained so that they cannot move, and can only see before them, being prevented by the chains from turning round their heads. Above and behind them a fire is blazing at a distance, and between the fire and the prisoners there is a raised way; and you will see, if you look, a low wall built along the way, like the screen which marionette players have in front of them, over which they show the puppets.
</Quote>

Along the raised way men carry statues and figures of animals above the wall, some talking. The prisoners see only the shadows these throw on the cave's back wall and hear only echoes, so they take the shadows for the things themselves: "the truth would be literally nothing but the shadows of the images."

One prisoner is freed and made to turn round. The firelight hurts his eyes, and when he is shown the carried objects he thinks the shadows were truer. He is dragged up the steep way into the sunlight and is blinded again. Slowly he learns to see: shadows first, then reflections in water, then the things themselves, then the moon and stars at night, and last the sun, which he understands to be the cause of the seasons and of everything he used to see. He would no longer care for the prizes the prisoners gave each other for guessing which shadow would come next; he would rather, in Homer's line, be "the poor servant of a poor master" on earth than live their life. If he went back down, his eyes would be full of darkness, he would lose the shadow-guessing contests, and the prisoners would say he had ruined his eyes by going up. "And if any one tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death." Plato's first readers knew a man who had tried: Socrates himself, who spent his life questioning Athenians about what they thought they knew, and whom <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring">the democracy put to death in 399</Ref>.

Socrates explains the image himself. The prison is the world we see; the fire is the sun; the climb is the soul's ascent to what can only be understood, not seen; and at the top, "seen only with an effort", is the good, "the universal author of all things beautiful and right", which anyone "who would act rationally either in public or private life must have his eye fixed" on.

### Education turns the soul round

Two consequences follow for the founders. The first is about education. Some teachers claim to put knowledge into a soul "like sight into blind eyes". The cave shows otherwise: the prisoner's eyes worked all along; they were pointed the wrong way, and he had to turn his whole body to see the fire. Education is the art of turning the whole soul round, not of filling it. A clever rogue proves the point: his "paltry soul sees the way to his end" sharply enough, so his power of sight is intact and serves bad ends. Turned the other way in youth, the same sharpness would see the truth.

### The compulsion to descend

The second consequence is about rule. Those who have reached the good will not want to come back down. They will think themselves already living "in the islands of the blest". The founders must not allow it: "they must be made to descend again among the prisoners in the den, and partake of their labours and honours, whether they are worth having or not." Glaucon objects that this is unjust, giving them a worse life when they could have a better. Socrates answers with the principle of the statue: the law aims at the happiness of the whole city, not of one class. And he gives the philosophers a reason addressed to them. In other cities philosophers grow up on their own, owe the city nothing, and are not asked to rule. These ones the city raised and educated to rule, so they owe it their turn:

<Quote source="Republic VII, 520c–d, trans. B. Jowett">
Wherefore each of you, when his turn comes, must go down to the general underground abode, and get the habit of seeing in the dark. When you have acquired the habit, you will see ten thousand times better than the inhabitants of the den, and you will know what the several images are, and what they represent, because you have seen the beautiful and just and good in their truth. […] Whereas the truth is that the State in which the rulers are most reluctant to govern is always the best and most quietly governed, and the State in which they are most eager, the worst.
</Quote>

The reluctance is the qualification. A man who wants office wants something from it, and men who want something from office "will be fighting about office", and the city with them. The only men who can be trusted with rule are those who have "another and a better life than that of a ruler", and only philosophy offers one. This is where the remark Socrates made against Thrasymachus in Book I ends up: there he said that good men take office only under a penalty, <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="socratess-answer-and-what-it-leaves-standing">to avoid being ruled by someone worse</Ref>. Book VII supplies what Book I lacked, a reason the reluctant men are the right ones: they know the good, and they want nothing the city can give.

**What the step assumes.** There is a good that can be known, a few people can come to know it, and knowing it is what qualifies a person to rule. Every earlier step needed rulers who could sort the metals, time the marriages and tell the right lies for the right reasons. This step says who could be trusted to do it.

<Aside title="The rulers' education, by age">
Book VII lays out the schooling that produces such rulers. Children learn arithmetic and geometry through play, never by force. From about seventeen to twenty, two or three years of hard physical training. At twenty the best are chosen and study the mathematical sciences together, to see how they connect. At thirty the best of those are chosen again for five years of *dialectic*, the art of giving and testing an account of what each thing is; Socrates holds it back until then because young men who learn to argue "like puppy-dogs" refute everything and end up believing nothing. At thirty-five they go back down into the cave for fifteen years of military commands and offices. At fifty those who have come through see the good itself, and from then on they spend most of their time in philosophy and take their turn at ruling. Women with the right natures follow the same course.
</Aside>

### How such a city would begin

Socrates ends the construction with the founding itself. The founders of such a city would take the existing city "as from a tablet" and "rub out the picture, and leave a clean surface" before drawing anything. In practice: "They will begin by sending out into the country all the inhabitants of the city who are more than ten years old, and will take possession of their children, who will be unaffected by the habits of their parents." Only children who have heard no other stories can be raised on the city's.

<Conclusion>
You can now rebuild the city of the *Republic* from its first premise and say what each step takes for granted. Need and differences of nature give the first city and its rule of one man, one art. Luxury gives war, war gives a professional guard, and the guard is the danger every later institution answers: censorship forms its character before it can judge, the tale of the metals makes the class order feel like nature and kinship, the camp leaves the guards nothing to take. Justice turns out to be the founding rule itself, each part doing its own, in the city and in a soul with three parts, where it is health and so out of the ring's reach. The three waves push unity to its limit and put rule in the hands of those who know the good, and the Cave says why those are the only men who will not use it for themselves, and why they must be made to rule. Four assumptions carry the whole: that natures differ and can be sorted, that most people must be shaped and told a story rather than persuaded, that unity is the highest good of a city, and that there is a good the few can know. The objection from care attacks the third, and Plato's text has no answer to it.

A city held together this way needs every generation sorted correctly and every marriage timed right. <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" /> takes up what happens the first time the rulers get it wrong, and how the best city declines, regime by regime, into tyranny.
</Conclusion>

Who and when: Plato wrote it around 380 BC, in the *Republic*. In the dialogue Socrates speaks it to Glaucon, in a conversation about founding an imaginary city; within the story, the tale is meant to be told first to the rulers, then to the soldiers, then to the people.

Records: nothing about any real city. It is a proposal, a story the founders would tell, and Glaucon at once doubts that the present generation would believe it.

Argues: that a founding story can make a class order feel natural and make citizens treat each other and the land as kin. It also argues, through the oracle, that the order matters: "when a man of brass or iron guards the State, it will be destroyed."

Leaves out: how the rulers are to tell a child's metal at birth, who checks the rulers' sorting, and what the producers will make of a story they are told and the rulers are only told "if possible". In Kallipolis the rulers lie to the ruled, and a private man who lies to the rulers commits the worse fault; the lie runs downward and is the rulers' monopoly. The two texts agree that a lie can be a remedy for a distrust argument cannot cure: the assembly's suspicion of its advisers, the many's inability to follow why they are governed. They assign it to opposite parties because they put the knower in opposite places. At Athens the one who knows serves a sovereign who does not, and has to deceive it to serve it; in Kallipolis the one who knows is the sovereign. Diodotus also describes the lie as a defect of the assembly that he would rather be without, while Socrates prescribes it as part of the design. One text reports a failure of speech among equals, the other builds on the assumption that speech among unequals will fail.

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "The tale of the metals sets up a caste system: rank is fixed by birth." Name the sentence of the tale that breaks the claim. Then say what the claim gets right.

<Solution>
The claim fails on the tale's second half. "A golden parent will sometimes have a silver son, or a silver parent a golden son", and the god's first command to the rulers is to watch for it: if a guardian's child has brass or iron in it, "nature orders a transposition of ranks, and the eye of the ruler must not be pitiful towards the child because he has to descend", and sons of craftsmen with gold or silver in them "are raised to honour". In a caste system the child's rank is the parent's; here it is the child's own nature, and the rulers' central duty is to move children against their parentage when the two disagree. The second wave confirms it: nobody among the guardians even knows who his parents are.

What the claim gets right: rank in Kallipolis is not chosen by the person who holds it, is not earned by effort or argued for in an assembly, and is presented as given by a god at birth. Those who are sorted are meant to accept the sorting as their nature, and the tale says that "the species will generally be preserved in the children", so in most cases children will in fact end up where their parents were. The order is mobile in principle and hereditary in the usual case, and it is not open to the people inside it to contest.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Set the number of philosophers to zero. The city is built, the auxiliaries are raised on the approved stories and live in their camp, the tale is told, but nobody in the city has seen the good. Which of the four virtues can the city still have, which can it not, and what does it then have to rely on to keep each part doing its own? What happens to it over time?

<Solution>
Courage survives. It was defined as the soldiers' preservation of the opinion the law implanted about what is to be feared, the dye fixed by education. It needs correct opinion, not knowledge, and the stories and training supply opinion.

Temperance survives for a while. It is agreement between rulers and ruled about who should rule, and that agreement too is opinion, which the tale was designed to produce.

Wisdom cannot exist. It was the knowledge that counsels "about the whole", held by the ruling class; with no one who knows the good, the city has at best rulers with correct opinions inherited from the founders.

Justice, each part doing its own, survives only as long as the opinions that hold the parts in place survive. Nobody can correct them, because nobody knows why they are correct. The city now relies entirely on its stories and on custom.

Over time it decays. The rulers must time the marriages and judge each child's metal, and those are judgments of knowledge, not of habit. Without knowledge, mistakes accumulate: a child with silver or iron is left among the rulers, the tale stops matching what people see, and the agreement about who should rule breaks down. Socrates draws the distinction this exercise leans on in Book V: opinion is enough to obey and to keep a good order once it exists, and not enough to found or repair one. That is why the third wave is the condition of the other two.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Derive the second wave from first principles, then derive the objection to it. (a) Starting only from "the greatest good of a city is unity" and "division begins with 'mine' and 'not mine'", derive the abolition of the guardians' private families and their common meals. (b) Starting from "what is common to the greatest number has the least care bestowed upon it", derive the opposite conclusion. (c) Show that the two arguments share a form and differ in one claim about human beings, and say what kind of evidence would decide between them.

<Solution>
(a) If unity is the greatest good, whatever divides the city is the greatest evil. Division starts when citizens disagree over what is "mine" and "not mine", when one man's gain is another's loss or one rejoices while another grieves. A private household is a "mine" that is not the city's: a man with his own wife, children and property has something to prefer to the city and to defend against it. So the guardians, the class whose loyalty the city most needs, must have no private household. Their women and children are held in common, so that each guardian says "mine" of the same people, and they eat together, so that even meals are not private. The city then feels a guardian's misfortune as a body feels a hurt finger.

(b) If what is common to many is cared for least, then making children common to a thousand fathers means no father cares for any child in particular; each expects another to do it. Affection spread across a thousand is "watery". The two things that make a person care for something, that it is his own and that it is his only one, are exactly what the common family takes away. So the abolition of the family weakens attachment, the bond of the city, and the city is less united, not more. Keep private families and property, and let use be shared through generosity.

(c) Both arguments have the form: people attach to X in such a way; so arrange the city to direct that attachment toward unity. Both agree that attachment is what holds a city together. They differ on one claim: whether attachment can be transferred from one's own to the whole without loss (Socrates), or whether it is strong only toward what is one's own and thins out when it is spread (the objection). Evidence that would decide it is evidence about real communities that have tried to share children and property: whether children raised in common are cared for as well as children raised by parents, and whether members of such communities show more loyalty to the whole than members of ordinary families do to their city. That is an empirical question about human nature, which neither argument settles by reasoning. The two sides do not start level on it: the objection already points to evidence anyone can check (shared duties left to others, many servants doing less than a few), while the dialogue offers none for the claim that attachment survives the transfer.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Someone claims: "Adeimantus's objection and Aristotle's are the same objection: both say the guardians are unhappy." Name two facts the claim fails to account for, and state what would have to be true for it to survive.

<Solution>
Fact 1: the text answers one and not the other. Adeimantus's objection is answered inside the dialogue with the painted statue: the founders aim at the happiness of the whole, not of one class. Aristotle attacks that answer itself: "the whole cannot be happy unless most, or all, or some of its parts enjoy happiness", since happiness is not like evenness, which a whole can have while its parts lack it. His objection begins where Socrates's reply ends.

Fact 2: most of Aristotle's objection is not about happiness at all. His main charge is about care and attachment: what is common to many is neglected, a thousand fathers make no father, affection becomes watery. He also says the producers' way of life is left undefined, so the city is "two states in one", and that the same souls always rule, which breeds faction among spirited men. None of these is about the guardians' contentment; they say the city would not be united or stable, the very goods it was built for.

For the claim to survive, it would have to be narrowed to one sentence of Aristotle's: his remark that the guardians are deprived of happiness. On that sentence the two critics agree about the fact. Even then they differ in kind: Adeimantus reports what the guardians lack by ordinary standards, and Aristotle argues that the founders' concept of a happy whole is incoherent.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Set two passages side by side. Book I: in a city of good men, people "would compete to avoid office as they now compete to win it", and good men rule only to avoid being ruled by someone worse. Book VII: "the State in which the rulers are most reluctant to govern is always the best and most quietly governed, and the State in which they are most eager, the worst." Where do the passages agree? What has been added between them? What judgment follows about why the philosopher must be compelled?

<Solution>
Agreement: both say that the right rulers do not want to rule, and that eagerness for office is a bad sign. Both rest on the Book I point that ruling, as a craft, brings the ruler no good of its own, so whoever seeks office seeks something else from it, money or honour.

What has been added: in Book I the reluctant ruler is any good man, and the only thing that moves him is a penalty, the prospect of being ruled by someone worse. By Book VII the reluctant ruler is a man who has seen the good. Two things follow that Book I lacked. First, he has knowledge of what ruling is for, so his reluctance comes with competence; Book I's good man was reluctant but not shown to know anything. Second, he has "another and a better life than that of a ruler", philosophy, so his reluctance has a cause: he is not indifferent to goods, he has a greater one. Book VII also adds a reason for compulsion addressed to him: the city educated him for this, so he owes it his turn.

Judgment: the philosopher must be compelled because the qualities that make him fit to rule make him unwilling to. He knows the good, and knowing it he prefers contemplating it to managing prisoners who will mock or kill him. A city that waited for him to volunteer would never be ruled by him, and a city ruled by volunteers would be ruled by those who want something from it, the "worst". Compulsion is therefore built into the design. The cost, which Glaucon names, is that the best men are given a worse life than they could have; the reply is the statue again, that the law aims at the whole.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The rulers must time the marriages and judge each child's metal. Suppose they get one judgment wrong: a child with silver or iron in him is raised among the rulers, or a golden child is left in the fields. Trace what happens over the next generation or two. Which of the four virtues fails first, and what kind of man and city do you expect to result?

<Solution>
Take the first case. A child of silver or iron is raised as a ruler. He has the rulers' education and the rulers' power but not their nature: in him reason does not rule spirit and appetite, so what he wants from office is honour, or, if he is iron, gain. He will not see what the rulers' dialectic is for, and he will judge the next children by his own lights, so the sorting gets worse, not better. Among the guardians, some will now value victory and honour above knowledge; others may want the gold and land they are forbidden.

The first virtue to fail is wisdom, because it lived in the smallest class and that class now contains men without it. Temperance follows: the agreement about who should rule breaks down, since the men of silver and iron among the rulers will not accept that they are ruled by reason, and the golden child left in the fields has a claim the order denies. Then the camp's rules weaken: rulers who want gold will divide the land and the houses among themselves and turn the citizens into their subjects, the "watch-dogs turned wolves" the camp was designed to prevent.

The likely result is first a city ruled by men who love honour and war more than wisdom, and after that, as the love of gold gets in, a city ruled by men who love wealth. You have just stated the opening of Book VIII: the account of how the best city decays, which Socrates puts in the mouths of the Muses, starting from marriages timed wrongly and children of the wrong metal raised as rulers, and passing through a regime of honour and a regime of wealth toward democracy and tyranny. <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" /> works through that sequence.
</Solution>

Three old men walk to the cave of Zeus on Crete and plan a real colony. They cannot have philosopher kings, so they have to say what a city can do with ordinary men: how much land each may hold, how laws should be worded to persuade before they compel, and what to do with a citizen who says there are no gods. The lesson reads the plan as Plato's own second thoughts.

The three men were met in <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-second-telling-two-mother-forms">the chapter on the decline of regimes</Ref>: they are the speakers of the *Laws*, Plato's last and longest dialogue, written in the 350s BC. One is an unnamed Athenian, who does nearly all the talking; the others are Cleinias, a Cretan, and Megillus, a Spartan. In Book III the Athenian looked back at history and found Sparta saved by a mixture of kings, elders and ephors. At the end of that book the talk changes from history to a commission. Cleinias tells the other two that most of Crete is sending out a colony to an empty site, that the city of Cnossus has been put in charge, and that he is one of ten men chosen to give it laws, "whether taken from the Cretan model or from any other". He asks them to found the city in speech first, so that he can use what they build.

Cnossus, the Knossos of the <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="what-the-palace-was">Bronze Age palace</Ref>, lies near the north coast of Crete; the cave where Zeus was said to have been hidden as an infant is on Mount Ida, in the middle of the island, a long day's climb away. The conversation fills that walk. Socrates is not in it. The *Laws* is the only one of Plato's dialogues from which he is absent, and the Athenian takes his place.

<Figure src="./figures/idaean-cave.jpg" alt="A wide, dark cave mouth at the foot of a grey limestone cliff streaked with scrub, a dirt path with old cart rails leading up to it" caption="The Idaean cave on Mount Ida, where Zeus was said to have been hidden as an infant: the end of the walk from Cnossus." float="right" width={380} />

The city of the *Republic* stood on one kind of person. Its rulers sorted every child by <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="the-tale-of-the-metals">the metal in its soul</Ref>; they told the rest of the city a tale, as a physician prescribes a drug, because the truth about why some rule would not be believed; they held no property and kept no families; and they ruled only because, having seen the good, they were <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="the-compulsion-to-descend">compelled to come back down</Ref>. Every institution assumed a ruler who knows. Socrates himself said the city waits on the chance that a philosopher is born to a king or a king turns philosopher.

A colony of Cretans cannot wait on that chance. So the question the walk has to answer is what a city can do when nobody in it knows the good.

The obvious answer is to keep the *Republic*'s institutions and staff them with the best men available. It fails at each one. A tale told by a ruler who does not know is only a lie. Common property and common families, imposed by men who cannot sort natures, are only confiscation. Compelling men to rule who have seen nothing is only force. What the colony needs is something that rules without depending on anyone's knowledge.

## Second best: the pattern kept, the knower removed

Before he gives the colony its land, the Athenian stops to say where this city stands among the possible ones. He compares the step to an unusual move in a Greek board game:

<Quote source="Laws V, 739a–e, trans. B. Jowett">
The next move in our pastime of legislation, like the withdrawal of the stone from the holy line in the game of draughts, being an unusual one, will probably excite wonder when mentioned for the first time. And yet, if a man will only reflect and weigh the matter with care, he will see that our city is ordered in a manner which, if not the best, is the second best. […] The first and highest form of the state and of the government and of the law is that in which there prevails most widely the ancient saying, that 'Friends have all things in common.' […] Whether such a state is governed by Gods or sons of Gods, one, or more than one, happy are the men who, living after this manner, dwell there; and therefore to this we are to look for the pattern of the state, and to cling to this, and to seek with all our might for one which is like this.
</Quote>

The first city is the *Republic*'s, described by its <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="second-wave-no-private-families">second wave</Ref>: wives, children and property in common, and even what is "by nature private, such as eyes and ears and hands", seeing and acting in common. It is not given up. It remains "the pattern". What changes is who could live in it: "Gods or sons of Gods". The *Republic* said philosophers.

The second city is the one the three men are building. Its citizens are to "distribute their land and houses, and not till the land in common, since a community of goods goes beyond their proposed origin, and nurture, and education". A third city is promised and never described in the work as it survives.

### The prayer for a young tyrant

The *Laws* also keeps the *Republic*'s formula for how the best city could begin. In Book IV the Athenian asks what a legislator would pray for if he could have any conditions, and answers for him:

<Quote source="Laws IV, 709e–710b, trans. B. Jowett">
He will say—'Give me a state which is governed by a tyrant, and let the tyrant be young and have a good memory; let him be quick at learning, and of a courageous and noble nature […]' Then our tyrant must have this as well as the other qualities, if the state is to acquire in the best manner and in the shortest time the form of government which is most conducive to happiness; for there neither is nor ever will be a better or speedier way of establishing a polity than by a tyranny.
</Quote>

Cleinias calls it "a monstrous doctrine". The claim is about speed of reform, not about who has a right to rule. A tyrant who happens to be temperate and happens to meet a great legislator can change a city's manners in a short time, by his own example, by praising and punishing. The Athenian ranks the starting points by that test alone: tyranny is easiest, then monarchy, then democracy, and oligarchy is hardest, because there power is "in the hands of a number of potentates" who must all be won over. Then he states the condition under which any of this works:

<Quote source="Laws IV, 711e–712a, trans. B. Jowett">
When the supreme power in man coincides with the greatest wisdom and temperance, then the best laws and the best constitution come into being; but in no other way.
</Quote>

That is the <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="third-wave-philosophers-must-rule">third wave</Ref> again: political power and wisdom meeting in one person. The Athenian has not given it up. He calls it a difficulty "rarely surmounted in the course of ages", and the colony is founded without it. The *Laws* keeps the philosopher king as a prayer and legislates for the case where the prayer is not granted.

### Why law: the age of Cronos

Without the knower, why law, and not the best men's judgment case by case? The Athenian answers with a story about the golden age under Cronos, the god who ruled before Zeus:

<Quote source="Laws IV, 713c–714a, trans. B. Jowett">
Cronos knew what we ourselves were declaring, that no human nature invested with supreme power is able to order human affairs and not overflow with insolence and wrong. Which reflection led him to appoint not men but demigods, who are of a higher and more divine race, to be the kings and rulers of our cities; he did as we do with flocks of sheep and other tame animals. For we do not appoint oxen to be the lords of oxen, or goats of goats […]. Still we must do all that we can to imitate the life which is said to have existed in the days of Cronos, and, as far as the principle of immortality dwells in us, to that we must hearken, both in private and public life, and regulate our cities and houses according to law, meaning by the very term 'law,' the distribution of mind.
</Quote>

The reason has changed from the *Republic*'s. There the trouble with ordinary rulers was ignorance: they do not know the good. Here it is corruption: no human being, however good, can hold supreme power without overflowing "with insolence and wrong". It is the principle the reader met in Book III, that too much authority overturns the mind as <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="sparta-saved-by-mixing">too large a sail</Ref> overturns a boat, stated now as a fact about human nature.

Men cannot be ruled by a higher race, as sheep are by shepherds. What they can obey is the divine element in themselves, mind, in its public form, law. The Greek makes it a pun: *nomos*, law, is the *dianomē*, the distribution, of *nous*, mind. A law is the reasoning of someone who understood, written down so that it can rule where no one who understands is present.

## Rulers as servants of the law

If law rules, someone must still hold office, and the question of who should rule comes back in a new form: on what title does a man administer laws he did not write?

### The interest of the stronger, rejected again

The Athenian first clears away the answer that the reader has heard <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="thrasymachus-the-law-is-what-the-rulers-interest-says-it-is">from Thrasymachus</Ref>: that whoever rules makes laws for his own advantage and calls obedience just. He restates it in three steps: the governing power makes the laws; every governing power, tyranny or democracy, makes its own continuance "the first or principal object of their laws"; whoever breaks them is punished as unjust. "This, then, is always the mode and fashion in which justice exists."

He calls it one of "those false principles" from Book III's list of <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-second-telling-two-mother-forms">seven claims to rule</Ref>, the principle of might. Then he says what happens where it governs. When one side wins a contest for power, it keeps every office and lives in fear of the losers. The Athenian's verdict on such cities, quoted in <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="socratess-answer-and-what-it-leaves-standing">the chapter on Thrasymachus</Ref>, is that they are "not polities but parties, and their notions of justice are simply unmeaning". In the *Laws* the sentence has a consequence: no one in the new city may hold office because he is rich, or strong, or tall, or well-born.

### The palm for obedience

What title is left?

<Quote source="Laws IV, 715b–d, trans. B. Jowett">
he who is most obedient to the laws of the state, he shall win the palm; and to him who is victorious in the first degree shall be given the highest office and chief ministry of the gods; and the second to him who bears the second palm; and on a similar principle shall all the other offices be assigned to those who come next in order. And when I call the rulers servants or ministers of the law, I give them this name not for the sake of novelty, but because I certainly believe that upon such service or ministry depends the well- or ill-being of the state. For that state in which the law is subject and has no authority, I perceive to be on the highway to ruin; but I see that the state in which the law is above the rulers, and the rulers are the inferiors of the law, has salvation, and every blessing which the Gods can confer.
</Quote>

From the author of the *Republic* one would expect the highest office to go to the wisest man. It goes to the most obedient. The *Republic*'s test was reluctance: "the State in which the rulers are most reluctant to govern is always the best". Both tests look for a man who will not use office for himself. They find him in opposite ways.

The change follows from the problem. Knowledge of the good cannot be checked by anyone who lacks it, which is everyone except the philosophers, and this city has none; obedience to written law can be checked by every citizen who can read the law. The qualification has moved out of the ruler and into the text he serves. Where the wisdom now lives is in the legislator, who writes the law and then stands outside the city he founds: Cleinias and his nine colleagues give the colony its laws, and the colony's magistrates administer them.

### A city named after a god

The new title also explains a puzzle from Book IV. When the Athenian asks which regime Sparta is, Megillus <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="sparta-saved-by-mixing">cannot say</Ref>: its ephors look tyrannical, its customs democratic, its elders aristocratic, its two kings the oldest monarchy in Greece. The chapter on the decline of regimes read his confusion as the mixture working, no part strong enough to rule alone. Cleinias adds that he cannot name the regime of Cnossus either, and the Athenian's reply gives the confusion a second meaning:

<Quote source="Laws IV, 712e–713a, trans. B. Jowett">
The reason is, my excellent friends, that you really have polities, but the states of which we were just now speaking are merely aggregations of men dwelling in cities who are the subjects and servants of a part of their own state, and each of them is named after the dominant power; they are not polities at all. But if states are to be named after their rulers, the true state ought to be called by the name of the God who rules over wise men.
</Quote>

A regime named after its ruling part, "democracy" after the people, "oligarchy" after the few, is a part ruling the rest, and Thrasymachus's description fits it. A regime in which no part can be named as ruler is one in which the part that writes the laws cannot write them for itself alone, so the law can be above everyone. What rules such a city is the law, and law, in the Cronos story, is the distribution of mind, the divine element in men; that is the god "who rules over wise men", and the city should carry his name rather than a faction's.

<KeyFact>
The palm for obedience needs the mixture. In a city ruled by one part, the most obedient citizen serves that part's law, which is justice as Thrasymachus described it; only where no part can write the laws for itself alone is obedience to the law something other than obedience to a faction.
</KeyFact>

## The free doctor: a law that gives its reasons

Law now rules, but law speaks to men who are not philosophers and will never see the reasons behind it. The *Republic* met the same problem with <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="two-kinds-of-lie">a medicinal lie</Ref>, prescribed by the rulers as physicians prescribe a drug. The *Laws* keeps the physician and splits him in two.

### The poet and the legislator

First, what a law must not be. A poet, the Athenian says, is "not in his right mind" when he composes; he imitates men of opposite characters and so contradicts himself. Asked to describe a funeral, he would praise an extravagant one for a rich patron and a stingy one for a miser. A legislator must "give not two rules about the same thing, but one only", and he cannot just order "a moderate funeral": he "must define what moderation is, and how much". A law is a single, exact command. The question is whether it must be nothing else.

### Two doctors

<Quote source="Laws IV, 720b–e, trans. B. Jowett">
The slave doctor prescribes what mere experience suggests, as if he had exact knowledge; and when he has given his orders, like a tyrant, he rushes off with equal assurance to some other servant who is ill […]. But the other doctor, who is a freeman, attends and practices upon freemen; and he carries his enquiries far back, and goes into the nature of the disorder; he enters into discourse with the patient and with his friends, and is at once getting information from the sick man, and also instructing him as far as he is able, and he will not prescribe for him until he has first convinced him.
</Quote>

The slave doctor, an assistant who learned medicine by watching his master, treats slaves; he has no reasons to give, only habits, and he gives orders "like a tyrant". The free doctor learned the art properly and treats free men, and a free man is owed an account of what is done to him. Legislators, the Athenian says, have two instruments, "persuasion and force", and with the multitude they have always used force "pure and simple". A citizen who is not a slave should get the free doctor's treatment.

### Simple law and double law

<Example title="the marriage law, in two forms">
**The simple form.** "A man shall marry between the ages of thirty and thirty-five, or, if he does not, he shall pay such and such a fine, or shall suffer the loss of such and such privileges." A command and a penalty. This is the slave doctor's prescription.

**The double form, step 1: the reason.** Every man wants not to "lie in the grave without a name", and that wish is "only the love of continuance". Human beings share in immortality the only way open to them, by leaving "children's children behind them".

**Step 2: what follows from the reason.** A man who refuses wife and children deliberately deprives himself of that share in immortality, and that is "impiety".

**Step 3: the command and the penalty.** He who obeys "shall be free, and shall pay no fine"; a man unmarried at thirty-five pays a yearly fine, "in order that he may not imagine his celibacy to bring ease and profit to him", and loses the honours the young pay to the old.

**What changed.** The command is the same in both forms. The double form adds the reason the legislator had for giving it, so that the citizen obeys as someone who has been told why. Even the penalty is explained: it removes the profit of disobeying.
</Example>

Megillus says the short form is the Spartan custom, and that he himself would choose the long one for every law. The Athenian then names what the long form has discovered. Music has preludes, "artistic beginnings intended to help the strain which is to be performed"; law, the highest strain, has never had one.

<Remark title="the preamble to a law">
A *preamble*, or prelude, is the persuasive address a legislator sets before a law's command, giving his reasons so that the citizen receives the command "by reason of this good-will" and "more intelligently". A complete law has two parts: the preamble and the command with its penalty, which the Athenian calls "the law pure and simple". Every law should have one, though not every law needs a long one.
</Remark>

### What the preamble keeps from the lie

The preamble concedes something the *Republic* withheld. There the many were told a tale because a true account of why some rule over them would not have been believed. The *Laws* gives them the legislator's reason and treats them as men who can follow it.

The concession has a limit. The preamble persuades citizens of what the legislator has already decided, and its reasons are not always arguments. When the colonists receive their land in Book V, the preamble tells them that "seeing that the earth is their parent, let them tend her more carefully than children do their mother. For she is a goddess and their queen." That is the first half of <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="the-tale-of-the-metals">the tale of the metals</Ref>, the land as the citizens' mother, now spoken openly as a preamble to a property law. The *Laws* drops the second half, the god-given ranks of metal. What it keeps is the claim that binds every citizen to the land and to each other.

## 5040 lots and a ceiling of four: bounding wealth that cannot be abolished

The *Republic* took property away from its guardians because guards with houses and land of their own would become <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="the-guardians-camp">"enemies and tyrants"</Ref> of the people they guard. The second-best city gives every citizen a farm, though not a plough: a later book has the lots worked by slaves who hand over part of the produce, so that the citizen's time is free for the city. The reader has seen what private property does when nothing bounds it: the oligarchy of Book VIII became <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="five-defects-and-the-drone">"not one, but two States"</Ref>, one of the rich and one of the poor, with drones in between. The *Laws* has to keep private households without letting them drift into two cities.

### A clean start, and a fixed number

The colony is founded on empty land, and the Athenian counts that as luck. Cities that must cancel debts or redistribute land can "neither allow the old ways to continue, nor yet venture to alter them"; to create such quarrels when there are none would be "superhuman folly and wickedness". The new city will be built "on freedom from avarice and a sense of justice", "upon this rock".

Then comes the number. The land, the houses and the citizens are divided alike, one lot to one household, and the lots are fixed for good. A fixed body of citizens must still be split many ways: into tribes, into units for war, into shares for taxes and for distributions. If the number of lots is awkward, some division leaves a remainder, and a remainder is a group that belongs nowhere. So the legislator needs the number that divides most ways:

<Quote source="Laws V, 737e–738a, trans. B. Jowett">
The number of our citizens shall be 5040—this will be a convenient number; and these shall be owners of the land and protectors of the allotment. […] The whole of number has every possible division, and the number 5040 can be divided by exactly fifty-nine divisors, and ten of these proceed without interval from one to ten: this will furnish numbers for war and peace, and for all contracts and dealings, including taxes and divisions of the land.
</Quote>

5040 is 1 × 2 × 3 × 4 × 5 × 6 × 7. Every number from one to ten divides it, and so does twelve: the city is to have twelve tribes, each named after a god, of 420 households each.

### Keeping the number

A fixed number of lots is only as good as the means of keeping the number of households fixed. The *Laws* names several. Each holder leaves his lot to one son only, "his best beloved". His other sons are given for adoption to citizens with no children; his daughters are married off under the marriage law. The highest magistracy judges the remaining cases of too many children or too few. Where births run too high, citizens "may be made to refrain", and where they run too low, births are encouraged "by rewards and stigmas". If all else fails there is "the old device often mentioned by us of sending out a colony".

The lots themselves can never be bought or sold. The colonists are told that the earth is sacred to the gods, and priests are to pray that whoever buys or sells a lot "may suffer the punishment which he deserves", and to write those prayers "on tablets of cypress-wood, for the instruction of posterity". No private person may hold gold or silver. The city has a coin "passing current among themselves, but not accepted among the rest of mankind". There are no dowries and no loans at interest.

### Why the very rich cannot be good

Behind these rules is an argument, and it is the *Laws*' reason for treating great wealth as a sign of vice rather than a reward of virtue.

<Example title="why the very rich are not good">
**The claim.** "Very rich and very good at the same time he cannot be, not, at least, in the sense in which the many speak of riches."

**Step 1: two ways of getting.** A good man acquires only from just sources. A man who does not care acquires from just and unjust sources alike, and so gets "more than double" what the good man gets.

**Step 2: two ways of spending.** A good man spends on what is honourable, the city's sacrifices, his friends, his household's proper needs. A man who spends "neither honourably nor disgracefully", a hoarder who keeps what he has, spends "only half as great" a sum.

**Step 3: compare the balances.** One man gets double and spends half; the good man gets less and spends more. The good man "cannot possibly be wealthier than he".

**Step 4: who ends up very rich.** Not the profligate, who is "in general profligate, and therefore very poor", and not the good man, who is never very rich and never very poor. The very rich man is the one who takes from every source and spends on nothing: perhaps not "utterly bad", but "a good man he never is".

**Step 5: why the city cares.** The laws aim at citizens "as friendly as possible to one another", and men "always at law with one another" cannot be friends. A city of great fortunes is a city of lawsuits.
</Example>

The argument is a claim about tendencies, not a proof that every great fortune is ill-gotten: an heir or a lucky finder escapes its first step. It is enough for the legislator's purpose, which is to say why a law should stop fortunes from growing instead of only punishing theft.

### Floor and ceiling

Equal lots do not make equal men, since colonists arrive with different goods and some will prosper. The Athenian accepts four property classes, with offices, contributions and distributions graded by class. The reader has met the arrangement in <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="solons-equality-to-each-by-worth">Solon's four classes</Ref>: shares proportioned to worth, the geometric equality. What the *Laws* adds is a bound at both ends:

<Quote source="Laws V, 744d–745a, trans. B. Jowett">
In a state which is desirous of being saved from the greatest of all plagues—not faction, but rather distraction;—there should exist among the citizens neither extreme poverty, nor, again, excess of wealth, for both are productive of both these evils. Now the legislator should determine what is to be the limit of poverty or wealth. Let the limit of poverty be the value of the lot; this ought to be preserved […]. This the legislator gives as a measure, and he will permit a man to acquire double or triple, or as much as four times the amount of this.
</Quote>

The two evils are two Greek words. Faction, *stasis*, is a quarrel between parties inside one city: they fight over its offices, but it is still one city they fight over. "Distraction", *diastasis*, is worse: the city stands apart into two cities, one of the rich and one of the poor, what Book VIII called <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="five-defects-and-the-drone">"not one, but two States"</Ref>. That is why both extremes are blamed: a class of the very poor and a class of the very rich are the two cities, and a bound at each end keeps every citizen inside one. Anything above four times the lot, however it was got, must be handed to the state and its gods. A man who keeps it may be denounced by anyone, and the informer receives half the excess.

The bound is a ratio, not a sum. However rich the city becomes, the richest citizen holds at most four times what the poorest holds, so the distance between them is fixed in advance. The *Laws* gives no reason for four rather than three or five. The number is a judgment about how much inequality a city of friends can carry.

## Aristotle's charge: property limited, births not

The scheme rests on one number staying fixed. The strongest objection to it came from Plato's own student. <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="outside-the-text-the-objection-from-care">Aristotle</Ref>, whose objection from care the reader met against the *Republic*, takes up the *Laws* in the same book of his *Politics*, and most of his complaints about it come back to the arithmetic.

### The objection at full strength

First, size. Aristotle rounds the 5040 to 5,000 and takes the citizens as the *Laws* makes them, men who do not work their own lots. A body of 5,000 such men, "together with their women and attendants, who will be a multitude many times as great", would need "a territory as large as Babylon", the byword for a vast city. "In framing an ideal we may assume what we wish, but should avoid impossibilities."

Second, and decisive in his view, births:

<Quote source="Aristotle, Politics II.6, 1265a38–b12, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
There is an inconsistency, too, in equalizing the property and not regulating the number of the citizens; the population is to remain unlimited […]. But greater care will be required than now; for among ourselves, whatever may be the number of citizens, the property is always distributed among them, and therefore no one is in want; but, if the property were incapable of division as in the Laws, the supernumeraries, whether few or many, would get nothing. One would have thought that it was even more necessary to limit population than property […]. The neglect of this subject, which in existing states is so common, is a never-failing cause of poverty among the citizens; and poverty is the parent of revolution and crime.
</Quote>

In an ordinary city an estate is split among the sons and everyone is a little poorer. In the *Laws* the lot passes whole to one heir, so every extra son gets nothing at all. The fixed lots that were meant to prevent poverty manufacture a class of landless citizens' sons, the poor who end regimes.

<Figure

Aristotle sharpens the worry in his next chapter, against Phaleas of Chalcedon, an earlier writer who proposed equal estates for all citizens. Equal land does not settle the matter, he answers, because "the avarice of mankind is insatiable; at one time two obols was pay enough; but now, when this sum has become customary, men always want more and more without end". The obol was a small silver coin. The beginning of reform is "not so much to equalize property as to train the nobler sort of natures not to desire more". On this point the *Laws* agrees with its critic. Book V itself calls poverty "the increase of a man's desires and not the diminution of his property", and the *Laws* works on desire with its preambles as well as on property with its bounds.

### What the Laws can answer

Against the letter of the objection, the *Laws* does not leave the population "unlimited": the one heir, adoption, restraint, rewards and stigmas and the colony are all in Book V. Against its substance it has little. Each outlet assumes what it needs: a colony needs empty land somewhere else and men willing to go, and restraint needs the preambles to work on the desire they address. The number 5040 is an aim, and the means of holding it are listed, not shown to suffice.

The Athenian anticipates a version of the complaint. A city of men who accept a fixed lot, beget children "in accordance with our ordinances" and give up gold is, he concedes, "as if the legislator were telling his dreams, or making a city and citizens of wax". His reply:

<Quote source="Laws V, 746b–c, trans. B. Jowett">
in matters which are not present but future, he who exhibits a pattern of that at which he aims, should in nothing fall short of the fairest and truest; and that if he finds any part of this work impossible of execution he should avoid and not execute it, but he should contrive to carry out that which is nearest and most akin to it; you must allow the legislator to perfect his design, and when it is perfected, you should join with him in considering what part of his legislation is expedient and what will arouse opposition; for surely the artist who is to be deemed worthy of any regard at all, ought always to make his work self-consistent.
</Quote>

That is the method of the whole work, applied to itself. The *Republic*'s city was the pattern and the *Laws*' city the nearest feasible thing to it; the *Laws*' city is in turn a pattern, to be cut down further when it meets real colonists. Read this way, Aristotle's charge is not that the *Laws* conceded too much to practice. It is that the *Laws* did not yet concede enough.

### A mixture narrower than it claims

One complaint is not about numbers. It is aimed at what the [KeyFact](#a-city-named-after-a-god) above rested on, the mixture: obedience to the law is more than service to a faction only where no part of the city can write the laws for itself. Book III said a well-governed city must mix the <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-second-telling-two-mother-forms">two mother forms</Ref>, monarchy and democracy, the rule of one and the freedom of the many. Aristotle sets that claim beside the offices the later books actually set up:

<Quote source="Aristotle, Politics II.6, 1266a1–7, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
In the Laws it is maintained that the best constitution is made up of democracy and tyranny, which are either not constitutions at all, or are the worst of all. But they are nearer the truth who combine many forms; for the constitution is better which is made up of more numerous elements. The constitution proposed in the Laws has no element of monarchy at all; it is nothing but oligarchy and democracy, leaning rather to oligarchy.
</Quote>

"Tyranny" is Aristotle's unkind word for the monarchic half, and the *Laws* gave him the word with its prayer for a young tyrant. His point is the next sentence. The city of the *Laws* has no king: its magistrates are elected, some of them then chosen by lot from among the elected. Without a ruling one, the parts left to mix are the few and the many, the rich and the rest. So the regime he finds there is "neither democracy nor oligarchy, but something in a mean between them", which he calls *polity*. He uses the word in a narrower sense than the Athenian: not any true constitution as against a party, but one regime among others, held by citizens rich enough to arm themselves as heavy infantry.

The mean also tilts. The rich are compelled by law to attend the assembly and vote for magistrates; the rest may stay at home. The rich will all be there and many of the rest will not, so the votes cast lean to the higher property classes, and they choose the officials. Sparta, by contrast, is praised by some for mixing three elements, its kings, its elders and its ephors, and Aristotle holds that more elements make the better mixture.

The charge is therefore not that the *Laws* has no mixture, but that its mixture is narrower than the one it claims. The two parts it mixes are not the two mother forms: the rule of one is missing, the few and the many stand in for it, and the few have their hand on the vote. To the extent that the rich decide, the condition of the KeyFact holds only in part, and the obedient citizen of the second-best city serves a law that one part had the larger share in writing.

## Might is right, answered by the priority of soul

The preambles persuade a citizen who shares the legislator's premises: that the earth is a goddess, that refusing children is impiety, that the gods punish the buyer of a lot. Book X turns to the citizen who denies the premises themselves. He holds one of three opinions: that there are no gods, that they exist but take no care of men, or that they can be bought off with sacrifices. The preambles lean on the gods again and again, so this citizen is not one offender among many. He is the one man they cannot reach.

### The materialist's doctrine

The Athenian states the doctrine before he answers it, and calls it "the wisest of all doctrines, in the opinion of many". Everything comes to be by nature, by chance or by art. Fire, water, earth and air exist by nature and chance; from them, moved "by chance and some inherent force", came sun, moon, stars, animals and plants, "not by the action of mind […] or of any God". Art came later, "mortal and of mortal birth", and legislation is "entirely a work of art". Then the political conclusion:

<Quote source="Laws X, 889e–890a, trans. B. Jowett">
these people would say that the Gods exist not by nature, but by art, and by the laws of states, which are different in different places, according to the agreement of those who make them; and that the honourable is one thing by nature and another thing by law, and that the principles of justice have no existence at all in nature, but that mankind are always disputing about them and altering them […]. These, my friends, are the sayings of wise men, poets and prose writers, which find a way into the minds of youth. They are told by them that the highest right is might, and in this way the young fall into impieties, under the idea that the Gods are not such as the law bids them imagine; and hence arise factions, these philosophers inviting them to lead a true life according to nature, that is, to live in real dominion over others, and not in legal subjection to them.
</Quote>

The reader has heard each part of this before. Justice as an agreement that differs from city to city is <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="a-bargain-among-the-weak">Glaucon's bargain among the weak</Ref>. Rule by whoever is stronger is Thrasymachus's thesis. Gods who rule wherever they can is what the Athenian envoys told the Melians: "of the gods we believe, and of men we know, that by a necessary law of their nature they rule wherever they can" (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="gods-and-spartans">Melos</Ref>). What Book X adds is a physics. The envoys put the gods on the side of the stronger. The materialist puts them on the side of convention, as human inventions, and gives the whole picture a ground: an order of causes in which matter comes first and everything made by mind, gods, art, law and justice, comes last.

### Persuasion before the penalty

Should the legislator simply threaten? The Athenian asks the question himself, and answers that a lawgiver should "infuse the spirit of persuasion into his words". He addresses the young atheist gently: "O my son, […] you are young, and the advance of time will make you reverse many of the opinions which you now hold." He has known many who held the opinion in youth, and "no one […] ever continued in the same until he was old". Cleinias adds the reason a long argument belongs in a law code at all: laws "when once written down are always at rest", so any citizen, "however dull", can go over them "again and again".

### Soul before body

The answer goes after the materialist's order of causes. "They affirm that which is the first cause of the generation and destruction of all things, to be not first, but last." If soul is older than body, then everything akin to soul comes first too: "thought and attention and mind and art and law will be prior to that which is hard and soft and heavy and light". The proof that soul is older runs through motion.

Step through the four frames and name the one premise the materialist would refuse, before reading the steps below.

The steps, as the Athenian gives them:

1. Of things in motion, some are moved by something else and move other things in turn; some move themselves.
2. A thing moved by another cannot be the beginning of change: "How can a thing which is moved by another ever be the beginning of change? Impossible."
3. Suppose, as the materialists say, that "all things were at rest in one mass". Nothing outside could start them. The first motion would have to be self-motion, which is therefore "the eldest and mightiest principle of change".
4. When we see a thing move itself, we say it is alive. And the definition of soul is "the motion which can move itself". So soul is the first origin of all motion, older than body, and body "is born to obey the soul, which is the ruler".

The weight of the proof rests on step 4. Steps 1 to 3 show at most that some self-mover must exist. A materialist can accept that and say the self-mover is matter, moving by its own forces, as his doctrine already said. The argument wins only if self-motion is soul by definition, and that is the point he denies.

From soul to gods is a short step for the Athenian. Soul moves the heavens; their motion is orderly, regular and circular, the motion most like mind; so the soul that moves them is the best soul, and the souls of sun, moon and stars are gods. "Will any one who admits all this venture to deny that all things are full of Gods?"

### What the argument buys the law

The political payoff is the conclusion Cleinias drew before the proof began: law and art "exist by nature, and no less than nature, if they are the creations of mind in accordance with right reason". If mind comes first in the order of things, a law that is the distribution of mind is not a convention imposed by the stronger. It belongs to the order of nature, and the man who calls justice mere agreement has the order backwards.

So the *Laws* answers Thrasymachus in a different way from the *Republic*. The *Republic* answered him with a portrait of the soul: the tyrant as <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-verdict-and-what-kind-of-answer-it-is">the most enslaved man</Ref>. The *Laws* answers him with a cosmology, and it has to, because the knower is gone. In a city whose rulers do not know the good, the only standard above the rulers is the law, and law can be above them only if mind is above matter. Book IV said as much in a line addressed to the colonists: "God ought to be to us the measure of all things, and not man". The good that philosophers were to see has become a god whom every citizen is to honour.

### The law on impiety

The persuasion has an end, and the law that follows it is as severe as anything in the *Republic*. Anyone who witnesses impiety must report it to the magistrates, and a magistrate who refuses to act is himself tried for impiety. An unbeliever of honest life, who erred "from want of understanding", is confined for at least five years in a prison called the House of Reformation, speaking to no one except members of the nocturnal council, a council of the city's senior officials set out in later books, who are to talk with him "with a view to the improvement of his soul's health". If he is convicted a second time, he dies. The clever, deceitful unbeliever, from whose ranks "sometimes come tyrants and demagogues", deserves "death many times over". No one may keep a shrine in a private house.

The *Republic* censored the stories children hear because <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="stories-come-before-reasons">character is formed before it can judge</Ref>. The *Laws* argues with adults first and then imprisons those it cannot persuade. The city that replaced the lie with the preamble still legislates what its citizens may believe.

<Conclusion>
You can now say what the *Laws* puts in the place of each institution the *Republic* built on a knower, why, and what each replacement concedes and keeps:

| The *Republic* | The *Laws* | What it concedes | What it keeps |
|---|---|---|---|
| philosophers rule by knowledge of the good | law rules; law is "the distribution of mind" | no human being can hold supreme power without "insolence and wrong" | wisdom is still the source of rule, now through the legislator |
| the most reluctant rule, compelled | the most obedient win the palm | knowledge cannot be checked by the ruled; obedience can | rulers who do not rule for themselves |
| a tale for the many | a preamble before every law | free men are owed the legislator's reason | the land as mother and goddess; persuasion toward what is already decided |
| no property for the guardians | 5040 inalienable lots, four classes, a ceiling of four | private households cannot be abolished | the first city as the pattern; no gold or silver; wealth suspect |
| the good, seen by the few | God as the measure; soul before body | the good cannot be every citizen's standard | belief is legislated; impiety is a crime |

The mixed constitution holds the second column together: where no part can write the laws for itself, the law can stand above the rulers. The scheme is weakest where Aristotle pressed it: the number of lots is fixed and the number of sons is not, and the mixture the law is meant to stand on has no monarchic part and leans to the rich.

Two questions stay open. Whether a mixture in which no one can name the ruler is a regime at all, or only a regime whose ruler is hidden, is the question <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" /> answers with a definition of a constitution. And a law that rules needs someone to apply it to cases its writer did not foresee, so "the rule of law" is always partly the rule of its interpreters; the medieval theory of law as reason in <Ref lesson="aquinas-law-and-kingship" /> takes that up. The core path continues with Aristotle's question of what a city is by nature, in <Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" />.
</Conclusion>

Apply the <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="solon-at-athens">sourcing questions</Ref> to the "servants of the law" passage: who wrote it and when, who speaks it in the dialogue and to whom, what it records, what it argues, what it omits. Then answer: what can the passage be used to establish about how far Plato had moved from the *Republic*, and what does it change that the speaker is a nameless Athenian rather than Socrates?

<Solution>
**Who and when.** Plato, in the 350s BC, near the end of his life, some thirty years after the *Republic*. **Speaker and audience.** An unnamed Athenian, speaking to a Cretan who must actually give a colony its laws and to a Spartan from the city held up as the model of moderation; the audience is old, practical and not philosophical.

**Records.** Nothing: it is a proposal for a city that does not exist. **Argues.** That office should go to the man most obedient to the laws, not to the rich, strong or well-born; that rulers are "servants or ministers of the law"; that a city where the law is above the rulers "has salvation", and one where it is not is "on the highway to ruin". **Omits.** Who writes the law the rulers serve, and with what title. The legislator stands outside the city he founds, and the passage is silent on him. It also omits knowledge as a qualification for office.

**Use.** Set beside the *Republic*'s "the State in which the rulers are most reluctant to govern is always the best", it establishes that by the end of his life Plato gave the title to office to obedience rather than to knowledge, for a city without philosophers. It cannot by itself establish that he abandoned the philosopher king, since the same book prays for wisdom and power to meet in one man. **The speaker.** Socrates was the man who questioned every law and every claim to know; putting the doctrine in his mouth would have made the philosopher the founder of a city of obedience. A nameless Athenian lets the proposal stand as a statesman's, not a philosopher's, and leaves open how far the author's own view is the speaker's.

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "In the *Laws*, Plato abandons the philosopher king." Name the steps of the text that break it, and state the claim that survives.

<Solution>
**The first city is kept.** Book V ranks the community of "friends have all things in common" first and says "to this we are to look for the pattern of the state, and to cling to this". The city of the *Laws* is ranked second against it.

**The formula is repeated.** Book IV: "When the supreme power in man coincides with the greatest wisdom and temperance, then the best laws and the best constitution come into being; but in no other way." The prayer for a young, temperate tyrant who meets a great legislator is the same union of power and wisdom.

**Wisdom still rules, by another route.** Law is "the distribution of mind": the legislator's understanding, written down. The colony is founded by men who claim to know what law should say.

**Belief is still governed.** Book X legislates what citizens may hold about the gods and imprisons those it cannot persuade.

**What survives.** The *Laws* does not count on the philosopher king. It treats the union of wisdom and power as a prayer "rarely surmounted in the course of ages", and it governs the actual city by written law and by rulers chosen for obedience, because no human being can hold supreme power without being corrupted by it.

<Exercise>
Probe the wealth ceiling at its limits. (a) Set it at one: no citizen may hold more than his lot. (b) Remove it: the floor stays, the ceiling goes. Using only the *Laws*' own arguments and the descent of regimes, say what kind of city each becomes, and what the answer shows about the choice of four.

<Solution>
**(a) Ceiling = floor.** Every household holds exactly its lot, and the four classes collapse into one. That is equality of possessions without the community of use: the first city's equality without its common life. The *Laws*' own reason against it is in Book V: colonists arrive with unequal goods, and some inequality is needed "in order that offices and contributions and distributions may be proportioned" to what each can bear. A city with one class cannot grade its burdens, so it must give equal shares to unequal men, the arithmetic equality <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-embroidered-robe">the descent called democracy's</Ref>. It also gives no room for the honest gain that the argument about the rich allows the good man.

**(b) No ceiling.** The floor protects the poorest lot, but fortunes can grow without limit. By the argument of Book V, the largest fortunes will belong to those who take from every source and spend on nothing, not to the good. By the descent of Book VIII, honoured wealth becomes the qualification for office: a city with a floor and no ceiling has the material for an oligarchy, the regime of "two States", even if it never passes a property qualification.

**Why four.** The ceiling must lie above one, so that classes and graded burdens are possible, and must exist, so that wealth cannot become rule. The *Laws* gives no argument for four rather than three or five. The ratio is a judgment about how much inequality a city of friends can bear, and what is argued is the form of the rule, a bound stated as a multiple of the poorest lot.

*A possible answer.*

**Preamble.** "Citizen, the lot you hold was not made by you and is not yours alone. <Exercise>
Set the materialist doctrine of Book X ("the principles of justice have no existence at all in nature"; "the highest right is might") beside three earlier texts: Thrasymachus's thesis that every ruling body makes laws for its own advantage; Glaucon's account of justice as <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" id="a-bargain-among-the-weak">a bargain among the weak</Ref>; and the Athenian envoys at Melos, "of the gods we believe, and of men we know, that by a necessary law of their nature they rule wherever they can". Where do the four agree, where does Book X go further, and what does the comparison show about why Plato's final answer is a cosmology?

<Solution>
**Agreement.** All four deny that justice is found in nature: it is made, by the stronger (Thrasymachus), by the weak as a compromise (Glaucon), or by "the agreement of those who make" laws, varying by city (Book X). All four agree that by nature the stronger rule: Thrasymachus says so of every regime, Glaucon of every man who could act without fear, the envoys of gods and men alike, Book X in "the highest right is might" and "real dominion over others".

**Where Book X goes further.** It supplies the ground the others lacked. Thrasymachus and Glaucon describe politics; the envoys appeal to the gods as the first case of the strong ruling. Book X gives a physics: matter moved by chance comes first, mind and its products come last, so the gods are themselves products of art and convention. The envoys put the gods on the side of the stronger. The materialist puts them on the side of convention, which removes them as a standard altogether.

**Judgment.** Each earlier answer to the thesis left its premise standing. The *Republic* answered with the soul's order, which persuades only a reader who already accepts that order is health. Once the doctrine claims that nature itself is matter and chance, any appeal to what is "by nature" just has to show that nature is not what the materialist says. So the last answer has to be about the order of causes: soul before body, mind before matter, law by nature. Plato's political answer becomes a cosmology because the thesis it answers had become one.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Someone claims: "Aristotle's population objection is decisive against the *Laws*." Name two facts the claim must account for, and say what would have to be true for it to be decisive.

<Solution>
**Fact 1.** The *Laws* does not leave the number unregulated. Book V provides one heir per lot, adoption of extra sons by childless households, restraint where births run high, rewards and stigmas where they run low, judgment by the highest magistracy, and a colony as the last resort, against Aristotle's "the population is to remain unlimited".

**Fact 2.** Aristotle accepts the principle. His complaint is that the *Laws* fixed property and neglected population, not that a city should not fix its citizen body: "it was even more necessary to limit population than property". In the *Politics* he goes on to propose limits on the number of children for his own best city. The objection is to the execution, not to the aim.

**What would make it decisive.** That the named remedies cannot hold the number in practice: that adoption and restraint cannot absorb the surplus, and that a city of fixed lots must either send out colonies it cannot found or let landless sons accumulate. That is a question about real cities, not about the text. A city with fixed citizen lots and a closed citizen body is the case to test it on, and the shrinking number of full Spartan citizens is the obvious one (<Ref lesson="sparta-as-test-case" />).

<Exercise>
The *Laws* says that a city named after its ruling part is "not a polity" but a faction in power, and that Sparta and Cnossus are true polities because their own citizens cannot name the ruling part. State the strongest objection you can to this claim. Then say what you would need to define before the dispute could be settled.

<Solution>
**The objection.** Every city, mixed or not, settles disputes somewhere: some body has the last word when the parts disagree. If Megillus cannot name it, that is a fact about his ignorance, not about Sparta. Either the mixture really has no last word, and then nobody is responsible and the city cannot decide, or it has one, and then the body that holds it is the real ruler and the "polity" is a faction that hides its rule better. On this view the unnameable regime is not the rule of law but the rule of whoever controls the decisive office, in the dress of the law.

**What must be defined.** What a constitution is: the whole arrangement of offices, or the office with the last word. If the first, a mixture is a constitution of its own kind and the Athenian is right that no part rules. If the second, every city has a ruler and the question is only which.

You have stated the question Aristotle answers by defining a constitution as the arrangement of the offices of a city, above all of the one that is supreme (<Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" />). Pressed to its limit, the objection becomes the claim that sovereignty cannot be divided at all, so that a mixed state is no state, which is how <Ref lesson="bodin-sovereignty" /> meets every mixed constitution.
</Solution>

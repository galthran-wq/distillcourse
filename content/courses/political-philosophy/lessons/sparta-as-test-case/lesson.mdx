Sparta had two kings, a council of elders, elected overseers and a citizen body of equals who ate together and trained for war; it also had a subject population many times larger that it held down by terror, and its citizen numbers fell until the city could no longer field an army. Plato and Aristotle built their theories of the mixed regime, of natural rule and of decline with this city in view, yet the Spartans wrote down no laws and no history, and everything known of them comes from foreigners. How do you test a theory against a city whose only witnesses are its admirers and its critics?

Behind those theories stands the question the earlier lessons kept asking: who should rule? The Greek answers were three. Those who know the good (<Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" id="third-wave-philosophers-must-rule">the philosopher king</Ref>); those whom nature made to rule (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery">rule by nature</Ref>); or no single part, but a regime mixed from several and held up by the middle (<Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="who-holds-the-mixture-up-the-middle">the middle</Ref>). A city's record can bear on that question only through claims it can confirm or break, and the theories below bear on the three answers through the regimes they explain. Three theories make such claims about Sparta, and two of them make more than one, so the tests number five. Plato's *Laws* and Aristotle's polity say that a regime mixed from several parts outlasts a simple one (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="sparta-saved-by-mixing">Sparta saved by mixing</Ref>). Aristotle's *Politics* says that rule runs through nature from the soul down to the slave, which gives two claims Sparta can test: that where master and slave are matched by nature they share an interest (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="friends-with-a-common-interest">friends with a common interest</Ref>), and that the husband rules his wife by nature (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="three-rules-in-one-household">three rules in one household</Ref>). And both men told stories of decline, which are two claims, not one: Plato's honour-loving regime that rots into a money-loving one, and Aristotle's city trained for war that <Ref lesson="aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life" id="the-spartan-critique-like-unused-iron">rusts like unused iron</Ref> once it has won. Each was stated with Sparta as its example; none was checked against the whole record, or against how far each witness to that record can be trusted.

The obvious way to run them is to describe Sparta's constitution and then hold each theory up to it. That fails at the first step, because nobody can simply describe Sparta. The Spartans kept even the size of their army secret, and the foreigners who describe them are admirers, critics and a biographer who wrote under Rome, each with a reason to see the city one way. So the test has two stages. First, what can be known, and on whose word. Then, for each theory, where the record fits it and where the record resists.

## What is known of Sparta, and who says so

A theory can be tested only against a record whose reliability is known. For Sparta the reliability varies witness by witness, so the witnesses come before the regime.

Sparta lay in the south-east of the Peloponnese, the large peninsula joined to the rest of Greece at Corinth, in the valley of the river Eurotas. The region around it was Laconia, and the state's own name was Lacedaemon. West of the valley rises the Taygetus range, a wall of mountains about 2,400 metres high, and beyond it lies Messenia, a broad fertile plain that Sparta conquered from its Greek inhabitants in the late eighth century and had to conquer again after a great revolt in the mid-seventh (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="competition-between-many-cities">Sparta's answer to land shortage</Ref>). To the north lay Arcadia and Argos, both hostile for most of Sparta's history.

### Five witnesses, none of them Spartan

Herodotus, the historian of the Persian wars who wrote at Athens in the 430s and 420s, reports what an exiled Spartan king told the Persian king Xerxes in 480. Thucydides, the exiled Athenian general who wrote the history of the Peloponnesian War (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice" id="the-historian">the historian</Ref>), saw Sparta as an enemy and says outright what he could not find out:

<Quote source="Thucydides V.68, trans. R. Crawley">
Owing to the secrecy of their government the number of the Lacedaemonians was not known, and men are so apt to brag about the forces of their country that the estimate of their opponents was not trusted.
</Quote>

The third witness is the only one who lived among Spartans. Xenophon (c. 430–354) was an Athenian soldier and writer, exiled from Athens, who served under Spartan commanders, lived for years under Spartan protection in the Peloponnese and was a friend of the Spartan king Agesilaus. His short *Constitution of the Lacedaemonians*, written in the 370s, praises the lawgiver chapter by chapter. The fourth is Aristotle, who collected the constitutions of Greek cities (his account of Sparta's is lost) and in *Politics* II.9, written in the 330s or 320s, examines Sparta's institutions one by one for their defects. The fifth is Plutarch (c. AD 46–120), a Greek from Boeotia who wrote biographies under the Roman Empire. His *Life of Lycurgus*, about the legendary lawgiver Sparta credited with its whole system, was written about four and a half centuries after Aristotle, from books now mostly lost, Aristotle's lost account among them.

<Remark title="what the record can establish">
Where the admirer and the critic agree on an institution, the institution can be taken as established. Where they agree on a defect, the defect is strong evidence, because the admirer had no reason to invent it. Where only Plutarch reports something, it is a tradition of uncertain age. Numbers are reports, never counts: Sparta kept none that an outsider could see.
</Remark>

### The regime the witnesses agree on

Set side by side, the witnesses agree on the following arrangement. Its parts divide into two questions: who governed, and who lived on whose labour.

**Who governed.** Two hereditary kings, from two royal houses, ruled together and commanded in war. A council of elders, the *gerousia*, held twenty-eight men over sixty, elected for life, and the two kings sat with them, thirty in all. Five *ephors*, the "overseers" whom <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="sparta-saved-by-mixing">the Laws called Sparta's third saviour</Ref>, were elected every year from all the citizens; they presided over the assembly, judged lawsuits over contracts, and every magistrate, the kings included, answered to them. The assembly of all citizens decided by shouting. Thucydides describes an ephor putting the question of war to it in 432 and declaring that he "could not determine which was the loudest acclamation (their mode of decision is by acclamation not by voting)"; Plutarch adds that ordinary citizens could not propose a measure, only accept or refuse what kings and elders put to them.

**Who lived on whom.** The full citizens called themselves the *homoioi*, "the equals" (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-hoplite-phalanx">the hoplites who stood in one line</Ref>). Each held a *kleros*, a lot of land, which he did not work. The lots were farmed by *helots*, the descendants of the Laconians and Messenians whom Sparta had conquered: serfs bound to the land rather than slaves bought and sold in a market, who handed over part of what they grew to the citizen whose lot they worked.

**The mess.** From that share every citizen paid a monthly contribution of barley, wine, cheese and figs to his *mess*, a company of about fifteen men who dined together every evening. The contribution was not optional: a citizen who could not pay it lost his place.

**The upbringing.** Boys were taken from their families at seven and raised in companies by the city, an upbringing called the *agoge*. A citizen was a man who had been through it, kept his lot and paid his mess.

**The free outsiders.** Around the territory lay the towns of the *perioeci*, the free but voteless "dwellers-around" (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="what-the-best-city-does-with-it">the class Aristotle's best city also keeps outside the citizens</Ref>), who traded, made things and fought in the army without any share in the government.

The witnesses also disagree, and the disagreements matter for the tests that follow. Plutarch says Lycurgus divided the land into nine thousand equal lots for the citizens, then reports that others said six thousand, with three thousand added later; Aristotle says only that some Spartans "have quite small properties, others have very large ones". Whether the equals were ever equal in land is therefore open. Plutarch describes the *krypteia*, the "secret" service in which young men were sent out at night to kill helots, and then refuses to believe the lawgiver founded it. And Aristotle and Plutarch disagree about whether Lycurgus ever tried to govern the women.

## The Great Rhetra: a mixture built in stages

Plato's *Laws* explained Sparta's mixture as the work of <Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="sparta-saved-by-mixing">three saviours in turn</Ref>: a god who gave two kings, "human wisdom" that added elders equal in weight, and a "third saviour" who added the ephors. Was the mixture designed, or did it grow? The one founding text the Spartans kept can be tested against that story.

### The document

Plutarch quotes an oracle that Lycurgus is said to have brought from Delphi, Apollo's sanctuary in central Greece, and that the Spartans called the *rhetra*, "the saying". It is the Great Rhetra, the nearest thing Sparta had to a written constitution, preserved as an oracle precisely because Sparta wrote its laws down nowhere else.

<Quote source="Plutarch, Life of Lycurgus 6, trans. A. Stewart and G. Long">
After you have built a temple to Zeus of Greece and Athene of Greece, and have divided the people into *tribes* and *obes*, you shall found a council of thirty, including the chiefs, and shall from season to season *apellazein* the people between Babyka and Knakion, and there propound measures and divide upon them, and the people shall have the casting vote and final decision.
</Quote>

The "chiefs" are the kings; the *obes* were smaller divisions of the people within the tribes, probably by village; *apellazein* means to call an assembly; Babyka and Knakion are a bridge and a stream, the open-air place where it met. Plutarch then reports a later addition:

<Quote source="Plutarch, Life of Lycurgus 6, trans. A. Stewart and G. Long">
But in later times, as the people made additions and omissions, and so altered the sense of the motions before them, the kings, Polydorus and Theopompus, added these words to the *rhetra*, "and if the people shall decide crookedly, the chiefs and elders shall set it right." That is, they made the people no longer supreme, but practically excluded them from any voice in public affairs, on the ground that they judged wrongly. However these kings persuaded the city that this also was ordained by the god.
</Quote>

Theopompus is the king of about 700 BC whom Aristotle credited with creating the ephors and who told his wife that the reduced power he left his sons would be "<Ref lesson="aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life" id="insult-hatred-fear-contempt-how-one-man-falls">more lasting</Ref>". Plutarch's next chapter dates the first ephors to the same reign, "about a hundred and thirty years after Lykurgus", and gives their purpose: Lycurgus's successors saw that "the powers of the oligarchy", the kings and elders, "were unimpaired", and "placed as a curb to it the power of the Ephors".

<Example title="the Great Rhetra under the sourcing questions">
The <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="solon-at-athens">four sourcing questions</Ref>: who wrote it, when and for whom; what it records; what it argues; what it leaves out.

**Who, when, for whom.** The text is archaic in form: a command in the god's voice, with place names that had changed by Plutarch's day. The quotation is Roman-era, in a biography written for educated Greeks and Romans around AD 100, and Plutarch names his authorities (Aristotle on the place names, the seventh-century Spartan poet Tyrtaeus for the rider). The text is old; the frame round it is late.

**What it records.** Three bodies: kings, a council of thirty that includes them, and an assembly that meets at fixed times. The council proposes ("propound measures"), the assembly decides ("the people shall have the casting vote and final decision").

**What it argues.** Nothing; it commands. Its authority is the god's, which is also how the rider was passed: the kings "persuaded the city that this also was ordained by the god".

**What it omits.** The ephors. The prediction in the Task probably named them, since they were the most powerful magistrates of classical Sparta, and the founding text does not know them. It also omits who decides when a decision is "crooked": the rider gives the answer to the kings and elders, who are one side of the dispute.

**What it can be used to establish.** That the regime was assembled in steps: first a council with the kings and a sovereign assembly; then, about a hundred and thirty years later and in one reign, two changes that pull opposite ways. The rider curbs the people, and it was passed by kings. The ephors curb the kings and elders. The *Laws*' story of successive saviours has each step curb the kings; the record shows the reign that created the curb on the kings also creating a curb on the people. Which of the two came first, the sources do not say. Plutarch tells the rider in one chapter and the ephors in the next, and nothing more than that order of telling supports calling the rider the first amendment.
</Example>

A mixture that grew as a settlement in which each side gave something up, rather than by one saviour after another, can still be stable; whether Sparta's was, and why, is the first theory's test.

## Theory one: the mixed constitution fits, for Aristotle's reason rather than Plato's

Does a regime mixed from several parts outlast a simple one? Sparta is the one Greek city where that prediction can be checked over centuries, because the record of its stability is better than the record of almost anything else about it.

### The prediction holds

Thucydides, an enemy with no reason to flatter, states it:

<Quote source="Thucydides I.18, trans. R. Crawley">
this city, though after the settlement of the Dorians, its present inhabitants, it suffered from factions for an unparalleled length of time, still at a very early period obtained good laws, and enjoyed a freedom from tyrants which was unbroken; it has possessed the same form of government for more than four hundred years, reckoning to the end of the late war
</Quote>

Four centuries without a tyrant and without a change of regime is unique in the Greek record. Every other Greek city whose history is known changed its constitution several times in that span: Athens went from aristocracy through Solon, a tyranny, Cleisthenes' democracy and two oligarchies. Aristotle's own test of a good mixture, that it can be <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="polity-oligarchy-and-democracy-fused">called either a democracy or an oligarchy</Ref>, was illustrated with Sparta, and Megillus, the Spartan speaker of the *Laws*, could not say which regime his city was (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="sparta-saved-by-mixing">the mixture working</Ref>). On the question whether the mixture lasted, the record is on the theory's side.

### Two explanations of one fact

The theory also says why it lasted, and here Plato and Aristotle give two different answers. The *Laws* (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="sparta-saved-by-mixing">the three saviours</Ref>) says each part checked the others' excess, so that the kingship was "compounded of the right elements and duly moderated". The explanation is a balance of virtues: the elders' age tempered the kings' pride, and the ephors curbed a power still "swelling and foaming".

Aristotle reached Sparta's mixture from a list of its defects, and the defects are the parts. The ephors have "authority in the highest matters" but are "chosen from the whole people", so "the office is apt to fall into the hands of very poor men, who, being badly off, are open to bribes"; recently some of them, bribed in an affair concerning the island of Andros, "did their best to ruin the state"; and "so great and tyrannical is their power, that even the kings have been compelled to court them". The elders sit for life, though "the mind grows old as well as the body"; many "are well known to have taken bribes"; they answer to no one; and the way they are elected is "childish", a man canvassing for an office the worthiest should hold "whether he chooses or not". The method itself is described only by the Roman-era biographer: each candidate walked through the assembly while judges shut in a nearby building noted which man drew the loudest shout. The kings are distrusted by the lawgiver himself, who sent "enemies with them in the same embassy" and held "the quarrels between the kings" to be "conservative of the state". No part, on this account, is virtuous enough to temper another.

Yet the same chapter says the mixture holds, and why:

<Quote source="Politics II.9, 1270b17–26, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
The Ephoralty certainly does keep the state together; for the people are contented when they have a share in the highest office, and the result, whether due to the legislator or to chance, has been advantageous. For if a constitution is to be permanent, all the parts of the state must wish that it should exist and the same arrangements be maintained. This is the case at Sparta, where the kings desire its permanence because they have due honour in their own persons; the nobles because they are represented in the council of elders (for the office of elder is a reward of virtue); and the people, because all are eligible to the Ephoralty.
</Quote>

This is <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="the-weight-that-turns-the-scale">the test of the weight that turns the scale</Ref> applied to one city: a regime lasts when the parts that want it outweigh those that do not. Each part has something it would lose under any other regime. The kings would lose their honours under an oligarchy, the leading families their council seats under a democracy, the people their chance of the ephorate under either. Aristotle even grants that the ephorate's effect may be "due to the legislator or to chance". The stability does not need anyone to have designed it.

The finding has a cost for the theory, and Aristotle states it in the same passage. A mixture that holds by interest rather than virtue can drift. The ephors' power had made the kings court them, and "the whole constitution has deteriorated, and from being an aristocracy has turned into a democracy". It still held. Stability, on this evidence, is a property of how shares are distributed, not proof that the city is well governed.

<KeyFact>
Aristotle's test counts only the parts that hold a share: kings, leading families, the citizen people. Sparta passes it inside a boundary drawn round the citizens. The helots, who worked the citizens' lots, are not a part on this count, and had every reason to want the regime gone.
</KeyFact>

## Theory two: the helots, the case natural slavery never classifies

The helots worked every lot, and the share of the crop they handed over paid every citizen's mess and freed him for training; the leisure Aristotle took to be the condition of any good city was their labour. Thucydides reckoned them the largest servile population of any Greek city. If a theory of natural mastery could find natural masters and natural slaves anywhere in Greece, it should find them here.

### What the theory predicts

The theory, as *Politics* I.6 sets it out (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="friends-with-a-common-interest">friends with a common interest</Ref>), makes two predictions. Where master and slave are matched by nature, "they are friends and have a common interest"; where the relation "rests merely on law and force the reverse is true". And capture in war proves nothing: Greeks "do not like to call Greeks slaves", which shows that by "slave" they "really mean the natural slave", since "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="aristotles-answer-some-are-slaves-everywhere-others-nowhere">Aristotle's answer</Ref>). A servile population bound to its masters by nature should be loyal, and should not need to be held by force.

### What the record shows

The helots were Greeks conquered in war and held on their own land. Thucydides says of them that "most of the Helots were the descendants of the old Messenians that were enslaved in the famous war; and so all of them came to be called Messenians". The record of how they were held is consistent across witnesses who agree on little else. Thucydides reports an episode of 424, when an Athenian garrison on the Messenian coast was drawing helots away:

<Quote source="Thucydides IV.80, trans. R. Crawley">
Indeed fear of their numbers and obstinacy even persuaded the Lacedaemonians to the action which I shall now relate, their policy at all times having been governed by the necessity of taking precautions against them. The Helots were invited by a proclamation to pick out those of their number who claimed to have most distinguished themselves against the enemy, in order that they might receive their freedom; the object being to test them, as it was thought that the first to claim their freedom would be the most high-spirited and the most apt to rebel. As many as two thousand were selected accordingly, who crowned themselves and went round the temples, rejoicing in their new freedom. The Spartans, however, soon afterwards did away with them, and no one ever knew how each of them perished.
</Quote>

The "policy at all times" is the enemy's verdict. Plutarch, the admirer five centuries later, describes the *krypteia*: young men sent into the countryside with daggers, hiding by day and killing helots on the roads by night, choosing "the strongest and bravest". He adds that Aristotle, in his lost account, attributed it to Lycurgus and reported that the ephors, on taking office, formally declared war on the helots "in order that it may be lawful to destroy them". Plutarch's own verdict is a refusal:

<Quote source="Plutarch, Life of Lycurgus 27, trans. A. Stewart and G. Long">
So it seems to have been well said that in Lacedaemon, the free man was more free, and the slave more a slave than anywhere else. This harsh treatment, I imagine, began in later times, especially after the great earthquake, when they relate that the Helots joined the Messenians, ravaged the country, and almost conquered it. I cannot impute this wicked act of the Krypteia to Lykurgus, when I consider the gentleness and justice of his general behaviour, which also we know was inspired by Heaven.
</Quote>

The earthquake struck Sparta in the 460s; the helots of Messenia rose and held out for years on Mount Ithome, the stronghold of their old kingdom.

### What Aristotle says about them, and what he never says

Aristotle knew all this; by Plutarch's report he wrote some of it down. In the *Politics* the helots appear in three places, and each time as a problem of control. In II.5 he warns that Plato's farmers would be "much more unmanageable and conceited than the Helots". In II.10 he explains why Crete's serfs are quiet "whereas the Helots are perpetually revolting". And in II.9, the chapter on Sparta, he opens with them:

<Quote source="Politics II.9, 1269a34–b12, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
That in a well-ordered state the citizens should have leisure and not have to provide for their daily wants is generally acknowledged, but there is a difficulty in seeing how this leisure is to be attained. The Thessalian Penestae have often risen against their masters, and the Helots in like manner against the Lacedaemonians, for whose misfortunes they are always lying in wait. […] Whereas all the neighbours of the Lacedaemonians, whether Argives, Messenians, or Arcadians, were their enemies. […] Besides, if there were no other difficulty, the treatment or management of slaves is a troublesome affair; for, if not kept in hand, they are insolent, and think that they are as good as their masters, and, if harshly treated, they hate and conspire against them. Now it is clear that when these are the results the citizens of a state have not found out the secret of managing their subject population.
</Quote>

From a theory of natural hierarchy, one would expect its author to treat the helots as his paradigm case: the one Greek regime built wholly on a servile class, which he admired in part and studied closely. The text does the opposite. In none of the three passages is the word "nature" applied to the helots, and nowhere in the *Politics* are they called slaves by nature. They are a "difficulty", a "troublesome affair", a "secret" the Spartans have not found. The theory of Book I is never brought to them.

Aristotle never draws the conclusion, but his own criteria draw it in three steps. First, a relation in which the slaves are "always lying in wait" for their masters' misfortunes is not friendship with a common interest; by I.6 it is "the reverse", the relation of law and force. Second, the helots were Greeks taken by conquest, and conquest, by the argument Aristotle accepted, confers no title. Third, his description of their temper, men who when not held down "think that they are as good as their masters", is a description of spirit, the quality that in his best city disqualifies men from serving as its farmers: he wanted those farmers "not all of the same race and not spirited", so that "there will be no danger of their making a revolution". The helots were one people, spirited, with free kinsmen across the border. Aristotle's best city reads as a design against the Spartan case. By his own marks, then, the helots were slaves by law whose nature was unknown, and their conduct was what Book I predicts of men enslaved against their nature.

### The reply on Sparta's behalf

The reply has two parts, both from inside the record. The first is Xenophon's. He begins his book with "the relatively sparse population, and at the same time the extraordinary power and prestige of the community", and explains both by obedience: "there is no state in the world in which greater obedience is shown to magistrates, and to the laws themselves, than Sparta". On that view the system did what it was built to do, and a theory that judges it by the helots' contentment judges it by an aim it never had. The second is Aristotle's own. He does not condemn the arrangement. He accepts in the same chapter that citizens must have leisure and someone must work the land, and his best city is farmed by unfree men too. The helots, besides, were serfs on their own land, not chattel sold abroad; Aristotle sets them beside the Thessalian Penestae, another conquered people kept on the land, and not beside the slaves of the market.

Both points stand, and neither rescues the theory. Xenophon's defence concerns what the system achieved, great power from few citizens, and the helots' hatred does not refute that. Aristotle's concerns the arrangement, which he endorses as a planner. What fails is the claim that the arrangement is natural, and the case shows how the claim behaves when it is applied. Tested on Sparta, the theory of natural slavery turns into advice on holding down men one has conquered, the thing Book I called "merely law and force".

## Theory three: two stories of decline, and the land law that decided between them

Sparta did fall. It won the Peloponnesian War in 404, ruled most of Greece for a generation, lost a single battle to Thebes at Leuctra in Boeotia in 371, and within two years had lost Messenia, which the Thebans freed, and half its land. Plato and Aristotle gave two different accounts of why such a regime declines, and the record can choose between them.

### Plato's story and Aristotle's

Plato's account is the honour regime's decay (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes" id="the-compromise-between-gold-and-iron">timocracy</Ref>). A regime that honours victory still loves gold underneath; its rulers hoard it in secret and "run away like children from the law"; in time the love of wealth comes out into the open, and timocracy becomes oligarchy. The witnesses confirm the symptom. Aristotle says that at Sparta "wealth is too highly valued", and that the citizens, finding the law's strictness "intolerable", "run away from the law into the secret indulgence of sensual pleasures", almost Plato's words. Xenophon, the admirer, admits it in the 370s:

<Quote source="Xenophon, Constitution of the Lacedaemonians 14, trans. H. G. Dakyns">
Now, if the question be put to me, Do you maintain that the laws of Lycurgus remain still to this day unchanged? that indeed is an assertion which I should no longer venture to maintain; knowing, as I do, that in former times the Lacedaemonians preferred to live at home on moderate means […] knowing further, as I do, that formerly they dreaded to be detected in the possession of gold, whereas nowadays there are not a few who make it their glory and their boast to be possessed of it.
</Quote>

Aristotle's own account is different in kind. He takes it, he says, from a charge already made in the *Laws*:

<Quote source="Politics II.9, 1271a41–b6, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
the whole constitution has regard to one part of virtue only,—the virtue of the soldier, which gives victory in war. So long as they were at war, therefore, their power was preserved, but when they had attained empire they fell, for of the arts of peace they knew nothing, and had never engaged in any employment higher than war.
</Quote>

That is the unused iron of Book VII. The same chapter names one art of peace Sparta never learned, paying for the state: "there is no money in the treasury, although they are obliged to carry on great wars, and they are unwilling to pay taxes", so that the lawgiver "has made his city poor, and his citizens greedy". A city that could fight but not finance a war could win an empire and not keep it.

Plato's story is a change of what the city loves, from honour to money. Aristotle's charge is a city that cannot change at all: trained for one thing, it has nothing to do when that thing is done. The charge names a weakness, not how the city lost its men; the same chapter supplies that second half, a law of property. Aristotle's account of the fall is the two together, and the record has to be asked about each.

### The mechanism: how a city of equals ran out of men

Book VIII of the *Politics* adds a point: the Spartans were once superior "only on the circumstance that they trained them when their only rivals did not". Once others trained too, training no longer made up for numbers, and numbers are what Sparta lost. Aristotle traces the loss to the law of property:

<Quote source="Politics II.9, 1270a15–34, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
While some of the Spartan citizens have quite small properties, others have very large ones; hence the land has passed into the hands of a few. And this is due also to faulty laws; for, although the legislator rightly holds up to shame the sale or purchase of an inheritance, he allows anybody who likes to give or bequeath it. Yet both practices lead to the same result. And nearly two-fifths of the whole country are held by women; this is owing to the number of heiresses and to the large dowries which are customary. […] Hence, although the country is able to maintain 1500 cavalry and 30,000 hoplites, the whole number of Spartan citizens fell below 1000. The result proves the faulty nature of their laws respecting property; for the city sank under a single defeat; the want of men was their ruin.
</Quote>

**Gifts moved lots to those who could pay.** A law that only let lots change hands could as easily scatter land as gather it; what made the transfers run one way was who stood on each side of them. The law shamed the sale of a lot, so a citizen in need could not openly sell. But he could give it, and nothing stopped a gift from being paid for. A man in debt, or short of the produce his mess required, could "give" his lot to a richer citizen in return for money or for the cancellation of what he owed. The lot went to whoever could pay, which is to say to a family that already had more than it needed; the giver's sons inherited nothing. That is the force of Aristotle's "both practices lead to the same result": the gift was a sale under another name.

**Bequest follows marriage.** A citizen who died with a daughter and no son left her his lot, and the law let him, or after him his heir, "bestow his heiress on any one whom he pleases". The most sought-after husband for a girl who brought land was a son of a house that already had it, and her lot joined that house's estate. Large dowries moved land the same way, out of the bride's family and into the family that could make the best match. Each marriage of this kind turned two holdings into one, and that is how "nearly two-fifths of the whole country" came to be held by women: not as soldiers' lots but as the property of heiresses and wives in the richest houses.

**Loss of land became loss of citizenship.** The third step is in a different part of the same chapter. A citizen's place depended on his mess dues: "those who cannot contribute are not allowed to retain their rights of citizenship". A man whose lot had gone by gift, or whose father's land had gone out with his sisters as dowries, had no crop to pay with, so he lost his mess, and with it his citizenship. The land stayed; the citizens disappeared from the roll, and nothing refilled it. Aristotle records a tradition that "in the days of their ancient kings, they were in the habit of giving the rights of citizenship to strangers, and therefore, in spite of their long wars, no lack of population was experienced"; classical Sparta admitted no one. Aristotle's word for the result, translated above as "the want of men", is *oliganthropia*, "fewness of men".

### The numbers, read as reports

Every figure for Sparta's citizen numbers is a report rather than a count. In 480, an exiled Spartan king tells Xerxes, in Herodotus's telling, that Sparta has "about eight thousand men". At Mantinea in Arcadia in 418, where Sparta defeated Argos and its allies, Thucydides reconstructs the Spartan line from its company structure and gets about 3,600 men, perioeci very probably among them, after warning that the true number "was not known". Aristotle puts the citizens "below 1000" and does not say when; since the same sentence has the city already sunk "under a single defeat", the figure is most likely the number in his own day, after Leuctra, around 340. He also records a tradition of "not less than 10,000" in early times.

Taken at face value, the reports give a fall of more than eight times in about a century and a half, while the country could, in Aristotle's estimate, have maintained thirty times as many soldiers as it had citizens. The face value is uncertain on every side: Herodotus's figure is a speech he gives a character; Thucydides counts an army, not a citizen roll; Aristotle's "able to maintain" is an estimate, and his "below 1000" may count only those fit to serve. No adjustment within those uncertainties reverses the direction or brings the fall down to anything modest. The direction is also corroborated without numbers: Aristotle's list of Sparta's factions includes Cinadon, "a brave man excluded from the honours of the state", who conspired against the Spartans in the reign of Agesilaus, around 399.

### Which story the record fits

Plato's story needs a change in what the city loves to come first and the decline to follow from it, so the test is a matter of dates: when the gold came in, and when the citizen body began to shrink.

Xenophon gives the gold no year, but he ties it to the empire. In the same chapter, beside the men who boast of their gold, he sets the "leading citizens" whose one ambition is "to live to the end of their days as governors-general on a foreign soil", and Sparta had governors in foreign cities only after it won the war in 404. Plutarch dates it outright:

<Quote source="Plutarch, Life of Lycurgus 29, trans. A. Stewart and G. Long">
But in the reign of Agis money found its way into Sparta, and, after money, selfishness and greed for gain came in, on account of Lysander, who, though himself incorruptible, yet filled his country with luxury and love of gold, as he brought back gold and silver from the wars, and disregarded the laws of Lykurgus.
</Quote>

Agis II was king from about 427 to about 400; Lysander was the admiral who destroyed the Athenian fleet in 405 and brought the spoils of the war home in 404. Plutarch wrote five centuries later and wanted Lycurgus's laws to have stood until one man undid them, so a single late date suits his hero. It is credible here because it agrees with Xenophon, who saw the change and had the opposite motive: gold boasted of is a defect in the city he wrote to praise.

The shrinking is dated by the two reports that come before 404: about 8,000 men in 480, about 3,600 in the Spartan line at Mantinea in 418. Neither is a citizen count. The first is a number Herodotus puts in an exiled king's mouth, the second Thucydides' reconstruction of an army that very probably included perioeci, so how far the roll had fallen by 418 is uncertain. But both reports are older than the gold, and the second is already less than half the first. The shrinking came before the gold, the reverse of Plato's order.

The same test of dates splits Aristotle's account. The single-virtue charge puts the fall after the empire: "so long as they were at war … their power was preserved". The defeat did come after 404, but the reports show the roll falling between 480 and 418, during the wars and before any empire, so the single virtue cannot have started the shrinking. The land law has no date to miss: it worked in every generation, through every gift and every heiress's marriage, and so predicts a roll falling from the earliest report on, which is what the reports, uncertain as they are, describe.

The land-law half also needs a city that keeps its laws while its laws undo it, the Spartans "continuing in the observance of his laws and no one interferes with them", in his words from Book VII. Xenophon's admission that they are "plainly disobedient … to the laws of their own lawgiver" seems to cut against that, but the laws he says they break are the ban on gold and the ban on living abroad. The laws Aristotle blames were kept: the shame on selling a lot with freedom to give or bequeath it, a father's free choice of a husband for his heiress, the loss of rights for unpaid mess dues. These drained the citizen body by working as written, while the training stayed perfect. The record confirms the land-law half as the mechanism and the single-virtue half only as the moment of the end: the love of gold was a symptom of the empire, the land law and the mess dues emptied the roll from the start, and once Sparta ruled, a city with no treasury and no art but war had nothing to absorb a loss, so the single defeat at Leuctra was enough. The "city of equals" had become an oligarchy of fewer and fewer, without any law being changed.

## Half the city without laws: the household hierarchy tested

Two-fifths of the land in women's hands is a fact about property. Aristotle also treats it as a failure of the household order his Book I called natural. Does that hierarchy behave at Sparta as a natural one should?

### The critic's account

<Quote source="Politics II.9, 1269b12–23, 1269b37–1270a8, trans. B. Jowett, rev. W. D. Ross">
For, a husband and a wife being each a part of every family, the state may be considered as about equally divided into men and women; and, therefore, in those states in which the condition of the women is bad, half the city may be regarded as having no laws. And this is what has actually happened at Sparta; the legislator wanted to make the whole state hardy and temperate, and he has carried out his intention in the case of the men, but he has neglected the women, who live in every sort of intemperance and luxury. […] This licence of the Lacedaemonian women existed from the earliest times, and was only what might be expected. For, during the wars of the Lacedaemonians, first against the Argives, and afterwards against the Arcadians and Messenians, the men were long away from home, and, on the return of peace, they gave themselves into the legislator's hand, already prepared by the discipline of a soldier's life […]. But, when Lycurgus, as tradition says, wanted to bring the women under his laws, they resisted, and he gave up the attempt.
</Quote>

He adds that "many things were managed by their women", that it makes no difference "whether women rule, or the rulers are ruled by women", and that in the Theban invasion "they were utterly useless and caused more confusion than the enemy".

### The admirer's account

Xenophon describes the same freedom as the lawgiver's design. Elsewhere girls sat at home and spun wool; Lycurgus, "believing that the highest function of a free woman was the bearing of children", made physical training "incumbent no less on the female than the male", with contests in running and strength, because "where both parents were strong their progeny would be found to be more vigorous". Plutarch goes further and contradicts the critic by name: "It is not true that, as Aristotle says, he endeavoured to regulate the lives of the women, and failed."

The two accounts agree on the facts: Spartan women were trained and were outside the men's daily discipline. That they had large authority in their households is Aristotle's report alone. They disagree on the cause, a design for strong mothers against a lawgiver who gave up, and on the value. The land evidence sits with the critic: two-fifths of the country passing through heiresses and dowries is a fact both about women's wealth and about the shrinking roll, and none of the admirers denies it.

### What would count against the household theory

For the helots, Aristotle's theory made a prediction the record could break: a natural master and slave are friends, and the record showed force. The household theory has to be held to a prediction of the same kind. Book I gave the husband *constitutional* rule over his wife, the rule over a part that has a voice of its own and must be persuaded rather than simply ordered (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="every-composite-has-a-ruling-part">every composite has a ruling part</Ref>), and made the inequality "permanent" because the woman's deliberative faculty is "without authority" (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="three-rules-in-one-household">three rules in one household</Ref>).

The obvious test is persistence. If the rule is founded in nature, it should not depend on the ruler's standing guard; when the men came home from the wars, it should have taken hold again. Aristotle's own account says it did not: the women's "licence" began while the men "were long away from home", and when Lycurgus tried to bring them under his laws "they resisted, and he gave up the attempt". On this test the household hierarchy held only while it was enforced, which is how a rule by custom and force behaves.

### Aristotle's reply

The test misreads the theory, and Aristotle's own text has the reply, in three steps. First, the rule is constitutional, not despotic. It works by persuading a part that can answer back, as reason must manage appetite, and persuasion can fail; the theory never promised a rule that runs itself the way the soul moves the body. Second, the theory says what such a part does when no one rules it. A deliberative faculty "without authority" judges, but its judgments are not fit to govern, and a household left to them should fall into disorder: the women, unruled for years, lived "in every sort of intemperance and luxury", and Aristotle calls the result "only what might be expected". Third, I.5's rule of evidence: nature's intentions are to be looked for "in things which retain their nature, and not in things which are corrupted". A lawgiver who "neglected the women" left half the city in that corrupted state, and a corrupted case does not count against the hierarchy. On Aristotle's terms Sparta does not refute the household theory; it illustrates it.

### Judging the reply

The reply is consistent with Book I, and it disposes of the persistence test: a rule by persuasion can lapse without being unnatural. Its cost shows when you ask which Spartan record would have counted against the theory. Wives who obeyed would be nature at work. Women who ran estates and held two-fifths of the land are an unruled part doing what such a part does. Every outcome has a place. Book I's rule about corrupted cases has the same gap wherever it is used: it tells you to set corrupted cases aside, but not how to recognise one (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="four-places-a-mark-could-be">four places a mark could be</Ref>). At Sparta the only sign of corruption on offer is the conduct the theory is meant to explain. The women's independence is the evidence that they were unruled, and their being unruled is the explanation of their independence.

A fair test would need a mark of a deliberation "without authority" that could be found before seeing who obeys whom, and Book I gives none, as it gave none for the natural slave's soul. The record even contains what a reader without the theory would take for deliberation that carried authority: "many things were managed by their women". Aristotle reads that as disorder, since it makes no difference "whether women rule, or the rulers are ruled by women", but the reading comes from the theory, not from the managing.

### Three answers to who should rule

The five rows do not bear equally on the three answers. Two bear on rule by nature, two on the mixed regime, and the timocracy row bears on rule by knowledge only from a distance: Plato's sequence of decline begins with the best city, ruled by philosophers, falling into timocracy, and Sparta was never that city, so the row's "partly" judges a later stage of the story and leaves its first step untested.

**Mastery by nature fails.** Two rows carry it. <Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery" id="mastery-by-nature-fails">Natural slavery was already judged to fail</Ref>, graded established, because no mark tells a natural slave from a captive. What Sparta adds is the case that verdict predicted: the one Greek regime built wholly on a servile class shows the relation Book I calls law and force, and the theory's author, who knew the case well, never calls the helots slaves by nature. The household row cannot make up for it, since a theory that no household could break has not been confirmed by this one. What Sparta does not test is the weaker claim that some are fitter than others to lead free men; <Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery">the module's earlier lesson</Ref> grants that claim, and the mixture that held is one way of acting on it, with yearly ephors chosen from the whole people.

**The mixed regime of the middle survives.** Two rows carry it, and they fit together. The mixture's stability fits the record for Aristotle's reason: every part held a share it would lose. The half of Aristotle's account of the fall that the dates confirm, the land law, is the one that destroyed exactly that condition. It gathered lots into a few houses and the mess rule struck the landless from the roll, so the body of citizens who held the people's share shrank from thousands to under a thousand. What vanished was the middle. Aristotle's polity rests on men of moderate property numerous enough to <Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" id="the-weight-that-turns-the-scale">turn the scale</Ref>, and the Sparta he describes had lost them: "some of the Spartan citizens have quite small properties, others have very large ones". The regime held while its parts had shares, and fell when too few men were left to hold them. The answer that survives is Aristotle's, in both its halves: give every part a share, and keep the citizen body broad. Sparta kept the first and lost the second.

**Rule by knowledge is untested.** No city in the record was ruled by philosophers. Sparta, with unwritten laws, an assembly that shouted and a government that hid its own numbers, was the furthest thing from a city governed by reasoned knowledge, so the record cannot test the philosopher king. That is a finding about the evidence: it leaves Plato's answer neither confirmed nor refuted.

### The objection from the witnesses

Every one of these witnesses was a foreigner: Athenians who admired or feared Sparta, a philosopher trained at Athens, a Roman-era biographer. Sparta's own voice survives in an oracle, a few lines of poetry and a collection of sayings. Modern historians call the resulting picture the *Spartan mirage*: the claim that the austere, equal, obedient Sparta of the sources is largely an image that outsiders built for their own arguments, Athenian oligarchs holding up a model, philosophers an example, and that the real city behind it is mostly out of reach.

He wears a close-fitting helmet with a tall curved crest; the front of the crest is smooth, whiter stone. The objection is right about what the record can establish: every institution above rests on foreign witnesses, and every number on reports. It does not damage the tests, because the theories were built on the same reports. Plato, Aristotle and Xenophon knew no other Sparta than the one these witnesses describe. A test of their theories against the evidence they themselves had is fair to the theories, even if it is not a history of Sparta. What the objection does limit is how far the verdict reaches: one city, seen from outside.

### The rival cause: fear of the helots

The second objection accepts the record and disputes the cause. Sparta's citizens lived among a subject people many times their number, who were "always lying in wait" for their misfortunes; Thucydides says Sparta's "policy at all times" was "governed by the necessity of taking precautions against them", and that no Greek city held more slaves. Men in that position close ranks. On this reading, what held kings, elders and people together for four centuries was the danger below them, and the mixture was the form their unity took rather than its cause. It is a reading with a following among modern interpreters, who trace even Sparta's caution abroad to its fear of a slave revolt.

The objection is strong, and it narrows the verdict, but it does not reverse it, for two reasons.

**Fear explains the wrong unity.** A common enemy explains why the citizens stood together against the helots. It does not settle the quarrel a common enemy leaves open, which of the citizens commands the others: a king could use the danger to claim more power as easily as he could hold back, and a people asked to fight could demand more of the government. What settled that quarrel, by Aristotle's account, was that each part had something under the existing regime that it would lose under any other, and the record of four centuries without a tyrant is a record of that quarrel staying settled.

**The fear belongs to the fall, not the stability.** Aristotle opens his chapter on Sparta with the helot danger and closes his account of its fall with the shrinking roll. He names both as causes of the end: a city that had to hold a hostile people down needed citizens to do it, and "the city sank under a single defeat" when too few were left. So his account does give fear a place, but in the fall, not in the four centuries before it. Where the record shows fear at work, in the end of the regime, the theory has already explained it; the rival has to carry the stability on fear alone, and there the first reason tells against it.

Together the two objections turn "the mixture explains Sparta's stability" into "the mixture fits it best", which is why the grade is on balance and not strong.

</Verdict>

<Conclusion>
Sparta, read through its witnesses, was a regime of two kings, a life council, five yearly ephors and an assembly of equals, living on lots worked by conquered Greeks and on messes that cost a fixed contribution, and it is known only from foreigners who admired or criticised it. Run against that record, the theories divide. The mixed constitution's stability fits, for Aristotle's reason rather than the *Laws*': every part was corrupt, and the regime held because each part had a share it would lose. Aristotle's account of the fall fits on its land-law half, which the reports date from before the empire, while the single virtue explains only why one defeat was enough; Plato's honour-to-money sequence catches only the symptom. Natural slavery does not fit, and the household hierarchy is left untested. So, of the module's three answers to who should rule, the mixed regime of the middle holds up best: Sparta lasted while every part had a share, and it ended when the land law shrank the citizen body. Mastery by nature fails by its own author's test, and rule by knowledge has no case in the record to test. The verdict is on balance, because the record is one city known only from foreigners, and fear of the helots may have held the citizens together where the lesson credits the shares.

You can now reconstruct the Spartan regime from its reports, say what each witness can establish, judge where each theory fits the record, and weigh a verdict against its strongest rival. The regime outlived its purpose: Messenia was lost for good in 369, and Sparta kept its upbringing, its messes and its few citizens for nearly two more centuries, until a foreign conqueror abolished the system in 188 BC; why a society holds on to an arrangement that no longer serves it is taken up in <Ref lesson="toynbee-challenge-and-response" />. The verdict's second half, keep the citizen body broad, is the one Sparta broke, and the next city kept it: Rome enlarged its citizen body as it conquered. A Greek hostage there sets Lycurgus's designed mixture beside the one Rome reached by trial and disaster, and asks whether a mixture can survive an empire: <Ref lesson="polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome" />.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Apply the sourcing questions to the rider Plutarch reports: "and if the people shall decide crookedly, the chiefs and elders shall set it right", added, he says, by the kings Polydorus and Theopompus, who "persuaded the city that this also was ordained by the god". Who is said to have added it, what does it record, what does it argue, what does it omit, and what can it be used to establish about whether Sparta's assembly was ever sovereign? Then say which of Aristotle's two names for Sparta, democracy or oligarchy, the rider supports.

<Solution>
**Who and when.** Two kings of the early seventh century or a little before, Theopompus being the king credited with the ephors; the report is Plutarch's, around AD 100, citing a poem of Tyrtaeus, a Spartan poet of the seventh century, as his evidence. The rider is therefore attested by a near-contemporary poem, through a late biographer.

**What it records.** That the assembly's "final decision" in the original text could now be overruled by the kings and the council when they judged it "crooked". Plutarch adds the occasion: the people had been amending motions, "additions and omissions", rather than simply voting them up or down.

**What it argues.** In its own words, nothing; it is a further command in the god's voice. Plutarch's gloss argues that it "practically excluded" the people.

**What it omits.** Any test of crookedness, and any body other than the kings and elders to apply it. The judges of the people's decisions are one party to the dispute.

**What it can establish.** That the assembly's sovereignty, if it ever existed, lasted only until the people began to use it independently, and that from then on the people could ratify or refuse but not reshape what the council proposed. It cannot establish how often the veto was used. Read with Plutarch's next chapter, it shows a regime mixed by amendment, in which the reign that curbed the people also curbed the kings and elders with the ephors; it cannot show that the people were curbed first, since the only evidence for that order is the order of Plutarch's chapters.

**Which name.** Oligarchy. Aristotle's oligarchic marks were election instead of lot and "a few" deciding the gravest matters; the rider puts the last word in the hands of thirty men. The ephorate, open to all and created in the same reign, is what gave the regime its democratic name, which is why Aristotle can say the constitution "from being an aristocracy has turned into a democracy".

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "Sparta proves Aristotle's theory of natural slavery: a master people ruled a slave people for three centuries." Name the steps that fail, and state what Sparta does show about the theory.

<Solution>
**Step 1: Aristotle never says it.** In the three passages where the *Politics* mentions the helots (II.5, II.9, II.10), they are a problem of management and revolt; the word "nature" is never applied to them, and they are nowhere called slaves by nature. The claim attributes to the theory an application its author did not make.

**Step 2: the title was conquest.** The helots were Greeks conquered in war. Aristotle accepted the objection that capture proves nothing about the captive, and reported that Greeks "do not like to call Greeks slaves". By his own argument, conquest is no title to natural mastery.

**Step 3: the relation was the reverse of friendship.** A natural relation, by I.6, is a friendship with a common interest. The helots were "always lying in wait" for their masters' misfortunes, "perpetually revolting", and were held by the krypteia and by massacres such as that of the two thousand in 424. I.6 calls such a relation "the reverse", one of "law and force".

**Step 4: their temper is the temper of free men.** Aristotle's own description, men who when not held down "think that they are as good as their masters", describes spirit, the trait his best city avoids in its farmers so that "there will be no danger of their making a revolution".

**What Sparta shows.** That the theory, when its author met the one case where it would have had to be applied, was not applied; and that by its own tests the helots' condition was slavery by law, whose relation to their nature is unknown. Three centuries of rule show that conquest can be maintained by force, which the theory never denied.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Two boundary cases for Aristotle's stability test ("all the parts of the state must wish that it should exist"). (a) Remove the ephors. Which part no longer wishes the regime to exist, and what did Aristotle say the ephorate did for it? Use the Great Rhetra's rider to say what that part had left. (b) Restore the ephors and remove the helots instead: the citizens must work their own lots. Which institutions survive, and what does the answer show about which part of the regime the mixture actually rested on?

<Solution>
**(a)** The people. Aristotle says "the people are contented when they have a share in the highest office", and that they wish the regime to last "because all are eligible to the Ephoralty". Without the ephors the people have only the assembly, and since the rider their decisions can be "set right" by kings and elders when judged "crooked": a right to shout yes or no to motions they cannot amend. On Aristotle's test the regime would then rest on two parts, kings and leading families, against a citizen body with no share it would lose. That is the condition in which, by Book V, the many form a party with a grievance. Plutarch's dating fits: the ephors came in the same reign as the rider, so the reign that took away the assembly's last word gave the people, in the same years, an office open to all; and Theopompus, who gave up power to create it, said what he left his sons was "more lasting".

**(b)** Very little survives. The messes depend on a monthly contribution from the produce of a lot the citizen does not work; the agoge depends on boys and men with no other occupation; standing readiness for war depends on leisure. A citizen who farms his own lot cannot train full-time or eat every evening in a mess. The constitution's governing parts, kings, elders, ephors and assembly, could survive in a city of working farmers; the way of life that defined the equals could not.

**What it shows.** Case (b) is the one the stability test never runs: the governing mixture survives without the helots, the citizens' way of life does not. So the regime's stability and the regime's existence rest on different parts, and only the first is counted. Aristotle's own best city is built the same way: it draws <Ref lesson="aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life" id="parts-and-conditions">its boundary round the equals</Ref> and leaves those who work the land outside it.

Take the three reported figures: about 8,000 men in 480 (Herodotus), about 3,600 in the Spartan line at Mantinea in 418 (Thucydides), fewer than 1,000 citizens in Aristotle's day, after Leuctra (Aristotle). (a) State the fall from the first to the last as a ratio and say what each figure can and cannot count. (b) Aristotle names two mechanisms of the fall: concentration of land through gift, bequest, heiresses and dowries, and loss of citizenship for unpaid mess dues. Can the numbers alone distinguish them? (c) What further evidence would?

<Solution>
**(a)** From about 8,000 to under 1,000 is a fall of more than eight times in about a century and a half. The figures are not alike. Herodotus's is a round number spoken by a character, a former Spartan king, in a scene the historian composed; it may count men of military age. Thucydides' is a reconstruction of one army from its company structure, with his own warning that the number "was not known", and it probably includes men who were not full citizens. Aristotle's is a report of citizens, undated in his text and placed after Leuctra only by inference from the "single defeat" in the same sentence; it may count only men fit for the field. None is a census. A defensible statement: the reports agree on a large fall, very likely several times over, and do not support any precise rate.

**(b)** No. Both mechanisms reduce the roll and both act through land: concentration takes lots from some families, and the mess rule converts the loss of a lot into the loss of citizenship. The roll alone records the result. What separates them is where the excluded men went. If concentration acted alone, men without lots might still have been citizens, poor ones; the mess rule is what turned them into non-citizens.

**(c)** Evidence of a class of men who had been citizens or citizens' sons and were not: men present in the city, Spartan by birth and upbringing, but outside the roll. Aristotle mentions one: Cinadon, "a brave man excluded from the honours of the state", who conspired in Agesilaus's reign. An account of who Cinadon's followers were, how many, and why they had lost their status would show the mess rule at work; evidence of rich families acquiring lots through marriages to heiresses would show concentration. The calculation sets a scale; only such evidence could weigh the two mechanisms against each other.

Check the verdict column of the ledger of five theories against the Spartan record. Without looking back at the body, write for each row "fits", "partly", "does not fit" or "untested", with one sentence of evidence and one sentence saying why that evidence bears on the theory (the warrant). Where you disagree with the lesson's verdicts, defend your own.

<Solution>
The lesson's verdicts with their evidence and warrants; the third and fifth rows each have a second defensible verdict.

**Mixed constitution: fits.** *Evidence:* "the same form of government for more than four hundred years" with "a freedom from tyrants which was unbroken". *Warrant:* the theory predicts that a regime giving each part a share outlasts a simple one, and no simple regime in the Greek record lasted so long; Aristotle's evidence that each part was corrupt shows the fit is for his reason, shares that would be lost, not the *Laws*' balance of virtues.

**Aristotle's account of the fall: fits, on its land-law half.** *Evidence:* the roll already falling between 480 and 418; the victory of 404, the defeat of 371, "the city sank under a single defeat; the want of men was their ruin". *Warrant:* the land law, working in every generation, predicts a roll falling from the start while the laws are kept "and no one interferes with them"; the single-virtue charge dates the fall after the empire and so explains why one defeat was enough, not the shrinking that came before it.

**Plato's timocracy: partly** (or "does not"). *Evidence:* gold hoarded and then boasted of (Xenophon 14; Aristotle's "secret indulgence"). *Warrant:* the symptom Plato predicts is attested; but the gold arrives with the empire after 404, and the fall in citizen numbers is driven by inheritance and mess dues. A reader who counts mechanism over symptom says "does not".

**Natural slavery: does not fit.** *Evidence:* the helots "always lying in wait", the krypteia, the two thousand who disappeared in 424; Aristotle never calls them slaves by nature. *Warrant:* the theory predicts friendship where slavery is natural; the record shows the relation I.6 calls law and force, and the theory's author did not apply it.

**Household hierarchy: untested** (or "fits", on Aristotle's terms). *Evidence:* the women's independence began while the men "were long away from home", Lycurgus "gave up the attempt", and "many things were managed by their women". *Warrant:* on Aristotle's terms the rule is constitutional and can fail, an unruled part with deliberation "without authority" falls into "licence", and a corrupted case does not count, so the record confirms the theory. But the only sign that Sparta was a corrupted case is the conduct the theory explains, so no record of any household could count against it; a theory that no evidence could break has not been tested by this evidence. A reader who accepts the corrupted-case clause can write "fits" and must then say what would ever have counted as a misfit.

<Exercise>
The lesson concluded, on balance, that the mixed regime of the middle is the Greek answer that survives Sparta, its stability explained by every part's holding a share. Attack the verdict. (a) State the strongest rival explanation in its best form, and name the passages that support it. (b) Say why the rival, at full strength, narrows the verdict rather than reverses it, or argue that it reverses it. (c) Aristotle's next chapter describes Crete: institutions like Sparta's, but chief magistrates "elected out of certain families, and not out of the whole people", subject farmers who are "contented" because the island has no hostile neighbours, and nobles who "get together a party among the common people and their own friends and then quarrel and fight with one another". Does Crete help either side? (d) What evidence would overturn the verdict rather than merely weaken it?

<Solution>
**(a) The rival.** Sparta held together through fear of the helots; the mixture was the form the citizens' unity took, not its cause. The passages: Aristotle opens his chapter on Sparta with the helots, who "have often risen against their masters" and are "always lying in wait" for their misfortunes, and adds that all Sparta's neighbours were its enemies; Thucydides says that Sparta's "policy at all times" was "governed by the necessity of taking precautions against them", and that Chios had more slaves than any city "except Lacedaemon"; the two thousand helots who vanished in 424 show what the precautions were. A city whose citizens are a small armed minority among a hostile majority has every reason to suppress its own quarrels, whatever its constitution.

**(b) Why it narrows.** Fear explains unity against the helots. It does not settle who among the citizens commands: a king could have used the danger to take more power, and the people could have demanded more of the council. That those quarrels stayed settled for four centuries is what the shares explain, each part having something it would lose under any other regime. Aristotle does give the fear a place, but in the fall: his analysis of Sparta begins with the helot danger and ends with a roll too small to survive "a single defeat", and he names both as causes of the end, not of the stability. On the stability itself the two accounts compete, and the shares explain what fear leaves open. So the rival weakens "the mixture explains the stability" to "the mixture fits it best", which is why the verdict is on balance and not strong. To argue that it reverses the verdict, you would have to show that the shares did no work, which is part (d).

**(c) Crete cannot decide between them.** Crete lacked both things the rival accounts point to. Its offices were closed to "certain families", so the people had no share, and its serfs were quiet, so there was no common danger. It fell into faction among the nobles, a state Aristotle calls "really a close oligarchy". The mixture reading predicts that faction from the closed offices; the fear reading predicts it from the absence of danger. Both causes are absent together, so the case cannot separate them. A city that settles the question would have to have one without the other.

**(d) What would overturn it.** Evidence that a closed group of families held Sparta's offices throughout its stable centuries, the ephorate included, so that its openness "to the whole people" was nominal and the people had no share to lose. Or a second Greek regime that gave its people no share and still lasted as long without a tyrant or a revolution, held together by an outside danger alone. Either would show that stability did not need the shares.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Aristotle explained Sparta's stability by every part's having a share, and its fall by a training for one virtue and a land law that shrank the citizen body. Take a city that mixes two chief magistrates elected each year with a king's command in war, a council of former magistrates sitting for life, and assemblies of the people with officers who can veto the magistrates; and that, unlike Sparta, kept enlarging its citizen body by granting citizenship to conquered peoples, and paid for its wars with coined money and tribute. Using what the Spartan case showed, predict which of Sparta's failures this city avoids and which it may not.

<Solution>
**Avoided: the shrinking citizen body.** Sparta's roll fell because citizenship depended on a lot and a mess contribution, and no one new was admitted; by the tradition Aristotle records, the early kings' habit of giving citizenship to strangers had kept numbers up "in spite of their long wars". A city that enfranchises the conquered does what early Sparta is said to have done and classical Sparta did not: it replaces its losses and grows, so a single defeat does not empty it.

**Avoided: the inability to pay for its wars.** Sparta had "no money in the treasury, although they are obliged to carry on great wars", and its citizens were "unwilling to pay taxes": one of the arts of peace a city trained only for war never learned. A city that pays for its wars with coin and tribute has learned that art, so winning an empire does not leave it unable to hold one.

**Kept, perhaps: the shares that hold the mixture.** The magistrates have honour and command, the council has its seats, and the people have the veto officers, so each part has a share it would lose, Aristotle's condition for permanence.

**Not avoided, perhaps: the single virtue and the drift of every simple part.** If the city is organised for war and conquest, Aristotle's iron sentence applies to it once it has nothing left to conquer; and a mixture held by interest can drift as Sparta's did from aristocracy towards democracy, as the balance of shares moves.

**The connection.** This is the comparison a Greek historian of Rome draws: Lycurgus's laws were "entirely sufficient" for "guarding their own country with absolute safety, and for preserving their own freedom", but for empire "the Spartan constitution is deficient, and that of Rome superior and better constituted for obtaining power" (<Ref lesson="polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome" />). Sparta's two further centuries without an empire return in <Ref lesson="toynbee-challenge-and-response" />.

A position on who rules a country, who may join it and what it owes is usually stated as a list of conclusions, and nobody can tell which premise each conclusion needs or what would make its holder give it up. May a state refuse citizenship to a man who meets every legal test but preaches that its constitution is void? May it require its language of an applicant whose parents were born elsewhere, and refuse nothing else? Does a citizen owe anything for what the state did before he was born? A position that answers these must name the premises it starts from, grade each against the record, derive each answer from named premises, and hold no answer more firmly than the weakest premise it uses.

<Ref lesson="the-argument-assembled">The argument assembled</Ref> reached an answer, on balance: liberal freedom is real and worth keeping, but it does not found itself. It lives on things it did not create: a nation, an elite held under law, an inherited culture it cannot produce on demand, and an economy of property and markets. A conclusion of that size cannot by itself decide the three questions above.

The obvious extension is a list of what the position holds, each item with its reason: "a state may control who joins, because a free state lives on a nation." That reason supports refusing the preacher of a void constitution far more strongly than refusing a fluent, employed applicant of another faith, yet the list gives both the same confidence and names no evidence that would make its holder give either up.

## A doctrine is a set of premises that can each be overturned, and of consequences no firmer than their weakest premise

The repair is to separate premises from conclusions. Each premise, a *postulate*, is stated with its grounds, its grade and what would overturn it. Each conclusion, a *thesis*, is derived from postulates named by number, so its strength can be read off the premises it uses and the loss of any one premise can be traced.

### What counts as a ground

A postulate rests either on a verdict reached against the record, a forecast checked against what followed or a claim against the evidence, or on an argument that survived the strongest objection made to it. A theory's following is not evidence: undivided sovereignty was the received theory, and the settlement of 1689 refuted it. The same tests were put to claims from the right and from the left, each judged claim counted once by whose claim it was and open items left out, and the failure rates came out almost equal: 59% of the right-leaning claims failed (73 of 123), and 58% of the left and liberal ones (38 of 66).

### The grades

Every grade below comes from a case already judged:

| Grade | What it means | A case that has it |
|---|---|---|
| **established** | the failure is internal to the text, or the evidence admits no reasonable rival | natural slavery fails by Aristotle's own concessions (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery">natural slavery</Ref>) |
| **strong** | the record decides it, and the best objection narrows it without reversing it | a kingdom with no settled answer to "who decides" goes to war (<Ref lesson="english-revolution-as-test">1642</Ref>) |
| **on balance** | the evidence leans one way and a serious rival reading remains | the mixed regime held Sparta together (<Ref lesson="sparta-as-test-case">Sparta</Ref>) |
| **open** | the evidence does not decide; the settling evidence is named | the genetic share of the gaps between groups in measured test scores (<Ref lesson="national-data-audited">the national data</Ref>) |
| **argued** | a premise about what is good: evidence can inform it and cannot decide it | P16, P17, and peace over war |

Three rules compose the grades. A postulate takes the grade of the verdicts that ground it, and where it has two halves of different strength, the weaker. A thesis is no firmer than the weakest premise it uses. An argued premise is not placed on the evidence scale: a thesis that uses one is graded by its weakest evidential premise, and its "may" or "should" stands only as firmly as the argument for the value premise. A postulate no thesis uses is a background premise: it frames the others and derives nothing.

### Two aims held in common

A thesis that says what a state should do needs a premise about what is good. Two such premises are held on nearly every side, so they are stated once and named wherever a thesis uses them. Offices should go to the capable: Plato, Aristotle, the elite theorists and the meritocratic liberals all hold it, and dispute only who the capable are and how they are found. Peace is better than war: graded argued, because it has dissenters, from the counter-revolutionary who called war "mysterious and divine" (<Ref lesson="maistre-and-bonald">Maistre</Ref>) to the syndicalist who praised violence for the energy it restored (<Ref lesson="sorel-reflections-on-violence">Sorel</Ref>) and the officer who saw in the trenches an ordeal that made a new kind of man (<Ref lesson="junger-storm-of-steel-and-the-worker">Jünger</Ref>).

## People are unequal in ability and equal in standing, social by nature, and always ruled by a few

What can a free state take as given about the people it governs and about who will govern them?

### Ability, society and envy

<Postulate
  grade="strong"
  grounds="capacity to lead differs partly by birth (strong); measured ability predicts adult outcomes within families (strong); every argument from unequal ability to ownership failed (established and strong)."
  overturnedBy="a criterion, stated in advance, that separates natural subordinates from free men and does not reduce to whoever was captured or whoever was born where."
>
People are unequal in ability, partly by birth, and equal in standing under law.
</Postulate>

People differ in the abilities that matter for work and for leading, partly by inheritance, and nothing in the difference makes one person another's owner or natural master. The factual half rests on twin studies in which genes account for about 30% of the variation in who comes to hold leading roles (<Ref lesson="aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery">natural slavery</Ref>), and on a test score taken in youth that predicts adult outcomes about as well between siblings, with family background held fixed (<Ref lesson="the-cognitive-elite">the cognitive elite</Ref>). The equal-standing half rests on failures. Natural slavery failed by Aristotle's own concession that nature gives the natural slave no mark. "Freedom among equals, slavery for outsiders" failed because the Greeks enslaved Greeks: "outsider" means whoever was captured or born elsewhere. Slavery as a "positive good" failed on the slave states' own record (<Ref lesson="locke-and-mill-on-empire">Locke and Mill on empire</Ref>).

The objection is that equal standing is a value, not a finding. The half claimed as a finding is negative: no criterion that picks out the natural subordinate has survived its test. The positive value is P16's.

<Postulate
  grade="strong"
  grounds="no solitary, pre-social stage of human life (established); states have lower rates of lethal violence than bands, tribes and chiefdoms (strong)."
  overturnedBy="a consistent finding that states have higher rates of violent death than non-state societies."
>
People are social by nature, and the state lowers violence; there was never a peaceful solitary stage.
</Postulate>

Humans have always lived in groups; the solitary natural man of the contract theorists never existed. In prehistoric bands and tribes about 2% of deaths were killings, chiefdoms ran higher and states run lower (<Ref lesson="rousseau-inequality-and-general-will">Rousseau against Hobbes</Ref>). The objection, that states make wars, misses that the comparison counts violent deaths per head, wars included.

<Postulate
  grade="on balance"
  grounds="envy is a measured motive for supporting redistribution, the weakest of three (on balance); a motive neither proves nor refutes the justice of what is demanded (strong)."
  overturnedBy="for the motive, failure of the envy effect to replicate in representative samples; for the logical half, nothing."
>
Envy is a real political motive, though it decides no question of justice.
</Postulate>

Survey studies in four countries, and a British replication, find that envy predicts support for taxing the rich alongside compassion and self-interest, at about a third to half of compassion's weight (<Ref lesson="ressentiment-and-envy">ressentiment and envy</Ref>). The objection from the right reads the demand for redistribution as ressentiment and would refuse it. It fails twice: a motive neither refutes nor proves the claim it drives, and the same transfers were proposed with no egalitarian motive: the German chancellor Bismarck's social insurance of the 1880s, and the American social scientist Charles Murray's proposal of 2006 for an annual grant of \$10,000 to every adult.

### Who rules and what checks them

<Postulate
  grade="on balance"
  grounds="an organised minority holds office, as a strong tendency rather than an iron law (strong); the minority stays capable only where office is contested and removable (on balance)."
  overturnedBy="closed, hereditary or co-opting rulers who kept their capacity across generations."
>
A few always rule; what matters is how they are selected and checked.
</Postulate>

Every society is governed by an organised minority, because a hundred who act together beat a thousand who act alone (<Ref lesson="mosca-ruling-class">Mosca's ruling class</Ref>), and inside parties and unions the leadership entrenches itself, as a tendency rather than a law (<Ref lesson="michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites">Michels and Pareto</Ref>). The weaker half sets the grade: the rulers are the most capable only where they compete and can be removed. Hereditary succession took Rome from the emperor Marcus Aurelius, a capable ruler, to his son Commodus, an incapable one: in a contemporary's words, "from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust". The objection, a union whose two organised parties alternated in office for decades, shows alternation between organised minorities: the postulate's second half at work.

<Postulate
  grade="strong"
  grounds="1642, two claimants and civil war (strong); 1660 and 1689, a limited crown that lasted (strong)."
  overturnedBy="showing that the limits of 1660 and 1689 were nominal."
>
Every order needs a settled last word; undecided sovereignty means war; the last word can be held by a composite body bound by law.
</Postulate>

A *last word* is the authority whose decision ends a dispute about who decides. In 1642 the English king and Parliament each claimed it, and the result was civil war. After 1689 a crown limited by Parliament held it jointly and lasted (<Ref lesson="english-revolution-as-test">the English Revolution</Ref>; the theory in <Ref lesson="bodin-sovereignty">Bodin</Ref> and <Ref lesson="hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty">Hobbes</Ref>). Hobbes's best reply, that king-in-parliament is one sovereign, fails because the settlement kept the divisions his theory called fatal: the Crown's executive, Parliament's purse, independent judges.

<Postulate
  grade="on balance"
  grounds="the state's share of output grew greatly across the democratising era (established); as a law of near-uninterrupted growth the thesis failed after 1990 (on balance); where the checks were removed, power grew (on balance to strong)."
  overturnedBy="power that stayed limited where the named checks were absent."
>
Power tends to grow unless it is checked by bodies it does not control.
</Postulate>

Government spending in fourteen industrial countries rose from about a tenth of output around 1870 to about two-fifths in 1980; Canada and New Zealand then cut it from about 53% to under 40%, through elected governments and voted budgets (<Ref lesson="jouvenel-on-power">Jouvenel</Ref>). So the claim fails as a law and holds as a tendency whose checks are named before the record is read: elections that can remove a government, courts that can annul its acts, budgets voted by others. Where they were removed, power grew: the Weimar Republic, Germany's democracy of 1919–1933, governed by emergency decree from 1930 with no end set (<Ref lesson="article-48-weimar">Article 48</Ref>), and a Hungarian majority in 2013 wrote back into the constitution provisions its court had annulled (<Ref lesson="hungarys-fundamental-law">Hungary's Fundamental Law</Ref>). Whether the growth came from power's appetite or from voters' demand is open.

## Belonging is received before it is chosen, and a free state lives on it without producing it

The contract theory says a people is those who agreed to be governed together. What does the record put in place of that answer?

### Where belonging comes from

<Postulate
  grade="strong"
  grounds="membership received, not chosen (strong); the original contract and tacit consent fail (strong)."
  overturnedBy="a body of states founded and kept by consent that individuals actually gave."
>
Belonging is inherited before it is chosen; the contract fails as an account of origins.
</Postulate>

A person is born into a language, a law and a set of loyalties before he can choose any of them (<Ref lesson="barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism">Barrès and Maurras</Ref>; <Ref lesson="filmer-patriarcha">Filmer</Ref>). The governments the Scottish philosopher David Hume surveyed began in conquest and usurpation, and staying in a country no more shows consent than a sleeping passenger consents to the voyage (<Ref lesson="hume-against-the-original-contract">Hume</Ref>). Inherited belonging licenses no inherited rank and no exclusion of newcomers: those steps need premises about blood (strong). P7 says how belonging begins, not who may join later.

<Postulate
  grade="on balance"
  grounds="the premise, whose motivational half its strongest liberal critic grants (on balance); Rome held together by a public worship (strong); integration by itself failed for the second generation of non-EU origin (strong)."
  overturnedBy="a cross-national series showing that trust and willingness to fund transfers to strangers do not fall where shared identity or religion weakens."
>
A free state lives on a solidarity its procedures do not produce.
</Postulate>

A free state asks its citizens to pay taxes for strangers, obey laws they voted against and hand over power when they lose, and they do it for people they count as "us". No voting rule or court creates that willingness; the procedures use it. A German constitutional lawyer put it in one sentence in 1967:

<Quote source="E.-W. Böckenförde, 'Die Entstehung des Staates als Vorgang der Säkularisation' (1967)">
<Original lang="de">
Der freiheitliche, säkularisierte Staat lebt von Voraussetzungen, die er selbst nicht garantieren kann.
</Original>

The free, secularised state lives on presuppositions it cannot itself guarantee.
</Quote>

Its strongest liberal critic, the German philosopher Jürgen Habermas, holds that the constitutional state can justify itself from its own resources, and that its dependence on solidarity is only motivational. That is the half P8 claims, and he grants it: "liberal societal structures are dependent on the solidarity of their citizens" (<Ref lesson="hungarys-fundamental-law">Hungary's Fundamental Law</Ref>). In the dispute of 384 over the pagan altar in the Roman Senate house, both sides acted as if the commonwealth turned on its public worship (<Ref lesson="altar-of-victory">the Altar of Victory</Ref>). The forecast that integration would follow by itself failed for Europeans born to parents from outside the EU, whose employment stands at 68% against about three-quarters, while it held for those of EU parentage (<Ref lesson="murray-strange-death-of-europe">the restrictionist case</Ref>). The grade is on balance, not strong, because the deciding evidence, trust and transfers measured where shared identity weakens, has not been gathered.

### What carries belonging

<Postulate
  grade="strong"
  grounds="culture persists (strong); regimes not explained by genes (strong); racial accounts of civilization fail (established)."
  overturnedBy="genetic divergence on relevant traits within the periods in which regimes changed, or values that change within a generation on demand."
>
Culture persists across generations; it is not carried by blood and not acquired on demand.
</Postulate>

The trust levels of a migrant's country of origin are still visible in his American descendants four generations later, and values change "rarely within two generations" (<Ref lesson="national-data-audited">the national data</Ref>). Nor is culture blood: one ancestry lived under opposite regimes within a lifetime, in the two Koreas, in Taiwan under martial law until 1987 and free since, and in the two Germanies, equal in income before the war and with the East at half the West's output per head by 1950. Every racial theory of civilization failed by its own criteria (<Ref lesson="gobineau-inequality-of-races">Gobineau</Ref>, <Ref lesson="chamberlain-and-grant">Chamberlain and Grant</Ref>, <Ref lesson="reich-ancient-dna-and-the-indo-europeans">ancient DNA</Ref>). The objection from the right: peoples differ in what they value (some, it is said, do not want freedom), and culture is made by genes. Only the first clause survives: the differences in values are cultural and persistent. "Do not want freedom" fails on the surveys: in each of eight Arab countries at least 73% agree that, whatever its problems, democracy is the best system of government. "Made by genes" fails on Taiwan and on time: genes and culture shape each other over millennia, and these regimes changed in years.

<Postulate
  grade="strong"
  grounds="Rome's public worship (strong); second-generation Muslim religiosity kept (strong)."
  overturnedBy="the third generation of Muslim-origin Europeans converging as Christians did."
>
Religion is a bond that has held a commonwealth and persists across generations where it is held.
</Postulate>

In France 91% of those raised Muslim keep the religion as adults, against 67% of those raised Catholic; across fifteen countries second-generation Muslims keep their parents' religiosity while Christians drift from theirs (<Ref lesson="murray-strange-death-of-europe">the restrictionist case</Ref>). The objection is that Western Europe's majority secularised, so religion is no lasting bond. That is conceded, and it is P8's case: the majority's bond weakened and nothing has been shown to take its place; in Habermas's words, the sources of solidarity "may dry up".

## Societies decline through causes that can be named, grow rich under property and law, and meet each other as powers

If belonging is slow and cannot be manufactured, the conservative's fatalism seems to follow: what cannot be rebuilt must run down. Does the record bear that out?

### Change and decline

<Postulate
  grade="on balance"
  grounds="redesigning a whole society at one stroke paid a heavy price in France after 1789, with war finance as a rival cause (on balance); designed constitutions and institutions have lasted (strong)."
  overturnedBy="a whole-society redesign at one stroke that paid no heavy price where war was absent."
>
Redesigning a whole society at one stroke is costly; designed institutions can last.
</Postulate>

The British statesman Edmund Burke forecast in 1790 that revolutionary France's paper money, issued on confiscated church land, would collapse and that a general would take power. Both happened, and both are costs of remaking property, money and command at once. He also missed twice (strong): the new departments became the instruments of Europe's most centralised administration, and France, "expunged out of the system of Europe" in his words, had armies beating the coalition within four years. The worst of the price has a rival cause, the war of 1792 and its finance, so the general claim is on balance (<Ref lesson="assignats">the assignats</Ref>). The counter-revolutionary Joseph de Maistre's wider claim, that nothing deliberated lasts, failed: the American constitution of 1787, the departments and the metric system were all designed and all lasted. Caution therefore scales with the size of a change; it is not a presumption against change (<Ref lesson="what-conservatism-is">what conservatism is</Ref>).

<Postulate
  grade="strong"
  grounds="laws of decline fail (strong; the racial law established); named mechanisms held; decline reversed (strong for parliaments, on balance for Europe after Rome)."
  overturnedBy="a law of decline stated in advance that predicts a dated fall."
>
Civilizations decline through mechanisms, not by fate, and decline can be reversed.
</Postulate>

Every law of decline tested failed: Plato's fixed chain of regimes (<Ref lesson="decline-of-regimes">the decline of regimes</Ref>), the Greek historian Polybius's cycle of constitutions (<Ref lesson="polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome">Polybius</Ref>), the German historian Oswald Spengler's civilizations as organisms with a fixed life-span (<Ref lesson="spengler-decline-of-the-west">Spengler</Ref>), the British historian Arnold Toynbee's law that civilizations rise by a creative minority's response to a challenge (<Ref lesson="toynbee-challenge-and-response">Toynbee</Ref>), and the French writer Arthur de Gobineau's decay by racial mixture. Named mechanisms held: rivalry for office wrecked Rome's mixed constitution, as Polybius forecast, and Weimar's parliament could block but not govern. Decline was reversed: Spengler forecast that parliaments would become empty pageantry, and by 2000 they governed across Western and then Eastern Europe; Europe recovered after Rome's collapse (on balance; <Ref lesson="the-borrowing-ledger">the borrowing ledger</Ref>). The objection, that decline is merely slow, forecasts nothing: a decline of any length and form cannot fail.

<Postulate
  grade="strong"
  grounds="the material forecasts of 1795 (longer lives, literacy, abolition) held (strong); the forecast that liberal democracy would spread failed from 2006 (strong)."
  overturnedBy="a sustained world fall in life expectancy and literacy."
>
Material progress is real but not a law of history.
</Postulate>

A background premise: no thesis uses it. World life expectancy rose from about 29 years in 1800 to 73 in 2023, as the French philosopher Condorcet forecast in 1795 (<Ref lesson="voegelin-gnosticism">Condorcet's forecasts</Ref>).

### Wealth and power between peoples

<Postulate
  grade="established"
  grounds="matched pairs and turns (established); institutions alone produce gaps as large as any between nations (established)."
  overturnedBy="a planned economy at parity with a matched market economy over decades."
>
Property and markets under law produce wealth; planning failed.
</Postulate>

South Korea's output per head in 2022 was about twenty-six times North Korea's; East Germany stood at about 56% of West Germany from 1950 to 1989, against parity in the 1930s (<Ref lesson="burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west">Burnham's forecast</Ref>). China and India grew two to three times as fast after their turns to markets, in 1978 and 1991, as before. The objection from the right, that every country that adopts capitalism grows uniformly richer, claims more than this postulate and fails: Argentina was level with Canada in 1913, at 60% of American output per head, and stood at 31% in 2022. Markets are necessary and not sufficient; secure property and an unbiased law decide (<Ref lesson="geography-or-institutions">geography or institutions</Ref>). The objection from the other side is China, which rose from 6% of American output per head in 1978 to 33% in 2022 under a party-state whose courts answer to the party. It does not reach the comparison the grade rests on, since China's growth began when it admitted markets and property, and it tests "under law" only at a level of income China has not reached.

<Postulate
  grade="on balance"
  grounds="(a) where no common authority exists, cities at war acted as the strong could (on balance); (b) no war between established democracies or between members of the European Union since 1950 (strong)."
  overturnedBy="for (a), a period of stable restraint among unequal powers with no common authority and no balance; for (b), a war between two established democracies."
>
Without a common authority, power decides between peoples; among free republics, peace has held.
</Postulate>

When Athens besieged the neutral island of Melos in 416 BC, its envoys said justice arises only between equals in power, and both sides' conduct in the war bore that out. Their further claim, that fairness protects no one, failed in 404, when Sparta refused to destroy a defeated Athens "which had done great service" (<Ref lesson="thucydides-power-and-justice">Melos and 404</Ref>). Among free states peace has held since 1950 (<Ref lesson="testing-the-clash">testing the clash</Ref>). The objection from the right is that if power decides, power gives right. As a theory of right it gives every successful rebel and every unopposed invader the same title, and leaves no ground to say anything ought to be otherwise (<Ref lesson="vitoria-right-to-the-americas">Vitoria</Ref>).

## Two premises are argued, not measured: freedom among citizens, and the worth of the West's achievement

None of the postulates so far says why freedom should be kept. Evidence can show what freedom and an inheritance cost and what they yield; it cannot show that the yield should be preferred to what someone else would choose instead. A premise about value is defended a different way: by showing that its rivals need something they cannot supply.

### Freedom among citizens

<Postulate
  grade="argued"
  grounds="every alternative needs someone entitled to decide others' good; no criterion for choosing that someone survived its test; where freedom was kept it did not cost what its critics forecast."
  overturnedBy="an argument, not evidence: a criterion, fixed in advance, for who may decide others' good that survives its own test."
>
Freedom among citizens is a good worth keeping.
</Postulate>

*Freedom among citizens* means that adults choose their own way of life within general laws, and choose and remove those who make the laws. The argument has three steps.

1. Every alternative needs someone entitled to decide for others what their good is: a philosopher, a hero, a wise minority, an emergency ruler, an authority for the common good.
2. No criterion for picking that someone, stated in advance, survived its test. Rule by knowledge has no case in the record to test (<Ref lesson="sparta-as-test-case">Sparta</Ref>). The hero cannot be told from the quack before the outcome (<Ref lesson="carlyle-heroes">Carlyle</Ref>, strong). The case for coercing men toward their good needs someone entitled to decide which ends are good, and who that "wise minority" is, is the point in dispute (<Ref lesson="stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity">Stephen against Mill</Ref>, strong). The emergency ruler of 1930–1932 had no criterion that kept his rule temporary (<Ref lesson="article-48-weimar">Article 48</Ref>, on balance). The authority for the common good has no check but its own good faith (<Ref lesson="vermeule-common-good-constitutionalism">common-good constitutionalism</Ref>, on balance).
3. So freedom among citizens is the one arrangement that needs no one entitled to decide others' good, and where it was kept it did not cost what its critics forecast: the British franchise widened from 1832 to 1928 without ending liberty or stability (<Ref lesson="british-franchise">the British franchise</Ref>, strong); the civil rights of 1948 became enforceable in courts (<Ref lesson="udhr-read-against-the-right">the Universal Declaration</Ref>, strong); and Hungary's governing alliance, which had written a constitution alone, was voted out in 2026 (<Ref lesson="hungarys-fundamental-law">Hungary</Ref>, strong).

The strongest objection, the post-liberal one, is met among the critics below. The question beneath it, what freedom is for once the old faith has gone, is [the death of God](/courses/nietzsche/lessons/death-of-god)'s. Whether the "protected subject" of the welfare state is less free than the subject of the old intermediate powers is the open value question inside P16 (<Ref lesson="jouvenel-on-power">Jouvenel</Ref>).

### The West's achievement

<Postulate
  grade="argued"
  grounds="political theory arose with the Greeks; science after 1400 (strong); wealth from 1300 (on balance); self-government after 1689 (strong); the cause is open."
  overturnedBy="for the factual half, a documented list of omitted non-European achievements after 1400 at a scale that changes the shares; for the value half, an argument that an achievement whose cause is unknown can be reproduced without the institutions it arose with."
>
The West's achievement is real and worth conserving.
</Postulate>

The factual half is graded by parts. Political theory arose in the Greek cities, where power was placed "in the middle" of a body of peers (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle">the Greek miracle</Ref>). After about 1400 the recorded discoveries in the sciences are overwhelmingly European, a share that survives correction by specialists in non-Western science (<Ref lesson="murray-human-accomplishment">human accomplishment</Ref>, strong). The lead in wealth opened in Italy around 1300 and became decisive in north-western Europe in the early modern period (<Ref lesson="the-borrowing-ledger">the borrowing ledger</Ref>, on balance). Self-government took its lasting form in the limited crown after 1689 and the franchise widened after 1832 (strong).

The claim from the right that the West was almost always ahead by a large margin fails (strong), with the collapse after Rome and the fast recovery conceded: writing, cities and law codes came first in Mesopotamia and Egypt, Rome was of one order with Han China (the Chinese empire of 206 BC–AD 220), and around 1000 the Islamic world and Song China were richer. The cause of the lead is open among five candidates: political fragmentation, the Church's breaking of the clans, a religious ethic, medieval institutions, and genetic selection. The genetic candidate's England-only form, that in England alone the rich out-bred the poor and spread their traits, fails on balance, because the rich out-bred the poor across much of the continent too.

The value argument: an achievement whose cause is unknown cannot be reproduced on purpose, a society cannot be redesigned at one stroke without cost (P11), and a culture cannot be put on at will (P9). So the achievement is kept by conserving the institutions and culture it arose with, including what Europe made of what it borrowed, paper and numerals built into systems that lasted. The rival thesis, that the lead was late luck in a shared Eurasian exchange (<Ref lesson="pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis">Pomeranz and Goody</Ref>), fails on timing (strong): Song China had fallen behind Italy by 1300.

## Nine theses follow, each no firmer than the weakest premise it uses

Seventeen premises still do not say whether a people may refuse an applicant or must guarantee a minimum income. Each answer has to be derived, with the value premise that licenses its "may" or "should" named, and its strength read off the weakest premise it uses.

### Who rules: T1 and T2

<Thesis from="P1, P2, P4, P5, P6, P16; common ground: offices to the capable; verdicts: 1642 and 1689, capacity with removability, Weimar, the Hungarian Fourth Amendment" grade="on balance">
A mixed, limited regime: a settled last word held under law, and an elite that can be removed.
</Thesis>

People live in societies, and the state lowers violence among them (P2), so an order is needed; it needs a last word (P5), and a few will hold it (P4). They stay capable only where office is contested and removable (P4, with P1 and the aim that offices go to the capable); unchecked, their power grows (P6); and no one is entitled to rule others without their consent (P16). So the last word is held by a composite body bound by law, with the few removable by the many. The settled last word under law is strong (P5); the whole is on balance, from P4 and P6.

<Thesis from="P4, P6, P7, P8, P9, P14, P16; verdict: liberal principles alone do not suffice" grade="on balance">
Liberal freedom does not found itself: it lives on a nation, an elite held under law, an inherited culture it cannot produce on demand, and an economy of property and markets.
</Thesis>

Freedom is worth keeping (P16). It depends on a solidarity it does not produce (P8), which is received (P7) and carried by a culture that cannot be acquired on demand (P9). Its rulers must be held by law (P4). Its wealth rests on property and markets under law (P14), and so does its freedom: a planning authority holds every livelihood under a power that no budget voted by others checks (P6). So keeping freedom means keeping these four. "On demand" is the whole of the claim: whether a free state can build a nation over generations is the third hard case below, and it is open. The grade is P4's and P8's, on balance.

### Membership: T3 and T4

<Thesis from="P7, P8, P9, P16; verdicts: Miller over Carens, integration by itself failed, the fiscal checks" grade="on balance">
A people may decide who joins and how many, and select by capacity to integrate, within duties of rescue, fair terms, and full inclusion of those it admits.
</Thesis>

<Example title="Deriving T3 and reading off its grade">
**The premises, with grades.** P7, belonging is received (strong). P9, culture persists and is not acquired on demand (strong). P8, a free state lives on a solidarity it does not produce (on balance). P16, freedom among citizens is worth keeping (argued). The verdict that a people's self-determination outweighs a right to free movement, within limits (on balance; <Ref lesson="miller-strangers-in-our-midst">Miller over Carens</Ref>).

**Steps 1–2.** From P7 and P9: the bond that makes a people is received and changes over generations. From P8: a free state depends on that bond and cannot produce it on demand. So who enters, how many, and how fast bear on what the state lives on.

**Step 3.** The "may" enters, from P16 together with the verdict. A people that governs itself governs its membership as it governs its laws (P16), and equal moral worth does not take that right away: it gives every person a right to *some* adequate place to live, not to *this* one (the verdict). Without P16 the thesis would state a fact and license nothing.

**Step 4.** The verdict also narrows the "may". It leaves duties of rescue to those whose rights cannot be protected at home, fair terms for migrants, and full inclusion of those admitted. The thesis carries those limits, or it says more than its verdict.

**Step 5.** *Selecting* by capacity to integrate rests on two findings: integration did not follow by itself for the non-EU second generation, and selected labour migration is a net fiscal gain while asylum and family migration in high-welfare states are a net cost (both strong; <Ref lesson="murray-strange-death-of-europe">the restrictionist case</Ref>).

**The grade.** The weakest evidential premises are on balance (P8 and the Miller verdict), so T3 is on balance, and its "may" stands as firmly as the argument for P16.
</Example>

<Thesis from="P1, P9, P16; verdicts: the racial accounts of civilization fail, the step from received membership to private rights only, regimes not explained by genes, within-group heritability cannot apportion a between-group gap, facts about heredity settle no policy" grade="strong">
Membership is cultural, not racial: open in principle, slow, and demanded rather than assumed.
</Thesis>

What holds a people is a culture (P9), not blood: the racial accounts failed (established), and regimes are not explained mainly by genes (strong). Equal standing (P1) forbids status by descent, and that "forbids" stands as firmly as the argument for P16, which supplies the value P1's negative finding leaves out. Culture is acquired slowly and not on demand (P9). So membership is open to anyone who takes on the culture, takes time, and is required, not presumed. The step holds however the open question of genetic shares in group gaps is answered: a group average does not fix an individual, within-group heritability cannot apportion a gap between groups (established; <Ref lesson="heritability-within-and-between-groups">within and between groups</Ref>), and facts about heredity settle no policy on either side (strong; <Ref lesson="gould-and-harden-on-heredity">what follows from heredity</Ref>). Both evidential premises are strong, so T4 is.

### Economy and public culture: T5 and T6

<Thesis from="P1, P3, P6, P14, P16; verdicts: the floor as common ground, the merit case against strict equality, heavy redistribution and poverty" grade="on balance">
A market economy with a guaranteed minimum, not redistribution aimed at equal outcomes.
</Thesis>

Markets under law produce wealth (P14), and they leave adults to choose their work and keep what they earn within general laws (P16). Abilities differ (P1), so equal outcomes need continuous correction by an authority whose power grows (P6), and strict equal-outcome schemes fall to the argument that fitting earnings to a pattern overrides what people freely did with their own (strong). A guaranteed minimum is accepted on every side: by groups in experiments who chose a distribution rule without knowing their own place in it, and by the merit side's own authors (on balance; <Ref lesson="the-cognitive-elite">the cognitive elite</Ref>). The floor sets the grade. Nor is it held down by fear of ruin: the claim that heavy redistribution makes a nation poor fails on balance, since the Nordic states tax 40–45% of output and stay among the richest. Between the floor and the liberal philosopher John Rawls's difference principle, which permits inequality only where it benefits the worst off, the question is open.

P3 is listed and does no work: envy decides nothing about justice, so deleting it leaves the derivation unchanged.

<Thesis from="P8, P9, P10, P16, P17; verdicts: no neutral civic state, the enforceable civil core, Rome's worship" grade="on balance">
A free state may favour in public life the religion and culture it historically lives on, while protecting religious liberty.
</Thesis>

Every state chooses an official language, holidays, a public history and terms of naturalisation: no civic state is neutral (strong; <Ref lesson="hazony-virtue-of-nationalism">Hazony</Ref>). The solidarity a free state lives on is carried by a culture and a faith that persist (P8, P9, P10; Rome's worship is the case), and the inheritance is worth conserving (P17), so the choice it must make anyway is made for what it lives on. Freedom (P16) and the enforceable civil rights bound it: favour, never compulsion. Whether writing the culture into the supreme law produces cohesion is open. The grade is P8's.

### The past and the outside: T7 and T8

<Thesis from="P7; verdicts: inherited guilt, parts (a) to (d)" grade="strong">
No inherited guilt; liability only through institutions that persist; repair owed to people now alive.
</Thesis>

Guilt attaches to a will, so it does not pass to descendants. Institutions are inherited (P7), so liabilities pass through estates, corporations and states that persist. Wrongs centuries old cannot be repaired by computing what would have happened without them: the baseline, and even who would have existed, are indeterminate. Wrongs done to people now alive are owed repair, as for the federal exclusion of Black neighbourhoods from mortgage insurance from the 1930s (<Ref lesson="inherited-guilt-and-reparations">inherited guilt</Ref>). Each step is strong. T7 takes no position on whether colonial rule left the colonised better or worse off overall, which is open (<Ref lesson="auditing-the-case-for-colonialism">the colonial ledger</Ref>).

<Thesis from="P5, P15; common ground: peace over war; verdicts: Melos, the republican peace, the right of the stronger, the empire charge against the EU" grade="on balance">
Between peoples, interest and power where no common authority exists; cooperation among free states.
</Thesis>

Without a settled last word, power decides: P5 applied between peoples, and P15's first half. Among free republics peace has held, P15's second half. Peace being better than war, a free state keeps the arms that make it costly to attack where power decides, and binds itself by treaty to free states, keeping a way out. The nationalist charge that a federation of nations, the European Union above all, is an empire fails as rule without consent or exit, since members joined by treaty and one has left (strong), and holds as compulsory jurisdiction (on balance). Power gives no right (<Ref lesson="vitoria-right-to-the-americas">Vitoria</Ref>, strong). The grade is P15's, and the "should" stands as firmly as the argument for peace over war.

### What is excluded: T9

<Thesis from="P1, P4, P6, P9, P11, P12, P15; verdicts: the decider, the racial nation, the conservative revolution, the unjust titles, natural slavery and the positive-good defence" grade="on balance">
The radical alternatives are excluded: the decider, the racial nation, the conservative revolution, conquest as a right, ownership of people.
</Thesis>

The *decider* is a ruler whose decision in the emergency is itself the source of law, and he has no check but himself (P4, P6). Its leading theorist, the German jurist Carl Schmitt, argued in 1932 that a constitution may deny the "equal chance" to parties that would use legality to abolish it; in 1933 he accepted the regime that did exactly that, on his own doctrine that whoever decided is law (<Ref lesson="the-right-in-1933">1933</Ref>, on balance). The racial nation is excluded because it fails its own tests (established); P1 and P9 say what takes its place. The *conservative revolution*, the interwar German right's call for a revolution to restore what liberalism had destroyed, is redesign at one stroke without a programme (P11), called for by a decline that can be reversed without it (P12): strong against its chief theorist (<Ref lesson="moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich">Moeller van den Bruck</Ref>), on balance for the movement. Conquest as a right turns power into right (P15; strong). Ownership of people contradicts P1: strong, and established against Aristotle's version. The constitution written by one majority is not on the list, since whether it produces cohesion is open; a majority overriding its court is already excluded by T1. The grade is the decider's, on balance; each part keeps its own.

</KeyFact>

## What follows in practice: seven permissions, nine prohibitions, three cases the theses do not settle by themselves

What may a government do under the doctrine, and what may it not?

**The doctrine allows:**

| Permission | Thesis | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| A people decides who joins and how many, selecting by capacity to integrate | T3 | on balance |
| Language, law and loyalty required as conditions of membership | T3, T4 | on balance |
| Refusing and removing those hostile to the constitutional order, Islamists above all on the asylum-route record, with refused applicants actually removed | T1, T3; the 2015 security verdict | on balance |
| Precedence for the national culture in public life: schools, calendar, civic symbols | T6 | on balance |
| Inequality of outcome; an elite rising by ability under competition and removability | T1, T5 | on balance |
| Markets and property with a guaranteed minimum | T5 | on balance |
| No inherited guilt for colonialism or slavery | T7 | strong |

**The doctrine forbids:**

| Prohibition | Thesis | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Status by descent: a citizen of non-European origin who has taken on the culture is a full member | T4 | strong; the "forbids" as firm as P16's argument |
| Ownership of persons; conquest as a source of right | T9 | strong; strong |
| Rule without consent or removability | T1, T9 | on balance |
| A majority overriding its constitutional court | T1 | on balance |
| Planned economies | T5 (P14 established) | on balance |
| Open borders as a right | T3 | on balance |
| Collective guilt by origin, in either direction | T7 | strong |
| Refusing rescue to those whose rights cannot be protected at home, or holding admitted residents in a lasting lower status | T3's limits | on balance |
| Refusing repair to living people wronged by an institution that persists | T7 | strong |

The third permission names Islamists because of the record of those it screens. Germany admitted about a million asylum seekers in 2015–16, by a decision of the executive with no vote in parliament (<Ref lesson="the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways">2015</Ref>). Of the 17 Islamist attacks in Germany since 2016, 12 were by men who came by the asylum route, and they caused 22 of the 23 deaths. That is a share of the Islamist attacks, not of all terrorism: far-right attackers also killed in those years, at Munich in 2016, Halle in 2019 and Hanau in 2020, and in the murder of the politician Walter Lübcke in 2019. Against about three million asylum applications in 2015–2024, the twelve are about four attackers per million, and only three were known Islamists beforehand. The failure the record shows is institutional: men obliged to leave were not removed (strong). So the permission is to screen for declared hostility and to enforce removal, not to suspect a group.

The first prohibition and the second permission are one thesis seen from two sides: a state may demand its language and law of anyone, and may not refuse someone who meets the demand because of where his parents were born.

### Hard case 1: screening by ideology or by religion

T3 allows selection by capacity to integrate, and P10 says religion persists across generations. Does that license screening applicants by religion?

For, at full strength: the solidarity P8 needs is carried partly by religion (P10); across fifteen European countries second-generation Muslims keep their parents' religiosity, and integration by itself failed for the second generation of non-EU parentage (both strong). If religion carries the bond and does not fade, a state selecting by capacity to integrate seems entitled to look at it. Against: T6 protects religious liberty; T4 and P1 forbid status by birth, and a faith is usually inherited; most adherents hold no hostile ideology. Nor does either finding separate religion from origin: the non-EU second generation differs from the EU one in origin and in much else besides faith, and no series holds those apart, so the record cannot say how much of the shortfall religion carries. Screening by declared hostility to the constitutional order, judged by aims, associations and conduct, follows from T1 and T3, on balance.

<Verdict
  grade="open"
  decidedBy="no series separates religion from origin, sex, education and declared ideology in predicting integration."
  overturnedBy="integration outcomes by cohort, employment by sex, second-generation attachment and suspect rates adjusted for age and sex, with religion separated from those four."
>
Whether a free state may screen applicants by religion as such, rather than by declared hostility to its constitutional order, is open.
</Verdict>

### Hard case 2: how many is too many

T3 lets a people decide the number; it does not supply one. The demand is large: about 900 million adults say they would like to leave their country permanently, and some tens of millions of them name Germany (<Ref lesson="carens-open-borders">Carens</Ref>, strong). What the doctrine supplies is a criterion, from T3 and P8: the number is too many when the integration of each arriving cohort, measured on its own record, stops converging on the population's, in employment by sex, in second-generation outcomes and in trust. The number itself cannot be derived until a series relating the size and composition of cohorts to their integration, with sex and origin separated, has been gathered.

### Hard case 3: whether a free state can build a nation over generations

T2 says a free state cannot produce its solidarity on demand; whether it can over generations decides how far T3 and T6 need to go. For: the Hispanic second generation in the United States speaks English almost universally, and attachment to the country rises with each generation (<Ref lesson="albions-seed-and-who-are-we">America's English inheritance</Ref>, strong and on balance); integration held for Europe's second generation of EU parentage (strong). The French Third Republic (1870–1940) is said to have made "peasants into Frenchmen" through schools and conscription while staying parliamentary, but the claim rests on a summary of one book, not on its evidence. Against: values change rarely within two generations (P9); integration by itself failed for the non-EU second generation (strong); whether Hungary's written presuppositions produced cohesion is unmeasured.

<Verdict
  grade="open"
  decidedBy="the cases for and against are each partial, and the strongest case for, the Third Republic, rests on a summary of one book, not on its evidence."
  overturnedBy="a checked account showing a nation built under free institutions within a generation or two, or a cohesion series that moves with a deliberate programme of schooling and not with the economic cycle."
>
Whether a free state can produce over generations the solidarity it cannot produce on demand is open.
</Verdict>

## Six critics: two answered, three answered in part, one left with a question the doctrine cannot yet close

Which premises do the doctrine's strongest opponents reject, and what would fall if they were right?

### Rawls: a just structure makes its own allegiance

Rawls holds that citizens raised under just institutions acquire a sense of justice that sustains them, so freedom needs no prior solidarity. That hits P8 and T2. The answer: his theory assumes a closed society entered by birth and places self-determination at the level of peoples, so it presupposes the bounded "we" it does not derive; and integration did not by itself form allegiance in the non-EU second generation. His route from just institutions to allegiance is itself untested (open).

<Verdict
  grade="on balance"
  decidedBy="his theory presupposes a bounded people; integration did not by itself produce allegiance."
  overturnedBy="support for just institutions forming at equal rates in groups with and without a prior shared identity."
>
Rawls's objection does not overturn P8; the stability route it relies on is untested.
</Verdict>

### Carens: birthright membership is a privilege

The political philosopher Joseph Carens holds that birthright citizenship is like feudal privilege, so control of membership is unjust. That hits T3. The answer: equal worth grounds a right to some adequate place, not to this one (on balance); his inference that few would move failed (strong); and his own public-order limit licenses control. Conceded: what a rich state owes people above the floor of rescue but far below its own options is open.

<Verdict
  grade="on balance"
  decidedBy="a right to some adequate place, not this one; the movers' inference failed."
  overturnedBy="an argument that equal worth grounds a right to enter this state and not only some adequate one, or evidence that under free access realised migration runs far below stated desire."
>
Carens's objection does not overturn T3; the duty to those above the floor of rescue stays open.
</Verdict>

### MacIntyre: a nation cannot replace a lost tradition

The moral philosopher Alasdair MacIntyre holds that modern moral language is the fragment of a lost tradition, so a nation and an inherited culture cannot supply what the tradition supplied. That hits P9 and T6, and through them P8. The answer is partial: T2 claims a loyalty that bears burdens for strangers, not a shared account of the good life. Whether that loyalty can outlast the lost account is unanswered (<Ref lesson="macintyre-after-virtue">After Virtue</Ref>); if it cannot, P8's supplement is thinner than T2 needs.

<Verdict
  grade="open"
  decidedBy="no evidence measures whether a burden-bearing loyalty survives the loss of a shared account of the good."
  overturnedBy="a cohesion series in societies that lost a shared religion, tracked against the willingness to bear burdens for strangers."
>
Whether the loyalty T2 relies on can outlast the tradition MacIntyre says was lost is open.
</Verdict>

### Habermas: the state justifies itself

His objection was met at P8: P8 claims only the motivational dependence, which he grants. How far a state can itself produce what it depends on is hard case 3.

<Verdict
  grade="on balance"
  decidedBy="P8 claims only the motivational dependence, which the objection grants."
  overturnedBy="the trust-and-transfers series P8 names, showing no fall where shared identity weakens."
>
Habermas's objection leaves P8 standing; it bears on hard case 3, which is open.
</Verdict>

### The post-liberals: liberalism failed by succeeding

At full strength: liberalism's premises, freedom from nature and from unchosen bonds, dissolve the family, the place and the faith that freedom lives on; the more fully liberalism succeeds, the faster it consumes its supports; so the remedy is to rule for the common good, not for freedom (<Ref lesson="deneen-post-liberalism">Deneen</Ref>, <Ref lesson="vermeule-common-good-constitutionalism">Vermeule</Ref>). That hits P16 and T1. The doctrine accepts the diagnosis's conclusion, that freedom does not found itself (T2), and denies the remedy: the common-good theory has no check of its own against a determination made in bad faith (on balance), and where a majority wrote its presuppositions into the constitution, it also overrode its court (strong). Whether that text produced cohesion is unmeasured, and the causal claim, that liberalism and not wealth or technology dissolved those bonds, is open: it needs cohesion series in liberal and non-liberal societies at equal income.

<Verdict
  grade="on balance"
  decidedBy="the remedy's safeguard lies in the good faith of the officials it would empower (on balance); the one majority that wrote its presuppositions into a constitution overrode its court (strong)."
  overturnedBy="a mechanism in the theory, not in officials' character, that can block a governing majority's determination."
>
The post-liberal remedy fails, on balance: it offers no check that could replace the removable rulers of T1.
</Verdict>

### The racial right: membership by descent tracks what carries culture

At full strength: abilities are heritable within populations (conceded, strong); nations' measured skills track their incomes (strong); no evidence excludes a genetic share in the gaps between groups (established); genes and culture shape each other; so membership by descent is membership by what carries culture. That hits T4 and P9. The answer: the racial nation fails by its own tests (established); the same people were free and unfree within a lifetime (strong); and T4, as shown there, does not need the genetic question answered.

<Verdict
  grade="established"
  decidedBy="the racial accounts fail on their own tests; within-group heritability cannot apportion a gap between groups."
  overturnedBy="an independent measure of ancestry that predicts the rise and fall of civilizations better than institutional or ecological causes."
>
The racial nation fails as a ground of membership; T4 does not rest on the genetic share being zero.
</Verdict>

## Five questions stay open, and each would move a named postulate or thesis

Where is the derivation most exposed, and what evidence would move it?

| Open question | What would settle it | What it would move |
|---|---|---|
| The genetic share of the gaps between groups in measured test scores | sibling-difference designs with measured local ancestry; within-family genetic studies of cognition in African-ancestry samples | nothing in T1–T9 (T4) |
| The cause of the West's rise | within-Europe designs separating the timing of clan dissolution, state competition and literacy; genetic scores from dated remains in England, the continent and China, 1000–1800 | P17's account of cause, not its worth; if the cause is the institutions T1 and T2 name, T2 gains |
| Hard case 3 | as stated in its verdict | T2's "on demand"; the scope of T3 and T6 |
| Hard case 2 | as stated there | the application of T3 |
| Hard case 1 | as stated in its verdict | the practice of T3; the limit of T6 |

Overturn P9 and T4 falls, with T2 and T3 losing a premise; overturn P16 and T1 to T6 lose the source of their "may" and "should". P8 sits under fewer theses than P6 (three against four), but the evidence that would decide it is named and has not been gathered: a series of trust and of willingness to fund transfers to strangers, measured where shared identity or religion weakens. Three of the six critics land on it.

<Conclusion>
The doctrine is seventeen postulates, fifteen graded on the record and two argued, and nine theses derived from them. The position they compose: a free state, limited, ruled by a removable few under a settled last word bound by law; living on a nation and an inherited culture it cannot produce on demand; controlling its membership within duties of rescue, fair terms and full inclusion; open to anyone who takes on its culture and closed to status by descent; with a market and a guaranteed minimum; favouring in public life the culture and faith it lives on, never compelling them; with no inherited guilt and repair owed to the living; armed where there is no common authority and bound by treaty to free states; closed to the decider, the racial nation, the conservative revolution, conquest and ownership of people. Two theses are strong and seven on balance, each of the seven resting on at least one on-balance premise; P8 is the one whose deciding evidence is named and not yet gathered. The three hard cases stay open, each with its settling evidence named; the racial nation fails; the post-liberal remedy fails on balance; and whether a burden-bearing loyalty can outlast a lost tradition is open. You can now take a proposal in any of these disputes, find the thesis it falls under, trace that thesis to its postulates and their grades, and say what evidence would overturn it. Nothing in this course comes next; the most radical answer to the question beneath P16, what freedom is for once the old faith has gone, is the subject of the Nietzsche course's lesson on Nietzsche and politics.
</Conclusion>

T4 says membership is cultural, open in principle and demanded rather than assumed; she has met the demand on every measure T3's criterion names, employment included, which is the measure on which women of her cohort most often fall short. Refusal on origin is forbidden (T4, strong; P1). Refusal on religion as such is not licensed: whether religion as such may be screened is hard case 1, open, and T6 protects religious liberty; the headscarf is a practice of that liberty and says nothing about hostility to the constitutional order. What could still justify refusal is declared hostility, shown by aims, associations or conduct, and nothing in the case shows it. The doctrine grants her application, at the grade of T3 and T4: the conditions on balance, the bar on refusing by origin strong.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
**Apply the doctrine: two associations.** One association of citizens preaches that the constitution is void and that the law must be replaced by religious law. Another is a congregation of the same faith that preaches no such thing. Which may the state ban, under which thesis, at what grade, and what would move the grade?

<Solution>
The first may be banned, under T1. T1 requires a settled last word held under law; an association that works to abolish the constitutional order attacks that last word. T3 does not decide it: T3 governs who is admitted, and these are citizens. The precedent is the criterion argued in Germany in 1932 by the jurist whose theory made the ruler's emergency decision the source of law: a constitution may deny the "equal chance" to parties that would use legality to abolish it. In 1933 he accepted the regime that did exactly that (<Ref lesson="the-right-in-1933">1933</Ref>). P16 limits the ban: freedom of association is the rule, so the ban reaches an association shown, by its aims, associations and conduct, to work for abolishing the constitutional order, not one that merely holds a belief the state rejects. The grade is T1's, on balance.

The second may not be banned. It preaches no hostility to the constitutional order; T6 protects religious liberty, and whether religion as such may be screened is hard case 1, open.

What would move the grade: for the first, T1's on-balance premises, P4 and P6, moving; for the second, the hard-case-1 series. If integration outcomes separated by religion, holding origin, education, sex and ideology fixed, showed religion itself predicting hostility, the question would move from open; a ban on worship would still meet T6's limit.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
**Apply the doctrine: two reparations claims.** (a) Descendants of people enslaved before 1865 claim reparation from the state. (b) People now living who were refused mortgage insurance in the 1960s because of the neighbourhood they lived in claim against the same state. What does T7 say about each, and why?

<Solution>
(a) As a claim of guilt, T7 refuses it: guilt attaches to a will and does not pass to people born later (strong). As a claim of liability against a state that has persisted since 1865, T7 does not exclude it in principle, since liability passes through institutions that persist (strong). But a remedy measured by what the claimants' position would have been without the wrong cannot be computed: the baseline is indeterminate, and the claimants themselves would not exist in the world without the wrong (strong). Supersession, the lapse of a once-just claim as circumstances change, is possible, not automatic. So the claim fails in its counterfactual form, and T7 says which form could succeed: a wrong whose effects run, through a persisting institution, to people now alive.

(b) That form is this case. The wrong was done by a persisting institution, the federal state, to people now alive. T7's third clause applies: repair is owed (strong), and refusing it is one of the doctrine's prohibitions.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
**Attack the doctrine at P8.** P8 is not the only on-balance premise, but it is the one whose deciding evidence is named and not yet gathered. Attack it at full strength from whichever side you think strongest, say which theses fall if the attack succeeds, and name the evidence that would decide it. Write it as a paragraph: claim, evidence, reasoning.

<Solution>
Possible answers. P8 sits under T2, T3 and T6.

**A liberal attack.** Habermas's reply, with the integration of the EU-parentage second generation and the Third Republic: a free state can produce its solidarity over generations through schools and equal citizenship. It succeeds if a diversity-and-trust series shows no fall in trust and transfers where shared identity weakens, and a checked account of the Third Republic shows a nation built under free institutions. Then T2's "on demand" still stands, but T3's case for selecting by capacity to integrate weakens and T6 loses its ground.

**An attack from the other side.** MacIntyre's: the loyalty P8 names cannot outlast the lost account of the good, so P8 understates the problem. It succeeds if a cohesion series shows the willingness to bear burdens for strangers falling wherever the shared faith went, faster than any free state restores it. Then T2's supplement is thinner than it needs, and keeping freedom (P16) asks for something no free state can supply.

Each attack is answered at the grade the record gives it now: the first fails on balance, the second is open. Neither is preferred to the evidence. The question beneath the second attack, what freedom is for when no shared faith says, is the one the Nietzsche course takes up in its lesson on Nietzsche and politics, which builds on its lesson on [the death of God](/courses/nietzsche/lessons/death-of-god).
</Solution>

A shepherd finds a ring that makes him invisible. He can take what he likes and nobody will ever know. If he keeps behaving well anyway, is he just, or merely a fool? Plato puts this test in the mouth of a young man who wants to be shown that justice is good for the one who practises it, not only for those he spares.

The question is older than the story. <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="justice-comes-down-to-the-agora-solons-poem-as-a-political-fact">The Greek cities</Ref> put power "in the middle" and cut their laws in stone in the public square, so that one text bound rich and poor and anyone could dispute it. They also left a visitor, Anacharsis, laughing that written laws are spiders' webs, catching the weak and torn by the strong. Laws made by men were made by somebody. Then justice, meaning keeping the laws and respecting what they protect, may be nothing but doing what suits whoever was strong enough to write them, and the just man is the one who has not yet found his ring.

The man who put the test on paper is Plato, an Athenian aristocrat (c. 428–348 BC) who wrote philosophy as dialogues, conversations in which he never speaks himself. The chief speaker is nearly always his teacher Socrates (469–399 BC), an Athenian who spent his life questioning his fellow citizens about what they claimed to know, and whom the Athenian democracy put to death in 399 on charges of impiety and corrupting the young. Plato's *Republic*, written around 380 BC, a generation after that execution, is one night's conversation about justice.

The conversation is set in Socrates's lifetime at the Piraeus, the harbour town of Athens a few miles from the city, in the house of Cephalus, a rich old foreigner who ran a shield factory there. One of the young men in the room is Plato's own brother Glaucon.

The book opens with the problem in miniature. Socrates is walking home from a festival when Cephalus's son Polemarchus catches him up with a crowd of friends and tells him: "are you stronger than all these? for if not, you will have to remain where you are." Socrates offers a third way, persuading them to let him go. "But can you persuade us, if we refuse to listen to you?" Force, persuasion, and the refusal to listen: the night that follows is about whether justice is anything more than the first of these wearing the clothes of the second.

The obvious reply to the shepherd is that injustice does not pay. Thieves get caught, liars lose their friends, the gods punish. Every one of those answers needs someone to find out. The ring is built to take that need away, and Glaucon, who tells the story, built it on purpose, because the answers Socrates had given earlier in the evening had not satisfied him.

## The ring of Gyges: justice with no witnesses

Glaucon wants a test that separates the just man from the man who only fears being caught. A test needs one thing changed and everything else held still. His is a story borrowed from a legend of Lydia, a kingdom in western Anatolia (modern Turkey), whose last king, Croesus, was a byword for wealth in Plato's day. Gyges, the founder of Croesus's royal house, is made a shepherd:

<Quote source="Republic II, 359d–360b, trans. B. Jowett">
According to the tradition, Gyges was a shepherd in the service of the king of Lydia; there was a great storm, and an earthquake made an opening in the earth at the place where he was feeding his flock. Amazed at the sight, he descended into the opening, where, among other marvels, he beheld a hollow brazen horse, having doors, at which he stooping and looking in saw a dead body of stature, as appeared to him, more than human, and having nothing on but a gold ring; this he took from the finger of the dead and reascended. Now the shepherds met together, according to custom, that they might send their monthly report about the flocks to the king; into their assembly he came having the ring on his finger, and as he was sitting among them he chanced to turn the collet of the ring inside his hand, when instantly he became invisible to the rest of the company and they began to speak of him as if he were no longer present. He was astonished at this, and again touching the ring he turned the collet outwards and reappeared; he made several trials of the ring, and always with the same result—when he turned the collet inwards he became invisible, when outwards he reappeared. Whereupon he contrived to be chosen one of the messengers who were sent to the court; whereas soon as he arrived he seduced the queen, and with her help conspired against the king and slew him, and took the kingdom.
</Quote>

The collet is the setting on top of a ring that holds its stone. Turned in toward the palm, it makes Gyges invisible; turned outward, it brings him back. The power is under his control, a twist of the finger, so he can vanish exactly when a crime needs no witness and reappear when he wants to be seen.

The story proves only that one shepherd, given the chance, became a murderer and a king. The argument is the step Glaucon takes next:

<Quote source="Republic II, 360b–d, trans. B. Jowett">
Suppose now that there were two such magic rings, and the just put on one of them and the unjust the other; no man can be imagined to be of such an iron nature that he would stand fast in justice. No man would keep his hands off what was not his own when he could safely take what he liked out of the market, or go into houses and lie with any one at his pleasure, or kill or release from prison whom he would, and in all respects be like a God among men. Then the actions of the just would be as the actions of the unjust; they would both come at last to the same point. And this we may truly affirm to be a great proof that a man is just, not willingly or because he thinks that justice is any good to him individually, but of necessity, for wherever any one thinks that he can safely be unjust, there he is unjust. […] If you could imagine any one obtaining this power of becoming invisible, and never doing any wrong or touching what was another's, he would be thought by the lookers-on to be a most wretched idiot, although they would praise him to one another's faces, and keep up appearances with one another from a fear that they too might suffer injustice.
</Quote>

### What the test holds fixed

Two rings make the experiment controlled. The two men have the same power and the same safety; they differ only in character. If character mattered, their paths would stay apart. Glaucon predicts they meet "at the same point", and concludes that people are just "of necessity": justice is a cost they bear because they cannot avoid it, not a good they want.

### What the test does not say

The ring is easily read as a remark about human weakness: people cheat when nobody is watching. Glaucon claims more than that, and the last sentence of the passage shows it. Suppose one man with the ring did stay honest. The onlookers would call him "a most wretched idiot" among themselves, because by their lights he would be making a mistake, passing up goods for nothing. They would still praise him to his face, out of fear of suffering injustice themselves. The claim is about what justice is worth to the person who practises it, and about what the praise of justice is: a precaution among people afraid of each other.

## One counterexample kills a definition

Glaucon tells the story because the first half of the evening had failed to answer it. That half began with the plainest question available: what is justice?

### The returned sword

Asked what his wealth has done for him, Cephalus answers that its best gift to a good man is that he "has had no occasion to deceive or to defraud others", and can die owing nothing to gods or men. Socrates turns this into a definition: justice is telling the truth and paying back what you owe. Then he tests it with one case. A friend, in his right mind, leaves his weapons with you; later he goes mad and asks for them back. Returning them would pay the debt, and nobody would call it just; telling such a man the whole truth would not be right either. Cephalus agrees and goes off, laughing, to see to the sacrifices.

One case was enough because of what was asked. Socrates wants what justice *is*, not what just people usually do. A definition says what every just act has in common, so a single just act it leaves out, or a single unjust act it lets in, sinks it. "Pay your debts" survives as advice and fails as an answer.

### Help friends, harm enemies

Polemarchus takes over the argument by quoting Simonides, a poet of two generations earlier whose lines Greeks repeated as moral wisdom: justice is giving each what is owed. What is owed, Polemarchus explains, is good to friends and harm to enemies. This was the morality of the Greek gentleman, and of most of the world before and since: loyalty to one's own. Socrates puts three pressures on it.

**Useless.** If justice is a skill, it must be a skill at something. Socrates asks who is best at doing good to friends in each situation. In sickness it is the physician, at sea the pilot; the better partner in laying bricks is the builder, in buying a horse the man who knows horses. Every time a thing is put to use, that use has its own expert, and the expert is the one you want. Justice is left only the case where nothing is being used: Polemarchus ends by saying it is useful for keeping money safe while it lies idle on deposit. Then justice is useful for things only while they are useless. Worse, a skill at guarding is also a skill at taking. The boxer best able to ward off a blow is best able to land one; the doctor who knows how to prevent a disease knows how to cause it. So if the just man is best at keeping money safe, he is best at stealing it. The joke has a point: a technique is neutral and can be turned either way, so justice cannot be only a technique.

**Misjudged.** People often take bad men for friends and good men for enemies. The rule then tells them to harm good men who have done no wrong. Polemarchus patches it: friends are those who really are good.

**Harm makes worse.** A horse that is harmed becomes a worse horse; a man who is harmed becomes worse at human excellence, and justice is a human excellence. A musician cannot by music make men unmusical, so the just man cannot by justice make men unjust. Justice harms nobody, enemies included.

Then Socrates asks whose saying it really was, and answers himself: some "rich and mighty man, who had a great opinion of his own power", Periander, tyrant of Corinth around 600 BC, or Xerxes, the Persian king who invaded Greece in 480. A morality of rewarding friends and punishing enemies belongs to someone strong enough to do both. Conventional morality has just been traced to a powerful man's interest, and a man in the room is waiting to say that this is the whole truth.

<Aside title="What Plato's first readers knew about the people in this room">
The *Republic* was read around 380 BC by Athenians who knew how the evening's company had ended. In 404 BC, after Athens lost its long war with Sparta, a junta of thirty Athenians took power with Spartan backing; it executed Polemarchus without trial and seized the family's shield factory. Five years later the restored democracy executed Socrates. A conversation about whether every regime calls its own interest "justice" was set in a house whose son one regime had killed, around a man another regime had killed.
</Aside>

## Thrasymachus: the law is what the rulers' interest says it is

Thrasymachus of Chalcedon, a Greek city on the Asian shore of the Bosporus, was a *sophist*: a travelling, paid teacher of persuasion, who trained ambitious young men to win in assemblies and law courts. He has sat through two refuted definitions, and now "he came at us like a wild beast, seeking to devour us".

### An answer forbidden in advance

He begins by banning answers: Socrates is not to say that justice is "duty or advantage or profit or gain or interest". Socrates replies that this is like asking what twelve is while forbidding twice six and three times four. If the true answer is on the forbidden list, the ban forbids the truth. Thrasymachus's own answer then uses a forbidden word: "I proclaim that justice is nothing else than the interest of the stronger." Asked what he means, he explains:

<Quote source="Republic I, 338d–339a, trans. B. Jowett">
Well, he said, have you never heard that forms of government differ; there are tyrannies, and there are democracies, and there are aristocracies? […] And the government is the ruling power in each state? […] And the different forms of government make laws democratical, aristocratical, tyrannical, with a view to their several interests; and these laws, which are made by them for their own interests, are the justice which they deliver to their subjects, and him who transgresses them they punish as a breaker of the law, and unjust. And that is what I mean when I say that in all states there is the same principle of justice, which is the interest of the government; and as the government must be supposed to have power, the only reasonable conclusion is, that everywhere there is one principle of justice, which is the interest of the stronger.
</Quote>

The argument has three steps. Every city has a ruling body: one man, a few, or the many. Each ruling body makes laws for its own advantage. Each calls obedience "just" and punishes disobedience as "unjust". So in any city "just" means "what serves those who rule here". He is not saying that rulers betray some true justice that stands above them. There is nothing else for justice to be.

This is Anacharsis's objection turned into a theory. Anacharsis said the strong tear through the web. Thrasymachus says the strong spun it. Putting the law "in the middle" changes nothing on his account: a law cut in stone in the square is the rulers' interest in a form everyone can read. Democracy is on his list. Where the many rule, the laws serve the many, and the rich man who breaks them is punished as unjust. Thrasymachus is not defending tyranny here; he is giving a theory of all law.

### Rulers make mistakes

Socrates's first reply is that rulers are fallible. Thrasymachus agrees that subjects ought to obey and that rulers sometimes misjudge their interest. Then a subject who obeys a mistaken law is just, and does what harms the stronger. Justice turns out to be the stronger's injury as often as his interest.

Cleitophon, a young man in the room who sides with Thrasymachus, offers a rescue: say that justice is what the stronger *thinks* is his interest. Thrasymachus refuses it. Accepting it would make justice mere obedience to whatever the rulers happen to believe, and he wants a claim about real advantage: that injustice actually pays. So he takes another way out. A physician who misdiagnoses is not, at the moment of the mistake, acting as a physician; a skilled man errs only when his skill fails him. In the same way the ruler "in the strict sense", as ruler, never errs, and always commands what is in fact his interest.

That escape has a price. Ruling is now a *craft* (in Greek, *techne*): a body of knowledge for reaching some end, like medicine or navigation.

<KeyFact>
If ruling is a craft, a claim to rule is a claim to know, as a claim to treat the sick is a claim to know medicine, and the question "who should rule?" becomes "who knows how?" (<Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" />).
</KeyFact>

## Whom does a craft serve? The physician, the pilot, the shepherd

Socrates takes the concession at its word and asks what crafts are for.

### The physician and the pilot

Medicine exists because the body has needs: it falls ill and must be cured. Medicine itself, considered as a craft, lacks nothing; it does not need another craft to perfect it. A craft that lacks nothing has no interest of its own to serve, so the only interest it can look after is its object's: medicine looks after the body. The physician in the strict sense is a healer of the sick, not a money-maker. The pilot in the strict sense is not a sailor who happens to be on board; his name means skill at commanding sailors, for the safety of the ship. Every craft rules its object and serves it. So the ruler in the strict sense, whom Thrasymachus has just introduced, looks to the good of the ruled.

### The shepherd

Thrasymachus does not dispute the step. He asks Socrates whether he has a nurse, since she has never wiped his nose or taught him "to know the shepherd from the sheep":

<Quote source="Republic I, 343b–c, trans. B. Jowett">
Because you fancy that the shepherd or neatherd fattens or tends the sheep or oxen with a view to their own good and not to the good of himself or his master; and you further imagine that the rulers of states, if they are true rulers, never think of their subjects as sheep, and that they are not studying their own advantage day and night. Oh, no; and so entirely astray are you in your ideas about the just and unjust as not even to know that justice and the just are in reality another's good; that is to say, the interest of the ruler and stronger, and the loss of the subject and servant; and injustice the opposite; for the unjust is lord over the truly simple and just: he is the stronger, and his subjects do what is for his interest, and minister to his happiness, which is very far from being their own.
</Quote>

He then lists the just man's losses. In a private partnership, when it is wound up, the unjust partner always comes away with more. In taxes, the just man pays more on the same income, and when the city hands something out he takes nothing. In office he neglects his own affairs, makes nothing from the public, and is hated by his friends for refusing them illegal favours. Then the case at its largest scale:

<Quote source="Republic I, 344a–c, trans. B. Jowett">
…that is to say tyranny, which by fraud and force takes away the property of others, not little by little but wholesale; comprehending in one, things sacred as well as profane, private and public; for which acts of wrong, if he were detected perpetrating any one of them singly, he would be punished and incur great disgrace—they who do such wrong in particular cases are called robbers of temples, and man-stealers and burglars and swindlers and thieves. But when a man besides taking away the money of the citizens has made slaves of them, then, instead of these names of reproach, he is termed happy and blessed, not only by the citizens but by all who hear of his having achieved the consummation of injustice. For mankind censure injustice, fearing that they may be the victims of it and not because they shrink from committing it.
</Quote>

The speech is strong for three reasons. It describes what anyone can observe: partnerships, tax rolls and public offices behave as he says often enough. It explains why justice is praised anyway: people blame injustice from fear of suffering it, not from any reluctance to commit it. And it explains the difference of scale, which a moralist has to explain away: the temple-robber is punished and the man who enslaves a whole city is congratulated.

### Socrates's answer, and what it leaves standing

The shepherd speech is a counterexample aimed at the craft argument. If a shepherd is a craftsman whose work ends at his own table, then a craft can serve the craftsman, and the step from "every craft serves its object" to "the ruler serves the ruled" fails. Socrates has to show that the profit is not part of the shepherd's craft.

He answers that Thrasymachus has been strict about the physician and loose about the shepherd. The shepherd as shepherd cares for the sheep; fattening them for the table belongs to the diner, selling them to the merchant. Every craftsman is paid, but payment comes from a separate craft, wage-earning, which accompanies all the others. The evidence is that nobody takes an office without pay: if ruling were its own reward, rulers would not need wages. So rulers are paid in money, in honour, or in a penalty. Good men want neither money nor honour for ruling; what moves them is the penalty for refusing, "to be ruled by one who is worse than himself". In a city of good men, Socrates adds, people would compete to avoid office as they now compete to win it.

This defends the craft argument against the shepherd: the profit belongs to the wage-earner in the man, not to the shepherd or the ruler in him, so ruling considered as a craft still serves the ruled. It leaves standing the shepherd speech's description of what rulers actually do. The two claims are about different things, one about ruling as a craft, one about the men who hold office, and both can be true at once. If both are, no actual city is ruled by a ruler in Socrates's strict sense, and Thrasymachus loses nothing by granting it, since his ledger of partnerships, taxes and tyrants was about actual cities all along. Plato never withdrew the description. In his last work, the *Laws*, written some thirty years later, his main speaker reports the same view and condemns the cities it describes:

<Quote source="Laws IV, 715b, trans. B. Jowett">
Now, according to our view, such governments are not polities at all, nor are laws right which are passed for the good of particular classes and not for the good of the whole state. States which have such laws are not polities but parties, and their notions of justice are simply unmeaning.
</Quote>

That is a verdict on such cities, not a denial that they exist. The next generation would sort regimes by exactly Thrasymachus's test, whether the rulers rule for the common good or their own (<Ref lesson="aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle" />).

Socrates does not try to close that gap. He sets aside the claim that justice is the interest of the stronger and turns to the new one, that the unjust life is better than the just, which "appears to me to be of a far more serious character". The quarrel has moved from what the law is to which life to live.

## Three ways injustice defeats itself, and what they do not show

Thrasymachus now goes further than calling injustice profitable. He refuses to call it a vice: injustice is "discretion", good judgment, and justice is "sublime simplicity", meaning gullibility. Socrates sees that this blocks the easy reply. You cannot argue from the shame of injustice with a man who ranks injustice with wisdom. Socrates has to show, on Thrasymachus's own terms, that injustice is a failure.

### Wanting more than one's share

The Greek word for the urge to have more than one's share, to outdo everyone, is *pleonexia*. Socrates gets Thrasymachus to agree that the just man tries to outdo only the unjust, while the unjust man tries to outdo everyone, just and unjust alike. Then he turns to crafts. A musician tuning a lyre does not try to tighten the strings beyond what another musician would; there is a right pitch, and he aims at it. He would only try to outdo someone who does not know music. A physician prescribing a diet behaves the same way. Whoever knows aims at the correct measure; whoever does not know overshoots everyone. So the unjust man, who tries to outdo all, resembles the ignorant, and the just man resembles the wise. Each, Socrates adds, is what he resembles. The conclusion Thrasymachus has to concede is the reverse of his own: justice is virtue and wisdom, injustice vice and ignorance.

Thrasymachus makes these admissions "with extreme reluctance; it was a hot summer's day, and the perspiration poured from him in torrents; and then I saw what I had never seen before, Thrasymachus blushing". He has been shamed. Whether he has been refuted is another matter. The argument assumes that having money and power is like tuning a string, that there is a correct amount and wanting more is overshooting it. That is exactly what Thrasymachus denies.

### Honour among thieves

<Example title="The robber band">
Socrates asks whether a city, an army, or "a band of robbers and thieves" could act together if its members wronged one another. Thrasymachus agrees that it could not.

Step 1. A gang that robs outsiders must not rob itself: its members share out the loot as agreed, keep watch for one another, and do not betray the plan.

Step 2. That restraint among members is justice, applied inside the gang. So a gang able to act at all has "some remnant of justice in them"; its members are "but half-villains". Men wholly unjust would turn on one another and could do nothing.

Step 3. The same holds for any body that acts: a city, an army, a family. Socrates extends it to a single person. A man in whom injustice rules is "not at unity with himself", and cannot act any more than a gang at war with itself can.

What it shows: injustice cannot be total and effective at once. What it does not show: that the just man is happier than an unjust one who keeps faith with his partners and preys on everyone else. That man is exactly the gang leader of Step 2.
</Example>

### What a thing is for

The last argument uses a tool. You can cut a vine branch with a dagger or a chisel, but not so well as with a pruning hook made for the purpose. Its *work* (Greek *ergon*) is what it alone does, or does best; its *excellence* (*arete*, the Greek word usually translated "virtue") is whatever makes it do that work well, a sharp edge for the hook, sight for the eye. The soul has a work too: to manage, command, deliberate, and to live. What is its excellence? Socrates reminds Thrasymachus that he has already answered: he conceded at the end of the pleonexia argument that justice is virtue, and injustice vice. So justice is the excellence of the soul. Then the just soul lives well, the one who lives well is happy, and injustice "can never be more profitable than justice".

The chain is valid, and it hangs on the concession won in the pleonexia argument, which was the weakest link of the night. Socrates says as much himself:

<Quote source="Republic I, 354b–c, trans. B. Jowett">
As an epicure snatches a taste of every dish which is successively brought to table, he not having allowed himself time to enjoy the one before, so have I gone from one subject to another without having discovered what I sought at first, the nature of justice. […] And the result of the whole discussion has been that I know nothing at all. For I know not what justice is, and therefore I am not likely to know whether it is or is not a virtue, nor can I say whether the just man is happy or unhappy.
</Quote>

He has won every exchange and lost the question.

## Glaucon restarts the challenge: why rewards cannot answer it

Thrasymachus has fallen silent. Glaucon has not been persuaded either, and says so: "Socrates, do you wish really to persuade us, or only to seem to have persuaded us, that to be just is always better than to be unjust?" He means to restate Thrasymachus's case at a level Socrates's arguments so far could not reach.

### Three kinds of good

Glaucon starts by sorting goods into three kinds. Some we want for their own sake, apart from anything that follows: harmless pleasures. Some we want both for themselves and for their results: sight, knowledge, health. Some we want only for their results and would avoid if we could: exercise, medical treatment, the ways of earning money.

Socrates puts justice in the second class, which he calls the highest. It outranks the first because a good there is worth having twice over: it would be worth having if nothing followed from it, and good things follow from it as well. The many, says Glaucon, put it in the third: a burden carried for its wages, "rewards and reputation". Glaucon wants to hear justice praised for itself, and to provoke the praise he will argue the many's case as hard as he can, in three claims: that justice is a compromise people accept because they are weak; that those who practise it do so unwillingly, from necessity; and that they are right to, because the unjust life is better than the just.

### A bargain among the weak

The first claim is an account of where justice comes from, which Glaucon presents as what people say:

<Quote source="Republic II, 358e–359b, trans. B. Jowett">
They say that to do injustice is, by nature, good; to suffer injustice, evil; but that the evil is greater than the good. And so when men have both done and suffered injustice and have had experience of both, not being able to avoid the one and obtain the other, they think that they had better agree among themselves to have neither; hence there arise laws and mutual covenants; and that which is ordained by law is termed by them lawful and just. This they affirm to be the origin and nature of justice;—it is a mean or compromise, between the best of all, which is to do injustice and not be punished, and the worst of all, which is to suffer injustice without the power of retaliation; and justice, being at a middle point between the two, is tolerated not as a good, but as the lesser evil, and honoured by reason of the inability of men to do injustice. For no man who is worthy to be called a man would ever submit to such an agreement if he were able to resist; he would be mad if he did.
</Quote>

By nature, on this account, the best thing is to wrong others without paying for it, and the worst is to be wronged without being able to hit back. Men who cannot have the first without risking the second agree to give up both, and call the agreement law and the law justice. Justice is a compromise between the best and the worst, valued as the lesser evil. This is a *contract theory*: justice exists because people agreed to it, and they agreed only because each was too weak to do better alone. <Ref lesson="hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant" /> starts from a similar bargain and builds a state on it.

The ring story is Glaucon's proof of the second claim, that those who practise justice do so unwillingly, from inability to do otherwise.

### Two genealogies, not one

Thrasymachus and Glaucon give opposite accounts of who made justice. They are easily merged into one view, "justice serves the strong", and the merger loses the argument. For Thrasymachus the strong make the law and the weak obey it, so justice is "another's good", the ruler's. For Glaucon the weak make the law to protect themselves from the strong, and a really strong man would be mad to sign it. One makes justice the tool of the rulers, the other the shield of the many.

What they share is the premise beneath both stories. By nature everyone wants more; justice is a convention imposed against that nature; so justice is not a good for the person who practises it.

A test separates them. Take a tax law whose burden falls on the poor: Thrasymachus explains it at once (the rich rule and wrote it), and Glaucon's bargain among equals has trouble. Take a law that sentences anyone who tries to make himself tyrant: Glaucon explains it at once (the many guarding their pact against the man strong enough to break it), and Thrasymachus has trouble, unless the many are themselves the "stronger" of a democracy.

### Two statues: being just and seeming just

The third claim, that the unjust life is better, needs the two lives compared with nothing to blur the comparison. Glaucon "isolates" the two men. The perfectly unjust man is skilful, like a good pilot who knows what his ship can do: he commits the greatest wrongs and is never found out, "for the highest reach of injustice is, to be deemed just when you are not". Beside him stands the perfectly just man, who wants "to be and not to seem good", in a line of the tragic poet Aeschylus. He must have no reputation for justice at all: if he seemed just, he would be honoured, "and then we shall not know whether he is just for the sake of justice or for the sake of honours and rewards". So let him be the best of men and be thought the worst. Socrates says Glaucon polishes the pair up "as if they were two statues".

  src="./figures/being-seeming.svg"

Cell 'is just, seems unjust': scourged, racked, bound, eyes burnt out, impaled. The other two cells are grey and marked 'not in the test'."

Then the outcome, which he puts in the mouths of those who praise injustice. The just man thought unjust "will be scourged, racked, bound—will have his eyes burnt out; and, at last, after suffering every kind of evil, he will be impaled". The unjust man thought just rules the city, marries into any family he likes, wins every contract, grows rich, helps his friends and harms his enemies, and makes such generous offerings that even the gods favour him. Which of the two is happier?

### What parents and poets praise

Adeimantus, Plato's other brother, now speaks for the first time and adds what Glaucon left out. Parents tell their sons to be just for the sake of reputation, offices and good marriages. Poets promise the just man oaks heavy with acorns and sheep weighed down by their fleeces, and in the next world an eternal feast. Wandering priests sell rites that wipe out a man's own or his ancestors' crimes, so even the gods' punishment can be bought off. A clever young man hearing all this concludes that he should put up a front of virtue and keep behind it "the subtle and crafty fox". Then Adeimantus makes the demand that the rest of the book has to meet:

<Quote source="Republic II, 367e, trans. B. Jowett">
And therefore, I say, not only prove to us that justice is better than injustice, but show what they either of them do to the possessor of them, which makes the one to be a good and the other an evil, whether seen or unseen by gods and men.
</Quote>

### Why rewards cannot answer the ring

Put the pieces together. Any defence of justice by its rewards says "be just, because something good follows". That places justice in the third class, wanted only for what it brings. Then anyone who can get what it brings without paying for it, by seeming just, has no reason left to be just. The two statues are built to give the unjust man every reward of seeming just and the just man every penalty of seeming unjust. Against that construction a defence by rewards loses before it starts. The gods do not rescue it: Adeimantus has already said that the poets make the gods open to bribes, so divine reward can be bought by the unjust as well.

What remains is a single kind of answer: a fact about what justice does inside the person who has it, true whatever follows from it and whoever sees it.

## Reading justice in large letters

Adeimantus asks about something nobody can see: what justice does inside a person, unseen by gods and men. Socrates answers with a method rather than an argument.

A short-sighted man is asked to read small letters from far away. If someone pointed him to the same letters written larger somewhere else, he could read those first and then go back to the small ones. Justice is spoken of both as the virtue of a single person and as the virtue of a whole city, and a city is larger than a person. So Socrates proposes to look first at a city, and to watch justice and injustice come into being by building one from its beginning. A city's parts and the relations between them can be seen, which a soul's cannot, and if the same pattern turns up in both, the city will show what the soul hides.

The building starts at once and from need: "no one is self-sufficing, but all of us have many wants". The city of the *Republic* is Socrates's answer to the ring.

<Conclusion>
You can now state the challenge that Plato set himself: if no one could ever find out, would anyone have a reason to be just? Thrasymachus says justice is the interest of whoever rules, written as law in every regime alike; Glaucon says it is a bargain the weak make because they cannot dominate, kept only from fear. The first half of the night shows definitions dying by counterexample, and Socrates's replies to Thrasymachus (rulers err, crafts serve their objects, injustice overreaches and divides itself, the soul has a work) win the exchanges without saying what justice is. Glaucon's sorting of goods shows why that was never going to be enough: any defence by rewards places justice among goods wanted only for what they bring, and the ring and the two statues remove what they bring.

What is left is a demand: show what justice does inside the person who has it. Socrates proposes to find out by building a city in speech and reading justice off it in large letters. <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" /> builds that city, class by class, and asks what each step assumes.
</Conclusion>

<Exercise>
Without looking back: name the three definitions of justice put forward in the *Republic* before Glaucon speaks, and for each, the case or argument brought against it and whether the definition survives. Then, from the lesson on the Greek cities: which phrase named the place where power and law were set so that anyone could dispute them, and why does Thrasymachus's thesis need the law to be something that was made and can be disputed?

<Solution>
1. Telling the truth and paying debts (Cephalus): does not survive. It is broken by the single case of the sword deposited by a friend who later goes mad and asks for it back; returning it pays the debt and is plainly wrong.
2. Giving each what is owed, read as helping friends and harming enemies (Polemarchus, from Simonides): does not survive. It is broken three ways. It makes justice useless, good only for keeping things not in use, and as good at stealing as at guarding. It makes us harm good men whenever we misjudge who our friends are. And harming a man makes him worse in human excellence, which justice cannot do.
3. Justice is the interest of the stronger (Thrasymachus): attacked first by the rulers' mistakes (obeying a mistaken law harms the stronger), then by the craft argument (a ruler in the strict sense serves the ruled). It survives. The craft argument is about ruling as a craft, and leaves standing the shepherd speech's account of what actual rulers do; the later arguments shame Thrasymachus into silence without refuting him, and Socrates ends the night confessing that he does not know what justice is. Glaucon restates the thesis at once.

The phrase is *es meson*, "into the middle": power, disputes and the law placed in the public square, at equal distance from every citizen (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" />). Thrasymachus's thesis presupposes exactly that arrangement. Only a law that some body of men *made*, rather than one received from the gods or from a sacred king, can be asked "whose interest does this serve?" Written public law made the question possible; Thrasymachus gives it his answer.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Apply the sourcing questions to Thrasymachus's regime passage ("the different forms of government make laws democratical, aristocratical, tyrannical, with a view to their several interests…"). Who wrote it, when, for whom, and in whose mouth? What does it record about Greek cities, what does it argue, and what does it leave out? Finally: what can a speech that Plato wrote for an opponent be *used* to establish about Greek opinion around 380 BC, and what can it not?

<Solution>
Who, when, for whom: Plato wrote it around 380 BC, a generation after the Athenian democracy executed Socrates and about twenty-five years after a junta of thirty had killed Polemarchus, the host's son. The dialogue is set earlier, in Socrates's lifetime, in the house of a rich foreign manufacturer at the Piraeus. The words are given to Thrasymachus, a real teacher of rhetoric from Chalcedon, speaking to Socrates and a room of young Athenians. Plato's readers were educated Athenians who knew how the company had ended.

What it records: that Greeks recognised three kinds of regime (rule by one man, by a few, by the many) and that each made its own laws and punished breaches of them as injustice.

What it argues: that "just" means nothing but "in the interest of whoever rules", so that one principle of justice holds everywhere, the interest of the stronger.

What it omits: any account of what the rulers' interest actually is or how they know it. The argument from mistakes exploits that gap at once. It also omits laws that seem to serve everyone, such as laws against murder.

Use: Plato wrote the speech in order to have it answered, so it cannot establish what the historical Thrasymachus believed word for word. It can establish that around 380 the view was current and forceful enough that Plato chose to state it at full strength in an opponent's mouth, and that he expected his readers to find it plausible: Glaucon says he hears it from "myriads of others". A text written to be refuted is good evidence that its target was a live opinion, and weak evidence for the exact wording of any one man's doctrine.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "Socrates refutes Thrasymachus in Book I. The craft argument shows that rulers serve the ruled." Name the step that fails, and say what Socrates himself says at the end of the book.

<Solution>
The craft argument is valid about the ruler *in the strict sense*, a figure Thrasymachus introduced to escape the argument from mistakes: a ruler considered only as a practitioner of ruling, who never errs. Of that figure, Socrates shows that as a craftsman he serves his object, as medicine serves the body. The claim fails at the step from that figure to actual rulers. Nothing in the argument shows that any real ruler practises ruling as a pure craft rather than as a means to his own gain. Thrasymachus's shepherd speech is aimed at exactly that gap: real shepherds fatten sheep for the table, real rulers study their own advantage "day and night". Socrates answers by separating the craft of ruling from the craft of earning wages, which again describes what ruling is, not what rulers do. Both accounts can be true together, and then no city has true rulers.

The later arguments do not close the gap either. The pleonexia argument shames Thrasymachus by assuming there is a correct amount of wealth and power to want; the function argument rests on the concession won there. Socrates says so himself: "the result of the whole discussion has been that I know nothing at all. For I know not what justice is", and so he cannot say whether the just man is happy. Thrasymachus is silenced, not refuted, and Glaucon reopens the case at once.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the ring backwards. Imagine a man who has the ring and still does no wrong. Glaucon says onlookers would call him "a most wretched idiot". Derive what the onlookers must believe about which class of goods justice belongs to for that judgment to follow. Then show that the same belief explains why, according to Adeimantus, parents and poets praise justice only for its rewards.

<Solution>
Step 1. Calling the man an idiot means judging that he chose badly: he gave up something good and received nothing in return.

Step 2. What he gave up is plain: the goods the ring would have let him take. For that to be a loss with nothing in return, his justice must have brought him nothing of its own. So the onlookers believe justice has no value in itself.

Step 3. If justice has no value in itself, it is either no good at all or good only for its results. The onlookers do practise it in public, so they think it good for something: its results, safety from the injustice of others and a good name. That is the third class, the goods wanted only for what they bring, like medicine.

Step 4. Under the ring the results are gone. Nobody can see him, so he earns no reputation, and he is in no danger of being wronged in return, since he is untouchable. A good of the third class with its results removed is pure cost. Hence "idiot".

Now Adeimantus. If everyone believes justice belongs to the third class, then anyone who wants a child to be just can only point to its results, the offices, marriages, harvests, eternal feasts, and to the punishments of injustice. Praising justice for itself would mean praising a second-class good, which is exactly what the belief denies exists. So the praise Adeimantus hears, and the onlookers' sneer, come from one premise. That is why Glaucon and Adeimantus ask for the opposite: justice shown to be a good of the second class, good in the soul whether seen or unseen.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Glaucon's bargain is made by people who "cannot avoid the one and obtain the other": they can neither wrong others safely nor avoid being wronged. Test it at its limits. (a) A single man who cannot dominate faces many who can. Does any "justice" in Glaucon's sense arise between them? (b) A single man strong enough to dominate faces a city of people who cannot. What does Glaucon's account predict, and which of the two genealogies, his or Thrasymachus's, describes what follows? (c) What do the two cases show about whether the genealogies are rivals or two parts of one picture?

<Solution>
(a) No bargain arises. The many lose nothing by wronging him and gain nothing by promising not to; he has nothing to offer them in exchange for their restraint. Glaucon's justice needs a rough equality of vulnerability: each must be able to suffer, and each must be able to do some harm. With one weak man among strong ones the condition fails, and he is simply at their mercy. Whatever rules they then lay on him are their interest, which is Thrasymachus's case.

(b) Glaucon's account predicts that the strong man will not join the bargain, since "no man who is worthy to be called a man would ever submit to such an agreement if he were able to resist". He will take what he likes, like Gyges, and make himself tyrant. Once he rules, he makes the laws, in his own interest, and punishes breaches as injustice: Thrasymachus's account describes the city from then on. The many, for their part, will try to restore their bargain by making the tyrant's attempt a crime, which is Glaucon's account again.

(c) The two cases show that each genealogy describes one configuration of power. Among rough equals, justice is Glaucon's mutual pact. Where one side is decisively stronger, it is Thrasymachus's rulers' interest. They rest on the same premise (by nature everyone wants more, and restraint is accepted only from inability) and differ in where the strength lies. So they are rivals as accounts of who makes justice in general, and complementary as descriptions of particular cities. Neither says justice is good for the person who practises it, which is what the *Republic* sets out to show.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Someone claims: "Thrasymachus and Anacharsis are saying the same thing: law serves the strong." Name two facts the claim fails to account for, and state what would have to be true for it to survive.

<Solution>
Fact 1: they disagree about what the strong do to the law. Anacharsis says the strong *tear through* it: the web catches the weak and breaks when the powerful push against it. The law, on his view, is meant to bind everyone and fails to bind the strong. Thrasymachus says the strong *wrote* it: the law works exactly as intended, and what it was intended to do is serve the rulers. For Anacharsis the strong man breaks the law; for Thrasymachus the strong man is the law.

Fact 2: the replies that answer one leave the other untouched. Solon's answer to Anacharsis (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" />) was that men keep agreements when neither side gains by breaking them, so a law fitted to the citizens will be obeyed by the strong too. That answers a complaint about enforcement. It does nothing against Thrasymachus, who can agree that the law is perfectly enforced and still say whose interest it enforces. Conversely, Socrates's argument from rulers' mistakes touches Thrasymachus (rulers may write laws that harm them) and is irrelevant to Anacharsis.

For the claim to survive it would have to be restated at the level of their shared conclusion: in practice the weak bear the law's burden and the strong do not. As a statement of outcome, both agree. As accounts of what law *is*, a failed common standard or a successful instrument of rule, they differ, and the difference decides what a defender of law has to answer.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Set two passages side by side: Thrasymachus's ledger of the just man's losses (he comes away from partnerships with less, pays more tax, neglects his affairs in office and is hated by his friends for refusing them favours) and Socrates's account of why good men rule at all (they take neither money nor honour, and rule only to avoid "being ruled by one who is worse"). Where do the two passages agree? Where do they disagree? Write the judgment the comparison supports.

<Solution>
Agreement: both say that public office, honestly held, costs the just man. Thrasymachus lists the costs: neglected private affairs, no profit from the public, friends lost by refusing illegal favours. Socrates grants the same thing from the other side: ruling brings the ruler no benefit of its own, which is why nobody takes office without pay and why good men must be compelled. They agree, too, that the eager office-seeker is usually after something other than the good of the ruled.

Disagreement: what the cost shows. For Thrasymachus it shows that justice is folly: the just man loses, so the clever man is unjust. For Socrates it shows what ruling is: a craft that serves its object, so that the true ruler is the one who rules reluctantly, and a city of good men would compete to avoid office.

Judgment: on the facts the two speakers agree, and the dispute is over interpretation. The evidence of the passages cannot decide it, because both readings fit the same facts. What would decide it is whether the reluctant ruler gets some good from ruling that is not money or honour, for instance the good of not living under a worse man, or a good in his own soul. Socrates names the first; the second is what Adeimantus's demand requires and what Book I has not supplied.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Adeimantus asks Socrates to show what justice does "to the possessor of" it, "whether seen or unseen by gods and men". Make a list of what you would need to know about a person's soul to answer him: what questions about its structure an answer would have to settle. Then say why Socrates turns to a city to answer a question about a soul.

<Solution>
A possible list. An answer would need to know:
1. Whether the soul has parts, or is one thing. If justice is to be something *in* the soul, the soul must have some structure for justice to be a state of.
2. What those parts want. Book I suggested an overreaching desire (pleonexia) and a capacity to deliberate and command; an answer needs to know whether these are different parts.
3. Which part should rule the others, and why that one. The function argument said the soul's work is to manage, command and deliberate; that makes ruling something the soul does to itself.
4. What happens when the wrong part rules. The robber-band argument said a soul at odds with itself cannot act; an answer must explain that disorder and show that it is bad for its owner, whether anyone sees it or not.
5. Whether the right order is the same thing as justice, so that the just man is better off even on the rack.

Socrates turns to a city because each item on the list concerns relations between parts that cannot be seen in a soul, while the parts of a city (who works, who fights, who decides) can be seen. If justice is the same pattern written in both, it can be read in the large letters first. <Ref lesson="kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king" /> builds that city and reads off from it a soul with three parts, one of which ought to rule. Your list is its inventory. You have also met the reason the city's rulers will have to be compelled to rule: Socrates said in Book I that good men take office only under the penalty of being ruled by someone worse.
</Solution>

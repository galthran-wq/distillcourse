In 431 BC Athens buries its first war dead and its leading citizen tells the crowd that their city is a school for all Greece, free and open, governed by the many. Four years later the same assembly votes to kill every man of a rebel ally and changes its mind overnight; eleven years after that its envoys tell a small neutral island that justice is a question only between equals in power. A single historian recorded all three speeches. The lesson asks how one city could say all three things, and what the speeches show about who should rule.

<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" /> ended with a city that had placed power "in the middle" of men who counted one another as equals, decided by speech in the open, and made justice a written public text that anyone could quote against a magistrate. Of all the cities of its time, Athens had gone furthest down that road. The natural expectation is that such a city would treat justice as binding wherever it acted, since it had built its own life on the claim that power must answer to a public standard.

The record shows the opposite, and the record is not an enemy's. The three speeches in the opening come from Athenians, addressed to Athenians or spoken in Athens's name, and the harshest of them explains itself without embarrassment. Two easy readings offer themselves: the city was hypocritical, saying fine things at home and doing ugly ones abroad; or the war corrupted a good democracy, so the first speech is the real Athens and the last a degenerate one. Both readings assume the three speeches contradict one another. Before deciding whether they do, you need to know what kind of evidence they are.

## A speech in this history is the argument the occasion demanded

Every claim below rests on speeches, so the first question is what a speech in this book records: what a man said, or something else?

### The historian

Thucydides was an Athenian of a wealthy family, old enough when the war began in 431 BC to follow it from the start. In 424 he was one of the city's generals, with a squadron in the north Aegean, and he failed to save Amphipolis, an Athenian colony on the north coast of the Aegean, from a Spartan attack. The city exiled him. He describes his position in one sentence:

<Quote source="Thucydides V.26, trans. R. Crawley">
I lived through the whole of it, being of an age to comprehend events, and giving my attention to them in order to know the exact truth about them. It was also my fate to be an exile from my country for twenty years after my command at Amphipolis; and being present with both parties, and more especially with the Peloponnesians by reason of my exile, I had leisure to observe affairs somewhat particularly.
</Quote>

His history breaks off in 411, seven years before the war's end; he did not finish it.

### What the speeches are

The obvious way to read a speech in a history is as a transcript. Thucydides rules that out himself, in the passage where he explains his method:

<Quote source="Thucydides I.22, trans. R. Crawley">
With reference to the speeches in this history, some were delivered before the war began, others while it was going on; some I heard myself, others I got from various quarters; it was in all cases difficult to carry them word for word in one's memory, so my habit has been to make the speakers say what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various occasions, of course adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what they really said.
</Quote>

For the events, by contrast, he says he trusted no single report, not even his own impressions, and tested each account "by the most severe and detailed tests possible". He wrote the whole, he says, "not as an essay which is to win the applause of the moment, but as a possession for all time".

<Remark title="What a speech in this history can establish">
A speech is the historian's reconstruction of the argument the occasion called for, held to the general sense of what was said. It is good evidence of what arguments could be made and answered in Athens, and of how a well-placed Athenian understood a decision. It is weak evidence of anyone's exact words. So "Pericles says" in what follows means "Pericles, in Thucydides' reconstruction, says"; the shorter form is used from here on.
</Remark>

### The war and the empire

The war is the Peloponnesian War, 431–404 BC, fought about fifty years after Plataea, the battle of 479 that ended the Persian invasion (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-hoplite-phalanx">the last date on the earlier timeline</Ref>). On one side stood Sparta, the state the translation also calls *Lacedaemon*, with its allies in the Peloponnese. On the other stood Athens with its empire.

The empire grew out of the Persian war. After 479 the cities of the Aegean islands and the coast of Asia Minor joined Athens to carry the fight against Persia, each contributing ships or money. Over the following decades most of them stopped sending ships and paid a yearly *tribute* in silver instead; Athens kept the fleet the money paid for, and a member that tried to leave was besieged and brought back. By 431 the allies were subjects in all but name. Thucydides puts the cause of the war in one sentence:

<Quote source="Thucydides I.23, trans. R. Crawley">
The growth of the power of Athens, and the alarm which this inspired in Lacedaemon, made war inevitable.
</Quote>

## The Funeral Oration: freedom among equals, and the empire that proves it

The first speech is the city's account of itself, given at the moment it had most reason to say what it was fighting for. What does an Athenian, speaking for the city to the city, say holds it together?

### A funeral by tribes

At the end of the war's first campaigning year, in the winter of 431/0, the Athenians buried their dead at public cost, by an ancestral custom Thucydides describes before the speech. The bones lay in a tent for three days. Then cypress coffins were carried in procession, one for each tribe, each holding the bones of that tribe's dead: the ten tribes Cleisthenes had made of the citizen body in 508/7 (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="cleisthenes-equality-one-share-each">each a third from the coast, the inland and the city</Ref>). One empty bier was carried "for the missing, that is, for those whose bodies could not be recovered". The city mourned as a body of tribes, and the man who spoke over them was "a man chosen by the state".

The man chosen was Pericles, the leading Athenian of his generation. He was one of the ten generals the Athenians elected every year, and they re-elected him again and again; he had argued for the war and planned its strategy. The helmet on his portrait is a general's. He died in 429, two and a half years into the war.

### The constitution in one paragraph

A war funeral is expected to praise the dead men's courage. Pericles postpones that. First, he says, he will describe "the form of government under which our greatness grew", because that is what the dead died for. The centre of the speech is one paragraph on the constitution:

<Quote source="Thucydides II.37, trans. R. Crawley">
Our constitution does not copy the laws of neighbouring states; we are rather a pattern to others than imitators ourselves. Its administration favours the many instead of the few; this is why it is called a democracy. If we look to the laws, they afford equal justice to all in their private differences; if no social standing, advancement in public life falls to reputation for capacity, class considerations not being allowed to interfere with merit; nor again does poverty bar the way, if a man is able to serve the state, he is not hindered by the obscurity of his condition. The freedom which we enjoy in our government extends also to our ordinary life. There, far from exercising a jealous surveillance over each other, we do not feel called upon to be angry with our neighbour for doing what he likes, or even to indulge in those injurious looks which cannot fail to be offensive, although they inflict no positive penalty. But all this ease in our private relations does not make us lawless as citizens. Against this fear is our chief safeguard, teaching us to obey the magistrates and the laws, particularly such as regard the protection of the injured, whether they are actually on the statute book, or belong to that code which, although unwritten, yet cannot be broken without acknowledged disgrace.
</Quote>

<Example title="What the constitution paragraph claims">
Take it sentence by sentence and state each claim plainly.

1. **Who rules.** "Its administration favours the many instead of the few; this is why it is called a democracy." Government is run in the interest of, and by, the many; the name *democracy* means rule (*kratos*) of the people (*demos*).
2. **One law for all.** In private disputes everyone has "equal justice": the same law, the same courts, whoever you are. This is isonomia, equal standing under the law, as <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="a-ruler-who-used-the-phrase">Maeandrius proclaimed it at Samos</Ref>.
3. **Office by capacity.** Public honours go by "reputation for capacity", and birth ("class considerations") is not allowed to interfere.
4. **Poverty no bar.** A poor man who can serve is not kept out.
5. **Private freedom.** Citizens do not police one another's private lives, not even with a disapproving look.
6. **Public obedience.** That freedom does not make them lawless, because they hold the magistrates and the laws, written and unwritten, in awe ("fear" here means reverence, not dread of punishment).

Now set the clauses against the two equalities of the earlier lesson: *arithmetic* equality, one share each whatever a man is worth, and *geometric* equality, shares in proportion to worth (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="two-equalities-the-city-could-not-reconcile">two equalities the city could not reconcile</Ref>). Clause 2 is arithmetic: each man counts for one before the law. Clause 3 is geometric: office goes to the more capable in proportion to capacity. Clause 4 changes what counts as worth: not wealth, which was the measure in Solon's four classes, but capacity, which a poor man can have.

So Pericles claims Athens has both equalities, and the claim is coherent because each governs a different sphere. Law and the vote are shared arithmetically; office is distributed geometrically, by a measure of worth the poor can meet. Whether practice matched the paragraph is another question; the paragraph is the city's statement of what it is.
</Example>

### The citizen who judges

The paragraph says who rules. The next part says what ruling demands of an ordinary citizen, and the answer is participation. A man who takes no part in public business is regarded "not as unambitious but as useless". Most citizens cannot propose policy, but "we Athenians are able to judge at all events if we cannot originate", and "instead of looking on discussion as a stumbling-block in the way of action, we think it an indispensable preliminary to any wise action at all".

This is the arrangement of the earlier lesson described by the people who lived in it. A claim placed in the middle must <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="what-a-claim-in-the-middle-requires">win over the men standing round it by speech</Ref>; Pericles says that the many cannot all speak well, but they can all listen and decide, and that the deciding is everyone's duty.

### What holds the city together

Clause 6 explains why free citizens obey: awe of the magistrates and the laws. A war funeral asks more than obedience. The men in the coffins went out and died, and awe of the law does not explain that. What makes a man who lives as he pleases risk his life for the city?

The enemy had an answer everyone knew: training. The Spartans, Pericles says, "from their very cradles by a painful discipline seek after manliness"; its citizens were drilled for war from boyhood. Athens has no such system, and Pericles makes that its boast: "at Athens we live exactly as we please, and yet are just as ready to encounter every legitimate danger", with courage "not of art but of nature". That takes away the obvious answer and leaves the question standing: if not drill, what?

His answer is love. The city is a model, "as a city we are the school of Hellas", and he tells the mourners to look at Athens's power and "feed your eyes upon her from day to day, till love of her fills your hearts". So the city stands on two supports: awe of the law makes free citizens obey, and love of the city makes them willing to die.

### What the speech says about those outside

The ideal so far describes relations among citizens. The same speech, read to the end, shows two other relations governed by something else.

The first is the empire. The proof of Athens's worth, Pericles says, is its power: the city "alone gives no occasion … to her subjects to question her title by merit to rule". He goes on:

<Quote source="Thucydides II.41, trans. R. Crawley">
We have forced every sea and land to be the highway of our daring, and everywhere, whether for evil or for good, have left imperishable monuments behind us.
</Quote>

The allies appear here as "subjects", and their subjection is part of the praise. The second relation is the women. After addressing the parents and the sons of the dead, Pericles gives the widows one sentence: "greatest will be hers who is least talked of among the men, whether for good or for bad". Speech in the middle, the instrument of the citizen's life, is for the women a thing to avoid.

Nothing in this is concealed or apologised for, and there is no reason to read it as an accusation. The oration is an account of freedom and equality among Athenian citizens. It says, in passing, that Athens rules other Greeks, and it does not say by what right beyond merit proved by power.

## Mytilene: the merciful side argues from interest

If the Funeral Oration describes how equals govern themselves, the next question is what such an assembly does when it must decide the fate of people who are not its equals. Four years later the Athenians faced that decision over Mytilene.

### A rebel ally and a decree

Mytilene was the chief city of Lesbos. Unlike most allies it paid no tribute; it kept its own fleet and walls, and its government was in the hands of a few rich families, an *oligarchy*, the rule of the few. In 428 it revolted and asked Sparta for help. A Spartan fleet set out, came too late, and in 427 the Athenian general Paches took the city. As food ran out, the oligarchs armed the common people for a sortie against the besiegers; once armed, the people refused to fight, demanded that the grain stores be shared out, and threatened to come to terms with Athens on their own. The oligarchs surrendered the town before the people could hand them over. Paches sent the leaders of the revolt to Athens.

The decision went to the *assembly*: every adult male citizen who chose to come, meeting on the Pnyx, a hill west of the Acropolis, deciding by show of hands after open debate.

The first assembly, "in the fury of the moment", voted to put to death "the whole adult male population of Mitylene, and to make slaves of the women and children" (the translation spells the name Mitylene), and sent a galley with the order to Paches. What swelled the anger, Thucydides says, was that Mytilene had revolted without being oppressed and had brought a Spartan fleet into Athens's sea. Then: "The morrow brought repentance with it and reflection on the horrid cruelty of a decree, which condemned a whole city to the fate merited only by the guilty." A second assembly was called the next day.

### Cleon: punishment is both just and useful

The man who had carried the first decree spoke again. Thucydides introduces Cleon as "the most violent man at Athens, and at that time by far the most powerful with the commons". His speech begins with democracy itself:

<Quote source="Thucydides III.37, trans. R. Crawley">
I have often before now been convinced that a democracy is incapable of empire, and never more so than by your present change of mind in the matter of Mitylene. Fears or plots being unknown to you in your daily relations with each other, you feel just the same with regard to your allies … entirely forgetting that your empire is a despotism and your subjects disaffected conspirators, whose obedience is ensured not by your suicidal concessions, but by the superiority given you by your own strength and not their loyalty.
</Quote>

The argument has four steps.

**The assembly is the wrong kind of judge.** Citizens who live without suspicion of one another carry the same ease into their dealings with subjects, which is a mistake, because subjects are not fellow citizens. Worse, the assembly reopens settled questions. "Bad laws which are never changed are better for a city than good ones that have no authority", and "ordinary men usually manage public affairs better than their more gifted fellows". The clever speakers who would reverse the decree have made the assembly "more like the audience of a rhetorician than the council of a city".

**Mytilene's guilt is deliberate.** Revolt implies oppression, and Mytilene was free, armed and honoured; so this was "deliberate and wanton aggression". The people share the guilt: "do not, while you condemn the aristocracy, absolve the people", for they fought alongside their rulers.

**Justice and interest coincide.** Punishing all is just, because the offence was voluntary, and useful, because it deters: "teach your other allies by a striking example that the penalty of rebellion is death". If failure costs a rebel little, every ally will revolt "upon the slightest pretext", and Athens will have to fight them one after another, risking money and lives to win back each time "a ruined town from which we can no longer draw the revenue upon which our strength depends". Pity, sentiment and indulgence are "the three failings most fatal to empire".

**The dilemma.** Cleon puts the assembly a choice it cannot evade:

<Quote source="Thucydides III.40, trans. R. Crawley">
For if they were right in rebelling, you must be wrong in ruling. However, if, right or wrong, you determine to rule, you must carry out your principle and punish the Mitylenians as your interest requires; or else you must give up your empire and cultivate honesty without danger.
</Quote>

### Diodotus: "not a court of justice"

The reply came from Diodotus, who had opposed the decree the day before. He is otherwise unknown; he appears in the history only here. From a contest between justice and interest one would expect the merciful speaker to argue from justice: the people of Mytilene did not revolt, so killing them is wrong. Diodotus refuses that ground.

<Quote source="Thucydides III.44, trans. R. Crawley">
The question before us as sensible men is not their guilt, but our interests. Though I prove them ever so guilty, I shall not, therefore, advise their death, unless it be expedient … we are not in a court of justice, but in a political assembly; and the question is not justice, but how to make the Mitylenians useful to Athens.
</Quote>

His argument also has four steps, and each answers one of Cleon's.

**Debate is the only way to handle the future.** Haste and passion are the enemies of good counsel; the future is uncertain and can be dealt with only through speech. The real trouble with the assembly is that it suspects every adviser of being bribed, so that plain good advice is suspected as much as bad and "the best counsellor" has "to lie in order to be believed"; and that the advisers answer for bad advice while the assembly that took it answers to no one.

**Death does not deter.** Cities have made death the penalty for lesser crimes, and men still commit them. Hope and desire are "invisible agents, far stronger than the dangers that are seen", and a city fighting for freedom or empire overrates its own chances. "It is impossible to prevent … human nature doing what it has once set its mind upon, by force of law or by any other deterrent force whatsoever."

**Equal punishment defeats its purpose.** Here Diodotus takes Cleon's own picture and reverses its cause. Cleon said leniency would leave Athens winning back ruined towns; Diodotus says severity will. If surrender and resistance are both punished with death, no rebel city has a reason to surrender, every one will hold out to the last, and Athens will pay for long sieges and win "a ruined town from which we can no longer draw the revenue which forms our real strength".

**The people are Athens's friends.** In every allied city the common people are on Athens's side against their oligarchs. Kill the people of Mytilene, who surrendered the town as soon as they had arms, and every oligarchy will have its people behind it at the next revolt. Hence his conclusion:

<Quote source="Thucydides III.47, trans. R. Crawley">
In short, I consider it far more useful for the preservation of our empire voluntarily to put up with injustice, than to put to death, however justly, those whom it is our interest to keep alive.
</Quote>

### Two ships

The vote was taken by show of hands, and "the show of hands was almost equal, although the motion of Diodotus carried the day". A second galley left at once. The first had about a day and a night's start, but its crew, Thucydides says, made "no haste upon so horrid an errand". The envoys of Mytilene supplied the second with wine and barley and promised a reward if it arrived in time; the rowers ate barley-cakes kneaded with oil and wine at the oar and slept in turns. Paches had just read the first decree and was preparing to carry it out when the second ship came in.

The leaders Paches had sent to Athens, "rather more than a thousand", were put to death on Cleon's motion. Mytilene's walls were pulled down and its fleet taken; most of the land of Lesbos was divided into lots for Athenian holders, who drew a rent from the Lesbians who went on farming it.

### What the debate settled, and what it never asked

Each speech contains an answer to the question who should rule, but it is an answer about Athens governing itself. Cleon wants settled laws and plain men, distrustful of clever speakers. Diodotus wants an assembly that hears advice calmly and shares the responsibility for what it decides. Neither asks whether the many of Athens should be deciding the fate of the many of Mytilene.

## Melos: justice only between equals in power

At Mytilene the speaker who wanted mercy set justice aside on the ground that an assembly is not a court. Eleven years later Athenian envoys set it aside as a matter of principle, to the face of the people they were about to conquer. What do they say when there is nothing left to pretend?

### A conference before the few

Melos was a small island in the southern Aegean, settled by colonists from Sparta. It had refused to join the Athenian alliance; it "at first remained neutral and took no part in the struggle", and became openly hostile after the Athenians raided its land in 426. In 416 an Athenian force of thirty ships and allied contingents landed and, before doing any damage, sent envoys.

Two details come first. The Melians did not let the envoys address their people; they heard them before "the magistrates and the few". The Athenians remark at once that they know why: so that "the multitude" cannot be won over by "seductive arguments". The second detail is the format. There are no set speeches. The two sides talk in short turns, question and answer, the only passage of the history written like a play. The Melians open by saying the outcome is fixed: the Athenians have come "to be judges in your own cause", so the result will be war if the Melians are in the right, and slavery if they give way.

### The thesis

The Athenians then state the ground rules.

<Quote source="Thucydides V.89, trans. R. Crawley">
For ourselves, we shall not trouble you with specious pretences—either of how we have a right to our empire because we overthrew the Mede, or are now attacking you because of wrong that you have done us—and make a long speech which would not be believed; and in return we hope that you, instead of thinking to influence us by saying that you did not join the Lacedaemonians, although their colonists, or that you have done us no wrong, will aim at what is feasible, holding in view the real sentiments of us both; since you know as well as we do that right, as the world goes, is only in question between equals in power, while the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.
</Quote>

"The Mede" is the Persians. The Athenians decline the two justifications an empire usually gives, that it earned its rule by a great service (defeating Persia) and that its victim deserved what came. They ask the Melians to decline theirs in turn, neutrality and innocence. What is left is the sentence the passage is known for. Call it the *Athenian thesis*: justice is a question that arises only between parties equal in power; where power is unequal, the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.

### What the thesis does not say

The sentence is usually remembered as "might makes right", and that reading gets it wrong in a way that matters.

"Might makes right" says that whatever the strong do is just: power confers the title. The envoys say something else. They do not call the conquest of Melos just; they have just refused to offer any justification. They say that the question of justice does not arise between a strong city and a weak one. Right exists, "between equals in power". Between unequals the word has nothing to apply to.

Why should justice need equal power? The earlier lesson gave the reason, in Solon's reply to Anacharsis: <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-objection-from-the-spiders-web">men keep agreements when neither side gains by breaking them</Ref>. Breaking an agreement gains nothing when the other side can retaliate and take back more than the breach won, so an agreement holds where each party can make the other pay for breaking it. Between equals that condition holds, so terms are made and kept and the language of right is in use. Between Athens and Melos it fails; Melos can make Athens pay nothing, so there is nothing to keep the terms. The thesis is Solon's condition for law, run between a strong and a weak city.

The envoys' own closing advice shows the difference. "Those who do not yield to their equals, who keep terms with their superiors, and are moderate towards their inferiors, on the whole succeed best." Moderation towards inferiors is recommended here as good policy, a way to succeed, and not as something the inferiors are owed. "Might makes right" would call the strong man's every act just; the Athenian thesis calls it neither just nor unjust, and judges it by whether it works.

### The Melians' replies

The Melians accept the ground rules under protest, "since you enjoin us to let right alone and talk only of interest", and try to beat the Athenians on interest. They make five replies, and three of them carry their case. The exchange runs in the order below.

**Fairness as everyone's protection.** The Melians' first argument is from Athens's own interest. Do not destroy "what is our common protection, the privilege of being allowed in danger to invoke what is fair and right", because Athens may one day need it: "your fall would be a signal for the heaviest vengeance". At full strength this is a serious point. Power changes hands, and a city that abolishes the appeal to fairness abolishes it for its own bad day too. The Athenians answer that they do not fear a rival empire such as Sparta if they fall; what they fear is their own subjects rising. Meanwhile both sides gain by Melos's submission: "you would have the advantage of submitting before suffering the worst, and we should gain by not destroying you".

**Neutrality.** Why not leave Melos neutral, a friend of neither side? Because "your friendship will be an argument to our subjects of our weakness, and your enmity of our power". An empire of islands rests on its reputation; an island left free advertises that Athens cannot or dare not take it. The Melians press the point from the other side: attack a neutral, and every neutral city will expect to be next and turn against Athens. The Athenians answer that the cities of the mainland, secure in their freedom, give them little alarm; islanders and restless subjects are the danger.

**Honour and hope.** The Melians say it would be "great baseness and cowardice" not to resist, and that the fortune of war is sometimes kinder than numbers suggest. The Athenians answer that this is not a contest between equals with honour as the prize, but a question of survival. Hope is "danger's comforter", affordable to those who can lose without being ruined; the weak who stake everything on it, and then turn to "prophecies and oracles", are destroyed by it.

### Gods and Spartans

The last two replies put the Melians' trust outside the conference room. The gods, they say, will grant them fortune as good as Athens's, "since we are just men fighting against unjust"; and Sparta, their mother city, is "bound, if only for very shame, to come to the aid of their kindred".

The answer about the gods turns the thesis from a remark about the present situation into a law of nature:

<Quote source="Thucydides V.105, trans. R. Crawley">
Of the gods we believe, and of men we know, that by a necessary law of their nature they rule wherever they can. And it is not as if we were the first to make this law, or to act upon it when made: we found it existing before us, and shall leave it to exist for ever after us; all we do is to make use of it, knowing that you and everybody else, having the same power as we have, would do the same as we do.
</Quote>

Two things in this passage carry the argument. The law covers gods as well as men, so the gods cannot be expected to side with the weak against it. And the Athenians claim no exemption: the Melians, with the same power, would do the same.

The answer about Sparta applies the thesis to Athens's enemy. The Spartans are "the worthiest men alive" at home, but in their dealings with others "of all the men we know they are most conspicuous in considering what is agreeable honourable, and what is expedient just". An ally comes to the rescue when it has "a decided superiority of power for action", not out of goodwill; Sparta is cautious, and Athens holds the sea.

Without looking back at the figure or the text, write the Athenian argument in three sentences of your own. A model answer. The Athenians argue that justice is a question only between parties equal in power; between a strong and a weak city the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must; and this is a law of nature for gods and men alike, which Athens did not invent and which Melos would follow in its place. The Melians reply first that fairness protects everyone, Athens included when its turn comes; the Athenians answer that they fear their own subjects more than any rival, and that submission profits both sides now. They reply second that the gods favour the just; the Athenians answer that the gods too rule wherever they can. They reply third that Sparta will come to help its kin; the Athenians answer that Spartans call the expedient just, will not risk a crossing to an island, and that Athens commands the sea.

### The end of Melos

The Athenians withdrew to let the Melians decide. The Melians refused to "deprive of freedom a city that has been inhabited these seven hundred years", trusting the gods and the Spartans. The envoys' parting words were a prediction: having staked most on Sparta, their fortune and their hopes, the Melians "will be most completely deceived".

No Spartan force came. The siege lasted into the winter; twice the Melians sallied out by night and took part of the Athenian siege lines. Then, Thucydides writes in one sentence, "some treachery taking place inside, the Melians surrendered at discretion to the Athenians, who put to death all the grown men whom they took, and sold the women and children for slaves, and subsequently sent out five hundred colonists and inhabited the place themselves."

## One principle, two domains

The three speeches now sit side by side: a city of free equals, an assembly deciding the fate of a subject city on grounds of interest, envoys stating that justice holds only among equals in power. Are they contradictory voices, the city's ideals and its practice at odds? Two facts suggest they are not.

### The thesis was old

The first fact is that the Melian thesis was not new in 416, or the product of a war gone bad. Before the war began, Athenian envoys at Sparta, defending the empire against the charges of Sparta's allies, said the same thing:

<Quote source="Thucydides I.76, trans. R. Crawley">
It follows that it was not a very wonderful action, or contrary to the common practice of mankind, if we did accept an empire that was offered to us, and refused to give it up under the pressure of three of the strongest motives, fear, honour, and interest. And it was not we who set the example, for it has always been law that the weaker should be subject to the stronger.
</Quote>

They added that justice is "a consideration which no one ever yet brought forward to hinder his ambition when he had a chance of gaining anything by might", and that "praise is due to all who … respect justice more than their position compels them to do". In 430, when the plague, an epidemic in the crowded city, and the Spartan army's raids on the farmland round it had turned the Athenians against the war, Pericles told them plainly what they held: "what you hold is, to speak somewhat plainly, a tyranny; to take it perhaps was wrong, but to let it go is unsafe". Cleon called the empire "a despotism" at Mytilene. At no point did the city claim that its empire rested on the equality of its assembly. Melos stated the old position more bluntly, to the people it was being used against.

### Equality among those who can answer back

The second fact is in the Funeral Oration itself. Its equality is an equality among Athenian citizens, and the Melian thesis says that justice holds among equals. These are the same principle seen from inside and from outside.

The earlier lesson ended on the observation that the polis's equality was always an equality among some, and that <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="equality-among-whom">"equal among whom?" was built into it from the start</Ref>. Inside the wall, citizens could hold one another to account: in the assembly, in the courts, in the vote at the end of each official's year. Among them, speech, law and shares were the rule. Outside the wall, Athens faced cities that could not hold it to account, and there, on its own speakers' account, the rule was power.

Mytilene is the hinge. There the inside decided the outside: an assembly of equals, arguing as equals do, settled the fate of a city that had no vote. The arguments it heard were both arguments of rulers about subjects. That is why the merciful speaker argued from interest. Once it was agreed that Mytilene was not an equal, interest was the only ground on which an Athenian could ask for mercy and be heard.

## The objection from compulsion

From the speech at Sparta before the war to the conference at Melos, Athens's own speakers stated the thesis and none rejected it. Can it be held consistently? The strongest objection turns the thesis's own premise against it.

The thesis says the strong rule wherever they can "by a necessary law of their nature". The Athenians at Sparta said they were compelled to take and keep their empire by fear, honour and interest. Fear may compel. But if honour and interest also compel, then every act done for gain or for glory is done under compulsion, and nothing is ever done voluntarily. Nobody can then be blamed for anything. The Mytilenians cannot be blamed for revolting, though Cleon demanded their deaths because their offence was "not involuntary, but of malice and deliberate". The Melians cannot be blamed for resisting. Diodotus came close to saying so: "it is impossible to prevent … human nature doing what it has once set its mind upon". Held consistently, the thesis dissolves the charges of guilt that the empire was enforced with.

### The Athenian reply

The Athenians could answer that they never needed blame. They needed only to predict, and at Melos their predictions came true: Sparta did not come, and hope failed those who trusted it. Their claim to moderation does not need blame either. If the strong are not obliged to be just, a strong city that restrains itself, that "respect[s] justice more than [its] position compels", deserves credit for doing more than it had to, and the Athenians claimed that credit.

The objection does not refute the thesis as a description of how powerful cities behave; the narrative bears it out more often than not. What it shows is narrower. The thesis cannot be held together with the language of guilt and punishment that Athens used whenever it put down a revolt. Whether the thesis is true between cities is a separate question, and it can be tested: on what happened when Athens itself became the weak party. Whether it stops at the city wall no war between cities can test.

## Does the arrangement judge Athens?

Thucydides almost never comments in his own voice on the speeches, so a verdict on Athens, if the history gives one, has to lie in the narrative: in what he sets next to what. Twice the placement is hard to miss.

### Two juxtapositions

The Funeral Oration is followed at once by the plague, the epidemic of 430 that struck Athens when the country people of Attica had crowded inside the walls to escape the Spartan army, and that returned in later years. Thucydides describes the symptoms and then the collapse of restraint. Men "coolly ventured on what they had formerly done in a corner"; they spent quickly because no one expected to live; and

<Quote source="Thucydides II.53, trans. R. Crawley">
Fear of gods or law of man there was none to restrain them.
</Quote>

Set this against clause 6 of the constitution paragraph. There, ease in private life did not make the Athenians lawless, because fear taught them "to obey the magistrates and the laws". The translation says "fear" in both passages; the Greek uses two near-synonyms, the first nearer to awe, the second nearer to dread. What the two passages share is a pairing, not a word: in each, fear is what holds men to the law. In the oration it holds them; a few pages later, under the plague, neither fear of gods nor law of man holds anyone.

The Melian dialogue closes Book V. Book VI opens with the Sicilian expedition (415–413), Athens's attempt to conquer Syracuse, the chief Greek city of Sicily, the large island south of Italy. The assembly voted for it with enthusiasm: "All alike fell in love with the enterprise", and "the few that liked it not, feared to appear unpatriotic by holding up their hands against it, and so kept quiet". It ended with the destruction of the whole force, fleet and army:

<Quote source="Thucydides VII.87, trans. R. Crawley">
This was the greatest Hellenic achievement of any in this war, or, in my opinion, in Hellenic history; at once most glorious to the victors, and most calamitous to the conquered. They were beaten at all points and altogether; all that they suffered was great; they were destroyed, as the saying is, with a total destruction, their fleet, their army, everything was destroyed, and few out of many returned home.
</Quote>

### The order as verdict

The first reading takes the placement as a judgment. The city that boasted of having left monuments everywhere "for evil or for good" loses, in a season, the respect for law it had just claimed as its distinction. The city that told the Melians the strong do what they can goes on, the next year, to attempt more than it can and is destroyed. On this reading the history punishes Athens for its pride without ever saying so, and the Melians' warning, "your fall would be a signal for the heaviest vengeance", comes true.

### The historian's own causes

The second reading notes that Thucydides himself gives other causes, and refuses the moral. Of the plague he says that "it first began, it is said, in the parts of Ethiopia above Egypt" and passed through Egypt and Persia's empire before reaching Athens: a disease with a course, not a punishment aimed at one city. Of Sicily he says that the expedition "failed not so much through a miscalculation of the power of those against whom it was sent", as through the failure of the leaders at home, who, "choosing rather to occupy themselves with private cabals for the leadership of the commons", did not support the army they had sent. And Athens, after Sicily, fought on for nine more years. A historian who thought the city doomed by its injustice would not have stressed that it might have won.

Both readings rest on the text. The first rests on what Thucydides placed; the second on what he said. If the work judges Athens, then, it judges by the course of events. No speaker in the history answers the envoys at Melos with an argument that defeats theirs; the test the thesis does meet comes from events after the history breaks off.

## Who should rule, according to each speaker

The speeches argue about how to treat other cities, but each speaker also shows, in passing, whom he would trust with the decision: the many, settled laws, a calm assembly, the stronger, the few. The historian twice says in his own voice which Athens he thought best governed.

### Seven answers

| Speaker | Setting | Who should rule | On what ground |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pericles | Funeral Oration, 431/0 | The many, with office by capacity, not birth or wealth | Equal law, judgment by all, love of the city |
| Cleon | Mytilene debate, 427 | Settled laws and ordinary men, not clever speakers | Clever men overrule the laws and ruin the city |
| Diodotus | Mytilene debate, 427 | An assembly that deliberates calmly and shares responsibility with its advisers | Only speech can deal with an uncertain future |
| The Athenian envoys | Melos, 416 | Between cities: the stronger | A law of nature for gods and men |
| The Melian magistrates | Melos, 416 | The few: they kept the envoys from the people | Shown by their act, not argued |
| Thucydides | On Pericles (II.65) | A democracy in name, led by its first citizen | Pericles led the many instead of being led by them |
| Thucydides | On the regime of 411 (VIII.97) | A mixed body of "the high and the low" | The best government Athens had in his time |

### The historian's own two judgments

After Pericles's death, Thucydides writes that Pericles, by his ability and known integrity, could "lead them instead of being led by them", and contradict the assembly to its face; "in short, what was nominally a democracy became in his hands government by the first citizen". His successors, "more on a level with one another, and each grasping at supremacy, … ended by committing even the conduct of state affairs to the whims of the multitude", and that produced "a host of blunders, and amongst them the Sicilian expedition".

The second judgment concerns 411, two years after the Sicilian disaster, when the assembly handed the government to a body called the Five Thousand, open to every citizen who could furnish his own armour as a hoplite, and abolished pay for public office. That pay, a daily allowance for serving on a council or a jury, was what let a poor citizen leave his work to hold office; without it, office fell to men who could afford to serve unpaid. Of this regime Thucydides says the Athenians "appear to have enjoyed the best government that they ever did, at least in my time. For the fusion of the high and the low was effected with judgment."

Neither judgment is a theory. Both point the same way: the best Athens he saw was neither the many ruling directly nor the few ruling alone, but the many led by one man of judgment, or a body in which the few and the many were mixed. Both answers return in the following lessons: rule by the one who knows is Plato's, and a mixture of the few and the many is Aristotle's.

## The thesis describes the war and fails as a rule: the record of 404

The objection from compulsion showed that the thesis cannot sit beside the language of guilt, and the arrangement showed that the historian does not argue against it. Neither settles whether it is true. Its sentence at Melos makes two claims, and the record of the war tests them separately.

### Two claims in one sentence

The first claim is a description: a city much stronger than another does with it what it can. It is tested by what strong cities did, on both sides of the war. The second is a rule: between unequal powers the appeal "to what is fair and right" is worth nothing, so fairness protects no one who is weak. It is tested by one kind of case, a city at the mercy of another whose appeal to fairness counted for something.

The two claims can come apart. Strong cities may nearly always do what they can, and the appeal may still count once; once is enough against a rule that says it never counts.

### The description holds on both sides

Athens acted as the thesis says, before Melos as well as there. Scione, a town on the northern coast of the Aegean, went over to Sparta in 423. When Athens retook it in 421 it "put the adult males to death, and, making slaves of the women and children, gave the land" to others. Melos followed five years later.

Sparta acted the same way, and its clearest case is one where an appeal to past service was made and refused. Plataea, the small town beside the battlefield of 479 (<Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-hoplite-phalanx">the battle that ended the Persian invasion</Ref>), was Athens's ally. Sparta and its ally Thebes, the largest city of Boeotia, the region on Athens's northern border, besieged it from 429 and took it in 427, the year of the Mytilene debate. The Plataeans pleaded that they alone of their region had fought the Persians, beside the Spartans and on their own soil. The Spartan judges put one question to each prisoner, whether he had done Sparta and its allies "any service in the war" then being fought, and killed every man: more than two hundred Plataeans and twenty-five Athenians. Thucydides gives the reason:

<Quote source="Thucydides III.68, trans. R. Crawley">
The adverse attitude of the Lacedaemonians in the whole Plataean affair was mainly adopted to please the Thebans, who were thought to be useful in the war at that moment raging.
</Quote>

And Sparta did not come for Melos, its own colony, as the envoys had predicted. On both sides a strong city did with a weak one what it could, and neither kinship nor past service stopped it. The envoys' claim that "you and everybody else, having the same power as we have, would do the same" is borne out by Athens's enemy. As a description of the war, the thesis holds.

### When Athens was the weak party

The rule needs a different case: a strong city brought to the mercy of others. Thucydides' history breaks off in 411, and the war's end is recorded in the history that continues it, written by Xenophon, an Athenian of the next generation. In 405 a Spartan fleet under Lysander destroyed the Athenian fleet at Aegospotami, a beach on the Hellespont, the strait between Europe and Asia Minor through which Athens's grain ships came. The news reached Athens at night, and no one slept. The Athenians mourned "not for the lost alone, but far more for their own selves, thinking that they would suffer such treatment as they had visited upon the Melians", and upon Scione and the other cities they had destroyed.

Lysander, sailing to blockade Athens, restored the surviving Melians to their island. Athens, starving, sent envoys to Sparta in 404. At the assembly of Sparta's allies, Corinth, the trading city on the isthmus that joins the Peloponnese to the rest of Greece, and Thebes demanded that Athens be destroyed: the treatment of Melos, applied to the city that had dealt it. Sparta refused.

<Quote source="Xenophon, Hellenica II.2.20, trans. C. L. Brownson">
The Lacedaemonians, however, said that they would not enslave a Greek city which had done great service amid the greatest perils that had befallen Greece.
</Quote>

The "greatest perils" were the Persian invasions. Athens lost the long walls that joined the city to its harbour and all but twelve of its ships, and had to follow Sparta "both by land and by sea wherever they should lead the way"; it kept its people and its city.

Set this beside the conference at Melos. The Melians had argued that fairness is "our common protection", which Athens might one day need, because "your fall would be a signal for the heaviest vengeance". The Athenians had answered that they did not fear a rival such as Sparta if they fell. In 405 they feared exactly that. In 404 the vengeance their enemies demanded was refused, on the ground of what Athens had once done for Greece, for a city that could no longer make anyone pay.

<KeyFact>
The service the envoys at Melos declined to plead, Athens's part in defeating the Persians, is the ground on which Sparta refused to destroy Athens in 404. The appeal they had called a "specious pretence" was the one their city was spared on.
</KeyFact>

### The strongest objection: interest spared Athens

At full strength, the objection sets Plataea beside 404. Each time Sparta judged a city at its mercy on the same ground, service against the Persians: in 427 the Plataeans pleaded it, and in 404 Sparta stated it itself. Each time Thebes wanted the city destroyed. In 427 Thebes was an ally Sparta needed in a war still being fought, and Sparta killed the Plataeans to please it. In 404 the war was won, and a destroyed Athens would have left Thebes and Corinth, Sparta's two strongest allies, as the strongest powers in Greece beside Sparta, with no counterweight to either. The terms fit the same calculation: Athens survived disarmed and bound to follow Sparta, a counterweight that Sparta controlled. Within a decade Sparta was at war with both Thebes and Corinth. On this reading the ground of past service was the decent form of a calculation, and the thesis survives intact: interest killed the Plataeans, and interest spared the Athenians.

### Why the verdict stands

The objection explains Sparta's choice. It does not show that the appeal counted for nothing, and nothing is what the thesis claimed. Two things in the record tell against it.

**The ground Sparta gave.** The decision was announced to an assembly of allies who wanted the opposite. A ground offered to such an assembly has to be one its hearers can be expected to accept. The envoys at Melos had dismissed appeals to past service as pretences that "would not be believed"; in 404 one was given, to Sparta's two strongest allies, as the reason for refusing them.

**The Melians restored.** Sparta had entered the war proclaiming itself the liberator of Greece, and returning the Melians to their island served that claim. But a display of that kind works only on an audience that counts what Athens did at Melos as a wrong to be undone, so even read as display it shows the appeal to fairness had force with the onlookers.

The Athenians' fear the night the news came is not a third. They expected the rule they had applied to Melos to be applied to them, which is what the thesis predicts for a fallen city. What the fear refutes is the envoys' answer to the Melians' first reply, that Athens did not fear a rival if it fell: the vengeance the Melians had foretold was expected by Athens and demanded by Corinth and Thebes. Only its refusal tells against the thesis, and the two reasons above are about that refusal.

### The grade

The verdict is split. As a description of how cities at war treated those at their mercy, the thesis is upheld on both sides. As a rule that fairness protects no one, it fails: when Athens was the weak party, the appeal its envoys had called worthless counted for something. That second finding is graded *on balance*, not *strong*, because in 404 the appeal and Sparta's interest pointed the same way, so the record cannot show that the appeal would have prevailed against interest; at Plataea, where the two pointed opposite ways, interest won. The verdict would be overturned by a contemporary account of the debate at Sparta that gave the counterweight as the reason and treated the appeal to service as a formula with no weight.

<Conclusion>
You can now set out the three positions and how one city held them. The Funeral Oration describes Athens as rule by the many with office by capacity, private freedom with public obedience: citizens obey from awe of the law and die for the city from love of it, not from Spartan drill. In the Mytilene debate the harsh speaker, Cleon, argued from justice and deterrence, and the merciful one, Diodotus, from interest alone, because once Mytilene was agreed to be a subject, interest was the only ground on which mercy could be asked; the vote was almost equal, and more than a thousand men died even so. At Melos the envoys argued that justice arises only between equals in power, which is not "might makes right" but Solon's condition for agreements run between a strong and a weak city; you can state the Melians' three main replies (fairness, the gods, Sparta) and the answer to each. The three speeches are one principle in two domains: equality, speech and law among those who can hold one another to account, power towards those who cannot. The historian's arrangement can be read as a verdict on Athens, but his own explanations do not support that reading; the thesis is judged instead on the record. As a description of how cities at war treated those at their mercy it held on both sides, at Scione and Melos and at Plataea. As a claim that fairness protects no one it failed in 404: Corinth and Thebes demanded the treatment Athens had given Melos, and Sparta refused its two strongest allies on the ground that Athens had "done great service", while Lysander restored the Melians. The Athenians' fear of that treatment refuted only the envoys' answer that they did not fear a rival's vengeance. The grade is on balance, because Sparta's restraint also served its interest.

What the envoys claimed for relations between cities, where there is no common law, leaves a harder question about the inside. If justice exists only where each party can make the other pay, then the law "in the middle" of a city may itself be only the interest of whoever is strongest there. <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring">The next lesson</Ref> takes that up, in an Athenian house during these same war years, where a teacher of rhetoric says it outright to Socrates.
</Conclusion>

The correct account: the envoys argued that the question of justice arises only between equals in power, and that between unequals the strong do what they can.

The step that fails is "makes right". "Might makes right" says that the strong man's power gives him a just title, so that his acts are just. The envoys say three different things. First, in V.89 they refuse to claim any right at all: they set aside "specious pretences" about having earned the empire or having been wronged, and say that "right, as the world goes, is only in question between equals in power". They deny that the question arises between Athens and Melos; they do not answer it in their own favour. Second, in V.105 they ground their conduct in necessity, not justice: gods and men "by a necessary law of their nature … rule wherever they can". Third, their closing advice, to be "moderate towards their inferiors", is offered as a maxim of success, not as a duty owed to the inferior.

The claim that survives: at Melos the Athenians argued that justice presupposes rough equality of power, as agreements hold only when each party can make the other pay for breaking them; that between unequal powers there is no justice to appeal to; and that the strong ruling where they can is a law of nature that the weak, in their place, would follow too.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
At Sparta before the war the Athenians said they had been compelled to take and keep their empire "under the pressure of three of the strongest motives, fear, honour, and interest". At Mytilene Diodotus said "it is impossible to prevent … human nature doing what it has once set its mind upon". Start from these two statements and work out what follows for Cleon's charge that Mytilene's revolt was "deliberate and wanton aggression" deserving death. Then say what the Athenians would have to add to keep both the thesis and the right to punish.

<Solution>
Step 1. If fear, honour and interest compel, then a city that revolts out of fear of Athens, or out of the hope of honour or gain, acts under compulsion.

Step 2. Mytilene revolted because it saw a chance of freedom and Spartan help. That is interest and honour, so on the Athenians' own principle the revolt was compelled.

Step 3. Diodotus adds that no penalty can stop human nature from doing what it has set its mind on. So the revolt was neither voluntary in the sense the thesis allows nor preventable.

Step 4. Cleon's charge needs the opposite. He says the offence "was not involuntary, but of malice and deliberate; and mercy is only for unwilling offenders". If the thesis is true, there are no willing offenders in his sense, and his ground for punishment is gone.

What the Athenians would need to add: either (a) a theory of punishment without blame, that killing rebels is a deterrent, a means to the empire's safety; but Diodotus has just argued that death does not deter, so this route needs an answer to him; or (b) a standard of justice that holds between ruler and subject, under which revolt is a wrong. The thesis rejects (b), since it says right arises only between equals in power. So the thesis can keep punishment only as policy, judged by its effects, which is exactly the ground on which Diodotus won.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Set the powers equal. Before the war, Athens and Sparta were roughly matched: one the strongest power at sea, the other on land. What does the Athenian thesis predict about how two such cities will deal with each other? Check the prediction against what the Athenian envoys at Sparta actually did in their speech before the war (they argued their case, claimed to rule with moderation, and urged the Spartans to settle differences by arbitration rather than break the truce, the peace treaty then in force between the two). What does this boundary case show about where the thesis draws its line?

<Solution>
Prediction: between equals in power, "right … is in question". The thesis predicts that two matched cities will use the language of justice with each other: make agreements, argue about whether they have been kept, offer arbitration, because each can make the other pay for breaking terms.

Check: that is what the Athenians did at Sparta. They did not tell the Spartans that the strong do what they can; they argued that their empire was neither unusual nor unreasonable, claimed credit for respecting justice "more than their position compels them", and pressed for arbitration under the existing truce. The thirty years' truce, whose breach began the war, was itself a treaty between equals.

What the probe shows: the thesis is not "there is no justice". It says justice exists exactly where each side can hold the other to account, which is Solon's condition for agreements being kept, from <Ref lesson="greek-miracle" id="the-objection-from-the-spiders-web">the earlier lesson</Ref>. The line falls at equality of power, not between cities and individuals or between Greeks and others. Melos falls on the far side of it; Sparta on the near side. The boundary case also shows the thesis's limit as a prediction: equality kept the language of justice in use, but it did not keep the peace, since the war came anyway.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Read two statements side by side. Pericles, in the Funeral Oration: "we do not feel called upon to be angry with our neighbour for doing what he likes". Cleon, at Mytilene: "Fears or plots being unknown to you in your daily relations with each other, you feel just the same with regard to your allies". Where do they agree about the Athenians, where do they disagree, and what judgment about the relation between democracy and empire does the comparison support? Write it as a short argued paragraph.

<Solution>
Agreement: both describe the same trait. Athenians live among one another without suspicion; they do not police their neighbours' private lives (Pericles) and do not fear plots from one another (Cleon).

Disagreement: Pericles presents the trait as the city's distinction, safe because it is paired with respect for magistrates and laws. Cleon presents it as a danger once it leaves the city: the same trust extended to subjects makes Athens lenient where it should be feared, and so "a democracy is incapable of empire".

Judgment, one defensible version: the comparison shows that the habits of equals are suited to relations among equals. Pericles describes them inside the wall and has no need to ask whether they fit outside it. Cleon asks, and answers that they do not: subjects are not neighbours, and rule over them rests on "superiority given you by your own strength and not their loyalty". Both speakers, then, agree with the thesis later stated at Melos that relations with subjects are governed by power rather than by the trust that holds among citizens; they disagree only about whether the assembly can keep the two domains apart. The Mytilene debate is the test case, and its almost equal vote suggests that the assembly found it hard.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The verdict above holds, on balance, that the Athenian thesis fails as a claim that fairness protects no one, on the record of 404. Attack it. Make the strongest case that Sparta's restraint in 404 was interest alone, using the trial of the Plataeans in 427, the peace terms imposed on Athens, and Sparta's claim since 431 to be the liberator of Greece. Then say which of the two reasons given for keeping the verdict your case answers and which it leaves standing, and what evidence would be needed to overturn the verdict.

<Solution>
The case for interest alone has three parts.

1. **A controlled comparison.** Plataea in 427 and Athens in 404 are as close to the same trial as history offers: the same judge (Sparta), the same ground (service against the Persians, pleaded by the Plataeans, who had fought on the very field of 479, and stated by Sparta itself in 404), the same demand from Thebes that the city be destroyed. The outcome is opposite. What changed is Thebes's value to Sparta: in 427 an ally needed in a running war, in 404 a large neighbour with the war won. The historian himself says Sparta acted at Plataea "to please the Thebans, who were thought to be useful". When one factor changes and the outcome changes with it while the ground stays fixed, the ground is not what decided.
2. **The terms.** Athens was disarmed, its long walls and fleet taken, and bound to "follow the Lacedaemonians both by land and by sea". That is a city kept as an instrument, not spared as a benefactor; gratitude for a service would not need the last clause. Sparta's war with Thebes and Corinth within a decade shows the rivalry the counterweight was kept for.
3. **The liberator's role.** Restoring the Melians and refusing to enslave a Greek city fit the claim Sparta had made since 431. A propaganda claim is an instrument of interest; keeping it costs little and buys allies.

What it answers and what it leaves standing. It answers most of the second reason: restoring the Melians can be read as a display. It does not answer all of it, since a display works only on onlookers who count Melos a wrong, so the appeal to fairness still had currency with them. It weakens the first reason: Sparta led the alliance and could refuse Thebes and Corinth by its own power, so the stated ground may have been a courtesy rather than what persuaded them. The Athenians' fear in 405 is no help to either side: it shows they expected the thesis to be applied to them, which the thesis predicts; what it refutes is only the envoys' claim not to fear a rival's vengeance.

What would overturn the verdict: evidence that the appeal had no weight. A contemporary account of the debate at Sparta that gives the counterweight as the reason and treats the service formula as cover would do it. So would a pattern: more cases like Plataea, where the appeal and Sparta's interest pointed opposite ways and interest won, with none where the appeal won. A single case where the appeal won against interest would do the opposite and raise the verdict from on balance to strong.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The Athenian envoys at Melos say justice is in question only between equals in power. Apply the thesis inside a single city. In democratic Athens the many outnumber the rich and decide in the assembly; in an oligarchy such as Mytilene before its revolt, the rich hold the offices and the arms. On the envoys' premise, what would "justice" in each city's laws turn out to be? Then say what the envoys themselves did not claim that this application claims.

<Solution>
Inside each city there are parties of unequal power: in Athens the many are stronger in the assembly, in an oligarchy the few are stronger in office and arms. If justice arises only between equals in power, the stronger party in each city is not bound by justice towards the weaker. It makes the laws to suit its interest, and the weaker obey because they must. "Justice" in each city's laws would then be the interest of whichever part is stronger there, written down as law and called just: in Athens the interest of the many, in an oligarchy the interest of the few.

What the envoys did not claim: they spoke of relations between cities, where there is no common law or court. Inside Athens they would have accepted the Funeral Oration's equality among citizens, which is an equality of men who can hold one another to account. The application above denies that even that equality is anything but power: the law in the middle becomes the stronger party's interest. That step, from the city wall to the city's own law, is the thesis a teacher of rhetoric puts to Socrates in <Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring" />, where justice is defined as the interest of the stronger in every regime.
</Solution>

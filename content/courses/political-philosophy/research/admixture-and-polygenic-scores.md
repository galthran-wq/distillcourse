# Research brief: admixture-and-polygenic-scores (Direct genetic tests: admixture studies and polygenic scores)

status: planned
module: heredity-and-civilization
type: core
goal: Evaluate the two designs that try to test a genetic contribution to the gap directly — regression on genomic ancestry and comparison of polygenic scores — stating what each has found, what confounds it, and what study would settle the question

Register rule (binding on the lesson): The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.

Group labels: the studies use self-identified race/ethnicity ("SIRE": Black or African-American,
White or European-American, Hispanic, Asian, biracial) and, separately, genomic ancestry estimated
against reference panels ("European", "African", "Amerindian", "East Asian" ancestry shares). The
lesson says once that both sets of categories are the authors', that the course adds none, and that
the two are different things: a self-identified group is a social category; an ancestry share is a
measured fraction of a person's genome. The lesson's whole argument depends on keeping them apart.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `adoption-studies-across-groups`: the placement confound — whatever travels
  with ancestry (age at placement, birth mother's circumstances, social classification) mixes
  rearing with parentage — and the hand-off that the question moved to measuring ancestry in the DNA
  of people raised in ordinary families; the 1976 one-vs-two-Black-parent comparison as a crude
  two-point ancestry test. From `heritability-within-and-between-groups`: SNP heritability (.15–.25
  for cognition), within-sibship shrinkage (cognition 22% in one large study, about none in another;
  education about half), genetic nurture, current scores predicting 5–10% of cognitive variance in
  European-ancestry samples, the within/between fallacy, the default hypothesis, and the 2024 proof
  that aggregate within-group data cannot partition a between-group difference (stated there in
  general; its admixture case is taught here). From `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`: the residual
  gap after SES and the ambiguity of controlling SES. From `what-intelligence-tests-measure`:
  measurement invariance (the admixture samples' tests are invariant across groups). From
  `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`: Spearman's hypothesis and Rushton and Jensen's admixture
  category, with their 2005 forecast that individual DNA admixture would settle the issue.
- **Contributes:** The one move: the two "direct" designs each measure something real — a small
  positive slope of scores on European ancestry within self-identified Black samples; a large gap in
  education-linked polygenic scores between ancestries — and neither measurement, as designed so far,
  can say how much of what it found is genetic, because the slope sums genetic and environmental effects
  of ancestry, and the scores lose most of their accuracy outside the ancestry they were built in and
  carry family and population effects inside it. Concepts introduced: `admixture-regression`,
  `polygenic-index`, `pgs-portability`. The lesson states the module's "what would settle it": a
  well-powered sibling-difference admixture design with observed colour and local ancestry, and
  within-family genetic studies of cognition in African-ancestry samples — with the residue no design
  removes.
- **Hands off:** To `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`: this lesson decides that neither "genes cause the
  gap" nor "there is no genetic difference" has been shown by the direct designs (**strong**); the
  empirical question is therefore **open** in both directions, and the course can say what evidence would
  close it (the module verdict in `national-data-audited` recalls this lesson's design); the next question is what would
  follow politically if it closed either way. One sentence in the `<Conclusion>`; the FORWARD exercise
  reaches it. (The ancient-DNA selection result is mentioned here only for what it does to one
  assumption of a 2021 bound; `reich-ancient-dna-and-the-indo-europeans` owns ancient DNA.)
- **Spine step:** "Heredity as an explanation of the gaps between peoples, tested premise by premise — what the tests measure, what heritability can and cannot imply, the hereditarian case at full strength, then the US gap, adoption, admixture and polygenic scores on the step from a real gap to a genetic one — then what follows politically whatever the answer, what ancient DNA shows, the rival explanations from geography and institutions, and the key datasets audited"
- Not module-final: no APPLICATION spec. `national-data-audited` recalls this lesson's "what would
  settle it" in the module's closing status; the statement is made here once.

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** two opposite readings of the direct genetic designs: (i) admixture regressions and
  polygenic scores show that genes cause part of the gap; (ii) they show there is no genetic difference.
- **Verdict:** **neither is shown.** Grade: **strong**.
- **What decides it** (dossier §§4–5, 9): admixture slopes are small and positive (PNC: r = 0.086, under 1%
  of variance; lasker2019, kirkegaard2019), and the slope sums the genetic and the environmental effects of
  ancestry, colourism and family circumstances included (Schraiber & Edge preprint l.301–316). The one
  within-family estimate is positive and not significant (Fuerst et al. 2023, Table 6: 0.62 ± 0.55, interval
  −0.46 to 1.70). Polygenic scores lose most of their accuracy outside European ancestry, and mean
  differences between ancestries cannot at present be read as genetic potential. Bird's 2021 bound (at most
  4.7–8.5 points "in either direction") holds only under neutrality and stated assumptions; it is a bound, not
  a measured zero.
- **Strongest objection:** from the hereditarian side, every genomic design that has been run points the
  same way (positive slopes, a positive sibling estimate, a score gap in the predicted direction), and
  consistent direction across designs is itself evidence. From the other side, Bird's bound caps any genetic
  share at a few points, which is close enough to none. Neither overturns the verdict. Direction across
  designs that share the same confound (ancestry correlates with appearance and family circumstance in all
  of them) is one result repeated, not several; the sibling estimate's interval includes zero. The bound
  rests on neutrality, which the 2026 ancient-DNA result shows is a testable premise rather than a given,
  and on national-IQ inputs the module audits.
- **What would overturn it:** a well-powered sibling-difference admixture design with observed skin colour
  and local ancestry, or within-family genome-wide studies of cognition in large African-ancestry samples
  (section 10). A significant result there, in either direction, would decide what this lesson cannot.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): Americans who identify as Black carry a share of their DNA from
European ancestors, and the share differs a good deal from one person to the next; if part of the
gap were genetic, people with more European ancestry should score slightly higher on average; if it
were wholly environmental, they should not, unless the environment itself tracks ancestry, through
skin colour or family circumstances; since 2019 several studies have run this test on large samples
whose DNA was read, and others have compared average genetic scores built from variants linked to
schooling; the lesson reads what they found and asks what design would make a result decisive.

The problem developed (L10a): the adoption studies could not separate a child's parentage from its
history, because history travelled with ancestry (`<Ref>` `adoption-studies-across-groups`, one
clause on the placement confound). The opening's design replaces the adoption agency with nature:
within one self-identified group, raised by their own families, ancestry varies person to person.
The opening already names the catch ("unless the environment itself tracks ancestry"); the lesson's
job is to find out how far each design gets past it. Anchors (L11): the American population history
behind the variation (European ancestry in African Americans averaging about a fifth — one clause, no
more, with no dating the dossier does not support; the ancient-DNA lesson owns population history);
Philadelphia (the PNC sample's city, chosen by its authors partly to avoid geographic confounding,
lasker2019 l.155–160).

1. **What "ancestry share" is.** Problem: how can a person's European ancestry be a number? Answer in
   three steps: reference panels of people whose recent ancestors all lived in one continent; at
   hundreds of thousands of genetic positions, allele frequencies differ between panels; a program
   finds the mixture of panels that best explains a person's genotype — the global ancestry share.
   In the PNC's self-identified Black participants it averaged 18.7% European with an SD of 11.7
   points (lasker2019 l.1093); among self-identified White participants 98.6%. Two things a newcomer
   must hold: self-identified race and ancestry share are different variables (a biracial person and a
   person who identifies as Black may have the same share); and the share is a fraction of the genome,
   not a count of "intelligence genes". KEEPER: about a fifth, varying by about twelve points.
2. **The first attempt: blood groups, and why a null was weak.** Problem: before DNA chips, the only
   measurable markers of ancestry were blood groups. The 1970s studies (40 and 44 adolescents; 144 twin
   pairs) found correlations near zero between European-typical markers and scores (dossier §4). Obvious
   reading: no ancestry effect. Why it fails: a handful of markers estimates a person's ancestry with
   large error, and measurement error pulls a correlation toward zero; and after generations of mixing,
   a particular blood-group gene says little about which other genes came from which continent — the
   1973 authors' own caution that it "may not … be a very strong test". The hereditarian side adds that
   the markers chosen differed little between continents; the 2019 PNC authors compute that the 1977
   correlation is about 58% of theirs, as attenuation would predict. Answer: a null from a noisy index is
   not a measured zero (dossier "must NOT": "older nulls were weak tests"). Figure 3. This section also
   anchors the 1996 task force's use of these studies as evidence against a genetic account (`<Ref>`
   back to the module's earlier mention if any; else one clause) — and shows why that use was weaker
   than it looked.
3. **Admixture regression: the slope and what it measures on its face.** Problem: with DNA chips,
   ancestry is measured precisely. Define `admixture-regression`: within one self-identified group,
   regress a test score on the ancestry share; the slope is the predicted difference in score between
   0% and 100% of the ancestry, in SD of the score. Worked example (figure 1, frames 1–2): the PNC's
   monoracial Black sample, n = 2,179, slope 0.78 SD per 0 → 100% European ancestry — about 0.08 SD
   (≈1.2 IQ points) per ten percentage points; correlation 0.086; adjusted R² 0.007 — under 1% of the
   variance. Corroboration: PING (n = 1,369; African ancestry −1.58 SD per 0 → 100% with self-identified
   group in the model), ABCD (Black subsample 1.06; dossier §4), NLSY97 (r = .137, parent-reported
   ancestry; [abstract]). Three samples, same sign. Why "under 1% of variance" and "a large effect"
   are both true: ancestry varies little inside the group (SD 11.7 points), so even a steep slope moves
   few people far. Set piece (figure 1, frame 3): the extrapolation — 0.78 × (98.6 − 18.7)% ≈ 0.62 SD,
   or, by the authors' route through r, 0.086 × 6.83 = 0.59 SD, against a gap of 0.98 SD: "consistent
   with 60%" of the gap; a separate formula gives "50–70%". This is the figure a 2024 critique reports as
   "over 60%" (the dossier's correction: it is the admixture extrapolation, not the polygenic-score
   figure). The reader must see the two assumptions the extrapolation adds — that the line runs straight
   far beyond the range of the data (most of the sample lies between 0 and 40%), and that the slope is
   genetic. Exercise 2 reproduces the number; exercise 3 derives r from the slope.
4. **The hereditarian reading, at full strength.** Problem: grant that one study proves little — what
   does the pattern across studies show? (a) The sign is the same in every genomic sample, with the
   self-identified group held constant, so the slope is not a by-product of comparing groups. (b) It
   survives parental SES, reduced by a third to a half (PING 36–48%; PNC 0.78 → 0.67; ABCD 1.06 →
   0.84), and controlling SES over-corrects under a genetic model, because parents' SES partly reflects
   their own genes (`<Ref>` `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment` for the SES ambiguity — the same
   ambiguity, now cutting the other way; both PING and PNC authors make the point). (c) It survives a
   colour covariate (0.78 → 0.83 with genotype-predicted skin colour, which itself added nothing). (d)
   The ancestry differences fall mostly on g and follow subtest g-loadings (`<Ref>`
   `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations` for Spearman's hypothesis), and the tests are invariant along the
   whole range of ancestry (`<Ref>` `what-intelligence-tests-measure`). (e) An education polygenic score
   statistically mediates part of the slope; colour does not. (f) The burden-of-proof argument: a
   correlation between genes and a trait is most simply explained by the genes; the classic
   counter-example — children with red hair systematically denied schooling — is a hypothesis, and "no
   one has ever shown" such deprivation in present-day America (Warne 2021, as a position). (g) The
   design was offered as a falsification test: in the PNC authors' words, a null "would indicate that
   an evolutionary-genetic explanation of the gap is unlikely" (l.150–156), and the result was not null.
   Then the proponents' own limits, quoted (the lesson quotes one or two of these; the brief lists them
   so the writer can choose): "the control can only be regarded as partial"; "we cannot completely rule
   out an incremental effect of color"; "we cannot rule out many types of confounding environmental
   variables with this research design"; "environmental familial models cannot be ruled out at this
   stage"; "A single dataset or study cannot settle a debate". Bearing: the evidence is real and
   consistent and, by its authors' own account, a first step.
5. **What the slope sums.** Problem: suppose everything in section 4 is granted — is the slope a
   genetic effect? The obvious attempt: control enough environmental variables and what remains is
   genetic. Why it fails, in two stages. (i) The algebra (Schraiber & Edge, as a fact of the method,
   not as a citation in the body): a person's expected score is a baseline plus their ancestry share
   times the sum of two effects — the genetic difference between the source populations and the
   environmental difference that goes with ancestry in this society. Regression recovers the sum; it
   cannot recover the parts, "or even their direction" (figure 2, a path diagram). (ii) The candidates
   for the environmental part: appearance and the way people are treated for it (colourism — "colour"
   here means how a person looks to others, which is not identical to the genotype); family wealth,
   which differs far more than parental education does; neighbourhood, segregation, school quality and
   pollutant exposure, concentrated by place; the history of who partnered with whom. Each control used
   so far reaches only part of these: SES as parental education alone (PNC); colour predicted from the
   genotype by a forensic tool rather than observed, with a correlation of only −0.39 with ancestry
   inside the Black sample; no appearance data at all (PING). Then the critics' reading at full strength
   (Bird, Jackson & Winston 2024, as a position): the predicted-colour covariate is "a noisy proxy of a
   genetic prediction that is already an imperfect proxy of actual skin color", the tool is unreliable
   for admixed populations, disadvantage reverberates through extended families and places, scores for
   education are biased in admixed individuals, and gene–environment interaction means a slope found in
   one environment need not hold in another — hence their conclusion, quoted: "no evidence that racial IQ
   gaps are genetic or intransigent can be taken from admixture studies". Their labels for the research
   ("racial hereditarian research", "pseudoquantitative") appear, if at all, only as quotation with
   "a 2024 critique" and the year. Then the reply from the other side: the critics name confounds rather
   than measure them; where colour and SES were measured, the slope survived; within families,
   appearance seems to matter little for outcomes (the PING authors' reading of economics sibling
   studies) and in NLSY97 colour did not predict g between siblings (β = .027, 225 pairs; [abstract] —
   and too few pairs to show much either way). And the reply to the reply: the algebra does not depend
   on which confound has been measured; a slope survives a weak control as easily as a weak confound.
   Answer, stated once: the admixture slope is a measured association between ancestry and scores of
   about 0.1 in correlation; it is the sum of whatever genetic and environmental effects travel with
   ancestry; cross-sectional controls narrow the environmental candidates without excluding them.
   (`<Ref>` `heritability-within-and-between-groups` for the general 2024 result; this is its
   admixture case, taught here.)
6. **Siblings: the design that removes the family.** Problem: what if ancestry varied while the family
   did not? Full siblings share both parents, so their expected ancestry is the same, but each inherits
   a random half of each parent's genome, so their realised shares differ by chance. A sibling-difference
   regression asks whether the sibling with more European ancestry scores higher. What it removes:
   everything shared by the family — parents' income, education and wealth, the neighbourhood and
   school, family-level colourism (dossier §4 "What would settle it", revised). What it does not remove:
   anything that responds to each sibling's own ancestry — above all how each looks and is treated (the
   2024 point that colourism "might lead to systematically different treatment as a function of ancestry
   fraction, even within sets of full siblings"). And what it costs: chance differences between siblings
   are small, so the test needs very large samples. The one attempt (ABCD, 2,291 siblings, all
   self-identified groups pooled): within-sibship slope 0.62 with a standard error of 0.55, against 0.93
   in the full sample — 33% smaller, not significantly different from zero, and with a 95% interval
   from about −0.5 to 1.7, which includes both zero and values larger than the full-sample slope. Its
   authors: such analyses are "highly underpowered and, as such, unreliable". Answer: inconclusive, in
   both directions (dossier §4 status: "underpowered and inconclusive"). Exercise 4 computes the
   interval and the sample size the test would need.
7. **Polygenic scores: how they are built.** Problem: the admixture design measures ancestry, not the
   genes that matter; could one measure those genes instead? Define `polygenic-index` (the course's
   term; "polygenic score" is the common name — give both once): a genome-wide association study
   estimates, in hundreds of thousands to millions of people, a tiny effect for each of millions of
   genetic variants on a measured outcome — mostly years of schooling, because it is recorded for
   everyone, not cognitive tests; a person's score sums their variants weighted by those estimates
   (figure 4, a step-through). Recall by `<Ref>` (`heritability-within-and-between-groups`): such
   scores predict 5–10% of cognitive variance in European-ancestry samples, and part of what they
   capture is not the person's own genes acting on the person — within sibships the education score's
   effect shrinks by about half, the cognitive score's much less. One clause only; that lesson taught
   it.
8. **Portability: a score loses its accuracy away from where it was built.** Problem: almost all the
   samples behind the weights are of European ancestry (roughly four-fifths of GWAS participants
   around 2019, for about a sixth of the world). Obvious expectation: genes are genes; a score should
   work for anyone. What the data show (define `pgs-portability`): the largest education score explains
   1.3% and 2.3% of the variance in two US samples of African ancestry — 89% and 85% less than in
   European-ancestry samples (dossier §5, Okbay supplement), more loss than differences in allele
   frequencies and in linkage between neighbouring variants alone predict; accuracy falls person by
   person with genetic distance from the training sample, not just between continents; it falls even
   within Europe, and within one ancestry it varies with age, sex and SES. The hereditarian papers report
   the same thing in their own data: the PNC score's validity in African-Americans was roughly half the
   European-American figure; ABCD's 0.16 against 0.26. Because: the variants a study detects are mostly
   markers that sit next to causal variants, and which marker sits next to which differs between
   populations; some variants exist in one population only; effects may depend on environment; and
   population structure leaks into the weights. Figure 5.
9. **Comparing average scores between ancestries.** Problem: the education score is lower on average
   in samples of African ancestry; is that a genetic gap in potential? Obvious attempt: read the
   score gap as a genetic gap. Why it fails, using the hereditarian papers' own numbers: in the PNC the
   score gap is d = 1.89 — nearly twice the measured gap in g (0.98) — so the raw score difference
   cannot be read as an expected difference in ability. The PNC authors therefore scaled it by the
   score's slope inside the African-American sample and arrived at "as much as 20%–25%" of the gap
   "naïvely explained" (their word); the ABCD authors write that it is "uncertain to what extent
   eduPGS differences correspond to causally relevant genetic differences". Then the comparison at
   continental scale (each as a position with its evidence): (a) an allele-frequency index built from
   education variants correlates about 0.9 with national average IQ across 26 populations ([abstract];
   ecological — `<Ref>` `national-data-audited` for the national IQ inputs); (b) the precedent that
   counsels caution: claimed genetic differences in height between northern and southern Europeans
   largely vanished when the weights were re-estimated in a more homogeneous sample — the signal had come
   from population structure in the original study; (c) the 2021 test: with effect sizes estimated
   within families, the Africa–Europe score difference shrank by about 95% and was no larger than random
   scores; education- and cognition-linked variants were no more differentiated between continents than
   matched control variants; under neutral evolution that differentiation allows at most 3.6–12% of the
   between-continent variance in measured national IQ to be additive genetic, an expected difference of
   at most 4.7–8.5 points, "in either direction" (figure 6). Its own caveats, stated with it: "a rough
   calculation" without uncertainty measures; additive effects equal across populations, no
   interactions; "best viewed as a rough upper bound"; low power — larger data could differ; national IQ
   inputs for Africa that the module audits (68.4); and continental, not US Black–White. And its
   key assumption, neutrality: if natural selection pushed cognition-linked variants in different
   directions in different regions, differentiation could exceed the neutral expectation. A 2026
   ancient-DNA study reports such directional change within West Eurasia over ten millennia
   ([abstract]; "it remains unclear how these relate to phenotypes that were adaptive in the past") — it
   does not test Africa against Europe, but it shows that selection on such scores is detectable, so the
   neutrality assumption is a testable premise, not a given (forward `<Ref>`
   `reich-ancient-dna-and-the-indo-europeans`, one clause). (d) The geneticists' summary on both sides of
   the policy argument: current education scores are "neither interpretable nor particularly meaningful"
   beyond prediction for between-ancestry comparison (a 2022 essay), a point the leading
   genetic-egalitarian writer accepts in reply ([abstract]). Answer: mean score differences between
   continental ancestries cannot at present be read as differences in genetic potential (dossier §5
   status, settled); and the bound is a bound under stated assumptions, not a measured zero (dossier
   "must NOT", environmental side).
10. **The weighing: the direct designs have shown neither a genetic cause nor its absence — and what
    would.** (Heading as a claim.) First set each reading at its strongest against the evidence of
    sections 3–9 and against the strongest objection (VERDICT: consistent direction across designs; the
    bound as a near-zero), and reach the verdict in the course's voice: neither is shown, **strong**. Then
    what would settle it. Problem: if the present designs cannot decide, which could? Build the
    answer as the chain of confounds and the design step that removes each (figure 7, the module's
    KEEPER table):
    - family-level confounds (parental SES, wealth, neighbourhood, family-level colourism) → a
      sibling-difference admixture regression, well powered — tens of thousands of admixed sibling
      pairs (exercise 4 shows why);
    - individual-level treatment by appearance → add observed (not genotype-predicted) skin colour and
      other visible traits measured on each sibling, and test ancestry at genome segments unrelated to
      pigmentation (local ancestry) separately from segments that affect appearance;
    - the score's portability and indirect effects → within-family genome-wide studies of cognition (not
      schooling) in large African-ancestry and admixed samples, scores validated within families in each
      ancestry, then compared (dossier §5 "What would settle it"; none exists at the needed size);
    - and what the critics of the whole approach accept as possible: identifying specific variants whose
      effects are consistent across genetic backgrounds and environments and have a plausible biological
      function (the 2024 paper's own example is a growth-factor variant explaining body size in dogs).
    The residue no design removes: treatment that responds to each person's own appearance can be
    narrowed, not eliminated; and gene–environment interaction means any answer is an answer for these
    environments — the genetic share of a difference can change or reverse in another one. The
    proponents' own programme (replicate in national samples; local admixture mapping; half- and
    adopted-sibling designs) and the critics' (no causal inference from global ancestry; specific
    variants only) overlap on the sibling and local-ancestry steps and disagree on what local ancestry
    can show — state the disagreement, do not resolve it. Close with both Status lines (dossier §4 and
    §5) and the two limits the module has established (`<Ref>` `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`):
    the gap is not wholly genetic as it stands; no evidence excludes a genetic share.
    `<Conclusion>`: the verdict in plain words: the direct genetic designs have not shown that genes cause
    part of the gap, and have not shown that there is no genetic difference; the course holds this as
    **strong**, and states the design that would decide it. Then the hand-off sentence (ARC).

Headings as claims: "An ancestry share is a measured fraction of a genome"; "Blood groups: a null from
a blunt instrument"; "A small slope and a large extrapolation"; "The slope as evidence for genes"; "The
slope sums two effects"; "Siblings remove the family, not the face"; "How a polygenic score is built";
"Scores travel badly"; "Average scores across ancestries"; "Neither a genetic cause nor its absence has
been shown".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The PNC slope and its extrapolation** (lasker2019 Table 5, l.1089–1105): 0.78 SD per 0 → 100%;
   r = 0.086; mean 18.7% (SD 11.7) vs 98.6%; 0.086 × 6.83 = 0.587 SD; 0.587/0.984 ≈ 60%. The reader
   sees how "under 1% of variance" becomes "60% of the gap" — and which two assumptions do the work.
   Worked in section 3, reproduced in exercise 2.
2. **The equation that sums** (Schraiber & Edge preprint l.301–316): expected score = baseline +
   share × (genetic difference + environmental difference). Rendered as words and one display equation
   with named terms; then as a path diagram (figure 2).
3. **The sibling estimate** (Fuerst 2023 Table 6): 0.62 ± 0.55 vs 0.93; interval −0.46 to 1.70.
   A worked `<Example>` of reading a non-significant estimate: what values it excludes (almost none) and
   what it would take to exclude some.
4. **The score gap larger than the trait gap** (lasker2019: d = 1.89 PGS vs 0.98 g): the one-line
   demonstration that a raw score difference cannot be a difference in expected ability.
5. **The 95% shrinkage** (bird2021 l.290–305): squared Africa–Europe score difference 0.068 with
   between-family weights, 0.0027 with within-family weights. The same genomes, different weights, the
   difference nearly gone — and the caveat that within-family weights are noisier and the test has low
   power.
6. **The bound's arithmetic** (bird2021 l.295–316): among-group variance from national means 68.4 and
   99.2; genetic among-group variance 8.5 / 19.9 / 28.4 points² → 3.6% / 8.4% / 12%; 4.7–8.5 points in
   either direction. Shown as a figure (6), with the inputs labelled so the reader sees that one input
   is the national-IQ table the module audits.
7. **The height precedent** (Sohail / Berg 2019): one paragraph telling the story — a celebrated
   north–south signal that disappeared with better weights — as the reason population comparisons of
   scores are handled with caution by geneticists on every side.
8. **Red hair** (Warne 2021 p. 490, quoting Jencks 1979): the thought experiment and the reply, set
   against the 2024 colourism-within-siblings point. A paired-argument set piece for section 5.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know (L11; at most three per section):** allele, genetic variant
  (single-nucleotide polymorphism — use "variant"); reference panel and global ancestry share (section
  1); local ancestry (which continent a particular stretch of a chromosome came from — section 10 only);
  regression slope (the predicted change in the score for a one-unit change in the predictor; the unit
  here is 0 → 100% ancestry, in SD of the score); correlation vs slope (section 3 depends on the
  difference); adjusted R² ("share of variance explained") — once; restricted range; measurement error
  attenuating a correlation (section 2); standard error and 95% interval (section 6, one plain sentence
  each); genome-wide association study; polygenic index/score; within-family (direct) effect vs
  population effect (`<Ref>` lesson 2); linkage between neighbouring variants (one clause: variants
  inherited together, in patterns that differ between populations); population structure/stratification
  (differences in ancestry within a study sample that make a variant look associated with an outcome
  because both differ by ancestry); Fst (say "how much a variant's frequency differs between populations"
  and name the statistic only in a figure label, if at all); neutral evolution / genetic drift (changes
  in allele frequencies by chance, without selection — one sentence); colourism (unequal treatment by
  skin tone; the course defines it once, without endorsing any estimate of its size).
- **Self-identified group vs ancestry share:** never write "Black people have X% European genes" as a
  fact about individuals; it is a sample average with large spread. Never write "African ancestry
  lowers scores" — the slope is an association in the samples named.
- **The "over 60%" figure:** a 2024 critique attributes "over 60%" to the PNC paper; that figure is
  the paper's admixture extrapolation (60%, l.1104) and its admixture formula (50–70%, l.1189), not its
  polygenic-score estimate (20–25%). The lesson keeps the three numbers apart and attributes each to its
  route (dossier §5; confirmed in the text for this brief).
- **The sibling interval:** the dossier writes that the sibling estimate's interval spans "zero to about
  the full-sample value"; computed from the reported SE it runs from about −0.46 to 1.70, i.e. well
  beyond the full-sample 0.93 on the upper side. The lesson uses the computed interval.
- **The sibling sample is pooled** across self-identified groups (ABCD Table 6 controls; all siblings);
  do not call it a within-family test "among Black Americans".
- **Do not re-teach** SNP heritability, within-sibship shrinkage, genetic nurture or the general 2024
  proof (`heritability-within-and-between-groups`), the SES ambiguity
  (`us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`), Spearman's hypothesis (`hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`),
  measurement invariance (`what-intelligence-tests-measure`), the placement confound
  (`adoption-studies-across-groups`). Each is a clause and a `<Ref>`.
- **Do not preview** the political question (`gould-and-harden-on-heredity`), ancient DNA beyond the
  one neutrality paragraph (`reich-ancient-dna-and-the-indo-europeans`), or the national-IQ audit
  (`national-data-audited`) beyond naming the bound's input as audited there.
- **Balance check (revision-prompt rule 6):** section 4 states the hereditarian reading with its own
  authors' caveats; section 5 states the critics' reading and then the reply to it and the reply to the
  reply; section 9 states the bound with its own caveats and the neutrality question it leaves open.
  Words to avoid in the course's voice: "debunked", "artefact" applied to the admixture slope (the slope
  is a measured association; the height signal is called an artefact because its own authors found it to
  be one), "flawed", "merely", "pseudo-", "fringe"; on the other side, "proves", "confirms the genetic",
  "the data show genes". "Naïvely" is the PNC authors' word for their own estimate; quote it as theirs.
- **Provenance (sourcing-drill metadata, never argument):** three of the admixture papers have authors
  affiliated with the Ulster Institute for Social Research and one appeared in *Mankind Quarterly*;
  the 2024 critique appeared in *American Psychologist* under a title that names its verdict;
  the 2021 bound in *American Journal of Physical Anthropology* under a title that states a verdict. If
  an exercise uses any of this, it applies the same treatment to every source and asks what the metadata
  predicts about use, not what it proves about evidence. The ABCD governance letter is data-access
  metadata only (see Sources).
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — (1) the admixture correlation about 0.1, under 1% of variance, with a
  slope that extrapolates to "60%" only under two assumptions; (2) the sibling estimate 0.62 ± 0.55;
  (3) 85–89% loss of score accuracy in African-ancestry samples. Everything else in figures or as
  order-of-magnitude words.

### DOSSIER CONSTRAINTS

Status lines the lesson must state, in substance verbatim:

> §4 **Status: open. Within self-identified US Black samples, more European genomic ancestry goes with
> slightly higher scores (r ≈ 0.09–0.14; under 1% of variance). The association is reduced but not
> removed by parental SES, and not removed by the colour measures used so far (genotype-predicted). The
> one within-family test is underpowered and inconclusive. Old blood-group studies found no association
> with weak tests. Latin-American studies that control social conditions find the association small or
> gone, on other outcomes.**

> §5 **Status: settled that current cognitive and education scores are partly inflated by indirect
> effects, assortative mating and stratification (EA strongly, cognition less), and lose most of their
> accuracy in African-ancestry samples; therefore settled that mean score differences between
> continental ancestries cannot now be read as genetic-potential differences. Open whether any genetic
> difference exists; no current PGS study can answer.**

(The Latin-American studies — infant screening, MMSE, dementia, all [abstract] and none childhood IQ
— get one sentence in section 5 as a corroborating pattern on other outcomes, labelled as such, or are
left out; they must not be presented as tests of the IQ gap.)

And the "What would settle it" paragraphs of §4 and §5, as revised in the dossier after the
Schraiber & Edge reading: the sibling design "removes family-level confounds — parental SES,
neighbourhood, family-level colourism — but not treatment that responds to each sibling's own
appearance … no design fully closes it"; "within-family GWAS of cognition in large African-ancestry and
admixed samples, scores validated within families in each ancestry, then compared. None exists at the
needed size."

Claims the lesson must NOT make (dossier "Claims the lessons must NOT make"), both directions:

- Hereditarian direction: "Admixture studies show genes cause the gap" — correlations under 1% of
  variance, confounded; the within-family estimate is non-significant. "Polygenic scores show lower
  genetic potential in Africans or Black Americans." "The gap is X% genetic" for any X (here: the
  PNC's 60% and 50–70%, and 20–25%, are reported only as the authors' estimates with their routes and
  caveats). "Invariance / Spearman's hypothesis shows the gap is genetic" (the PNC's Jensen effects are
  reported as their finding, not as proof).
- Environmental direction: "Admixture and adoption studies show no ancestry effect" — recent genomic
  studies find small positive associations; older nulls were weak tests. "Polygenic scores have shown no
  genetic difference" — Bird 2021 is a bound under neutrality and stated assumptions, not a measurement
  of zero. "Science has shown the gap is entirely environmental" / "genes play no role".

How each is refuted in the body (L13): "admixture shows genes cause the gap" in section 5 (the slope
sums two effects; the sibling test is inconclusive); "PGS show lower genetic potential" in sections
8–9 (portability; the score gap exceeds the trait gap; the authors' own uncertainty); "admixture shows
no ancestry effect" in section 2 (weak blood-group tests) and section 3 (consistent positive genomic
slopes); "PGS have shown no genetic difference" in section 9 (a rough upper bound under neutrality,
equal effects and a contested input, with low power; neutrality testable and not tested between
Africa and Europe). Each in the schema: claim, rejection, why it fails, replacement, discriminating
example (the 1977 r = 0.05 vs the PNC's 0.086; d = 1.89 vs 0.98; the bound's 4.7–8.5 "in either
direction").

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** say in two sentences what an admixture regression's slope is, and
   name the two effects it sums.
2. **Research-shaped: reproduce a published number (E6d).** Given the PNC figures (slope 0.78 SD per
   0 → 100% European ancestry; r = 0.086; mean ancestry 18.7%, SD 11.7, in the self-identified Black
   sample; 98.6% in the White sample; observed gap 0.98 SD), reproduce the "60% of the gap" by both
   routes (slope × difference in ancestry; r × difference in SD units). Then name the two assumptions the
   number needs, and identify which figure a later critique reported as "over 60%" and which it did not.
3. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** show that a slope of 0.78 SD per unit of ancestry and an
   ancestry SD of 0.117 imply a correlation of about 0.09 and a variance share under 1% (r ≈ slope ×
   SD_x / SD_y with SD_y ≈ 1). Explain why "explains under 1% of the variance" and "consistent with 60%
   of the gap" are both true, and what each sentence would wrongly suggest on its own.
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** the sibling estimate 0.62 (SE 0.55, 2,291 siblings) against 0.93 in the
   full sample. Compute the 95% interval; say which hypotheses it excludes. Then estimate how many
   siblings would be needed to bring the standard error to 0.15 (SE scales as 1/√n: about 13 times as
   many, ~30,000) and say what such a study would still not remove.
5. **Claim to refute (E6b, hereditarian direction):** "Black Americans' education polygenic score is
   1.9 SD below White Americans', so their genetic potential for education is lower." Name the steps
   that fail (score gap larger than the trait gap; 85–89% loss of accuracy; indirect effects and
   stratification in the weights; the authors' own "uncertain"), and state what the score difference
   does establish.
6. **Claim to refute (E6b, environmental direction):** "Genetic studies have shown there is no genetic
   difference: polygenic scores show no selection between Africa and Europe, and the blood-group studies
   found no link between ancestry and IQ." Name the steps that fail (a rough upper bound under
   neutrality, equal effects and a contested national-IQ input, with low power and "either direction";
   blunt markers; the genomic slopes), and state what the 2021 test does establish.
7. **Corroboration and argument (document reasoning + CER):** two short passages — the 2024 critique on
   the colour covariate and parental education, and the PNC authors' own limitations paragraph
   (l.1189–1201) with the red-hair reply from a 2021 review. State where they agree (both call the
   controls partial; both say global admixture cannot settle it), where they disagree (burden of proof;
   whether unmeasured confounds should be presumed), and design the one measurement that would most
   reduce the disagreement (observed colour within siblings).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`:** suppose the study in section 10 were run with
   100,000 sibling pairs and observed colour, and came back (a) with a within-family slope
   indistinguishable from zero, or (b) with a slope implying a genetic difference of a few IQ points.
   For each of four policy claims made by writers on the right and the left (given in the exercise:
   group-conscious remedies are justified; outcomes should be expected to differ by group; individuals
   should be judged as individuals; genetic luck calls for compensation), say whether result (a) or (b)
   would change it, and why. The solution shows that most of the claims survive either result because
   they rest on a value premise, and says in one sentence that the next lesson sets those premises side
   by side, with a `<Ref>`.

9. **Attack the verdict:** The lesson holds as **strong** that the direct designs show neither a genetic
   cause of the gap nor its absence. State the best case that they do show a genetic contribution (the
   direction of every slope, the sibling point estimate, the score gap), then the best case that they show
   none worth the name (the neutral bound). For each, say what evidence would be needed to overturn the
   verdict. The solution shows that both cases rest on a premise the designs cannot check (no confounding by
   appearance and family; neutrality), and names the section-10 design that would remove it.

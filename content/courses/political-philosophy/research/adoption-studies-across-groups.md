# Research brief: adoption-studies-across-groups (Adoption studies across groups: the Minnesota study, the English nurseries and Eyferth's children)

status: planned
module: heredity-and-civilization
type: core
goal: Evaluate the transracial adoption and cross-parentage studies as tests of the genetic and environmental accounts of the gap, and explain why each side reads the same data as support

Register rule (binding on the lesson): The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.

Group labels: the studies use the census and social categories of their time and place ("Black",
"White", "interracial", "black/white", "Asian/Indian", "West Indian or African", "mixed race",
the German "Besatzungskinder" of Black and White fathers). The lesson says once, at first use, that
these are the authors' categories and that the course adds none; inside each study's numbers it uses
that study's own labels. "Interracial" in the Minnesota papers means a child with one Black and one
White birth parent (66 Black fathers, 2 Black mothers: scarr1976 Table 10 note, l.481); the larger
"black/interracial" group of 130 also includes children with one parent of unknown, Asian or Indian
background (scarr1976 l.462–464).

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`: the gap's size (about 0.75 SD in recent
  child norms, about 1 SD in adult norms) and its widening with age; the Flynn effect (scores rising
  about 3 points a decade, so that an older test normed earlier gives higher scores than a newer one —
  the reason every retest on a revised test "loses" points); the residual gap after measured SES
  (about 6 points of the child WISC gap) and why controlling SES is ambiguous; the status that the gap
  is not wholly genetic as it stands. From `heritability-within-and-between-groups`: the
  within/between fallacy (two pots of seed), the default hypothesis, heritability of g rising with age
  (about .41 at nine to .66 at seventeen), shared-family environment fading by adolescence. From
  `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`: Rushton and Jensen's ten categories of evidence, of which
  transracial adoption is one they score for the hereditarian model, and the Bell Curve's treatment of
  the Minnesota study (that lesson states the case; this one tests it).
- **Contributes:** The one move: an adoption study looks like the experiment the question needs —
  rearing held constant, parentage varied — but it can answer the question only if placement is
  independent of ancestry, and in every study the lesson reads it is not, or the samples are too small
  to tell. Concepts introduced: `transracial-adoption-study` (a design comparing children of
  different ancestry raised in similar adoptive homes, decisive only if placement is independent of
  ancestry) and `placement-confound` (any difference in pre-adoptive history, age at placement,
  number of placements or birth-mother characteristics that travels with the child's ancestry). The
  lesson shows, on the same numbers, the hereditarian reading (the ordering of groups survives into
  adolescence in the same homes) and the environmental reading (the ordering of placement histories
  matches the ordering of scores; the norms changed; attrition inflated one group) at full strength,
  then the residue both readings leave.
- **Hands off:** To `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`: adoption cannot separate parentage from
  placement, and the study that could (adoption at birth, placement independent of ancestry,
  birth-parent scores, testing in adulthood) will not be run; the question moved to designs that
  measure ancestry directly in the DNA of people raised in ordinary families. One sentence in the
  `<Conclusion>` says so; the FORWARD exercise reaches it. What this lesson decides: neither the
  Minnesota study nor the Eyferth and Tizard studies decide the cause of the gap (**strong**); what passes
  on is the question itself, now put to genomic designs.
- **Spine step:** "Heredity as an explanation of the gaps between peoples, tested premise by premise — what the tests measure, what heritability can and cannot imply, the hereditarian case at full strength, then the US gap, adoption, admixture and polygenic scores on the step from a real gap to a genetic one — then what follows politically whatever the answer, what ancient DNA shows, the rival explanations from geography and institutions, and the key datasets audited"
- Not module-final: no APPLICATION spec.

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** two opposite readings of the adoption record: (i) the Minnesota study shows the gap
  surviving a good environment (the ordering 109/106/99/89 at seventeen); (ii) the Eyferth and Tizard
  studies show no ancestry effect.
- **Verdict:** **neither study decides.** Grade: **strong**.
- **What decides it** (dossier §6 and §4; scarr1976, weinberg1992, the Waldman et al. 1994 reply, tizard1974,
  Neisser 1997, Thomas 2017): in Minnesota, placement history, age at placement (9 against 32 months for the
  mixed and two-Black-parent groups) and attrition travel with ancestry; birth parents' scores were never
  measured; and the study's own authors deny the hereditarian reading ("consistent with both"). Eyferth and
  Tizard are small, tested children young (when shared environment weighs most), and are confounded (the
  fathers screened by the army; the nurseries' tiny cells, with no adopted child of two Black parents); the
  primary Eyferth numbers are unread and the two secondary versions disagree on group labels.
- **Strongest objection:** from the hereditarian side, the Minnesota ordering held across a decade and new
  tests, and every confound would have to line up with ancestry to explain it away; from the environmental
  side, two independent studies with roughly equal care both found near-zero differences. It does not
  overturn the verdict. Each confound does line up with ancestry in the authors' own tables, which is why
  neither reading can be preferred on these data; and two small studies with young testing do not add up to a
  strong null. The verdict applies one standard to both readings: a design confounded with the variable of
  interest decides nothing in either direction.
- **What would overturn it:** adoption at birth, placement documented independent of ancestry, birth-parent
  scores measured, testing in adulthood. The register and the dossier agree such a study is unlikely ever to
  be run.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): if the gap came wholly from homes, children of one group raised
from infancy in homes of the other should score like the children they grow up beside; if part were
genetic, some difference should remain; in the 1970s a Minnesota team followed about 130 adopted
Black and mixed-race children, and some adopted White children, in White middle-class families,
testing them at about seven and again at seventeen; it looks like the experiment the question needs,
and both sides cite it; the lesson reads it, and two smaller studies from England and Germany, to find
out how.

The newcomer's problem, from the previous lesson (one sentence, `<Ref>`
`us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`): measured family circumstances account for part of the gap and
leave part, and controlling for them is ambiguous because parents' circumstances are partly an effect
of parents' ability. An adoption study seems to cut that knot: the adopted child has its birth
parents' genes and its adoptive parents' home. Anchors (L11): Minnesota in the 1960s–70s, where
agencies placed many Black and mixed-race children with White families because too few Black families
were available to adopt (scarr1976 l.97–106; the Black share of the state's population was 0.9% in
1970); England's voluntary-society residential nurseries of the late 1960s; occupied West Germany
after 1945.

1. **What everyone agrees adoption does.** Problem: before asking about groups, does moving a child
   to a richer home change its score at all? Obvious attempt: compare adoptees with the general
   population. Why it is not enough: adoptees are a selected group (healthy enough to be placed) and
   their adoptive homes are atypically good. Answer (dossier §6, Nisbett 2012 l.433–448): comparisons
   with siblings left at home or with children adopted into poorer homes show gains of about 12
   points, and up to 18 where the comparison is a deprived institution — within groups, a settled
   finding. Both sides grant it (the Bell Curve: the age-7 result "pointed to a considerable impact of
   the home setting", l.3212). The disagreement is not whether homes matter but whether equal homes
   equalise groups. This seam turns the opening's question from "do homes matter?" to "do groups
   differ when homes are the same?" — the question an adoption design can only answer under a
   condition.
2. **The experiment the question needs, and the one that was run.** Problem: what would make an
   adoption study decisive? Obvious attempt: take Black and White adoptees in similar homes; any
   remaining difference is genetic. Why it fails: a remaining difference has three candidate sources —
   birth parentage (genes and the prenatal environment, which cannot be separated: Black mothers in
   the 1970s were at greater risk of prematurity and nutritional deficiency, scarr1976 l.747–757), the
   child's history before placement, and how the child is treated as a member of a group after it.
   Answer: the design is decisive only if (i) children are placed at birth, (ii) placement is
   independent of ancestry — no group placed later, more often, or into poorer homes — (iii) birth
   parents' abilities are known, and (iv) children are tested when family effects have faded. Define
   `transracial-adoption-study` and `placement-confound` here (one section, two terms; "cross-fostering"
   is the animal-genetics name the Minnesota authors used, scarr1976 l.33–39 — give it as a clause).
   KEEPER: the four conditions, used as a checklist for every study in the lesson (figure 7 is that
   checklist filled in).
3. **Minnesota at seven: a group that scored above the White average.** Problem: what did the
   first testing show, and what can it carry? The design (scarr1976): 101 families, adoptive parents
   with 15–17 years of schooling and Wechsler scores near 120; every child over four tested at home;
   the scorer blind to race and adoptive status. The numbers (figure 2, frame 1): biological children
   116.7; White adoptees 111.5; Black/interracial adoptees 106.3 (130 children) — about one SD above
   the 90 typical of Black children reared in the region; those placed in the first year 110. Within
   the 130: one White birth parent 109.0 (68), two Black birth parents 96.8 (29). Then the confound,
   in the authors' own table: the 29 had been placed at 32 months on average against 9, had spent less
   time in the home, had more and poorer prior placements, and their birth mothers had 10.9 years of
   school against 12.4 (Table 10). Worked example: the two regressions in both orders — 35% of the
   variance explained, 24.5 points of it shared between "biological" and "social" variables, birth
   mother's race uniquely 3% (figure 4). The authors' reading: rearing moved scores by at least
   10–20 points; genes and placement "largely confounded"; race of the birth mother cannot be read as
   genes alone. Two checks a reader would ask for, and the study made: an expectancy test (12 mixed
   children wrongly believed by their parents to have two Black parents scored 108.6, the correctly
   classified 108.4: parents' beliefs did not move scores) and a self-selection check. Bearing: at
   seven the study answers the malleability question clearly and the group question not at all.
4. **Minnesota at seventeen: everyone lost points, the order stayed.** Problem: ten years later the
   numbers look like a different study. Obvious attempt: read the drop (Black/interracial 106.3 →
   96.8) as the fading of a home effect. Why it fails as stated: everyone dropped — fathers by 4.6,
   mothers by 5.5, biological children by 7.0 — because the children were retested on newer tests
   normed later (WISC-R/WAIS-R short form), and a test normed later reads lower (`<Ref>`
   `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment` for the Flynn effect; WAIS → WAIS-R averages 6.8 points,
   weinberg1992 l.660–664); the Black/interracial adoptees' drop did not differ significantly from
   the biological children's (p = .156). Answer: comparisons must be made between groups at the same
   testing, and there the ordering persisted: biological 109.4, White adoptees 105.6, Black/white 98.5,
   Black/black 89.4 (figure 2, frame 2). Birth mother's race "remained the best single predictor"
   with other variables controlled — and the authors add that it may stand largely for "unmeasured,
   social characteristics". Set piece: the authors' own test (l.813–825) — what a genetic-only model
   predicts (adoptees decline more than biological children; Time 2 IQ tracks birth parents' education
   but not adoptive parents'; placement history no longer matters) and why the data did not fit. The
   reader should notice what the test does and does not exclude: it rejects a genes-only model; it does
   not reject a mixed one (exercise 7). Pitfall to state: at 17 only 16 White adoptees were retested.
5. **The hereditarian reading, at full strength.** Problem: grant every confound — what does the
   follow-up still show? The reading, built from its proponents (positions, not names, in the body):
   (a) in the same upper-middle-class homes, at both ages, the four groups line up in the order of
   their African ancestry — biological, White adoptee, mixed, two-Black-parent — "precisely what would
   be expected from genetic theory" (quoted in the reply as Lynn 1994's sentence); (b) the gap between
   two-White-parent and two-Black-parent adoptees at 17 is about 16–17 points, the size of the national
   gap, and the two-Black-parent children's 89 is no higher than the regional Black mean — "Black babies
   adopted by White parents registered no IQ gains" (Lynn 1997, as quoted by Neisser 1997); "Whatever
   the environmental impact may have been, it cannot have been large" (Bell Curve l.3214); (c) the age
   confound is weaker than it looks: on this reading age at adoption has little effect on IQ after
   seven, and East Asian children adopted late and often malnourished score at or above the White
   norm (three studies, 19–141 children each; `[secondary]`); (d) shared-family effects are largest
   in childhood and smallest by late adolescence (`<Ref>` `heritability-within-and-between-groups`
   for heritability rising with age), so the age-17 scores are the informative ones and the age-7
   advantage was the expected temporary one; (e) the misclassified children (section 3) rule out the
   parents' expectations as the cause. One proponent's quantitative claim — that genetic variation
   explains "about 70%" of the difference — is reported as a claim and is on the dossier's
   not-to-state list. Close the section with the strongest concession from that side: a 2021 review
   that argues the gap is partly genetic calls the study's evidence "ambiguous", concurs with its
   authors, and leaves adoption out of its own case (Warne pp. 490–491).
6. **The environmental reading, at full strength.** Problem: grant the ordering — what produces it?
   (a) The placement histories line up in the same order as the scores: White adoptees placed with
   fewer, better prior placements and longest in the home; two-Black-parent children latest, most
   moved, shortest in the home (Waldman Table 1; figure 3 sets the two orderings side by side).
   Placement history explains 32% of childhood and 13% of adolescent score variance; once it is held
   constant, the difference among the adoptee groups shrinks to a statistical trend. (b) The study's
   Asian/Indian adoptees scored between the mixed and the Black groups (101 and 96), the opposite of
   the order a genetic ranking predicts for East Asian ancestry. (c) Test changes must be corrected
   child by child, by test and norming year; until then raw follow-up means are "relatively
   meaningless" (Neisser 1997). (d) Attrition: nine of the 25 White adoptees were not retested, and
   the 16 who were had scored 117.6 at seven against 111.5 for all 25; allowing for regression to the
   mean (correlation .63 between the two testings) their age-17 mean is inflated by about 3.8 points;
   adjusted, the White–two-Black-parent gap is 11.7 points at 17 against 14.7 at 7 — no widening
   (worked example; figure 2, frame 3). (e) Asymmetry: a mean above the population shows rearing
   helped; a mean at or below it does not show rearing failed, because adoptees carry pre-adoptive
   adversity. (f) The adoptees were socially classified as Black in schools and neighbourhoods;
   that experience was not measured and goes with the ancestry ordering. (g) A failure of one
   intervention to close a gap is not evidence for a genetic cause ("One cannot demonstrate the validity
   of a scientific hypothesis by default", Waldman l.571). The authors' verdict: the balance, "although
   not conclusive", favours a predominantly environmental account (l.664–666).
7. **Where each reading meets its strongest objection.** Problem: each reading leaves a residue the
   other can use. Set them as pairs, with the evidence for each reply:
   - Against (b) of the environmental reading: the study's "Asian/Indian" group was not an East Asian
     sample — of its 21 children at least 7 were Canadian or Ecuadorian Indian and 8 Korean or
     Vietnamese (scarr1976 Table 2), and the group was placed latest of all, at 61 months on average
     (Table 5). It tests late placement as much as ancestry, and so it supports the environmental side's
     general point about placement while weakening its specific point about ranking. (This observation
     is the course's own reading of the authors' tables; state it as such, with the numbers, and let the
     reader check it in exercise 7 if the writer moves it there.)
   - Against (c) of the hereditarian reading: inside this study, age at placement still correlated
     −.30 with scores at 17 and placement variables still explained 13% of adolescent variance
     (weinberg1992 Table 3; Waldman Table 2) — the general claim that age at adoption stops mattering
     after seven does not hold in this sample.
   - Against (d) of the environmental reading (attrition): the adjustment rests on a correlation from
     16 children and a regression-to-the-mean model; after it, a gap of about 12 points (0.8 SD) between
     two-White-parent and two-Black-parent adoptees remains.
   - Against (a)/(b) of the hereditarian reading: the two-Black-parent group differs from the others on
     every measured pre-adoptive variable and on birth mothers' schooling; birth parents' test scores
     were never measured (Warne's "most problematic shortcoming"); and "parentage" includes the prenatal
     environment.
   - The interpretation with the widest assent, stated in one place: no clear evidence for the genetic
     hypothesis; evidence against "certain versions of the environmental hypothesis" — the mere fact of
     growing up in a middle-class home apparently does not by itself raise adolescent scores (Neisser
     1997; pending the Flynn corrections, which were never published). Both sides can quote it, and both
     have.
   KEEPER: the age-17 ordering (109/106/99/89) set beside the placement ages (9 vs 32 months for the
   mixed and two-Black-parent groups) — the one pair of numbers that shows why both readings exist.
8. **The English nurseries: equal care, small numbers.** Problem: could a study escape the placement
   confound by putting children of different ancestry in the same institution from infancy? The design
   came closer than Minnesota on condition (ii): children were placed in whichever nursery had a
   vacancy, nobody assessed their or their parents' intelligence before placement, the nurseries were
   isolated and the observers saw no colour prejudice in them, and fathers' manual or non-manual work
   did not differ by group. Results (figure 5): at 2–4 years no difference except a non-verbal score
   favouring the non-White children; at 4½, in the institutions, White 101.2 (10), mixed 109.3 (7),
   Black 105.6 (8); among the adopted, White 113.0 (17) and mixed 119.9 (7) — no child with two Black
   parents was adopted. None of the differences is statistically significant. What it can carry: it
   fails condition (iv) badly (4½ years old) and its samples are so small that a difference of nearly
   ten points would not reach significance (exercise 5 computes it); the "Black" children had West
   Indian or African parents, a migrant population in 1960s England, whose selection is unknown (one
   estimate puts its possible effect at a few points: `[secondary]` via Nisbett 2009). Each side's
   reading: a result in the "wrong" direction for a genetic ordering, under the best placement control
   of any study; or a test at an age when family effects dominate, too small to detect anything but a
   large difference. The author's own sentence ("genetic aspects were neither controlled nor adequately
   known") bounds both.
9. **Black families and White families: the 1986 comparison (secondary only).** Problem: hold the
   child's ancestry roughly constant and vary the adoptive family instead. Middle-class Black and White
   families adopted Black and mixed-race children; tested at about 7–8½. Reported results (dossier §6,
   `[secondary]`; four accounts, figure 6): children in White families scored about 13–13.5 points
   higher (117 vs 104 in two accounts); mixed and two-Black-parent children did not differ
   significantly — "virtually the same" in one account, 5.1 points in favour of the mixed children
   across both kinds of family in another, 107 (mixed) vs 109 (two Black parents) in White families in
   a third. The same reply at 17 is unavailable: no follow-up was published. Environmental reading:
   adoptive-family culture (test-taking style, maternal support during tasks, weinberg1992 l.709–714)
   moved scores by most of a national gap, and partial European ancestry gave no advantage.
   Hereditarian reading: age 7–8 is when family effects are largest; the sample is small (46 in one
   account). The reply to that (Nisbett 2009 App. B): the heritability curve the objection cites is
   nearly flat between 7 and 17. The lesson states that the primary paper is unread and that the
   accounts disagree on the children's age and on how the ancestry comparison is broken down.
10. **Children of Black and White soldiers in occupied Germany (secondary only).** Problem: a
    natural experiment with the mother held constant — illegitimate children of White German women,
    fathered by White or Black soldiers of the occupation forces after 1945, raised in Germany.
    Reported result: no difference — about 97 for both groups. Then the evidence problem (figure 6,
    the lesson's sourcing set piece): five secondary accounts; four give the children of Black fathers
    96.5 and of White fathers 97 or 97.2 (Waldman et al. 1994 citing a 1984 review; Rushton & Jensen
    2005 with group sizes 98 and 83; Nisbett 2009 in his chapter), one gives the opposite labelling
    (Nisbett 2009, his own appendix); a sixth (Neisser et al. 1996) gives only "no mean difference", and
    a seventh (Nisbett et al. 2012) adds that the groups also matched on measured g. The accounts agree
    on the substance (no difference worth the name) and disagree on a detail that shows none of them is
    being quoted from the 1961 paper beside the writer. What the design can carry: (i) Black soldiers
    were a screened group — Army pre-induction tests rejected about 30% of Black and 3% of White
    recruits (`[secondary]`, via Rushton & Jensen 2005) — so their children's genetic expectation is
    above the Black American mean, by no more than about 3 points on one estimate (`[secondary]`, via
    Nisbett 2009 and the dossier); (ii) some "Black" fathers were French North African soldiers
    (20–25% on one account; "very small" effect on another); (iii) the children were 5–13 years old
    (one account) or "adolescents" (another); (iv) mixed-race children in post-war Germany met
    prejudice, which would bias against them. Environmental reading: the strongest piece of "direct"
    evidence the 1996 task force could cite. Hereditarian reading: ambiguous for (i)–(iii). Both
    readings agree it is small, old, unread in the original by most who cite it, and unreplicated.
11. **The weighing: no adoption study decides the cause of the gap, in either direction.** (Heading as a
    claim; it replaces "What adoption cannot settle".) Set each reading at its strongest (the ordering that
    survived a decade; the two near-zero differences) against the four conditions and reach the verdict in the
    course's voice: neither decides, **strong**. Problem: put the four studies through the four
    conditions of section 2 (figure 7). Minnesota fails (ii) and (iii) and is small at (iv); the
    nurseries pass (ii) as far as anyone could check and fail (iv); the 1986 comparison is unread and
    young; the German study is unread, young, and screened. Status (dossier §6, stated in substance
    verbatim): open; the data are few, small and confounded with pre-adoptive history, and the original
    authors themselves say they cannot separate genes from environment. Both claims the dossier forbids
    are refuted explicitly here (L13 refutation schema, one paragraph each, see Pitfalls). Then the
    study that would settle it (dossier §6 "What would settle it"): a large adoption-at-birth study with
    documented independence of ancestry from placement quality, birth-parent scores, tested in
    adulthood — and why it will not be run (placement at birth cannot be randomised; one review of
    these studies concludes no fully representative one is ever likely). The hand-off: the remaining
    route is to measure ancestry itself, in the DNA, among people raised in ordinary families, and see
    whether it predicts scores — with its own version of the placement problem (one sentence, forward
    Ref `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`).
    `<Conclusion>`: the verdict in plain words: the Minnesota study does not show the gap surviving a good
    environment, and the German and English studies do not show that ancestry has no effect; no adoption
    study decides the cause of the gap, and the course holds this as **strong**. Then what the reader can now
    do (put any adoption study through the four conditions) and the hand-off sentence above.

Headings as claims: "Adoption raises scores; the question is whether it equalises them"; "The

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know (L11; at most three per section):** adoption study and
  cross-fostering (section 2); placement history, selective placement (agencies matching better-
  educated birth mothers to better-educated adoptive families: birth mother's education correlated
  .22–.26 with adoptive parents', scarr1976 l.647–663); norms and renorming (`<Ref>` the Flynn effect,
  one clause); short form (four subtests correlating above .90 with the full test); regression to the
  mean (a group selected for high scores on one occasion scores closer to average on the next —
  needed for the attrition argument; one sentence and the example); attrition; statistical
  significance and sample size (one plain sentence: "not significant" here means a difference this
  size could easily arise by chance with these few children, not that there is no difference); analysis
  of covariance (say "comparing the groups after statistically equalising their placement histories" —
  do not name the technique); "socially classified Black" (the authors' term for any child with at
  least one Black birth parent); WPPSI, Reynell scales (one clause each or omit); the occupation
  (Allied occupation of Germany after 1945, one half-sentence).
- **Numbers that invite confusion:** the Black/interracial mean at 17 (96.8) equals the Black/black
  mean at 7 (96.8) — never write "96.8" without its group and age. White adoptees: 25 at 7, 16 at 17,
  and those 16 had averaged 117.6 (not 111.5) at 7. The biological children's 116.7 is in the OCR as
  "11,6.7" (scarr1976 l.383). Rushton & Jensen round to 117/112/109/97 and 109/106/99/89 and give 56
  correctly classified mixed children where the paper has 43; the lesson uses the papers' own figures.
  "Interracial" is not "half-European" in every case: the 130 include children with one parent of
  unknown, Asian or Indian background; the Black/white comparison uses only the 68 and 29.
- **Do not state** that the Asian/Indian group is an East Asian sample (section 7). Do not state that
  Lynn's or Levin's papers say anything beyond the sentences quoted in the 1994 reply and the 1997
  reply (they are unread).
- **Eyferth and Moore are unread.** Every number from them carries "reported" and the figure shows
  all versions; never pick one version silently. The dossier recorded two conflicting versions of
  Eyferth (Waldman 1994 vs Nisbett 2009 App. B); this brief found that four of five secondary accounts
  agree (children of Black fathers 96.5, of White fathers 97–97.2) and that Nisbett 2009 contradicts
  his own chapter (p. 136, l.4668–4672) in his appendix (p. 304, l.10566). The lesson says: reported
  near 97 for both groups; the accounts differ on which group had the half-point edge; the original
  is not in hand.
- **Tizard's numbers:** use Tizard 1974 (Table 2). Nisbett 2009's 103/108/106 come from a 1972 paper
  and do not match the 1974 table; do not mix them.
- **Lesson's own boundary (no repetition):** the Flynn effect, heritability by age, the within/between
  fallacy and SES ambiguity are recalled with `<Ref>` and a clause, never re-explained. Rushton and
  Jensen's ten categories and the Bell Curve's chapter 13 are `<Ref>` `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`;
  this lesson tests the adoption category only. The blood-group studies and genomic admixture are the
  next lesson's: the German study's "ancestry" comparison is by father's race, and the lesson does not
  preview DNA-based work beyond the hand-off sentence.
- **Balance check (revision-prompt rule 6):** each of sections 5 and 6 opens with its reading's
  strongest form and ends with its own authors' concession; section 7 gives each its strongest
  objection. No section closes on one side's last word without the other's reply. The lesson does not
  say which reading is better; it says what each needs to assume and what would decide between them.
  Words to avoid in the course's voice: "debunked", "refuted" (of a reading as a whole), "flawed
  study", "fails to", "merely". "Flaws acknowledged by the authors" is acceptable only as a report of
  what the authors acknowledged, with the acknowledgement quoted.
- **Provenance:** journals (*Intelligence*, *American Psychologist*, *Nature*) and any funding are
  not arguments; no provenance appears in this lesson's body. If an exercise uses provenance, it is in a
  sourcing drill and applies the same treatment to every source.
- **Group labels:** the sources' own (see the paragraph under the register rule). Never "race" as the
  course's own noun for a population in this lesson; "the children's ancestry" or the study's label.

### DOSSIER CONSTRAINTS

Status line the lesson must state, in substance verbatim (dossier §6):

> **Status: open; the data are few, small and confounded with pre-adoptive history, and the original
> authors themselves say they cannot separate genes from environment.**

And from "Overall answer" item 5: "The evidence offered against (blood-group studies, Eyferth, Tizard,
Moore, MTRAS reinterpretations) is small, old, and in places confounded." (The lesson needs the
adoption half of that sentence; the blood-group half belongs to the next lesson.)

Claims the lesson must NOT make (dossier "Claims the lessons must NOT make"), both directions:

- Hereditarian direction: "The Minnesota study proves the gap survives a good environment" — its
  authors deny it; confounded by age at adoption and placement history. Also: "The gap is X% genetic"
  for any X — including Rushton & Jensen's 50–80% or the survey mean of ~49% (here: Levin's "about
  70%" is reported only as his claim, inside the reply's quotation).
- Environmental direction: "Admixture and adoption studies show no ancestry effect" — recent genomic
  studies find small positive associations; older nulls were weak tests. Also: "Science has shown the
  gap is entirely environmental" / "genes play no role" — the APA task force said no adequate
  explanation exists. And: "SES explains the gap" — do not let the adoption gains (12–18 points within
  groups) be turned into a claim that home environment explains the group gap.

How each is refuted in the body (L13): the Minnesota claim in section 7 (norm change, attrition,
placement ordering, the authors' and a hereditarian reviewer's agreement that the study cannot decide);
the "no ancestry effect" claim in section 11 (the nulls are from small, young, screened or unread
samples; the one study with a follow-up shows an ordering; absence of a significant difference in
n = 7–25 is not evidence of no difference). Each refutation in the schema: claim, rejection, why it
fails, replacement ("open; confounded"), a discriminating example (the 16 retested White adoptees; the
Tizard power calculation).

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** name the four conditions an adoption study must meet to separate
   parentage from rearing, and for the Minnesota study say which it met.
2. **Claim to refute (E6b, hereditarian direction):** "The Minnesota study proves the gap survives a
   good environment: at seventeen, Black adoptees raised in White middle-class homes scored 89 and
   White adoptees 106." Name every step that fails (test and norm change affecting all groups; the 16
   retested White adoptees; placement histories ordered like the scores; birth-parent scores unknown;
   the authors' and a hereditarian reviewer's own conclusions) and state the claim the data do support.
3. **Claim to refute (E6b, environmental direction):** "Adoption studies show no ancestry effect: in
   the English nurseries and among the German occupation children, children of Black and White parents
   scored the same." Name what fails (ages 4½ and 5–13; n of 7–25 per cell; the German original unread
   and its accounts inconsistent; screening of fathers; the one study with a follow-up shows an
   ordering) and state the claim the data support.
4. **Research-shaped: reproduce a published re-analysis (E6d).** From the follow-up table (given in
   the exercise: White adoptees 111.5 for all 25 at seven, 117.6 for the 16 retested, 105.6 at
   seventeen; Black/white 98.5; Black/black 89.4; correlation between testings .63), compute the
   attrition-adjusted White mean and the two adjusted gaps; then state the assumption the adjustment
   makes and one reason a hereditarian reader would doubt it (16 children; a single correlation).
5. **Boundary probe (E6a):** the nurseries' institutional means (White 101.2, n = 10, SD 10.7; mixed
   109.3, n = 7, SD 8.2; Black 105.6, n = 8, SD 10.3). Estimate the standard error of the White–Black
   difference and the smallest difference the study could have called significant. What does "no
   significant difference" establish at this sample size, and what would it establish at n = 1,000 per
   group? (The computation is unfamiliar to the reader — it is the one place arithmetic serves the
   argument; the solution works it in full.)
6. **Sourcing drill and corroboration (document reasoning):** the German occupation-children study in
   five secondary accounts (the figure-6 table, reproduced). For each account: what it reports, what it
   omits, whether it cites the original or another review. Then: which claim can a careful writer make
   from the five together, and what single document would settle the half-point dispute?
7. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** the follow-up's authors list three predictions of a model in
   which only genes matter, and show the data fail them. Write the predictions of a model in which the
   gap among the adoptee groups is partly genetic and partly due to placement history, for the same
   three observations; show that the data fit both the authors' model and this one, and name the one
   measurement that would discriminate (birth parents' scores; or placement history equal across
   groups). Include in the solution the composition and placement age of the study's "Asian/Indian"
   group and what it does to the ranking argument.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`:** the 1976 comparison of adoptees with one
   versus two Black birth parents is an ancestry test with two values (roughly half versus all African
   ancestry by parentage). Say what a design gains by replacing "number of Black parents" with each
   person's measured share of ancestry read from DNA, studied in people raised by their own families;
   which part of the placement problem disappears; and which link between ancestry and environment
   would still be there (appearance and how a person is treated; family circumstances that follow
   ancestry). The solution says in one sentence that this design is the next lesson's subject, and that
   its central dispute is exactly the link the reader named, with a `<Ref>`.

9. **Attack the verdict:** The lesson holds as **strong** that no adoption study decides the cause of the
   gap. Choose one side and state its best case against the verdict: either that the Minnesota ordering,
   held across ten years and new tests, is too consistent with the national pattern to be produced by
   placement alone, or that Eyferth and Tizard, independent and both near zero, together make a real null.
   Then say what evidence would be needed to overturn the verdict for that side. The solution names the
   register's falsifier (adoption at birth, placement independent of ancestry, birth-parent scores,
   testing in adulthood) and shows that the case chosen fails at least one of the four conditions.

Mix check: retrieval 1 (≈12%); document reasoning 4, 6 (25%); synthesis and argument 2, 3, 7, 8 (50%);
one numerical boundary probe (5) justified under the numbers rule. E6 floor: claims to refute (2, 3),
derivation (7), boundary probe (5), research-shaped (4) — four of the four kinds; one warm-up. E3: 2
and 8 recall the Flynn effect and the residual gap (`us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`); 7 recalls
the default hypothesis.

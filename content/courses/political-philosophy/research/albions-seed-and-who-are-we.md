# Research brief: albions-seed-and-who-are-we (Albion's Seed and Who Are We?: America's English inheritance)

status: planned
module: nation-and-immigration
type: core
goal: Analyze Fischer's argument that four British folkways shaped American culture and Huntington's argument that an Anglo-Protestant core culture defines American identity, and evaluate the evidence and the objections

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`: Jay's claim in
  Federalist 2 that Americans were already "one united people" — same ancestors, language,
  religion, principles of government — stated as a factual claim about 1787 and left untested;
  the extended republic as a design that does not need a homogeneous people. From
  `the-nation-and-its-defenders`: the pre-political "we" (`pre-political-loyalty`), Scruton's
  unchosen home (`oikophilia`), and the fork it ended on — is the bond a culture or a set of
  institutions? From `renan-what-is-a-nation`: the `daily-plebiscite` (memory plus will, not
  race or language). From `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`: `mores` ranked above laws,
  and the New England township as their school. From `chamberlain-and-grant`: Grant's
  "native American" as Nordic, the 1924 national-origins quotas, `nordicism` — the
  ancestry-based account of American identity that both authors of this lesson reject. From
  `english-revolution-as-test` and `filmer-patriarcha`: Puritan against Royalist, and Filmer's
  patriarchal kingship — both reappear here as living cultures carried across the Atlantic.
  From `burke-reflections`: Burke as the defender of inherited English liberty (his 1775
  speech on conciliation is quoted by both authors).
- **Contributes:** Two answers to the question Jay's sentence opens — what held together a
  people who soon no longer shared ancestors? Fischer: not one English culture but four British
  regional cultures ("folkways"), carried by four migrations, each with its own idea of
  liberty, persisting because newcomers assimilate to the region they enter; America's freedom
  is the tension among the four. Huntington: one Anglo-Protestant core culture (English
  language, English institutions, dissenting Protestantism), whose product is the American
  Creed; settlers made it and immigrants joined it; a creed alone cannot hold a nation, and the
  core is now challenged by multiculturalism, weak assimilation, Mexican immigration and a
  denationalised elite. Both reject the two accounts most Americans give of themselves (a
  "nation of immigrants"; a nation defined by a creed anyone can adopt), and both reject
  ancestry. The lesson then puts the objections: the historians' (selection, the omitted
  Africans and Indians, the regions' internal variety), the ethnic-pluralist, the creed as
  self-standing, and the data on Hispanic assimilation since 2004.
- **Hands off:** (i) `america-and-the-english-decline` (next): if America's culture is
  England's inheritance, did the heir inherit the parent's decline? Huntington himself supplies
  both halves of the question — the United Kingdom "could follow the Soviet Union into
  history", yet dissenting Protestantism "faded in England" while it flourished in America.
  (ii) `powell-raspail-and-camus`: Huntington's "immigration with or without assimilation"
  and his Bosnia–California comparison are the American version of the European fear the
  lesson after next examines. (iii) `carens-open-borders`: a nation defined by its creed alone
  is the premise the open-borders argument needs; Huntington is its strongest denial.
  (iv) `miller-strangers-in-our-midst`: Miller's civic / social / cultural integration is a
  liberal-nationalist reply to Huntington's Anglo-conformity. What this lesson decides and
  does not pass on: Jay's one people is false as stated (strong), and Huntington's 2004
  forecast is judged criterion by criterion (failed on language; attachment rising with
  generation; persistent identity and the education stall partly or wholly borne out).
  What passes on is the narrower decline thesis — an elite that cannot say why its
  inheritance deserves defence — which `america-and-the-english-decline` finds open.

## VERDICT

Two claims are judged here, and the lesson states both verdicts in its own voice.

**(a) Jay's claim in Federalist 2** that Americans of 1787 were one people with the "same
ancestors … same language … same religion". Verdict: **false as stated. Grade: strong.**
Decided by Fischer's four British regional cultures, each with its own religion, rank order
and idea of liberty, and by the population Jay's sentence leaves out: Africans (about one
in six of the 1790 census), Indians outside the count, Germans in Pennsylvania, the Dutch in
New York. "Same religion" holds only at the level of "Protestant", and Huntington's own 1790
shares (estimates from surnames) put a fifth of whites outside "British". Strongest
objection: Jay meant the free, English-speaking Protestant majority that would make the
government, and on that majority his sentence is roughly true. It does not overturn the
verdict: the claim as written is about "one united people", and even within that majority
Fischer's four folkways differ on exactly the bonds Jay names (religion, manners,
principles of government). Would be overturned by: evidence that the four regional
cultures were a historian's construct, with no difference in religion, rank or law among
the four hearths in 1787.

**(b) Huntington's 2004 forecast** that Hispanic, chiefly Mexican, immigration would not
assimilate. This is a liberal assimilation forecast set against a restrictionist one, and
the record splits by criterion:
- *Language:* the forecast **failed**. English proficiency is near-universal by the second
  generation. Grade: **strong**.
- *Attachment to the United States* **rises with generation** (Citrin et al. 2007), against
  the "threat to American identity" claim. Grade: **on balance**.
- *A persistent ethnic identity, endogamy and residential concentration into the fourth
  generation* are **partly borne out**: "the majority of fourth generation Mexican Americans
  continue to live in Hispanic neighborhoods, marry other Hispanics, and think of themselves
  as Mexican" (Telles & Ortiz 2008, publisher's summary — secondary to the book; bind from
  the book before quoting). Ethnic attrition (descendants who stop identifying as Hispanic)
  may bias that sample toward those who kept the identity. Grade: **on balance**.
- *Education* stalls after the second generation and declines in the third and fourth:
  **borne out**. Grade: **on balance**.

Strongest objection to (b): the restrictionist reply that language is the easiest criterion
and identity and schooling the decisive ones, so the forecast's core held. It narrows the
verdict but does not overturn it: Huntington himself made language the test ("only if they
dream in English"), and on that test his forecast failed; on schooling it held, and the
verdict says so. From the other side: Telles and Ortiz attribute the education stall to
exclusion, not to a refusal to assimilate. That is a dispute about the cause, which the
verdict does not decide; it records the outcome Huntington forecast. Would be overturned by:
third- and fourth-generation data, corrected for ethnic attrition, showing schooling at
parity with non-Hispanic whites (against the education item), or English loss in the third
generation (against the language item).
- **Spine step:** "The live question: what a nation is and who may join it — Scruton and
  Miller on the pre-political loyalty, America as England's heir from the Federalist to Who
  Are We?, the prophecies of displacement from Powell to Camus, Carens and Miller on borders,
  Murray on Europe — and 2015 as the test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "Fewer than a fifth of Americans today have British
ancestors, yet their country's law, language, religion of the book and habits of
self-government came from Britain. A historian in 1989 argues that four waves of migrants from
four regions of seventeenth-century Britain brought four different ways of life that still
divide the country; a political scientist in 2004 argues that a single Anglo-Protestant
culture, not a creed anyone could adopt, made America what it is, and asks whether it will
survive. The lesson follows both arguments and what they say about how a people is held
together."

The newcomer's problem, stated from the previous lesson: Jay said Americans were one people
because they shared ancestors, language and religion (`<Ref>`
federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic). Within a century and a half most Americans
no longer shared those ancestors. Yet the country did not break into its ancestries. Something
other than descent held it — and the lesson's two authors say that something was a culture
brought from Britain, and disagree about whether it was one culture or four.

1. **What could hold a people that does not share ancestors?** The obvious attempts, as the
   material itself lists them (Fischer, Introduction l.662–675): the frontier made Americans
   (the frontier thesis); they were made by the mixing of many immigrant peoples (the
   "nation of immigrants", the ethnic-pluralist model); they hold one creed anyone can adopt.
   Why each fails for both authors: the frontier did not make the same culture on every
   frontier (Fischer: backcountry ways "did not develop on other frontiers", l.7601); the
   immigrant model cannot explain why newcomers ended up speaking English and voting in
   regional patterns older than their arrival; the creed account cannot say where the creed
   came from. Answer (shared): something brought by the first settlers and absorbed by later
   arrivals. Seam: both therefore revive the oldest answer — the inheritance ("germ") thesis —
   without its racial form (`<Ref>` chamberlain-and-grant: Grant had made the "native
   American" a Nordic; neither author makes identity a matter of descent).
2. **Fischer: why four cultures and not one?** Obvious attempt: the colonists were "English",
   so they brought "English culture". Fischer's answer is the definition and the four
   migrations. A folkway is "the normative structure of values, customs and meanings" of a
   culture (l.695), studied in 24 "ways" from speech and building to rank, order, power and
   freedom (l.739–747). Then derive the four from four variables the material gives: the
   region of origin (each American hearth drew about 60 percent of its migrants from one
   British region), the religion (Puritan, Anglican, Quaker, border Presbyterian and
   unchurched), the rank (middling families; a Royalist elite with servants; artisans and
   farmers; poor and proud borderers), and the moment of departure (1629–40, 1642–75,
   1675–1725, 1718–75 — each carried the quarrels of its own decade). Because the four
   variables differ together, four cultures arrive, not one. Keeper numbers: the four waves
   and dates; the 20-percent sentence.
3. **Four ideas of liberty.** This is the political core and the course's own thread. Obvious
   attempt: "liberty" means one thing, Locke's (`<Ref>` locke-property-and-consent). Fischer:
   four. *Ordered* liberty (Massachusetts): Winthrop's Little Speech (1645) — natural liberty
   "to do evil as well as good" against civil liberty "to that only which is good, just and
   honest", exercised "in a way of subjection to authority"; the 1636 exchange in which
   Massachusetts granted hereditary honour but refused hereditary power. *Hegemonic* liberty
   (Virginia): freedom as "the power to rule, and not to be overruled by others", compatible
   with slavery; Filmer's patriarchalism living on in the Virginia cousinage (`<Ref>`
   filmer-patriarcha); Burke's observation that slaveholders were the proudest of their
   freedom. *Reciprocal* liberty (Delaware Valley): Penn's golden-rule freedom extended even to
   error, the Quaker antislavery that followed from it. *Natural* liberty (backcountry): freedom
   from ordering institutions, Patrick Henry's state of nature learned "not from treatises"
   (l.7760) but from border custom, and "not a reciprocal idea" (l.7764). Then Fischer's
   conclusion (l.8740–8746): each began "noble" and "defective" — persecution, slavery,
   withdrawal, anarchy — and the plurality made American freedom "more open and expansive than
   any unitary tradition". Seam: the course has met each idea as a theory (Winthrop's
   covenant liberty in the English Revolution, Filmer's patriarchy, Locke's state of nature);
   here they are habits of four populations.
4. **Do folkways persist, and how would we know?** Obvious attempt: they faded within a
   generation of independence. Fischer's evidence that they did not: dialect regions, building
   types, the presidency by cultural ancestry, party coalitions that line up region against
   region from Washington to the 1980s, the 1964 counties that had voted antislavery a century
   earlier, the 1921 restriction bill passed "as one" by all four regions (l.8545), late
   twentieth-century homicide and tax maps that repeat the eighteenth-century order; and the
   mechanism — newcomers assimilated to the region they entered, not to a generic America
   (l.8541ff). He names five candidate explanations of persistence and does not choose one
   (l.8712). **First OBJECTION enters here, at full strength (the historians', stated as the
   course's own):** the evidence is selected to fit (the Virginia "cavaliers" were a small
   elite; most migrants to Virginia were servants, by Fischer's own count more than
   three-quarters); each region was internally divided; Africans, Indians, the Dutch, Germans
   and Scots made the culture too, and "that culture created slavery" (l.8007ff) reverses a
   causation many historians hold the other way; a 1980s map that resembles a 1750s map is a
   correlation over two centuries of migration, not a proof of transmission. The lesson
   concedes the last point most: persistence is shown as pattern, and Fischer himself leaves
   the mechanism open. What survives the objection: a regional map of liberty, which explains
   American *divisions* better than American *unity* — the question Huntington takes up.
5. **Huntington: settlers, not immigrants.** Obvious attempt: America is "a nation of
   immigrants". Huntington's reply is a distinction: settlers found a society from a compact or
   charter; immigrants join a society that already exists (l.2300–2313 — "Before immigrants
   could come to America, settlers had to found America"; the word "immigrant" entered English
   only in the 1780s). The settlers' culture is the core: the English language, English
   institutions (fundamental law limiting government, separation of powers, a two-house
   legislature, local representation, militia over standing army — Tudor patterns, l.3366ff),
   and the values of dissenting Protestantism. The counterfactual carries the argument
   (l.3284–3290): settled by French, Spanish or Portuguese Catholics, it "would be Quebec,
   Mexico, or Brazil". Sourcing seam (do it in the body, briefly): Huntington cites Fischer's
   "Albion's seed" sentence (l.2442–2452) for *one* core; Fischer wrote it for *four*.
   **Objection, at full strength:** the settler/immigrant line depends on where one stands —
   Spanish settlers founded St Augustine (1565) and Santa Fe (1610) before Jamestown's culture
   reached them; Africans arrived from 1619 as neither settlers nor immigrants; the Indians
   were there first. Huntington's reply, from the text: the doctrine of "first effective
   settlement" (l.2391) — what counts is who set the institutions of the society others later
   joined. The lesson says what the reply achieves: it answers the objection by narrowing the
   claim. "Effective" means "set the institutions later arrivals joined", so the distinction
   now records institutional precedence, which the record supports, and no longer says who
   was there first, which it never could.
6. **Where the Creed comes from.** Obvious attempt: from the Enlightenment — America as a
   Lockean, liberal fragment of Europe (the reading Huntington names as his target; scholar in
   References). Huntington: the Creed (liberty, equality, individualism, representative
   government, property — named in 1944 by a Swedish economist's study) grew out of dissenting
   Protestantism; America is "the child of the Reformation", "a process under way in 1632 when
   Locke was born"; "the American Creed … is Protestantism without God" and the civil religion
   "Christianity without Christ" (l.3794, 5698); Burke's "dissidence of dissent" (l.3553).
   Why it matters for the lesson's question: a creed can be adopted by anyone anywhere; a
   culture is joined by assimilation. Hence ch. 12 (l.17453ff): "A creed alone does not a
   nation make" — five reasons (few nations rest on ideology alone and those that did
   dissolved when coercion ended; ideologies change easily; a creed says how to govern but not
   who the members are or where the borders run; principles lack the emotional pull of kith,
   kin and culture; the Creed is itself a product of the culture, so "a multicultural America
   will, in time, become a multicreedal America"). One clause with `<Ref>` the-nation-and-its-defenders:
   this is the culture branch of that lesson's fork. One clause with `<Ref>` renan-what-is-a-nation:
   Huntington reads Renan's plebiscite as the daily renewal of an inherited past, "a nation with
   the soul of a church" — not a contract.
7. **Culture, not race — the distinction Huntington insists on.** Obvious misreading (refute,
   L13): "an Anglo-Protestant core means a white or British-descended America". Huntington's
   own text: culture is changeable, ancestry is not (ch. 2; Kallen's "cannot change their
   grandfathers" is the view he rejects); Germany and Japan changed their cultures after 1945;
   the core is open to descendants of any ancestry who adopt it — Americans "need not choose
   between a WASP ethnic identity and an abstract civic identity". Contrast with Grant (`<Ref>`
   chamberlain-and-grant): Grant's America was a breed and immigration a biological
   replacement; Huntington's is a culture and immigration a question of assimilation. Report,
   as fact, that Huntington also forecasts a white nativist reaction (ch. 11) and does not
   endorse it.
8. **Will the core survive? The 2004 diagnosis, then the record.** Huntington's challenges:
   the "deconstructionists" (group rights, bilingualism, multiculturalism; ch. 7); weaker
   assimilation (ch. 8: "immigration with or without assimilation" l.9517 — converts,
   sojourners, "ampersands" with dual loyalties; the nine past facilitators — dispersion, the
   1924–65 pause, war service, the Americanization movement — all weakened); Mexican
   immigration's six differentia (contiguity, numbers, illegality, regional concentration,
   persistence, historical presence) and its assimilation measured on six criteria (language,
   education, occupation and income, citizenship, intermarriage, identity — Tables 9.1–9.2);
   the elite's denationalization against a patriotic public (ch. 10); the closing sentence
   ("only if they dream in English"); the three futures — cosmopolitan, imperial, national
   (l.18970). **Second OBJECTION, at full strength (empirical; scholars in References):** the
   data after 2004. Studies that tested the claim found English acquired by the second
   generation and near-universal by the third, attachment to the United States rising with
   generation, intermarriage high; one long panel study found the educational gap stalling
   across generations — partial support for Huntington on one criterion, not on language or
   identity. **Third OBJECTION (normative, from the ethnic-pluralist side, inside his own
   sources):** the inheritance he praises carried hierarchy as well as freedom (Fischer's own
   four defects; the 1790 "free white persons" law Huntington himself reports), so a culture
   defined by its settlers' core is not neutral among the people who later joined it; the
   Creed can be held as a standard against that culture, as the civil-rights movement held it.
   Huntington's reply, from the text: the Creed's universalism is itself the culture's product
   and survives only while the culture does. The lesson says what that reply does and does not
   answer: it answers the claim that the Creed can stand without the culture (a causal claim
   the course does not test here), and it does not answer the charge that the terms of joining
   were set by one group — his text leaves that charge standing. The data are weighed in
   link 9.
9. **The weighing: Jay's one people was false as stated, and Huntington's forecast failed on
   language and held on schooling.** Set each claim against its strongest objection and the
   evidence, as in ## VERDICT. (a) Jay: the four folkways (links 2–4), the 1790 census (about
   one in six enslaved) and Huntington's own shares; the objection that Jay meant the
   Protestant majority, and why it fails (the four regions differ on the very bonds he names).
   Verdict: false as stated, **strong**. (b) Huntington's six criteria (figure 6) against the
   post-2004 record, criterion by criterion: language failed (**strong**); attachment rises
   with generation (**on balance**); identity, endogamy and concentration into the fourth
   generation partly borne out, with the attrition caveat (**on balance**); education stalls
   and declines (**on balance**). The restrictionist objection (language is the easy test) and
   the exclusion objection (the stall has another cause) each in two sentences, and why
   neither moves the outcome recorded. Then the frame the two authors give it: Fischer — a
   people held by inherited practices that newcomers enter region by region, plural and freer
   for being plural; Huntington — by one culture that newcomers join, singular and fragile once
   joining stops. Both against ancestry, both against "creed alone"; the record so far shows
   joining by language and attachment and lagging by schooling, which is neither author's
   prediction in full.

   **Conclusion.** State the verdicts in plain words: Jay's sentence was false as stated
   (strong); Huntington's forecast failed on language (strong); attachment to the United
   States rises with generation, against his "threat" claim (on balance); and it was partly or wholly borne out on persistent identity and on schooling (on
   balance each). Hand-off: if the culture came from England, and England has since lost much
   of what it gave (Huntington, l.3295–3302, 3366–3373), does the heir inherit the loss? The
   next lesson judges the broad decline forecast and finds the narrow one — an elite that
   cannot say why its inheritance deserves defence — open.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first use (L11), no more than three per section:** *folkway* (Fischer's
  term, defined); *Puritan* (English Calvinists who wanted the Church of England purified;
  Massachusetts 1630s); *cavalier* (supporter of Charles I in the Civil War — `<Ref>`
  english-revolution-as-test); *indentured servant* (bound to labour for a term, usually four
  to seven years, in exchange for passage); *Quaker* (the Society of Friends; inner light, no
  clergy, refusal of oaths and of hat honour); *borderers* (people of the Anglo-Scottish border
  and Ulster; "Scotch-Irish" is the American name Fischer rejects); *town meeting*, *vestry*,
  *General Court*; *Great Migration* (1629–40); *the American Creed*; *civil religion*;
  *Anglo-conformity* ("tomato soup"); *ampersand* (Huntington's word for a migrant of dual
  attachment); *Bilingual Education Act* (1968); *Proposition 187* (California 1994 measure
  denying public services to illegal immigrants); *matrícula consular*. Places: East Anglia,
  the North Midlands, the Delaware Valley, the Appalachian backcountry — a locator figure
  (FIGURE PLAN 1).
- **Who Are We? is read in full (index 76).** Any surviving phrase in other briefs that it was
  "not obtained" is wrong; this lesson introduces `anglo-protestant-core` and
  `american-creed`.
- **The real misreadings (L13), one slot each at most:** (a) "Huntington defines America
  racially" — refuted from ch. 2 and ch. 4 as in link 7; (b) "Fischer says Americans are
  English" — he says four distinct British regional cultures, and more plural than any single
  one. No invented strawmen beyond these.
- **Contested claims taught as arguments (co-contested):** the settler/immigrant distinction,
  the Creed's Protestant origin, the Mexican assimilation lag, persistence of folkways. Each is
  stated at full strength, then met with its objection. Huntington discloses his own stance
  ("patriot and scholar", l.521); the lesson reports it, it does not use it to dismiss him.
- **Data are dated.** Huntington's figures stop around 2000–2003 (2000 census, Gallup, WVS
  1990s). The body must not present them as current; the objection paragraph supplies later
  findings with their dates and sources. Fischer's "today" is 1988–89.
- **Huntington's numbers to verify:** the 1790 shares (60/80/98 percent) are estimates from
  surname studies — say "estimates"; Table 3.1 is garbled in OCR; the ISSP and WVS rankings are
  1990s surveys.
- **Fischer's own words that may jar** ("cavalier", "the warrior ethic", border "macocracy"):
  his terms, quoted as his. Period racial terms in quoted sources: quote accurately with
  provenance, never paraphrase into modern labels.
- **Do not re-teach:** Jay's Federalist 2 (Ref + clause), Tocqueville's mores and township
  (Ref + clause — Fischer's claim that the town meeting was transplanted from East Anglia may
  be set against Tocqueville's view of the township in one sentence), Renan's plebiscite, Grant's
  Nordicism, Filmer's patriarchy, the Civil War (`<Ref>` english-revolution-as-test for the
  English one).
- **Do not run ahead:** the UK-decomposition passage (ch. 1, l.970–1000), the "faded in England"
  remark used as a thesis about decline, and the Bosnia–California comparison belong to the
  next two lessons; here they are at most a forward clause.
- **Register.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right. This applies to
  "nativist", "deconstructionist", "multiculturalist" (Huntington's words — attribute them)
  and to the reviews of Huntington (report them as positions, with their evidence).
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — the four migrations' dates; "less than 20 percent"; Huntington's
  1790 shares (as estimates). Everything else in tables or the figure. Exact days only for
  Winthrop's speech if needed (3 July 1645 is not a contingency point: decade is enough).
- **Dossier thin spots:** the objection literature is outside the dossier (named above);
  Tocqueville's "point of departure" chapter (Democracy in America I.2) is not in the dossier
  (the staged shard covers the Introduction, ch. III–IV only) — recall Tocqueville only through
  the earlier lesson.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (the only mechanical one):** for each of the four migrations give the
   British source region, the American hearth, the decade of departure and the idea of
   liberty Fischer assigns to it.
2. **Document reasoning — the sourcing drill on Winthrop's Little Speech (1645):** metadata
   (the deputy governor, speaking to the General Court after his own impeachment trial was
   dropped; a Puritan audience). Predict what such a speaker in that setting would say about
   liberty; then read: what the speech RECORDS (an official doctrine of liberty), ARGUES
   (liberty as freedom to do good under authority), OMITS (the people it excluded — dissenters
   banished from the colony); what it can be USED to establish about Massachusetts and what it
   cannot establish about later New England.
3. **Corroboration (E3, from `federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`):** Jay (1787),
   Fischer's ancestry figures, and Huntington's 1790 shares on the question "were Americans one
   people in 1787?" Set the three side by side, state where they agree (British and Protestant
   majority among whites) and where they disagree (one culture or four), and write a
   one-paragraph judgment with its evidence.
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** apply Huntington's settler/immigrant distinction to two cases it
   was not built for — Spanish settlers in New Mexico (1598–1610) and Africans brought to
   Virginia from 1619. Which category does each fall into, and what does the "first effective
   settlement" doctrine have to assume to keep the distinction? The solution shows the
   distinction holds only if "effective" means "set the institutions later arrivals joined",
   and says what that concedes.
5. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Huntington's Anglo-Protestant core is an ethnic definition of
   America: only the descendants of the settlers can belong to it." Name the step that fails
   (culture vs ancestry, ch. 2; the core joined by assimilation; the explicit rejection of
   Kallen's "grandfathers"), state what Huntington does claim, and say which objection to him
   survives the correction (that the culture's terms of joining are set by one group).
6. **Research-shaped (E6d):** design the test of one of Huntington's six criteria with data
   published after 2004 (for example, English use at home by generation for Mexican Americans
   in the American Community Survey). State the measure, the comparison groups (first,
   second, third generation; other immigrant groups), what result would support his 2004
   forecast and what would refute it, and one confounder (selective return migration; ethnic
   attrition — later-generation descendants who stop identifying as Hispanic). End with what the
   design can and cannot establish.
7. **Synthesis — counter-argument stem:** "America's liberty was never one idea." Write the
   claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph for it from Fischer, then name two facts Huntington would
   say it fails to account for (the shared English language and institutions all four regions
   used; the one Creed all four came to profess) and state what would have to be true for
   Fischer's claim to survive them.
8. **Attack the verdict:** State the strongest case that Huntington's forecast held — that
   identity, endogamy, residential concentration and schooling, not language, are what decide
   whether a people has been joined, and that Telles and Ortiz's fourth generation shows all
   four lagging. Then say what evidence would be needed to overturn the verdict that the
   forecast failed on language and attachment: name the measure (e.g. English use at home and
   national attachment by generation in a survey that follows descendants who stop
   identifying as Hispanic), the comparison group, and the result that would reverse each
   item. The solution grants that the education item already stands with Huntington, shows
   why ethnic attrition cuts both ways (it inflates measured persistence of identity and may
   understate measured schooling), and states that the language verdict would fall only with
   English loss in the third generation, which no study reports.
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `america-and-the-english-decline`:** Huntington writes that the
   dissenting Protestantism the settlers brought "faded in England" while it took "new life"
   in America, and that the Tudor institutions "fundamentally changed in the United Kingdom"
   but persisted in the United States. In the same book he wonders whether the United Kingdom
   might "follow the Soviet Union into history". From these three remarks, construct the two
   opposite answers to the question "if America is England's heir, will it share England's
   decline?" The solution says the next lesson assembles the first answer (the heir carries the
   cause) from several writers and tests it against the second (the heir kept what the parent
   lost), with a `<Ref>`.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The whole module, by concept id: `anacyclosis` and
  `checks-and-balances`, with Polybius's reading of Roman religion as the
  thing "which keeps the Roman commonwealth together" and the oath that keeps
  Roman officials honest (`polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`, VI.56);
  `res-publica`, `populus`, `natural-law` and Cicero's civil religion — "no
  new gods or alien gods unless recognized by the State", the gods as
  witnesses of oaths, virtues deified (all `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`,
  whose Laws II hand-off paragraph this lesson cashes);
  `hellenization` and the Church as "the Roman Empire consecrated by the
  Gospel" (`hellenization-of-christianity`); `representation-existential`,
  `civil-theology`, `political-theology`, Celsus's warning and Eusebius's
  one-emperor theology (`constantine-and-representation`); `two-cities` and
  XIX.17's "the heavenly city preserves the diverse laws and customs… so long
  only as no hindrance to the worship of the one true God"
  (`augustine-two-cities`). The reader has met Ambrose in one sentence
  (Voegelin's "serve" formula, previous-but-one lesson) and Symmachus not at
  all.
- **Contributes:** One move: two real documents of 384 — a pagan prefect's
  petition and a bishop's two letters to a thirteen-year-old emperor — are
  read with the module's tools, and each tool is judged by whether it
  predicts what the documents say and do. Civil theology predicts Symmachus
  almost sentence for sentence; existential representation predicts Ambrose's
  "you serve God" and nothing of his threats; natural law is claimed by both
  sides in opposite senses and decides nothing; the two cities (written
  forty years later) describe neither party's conduct; and the record
  departs from every idealisation — a bishop who threatens, an emperor who
  negotiates, a cult that outlived its altar. The module question is then
  answered: the documents bear out the civil-theology account of what held
  Rome together, and both sides held it — the Christian victory replaced
  Rome's god without separating the city from public worship (**strong**).
- **Hands off:** The module closes, having decided its question: what held
  the commonwealth together was a public worship, and 384 changed which
  worship, not whether there was one (**strong**; see VERDICT). What passes
  on is the third body the verdict leaves standing — a Church with its own
  sanction over the ruler. The forward lines are: a Church that
  claims the emperor "within" it and not above (Aquinas's law above kings,
  `aquinas-law-and-kingship`); the question whether a state can live on
  diverse cults or needs one (Bodin's politique toleration,
  `bodin-sovereignty`); the Symmachus argument that a people's ancestral
  rites are the condition of its existence, which returns as Burke's
  inheritance and Scruton's home (no names in the body). The spine moves to
  "Christendom builds a law above kings".
- **Spine step:** "A Jewish movement becomes Europe's religion by being remade
  in Greek concepts and Roman forms; Constantine's empire and the Church fight
  over who represents the truth; Augustine denies that any earthly city can be
  just"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):**
  - *Artefact:* the 384 exchange — Symmachus, Relatio 3 (the petition of the
    prefect of Rome to Valentinian II, Theodosius and Arcadius), and Ambrose
    of Milan, Epistles 17 (to Valentinian, before the consistory heard the
    petition) and 18 (the point-by-point reply once Ambrose had obtained the
    text). NOT in the dossier; pointers and licence below.
  - *Module results used:* `civil-theology` (Polybius/Varro/Cicero: the
    city's gods as the condition of its existence, religion as what holds
    the commonwealth and keeps oaths — Symmachus's whole case);
    `representation-existential` (Ambrose Ep. 17: all men serve the emperor,
    the emperor serves God — temporal power de-divinized); `political-theology`
    (what Ambrose does NOT argue: no "one God, one emperor"; and what he
    does: the emperor is a Christian who happens to hold the office);
    `natural-law` (Symmachus: custom and the one secret reached by many
    roads; Ambrose: truth known by God's voice against conjecture — the same
    appeal to something above statute, pointing opposite ways; and Cicero's
    "no new gods unless the State recognizes them" now cutting against the
    old gods); `two-cities` (XIX.17 tested: did the heavenly city preserve
    Rome's diverse customs "whereby earthly peace is secured", or call them a
    hindrance to worship?); `hellenization` (the bishop arguing like a
    Roman advocate, the Church as Empire's heir).
  - *Departures from the idealisation the lesson must name:* (1) Ambrose
    threatens excommunication-by-withdrawal of the clergy (Ep. 17: the emperor
    may come to church and find no bishop, or one who resists) — the
    "service" formula is backed by a sanction Voegelin's account of
    de-divinized rule does not mention; (2) the emperor negotiates: a boy
    with a divided court, a usurper (Maximus) in Gaul, a mostly Christian
    senate and a pagan prefect, who refuses in 384 but whose successors are
    petitioned again (391, 393) — representation is a bargaining position,
    not a doctrine; (3) the cult outlived its altar: Symmachus and the pagan
    senators kept petitioning for a decade; Eugenius (393) partly conceded;
    Prudentius was still refuting Symmachus in verse c. 402; the "do-ut-des"
    reasoning returned in 410 as the pagan charge against Christianity
    (`<Ref>` `augustine-two-cities`); (4) Ambrose's "cheap argument" from the
    harvest of 384 is the same kind of argument as Symmachus's from the famine
    of 383 — the Christian bishop reasons do-ut-des when it suits; (5) the
    "one road" argument for tolerance is made by the party that had enforced
    its own cult by law for four centuries, and Ambrose says so; (6) XIX.17
    is written forty years AFTER the case it is tested on, by a man who knew
    the outcome.

## VERDICT

Module verdict (research/verdicts/REGISTER.md, module 2), passed in this
lesson. Module question: what holds a commonwealth together — its
constitution, its justice, or its gods — and what happened to that bond when
Rome's gods were replaced?

- **Claim judged:** the civil-theology account — that Rome's commonwealth was
  held together by its public worship (Polybius VI.56, Varro, Cicero Laws
  II) — tested on the documents of 384.
- **Verdict: borne out, and held by both sides. Grade: strong.** Symmachus
  argues that the ancestral cult is the condition of Rome's existence
  (Relatio 3 §9–10: "this worship subdued the world to my laws"). Ambrose
  does not answer that the commonwealth can do without a public worship; he
  answers that it must have the right one. He reasons do-ut-des himself (the
  good harvest of 384 against Symmachus's famine of 383, Ep. 18 §17–21), and
  he backs "the emperor serves God" (Ep. 17 §1) with the threat that the
  emperor will find no bishop in his church (Ep. 17 §13). The Christian
  victory replaced Rome's god; it did not separate the city from worship.
  Augustine's later "the heavenly city preserves the diverse laws and
  customs of the nations" (XIX.17) describes neither party's conduct in
  384. Natural law decides nothing in the case, because both sides claim it
  in opposite senses (Relatio 3 §10, "one road"; Ep. 18 §8, truth by God's
  voice).
- **Strongest objection:** the record shows bargaining — a boy emperor, a
  divided court, a usurper in Gaul — so the outcome may reflect power, not
  either theory. It does not overturn the verdict: the bargaining was over
  the cult itself and lasted a decade (391, 393, Prudentius c. 402), and
  both sides acted as if the public worship were what the commonwealth
  turned on.
- **What would overturn it:** evidence that the court treated the altar as a
  pure patronage question, indifferent to doctrine.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: in 382 a Christian emperor
removed a pagan altar from the Senate house and cut off the funds for the old
rites; two years later the prefect of Rome petitioned the boy emperor to put
it back, and the bishop of Milan wrote two letters telling him he could not;
both texts survive; the lesson reads them against everything the module has
argued.

1. **What exactly was removed, and why did it matter?** The obvious attempt:
   a statue. Anchor the object: an altar and a gilded statue of Victoria
   (Victory, winged, from Tarentum) placed in the Curia by Augustus after
   Actium (29 BC), at which senators burned incense and swore their oath of
   loyalty to the emperor at the start of each session. Removed once by
   Constantius II in 357, restored by Julian (361), left by his successors,
   removed again by Gratian in 382 — together with the state stipends of the
   Vestals and priests and the temple lands, and (it is reported) the
   emperor's own refusal of the title pontifex maximus. Then the module's
   first tool: this is Cicero's civil theology in its most literal form
   (`<Ref>` `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`: the gods made "members" of the citizens'
   association "as judges or witnesses" of oaths; the state alone recognizes
   gods). Taking the altar away did not forbid anyone's worship; it removed
   the god from the place where the city's representatives swore. In the
   vocabulary of `constantine-and-representation` (`<Ref>`): the empire's
   transcendental representation was changing in the room where its
   existential representation sat. Anchors: Gratian (emperor in the West
   367–383, murdered at Lyon by Maximus's men in 383); Valentinian II (his
   half-brother, emperor at thirteen, court at Milan, Arian mother Justina);
   Theodosius (emperor in the East, 379–395); Quintus Aurelius Symmachus
   (c. 345–402, senator, orator, prefect of the city of Rome in 384);
   Ambrose (c. 340–397, a provincial governor made bishop of Milan by
   acclamation in 374).
2. **What did Symmachus ask for, and on what grounds?** Read the Relatio in
   order, metadata first (the sourcing drill: author, date, audience — three
   emperors, in form; one boy, in fact — purpose, what it omits). The
   requests: restore the altar; restore the stipends and the exemptions of
   the Vestals and priests; restore the temple lands to their trusts. The
   grounds, in his order: (a) ancestral custom — "we ask for peace for the
   gods of our fathers and of our country"; (b) Rome personified: respect my
   years, "let me use the ancestral ceremonies, for I do not repent of them…
   this worship subdued the world to my laws, these rites repelled Hannibal
   from the walls and the Senones from the Capitol"; (c) the many roads:
   "it is just that all worship be considered as one; we look on the same
   stars, the sky is common… What difference does it make by what wisdom each
   seeks the truth? We cannot attain to so great a secret by one road"
   (uno itinere non potest perveniri ad tam grande secretum); (d) the famine
   of 383 as sacrilege's wage — "the public famine" followed the confiscation;
   (e) the legal point: endowments given by the dying are property, and the
   fisc that seizes them steals. Then the tool: every one of these is Cicero
   Laws II in petition form — ancestral rites preserved, the gods the
   condition of the city's deeds, Delphi's "keep the best of your ancestors'
   customs". **Civil theology predicts the document.** Note too what it does
   not say: nothing about the truth of the gods, everything about Rome's
   existence and the sanctity of property.
3. **Why does the "one road" argument not win?** The obvious reading today casts Symmachus as pleading for tolerance and
   Ambrose as refusing it. Put the "one
   road" sentence at full strength — it is the most quoted line of the whole
   affair — and then let Ambrose answer it on two levels. Factually (Ep. 17,
   18): restoring the altar means every senator present at the oath,
   Christian senators included, is made party to a sacrifice; the Christian
   majority of the senate protested and was not consulted; the "tolerance"
   asks the state to fund one cult, not to permit many. On principle (Ep.
   18): "what you do not know, we know from the voice of God; what you seek
   by conjecture, we have found" — i.e. the one-road argument assumes the
   secret is unreached, and the Christian claims it has been reached. The
   lesson's own observation: the appeal to "one secret, many roads" is an
   appeal above statute to something like a natural order of worship — and
   Ambrose's appeal to a truth above Rome's customs is also an appeal above
   statute. **Natural law is claimed by both** (`natural-law` reinforced), in
   Symmachus's form as the ancestral and customary, in Ambrose's as the
   revealed and universal — the Laelius/Philus axis (`<Ref>` `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`: custom varies, true law is
   one) turned so that the
   pagan holds the custom end and the Christian the one-law end. Cicero's own
   clause, "no new gods unless recognized by the State", now cuts against
   the old gods, because the state has changed its recognition.
4. **What did Ambrose actually argue — and does representation predict it?**
   Ep. 17's principle: "as all men under Roman rule serve you, so you serve
   almighty God and the holy faith." Set beside the module's tool (`<Ref>`
   `constantine-and-representation`): the subjects' service makes the emperor
   their existential representative; above that temporal sphere he serves
   God — not as God's image (no Eusebian "one God, one emperor"), but as a
   Christian who holds an office. **Existential representation predicts the
   formula exactly, and political theology is absent** — Ambrose never
   argues that monarchy mirrors God; he argues that this emperor is baptised.
   Then Ep. 18's answers to Rome's speech: the gods did not save Rome at the
   Allia or from Hannibal — "the legions make Victoria, not Victoria the
   Empire" (the line Voegelin paraphrases "contemptuously"); the famine was
   everywhere and the next harvest was good; Rome does not blush to change
   in her old age, as the world has; progress — day begins dim and brightens.
   Observe the register: a Roman advocate's point-by-point *refutatio*, with
   Livy's history quoted back at a Livian. The Church arguing as the Empire's
   heir (`hellenization` reinforced: Harnack's "Roman Empire consecrated by
   the Gospel", `<Ref>`).
5. **First departure: the bishop who threatens.** Ep. 17 ends with a
   sanction: if the emperor grants the petition, the bishops will not
   tolerate it; "you may come to the church, but you will find no priest
   there, or you will find one who resists you". The tool said the emperor
   *serves* God; the document says a bishop will withdraw the Church from an
   emperor who disobeys a bishop. State the departure precisely: the
   de-divinization of temporal power (`<Ref>`) is here enforced by a new
   power with its own sanction — excommunication — which the 384 documents
   introduce into imperial politics and which the Varro/Cicero theory, the
   Eusebian theory and Voegelin's "service" formula all leave out. The 386
   line "the emperor is within the Church, not above it" (Sermon against
   Auxentius §36 — date and occasion in half a sentence) names what 384 did.
   Harnack's frame (`<Ref>`): the conscientious Christian emperor is bound
   to intolerance — true, but the documents show the *bishop* supplying the
   conscience.
6. **Second departure: the emperor who negotiates.** The idealisations — a
   society represented by its ruler, a ruler who serves God — have one actor
   on each side. The record has a thirteen-year-old with an Arian mother, a
   consistory divided, a usurper in Gaul whose recognition he needed, a
   senate mostly Christian and a pagan prefect, a bishop who was also the
   court's best diplomat (Ambrose had gone to Maximus as Valentinian's envoy
   in 383). Valentinian refused in 384; the petition came back in 391 (to
   Valentinian, refused), in 393 (to the usurper Eugenius, who gave temple
   funds to pagan senators as private gifts — Ambrose's Ep. 57 protests), and
   fell with Eugenius at the Frigidus in 394. **Representation is a
   bargaining position, not a doctrine**: who represented Roman society in
   384 was being decided by whose petitions were granted. The sourcing
   drill's last question — "how does provenance change what the source can
   be USED to establish?" — applies to Ambrose's own letters: a bishop's
   account of his own victory.
7. **Third departure: the cult that outlived its altar.** From "Ambrose won"
   (Voegelin's phrase) one expects the end of Roman paganism. The record:
   Symmachus consul in 391; the Theodosian laws of 391–392 banning sacrifice
   needed because sacrifice continued; a Christian poet still refuting
   Symmachus's Relatio stanza by stanza c. 402 (Prudentius, Contra
   Symmachum); and in 410 the pagan charge that Rome fell because the rites
   were abandoned — Symmachus's do-ut-des argument from the famine, now about
   the sack — which is what the City of God was written to answer (`<Ref>`
   `augustine-two-cities`). The ancestral cult of a city, the lesson notes,
   does not die when its funding does; it dies when its *people* change what
   they love — Augustine's second definition (`<Ref>` XIX.24), which was
   written to explain exactly this.
8. **The two cities tested — does XIX.17 describe 384?** Augustine's rule:
   the heavenly city preserves the nations' diverse laws and institutions
   "whereby earthly peace is secured… so long only as no hindrance to the
   worship of the one supreme and true God is thus introduced". Apply it:
   was the Altar of Victory a diversity "whereby earthly peace is secured"
   (Symmachus: it is the condition of Rome's existence) or a hindrance to
   worship (Ambrose: Christian senators compelled to attend a sacrifice)? The
   documents answer the question of fact in Ambrose's favour on the oath
   (participation was compelled) and in Symmachus's on the stipends and
   lands (no Christian was made to worship by a Vestal's pension). XIX.17
   therefore predicts a split verdict — remove the altar, leave the
   endowments — which is NOT what happened: Gratian took everything, and
   Ambrose defended taking everything, arguing from the fisc and from
   "idolatry" rather than from hindrance. Name the departure: **the two
   cities describe neither party's conduct in 384**: neither side proposed
   a shared earthly peace with no shared religion — Symmachus asked for the
   old public cult, Ambrose for a Christian ruler who funds none but the
   true one. Written forty years later by a man who knew the outcome, XIX.17
   is calmer than either 384 document and describes a city neither party
   wanted.
   Augustine's own happy emperor (V.24) "makes his power the handmaid of
   God's majesty"; Ambrose's emperor is told what that handmaid may fund.
9. **THE WEIGHING — "Both sides held that Rome stood on its public worship;
   384 changed the god, not the bond."** Lay the four frames against the
   two texts in a table (set piece 8). Civil theology: predicts Symmachus wholly —
   and, the decisive point, predicts Ambrose too: he never argues that the
   commonwealth can do without a public worship, only that it must have the
   right one; he argues do-ut-des from the harvest of 384 and backs "you
   serve God" with the threat of an empty church. Representation: predicts
   Ambrose's formula and the de-divinized emperor; misses the bishop's
   sanction. Natural law: claimed by both in opposite senses, so it decides
   nothing in the case. Two cities: describes neither party's conduct, and
   is written after the fact. Then the strongest objection at full
   strength — the outcome was bargaining among a boy emperor, a divided
   court and a usurper, so power, not any theory, decided it — and the
   answer: the bargaining was over the cult itself, it went on for a decade
   (391, 393, Prudentius c. 402), and both sides acted throughout as if the
   public worship were what the commonwealth turned on. Verdict in the
   course's voice: the civil-theology account of what held Rome together is
   borne out, and held by both parties (**strong**); the Christian victory
   replaced Rome's god and kept the city bound to a worship. Say what would
   overturn it: evidence that the court treated the altar as a patronage
   question, indifferent to doctrine. What the verdict leaves standing, for
   the next module: the body that now held the worship — a Church with its
   own sanction over the ruler, which no theory in the module predicted.

Headings that fall out: "An altar, a statue, an oath: what Gratian removed";
"Symmachus's petition: the city's gods as the condition of the city";
"Many roads to one secret — and why it did not win"; "Ambrose's principle:
you serve God"; "A bishop who threatens"; "An emperor who negotiates"; "A
cult that outlived its altar"; "The two cities tested on 384"; "Both sides held
that Rome stood on its public worship; 384 changed the god, not the bond".
The `<Conclusion>` states the verdict in plain words with its grade — what
held Rome together was a public worship, both the pagan prefect and the
Christian bishop argued as if it were, and the Christian victory replaced
the god without freeing the city from worship (strong) — then hands off:
the next module opens with that worship's new keeper, a Church that claims
a law above kings (`aquinas-law-and-kingship`).

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The altar's own history as a timeline with spans** (29 BC placed by
   Augustus; 357 removed by Constantius; 361 restored by Julian; 382 removed
   by Gratian; 384, 391, 393 petitions; 394 Frigidus; 410 sack). Anchors the
   whole lesson; the reader sees the object's life before the argument.
2. **Rome's speech in Symmachus** (Relatio 3 §9–10): "respect my years… let
   me use the ancestral ceremonies, for I do not repent of them… this worship
   subdued the world to my laws, these rites repelled Hannibal from the walls
   and the Senones from the Capitol." Quote short (PD, de Romestin), then
   Ambrose's Rome answering (Ep. 18 §7): she blushes not to change; the geese,
   not the gods, saved the Capitol; the legions won. The same personification
   made to say opposite things — the lesson's central document-reasoning
   exercise done in front of the reader.
3. **"We cannot attain to so great a secret by one road"** (Relatio 3 §10)
   parsed clause by clause: same stars / same sky / different wisdoms / one
   secret — then Ambrose's reply (Ep. 18 §8) parsed the same way: not known /
   known by God's voice; sought by conjecture / found by truth. Two appeals
   above statute, pointing opposite ways.
4. **Ambrose's "serve" sentence** (Ep. 17 §1) set beside the Mongol Order of
   God (`<Ref>` `constantine-and-representation`, one clause) and Eusebius's
   "one God, one emperor": three formulas for ruler and God, and only one of
   them speaks of service rather than rule.
5. **The threat** (Ep. 17 §13): "you may come to the church, but you will find
   no priest there, or you will find one who resists you." Quote exactly (PD),
   then name what kind of act it is in the module's terms: a sanction the
   theory of de-divinized rule did not include.
6. **Famine and harvest as do-ut-des on both sides** (Relatio 3 §15–17; Ep.
   18 §17–21): Symmachus — sacrilege caused the famine; Ambrose — the famine
   was general and this year's harvest is abundant, so the gods were not the
   cause. Show that Ambrose's refutation accepts the premise (harvests show
   divine favour) to deny the conclusion; Voegelin's "cheap argument". Say
   what that makes it: not a refutation of do-ut-des reasoning but the same
   reasoning pointed the other way — evidence for the module verdict that
   both sides held the commonwealth to depend on its worship.
7. **The Vestals' stipends** (Relatio 3 §11–14; Ep. 18 §11–16): seven virgins
   with privileges and public pay vs the Church's unpaid virgins; Symmachus on
   the sanctity of legacies vs Ambrose on the fisc's right. The only part of
   the exchange about *property*, and the part XIX.17's "earthly peace" test
   actually turns on.
8. **The four frames × two documents grid**, filled in the lesson's last
   section (civil theology / representation / natural law / two cities ×
   Symmachus / Ambrose / what happened), with the departures in the margin.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know — anchor at first mention:** Curia (the
  Senate house in the Forum; the brick hall standing today is Diocletian's
  rebuilding); Victoria (the goddess Victory; her altar and statue); prefect
  of the city (the emperor's governor of Rome, a senator); consistory (the
  emperor's council); Vestals (six, not seven in most sources — Symmachus
  says seven; the priestesses of the hearth-goddess, state-maintained);
  pontifex maximus; Arian (the reader has Arius from
  `constantine-and-representation`; `<Ref>`); Milan (the Western court's
  seat in the 380s, not Rome); Maximus (Magnus Maximus, usurper in Britain
  and Gaul 383–388); Eugenius (usurper 392–394); the Frigidus (the battle in
  394 where Theodosius destroyed Eugenius); the Senones and the Allia (the
  Gauls who sacked Rome in 390 BC; the geese of the Capitol); Hannibal at the
  walls (211 BC); fisc (the imperial treasury); Prudentius (Spanish Christian
  poet, c. 400); excommunication (exclusion from the sacraments — explain in
  a clause; the word may not be in Ambrose, the thing is).
- **The documents are not in the dossier.** The writer must fetch NPNF 2.10
  (CCEL/New Advent) and verify every quotation against de Romestin's text;
  the wordings given above are the brief-writer's recollection and MUST be
  checked. Keep each quotation to one or two sentences.
- **"The emperor is within the Church, not above it" is from 386, not 384**
  (Sermon against Auxentius §36). Course.yaml's key_idea attaches it to the
  exchange; the lesson may use it only with its date and occasion, or
  substitute Ep. 17's "serve" formula. Report this as a grounding gap.
- **Gratian and the pontifex maximus title:** Voegelin states it; the main
  source is Zosimus (a hostile pagan historian c. 500) and modern scholars
  (Cameron 2011) doubt it. Write "is reported to have refused".
- **"Pagan party" and "pagan revival":** Voegelin's narrative (and much
  older scholarship) makes 384–394 paganism's last battle; the revisionist
  view holds the senate was already largely Christian and the "revival"
  slight. Present the petition as the act of a senatorial minority with
  Symmachus as its advocate, as Ambrose himself says (Ep. 17: the Christian
  senators are the majority) — which is the primary evidence — and leave the
  historiography to References.
- **Register.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Do not moralise in either direction:** the lesson casts neither man as
  hero or villain; the lesson's job is to say what each asked,
  on what grounds, and which theory predicts it. "No some-believe strawmen":
  the "one road" argument is given at full strength and answered on its own
  terms.
- **The numbering of Ambrose's letters** differs between NPNF (17, 18, 57) and
  the modern CSEL edition (72, 73, 72a for the Relatio; Ep. 57 = extra coll.
  10). Give both in References; use NPNF's in the body since that is the text
  the reader can open.
- **Do not re-teach** Varro, Celsus, Eusebius, Harnack's "Empire consecrated",
  Scipio's definition, or XIX.17's derivation — each a `<Ref>` plus a clause.
  This lesson has nothing new to introduce (course.yaml: `introduces: []`)
  and everything to apply.
- **Numbers:** keepers — 382 (removal), 384 (the exchange), 391 (Theodosian
  ban). Seven Vestals and the famine are Symmachus's figures, quoted as his.
- **Names only in References:** de Romestin (translator), Voegelin (except as
  author of the representation tool via `<Ref>`), Harnack (here a scholar;
  `<Ref>` the earlier lesson instead), Lilla, Cameron, McLynn, Sheridan,
  Barrow, Fortin, Zosimus (may be named as the *source* of the pontifex
  report in a text-condition sentence). Symmachus, Ambrose, Augustine,
  Prudentius, Cicero, Varro, Celsus, Eusebius are material or objects.
- **Dossier gap, stated plainly in the lesson's References:** the primary
  texts were fetched from NPNF 2.10 at writing time, not from the research
  dossier; the Theodosian Code and Prudentius are cited, not quoted.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (warm-up, the only one).* List Symmachus's five grounds and,
   beside each, the clause of Cicero's Laws II it realizes and the module concept it
   instantiates (E3 reach-back to `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law` and,
   for the oath, Polybius VI.56 in `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`).
2. *Document reasoning — the sourcing drill on the Relatio.* Metadata: a
   pagan senator, prefect of the city, writing in 384 to three emperors (one
   a boy at an Arian court) for a senatorial minority, after a famine and a
   murder. Prediction before reading: what will he claim, what will he omit?
   Then records / argues / omits (he never argues the gods are true; he never
   mentions the Christian senators). Then USE: what can the Relatio
   establish about Roman religion in 384, and what only about the arguments
   a pagan thought a Christian court would accept?
3. *Document reasoning — paired corroboration.* Ep. 17 says the Christian
   senators are the majority and protested; Relatio 3 speaks for "the Senate".
   Set the two claims side by side; what third kind of evidence would
   decide between them (the senate's composition from inscriptions and
   letters — not in the dossier); and how does each author's provenance
   change what his claim can be USED for?
4. *Boundary probe.* Set the sanction to zero: an Ambrose who argues Ep. 17's
   "you serve God" and Ep. 18's refutations but has no power to withhold the
   sacraments. Does the module's representation tool predict a different
   outcome? What does the exercise show about where the tool stops and
   power begins?
5. *Claim to refute.* "Symmachus argued for religious toleration and Ambrose
   against it." Name the failing step with the oath (participation compelled),
   the stipends (one cult funded) and Laws II.8 ("no new gods unless the
   State recognizes" — the principle Symmachus's own tradition had enforced
   for four centuries); state what Symmachus did argue for (the preservation
   of a state cult) and what Ambrose did argue against (state funding of it),
   and where genuine toleration would have sat between them.
6. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* Using Augustine XIX.17 and XIX.24 alone
   (`<Ref>` `augustine-two-cities`), derive the ruling Augustine's theory
   gives on each of the three requests (altar, stipends, lands) — then compare
   to Gratian's acts and Ambrose's defence, and state in one sentence why the
   theory and the record diverge (the theory is about what the heavenly city
   *tolerates*; the record is about what a Christian *state* funds).
7. *Synthesis — causal ranking.* Rank as necessary / enabling / precipitating
   the causes of the altar's permanent removal that the documents and
   timeline supply: Gratian's decision of 382; the Christian majority in the
   senate; Ambrose's sanction; Valentinian's need of Theodosius against
   Maximus; the Frigidus (394). Defend the ranking; the solution shows that
   the 384 exchange decided less than its fame suggests and the battlefield
   more.
8. *Attack the verdict.* The lesson judges (strong) that both parties to
   384 held the commonwealth to stand on its public worship. State the
   strongest objection in its best form — that the outcome was decided by
   bargaining at a weak court (a boy emperor, a usurper in Gaul, a bishop
   who was also the court's envoy), so the documents record the arguments
   each side thought would work, not what anyone believed held Rome
   together — and say what evidence would be needed to overturn the
   verdict: a record that the court handled the altar as patronage,
   indifferent to doctrine (for example, the stipends restored to a
   Christian claimant, or the altar traded for the usurper's recognition
   with no word about worship).
9. **FORWARD (E7)** → `aquinas-law-and-kingship`. Ambrose told an emperor what
   he might not fund and Augustine told him what his power should serve. State
   in one sentence the principle both presuppose — that a ruler is bound by a
   law he did not make and cannot repeal — and then ask what that law *is*
   for a Christian ruler: Cicero's right reason in nature (`<Ref>`
   `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`), God's revealed command, or both in some order. The
   solution says: the next module opens with a theologian who answers a king
   by ranking exactly these — eternal, natural, divine and human law — and
   that "an unjust law is no law" (which the reader has from Laws II.5) is
   its first consequence.

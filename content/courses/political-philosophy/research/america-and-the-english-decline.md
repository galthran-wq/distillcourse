## THE DOSSIER GAP (read before writing)

The dossier contains **no single statement of the thesis** that America's condition continues
England's decline. course.yaml's notes say so and ask that it be assembled from Burnham
(index 70), Huntington (76), Murray (75), Scruton (44), Spengler (11), Toynbee (33) and Lasch
(62). The lesson must therefore (a) say plainly, once, that it is assembling the argument
from several writers, none of whom states it whole; (b) attribute each piece to the writer

(d) never present "some observers argue" as if a school existed with a manifesto. The opening
("Some observers argue …") is course.yaml's and stays verbatim; the first paragraph after it
names who the observers are and what each contributes.

Also absent from the dossier, and named here only so the writer knows the gap (obtain only
if needed; otherwise omit, never paraphrase from memory): the British "declinist" literature
(e.g. M. Wiener, English Culture and the Decline of the Industrial Spirit, 1981; C. Barnett,

Edgerton, The Rise and Fall of the British Nation, 2018); the American decline debate (P.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "Britain in 1900 ruled a quarter of the world; by 1960 it had
given up its empire, its industrial lead and, said many of its own writers, its confidence.
Some observers argue that the United States, built on English law, language and religion, is
now following the same course, for the same reasons. Others say the comparison confuses a
power with a civilization, and that America's problems are its own. The lesson lays out the
thesis in its strongest form and puts the objections."

(Check the opening's numbers: the British Empire at its greatest extent, around 1920, covered
roughly a quarter of the world's land and population; "by 1960 … given up its empire" is
loose — India 1947, most of Africa 1957–1968, Aden 1967; the writer keeps the opening verbatim
and gives the decolonisation span correctly in the body.)

The newcomer's problem: the previous lesson established that American institutions and
habits came largely from Britain. A reader who knows that Britain then lost an empire, an
industrial lead and, by many accounts, its self-belief will ask whether the inheritance
included whatever caused the loss. The lesson builds that argument from the course's own
authors and tests it.

1. **What exactly declined in Britain?** Obvious attempt: "Britain declined" — one thing.
   Split it into four, because the thesis needs to say which one America inherits: (a)
   territory and reach (the empire's dissolution, 1947–68; Suez 1956 as the moment the United
   States stopped Britain and France by diplomatic and financial pressure — Burnham l.2325–2339);
   (b) relative economic weight (the industrial lead lost to the United States and Germany
   before 1914); (c) the established religion and the institutions built on it (Scruton's
   Anglican Church from which faith seeps "out from the hidden pores", l.6481ff; Pusey's 1825
   warning, via Murray); (d) the governing class's confidence (Burnham's "will to survive";
   Scruton's national myths moving from glory to sacrifice, l.1400ff). Answer: the thesis must
   be about (c) and (d) — causes in the culture — because (a) and (b) are relative to rivals
   and could happen to any power. Keeper: Suez 1956.
2. **Why would an heir inherit a decline?** Obvious attempt: because heirs resemble parents.
   Why that fails: inheritance of features does not by itself transmit a fate; Britain's
   territorial loss followed two world wars, debts and the rise of larger powers — circumstances
   America did not inherit. Answer, and the crux of the lesson: the thesis holds only if the
   *cause* of Britain's decline lay in something the culture carried — and then the heir
   carries it too. So the lesson's real question is: what, in the inherited culture, is
   claimed to cause decline? Draw this as the lesson's first figure (cause in circumstances →
   no inheritance; cause in culture → inheritance).
3. **The causes in the culture, as the dossier's writers state them.** Build the argument map
   one premise at a time, each with its supplier:
   - *The religion that became a creed.* Huntington: the American Creed is "Protestantism
     without God" (`<Ref>` albions-seed-and-who-are-we); the creed outlives the faith that made
     it. Burnham names "the decay of religion" first among the causes he suspects (l.2551).
     Scruton: in England the Church kept the structure while the faith seeped out. Premise: a
     culture whose creed has lost its faith keeps the principles and loses the reasons.
   - *The ideology that reconciles a civilization to its contraction.* Burnham: liberalism,
     strongest "in the United States and Great Britain" (l.2077), turns each loss into a
     victory and comforts the patient (l.2551–2575) (`<Ref>`
     burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west). Premise: the two countries share the
     ideology that excuses retreat.
   - *The elite that has seceded.* Lasch, reading a British satire of 1958: "Young's
     imaginative projection of postwar trends in Great Britain sheds a great deal of light on
     similar trends in the United States" (l.431) — an aristocracy of talent without noblesse
     oblige (`<Ref>` lasch-revolt-of-the-elites); Huntington's denationalised elite against a
     patriotic public (ch. 10). Premise: both countries are governed by a credentialed class
     with weaker ties to the nation than the public has.
   - *Immigration nobody chose.* Britain's post-war immigration, never put to voters as a
     programme (Murray, ch. 1 — one clause, forward Ref); Huntington's Mexican chapter for
     America (`<Ref>` albions-seed-and-who-are-we). Premise: in both, the composition of the
     people changed by decisions the people did not take. (The next lesson takes this premise
     up on its own; here it stays one line.)
   Answer: the assembled thesis — the United States carries the same creed-without-faith, the
   same liberal ideology of contraction, the same meritocratic elite and the same unchosen
   immigration that preceded Britain's decline; therefore it will follow. Name it once as the
   course's label (`anglosphere-decline-thesis`). Say in the body that no one of these writers
   stated it whole.
4. **What does the thesis forecast?** A thesis is only as good as what it predicts. From
   Britain's path, in order: loss of reach (allies and bases given up, as Burnham's atlas showed
   for Britain); loss of confidence in the governing class; fragmentation of the national
   identity — Huntington's own UK passage (l.970–1000: devolution, the English calling
   themselves English, the Union Jack "disassembled into its separate crosses") and his
   parallel: the UK, the USSR and the US were all "unions". Answer: the forecast is contraction
   plus fragmentation, on a timescale of decades.
5. **Spengler's frame: one Civilization, England first.** Obvious attempt: Spengler's
   morphology supports the thesis. It supports the shared fate and rejects the mechanism.
   Spengler's unit is "West-European-American": one Civilization (`<Ref>`
   spengler-decline-of-the-west). England was the first part of it to pass from Culture to
   Civilization — "England is therefore its birthplace" (l.1942); "France and England have
   already taken the step" (l.2104); battles fought "in the name of this England of
   Parliamentarianism, business morality and journalism" (l.7697). America did not inherit
   England's late phase; it was born in it. And Spengler's forecast is not gentle contraction
   but Caesarism — Rhodes, "expansion is everything", "the first precursor of a Western type of
   Caesars" (l.2316–2350). Answer: in Spengler's terms the thesis is half right (same phase,
   same fate) and wrong about the cause (not a disease passed down, a stage reached together)
   and about the outcome (expansion and Caesars, not retreat).
6. **Toynbee's frame: nations are not the unit; the leader changes.** Toynbee's first test was
   England itself: English history is not intelligible on its own, only as a chapter of the
   West's (`toynbee-s1.txt` l.8–30) — so "England's decline" and "America's decline" are not
   two diseases but two moments in one civilization's story (`<Ref>`
   toynbee-challenge-and-response). England led the West's "third chapter" after a withdrawal
   behind the Channel — parliament made efficient, then the Industrial Revolution
   (`toynbee-s5.txt` l.2505–2545); leadership passes to the most pressed march (the
   transference of stimulus), and America enters the fourth great war "as a leading
   belligerent" (`toynbee-s21.txt` l.128–150). Breakdown is a failure of self-determination in
   the creative minority, and loss of territory is its consequence, not its cause. Answer: on
   Toynbee's terms the right question is not "is America inheriting England's disease?" but
   "has America's governing minority stopped responding creatively and become a dominant
   one?" — and Toynbee refuses to say that any civilization's breakdown is determined.
7. **The OBJECTIONS, each at full strength, from wherever it comes.**
   (a) *A power is not a civilization.* Toynbee: geographical expansion is "no criterion
   whatever" of growth (`toynbee-s5.txt` l.146–166); Burnham himself concedes that ending rule
   over non-Western societies differs from losing Western ones (l.444). Britain's loss of empire
   was the loss of a power's reach, and its law, language and parliament spread further after
   the empire than during it. The thesis, if it is about culture, cannot use the atlas as its
   evidence. (Recall `what-measures-greatness`: power is one measure among three.)
   (b) *The heir kept what the parent lost.* Huntington, the thesis's chief supplier, says the
   opposite of inheritance on the decisive point: dissenting Protestantism "faded in England"
   but took "new life" in America; Tudor institutions changed in the UK but persisted in the US
   (l.3295–3302, 3366–3373); on religious belief the United States ranked 1.7 and Great Britain
   11.6 in the 1990s survey he reports (l.4826–4834); Americans' willingness to fight for their
   country far exceeded Britons'. If the cause of decline was the loss of faith, America
   lacked the cause. The lesson then gives the reply from later data (writer to fetch and
   date): American religious membership and practice have fallen since about 2000 — the gap is
   narrowing — so the objection is strongest for the twentieth century and weaker for the
   twenty-first.
   (c) *Demography and power.* America's population has kept growing (by immigration and,
   longer than Europe, by fertility); its share of world output has stayed roughly constant for
   decades and its military spending exceeds that of the next several powers combined (writer
   fetches the series; numbers bound in the sentence or the figure). Britain's relative decline
   showed in these numbers well before 1914; America's, so far, mostly does not. Reply from the
   thesis: these are measures of power, and the thesis is about will and belief — which
   returns to objection (a).
   (d) *Decline talk is a constant of both countries.* Inside the dossier: Spengler forecast
   the West's winter in 1918; Burnham, in 1964, extrapolated that if the contraction went on
   "over the next several decades" "the West will be finished" (l.484) — and closed by
   suspecting his own book was a sign of recovery (l.2575). The record since 1964 includes the
   reverse of his atlas: after 1989 most of the eastern Europe he listed as lost rejoined
   Western alliances and institutions. A forecast of decline that has been made in every
   generation and is not falsified by any reversal has to explain why this time is different.
   (Outside the dossier, if obtained: the essay that counted the waves of American declinism and
   the histories of British "declinism" — see THE DOSSIER GAP.)
   (e) *America's problems are its own.* The divisions the previous lesson found — four
   regional cultures, slavery and its legacy ("that culture created slavery", Fischer
   l.8007ff), the federal structure — have no English counterpart. An English observer in 1968
   called American race relations "that tragic and intractable phenomenon" that Britain was
   about to import (next lesson) — the direction of inheritance can run the other way.
   Where the lesson concedes: (a) and (d) are strong against the thesis as a forecast of
   *contraction*; (b) is strong for the twentieth century and weakening; the thesis survives
   best in its narrow form — that the two countries share an elite and an ideology that
   find it hard to say why their own inheritance deserves defending.
8. **What evidence would decide it?** Burnham's own test, turned on Burnham: the mark of an
   ideology, he wrote, is that its holder "can't lose"; ask "what evidence might conceivably
   prove you wrong?" (ch. 6, summary). Apply it: the thesis must name indicators, the direction
   each should move if it is true, and a horizon. Build the table with the reader (FIGURE PLAN
   5): religious practice; fertility; share of world output; military reach and alliances;
   the gap between elite and public opinion on national identity; assimilation of immigrants;
   cohesion of the union (the UK's devolution against any American analogue). For each row,
   what the thesis predicts, what the objections predict, and what the record since 1964 (or
   2004) shows so far. Answer: the narrow thesis (shared elite, shared ideology) is testable
   only through measures of belief and allegiance, which move slowly and are measured badly;
   the broad thesis (America will contract as Britain did) has so far been contradicted by the
   power indicators. The lesson stops there and hands the immigration premise to the next
   lesson.

Headings as claims (suggested): "Britain lost four different things"; "An heir inherits a

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **Burnham's atlas** (l.416–438): the 1921 school atlas, the double-page 1914 map
   "Colonies, Dependencies and Trade Routes", the imagined maps after it, "a film winding in
   reverse". Then set the 1914 map against Toynbee's sentence that "painting the map red" is no
   criterion of growth — the two authors in one paragraph, disagreeing about what the map
   measures.
2. **Suez, 1956** (Burnham l.2325–2339): the heir acting against the parent — the United States
   "acted directly against" Britain and France "even though they were actively engaged on the
   field of battle". Date and outcome in one sentence (British and French landing in Egypt in
   November 1956, withdrawn under US financial and diplomatic pressure); Burnham's reading (the
   West lost the Isthmus); the alternative reading (the US preferring decolonisation and the
   UN to a failing imperial operation). The worked example of one event read by both sides.
3. **Huntington's three unions** (l.970–1000): UK 1707, US 1776, USSR 1918; the Union Jack
   coming apart into its crosses; the forecast that the UK "could follow the Soviet Union into
   history". Then the record since 2004 in one clause (the 2014 Scottish independence referendum
   rejected independence, 55 to 45 percent; the union still stands) — a forecast checked.
4. **"Faded in England … new life on the new continent"** (l.3295–3302) and the religiosity
   table (l.4826–4834: US 1.7, Great Britain 11.6): the strongest single objection, supplied by
   the thesis's own chief witness.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **No invented author.** The thesis is assembled; the lesson says so once, plainly, and
  attributes every piece. Do not write "declinists argue", "Anglosphere theorists hold" or any
  phrase implying a school. The course label `anglosphere-decline-thesis` is introduced as the
  course's name for the assembled argument.
- **Burnham's thesis is about the West, not about England.** He groups the US and Britain
  (l.2077) and counts British losses (the bases, Suez), but he never argues that America
  inherited England's decline; the lesson uses him for the "ideology of contraction" premise
  and for Suez, not as a proponent of the inheritance thesis. Same for Spengler (the unit is
  "West-European-American") and Toynbee (the unit is the civilization).
- **Huntington is a witness for both sides.** He supplies the inheritance and the UK forecast,
  and the strongest objection (the heir kept what the parent lost). Present both; do not
  quote one half without the other.
- **Murray's lesson comes later.** Use only his British observations (census decade, Pusey,
  "never chosen"); do not reconstruct the two concatenations, the "tiredness" or the guilt
  argument here — forward Ref.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** *Suez crisis* (1956: Britain, France and Israel attack Egypt after
  Nasser nationalises the canal; withdrawn under American pressure); *decolonisation*
  (1947–68 for most of the British Empire); *devolution* (the Scottish and Welsh parliaments,
  1999); *anglosphere* (Scruton's word for the countries that inherited English common law —
  define at first use); *Tudor institutions* (Huntington's term — the sixteenth-century
  English pattern of fundamental law, two-house legislature, local representation, militia);
  *meritocracy* (`<Ref>` lasch-revolt-of-the-elites); *transference of stimulus* (Toynbee —
  define in a clause). No more than three new terms per section.
- **Do not re-teach** Spengler's morphology, Toynbee's mechanism, Burnham's nineteen
  propositions, Lasch's inversion of Ortega, Huntington's core culture — each one clause and a
  `<Ref>`. The lesson's new matter is the assembly and the test.
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — Suez 1956; the 1990s religiosity ranks (1.7 vs 11.6, with "on a
  ranking of fifteen countries' answers to seven questions about belief" bound in the
  sentence); one power indicator of the writer's choosing (share of world output or military
  spending) with its year and source. Empire-area and industrial-share figures, if used,
  belong in a figure with sources (verify; the commonly quoted "a quarter of the world" refers
  to about 1920).
- **Dates:** "Britain in 1900 ruled a quarter of the world" is course.yaml's; the body gives
  the c. 1920 peak if it gives a figure at all.
- **Register.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning
  or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a
  label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. "Decline",
  "suicide", "contraction" are the authors' words — attribute them; the lesson's own sentences
  use neutral verbs ("lost", "gave up", "fell").
- **Contested claim (co-contested):** whether America is declining is a live empirical and
  political quarrel. The lesson does not decide it; it decides which version of the claim the
  evidence so far bears on and which it does not.
- **Dossier thin:** stated in THE DOSSIER GAP above. The writer must not fill the gap with
  remembered quotations from books outside the dossier.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (E3, earlier lessons):** name the writer who supplies each of the thesis's four
   premises in this lesson, and the earlier lesson where each writer's own argument was
   reconstructed.
2. **Document reasoning — the sourcing drill on Burnham's atlas passage** (l.416–438):
   metadata (an American conservative editor, 1964, lectures turned book, at the height of
   decolonisation); predict what such a source will count and what it will leave out; then:
   what it RECORDS (the territorial reach of European states, 1914–1964), ARGUES (that the
   shrinkage is suicide, caused by loss of will), OMITS (whether the peoples ruled consented;
   whether Western institutions spread while rule receded — which Burnham himself half-concedes
   at l.444). What can it be USED to establish about the West's will?
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** run the thesis on a case it was not built for — Canada or
   Australia, which inherited the same law, language and religion. Does the thesis predict
   their decline too? If yes, what would the evidence have to show; if no, which premise
   (creed, ideology, elite, immigration) is missing there? The solution shows that the thesis
   as stated is about the Anglosphere, not about America, and that its test must include the
   other heirs.
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Huntington's own evidence proves that America inherited
   England's decline." Name the step that fails (his religiosity data and the "faded in
   England" passage cut the other way; his UK forecast is about Britain, and his American
   forecast rests on immigration and the elite, not on inherited loss of faith), and state what
   in his book does support a weaker version.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** derive Spengler's forecast for America from his
   premises alone (one Civilization; England first into Civilization; Rhodes; Caesarism) and
   compare it with the inheritance thesis's forecast. State the one observation that would
   tell them apart.
6. **Research-shaped (E6d):** choose two rows of the decision table (FIGURE PLAN 5), fetch the
   series for both countries from the named sources (World Bank / Maddison for output; Gallup
   and British Social Attitudes for religion), and write what each shows since 1964, with an
   uncertainty statement (comparability of the measures) and a one-sentence verdict on which
   version of the thesis the rows bear on.
7. **Synthesis — counter-argument stem:** "Talk of decline is as old as the countries; it tells
   us nothing." Name two facts the claim fails to account for (the empire did end; religious
   practice in Britain did fall, and in America has been falling since about 2000) and state
   what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that decline talk is equally frequent in
   periods that later proved to be decline and periods that did not).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `powell-raspail-and-camus`:** of the thesis's four premises, one —
   immigration nobody chose — is checkable with censuses rather than surveys of belief. In 1968
   an English politician forecast that Britain's "Commonwealth immigrants and their
   descendants" would number three and a half million within fifteen or twenty years and five
   to seven million by 2000. Say what data would test that forecast, and what category problem
   the test would meet. The solution says the next lesson runs the test and finds the category
   problem (censuses count ethnic groups, not "immigrants and descendants"), with a `<Ref>`.

# Research brief: aquinas-law-and-kingship (Aquinas on law and kingship: natural law, unjust law and tyranny)

status: planned
module: christendom-to-sovereignty
type: core
goal: Analyze Aquinas's hierarchy of laws and his account of kingship and tyranny, and explain what obedience is owed to an unjust law

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`: true law is right reason in agreement
  with nature, one and eternal, and an unjust statute is not a law (Laelius's reply to Carneades);
  a tyranny is no commonwealth at all. From
  `augustine-two-cities`: no earthly city is just; rulers "serve"; slavery is a consequence of
  sin, not of nature; the earthly peace is a truce the pilgrim uses. From
  `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` and `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`: the
  six regimes, rule of law against the best man, man as political animal, natural slavery. From
  `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`: the mixed constitution (consuls, senate, people, each able
  to block the others) as the arrangement that arrests the cycle of regimes. From
  `altar-of-victory` (the previous module's close): Ambrose's "the emperor is within the Church,
  not above it" — the claim De Regno I.15 turns into a doctrine. The reader has NOT met
  scholastic method, the Summa, or any medieval institution.
- **Contributes:** The one move is to show how a Christian who has just received Aristotle's
  Politics (translated into Latin c. 1260) fits Aristotle's naturally good city inside
  Augustine's fallen world: law is reason's ordinance, descending from God's plan through
  natural law to human statute, so that a king is bound by a law he did not make — and yet an
  unjust law is usually to be obeyed and a tyrant is never to be killed by a private hand. The
  lesson's internal seam is the gap between ST I-II 96.4 (obey an unjust law to avoid scandal)
  and De Regno I.7 (deposition belongs to the public authority that appointed the king).
- **Hands off:** The open problem is enforcement: Aquinas exempts the sovereign from the
  coercive force of his own law (96.5) and sends the subject of a tyrant to "public authority"
  or to God — so WHO judges the king, and what happens when two authorities (pope and king, king
  and estates) each claim "care of the community"? `machiavelli-effectual-truth` answers by
  changing the question (what works, not what ought); `bodin-sovereignty` answers that no
  subject judges and the question "who are the rulers" is replaced by "what is a state". Vitoria
  (`vitoria-right-to-the-americas`, empire module) is this lesson's natural law applied to Spain
  in America, and `vermeule-common-good-constitutionalism` (post-liberal module) takes this
  lesson's definition of law as the anchor of every term it uses — forward hooks only, one
  clause each.
- **Spine step:** "Christendom builds a law above kings; Machiavelli breaks it; the wars of
  religion force one sovereign power above every faction — Bodin's definition, Hobbes's
  derivation from fear, Filmer's from fatherhood — and the English Revolution tests them"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): a king asks a theologian how to rule; the answer binds the
king to a law he did not make, calls a violating law no law — and tells subjects usually to obey
anyway. Anchor at once (L11): the theologian is Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), a Dominican friar
teaching at Paris and Naples; the king is the king of Cyprus, to whom the short unfinished
treatise On Kingship (De Regno, c. 1265) is addressed; the large work is the Summa Theologiae, a
textbook for teachers of theology, whose "Treatise on Law" (qq. 90–97 of the First Part of the
Second Part) is where the hierarchy of laws is argued. The newcomer's problem: after Augustine
(`augustine-two-cities`) said no earthly city is just and the ruler's peace is a truce, and
after Aristotle's Politics arrived in Latin (c. 1260) saying the city is natural and good, a
Christian king needs to know which is true of HIS kingdom.

1. **What is a law at all?** Obvious attempt: a law is whatever the one who holds power commands
   — the Roman jurists' maxim "what pleases the prince has the force of law", and behind it
   Thrasymachus (`thrasymachus-and-the-ring`, Ref). Why it fails: then a command to worship an
   idol is law, and a father's order to his household is law. Answer, built one clause at a time
   from q.90 aa.1–4: law is a rule and measure of acts, so it belongs to REASON (a.1 — the maxim
   holds "only if the will accords with reason, else lawlessness rather than law", ad 3); its
   end is the COMMON GOOD, because law regards the happiness of the whole community (a.2); its
   author is "him who has CARE OF THE COMMUNITY", the whole people or its vicegerent, because
   coercive power vests there — a father commands but does not legislate (a.3 ad 3); and it must
   be PROMULGATED, because a measure must be applied to those it measures (a.4). Here introduce
   the quaestio form briefly (title as yes/no question, objections, sed contra, respondeo,
   replies) so the reader can read the rest — the full worked example waits for 96.4.
2. **Whose reason? Four laws, or why human law is not enough.** Obvious attempt: the king's
   reason suffices. Fails: q.91 a.4 gives four reasons — man's end exceeds his natural faculty;
   human judgments conflict; human law cannot see interior acts; it cannot forbid every evil
   without destroying goods. Answer: the four laws, each as the answer to one of these — ETERNAL
   law is God's plan of government for the universe (a.1), NATURAL law is "nothing else than the
   rational creature's participation of the eternal law" (a.2 — the sun known by its rays, q.93
   a.2), HUMAN law is the particular determination of natural law's indemonstrable precepts
   (a.3), DIVINE law is revelation supplying what the other three cannot (a.4). Every law has
   force only as it partakes of the eternal (q.93 a.3) — this is the sentence that binds the
   king.
3. **What does natural law actually say?** Obvious attempt: a code of rules written in every
   heart. Fails: peoples disagree (Caesar's Germans did not think theft wrong, q.94 a.4).
   Answer: natural law is to practical reason what the first indemonstrable principles are to
   speculative reason; its first precept "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be
   avoided" (q.94 a.2 ~l.348) and three tiers of inclination (life — shared with all things; sex
   and offspring — with animals; truth about God, society, not offending one's fellows — proper
   to reason). General principles are the same for all; secondary conclusions hold for the most
   part and fail in a few cases (the deposit not returned to one who will arm against his
   country) or in knowledge (passion, custom). Natural law changes by addition, never by
   subtraction (a.5): common possession and universal freedom are "of natural law" only in that
   nature did not bring in the contrary — property and slavery are "devised by human reason"
   (a.5 ad 3). Seam with earlier lessons: this is a third answer to the natural-slavery question
   — not nature (Aristotle, Ref), not sin alone (Augustine, Ref), but human reason's addition.
   Cicero's "true law is right reason" (Ref `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`) is here given a structure: a
   first precept, tiers, and a theory of error.
4. **How does "do no harm" become "the thief pays fourfold"?** Obvious attempt: deduce every
   statute from natural law. Fails: natural law says the evildoer should be punished but not
   WHICH penalty; nature says nothing about which side of the road. Answer (q.95 a.2): two modes
   of derivation — by CONCLUSION (do not kill, from do no harm; these have natural law's force
   and make the law of nations, a.4) and by DETERMINATION (the particular penalty; these have
   "only the force of human law"), the craftsman specifying "a house" into this house.
   Consequences the reader can now see as forced: human law does not forbid all vices but the
   graver ones from which most can abstain, chiefly harms to others (q.96 a.2 — new wine in old
   bottles; "he that violently bloweth his nose bringeth out blood"); law is better than judges
   because few wise lawmakers are easier to find than many wise judges and law deliberates in
   advance without love or hatred (q.95 a.1 ad 2 — Aristotle's rule-of-law argument Ref'd,
   `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`); custom has the force of law because repeated deeds, like
   speech, declare the people's will (q.97 a.3), and law should rarely change because custom is
   obedience's chief support (q.97 a.2).
5. **The seam: what if the king's law breaks natural law?** Here the full WORKED QUAESTIO, q.96
   a.4 "Whether human law binds a man in conscience?": the objections (power from God binds;
   Peter says obey; a human superior cannot bind conscience…) stated at strength; sed contra (1
   Pet 2:19 suffering for conscience); respondeo — a law is just by END (common good, not the
   ruler's "cupidity or vainglory"), AUTHOR (within the power committed), FORM (burdens
   proportionately equal); a law failing these is "an act of violence rather than law", "seems
   to be no law at all" (Augustine), and "does not bind in conscience, EXCEPT PERHAPS IN ORDER
   TO AVOID SCANDAL OR DISTURBANCE, for which cause a man should even yield his right" (Matt
   5:40–41); a law against the Divine good (idolatry) "must nowise be observed" (Acts 5:29);
   replies. Explain "scandal" in its technical sense (an occasion of sin or disorder for others,
   not embarrassment). The reader must see the two-level answer: not-binding-in-conscience and
   yet-to-be-obeyed are compatible because the obligation to obey now comes from the duty not to
   cause disorder, not from the law. Add q.96 a.5: the sovereign is exempt from the law's
   COERCIVE force (no one can coerce himself) but subject to its DIRECTIVE force by his own will
   — "obey the law that thou makest thyself".
6. **Who should rule — one? And why is a tyrant worst?** Obvious attempt: the reader's Aristotle
   (six regimes, polity best for most cities, Ref). Aquinas's answer in De Regno I.2–4: the good
   of a multitude is unity (peace); what is itself one produces unity best, as nature's one
   heart and one God show, so rule by one is best (I.3); tyranny, the contrary of the best, is
   worst, since unity of force magnifies evil, and democracy is the most tolerable of the bad
   forms (I.4); the tyrant forbids friendship, marriage, shared meals and wealth to forestall
   conspiracy (I.4 [26–28]); Rome's freedom made it vigorous and its civil wars made emperors
   (I.5–6). Then the Summa's own concession: q.95 a.4 ranks the mixed regime best (and q.105
   a.1, if fetched, makes Moses's polity a mixture — flag the dossier gap). The mixture is the
   one the reader met in Rome (`polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`, Ref, one clause); Aquinas's
   precedent for it is Hebrew, not Roman. Say the seam
   plainly: the two texts answer two questions — which simple form is best (one), and which
   arrangement of offices is safest (mixed) — and the lesson should not pretend they are one
   answer.
7. **What may be done about a tyrant?** This is where the strongest OBJECTION enters, from
   inside the text and from the reader's Cicero: if an unjust law is violence and a tyrant's
   rule is "perverted" (q.92 a.1 ad 4), then killing him is self-defence — Aioth slew Eglon and
   was made a judge (I.7 [45]). Aquinas's reply at full length (I.7 [44–52]): tolerate a mild
   tyranny, because the remedy often fails and, when it succeeds, breeds faction or a worse
   tyrant (the old woman of Syracuse [44]); Aioth killed a foreign enemy, not a ruler, and the
   Theban legion did not resist though armed [46]; private tyrannicide is forbidden because the
   wicked would use it against good kings too [47]; action belongs to PUBLIC AUTHORITY [48] — a
   multitude that appointed its king may depose or restrain him without breaking faith, since he
   broke the covenant first [49]; otherwise appeal to a superior, and if none, to God, who gives
   a tyrant "in my wrath" for the people's sins [50–52]. Now set 96.4 and I.7 side by side: the
   first tells the subject of an unjust LAW to obey for order's sake, the second tells the
   PEOPLE that appointed a king that they may remove him. The lesson concedes the gap and names
   what closes it: Appendix 6 (a usurper by violence has no authority and may be repelled; an
   unworthy but lawful ruler is obeyed; commands to sin are refused). The reply the course
   states as its own: Aquinas has placed enforcement of the law above kings in a public
   authority he never specifies — a pope (I.15 [110–112]), an electing multitude, a superior
   lord — and that unspecified judge is what the rest of this module fights over.

Headings fall out as claims: "Law is an ordinance of reason, or the prince's pleasure is
lawlessness"; "Four laws because human law cannot see inside a man"; "Natural law has one
precept, three inclinations and a theory of error"; "Conclusion and determination: how a statute
inherits force"; "An unjust law is violence — and is usually obeyed"; "One ruler is best and a
tyrant worst"; "Who may act against a tyrant: public authority, never a private hand".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The definition parsed clause by clause** (q.90 aa.1–4): each of the four clauses shown as
   the conclusion of one article, with the counterexample it excludes (a tyrant's whim — reason;
   a privilege for the king's purse — common good; a father's house rule — care of the
   community; a secret decree — promulgation). Quote a.1 ad 3 on the Roman maxim. Figure 2
   below.
2. **The worked quaestio: q.96 a.4** — reproduce the architecture: title, three objections in
   the reader's words, the sed contra, the respondeo with the three tests, the "except perhaps
   to avoid scandal" clause, the idolatry exception, the replies. Keep quotations under two
   sentences each. This is the lesson's centre and its `<Example>`.
3. **Conclusion vs determination** (q.95 a.2 ~l.1551–1589): "one must not kill" derived from "do
   harm to no one" vs "the evildoer should be punished" determined into THIS penalty; the
   house-builder analogy; the consequence that determinations "have only the force of human law"
   — so a different penalty elsewhere is not a different natural law.
4. **The old woman of Syracuse** (De Regno I.7 [44], Kenny l.227): quote her three-tyrant speech
   in full (it is short) as the argument against hasty tyrannicide; pair with Damon and Pythias
   (I.11 [77]) as the argument that even a tyrant wants friends — hence why tyranny "does not
   pay".
5. **Man without hair, horns or claws** (I.1 [5–7]): the argument from neediness to society and
   from society to a ruler — "the proper divides, the common unites" [8–9]. The lesson's first
   De Regno passage; shows the method of stacking arguments from nature, experience and
   Scripture.
6. **The sovereign exempt from coercion, not direction** (q.96 a.5 ad 3) with the besieged-city
   case (q.96 a.6: if the gates must stay shut by law and the defenders are outside, "necessity
   knows no law") — the two places where Aquinas lets law bend without breaking.
7. **Augustine's corrupt electorate** (q.97 a.1): a moderate people may elect its magistrates; a
   people selling its votes forfeits the right — the one text where Aquinas makes the form of
   government depend on the people's condition; a ready seam to Machiavelli's corrupt city
   (I.55) in `machiavelli-discourses-republic`.
8. **Fear as water under pressure** (I.11 [81]) and the tyrant (Aquinas's "hypocrite", an actor) "who plays a king on
   stage" ([83]) — the passages Machiavelli's Prince XVII–XIX will answer; set them up here so
   the next lesson's objection is a recall, not a new teaching.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first mention (L11):** Summa Theologiae (a textbook for teachers of
  theology, c. 1265–74, in "questions" each split into "articles"); quaestio / article /
  objection / sed contra ("on the contrary", the authority cited for the opposite view) /
  respondeo ("I answer that") / reply to objection; De Regno (On Kingship, c. 1265, for the king
  of Cyprus — unfinished; the second half of the transmitted text was continued by another
  Dominican, Ptolemy of Lucca, so quote only Book I and II.1–4); Dominican (a preaching order
  founded 1216); "the Philosopher" (Aquinas's name for Aristotle); Isidore (seventh-century
  bishop of Seville, author of a reference work Aquinas quotes); Gratian (twelfth-century
  compiler of church law); Romans 13 ("the powers that be are ordained of God" — the proof-text
  for obedience); Theban legion (soldiers said to have been martyred c. 300 rather than resist
  an emperor's order); Aioth and Eglon (Judges 3: an Israelite kills the Moabite king oppressing
  Israel); Dionysius of Syracuse (fourth-century BC tyrant); "scandal" (technical: an occasion
  of sin or disorder for others); "law of nations" (ius gentium: rules all peoples share, e.g.
  just buying and selling, q.95 a.4); "determination"; "promulgation"; "fomes" and "synderesis"
  (AVOID — not needed); "vicegerent" (one acting in another's place).
- **Do not re-teach** Aristotle's six regimes, the rule-of-law argument, natural slavery,
  Cicero's natural law or Augustine's two cities — one clause plus `<Ref>` each (L0e). The
  lesson's new matter is the DESCENT of law and the TWO-LEVEL account of obligation.
- **The real misreading (L13):** "Aquinas says an unjust law is no law, so it may be disobeyed."
  Refute with 96.4's own clause and the besieged-city case: the thesis is about what binds in
  conscience, not about what to do. Do not invent a second "some believe".
- **Do not present the mixed regime as Aquinas's settled answer** — q.95 a.4 is one line, q.105
  a.1 is not in the dossier (see Sources). State what the dossier supports and flag the rest in
  References.
- **Summary vs text:** the summary's "q.90 a.4 corpus: definition" is a gathering of four
  conclusions, not a quotation from a.4 alone — say "gathered at the end of a.4". The summary
  paraphrases I.7 [49] as "a multitude with the right to appoint a king may depose or restrain
  him" — the Kenny text says the multitude does not act "faithlessly" in deposing a king it set
  up, "even though it had previously subjected itself to him in perpetuity", because he deserved
  it by not keeping faith — quote from the text, not the summary.
- **Kenny OCR:** paragraph [46] contains stray quotation marks and a mis-cited "2 Sam 14:5–6"
  (should be 2 Kings 14:5–6) — do not reproduce the citation.
- **Theology vs politics:** De Regno I.15 subordinates kings to "the priesthood of Christ's
  vicar" in matters of the ultimate end — report it as what the text says (it is the medieval
  answer to "who judges the king"), neither inflate it into a theory of papal supremacy nor drop
  it; it is exactly what Bodin's "new question" will refuse.
- **Thin places in the dossier:** secondary reading on Aquinas is Strauss ch. IV (8 pages) and
  the Strauss–Cropsey chapter (OCR); no Marsilius text (the Strauss–Cropsey volume has a
  Marsilius chapter, ll. 13657–14603, which the writer may consult for a one-clause forward
  pointer to a medieval rival who gave the legislative power to the people, but not teach).
  Say in References that the lesson reconstructs from the two primary texts.
- **Register:** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or
  approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label
  may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. The lesson reports what the
  arguments establish and where they leave the judge of kings unnamed.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (mechanical, the only one):** apply the four-element test (Figure 2) to
   three putative laws — a decree exempting the royal household from a tax, a secret order to
   arrest a critic, a custom of a free people observed for a century — and say for each which
   clause fails or passes and whether, by q.97 a.3, the custom counts as law.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** q.90 a.1 ad 3 answering the maxim "what pleases the
   prince has the force of law". Author, date, audience (teachers of theology at a university),
   what it records (a Roman-law maxim in current use), what it argues, what it omits (who
   decides whether the prince's will "accords with reason"); what it can be USED to establish
   about the standing of Roman law in 1270 and what it cannot.
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** a law passes all three tests of 96.4 and the only person able to
   coerce is the king who made it (96.5). State what binds him, in Aquinas's terms, and what
   happens to "unjust law is violence" when the author of the violence is the only enforcer.
   (The solution names the directive force, the public authority of I.7 [48–49], and the appeal
   to God — and says plainly that the enforcement question is left to the next lessons.)
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Aquinas teaches that a subject may disobey any unjust law." Name
   the step that fails (the "except to avoid scandal" clause; the distinction between binding in
   conscience and the duty of order; the idolatry exception as the only unconditional case).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** run the conclusion/determination distinction (q.95
   a.2) over Caesar's Germans who did not think theft wrong (q.94 a.4): is their error in a
   first principle, a conclusion, or a determination — and what does the answer imply for
   whether natural law is "the same for all" (q.94 a.4's distinction between rectitude and
   knowledge)?
6. **Research-shaped / corroboration (E6d, E3):** set three passages side by side — ST I-II 96.4
   (obey an unjust law to avoid scandal), De Regno I.7 [47–49] (deposition by the multitude that
   appointed), Appendix 6 (a usurper may be repelled) — and construct the single consistent
   position they allow, or show where none exists. Then compare with Cicero's "unjust statutes
   are not laws" (`cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`, Ref): what Aquinas adds (the scandal clause, the public
   authority) and why.
7. **Synthesis / counter-argument (E3 from `augustine-two-cities`):** "Aquinas has quietly
   reversed Augustine: the city is natural and good again." Name two facts the claim fails to
   account for (slavery and property as additions of reason after the Fall, q.94 a.5 ad 3; the
   king subject to the priesthood for the ultimate end, I.15) and state what would have to be
   true for it to survive.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `bodin-sovereignty`:** Aquinas's law needs an author "who has care of the
   community" (q.90 a.3) and a public authority to act against a tyrant (I.7 [48]). Take a
   kingdom in which a pope, a king and an assembly of estates EACH claim that care, and each
   claims the others' laws fail the test of "author". Using only this lesson's definitions, say
   whose law binds — and state why the definition cannot answer. The solution says in one
   sentence that this is the question the Wars of Religion force and that `bodin-sovereignty`
   answers it by asking a new question (what a state is), with a `<Ref>`.

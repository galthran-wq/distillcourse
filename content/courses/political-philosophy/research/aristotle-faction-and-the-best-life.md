# Research brief: aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life (Aristotle on why regimes fall and what a city is for)

status: drafted

goal: Explain Aristotle's causes of faction and his account of the best life the city exists to make possible

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`: the sixfold table, the two partial justices (III.9), polity and the middle, the stability test "all the parts must wish it to exist"; the FORWARD exercise there left the reader with a polity in which an election went against expectation and the loser invoking his partial justice. From `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`: the city as natural and for the sake of living well; the climate theory (VII.7) and the "spiritless farmers". From `decline-of-regimes`: Plato's account of regime change as a psychology, and Aristotle's objection to it. From `thucydides-power-and-justice`: Mytilene's revolt from Athens and the debate over it (V.4 traces that war's start to a quarrel over heiresses — a case the reader can now place); Athens's losses in the war and in Sicily (V.3's "Athens after its hoplite losses"); the Athenian thesis at Melos — the strong "rule wherever they can" — which VII.2's critique of cities that aim at domination answers; and the historian's claim that civil strife will recur "as long as the nature of mankind remains the same" (III.82, Corcyra — not taught there in the body; quoted in this lesson's Exercise 4 with its metadata).
- **Contributes:** One move: a general theory of why cities fall from inside — every regime rests on a partial conception of equality, and faction begins in the mind of whoever feels slighted by it; a state of mind, a motive (gain, honour, fear) and an occasion (insolence, fear, contempt, disproportionate growth, a quarrel among notables over a trifle) make a revolution; what destroys preserves when reversed, so the remedies are the causes run backwards, above all "guard against small lawbreaking" and an education fitted to the regime. Then the question under it: what is the city FOR — not conquest, not mere life, but virtuous activity in leisure — and what the best city therefore needs in size, site and citizens.
- **Hands off:** The module's theory is now complete and `sparta-as-test-case` tests it on a real regime (Aristotle's own Sparta: helots, women, oliganthropia, a single-virtue constitution; the Book VII critique of a city built for war). The forward objection to carry (course.yaml notes): Machiavelli's Discourses I.4, that the quarrel of the nobles and the people was the source of Rome's liberty — faction as the engine of freedom rather than the disease of the city. Aristotle answers part of it here (his iron sentence is about a city built for war, and his remedies are laws a faction's grievance teaches) and not its core (whether a quarrel can be kept inside institutions); that is decided in `machiavelli-discourses-republic` (conflict channelled through institutions produced liberty at little cost, conflict outside them destroyed the Republic — on balance). The theory of stasis itself is tested on Sparta in `sparta-as-test-case`. The common good as the measure of law is `aquinas-law-and-kingship`'s; the climate theory and the question of who may be a citizen of the best state return in `vitoria-right-to-the-americas`.
- **Spine step:** "Aristotle grounds rule in nature: man is a political animal, hierarchy runs through all things, some are slaves by nature — and he admits the theory's difficulty himself; the stable city is the one the middle governs"

Read in order:
  - V.1 (1301a–1302a, text l. ~440–560): every regime rests on a conception of justice and equality — "democracy arose from men thinking that if they are equal in any respect they are equal absolutely … oligarchy from the notion that those who are unequal in one respect are in all respects unequal"; "both are partly just, but absolutely unjust", so both cause faction; the inferior revolt to be equal, the equal to be superior; numerical vs proportional equality — "the excess of three over two is numerically equal to the excess of two over one; 4:2 = 2:1 is proportional" (l. 519–530); a regime should use both; the two kinds of change (of the constitution; of who holds it); "the government of the middle class is safest of the imperfect forms".
  - V.2 (1302a–b, text l. ~585–600): THE ANATOMY OF FACTION — the state of mind (the sense of inequality); the objects (gain and honour, or their opposites, loss and dishonour); the occasions — insolence, fear, excessive predominance, contempt, disproportionate increase, election intrigue, carelessness, neglect of trifles, dissimilarity of elements. Quote the list; it is the concept `stasis`.
  - V.3 (1302b–1303b, text l. ~600–700): each occasion with its case — insolence and gain in the magistrates; honour (Harmodius; the Peisistratidae); predominance (why ostracism); fear (the Rhodian notables); contempt (Thebes after Oenophyta; Syracuse before Gelon); disproportionate growth — "a state like a body whose parts must grow in proportion, or it will perish", "a foot four cubits long and the rest of the body two spans", Tarentum, Argos and Athens changing regime after the loss of their notables in war; dissimilarity of race and place — Sybaris, Thurii, Byzantium, Amphipolis; "the impediment of a ditch may break a regiment"; the Piraeus more democratic than the city.
  - V.4 (1303b–1304b, text l. ~725–800, checked): "trifles" — "revolutions are not about trifles, but spring from trifles" because great interests are at stake; Syracuse's constitution changed by "a love-quarrel of two young men" in office (l. 732–733); Hestiaea after the Persian war — a dispute over an inheritance; Delphi — a quarrel about a marriage (the suitor who read an omen in a broken pledge); Mytilene — a dispute about heiresses began the Athenian war; Phocis — an heiress between Mnaseas and Euthycrates; Epidamnus — a marriage; the power of a section (Argos, the Areopagus after the Persian wars, the sailors of Salamis); force and fraud.
  - V.5–7 (1304b–1307b): democracies fall by demagogues (the early tyrants were demagogue-generals; Cos, Rhodes, Heraclea, Megara); oligarchies by oppressing the people, by exclusion of rich outsiders, by oligarchs turning demagogue, by extravagance and mercenaries, by an oligarchy within the oligarchy, by rising valuations; aristocracies and polities by few sharing honours, by ill-mingling, by small changes — Thurii's repeal of one law on generals' re-election led to a dynasty; "if each part is little, the whole is little" as the fallacy of ignoring small changes.
  - V.8–9 (1307b–1310a, text l. ~1100–1420): PRESERVATION — opposites produce opposites; "guard against the beginning of change" and "small lawbreaking", "for the lawless spirit creeps in unnoticed, as expenditure in small sums"; do not trust the rigging devices; treat the excluded fairly; short tenures; "invent terrors and bring distant dangers near, like night sentinels" (l. 1238); calibrate the census to the city's wealth; no disproportionate honour; make office unprofitable — "people do not mind being excluded from government, but they resent seeing rulers steal"; the nose that varies from the ideal "ceases to be a nose" (l. 1393–1398) — do not push a regime's principle to its extreme; the oligarchic oath "I will be an enemy to the people" replaced; THE GREATEST PRESERVATIVE — "education adapted to the constitution … the laws are useless if the citizens are not trained in the spirit of the constitution"; "living as one likes" is a false freedom, "living by the constitution" is salvation (1310a).
  - V.10–12 (1310a–1316b): kingship rests on merit and protects the notables; tyranny arises from demagogues and protects the people against the notables — "a compound of extreme oligarchy and extreme democracy"; the motives of conspiracy — insult, fear, contempt, ambition — with cases (Harmodius and Aristogeiton, Pausanias and Philip, Sardanapalus seen carding wool, Dion); Hesiod's "potter hates potter"; V.11 THE TWO WAYS A TYRANNY SURVIVES — repression (the Persian handbook: cut down the tallest, forbid common meals and clubs and education, spies and "female detectives", keep the people poor by great works like the Pyramids and by war; summed as "humble, distrust, disable") or "playing the king" — seem a steward of the public money, honour the good, be "half virtuous, half wicked"; Theopompus's remark on the ephors ("I hand on the kingship more lasting") for the limited kingship; V.12 tyrannies are short-lived (Orthagoras at Sicyon a century; the Cypselids; the Peisistratids) and the critique of Plato Republic VIII (recall, `<Ref>` `decline-of-regimes`).
  - VI.2–5 (1317a–1320b): democracy's principle is liberty — majority rule and "living as one likes"; the best democracy is agrarian (too busy to attend assembly, content to elect and audit); surplus revenue poured into doles is "water into a leaky cask" — help the poor to a small farm instead. One paragraph; recalls polity.
  - VII.1–3 (1323a–1325b, `politics-s4-books7-8.txt`): the best regime presupposes the best life; three classes of goods (external, body, soul); happiness is proportional to virtue and virtue is not got from external goods but the reverse (the lyre: "performance owes to the performer"); the city's virtue is the same in kind as a man's; VII.2 the two opinions — the political life vs the philosophic — and the critique of cities that aim at war and domination: the Spartans and Cretans trained for war; Carthage honours by campaigns; the Scythian cup; such a constitution "aims at might without right"; "a physician is not expected to persuade or coerce his patients, nor a pilot the passengers" (1324b); an isolated city may be happy; VII.3 happiness is activity, and the contemplative life is a kind of action.
  - VII.4–7 (1325b–1328a): the city's size — "great" is by function, not headcount; the limit is "the largest number which suffices for the purposes of life and can be taken in at a single view" — too many and "who can be the general … or the herald, unless he have the voice of a Stentor?"; citizens must know one another's characters to judge and elect; territory surveyable; the sea — harbours outside the city; VII.7 the climate theory (recall, `<Ref>` `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`, one clause) and the critique of Plato's guardians who are "fierce to strangers" — spirit is stirred against friends who slight us, not against strangers.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): cities are overthrown by their own citizens far more often than by enemies; Aristotle collects the cases — a love quarrel in Syracuse, an heiress at Delphi, a Spartan land shortage — and asks what they have in common; draw out his theory of faction and preservation, then turn to what the city is for. The lesson is empirical in method (cases first, theory from them — Aristotle's own order in Book V) and teleological in its last third (the end first, the city derived from it — Book VII's order).

1. **What do a love affair, a dowry and a ditch have in common?** Open with three cases told in two sentences each: Syracuse, where two young magistrates fell out over a boy and the city split behind them; Delphi, where a suitor read an omen in a mishap at his betrothal and the bride's family, insulted, planted temple treasure on him; Epidamnus, a marriage quarrel that ended with a change of constitution. The obvious attempt: great cities fall from great causes. Why it fails: Aristotle's own sentence — "revolutions are not about trifles, but spring from trifles", because when the ruling men quarrel the whole city takes sides. The answer: look not at the occasion but at the state of mind that made the city ready. Because: a spark finds a city already divided or it finds nothing.
2. **Every regime rests on a partial equality, and faction begins with the slighted.** Recall III.9 (`<Ref>` `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`): the democrat's "equal in freedom, so equal in all", the oligarch's "superior in wealth, so superior in all". V.1 generalises: each is "partly just but absolutely unjust", so each regime carries the seed of its own faction — those denied equality rise to get it, those denied superiority rise to get that. The two equalities named exactly: numerical (3 − 2 = 2 − 1) and proportional (4 : 2 = 2 : 1); a sound regime uses both; "the middle-class government is safest". Concept `stasis`, first half. `<Ref>` `greek-miracle`: the two equalities the Greeks could not reconcile are now the two engines of civil war. Because: a city whose justice is a part treats some of its members as less than they think they are, and that is the "state of mind" in which faction begins.
3. **The anatomy of faction: a state of mind, a motive, an occasion.** V.2–3: the state of mind (the sense of being unequally treated); the motives (gain and honour, or escape from loss and dishonour); the occasions — insolence, fear, contempt, excessive predominance, disproportionate growth, election intrigue, neglect, small changes, dissimilar peoples. Each occasion gets its case in one sentence (the table, figure 2). Dwell on two: disproportionate growth — "a body whose parts must grow in proportion", "a foot four cubits long"; Tarentum, Argos and Athens, where the loss of the notables in war let the people in — and dissimilarity — "the impediment of a ditch may break a regiment", the Piraeus more democratic than the city. Because: the theory is a classification of cases, and its test is whether every case the reader can find fits one of the boxes (exercise 4 asks exactly this).
4. **How each regime falls — and why what destroys also preserves.** V.5–7 in one section: democracies fall by demagogues, who were generals in the old days and became tyrants; oligarchies by oppressing the people, by shutting out rich newcomers, by oligarchs turning demagogue, by extravagance and hired soldiers, by an oligarchy inside the oligarchy, by the census falling out of step with the city's wealth; aristocracies and polities by too few sharing honours and by small changes — Thurii repealed one law on generals' re-election and got a dynasty. Then V.8's principle: "opposites produce opposites", so the remedies are the causes reversed. Because: a theory of causes that is also a theory of cures is a theory, not a list.
5. **The remedies — and why the first of them is "guard small lawbreaking".** V.8–9: the lawless spirit "creeps in unnoticed, as expenditure in small sums" — so watch trifles; do not trust the rigging devices (fines on the rich for absence, none on the poor — recall IV.13); treat the excluded with justice and bring their leaders into office; keep tenures short; "invent terrors and bring distant dangers near, like night sentinels"; recalibrate the census; let no man rise too far too fast; make office unprofitable — "people do not mind being excluded from government, but they resent seeing rulers steal"; replace the oligarchic oath "I will be an enemy to the people"; do not push the regime's own principle to the point where, like a nose stretched past the ideal, it "ceases to be a nose"; and above all, "education adapted to the constitution" — laws are useless if the citizens are not formed in the regime's spirit, and "living as one likes" is not freedom but the regime's dissolution. Mark the dry seam: from a theory of faction one expects the remedy to be force; Aristotle's first remedy is attention to small things and his last is education — the regime is preserved in the soul, which is Plato's thesis (`<Ref>` `decline-of-regimes`) made into a rule of statecraft. Because: faction begins in a state of mind, so prevention works on the mind.
6. **Tyranny: why it is short and how it lasts — the course's first handbook of power.** V.10–12: kingship protects the notables, tyranny protects the people against them — "a compound of extreme oligarchy and extreme democracy"; it falls by hatred and contempt (the tyrant seen carding wool with his women; "potter hates potter"); it survives in two opposite ways — the Persian handbook (cut down the tallest; forbid common meals, clubs and schools; spies; keep the people busy and poor with great works and wars; "humble, distrust, disable") or "playing the king" (keep the public accounts, honour the good, show piety, be "half virtuous, half wicked"). Then the durations: Orthagoras's house at Sicyon a century, the Cypselids three generations, the Peisistratids thirty-odd years — tyrannies are the shortest-lived regimes. The STRONGEST OBJECTION enters here and reaches forward: the lesson concedes that Aristotle's "play the king" is advice to a tyrant on how to last, and that a later writer will read all of Book V as the manual Aristotle pretended it was not (`<Ref>` `machiavelli-effectual-truth`); Aristotle's answer, as the text has it, is the measure — the tyrant who "plays the king" is "half virtuous", and the regime that lasts longest is the one that approaches its true form. Because: a science of preservation cannot refuse to say how bad regimes last; its judgment is in the ranking, not the silence.
7. **What is the city for? Not conquest.** VII.1–3: the best regime presupposes the best life, and the best life is virtuous activity — external goods are instruments, not the end ("performance owes to the performer, external goods are the lyre"). Then the two opinions on the best life — the political (ruling) and the philosophic (contemplation) — and the critique of the cities that chose war: Sparta and Crete trained for it, Carthage honours by campaigns, the Scythian drinks from a cup only if he has killed; a regime aimed at domination "aims at might without right", and a city may be happy alone, as a man may. Concept `common-good`: the end a true regime serves is the good life of the whole, and a regime that serves conquest serves a part of a part. Backward seam, one sentence with `<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-justice`: "might without right" is Aristotle's name for the Athenian envoys' thesis, and his reply is not that the thesis misdescribes what powers do but that a city which lives by it has mistaken its end — the envoys' Athens, which "fell in love" with Sicily, is the case. "War must be for the sake of peace, business for leisure, the necessary for the noble" (VII.14). THE SECOND OBJECTION, at full strength: Machiavelli will say that the quarrel of senate and people — faction — made Rome free and great, and that a republic built for peace cannot defend itself (Discourses I.4; `<Ref>` `machiavelli-discourses-republic`). The lesson states it at full strength and then says, as a fact about the argument, how much of it Aristotle answered: the "built for peace cannot defend itself" half is answered ("like unused iron" describes a city built for war that wins, and the best city must be "temperate and brave, and able to endure"); the core — that a quarrel kept inside institutions makes liberty — is not, because Book V treats every faction as a state of mind to be prevented and never considers a quarrel given an office of its own. Where that is decided: `machiavelli-discourses-republic`. Because: a theory of the city's end decides what counts as its success, and that decision is where Aristotle and Machiavelli part.
8. **The best city derived from its end: size, site, citizens.** VII.4–10: "great" by function, not headcount; the limit of size is what "can be taken in at a single view" — too many and the general cannot command nor the herald be heard without Stentor's voice, and the citizens cannot know each other's characters to elect and judge (the feast argument of III.11 needs a city of this size — `<Ref>`); a surveyable territory, harbours outside the walls, a moderate navy manned by perioeci; the parts of the city are the warriors and the councillors — the same men young and old — and the farmers, craftsmen and labourers are "conditions, not parts"; the land is the citizens' and worked by slaves or barbarian perioeci "spiritless and of mixed race, so that they will not combine" (recall the climate theory, `<Ref>` `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`, one clause — the lesson names what the theory is used for and does not moralise); common meals; education public and uniform because "the citizen belongs to the state"; leisure, not amusement, is the end — "to be always seeking after the useful does not become free and exalted souls". The lesson's dry seam: the best city's leisure is bought by the exclusion of everyone who works, which is the household's hierarchy from Book I written large. `<Conclusion>`: the reader can now classify a case of civil strife by state of mind, motive and occasion, run the remedy as the cause reversed, and state the end against which Aristotle ranks regimes; hand-off — the next lesson runs the module's theory on a real regime, Sparta, and decides which Greek answer survives it (`sparta-as-test-case`).

Headings that fall out: "Revolutions spring from trifles, not about them" · "Two equalities, two engines of civil war" · "A state of mind, a motive, an occasion: the anatomy of faction" · "How each regime falls; what destroys, reversed, preserves" · "Guard small lawbreaking; educate for the regime" · "Why tyranny is short, and the two ways it lasts" · "What the city is for: not conquest" · "A city you can take in at one view: size, site and who counts".

2. **Numerical and proportional equality** (V.1, 1301b): 3 − 2 = 2 − 1 vs 4 : 2 = 2 : 1 — the two equalities of `greek-miracle` given Aristotle's arithmetic and attached to democrat and oligarch; build figure 1. The one computation in the lesson, and it IS the argument (playbook-history rule 4).
3. **The foot four cubits long** (V.3, 1302b–1303a): a body whose parts grow out of proportion "perishes or changes into another animal"; Tarentum after its defeat by the Iapygians, Argos after Cleomenes' slaughter of the notables, Athens after its hoplite losses in the Peloponnesian war — the people came in because the notables were dead. One paragraph; the case for "disproportionate growth".
4. **The ditch and the Piraeus** (V.3, 1303b): "as in war the impediment of a ditch, though ever so small, may break a regiment", so every difference divides a city; the Piraeus more democratic than the upper city. Two sentences; the case for "dissimilarity".
5. **The night sentinels and the nose** (V.8–9, 1308a, 1309b): statesmen "should invent terrors and bring distant dangers near, that the citizens may be on their guard, like night sentinels"; a nose that departs from the straight is still a nose until the excess makes it not one — "push a democracy or an oligarchy to its extreme and it ceases to be a constitution". Two images for two remedies; `<Quote>` the nose.
6. **The Persian handbook and "playing the king"** (V.11, 1313a–1315b): the two recipes as a two-column table (figure 5) — cut down the tallest, forbid meals and clubs and schools, spies and "female detectives", the Pyramids, war ("humble, distrust, disable") vs steward of the public money, honour the good, visible piety, "half virtuous, half wicked". Then Theopompus to his wife on the ephors — "I hand on the kingship to my sons more lasting". Worked as the lesson's `<Example>` of "what destroys, reversed, preserves".
7. **Might without right** (VII.2, 1324a–b): Sparta and Crete trained for war, Carthage's armlets for campaigns, the Scythian's cup, the Iberians' obelisks for enemies killed; "a physician is not expected to persuade or coerce his patients, nor a pilot the passengers"; a regime of domination is unjust to its neighbours and ruins itself in peace — "like unused iron" (VII.14). Quote the iron sentence; it is the module's hand-off to `sparta-as-test-case`.
8. **Stentor's voice** (VII.4, 1326a–b): a city of ten thousand craftsmen is not a city; "a ship a span long or a quarter-mile long is no ship"; the limit is the number that "can be taken in at a single view" — else no general, no herald, no knowledge of each other's characters for elections and lawsuits. Build figure 7; it is the condition the feast argument (III.11) silently needed.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), in chain order:** stasis (civil strife, faction — anchor as the concept id and keep the Greek once; Plato's "discord vs war" from Republic V may be recalled in one clause); Syracuse (the great Greek city of Sicily), Delphi (the oracle's sanctuary — also a city), Hestiaea, Mytilene (chief city of Lesbos), Epidamnus (a colony on the Adriatic), Thurii (an Athenian-founded colony in Italy), Tarentum (a Spartan colony in Italy), the Iapygians, Argos, the Piraeus (recall), Sybaris, Byzantium, Amphipolis — ONE clause each, and only those the lesson actually uses (cut the rest; L12a); demagogue (a "leader of the people" — in Aristotle, one who flatters the assembly); Harmodius and Aristogeiton (the tyrannicides of Athens, 514 — one clause), the Peisistratids (Athens's tyrant house, c. 560–510), Orthagoras and the Cypselids (tyrant houses of Sicyon and Corinth), Sardanapalus (an Assyrian king of legend), Theopompus (a Spartan king, c. 700, credited with the ephors — anchor here, reused in `sparta-as-test-case`); the census (the property assessment that fixes who may hold office); perioeci (recall from the household lesson); Stentor (the loud-voiced herald in Homer); the Islands of the Blest (the Greek paradise); Carthage, Scythians, Iberians (one clause each when VII.2 lists them).
- **Cases are scenes or judgments, never data rows (L12d).** The three trifles are told; the V.5–7 lists are a table; the tyrant durations are one sentence with the three names. Do not reproduce Aristotle's full case-lists — select two per occasion at most and say so in a note ("Aristotle gives a dozen; two are enough to see the box").
- **"Education adapted to the constitution" is the lesson's keeper line, not force.** QA will check that the preservation section ends on it and that the lesson marks it as the point where Aristotle agrees with Plato on the regime-in-the-soul (`decline-of-regimes`).
- **The tyrant's handbook is Aristotle's, and the lesson must not editorialise.** The "play the king" advice is stated as the text gives it; the forward Machiavelli objection is one paragraph and one Ref. The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Machiavelli's Discourses I.4 is a FORWARD objection** (course.yaml notes): the lesson names the thesis as "a later republican's", states it at full strength in two sentences, does not teach it, and lets Aristotle's iron sentence stand as the reply the text already contains.
- **The climate theory and the "spiritless farmers" are recalled, not re-taught** (course.yaml notes): one clause with `<Ref>`; here they appear as "who can be a citizen of the best state" — the lesson names what the theory is used for and does not moralise.
- **Book VI (varieties of democracy and oligarchy, the offices catalogue)** is one paragraph — the agrarian democracy and the leaky cask — nothing more.
- **Book VIII (education, music) is one paragraph** — public education, leisure as the end; no modes, no catharsis. The text breaks off at VIII.7; say nothing of it.
- **Numbers:** the 3 − 2 / 4 : 2 arithmetic is the one computation and IS the argument; the tyrant durations to the generation ("a century", "three generations", "about thirty years"); nothing else. No citizen counts (Sparta's are the next lesson's).
- **Summary vs primary:** spot-checked V.1 (text l. 519–530), V.2 (l. 590–596), V.4 (l. 732–770), V.8 (l. 1238), V.9 (l. 1393–1398), VII.14 (s4 l. ~780–800) match. The s3 shard's Book VI ends at ch. 8; VII begins in s4.
- **Thin in the dossier:** the Nicomachean Ethics (happiness, virtue as a mean, the contemplative life) is not in the dossier — VII.1–3 and 13–15 are read as they stand and the lesson defines what it needs. Machiavelli's text is in the dossier but belongs to module 3; only the I.4 thesis is used. The Strauss–Cropsey chapter is unread.
- **Nothing is a demo.**

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up, the one mechanical item).** List the three components of faction (state of mind, motive, occasion) and name four occasions with one case each. From `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` (E3): state the two partial justices of III.9 and say which one a slighted oligarch invokes.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill) on V.4's Syracuse case.** Author, genre (lecture notes collecting cases from local histories now lost), audience; what the passage records (that a Syracusan constitution changed c. 5th century after a quarrel in office), what it argues (trifles as sparks), what it omits (the names, the regime before and after, the source). Closing: what can a two-sentence anecdote in Aristotle be USED to establish about Syracuse — and what about Aristotle's method?
3. **Claim to refute.** "Aristotle's theory of faction says that inequality causes revolution." Name the step that fails (every regime is unequal in some respect; what causes faction is a PARTIAL conception of equality that the slighted party rejects — a state of mind, not a distribution) and state the surviving claim with its consequence (the remedy works on the mind: justice to the excluded, education for the regime).
4. **Research-shaped task: classify a case.** Take one case the lesson did not use — Thurii's repeal of the re-election law (V.7), the Rhodian notables' fear (V.3), Hestiaea's inheritance (V.4), or (E3, the richest option) the civil war at Corcyra as Thucydides describes it (III.82, Gutenberg #7142 l. 6860–6905 of research/texts/thucydides-full-pg7142.txt: the popular leaders calling in Athens and the oligarchs Sparta; "Words had to change their ordinary meaning … Reckless audacity came to be considered the courage of a loyal ally"; "as long as the nature of mankind remains the same" — the exercise quotes four to six lines and gives the metadata: an exiled Athenian general writing c. 400 about a Corinthian colony off north-west Greece in 427) — and place it in figure 2's three columns, defending each placement from the text. Then design the test that would show the theory is more than a sorting of names: name what finding (a revolution with no prior sense of inequality among its makers) would falsify it.
5. **Boundary probe: the state of mind at zero.** A city in which no one feels unequally treated — Aristotle's polity of "equals and similars". Can any occasion (insolence, a love quarrel, a lost war) start a faction? Run V.2's three components with the first set to zero; then set the middle class to zero and the state of mind to maximum (a city of rich and poor only) and say which occasion is then sufficient. Solution: the theory makes the state of mind necessary and the occasion only precipitating — which is exactly the causal ranking of exercise 6.
6. **Causal ranking (synthesis).** For one case (Athens after the Peloponnesian war's hoplite losses, V.3), rank the loss of the notables, the democratic conception of equality, and the existing assembly institutions as necessary / enabling / precipitating for the change Aristotle records, and defend the ranking. "Possible answers": two defensible rankings.
7. **Counter-argument stem (synthesis, FORWARD-adjacent).** "A later republican claims that faction between the few and the many was the source of Rome's liberty and greatness." Name two facts in Book V the claim must account for (that Aristotle's remedy for faction is justice to the excluded, which is also a way of letting the many in; that tyranny arises precisely from the unmediated quarrel of notables and people) and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that a city can institutionalise the quarrel without its becoming a state of mind — the tribunes' veto as a device, which the reader will meet as one of Rome's checks in `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome` and as the engine of Rome's liberty in `machiavelli-discourses-republic`).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `sparta-as-test-case`.** Aristotle says a city trained for one virtue, war, "like unused iron loses its temper in peace", and that a regime is stable only if "all the parts wish it to exist". Take a city whose citizen body is a few thousand men living off a subject population many times larger, trained from age seven for war, with women holding much of the land: list which of Book V's occasions it carries (fear, predominance of a part, disproportionate growth, dissimilarity) and which of its parts can be said to "wish the regime to exist". The solution says in one sentence that the reader has just drafted Aristotle's own account of Sparta in Politics II.9 and VII.14 — the next lesson sets it against the record, `<Ref>`.

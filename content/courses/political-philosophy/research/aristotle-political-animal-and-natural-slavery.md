# Research brief: aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery (Aristotle on the household: the political animal, natural hierarchy and natural slavery)

status: planned
module: greek-discovery
type: core
goal: Analyze Aristotle's argument that the city is natural and that some men are slaves by nature, and locate the difficulty he concedes inside it

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `greek-miracle`: the polis as the community that decides by speech; the two equalities (by merit / one share each). From `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`: Plato's city built from need and the division of labour, its three classes, its unity premise, and Aristotle's "all say mine" already heard as the external objection. From `decline-of-regimes`: the regime as a psychology; Aristotle's objection to Plato's cycle. The reader has not met Aristotle as a thinker in his own right; this is where he is anchored.
- **Contributes:** One move: the city is natural — not a contract, not a fever — because it grows from the household and is the end at which the household aims, and because man alone has speech about the just; and the same "nature" that makes the city makes a hierarchy of ruler and ruled run through everything, down to the master and the natural slave. The lesson's centre (course.yaml notes) is the INTERNAL difficulty: Aristotle reports the objection that slavery is only by law and force, answers it, and then concedes that nature "often fails" to make the bodies and souls his theory needs — so there is no mark by which a natural slave can be recognised, war captives may be the wrong men, and a master may have the slavish soul. Planned as a reversal seam: the theory is built at full strength, then the text itself undoes its application.
- **Hands off:** If hierarchy is natural but cannot be read off anyone, who should rule a city of free men? `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` takes the citizen, the six regimes and the middle. The climate theory of Greek and barbarian (VII.7) returns there and in `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life` as the question of who may be a citizen of the best city. The natural-slavery argument is the backward Ref for `vitoria-right-to-the-americas` (Vitoria rejects the title), for `augustine-two-cities` (slavery by sin, not nature), for `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` (equality of ability) and for `strauss-natural-right-and-history` (the remark that Aristotle did conceive the injustice of slavery). Chrematistics returns in `aquinas-law-and-kingship` (usury) and `locke-property-and-consent`.
- **Spine step:** "Aristotle grounds rule in nature: man is a political animal, hierarchy runs through all things, some are slaves by nature — and he admits the theory's difficulty himself; the stable city is the one the middle governs"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): Aristotle begins not with a city but with a household — man, woman, children, slaves; each relation of ruler and ruled in it is natural; the city grows from such households as an oak from an acorn; then he faces the objection, reported from his own contemporaries, that slavery exists only by law and force; follow his answer and the place where he admits it does not quite work. The lesson builds the theory to full strength first — the reader must feel its force before the seam — and the seam is the text's own.

1. **Why start with a household rather than a city — and what "natural" is going to mean.** The obvious attempt (Plato's, `<Ref>` `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`): build the city from need and the division of labour, as an artefact. Why Aristotle refuses it: a thing is understood by watching it grow from its least parts, and the least parts of a city are two pairings nature itself makes — male and female for generation, ruler and ruled for preservation. The answer: household → village → city, and the city is not just the last and biggest but the END: "the nature of a thing is its end", what it is when its growth is complete. The acorn/oak image is the lesson's, not the text's (say so — the text's images are the Delphian knife and the dead hand). Because: if the city is the completed form of something that grows by itself, it is natural in the way an adult body is, not in the way a hut is. Assumption named: nature is purposive — things have ends.
2. **Man is a political animal because he has speech — the polis of `greek-miracle` gets its definition.** Obvious attempt: bees and herd animals are social too. Why it is not enough: they have voice, which signals pleasure and pain; man alone has speech, which "sets forth the expedient and the inexpedient, and likewise the just and the unjust" — and a shared perception of good and evil, just and unjust, is what MAKES a household and a city. Then the priority of the whole: a hand cut from the body is a hand in name only; the man who needs no city is "a beast or a god"; perfected by law he is the best of animals, separated from it the worst, "armed with intelligence". Concept `political-animal`. `<Ref>` `greek-miracle` in one clause: the community that puts its affairs "in the middle" and decides them by speech is, on this account, what human beings are for. Because: an animal whose defining capacity is arguing about justice can only exercise it in a community that argues about justice.
3. **The ladder of rule: hierarchy runs through everything.** From the household's three relations Aristotle generalises (I.5): wherever there is a composite, one part rules — soul over body "despotically", intellect over appetite "constitutionally and royally"; man over tame animals, who are better off ruled; male over female; and so, "of necessity, extends to all mankind". The method sentence that will matter at the seam: "we must look for the intentions of nature in things which retain their nature, and not in things which are corrupted" — so study the man in the most perfect state of body and soul, where the hierarchy is visible, "although in bad or corrupted natures the body will often appear to rule over the soul". Because: if the hierarchy is nature's, it must show in nature's best specimens, and the corrupted cases are set aside as evidence. Heading: "A ladder of rule from soul and body down to master and slave".
4. **The natural slave defined — at full strength.** Who is at the bottom of the ladder? One "who can be, and therefore is, another's", and who "participates in rational principle enough to apprehend, but not to have, such a principle" — above the tame animal, which cannot even apprehend; an instrument of action, not production (the shuttle makes cloth, the slave helps the master live). Daedalus's statues: if tools moved by themselves, no one would need slaves. And it is good for him: "it is better for them, as for all inferiors, that they should be under the rule of a master"; where the relation is natural, master and slave "are friends and have a common interest". Concept `natural-slavery`, first half. Assumption named: a soul can lack the ruling part and still be human. Register rule (the two traps): state it as Aristotle states it, without softening and without an adjective.
5. **The objection Aristotle reports — "slavery is against nature" — and his first answer.** I.3 and I.6: "others affirm that the rule of a master over slaves is contrary to nature, and that the distinction between slave and freeman exists by law only, and not by nature; and being an interference with nature is therefore unjust." At full strength, as Aristotle gives it: jurists "detest the notion that, because one man has the power of doing violence and is superior in brute strength, another shall be his slave"; "what if the cause of the war be unjust?"; "no one would ever say that he is a slave who is unworthy to be a slave — were this the case, men of the highest rank would be slaves" if captured. Aristotle AGREES with all of this: slavery by law is "a sort of convention", and conquest proves nothing. His answer: the convention's defenders and attackers both really mean natural slavery — Greeks will not call Greeks slaves, "confining the term to barbarians", which is to say "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere". Because: the only slavery that can be just is the natural kind; the legal kind is just only when it happens to coincide with it.
6. **THE SEAM — the text undoes its own application.** From the theory one would expect a mark by which the natural slave is known. The record is the opposite. (a) Bodies: "Nature would like to distinguish between the bodies of freemen and slaves … but the opposite often happens — that some have the souls and others have the bodies of freemen." (b) Souls: if men differed as the statues of the gods differ from men, all would agree; "but the beauty of the body is seen, whereas the beauty of the soul is not seen" — the one thing that would decide is the one thing not visible. (c) Birth: Greeks think good begets good, "but this is what nature, though she may intend it, cannot always accomplish". (d) War: the captive may be the better man. Draw the consequences the text does not draw but licenses: there is no procedure by which a city could tell a natural slave from a legal one; every actual slave is a legal slave whose natural status is unknown; and since "some have the souls of freemen" in servile bodies and vice versa, a master may be the one with the slavish soul — in which case, by Aristotle's own criterion, the relation is "merely law and force" and "the reverse" of friendship. Aristotle's own conclusion: "all are not either slaves by nature or freemen by nature" — the theory survives as a theory, and the institution it was meant to justify is left standing on convention. The course's judgment, stated dryly: the difficulty is internal; it is Aristotle's, and he saw it (`<Ref>` forward: the course will meet the claim that he "could not have conceived of the injustice of slavery" and the reply that he did, `strauss-natural-right-and-history`). Because: a hierarchy "of nature" that cannot be read off any natural sign cannot be applied without the force it was meant to replace.
7. **Greek and barbarian: the climate theory as the attempted mark.** If souls cannot be seen one by one, perhaps peoples can be sorted. VII.7 at full strength: Europeans spirited but unintelligent, Asians intelligent but spiritless and "therefore always in a state of subjection and slavery", Greeks between, "high-spirited and also intelligent", able "to rule the world" if one state. I.2's "it is meet that Hellenes should rule over barbarians" and I.8's "naturally just" war against those "intended by nature to be governed, who will not submit". Concept `greek-barbarian`. The STRONGEST OBJECTION to this, from the Greek side itself: Aristotle has just said that Greeks are enslaved too and that the line between Greek and barbarian is a convention of naming ("some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" cuts both ways); a sorting by birthplace is as arbitrary as sorting numbers into ten thousand and the rest; and the best city of Book VII needs its farmers "spiritless" and "of mixed race" so that they will not combine — a policy, not a finding. The lesson concedes that the climate theory is Aristotle's and that it is where the theory of natural slavery goes to look for its mark; it does not moralise and it does not pretend the mark is found. `<Ref>` forward: the theory's rejection as a title to conquest (`vitoria-right-to-the-americas`) and its reappearance as the question of who can be a citizen of the best state (`aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`).
8. **Wealth-getting: the other thing the household must not do without limit.** Why here? Because the household's end is living well, and the same teleology that ranks souls ranks ways of acquiring. A shoe can be worn or exchanged; barter is natural; money arises as a measure (wine for corn, metal by weight, then stamped); retail trade turns the measure into the end, and the end has no limit — Midas starving on gold. Medicine's end (health) is unlimited but its means are limited; money-making reverses this. The unlimited desire "springs from caring for living only, not living well". Usury is the most unnatural: "money born of money" (tokos, "offspring"). Concept `chrematistics`. Because: a hierarchy of natural ends makes acquisition a servant; an acquisition without end has no place on the ladder and so is "against nature" in exactly the sense slavery by force is. `<Conclusion>`: the reader can now reconstruct the chain from the household to the city and the ladder of rule, state the natural-slave argument and the four places it admits failure, and say what the climate theory was for. Open: if hierarchy is natural but no one can be sorted by it, who among free men should rule — `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`.

Headings that fall out: "Begin with what grows: household, village, city" · "Speech about the just: why man is a political animal" · "A ladder of rule from soul and body down to master and slave" · "The natural slave: one who apprehends reason without having it" · "Slavery by law: the objection Aristotle reports and accepts" · "Where the theory fails on its own terms: no mark, no sign, no test" · "Greek and barbarian: the climate theory as the missing mark" · "Getting without limit: chrematistics and the birth of money from money".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), in chain order:** Aristotle (384–322; Plato's pupil for twenty years, tutor to Alexander, founder of the Lyceum at Athens; the Politics is lecture-notes, compressed, c. 335–322 — one sentence, no more); Bekker numbers (the page/column/line of the 1831 edition, e.g. 1253a — one clause at first citation, then use them); oikos / household (a man, his wife, children, slaves, and property — not a nuclear family); village (several households, "colonies" of one); polis (recall `greek-miracle`); telos / end ("the nature of a thing is its end" — define at first use, keep the Greek once); despotic / royal / constitutional rule (master–slave, father–child, husband–wife and citizen–citizen — the three kinds the lesson uses throughout); Daedalus and Hephaestus (the mythical craftsman and the smith-god whose made things moved); barbarian (anyone who does not speak Greek — anchor as a linguistic word before VII.7 gives it a climate); Hellenes (the Greeks' own name); Theodectes (a tragedian; one clause); perioeci (the free non-citizen "dwellers-around" of Sparta and Crete — anchor at VII.9 and Ref `sparta-as-test-case`); chrematistics (anchor as the concept id: wealth-getting without limit); tokos (interest; literally "offspring"); Midas; Thales (recall `greek-miracle`); Dionysius (tyrant of Syracuse, c. 400 — one clause); Euripides (when I.2 quotes him).
- **The two traps are a register rule (course tone).** Neither airbrush nor dismiss. The theory is stated in Aristotle's words at full strength; the difficulty is stated in Aristotle's words at full strength; the lesson adds no adjective in either direction. QA will fail "disturbing", "unfortunately", "of course", and equally "merely conventional", "common sense", "obviously".
- **The internal difficulty is the centre (course.yaml notes), and it is INTERNAL.** The lesson's reversal is not "modern readers object"; it is "Aristotle's own text says the opposite often happens". The four concession points (bodies, visible souls, birth, war) are all in I.5–6 and must be quoted, not paraphrased. The inference "the master may be the one with the slavish soul" is the course's own and is marked as such ("Aristotle does not draw this consequence; his premises do").
- **Natural slavery is not racial slavery.** Aristotle denies that conquest or ethnicity as such makes a slave (I.6); the climate theory (VII.7) is a separate, later attempt to find the mark by peoples, and the lesson keeps the two apart and shows that VII.7 contradicts I.6's "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" when read strictly. Do not import the early-modern debate here; `vitoria-right-to-the-americas` has it.
- **Women and children (I.12–13):** one paragraph, in the text's words — the wife ruled "constitutionally with permanent inequality", with a deliberative faculty "without authority"; the lesson states it, places it on the ladder, and moves on; `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` will note that Aristotle excludes women from citizenship without re-arguing. Not a separate section.
- **Do not re-teach Plato's city** — `<Ref>` `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king` where Aristotle's method departs from it, and recall "all say mine" in one clause. Do not teach II.2–5 again.
- **Chrematistics is one section, not a lesson on economics.** No modern terms (capital, interest rate); the course returns to usury in `aquinas-law-and-kingship` and to acquisition in `locke-property-and-consent`.
- **Numbers:** none load-bearing; Aristotle's dates to the decade. No slave-population figures (the dossier has none and the lesson needs none).
- **Summary vs primary:** all spot-checked pointers (I.5 at text l. 280–330, I.6 at l. 340–420, VII.7 at s4 l. 485–500) match; the OCR carries Ross's footnotes inline — do not quote a footnote as text. Jowett's "rational principle" is logos; the writer may gloss once.
- **Thin in the dossier:** the Nicomachean Ethics (which the Politics assumes for "virtue", "the mean", distributive justice) is not in the dossier; the lesson defines what it needs in situ and does not cite the Ethics. The Strauss–Cropsey chapter is unread. No Vitoria text is to be used here.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up, the one mechanical item).** Reconstruct the household → village → city chain and say at each step what "for the sake of" names (daily needs, more than daily needs, living well). From `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king` (E3): state Plato's starting point for the city in one sentence and say which word in Aristotle's I.2 is the direct reply ("nature is the end" against "need").
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill) on I.6 (1255a3–b15).** Author (Aristotle, c. 330, a resident alien in Athens who owned slaves — his will freeing some survives in Diogenes Laertius, if the writer wants one clause), genre (lecture notes), audience (students of a school, not citizens in assembly); what the passage records (that jurists and philosophers in his day disputed slavery's justice), what it argues, what it omits (any procedure for telling the natural slave). Closing: what can the passage be USED to establish about fourth-century Greek opinion — and what does the fact that the objection is reported in Aristotle's own words change about the charge that antiquity "could not conceive" the injustice of slavery?
3. **Claim to refute.** "Aristotle justifies slavery as it existed in Greece." Name the step that fails (he denies that conquest confers title and that Greek/barbarian as such does; he says "all are not either slaves by nature or freemen by nature"; every actual slave was a legal slave whose status by his criterion is unknown) and state the claim that survives (he justifies an institution no actual slaveholder could show he was in).
4. **Boundary probe — the degenerate case named in the brief.** Suppose a master whose soul is the servile kind and a slave whose soul is free: Aristotle's own "some have the souls and others the bodies of freemen". Run I.5–6 on this pair. Which of Aristotle's sentences says what the relation then is ("merely law and force"; "the reverse" of friendship)? What does Aristotle's own theory require to be done, and who in the city could do it? Solution: nothing in the theory tells anyone how to find out, which is the difficulty the lesson calls internal.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped: VII.7 against I.6.** Take I.6's "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" and VII.7's "Asians … always in a state of subjection and slavery". Derive the contradiction if both are read strictly (a people cannot be slaves "by climate" if slavery is a property of individual souls that birth does not fix — "nature cannot always accomplish" good from good), then state the weaker reading of VII.7 that survives (a tendency, not a mark). Solution says which reading the Book VII best city's "spiritless farmers of mixed race" requires.
6. **Causal ranking (synthesis).** Rank the three reasons Aristotle gives for the city's naturalness — growth from the household, the city as the end of that growth, speech about the just — as necessary / enabling / sufficient for his conclusion that man is a political animal, and say which one Plato's artefact-city lacks. Defend in a paragraph.
7. **Paired-source corroboration.** Set Aristotle's I.2 ("he who is without a state … a beast or a god") beside Vernant's thesis from `greek-miracle` (reason as "a creature of the city") as the course stated it. Where do they agree (speech and the city make each other), where do they differ (for Aristotle this is nature; for the course's account of the Greek miracle it was a historical rearrangement after the palaces fell), and what judgment follows — is the political animal a fact about humans or about Greeks after 800 BC? "Possible answers" with two defensible positions.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`.** Aristotle says the household is a monarchy (one rules by nature), and the city is a community of free men who rule "in turn". If no free man can be shown by nature to be another's superior (the result of section 6), state what must decide who rules among them — and whether it can be nature at all. The solution says in one sentence that the reader has arrived at Book III's question, "who should be sovereign — the many, the rich, the good, the one best man, or the law?", `<Ref>` the next lesson; and that the answer Aristotle gives there ("the law is reason without desire") is the ladder's top rung with no man on it.

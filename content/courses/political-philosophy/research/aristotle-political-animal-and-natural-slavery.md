# Research brief: aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery (Aristotle on the household: the political animal, natural hierarchy and natural slavery)

goal: Analyze Aristotle's argument that the city is natural and that some men are slaves by nature, and locate the difficulty he concedes inside it

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `greek-miracle`: the polis as the community that decides by speech; the two equalities (by merit / one share each). From `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`: Plato's city built from need and the division of labour, its three classes, its unity premise, and Aristotle's "all say mine" already heard as the external objection. From `decline-of-regimes`: the regime as a psychology; Aristotle's objection to Plato's cycle; the mixed constitution (introduced there). From `thucydides-power-and-justice`: the Athenian thesis — "of men we know, that by a necessary law of their nature they rule wherever they can" (V.105) — and its outcome at Melos, where the grown men were killed and "the women and children sold for slaves": enslavement by conquest, of Greeks, by the Greek city that called itself the school of Hellas; also the Funeral Oration's ideal of the citizen who takes part in public affairs ("useless" if he does not), which Aristotle's political animal turns into a claim about human nature. From `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`: justice as the interest of the stronger. The reader has not met Aristotle as a thinker in his own right; this is where he is anchored.
- **Contributes:** One move: the city is natural — not a contract, not a fever — because it grows from the household and is the end at which the household aims, and because man alone has speech about the just; and the same "nature" that makes the city makes a hierarchy of ruler and ruled run through everything, down to the master and the natural slave. The lesson's centre (course.yaml notes) is the INTERNAL difficulty: Aristotle reports the objection that slavery is only by law and force, answers it, and then concedes that nature "often fails" to make the bodies and souls his theory needs — so there is no mark by which a natural slave can be recognised, war captives may be the wrong men, and a master may have the slavish soul. Planned as a reversal seam: the theory is built at full strength, then the text itself undoes its application, and the lesson closes on the verdict, in four parts along Aristotle's own line between political and despotic rule (round 2): people differ in the capacity to lead, partly by birth (strong); that the most capable should rule is common ground; that whoever rules is the most capable fails as a rule and holds where office is contested and removable (on balance); that some may be owned fails by Aristotle's own concessions (established) — and the defence "freedom among equals, slavery for outsiders" fails with it.
- **Hands off:** Decided here, in four parts: capacity to lead differs, partly by birth (strong); the most capable should rule (common ground); whoever rules is not therefore the most capable, except where office is contested and removable (on balance — the elite-theory side is in `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`); natural slavery fails as a justification of any actual mastery (established), and so does the line drawn at outsiders. The failure is in the step from political to despotic rule. Passed on: if hierarchy is natural but cannot be read off anyone, who should rule a city of free men? The theory is then run on a real servile population, the helots, in `sparta-as-test-case`. `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` takes the citizen, the six regimes and the middle. The climate theory of Greek and barbarian (VII.7) returns there and in `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life` as the question of who may be a citizen of the best city. The natural-slavery argument is the backward Ref for `vitoria-right-to-the-americas` (Vitoria rejects the title), for `augustine-two-cities` (slavery by sin, not nature), for `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` (equality of ability) and for `strauss-natural-right-and-history` (the remark that Aristotle did conceive the injustice of slavery). Chrematistics returns in `aquinas-law-and-kingship` (usury) and `locke-property-and-consent`.
- **Spine step:** "Aristotle grounds rule in nature: man is a political animal, hierarchy runs through all things, some are slaves by nature — and he admits the theory's difficulty himself; the stable city is the one the middle governs"

## VERDICT

Lesson verdict (research/verdicts/REGISTER.md, Module 1; **round 2: the single verdict is replaced by four graded parts**). Binding: state each part no more and no less firmly.

**The hinge is Aristotle's own distinction between two kinds of rule.** *Political* (constitutional) rule is rule over the free and equal, who rule and are ruled in turn; *despotic* rule is mastery, the rule of an owner over what he owns. "The rule of a master is not a constitutional rule … For there is one rule exercised over subjects who are by nature free, another over subjects who are by nature slaves … constitutional rule is a government of freemen and equals" (I.7, `research/texts/aristotle/politics-s1-books1-2.txt` l.418–426); in constitutional states "the citizens rule and are ruled by turns" (I.12, l.862–866); "there is a rule of another kind, which is exercised over freemen and equals by birth — a constitutional rule, which the ruler must learn by obeying" (III.4, `research/texts/politics-s2-book3-4.txt` l.308–316); regimes that rule in the rulers' interest "are despotic, whereas a state is a community of freemen" (III.6, l.501–504). The claim "some men are slaves by nature" runs four claims together. Three are about political rule and one is about despotic rule, and they come out differently.

1. **People differ in their capacity to lead, partly by birth. → Holds. Grade: strong.**
   - *Evidence.* Twin studies: among 213 pairs of adult male twins from the Minnesota Twin Registry (119 identical, 94 fraternal), genetic factors accounted for about 30% of the variance in leadership role occupancy (95% CI .14–.44), the rest non-shared environment, shared family environment negligible (Arvey, Rotundo, Johnson, Zhang & McGue, *Leadership Quarterly* 17, 2006, `research/texts/round2-a/leadership/arvey-2006-leadership-role-occupancy-male-twins.txt` l.20–26, 238–244, 709–710, 723–731, 753–755); in 196 pairs of female twins the estimate was 32% (CI .16–.48), not different from the men's (Arvey, Zhang, Avolio & Krueger, *J. Applied Psychology* 92, 2007, `arvey-2007-leadership-role-occupancy-women.txt` l.20–22, 562–568). Meta-analyses: across 222 correlations from 73 samples, personality predicts leadership — extraversion ρ = .31, conscientiousness .28, neuroticism −.24, openness .24; for emergence as a leader extraversion and conscientiousness each .33; the five traits together R = .48 (.53 for emergence, .39 for effectiveness) (Judge, Bono, Ilies & Gerhardt, *J. Applied Psychology* 87, 2002, `judge-bono-ilies-gerhardt-2002-personality-leadership.txt` l.18–25, 342, 430–434, 557–563, 634; note the file's encoding: "!" is ρ and `"` is a minus sign); across 151 samples intelligence correlates with leadership .21, .27 corrected for range restriction — "considerably lower than previously thought" (Judge, Colbert & Ilies, *J. Applied Psychology* 89, 2004 — **abstract only**, `judge-colbert-ilies-2004-intelligence-leadership-abstract.txt` l.13–23).
   - *Limits, stated with the evidence.* The twin studies measure occupancy of leadership roles, self-reported, not good leadership; heritability is a population statistic (`the-cognitive-elite` defines it), so "partly by birth" means differences among people are partly genetic, not that any person's capacity is fixed; the correlations are moderate. Strong, not established: consistent across two twin samples and two meta-analyses, moderate in size.
2. **The most capable should rule. → Common ground, not a dispute. No grade.** Plato ("Until philosophers are kings … cities will never have rest from their evils", *Republic* V 473c–d, `research/texts/republic-s3-books5-6.txt` l.1506–1511), Aristotle (aristocracy, rule "of the best", III.7, `politics-s2-book3-4.txt` l.523–526; offices by merit as the aristocratic element of the mixture), Mosca (ruling minorities ought to be, and claim to be, distinguished by "a certain material, intellectual or even moral superiority", `research/texts/mosca/the-ruling-class.txt` l.810) and meritocratic liberals (Jefferson's "natural aristocracy", via `the-cognitive-elite`) all hold it. The dispute is over who the capable are, how they are found and what they may do — parts 3 and 4.
3. **Whoever actually rules is the most capable. → Fails as a general rule; holds where there is competition and removability. Grade: on balance.**
   - *Against it, from the course's own authors.* Plato's founding premise is that the cities as they are not ruled by the wise — that is why they "never have rest from their evils" (l.1506–1511). Aristotle's deviant regimes are rule by the one, few or many for their own interest (III.7, l.508–525). Thucydides: after Pericles his successors, "more on a level with one another, and each grasping at supremacy, … ended by committing even the conduct of state affairs to the whims of the multitude", which "produced a host of blunders" (II.65, `research/texts/thucydides-full-pg7142.txt` l.4396–4402); Cleon, "the most violent man at Athens, and at that time by far the most powerful with the commons" (III.36, l.5936–5939). Hereditary succession: Marcus Aurelius was succeeded by his son Commodus, and Cassius Dio writes that "our history now descends from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust" (Dio 72[71].36.4, Loeb tr. Cary, `research/texts/round2-a/leadership/cassius-dio-72-33-36-marcus-gold-to-iron.txt` l.14; on Commodus, "his great simplicity, however, together with his cowardice, made him the slave of his companions", 73[72].1.1–2, `cassius-dio-73-1-4-commodus.txt` l.5 — LacusCurtius page marked "not yet been proofread": check against the printed Loeb before quoting). The elite theorists say the same of ruling minorities: labels drift from capacity, closed aristocracies decay, the capable accumulate below (Pareto §§2052–2057; Michels on co-optation and selection for loyalty — `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`, second verdict).
   - *Where it holds.* Where office is contested and holders can be removed: Pareto's open elite that "assimilates" the able below is the hardest to overthrow (§2179); Sparta's yearly ephors chosen "from the whole people" were part of the mixture that held (`sparta-as-test-case`). This is the same result as the module verdict — the mixed regime with a share for every part survives — reached from the side of capacity.
   - *On balance, not strong:* the cases are illustrations, not a count; "holds where there is competition" is a tendency the course supports from the module-1 and module-7 verdicts, not a measured law.
4. **Some men are by nature fit to be owned (despotic rule over them is just). → Fails, by Aristotle's own concessions. Grade: established** (the failure is internal and textual). This is the verdict the drafted lesson already reaches; it stands unchanged:
   - *What decides it:* Aristotle's own sentences in I.5–6. Nature "would like to distinguish between the bodies of freemen and slaves … but the opposite often happens"; "the beauty of the soul is not seen"; good from good "is what nature, though she may intend it, cannot always accomplish"; war captives may be the wrong men ("what if the cause of the war be unjust?"; "men of the highest rank would be slaves"). So no mark picks out a natural slave, every actual slave is a slave by law whose nature is unknown, and the master may be the one with the slavish soul — in which case I.6 calls the relation "merely law and force", "the reverse" of friendship. A justification that cannot pick out its object justifies no actual slavery.
   - *Strongest objection:* a justification need not supply a test; and Books III and VII supply a mark after all — whole peoples "more servile in character" (III.14), so a sorting by peoples right on the whole is the most any practice can have.
   - *Why it does not overturn the verdict:* I.6 makes justice turn on whether this master and this slave are matched by nature; a right no one can show he holds over any given man licenses nothing; and a sorting by peoples, on Aristotle's own "similar differences in the different tribes of Hellas" (VII.7) and "cannot always accomplish" (I.6), sweeps in men who can deliberate.
   - *What would overturn it:* a criterion, stated in advance, that separates natural slaves from free men and does not reduce to "whoever was captured" or "whoever was born where".

**How the four fit (the sentence the lesson must make plain):** parts 1–3 are about *political* rule — who among the free and equal should hold office, and how to find them; part 4 is about *despotic* rule. Unequal capacity, which is real (1), can ground at most the claim to hold office among equals, in turn and removably (2–3); it cannot ground ownership of a man (4). Aristotle's own distinction of the two kinds of rule is where the step from "people differ" to "this man may own that one" breaks.

**Objection (author's review): "freedom among equals, slavery for outsiders" — the Greek/barbarian line held consistently.** Required: the lesson states it at full strength, with its best authors, before answering it; it does not change part 4.
- *At full strength.* The Greeks did not count foreigners as equals, and the objection says they were consistent: political rule among those who share a language, laws and gods; mastery over those who do not. Aristotle states the line twice: among barbarians "there is no natural ruler … they are a community of slaves", "Wherefore the poets say, 'It is meet that Hellenes should rule over barbarians'; as if they thought that the barbarian and the slave were by nature one" (I.2, quoting Euripides, *Iphigenia at Aulis*, `politics-s1-books1-2.txt` l.50–61); "Hellenes do not like to call Hellenes slaves, but confine the term to barbarians" (I.6, l.376–377); and barbarians are "more servile in character than Hellenes" (III.14, `politics-s2-book3-4.txt` l.1180). On this view equality is a relation among members, not a property of human beings; a people owes political rule to its own and nothing comparable to outsiders who share neither its language nor its culture; enslaving such outsiders is no great wrong and may even benefit them, as I.5 says rule benefits those fit to be ruled.
- *Answered.* (i) *The line was not held.* Greeks enslaved Greeks: the helots were Greeks of Messenia held by Sparta (`sparta-as-test-case`); at Melos the Athenians killed the men and "sold the women and children for slaves" (Thucydides V.116, `research/texts/thucydides-melian-dialogue.txt` l.295); and Plato has to legislate against the practice — "Do you think it right that Hellenes should enslave Hellenic States …? … no Hellene should be owned by them as a slave" (*Republic* V 469b–c, `republic-s3-books5-6.txt` l.1266–1275), which a rule that was already kept would not need. (ii) *Aristotle concedes the line does not track nature.* I.6 says Greeks "really mean the natural slave", for "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" — a property of persons, not peoples; VII.7 finds "similar differences in the different tribes of Hellas"; I.6's "cannot always accomplish" means a people holds souls of both kinds (the lesson's climate section already shows this). On Aristotle's own terms the line by birthplace is "whoever was born where" — the criterion part 4's overturn-condition excludes. (iii) *The principle is symmetric.* If enslaving outsiders who share neither language nor culture is no great wrong, it was no great wrong when Rome did it to the ancestors of Europeans: Caesar sold the whole town of the Atuatuci, "53,000 persons" by the buyers' count (*Gallic War* II.33, `research/texts/round2-a/slavery/caesar-bg-2-33-mcdevitte.txt`), and Plutarch says that in Gaul he took a million prisoners (*Caesar* 15, `plutarch-caesar-15-long.txt` — an ancient round figure, not a count); the English word *slave* comes, on the traditional derivation, from *Slav*, because Slavs were so often enslaved in the Middle Ages (`wiktionary-slave-etymology.txt` [secondary; an alternative derivation exists]); Viking raiders sold captives from the British Isles and the Slavic east [UNVERIFIED: standard history; bind before use]; and Barbary corsairs took, on one historian's estimate, between one million and 1.25 million European Christians into slavery between 1530 and 1780 (R. C. Davis, *Christian Slaves, Muslim Masters*, 2003 [secondary: the book not read; the figure and its method — a replacement-rate extrapolation from 1580–1680 — from the Ohio State release `davis-2003-osu-news-release-2004.txt` l.28–31 and the *Guardian* report `davis-2003-guardian-carroll-2004-mg-reprint.txt` l.7–21, where another historian says the figures "sound a bit dodgy" and may be exaggerated]). A principle that licenses all of these licenses whoever is stronger; it is the Athenian thesis at Melos (`thucydides-power-and-justice`), not a principle of freedom. (iv) *"Beneficial to them."* One clause: I.6 itself says that where master and slave are not matched by nature the relation is "the reverse" of a common interest; the record of what enslavement did to the enslaved and their societies is weighed in module 14 (`<Ref>` `auditing-the-case-for-colonialism`, the slave-trades finding).
- *Grade:* the historical premise (the Greeks held the line) **fails — established** (the counter-cases are in the course's own primary texts); the principle as a justification of mastery **fails — strong**, because it reduces to a criterion of birthplace, which Aristotle himself says does not track the nature on which his justification depends, and because, applied consistently, it licenses the enslavement of the people who hold it.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): Aristotle begins not with a city but with a household — man, woman, children, slaves; each relation of ruler and ruled in it is natural; the city grows from such households as an oak from an acorn; then he faces the objection, reported from his own contemporaries, that slavery exists only by law and force; follow his answer and the place where he admits it does not quite work. The lesson builds the theory to full strength first — the reader must feel its force before the seam — and the seam is the text's own.

1. **Why start with a household rather than a city — and what "natural" is going to mean.** The obvious attempt (Plato's, `<Ref>` `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`): build the city from need and the division of labour, as an artefact. Why Aristotle refuses it: a thing is understood by watching it grow from its least parts, and the least parts of a city are two pairings nature itself makes — male and female for generation, ruler and ruled for preservation. The answer: household → village → city, and the city is not just the last and biggest but the END: "the nature of a thing is its end", what it is when its growth is complete. The acorn/oak image is the lesson's, not the text's (say so — the text's images are the Delphian knife and the dead hand). Because: if the city is the completed form of something that grows by itself, it is natural in the way an adult body is, not in the way a hut is. Assumption named: nature is purposive — things have ends.
2. **Man is a political animal because he has speech — the polis of `greek-miracle` gets its definition.** Obvious attempt: bees and herd animals are social too. Why it is not enough: they have voice, which signals pleasure and pain; man alone has speech, which "sets forth the expedient and the inexpedient, and likewise the just and the unjust" — and a shared perception of good and evil, just and unjust, is what MAKES a household and a city. Then the priority of the whole: a hand cut from the body is a hand in name only; the man who needs no city is "a beast or a god"; perfected by law he is the best of animals, separated from it the worst, "armed with intelligence". Concept `political-animal`. `<Ref>` `greek-miracle` in one clause: the community that puts its affairs "in the middle" and decides them by speech is, on this account, what human beings are for. Because: an animal whose defining capacity is arguing about justice can only exercise it in a community that argues about justice.
3. **The ladder of rule: hierarchy runs through everything.** From the household's three relations Aristotle generalises (I.5): wherever there is a composite, one part rules — soul over body "despotically", intellect over appetite "constitutionally and royally"; man over tame animals, who are better off ruled; male over female; and so, "of necessity, extends to all mankind". The method sentence that will matter at the seam: "we must look for the intentions of nature in things which retain their nature, and not in things which are corrupted" — so study the man in the most perfect state of body and soul, where the hierarchy is visible, "although in bad or corrupted natures the body will often appear to rule over the soul". Because: if the hierarchy is nature's, it must show in nature's best specimens, and the corrupted cases are set aside as evidence. Heading: "A ladder of rule from soul and body down to master and slave".
4. **The natural slave defined — at full strength.** Who is at the bottom of the ladder? One "who can be, and therefore is, another's", and who "participates in rational principle enough to apprehend, but not to have, such a principle" — above the tame animal, which cannot even apprehend; an instrument of action, not production (the shuttle makes cloth, the slave helps the master live). Daedalus's statues: if tools moved by themselves, no one would need slaves. And it is good for him: "it is better for them, as for all inferiors, that they should be under the rule of a master"; where the relation is natural, master and slave "are friends and have a common interest". Concept `natural-slavery`, first half. Assumption named: a soul can lack the ruling part and still be human. Register rule (the two traps): state it as Aristotle states it, without softening and without an adjective.
5. **The objection Aristotle reports — "slavery is against nature" — and his first answer.** I.3 and I.6: "others affirm that the rule of a master over slaves is contrary to nature, and that the distinction between slave and freeman exists by law only, and not by nature; and being an interference with nature is therefore unjust." At full strength, as Aristotle gives it: jurists "detest the notion that, because one man has the power of doing violence and is superior in brute strength, another shall be his slave"; "what if the cause of the war be unjust?"; "no one would ever say that he is a slave who is unworthy to be a slave — were this the case, men of the highest rank would be slaves" if captured. Aristotle AGREES with all of this: slavery by law is "a sort of convention", and conquest proves nothing. Mark the backward link in one sentence with `<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-justice`: the position Aristotle reports — that one party "identifies justice with … the mere rule of the stronger" — is the Athenian thesis at Melos, and Melos is the case the reported objection describes: Greeks taken in a war whose justice the victors did not even claim, then sold. Do not re-tell Melos; one clause of reminder. His answer: the convention's defenders and attackers both really mean natural slavery — Greeks will not call Greeks slaves, "confining the term to barbarians", which is to say "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere". Because: the only slavery that can be just is the natural kind; the legal kind is just only when it happens to coincide with it.
6. **THE SEAM — the text undoes its own application.** From the theory one would expect a mark by which the natural slave is known. The record is the opposite. (a) Bodies: "Nature would like to distinguish between the bodies of freemen and slaves … but the opposite often happens — that some have the souls and others have the bodies of freemen." (b) Souls: if men differed as the statues of the gods differ from men, all would agree; "but the beauty of the body is seen, whereas the beauty of the soul is not seen" — the one thing that would decide is the one thing not visible. (c) Birth: Greeks think good begets good, "but this is what nature, though she may intend it, cannot always accomplish". (d) War: the captive may be the better man. Draw the consequences the text does not draw but licenses: there is no procedure by which a city could tell a natural slave from a legal one; every actual slave is a legal slave whose natural status is unknown; and since "some have the souls of freemen" in servile bodies and vice versa, a master may be the one with the slavish soul — in which case, by Aristotle's own criterion, the relation is "merely law and force" and "the reverse" of friendship. Aristotle's own conclusion: "all are not either slaves by nature or freemen by nature" — the theory survives as a theory, and the institution it was meant to justify is left standing on convention. The course's judgment, stated dryly: the difficulty is internal; it is Aristotle's, and he saw it (`<Ref>` forward: the course will meet the claim that he "could not have conceived of the injustice of slavery" and the reply that he did, `strauss-natural-right-and-history`). Because: a hierarchy "of nature" that cannot be read off any natural sign cannot be applied without the force it was meant to replace.
7. **Greek and barbarian: the climate theory as the attempted mark.** If souls cannot be seen one by one, perhaps peoples can be sorted. VII.7 at full strength: Europeans spirited but unintelligent, Asians intelligent but spiritless and "therefore always in a state of subjection and slavery", Greeks between, "high-spirited and also intelligent", able "to rule the world" if one state. I.2's "it is meet that Hellenes should rule over barbarians" and I.8's "naturally just" war against those "intended by nature to be governed, who will not submit". Concept `greek-barbarian`. The STRONGEST OBJECTION to this, from the Greek side itself: Aristotle has just said that Greeks are enslaved too and that the line between Greek and barbarian is a convention of naming ("some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" cuts both ways); a sorting by birthplace is as arbitrary as sorting numbers into ten thousand and the rest; and the best city of Book VII needs its farmers "spiritless" and "of mixed race" so that they will not combine — a policy, not a finding. The lesson concedes that the climate theory is Aristotle's and that it is where the theory of natural slavery goes to look for its mark; it does not moralise, and it says the mark is not found: whether Aristotle meant VII.7 as a mark or a tendency is an interpretive dispute the lesson may leave as one, but on either reading it cannot pick out the individual natural slave I.6's test requires — that is decided in the weighing (step 9). `<Ref>` forward: the theory's rejection as a title to conquest (`vitoria-right-to-the-americas`) and its reappearance as the question of who can be a citizen of the best state (`aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`).
8. **Wealth-getting: the other thing the household must not do without limit.** Why here? Because the household's end is living well, and the same teleology that ranks souls ranks ways of acquiring. A shoe can be worn or exchanged; barter is natural; money arises as a measure (wine for corn, metal by weight, then stamped); retail trade turns the measure into the end, and the end has no limit — Midas starving on gold. Medicine's end (health) is unlimited but its means are limited; money-making reverses this. The unlimited desire "springs from caring for living only, not living well". Usury is the most unnatural: "money born of money" (tokos, "offspring"). Concept `chrematistics`. Because: a hierarchy of natural ends makes acquisition a servant; an acquisition without end has no place on the ladder and so is "against nature" in exactly the sense slavery by force is.
9. **The weighing — round 2: "Some may lead free men; none may own them".** (Last body section; heading states the verdict as a claim. The REVISION block's GAIN 1–8 is binding: the hinge, parts 1–4 and the outsiders objection, in that order; the older text below is part 4 and stays as its content.) The claim: some men are slaves by nature, so mastery over them is just. The decision, recalled from step 6 in three sentences: no mark; every actual slave a slave by law; the master may be the slavish one, which I.6 calls "law and force". The strongest objection at full strength: a justification need not supply a test — Aristotle claims only that natural slaves exist and that ruling them is just; and III.14/VII.7 offer a sorting by peoples that is right on the whole. The answer: I.6 makes justice turn on whether this master and this slave are matched by nature, so a right no one can show he holds over any given man licenses no act of mastery; and a sorting by peoples, on Aristotle's own "similar differences in the different tribes of Hellas" and "cannot always accomplish", sweeps in men who can deliberate — by I.6, enslaved by force. What survives: the premise that people differ in ability; the failure is in the step from differing ability to a right of mastery. Grade: established, because the failure is internal and textual. What would overturn it: a criterion, stated in advance, that separates natural slaves from free men and does not reduce to "whoever was captured". `<Conclusion>`: states the verdict in plain words with its grade (the argument that some men are slaves by nature and may justly be mastered fails — established — on Aristotle's own sentences; the premise that people differ in ability stands), then what the reader can now do (reconstruct the chain from the household to the city and the ladder of rule, state the natural-slave argument and the four places it admits failure, say what the climate theory was for), then the hand-off: among free men no one can be shown by nature to be another's master, so who among them should rule — `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`.

Headings that fall out: "Begin with what grows: household, village, city" · "Speech about the just: why man is a political animal" · "A ladder of rule from soul and body down to master and slave" · "The natural slave: one who apprehends reason without having it" · "Slavery by law: the objection Aristotle reports and accepts" · "Where the theory fails on its own terms: no mark, no sign, no test" · "Greek and barbarian: the climate theory as the missing mark" · "Getting without limit: chrematistics and the birth of money from money" · "Natural slavery fails as a justification, by Aristotle's own concessions".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), in chain order:** Aristotle (384–322; Plato's pupil for twenty years, tutor to Alexander, founder of the Lyceum at Athens; the Politics is lecture-notes, compressed, c. 335–322 — one sentence, no more); Bekker numbers (the page/column/line of the 1831 edition, e.g. 1253a — one clause at first citation, then use them); oikos / household (a man, his wife, children, slaves, and property — not a nuclear family); village (several households, "colonies" of one); polis (recall `greek-miracle`); telos / end ("the nature of a thing is its end" — define at first use, keep the Greek once); despotic / royal / constitutional rule (master–slave, father–child, husband–wife and citizen–citizen — the three kinds the lesson uses throughout); Daedalus and Hephaestus (the mythical craftsman and the smith-god whose made things moved); barbarian (anyone who does not speak Greek — anchor as a linguistic word before VII.7 gives it a climate); Hellenes (the Greeks' own name); Theodectes (a tragedian; one clause); perioeci (the free non-citizen "dwellers-around" of Sparta and Crete — anchor at VII.9 and Ref `sparta-as-test-case`); chrematistics (anchor as the concept id: wealth-getting without limit); tokos (interest; literally "offspring"); Midas; Thales (recall `greek-miracle`); Dionysius (tyrant of Syracuse, c. 400 — one clause); Euripides (when I.2 quotes him).
- **The two traps are a register rule (course tone).** Neither airbrush nor dismiss. The theory is stated in Aristotle's words at full strength; the difficulty is stated in Aristotle's words at full strength. The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **The internal difficulty is the centre (course.yaml notes), and it is INTERNAL.** The lesson's reversal is not "modern readers object"; it is "Aristotle's own text says the opposite often happens". The four concession points (bodies, visible souls, birth, war) are all in I.5–6 and must be quoted, not paraphrased. The inference "the master may be the one with the slavish soul" is the course's own and is marked as such ("Aristotle does not draw this consequence; his premises do").
- **Natural slavery is not racial slavery.** Aristotle denies that conquest or ethnicity as such makes a slave (I.6); the climate theory (VII.7) is a separate, later attempt to find the mark by peoples, and the lesson keeps the two apart and shows that VII.7 contradicts I.6's "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" when read strictly. Do not import the early-modern debate here; `vitoria-right-to-the-americas` has it.
- **Women and children (I.12–13):** one paragraph, in the text's words — the wife ruled "constitutionally with permanent inequality", with a deliberative faculty "without authority"; the lesson states it, places it on the ladder, and moves on; `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` will note that Aristotle excludes women from citizenship without re-arguing. Not a separate section.
- **Do not re-teach Plato's city** — `<Ref>` `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king` where Aristotle's method departs from it, and recall "all say mine" in one clause. Do not teach II.2–5 again.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up, the one mechanical item).** Reconstruct the household → village → city chain and say at each step what "for the sake of" names (daily needs, more than daily needs, living well). From `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king` (E3): state Plato's starting point for the city in one sentence and say which word in Aristotle's I.2 is the direct reply ("nature is the end" against "need").
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill) on I.6 (1255a3–b15).** Author (Aristotle, c. 330, a resident alien in Athens who owned slaves — his will freeing some survives in Diogenes Laertius, if the writer wants one clause), genre (lecture notes), audience (students of a school, not citizens in assembly); what the passage records (that jurists and philosophers in his day disputed slavery's justice), what it argues, what it omits (any procedure for telling the natural slave). Closing: what can the passage be USED to establish about fourth-century Greek opinion — and what does the fact that the objection is reported in Aristotle's own words change about the charge that antiquity "could not conceive" the injustice of slavery?
3. **Claim to refute (E3 from `thucydides-power-and-justice`).** "Aristotle justifies slavery as it existed in Greece — for instance the enslavement of the Melians." Name the step that fails (he denies that conquest confers title and that Greek/barbarian as such does; he says "all are not either slaves by nature or freemen by nature"; every actual slave was a legal slave whose status by his criterion is unknown) and state the claim that survives (he justifies an institution no actual slaveholder could show he was in). The Melian case makes the failure concrete: Greeks, taken by a city that claimed no justice for the war, sold — by I.6 the paradigm of slavery "merely by law and force".
4. **Boundary probe — the degenerate case named in the brief.** Suppose a master whose soul is the servile kind and a slave whose soul is free: Aristotle's own "some have the souls and others the bodies of freemen". Run I.5–6 on this pair. Which of Aristotle's sentences says what the relation then is ("merely law and force"; "the reverse" of friendship)? What does Aristotle's own theory require to be done, and who in the city could do it? Solution: nothing in the theory tells anyone how to find out, which is the difficulty the lesson calls internal.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped: VII.7 against I.6.** Take I.6's "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere" and VII.7's "Asians … always in a state of subjection and slavery". Derive the contradiction if both are read strictly (a people cannot be slaves "by climate" if slavery is a property of individual souls that birth does not fix — "nature cannot always accomplish" good from good), then state the weaker reading of VII.7 that survives (a tendency, not a mark). Solution says which reading the Book VII best city's "spiritless farmers of mixed race" requires.
6. **Causal ranking (synthesis).** Rank the three reasons Aristotle gives for the city's naturalness — growth from the household, the city as the end of that growth, speech about the just — as necessary / enabling / sufficient for his conclusion that man is a political animal, and say which one Plato's artefact-city lacks. Defend in a paragraph.
7. **Paired-source corroboration.** Set Aristotle's I.2 ("he who is without a state … a beast or a god") beside the account of the polis in `greek-miracle` (political argument as the by-product of a historical rearrangement after the palaces fell — stated there as the course's own; no scholar named in the exercise) and Pericles' "we regard him who takes no part in these duties not as unambitious but as useless" (`thucydides-power-and-justice`). Where do they agree (speech and the city make each other; the man outside public life is less than a man — "useless", "a beast or a god"), where do they differ (for Aristotle this is nature; for the Greek-cities lesson it was a historical rearrangement; for Pericles a boast about one city), and what judgment follows — is the political animal a fact about humans or about Greeks after 800 BC? "Possible answers" with two defensible positions.
8. **Attack the verdict (part 4).** The lesson judged that natural slavery fails as a justification, and graded the verdict established. State the strongest objection in its best form — a justification need not supply a test of who falls under it, and Books III and VII offer a sorting by peoples right on the whole — and say what evidence or argument would be needed to overturn the verdict. Solution: the objection at full strength (a claim's truth vs our ability to apply it; III.14 "by nature slaves"; VII.7); why it fails against a justification of mastery (I.6 ties justice to the particular pair; a right no one can show licenses nothing; VII.7's own "similar differences in the different tribes of Hellas"); what would overturn it — a criterion stated in advance that separates natural slaves from free men and reduces neither to "whoever was captured" nor to "whoever was born where", the second being the objection's own sorting.
8b. **Attack part 3 (round 2).** State at full strength the view that those who rule are on the whole the most capable (selection by success; Pareto's circulation of elites), and say what evidence would overturn the verdict that this fails as a general rule. Solution: the drift of label from capacity is Pareto's own (§2052); Thucydides II.65 and III.36; Marcus to Commodus; what would overturn it — closed, hereditary or co-opting rulers who kept their capacity across generations; and where the claim holds (contested, removable office).
8c. **The outsiders objection (round 2).** "The Greeks were consistent: freedom among equals, slavery for outsiders." State the view in its best form from I.2 (Euripides) and I.6, then test it against three facts (the helots; Melos; *Republic* V 469b–c) and one symmetry (Caesar's sale of the Atuatuci; the *Slav* etymology; the Barbary captives, with the estimate's method and dissent stated). Solution: the historical premise fails (established); the principle reduces to "whoever was born where", which I.6 and VII.7 deny tracks nature, and applied consistently licenses the enslavement of those who hold it (strong).
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`.** Aristotle says the household is a monarchy (one rules by nature), and the city is a community of free men who rule "in turn". If no free man can be shown by nature to be another's superior (the result of section 6), state what must decide who rules among them — and whether it can be nature at all. The solution says in one sentence that the reader has arrived at Book III's question, "who should be sovereign — the many, the rich, the good, the one best man, or the law?", `<Ref>` the next lesson; and that the answer Aristotle gives there ("the law is reason without desire") is the ladder's top rung with no man on it.

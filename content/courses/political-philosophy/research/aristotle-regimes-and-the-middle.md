# Research brief: aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle (Aristotle on who should rule: regimes, the multitude and the middle class)

status: planned
module: greek-discovery
type: core
goal: Analyze Aristotle's six regimes and his answer to who should be sovereign, including his case for the rule of law and the middle

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`: the city as natural, the ladder of rule, the three kinds of rule (despotic, royal, constitutional), and the result that no free man can be shown by nature to be another's superior — the FORWARD exercise there left the reader asking what then decides who rules. From `decline-of-regimes`: Plato's five regimes and the concept `regime-cycle`; the concept `mixed-constitution`, introduced there from Laws III's Sparta, and Megillus's wall (the FORWARD exercise there asked what must be defined before a mixture can be called a regime). From `thucydides-power-and-justice`: a real assembly at work — Pericles' "we … are able to judge at all events if we cannot originate", Cleon's "audience of a rhetorician", Diodotus's advisers who bear the blame while the audience does not, the overnight reversal on Mytilene; and Thucydides' own verdicts on who should rule Athens — under Pericles "nominally a democracy … government by the first citizen" (II.65), and the Five Thousand of 411 as the best government Athens had in his time because "the fusion of the high and the low was effected with judgment" (VIII.97). From `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`: "every regime legislates for its rulers' interest". From `greek-miracle`: the two equalities and Cleisthenes' Athens.
- **Contributes:** One move: "who should rule" is answered by first asking what a regime IS (the arrangement of offices, above all the supreme one) and who a citizen is (one who shares in judging and deciding), then classifying regimes by number AND by end (six, not Plato's five), then showing that each of the five claimants — the many, the rich, the well-born, the one best man, the law — leads to injustice if taken alone, and settling on: the law should be sovereign where it can, the many deliberate better together than any one of them (the feast), the pre-eminent man is either made king or ostracised, and the regime most cities can actually have is polity — a mixture of oligarchy and democracy resting on a large middle class.
- **Hands off:** A city ranked by how it decides can still fall; what makes it fall from inside, and what the city is for, is `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`. The charge that a mixture is no regime at all (Laws IV's "parties, not polities"; Bodin later) is carried as an objection and handed to `bodin-sovereignty`. The six regimes and polity are the backward Refs for `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome` (six simple forms in a cycle, and Rome's consuls, senate and people as a mixture that checks itself), `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law` (Scipio's three simple forms and the cycle recalled), `aquinas-law-and-kingship` (kingship as the best simple form) and `machiavelli-discourses-republic`. The wisdom of the multitude returns in `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township` and `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`.
- **Spine step:** "Aristotle grounds rule in nature: man is a political animal, hierarchy runs through all things, some are slaves by nature — and he admits the theory's difficulty himself; the stable city is the one the middle governs"

  - III.8 (1279b–1280a) and IV.4 (1290a–b): "the real difference between democracy and oligarchy is poverty and wealth"; if 1300 citizens had 1000 rich ruling 300 free poor, it would not be a democracy; the Ethiopian offices by stature; number is an accident.
  - III.9 (1280a–1281a): each party holds a part of justice — the democrats infer equality in all from equality in freedom, the oligarchs superiority in all from superiority in wealth; the state is not an alliance for exchange and defence (Tyrrhenians and Carthaginians with trade treaties are not one state; Corinth and Megara with touching walls are not one; Lycophron's law as "mere surety"); the state exists "for the sake of noble actions", a community of families "in a perfect and self-sufficing life"; "a state is not a mere society having a common place … for the prevention of mutual crime and for the sake of exchange" — the direct answer to Glaucon's contract (`<Ref>` `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`).
  - III.10 (1281a): WHO SHOULD BE SOVEREIGN — the multitude, the wealthy, the good, the one best man, the tyrant, or the law? Each in turn: if the many, they will divide the rich's property "and this is not unjust?" ("it is the uttermost injustice"); if the few rich, the same in reverse; if the good, all others are dishonoured; if one, the same; if the law — "but what if the law is oligarchic or democratic?" Quote the chain; it is the lesson's hinge.
  - III.11 (1281a–1282b, text l. ~760–860): THE WISDOM OF THE MULTITUDE — "the many, of whom each individual is but an ordinary person, when they meet together may very likely be better than the few good, if regarded not individually but collectively, just as a feast to which many contribute is better than a dinner provided out of a single purse"; "they become in a manner one man, who has many feet, and hands, and senses"; the user judges the house better than the builder, the guest the feast better than the cook (l. 858); so give the many the assembly and the courts (election and audit), not the highest offices; and over all — "laws, when good, should be supreme".
  - III.12–13 (1282b–1284b): claims to office must rest on elements of the state (freedom, wealth, virtue, birth); better-born flute-players do not get better flutes; commensurability of height and virtue absurd; the pre-eminent individual "a god among men" (1284a, text l. 1059) — no law for him; Periander's advice to Thrasybulus, cutting down the tallest ears of corn; the Argo refusing Heracles; Antisthenes' lions ("where are your claws?"); ostracism as "political justice" in imperfect regimes.
  - III.14–17 (1285a–1288b): five kinds of kingship (Spartan generalship for life, barbarian, elective tyranny/aesymnetia, heroic, absolute); best man or best laws? — the Egyptian doctor may alter treatment after the fourth day; the many "are less easily corrupted", like more water; the historical sequence kingship → oligarchy → tyranny → democracy (1286b); III.16 THE RULE OF LAW — "he who bids the law rule may be deemed to bid God and Reason alone rule, but he who bids man rule adds an element of the beast; for desire is a wild beast, and passion perverts the minds of rulers, even when they are the best of men. The law is reason unaffected by desire" (1287a, text l. ~1410–1418); the physician who treats himself; the king's many eyes, ears, hands; III.17 absolute kingship fits only a people that produces one pre-eminent in virtue.

  - IV.3–6 (1289b–1293a): why there are many regimes (the city has many parts); the two-winds / two-modes view that only democracy and oligarchy exist; the demagogue as the tyrant's flatterer; decree-rule by demagogues "is not a constitution" (1292a); varieties of democracy and oligarchy (one figure, no section).
  - IV.7–9 (1293a–1294b): aristocracy and polity as further forms; polity is "a fusion of oligarchy and democracy" (1293b); three modes of mixing (combine both rules; take the mean; take one from each — e.g. election from oligarchy, no property qualification from democracy, as at Sparta); the sign of a good mixture — "it may be called either a democracy or an oligarchy", as Sparta is called both (1294b, text l. ~2283 region "also in the mean"); all parts must be willing it to last.
  - IV.11 (1295a–1296b, text l. ~2389–2460): THE MIDDLE — "a city ought to be composed, as far as possible, of equals and similars; and these are generally the middle classes"; the very rich "grow into violent and great criminals", the very poor "into rogues and petty rascals"; the rich will not obey and know not how, the poor are too degraded to command; the middle "least likely to shrink from rule, or to be over-ambitious for it"; Phocylides, "many things are best in the mean; I desire to be of a middle condition in my city" (l. 2417–2421); "where the middle class is large, there are least likely to be factions"; large states less factious because the middle is large; "the best legislators have been of a middle condition — Solon, Lycurgus, Charondas" (l. 2452–2455); why polity is rare — the middle is seldom numerous and the two extremes fight for the regime as a prize. ## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): five candidates claim the right to rule a city — the many, the rich, the well-born, the one best man, the law; each claim taken alone leads to something unjust, and a city can still be ranked by how it decides; follow his classification of regimes and the answer he settles on. The lesson runs as Book III runs: two definitions, a table, a tournament of claims, and an answer in three parts.

1. **Before "who should rule": what is being ruled, and who counts?** Obvious attempt: the city is the people in a place. Why it fails: the same people on the same ground are a different city when the constitution changes (the chorus that is comic one day and tragic the next) — so the debts of a tyranny can be repudiated. The answer: the constitution IS the city's identity, and a constitution is "the arrangement of offices, especially the supreme one". Then the citizen: not residence, not descent (which regresses — the first citizens had no citizen parents; Gorgias's joke), but "one who shares in judging and deciding" — a definition that "fits democracy best" and that the lesson flags as already loaded. Because: the question "who should rule" has to name what "rule" is (holding the supreme office) and who is eligible to ask (those who share in judging), before any claim can be weighed.
2. **The good man and the good citizen.** Obvious expectation: the same thing. Why not: a ship's crew has rowers, pilots and look-outs, each with a different excellence and one common end — safe voyage; the citizen's excellence is relative to the regime he serves, so a good citizen of an oligarchy is a bad one of a democracy; only in the best regime, and only for the ruler, do the two coincide. Corollary that will matter: "he who has never learned to obey cannot be a good commander" — the citizen of a free city must know both. Because: if "good citizen" is regime-relative, ranking regimes cannot be done by asking which produces good citizens; it needs an external standard — the common good.
3. **Six regimes, not five: number AND end.** Plato's five (`<Ref>` `decline-of-regimes`) were a descending psychology. Aristotle's grid: rulers one / few / many, crossed with for the common good / for the rulers' own — kingship, aristocracy, polity vs tyranny, oligarchy, democracy. Concept `six-regimes`. Then the correction that makes the grid honest: number is an accident; "the real difference between democracy and oligarchy is poverty and wealth" — if a thousand rich ruled three hundred poor it would still be an oligarchy. Because: Thrasymachus's "every regime legislates for its rulers" (`<Ref>` `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`) is accepted as the definition of the three perversions — and denied as a definition of the three true forms, which is the whole dispute.
4. **Each claimant's justice is partial — and the state is not a contract.** III.9: the democrat argues "equal in freedom, so equal in everything"; the oligarch "superior in wealth, so superior in everything"; each holds a part of justice and takes it for the whole. `<Ref>` `greek-miracle`: these are the arithmetic and geometric equalities with a claimant attached to each. Then the standard that judges them: the state is not an alliance for trade and defence — trading partners with treaties are not one state, and Corinth and Megara would not be one if their walls touched; a law that is "mere surety" for mutual non-injury (Lycophron) is Glaucon's contract (`<Ref>` `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`) and Aristotle rejects it in one clause: the state exists "for the sake of noble actions", a community of households "in a perfect and self-sufficing life". Because: only an end beyond the members' interests can judge among their claims.
5. **The tournament: five claimants, five injustices.** III.10, run as a chain in Aristotle's words: let the many be sovereign — they divide the rich's property by majority vote; "is not this unjust?" — "it is the uttermost injustice"; let the rich — they do the same in reverse; let the good — everyone else is dishonoured; let the one best man — still more so; let the law — "but what if the law is oligarchic or democratic?" Nothing survives taken alone. The heading is the question; the section's bearing is that the answer must be a combination of the claims, each where it is strongest.
6. **Answer, part one: the multitude together is wiser than any one of them — but at what?** III.11: "a feast to which many contribute is better than a dinner provided out of a single purse"; the many "become in a manner one man, who has many feet, and hands, and senses"; the user judges the house better than the builder, the guest the feast better than the cook. So the many should have the assembly and the courts — electing and auditing — where collective judgment is at stake, and not the highest offices, where expertise is. Concept `wisdom-of-the-multitude`. Mark the condition Aristotle attaches and the lesson must keep: "not every multitude" — one too debased is a different case. Backward seam, one sentence with `<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-justice`: Pericles had claimed the same division of labour for Athens — the citizens "are able to judge at all events if we cannot originate" — and the reader has also seen the case against it, an assembly that voted a massacre in anger and reversed it the next day by a show of hands "almost equal". The feast argument is Aristotle's answer to which of those two pictures holds, and his condition ("not every multitude") concedes that the second can. Because: judgment of a result is different from skill at producing it, and the many are the users of the city.
7. **Answer, part two: the pre-eminent man, and why the law should rule where it can.** III.13: a man so superior in virtue that he is "a god among men" cannot be made an equal; a city must either make him king or get rid of him — Periander's silent advice to Thrasybulus, cutting down the tallest ears of corn; the Argo that would not carry Heracles; Antisthenes' lions asking the hares "where are your claws?"; ostracism as "political justice" in the imperfect city. III.15–16, the STRONGEST case against the rule of law stated first and at full strength: the best man can see the case the law cannot — the Egyptian doctor may change the treatment on the fourth day, the law is a general rule and cases are particular, a written rule cannot deliberate. Then the reply: "he who bids the law rule bids God and Reason alone rule, but he who bids man rule adds an element of the beast … The law is reason unaffected by desire"; the king cannot see everything, so he needs "many eyes, ears, hands" — friends, who are then rulers too; the many are "less easily corrupted", as more water is harder to spoil. Concept `rule-of-law`. The lesson's reversal seam: from Book I's ladder (`<Ref>` `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`) one would expect the top rung to be occupied by the best man; Aristotle puts law there — the rung with no man on it. Because: the best man's advantage over the law is exactly the faculty — desire — that corrupts him.
8. **Answer, part three: polity and the middle — the best regime most cities can have, and the OBJECTION it carries.** IV.1 reframes the question: political science must know the best absolutely, the best under conditions, how to save any regime, and the best for most. IV.8–9: polity is "a fusion of oligarchy and democracy" — take election (oligarchic) and no property bar (democratic); the sign of a good mixture is that it "may be called either", as Sparta is called both (the Megillus wall of `decline-of-regimes`' FORWARD exercise, now named: not being able to name the ruling part is the merit). IV.11: who holds it up — the middle class, "equals and similars", who neither covet rule nor shrink from it; the very rich cannot obey, the very poor cannot command; "where the middle class is large there are least likely to be factions"; Solon, Lycurgus, Charondas were middling men; Phocylides' prayer. IV.12: the part that wants the regime must be stronger than the part that does not — quality (wealth, birth) against quantity (numbers), with the middle as the weight that turns the scale. Concept `polity`. One backward clause with `<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-justice`: the historian of the war had already called the best government Athens had in his time the one in which "the fusion of the high and the low was effected with judgment" — a mixture of the few and the many, praised a generation before Aristotle named it. Then the objection at full strength, from both ends of the course: Plato — a regime named for a part is "a party, not a polity", and a mixture is only a regime if it has a sovereign that is none of the parts (Laws IV); Bodin, two thousand years on — a mixed state is no state, because the power that makes law and is bound by none cannot be shared (`<Ref>` `bodin-sovereignty`, one clause). Aristotle's reply, as the text gives it: the three elements (deliberative, offices, courts) can be assigned to different parts and the constitution is that assignment; and his own test is empirical — "all the parts must wish it to exist". The lesson concedes that Aristotle never says who decides when the parts disagree, and hands that to the sovereignty module. `<Conclusion>`: the reader can now define a regime and a citizen, draw the sixfold table, run the tournament, and state the three-part answer; open — what makes even a polity fall from inside, and what the city is for (`aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`).

Headings that fall out: "A city is its constitution; a citizen is one who shares in judging" · "The good citizen is relative to the regime; the good man is not" · "Six regimes: number and end, and the real line is rich and poor" · "Each party's justice is a part; the state is not a contract" · "Five claimants, five injustices" · "The feast: what the many judge better than the expert" · "A god among men, or the law: why the top rung has no man on it" · "Polity and the middle: the best regime most cities can have".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), in chain order:** politeia (constitution/regime — Aristotle's word for both "the arrangement of offices" and the regime's whole way of life; anchor once, then use "constitution" or "regime" consistently); "indefinite office" (sitting in the assembly or a jury — not a magistracy); Larissa and Gorgias (a Thessalian city; a famous orator — one clause); Babylon; Jason of Pherae (a Thessalian tyrant, c. 370 — one clause); kingship / aristocracy / polity / tyranny / oligarchy / democracy as Aristotle uses them — above all POLITY (politeia used as the name of one regime, the mixed one; flag the double use of the word once) and DEMOCRACY (the rule of the free poor for their own interest — a perversion in Aristotle's scheme, unlike the modern word; one sentence of warning at the table); Lycophron (a sophist, one clause); Corinth and Megara (neighbouring cities on the Isthmus); Periander and Thrasybulus (tyrants of Corinth and Miletus, c. 600); the Argo and Heracles; Antisthenes (a follower of Socrates — his fable of the lions); ostracism (the Athenian ten-year banishment by vote on potsherds — anchor here even if `decline-of-regimes` showed an ostrakon); aesymnetes (elective tyrant, one clause if III.14 is used at all); Phocylides (an Ionian poet, c. 550); Charondas (lawgiver of Catana in Sicily — recall from `greek-miracle` if Vernant's Charondas was used); Lycurgus (Sparta's legendary lawgiver — anchor here in one clause as legendary; `sparta-as-test-case` has the full treatment).
- **"Democracy" is a perversion in this table.** The newcomer's largest confusion. State at the table that Aristotle's democracy is rule by the poor for the poor, that his polity is closer to what a modern reader means by a constitutional republic, and that his feast argument is a defence of popular judgment, not of democracy in his sense. Say it once; no modern analogies.
- **The feast is not "the wisdom of crowds" without limit.** Aristotle's condition — not every multitude, and not the highest offices — is part of the concept. The lesson keeps it.
- **Do not teach the varieties of democracy and oligarchy (IV.4–6) or the appointment-mode taxonomy (IV.15)** beyond one figure each; IV.15 is textually corrupt (Ross) and the lesson says nothing of it.
- **Register.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Do not re-teach Thucydides.** The Funeral Oration's "judge if we cannot originate", the Mytilene vote and VIII.97 are single clauses with `<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-justice`; no quotation longer than a phrase.
- **Do not re-teach Sparta.** Sparta appears as the example of a mixture "called either name" and as Lycurgus's middling condition; everything else is `sparta-as-test-case`.
- **Do not re-teach the Laws (extra).** The Laws IV objection is given in two sentences with the core reader in mind; the `platos-laws-second-best-city` Ref is optional reading.
- **Bodin is one clause.** The objection is named and handed on; nothing about sovereignty is taught here.
- **Numbers:** the 1300 / 1000 / 300 case is Aristotle's own worked example and stays (bound in its sentence); nothing else. No "Athens had 30,000 citizens".
- **Summary vs primary:** spot-checked III.11 (text l. 767–777, 858), III.13 (l. 1059), III.16 (l. 1414–1416), IV.11 (l. 2389–2458) all match. Book IV chs. 14–16 are in the s3 shard, not s2.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up, the one mechanical item).** Draw the sixfold table from memory and place on it Plato's five regimes from `decline-of-regimes` (E3), saying which cell Plato's "aristocracy" (the city in speech) occupies and why Plato's "timocracy" has no cell.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill) on III.11 (the feast), with a paired source from `thucydides-power-and-justice` (E3).** Author (Aristotle, a resident alien who could not vote in the Athens whose assembly he describes), genre, audience; what the passage records (that assemblies and courts of ordinary men existed and worked), what it argues (collective judgment beats the expert at judging, not at making), what it omits (which multitude — Aristotle's own condition). Then set beside it Pericles' "we … are able to judge at all events if we cannot originate" and Cleon's "more like the audience of a rhetorician than the council of a city": which of the two does the feast argument support, and which does Aristotle's "not every multitude" concede? Closing: what can the passage be USED to establish about Aristotle's view of Athenian democracy, and what can it not (that he favoured it — he classes it as a perversion two chapters earlier)?
3. **Claim to refute.** "Aristotle argues that the law should always rule rather than the best man." Name the step that fails (III.13 and III.17: where a man is "a god among men" there is no law for him and kingship is right; the rule of law is the answer where no such man exists — which Aristotle thinks is nearly always) and state the surviving claim.
4. **Boundary probe: the middle class at zero.** A city of only rich and poor, with the three elements (assembly, offices, courts) assigned by Aristotle's third mode of mixing. Using IV.11–12, derive what happens (the two extremes fight for the regime "as a prize", the winners found a democracy or an oligarchy, "never a polity"); then set the middle to the whole (a city of equals and similars with no rich or poor at all) and say what Aristotle's own text implies about that case (it is the city his definition of polity describes, and Plato's second-best city with a cap of four — `<Ref>` `platos-laws-second-best-city` optional).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped: ostracism from the sixfold table.** Start from "a constitution is the arrangement of offices among equals" and "a man may exist whom no law fits". Derive why a regime of equals must either exile him or cease to be that regime, and why Aristotle calls the exile "political justice" only in imperfect regimes. Solution shows where the derivation uses III.13's premise and where it uses III.17's.
6. **Counter-argument stem (synthesis).** "Someone claims that polity is just Plato's second-best city with a different name." Name two facts the claim fails to account for (polity rests on a class, the middle, not on a legislator's lots and ceiling; it is defined by being nameable as either of two regimes, not by a sovereign law above all parts) and state what would have to be true for it to survive (that the middle class and the four-class ladder pick out the same men). For the core reader who skipped the Laws: the solution supplies the two Laws facts in one sentence each.
7. **Paired-source corroboration.** Set Thrasymachus's regime table (`thrasymachus-and-the-ring`) beside Aristotle's III.7. Where do they agree (the three perversions ARE Thrasymachus's description), where do they disagree (that there are three other regimes), and what evidence would settle whether the "true forms" exist anywhere (Aristotle's own answer: Sparta is called both names — test it in `sparta-as-test-case`). Written judgment.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`.** Aristotle says the stability test of any regime is that "all the parts must wish it to exist". Take a polity in which the poor accept election because they expect to win and the rich accept no property bar because they expect to be elected; state what happens the first time an election goes against expectation, and which of the two partial justices (III.9) the loser will invoke. The solution says in one sentence that the reader has described the "state of mind" with which Book V's account of faction begins — the slighted party's sense of unequal treatment — `<Ref>` the next lesson.

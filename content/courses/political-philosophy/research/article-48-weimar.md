# Research brief: article-48-weimar (Article 48 and the end of the Weimar Reichstag, 1930–1933)

status: planned
module: crisis-of-liberal-europe
type: core
goal: Apply Schmitt's theory of the exception and his distinction between commissarial and sovereign dictatorship to the record of presidential decrees and Reichstag laws, and judge whether the theory described the collapse or helped produce it

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The whole module, now to be cashed out: Mann's "democracy =
  politicisation" and the unpolitical Bürger (`mann-reflections`); Ortega's
  mass-man, his claim that Parliaments lose prestige when they lose tasks, and
  that after 1848 coups replace revolutions (`ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`);
  Schmitt's friend–enemy criterion, the internal enemy and protego ergo obligo
  (`schmitt-concept-of-the-political`); and above all "sovereign is he who
  decides on the exception", norm vs decision, Article 48 read as controlled
  precondition and unlimited content, and commissarial vs sovereign
  dictatorship (`schmitt-political-theology`). From earlier modules: Bodin's
  "no subject may judge the sovereign" (`bodin-sovereignty`); the English
  Revolution read against Hobbes as the course's first application of a
  sovereignty theory to a record (`english-revolution-as-test`); Maistre's
  count of revolutionary laws as a measurable claim (`assignats`); Tocqueville's
  people who "shake off dependence just long enough to select their master"
  (`tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`).
- **Contributes:** The module's application (L16). The artefact is a table —
  Reichstag laws against Article 48 decrees per year 1930–1932, with the
  Reichstag's sitting days — and one event, the Preussenschlag of 20 July
  1932 with Schmitt's brief before the Staatsgerichtshof. The lesson sets
  both against the theory and names where the record departs: a parliament
  that abdicated by procedure, a president who decided nothing, a movement
  that took power legally. The reader leaves able to read a constitutional
  clause with Schmitt's three questions, to say which dictatorship Weimar's
  was, and to argue both answers to the lesson's question — did the theory
  describe the collapse or help produce it?
- **Hands off:** The presidential cabinets and the leader who followed are the
  stage that the next module's first thinker calls Caesarism, the end of
  democracy in money and the Press (`spengler-decline-of-the-west`). The
  question "could a constitution defend itself against a party using its own
  legality?" returns in `udhr-read-against-the-right` (a declaration that binds
  no sovereign) and in `the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways` (states deciding an
  exception at a border). Schmitt himself returns in `voegelin-gnosticism` as
  the rival reader of secularisation.
- **Spine step:** "Liberal Europe breaks: Mann sets Kultur against Zivilisation,
  Ortega diagnoses the mass-man, Schmitt names the political as friend and
  enemy and the sovereign as he who decides the exception — and Weimar is the
  case"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):**
  - *Artefact:* (1) the yearly counts, 1930–1932, of laws passed by the
    Reichstag, of emergency decrees issued by the Reich president under
    Article 48 §2, and of days the Reichstag sat; (2) the text of Article 48
    as promulgated (11 August 1919); (3) the Preussenschlag — the decree of 20
    July 1932 appointing Chancellor Papen Reich Commissioner for Prussia and
    deposing the Prussian ministry, and the Staatsgerichtshof's judgment of 25
    October 1932, with Schmitt as counsel for the Reich.
  - *Module results it uses:* `state-of-exception` and `decisionism` (who
    decides the exception exists, the measures, its end; norm presupposes a
    normal situation); the commissarial/sovereign distinction (taught in
    `schmitt-political-theology`, link 7); `friend-enemy-distinction` and the
    internal enemy, with the "equal chance" doctrine (Schwab intro III);
    `sovereignty` (Bodin: whoever can refuse is sovereign); `mass-man`
    (Ortega's Parliaments that lose tasks; coups replace revolutions);
    `kultur-vs-zivilisation` (Mann's unpolitical German as the voter who
    chose the presidential regime — a claim to test, not assert).
  - *Departures the lesson must name:* (a) the exception was not DECLARED
    against a parliament but tolerated BY it — the SPD's toleration policy
    from October 1930 kept the Reichstag from using its §3 right to demand
    repeal, so the "decision" was a procedural abstention; (b) the president
    who "decided" signed what chancellors and a circle of advisers placed
    before him — the sovereign the theory needs was not one person deciding;
    (c) the party Schmitt's equal-chance doctrine was written to exclude came
    to power by the constitution's own legality (appointment 30 January 1933;
    Article 48 decree of 28 February; Enabling Act of 23 March by a two-thirds
    vote), and the author of the theory joined it in May; (d) the exception
    lasted three years and became the norm — the theory has no clock; (e) the
    court that ruled on the Preussenschlag split the decision the theory says
    cannot be split (the commissioner lawful, the deposed ministry still
    Prussia's representative).

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (verbatim from course.yaml): between 1930 and 1932 the German
parliament passed fewer and fewer laws while the president signed more and
more emergency decrees under one article of the constitution; by 1932 the
Reichstag met for a handful of days; the jurist who had defined sovereignty by
the exception was advising the government. The lesson sets the counts against
the theory.

1. **What did the article say, and what did its drafters leave out?** Before
   the counts, the clause. The reader has §2's two sentences from the last
   lesson; now the whole of Article 48 is read with the sourcing drill:
   metadata (Weimar National Assembly, August 1919, a constitution written
   during a civil war in the capital's streets and a revolution in Bavaria;
   audience: a republic that had just put down armed risings with the army);
   prediction (what would such drafters give the president?); then the five
   paragraphs. The obvious reading — the legalistic one — is that the second
   sentence of §2 LIMITS the first: the president may suspend seven named
   rights and nothing more (enumeratio ergo limitatio). Schmitt's reading
   (Schwab l.1127–1167): the two sentences were drafted by different
   committees; the first originally began with "armed force" and was
   reordered out of squeamishness; the second says only that IF rights are
   suspended, these are the ones — it says nothing about what else may be
   done. The lesson gives both readings at full strength and notes what both
   agree on: §3 gives the Reichstag the power to demand repeal, and §5
   promised a law regulating the details that was never passed in fourteen
   years. The "because" of the whole lesson sits here: the clause's content
   was unlimited (Ref `schmitt-political-theology`, link 2), its precondition
   controlled — by a body that would have to want to control it. Heading:
   "Article 48 as written: a controlled gate and an open field".

2. **What do the counts show?** The artefact, in a figure and a short table:
   laws against decrees, year by year, with sitting days (the three keeper
   numbers are the 1932 row: five laws, sixty-six decrees, thirteen sitting
   days — bound in the sentence as "a parliament that met for less than a
   fortnight in the year"). The obvious reading: the president seized power.
   It fails on the dates and the mechanism. Effective parliamentary government
   ended in March 1930 when the Müller cabinet — the last with a Reichstag
   majority — fell over a fraction of a percentage point on unemployment
   insurance contributions (Muller l.3687; Schmitt's own "tip of a
   socioeconomic iceberg"); Brüning was appointed WITHOUT a majority, used
   §2 to enact his finance programme in July 1930 when the Reichstag rejected
   it, the Reichstag DEMANDED REPEAL under §3 — and Brüning dissolved it. The
   election of 14 September 1930 returned a Reichstag in which the NSDAP had
   gone from a dozen seats to over a hundred (bind: from under three percent
   to over eighteen percent of the vote). From then the SPD, the largest
   party, "tolerated" Brüning: it voted against every motion to demand repeal
   of a decree, because the alternative was a dissolution and a worse
   Reichstag. The curve in the figure is that policy. Ref
   `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses` (XIV.5): a Parliament that loses tasks loses
   prestige — here it GAVE the tasks away. Heading: "Fewer laws, more decrees:
   the counts and what produced them".

3. **Who decided the exception?** Schwab's three questions (Ref
   `schmitt-political-theology`): who decided it existed, who the measures,
   who its end. The theory expects one answer: the president. The record
   gives three bodies, none deciding. (i) The president: Hindenburg, eighty-
   two in 1930, signed decrees drafted in the chancellery; the decision
   "that" there was an exception was made by whoever chose to govern by
   decree — Brüning, then Papen, then Schleicher — and by the circle around
   the president (Schleicher at the Defence Ministry, Meissner the state
   secretary, Oskar von Hindenburg) who chose the chancellors. (ii) The
   measures: drafted by ministries, as laws would have been; the content of
   the decrees was budgets, tariffs, wage cuts, press restrictions — ordinary
   legislation by other means, not a response to a situation "no norm applies
   to". (iii) The end: never decided; the exception had no clock, and by 1932
   it was the normal situation — the thing the theory says a norm needs and
   says the sovereign restores. The seam here must SAY the reversal: from
   "sovereign is he who decides on the exception" one expects to find a
   decider; the record shows an exception without a decision, produced by a
   parliament's abstention and a presidency's signature. Schmitt's own 1931
   diagnosis (Muller l.3699–3755) is put beside this at full strength: the
   state and society had interpenetrated ("total state"); parties had become
   rigid subcultures; parliament was "a mere reflection of the pluralistic
   partitioning of the state itself" and could no longer form a will; so the
   constitution's guardian (Hüter der Verfassung) must be the one organ
   elected by the whole people, the president. On his account the record is
   not a departure from the theory but its confirmation: parliament could not
   be sovereign, and something had to be. Heading: "Who decided? Three bodies
   and no decision".

4. **Commissarial or sovereign?** The distinction (Ref
   `schmitt-political-theology`, link 7). Brüning's decrees are the
   commissarial case on their face: finance measures to keep the state solvent
   so the constitution could resume — and Brüning said so, and resigned in May
   1932 when Hindenburg withdrew his signature. Papen's six months are the
   test: a chancellor with the support of about a tenth of the Reichstag (bind
   it: the DNVP's seats against the whole), governing wholly by decree,
   planning a "New State" with a reformed franchise and an upper house — the
   constitution to be replaced, not restored. Schmitt's "equal chance"
   (Legalität und Legitimität, July 1932; Schwab l.1209–1249): a constitution
   does not will its own destruction, so the chance to win power legally
   belongs only to parties that accept it; the Nazis and Communists should be
   denied it; the president's "full assertion of his constitutional
   prerogatives and sovereign powers" is the means. The lesson shows what this
   is: an argument that the commissarial dictator must be allowed to act as a
   sovereign one toward the constitution's enemies in order to remain
   commissarial toward the constitution — and that the theory has no criterion,
   while the exception lasts, for telling which he is (the derivation the
   last lesson's exercise 5 ran). Lord Balfour's England, "so fundamentally at
   one that they can safely afford to bicker" (Schwab l.1232–1237), is the
   condition Schmitt says Germany lacked — Ref `british-franchise` for the
   contrast the reader has already studied. Heading: "Which dictatorship was
   it? The equal chance and the missing criterion".

5. **The Preussenschlag: the theory in court.** The one event the lesson
   works in full, with the sourcing drill on the decree and the judgment. 20
   July 1932: by decree under Article 48, Papen is made Reich Commissioner for
   Prussia — two-thirds of Germany's territory and population, with the
   largest police force in the Reich, governed since 1920 by an SPD-led
   coalition — and the Braun–Severing ministry is deposed; a state of military
   emergency over Berlin. The stated ground: the Prussian government's failure
   to keep order after the Altona street battle of 17 July (about eighteen
   dead), that is, Article 48 §1 — a Land failing its duties. The ministry
   does not resist (Severing yields "only to force", and the force is one
   police officer); it goes to the Staatsgerichtshof in Leipzig. There, in
   October, the theory argues its own case: Schmitt for the Reich — the
   president as guardian of the constitution decides whether a Land has
   failed, and a government that tolerates an armed party on its left while
   fighting one on its right has failed; Prussia's counsel (Brecht, Anschütz,
   Heller) — §1 was not met, the Reich may execute against a Land but not
   replace its organs, and the president cannot be the judge of his own
   decision. Judgment, 25 October: the commissioner's appointment was lawful
   under §2, the deposition of the ministry was not; Prussia keeps two
   governments, one with the powers and one with the title. Read against the
   theory: the court did exactly what Schmitt says cannot be done — it split
   the decision and derived part of it from a norm; and the Reich obeyed the
   half it lost, which on Schmitt's own account means the court, not the
   president, had the last word that day. Read with Ortega (ch. XIII): a coup
   d'état by decree against an elected government, with no revolution. Read
   with Bodin (Ref `bodin-sovereignty`): a subject organ — a Land — judging
   the sovereign's act, which Bodin says no subject may do. Heading: "20 July
   1932: the exception argued before a court".

6. **A movement that took power legally.** The sequence of 30 January to 23
   March 1933, told as a chain of legalities: appointment by the president
   under Article 53 (the chancellor the "equal chance" was written to
   exclude); the Reichstag fire; the decree of 28 February under Article 48
   §2, suspending the seven enumerated rights — the legalistic reading
   observed to the letter, "until further notice"; an election in which the
   NSDAP still lacked a majority; the Enabling Act of 23 March passed by the
   two-thirds Article 76 required, with the Centre's votes and the Communists'
   seats empty — parliament using its one surviving power, amendment, to end
   itself. Then Schmitt: joined the party in May 1933; drafted for the
   Gleichschaltung of the Länder — the Preussenschlag made general; wrote on 1
   August 1934, after the killing of the SA leadership and of his own patron
   Schleicher, that "the Führer protects the law". Reported as fact bearing on
   the theory's reception (Strong l.127–141; Schwab l.919–939; Fawcett
   l.9287–9310), dated, once. The lesson's question follows directly: the
   equal-chance doctrine said a constitution may deny legality to its enemies;
   the Enabling Act was the enemies using legality against the constitution;
   and the man who wrote the doctrine concluded that the new authority, being
   established, was good (Ref `maistre-and-bonald`, Maistre's "any government
   is good once established", via `schmitt-political-theology` link 6).
   Heading: "January to March 1933: legality against the constitution".

7. **Did the theory describe the collapse or help produce it?** Both readings
   at full strength, the reader left to weigh (course tone; co-objection).
   *Described:* the counts are what the 1931 essay said they would be — a
   parliament of blocs that can refuse but cannot will; the equal-chance
   warning came true in the one way it foresaw (a party closing the door
   behind itself); the exception DID reveal where authority lay — not in the
   Reichstag, which could have ended the decrees any month and did not. On
   this reading the theory is a diagnosis that Weimar's defenders refused to
   read (and the "power of absence" — a right with no liberal-democratic
   conservatism to hold the centre — is the structural reason the diagnosis
   had no taker). *Produced:* a theory that locates the state's reality in the
   exception and calls parliament a "discussing class" gave every chancellor
   from Brüning on a doctrine for not seeking a majority; the latitudinarian
   reading of §2, pressed by Schmitt since 1924, made the decree a substitute
   for the law rather than a measure against chaos; the Preussenschlag, argued
   by Schmitt, removed the one government that had the police to resist the
   party he wanted banned; and a theory in which "a wrong decision is still
   valid" and only participants can name the enemy had nothing to say when
   the participant who named the enemy was the enemy. The honest middle, as
   the course's own: the theory described a parliament that could not act and
   prescribed a president who, in the event, did not decide — and so
   delivered the exception to whoever would. Ref `english-revolution-as-test`:
   the reader has seen a sovereignty theory confirmed by a civil war it did
   not cause; here the author was in the room. Ref `mann-reflections`: Mann's
   German who prefers Obrigkeit to politics is one explanation offered for
   the voter who chose the presidential regime — state it as a claim the
   reader may test against the election figures (the presidential election
   of 1932 re-elected Hindenburg with the SPD's votes, against Hitler), not as
   established. Heading: "Diagnosis or prescription? Two readings of the
   record".

Headings as claims: "Article 48 as written: a controlled gate and an open
field"; "Fewer laws, more decrees: the counts and what produced them"; "Who
decided? Three bodies and no decision"; "Which dictatorship was it?"; "20 July
1932: the exception argued before a court"; "January to March 1933: legality
against the constitution"; "Diagnosis or prescription?".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **Article 48 dissected** — the five paragraphs set out, each with its
   actor (president / Reichstag / Land) and its verb (may undertake; must
   inform; must repeal on demand; may provisionally; shall regulate — never
   did). Then §2's two sentences in both readings, with the drafting history
   (Schwab l.1127–1167) as the evidence. The sourcing drill run on it in full:
   metadata, prediction, records / argues / omits (it omits any time limit and
   any definition of "considerably disturbed"), use.
2. **The table and the curve** — laws, decrees, sitting days, 1930–1932, with
   the two 1930 Reichstags (before and after 14 September) and the three
   chancellors as spans. The worked reading: the 1930 row (98 laws, 5
   decrees) is a parliament still legislating; the 1931 row (34 against 44) is
   toleration; the 1932 row (5 against 66, 13 days) is a parliament that
   exists to not meet. Then the counterfactual-deletion test on each number:
   which claim fails without it?
3. **The fall of the Müller cabinet, March 1930** (Muller l.3687 and Schmitt's
   "tip of a socioeconomic iceberg"): a coalition of five parties breaks over
   whether unemployment insurance contributions rise by a quarter of a
   percentage point or benefits fall — told in four sentences as the moment
   parliamentary government ended, a year before Schmitt's essay said it
   could not be sovereign.
4. **Schmitt's 1931 diagnosis parsed** (Muller l.3699–3755): the dualistic
   state → the legislative state → the total state; parties as "rigid, ongoing,
   permanent institutions"; parliament as "a mere reflection of the pluralistic
   partitioning of the state" — reconstructed as a four-step argument ending in
   the president as guardian, with each step tested against the counts.
5. **Schwab's three questions run on the record** — who decided the exception
   existed (Brüning's choice to govern by decree; the Reichstag's abstention),
   the measures (ministries drafting ordinary legislation), its end (nobody;
   or Hitler's decree of 28 February, which was never repealed). A table with
   the theory's expected answer and the record's actual one in each row.
6. **The Preussenschlag in three documents**: the decree of 20 July (its
   recital of §1 and §2); Severing's reported words and the single officer
   (the "force" he yielded to); the judgment's two holdings. Then the two
   briefs reconstructed as arguments (Reich: the guardian judges the Land's
   failure; Prussia: no one judges his own cause — Ref
   `locke-property-and-consent`, "judge in his own cause", and Ref
   `bodin-sovereignty`). The reader judges which theory of sovereignty the
   court applied — and whether it applied one at all.
7. **The 1933 chain of legalities** — Article 53 (appointment); Article 25
   (dissolution); Article 48 §2 (the decree of 28 February, with the seven
   articles listed and their content: person, home, post, speech, assembly,
   association, property); Article 76 (two-thirds; the Enabling Act's vote,
   444 to 94, with the arithmetic of who was absent). Each link legal; the
   chain the end of legality. The Centre's vote and Kaas's reasons as the
   one place where a "decision" in Schmitt's sense was taken — by a party.
8. **"Der Führer schützt das Recht"** (1 August 1934; Strong l.740–750):
   reported in one paragraph — the killings of 30 June–2 July, Hindenburg's
   telegram, Schmitt's article — as the terminus of "any government is good
   once it is established". Dated, factual, no epithet.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know, and where to anchor them.** Reichstag
  (link 1: the elected parliament of the Reich, by proportional
  representation). Reich president / Reichspräsident (link 1: directly
  elected for seven years; Ebert 1919–25, Hindenburg 1925–34 — the field
  marshal of the war, aged 77 at election). Chancellor (link 2: head of
  government, appointed by the president, dependent on the Reichstag's
  confidence under Article 54). Land / Länder (link 1: the federal states —
  Prussia, Bavaria and fifteen others). Notverordnung (link 2: emergency
  decree). Staatsgerichtshof (link 5: the constitutional court for disputes
  between Reich and Länder, sitting at the Reichsgericht in Leipzig).
  Reichsrat (link 5: the chamber of Land governments). Preussenschlag (link
  5: "the blow against Prussia", the historians' name). Tolerierung /
  toleration policy (link 2). Präsidialkabinett / presidential cabinet (link
  2). Enabling Act (link 6). Gleichschaltung (link 6: "bringing into line"
  — the 1933 laws subordinating the Länder). SA, KPD, SPD, NSDAP, DNVP, Centre
  (links 2, 5, 6: spell each out once with one clause — the Nazi party's
  uniformed paramilitary; the Communists; the Social Democrats; the Nazis; the
  nationalist right; the Catholic party). Altona (link 5: a Prussian city
  beside Hamburg). Legalität und Legitimität (link 4: Schmitt's 1932 book;
  legality = conformity to the rules; legitimacy = title to rule). Hüter der
  Verfassung (link 3: "guardian of the constitution", Schmitt's 1931 title).
  Enumeratio ergo limitatio (link 1, Latin: "enumeration, therefore
  limitation" — the legal maxim that a list is exhaustive). People: Brüning
  (link 2: Centre party, chancellor March 1930–May 1932), Papen (link 4: a
  Centre dissident with the DNVP's backing, June–November 1932), Schleicher
  (link 3: the political general, December 1932–January 1933), Braun and
  Severing (link 5: Prussia's SPD prime minister and interior minister),
  Hermann Heller (link 5: the socialist jurist who argued against Schmitt;
  died in exile 1933), Anschütz (link 1: the leading commentator on the
  constitution). Three new terms per section (L11c) — every link here is
  over budget; the lesson needs subheadings and a glossary-style first
  mention for the party abbreviations, perhaps in the Reichstag-composition
  figure's legend.
- **Numbers (playbook-history rules 1–4).** This is the one lesson in the
  module built on a series, and it lives in the figure and the table; prose
  states what the series means. Keeper numbers: the 1932 row (5 / 66 / 13);
  the NSDAP's jump in September 1930 (from under 3 to over 18 percent of the
  vote); the Enabling Act's two-thirds. Everything else is order-of-magnitude
  words. Every number bound in the sentence (unit, base, comparison). Where
  sources differ on what counts as a decree, the lesson says so in one clause
  and gives the range. The computation that is legitimate here (rule 4) is
  the reader's own count of one year of the Reichsgesetzblatt as a check on
  the published series — exercise 7.
- **Dates:** this is the lesson where contingency IS the point, so the exact
  day appears for 14 September 1930, 20 July 1932, 25 October 1932, 30 January,
  28 February, 5 March and 23 March 1933, and 1 August 1934. Everything else
  to the month or year.
- **Do not teach the Depression.** One clause (the slump from autumn 1929;
  unemployment at about a third of the workforce by early 1932 — bound) is
  the context; the lesson is about the constitution, not the economy, and
  the Borchardt debate on Brüning's room for manoeuvre (Muller note 5) goes in
  References at most.
- **Do not moralise in either direction.** The two readings of link 7 are
  both the course's own and both complete. Schmitt's 1933–34 conduct is
  reported, dated and once; "Nazi jurist" never appears as an epithet; nor
  does any sentence that excuses. Equally, do not present the "toleration
  policy" as cowardice — give the SPD's reason (a worse Reichstag after any
  dissolution) as it gave it.
- **A single-cause reading is the real misconception (L13):** "Article 48
  destroyed Weimar." Refute it with the record: Ebert used Article 48 well
  over a hundred times in 1919–25 (verify the count in Kolb or Evans; "about
  135" is the usual figure) and the republic stabilised; the article was an
  instrument, and the lesson's question is who held it and why no one took it
  back. The refutation is also the setup for both readings in link 7.
- **The "president who decided nothing" is an interpretive claim**, not a
  fact: Hindenburg DID refuse Brüning his signature in May 1932, did refuse
  Hitler the chancellorship in August and November 1932, and did appoint him
  in January 1933. State the departure as the course's own reading ("the
  decisions that mattered were who to appoint, and were taken by a circle"),
  give the counter-evidence, and let the reader weigh.
- **Scholars live in References only:** Bracher, Mommsen, Kolb, Evans,
  Winkler, Bendersky, Kennedy, Dyzenhaus, Harold James, Borchardt, Fawcett,
  Muller, Schwab, Strong, Agamben. Anschütz, Thoma, Heller, Brecht, Nawiasky,
  Bilfinger, Jacobi are participants in the record (counsel, commentators)
  and may be named as such with an anchor. Lord Balfour and Bagehot: Schmitt's
  witnesses (via Schwab) — name as his.
- **Where the dossier is thin — say so in References:** the counts, the full
  Article 48, the Preussenschlag decree and judgment, Schmitt's brief,
  Legalität und Legitimität, Der Hüter der Verfassung beyond Muller's
  excerpt, the 1933 decrees and the Enabling Act text are ALL outside the
  dossier and must be obtained as listed. The dossier's own anchors are Schwab
  intro III, Strong l.223–235 and l.698–765, Muller ch. 7, Fawcett ch. 5 iii
  and 6 iv, and Schmitt's PT ch. 1 l.1655–1680. The 1931 excerpt in Muller is
  the ONLY Schmitt text from the crisis years the dossier holds; everything
  else of his from 1930–34 is reported.
- **Where sources differ:** the date of Schmitt's party entry (Muller: "late
  April"; Strong, Fawcett: May 1933 — the application and the admission; say
  "spring 1933" or "1 May 1933" with a note); the Altona dead (17–19); the
  decree counts (±2 by what is counted). Give the range or the rounded word.
- **Do not re-teach** the exception, the two dictatorships, the friend–enemy
  criterion, Ortega's Parliaments, Bodin: Ref and a clause each. This lesson
  introduces no concept id; it reinforces six.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** State the three rows of the 1930–1932
   table from memory (laws, decrees, sitting days) and name the policy that
   produced the 1931 row. Then recall from `schmitt-political-theology` the
   difference between commissarial and sovereign dictatorship in one sentence
   (E3).
2. **Document reasoning / sourcing drill:** The decree of 28 February 1933
   (its recital and its list of suspended articles), with metadata (the
   president, on the chancellor's draft, the morning after the Reichstag
   fire, six days before an election). Predict what such a decree would
   invoke; read; records / argues / omits (it omits any time limit — "until
   further notice"); what can it be USED to establish (that the legalistic
   reading of §2 was observed to the letter) and what can it not (who set the
   fire)?
3. **Claim to refute:** "Article 48 destroyed the Weimar Republic: a
   constitution with such a clause was doomed." Name the failing step (Ebert
   used the clause over a hundred times in 1919–25 and the republic
   stabilised; the clause had a repeal mechanism in §3 that the Reichstag
   never used after 1930; the collapse ran through choices — Brüning's
   appointment without a majority, the SPD's toleration, the Enabling Act's
   two-thirds — that the clause did not make). Then state what remains true
   (§5's law was never passed; the content was unlimited).
4. **Boundary probe:** Set the Reichstag's §3 power to its maximum — a
   parliament that demands repeal of every decree the day it is issued. Who
   is sovereign in Schmitt's sense then, and what does the president's
   "decision on the exception" amount to? Then set it to zero — a parliament
   that never demands repeal and never meets. Show that in both limits the
   theory's answer is clear and that the record sat at neither: the Reichstag
   COULD and did not. What does the theory say about an organ that holds the
   power to decide and declines? (It says nothing; that is the finding.)
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** From the equal-chance doctrine — a
   constitution does not will its own destruction, so legality belongs only to
   parties that accept it — derive who, in the Weimar constitution, would have
   had to decide which parties those were, and under which article. Then
   derive what that decider becomes, by Schmitt's own definitions, the moment
   he excludes a party (the one who decides the internal enemy — the political
   entity itself). The solution shows the doctrine makes the president a
   sovereign dictator toward the parties in order to remain a commissarial
   one toward the constitution, and that the theory cannot say where one
   ends and the other begins.
6. **Causal ranking (synthesis):** Rank as necessary / enabling / precipitating
   the following in the end of the Reichstag: the unlimited content of Article
   48 §2; the proportional-representation Reichstag of September 1930; the
   SPD's toleration policy; Brüning's appointment without a majority; the
   Preussenschlag; the Depression. Defend the ranking from the record, and
   state which of the six the theory of the exception predicts and which it
   cannot see.
7. **Research-shaped task:** Using the digitised Reichsgesetzblatt 1932 Teil I
   (ALEX), count the ordinances headed as issued by the Reich president "auf
   Grund des Artikels 48" and compare with the published figure of 66. Report
   the count, the discrepancy, and the two decisions you had to make (does an
   amending decree count? does a decree under §1 count?). Then state what the
   discrepancy shows about every published series — and whether the lesson's
   claim survives a range of ±5.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `spengler-decline-of-the-west`:** The sequence
   parliament → presidential cabinets → a leader governing by decree and
   acclamation is, the next lesson's author will say, the normal late stage of
   a Civilization — democracy exhausting itself in money and the Press,
   replaced by personal power. Using only the record this lesson has laid out,
   name two features of 1930–33 that fit such a stage and two that do not (a
   party built from the mass, not a general; a legal transfer; a parliament
   that voted for it). The solution says the reader has stated the test the
   next module runs on the theory of Caesarism, and Refs
   `spengler-decline-of-the-west`.

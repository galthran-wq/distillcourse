# Research brief: assignats (The assignats: Burke's forecast against the record)

status: planned
module: contract-revolution-reaction
type: core
goal: Test Burke's and Maistre's predictions about the Revolution's paper money and its legislation against the recorded depreciation of the assignat and the count of laws, and say where the theory and the record part

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The whole module, as predictions. From `burke-reflections`:
  the epistemic thesis (no generation knows enough to design; prejudice as the
  general bank of ages), the forecast ledger (Terror, the general, paper money,
  departments that will not obey Paris), the "nation of gamesters" and "square
  republics". From `maistre-and-bonald`: Providence uses men; no nation gives
  itself a constitution; "there is no such thing as man"; written law is "paper";
  the count of laws and "three constitutions in five years" held for here. From
  `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`: the rational state as what is understood
  after the fact, actuality against mere existence, a constitution that "cannot
  be given a priori" (§274), codification as an achievement (§211), and the
  question that lesson's close left for this one — which of the Revolution's
  products is "paper" and which could be "rational actual". From
  `donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship` (the prerequisite): `dictatorship-of-the-sabre`
  — when legality cannot save society, the only choice is between a
  dictatorship from below and one from above — and the thermometer's "million
  eyes and ears" of administrative centralisation; the record of 1793–1804 (the
  Terror's committees, the Directory's coups against its own legislature, the
  general of 1799) is the case his dilemma was drawn from. From
  `hume-against-the-original-contract`: `government-founded-on-opinion` — and
  credit is opinion: a paper currency is worth what holders believe the issuing
  government's word is worth; "Antiquity always begets the opinion of right" and
  time turns a title begun in force into right (the buyers of Church lands). From
  `herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation`: the `plurality-of-cultures` — laws made
  "for all times and peoples" fit no people — set against the metric system
  decreed "for all peoples, all times" (one clause). From
  `locke-property-and-consent`: money as the convention
  that lifts the limits on property (Sect. 46–50), and property as what the
  legislative may not take without consent (Sect. 138–140) — the confiscation of
  Church lands is the case. From `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: "the
  word finance is a slavish word" (III.15).
- **Contributes:** The module's application (L16): two real artefacts — the
  assignat and its depreciation 1790–96, and the legislative output of the
  revolutionary assemblies — read against two checkable forecasts. Burke in 1790
  said a currency on confiscated Church land would end as Law's Mississippi had,
  in worthless paper and a state living by force; Maistre in 1797 counted 15,479
  laws and three constitutions in five years and said no society could absorb
  them, that "to destroy is not to make". The lesson checks both, and then names
  where the record departs from the critics: the institutions of the Revolution
  that lasted.
- **Hands off:** The module closes. What stays open is the question the whole
  module raised and the next will answer with a fact rather than a theory: if
  the Revolution's designs partly failed and partly endured, what decides which
  — and Tocqueville's answer that the levelling the Revolution accelerated is a
  "providential fact" no one designed (`tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`).
  The epistemic thesis returns in `what-conservatism-is` and
  `oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics` with this lesson's ledger as its evidence;
  Donoso's dilemma returns in `schmitt-political-theology` and is tested again on
  an emergency article in `article-48-weimar`.
- **Spine step:** "Government by consent (Locke) is doubted by Hume and
  radicalised by Rousseau's general will; when France tries to frame a government
  from principles, Burke, Maistre and Donoso Cortés answer that no generation
  knows enough, Herder and Fichte put the people before the state, and Hegel
  absorbs both sides into the rational state"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):**
  - *Artefact:* (1) the assignat — the note itself (a photograph of a real one),
    what it was (a bond on the confiscated "national property", made legal
    tender in April 1790, issued in rising quantities until the plates were
    broken in February 1796) and the recorded depreciation series — the value in
    specie of 100 livres in assignats, month by month, from the official
    departmental tables the Directory published in 1796–97; (2) Maistre's
    count — the table of laws passed by the three assemblies (2,557 + 1,712 +
    11,210 = 15,479; Considerations ch. 7 p. 99) and the three constitutions
    (1791, 1793, 1795).
  - *Module results it uses:* `entailed-inheritance` and `prejudice` (Burke's
    epistemic case — the forecast is its test); `providence-in-history` and
    `no-such-thing-as-man` (Maistre's thesis that legislation for "man" is paper
    and that what endures was not deliberated); `rational-state` (Hegel's
    actuality vs existence as the test for what lasted); `labour-theory-of-property`
    and Locke's taxation-by-consent (the confiscation as the case);
    `dictatorship-of-the-sabre` (Donoso's dilemma as the lens on 1793–1799: the
    Terror as the dictatorship that claimed to come from below, Brumaire as the
    one from above, the Code made under it) and `government-founded-on-opinion`
    (the assignat's value as opinion of the state's credit); the sourcing drill
    from `english-revolution-as-test` and `altar-of-victory`.
  - *Departures the lesson must name:* (a) the assignat did collapse, roughly as
    Burke said — but by a route he did not predict (war finance after 1792 and
    the Terror's price controls, not the land-bank's structure alone), and the
    sale of Church lands itself was never reversed — Maistre advised keeping it
    (ch. 10); (b) the departments, which Burke said would be "so many
    independent republics" that would not obey Paris (S1 l.1719–1760; S3
    l.1715–1777), became the instrument of the most centralised administration
    in Europe and exist today; (c) the metric system, decreed by the Convention
    (1795) — "made for man", for "all peoples, all times" — is the world's
    measure; (d) the Civil Code (1804) codified the Revolution's civil law and
    outlasted every regime — but under a single ruler, which Maistre could claim
    as his "legislator who says fiat" and Burke as his general become master;
    (e) the count of laws is a measure of output, not of durability: most of the
    15,479 were decrees of administration, and the thesis "nothing designed
    endures" needs a criterion for what counts as design; (f) Donoso's dilemma
    fits 1799 (a general ended an assembly that could no longer govern) but not
    its sequel: the dictatorship "from above" kept and codified the Revolution's
    civil law rather than restoring the old order, so the sabre preserved what
    Donoso's dagger had won. State each departure
    plainly and let the reader weigh which theory the record favours where.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: in 1790 the National Assembly
confiscated the lands of the Church and issued paper notes backed by them; Burke
wrote that same year that the scheme would end as John Law's Mississippi scheme
had, in worthless paper and a government living by force; Maistre later counted
the laws the revolutionary assemblies passed and said no society could absorb
them. Both forecasts can be checked. The lesson checks them.

1. **What was an assignat, and why did the Assembly need one?** The problem as
   the Assembly met it: a state bankrupt (Burke gives Necker's deficit — bind
   it: about 56 million livres a year, roughly a tenth of revenue, by Burke's
   own figures from Necker, S2 l.1786–1829), a tax system that had just
   collapsed with the regime that collected it, and a Church owning perhaps a
   tenth of the land. The obvious remedy: sell the Church's lands. Why it
   fails at once: "to bring this unwieldy mass of landed property … at once
   into market was obviously to defeat the profits … by depreciating the value
   of those lands, and indeed of all the landed estates throughout France" (S2
   l.1877 ff.). The Assembly's answer: issue notes secured on the lands —
   *assignats*, so called because each was "assigned" to a portion of the
   national property, redeemable by buying land with them, bearing interest at
   first — then, in April 1790, made legal tender. Define the term and show the
   note (the sourced figure) here: what the reader can see on it — the face
   value, "Domaines nationaux", the law that authorised it, the penalty for
   forgery. Heading as question: *How do you pay a state's debts with land you
   cannot sell?*
2. **What did Burke predict, and on what grounds?** Not merely "it will
   depreciate" — a forecast with a mechanism. (a) Paper credit rests on prior
   cash and prosperity, not the reverse: the English note is accepted "of
   choice", convertible, and "of value in commerce because in law it is of
   none"; the assignat is "the creature not of credit, but of power" (S4
   l.1258–1284). (b) A land-bank "has commonly ended in bankruptcy" because land
   is not liquid; a proper one would first strike a net surplus and vest it in
   trustees (the worked model, S4 l.1376–1398); the Assembly did not even
   state the encumbrances, and the pensions owed to the dispossessed clergy
   exceeded the estates' income (S4 l.1455–1457) — "Never did a state … enrich
   itself by the confiscations of the citizens". (c) The early signs: sixteen
   million sterling issued, "an almost immediate depreciation of five per cent,
   which in a little time came to about seven"; tax collectors receiving coin
   and paying the treasury in paper (S4 l.1524–1545). (d) The social forecast:
   "a nation of gamesters" — "what he receives in the morning will not have the
   same value at night"; the peasant who finds his note "seven per cent the
   worse for crossing the way" and stops bringing corn; towns forcing the
   country; "the murders of Paris and St. Denis may be renewed through all
   France" (S3 l.2087–2134). (e) The comparison case: John Law (anchor: the
   Scottish financier whose bank and Mississippi company collapsed in 1720) —
   whose "generous delusions" at least aimed at commerce and used no force;
   the assignat "entices the smell of a mole" and adds compulsion (S4
   l.1564–1604). Burke's forecast, stated in one sentence the record can test:
   compulsory paper on confiscated land will depreciate toward nothing, breed
   speculation, set town against country, and leave the state ruling by force.
   Put the mechanism in the previous lessons' terms in one sentence: a note
   accepted "of choice" rests on the holder's opinion of the issuer's interest
   and right (`<Ref>` hume-against-the-original-contract); a note forced at par
   replaces that opinion with the law's penalty — which is why Burke calls it
   "the creature not of credit, but of power".
3. **What does the record show?** The depreciation series as a figure (the
   lesson's primary data chart), with the sourcing drill applied to the source
   first: the official departmental tables of 1797 — who drew them up (the
   Directory's administration, to settle debts contracted in assignats), for
   what purpose (to convert nominal sums to real ones, month by month), what
   they record (a legal exchange rate fixed after the fact from market
   evidence), what they omit (regional variation is in the tables; black-market
   rates, the price controls of 1793–94 that masked the fall). Then the shape:
   a slow slide to 1792; a sharp fall with war (April 1792) and the overthrow
   of the king; a partial recovery under the Terror's "maximum" and the death
   penalty for refusing paper (bind: the law of the general maximum, September
   1793); collapse after Thermidor (1794) when controls ended; near zero by early
   1796; the plates broken in February 1796; the mandats that replaced them
   failed within months; paper demonetised 1797. Two keeper numbers in prose,
   bound. Computation inside the inquiry only: one calculation — e.g. what a
   fixed salary of 1,000 livres in paper bought in specie at three dates, ending
   in a prose interpretation ("a creditor paid in 1795 received a few per cent
   of his loan") and an uncertainty statement (the tables are post hoc and
   legal, not market). The verdict on Burke's mechanism: the direction and the
   end he got right in 1790; the route ran through war and the Terror's
   controls, which he did not foresee; and the "nation of gamesters" (speculation
   in biens nationaux, fortunes made by buying land with depreciated paper) is
   in the record — as is the town–country conflict over grain. Mark the seam
   (L10b): from a forecast made before any of it, one expects vindication; the
   record gives vindication of the end and not of the path.
4. **What did Maistre count, and what did he claim it showed?** The table (ch.
   7 p. 99): three assemblies, six years, 15,479 laws — Maistre's source a count
   "made in France, reported in a foreign gazette for February 1796", which he
   calls "honest enough" beside a journalist's claim of two million. Apply the
   drill to Maistre's number: a count of acts (laws and decrees undistinguished),
   from a hostile gazette, in a pamphlet whose argument needs the number large.
   Then his inference: "Why so many laws? Because there is no legislator"; "a
   real legislator does not fumble around; he says fiat and the machine goes";
   "to destroy is not to make"; "Quod caret alterna requie durabile non est" —
   what lacks alternation of rest will not endure; and "three constitutions in
   five years" (1791, 1793, 1795) as the proof that nothing deliberated holds.
   State the claim in a testable form: the volume of legislation measures the
   absence of a legislator, and what is made by deliberation does not last.
5. **What does the record show for Maistre?** Two columns. For him: the
   constitution of 1795 he was writing against fell in 1799 to a general
   (Burke's forecast too); the 1793 constitution was never applied; the 1791
   one lasted a year; the Directory's own festivals failed as he said (ch. 5).
   Against him: the departments of 1790 — drawn, as Burke mocked, "with a
   theodolite" into "square republics" — survived every regime since and are
   the frame of French administration today; the metric system decreed in
   1795 for "all peoples and all times" — legislation for "man" in Maistre's
   exact sense, and for no people in particular against Herder's plurality of
   peoples (`<Ref>` herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation, one clause) — is the
   world's measure; the sale of Church lands was never reversed, and Maistre
   himself (ch. 10) said it must not be — the buyers' title, begun in
   confiscation, became right by possession and interest, as the module's
   sceptic said titles do (`<Ref>` hume-against-the-original-contract). Then the
   hardest case, stated for both sides: the Civil Code of 1804, which gave the
   Revolution's civil law (equality before the law, civil marriage, partible
   inheritance, the end of feudal tenures) a form that outlasted every French
   regime — but was made under one ruler by a commission of four, not by an
   assembly; Maistre can claim it as a legislator who "says fiat", Burke as the
   general become master; and yet its content was the Assembly's. The lesson
   does not resolve this; it names it. Then Donoso's lens, one paragraph
   (`<Ref>` donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship): he called the First Republic "a
   gigantic dictatorship without end" and framed every crisis as a choice
   between a dictatorship from below and one from above. The record of
   1793–1799 fits the frame (the Committee of Public Safety ruling by decree in
   the name of the people; the Directory purging its own legislature in 1797
   and 1798; the general of 1799), and breaks it at the next step: the
   dictatorship "from above" did not restore what the one "from below" had
   destroyed — it codified the Revolution's civil law and kept the departments,
   now under prefects, the "million ears" of Donoso's thermometer.
6. **Where theory and record part.** The ledger (the lesson's synthesis figure):
   forecast / ground / what happened / verdict, for Burke's four and Maistre's
   two. Then the departures, each stated as a departure from a specific
   premise: (a) Burke's mechanism was financial (land-bank structure); the
   collapse was fiscal-military (war from 1792) — the theory under-predicted the
   cause and over-predicted the social break (France did not dissolve into
   warring municipalities); (b) "nothing designed endures" needs a criterion of
   design — the departments were designed on a map; (c) Maistre's count is a
   measure of output, and output in a revolution is not a measure of
   durability; (d) Hegel's test (`<Ref>`): what lasted was what came to be
   recognised as articulating the nation's life — codified law, uniform
   administration, uniform measures — which is neither Burke's inheritance nor
   Maistre's Providence but something a people found rational after the fact.
   State the course's own conclusion as a judgment, not a verdict: the
   epistemic case predicts the failure of designs that ignore what a society
   already is (a currency), and fails to predict the success of designs that
   give a society tools it then uses (a map, a measure, a code). Let the reader
   weigh whether that distinction saves Burke or refutes him.
7. **What the reader can now do, and what stays open.** Read a forecast
   against a series with the drill applied to both; state which of the module's
   theses the record bears out and which it breaks. Open: the next module asks
   not whether the Revolution's designs lasted but what the Revolution was a
   symptom of — a levelling that no one designed and no one stopped (`<Ref>`
   tocqueville-equality-and-the-township).

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The assignat in hand**: the sourced photograph read as an object — face
   value, "Domaines nationaux", the authorising law's date, "La loi punit de
   mort le contrefacteur" (verify on the chosen note), the Republic's symbols
   after 1792 replacing the king's. One paragraph of reading before any theory.
2. **Burke's proper land-bank (S4 l.1376–1398)** as a step-through: gross value
   → charges → encumbrances → net surplus → trustees → parcels sold or stock
   subscribed — then the Assembly's actual sequence beside it (confiscation →
   compulsory paper → charges discovered later), so the reader sees exactly what
   Burke says was skipped.
3. **"Our paper is of value in commerce, because in law it is of none" (S4
   l.1274–1275)** parsed: convertibility, choice, no enforcement — three
   properties the assignat lacked, with the April 1790 legal-tender decree as
   the moment it lost the third.
4. **The peasant at market (S3 l.2111–2134)**: at par when he sells corn, seven
   per cent worse when he buys — the first appearance of the town–country wedge;
   then the record's grain riots and the maximum as what followed.
5. **Law's Mississippi against the assignat (S4 l.1564–1604)**: two columns —
   what Law's scheme rested on (East India and African trade, the farmed
   revenues, "the whole range of the two hemispheres") and what the assignat
   rested on ("feeding France from its own substance"); "in their fraud there
   was no mixture of force" as the decisive difference; the church bells coined
   as "alchemy".
6. **The depreciation series as the lesson's data figure**, with the sourcing
   drill on the Directory's tables first, two bound keeper numbers in the
   caption, and the one permitted calculation (a fixed sum in paper at three
   dates) worked in prose with its uncertainty.
7. **Maistre's table of laws (ch. 7 p. 99)** reproduced as a bar chart with its
   provenance under it (a count in a foreign gazette, February 1796; "honest
   enough"), the sum verified (2,557 + 1,712 + 11,210), and the per-month rate
   the reader can compute inside the inquiry — followed by the question what a
   "law" was in the count.
8. **Three constitutions in five years — and a fourth**: 1791 (one year),
   1793 (never applied), 1795 (four years), 1799 (Bonaparte) — a timeline with
   spans, not ticks (playbook-history: duration and simultaneity), with the
   departments (1790–today), the metric law (1795–today) and the Code
   (1804–today) drawn as the long bars beneath — the figure that states the
   departure.
9. **The ledger**: six forecasts, ground, outcome, verdict — Burke's paper
   money, nation of gamesters, departments, general; Maistre's laws, three
   constitutions.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or
  idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only
  through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with
  who applied it and when.
- **Do not re-teach Donoso, Hume or Herder.** Each enters as a lens with a `<Ref>`
  and a clause; the lesson's new matter remains the two artefacts and the
  forecasts of Burke and Maistre. *Fructidor* (September 1797, the Directory's
  purge of the royalist majority in its own councils) and *Brumaire* (November
  1799, Bonaparte's coup) are anchored in one clause each if Donoso's paragraph
  uses them.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** *assignat* (define as above at first use);
  *biens nationaux / national property* (the confiscated lands of the Church,
  from November 1789, later the émigrés' and the crown's); *legal tender*
  (money a creditor must accept — the April 1790 decree); *Necker* (Louis
  XVI's finance minister, Genevan banker, dismissed 1790 — one clause);
  *the maximum* (the Convention's price and wage controls, September 1793 –
  December 1794); *Thermidor* (`<Ref>` maistre-and-bonald); *the Directory*
  (the executive of five under the 1795 constitution, 1795–99); *mandats
  territoriaux* (the land-backed paper that replaced assignats in March 1796
  and failed by the summer); *John Law* and *the Mississippi scheme*; *the
  Constituent Assembly / Legislative Assembly / Convention* (the three bodies of
  Maistre's table — one clause each with dates); *the Civil Code* (the Code
  Napoléon, 1804); *the metric system* (the Convention's decimal measures, the
  metre as a fraction of the meridian — one clause); *département* (`<Ref>`
  burke-reflections, where Burke's "square republics" anchored it); *Caron's
  tables / the tableaux de dépréciation* (what they are, who made them, why).
- **Numbers only inside the source inquiry (playbook-history rules 1–4).**
  Every number in prose is bound in its sentence (unit, base, date, source);
  the series lives in the figure; two or three keeper numbers; the single
  computation is the historical argument (what paper bought at three dates)
  and ends in interpretation and uncertainty. Burke's and Maistre's figures
  are THEIRS — "by Burke's figures from Necker", "by the count Maistre took
  from a gazette" — never the course's own. Do not reconcile livres and
  sterling beyond Burke's own conversions; do not compute inflation rates from
  a post hoc legal table as if it were a price index.
- **Verify every date and value against the chosen compilation before writing
  it.** The brief's "expected shape" in Sources is orientation, not data. If the
  writer cannot obtain Caron or the Seine table, use Marion or Thiers and say
  which; if only White is to hand, use his figures with his provenance stated
  (a hard-money pamphlet of 1876) and treat them as a sourcing-drill object.
- **The assignat's fall had several causes**: Burke's mechanism (over-issue on
  an illiquid, over-charged security), war finance from 1792, the Terror's
  controls (which masked the fall and then released it), and the flight of
  specie. The lesson credits Burke with the direction and end, not with the
  causal path; no single-cause story (a real L13 target: "the assignat failed
  because paper money always fails" — White's thesis — is the misconception to
  refute, with the English wartime suspension of 1797–1821 as the discriminating
  case if the writer can anchor it in one sentence).
- **Maistre's 15,479 is a count of acts.** Most were administrative decrees;
  the thesis it is made to carry (volume shows the absence of a legislator) is
  Maistre's inference, not a fact in the table. Say so; do not dismiss it — the
  number is real and the inference is the argument to weigh.
- **The Code cuts both ways** and the lesson must say so (link 5) rather than
  claim it for one side. Likewise the sale of Church lands: Burke's "Never did a
  state enrich itself by confiscation" is about the state's finances (true: the
  assignat lost the value the lands were meant to secure) while the social fact
  (a new class of proprietors) endured — distinguish the two claims.
- **Do not re-teach Burke's or Maistre's arguments**; recall each with a `<Ref>`
  and one clause, then test. Do not re-run the Rousseau–Hegel debate; Hegel
  enters only as the test criterion (actuality vs existence) in one paragraph.
- **No moralising about the Revolution in either direction**: the lesson is a
  test of forecasts; its verdicts are about predictions, not about the
  Revolution's justice.
- **Images:** a photograph of a note passes the visible-not-legible test only
  if the reader is asked to SEE something (the printed face value against the
  recorded real value; the Republic's emblems replacing the royal; the forgery
  warning) — the caption must name it. A scan of a page of Caron's tables is a
  defect (text); the series is redrawn as the course's own chart from the
  table's numbers, with the table cited.
- **Names ONLY in References:** Caron, Marion, Harris, Thiers, A. D. White,
  E. N. White, Aftalion, Bordo, Aulard, Lebrun, Muller, Hamilton (SEP), Strauss.
  Thinkers and actors who may be named: Burke, Maistre, Hegel (`<Ref>`),
  Necker, Calonne (Burke's note 133), John Law, Bonaparte (as an actor), the
  Directory, the Convention. The Directory's tables are a named object.
- **Dossier gaps:** the depreciation series, the departmental tables, the dates
  of the lasting institutions, and John Law are NOT in the dossier (all public
  domain; pointers above). S. E. Harris's copyright status must be checked. The
  course.yaml note's "Bibliothèque nationale" is Gallica (the BnF's digital
  library): the Caron volume and the Seine table are the items to find there.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval, warm-up (E3).* State Burke's forecast about the assignat in one
   sentence with its mechanism (S4 l.1258–1284, 1366–1398) and Maistre's about
   the laws with its inference (ch. 7), then name which concept from
   `burke-reflections` and which from `maistre-and-bonald` each forecast is a
   test of, and which of Donoso's two dictatorships (`<Ref>`
   donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship) the general of 1799 would count as.
2. *Research-shaped task (the one computation).* Using the depreciation table
   in the lesson's figure, take a debt of 1,000 livres contracted in coin in
   1790 and repaid in assignats at face value in January 1793, January 1795 and
   January 1796: what did the creditor receive in specie at each date? End with
   a prose interpretation (who gained — debtors, buyers of national property,
   the state), an uncertainty statement (the table is a legal rate fixed after
   the fact), and a corroborating non-quantitative source (Burke's peasant at
   market, S3 l.2111–2134, or a contemporary complaint the writer anchors).
3. *Document reasoning (sourcing drill).* Maistre's law count (ch. 7 p. 99 and
   n.2): author, date (1796–97, in exile), audience (French royalists and
   moderates), what it records (a gazette's count of acts), what it argues
   (volume shows no legislator), what it omits (what counted as a "law"; that a
   revolution governing by decree produces acts as a parliament does not), and
   what it can be USED to establish — the scale of revolutionary legislation
   and Maistre's method, not the durability of any act.
4. *Plausible false claim to refute.* "The assignat proves Burke right: paper
   money not backed by metal always collapses." Name the failing steps: Burke's
   own argument is about compulsion and the illiquid, over-charged security, not
   about paper as such — he praises the Bank of England's paper (S4
   l.1258–1284); and the discriminating case the solution supplies in one
   sentence: England suspended convertibility in 1797 and its notes held for
   twenty years — the difference being choice and credit, which is Burke's own
   criterion.
5. *Boundary probe.* Apply "nothing made by deliberation endures" (Maistre ch.
   6–7) to the metric system: decreed by the Convention in 1795 for "all
   peoples", resisted, made compulsory in 1840, now universal. What does the
   thesis have to say about it, and what criterion of "design" would let it
   survive? The reader should find that the thesis either excludes tools and
   measures (then it says nothing about administration either, and the
   departments escape too) or fails.
6. *Synthesis / causal ranking.* Rank as necessary / enabling / precipitating
   the causes of the assignat's collapse — over-issue on an over-charged
   security (Burke's mechanism), war from 1792, the maximum and its repeal, the
   flight of specie — and defend the ranking with one piece of evidence each
   from the lesson's figure and Burke's text. The model answer argues the
   warrant, not just the verdict (E4).
7. *Counter-argument (change and continuity).* Someone claims that since the
   departments, the metric system and the Code lasted, Burke and Maistre were
   simply wrong that design cannot endure. Name two facts the claim fails to
   account for (the three constitutions did fall; the Code was made under one
   ruler, not an assembly) and state what would have to be true for it to
   survive (a criterion separating designs that give a society tools from designs
   that remake it — which neither Burke nor Maistre states and Hegel's actuality
   gestures at, `<Ref>`).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`.** The buyers of
   national property kept their land through every regime, including the
   Restoration, which compensated the émigrés rather than evicting the buyers.
   Ask what this did to the distribution of land in France and to the old
   nobility's position — and notice that the Revolution's most durable effect
   may be one no assembly decreed: a levelling of conditions. The solution says
   in one sentence that the next module's French aristocrat will call exactly
   that a "providential fact" and ask what makes it bearable, with the `<Ref>`.

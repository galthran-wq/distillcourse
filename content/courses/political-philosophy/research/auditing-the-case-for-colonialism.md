# Research brief: auditing-the-case-for-colonialism (Auditing the Case for Colonialism: three cited claims re-checked)

status: planned
module: empire-and-guilt
type: core
goal: Apply the sourcing drill to three empirical claims in the Case for Colonialism and its critics — the slave-trade trust effect, colonial-era stature in Kenya, real wages in British Africa — by reading what the cited studies actually found

- **Hands off:** The module ends with its verdict: the titles from discovery,
  donation, empire, unbelief and natural slavery fail (strong), and no lasting
  rule over a self-governing people rests on the limited title (on balance);
  a general ledger of net benefit or net harm is open, and by kind of colony
  the evidence leans one way for settlement colonies and islands and the
  other, without settling, for mainland sub-Saharan Africa; guilt does not pass
  and liability can (strong). The course's next module opens a new question
  (`the-nation-and-its-defenders`: who "we" are), and the closing synthesis
  (`the-argument-assembled`) returns to "the West's judgment of its conquests"
  as one of three thematic halts. The one forward exercise reaches
  `powell-raspail-and-camus`, where three prophecies of displacement are

- **Spine step:** "The West judges its own conquests: Vitoria's titles, Locke's waste land, Burke's impeachment of Hastings, Mill's tutelage, the colonial balance sheet, and whether guilt is inherited"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):** The artefact is three peer-reviewed studies as
  cited on two sides of a public dispute — by Gilley's 2022 Response ("Lazy
  Reflexive Citations", "Health and Education", "Slavery") and by Kendhammer's
  2017 op-ed (and Taylor's 2018 critique for the Nunn claim). The module
  results it uses: `cost-benefit-of-empire` (what a "benefit" or "harm" entry
  needs: a measured variable, a population, a period, a comparison);
  `counterfactual-development` (what comparison each study actually makes — a
  cross-section with controls, a before/after boundary, a trend against London);
  `subjective-legitimacy` indirectly (the first study's variable is trust in
  neighbours and institutions — a measure of what rule left behind in
  attitudes); `supersession` (a reparations calculation needs a present effect
  — the first study is the kind of evidence such a claim would cite); and the
  sourcing drill from `docs/playbook-history.md`. Departures from the
  idealisation the lesson must name: (1) the studies measure proxies — survey
  answers about trust, army recruits' heights, unskilled urban wages — not
  "benefit"; (2) their populations are selected — Afrobarometer respondents in
  the countries surveyed, men who passed a recruiting board, wage-earners in
  towns — not "Africans"; (3) their periods and scopes differ from the
  dispute's — the slave trades 1400–1900 lie almost entirely before the 1824
  line the Case for Colonialism draws for itself; (4) both citers compress, and
  the lesson's finding is about the direction and size of each compression, not
  about who was right about colonialism; (5) computation appears only where it
  is the argument — what "one to two per cent of explained variance" means
  beside a standardised coefficient, what a welfare ratio of one means for a
  family — and ends in prose, an uncertainty statement and a corroborating
  passage.

## VERDICT

Binding: research/verdicts/REGISTER.md, module 14, module verdict (hosted here).
Module question: by what right did Europeans rule other peoples, did their rule
leave those peoples better or worse off, and does anything pass to the
descendants of either side?

- **Verdict and grades.** **Strong** on the failed titles, **on balance** on
  the limited title; **open** on a general ledger, **on balance** by kind of
  colony; **strong** on inheritance.
- **(1) Title.** The titles from discovery, papal donation, the Emperor,
  unbelief and natural slavery fail, on Vitoria's premises and on the consent
  premise the course builds (**strong**; `<Ref>` vitoria-right-to-the-americas).
  On Vitoria's own premises there was a limited title to intervene: defence of
  the innocent against tyranny and human sacrifice "even if the victims
  consent", and the rights of travel and trade. Whether a lasting conquest
  could rest on it: **no, on balance** — the conqueror judged its own case.
  Locke (ch. XVI: conquest is no origin of government) and Burke ("arbitrary
  power … neither any man can hold nor any man can give") give no title to
  lasting rule over a people that governed itself. The behavioural
  "legitimacy" offered instead does not show consent; it shows that legitimacy
  was graded, not nil (`<Ref>` colonial-balance-sheet).
- **(2) Ledger.** The burden lies equally on anyone who asserts a net effect in
  either direction.
  - A general verdict of net benefit or net harm is **not established either
    way: open**.
  - Where Europeans settled, and on islands, colonial tenure goes with higher
    income today. Islands with longer colonial tenure, instrumented by wind
    patterns, are richer and have lower infant mortality, and years after 1700
    count for more (Feyrer & Sacerdote, NBER w12546, 2006/*REStat* 2009;
    abstract `research/texts/verdict-audit/nber-w12546-abstract.txt`).
    Colonial European settlement shows "a strong and uniformly positive
    relationship" with development, even at low settler shares (Easterly &
    Levine, NBER w18162, 2012/2016; abstract
    `research/texts/verdict-audit/nber-w18162-abstract.txt`). **On balance**:
    an instrumented design on islands and a cross-country association.
    Islands are not the African mainland.
  - For sub-Saharan Africa the one Africa-specific assessment, Heldring &
    Robinson (NBER WP 18566, 2012;
    `research/texts/verification-2026-11/heldring-robinson-2012-nber-w18566.txt`),
    concludes that colonialism "probably had a uniformly negative effect on
    development in Africa" (abstract, l.51). It is a qualitative review
    against stated counterfactuals, which its authors call "a very subjective
    and speculative view" (l.936), and it also holds that "in the world
    context, colonialism had very heterogeneous effects" (l.45). Easterly &
    Levine's finding cuts against the extractive-institutions mechanism it
    relies on. **Open**, with the best assessment leaning negative. What would
    settle it: a border or timing discontinuity design on the mainland.
  - Measured effects differ by kind of colony. Real urban wages rose in every
    British colony studied, though East Africa stayed near subsistence for
    decades (Frankema & van Waijenburg 2012). Heights in Kenya did not break
    trend at colonization (Moradi 2009). Both are this lesson's audited claims.
  - The slave trades, mostly before the Case's 1824 boundary, left a real and
    lasting effect. In the one causal estimate on income it is large: a
    one-standard-deviation smaller slave export goes with mean income of
    $1,864 instead of $1,249, a 50% increase (Nunn 2007, OLS, "for purely
    illustrative purposes"; the IV estimates are larger;
    `research/texts/audit-right-shading/nunn-2007-slave-trades-mpra-wp.txt`
    l.780–789). On trust there is an effect whose size the explained-variance
    share does not give (this lesson's claim one).
- **(3) Inheritance.** Liability can pass, guilt cannot (**strong**; `<Ref>`
  inherited-guilt-and-reparations).
- **Strongest objection.** From one side: the burden lies on whoever claims
  alien rule was beneficial, so "open" lets the Case off. From the other: the
  instrumented island design and the settlement association meet the course's
  own test of a credible counterfactual, so "open" lets the critics off.
- **Why it does not overturn the verdict.** One standard answers both: each
  claim of net effect carries its own burden, and neither has met it across
  colonies. The island design meets the test for islands; the settlement
  association is an association; the Africa assessment is, by its authors'
  account, subjective. No design covers many colonies of different kinds with
  results in one direction.
- **What would overturn it.** A credible counterfactual design with results in
  one direction across many colonies of different kinds; for mainland
  sub-Saharan Africa, a border or timing discontinuity design.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim. Develop it: the reader has watched
two sides accuse each other of citing studies they have not read
(`<Ref>` colonial-balance-sheet, one clause). The newcomer's attempt is to trust
the side he already prefers, or to count citations. Both fail: a citation is a
claim about a text — "this paper found X" — and a text can be opened. The
lesson opens three.

1. **The drill, set up once for a study rather than a document.** Question:
   what does one check? Attempt: whether the study "supports" the claim.
   Fails: "supports" hides four separate questions. Metadata first: who wrote
   it, when, where it appeared (a peer-reviewed economics or economic-history
   journal; what that does and does not guarantee); what was measured (the
   variable — a survey answer, a height, a wage); on what data (population,
   period, sample, how collected); against what comparison (a cross-section
   with controls; a before/after boundary; a trend against a benchmark). Then
   the two claims side by side: what the authors claim; what the citer claims
   they found. Prediction before reading (`<Task>`): from the title, venue and
   year alone, what would you expect each study to show, and what would you
   expect it to be silent about? Because: the gap between the authors' claim
   and the citer's is what an audit measures, and it has a direction.

2. **Claim one — the slave trades and mistrust.** As cited: by the op-ed, as
   evidence that colonialism's legacy includes a lasting mistrust; by the
   Response, as showing that "slavery explains almost nothing" — "between one
   and two percent" of explained variation, "a rounding error". The study:
   survey answers about trust from the Afrobarometer round of 2005, matched to
   the respondent's ethnic group's recorded slave exports between 1400 and
   1900; a negative relation that survives controls and an instrument
   (distance from the coast; `<Ref>` national-data-audited for what an
   instrument is). What the lesson adds to what the reader holds — figure 2:
   "variance explained" (recalled from `<Ref>` national-data-audited, where the
   square of a correlation was read as the share of spread a line accounts for)
   is here the share of the spread in trust answers across individual
   respondents, not across country means; the new step is the standardised
   coefficient beside it — a variable can shift the average answer by a tenth
   of a standard deviation (the
   standardised coefficient the paper reports, to be verified) and still
   account for only one or two per cent of the spread, because most of the
   spread is between individuals for other reasons; small share and real
   effect are both true at once. Then what survives: a real, robust, modest
   effect of the slave trades on a measure of trust — the paper's own claim,
   verified in its magnitude paragraph. What does not: the op-ed's step from
   "slave trade" to "colonialism" (the trades lie almost entirely before 1824,
   the Response's own boundary); the Response's "explains almost nothing", if
   the paper's own magnitude discussion treats the effect as substantial — a
   small R² share is not "nothing" (verify which the paper says). What remains
   judgment: how much a tenth of a standard deviation in trust weighs against
   anything else on a ledger. Because: the same table supports two sentences,
   and the drill shows which word in each is the compression.

3. **Claim two — heights in Kenya.** As cited: by the Response, for two
   findings — no significant change across the colonization boundary, and
   improvement during the colonial period from health infrastructure. The
   study: heights from army recruitment records, by birth cohort, with
   civilian survey heights for later cohorts; height as a thermometer of
   childhood — net nutrition after disease — read when the man is measured
   twenty years later. Selection: who joins an army (healthier, taller,
   from recruiting regions; the author's own tests). What survives: the two
   quoted sentences, if they are the author's (verify pp. 719–720, 746) and
   the series they summarise. What does not: "colonialism improved health"
   without the pre-colonial cohorts (the boundary finding says the
   colonization moment did not change the trend) and without the post-1963
   cohorts (what happened after — read the paper); and "army recruits" standing
   for "most Kenyans" without the author's bridge to the civilian data. The
   standard turned round: the Response's own rule is comparative trends
   against a regional or world trendline — did Kenyan cohorts gain faster or
   slower than African or world cohorts of the same decades? If the paper
   does not say, the Response's rule cannot be applied with it. What remains
   judgment: whether a people whose children grew taller under a regime were
   benefited by the regime or by the century.

4. **Claim three — real wages in British Africa.** As cited: by the op-ed
   (read the sentence — what harm was it cited for?); by the Response, for
   "Real wages increased during the colonial era in all of the countries we
   studied" and growth "in line and sometimes even outpaced" London's
   nineteenth-century unskilled wages. The study: unskilled urban wages
   against the cost of a bare-bones basket — the welfare ratio — in a set of
   British colonies, 1880–1965; its own argument is a divergence: West Africa
   well above subsistence and rising, East Africa near subsistence for
   decades. The one computation, inside the inquiry (figure 4): a welfare
   ratio of 1 means a year's wage buys one family's barest basket and
   nothing else; a ratio of 2 or 3 is what the West African series shows at
   its height (verify the values); the East African series near 1 means a
   wage-earner's family lived at the edge — then the interpretation in prose,
   the uncertainty (basket composition; urban wage-earners only; a minority
   of the population), and a corroborating non-quantitative source if the
   writer has one (the paper's own discussion of settler land policy in
   Kenya). What survives: rising real urban wages in every colony studied —
   the Response's quotation is in the paper. What does not: "living standards
   rose for Africans" (the rural majority is unmeasured; the paper measures
   towns); and, on the other side, "wages were held at subsistence" (true-ish
   for East Africa for part of the period, false for the West). What remains
   judgment: whether the West/East gap — the paper's thesis that it was
   policy, not geography — is a count against colonialism in general or a
   count for one kind of colonial economy over another.

5. **What re-checking changes.** The KEEPER table (figure 3): three claims ×
   four columns — as the Response cites it | as the op-ed (or Taylor) cites it
   | what the study states | what it can be used to establish. Then the
   audit's finding, stated as the course's own: both citers compressed; the
   direction of each compression (toward "harm" or toward "benefit"); the size
   of each (a scope shift of a century; a population shift from recruits to a
   nation; a headline swapped for a secondary finding). The lesson says, claim
   by claim and once the papers are verified, which compression is larger and
   whether they are symmetric — by the same standard for both citers — and
   names the sentence of the paper that decides it. The second-order lesson: a balance sheet's
   entries are each a proxy with a population and a period; the aggregation
   into "on balance" is where argument, not data, lives — which is what the
   previous lesson said the two sides measure differently.

6. **Departures named.** The idealisation (a ledger of measured benefits and
   harms against a counterfactual) departs from the artefact in the five ways
   the APPLICATION spec lists; state them in the body as the section's
   bearing. One paragraph: every judgment of a conquest in the module has
   rested on an empirical premise — that the Indians had cities and laws, that
   Bengal had law and property, that improvement was "actually effected", that
   a wage rose.

7. **The weighing — the titles fail, the general ledger is open, and liability
   passes while guilt does not.** The module's verdict, reached in the
   lesson's last body section (see VERDICT), one part at a time and with one
   standard for both sides. (1) Title: recall the failed titles (strong) and
   the limited title Vitoria left; the conqueror judged its own case, so no
   lasting rule over a self-governing people rests on it (on balance); Locke's
   ch. XVI and Burke's "arbitrary power" sentence agree. (2) Ledger: state the
   burden rule first — whoever asserts a net effect in either direction carries
   it. Then the designs the reader can now read with the drill: the island
   design (Feyrer & Sacerdote) and the settlement association (Easterly &
   Levine) for settlement colonies and islands (on balance); Heldring &
   Robinson for the African mainland, with its design and its own hedge
   ("very subjective and speculative") stated in the sentence that uses it
   (open, leaning negative); this lesson's three audited claims as the
   measured effects by kind of colony; Nunn's income estimate for the slave
   trades ($1,249 against $1,864, OLS, "for purely illustrative purposes").
   Verdict: a general ledger is open; by kind of colony, on balance. Set the
   strongest objection from both sides ("open" lets the Case off; "open" lets
   the critics off) and answer both with the one burden rule. (3)
   Inheritance: liability can pass, guilt cannot (strong; recall, do not
   re-argue). Close the section with what would overturn the open finding: a
   credible counterfactual design with one-directional results across many
   kinds of colony; for the mainland, a border or timing discontinuity
   design.

Headings fall out as: "A citation is a claim about a text"; "What one to two
per cent of the variance means"; "Height as a record of childhood"; "A year's
wage against a basket"; "Three claims, four columns"; "What the ledger's
entries are made of"; "No title for lasting rule; no general ledger yet; a debt
without guilt".

**Conclusion.** States the module verdict in plain words with its grades:
Europeans' titles from discovery, papal grant, empire, unbelief and natural
slavery fail (strong), and the one limited title Vitoria left — intervention
to defend the innocent — gave no right to lasting rule over a people that
governed itself (on balance); whether colonial rule left the ruled better or
worse off is not established either way across colonies (open), while by kind
of colony the evidence favours settlement colonies and islands (on balance) and
leans negative, without settling, for mainland sub-Saharan Africa; and liability
can pass to descendants where guilt cannot (strong). Then the hand-off: the
course turns to who "we" are (`the-nation-and-its-defenders`); the synthesis
returns to this verdict.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** Afrobarometer (a survey of public attitudes
  across African countries, begun 1999); standardised coefficient (the effect
  of a one-standard-deviation change in the cause, in standard deviations of
  the outcome); R² / variance explained and instrumental variable — both
  recalled by `<Ref>` national-data-audited, never re-defined at length; King's
  African Rifles (the British colonial regiment in East
  Africa); Demographic and Health Surveys; birth cohort; welfare ratio and
  bare-bones basket; Blue Books (colonial governments' annual statistical
  returns); Gold Coast (Ghana), Nyasaland (Malawi); peer review; AER and JEH
  as journals. Three per section at most; each defined in the sentence where
  it first appears.
- **Computation only inside source inquiry (playbook-history rule 4).** The
  lesson's arithmetic is limited to: what a share of variance means beside a
  standardised coefficient; what a welfare ratio means. Each ends in a prose
  interpretation, an uncertainty statement and, where available, a
  corroborating passage. No recomputed regressions, no invented sample
  sizes.
- **The three papers must be obtained and read.** Open versions exist (NBER
  14783; CGEH WP 24; Moradi's paper is behind the JEH paywall — a university
  library or the author's page). If one cannot be had, the lesson audits two
  and says in References which it could not read — an audit lesson that
  cites unread papers has failed its own test.
- **Both citers' texts must be read.** The Response is in the dossier; the
  op-ed and Taylor are not. Without Kendhammer's sentences the lesson cannot
  state what he cited each paper for; "as the Response says Kendhammer said"
  is the lazy citation the lesson is about. Obtain the op-ed.
- **Naming.** Gilley, Kendhammer and Taylor are the authors of the texts under
  examination and may be named in the body as such (L8a(b)); the studies are
  named by their authors' names as the names of the papers (Nunn and
  Wantchekon's study), as the previous lesson named Abernethy's test. Nobody
  is cited as an authority: every sentence about a paper reports what the
  paper says at a stated place.
- **Verify every quotation the Response attributes to a paper against the
  paper.** The Moradi sentences, the Frankema–van Waijenburg sentences and
  the "one to two percent" figure are the audit's object; the brief reports
  them as the Response gives them, not as verified.
- **The audit's finding and the module verdict are two things.** The audit's
  finding is about compressions and their direction: "the op-ed moved the
  period; the Response moved the magnitude" is the kind of sentence it allows,
  once verified. Three audited claims cannot carry "Gilley was right" or "the
  critics were right" overall; the module verdict in the last section says
  what the wider evidence does carry — open on a general ledger, on balance by
  kind of colony — and states the same burden for both sides. The
  register rule: The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Dates:** to the decade except 2005 (the survey round), 1880–1965 and
  1400–1900 (the studies' own periods), 2009/2011/2012 (publication), 2017 and
  2022 (the dispute).
- **Do not re-teach** the balance-sheet lesson's three failures or Waldron's
  models; one `<Ref>` clause each where the audit touches them.
- **Dossier gap, by design:** none of the three studies, neither critic's
  text. The brief gives full citations; the lesson cannot be written from the
  dossier alone.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (E3, the one warm-up).** Name the three failures the Case for
   Colonialism charges its critics with (`<Ref>` colonial-balance-sheet) and
   say which of this lesson's three claims bears on which failure.
2. **Document reasoning — a fourth citation drilled.** From the Response's
   "Health and Education": Cappelli and Baten's 284 country-decades, 1730–1970,
   British colonialism positive on human capital, others neutral. Fill in the
   drill card from the working paper's abstract (CEPR DP 11273, 2016 — obtain):
   what "human capital" is measured by (age-heaping as a numeracy proxy —
   verify), on what data, against what comparison; then state what the finding
   can and cannot be used to establish.
3. **Boundary probe.** Construct a case where a cause has a large average
   effect and explains almost none of the variance (a rare, concentrated
   exposure in a large, heterogeneous population). State what "explains almost
   nothing" means in that case and why it is not "has no effect"; then state
   the opposite boundary — a variable with a large R² share and a trivial
   effect per unit (a near-constant that happens to track the outcome). Solution
   shows the two numbers answer different questions.
4. **Claim to refute.** "The slave-trade study shows that colonialism caused
   African mistrust." Name the two steps that fail (the period — 1400–1900
   against the 1824 line; the variable — slave exports, with colonial rule as a
   control, not the treatment) and the one sentence of the paper that would
   have to read differently for the claim to stand.
5. **Derivation inside source inquiry.** Using the paper's definition of the
   welfare ratio, state what ratios of 1, 2 and 3 mean for a Gold Coast
   labourer's family in a year; then write the uncertainty statement (basket
   composition; urban wage-earners only; the share of the population they were)
   and name the corroborating passage you would look for.
6. **Research-shaped task.** Design the test that would distinguish the
   Response's reading of the Kenyan heights ("colonialism improved nutrition")
   from the critics' ("the century improved nutrition everywhere"): what data
   would each need (African and world cohorts of the same decades; the
   post-1963 civilian series; a never-colonised comparator's heights, with the
   comparator problem from the previous lesson named), and what result would
   decide between them.
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `powell-raspail-and-camus`.** A speech or a projection says
   what share of a population a group will be in fifteen or twenty years, or in
   2050. Before reading its number, run the metadata step of the drill: list the
   assumptions such a forecast must make (fertility by group over decades;
   migration flows; who counts as a member of the group across generations;
   mixed descent) and what later record would test it. The solution says the
   next module's lesson on the prophecies of displacement checks three such
   forecasts against the censuses and states what a demographic projection can
   and cannot say, with a `<Ref>` (and that `murray-strange-death-of-europe`
   reads the ONS, Pew and Kaufmann projections the same way).
8. **Synthesis — the audited entry.** Write the balance-sheet entry for one of
   the three claims as the drill leaves it: the claim as made, the evidence as
   the paper gives it, the reasoning that connects or fails to connect them,
   and the one sentence stating what remains a judgment the data cannot make.
9. **Attack the verdict.** The module finds a general ledger of colonial rule
   open, leaning negative for mainland sub-Saharan Africa and positive for
   settlement colonies and islands. Choose one side and state its strongest
   objection in its best form — either "the burden lies on whoever defends
   alien rule, and Heldring & Robinson meet any fair standard, so the verdict
   should be net harm", or "Feyrer & Sacerdote's instrumented design is the
   only credible counterfactual in the literature and it points to benefit,
   so the verdict should be net benefit". Then say what evidence would be
   needed to overturn the open finding: name the design (a border or timing
   discontinuity on the African mainland; a credible counterfactual across
   many kinds of colony), the data it would need, and the result that would
   decide it. The solution applies one burden rule to both objections and
   shows why each falls short of "established".

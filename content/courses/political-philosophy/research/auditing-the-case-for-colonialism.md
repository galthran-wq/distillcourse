# Research brief: auditing-the-case-for-colonialism (Auditing the Case for Colonialism: three cited claims re-checked)

status: planned
module: empire-and-guilt
type: core
goal: Apply the sourcing drill to three empirical claims in the Case for Colonialism and its critics — the slave-trade trust effect, colonial-era stature in Kenya, real wages in British Africa — by reading what the cited studies actually found

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `colonial-balance-sheet`: the three concepts
  (cost–benefit of empire, counterfactual development, subjective legitimacy),
  the two sides' standards (absolute levels vs comparative trends; scope from
  1824), and the "lazy citations" charge made both ways — the forward exercise
  there asked what one would need to check about the Lumumba citation. From
  `inherited-guilt-and-reparations`: supersession and the reparations case,
  both of which rest on empirical premises about what the wrong did and what
  it does now. From the whole course: the sourcing drill (metadata → prediction
  → records/argues/omits → use, not bias → corroboration), used on texts since
  module 1 and on a trial in `burke-against-hastings`; here on peer-reviewed
  studies.
- **Contributes:** The module's method applied to its own material: three
  claims, three cited studies, read for what they measured, on what data,
  against what comparison, and whether the claim as made survives. The result is
  a table of compressions — each citer's, in which direction — and a statement
  of what remains a judgment the data cannot make.
- **Hands off:** The module ends. The course's next module opens a new question
  (`the-nation-and-its-defenders`: who "we" are), and the closing synthesis
  (`the-argument-assembled`) returns to "the West's judgment of its conquests"
  as one of three thematic halts. The one forward exercise reaches
  `murray-strange-death-of-europe`, where demographic projections are read with
  their assumptions by the same drill.
- **Spine step:** "The West judges its own conquests: Vitoria's titles, Locke's waste land, Burke's impeachment of Hastings, Mill's tutelage, the colonial balance sheet, and whether guilt is inherited"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):** The artefact is three peer-reviewed studies as
  cited on two sides of a public dispute — by Gilley's 2022 Response ("Lazy
  Reflexive Citations", "Health and Education", "Slavery") and by Kendhammer's
  2017 op-ed (and Taylor's 2018 critique for the Nunn claim). The module
  results it uses: `cost-benefit-of-empire` (what a "benefit" or "harm" entry
  needs: a measured variable, a population, a period, a comparison);
  `counterfactual-development` (what comparison each study actually makes — a
  cross-section with controls, a before/after boundary, a trend against London);
  `subjective-legitimacy` indirectly (the first study's variable is trust in
  neighbours and institutions — a measure of what rule left behind in
  attitudes); `supersession` (a reparations calculation needs a present effect
  — the first study is the kind of evidence such a claim would cite); and the
  sourcing drill from `docs/playbook-history.md`. Departures from the
  idealisation the lesson must name: (1) the studies measure proxies — survey
  answers about trust, army recruits' heights, unskilled urban wages — not
  "benefit"; (2) their populations are selected — Afrobarometer respondents in
  the countries surveyed, men who passed a recruiting board, wage-earners in
  towns — not "Africans"; (3) their periods and scopes differ from the
  dispute's — the slave trades 1400–1900 lie almost entirely before the 1824
  line the Case for Colonialism draws for itself; (4) both citers compress, and
  the lesson's finding is about the direction and size of each compression, not
  about who was right about colonialism; (5) computation appears only where it
  is the argument — what "one to two per cent of explained variance" means
  beside a standardised coefficient, what a welfare ratio of one means for a
  family — and ends in prose, an uncertainty statement and a corroborating
  passage.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim. Develop it: the reader has watched
two sides accuse each other of citing studies they have not read
(`<Ref>` colonial-balance-sheet, one clause). The newcomer's attempt is to trust
the side he already prefers, or to count citations. Both fail: a citation is a
claim about a text — "this paper found X" — and a text can be opened. The
lesson opens three.

1. **The drill, set up once for a study rather than a document.** Question:
   what does one check? Attempt: whether the study "supports" the claim.
   Fails: "supports" hides four separate questions. Metadata first: who wrote
   it, when, where it appeared (a peer-reviewed economics or economic-history
   journal; what that does and does not guarantee); what was measured (the
   variable — a survey answer, a height, a wage); on what data (population,
   period, sample, how collected); against what comparison (a cross-section
   with controls; a before/after boundary; a trend against a benchmark). Then
   the two claims side by side: what the authors claim; what the citer claims
   they found. Prediction before reading (`<Task>`): from the title, venue and
   year alone, what would you expect each study to show, and what would you
   expect it to be silent about? Because: the gap between the authors' claim
   and the citer's is what an audit measures, and it has a direction.

2. **Claim one — the slave trades and mistrust.** As cited: by the op-ed, as
   evidence that colonialism's legacy includes a lasting mistrust; by the
   Response, as showing that "slavery explains almost nothing" — "between one
   and two percent" of explained variation, "a rounding error". The study:
   survey answers about trust from the Afrobarometer round of 2005, matched to
   the respondent's ethnic group's recorded slave exports between 1400 and
   1900; a negative relation that survives controls and an instrument
   (distance from the coast). The explanation the lesson owes the newcomer —
   figure 2: "variance explained" is the share of the spread in trust answers
   across respondents that the regression attributes to a variable; a variable
   can shift the average answer by a tenth of a standard deviation (the
   standardised coefficient the paper reports, to be verified) and still
   account for only one or two per cent of the spread, because most of the
   spread is between individuals for other reasons; small share and real
   effect are both true at once. Then what survives: a real, robust, modest
   effect of the slave trades on a measure of trust — the paper's own claim,
   verified in its magnitude paragraph. What does not: the op-ed's step from
   "slave trade" to "colonialism" (the trades lie almost entirely before 1824,
   the Response's own boundary); the Response's "explains almost nothing", if
   the paper's own magnitude discussion treats the effect as substantial — a
   small R² share is not "nothing" (verify which the paper says). What remains
   judgment: how much a tenth of a standard deviation in trust weighs against
   anything else on a ledger. Because: the same table supports two sentences,
   and the drill shows which word in each is the compression.

3. **Claim two — heights in Kenya.** As cited: by the Response, for two
   findings — no significant change across the colonization boundary, and
   improvement during the colonial period from health infrastructure. The
   study: heights from army recruitment records, by birth cohort, with
   civilian survey heights for later cohorts; height as a thermometer of
   childhood — net nutrition after disease — read when the man is measured
   twenty years later. Selection: who joins an army (healthier, taller,
   from recruiting regions; the author's own tests). What survives: the two
   quoted sentences, if they are the author's (verify pp. 719–720, 746) and
   the series they summarise. What does not: "colonialism improved health"
   without the pre-colonial cohorts (the boundary finding says the
   colonization moment did not change the trend) and without the post-1963
   cohorts (what happened after — read the paper); and "army recruits" standing
   for "most Kenyans" without the author's bridge to the civilian data. The
   standard turned round: the Response's own rule is comparative trends
   against a regional or world trendline — did Kenyan cohorts gain faster or
   slower than African or world cohorts of the same decades? If the paper
   does not say, the Response's rule cannot be applied with it. What remains
   judgment: whether a people whose children grew taller under a regime were
   benefited by the regime or by the century.

4. **Claim three — real wages in British Africa.** As cited: by the op-ed
   (read the sentence — what harm was it cited for?); by the Response, for
   "Real wages increased during the colonial era in all of the countries we
   studied" and growth "in line and sometimes even outpaced" London's
   nineteenth-century unskilled wages. The study: unskilled urban wages
   against the cost of a bare-bones basket — the welfare ratio — in a set of
   British colonies, 1880–1965; its own argument is a divergence: West Africa
   well above subsistence and rising, East Africa near subsistence for
   decades. The one computation, inside the inquiry (figure 4): a welfare
   ratio of 1 means a year's wage buys one family's barest basket and
   nothing else; a ratio of 2 or 3 is what the West African series shows at
   its height (verify the values); the East African series near 1 means a
   wage-earner's family lived at the edge — then the interpretation in prose,
   the uncertainty (basket composition; urban wage-earners only; a minority
   of the population), and a corroborating non-quantitative source if the
   writer has one (the paper's own discussion of settler land policy in
   Kenya). What survives: rising real urban wages in every colony studied —
   the Response's quotation is in the paper. What does not: "living standards
   rose for Africans" (the rural majority is unmeasured; the paper measures
   towns); and, on the other side, "wages were held at subsistence" (true-ish
   for East Africa for part of the period, false for the West). What remains
   judgment: whether the West/East gap — the paper's thesis that it was
   policy, not geography — is a count against colonialism in general or a
   count for one kind of colonial economy over another.

5. **What re-checking changes.** The KEEPER table (figure 3): three claims ×
   four columns — as the Response cites it | as the op-ed (or Taylor) cites it
   | what the study states | what it can be used to establish. Then the
   finding stated as the course's own and not as a verdict on colonialism:
   both citers compressed; the direction of each compression (toward "harm"
   or toward "benefit"); the size of each (a scope shift of a century; a
   population shift from recruits to a nation; a headline swapped for a
   secondary finding) — the reader judges which compressions are larger and
   whether they are symmetric. The second-order lesson: a balance sheet's
   entries are each a proxy with a population and a period; the aggregation
   into "on balance" is where argument, not data, lives — which is what the
   previous lesson said the two sides measure differently.

6. **Departures named, and what stays open.** The idealisation (a ledger of
   measured benefits and harms against a counterfactual) departs from the
   artefact in the five ways the APPLICATION spec lists; state them in the
   body as the section's bearing. Then one paragraph: the module began with a
   theologian asking by what title Spain held America and ends with a reader
   checking footnotes; what connects them is that every judgment of a
   conquest has rested on an empirical premise — that the Indians had cities
   and laws, that Bengal had law and property, that improvement was "actually
   effected", that a wage rose — and the method for testing such premises is
   the only part of the module's result that is not itself contested. The
   course turns next to a different question (who "we" are); the closing
   synthesis returns to this one.

Headings fall out as: "A citation is a claim about a text"; "What one to two
per cent of the variance means"; "Height as a record of childhood"; "A year's
wage against a basket"; "Three claims, four columns"; "What the ledger's
entries are made of".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** Afrobarometer (a survey of public attitudes
  across African countries, begun 1999); standardised coefficient (the effect
  of a one-standard-deviation change in the cause, in standard deviations of
  the outcome); R² / variance explained; instrumental variable (one sentence,
  no more); King's African Rifles (the British colonial regiment in East
  Africa); Demographic and Health Surveys; birth cohort; welfare ratio and
  bare-bones basket; Blue Books (colonial governments' annual statistical
  returns); Gold Coast (Ghana), Nyasaland (Malawi); peer review; AER and JEH
  as journals. Three per section at most; each defined in the sentence where
  it first appears.
- **Computation only inside source inquiry (playbook-history rule 4).** The
  lesson's arithmetic is limited to: what a share of variance means beside a
  standardised coefficient; what a welfare ratio means. Each ends in a prose
  interpretation, an uncertainty statement and, where available, a
  corroborating passage. No recomputed regressions, no invented sample
  sizes.
- **The three papers must be obtained and read.** Open versions exist (NBER
  14783; CGEH WP 24; Moradi's paper is behind the JEH paywall — a university
  library or the author's page). If one cannot be had, the lesson audits two
  and says in References which it could not read — an audit lesson that
  cites unread papers has failed its own test.
- **Both citers' texts must be read.** The Response is in the dossier; the
  op-ed and Taylor are not. Without Kendhammer's sentences the lesson cannot
  state what he cited each paper for; "as the Response says Kendhammer said"
  is the lazy citation the lesson is about. Obtain the op-ed.
- **Naming.** Gilley, Kendhammer and Taylor are the authors of the texts under
  examination and may be named in the body as such (L8a(b)); the studies are
  named by their authors' names as the names of the papers (Nunn and
  Wantchekon's study), as the previous lesson named Abernethy's test. Nobody
  is cited as an authority: every sentence about a paper reports what the
  paper says at a stated place.
- **Verify every quotation the Response attributes to a paper against the
  paper.** The Moradi sentences, the Frankema–van Waijenburg sentences and
  the "one to two percent" figure are the audit's object; the brief reports
  them as the Response gives them, not as verified.
- **No verdict on colonialism.** The lesson's finding is about compressions
  and their direction. "Gilley was right" or "the critics were right" is the
  conclusion the drill forbids; "the op-ed moved the period; the Response
  moved the magnitude" is the kind of sentence it allows, once verified.
- **Dates:** to the decade except 2005 (the survey round), 1880–1965 and
  1400–1900 (the studies' own periods), 2009/2011/2012 (publication), 2017 and
  2022 (the dispute).
- **Do not re-teach** the balance-sheet lesson's three failures or Waldron's
  models; one `<Ref>` clause each where the audit touches them.
- **Dossier gap, by design:** none of the three studies, neither critic's
  text. The brief gives full citations; the lesson cannot be written from the
  dossier alone.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (E3, the one warm-up).** Name the three failures the Case for
   Colonialism charges its critics with (`<Ref>` colonial-balance-sheet) and
   say which of this lesson's three claims bears on which failure.
2. **Document reasoning — a fourth citation drilled.** From the Response's
   "Health and Education": Cappelli and Baten's 284 country-decades, 1730–1970,
   British colonialism positive on human capital, others neutral. Fill in the
   drill card from the working paper's abstract (CEPR DP 11273, 2016 — obtain):
   what "human capital" is measured by (age-heaping as a numeracy proxy —
   verify), on what data, against what comparison; then state what the finding
   can and cannot be used to establish.
3. **Boundary probe.** Construct a case where a cause has a large average

# Research brief: augustine-two-cities (Augustine's two cities and the earthly peace)

status: planned
module: rome-and-christianity
type: core
goal: Analyze Augustine's account of the two cities and his verdict that no earthly commonwealth can be just, and weigh what it leaves to politics

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Scipio's definition — a people is a multitude bound by
  agreement about right and shared advantage — Laelius's natural law with God
  as its author, a tyrant's city as no commonwealth, Cicero's "we retain only
  the form of the commonwealth, but have long since lost its substance"
  (Rep. V.1–2, which Augustine himself quotes in II.21), and the Roman
  justification of empire by the ladder of rule (God over man, mind over
  body, master over slave) (all `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`);
  Polybius's forecast that Rome would decay through wealth and flattery of
  the people, and his functional reading of Roman religion
  (`polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`, VI.56–57) — the pagan charge of 410 is
  the same functional reading turned against the Christians; Aristotle's natural slavery and its admitted
  difficulty (`aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`); the struggle
  for representation, Varro's "the city is prior to its gods", Eusebius's one
  emperor for one God and its collapse, so that the question "what truth does
  Roman society now represent?" stands open (`constantine-and-representation`).
- **Contributes:** One move: mankind is sorted not by city or nation but by
  what it loves — two loves make two cities, intermingled until the end — and
  from that sorting the whole of politics is re-described: the lust of ruling
  is the earthly city's engine (Cain, Romulus), virtue is rightly ordered
  love, slavery is the wage of sin not of nature, Rome by Scipio's own
  definition never was a republic, and a people is re-defined by the objects
  of its love so that Rome can be a people after all — a lower one; what is
  left to rulers is the earthly peace, which the pilgrim city uses and prays
  for without calling it justice.
- **Hands off:** Three doors. (1) The test case: a Christian emperor and a
  pagan senator in 384 — does Augustine's "the heavenly city preserves the
  diverse laws and customs of the nations" describe what Ambrose actually
  did? (`altar-of-victory`). (2) The verdict "pagan virtue is splendid vice"
  is the judgment Machiavelli will reverse — Discourses II.2's charge that
  this teaching "made the world feebler" is the objection carried here and
  answered in `machiavelli-effectual-truth` / `machiavelli-discourses-republic`.
  (3) Aquinas will put justice back into the earthly city by way of natural
  law (`aquinas-law-and-kingship`). Plant XIX.17, XIX.24 and XIX.25 as the
  three sentences later lessons quote.
- **Spine step:** "A Jewish movement becomes Europe's religion by being remade
  in Greek concepts and Roman forms; Constantine's empire and the Church fight
  over who represents the truth; Augustine denies that any earthly city can be
  just"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: Rome sacked by Goths, pagans
blaming the new religion; a North African bishop answers with a history of
the whole human race as two cities, one built on love of self and one on love
of God, mixed until the end; on the way he takes Cicero's definition of a
republic and shows that by its own terms Rome never was one.

1. **What does the sack of Rome prove, and to whom?** The obvious attempt:
   Rome fell because it abandoned the gods that had protected it — the pagan
   charge of 410, which is exactly Celsus's prediction come true (`<Ref>`
   `constantine-and-representation`: a universal creed dissolves the national
   cults the empire is built from). The Eusebian Christian is in the worse
   position: if the Christian empire was God's kingdom arriving, 410 refutes
   it. Augustine's answer refuses both frames: Rome's fortunes prove nothing
   about God, because Rome was never the kind of thing whose fortunes could.
   Anchors: Alaric and the Goths, 24 August 410, three days; Augustine
   (354–430), bishop of Hippo on the African coast (modern Annaba, Algeria),
   writing the twenty-two books between 413 and 426; the pagan senatorial
   party who blamed the ban on the old rites (the Altar of Victory had been
   refused in 384 — forward clause). The lesson's register: this is a
   livre de circonstance that became a theory of history.
2. **How can the whole human race be two cities?** Not by birth, place or
   law — the reader's three inherited ways of sorting men (Greek/barbarian,
   citizen/alien, Roman/provincial) — but by love. The derivation runs
   through psychology first (XIV.6–9): the passions are acts of will, and
   the will is love — desire, joy, fear and grief are love seeking,
   enjoying, fleeing, suffering. So a man is what he loves. Then the first
   sin (XIV.13): pride, the will's turning from the unchangeable good to
   itself — "the very lifting up was already a fall". Then the sorting
   (XIV.28, `<Quote>`): two cities formed by two loves, self to the contempt
   of God, God to the contempt of self; "in the one, the princes and the
   nations it subdues are ruled by the love of ruling; in the other, the
   princes and the subjects serve one another in love". Because: if the
   passions are loves and love has an object, mankind divides by its
   ultimate object into exactly two — and the two are not visible, since
   "the city of God ran its course not in light but in shadow" and the
   Church itself is a dragnet with bad fish in it (XVIII.49). Define the
   concept `two-cities` here, with the caution the text itself gives: the
   earthly city is not the state and the heavenly city is not the Church.
3. **Why does the earthly city begin with a murder?** Cain founded a city;
   Abel "being a sojourner, built none" (XV.1); Romulus killed Remus (XV.5).
   The obvious reading is a moral: founders are murderers. Augustine's point
   is structural (XV.5): rule and glory are *diminished by sharing*, goodness
   is *increased* by it — so a city built on the love of ruling must be
   divided against itself from its first act. Name the engine: **libido
   dominandi**, the lust of ruling, which "lays waste men's hearts with the
   most ruthless dominion" (XIX.15) and "as civil wars bear witness" rules
   the soul of tyrants (XIV.15). Then the fairest paragraph (XV.4): the
   earthly city "desires earthly peace" and wages war to get it; its goods —
   victory, peace, prosperity — "are good things, and without doubt the gifts
   of God"; its fault is to love them as the only goods. And its religion
   (XV.7): the earthly city worships gods to help it rule — "the good use the
   world that they may enjoy God; the wicked would fain use God that they may
   enjoy the world". The reader should hear Varro's "the city is prior to its
   gods" (`<Ref>`) turned from a description into an indictment.
4. **Then what is virtue, if the passions are not to be killed?** The Stoic
   sage (the reader has Laelius's Stoic law; one clause) feels nothing;
   Augustine says this is not health but numbness: "a thing is not right
   because inflexible, nor healthy because insensible"; Christ wept (XIV.9).
   **Ordo amoris**: "virtue is the order of love" (XV.22) — the miser's gold
   is good; the fault is in the man who loves it out of order. Because:
   if love is the will, virtue cannot be the absence of will but its right
   direction; a passion is good or bad by its object and its rank. This
   link is short but load-bearing: it is why Augustine can grant the earthly
   city real goods (link 3) and still deny it justice (link 6).
5. **Slavery, rule and the household — is hierarchy natural?** The reader
   arrives with Aristotle's natural slave (`<Ref>`) and Cicero's ladder
   (`<Ref>` `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`: God over man, mind over
   body, master over slave, Rome over provinces). Augustine takes the ladder and cuts it
   (XIX.15): God gave man dominion "not man over man, but man over the
   beasts"; the righteous were shepherds of cattle, not kings of men;
   slavery "is a name introduced by sin and not by nature" — the wage of sin,
   just as a punishment, never as a nature; and "it is a happier thing to be
   the slave of a man than of a lust". Then the inversion of rule (XIX.14):
   "even those who rule serve those whom they seem to command"; "he who loves
   to govern rather than to do good is no bishop" (XIX.19). Because: if the
   only natural dominion is over beasts, every rule of man over man is
   post-lapsarian — a remedy for disorder, not the expression of a natural
   rank. State the consequence the reader will feel: Aristotle's difficulty
   (how do you tell a natural slave?) is dissolved by denying there is one;
   the price is that *every* master is ruling by a title sin created.
6. **Scipio's definition tested: Rome never was a republic.** Now the set
   piece the module has been building toward (XIX.21). Scipio's definition
   (`<Ref>`): a people is a multitude bound by agreement about right and
   shared advantage. Augustine's syllogism, six steps: a republic is the good
   of a people → a people is bound by agreement about right → where there is
   no justice there is no right ("the unjust inventions of men are neither to
   be considered nor spoken of as rights") → justice gives each his due →
   God is owed worship, and Rome gave it to demons → so no justice, no right,
   no people, no republic — "only some promiscuous multitude unworthy of the
   name of people". Then Augustine's use of Cicero's own Book III: Philus's
   case that empire needs injustice, Laelius's reply that servitude benefits
   the provincials "as God rules man, the soul the body, reason the passions"
   — and the turn: "when a man does not serve God, what justice can we
   ascribe to him, since his soul cannot exercise a just control over the
   body?" The ladder needs God at the top to hold at any rung; Rome removed
   God, so every rung is injustice. The lesson must show the reader that
   Augustine is not importing a Christian premise but using Laelius's
   ("God is the author of this law") against Scipio.
7. **OBJECTIONS at full strength — two from outside, one from inside.**
   (a) *The Roman's* (as the course's own, from Voegelin III.4 and Cicero):
   Augustine could not understand Varro because Varro's gods were never meant
   to be true in Augustine's sense; the city instituted them as the city's
   own self-representation, and a definition of justice that requires
   worship of the true God was not Scipio's — Scipio's *ius* was the agreed
   right of Roman citizens. Augustine has changed the meaning of "right" and
   then found Rome wanting by it. (b) *The worldly charge*, first in the form the pagans of 410 meant it
   (as the course's own): a religion that enrols its believers in a higher
   country gives them two citizenships, and the city's unconditional claim
   on their devotion is weakened — the political, not the superstitious,
   version of "Rome fell because it left its gods", and Polybius's
   religion-as-device seen from the losing side (`<Ref>`
   `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`). Then its sharpest later statement
   (Machiavelli, Discourses II.2, `<Quote>` short): a religion that glorifies "men of a
   humble and contemplative, rather than of an active life" and makes men
   "brave to suffer rather than to do" has "made the world feebler, and
   given it over as a prey to wicked men". Augustine's available reply: the
   judge takes the bench though he may torture the innocent (XIX.6); the
   just war is fought, and lamented (XIX.7); the earthly city's goods are
   real and the pilgrim obeys its laws (XIX.17) — nothing in the two cities
   forbids the active life; it forbids calling it justice. Whether that
   suffices is left to the reader; Machiavelli's own qualification (the
   fault is of the *interpreters*) is quoted too. (c) *The internal one*
   (course.yaml note): see link 8.
8. **The second definition: what Rome was, if not a republic.** XIX.24: "if
   we discard this definition… a people is an assemblage of reasonable
   beings bound together by a common agreement as to the objects of their
   love" — then "the Roman people is a people, and its good is without doubt
   a commonwealth", and so are Athens, Egypt, Babylon; peoples are ranked
   "superior in proportion as [bound] by higher interests". Parse what
   changed: *ius* dropped, *love* substituted, justice turned from a
   condition of existence into a measure of rank. Then the internal
   objection at full strength: if Rome is a people and a republic by the
   second definition, what work is "justice" now doing? Augustine's own
   answer is in the same chapter's last sentence — the ungodly city "is void
   of true justice" — and in XIX.25: its virtues "are rather vices than
   virtues… inflated with pride" (**splendid vices**, the course's name;
   note that the Latin phrase *splendida vitia* is not Augustine's own words
   but the tradition's summary of this chapter — say so). So justice has
   moved from "what makes a people" to "what a people lacks", and the
   earthly city is now describable without being justified. The reader
   should be able to say which definition does which job.
9. **What is left to politics — the earthly peace.** XIX.12–13: "every man
   seeks peace by waging war, but no man seeks war by making peace"; even the
   robber chief keeps peace with his band and "would lift his head in open
   day as a king" if a city submitted; peace is "the tranquillity of order",
   order "the distribution which allots things equal and unequal, each to its
   own place"; the ladder of peaces from the body to the heavenly city. Then
   XIX.17 (`<Quote>`): the two cities share the use of earthly peace but not
   the laws of religion; the heavenly city "calls citizens out of all
   nations… not scrupling about diversities in the manners, laws, and
   institutions whereby earthly peace is secured… so long only as no
   hindrance to the worship of the one supreme and true God is thus
   introduced". And XIX.26: "we also enjoy the peace of Babylon" and pray for
   it. Because: a city that cannot be just can still be *ordered*, and order
   is a real good the pilgrim needs; so rulers are left everything except
   the claim that their rule is justice — and the one condition, "so long
   only as", is where the next lesson's test begins. State the seam: from
   "no earthly city is just" one expects withdrawal; the record (Augustine
   the bishop, the judge on the bench, the emperor admonished) shows the
   opposite — the heavenly city in politics up to its elbows, denying only
   that politics saves.
10. **Close:** the reader can now run the two definitions, name the engine
    and the measure (libido dominandi, ordo amoris), state why slavery is
    penal, and say what Augustine leaves to rulers. Open problem: a bishop
    who has just read XIX.17 is handed a petition to restore a pagan altar
    "whereby earthly peace is secured" — does he preserve the diversity or
    call it a hindrance to worship? That is 384, next lesson, and it
    happened before Augustine wrote.

loves, two cities"; "The earthly city begins with a murder: the lust of

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **Cain and Romulus** (XV.1, XV.5; book15 lines ~8, 28): the two founding
   murders set side by side, then the principle — rule shrinks when shared,
   goodness grows when shared — as the test that separates the two cities'
   goods. Recreate as a two-column comparison.
2. **"Two cities have been formed by two loves"** (XIV.28): the full sentence
   in a `<Quote>`, then a four-row table from the same chapter — love,
   glory, rule, wisdom — earthly vs heavenly (summary 17 `figures`: "table
   contrasting the two cities by love, glory, rule, wisdom").
3. **The judge who tortures the innocent** (XIX.6; book19 line 42–44): the
   accused is tortured to find whether he is guilty, confesses falsely, is
   executed innocent; "will a wise judge take his seat on the bench or no?
   Beyond question he will" — the tragic necessity of office. The lesson's
   answer-in-advance to Machiavelli.
4. **The robber chief** (XIX.12; line 68): peace with his comrades, with his
   household, with his own body (Cacus); "if a city submitted… he would lift
   his head in open day as a king". Then "pride apes God". A worked example
   that even perversion seeks peace — used to derive "peace is the
   tranquillity of order".
5. **The ladder of peaces** (XIX.13; line 74–78): body → irrational soul →
   rational soul → body and soul → man and God → man and man → household →
   city → heavenly city; "the tranquillity of order". Draw it; it is the
   course's hierarchy diagram in Augustine's hands.
6. **Scipio's definition tested — the six-step syllogism** (XIX.21; line
   110–112) with Cicero's text beside it (`<Ref>`): republic → people → right
   → justice → due to God → Rome worshipped demons → no republic. Then the
   Philus/Laelius exchange as Augustine reports it, and the turn.
7. **The two definitions side by side** (XIX.21 vs XIX.24; lines 110, 130):
   Scipio's (right + advantage) and Augustine's (common objects of love);
   where Rome falls under each; what "justice" does in each (summary 17
   figures: "two definitions of people/republic compared").
8. **Machiavelli's charge** (Discourses II.2; discourses.txt 5925–5977):
   quote "brave to suffer rather than to do" and "made the world feebler";
   then his own hedge about the interpreters; then Augustine XIX.6–7 read as
   a reply. One case, argued both ways.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know — anchor at first mention:** Goths and
  Alaric (a Germanic people in Roman service, whose king sacked Rome in 410);
  Hippo (Hippo Regius, a port in Roman Africa; Augustine its bishop 395–430);
  Manichees, Donatists (if mentioned at all — one clause; better omitted);
  Stoic apatheia (freedom from passion as the sage's ideal — the reader has
  Stoics only as one clause from Laelius); Pelagius (omit); Cain and Abel,
  Romulus and Remus (one half-sentence each — the reader may not know
  Genesis 4); Babel/Babylon ("confusion"; the Mesopotamian city Scripture
  makes the type of the proud city); Jeremiah 29:7 ("seek the peace of the
  city" — the exiles told to pray for Babylon); the dragnet (Matthew 13:47:
  the kingdom as a net full of good and bad fish — the visible Church is not
  the city of God); eschatological / the "last judgment" (say "the end of
  time" and leave the Greek); Varro's 288 sects (a combinatorial list, not
  real schools); pilgrim/sojourner (peregrinus: a resident alien, not a
  traveller — the technical sense matters); demons (Augustine's word for the
  pagan gods: real spirits, wicked; the reader must know he means it
  literally); Eusebius (`<Ref>` previous lesson); Orosius (Augustine's pupil,
  History Against the Pagans, 417 — only if the Lilla irony is used).
- **Register.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Do not re-teach** Polybius's forecast and religion-as-device, Scipio's
  definition, Laelius's natural law, Aristotle's
  natural slavery, Eusebius or Varro: each is a `<Ref>` plus one clause. The
  lesson's labour is Augustine's use of them.
- **The two cities are not Church and state.** The commonest misreading (L13
  — a real one): Augustine says the city of God "ran its course in shadow",
  the Church is a dragnet with the reprobate in it (XVIII.49), and the
  earthly city includes bad Christians. Refute once, explicitly, with XVIII.49
  and XIX.17 ("the part of it which sojourns on earth").
- **"Splendid vices"** is the tradition's phrase, not a quotation of XIX.25;
  say so when introducing the concept id `splendid-vices`.
- **Dods's archaic prose**: quote short; paraphrase the long periods;
  "obnoxious" in XIX.17 means "exposed to", not "offensive" — gloss it or
  avoid it.
- **Books I–XIII and XX–XXII were not read by the swarm**: the 410 opening,
  II.21's promise and V.24–26 (the happy emperor) are cited from New Advent
  only at those points; say nothing else about them.
- **Where the summary and text differ:** summary 17's "line" pointers are
  chapter-lines of the New Advent flattenings; XIV.15 (lust of ruling) is at
  book14 line 132, XIV.28 at 190; XV.1 is at book15 line 12, XV.5 at 28,
  XV.7 at 36, XV.8 at 44, XV.22 at 130; XVIII.2 at book18a line 16, XVIII.24
  at 186; book19 chapter lines are listed in this brief. Verify by grep
  before citing a line.
- **Augustine on empire** (XVIII.22: Rome brought the world "into one
  fellowship of government and laws"; XIX.7: the Latin language imposed "as
  a bond of peace" at the price of wars) is two-sided; present both halves
  and do not make him either an imperialist or an anti-imperialist.
- **Do not moralise** the pagan virtue question in either direction: the
  verdict is Augustine's; Machiavelli's reversal is coming; the reader
  weighs.
- **Numbers:** keepers — 410 (the sack), 413–426 (composition), the six
  steps of XIX.21. Everything else to the decade.
- **Names only in References:** Dods (translator), Voegelin (except as the
  author of "representation" if the term is recalled — then a `<Ref>` to the
  previous lesson, not a name), Lilla, Strauss, Fortin, Harnack (here a
  scholar), Adamson, Orosius (may be named as an object in the Lilla irony:
  "a pupil of Augustine's wrote a history proving life had improved since
  Christ" — the course's own sentence).

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (warm-up, the only one).* Write both definitions of a people
   from memory and, for each, state in one clause where Rome falls and why.
2. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill on XIX.21.* Metadata: a bishop in
   Roman Africa, c. 425, writing against pagans who blame Christianity for
   410, quoting a pagan statesman's dialogue of 54 BC. Predict what such a
   writer will take from Cicero and what he will omit; then read; then: what
   does XIX.21 record (Cicero's text, otherwise lost), what does it argue,
   what does it omit (Scipio's own sense of *ius*)? What can the chapter be
   USED to establish about Cicero, and what only about Augustine?
3. *Boundary probe.* Take the second definition and set the "reasonable
   beings" clause aside: a band of robbers agrees on the objects of its love
   (XIX.12). Is it a people? Augustine's own answer in the chapter ("if only
   it is an assemblage of reasonable beings and not of beasts"); what work the
   clause does; and whether the robber chief's would-be kingdom passes.
4. *Claim to refute.* "Augustine's two cities are the Church and the Roman
   state." Name the failing step with XVIII.49 (the dragnet) and XIX.17 ("the
   part of it which sojourns on earth"); state what the two cities are sorted
   by, and give one member of each who would be misfiled by the false claim.
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* From "virtue is the order of love"
   (XV.22) and "every man seeks peace by waging war" (XIX.12), derive
   Augustine's position on a *just war* without reading XIX.7 — what must he
   say about its cause, its conduct and the victor's feeling? Then check
   against XIX.7 ("if he remembers that he is a man") and against Cicero's just-war clauses (`<Ref>`
   `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`): what did Augustine keep,
   and what did he add?
6. *Synthesis — counter-argument stem.* Someone claims (Machiavelli,
   Discourses II.2): "This teaching made the world feeble and handed it to
   wicked men." Name two facts in Augustine's text the claim fails to account
   for (the judge on the bench, XIX.6; the earthly city's goods as "gifts of
   God", XV.4) and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive
   (that calling political goods "not justice" in fact weakens the will to
   defend them — an empirical claim about believers, which the course will
   test in the Machiavelli lessons).
7. **FORWARD (E7)** → `altar-of-victory`. XIX.17 says the heavenly city
   preserves the nations' diverse laws and institutions "so long only as no
   hindrance to the worship of the one supreme and true God is thus
   introduced". A pagan senator petitions a Christian emperor to restore a
   state-funded altar and the priestly stipends the state has always paid.
   Using XIX.17 alone, write the two opposite rulings a bishop could derive
   (preserve a custom "whereby earthly peace is secured"; refuse a
   "hindrance to worship") and name the single question of fact that decides
   between them. The solution says: in 384 a bishop of Milan derived one of
   them, in two letters the next lesson reads.
8. *Document reasoning — change and continuity (E3 reach-back).* Aristotle's
   natural slave (`<Ref>`), Cicero's "unjust slavery when men are slaves who
   could govern themselves" (`<Ref>`), Augustine's "a name introduced by sin
   and not by nature": for each, what changed in the *ground* of slavery, and
   what persisted in the *practice* each author accepts? One sentence per
   author; then the pattern.

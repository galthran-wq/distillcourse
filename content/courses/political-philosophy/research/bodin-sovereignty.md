# Research brief: bodin-sovereignty (Bodin's sovereignty: the commonwealth of two religions)

status: planned
module: christendom-to-sovereignty
type: core
goal: Analyze Bodin's definition of sovereignty and its attributes, and explain why he rejects the mixed constitution and any right of resistance

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: law as an ordinance of reason by one who has
  "care of the community"; the king bound by natural law; deposition by the public authority
  that appointed him; the unnamed judge of kings (pope, multitude, superior) — the problem this
  lesson's "new question" dissolves. From `machiavelli-effectual-truth` and
  `machiavelli-discourses-republic`: the effectual-truth standard; treaties kept only while
  useful; fear safer than love; Rome as a mixture made by tumult. From
  `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` and `cicero-mixed-constitution`: the six regimes; polity;
  Rome as the mixed constitution of consuls, senate and tribunes (Scipio's case). From
  `platos-laws-second-best-city` (extra — Ref only): Sparta as the first mixture. From
  `constantine-and-representation` and `altar-of-victory`: the empire and the Church contesting
  who represents society — the medieval question in its late-antique form.
- **Contributes:** The one move: when two religions each claim the Church's authority to bind a
  king, "who are the rulers and what are their powers" has two answers and no decision
  procedure; Bodin asks instead "what is a state", answers "families under a sovereign power",
  and defines that power so sharply — absolute, perpetual, marked above all by making law for
  all without their consent — that a mixed state becomes a contradiction, a subject who judges
  the sovereign becomes a contradiction, and toleration of two religions becomes a policy of the
  state's survival rather than a concession to conscience. The lesson reconstructs the
  incommunicability argument and tests it on Rome, Venice and England as Bodin did.
- **Hands off:** Two problems stay open. (i) Bodin's sovereign holds his power from God,
  modelled on the father, and commonwealths begin in force (I.6) — so WHY would equals ever
  submit, and what makes force into obligation? `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` derives
  the sovereign from the subjects' own fear and consent. (ii) Bodin binds the sovereign by
  covenants and property yet lets no subject judge him — the unresolved tension between right
  and fact (a tyranny counts as a commonwealth though the definition says "rightly ordered");
  `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty` removes the limits, `english-revolution-as-test` watches a
  kingdom whose two bodies each claim to make law. The one-sentence forward hook to the crisis
  module: the twentieth-century jurist who will say Bodin's decisive passage is not "absolute
  and perpetual" but the marks of sovereignty in I.10, because they settle who decides when the
  law is suspended (`schmitt-political-theology`).
- **Spine step:** "Wars of religion force a new answer: one sovereign power above every faction,
  bound by no law of its own making — Bodin's definition, Hobbes's derivation from fear, and the
  English Revolution as the test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): France in 1576 — two religions, an unpaid army, a generation of
pamphlets on killing tyrants; a lawyer and royal deputy asks a new question and coins a new
word. Anchor (L11) at once: Jean Bodin (c. 1530–1596), a lawyer of Angers, Toulouse and Paris;
the Six Books of the Commonwealth (Six livres de la République, 1576); the Wars of Religion —
the civil wars between French Catholics and Protestants (Huguenots) that began in 1562 and ran
to 1598, the St Bartholomew's Day massacre of Huguenots in Paris (August 1572) four years before
the book; the Estates of Blois (December 1576), the assembly of the three orders — clergy,
nobility and "third estate" of commoners — at which Bodin sat for the Vermandois and refused the
king the money for a new war against the Protestants. The pamphlets are the Huguenot resistance
tracts of 1573–79 arguing that lesser magistrates may resist a king who breaks his compact with
the people. The newcomer's problem: every answer the reader has met — Aquinas's public
authority, the pope, the estates, the mixed constitution — gives a party to the war a title to
act.

1. **Why can the old question no longer be answered?** Obvious attempt: ask who the rightful
   rulers are and what powers they hold (the question every author from Aristotle to Aquinas
   asked, Ref). Why it fails in 1576: the two religions each claim the Church's authority to
   bind the king, so "who are the rulers" returns two answers and the question has no judge; the
   king owes the Protestants protection and the Catholics orthodoxy. Answer: ask instead what a
   state IS. The definition (I.1): "the rightly ordered government of a number of families, and
   of those things which are their common concern, by a sovereign power" — three elements
   (families, common concern, sovereign power); "happy" is not essential (Marseilles was poor
   and beset yet best ordered); the end must be known before the means (the bad archer). Then
   why the family and not the individual is the unit (I.2–5), and why force, not virtue, founded
   the first states (I.6 — Nimrod "the mighty hunter"). Seam: Aquinas placed the ruler inside a
   hierarchy of ends with the Church at the top (Ref); Bodin's definition has no place above the
   sovereign power.
2. **What makes a heap of families ONE state?** Obvious attempt: shared place, laws, religion.
   Fails: Ragusa is a state and the Swiss leagues are not, though the Swiss share more; "a ship
   is no more than a load of timber unless there is a keel" (I.2 l.1755); three households under
   a sovereign are as much a commonwealth as the Turkish empire ("the mouse and the elephant",
   Ragusa, l.1764). Answer: SOVEREIGNTY (I.8) — "that absolute and perpetual power vested in a
   commonwealth … majestas", which "no jurist or political philosopher has in fact attempted to
   define". Then the two adjectives derived as answers: PERPETUAL, because a power given for a
   term returns, so the holder was a lieutenant — the Roman dictator, the regent (King John of
   France ratifying every act of his son's regency on his return from English captivity, so the
   acts needed ratifying); ABSOLUTE, because whoever can limit the power in competence is the
   real sovereign — yet "absolute" does NOT mean free of all law: above positive law (his own
   and his predecessors'), bound by the law of God and of nature, by the fundamental laws of the
   realm (the Salic law of succession), by his covenants with subjects and other princes (a
   covenant binds both parties), and by subjects' property — he may not tax without consent
   (Comines at Tours 1484; Dionysius to his mother: he could exempt her from Syracuse's laws but
   not from God's and nature's). The sovereign cannot bind himself by his own law, because no
   obligation arises from the promisor's will alone — hence French edicts end "for such is our
   good pleasure".
3. **How do you TELL who is sovereign?** Obvious attempt: the one who judges, appoints officials
   and sits in council (the reader's Aristotle: deliberative, offices, courts). Fails (I.10):
   all three are delegated every day to men who remain subjects — jurisdiction most of all; a
   mark of sovereignty must be INCOMMUNICABLE, something that, given to a subject, makes him no
   longer a subject (as God cannot create another God). Answer: "the principal mark of sovereign
   majesty and absolute power is the right to impose laws generally on all subjects regardless
   of their consent" (l.2696) — law as COMMAND (distinguished from right/equity, and from
   custom, which grows by the prince's sufferance and which law can break but not conversely:
   "custom is king, law is tyrant"); and every other mark is contained in it — war and peace,
   the appointment of great officers, final appeal and pardon, faith and homage, coinage,
   taxation. These are inalienable and imprescriptible like the public domain: never granted to
   a subject or a foreigner.
4. **Then can sovereignty be shared? The mixed state reconstructed and refuted (II.1).** Obvious
   attempt, at full strength and from the reader's own lessons: Rome mixed consuls (kingly),
   senate (aristocratic) and people (democratic), and the mixture's mutual checks made it stable
   for centuries (Ref `cicero-mixed-constitution`; `machiavelli-discourses-republic`); Venice
   does the same with Doge, Senate and Great Council; "Contarini, Machiavelli, and many others
   have held the same opinion … it goes back … to Herodotus". Bodin's argument, step by step:
   (a) the marks are indivisible (link 3); (b) whoever makes law for all the rest is sovereign;
   (c) power exercised by an official is held in trust and remains the grantor's; therefore (d)
   ROME: the consuls could not make law, declare war or peace, appoint officials, pardon, or
   spend public money — "the Constable in this realm and the great Pasha in Turkey have ten
   times the power of the two Consuls put together, yet they are no more than the subjects and
   slaves of the prince"; the senate's decisions "had no force unless confirmed by the people";
   the censors who chose senators were themselves elected by the people — so "the Roman
   constitution, from the moment that the kings were expelled, was popular" (except the two
   years of the Decemvirate); (e) VENICE: Contarini himself says the Great Council makes law —
   but the Great Council is a small hereditary minority of nobles, so Venice is an aristocracy,
   "condemned out of his own mouth"; (f) FRANCE and ENGLAND: the estates and Parliament kneel,
   petition and counsel; their consent to taxation is a covenant the king has made, not a share
   in making law — so monarchies; to call France a mixture is treasonable; (g) the one real case
   — a commonwealth where two bodies genuinely share the marks (Denmark, Sweden) — is not a
   mixture but a war waiting to be fought, "only arms could resolve" it. Objection answered in
   the text: the stability the mixed-state authors prized is real, but it belongs to the FORM OF
   GOVERNMENT, not of state (link 5).
5. **Form of state vs form of government (II.2, II.7, VI.4).** The distinction as the answer to
   "then where did Rome's balance go?": WHERE sovereignty lies (one / few / many — always
   simple) vs HOW it is exercised (royally — subjects obey the prince, the prince obeys God and
   nature, liberty and property secured; despotically — lord of persons and goods by just
   conquest; tyrannically — natural law set at naught; and by whether offices are distributed to
   all or to a few). Rome: "a democracy governed aristocratically" (l.1043–1079); France,
   England, Spain: "monarchies governed democratically" where the prince "distributes lands,
   magistracies" across estates (l.3616) — and that, says Bodin, is the most stable arrangement
   there is. So the mixed constitution survives as a description of government and dies as a
   description of sovereignty. Headline the 3×3 grid.
6. **Is a tyrant a king, and may he be killed?** Obvious attempt: Aquinas's (Ref) — a multitude
   that appointed its king may depose him; the resistance tracts' — lesser magistrates may
   resist on the people's behalf. Bodin's king-vs-tyrant list (II.4: ~20 paired traits; tyranny
   is a matter of degree; "no prince lacks a vice nor tyrant a virtue") grants the distinction
   and then refuses every consequence (II.5): "the subject is never justified in any
   circumstances in attempting anything against his sovereign prince, however evil and
   tyrannical he may be"; he may refuse commands against God and nature, but must then "seek
   refuge in flight, go into hiding or suffer death"; a FOREIGN prince may pursue a tyrant by
   arms; a USURPER who has not been accepted may be killed (Solon: after trial if unarmed; the
   Lex Valeria: on sight if he holds the citadel); against a NON-sovereign ruler (the Roman
   emperor, the German emperor as first magistrate of an aristocracy) legal deposition is
   possible. The reason: the prince's person is more sacred than a father's, "ordained and set
   over his subjects by God"; and the reductio — "What a great number of tyrants would be
   discovered if one might kill them": the heavy taxer to the vulgar, the ruler against the
   people's wishes to Aristotle, the man with a bodyguard. Then the OBJECTION at full strength,
   from Bodin's own premises (the resistance tracts' argument, stated as the course's own):
   Bodin says covenants bind both parties and the prince may not tax without consent — so a
   prince who breaks his covenant and taxes without consent has broken a bond that binds HIM;
   what follows for the subject? Bodin: nothing but remonstrance (III.4–5: the magistrate
   refuses what is against God and nature, remonstrates repeatedly, then obeys — or refuses to
   help enact a new iniquitous law at registration) — the covenant binds, but no subject judges
   whether it is broken. The lesson concedes the tension Tooley names, stated as the course's
   own observation: a tyranny is counted a commonwealth though the definition says "rightly
   ordered", while a robber band is not; right and fact never meet.
7. **Two religions in one kingdom.** Obvious attempt (the parties'): enforce the true one. Bodin
   (IV.7): factions ruin every state and worst a popular one; forbid religious DISPUTATION,
   since argument inflames; "prefer toleration to coercion"; "no one can be forced to believe
   against his will" (Theodoric, l.6981); atheism is worse than superstition and persecution
   breeds scepticism, so the state must secure the conditions of SOME piety; the Ottoman sultan
   permits four religions in his seraglio. This is politique toleration — grounded in the
   state's survival and in religion's civic use, not in a right of conscience (the contrast with
   the next module's Locke is a forward clause). Close with V.1 in one paragraph: government
   must suit the people (north by force, middle by justice, south by religion; mountain peoples
   make popular states) — the reader's Aristotle VII.7 (Ref) recast without necessity: "this
   compulsion is not of the order of necessity"; laws and custom reshape nature; converting the
   Swiss cantons to monarchy would be a mistake though monarchy is "absolutely the best". And
   Bodin's own life as the test of his doctrine (Tooley Intro I): he blocked the king's war
   subsidy at Blois in the third estate's name — the estates petitioning — and in 1588 joined
   the Catholic League "out of prudence", an association that stood for everything he condemned.

Headings as claims: "Two religions make 'who are the rulers' unanswerable"; "A commonwealth is
families under a sovereign power — the keel, not the timber"; "Sovereignty is absolute and
perpetual, and bound by God, nature, covenant and property"; "The marks of sovereignty must be
incommunicable: law-making contains the rest"; "There is no mixed state: Rome, Venice and
England re-classified"; "Form of state and form of government: a monarchy governed
democratically"; "A tyrant is still the sovereign: no subject may judge him"; "Two religions:
tolerate, because the state must survive".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The new question** (Tooley Intro II.1, l.418–425): quoted in two sentences — new questions
   mark turning points; the middle ages asked only "who are the rulers and what are their
   powers"; Bodin asked "what is a state and how is it constructed". The lesson's first move;
   state it as the course's own reading with the source in References.
2. **The keel and the load of timber; the mouse and the elephant** (I.2 l.1755–1764): a ship
   without a keel is timber; three households under a sovereign are as much a state as the
   Turkish empire; Ragusa and the Turks. Show why sovereignty, not size or wealth, is the
   essence.
3. **The definition of sovereignty parsed** (I.8 l.2406–2470): "absolute and perpetual";
   perpetual via the dictator and King John's ratification; absolute via its four limits (God
   and nature, fundamental laws, covenants, property); "for such is our good pleasure";
   Dionysius's mother. Worked as a two-column "bound / not bound".
4. **The incommunicability test** (I.10 l.2600–2900): take Aristotle's three parts (counsel,
   offices, justice) and show each handed to a subject who stays a subject; then law-making
   handed to a subject — he is no longer a subject; then nest the other marks inside it (a
   declaration of war is a law; coinage — nummus from nomos; "God cannot create another God").
   The clemency statistic (of a hundred criminals two are tried, one convicted — so pardons are
   dangerous) only as an aside.
5. **Rome re-classified, step by step** (II.1 l.3458–3530): the authorities named; "reason and
   common sense compels me"; the consuls' powers listed and found delegated ("the Constable …
   and the great Pasha have ten times the power"); the senate's decisions needing confirmation;
   the censors elected by the people; the verdict "popular"; then Venice — Contarini "condemned
   out of his own mouth"; then France's kneeling estates. This is the lesson's `<Example>` and
   Figure 5.
6. **"What a great number of tyrants would be discovered if one might kill them"** (II.5
   l.4076–4090): the sentence, the three examples (heavy taxes, against the people's wishes, a
   bodyguard), and the one permission (foreign princes). Pair with Aquinas I.7 (Ref) as the door
   that closes.
7. **The magistrate's dilemma** (III.4–5): mandates against God and nature refused;
   unjust-but-legal orders — remonstrate, repeatedly, then obey; refuse to help register a new
   iniquitous law. "The magistrate is the life of the law." Shows how Bodin keeps a conscience
   inside the state without a right of resistance.
8. **Theodoric: "no one can be forced to believe"** (IV.7 l.6981) with the Ottoman seraglio and
   the ban on disputation — and, as the reader's test of the doctrine against the life, Blois
   1576 (Tooley Intro l.327–360) and the League in 1588.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms and names to anchor (L11):** Huguenots (French Protestants, Calvinist); Wars of
  Religion (1562–98; eight wars; St Bartholomew's Day 1572); the Estates-General and the three
  estates; Blois (1576–77); Parlement of Paris (the high court that registered royal edicts —
  NOT a parliament); "registration" (an edict had force when the Parlement entered it in its
  registers); the politiques (the party of Catholics who put the state's peace before religious
  unity); the Catholic League (1584–98, the militant Catholic party); Salic law (the rule
  excluding women and their lines from the French crown); the royal domain (crown lands and
  rights, inalienable); monarchomachs (the "king-fighters" — the resistance pamphleteers; use
  the word once if at all); majestas; Ragusa (Dubrovnik — a small aristocratic republic);
  Contarini (Venetian patrician whose book on Venice's constitution Bodin dismantles); Constable
  (France's chief military officer); Pasha; the Decemvirate (Ref to the Discourses lesson);
  Comines at Tours 1484; Theodoric (Ostrogothic king of Italy c. 500). Keep three per section.
- **Do not re-teach** Aristotle's regimes or citizen, Cicero's mixed Rome, Machiavelli's Rome,
  or Aquinas's tyranny chapter — recall each with a `<Ref>` and one clause; the
  re-classification in link 4 ASSUMES the reader holds Scipio's picture of Rome and can watch it
  fall.
- **Summary vs text:** Tooley's Introduction gives Bodin's doctrine in Tooley's words; the body
  must quote BOOK text (I.8 l.2406, I.10 l.2696, II.1 l.3458ff, II.5 l.4076ff) for Bodin's
  claims and reserve the Introduction for the "new question" framing and the biography. The
  summary says Bodin bars taxation "without consent" — the text (I.8; VI.2 l.8956–8961) says so,
  but as a consequence of property being natural, not as a share in sovereignty; keep that
  distinction or the mixed-state refutation collapses.
- **The real misreading (L13):** "Bodin's sovereign is above all law." Refute from I.8's four
  limits in the text; "absolute" means above positive law. One slot, no second strawman.
- **Right vs fact:** state the tension (a tyranny is a commonwealth; a robber band is not; the
  definition says "rightly ordered") as the course's own, cite Tooley in References, and do not

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (the only mechanical one):** classify by FORM OF STATE and FORM OF
   GOVERNMENT, with the mark that decides it: Rome after the kings; Venice; the Swiss cantons
   (trick-free: a league, not a state); the Holy Roman Empire (an aristocracy of Electors,
   Princes and Cities with the emperor as first magistrate); France in 1576.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** "What a great number of tyrants would be discovered
   if one might kill them" (II.5). Author (a royal officer and deputy), date (1576, four years
   after the massacre, during the Blois Estates), audience (French readers of both religions),
   what it argues, what it OMITS (the Huguenot case that the king broke his covenant in 1572),
   what it can be USED to establish about the resistance debate of the 1570s and what it cannot
   (whether Bodin thought Charles IX a tyrant).
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** a sovereign grants away, forever, the right to tax — or the right
   to make law for one province — to a subject. By I.8 and I.10, is he still sovereign, is the
   grant valid, and what does the "imprescriptible like the public domain" clause do to the
   grant? Then the degenerate case Bodin names: two bodies each genuinely holding a mark
   (Denmark). The solution shows the definition does not describe such a state — it predicts a
   war.
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Bodin's sovereign is above all law." Name the four limits in I.8
   and the step the claim confuses (positive law with law as such); then state what Bodin does
   NOT give the subject (any judge of whether the limits are kept).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** apply the incommunicability test to England in 1576
   as Bodin saw it — the king makes law? Parliament consents to taxes and petitions — and derive
   his classification (monarchy governed democratically). Then state the single observation that
   would FALSIFY it on Bodin's own terms (Parliament making law for all without the king, or
   commanding the armed forces — IV.1). The solution says in one sentence that this is what the
   application lesson watches for.
6. **Corroboration (E3 from `cicero-mixed-constitution`):** Scipio's Rome (Rep. I–II: the
   mixture as the best regime because of mutual checks) and Bodin's Rome (II.1: popular,
   governed aristocratically) describe the same institutions. Set two short excerpts side by
   side, state what each takes "the people confirm the senate's decrees" to be evidence of, and
   judge which account the facts of the tribunate (Ref `machiavelli-discourses-republic`)
   support better.
7. **Synthesis / counter-argument:** "Bodin only renamed the mixed constitution 'form of
   government' and changed nothing." Name two consequences that DO change (no body may legally
   resist the law-maker; a dispute over who makes law is settled by arms, not adjudicated) and
   state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that no one in France ever claimed
   the estates shared in law-making — which the body says was called treasonable).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`:** Bodin says commonwealths began in
   force (I.6) and that the sovereign's authority is from God and modelled on the father's, "not
   appointed by children". Take away God and the father: state why force alone cannot generate
   an OBLIGATION to obey (a captive obeys while held and no longer), and say what kind of act by
   the subjects themselves would have to replace it. The solution says in one sentence that the
   next lesson derives the sovereign from exactly such an act — a covenant of every man with
   every man — with a `<Ref>`.

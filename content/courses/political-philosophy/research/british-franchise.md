# Research brief: british-franchise (The British franchise 1832–1928 as a test of the democracy critics)

status: planned
module: democracy-and-its-critics
type: core
goal: Apply Tocqueville's law of franchise extension and the fears of Carlyle, Stephen and Arnold to the sequence of British Reform Acts, and judge what the record bears out

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Every position of the module, each by `<Ref>` and one clause: Tocqueville's franchise rule (Vol. I ch. IV) and his remark that aristocracies which see the inevitable bid for the people's goodwill (`equality-of-conditions`); the two dangers and the remedies (`tyranny-of-the-majority`, `soft-despotism`, `art-of-association`); Carlyle's "ballot-boxes alter the figure of our Quack" and "Democracy, which means despair of finding any Heroes" (`hero-worship`); Mill's social tyranny and the `harm-principle`, with Arnold's "doing as one likes" from `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`; Stephen's `coercion-by-opinion`, parliament as "counting heads instead of breaking heads", and his verdict on universal suffrage — power "has changed its shape but not its nature", leaders always rule, and to "what do you propose to substitute?" the answer "Nothing" (`stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity`); Marx's Chartists, the proletariat's "conquest of political power" and measure 2, the graduated income tax (`class-struggle`); and, from the prerequisite `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government`, the `fragility-of-popular-government` with the dated British forecasts this lesson tests — Maine's (1885) that power will pass from the Commons to the Cabinet and from the Cabinet to party committees chosen for "the adroit management of local political business", and that a mass electorate will be conservative rather than radical; Lecky's (1896) that franchise extension is driven by "a competition for power and popularity between rival factions" rather than by demand from below, that graduated taxation is "the supreme danger of democracy", and that "in fifty years' time" the Commons will have lost the power of making and unmaking ministries to a strong executive. From `burke-reflections`: the "arithmetic of 24 million against 200,000" and property in the representation — the aristocratic constitution the Acts dismantled. From `english-revolution-as-test` and `assignats`: the module-application method — a theory's prediction laid against a record, departures named.
- **Contributes:** The module's L16 application. One real artefact — five Acts of Parliament and the share of adults each left with a vote — read against the module's predictions — Tocqueville's, Carlyle's, Arnold's, Stephen's, Marx's, Maine's and Lecky's — most of them made while the Acts were being debated. The lesson judges, prediction by prediction, what the record bears out, and names where the artefact departs from every theory in the module: a landed aristocracy that led the extensions, a ratchet that took a century to run, and a sequel (parties, press, welfare state) that confirms the forecast of soft despotism in one column and refutes it in the next.
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):**
  - *Artefact:* the Representation of the People Acts of 1832, 1867, 1884 (with the 1885 Redistribution Act), 1918 and 1928, and after each the share of the adult population of the United Kingdom entitled to vote. The series is the artefact; the Acts' texts and the Hansard debates are the documents read against it.
  - *Module results used:* `fragility-of-popular-government` (Maine's Cabinet and committee forecast; Lecky's party-competition mechanism, his graduated-taxation forecast and his fifty-year forecast); `coercion-by-opinion` (Stephen: universal suffrage changes the shape of power, not its nature; counting heads); `equality-of-conditions` (Tocqueville's "invariable rule", Vol. I ch. IV, and the Maryland remark); `soft-despotism` and `tyranny-of-the-majority` (Vol. II Pt IV chs. V–VII: what the state absorbs after each extension, and the remedies — associations, press, courts, forms); `hero-worship` (Carlyle, Heroes VI and Past and Present I.iv, III.xiii); `harm-principle` and Arnold's reply (doing as one likes needs the State as best self); `class-struggle` (Marx, Manifesto I and IV: the proletariat's party; III.1: the aristocratic "alms-bag").
  - *Departures to name:* (1) the agents — Whig and Conservative aristocrats (Grey 1832; Peel accepting it in 1834; Derby and Disraeli 1867; Salisbury 1884; Baldwin 1928) extended the vote, not the class below the line and not its party — which fits Lecky's mechanism (party competition) better than Tocqueville's (pressure from below) or Marx's (class struggle); (2) the tempo — Tocqueville's "no stop can be made short of universal suffrage" ran for ninety-six years with four stops of ten to thirty-five years; (3) the sequel — the state grew into schooling, insurance and pensions as Tocqueville foresaw, but so did the counterweights he prescribed (unions, friendly societies, chapels, a free press, the courts, the Lords' veto until 1911), which no one designed; (4) the content — "universal" in 1928 still carried plural votes and university seats until 1948–50, so the series the lesson plots is adults *entitled*, not an equal franchise; (5) the fragility forecast — British popular government did not break down after 1867, 1884, 1918 or 1928, and survived two world wars; Maine and Lecky's forecast was right about where power moved inside it (Cabinet, party machine, a graduated tax, the Lords' veto cut in 1911) and wrong, for Britain, about its survival. Whether the Continent after 1918 bears the forecast out is the next modules' question (`article-48-weimar`), not this lesson's.
- **Hands off:** The lesson decides the module's question for Britain: the critics were right about where power would move inside a mass democracy (Cabinet, party machine, graduated taxation) and wrong that it would break (**strong**); pressure from below forced the question and party competition answered it (**on balance**). Two questions pass on. Whether the fragility forecast holds where the habits Britain had were missing passes to `article-48-weimar`, where it meets a parliament that did fail. Whether the welfare state is Tocqueville's tutelary power is **open** and passes to `deneen-post-liberalism`. The next module opens with `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`, whose crowd theory treats electorates as crowds and the party committee as their master — the forward exercise lands there. `mosca-ruling-class` and `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites` turn the sequel column's party machine into a law (every organisation is ruled by a minority); Spengler's "democracy = plutocracy; the Press makes truth; money decides elections" (`spengler-decline-of-the-west`), Mann's freedom against the vote (`mann-reflections`) and Ortega's "hyperdemocracy" (`ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`) are the later restatements.
- **Spine step:** "Democracy arrives as a fact and is judged: Tocqueville foresees soft despotism, Carlyle demands heroes, Mill defends liberty and Stephen answers him, Marx predicts class war, Maine and Lecky call popular government fragile — and the British franchise tests who was right"

## VERDICT (module verdict, from research/verdicts/REGISTER.md)

**Claim judged.** The module's question: were the nineteenth-century critics right that a mass franchise would endanger liberty, property and stable government? Tested on the one record most of them wrote about: Britain, 1832–1928.

**Verdict.** On the British record the critics were right about how power would move inside a democracy and wrong about stability. Grades by part:
- *Fragility* — **strong** that the forecast failed for Britain. Popular government did not break down after 1867, 1884, 1918 or 1928, and it survived two world wars. Carlyle's forecast was framed so that no outcome could refute it.
- *Power and taxation* — **strong** that the taxation forecast arrived. Maine and Lecky were right that power would pass to the Cabinet and the party machine, and that graduated taxation would come. Lecky's danger arrived (death duties 1894, the 1909 supertax), and his remedy, the Lords' veto, was the institution removed in 1911 to let it through. Whether that was the "catastrophe" he feared is a value judgment, not a finding: his admirers point to the long-run growth of taxation; his critics point to a democracy that taxed the rich and did not break. The lesson states both readings and does not choose.
- *Direction and administration* — Tocqueville was right about the direction (the vote was never taken back once widened) and about the growth of administration (schooling 1870, pensions 1908, national insurance 1911). He was wrong about the tempo: ninety-six years, four pauses.
- *Mechanism* — **on balance**: both accounts are partly borne out. Pressure from below moved the decision in 1832: the Swing riots shifted voters toward pro-reform candidates (Aidt & Franck 2015). In 1866–67 pressure made reform unavoidable. Party competition set the timing and the terms in 1867 and 1884, through aristocratic and Conservative ministries — Lecky's mechanism and Tocqueville's Maryland aside. Pressure from below forced the question; party competition answered it. None of the five Acts fits Marx's mechanism as he stated it.
- *Tutelage* — **open**. Tocqueville's counterweights appeared without a legislator: unions, friendly societies, chapels, a free press, the courts. Several were later absorbed or displaced: friendly societies lost their role as approved societies after the National Insurance Act 1946, and chapel attendance collapsed [secondary: standard history; bind before use]. Whether the welfare state is soft despotism is carried to `deneen-post-liberalism`. What would settle it: evidence on whether associational life and electoral turnover shrank as the state's functions grew.

**What decides it.** The series of five Acts and the share of adults entitled after each (House of Commons Library RP 13/14, bound before any share is written); the governing ministries that passed each Act; the agitations before 1832 and 1866–67, with Aidt & Franck (2015) for 1832; the sequel — the Cabinet's control of legislation, Central Office and the caucus, the 1894 death duties, the 1909 budget, the Parliament Act 1911; and the plain fact that no extension was reversed and no breakdown of popular government followed any of them.

**Strongest objection.** Britain was exceptional — old parliamentary habits, no defeat, no revolution — so one survival does not refute a general claim. This limits the verdict to Britain before 1945; it does not overturn it. The critics made dated forecasts about Britain in particular (Maine 1885, Lecky 1896, Carlyle 1867 if "Shooting Niagara" is used), and those failed. The Continent is tested at Weimar (`article-48-weimar`).

**What would overturn it.** For fragility: a breakdown of British popular government traceable to the widened franchise, or evidence that its survival depended on a restriction the Acts left in place (plural voting, the Lords' delay) rather than on the mass electorate. For the mechanism: a showing that the terms of 1867 and 1884 moved with the agitation, amendment by amendment, rather than with the parliamentary contest — or that 1832 was decided without the pressure Aidt & Franck measure. For tutelage: the evidence named above.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: Tocqueville's "cannot stop short of universal suffrage"; Britain widened the vote in 1832, 1867, 1884, 1918 and 1928, each time after a debate in which the module's critics said what would follow; the five Acts and what they did to the electorate laid against the predictions.

1. **What exactly did Tocqueville predict, and how would we know if he was right?** The obvious test — five extensions, no reversal, prediction confirmed — is too cheap, and the lesson says why. Parse the rule (ll. 986–996) into three claims: a *direction* (once touched, the qualification goes to zero), a *mechanism* (each concession strengthens democracy and multiplies the ambition of those just below the line, "in exact proportion to the great number of those who are above it"), and a *tempo* ("no stop can be made"). Each is testable on different evidence: the direction on the series; the mechanism on *who agitated* before each Act; the tempo on the gaps between Acts. The mechanism has two rivals in the module, each a `<Ref>` and a clause: Marx's class struggle (`marx-manifesto`) and Lecky's party competition — extension "not … the result of any spontaneous demand … but … a competition for power and popularity between rival factions" (`maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government`). Three mechanisms, one record: the record has to separate them. The method is the one `assignats` used on Burke: a forecast is checked, not admired.
2. **The artefact: five Acts and one series.** Give the series as the record, in one figure and one sentence of prose each, every number bound — share of the adult population of the United Kingdom (both sexes, 21 and over) entitled to vote, with the base stated. Approximate shape the writer verifies against the Commons Library paper before writing: before 1832 about one adult in twenty; after 1832 about one in fourteen (roughly one adult man in five in England and Wales — the £10 householder); after 1867 about one in six (roughly one man in three — household suffrage in the boroughs); after 1884 about two in seven (roughly two men in three — the counties brought level); after 1918 about three in four (all men at 21, women at 30 with a property qualification); after 1928 near all (women at 21; plural voting and university seats survive to 1948–50). State the qualification in Carlyle's and Disraeli's own words where they have one ("Tenpound Franchisers"; "household suffrage"). Prose says what the series means: a staircase with four landings, not a slope. Computation only inside source inquiry (link 7 and exercise 3): the share depends on the base chosen, and the base is a decision.
3. **Who extended the vote — and does the mechanism fit?** Here is the first departure, and the lesson must earn it with names and offices. 1832: Earl Grey's Whig ministry, a cabinet of peers, after the Swing riots of 1830, the Birmingham Political Union and the Bristol riots; Macaulay's "reform, that you may preserve". The Swing riots shifted voters toward pro-reform candidates in the 1831 election (Aidt & Franck 2015, References): in 1832 pressure from below moved the decision. 1834: Peel's Tamworth Manifesto accepts the Act for the Conservatives. 1838–48: the Chartists — the men "below the appointed rate" — petition three times with the Six Points and are refused; Kennington Common, April 1848, disperses in the rain two months after the Manifesto named them. 1866–67: the Reform League in Hyde Park (Arnold's railings); then Derby's and Disraeli's *Conservative* government passes household suffrage in the boroughs, outbidding Gladstone — Derby's "leap in the dark"; Lowe's "prevail on our future masters to learn their letters". 1884–85: Gladstone's county franchise, with Salisbury's Conservative Lords trading assent for the Redistribution Act. 1918: a wartime coalition; 1928: Baldwin's Conservative government. So: the direction is Tocqueville's; the pressure is his mechanism in 1832 and 1866–67; the timing and the terms are his *aside* — the Maryland remark that aristocracies who see the inevitable bid for the people's goodwill, so that "the democratic impulse was strongest where aristocracy was firmest". The course's own statement of the British right's choice (Fawcett's thesis, References): after 1830 it chose to "accept parity and win on liberalism's terms"; Salisbury, who in 1862 wrote of "the insane passion for equality", built the mass party that made the widened franchise safe for his side — "concede rather than battle in vain". Then Marx's prediction against the same facts: the proletariat's party (the Chartists) failed; the vote came from above and from the contest between two propertied parties; Marx's own III.1 — the aristocracy waving "the proletarian alms-bag" — described Disraeli better than it mocked him. State both readings: that a bourgeois state conceded under pressure (Marx confirmed), or that a committee of the propertied did what the revolution was to do (Marx refuted). Then the third reading, which the module's last critic offered as a general law: Lecky's — parties extend the vote to win the new voters ("calculations of this kind have been the chief motives of all our recent degradations of the suffrage"; "A weak Conservative Government is often tempted to outbid its rival"). Disraeli's outbidding of Gladstone in 1867 and the Conservative-Liberal bargain of 1884–85 fit Lecky's mechanism best; the agitations before 1832 and 1866–67 fit Tocqueville's; none of the five Acts fits Marx's as he stated it. Pressure from below forced the question; party competition answered it. Exercises 4 and 5 weigh the three.
4. **The tempo: a century, with four stops.** The second departure. From 1832 to 1928 is ninety-six years; the gaps are thirty-five, seventeen, thirty-four and ten. "No stop can be made" was false four times, and each stop was defended in the terms the module has met: Burke's property in the representation (Ref) in 1832; Lowe's and Bagehot's fear for "deference" in 1867; the Lords' veto until the Parliament Act of 1911 — Tocqueville's "forms" doing exactly what Pt IV ch. VII says forms do, giving the weak (here the propertied minority) time to look about. The mechanism's "irritated ambition" is visible before 1832, 1867 and 1918 and almost absent before 1884 and 1928, which came from the governing parties' own calculation. Judgment: the rule holds as a direction, fails as a tempo, and its mechanism explains the pressure but not the timing.
5. **What the critics said would follow — and what did.** Lay each prediction against the sequel, one paragraph each, in the dry register; each paragraph ends on what the record shows, and link 8 adds them up.
   - *Carlyle:* ballot-boxes change the Quack's figure, not his substance; democracy is "despair of finding any Heroes". After 1867 Britain was governed in turn by Gladstone, Disraeli, Salisbury, Lloyd George, Baldwin, Churchill — the exercise asks the reader which of these Carlyle's own criterion (sincerity, recognised by a people able to recognise it) would count as heroes, and notes that the prediction, as stated, no outcome could refute (Ref `carlyle-heroes` link 8). "Shooting Niagara", if used, is the dated version: the 1867 Act as a plunge; what followed was the 1870 Education Act — Lowe's "learn their letters" made law.
   - *Arnold:* "doing as one likes" without deference tends to anarchy unless the State becomes the best self. The anarchy did not come; the State did — compulsory elementary schooling 1870–91, school boards, inspectors (Arnold's own profession). Whether that was culture or "machinery" is a question of value this record cannot settle, and the lesson says so in one clause.
   - *Stephen:* the franchise is "counting heads instead of breaking heads"; the minority obeys because it would lose; and universal suffrage changes the shape of power, not its nature — leaders always rule. Confirmed as description — no extension was resisted by force after 1832, and the sequel column's party machines are his "leaders of one kind or another" — and contested as inference: a society that *can* count heads has already agreed not to break them, which is the liberal's point (Ref `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`). Say plainly that "leaders always rule" is a claim no franchise could refute — its strength and its limit (Ref `stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity`).
   - *Maine (1885):* power will move from the Commons to the Cabinet and from the Cabinet to the party organisation; a mass electorate will be conservative, not radical; popular government is fragile. The first is the confirmation column's party machine (Central Office, the caucus, the whip), and the Cabinet's control of legislation grew; the second is contested — the Conservatives governed for most of 1886–1905 and 1918–45 on the widened franchise, and the same widened electorate returned the governments that legislated pensions and national insurance (elected 1906) and the welfare state (elected 1945) — the record splits, and the lesson says it splits; the third failed for Britain, which Maine himself had partly excepted ("the British Constitution … two centuries"). Ref `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government`.
   - *Lecky (1896):* graduated taxation as "the supreme danger"; a strong upper chamber as the condition of democracy "without catastrophe"; the Commons, "in fifty years' time", stripped of the power to make and unmake ministries. The record, in order: the graduated death duties of 1894 (already passed when he wrote); the 1909 budget's supertax and land taxes, rejected by the Lords; two elections in 1910; the Parliament Act of 1911, which ended the Lords' veto over money bills and cut it to a two-year delay over others — his danger arrived, and his remedy was the institution removed to let it through. The fifty-year forecast (to 1946) is mixed: Commons votes still brought down ministries (January and October 1924), and the Norway debate of May 1940 forced Chamberlain out though he won the division, while the executive's control of the House through party discipline grew. Lecky's forecast that a persistent demand for women's suffrage would triumph (v2 ch. X pp. 457–461 — quote only after locating the sentence) was borne out in 1918 and 1928. Name the forecast that held, the one that failed, the one that is mixed.
   - *Marx:* class struggle drives the extension; the proletariat's party conquers power. The vote tracked industrial class formation (boroughs 1867, counties 1884, the mass army 1918), and the Labour Party held office in 1924 — without the "forcible overthrow" of 1848; the ten measures partly arrived by statute (Ref `marx-manifesto` exercise 5).
   - *Tocqueville:* the second danger. After each extension the state absorbed what Pt IV ch. V listed — schooling (1870), old-age pensions (1908), national insurance (1911), the welfare state (1945–48): "charity, education… savings" — and the parties became machines (Birmingham's caucus in the 1870s; the Primrose League's 1.5 million; Central Office; the manifesto, Tamworth's invention), the press a mass press (stamp duty repealed 1855; the Daily Mail 1896 — "nothing but a newspaper can drop the same thought into a thousand minds"). That is the confirmation column.
6. **The refutation column: the remedies arrived without a legislator.** Here the lesson names the third departure. Tocqueville's ch. VII prescriptions appeared on their own: civil associations (trade unions legalised 1871–75, friendly societies, chapels, the co-operative movement — the "artificial aristocrats" that "cannot be disposed of at pleasure"); a free and multiplied press; independent courts; the forms (the Lords until 1911, the monarchy's "dignified parts"); and elections that changed governments. The soft despotism of ch. VI is "republican in its head and ultra-monarchical in all its other parts"; Britain in 1928 was republican in its head and plural everywhere else. Whether the welfare state is the tutelary power or its opposite (a people providing for itself through its own majority) is the contested claim the lesson states as an argument with both sides' evidence and grades **open**, with what would settle it: whether associational life and electoral turnover shrank as the state's functions grew. Add the later record in one sentence: several counterweights were absorbed or displaced — friendly societies lost their role as approved societies after the National Insurance Act 1946, and chapel attendance collapsed [secondary; bind before use]. `deneen-post-liberalism` takes it up. Tocqueville's own hedge is the fair close: Pt IV ch. IV predicts that England's classes will *reverse* their positions on centralisation; by 1911 they had.
7. **What the series can and cannot establish — computation inside source inquiry.** Three decisions change the numbers: the base (all adults; adult men; England and Wales vs the United Kingdom — Ireland's inclusion lowers every pre-1918 share); the count (registered electors, not eligible adults; plural voters counted once or more); the year (the register lags the Act). Show one recomputation — 1867 on the adult-male base against the all-adult base — and say how much the "lag" claim moves (from "a century" to "sixty years" if women are excluded from the denominator, which is itself a judgment about what "universal" meant in 1867). End on the corroborating non-numeric source: the Charter's Six Points checked off against the Acts — five achieved, one never — as the independent measure of how far 1838's "below the line" got by 1918.
8. **The weighing: right about where power moved, wrong that it would break.** The section sets the module's question against the record and its strongest objection and reaches the verdict. First the parts, each graded. *Fragility* (**strong**): popular government did not break down after 1867, 1884, 1918 or 1928 and survived two world wars; Maine and Lecky's dated forecast about Britain failed, and Carlyle's was framed so that no outcome could refute it. *Power and taxation* (**strong**): power passed to the Cabinet and the party machine, as Maine said; graduated taxation arrived, as Lecky said, and his remedy, the Lords' veto, was removed in 1911 to let it through. Whether that was the "catastrophe" he feared is a value judgment: state both readings (his admirers: the long-run growth of taxation; his critics: a democracy that taxed the rich and did not break) and say the record cannot choose between them. *Direction* (Tocqueville right; never reversed) and *administration* (right: 1870, 1908, 1911); *tempo* wrong (ninety-six years, four stops). *Mechanism* (**on balance**): pressure from below moved 1832 (the Swing riots, Aidt & Franck) and made 1866–67 unavoidable; party competition set the timing and the terms in 1867 and 1884 — pressure forced the question, party competition answered it; Marx's mechanism fits none of the five Acts. Stephen's "leaders always rule": confirmed as description, beyond refutation in its general form. Arnold: the State came, anarchy did not. Then the strongest objection, in its best form: Britain was exceptional (old parliamentary habits, no defeat, no revolution), so one survival does not refute a general claim. Answer: this limits the verdict to Britain before 1945 and does not overturn it, because the critics' forecasts were dated forecasts about Britain in particular, and those failed; the general claim is tested where the habits were missing, at Weimar. Then what would overturn the verdict (a franchise-driven breakdown; a showing that survival rested on the restrictions the Acts left, not on the mass electorate). Last, the one part graded **open**: tutelage — the counterweights came without a legislator, several were later absorbed (friendly societies after 1946; chapel attendance [secondary; bind]), and what would settle whether the welfare state is soft despotism is evidence on whether associational life and electoral turnover shrank as the state's functions grew. The ledger (figure 5) is filled in here.

**Conclusion.** States the verdict in plain words with its grades: on the British record the critics were right about how power would move inside a mass democracy — to the Cabinet, the party machine and a graduated tax (**strong**) — and wrong that it would break (**strong**, for Britain); pressure from below forced each question and party competition answered it (**on balance**); whether the state that grew is Tocqueville's tutelary power is **open**. Then what the reader can now do (split a forecast into direction, mechanism and tempo, lay each against a record, and name the departures), and the hand-off: in 1914 the parliamentary Europe this record describes went to war with itself; the next module's writers explain mass politics by the crowd, the race and the nation, and the fragility forecast meets a parliament that did fail at Weimar (`article-48-weimar`).

Headings as claims: "Three claims in one sentence: direction, mechanism, tempo"; "The artefact: a staircase with four landings"; "Peers, baronets and a Jew who led a Tory party widened the vote" — or, drier, "Who widened it: aristocrats and Conservatives"; "A century, with four stops"; "Seven forecasts against one sequel"; "The remedies nobody designed"; "What the numbers can and cannot say"; "Right about where power moved, wrong that it would break".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Every number bound, every base stated** (playbook-history rules 1–2). "Share of the adult population of the United Kingdom, both sexes, 21 and over, entitled to vote" — or the adult-male share for the three nineteenth-century Acts, said so each time. Never a raw electorate count in prose; counts live in the figure and the source note. The approximate shares in link 2 are the brief's expectation, not the lesson's authority: the writer takes the figures from the Commons Library paper and Evans and records the discrepancy if they differ. Round to the precision the claim supports ("about one in six", not "16.4%").
- **Ireland and Scotland.** The Acts came in trios (England and Wales; Scotland; Ireland) with different thresholds until 1884; the UK series is the honest base for 1832–1918 and the writer must say that Ireland's inclusion lowers every pre-1884 share. After 1922 the base is the UK without the Free State.
- **"Universal" in 1928 is not "equal":** plural voting (business premises; university seats) until the Representation of the People Act 1948 and the 1950 election. One clause; it is a departure.
- **Women.** 1918 (30, with a property qualification or a graduate's) and 1928 (21) are two Acts, not one; the suffragist and suffragette campaigns are anchored in one clause each (the NUWSS 1897; the WSPU 1903; the 1913 Derby) and not narrated. No moralising about the exclusion, no congratulation on the inclusion; the lesson records what the base was and when it changed.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** Whig and Tory / Liberal and Conservative (the two parties; "Conservative" from 1830 — Croker's coinage in the Quarterly Review, via Muller); the £10 householder; borough and county seats; rotten boroughs (constituencies with a handful of electors, abolished 1832); household suffrage; the Chartists and the Six Points (Ref `carlyle-heroes` for the Charter's first mention; re-anchor in a clause); the Reform League (1865); the Primrose League (1883, the Conservative mass organisation); the Parliament Act 1911 (ending the Lords' absolute veto); the Tamworth Manifesto (1834, the first party programme addressed to electors); the stamp duty on newspapers (repealed 1855); the Daily Mail (1896, the first halfpenny mass daily); Hansard (the record of debates); the Education Act 1870 (school boards; compulsory attendance by 1880, free by 1891); old-age pensions 1908; National Insurance 1911; Grey, Peel, Derby, Disraeli, Gladstone, Salisbury, Lloyd George, Baldwin — each with office and date at first mention.
- **Names in the heading** — avoid the clever heading about Disraeli; the course's register is dry. Disraeli's background enters only if the lesson uses it as evidence (it is not).
- **Do not re-teach** any of the five theorists; every prediction is a `<Ref>` plus the one quotation that states it. The lesson's new material is the record and the judgment.
- **The register rule.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Do not declare a winner before the evidence**, and do not let the "confirmation column" become a lament or the "refutation column" a celebration. The verdict is reached in link 8, after the evidence, at the register's grades and no firmer. The contested claim — is the welfare state soft despotism — is taught as an argument with both sides' evidence (absorption of functions; persistence of associations and elections, and their later absorption), graded **open** with what would settle it, and handed to `deneen-post-liberalism`. Lecky's "catastrophe" is a value judgment: both readings, no choice between them.
- **Fawcett is a left-liberal author** and Evans a standard but copyrighted survey; their theses are stated as the course's own and they are named in References only. Johnston (the Commons Library author) likewise. Stephen, Arnold, Carlyle, Marx, Tocqueville, Macaulay, Lowe, Derby, Disraeli, Salisbury are primary voices and are named.
- **Carlyle's "Shooting Niagara"** is not in the dossier; it is public domain and the strongest Carlyle document for this lesson. If used, the writer reads it (Gutenberg) and quotes two clauses; if not, the 1840/1843 passages suffice and the lesson says Carlyle's prediction predates every Act but 1832.
- **Numbers in the Muller headnote and Fawcett** ("500k → 750k → 2.2m") are electorate counts whose base and year are unstated; do not reproduce them without checking.
- **Computation** appears only in link 7 and exercise 3, and ends in an interpretation and an uncertainty statement (playbook-history rule 4). No plug-constant arithmetic.
- **Dossier gaps (say so in the lesson's References, not its body):** the record side is wholly outside the dossier (Commons Library paper; Hansard; the Acts; Evans; the Charter; "Shooting Niagara"; Bagehot). Arnold's text is an anthology excerpt (public-domain full text on Gutenberg if more is wanted); Stephen, Maine and Lecky are staged in full (indices 95–97).

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval / chronology (warm-up, E3).** Place the module's texts (Democracy in America I 1835 and II 1840; Heroes 1841 and Past and Present 1843; the Manifesto 1848; On Liberty 1859; Culture and Anarchy 1869; Liberty, Equality, Fraternity 1873; Popular Government 1885; Democracy and Liberty 1896) on the staircase of figure 1, and say for each which Acts its author had seen when he wrote. Closed book; the point is that only Maine and Lecky saw 1884, no one in the module saw 1918, and the most pessimistic forecasts were written latest, by the authors who had seen the most extensions.
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill.** Robert Lowe's sentence of 15 July 1867 ("…prevail on our future masters to learn their letters"): speaker (a Liberal who had led the opposition to reform), date (the week the Bill passed the Commons), audience (the House that had passed it), purpose; what it records (a fear of the new electorate), what it argues (education as the remedy), what it omits (that his own party would legislate it in 1870, and that "masters" assumes the vote is power — Stephen's point); what it can be used to establish (how the governing class understood 1867) and what it cannot (what the new voters wanted).
3. **Research-shaped task — computation inside source inquiry.** From the Commons Library tables, compute the 1867 share on two bases — adult men in England and Wales; all adults in the United Kingdom — and state how the "lag behind Tocqueville's rule" changes with the base. End with an interpretation (which base matches what "universal suffrage" meant to Tocqueville in 1835 and to the Chartists in 1838), an uncertainty statement (registers lag; plural voters; Ireland), and the corroborating non-numeric source (the Six Points checklist).
4. **Claim to refute.** "The 1867 Act confirms Marx: the working class won the vote by class struggle." Name the failing steps (the proletariat's own party had failed in 1848; the Act was passed by a Conservative government of landowners outbidding a Liberal one; the pressure of 1866 was real but the timing and the terms were the parties'), then state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that the parties conceded *because* of the pressure — which is also Tocqueville's mechanism, so the evidence does not separate them), and what evidence would separate all three mechanisms — Marx's, Tocqueville's, Lecky's party competition (e.g. whether the terms of 1867 moved with the Reform League's agitation or with the parliamentary contest between Disraeli and Gladstone, amendment by amendment). The solution states the lesson's finding: pressure forced the question, party competition answered it (**on balance**).
5. **Boundary probe.** Set the "aristocracy that bids for goodwill" to zero: a propertied class that refuses every extension (Guizot's France, 1830–48, "enrichissez-vous"). Using Tocqueville's rule and his Maryland aside, what does the theory predict — and what happened in February 1848 (Ref `marx-manifesto` set piece 2)? Then state what the British record adds: the rule's direction held in both countries; the route (concession or revolution) depended on the variable Tocqueville put in an aside.
6. **Causal ranking (synthesis).** Rank the causes of the 1867 Act as necessary / enabling / precipitating — industrial urbanisation and the growth of the skilled artisan class; the death of Palmerston (1865) and the party contest; the Reform League and Hyde Park; the American Civil War's effect on British opinion about democracy — and defend the ranking in a paragraph that names the evidence and the warrant. Possible answers, not the answer.
7. **Counter-argument stem.** "The welfare state of 1908–48 is Tocqueville's tutelary power." Name two facts the claim must account for (the associations that grew alongside it — unions, friendly societies, the co-operative movement — and the alternation of governments by election that removed the party that built it in 1951 and restored it later), and state what would have to be true for the claim to hold (that those associations and alternations had stopped mattering to how people lived — the test Tocqueville himself sets in ch. VI, "subjection in minor affairs"). The solution grades the claim **open** and names the evidence that would settle it: whether associational life and electoral turnover shrank as the state's functions grew; `deneen-post-liberalism` returns.
8. **Attack the verdict.** The lesson concludes that the critics' fragility forecast failed (**strong**, for Britain). State the strongest objection in its best form — Britain was exceptional (centuries of parliamentary habit, no defeat in war, no revolution, an aristocracy that conceded in time), so its survival tells nothing about mass democracy as such — and say what evidence would be needed to overturn the verdict, not merely to limit it: e.g. a showing that British popular government survived because of the restrictions the Acts left in place (plural voting and university seats to 1948–50, the Lords' delay after 1911) rather than with the mass electorate, or a breakdown traceable to the widened franchise. The solution says why the objection limits the verdict to Britain before 1945 and does not overturn it (the forecasts were dated and about Britain), and where the general claim is tested (`article-48-weimar`).
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`.** From the sequel column — the Primrose League's 1.5 million, Central Office, the caucus, the Tamworth-style manifesto, the halfpenny daily — and from Maine's wire-puller, state in two sentences what an elector of 1900 actually chose between and who framed the choice, and say whether the elector behaved more like a juror weighing evidence or like one of a crowd answering to repetition and a name. The reader will describe a party committee and a mass press deciding what the vote is about; the solution says in one sentence, with a `<Ref>` to `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`, that the next module opens with a theory that treats electorates as crowds and calls the electoral committee their master — and asks what such a theory can and cannot explain.

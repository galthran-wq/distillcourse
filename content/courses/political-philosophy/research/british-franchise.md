# Research brief: british-franchise (The British franchise 1832–1928 as a test of the democracy critics)

status: planned
module: democracy-and-its-critics
type: core
goal: Apply Tocqueville's law of franchise extension and the fears of Carlyle, Stephen and Arnold to the sequence of British Reform Acts, and judge what the record bears out

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Every position of the module, each by `<Ref>` and one clause: Tocqueville's franchise rule (Vol. I ch. IV) and his remark that aristocracies which see the inevitable bid for the people's goodwill (`equality-of-conditions`); the two dangers and the remedies (`tyranny-of-the-majority`, `soft-despotism`, `art-of-association`); Carlyle's "ballot-boxes alter the figure of our Quack" and "Democracy, which means despair of finding any Heroes" (`hero-worship`); Mill's social tyranny and the `harm-principle`, with Stephen's "counting heads instead of breaking heads" and Arnold's "doing as one likes" from `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`; Marx's Chartists and the proletariat's "conquest of political power" (`class-struggle`). From `burke-reflections`: the "arithmetic of 24 million against 200,000" and property in the representation — the aristocratic constitution the Acts dismantled. From `english-revolution-as-test` and `assignats`: the module-application method — a theory's prediction laid against a record, departures named.
- **Contributes:** The module's L16 application. One real artefact — five Acts of Parliament and the share of adults each left with a vote — read against five predictions made while the Acts were being debated. The lesson judges, prediction by prediction, what the record bears out, and names where the artefact departs from every theory in the module: a landed aristocracy that led the extensions, a ratchet that took a century to run, and a sequel (parties, press, welfare state) that confirms the forecast of soft despotism in one column and refutes it in the next.
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):**
  - *Artefact:* the Representation of the People Acts of 1832, 1867, 1884 (with the 1885 Redistribution Act), 1918 and 1928, and after each the share of the adult population of the United Kingdom entitled to vote. The series is the artefact; the Acts' texts and the Hansard debates are the documents read against it.
  - *Module results used:* `equality-of-conditions` (Tocqueville's "invariable rule", Vol. I ch. IV, and the Maryland remark); `soft-despotism` and `tyranny-of-the-majority` (Vol. II Pt IV chs. V–VII: what the state absorbs after each extension, and the remedies — associations, press, courts, forms); `hero-worship` (Carlyle, Heroes VI and Past and Present I.iv, III.xiii); `harm-principle` and its two replies (Stephen: parliament as disguised compulsion; Arnold: doing as one likes needs the State as best self); `class-struggle` (Marx, Manifesto I and IV: the proletariat's party; III.1: the aristocratic "alms-bag").
  - *Departures to name:* (1) the agents — Whig and Conservative aristocrats (Grey 1832; Peel accepting it in 1834; Derby and Disraeli 1867; Salisbury 1884; Baldwin 1928) extended the vote, not the class below the line and not its party; (2) the tempo — Tocqueville's "no stop can be made short of universal suffrage" ran for ninety-six years with four stops of ten to thirty-five years; (3) the sequel — the state grew into schooling, insurance and pensions as Tocqueville foresaw, but so did the counterweights he prescribed (unions, friendly societies, chapels, a free press, the courts, the Lords' veto until 1911), which no one designed; (4) the content — "universal" in 1928 still carried plural votes and university seats until 1948–50, so the series the lesson plots is adults *entitled*, not an equal franchise.
- **Hands off:** `mann-reflections` opens the next module with a thinker for whom "Freiheit/Stimmrecht" (freedom vs the vote) is the antithesis itself; Spengler's "democracy = plutocracy; the Press makes truth; money decides elections" (`spengler-decline-of-the-west`) and Ortega's "hyperdemocracy" (`ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`) are the next restatements of what this lesson's sequel column shows; `deneen-post-liberalism` returns to soft despotism.
- **Spine step:** "Democracy arrives as a fact and is judged: Tocqueville foresees soft despotism, Carlyle demands heroes, Mill defends liberty, Marx predicts class war — and the British franchise tests who was right"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: Tocqueville's "cannot stop short of universal suffrage"; Britain widened the vote in 1832, 1867, 1884, 1918 and 1928, each time after a debate in which the module's critics said what would follow; the five Acts and what they did to the electorate laid against the predictions.

1. **What exactly did Tocqueville predict, and how would we know if he was right?** The obvious test — five extensions, no reversal, prediction confirmed — is too cheap, and the lesson says why. Parse the rule (ll. 986–996) into three claims: a *direction* (once touched, the qualification goes to zero), a *mechanism* (each concession strengthens democracy and multiplies the ambition of those just below the line, "in exact proportion to the great number of those who are above it"), and a *tempo* ("no stop can be made"). Each is testable on different evidence: the direction on the series; the mechanism on *who agitated* before each Act; the tempo on the gaps between Acts. The method is the one `assignats` used on Burke: a forecast is checked, not admired.
2. **The artefact: five Acts and one series.** Give the series as the record, in one figure and one sentence of prose each, every number bound — share of the adult population of the United Kingdom (both sexes, 21 and over) entitled to vote, with the base stated. Approximate shape the writer verifies against the Commons Library paper before writing: before 1832 about one adult in twenty; after 1832 about one in fourteen (roughly one adult man in five in England and Wales — the £10 householder); after 1867 about one in six (roughly one man in three — household suffrage in the boroughs); after 1884 about two in seven (roughly two men in three — the counties brought level); after 1918 about three in four (all men at 21, women at 30 with a property qualification); after 1928 near all (women at 21; plural voting and university seats survive to 1948–50). State the qualification in Carlyle's and Disraeli's own words where they have one ("Tenpound Franchisers"; "household suffrage"). Prose says what the series means: a staircase with four landings, not a slope. Computation only inside source inquiry (link 7 and exercise 3): the share depends on the base chosen, and the base is a decision.
3. **Who extended the vote — and does the mechanism fit?** Here is the first departure, and the lesson must earn it with names and offices. 1832: Earl Grey's Whig ministry, a cabinet of peers, after the Birmingham Political Union and the Bristol riots; Macaulay's "reform, that you may preserve". 1834: Peel's Tamworth Manifesto accepts the Act for the Conservatives. 1838–48: the Chartists — the men "below the appointed rate" — petition three times with the Six Points and are refused; Kennington Common, April 1848, disperses in the rain two months after the Manifesto named them. 1866–67: the Reform League in Hyde Park (Arnold's railings); then Derby's and Disraeli's *Conservative* government passes household suffrage in the boroughs, outbidding Gladstone — Derby's "leap in the dark"; Lowe's "prevail on our future masters to learn their letters". 1884–85: Gladstone's county franchise, with Salisbury's Conservative Lords trading assent for the Redistribution Act. 1918: a wartime coalition; 1928: Baldwin's Conservative government. So: the direction is Tocqueville's; the agency is not his mechanism but his *aside* — the Maryland remark that aristocracies who see the inevitable bid for the people's goodwill, so that "the democratic impulse was strongest where aristocracy was firmest". The course's own statement of the British right's choice (Fawcett's thesis, References): after 1830 it chose to "accept parity and win on liberalism's terms"; Salisbury, who in 1862 wrote of "the insane passion for equality", built the mass party that made the widened franchise safe for his side — "concede rather than battle in vain". Then Marx's prediction against the same facts: the proletariat's party (the Chartists) failed; the vote came from above and from the contest between two propertied parties; Marx's own III.1 — the aristocracy waving "the proletarian alms-bag" — described Disraeli better than it mocked him. State both readings: that a bourgeois state conceded under pressure (Marx confirmed), or that a committee of the propertied did what the revolution was to do (Marx refuted); exercise 5 weighs them.
4. **The tempo: a century, with four stops.** The second departure. From 1832 to 1928 is ninety-six years; the gaps are thirty-five, seventeen, thirty-four and ten. "No stop can be made" was false four times, and each stop was defended in the terms the module has met: Burke's property in the representation (Ref) in 1832; Lowe's and Bagehot's fear for "deference" in 1867; the Lords' veto until the Parliament Act of 1911 — Tocqueville's "forms" doing exactly what Pt IV ch. VII says forms do, giving the weak (here the propertied minority) time to look about. The mechanism's "irritated ambition" is visible before 1832, 1867 and 1918 and almost absent before 1884 and 1928, which came from the governing parties' own calculation. Judgment: the rule holds as a direction, fails as a tempo, and its mechanism explains the pressure but not the timing.
5. **What the critics said would follow — and what did.** Lay each prediction against the sequel, one paragraph each, in the dry register, no winner declared in advance.
   - *Carlyle:* ballot-boxes change the Quack's figure, not his substance; democracy is "despair of finding any Heroes". After 1867 Britain was governed in turn by Gladstone, Disraeli, Salisbury, Lloyd George, Baldwin, Churchill — the exercise asks the reader which of these Carlyle's own criterion (sincerity, recognised by a people able to recognise it) would count as heroes, and notes that the prediction, as stated, no outcome could refute (Ref `carlyle-heroes` link 8). "Shooting Niagara", if used, is the dated version: the 1867 Act as a plunge; what followed was the 1870 Education Act — Lowe's "learn their letters" made law.
   - *Arnold:* "doing as one likes" without deference tends to anarchy unless the State becomes the best self. The anarchy did not come; the State did — compulsory elementary schooling 1870–91, school boards, inspectors (Arnold's own profession). Whether that was culture or "machinery" is Arnold's own question, left to the reader.
   - *Stephen:* the franchise is "counting heads instead of breaking heads"; the minority obeys because it would lose. Confirmed as description — no extension was resisted by force after 1832 — and contested as inference: a society that *can* count heads has already agreed not to break them, which is the liberal's point (Ref `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`).
   - *Marx:* class struggle drives the extension; the proletariat's party conquers power. The vote tracked industrial class formation (boroughs 1867, counties 1884, the mass army 1918), and the Labour Party held office in 1924 — without the "forcible overthrow" of 1848; the ten measures partly arrived by statute (Ref `marx-manifesto` exercise 5).
   - *Tocqueville:* the second danger. After each extension the state absorbed what Pt IV ch. V listed — schooling (1870), old-age pensions (1908), national insurance (1911), the welfare state (1945–48): "charity, education… savings" — and the parties became machines (Birmingham's caucus in the 1870s; the Primrose League's 1.5 million; Central Office; the manifesto, Tamworth's invention), the press a mass press (stamp duty repealed 1855; the Daily Mail 1896 — "nothing but a newspaper can drop the same thought into a thousand minds"). That is the confirmation column.
6. **The refutation column: the remedies arrived without a legislator.** Here the lesson names the third departure. Tocqueville's ch. VII prescriptions appeared on their own: civil associations (trade unions legalised 1871–75, friendly societies, chapels, the co-operative movement — the "artificial aristocrats" that "cannot be disposed of at pleasure"); a free and multiplied press; independent courts; the forms (the Lords until 1911, the monarchy's "dignified parts"); and elections that changed governments. The soft despotism of ch. VI is "republican in its head and ultra-monarchical in all its other parts"; Britain in 1928 was republican in its head and plural everywhere else. Whether the welfare state is the tutelary power or its opposite (a people providing for itself through its own majority) is the contested claim the lesson states as an argument with both sides' evidence and leaves open — `deneen-post-liberalism` takes it up. Tocqueville's own hedge is the fair close: Pt IV ch. IV predicts that England's classes will *reverse* their positions on centralisation; by 1911 they had.
7. **What the series can and cannot establish — computation inside source inquiry.** Three decisions change the numbers: the base (all adults; adult men; England and Wales vs the United Kingdom — Ireland's inclusion lowers every pre-1918 share); the count (registered electors, not eligible adults; plural voters counted once or more); the year (the register lags the Act). Show one recomputation — 1867 on the adult-male base against the all-adult base — and say how much the "lag" claim moves (from "a century" to "sixty years" if women are excluded from the denominator, which is itself a judgment about what "universal" meant in 1867). End on the corroborating non-numeric source: the Charter's Six Points checked off against the Acts — five achieved, one never — as the independent measure of how far 1838's "below the line" got by 1918.
8. **Verdict as judgments.** Tocqueville: right on direction and on administrative growth; wrong on tempo and on agency, and right about agency in his aside. Carlyle: unfalsifiable as stated, and his specific 1867 fear answered by the Education Act he would have called Morrison's Pill. Arnold: the State came, anarchy did not — whether as best self is his question. Stephen: confirmed as description, contested as inference. Marx: direction confirmed, mechanism contested, cure not needed for the outcome he named. Then what the reader can now do (apply a forecast's three parts to a record and name the departures), and the hand-off: in 1914 the parliamentary Europe this record describes went to war with itself, and the next module's three writers watched parliaments fail.

Headings as claims: "Three claims in one sentence: direction, mechanism, tempo"; "The artefact: a staircase with four landings"; "Peers, baronets and a Jew who led a Tory party widened the vote" — or, drier, "Who widened it: aristocrats and Conservatives"; "A century, with four stops"; "Five predictions against one sequel"; "The remedies nobody designed"; "What the numbers can and cannot say"; "Who was right, and about what".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Every number bound, every base stated** (playbook-history rules 1–2). "Share of the adult population of the United Kingdom, both sexes, 21 and over, entitled to vote" — or the adult-male share for the three nineteenth-century Acts, said so each time. Never a raw electorate count in prose; counts live in the figure and the source note. The approximate shares in link 2 are the brief's expectation, not the lesson's authority: the writer takes the figures from the Commons Library paper and Evans and records the discrepancy if they differ. Round to the precision the claim supports ("about one in six", not "16.4%").
- **Ireland and Scotland.** The Acts came in trios (England and Wales; Scotland; Ireland) with different thresholds until 1884; the UK series is the honest base for 1832–1918 and the writer must say that Ireland's inclusion lowers every pre-1884 share. After 1922 the base is the UK without the Free State.
- **"Universal" in 1928 is not "equal":** plural voting (business premises; university seats) until the Representation of the People Act 1948 and the 1950 election. One clause; it is a departure.
- **Women.** 1918 (30, with a property qualification or a graduate's) and 1928 (21) are two Acts, not one; the suffragist and suffragette campaigns are anchored in one clause each (the NUWSS 1897; the WSPU 1903; the 1913 Derby) and not narrated. No moralising about the exclusion, no congratulation on the inclusion; the lesson records what the base was and when it changed.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** Whig and Tory / Liberal and Conservative (the two parties; "Conservative" from 1830 — Croker's coinage in the Quarterly Review, via Muller); the £10 householder; borough and county seats; rotten boroughs (constituencies with a handful of electors, abolished 1832); household suffrage; the Chartists and the Six Points (Ref `carlyle-heroes` for the Charter's first mention; re-anchor in a clause); the Reform League (1865); the Primrose League (1883, the Conservative mass organisation); the Parliament Act 1911 (ending the Lords' absolute veto); the Tamworth Manifesto (1834, the first party programme addressed to electors); the stamp duty on newspapers (repealed 1855); the Daily Mail (1896, the first halfpenny mass daily); Hansard (the record of debates); the Education Act 1870 (school boards; compulsory attendance by 1880, free by 1891); old-age pensions 1908; National Insurance 1911; Grey, Peel, Derby, Disraeli, Gladstone, Salisbury, Lloyd George, Baldwin — each with office and date at first mention.
- **Names in the heading** — avoid the clever heading about Disraeli; the course's register is dry. Disraeli's background enters only if the lesson uses it as evidence (it is not).
- **Do not re-teach** any of the five theorists; every prediction is a `<Ref>` plus the one quotation that states it. The lesson's new material is the record and the judgment.
- **Do not declare a winner before the evidence**, and do not let the "confirmation column" become a lament or the "refutation column" a celebration. The contested claim — is the welfare state soft despotism — is taught as an argument with both sides' evidence (absorption of functions; persistence of associations and elections) and handed to `deneen-post-liberalism`.
- **Fawcett is a left-liberal author** and Evans a standard but copyrighted survey; their theses are stated as the course's own and they are named in References only. Johnston (the Commons Library author) likewise. Stephen, Arnold, Carlyle, Marx, Tocqueville, Macaulay, Lowe, Derby, Disraeli, Salisbury are primary voices and are named.
- **Carlyle's "Shooting Niagara"** is not in the dossier; it is public domain and the strongest Carlyle document for this lesson. If used, the writer reads it (Gutenberg) and quotes two clauses; if not, the 1840/1843 passages suffice and the lesson says Carlyle's prediction predates every Act but 1832.
- **Numbers in the Muller headnote and Fawcett** ("500k → 750k → 2.2m") are electorate counts whose base and year are unstated; do not reproduce them without checking.
- **Computation** appears only in link 7 and exercise 3, and ends in an interpretation and an uncertainty statement (playbook-history rule 4). No plug-constant arithmetic.
- **Dossier gaps (say so in the lesson's References, not its body):** the record side is wholly outside the dossier (Commons Library paper; Hansard; the Acts; Evans; the Charter; "Shooting Niagara"; Bagehot). Stephen's and Arnold's texts are anthology excerpts; their public-domain full texts are on Gutenberg if more is wanted.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval / chronology (warm-up, E3).** Place the module's five texts (Democracy in America I 1835 and II 1840; Heroes 1841 and Past and Present 1843; the Manifesto 1848; On Liberty 1859; Culture and Anarchy 1869; Liberty, Equality, Fraternity 1873) on the staircase of figure 1, and say for each which Acts its author had seen when he wrote. Closed book; the point is that only Stephen and Arnold wrote after 1867 and no one in the module saw 1884.
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill.** Robert Lowe's sentence of 15 July 1867 ("…prevail on our future masters to learn their letters"): speaker (a Liberal who had led the opposition to reform), date (the week the Bill passed the Commons), audience (the House that had passed it), purpose; what it records (a fear of the new electorate), what it argues (education as the remedy), what it omits (that his own party would legislate it in 1870, and that "masters" assumes the vote is power — Stephen's point); what it can be used to establish (how the governing class understood 1867) and what it cannot (what the new voters wanted).
3. **Research-shaped task — computation inside source inquiry.** From the Commons Library tables, compute the 1867 share on two bases — adult men in England and Wales; all adults in the United Kingdom — and state how the "lag behind Tocqueville's rule" changes with the base. End with an interpretation (which base matches what "universal suffrage" meant to Tocqueville in 1835 and to the Chartists in 1838), an uncertainty statement (registers lag; plural voters; Ireland), and the corroborating non-numeric source (the Six Points checklist).
4. **Claim to refute.** "The 1867 Act confirms Marx: the working class won the vote by class struggle." Name the failing steps (the proletariat's own party had failed in 1848; the Act was passed by a Conservative government of landowners outbidding a Liberal one; the pressure of 1866 was real but the timing and the terms were the parties'), then state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that the parties conceded *because* of the pressure — which is also Tocqueville's mechanism, so the evidence does not separate them). Judgment, not verdict.
5. **Boundary probe.** Set the "aristocracy that bids for goodwill" to zero: a propertied class that refuses every extension (Guizot's France, 1830–48, "enrichissez-vous"). Using Tocqueville's rule and his Maryland aside, what does the theory predict — and what happened in February 1848 (Ref `marx-manifesto` set piece 2)? Then state what the British record adds: the rule's direction held in both countries; the route (concession or revolution) depended on the variable Tocqueville put in an aside.
6. **Causal ranking (synthesis).** Rank the causes of the 1867 Act as necessary / enabling / precipitating — industrial urbanisation and the growth of the skilled artisan class; the death of Palmerston (1865) and the party contest; the Reform League and Hyde Park; the American Civil War's effect on British opinion about democracy — and defend the ranking in a paragraph that names the evidence and the warrant. Possible answers, not the answer.
7. **Counter-argument stem.** "The welfare state of 1908–48 is Tocqueville's tutelary power." Name two facts the claim must account for (the associations that grew alongside it — unions, friendly societies, the co-operative movement — and the alternation of governments by election that removed the party that built it in 1951 and restored it later), and state what would have to be true for the claim to hold (that those associations and alternations had stopped mattering to how people lived — the test Tocqueville himself sets in ch. VI, "subjection in minor affairs"). Leave it open; `deneen-post-liberalism` returns.
8. **FORWARD (E7).** From the sequel column — the Primrose League's 1.5 million, Central Office, the Tamworth-style manifesto, the halfpenny daily — state in two sentences what an elector of 1900 actually chose between and who framed the choice. The reader will describe a party machine and a mass press deciding what the vote is about; the solution says in one sentence, with a `<Ref>` to `spengler-decline-of-the-west`, that the civilization module meets a thinker who calls this "democracy = plutocracy; the Press makes truth" and asks whether he describes it or merely despises it.

# Research brief: burke-against-hastings (Burke's impeachment of Warren Hastings: a conservative against empire)

status: planned
module: empire-and-guilt
type: core
goal: Analyze Burke's case against Hastings — that arbitrary power cannot be held or given, and that an empire is a trust — and explain why the defender of inherited institutions became the prosecutor of British rule in Bengal

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `burke-reflections`: liberty as an entailed inheritance,
  prejudice as "the general bank and capital of nations and of ages", the
  little platoon, natural aristocracy, society as a partnership "between those
  who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born" — and that
  lesson's note that "Burke's India (Hastings) is held for the empire module".
  From `locke-and-mill-on-empire`: the East India Company anchored (charter
  1600, Plassey 1757, the 1765 revenue grant, Crown 1858), tutelage and the
  "strategies of exclusion", Mill's "highest moral trust" and "a choice of
  despotisms", and Burke's "schoolboys without tutors" as a forward pointer.
  From `cicero-natural-law` and `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: a law above rulers
  by which statutes are judged — Burke's one explicit natural-law passage
  stands on it. From `vitoria-right-to-the-americas`: the prince as a judge
  between two commonwealths, never a prosecutor.
- **Contributes:** The module's reversal seam and one principle. Expectation:
  the defender of hierarchy and inheritance should defend the conqueror.
  Record: he prosecutes him for seven years. The principle: arbitrary power
  "is a thing which neither any man can hold nor any man can give"; all power
  over others is a trust under law; a conquered people is governed "upon their
  own principles and maxims, and not upon ours". The lesson shows that the
  Hastings speech and the Reflections are one theory of inherited order,
  turned on the Company instead of the Jacobins.
- **Hands off:** Burke's speech is itself a ledger — misery to Bengal, corruption
  flowing back to Britain, the "star from the West" that a good empire might
  have been — with no counterfactual and no comparison. `colonial-balance-sheet`
  asks what a cost–benefit accounting of empire would need that an eighteenth-
  century prosecutor's does not, and whether it can be done at all.
- **Spine step:** "The West judges its own conquests: Vitoria's titles, Locke's waste land, Burke's impeachment of Hastings, Mill's tutelage, the colonial balance sheet, and whether guilt is inherited"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim. Then the seam at once, in the dry
register: from the Reflections the reader expects (`<Ref>` burke-reflections, a
clause: inheritance, prejudice, hierarchy) that Burke will defend an English
governor against the claims of a conquered people; the record is that he spent
fourteen years on India and seven on this trial, and lost friends over it. The
lesson's problem: by what standard may English peers judge what an Englishman
did in Bengal to Indians — and why did this man think they must?

1. **What is on trial, and why by impeachment.** Anchor Hastings in a sentence
   (a Company servant from boyhood; Governor of Bengal 1772; first
   Governor-General under the Regulating Act of 1773; home in 1785; impeached by
   the Commons in 1787; the Lords sit as a court in Westminster Hall from
   February 1788). Question: why a parliamentary trial rather than a court?
   Attempt: because the offences were abroad and no court had jurisdiction.
   Burke's answer is larger: impeachment is "the great circulation of
   responsibility, by which (excepting the supreme power) no man, in no
   circumstance, can escape the account which he owes to the laws"; "Other
   constitutions are satisfied with making good subjects; this is a security
   for good governors". And the stakes are not one man: "whether millions of
   mankind shall be made miserable or happy". The crimes are "of forethought",
   a system of acts plus "a connected system of maxims invented to justify
   them" — so the trial is of a doctrine, which is why a lesson in political
   philosophy reads it. Because: the Lords judge by "natural, immutable, and
   substantial justice", not by the technical rules of a municipal court — the
   empire must be brought under justice, not justice shrunk to the island.

2. **What kind of thing is the Company?** Question: how does a trading company
   come to be tried for governing? Recall the anchor (`<Ref>`
   locke-and-mill-on-empire, one clause). Burke's two sources of its power: the
   British charters, and the dewanny — the Mughal emperor's grant in 1765 of
   the revenue administration of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa, after Plassey (1757)
   had made the Company the real power. By accepting the dewanny the Company
   became an officer of the Mughal empire, bound to govern by the natives'
   laws; Britain guaranteed those duties by "a virtual act of union". Hence
   "the East India Company in Asia is a state in the disguise of a merchant",
   and its service "a system of public offices in the disguise of a
   counting-house". Then the structural diagnosis: the English in India are
   "a nation of placemen; they are a commonwealth without a people; they are a
   state made up wholly of magistrates" — nothing "to watch, to inspect, to
   balance against the power of office"; young men on small pay with "unbounded
   license to plunder", "school-boys without tutors, minors without guardians";
   covenants everyone breaks a little, so that "he who has taken but one penny
   of unlawful emolument… does not dare to complain"; the banian, a native
   agent-creditor who rules his nominal master. Because: without a people, no
   check; without a check, office is the only power — this is why Burke
   charges a system, and why he says the structure made the crime possible and
   Hastings made it a principle. (Seam to Mill: the previous lesson's "choice
   of despotisms" diagnoses the same absence — no one who hears, sees or
   knows.)

3. **Whose laws apply in Bengal?** Question: an English court judging acts
   done among Hindus and Muslims — by English law? Burke: no — "we must govern
   them upon their own principles and maxims, and not upon ours". Caste as the
   fusion of religion, law and honour: "To speak to an Indian of his caste is
   to speak to him of his all." Then the sentence that reverses the stadial
   scale of the last lesson: "God forbid we should pass judgment upon people who
   framed their laws and institutions prior to our insect origin of
   yesterday!" The six eras (Hindu; Arab; Tamerlane; Akbar; the independent
   Subahs; the British) argued to one conclusion — in every conquest before the
   British the Hindu rajahs kept rank, forts and rights, "a republic of
   princes with a great chief at their head"; Tamerlane "conquered the prince,
   not the country". Flag in the body, once: Burke's Bengal before Plassey is a
   prosecutor's picture — stable, lawful, ancient — and historians have
   contested it; the lesson needs only his premise that India had law and
   property, which the defence itself conceded by claiming the opposite
   principle.

4. **The defence: "the constitution of Asia is despotism."** Give Hastings at
   full strength, from Burke's quotation and with the sourcing caveat that
   this is the prosecutor quoting: "the whole history of Asia is nothing more
   than precedents to prove the invariable exercise of arbitrary power";
   "Slaves I found them, and as slaves I have treated them. I was a despotic
   prince"; "I know the constitution of Asia only from its practice". The
   point of the defence is serious: a governor must govern a people by the
   constitution it has, and Bengal's was absolute power — the same premise as
   Burke's "their own principles", pointed the other way. Burke's reply is in
   three steps, each a section or a sub-heading:
   (a) **Geographical morality rejected.** The defence has "formed a plan of
   geographical morality, by which the duties of men… are not to be governed by
   their relation to the great Governor of the Universe, or by their relation
   to mankind, but by climates, degrees of longitude, parallels, not of life,
   but of latitudes: as if, when you have crossed the equinoctial, all the
   virtues die, as they say some insects die when they cross the line"; "the
   laws of morality are the same everywhere". Define the concept here.
   (b) **Arbitrary power cannot be held or given.** "The East India Company
   have not arbitrary power to give him; the king has no arbitrary power to
   give him; your Lordships have not; nor the Commons, nor the whole
   legislature… arbitrary power is a thing which neither any man can hold nor
   any man can give." Why: "We are all born in subjection… to one great,
   immutable, preëxistent law, prior to all our devices and prior to all our
   contrivances" — the law that "gives to our conventions and compacts all the
   force and sanction they can have" (`<Ref>` cicero-natural-law /
   aquinas-law-and-kingship: a clause — this is Burke's natural law, the one
   place he argues from it rather than from prescription). Conquest confers no
   arbitrary right: the conqueror steps into the displaced sovereign's duties.
   "Law and arbitrary power are in eternal enmity. Name me a magistrate, and I
   will name property; name me power, and I will name protection… We may bite
   our chains, if we will, but we shall be made to know ourselves, and be
   taught that man is born to be governed by law." Then the distinction that
   does the legal work: supreme power is unaccountable but not therefore
   arbitrary; and there is no "intermediate arbitrary power" — a delegate who
   acts without law yet claims the protection of all laws is "a wild beast (for
   such is a man without law) loose upon the people". Define empire-as-trust
   here: every grant of power carries "a proportionable degree of
   responsibility"; "In every patent of office the duty is included."
   (c) **Asia had law.** The Koran as a law the Grand Seignior cannot tax, kill
   or make war against without the fetwah (a jurist's ruling); the Mughal
   province's partitioned powers (governor, treasurer, judge, registrar); the
   Hindu Shaster; and Tamerlane's Institutes — twelve maxims Burke reads to the
   Lords to prove the Mughal founder "rooted empire in morality, religion and
   law": an empire without law is "a naked man… a house which hath neither
   roof nor gates". Burke's admission that he has never seen Genghis Khan's
   code (l.1363–4) goes in — it is a sourcing lesson in itself. "Let him fly
   where he will, from law to law; law, I thank God, meets him everywhere."
   Because: the defence needed Asia to be lawless; if it had law, Hastings
   broke the law of the country he governed as well as the law of the country
   that sent him.

5. **Bribery as a system.** Question: why does a trial of a governor turn into
   an account of presents? Burke's thesis: "Corruption and arbitrary power are
   of natural unequivocal generation, necessarily producing one another"; "there
   never was a man who thought he had no law but his own will, who did not soon
   find that he had no end but his own profit". Avarice is the basest form
   because it "can wear no specious virtue": "money is the beginning, the
   middle, and the end". Three obligations converge on "take no presents"
   (the moral duty of office, the Act of 1773, the covenant he signed). Then
   two cases told as cases: **Munny Begum** — the widow of a former Nawab, whom
   Hastings in 1772 made guardian of the minor Nawab and manager of his
   household, and from whom (the Commons charged) he took a present; the £200 a
   day "entertainment" charged to the Nawab's treasury for the Governor's
   visit while the Nawab's pensioners were cut — the vulture "who feeds upon
   the prostrate, the dying, and the dead" (bind the number: a sum per day set
   against stipends stopped). And **Debi Sing and Rungpore** — a revenue farmer
   of notorious record, re-appointed through Gunga Govind Sing (Hastings's
   banian, "a name at the sound of which all India turns pale"), who farms the
   districts of Dinagepore and Rungpore; the chain Burke makes the Lords
   follow: a bribe paid for the farm → exactions above the assessed revenue →
   loans at 600 per cent → forced sales of land, cattle, children → torture of
   the ryots (the peasants) → flight and revolt: "It was not a rigorous
   collection of revenue, it was a savage war made upon the country." The
   Paterson report (a Company commissioner who recorded the tortures and was
   then made the accused) as the record Burke reads from. And the moral-economic
   turn: the peasants' "mashed and disabled hands" furnished the investment
   for Britain's China trade — the tea at Westminster. Why bribery by a
   governor is not theft: the price is "the properties, the liberties, and the
   lives of an whole people", recouped "four times forty" from them. Because:
   this is how the structural diagnosis (link 2) becomes a crime — the man at
   the top removed the Company's own checks and appointed the men who paid him.

6. **"I impeach him in the name of…" — and what happened.** The five names
   (figure 6): the Commons whose trust he betrayed; the Commons whose national
   character he dishonoured; "the people of India, whose laws, rights, and
   liberties he has subverted"; "those eternal laws of justice which he has
   violated"; "human nature itself". The last two are the natural-law
   argument's signature. Then the record: the trial sat on and off for seven
   years; the Lords acquitted Hastings on every charge on 23 April 1795; Burke
   wrote to his executor that his India work should be his monument —
   "Remember! Remember! Remember!" (dates: 1788, 1795, Burke's death 1797; no
   others).

7. **How it fits the Reflections — one theory, two targets.** Question: did the
   prosecutor of 1788 contradict the author of 1790? The course's own reading
   (Mehta in References): no — both defend an inherited order against men who
   treat a country as material. The Company and the Jacobins are both
   dislocators: the Jacobins separate "the moral France from the geographical";
   the Company redraws Bengal on a map and lets "birds of prey and passage",
   "wave after wave" of young men, take fortunes home and buy their way into
   English families and Parliament — "Indianism and Jacobinism… the first is
   the worst by far" (1796). The partnership of generations (`<Ref>`) applies
   to Bengal: earlier conquerors "made the conquered country their own…
   Fathers there deposited the hopes of their posterity"; the British do not,
   and are to Bengal what the revolutionaries are to France — "the flies of a
   summer". The reversed childhood: for Mill (`<Ref>`) the Indians are children
   under tutelage; for Burke the Company's servants are "schoolboys without
   tutors" and the Indians a people with laws older than England's. And the
   braided danger: "Today the Commons of Great Britain prosecutes the
   delinquents of India: tomorrow the delinquents of India may be the Commons
   of Great Britain" — empire corrupts the constitution at home. Because: an
   inherited order is a particular order, and particularity is what a
   conqueror's arbitrary power and a legislator's abstract rights both erase.

8. **The OBJECTION at full strength — he objected to the Company, not to
   empire.** The Whig-imperial reading: Burke never asked that Britain leave
   India; he pleaded for good government, never Indian self-government; his
   remedy was a parliamentary commission (Fox's India Bill, 1783) — more
   British control, not less; his Bengal is an idealised society of ancient
   law that needed only honest administration; his evidence is prosecutorial
   and the Lords acquitted; the natural-law passage is the exception in a
   corpus that argues from prescription and circumstance; and "their own
   principles" is a conservative's reason for keeping Indians under the
   institutions they had, princes and all. The reply the lesson gives him: a
   trust is itself a theory of empire — compare its content with Mill's: Mill's
   trust is to improve a people toward a stage; Burke's is to preserve a
   people's own laws and property under the law that binds both; and the
   "eternal separation" sentence from 1783 — if Britain cannot govern India
   without governing itself ill, "a ground is laid for their eternal
   separation, but none for sacrificing the people of that country to our
   constitution" — is a conditional case for leaving. Leave the reader with
   both: the conservative against this empire, and the conservative who could
   not imagine India governing itself.

   country" and the China-trade hands.
7. **"I impeach him in the name of…"** (shard4 l.1565–1582) as a five-row

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), no more than three new institutions per section:**
  impeachment (the Commons accuse, the Lords try); Westminster Hall; Governor-
  General; the Regulating Act 1773; Nawab/Nabob (the Mughal governor of Bengal,
  by 1772 a pensioner of the Company); dewanny/diwani (the revenue
  administration granted in 1765); zemindar (a hereditary revenue-holder and
  landlord); ryot (a peasant cultivator); banian (a native agent and banker to
  a Company servant); peshcush (a present on appointment); subahdar (a provincial
  governor); fetwah (a Muslim jurist's ruling); Mogul/Mughal; "Gentoo" (the
  period word for Hindu — keep inside quotations); Tamerlane (Timur, d. 1405;
  his descendant Babur founded the Mughal line); Akbar (r. 1556–1605); Plassey
  (1757); Nundcomar (Nandakumar, a Brahmin who accused Hastings of taking
  bribes in 1775 and was hanged for forgery that year by the Supreme Court
  under Impey); Munny Begum; Gunga Govind Sing; Debi Sing; Rungpore and
  Dinagepore (districts of northern Bengal "as large as the northern counties
  of England", shard3 l.1610). Period spellings inside quotations; one modern
  gloss in prose.
- **Hastings is heard only through Burke.** Say so where his defence is
  quoted; the Debrett Minutes are PD for a writer who wants a direct quotation.
  The lesson analyses an argument; it does not re-try the man. Historians
  divide on Hastings (Marshall) — one clause, References.
- **Burke's India is a prosecutor's India.** The six eras and the praise of
  Hindu institutions idealise; index 25 pedagogy flags "orientalist assumptions
  about caste and 'Gentoo' character". The lesson uses only the premise the
  argument needs (India had law and property) and says the picture is
  contested.
- **Numbers:** Burke's lacs and pounds are rhetorical weights. Keep two: £200 a
  day (bound against stopped pensions) and 600 per cent (bound as the loan
  rate in the Rungpore chain). Dates: 1757, 1765, 1773, 1788, 1795 — everything
  else to the decade.
- **Do not re-teach** the Reflections (inheritance, prejudice, partnership) or
  Mill's tutelage; one `<Ref>` clause each. Do not run the Fox's India Bill
  speech as a second set — one sentence from it.
- **Register:** the speech is forensic oratory; the course is not. Quote the
  invective short, in `<Quote>`, and keep the course's own sentences
  declarative. No "hero", no "hypocrite".
- **Mehta, Marshall, O'Brien, Bromwich, Macaulay (as historian) only in
  References.** "The Reflections and the Hastings speech as one theory" is
  stated as the course's own.
- **Dossier gaps:** Fox's India Bill speech (PD, obtain); Hastings's own
  defence (PD, Debrett); the acquittal and the trial's length (public record);
  the closing speech of 1794 (not needed). The Gutenberg number discrepancy
  (#13968 in the meta vs #26159 in sources.yaml).
- **Strauss–Cropsey** Burke chapter: file empty at brief time.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (E3, the one warm-up).** Place Plassey, the
   dewanny, the Regulating Act, Burke's opening speech, the Reflections and the
   acquittal relative to 1789; then state in one clause each what Mill's "highest
   moral trust" and Burke's "trust" oblige a ruler to do.
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill on Hastings's defence as Burke quotes
   it.** Metadata: a prosecutor reading the accused's minute to the court;
   predict what the quotation will include and leave out; then: what the
   passage records (that Hastings claimed the principle), what it argues, what
   it omits (the context of the Benares revolt), and what it can be USED to
   establish — the doctrine on trial, not the conduct.
3. **Paired corroboration.** Burke reads Tamerlane's Institutes as proof that
   Asia had law, and admits he has never seen Genghis Khan's code. What does
   the admission establish about the standard of evidence in the speech, and
   does the argument "Asia had law" need the Institutes at all (it does not:
   the Koran and the Mughal provincial offices carry it)?
4. **Boundary probe.** Suppose a conquered people genuinely without law or
   property — Burke's own aside about "gangs of savages like the Guaranies and
   the Chiquitos". Which of his two arguments survives: "govern them by their
   own principles" (lapses — there are none to govern by) or "arbitrary power
   can neither be held nor given" (survives — it rests on the pre-existent law,
   not on theirs)? Solution shows why the natural-law argument is the one the
   case cannot do without.
5. **Claim to refute.** "Burke opposed British rule in India." Name the step
   that fails (Fox's India Bill proposed more parliamentary control, not
   withdrawal; the trust is a theory of how to hold an empire) and the one
   sentence that complicates the refutation (the "eternal separation"
   conditional).
6. **Derivation the lesson skipped.** Mill's "choice of despotisms" and Burke's
   "commonwealth without a people" both diagnose that no one in Bengal watches
   office. Run the two diagnoses to their remedies (a permanent trained
   delegated body | law, record and impeachment as the circulation of
   responsibility) and state what each remedy assumes about the governed
   (that they cannot yet judge | that their laws already judge).
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `colonial-balance-sheet`.** Write Burke's ledger as he
   gives it: the costs to Bengal (the Rungpore chain), the costs to Britain
   (the delinquents of India in the Commons), the benefit he grants (the "star
   from the West" that good government could have been). Then name what a
   cost–benefit accounting of empire would demand that his omits — a
   counterfactual ("compared to what?") and a comparison across cases. The
   solution says the next lesson supplies both and asks whether they can be
   had, with a `<Ref>`.
8. **Synthesis — claim–evidence–reasoning.** "The Hastings speech contradicts
   the Reflections." Argue it both ways in one paragraph each (the natural-law
   passage against prescription; the inherited order against dislocators), then
   judge, naming which passage decides it for you.

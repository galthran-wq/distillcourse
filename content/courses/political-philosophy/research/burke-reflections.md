# Research brief: burke-reflections (Burke's Reflections: inheritance, prejudice and the partnership of generations)

status: planned
module: contract-revolution-reaction
type: core
goal: Analyze Burke's case against the Revolution as a failure of knowledge before it is a failure of morals, and his account of what an inherited constitution knows

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `locke-property-and-consent`: consent as the only ground of
  government, the trust that can be forfeited, the right of resistance and the
  appeal to heaven — the doctrine Price will claim 1688 established. From
  `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: the general will, total alienation,
  "forced to be free", the Legislator, and the problem the last exercise left —
  whether a people can deliberate a government into being. From the Plato and
  Aristotle modules: `mixed-constitution` (`platos-laws-second-best-city`,
  `cicero-mixed-constitution`) and Aristotle's rule of law; from
  `english-revolution-as-test`: 1642–1660 and the Restoration, so 1688 is the
  next English date. From `cicero-natural-law` and `aquinas-law-and-kingship`:
  `natural-law`, which Burke will invoke for prescription.
- **Contributes:** One move: Burke's case against the Revolution is epistemic
  before it is moral. No generation knows enough to design a society from
  principles; an inherited constitution carries more reason than any
  individual can see, latent in "prejudice", custom and the accumulated
  adjustments of ages; so the French, by starting from "the rights of men",
  have thrown away the only knowledge available and will end in force — the
  gallows, then a general. The lesson's centre is the argument from ignorance
  (what the constitution knows that its critics do not) and the forecast it
  produced in 1790.
- **Hands off:** Burke argues from history and consequences; Maistre will say
  he conceded too much — the English constitution is not a model but an
  accident, and no constitution at all is made by men (`maistre-and-bonald`,
  the Act of Settlement quarrel). The deeper handoff is Strauss's question,
  stated here as the course's own: if the standard of a good constitution is
  that it grew, where is the standard by which growth is judged? That is the
  road to Hegel (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`). Burke's forecasts (paper
  money, the departments that will not obey Paris, the general who becomes
  master) are the predictions the module's application `assignats` tests.
  Burke's India (the Hastings impeachment, "geographical morality") is HELD for
  `burke-against-hastings` in the empire module — one sentence here, no more.
- **Spine step:** "Government by consent (Locke) and the general will (Rousseau)
  make a revolution thinkable; Burke, Maistre and Bonald answer that no
  generation knows enough to design a society, and Hegel absorbs both sides into
  the rational state"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: November 1789, a London preacher
tells his congregation that England's own revolution of 1688 gave the people
three rights — to choose their governors, to cashier them for misconduct, to
frame a government for themselves; a Whig MP who defended the American colonists
answers that England claimed none of these, that France is about to find out
why, and that a people who design a society from principles will get Terror and
then a general.

1. **Did 1688 establish a right to choose, cashier and frame?** The reader has
   Locke's trust and right of resistance (`<Ref>` locke-property-and-consent,
   one clause): on that account Price looks right. Burke's method is a close
   reading of the statutes: the Declaration of Right (1689) and Act of
   Settlement (1701) bind the succession and the liberties together in one
   instrument; the Lords and Commons of 1688 did not elect a king but filled a
   vacancy with the next Protestant heirs and declared the crown hereditary in
   their line — "a small and temporary deviation" to preserve the hereditary
   principle; "Privilegium non transit in exemplum" (a privilege does not pass
   into a precedent). The revolution was "made to preserve our ancient
   indisputable laws and liberties", "a revolution not made but prevented". Why
   the obvious reading fails: Price reads a right where the record shows a
   necessity — and "a necessity that is not chosen, but chooses" (S2
   l.1067–1078) is not a title anyone can invoke at will. Heading as claim:
   *1688 was a deviation to preserve the rule, not a right to break it*.
2. **If not from principles, where do English liberties come from?** Obvious
   answer: from "the rights of men". Burke's: from the fathers. From Magna
   Carta to the Declaration of Right the English "claim and assert our
   liberties as an entailed inheritance derived to us from our forefathers, and
   to be transmitted to our posterity" — "the rights of Englishmen", not of man.
   Define *entail* at first use (a settlement of land that no present holder
   may sell or break; he is a life-tenant). The payoff: "in what we improve we
   are never wholly new; in what we retain we are never wholly obsolete"; "a
   state without the means of some change is without the means of its
   conservation" — reform confined to "the peccant part". This is the "method
   of nature": a permanent body of transitory parts, the whole never old, never
   young. Ask here, as a reader would, why an inherited right is safer than a
   declared one — the next link answers.
3. **What does an inherited constitution know that its designers could not?**
   The epistemic core (goal). Burke's own words: "we are afraid to put men to
   live and trade each on his own private stock of reason; because we suspect
   that this stock in each man is small, and that the individuals would do
   better to avail themselves of the general bank and capital of nations and of
   ages." *Prejudice* — define it in Burke's sense, not ours: a settled judgment
   whose reasons are latent — "prejudice, with its reason, has a motive to give
   action to that reason, and an affection which will give it permanence";
   "through just prejudice, his duty becomes a part of his nature". Then the
   consequence for statecraft: "the science of constructing a commonwealth … is
   not to be taught a priori"; it is "experimental", its effects visible only
   "in the remoter operation"; "call in the aid of the farmer and the physician
   rather than the professor of metaphysics". So the Assembly's error is not
   wickedness but ignorance armed: "the metaphysics of an undergraduate and the
   mathematics and arithmetic of an exciseman". State the claim's structure
   precisely: not "the old is good" but "we cannot know enough to replace it
   wholesale, and what has lasted has passed tests we cannot enumerate". The
   reader should see this as an argument about distributed knowledge, then
   meet the *self-application* question (SEP 3.4) later in link 8.
4. **What is wrong with "the rights of men"?** Burke does not deny rights; he
   distinguishes real from metaphysical. The real rights: to justice, to the
   fruits of one's industry, to inheritance, to instruction — "in this
   partnership all men have equal rights; but not to equal things": five
   shillings and five hundred pounds have equal right to their shares. A share
   in political power is "a thing to be settled by convention", not an original
   right, because civil society is itself a convention in which men "surrender"
   the right to judge their own cause (Locke's own premise, `<Ref>`, turned).
   Hence "government is a contrivance of human wisdom to provide for human
   wants", and "the restraints on men, as well as their liberties, are to be
   reckoned among their rights". Metaphysical rights fail by their own
   perfection: "their abstract perfection is their practical defect. By having a
   right to everything they want everything"; they enter society like rays of
   light "refracted from their straight line" through "a dense medium"; "in
   proportion as they are metaphysically true, they are morally and politically
   false". Set this directly against Rousseau's I.6 (`<Ref>`): the demand to
   remain "as free as before" is the metaphysical right par excellence.
5. **Who should rule, then, if not 24 million?** Burke's answer is not
   hereditary right but *natural aristocracy*: "there is no qualification for
   government but virtue and wisdom, actual or presumptive" — but these are
   found where leisure, education, property and the habit of command have
   formed them; careers open "but not indifferently to every man"; "the temple
   of honor ought to be seated on an eminence". Property must be represented
   "out of all proportion" and in great masses as a "natural rampart", hereditary
   wealth being "the ballast in the vessel of the commonwealth". Then the
   arithmetic: "Twenty-four millions ought to prevail over two hundred
   thousand. True; if the constitution of a kingdom be a problem of arithmetic"
   — the will of the many often differs from their interest. The Assembly's
   composition (country lawyers, curates, stock-jobbers; the landed interest
   absent) is the evidence; the little platoon is the mechanism: "to love the
   little platoon we belong to in society, is the first principle (the germ as
   it were) of public affections" — affection ascends from family and
   neighbourhood to nation and cannot be commanded for "chequer No. 71". This is
   the seam where the lesson must be careful (L10b): from "no qualification but
   virtue and wisdom" one expects a meritocracy; Burke's conclusion is a landed
   one, and the lesson says so without moralising.
6. **What holds a society together if not the contract and the rights?** The
   manners and the religion the Assembly is dismantling. 6 October 1789 and the
   queen; "the age of chivalry is gone. That of sophisters, economists, and
   calculators has succeeded"; "all the decent drapery of life is to be rudely
   torn off"; "on this scheme of things, a king is but a man; a queen is but a
   woman; a woman is but an animal". The two supports of European civilisation:
   "the spirit of a gentleman and the spirit of religion"; learning, which they
   sustained, will be "trodden down under the hoofs of a swinish multitude"
   (read the sentence in context: the subject is learning, the fear is that
   without rank and church nothing protects it). Religion is "the basis of civil
   society"; the state is "consecrated" so that those who hold power know they
   hold it in trust, and so that no generation thinks itself free to break the
   entail: society is "a partnership in all science, a partnership in all art,
   a partnership in every virtue … between those who are living, those who are
   dead, and those who are to be born" — not "a partnership agreement in a trade
   of pepper and coffee". Place this beside the three contracts the reader knows
   (Hobbes's covenant, Locke's trust, Rousseau's total alienation — one clause
   each with `<Ref>`s): Burke's contract has parties who cannot be present, so
   it cannot be renegotiated by those who are. "A perfect democracy is the most
   shameless thing in the world" because infamy is divided by numbers.
7. **What did Burke predict, and on what grounds?** The forecast, with its
   grounds, as the test of the epistemic thesis. (i) Terror: "at the end of
   every visto, you see nothing but the gallows" — because once manners and
   religion are stripped, "nothing is left which engages the affections on the
   part of the commonwealth", and government is left with force; "kings will be
   tyrants from policy, when subjects are rebels from principle". (ii) The
   general: an army that deliberates becomes "a military democracy", and "the
   person who really commands the army is your master" (S4 l.829–837). (iii)
   Paper money: a currency on confiscated Church land breeds "an oligarchy of
   money-managers" and "a nation of gamesters" (S3 l.1982–2203) — one sentence,
   RESERVED for `assignats`. (iv) The departments will not obey Paris (S1
   l.1719–1760). Written in 1790, before the Terror (1793), before the execution
   of the king, before Bonaparte (1799). The lesson states what came true and
   where (i, ii), and holds iii and iv for the application lesson, which tests
   them.
8. **The objections, at full strength.** Three, in this order. (a) **Paine and
   Wollstonecraft (1790–91):** the dead cannot bind the living — "the vanity and
   presumption of governing beyond the grave is the most ridiculous and insolent
   of all tyrannies"; a constitution that cannot be produced does not exist;
   Burke "pities the plumage, but forgets the dying bird"; his reverence for
   rank is sympathy for the queen and none for the poor; and his contempt for
   reason ("a mortal antipathy to reason") leaves no way to tell a good
   prejudice from a bad one. (b) **The Whig/Tory puzzle:** the same man defended
   the American colonists, prosecuted Hastings for arbitrary power in India
   (one sentence; `<Ref>` burke-against-hastings), and now defends the French
   monarchy — is this consistency ("varying his means to secure the unity of
   his end", S4 l.1801–1818) or apostasy? Give Burke's own answer (the
   Americans claimed the rights of Englishmen, not of men; the Assembly
   destroys what the colonists defended) and the critics' (he defends whatever
   is established). (c) **The self-application problem:** if no general
   principle can be applied without circumstance, "distrust general principles"
   is itself a general principle; and the probabilistic version — why believe
   that the unforeseen effects of change are more likely bad than good? Burke's
   best reply is in the text: not "change is bad" but "a disposition to preserve,
   and an ability to improve, taken together" — reform "in the style of the
   building"; the argument is about the burden of proof and the rate of change,
   not a ban. The lesson concedes that this leaves the line between repair and
   innovation to judgment, which is where Fawcett's objection (one group's
   perilous innovation is another's prudent repair) lands.
9. **Where the argument leads next.** State the course's own forward objection
   (Strauss in References): Burke made the good constitution one that *grew*
   rather than one that was *designed* — natural in its manner of production,
   not in its end. If the standard is in the process, nothing outside the
   process can judge it; Providence becomes the current of events, and a
   constitution is justified by having lasted. That is the premise Hegel will
   generalise (`<Ref>` hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state) and Maistre will push
   further than Burke would go — "a constitution which is made for all nations
   is made for none", and the English constitution is no model but an accident
   (`<Ref>` maistre-and-bonald). Close by saying what the reader can now do:
   state Burke's case in its epistemic form and locate where it rests on
   judgment.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **Price's three rights against the statutes** (S1 l.435–833): a three-row
   table — the claimed right / what the Declaration of Right and Act of
   Settlement actually say / Burke's inference — ending on "Privilegium non
   transit in exemplum" and "a revolution not made but prevented". The method
   (close reading of a quoted opponent and of statute) is itself the lesson's
   first worked example of document reasoning.
2. **The entailed inheritance** (S1 l.1004–1096): quote "we claim and assert
   our liberties as an entailed inheritance"; parse the legal image (entail,
   life-tenant, waste) and derive why a life-tenant may improve but not alienate;
   then "in what we improve we are never wholly new".
3. **"Twenty-four millions ought to prevail over two hundred thousand. True; if
   the constitution of a kingdom be a problem of arithmetic"** (S1 l.1719–1728)
   with the composition of the Tiers État (S1 l.1324–1451) as the evidence: the
   two numbers bound (the French population against the privileged orders),
   then Burke's point that will and interest diverge, then the "lamp-post for
   its second" line.
4. **Prejudice and the general bank** (S2 l.706–727): quote whole and
   reconstruct as a three-step argument (each man's stock of reason is small;
   the stock of ages is large and tested; a prejudice carries its reason latent
   and adds motive and affection). Pair with "we have made no discoveries … in
   morality".
5. **"Sophisters, economists, and calculators" and the drapery** (S2
   l.273–397): the 6 October scene in two sentences (date, what happened — the
   crowd at Versailles, the queen's flight through the palace), then the
   argument the rhetoric carries: manners are "the supports of law", and a
   politics that strips them must rule by "the gallows". Then "swinish
   multitude" (l.437–439) in its sentence, with what the sentence is about.
6. **Real rights vs metaphysical rights** (S1 l.1964–2097): the list of real
   rights; "equal rights; but not to equal things" with the five shillings and
   five hundred pounds; the refracted rays; "in proportion as they are
   metaphysically true, they are morally and politically false" — parsed as a
   claim about application, not truth.
7. **The partnership of the living, the dead and the unborn** (S2 l.1043–1078)
   set beside the three contracts the reader has (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau): who
   the parties are, whether they can be present, whether the contract can be
   revised — the one-line consequence: "the temporary possessors and
   life-renters" may not "cut off the entail".
8. **The forecast ledger** (S2 l.383–384; S4 l.829–837; S1 l.1719–1760; S3
   l.1982–2203): four predictions, each with Burke's stated ground and the
   year it was written, against what happened (Terror 1793–94; Bonaparte
   1799) — with iii and iv marked "tested in `assignats`".
9. **Paine's dying bird and Wollstonecraft's antipathy to reason** — two short
   quotations, dated (1790, 1791), with the sourcing drill applied (author,
   audience, what each argues and omits).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** *Whig* and *Tory* (the two parliamentary
  connections since the 1680s — roughly the party of 1688 and parliamentary
  supremacy against the party of crown and church; Burke a Whig — one sentence);

  location — no more); *Declaration of Right (1689)* and *Act of Settlement
  (1701)* (one clause each); *entail*; *prejudice* in Burke's sense (define,
  then use only in that sense); *the National Assembly* (the body the Estates-
  General became in June 1789; by 1790 it had confiscated Church lands and was
  writing a constitution); *6 October 1789* (the Paris crowd's march to
  Versailles that brought the royal family to Paris); *the 83 departments*
  (the Assembly's redrawing of France in 1790 into roughly equal units replacing
  the old provinces); *Tiers État*; *natural aristocracy*; *little platoon*;
  *Hastings* (one sentence, `<Ref>` forward: Warren Hastings, Governor-General
  of Bengal, whom Burke impeached in 1788 for arbitrary rule). *Paine* (Thomas
  Paine, author of Common Sense, replied in Rights of Man, 1791);
  *Wollstonecraft* (Mary Wollstonecraft, replied within weeks in A Vindication
  of the Rights of Men, 1790). All may be named: they are primary interlocutors.
- **Dates:** 1688–89, 1789–90 exact (contingency is the point: Burke wrote
  before the Terror); everything else to the decade.
- **"Swinish multitude" is about learning**, not an epithet for the people;
  quote the whole sentence or not at all. Likewise "a woman is but an animal"
  is Burke stating the revolutionaries' "scheme of things", not his own view —
  a real and prevalent misreading, worth one refuting clause (small L13 slot).
- **Burke is not a reactionary and not a divine-right monarchist**: he defends
  1688, reform "in the style of the building", and traditional liberties
  (SEP 1.3; Lilla). The lesson says what he is in his own words (S3 l.819–830)
  and leaves "conservative" as a later label (the word dates from the 1830s —
  one clause; `what-conservatism-is` does the taxonomy).
- **Do not teach the assignat passages here** beyond one sentence; do not run
  the depreciation numbers; the application lesson owns them.
- **Do not teach Hastings**; one sentence under the Whig/Tory puzzle.
- **Natural aristocracy is not a defence of birth alone** — Burke's criterion
  is "virtue and wisdom, actual or presumptive" and property is defended as
  ballast and rampart, not as merit; state both halves. The occupations sneer
  (hairdresser, tallow-chandler, S1 l.1636–1645) is in the text; quote it if
  used and let the reader weigh it.
- **Period prejudice:** antisemitic phrases (S1 l.1568–1584, 1623; S2
  l.606–632, 1340) are not needed by any argument and should be left out
  (L12a); if the "Jew brokers" sentence is quoted for the sociology, the
  lesson names it as period prejudice in one clause and moves on.
- **The forecast is partly retrospective glory**: Burke also predicted things
  that did not happen (the departments would not obey Paris; the Revolution
  could not pay a biscuit). The ledger shows both columns; the application
  lesson judges.
- **"Epistemic before moral"** is the course's organising thesis (SEP 1.2,
  Hamilton; Oakeshott and Hayek later). State it as the course's own and ground
  it in S1 l.2045–2064 and S2 l.706–727; do not name Hamilton or Hayek in the
  body.
- **Strauss's historicism charge is a forward hook**, one paragraph; the
  argument is run in the Hegel lesson. Do not name Strauss.
- **Names ONLY in References:** Strauss, Mansfield, Muller, Hamilton (SEP),

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval, warm-up (E3).* Give Price's three rights and, for each, the
   statute or fact Burke sets against it; then say which of Locke's chapters
   (`<Ref>`) Price is in effect quoting and why Burke thinks 1688 does not fit
   it.
2. *Document reasoning (sourcing drill).* The "general bank and capital of
   nations and of ages" passage: author, date, audience (an open letter to a
   young Frenchman, published in London for an English readership), what it
   records (nothing), what it argues (an epistemic claim about distributed
   reason), what it omits (any way to tell a prejudice that carries latent
   reason from one that carries none), and what it can be USED to establish —
   the structure of Burke's case, not the merit of any particular prejudice.
3. *Plausible false claim to refute.* "Burke's argument is that old
   institutions are good because they are old." Name the failing step: the
   argument is about knowledge (S1 l.2045–2064, S2 l.706–727) and the rate and
   manner of change (S3 l.819–830, S4 l.1778–1790), and Burke explicitly
   demands reform of the "peccant part" (S1 l.646–667) — age is evidence of
   tested function, not a value.
4. *Boundary probe (self-application).* Take Burke's maxim that no general
   principle can be applied without circumstance and apply it to itself. Then
   take his probabilistic claim that the remote effects of innovation are
   more often bad than good, and ask what evidence could support it. The
   reader should find that the argument survives only as a claim about burden
   of proof and rate, not as a rule — and that this is what S3 l.819–830 says.
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* From "all men have equal rights; but not to
   equal things" (S1 l.1981–1984) and "a share in power is conventional" (S1
   l.1991–2028), derive Burke's position on the franchise, and then test it
   against his own "virtue and wisdom, actual or presumptive" (S1 l.1652–1674):
   does the criterion license the landed conclusion, and what further premise
   is needed?
6. *Synthesis / counter-argument (Paine).* Someone claims, with Paine, that
   Burke's partnership of generations is "the tyranny of the dead over the
   living". Name two facts the claim fails to account for (the life-tenant may
   improve; the unborn are parties too, so the living are trustees not slaves)
   and state what would have to be true for it to survive (that only present
   consent can bind — which is Locke's and Rousseau's premise, and exactly what
   Burke denies).
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `maistre-and-bonald`.** Burke says the English
   constitution is the model France should have "improved into" and that 1688
   was a necessity rightly handled. Suppose a critic grants Burke everything
   about design and prejudice and then asks: if no generation can design a
   constitution, how did the English come by theirs — and if the answer is
   "accident and growth", on what ground can England be a model for anyone?
   The reader should reach the thought that Burke's argument, pushed one step,
   denies that any constitution is made by men at all. The solution says in
   one sentence that the next lesson's Savoyard exile takes exactly that step
   and quarrels with Burke over the Act of Settlement, with the `<Ref>`.

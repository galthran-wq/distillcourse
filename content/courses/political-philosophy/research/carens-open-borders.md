# Research brief: carens-open-borders (Carens: the case for open borders)

status: planned
module: nation-and-immigration
type: core
goal: Analyze the argument that borders should generally be open from three liberal starting points, and the communitarian reply it answers

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `the-nation-and-its-defenders` the reader holds the premise under attack: a bounded "we" prior to politics, with a right to decide who joins, held by a conservative and a liberal nationalist; Scruton's "to include is to exclude"; Miller's weak cosmopolitanism and compatriot partiality. From earlier modules the three starting points Carens uses are already in hand as the reader's own equipment: Locke's `labour-theory-of-property` and `consent` (`locke-property-and-consent`), Mill's `harm-principle` and the utilitarian weighing of everyone's good (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`), and Rawls as the orthodoxy Scruton answered — "distributed by whom?", the Philosophical Appendix (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`). They have never read Rawls; that gap is stated, not hidden.
- **Contributes:** One move: that the right to exclude is not a premise liberalism shares but a conclusion it cannot reach. From three liberal starting points that disagree about almost everything else — Nozick's property rights, Rawls's hypothetical contract, utilitarian aggregation — the same conclusion follows: free movement is a basic liberty, restriction needs justification, and birthright citizenship in a rich country is the modern form of feudal privilege. The lesson reconstructs all three routes at full strength, locates the one restriction Carens allows (public order, narrowly), and then takes the strongest reply from inside liberalism's own house — Walzer's communitarian case for membership — and shows how Carens answers it.
- **Hands off:** Carens has shown that the pre-political "we" cannot ground exclusion *from liberal premises*. He has not shown that there is no other ground. The next lesson (`miller-strangers-in-our-midst`) accepts Carens's premise of moral equality and argues that a people's self-determination over membership follows anyway; Murray's lesson asks whether the "we" either of them assumes still exists. The open problem the reader carries: Carens concedes nonideal theory — national security, the sheer size of demand — and the reader should leave asking how large that concession is.
- **Spine step:** "The live question: what the nation is, who may join it, and whether the West can survive on liberal principles alone — Scruton, Miller and Murray against Carens, Deneen against liberalism itself, and 2015 as the test"

  - "The Communitarian Challenge" pp. 264–270: the "wrenched out of context" objection answered (liberal theories once excluded women and workers; principles have implications their authors did not see — that is what makes social criticism possible, p. 265); **Walzer's membership argument** (states "simply free to take strangers in (or not)", constrained by mutual aid, the duty to offer guest workers citizenship, and no expulsion of inhabitants); Carens's four replies — (a) the **internal-migration test** (New York vs Waycross, Georgia; Seattle vs Vancouver; California keeping out poor Oklahomans in the Depression, l. 797–816 — cities and states are open yet distinctive, and we deny them the right to exclude); (b) **the club analogy and the public/private line** — the white shopkeeper may exclude from his home but not his store (l. 872); (c) if guest workers must be offered citizenship, liberal principles equally forbid restrictive immigration; (d) the **paradox of liberal culture** — our culture is liberal, so starting from our tradition means starting from universal equal worth; Walzer himself makes universal claims.
  - Conclusion pp. 270–271: open borders as a goal; current restrictions "like feudal barriers to mobility… protect unjust privilege" (l. 963); members keep special rights; **"If people want to sign the social contract, they should be permitted to do so"**; most people do not love to move (l. 983), so claims to move are rarely frivolous; open immigration changes a community's character but leaves it a character; Forsyth County whites have no right to exclude to preserve their way of life.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: borders have guards, the guards have guns, a Mexican farm worker who crosses is arrested, and a Canadian philosopher asks by what right. The newcomer meets it as a demand for justification where none is usually thought needed: everyone assumes a state may exclude; the lesson asks what argument that assumption rests on.

1. **Why isn't sovereignty enough?** The obvious attempt: a state controls its territory (the reader has `sovereignty` from `bodin-sovereignty`), so it may exclude. It fails because sovereignty tells us *who decides*, not *what is right*: Bodin's sovereign may make any law, but the course has spent twenty lessons asking which laws are just. Carens's move is to ask the question *inside* the theories liberals already hold about justice. Because: if liberalism cannot justify the guards from its own premises, the guards need a premise liberalism does not have — which is what last lesson's "we" claimed to be.
2. **Route one — property.** Start from the reader's Locke (`<Ref locke-property-and-consent>`): I own what I have laboured on; the state exists to protect that. Attempt: "It's our country" — the nation owns its land. It fails because a *collective* property right over the whole territory is exactly what the entitlement theory denies: if the people as a whole own the land, they may also redistribute it, which Nozick's whole theory forbids. The farmer who hires the Mexican workers owns his farm; the state protects his contract and the worker's; a citizen has no right to protection from competitive disadvantage. Because: rights to exclude attach to owners, as individuals, over their property — and nobody owns the country.
3. **Route two — the contract behind a veil.** Introduce Rawls's device with the caveat: imagine choosing the rules of a society without knowing your place in it (your class, talents, religion); you would protect basic liberties first and permit inequality only where it helps the worst off. Carens's step: *where you were born* and *whether you already hold a passport* are facts of exactly the kind the veil hides — "arbitrary from a moral point of view". Attempt to stop it: Rawls meant a closed society. Carens concedes this and extends him anyway, with the argument that the parties would see free movement as a basic liberty — like religious freedom, it may be essential to a plan of life (love, faith, work, culture), and within a society we already treat free movement so. Because: a liberty that someone's whole life may depend on is not a liberty the parties would trade away for the comfort of those already inside.
4. **The one brake: public order.** Attempt: then any fear justifies closing the border. Carens disciplines it with Rawls's test: liberty may be restricted only for the sake of liberty; restriction needs a "reasonable expectation" of damage based on "evidence and ways of reasoning acceptable to all"; only to the extent necessary; and the riots of resentful citizens do not count — a just liberty cannot be withdrawn because others will act unjustly in response. Nonideal theory widens the brake a little (national security; the sheer size of demand) and Carens's history narrows it again: the same fears were voiced about Catholics, Jews, Asians and Africans, and were wrong. Because: a theory of justice cannot let the fear of the unjust set the terms.
5. **The three usual reasons, answered.** Birth entitles — arbitrary. Citizens' prosperity — only if they would fall below the level the excluded would have had, and liberty has priority anyway. Culture — this is where the course's own thread returns: to protect a way of life by exclusion is *perfectionism*, ranking one form of life above others, and no one behind the veil accepts it; Carens's example is Athenian culture, which rested on slavery (`<Ref aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery>` — the reader has met the premise it rested on). Because: the veil forbids ranking lives by the culture they come from.
6. **Route three — the calculus.** The reader's Mill: everyone counts for one (`<Ref mill-liberty-and-its-limits>`). An alien's gain counts the same as a citizen's loss; labour mobility raises aggregate welfare; the costs to citizens count but are unlikely to outweigh. The only interesting problem is which preferences count — the sadist's, the White Australia voter's — and even on the crudest count, Carens says, the balance favours much more openness than any state allows. Because: aggregation has no place to hide a passport.
7. **The strongest OBJECTION — from the communitarian.** Here Walzer enters at full strength, and the seam must say the register changes: Carens's three theories all treat the individual as prior to the community; Walzer denies it. A political community is like a club or a neighbourhood: its members made it, it has a character, and self-determination means the members decide who joins; strangers are owed mutual aid, guest workers must be offered citizenship, no one may be expelled — but admission is the community's to give. This is the last lesson's premise in liberal dress. Carens's four replies, in order: (a) cities and provinces are open and distinctive — if Georgia may not keep out New Yorkers nor Oregon Californians, the distinctiveness of a way of life does not depend on closure; (b) the club analogy crosses the public/private line — the shopkeeper may exclude from his home but not his store, and the state is the store; (c) Walzer's own concession about guest workers proves the principle; (d) the paradox: Walzer says start from *our* culture — but our culture is liberal, so its tradition commits us to the equal worth of the stranger; Walzer himself makes universal claims. The lesson gives Walzer's position two paragraphs before any reply.
8. **What Carens concedes and refuses.** Concedes: this is ideal theory; nonideal conditions (security, numbers) license some restriction; members keep special rights and obligations; open borders would change a community's character. Refuses: that any of this grounds the present level of closure, which protects privilege "like feudal barriers". "If people want to sign the social contract, they should be permitted to do so." Close on the exact shape of the standing disagreement: Carens has shown exclusion cannot be derived from liberal premises; he has not shown there is no other premise — and the reader already knows one candidate (last lesson's pre-political "we"), which the next lesson will try to make liberal.

Headings fall out as claims: "Sovereignty says who decides, not what is right"; "Nobody owns the country"; "Birthplace goes behind the veil"; "Public order is the only brake, and it is narrow"; "Culture, prosperity and birth: the three usual reasons answered"; "Everyone counts for one"; "The club and the store: Walzer's challenge"; "What the argument concedes".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The guards and the guns** (p. 251, l. 52–68). Open with Carens's three images — Haitians in leaky boats, Salvadorans in the Arizona desert, Guatemalans in sewer pipes (anchor: 1980s Central American and Caribbean migration to the United States, the article's present) — and the question "What gives anyone the right to point guns at them?" Then state the conventional answer in one sentence before dismantling it.
2. **Feudal privilege parsed** (p. 252, l. 90–91; p. 270, l. 963). Work the analogy as a parallel: a noble's son inherits land, status and immunity by birth; a citizen of a rich country inherits a labour market, a legal order and a life expectancy by birth. The disanalogies the reader must find (the citizen's status is not legally hereditary within the state; it is exclusive only at the border) are the exercise material.
3. **The farmer and the Mexican workers** (pp. 253–254). Reconstruct as a three-party case — farmer, worker, citizen competitor — and ask whose rights the minimal state protects. Show that "it's our country" requires a fourth party, the nation as owner, that the theory does not contain.
4. **Building the global original position** (pp. 255–258). A `<FigureSequence>` set piece: list what the veil hides in Rawls (class, talents, conception of the good), then add Carens's two items (birthplace; present citizenship), then ask the parties the one question — admit free movement as a basic liberty or not. Then Carens's concession (Rawls's closed system, l. 240) and his reply: the device's critical power depends on not reading it as an interpretation of existing values; the Forsyth County racists (Georgia, 1987: an all-white county whose residents attacked a civil-rights march) are still treated as free and equal under it, so shared liberal values are not its precondition.
5. **The public-order test applied** (pp. 259–260, l. 428–458). Take three candidate threats — a feared collapse of public services; the hostility of current residents; a security risk — and run each through the four conditions (reasonable expectation; evidence acceptable to all; only to the extent necessary; not the antagonistic reaction of citizens). Show which passes, which fails and why. This is the lesson's worked example and the model for an exercise.
6. **Athens and perfectionism** (pp. 261–262, l. 589–594). Carens's culture argument: the claim "our culture is worth protecting by exclusion" is perfectionism; his example is Athenian culture, which "many believed" required slavery — the reader has read the argument that it did (`<Ref aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery>`). One paragraph; the point is formal (the veil forbids ranking lives), not historical.
7. **Oregon, California and Waycross, Georgia** (pp. 266–267, l. 797–816). The internal-migration test as a list of real cases: Depression-era California turning back poor Oklahomans (the "Okies", 1930s — the "bum blockade" of 1936); Oregon's resentment of Californians; New York vs Waycross; Seattle vs Vancouver. Then the inference: distinctive, and open. Carens's own concession that internal migration strains localities is part of the set piece.
8. **The shopkeeper** (p. 268, l. 872). One paragraph: the white shopkeeper may not exclude Black customers from his store though he may from his home; freedom of association governs the private sphere, equal treatment the public; the state is public. The hinge of the reply to Walzer.
9. **The paradox of liberal culture** (pp. 268–269). The Buddhist monk in medieval Japan would find Rawls and Nozick unintelligible; we would not; we are products of a liberal culture; so Walzer's "start from our tradition" lands on universal equal worth. Short.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first mention** (L11): *original position* and *veil of ignorance* (defined in one sentence each, as Carens states them, with the no-Rawls caveat); *ideal vs nonideal theory* (what justice requires when everyone complies vs when they do not); *difference principle* (inequalities permitted only if they benefit the worst off); *priority of liberty*; *entitlement theory* / *minimal state* (Nozick: a state limited to protecting persons, property and contracts); *perfectionism* (a theory that ranks ways of life and lets the state promote the best); *communitarian* (the position that the community and its shared meanings are prior to the individual's rights); *Forsyth County* (set piece 4); *Okies* (set piece 7); *White Australia policy* (the restriction of non-European immigration to Australia, 1901–1960s/70s); *guest workers* (Walzer's *metics* — temporary labour admitted without a path to citizenship; the German *Gastarbeiter* of 1961–73 — one clause, Murray's lesson gives the history); *brain drain* (the emigration of a poor country's trained people).
- **No Rawls in the dossier.** Every statement about Rawls is "as Carens states it"; do not import the two principles in their exact wording from memory without flagging it as the standard formulation outside the dossier. Miller's one clause (ch. 1 pp. 14–15) that Rawls's "realistic utopia" eliminated immigration as a problem may be used to say Rawls never addressed the question.
- **Do not put Miller's replies here.** The cantilever argument, the park warden, territorial jurisdiction and Abizadeh belong to `miller-strangers-in-our-midst`. The only reply this lesson answers is Walzer's (communitarian), because Carens answers it himself. Scruton's "to include is to exclude" is recalled by Ref as the sentence Carens turns around, not re-argued.
- **Full strength means full strength.** The course has read conservatives at length; this is the liberal position taught as its author would want. No "some would say" hedging inside the reconstruction; the objection comes in its own section, at equal strength, and the concessions are Carens's own, stated as his.
- **Walzer at full strength too.** The reader is predisposed to Walzer (he restates last lesson's premise); give his analogies their force before Carens's replies, and do not let (d), the paradox, read as a trick — Carens offers it as the most important reply and the writer should present the counter (that a tradition may contain universal principles *and* a practice of membership, both authentically its own) as a question the lesson leaves open.
- **Contested empirical claims**: "mainstream economics holds that free labour mobility maximises aggregate gains" is Carens's report, 1987; state it as his report and note that the fiscal and wage effects are contested — Murray's lesson will meet the studies. Do not resolve it here.
- **Dates**: 1987 is the article's present (the reader should be told once that the cases — Haitians, Salvadorans, Guatemalans, Forsyth County — are the 1980s). Decade precision elsewhere.
- **Thin spots**: the utilitarian section is two pages and Carens treats it lightly; do not inflate it — one section of ~250 words. The Nozick section rests on Carens's reading of Nozick's last part (voluntary communities); the writer cannot check it against Nozick and should say the reading is Carens's.
- **Scan quality**: the text has run-together words; take quotations from the PDF (`aliens-and-citizens-1987.pdf`) when the .txt is garbled.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (E3, earlier lessons)*: Name the three starting points Carens uses and, for each, the earlier lesson that gave the reader the premise (`locke-property-and-consent`; Rawls via `scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`'s Appendix; `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`), and state in one sentence why the convergence of three rivals strengthens the conclusion.
2. *Derivation the lesson did not do*: Run the Nozickian route for *emigration* rather than immigration. Show that the same premises forbid a state from stopping its citizens leaving (Carens treats exit and entry symmetrically) — and note what this route cannot say about the brain drain, which a later lesson will press (`miller-strangers-in-our-midst`, Haiti).
3. *Boundary probe*: Take the public-order restriction to its limit. Suppose unlimited immigration would, by the cumulative effect of entirely just actions, overwhelm courts and schools so that *no one's* basic liberties could be protected. Show that Carens's test now licenses restriction — and identify exactly which of the four conditions did the work (the damage is to liberty itself, and it is not the antagonism of citizens). Then vary the case: the damage is riots by residents. Which condition fails, and why does Carens insist on it?
4. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill*: Carens's sentence "citizenship in Western liberal democracies is the modern equivalent of feudal privilege". Metadata (a Canadian political theorist, 1987, in a Catholic university's journal of political philosophy, writing for other theorists). Predict, then read: what does it record (nothing), argue (an analogy of inherited life chances), omit (that the status is not hereditary *within* the state and that feudal privilege was enforced against fellow members)? What can it be USED to establish — and what would the analogy need to survive as more than rhetoric?
5. *Claim to refute*: "Carens's argument proves too much: if birthplace is morally arbitrary, so is being born into a family, so parents may not favour their own children." Name the step that fails (the veil governs the basic structure of institutions, not personal relationships — the public/private line Carens himself draws against Walzer's club) and say what the claim gets right (Carens does need that line, and the next lesson's author will say it is drawn in the wrong place).
6. *Synthesis — counter-argument stem*: Someone claims that Walzer's communitarian challenge simply restates last lesson's pre-political "we" and therefore Carens's four replies refute Scruton and Miller as well. Name two facts the claim fails to account for (Scruton denies that the community is a *club* — it is a home no one joined by choice; Miller accepts Carens's premise of equal worth and argues from self-determination, not shared meanings), and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive.
7. **FORWARD (E7)** → `miller-strangers-in-our-midst`: Carens argues from free movement *within* a state to free movement *between* states (Oregon to Washington, so Washington to British Columbia). Write down what the internal right protects a person *against*, and ask whether a border does the same thing to the person it stops. The solution says the reader has found the fork Miller's "cantilever" argument turns on — that internal free movement protects minorities against domination by their own state (apartheid, ghettos, forced resettlement) while a border traps no one — and that the next lesson names and tests it.

# Research brief: carens-open-borders (Carens: the case for open borders)

status: planned
module: nation-and-immigration
type: core
goal: Analyze the argument that borders should generally be open from three liberal starting points, and the communitarian reply it answers

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `the-nation-and-its-defenders` the reader holds the premise under attack: a bounded "we" prior to politics, with a right to decide who joins, held by a conservative and a liberal nationalist; Scruton's "to include is to exclude"; Miller's weak cosmopolitanism and compatriot partiality. The American lessons have since tested what the bond is made of — Jay's "one united people" and Madison's `extended-republic` (`federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`), the `anglo-protestant-core` against the `american-creed` (`albions-seed-and-who-are-we`) — and the lesson just before this one read the restrictionist pole at full strength: Powell's, Raspail's and Camus's `prophecy-of-displacement` and `replacement-as-argument`, checked against the record (`powell-raspail-and-camus`). Carens is the opposite pole, and the seam can say so. The three starting points Carens uses are the reader's own equipment: Locke's `labour-theory-of-property` and `consent` (`locke-property-and-consent`); Mill's `harm-principle` and the utilitarian weighing of everyone's good (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`); and Rawls, read in full in `rawls-theory-of-justice` — the `original-position` and veil, the two principles, the priority of liberty, the `difference-principle`, the `natural-lottery` ("arbitrary from a moral point of view") — together with the conservative's "distributed by whom?" (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`).
- **Contributes:** One move: that the right to exclude is not a premise liberalism shares but a conclusion it cannot reach. From three liberal starting points that disagree about almost everything else — Nozick's property rights, Rawls's hypothetical contract, utilitarian aggregation — the same conclusion follows: free movement is a basic liberty, restriction needs justification, and birthright citizenship in a rich country is the modern form of feudal privilege. The lesson reconstructs all three routes at full strength, locates the one restriction Carens allows (public order, narrowly), and then takes the strongest reply from inside liberalism's own house — Walzer's communitarian case for membership — and shows how Carens answers it.
- **Hands off:** Carens has shown that the pre-political "we" cannot ground exclusion *from liberal premises* as he reads them. He has not shown that there is no other ground. This lesson does not decide the dispute of principle; it decides one of Carens's empirical premises (## VERDICT: few would move — the premise holds, the inference fails, strong), and that verdict answers the question his nonideal concession raises: the "sheer size of demand" is large, and the concession is large with it. The principle is decided in the next lesson (`miller-strangers-in-our-midst`), which accepts Carens's premise of moral equality and finds that it does not entail a right to cross borders freely — it grounds a right to *some* adequate place, not to *this* one (Miller over Carens, on balance); Carens's global original position bypasses Rawls's own placement of self-determination at the level of peoples (§58). Murray's lesson (`murray-strange-death-of-europe`) asks whether the "we" either of them assumes still exists, and the module's application (`the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways`) puts all three to one year's record and carries the module's four-part verdict (procedure, security, principle, integration); its principle part finds that Carens's own brake, the public-order restriction, licensed more control in 2015 than was exercised.

## VERDICT

This lesson hosts a **forecast check** on Carens's side, judged in the course's voice. The dispute of principle it stages (Carens against Walzer, and against the bounded "we") is decided in `miller-strangers-in-our-midst`.

**Claim judged.** Carens (1987, p. 270, text l. 980–988): open borders threaten a community's character "only because we assume that so many people would move if they could. If the migrants were few, it would not matter"; and, after Walzer, "most human beings do not love to move".

**Verdict.**
- The premise **holds as stated**: about 84–85 percent of adults do not wish to emigrate.
- The inference that the movers would be "few" **fails**. 15–16 percent of adults, about 900 million people, say they would like to leave their country permanently (Gallup World Poll, 2023 and 2025). With roughly 3–5 percent of would-be migrants naming Germany, those who would like to move to Germany number about 27–45 million, a third or more of its population; the United States figure (about 134 million in 2025) exceeds a third of its.
- **Grade: strong.** Not established, because a stated wish is not a move.

**What decides it.** The Gallup series (an annual sample of about 145,000 adults in some 140 countries) and the one case in which moves were cheap and legal: after the EU's 2004 enlargement realised flows ran *above* the official forecast (the Home Office forecast 5,000–13,000 a year for the UK; judged in `murray-strange-death-of-europe`).

**Strongest objection.** Gallup itself warns that "desire to migrate is not the same as intent to move", and the share with plans is "much lower"; Carens's claim is about who would actually move. It does not overturn the verdict: the claim needs movers to be "few", and a small fraction of 900 million is still tens of millions; and where the cost of moving fell to near zero (2004), realised migration exceeded the forecast instead of falling below it. Carens's own nonideal clause — "the sheer size of demand" may justify some restriction — concedes the point in advance.

**Would be overturned by:** evidence that under free access realised migration runs an order of magnitude below stated desire.
- **Spine step:** "The live question: what a nation is and who may join it — Scruton and Miller on the pre-political loyalty, America as England's heir from the Federalist to Who Are We?, the prophecies of displacement from Powell to Camus, Carens and Miller on borders, Murray on Europe — and 2015 as the test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: borders have guards, the guards have guns, a Mexican farm worker who crosses is arrested, and a Canadian philosopher asks by what right. The newcomer meets it as a demand for justification where none is usually thought needed: everyone assumes a state may exclude; the lesson asks what argument that assumption rests on.

1. **Why isn't sovereignty enough?** The obvious attempt: a state controls its territory (the reader has `sovereignty` from `bodin-sovereignty`), so it may exclude. It fails because sovereignty tells us *who decides*, not *what is right*: Bodin's sovereign may make any law, but the course has spent its whole length since Aquinas asking which laws are just. Carens's move is to ask the question *inside* the theories liberals already hold about justice. Because: if liberalism cannot justify the guards from its own premises, the guards need a premise liberalism does not have — which is what last lesson's "we" claimed to be.
2. **Route one — property.** Start from the reader's Locke (`<Ref locke-property-and-consent>`): I own what I have laboured on; the state exists to protect that. Attempt: "It's our country" — the nation owns its land. It fails because a *collective* property right over the whole territory is exactly what the entitlement theory denies: if the people as a whole own the land, they may also redistribute it, which Nozick's whole theory forbids. The farmer who hires the Mexican workers owns his farm; the state protects his contract and the worker's; a citizen has no right to protection from competitive disadvantage. Because: rights to exclude attach to owners, as individuals, over their property — and nobody owns the country.
3. **Route two — the contract behind a veil.** Recall Rawls's device in two sentences with `<Ref rawls-theory-of-justice>` (the reader has the whole argument): parties choosing the rules of a society without knowing their place in it protect equal basic liberties first and permit inequality only where it helps the least advantaged, because talents and starting places are "arbitrary from a moral point of view". Carens's step: *where you were born* and *whether you already hold a passport* are facts of exactly the kind the veil hides — "arbitrary from a moral point of view". Attempt to stop it, at full strength and from Rawls's own text: Rawls built his theory for a society "conceived for the time being as a closed system isolated from other societies" (§2), and when he did extend it (§58) he convened a second original position of *representatives of nations*, whose first conclusion was the equality of peoples and "the right of a people to settle its own affairs" — self-determination, not free movement. Carens concedes the first point and answers the second by the logic of the device itself: if class and talent go behind the veil because they are arbitrary, birthplace must too, and a device whose critical power is limited to what its author applied it to is no longer critical. The lesson states both and says what the reply achieves: it shows the device *can* be run over individuals across borders; it does not show why Rawls was wrong to run it over peoples, which is the objection as put. That is the point the next lesson decides (`miller-strangers-in-our-midst`: the global original position bypasses §58); Carens then extends Rawls with the argument that the parties would see free movement as a basic liberty — like religious freedom, it may be essential to a plan of life (love, faith, work, culture), and within a society we already treat free movement so. Because: a liberty that someone's whole life may depend on is not a liberty the parties would trade away for the comfort of those already inside.
4. **The one brake: public order.** Attempt: then any fear justifies closing the border. Carens disciplines it with Rawls's test: liberty may be restricted only for the sake of liberty; restriction needs a "reasonable expectation" of damage based on "evidence and ways of reasoning acceptable to all"; only to the extent necessary; and the riots of resentful citizens do not count — a just liberty cannot be withdrawn because others will act unjustly in response. Nonideal theory widens the brake a little (national security; the sheer size of demand) and Carens's history narrows it again: the same fears were voiced about Catholics, Jews, Asians and Africans, and were wrong. Because: a theory of justice cannot let the fear of the unjust set the terms.
5. **The three usual reasons, answered.** Birth entitles — arbitrary. Citizens' prosperity — only if they would fall below the level the excluded would have had, and liberty has priority anyway. Culture — this is where the course's own thread returns: to protect a way of life by exclusion is *perfectionism*, ranking one form of life above others, and no one behind the veil accepts it; Carens's example is Athenian culture, which rested on slavery (`<Ref aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery>` — the reader has met the premise it rested on). Because: the veil forbids ranking lives by the culture they come from.
6. **Route three — the calculus.** The reader's Mill: everyone counts for one (`<Ref mill-liberty-and-its-limits>`). An alien's gain counts the same as a citizen's loss; labour mobility raises aggregate welfare; the costs to citizens count but are unlikely to outweigh. The only interesting problem is which preferences count — the sadist's, the White Australia voter's — and even on the crudest count, Carens says, the balance favours much more openness than any state allows. Because: aggregation has no place to hide a passport.
7. **The strongest OBJECTION — from the communitarian.** Here Walzer enters at full strength, and the seam must say the register changes: Carens's three theories all treat the individual as prior to the community; Walzer denies it. A political community is like a club or a neighbourhood: its members made it, it has a character, and self-determination means the members decide who joins; strangers are owed mutual aid, guest workers must be offered citizenship, no one may be expelled — but admission is the community's to give. This is the premise of `the-nation-and-its-defenders` in liberal dress. Carens's four replies, in order: (a) cities and provinces are open and distinctive — if Georgia may not keep out New Yorkers nor Oregon Californians, the distinctiveness of a way of life does not depend on closure; (b) the club analogy crosses the public/private line — the shopkeeper may exclude from his home but not his store, and the state is the store; (c) Walzer's own concession about guest workers proves the principle; (d) the paradox: Walzer says start from *our* culture — but our culture is liberal, so its tradition commits us to the equal worth of the stranger; Walzer himself makes universal claims. The lesson gives Walzer's position two paragraphs before any reply.
8. **What Carens concedes and refuses.** Concedes: this is ideal theory; nonideal conditions (security, numbers) license some restriction; members keep special rights and obligations; open borders would change a community's character. Refuses: that any of this grounds the present level of closure, which protects privilege "like feudal barriers". "If people want to sign the social contract, they should be permitted to do so." State the exact shape of the standing disagreement: Carens has shown exclusion cannot be derived from liberal premises as he reads them; he has not shown there is no other premise — and the reader already knows one candidate (the module's opening premise, the pre-political "we"), which the next lesson makes liberal. How much the nonideal concession concedes depends on how many would come, which the next step checks.
9. **The weighing — "Most people do not love to move": the premise holds, the inference fails.** The forecast check (rule 7; 300–500 words of lesson). *The forecast, dated:* Carens 1987, p. 270 — open borders threaten a community's character only "because we assume that so many people would move if they could"; "most human beings do not love to move", so the migrants would be few. *The record:* the Gallup World Poll — 16 percent of adults worldwide (over 900 million) said in 2023 that they would like to leave their country permanently, up from 12–15 percent in 2011–18; 15 percent (about 900 million) in 2025, the United States named by 15 percent of them (about 134 million), Germany, the UK, France and others by about 3–5 percent each. For Germany that is about 27–45 million people. The one natural experiment with cheap legal movement, the 2004 enlargement, ran above its forecast (one clause, `<Ref murray-strange-death-of-europe>` forward). *The strongest objection:* a wish is not a move (Gallup's own caveat); the reply: "few" fails even at a small fraction of the wish, and the cheap-move case went above forecast, not below. *Verdict and grade:* the premise holds as stated (about 84 percent do not wish to move); the inference that the movers would be few fails. **Strong.** Close the step on what it does to the argument: Carens's nonideal clause ("the sheer size of demand") was written for exactly this case, so his own theory licenses more restriction than his ideal theory suggests — how much is the principle the next lesson decides.

**Conclusion.** State the verdict in plain words with its grade: most people do not wish to move, as Carens said, but those who do number some 900 million, so the movers under open borders would not be few — strong, though not established, because a wish is not a move. Then the hand-off: Carens has shown that exclusion cannot be derived from his reading of liberal premises; whether equal moral worth entails a right to cross borders at all is decided next, in `miller-strangers-in-our-midst`.

Headings fall out as claims: "Sovereignty says who decides, not what is right"; "Nobody owns the country"; "Birthplace goes behind the veil"; "Public order is the only brake, and it is narrow"; "Culture, prosperity and birth: the three usual reasons answered"; "Everyone counts for one"; "The club and the store: Walzer's challenge"; "What the argument concedes"; "Most people do not love to move — but 900 million would".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The guards and the guns** (p. 251, l. 52–68). Open with Carens's three images — Haitians in leaky boats, Salvadorans in the Arizona desert, Guatemalans in sewer pipes (anchor: 1980s Central American and Caribbean migration to the United States, the article's present) — and the question "What gives anyone the right to point guns at them?" Then state the conventional answer in one sentence before dismantling it.
2. **Feudal privilege parsed** (p. 252, l. 90–91; p. 270, l. 963). Work the analogy as a parallel: a noble's son inherits land, status and immunity by birth; a citizen of a rich country inherits a labour market, a legal order and a life expectancy by birth. The disanalogies the reader must find (the citizen's status is not legally hereditary within the state; it is exclusive only at the border) are the exercise material.
3. **The farmer and the Mexican workers** (pp. 253–254). Reconstruct as a three-party case — farmer, worker, citizen competitor — and ask whose rights the minimal state protects. Show that "it's our country" requires a fourth party, the nation as owner, that the theory does not contain.
4. **Building the global original position** (pp. 255–258). A `<FigureSequence>` set piece: list what the veil hides in Rawls (class, talents, conception of the good), then add Carens's two items (birthplace; present citizenship), then ask the parties the one question — admit free movement as a basic liberty or not. Then Carens's concession (Rawls's closed system, l. 240) and his reply: the device's critical power depends on not reading it as an interpretation of existing values; the Forsyth County counter-demonstrators (Georgia, 1987: residents of an all-white county who attacked a civil-rights march — Carens calls them "white racists"; the lesson quotes the phrase as his) are still treated as free and equal under it, so shared liberal values are not its precondition.
5. **The public-order test applied** (pp. 259–260, l. 428–458). Take three candidate threats — a feared collapse of public services; the hostility of current residents; a security risk — and run each through the four conditions (reasonable expectation; evidence acceptable to all; only to the extent necessary; not the antagonistic reaction of citizens). Show which passes, which fails and why. This is the lesson's worked example and the model for an exercise.
6. **Athens and perfectionism** (pp. 261–262, l. 589–594). Carens's culture argument: the claim "our culture is worth protecting by exclusion" is perfectionism; his example is Athenian culture, which "many believed" required slavery — the reader has read the argument that it did (`<Ref aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery>`). One paragraph; the point is formal (the veil forbids ranking lives), not historical.
7. **Oregon, California and Waycross, Georgia** (pp. 266–267, l. 797–816). The internal-migration test as a list of real cases: Depression-era California turning back poor Oklahomans (the "Okies", 1930s — the "bum blockade" of 1936); Oregon's resentment of Californians; New York vs Waycross; Seattle vs Vancouver. Then the inference: distinctive, and open. Carens's own concession that internal migration strains localities is part of the set piece.
8. **The shopkeeper** (p. 268, l. 872). One paragraph: the white shopkeeper may not exclude Black customers from his store though he may from his home; freedom of association governs the private sphere, equal treatment the public; the state is public. The hinge of the reply to Walzer.
9. **The paradox of liberal culture** (pp. 268–269). The Buddhist monk in medieval Japan would find Rawls and Nozick unintelligible; we would not; we are products of a liberal culture; so Walzer's "start from our tradition" lands on universal equal worth. Short.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first mention** (L11): *original position*, *veil of ignorance*, *difference principle*, *priority of liberty* — all introduced in `rawls-theory-of-justice`: re-anchor each with one clause and the Ref (L11d), never re-define at length; *ideal vs nonideal theory* (what justice requires when everyone complies vs when they do not — Rawls's distinction, introduced there too; one clause); *entitlement theory* / *minimal state* (Nozick: a state limited to protecting persons, property and contracts); *perfectionism* (a theory that ranks ways of life and lets the state promote the best); *communitarian* (the position that the community and its shared meanings are prior to the individual's rights); *Forsyth County* (set piece 4); *Okies* (set piece 7); *White Australia policy* (the restriction of non-European immigration to Australia, 1901–1960s/70s); *guest workers* (Walzer's *metics* — temporary labour admitted without a path to citizenship; the German *Gastarbeiter* of 1961–73 — one clause, Murray's lesson gives the history); *brain drain* (the emigration of a poor country's trained people).
- **Rawls is recalled, not re-taught, and is checked against his own text.** The reader has `rawls-theory-of-justice`; this lesson quotes Rawls only where Carens's move depends on the wording (§2's closed system, §34's public-order test, §39's liberty for liberty's sake, §58's self-determination of peoples) and keeps the rest to a clause and a Ref. ## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (E3, earlier lessons)*: Name the three starting points Carens uses and, for each, the earlier lesson that gave the reader the premise (`locke-property-and-consent`; `rawls-theory-of-justice`; `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`), and state in one sentence why the convergence of three rivals strengthens the conclusion.
2. *Derivation the lesson did not do*: Run the Nozickian route for *emigration* rather than immigration. Show that the same premises forbid a state from stopping its citizens leaving (Carens treats exit and entry symmetrically) — and note what this route cannot say about the brain drain, which a later lesson will press (`miller-strangers-in-our-midst`, Haiti).
3. *Boundary probe*: Take the public-order restriction to its limit. Suppose unlimited immigration would, by the cumulative effect of entirely just actions, overwhelm courts and schools so that *no one's* basic liberties could be protected. Show that Carens's test now licenses restriction — and identify exactly which of the four conditions did the work (the damage is to liberty itself, and it is not the antagonism of citizens). Then vary the case: the damage is riots by residents. Which condition fails, and why does Carens insist on it?
4. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill*: Carens's sentence "citizenship in Western liberal democracies is the modern equivalent of feudal privilege". Metadata (a Canadian political theorist, 1987, in a Catholic university's journal of political philosophy, writing for other theorists). Predict, then read: what does it record (nothing), argue (an analogy of inherited life chances), omit (that the status is not hereditary *within* the state and that feudal privilege was enforced against fellow members)? What can it be USED to establish — and what would the analogy need to survive as more than rhetoric?
5. *Claim to refute*: "Carens's argument proves too much: if birthplace is morally arbitrary, so is being born into a family, so parents may not favour their own children." Name the step that fails (the veil governs the basic structure of institutions, not personal relationships — the public/private line Carens himself draws against Walzer's club) and say what the claim gets right (Carens does need that line, and the next lesson's author will say it is drawn in the wrong place).
6. *Synthesis — counter-argument stem*: Someone claims that Walzer's communitarian challenge simply restates the pre-political "we" of `the-nation-and-its-defenders` and therefore Carens's four replies refute Scruton and Miller as well. Name two facts the claim fails to account for (Scruton denies that the community is a *club* — it is a home no one joined by choice; Miller accepts Carens's premise of equal worth and argues from self-determination, not shared meanings), and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive.
7. *Attack the verdict*: State in its best form the case that Carens's "few would move" survives the Gallup data — desire is not intent, intent is not a move, moving costs money and ties that a survey answer does not, and the realised flows of 2004 came from countries with free access but also with large wage gaps that most of the world's would-be migrants share. Then say what evidence would be needed to overturn the verdict: under what conditions of access, measured how, and what ratio of realised moves to stated desire would make the movers "few". The solution grants that the grade is strong and not established for exactly the first reason, and states that the verdict would fall only if realised migration under free access ran an order of magnitude below stated desire — which the one cheap-move case does not show.
8. **FORWARD (E7)** → `miller-strangers-in-our-midst`: Carens argues from free movement *within* a state to free movement *between* states (Oregon to Washington, so Washington to British Columbia). Write down what the internal right protects a person *against*, and ask whether a border does the same thing to the person it stops. The solution says the reader has found the fork Miller's "cantilever" argument turns on — that internal free movement protects minorities against domination by their own state (apartheid, ghettos, forced resettlement) while a border traps no one — and that the next lesson names and tests it.

# Research brief: carlyle-heroes (Carlyle: hero-worship, the cash-nexus and the aristocracy of talent)

status: planned
module: democracy-and-its-critics
type: core
goal: Analyze Carlyle's argument that history is the work of great men, that society rests on reverence for them, and that laissez-faire has replaced every bond with cash payment

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From the two Tocqueville lessons: a levelled world in which "nothing rises and nothing falls", the fear of a mild administration over a flock, and remedies that are all *institutions* (`equality-of-conditions`, `soft-despotism`, `art-of-association`). From `decline-of-regimes`: Plato's `democratic-man`. From `burke-reflections`: `natural-aristocracy` (leisure, property and habit of command fit some to rule). From `machiavelli-effectual-truth`: the `armed-prophet` who compels belief by force. From `english-revolution-as-test`: Cromwell as a sovereignty without legitimacy. Each is a `<Ref>` and a clause.
- **Contributes:** The module's one anti-institutional answer. Where Tocqueville asks what can hold a majority back, Carlyle asks who is worth obeying, and answers that every society is a *Hero-archy*, that an age which cannot recognise its great men is governed by quacks whatever its ballot boxes, and that laissez-faire England has dissolved every bond but cash payment. The lesson reads the 1840 lectures and the 1843 tract as one argument and names honestly what it asserts and never proves.
- **Hands off:** Mill (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`) answers that genius needs liberty, not reverence — "all that genius can claim is freedom to point out the way" — and the lesson closes on that reply, developed next lesson. Marx (`marx-manifesto`) borrows the cash-nexus and labels exactly this critique "feudal socialism — half lamentation, half lampoon"; the forward exercise lands the reader there. `british-franchise` tests "ballot-boxes alter the figure of our Quack" against 1867. Later: Ortega's noblesse oblige (`ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`) and the `nietzsche-on-the-right` extra lesson, which records Nietzsche's contempt for Carlyle.
- **Spine step:** "Democracy arrives as a fact and is judged: Tocqueville foresees soft despotism, Carlyle demands heroes, Mill defends liberty, Marx predicts class war — and the British franchise tests who was right"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: 1840, a Scottish historian, a London audience; the history of the world as biography; worship of great men the one thing that never died out; a society that has lost it governed by quacks; three years later England starving amid plenty, payment the only tie.

1. **If no one is born to rule, who should?** The reader arrives with Tocqueville's levelled world (Ref): ranks gone, "nothing rises and nothing falls", and a set of institutional counterweights. Carlyle's first move denies the premise. Society is not a field of equals held in check; it is a *Hero-archy* — a graded order of reverence — or it is nothing. "Universal History… is at bottom the History of the Great Men"; what the mass achieves is "the outer material result… of Thoughts that dwelt in the Great Men". The "because": worship is "transcendent wonder", wonder is the root of religion, and religion is "what a man practically believes" — so a society's dignities are "bank-notes" that circulate only while backed by real gold (admiration of real worth). Revolutions come "when most of the notes are forged".
2. **Does the age make the man, or the man the age?** The obvious modern answer — the great man is a "creature of the Time", the fuel would have burned anyway — is the one Carlyle attacks first, because if it were true the whole hero-archy would be an illusion. His reply is an image, not an argument: the great man is "the lightning", the time "dry fuel… waiting for the lightning"; "those are critics of small vision who cry: See, is it not the sticks that made the fire?" The lesson names this honestly (key_idea 5): an assertion defended by metaphor and by a counter-example (hero-worship survived even in Voltaire's Paris — the ladies plucking hairs from his fur in 1778). State what would count as evidence either way and that Carlyle offers neither.
3. **How do you tell a hero from a quack?** The reader needs a criterion, and Carlyle gives one: *sincerity*. Not a claimed sincerity — "the Great Man… cannot help being sincere" — but the inability to evade "the great Fact of Existence". Worked case: Mahomet (Lecture II) against the Impostor theory — "a false man cannot build a brick house", let alone a religion that has lasted twelve centuries; Kadijah "believed in me when none else would"; the patched cloak and the repaid three drachms. Then the uncomfortable corollary he does not hide: Nature is the umpire — "you must first get your sword" — and "in the long run it conquers nothing that does not deserve to be conquered": wheat survives, chaff does not. Name the might-makes-right strand as his, quote it, and set beside it the Christian and Thrasymachean versions the reader knows (Ref `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`): Carlyle says Nature ratifies right by success; Thrasymachus says success *is* right. Not the same claim; the difference is Carlyle's whole wager.
4. **What is a king, then, and what are ballot boxes for?** Lecture VI: "the business of all social procedure is to find the Ablest Man… and enthrone him"; every relation of authority is either "a Divine Right or a Diabolic Wrong". His etymology — King = *Kön-ning*, Can-ning, Able-man — is false (the writer says so in a clause; the word is from a root meaning kin), and he leans on it. Liberty and Equality are a "transitional" reaction to forged authority, not a goal. The decisive passage for this module: "if we are as Valets, and do not know the Hero when we see him, what good are all these? A heroic Cromwell comes; and for a hundred and fifty years he cannot have a vote from us… By ballot-boxes we alter the *figure* of our Quack; but the substance of him continues." The Valet-World (from the proverb "no man is a hero to his valet" — because the valet is a valet). Cromwell is the test (Ref `english-revolution-as-test`): dismissing the Rump was "Reality sweeping the Formulas away"; "the same power that made you a Parliament made me Protector". Say what this makes of the course's sovereignty module: Hobbes grounded authority in protection, Burke in inheritance, Carlyle in *recognition* — the governed must be able to see worth.
5. **England 1843: what does a nation look like when the notes are forged?** Past and Present begins from a fact, not a theory: "England is full of wealth… yet England is dying of inanition." The official return for Lady-day 1842 counts 1,429,089 paupers in England and Wales — roughly one person in eleven of a population near sixteen million (bind it in the sentence; the exact figure lives in the figure). The workhouse "Bastille" at St Ives where able men sit idle; Stockport parents who poisoned three children for the burial money; Peterloo (Manchester, 1819 — a reform meeting charged by cavalry) remembered as the people's one question, "What do you mean to do with us?" The Sphinx: a nation that mistakes Semblance for Fact perishes; "there is nothing else but justice"; noble conservatism conserves the just, ignoble conserves the parchment.
6. **What dissolved the bonds? The cash-nexus.** The mill-owner's "Did I not pay them?" is Cain's answer; the Irish widow refused by every charity "proves her sisterhood" by dying of typhus and infecting seventeen — "she was not their sister". "Cash-payment is not the sole nexus of man with man — how far from it!" (the phrase he coined to deny it, and that Marx would borrow to assert it; Ref forward). Two gospels rule: Mammonism (the working rich, who believe in nothing but success) and Dilettantism (the idle rich, who believe in nothing at all). Laissez-faire is "the Gospel of Despair"; "supply-and-demand is not the one Law of Nature"; over-production is really mal-distribution; horses are fed and men are not. Here the reader meets Carlyle's redefinition of liberty (III.xiii): Gurth the thrall with his brass collar was better off than the free labourer with "liberty to die by starvation"; true liberty is "that a man be made to walk the right path". State it at full strength and note what it costs (a liberty defined by the one who decides the path).
7. **What is to be done — and why not the vote?** Not "Morrison's Pill": there is no measure, only a change of regimen; "given a world of Knaves, to produce an Honesty" is impossible by "Reform Bill, ballot-box or Charter". Hence "Democracy, which means despair of finding any Heroes to govern you". The positive programme has two halves. (a) The *Aristocracy of Talent*: government by the able — but the able cannot be machine-selected; "the eye for talent presupposes reverence" (Bobus Higgins the sausage-maker cannot recognise a better man), so reform "begins at home". (b) The *Captains of Industry*: the mill-owner who becomes a "Law-ward", a Chivalry of Work, bound to his men by permanent contract rather than day-wage (Plugson of Undershot the buccaneer against Taillefer the Norman who sang at Hastings); and a state that organises — inspectors, sanitation, a Teaching Service, an Emigration Service — "the army proves a state can organise". Note the structure: Carlyle's remedies are *persons* where Tocqueville's were *institutions*.
8. **OBJECTION at full strength — Mill.** On Liberty III (Ref forward): genius is "the salt of the earth" and the age's danger is the "ascendancy of mediocrity" — so far Mill agrees. But "the initiation of all wise or noble things comes… from individuals" only where they are *free*: genius "can only breathe freely in an atmosphere of freedom"; and the demand for reverence is exactly the "hero-worship which applauds the strong man of genius for forcibly seizing on the government of the world… All that genius can claim is freedom to point out the way." Mill's second blade: the Valet-World argument is unfalsifiable — if the people choose badly it proves they are valets, if well it proves a hero appeared; nothing could count against it. Carlyle's reply, from his own text: freedom "to point out the way" to men who cannot see is "liberty to die by starvation" in another dress; and Mill's own worry about mediocrity concedes the diagnosis. Where it gives way: the criterion. Sincerity is tested by success, success by Nature's verdict, and Nature's verdict is read by the one who already believes — a circle the lesson shows rather than denounces. Then the two forward objections in a paragraph each: Marx's label (the aristocracy "waved the proletarian alms-bag in front for a banner" and the people "saw on their hindquarters the old feudal coats of arms") — the critique of capitalism is right and the cure is the past; and Nietzsche's (Ref `nietzsche/skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man`) — a man who shouts his sincerity is not sincere.

Headings as claims: "Society is a hero-archy or it is counterfeit"; "Lightning and dry fuel: what the thesis asserts and never proves"; "Sincerity, and Nature as umpire"; "By ballot-boxes we alter the figure of our Quack"; "England starving amid plenty"; "Cash-payment the sole nexus"; "No Morrison's Pill: Captains of Industry and an aristocracy of talent"; "Mill's reply: genius needs liberty, not reverence".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **Bank-notes and gold** (Heroes I, 347–367): dignities as notes, gold as real worth, forgery as the cause of revolutions — reconstructed in three steps and then applied to 1789 as Carlyle applies it (Lecture VI: Luther, Cromwell, the French Revolution as three acts of Protestantism — revolts against *false* sovereigns preparing true ones).
2. **Lightning and fuel** (I, 369–406): the two causal models of history set side by side, with Carlyle's one piece of evidence (Voltaire in Paris, 1778) and the question what evidence would decide it.
3. **Mahomet's sincerity** (II): the Impostor theory stated as Carlyle's contemporaries held it; "a false man cannot build a brick house"; Kadijah; "if the Sun stood on his right hand and the Moon on his left"; the mended cloak. Then "you must first get your sword" — and the wheat/chaff reading of twelve centuries of Islam. Flag the dated and condescending treatment of Islam in one sentence; use only what the argument needs.
4. **Cromwell and the Valet-World** (VI, 646–693, 1155–1208): Cromwell's reputation 1660–1840 as the lesson's worked case of a nation unable to recognise its hero — the gibbeted body, Hume's verdict, "for a hundred and fifty years he cannot have a vote from us"; the Rump dismissed. Ref `english-revolution-as-test` for the facts; this lesson supplies only Carlyle's reading.
5. **Midas and the workhouse** (P&P I.i): the St Ives workhouse scene (fifty able men sitting enchanted in the sun), the Stockport case, the Lady-day return; read as Carlyle reads them — wealth "enchanted", belonging to nobody.
6. **The Irish widow** (III.ii, from Dr Alison's Edinburgh report): refused by every charity, dies of typhus, infects seventeen — "proves her sisterhood by dying". Carlyle's one argument that cash is not the only bond: the bond exists whether acknowledged or not, and collects its debt.
7. **Gurth's collar and the two liberties** (III.xiii, 7507–7541): the thrall of Ivanhoe with his brass collar who "deserved" his master Cedric, against "liberty to die by starvation"; "true liberty… that a man be made to walk the right path". Set the two definitions as a table against Hobbes's silence of the law (Ref `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`) and Mill's (forward).
8. **Plugson of Undershot** (III.x) and the Captain of Industry (IV.iv): the buccaneer who has "made a thousand pounds" and dismisses his men, against the Norman Taillefer and the "Law-ward"; the ladder from day-wage to permanent contract. Then Morrison's Pill and Bobus Higgins (I.iv–v) as the two reasons there is no measure.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Rhetoric, not argument from premises.** Carlyle asserts and persuades by capitalised abstractions (Sincerity, Cant, Mammonism, Flunkyism, Valetism, Quack), recurring images and invented mouthpieces (Sauerteig, Teufelsdröckh, Plugson, Bobus, Sir Jabesh Windbag). The lesson must gloss every coinage at first use and must say which voices are Carlyle's own puppets. The method itself is key_idea 5: show, once, a place where the argument is carried by an image alone (link 2) and name it.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** Peterloo (St Peter's Field, Manchester, 16 August 1819 — a reform crowd of some sixty thousand charged by yeomanry, about a dozen killed); the Reform Bill (1832 — the vote to most middle-class householders; Ref nothing, `british-franchise` does it); the People's Charter (1838 — six points, universal male suffrage first) and Chartism (the mass movement for it, 1838–48); the New Poor Law (1834 — relief only inside workhouses, hence "Bastilles"); the Corn Laws (tariffs on grain, repealed 1846); laissez-faire and "supply-and-demand" (political economy's maxims; name Bentham and the "Greatest-Happiness Principle" in a clause); Mammon (the New Testament's personified wealth); Dilettante (an idler in matters that demand earnestness); Igdrasil (the Norse world-tree, Carlyle's image of the past living in the present); Valet (the proverb); Kön-ning (the false etymology); Mahomet (Carlyle's spelling — keep it in quotations, use "Muhammad" in prose); Lady-day (25 March, a quarter-day for official returns); Ivanhoe's Gurth (Scott's swineherd, 1819).
- **Numbers.** Only the Lady-day return is load-bearing (the "England starving amid plenty" claim fails without it); bind it as a share of the population in the same sentence and leave the exact figure to the figure. Do not import electorate figures (next-but-one lesson) or wage data Carlyle does not give.
- **The Quashee passage (P&P IV.v) and the 1849 "Occasional Discourse".** Carlyle's contempt for emancipated West Indian slaves entangles the wage-slavery critique with a defence of hierarchy by race. The lesson does not hide it and does not moralise: one paragraph in link 6 or 7 that quotes a clause, states plainly that the argument for being "made to walk the right path" was applied by its author to Black freedmen, and lets the reader weigh what the Gurth argument licenses. Nothing more; nothing less.
- **The might-makes-right strand** (Nature as umpire; "strength surviving is the measure of worth", Lecture IV) is real and should be quoted, not softened; equally, Carlyle's "Divine Right" is explicitly a *moral* claim (the Able-man is the one who sees what is right). Keep both.
- **Cromwell.** Do not re-tell the English Revolution (Ref `english-revolution-as-test`); use only Carlyle's reading and his reputation argument. Note that Carlyle's "Theocracy" as Cromwell's aim is Carlyle's thesis, contested; References.
- **Carlyle and Mill were friends** (Mill lent him the French Revolution manuscript his maid burned; Mill reviewed Heroes); the objection in link 8 is a quarrel inside a friendship, which is why Mill's hero-worship sentence is pointed. One clause.
- **Do not present "proto-fascist" as settled.** The charge (via the Nazi-era cult of Carlyle's Frederick) exists; name it as a charge in the Conclusion or References, with the reply that Carlyle's hero is defined by sincerity and justice, not by will. `nietzsche-on-the-right` carries the reception problem for Nietzsche; this lesson need not for Carlyle.
- **Disraeli and Young England.** Marx's III.1 names "Young England"; the lesson anchors it in a clause (Disraeli's group of young Tory MPs, early 1840s, whose novels — *Sybil* 1845, "two nations" — made the same Condition-of-England argument) and holds the rest for `marx-manifesto`.
- **Do not re-teach:** Burke's natural aristocracy (one clause: Burke's rests on property and leisure, Carlyle's on ability alone — the shift the SEP calls "from substance to function"); Machiavelli's armed prophet (one clause: Carlyle's Cromwell is Machiavelli's Moses with sincerity added); Tocqueville's soft despotism (Ref).
- **L8a:** Hamilton (SEP), Fawcett, Alison (as the source Carlyle quotes — may be named as Carlyle names him), Scott (as the author of Ivanhoe — a named object, fine). Mill and Marx are primary and may be named. "Carlyle argues" is the body's only authority voice.
- **Dossier:** full for both books; the Fourth Estate / Chinese examination passages of Lecture V are in the shard `heroes-s5-lecture5-man-of-letters.txt` (no sidecar — the file is there). Carlyle's "Shooting Niagara" (1867), the direct response to the Second Reform Act, is NOT in the dossier (public domain, Gutenberg) — `british-franchise` may want it; flag, do not use here.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up).** State Carlyle's criterion for a hero and the three places in the lesson where it is tested (Mahomet, Cromwell, Rousseau), and say in one clause what Rousseau's case shows about the criterion (a sincere man can set the world on fire — sincerity is necessary, not sufficient, by Carlyle's own account).
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill.** The Lady-day 1842 return ("In-door 221,687, Out-door 1,207,402, Total 1,429,089"): who produced it (the Poor Law Commissioners), for whom, why; what it records (relief given on one day), what it argues (nothing), what it omits (the destitute who got no relief; seasonal variation; Ireland and Scotland); what Carlyle uses it to establish and whether it bears that weight. Bind the figure to a population base in the solution.
3. **Boundary probe.** Set reverence to zero: a nation of perfect Valets, with a sincere Able-man present. By Carlyle's own argument (Bobus Higgins; "the eye for talent presupposes reverence"), can he be found at all — and if not, what does "reform begins at home" ask of each reader, and why does Carlyle say this is not a measure? Then set the Able-man to zero: what does the hero-archy become (a forged issue), and does the theory then predict revolution or soft despotism (Ref Tocqueville)?
4. **Claim to refute.** "Carlyle wants a strong state." Name the step that fails: the state proposals of P&P IV.iii are means for Captains, not a sovereign; his remedy is recognition of persons, not organisation of offices ("constitutions plentiful as blackberries"); and his liberty is being "made to walk the right path" by a *man* one reveres, not by an administration — the opposite of the tutelary power.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped — FORWARD (E7).** Read Carlyle's indictment of the Unworking Aristocracy and of cash-payment (III.viii–x) and write, in two sentences, what a critic who shared the diagnosis but believed the cure lay in the future rather than the past would say about it. The solution gives Marx's "half lamentation, half lampoon… the old feudal coats of arms" with a `<Ref>` to `marx-manifesto`, where the reader will find the very phrase "cash payment" in his hands.
6. **Counter-argument stem (synthesis).** "Cromwell's reputation 1660–1840 proves the Valet-World thesis." Name two facts the claim must account for (regicide and military rule gave reasons to condemn him that are not failures of recognition; his reputation rose in the 1840s without any change in the electorate's reverence — Carlyle's own lectures did it), and state what would have to be true for it to survive (that the Victorian revaluation itself was an act of hero-worship, which is Carlyle's claim).
7. **E3 — earlier lessons.** Burke's natural aristocracy (Ref `burke-reflections`) and Carlyle's aristocracy of talent both deny that election finds rulers. State what each grounds rule in (property, leisure, habit of command / ability and sincerity), what each therefore keeps of the hereditary peerage (all of it / "the Unworking Aristocracy" dismissed), and which of them Tocqueville's inheritance machine (Ref `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`) leaves standing.

# Research brief: cicero-mixed-constitution (Cicero's res publica and the mixed constitution)

status: planned
module: rome-and-christianity
type: core
goal: Analyze Cicero's definition of the republic and his case that Rome's mixed constitution is the best regime

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The reader arrives with Plato's regime cycle and democratic man
  (`decline-of-regimes`), the mixed constitution as the Laws' Spartan model
  (`platos-laws-second-best-city`, extra — so recall it with one clause, never
  assume it was read), Aristotle's six regimes and his answer "the law and the
  middle" (`aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`), and the Spartan test case
  (`sparta-as-test-case`), where a mixed regime was already weighed against
  the record. They have never met a Roman text, a Roman institution, or the
  idea that a people can be *defined*.
- **Contributes:** One move: the commonwealth is defined before it is ranked —
  res publica is res populi, and a populus is a multitude bound by agreement
  about right and common advantage — and from that definition the three simple
  forms, their perversions and the cycle are derived, so that Rome's mixture,
  built by many generations, comes out as the regime that leaves no station
  dissatisfied and so no reason for the ball to be snatched.
- **Hands off:** Two problems stay open. (1) The definition has "agreement
  about right" in it: what if right is only convention? Philus will say so
  next lesson (`cicero-natural-law`). (2) The same definition, taken strictly,
  is the knife Augustine uses to say Rome never was a republic
  (`augustine-two-cities`, XIX.21): the writer plants the clause *iuris
  consensu* clearly enough that the reader can be handed it back later.
- **Spine step:** "Rome turns Greek theory into Roman practice: Cicero defines
  the republic as the people's affair bound by agreed right, defends the mixed
  constitution, and asserts a natural law above all statutes"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: a garden outside Rome in 129 BC,
a general who destroyed Carthage asked which form of government is best; he
defines a commonwealth first, lets friends argue for the three forms, then
says Rome — none of them and all three — is best.

1. **Why should a general answer, and why in a garden?** The obvious attempt:
   this is a philosopher's question (the reader has three lessons of Plato
   and three of Aristotle), so a philosopher should answer. Cicero's preface
   (I.i–vii) refuses: virtue "exists only in use", and its noblest use is
   governing a state; a pilot who will not take the helm in calm weather
   cannot take it in a storm; the lawgiver who compels by penalty what the
   philosopher persuades a few of ranks above him. Because: the question is
   not what is best in speech but what has held a real city together. Anchor
   here: Scipio Aemilianus (c. 185–129 BC), destroyer of Carthage in 146 and
   of Numantia in 133; the Latin holidays of 129; Cicero writing in 54–51 BC,
   a generation of civil war later, and setting the talk in the past so as
   to offend no one living (Keyes p. 2–3 n. 4). Then the seam Laelius
   supplies (I.xix.31–32): the young men ask how two suns could be seen;
   Laelius asks why one state has "almost two senates and two separate
   peoples" — Tiberius Gracchus was killed four years before. The real
   question is the Gracchan split.
2. **What is a commonwealth at all?** The obvious attempt is to rank the
   forms straight away. Scipio's method (I.xxiv.38): agree what the name
   means before arguing about the thing. Res publica = res populi, "the
   property of a people"; and a people is not any crowd but a multitude
   "associated in an agreement with respect to justice and a partnership for
   the common good" (I.xxv.39). Parse the clauses: (a) a number, (b) bound by
   consent about *ius*, (c) sharing advantage. Because: without (b) and (c)
   there is only a crowd, and a crowd owns nothing. Add the origin: men
   associate from an implanted sociability, not from weakness (I.xxv.39–40)
   — the reader has Aristotle's political animal; one clause and a `<Ref>`.
   Corollary (I.xxvi.41–42): every people must have a *consilium*, a
   deliberating head, traceable to the founding cause — held by one, by the
   chosen few, or by all. That is where the three forms come from: they are
   the three places the head can sit.
3. **Which of the three heads is best?** Each is tolerable only while the
   bond of justice holds, and each has a flaw written into it
   (I.xxvi–xxviii.42–44): under a king the rest have too small a share in
   law and counsel; under the best men the many have too little liberty
   ("like slavery for a people" even in well-governed Massilia); under the
   people equality itself is unfair, because it gives no gradation to merit.
   Then the three speeches at full strength (I.xxxi–xxxv): the democrat —
   "what is a state but a partnership in right?" (quid est civitas nisi iuris
   societas), law gives equal right so citizens need equal rights; the
   aristocrat — nature makes the best rule, a pilot chosen by lot from the
   passengers is ruin, equal honour for unequal worth is "the most unequal
   equality"; the monarchist (Scipio himself) — one Jupiter, Rome's own
   kings, reason ruling the soul as a king (Archytas, I.xxxviii.59–60), even
   Laelius's own estate obeys one overseer (I.xxxix.61), and in war Rome
   itself obeys one man, the dictator. The reader should feel each wins its
   round. Scipio's verdict: if a simple form must be chosen, kingship; but
   no simple form is best.
4. **Why does no simple form last?** Because each slides into its neighbour
   (I.xxviii.44, I.xlii–xliv.65–68): a king who turns unjust is at once a
   tyrant (I.xlii.65); the aristocracy of Massilia has its neighbour the
   Thirty at Athens; a free people drunk on liberty — Plato's democratic man
   adapted here almost word for word (I.xliii.66–67: magistrates called
   tyrants unless indulgent, fathers fearing sons, asses crowding the street)
   — breeds the protector who becomes a tyrant. `<Ref>` to
   `decline-of-regimes` with one clause; do not re-teach. The ball
   (I.xliv.68): power snatched from kings by tyrants, from tyrants by
   aristocrats or people, from them by faction or tyrant. Cicero's reading
   of his own cycle: not a law of nature but what happens when a station has
   no reason to stay put.
5. **What stops the ball?** The fourth form (I.xxix.45, I.xlv.69): a balanced
   mixture in which there is something regal (consuls/dictator), something
   given to the authority of the leading men (senate), and some matters
   reserved to the judgment and will of the multitude (assemblies,
   tribunes). Because (I.xlv.69): when every citizen is "firmly established
   in his own station" there is no perverted form next door to fall into
   and no one with a motive to move the ball. The formula from Laws III.12:
   power in the people, authority in the senate. Note the asymmetry the
   reader has from `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`: Aristotle's polity
   mixes rich and poor; Cicero's mixture distributes *functions* (power,
   authority, liberty). State the difference in one sentence.
6. **How do we know the mixture works — Rome king by king (Book II).** The
   obvious attempt: design the best mixed constitution on paper (that is
   what the reader expects after Plato's Laws). Cato's thesis (II.i–ii): the
   Roman constitution is better than the one-lawgiver states (Minos,
   Lycurgus, Solon) because it was built "by many men in many generations"
   and tested by experience. Then the aetiology, each generation adding one
   institution: Romulus — senate and auspices (II.ix–x); the interregnum —
   kings chosen for virtue, not birth (II.xii); Numa — religion and clemency
   (II.xiv); Tullus — fetial law, so war needs a declaration (II.xvii);
   Servius — the centuriate assembly where all vote but the wealth-weighted
   centuries decide, "that the greatest number should not have the greatest
   power" (II.xxii.39–40); Tarquin's rape of Lucretia and Brutus — "no one
   is a mere private citizen when liberty is at stake" (II.xxv.46); consuls
   and the right of appeal, provocatio, "the root of Roman liberty"
   (II.xxxi); the secession of the plebs and the tribunes, born of a debt
   crisis (II.xxxiii); the Decemvirate of 451–450 as the counter-case — a
   state tilted toward its leaders cannot last (II.xxxvii). Tubero's
   objection (II.xxxviii–xxxix): you promised a theory and gave us a
   history. Scipio's reply: nature, not Rome, supplies the model; Rome is
   the illustration. This is the lesson's methodological payoff: Cicero
   wants the reader to see the mixture *grow*, not be designed — a point the
   course will meet again in Burke (do not name him; one forward sentence
   is enough).
7. **OBJECTION, at full strength — two of them.** (a) From inside the text
   (II.xlii–xliv and V.1–2): concord is a chord of unlike orders that
   "cannot exist without justice" — so the whole edifice presupposes that
   justice is real and shared; if justice is only the stronger's interest
   (the reader has Thrasymachus), the definition of a people is empty. Cicero
   himself parks this for Book III; the lesson does the same, ending the
   link with the question open. (b) From the record: Cicero writes in the
   50s BC that the republic has become a painting whose colours nobody
   refreshed — "we retain the name of a commonwealth but have lost the thing
   itself" (V.1–2). The best regime, by its author's own account, had
   already failed when he described it. The reply the lesson can give him:
   V.1 blames the loss of men and customs, not the design — but then the
   design did not protect itself, which is exactly the charge Bodin and
   Hobbes will press (forward sentence, no names needed: "later writers who
   deny that a mixed state is a state at all").
8. **Why is the statesman's reward not here (Book VI)?** The Dream of Scipio
   closes the work and the lesson: those who have preserved or enlarged the
   fatherland have a place in heaven (VI.13); the empire is a small island
   on a point of earth, fame cannot cross the Caucasus or the Ganges
   (VI.16–22); the soul is a self-mover, hence immortal (VI.25–26). Because:
   a republic needs men who will serve it without the republic being able to
   pay them — Cicero's answer to Glaucon's ring (`<Ref>`
   `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`, one clause). Keep it short; it is the lesson's
   coda, and it is the one part of the text that stayed in continuous
   circulation (through Macrobius), while the constitutional books lay under
   a Psalms commentary for fourteen centuries.

Headings that fall out: "Why a general, and why in a garden"; "A commonwealth
is the property of a people"; "Three places to put the head, and the flaw in
each"; "Why the ball never stops"; "The fourth form: power, authority,
liberty"; "Rome king by king: a constitution nobody designed"; "The chord
needs justice — and the colours have faded"; "The Dream: why the reward is
not here".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The two suns and the two senates** (Rep. I.xiii–xix; txt 2160–2170). The
   dialogue's pretext is a parhelion report; Laelius's lawyerly joke about an
   interdict giving both suns possession in common; his reframe to the
   Gracchan split. Tell it as the lesson's scene-setting, with the 133 BC
   killing anchored in one clause.
2. **Scipio's definition parsed clause by clause** (I.xxv.39; txt 2524–2545).
   Quote the two sentences (short, PD in the US), then a three-row table:
   multitude / consensus about ius / community of advantage — with the
   counterexample each clause excludes (a crowd at a market; a gang that
   agrees on everything but right; allies who share nothing). Mark the
   middle clause: it is the one Augustine will test.
3. **The three speeches as an argument map** (I.xxxi–xxxv). Each speaker's
   premise, inference, and best line (iuris societas; the pilot by lot;
   Laelius's overseer at Formiae). Then Scipio's rejection of each alone.
4. **The ball** (I.xliv.68; txt 3962–3976) drawn against Plato's chain from
   `decline-of-regimes`: Plato's five regimes in fixed order; Cicero's ball
   can go from tyrant to aristocrats OR to the people, i.e. the sequence is
   not fixed — a cycle with branches.
5. **Rome king by king** (Book II): a table of king → institution added →
   what it fixed (Romulus: senate/auspices → counsel; Numa: religion →
   clemency; Servius: centuries → "not the most should have the most";
   consuls + provocatio → liberty; tribunes → the plebs' lightning-rod;
   Decemvirate → the counter-case). The reader should be able to say which
   element of the fourth form each item belongs to.
6. **The faded painting** (V.1–2; txt 9076–9096). Quote "we retain the name
   of a commonwealth but have lost the thing itself" and set it against the
   dates: dialogue 129 BC, composition 54–51 BC, Caesar crosses the Rubicon
   49 BC. The best regime described as already lost.
7. **The palimpsest** (Keyes Intro, txt 378–395). Vat. lat. 5757: the Republic
   scraped off in the seventh century so Augustine's commentary on the Psalms
   could be written over it; Angelo Mai read the under-text in 1820;
   Books III, V and VI survive mostly as quotations in Augustine and
   Lactantius. The lesson's own text-criticism moment, and the irony that
   Augustine preserved the definition he attacked.
8. **The Dream's scale** (VI.16–22): the empire "a small island", fame unable
   to cross the Caucasus or the Ganges, a twentieth of the great year not yet
   passed since Romulus's eclipse. One figure, one paragraph.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know — anchor at first mention:** res publica
  (literally "the public thing"; translate once, then use "commonwealth");
  populus; consilium (the deliberating head); senate (a council of elders,
  ex-magistrates, with authority but not command); consul (one of two annual
  chief magistrates, each able to block the other); dictator (an emergency
  magistrate for six months, "master of the people"); tribune of the plebs
  (an officer of the commons, inviolable, able to veto; created after the
  plebs walked out of the city in the 490s); provocatio (the right to appeal
  a magistrate's sentence to the people); auspices (reading the gods' consent
  from birds before public acts); centuriate assembly (the army voting in
  wealth-ranked blocks); the Decemvirate (a board of ten who wrote the Twelve
  Tables in 451–450 BC and would not step down); Gracchi (Tiberius, tribune
  133 BC, killed by senators in a riot; Gaius, 121); Carthage (Rome's rival
  across the sea from Sicily, destroyed 146 BC); Massilia (Marseille, a Greek
  colony governed by a council); the Thirty (the Athenian junta of 404 BC —
  the reader has not met them: half a sentence). Plato's Laws Sparta is in an
  `extra` lesson: recall the mixed constitution from `platos-laws-second-best-
  city` with one clause and the concept's definition, assuming nothing more.
- **Do not re-teach** Plato's democratic man or Aristotle's six regimes;
  `<Ref>` each with one clause. The new thing is the *definition* and the
  *historical method*.
- **Polybius** supplied the cycle; the course.yaml note says keep him in
  References. The body says "the cycle of constitutions Cicero took from the
  Greek historians", nothing more.
- **Kingship as best simple form** (I.xxxv.54, II.xxiii) surprises readers
  who expect a republican to prefer the people. Keep it; it is in the text,
  and it is why Augustine and later monarchists could quote Cicero.
- **The text is fragmentary.** Book I has lacunae (I.xxv–xl); Books III, V
  and VI survive largely in quotations; II.xxiii–xl is patchy. Say so once;
  never present a reconstructed passage as continuous.
- **Numbers:** keep three — 129 BC (dialogue), 133 BC (Tiberius Gracchus),
  89 of 193 centuries (Servius's system). The 54–51 BC composition and 1820
  discovery are anchors, not keepers.
- **Dossier thinness:** the dossier has no Polybius, no Livy, no modern
  Roman-history survey; institutional anchors (consuls, tribunes, provocatio)
  must be stated from the Cicero text and general knowledge, and the writer
  should keep them to the half-sentence L11 requires rather than narrate the
  early Republic. The Strauss–Cropsey chapter file was empty at brief time.
- **Names only in References:** Keyes (translator), Polybius, Panaetius,
  Holton (Strauss–Cropsey), Strauss, Adamson, Zetzel/Ziegler editions if
  cited. Macrobius and Lactantius may be named as the *transmitters* of the
  text in the palimpsest set piece (they are objects of the story, not
  authorities).
- **Do not moralise** the Gracchan crisis or Cicero's own politics; the
  lesson reads a text, not Cicero's career.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (warm-up, the only one).* Write Scipio's definition from memory
   and name the clause each of these fails: a Roman mob in the forum; a
   pirate crew with a written code; Rome and Carthage under treaty. (Rep.
   I.xxv.39.)
2. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill.* The passage is written in 54–51 BC
   and set in 129 BC, in the mouth of a man dead before the author was born.
   Author, date, audience, purpose; what the choice of setting lets Cicero
   say and what it lets him avoid (Keyes p. 2–3 n. 4: to offend no one living);
   what the dialogue can and cannot be USED to establish about the Rome of
   129 — e.g. it is evidence of what an optimate of the 50s thought the
   Republic had been.
3. *Boundary probe.* Take the fourth form and set one element to zero: a
   Rome with consuls and senate but no tribunes (the Decemvirate, II.xxxvii);
   a Rome with assemblies and tribunes but no senate authority. For each,
   which perversion does Cicero's own theory predict, and does Book II
   supply a case?
4. *Claim to refute.* "Cicero's mixed constitution is Aristotle's polity
   under a Latin name." Name the step that fails: Aristotle mixes *classes*
   (rich and poor) and rests on a middle; Cicero mixes *functions* (power,
   authority, liberty) and rests on each station being satisfied. Use
   `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` (E3).
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* Run the cycle backwards: Cicero says a
   people "given a taste of liberty under monarchy is excited, not satisfied"
   (II.xxviii). Derive from the three flaws (I.xxvi–xxviii) why a *partial*
   grant of liberty is less stable than either none or the full mixture — and
   then say what the tribunate was for (Laws III.23–26).
6. *Synthesis — causal ranking.* Cicero's own diagnosis in V.1–2 blames lost
   customs and lost men. Rank, as necessary / enabling / precipitating, three
   candidate causes of the Republic's failure that the text itself supplies:
   the Gracchan split (I.xix), the centuriate assembly's wealth-weighting
   (II.xxii), the dictatorship as a standing precedent for one-man rule
   (I.xl.63). Defend the ranking; the solution shows that the text gives
   evidence for each and settles none.
7. **FORWARD (E7)** → `cicero-natural-law`. Scipio ends Day One saying
   concord "cannot exist without justice" (II.xlii.69). Suppose a sceptic
   grants everything in Book I and II but denies that justice is anything
   but custom: write the sentence in the definition of a people that his
   denial deletes, and state what is left of the res publica without it. The
   solution says: this is Philus's brief next lesson, and the clause is
   *iuris consensu*.
8. *Document reasoning — corroboration (optional 8th).* Set II.xxii (all
   vote, the rich decide) beside the democrat's "law gives equal right"
   (I.xxxii.49): do the two passages conflict, and how does Cicero's "most
   unequal equality" (I.xxxiv.53) reconcile them? What does that tell you
   about what "liberty" means in the fourth form?

# Research brief: cicero-natural-law (Cicero on natural law: Carneades's challenge and Laelius's reply)

status: planned
module: rome-and-christianity
type: core
goal: Analyze the case that justice is merely convention and Cicero's answer that true law is right reason in agreement with nature

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Thrasymachus and Glaucon — justice as the stronger's
  interest, the ring, the contract born of weakness
  (`thrasymachus-and-the-ring`); Aristotle's natural slavery and the climate
  theory (`aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`); Scipio's
  definition of a people as bound by agreement about right, and Scipio's own
  promise that Day Two will prove no commonwealth can stand without justice
  (`cicero-mixed-constitution`).
- **Contributes:** One move: the conventionalist case is given its Roman
  form — variety of customs, Rome grew by injustice, the wise man who will
  not seize an advantage is a fool — and answered not by consequences but by
  a definition of law that stands above every statute: right reason in
  agreement with nature, one for Rome and Athens, with God as its author, so
  that an unjust statute is no more a law than a robber band's rules. The
  same move licenses the Roman empire (rule over those who are better off
  ruled) and condemns the tyrant's city as no commonwealth at all.
- **Hands off:** Three doors open. (1) If true law is one for all nations,
  what are the gods of one nation — civil religion as convention or as part
  of the natural order? Laws II says a city must have gods and must not admit
  new ones; `constantine-and-representation` and `altar-of-victory` test that.
  (2) The justification of empire by natural hierarchy is exactly the premise
  Augustine will grant and then turn (XIX.21: "servitude may be advantageous
  to the provincials… why does God rule man, the soul the body?") —
  `augustine-two-cities`. (3) The hierarchy of laws, "an unjust law is no
  law", just war, the equality of all men: Aquinas, Vitoria, Burke on
  Hastings, Strauss, all later. Plant the three sentences the later lessons
  will quote.
- **Spine step:** "Rome turns Greek theory into Roman practice: Cicero defines
  the republic as the people's affair bound by agreed right, defends the mixed
  constitution, and asserts a natural law above all statutes"

## Sources to consult

- **Cicero, On the Republic III** — summaries.json index 39; text
  `research/texts/cicero/de-republica-de-legibus-loeb.txt`. Keyes (1928) is
  public domain in the US only: **short quotations, paraphrase the rest.**
  Book III is heavily fragmentary (quoted by Lactantius and Augustine);
  Keyes's headnote (txt ~7230–7290) explains the lacunae. Read:
  - III.3–7: civilization from reason and speech; statesman vs philosopher.
  - III.8–31 (txt ~7269–7960), Philus's brief for Carneades: Carneades argued
    for and against justice on consecutive days at Rome (the 155 BC embassy;
    Keyes n. and the course opening); justice conventional, not natural,
    since if natural it would be uniform like heat and cold (III.13); the
    tour of customs (III.9: Egyptians worship a bull, Taurians and
    Carthaginians sacrifice men, Gauls think it disgraceful to grow grain by
    hand, Rome forbids olives and vines across the Alps "to raise the price
    of our own", txt 7427–7430); Rome's own changing laws (III.10 Voconian
    law, txt 7481–7493); "not nature but weakness is the mother of justice",
    a pact of mutual fear (III.13); the pirate's answer to Alexander (III.14,
    txt 7639–7670); the just man reputed wicked vs the wicked reputed just
    (III.17); no state would choose "just servitude over unjust empire"
    (III.17–18); wisdom bids expansion, justice restraint, "Rome grew by
    wisdom not justice" (III.15, 24); the Numantine treaty (III.28, txt
    7853–7893, Mancinus); the seller who discloses faults is a "just fool";
    the shipwreck plank (txt 7906).
  - III.33–35 (txt 7962–8010), Laelius: "True law is right reason in
    agreement with nature; it is of universal application, unchanging and
    everlasting… there will not be different laws at Rome and at Athens…
    one eternal and unchangeable law… one master and ruler, that is, God,
    over us all, for he is the author of this law, its promulgator, and its
    enforcing judge." Then just war (III.34–35, txt 7990–8010): never
    undertaken "except in defence of its honour or its safety"; unjust if
    "undertaken without provocation"; none just "unless it has been
    proclaimed and declared, or unless reparation has first been demanded";
    "our people by defending their allies have gained dominion over the whole
    world". A state should be founded to be eternal; its death is a
    punishment (txt 8011–8030).
  - III.36–37 (txt 8050–8119), the natural-slavery justification of empire,
    preserved by Augustine: "dominion has been granted by Nature to
    everything that is best, to the great advantage of what is weak… why
    else does God rule over man, the mind over the body, and reason over
    lust?"; the mind rules the body "as a king governs his subjects", lust
    "as a master rules his slaves"; "there is a kind of unjust slavery,
    when men are slaves [who could govern themselves]" (txt 8116–8119).
  - III.43–45: a tyrant's city is no res publica (Syracuse under Dionysius,
    Athens under the Thirty, Rome under the decemvirs); "a people is a
    partnership in justice"; III.45: unrestrained popular rule is no
    commonwealth either, "the mob is a tyrant… a monster borrowing the name
    of people"; III.47 Rhodes.
- **Cicero, On the Laws I–II** (same file; Laws I from txt ~11700, Laws II
  from ~13700). Read:
  - I.15–19: why law must come from nature, not the praetor's edict or the
    Twelve Tables; "law is the highest reason implanted in nature, commanding
    what ought to be done and forbidding the opposite"; written statutes are
    law only in the popular sense (lex/legere vs nomos).
  - I.21–27: divine government conceded even by the Epicurean Atticus; reason
    common to gods and men → one commonwealth of gods and men.
  - I.28–34 (txt 12126–12142): "no single thing is so like another, so
    exactly its counterpart, as all of us are to one another… however we
    may define man, a single definition will apply to all"; no race cannot
    attain virtue given a guide; reason → right reason → law → justice.
  - I.40–41: conscience, the Furies as remorse, the lone man meeting a
    wealthy helpless traveller in a desert place (the Gyges test re-run;
    `<Ref>` the ring with one clause).
  - I.42–47 (txt ~14100–14300): against positivism — if statutes or utility
    made justice, "the votes of fools" could make robbery, adultery and
    forged wills just; the Thirty at Athens and Sulla's laws were enacted and
    unjust; the honourable and base are natural as the virtue of a tree or a
    horse is natural.
  - II.2.5 (txt 13843–13862): two fatherlands, one by nature and one by law
    (Cato of Tusculum and Rome); "for her it is our duty to die".
  - II.4.8–10: law is "not a product of human thought nor an enactment of
    peoples but something eternal", the primal mind of God, older than
    writing (Horatius Cocles held the bridge under no statute; Lucretia's
    rape was wrong before any written law forbade it).
  - II.5.11–13 (txt 14255–14275): the deadly statutes of nations "no more
    deserve to be called laws than the rules a band of robbers might pass in
    their assembly", or an unskilled doctor's poison a prescription; "Law is
    the distinction between things just and unjust, made in agreement with
    Nature".
  - II.6.14: true law cannot be repealed; the senate struck down the Titian,
    Apuleian and Livian laws in one sentence, proving they were never laws.
  - II.7.15–16 (txt 14478–14500): the proem to the religious code — citizens
    must believe the gods rule; belief is useful: oaths, treaties, deterrence,
    "how sacred an association of citizens becomes when the immortal gods are
    made members of it, either as judges or as witnesses". And II.8.19 (txt
    14564–14580): "No one shall have gods to himself, either new gods or
    alien gods, unless recognized by the State… They shall preserve the rites
    of their families and their ancestors." Read these two for the hand-off
    only (one paragraph in this lesson); they are the frame `altar-of-victory`
    tests.
  - II.11.28 (txt 15052–15062): deify virtues, not vices — Athens's temple to
    Disgrace and Insolence, Rome's altar to Fever; II.14.34 (txt 15367): in
    war "justice and good faith shall be supreme".
- **Augustine, City of God XIX.21** — index 17; `research/texts/cityofgod-

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: a Greek sceptic argued at Rome on
consecutive days first that justice is natural and then that it is not, and
was asked to leave; Cicero gives the second speech to a friend — different
laws everywhere, the wise man who does not seize an advantage is a fool, Rome
grew great by injustice — and then the reply that founded the idea of a law
above the law.

1. **Why is a Roman general's circle arguing against justice at all?** The
   obvious attempt: Day One (previous lesson) settled that Rome's mixture is
   best. But Scipio closed Day One by saying concord "cannot exist without
   justice" (`<Ref>` `cicero-mixed-constitution`, one clause) — so the whole
   Day-One argument is hostage to whether justice is real. Anchor Carneades
   (c. 214–129 BC, head of the sceptical Academy at Athens) and his visit to
   Rome in 155 BC on an embassy, where he argued both sides on successive days
   and Cato had the philosophers sent home; and Philus, who agrees to argue
   the brief he disowns "as one touches pitch" so that the reply can be
   heard. Because: a case must be heard at full strength before it can be
   answered, and this is the lesson's own rule too.
2. **Philus's case: the variety of customs.** If justice were natural it
   would be the same everywhere, like heat and cold (III.13); instead Egypt
   worships a bull, Carthage sacrifices children, the Gauls reap other men's
   fields, and Rome herself forbids the Gauls to grow olives to keep her
   prices up (III.9). Even Roman law changes: the Voconian law made it
   unjust for a daughter to inherit what was just the year before (III.10).
   The reader has met the argument from variety nowhere yet in this course;
   state it cleanly. Where it fails (hold the reply for link 6): variety of
   *opinion* about justice is not variety of *justice*; the Greeks had
   already noted that men disagree about weights too — but that reply is
   not available until "nature" has been distinguished from "custom", which
   is link 5's job.
3. **Philus's case: utility against justice.** The sharper half. "Not nature
   but weakness is the mother of justice" — a pact of mutual fear (III.13;
   the reader hears Glaucon: one `<Ref>` clause). Wisdom bids a people
   expand, justice bids it hold back; "Rome grew by wisdom, not justice"
   (III.15, 24) — the Roman twist that makes the brief dangerous in
   Scipio's garden. Then the pirate to Alexander: the same wickedness makes
   you harass the whole world (III.14). Then the two thought-experiments: a
   seller who tells the buyer the house is unhealthy and the slave a thief
   is a just fool (III.28 via Lactantius); two shipwrecked men and one plank
   — does the just man drown? (txt 7906). Then the Numantine treaty: Mancinus
   kept faith and was handed over; Pompeius broke it and prospered. No state
   would choose "just servitude over unjust empire" (III.17–18). This is
   Thrasymachus in Roman dress and the lesson should say so in one sentence
   — the standing realist challenge the course keeps answering.
4. **Why can the reply not be made from consequences?** The obvious attempt:
   show that justice pays (the honest trader keeps customers; Rome's good
   faith won allies). Philus has blocked it: the just man reputed wicked is
   tortured and blinded, the wicked man reputed just is honoured (III.17) —
   exactly Glaucon's perfectly unjust man who seems just. Any defence from
   payoff concedes that justice is instrumental, which is Philus's thesis.
   Because: a conventionalist can always re-describe a reward as the reason.
   The reply must therefore say what justice *is*, not what it earns.
5. **Laelius's reply: a law that is not a statute.** The lacuna here is real
   (Laelius's speech survives in fragments); the lesson builds his reply
   from III.33 and Laws I–II, saying so. First the distinction: the word
   "law" names two things — the statutes a people votes (the Twelve Tables,
   the praetor's edict, which occupy "a small and narrow corner", Laws I.17)
   and "the highest reason implanted in nature, commanding what ought to be
   done and forbidding the opposite" (Laws I.18). Then the chain (Laws I.23,
   33): reason is shared by gods and men → right reason → law → justice →
   one commonwealth of gods and men. Then the sentence (Rep. III.33): true
   law is right reason in agreement with nature, universal, unchanging,
   everlasting; it cannot be repealed by senate or people; there will not be
   one law at Rome and another at Athens; God is its author, promulgator and
   judge. Parse the clauses: (a) reason not will; (b) nature not enactment;
   (c) one not many; (d) God not the people as source. Because: only a law
   of this kind can be the standard by which the statutes are judged — and
   the reader already judges statutes (link 6 will show them doing it).
6. **How does the reply meet the two halves of Philus's case?** Against
   variety: the test of nature vs custom (Laws I.28–33) — "no single thing
   is so like another… as all of us are to one another"; one definition
   applies to every man; reason is common to all, and only "bad habits and
   false beliefs" twist minds; so variety is corruption of custom, not
   evidence against nature (the Greeks' lesson that the right way is found
   by appeal from the ancestral to the older — nature as the ancestor of all
   ancestors — may be stated as the course's own, with Strauss in
   References). Against utility: the reductio (Laws I.42–45) — if votes or
   utility made justice, then "the votes of fools" could make robbery,
   adultery and forged wills just; the Thirty's decrees and Sulla's were
   enacted and unjust; a statute that commands poison is no more a law than
   a robber band's assembly rules or a quack's prescription (Laws II.5.13).
   The reader has done the work the argument needs: they called the Thirty
   unjust before any definition told them to. Here the lesson's one
   `<KeyFact>`: an unjust statute is not a law — the sentence Aquinas,
   Vitoria and Burke will quote (forward clause, no names).
7. **What follows for the city — three consequences, each at full
   strength.** (a) *A tyrant's city is no commonwealth* (III.43–45): Syracuse
   under Dionysius was magnificent and "no res publica"; a people is a
   partnership in justice, so where justice is gone there is no people; and
   the same holds for the mob, "a monster borrowing the name of people"
   (III.45). Tie back to Scipio's definition: *iuris consensu* now means
   consent about *true* right, not any agreed rule. (b) *Just war* (III.34–35):
   only for defence or redress, declared, after reparation has been demanded,
   with faith kept even to an enemy (Laws II.14.34); and the Roman gloss, "by
   defending their allies our people have gained dominion over the whole
   world". (c) *Empire as rule over those better off ruled* (III.36–37):
   nature grants dominion to the best for the advantage of the weak — God
   over man, mind over body, reason over lust; the mind rules the body as a
   king his subjects and lust as a master his slaves; so provinces are
   rightly ruled when "lawless men are prevented from doing harm", and there
   is unjust slavery only when the enslaved could govern themselves. Keep
   this at full strength: it is Aristotle's natural slavery in Roman dress
   (`<Ref>` `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`, one clause),
   now doing the work of justifying an empire — and the reader should feel
   that the same premise that condemns the tyrant licenses the conqueror.
8. **OBJECTION, at full strength, from two sides.** From Philus's side,
   still standing: Laelius has defined law so that its author is God and its
   promulgator is reason — but the sceptic asked for *evidence* that such a
   law exists, and received a definition and a feeling of conscience (Laws
   I.40). Cicero himself, as a member of the sceptical Academy, elsewhere
   doubts what Laelius here asserts (state as the course's own; Strauss in
   References). From the other side, a reader who grants natural law:
   Laelius's third consequence shows that "right reason" at Rome concluded
   that Rome should rule — a law "the same at Rome and at Athens" that
   happened to put Rome on top. The lesson concedes the force of both and
   gives Cicero's one reply: natural law condemns Rome too when she breaks
   faith (the Numantine case cuts both ways; Laws I.42 names Sulla), so the
   standard is not Rome's interest even if Rome's advocates used it.
9. **What about the gods of one city?** The hand-off. Laws II builds a
   religious code for Scipio's best state: citizens must believe the gods
   watch (useful for oaths and treaties, II.7.15–16); no one may have "new
   gods or alien gods unless recognized by the State" (II.8.19); ancestral
   rites are kept; virtues may be deified, vices never (II.11.28). The
   lesson states the tension in one paragraph and leaves it: one law for all
   nations, but each city its own gods. A sect that refuses the city's gods
   because its God is the only one will test exactly this
   (`constantine-and-representation`).

Headings that fall out: "Why Scipio's garden must hear the case against
justice"; "Different laws everywhere: the argument from custom"; "Weakness is
the mother of justice: the argument from advantage"; "Why consequences cannot
answer"; "Two things called law"; "True law is right reason in agreement
with nature"; "Robbers' rules are not laws"; "Three consequences: the tyrant,
the just war, the empire"; "A law the same at Rome and Athens — and Rome on
top"; "One law, many gods".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **Carneades at Rome, 155 BC** (Keyes n. at txt ~7276; III.8). Two speeches
   on two days, the second undoing the first; Cato moves the senate to send
   the Greek embassy home quickly "lest the young men be corrupted". Tell it
   in four sentences as the opening scene; then Philus's "I will argue the
   brief I do not hold".
2. **The tour of customs** (III.9; txt 7420–7435). Egypt's Apis bull, Taurian
   and Carthaginian human sacrifice, Gauls reaping others' fields, Rome
   forbidding Transalpine olives and vines "so that our own may be worth
   more". Quote only the Roman item; it is the sting.
3. **The pirate and Alexander** (III.14; txt 7639–7670): "the same wickedness
   that drives you to harass the whole world"; Augustine retells it in City
   of God IV.4 (note only).
4. **The plank and the just fool** (III.28–29 via Lactantius; txt ~7900–7910):
   two men, one plank; the seller who discloses the slave's thefts. Work as a
   pair: in each, what does Philus say the "wise" man does, and what does he
   say the "just" man does? The reader produces the conventionalist's
   definition of wisdom from the cases.
5. **Laelius's sentence parsed clause by clause** (III.33; txt 7966–7988).
   Quote the first sentence and the "not different laws at Rome and at
   Athens" sentence (short); a four-row parse: reason / nature / one /
   God — against the statute's will / enactment / many / people.
6. **The robber band's assembly** (Laws II.5.11–13; txt 14255–14275). Quintus
   concedes step by step: a thing whose absence makes a state no state is a
   good; a state without law is no state; so law is a good; so a deadly
   statute is not law. Recreate the mini-dialogue; it is the course's first
   occurrence of "an unjust law is no law".
7. **The Numantine treaty** (III.28; txt 7853–7893). Mancinus made a treaty
   to save his army, kept faith, and was handed to the enemy naked; Pompeius
   broke his and kept his standing. Both Philus and Laelius use it — Philus
   to show justice does not pay, Laelius to show faith is owed anyway. One
   case, two readings: the sourcing drill applied to an argument.
8. **The empire argument reconstructed from Augustine** (III.36–37; txt
   8050–8119, with Keyes's note that the fragments come via De Civ. Dei
   XIX.21). Lay out the analogy as a ladder: God over man, mind over body
   (king over subjects), reason over lust (master over slaves); then the
   inference to provinces; then the one concession ("there is a kind of
   unjust slavery, when men are slaves [who are capable of self-rule]").

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know — anchor at first mention:** the Academy
  (Plato's school at Athens, by Carneades's time sceptical); sceptic (one who
  argues both sides and suspends judgment); Stoics (the Greek school that
  held the cosmos is governed by reason and that living by nature is the
  good — Laelius's doctrine is Stoic; one clause suffices); Epicurean
  (Atticus, in the Laws, holds the gods do not govern — hence the joke about
  his "treason" when he concedes it); praetor's edict and the Twelve Tables
  (Rome's two forms of positive law: the annual magistrate's rules and the
  450 BC code); the Voconian law (169 BC, limiting women's inheritance);
  Numantia (a Spanish hill town that beat Roman armies; Mancinus's treaty
  137 BC, Scipio destroyed it 133); Sulla (dictator 82–79 BC, whose
  proscription laws Cicero cites as enacted injustice); the Thirty (Athens,
  404 BC); lex/nomos (Cicero's etymologies: Latin lex from legere, "to
  choose"; Greek nomos from "to distribute" — say once); fetial law
  (priests who declared war formally); provincials (the inhabitants of
  conquered territory under a Roman governor).
- **The text is fragments.** Rep. III survives mostly in Lactantius and
  Augustine; the Laws stop at III.49 and Books IV–VI are lost. Say so once,
  up front; never stitch a lacuna silently. Keyes's bracketed restorations
  are conjectures.
- **Where the summary and the text differ:** the summary's "III.22 natural
  law passage (p. 211)" is Keyes's section XXII of Book III = III.33 in the
  standard numbering (txt 7966); cite as III.33 (Keyes XXII). Likewise
  "III.23 just war" = III.34–35; "III.25 natural slavery" = III.36–37.
- **Do not re-teach** Glaucon's contract or Aristotle's natural slavery;
  `<Ref>` each. The lesson's additions are the *Roman* form (empire grew by
  injustice) and the *definition* of law.
- **The egalitarian passage** (Laws I.28–30) is often read as a charter of
  human equality. Present the text, then the standing dispute as the course's
  own: it is arguing kinship and sociality, and Cicero elsewhere ranks men
  unequal (III.36–37 in this very lesson). Do not resolve it for the reader;
  an exercise does.
- **Keep the imperial justification at full strength** (course.yaml note):
  it is not an embarrassment to be hurried past; it is the argument Vitoria
  will reject and Augustine will grant and overturn. No moralising in either
  direction.
- **Civil religion** (Laws II) is a hand-off paragraph here, not a section;
  `altar-of-victory` reads it in full.
- **Numbers:** 155 BC (Carneades), 137/133 BC (Numantine treaty, Numantia
  destroyed) — anchors; no keeper numbers in this lesson beyond dates.
- **Names only in References:** Keyes, Lactantius (as transmitter; may be
  named in the text-condition sentence as an object), Strauss, Holton,
  Adamson, Polybius. Carneades, Philus, Laelius, Scipio, Cato are characters
  in the material and are named freely.
- **Dossier thinness:** no Lactantius, no Polybius, no Stoic primary text in
  the dossier; the Stoic background must be one clause. The Strauss–Cropsey
  chapter file was empty at brief time.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (warm-up, the only one).* Reconstruct Laelius's sentence in
   four clauses from memory (reason / nature / one / God) and, for each,
   name the clause of Philus's brief it answers.
2. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill.* Most of Book III reaches us as
   quotations in a Christian writer arguing against pagan Rome (Lactantius)
   and in Augustine's attack on Scipio's definition. Metadata first; predict
   what such transmitters would preserve and omit; then: what can the
   surviving Book III be USED to establish about what Cicero argued, and what
   must stay uncertain (e.g. the balance between Philus and Laelius)?
3. *Boundary probe.* Set Laelius's fourth clause to zero: a natural law with
   no God as author or judge. What remains of the reply to Philus — does the
   reductio (robbers' rules are not laws) still work without a divine
   lawgiver, and if so, what is doing the work instead? (The solution
   observes that the reductio runs on the reader's own judgment and
   Cicero's definition of law as reason; the God-clause supplies the
   *sanction* — Laws I.40's conscience, II.7's oaths — not the
   *distinction*. Forward clause to the Hobbes lesson, where laws of nature
   without a sword are "but words".)
4. *Claim to refute.* "Laws I.28–30 shows that Cicero held all men equal and
   therefore condemned slavery and empire." Name the failing step with
   III.36–37 in hand; state what the likeness passage does establish
   (one definition of man, shared reason, capacity for virtue) and what it
   does not (equal fitness to rule).
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped — the second proof.* Cicero says a state
   is founded to be eternal and its death is a punishment (III.34). Derive
   from that and from "a tyrant's city is no commonwealth" (III.43) what he
   must say happened to Rome under the decemvirs and under Sulla: did the
   res publica *die* or *pause*? Use the definition of a people to decide.
6. *Synthesis — counter-argument stem.* Someone claims: "Natural law is
   just the ideology of the strongest state — a law 'the same at Rome and at
   Athens' that happens to make Rome the ruler." Name two facts the claim
   fails to account for (the Numantine judgment against Rome; Laws I.42
   naming Sulla's enactments unjust) and state what would have to be true for
   the claim to survive (that the standard never in practice condemned a
   Roman action — testable against the text).
7. **FORWARD (E7)** → `augustine-two-cities`. Take Scipio's definition
   (`<Ref>` `cicero-mixed-constitution`) and Laelius's result together: a
   people is bound by agreement about right, and right is what agrees with
   true law, whose author is God. Now ask the strict question: did Rome, which
   worshipped gods Laelius's own argument says are not the author of law,
   ever have "agreement about right" in this sense? Write the syllogism out.
   The solution says: this is the argument of City of God XIX.21, and the
   next-but-two lesson will show Augustine running it to the conclusion that
   Rome never was a republic — and then changing the definition.
8. *Document reasoning — corroboration (E3 reach-back).* Set Philus's
   "weakness is the mother of justice" beside Glaucon's contract in
   `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`: list what is the same, what Philus adds
   (Rome; the plank; empire), and which of Socrates's first replies, if any,
   Laelius reuses.

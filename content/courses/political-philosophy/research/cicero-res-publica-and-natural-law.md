# Research brief: cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law (Cicero: the people's affair and the law above the law)

status: planned
module: rome-and-christianity
type: core
goal: Analyze Cicero's definition of the commonwealth as a people bound by agreement about right, Carneades's case that justice is convention, and Laelius's reply that true law is right reason in agreement with nature

This brief replaces the briefs of the two dropped planned lessons
`cicero-mixed-constitution` and `cicero-natural-law` (their files stay in the
dossier until the caller deletes them; never `<Ref>` those slugs). The mixed
constitution and the cycle are now taught in `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`;
here they are recalled with a `<Ref>` and only what Cicero adds is taught.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`: the six forms and the
  cycle with its generational engine (`anacyclosis`), the mixture of consuls,
  senate and people that blocks each part (`checks-and-balances`,
  `mixed-constitution`), Lycurgus's design by reason against Rome's
  constitution learned "from knowledge gained in disaster", religion as a
  civic device (VI.56), Polybius's test of a constitution by what it achieves
  (Rome beats Sparta because it can conquer), the centuriate assembly's 89
  of 193 votes, and the open question that lesson's FORWARD exercise left:
  does a commonwealth also have to be just? From `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`:
  Thrasymachus's justice as the rulers' interest, Glaucon's bargain of the
  weak, the ring, the two statues (the just man thought unjust, the unjust
  thought just), the robber band's "remnant of justice", and the Thirty
  (Athens, 404) as Polemarchus's killers (`justice-as-advantage-of-the-
  stronger`). From `thucydides-power-and-justice`: the Athenians at Melos —
  justice only between equals in power (`athenian-thesis`). From
  `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`: the political animal and
  natural slavery with its admitted difficulty. From `decline-of-regimes`:
  Plato's democratic man. The reader has never met a Roman author, a Stoic, a
  sceptic, or the idea that a people can be *defined*.
- **Contributes:** One problem — does a commonwealth need justice? — taken in
  two steps. First the definition: a commonwealth is the affair of a people,
  and a people is not any crowd but a multitude bound by agreement about
  right and a partnership of advantage, so justice is written into what a
  commonwealth *is*. Then the attack the definition invites: Carneades's case,
  given to Philus, that justice is convention and folly — customs differ,
  justice is a pact of the weak, and Rome grew great by taking what was not
  its own. Laelius answers not by showing that justice pays but by a
  definition of law above every statute: right reason in agreement with
  nature, one at Rome and Athens, God its author — so that an unjust statute
  is no more a law than robbers' rules, a tyrant's city is no commonwealth
  at all, war is just only for defence or redress, and (the same premise,
  pointing the other way) Rome may rule those who are better off ruled.
- **Hands off:** (1) If true law is one for all nations, what of the gods of
  one city? Laws II requires a city to keep its ancestral rites and admit "no
  new gods or alien gods unless recognized by the State" — a sect whose God
  is the only one tests exactly this (`constantine-and-representation`,
  `altar-of-victory`). (2) Scipio's definition, taken strictly with Laelius's
  God, is the knife a bishop will use to prove Rome never was a commonwealth
  (City of God XIX.21, `augustine-two-cities`); plant *iuris consensu* clearly
  enough to be handed back. (3) "An unjust law is no law", just war and the
  natural-slavery title to empire return in `aquinas-law-and-kingship`,
  `vitoria-right-to-the-americas`, `burke-against-hastings` and
  `strauss-natural-right-and-history` (forward clauses, no names in the body
  beyond the slug's thinker). The next lesson in course order is
  `hellenization-of-christianity`, whose Inherits clause relies on this
  lesson's "one law for all, each city its own gods".
  (4) What this lesson decides and what it passes on (register, module 2, no
  verdict): it settles two partial points — the diversity of customs does
  not prove that justice is convention, and Laelius's "rule over those
  better off ruled" inherits natural slavery's failure. Whether a natural law
  exists, against convention, is not decided here; it is the course's long
  arc, and the modern grounds for denying it are judged in
  `strauss-natural-right-and-history`.
- **Spine step:** "Rome turns Greek theory into practice: Polybius explains its
  rise by a mixed constitution that arrests the cycle of regimes, and Cicero
  defines the commonwealth as a people bound by agreed right under a natural
  law above every statute"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: a Greek philosopher at Rome as
an envoy in 155 BC speaks two days running, for justice and then against it,
and Cato has him sent home; a century later Cicero, in a garden dialogue set
in 129 BC, asks what a commonwealth is and then must face the second speech —
every people has different laws, the wise man who passes up an advantage is a
fool, Rome grew great by taking what was not its own; the lesson follows the
definition and the reply that put a law above every statute.

1. **Why a statesman, why a dialogue, why 129 BC?** Short. The obvious
   attempt: after two Greek philosophers, a philosopher should answer.
   Cicero's preface refuses: virtue "exists only in use", and the man who
   compels by law what philosophers persuade a few of ranks above them; a
   pilot who will not take the helm in calm cannot take it in a storm.
   Anchors: Cicero (106–43 BC, consul in 63, writing in 54–51 BC as the
   republic slid toward civil war); the dialogue set in 129 BC in the
   gardens of Scipio Aemilianus (destroyer of Carthage in 146; the reader
   met him as Polybius's patron, `<Ref>` `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`) —
   and Laelius's own line that Scipio used to discuss the state "with
   Panaetius … in company with Polybius". The pretext: two suns reported in
   the sky; Laelius turns it to "two senates and two separate peoples" — the
   killing of Tiberius Gracchus four years earlier (one clause; the reader
   has him from the Polybius lesson's objection). Because: the question is
   asked by men who must hold a divided city together, not by a school.
2. **What is a commonwealth?** The obvious attempt is to rank the forms at
   once, as the reader has seen Plato, Aristotle and Polybius do. Scipio's
   method: agree what the name means first. *Res publica* is *res populi*,
   "the property of a people"; a people is not any crowd but a multitude
   "associated in an agreement with respect to justice and a partnership
   for the common good" (Rep. I.39). Parse the three clauses as a worked
   Example: (a) a number; (b) bound by consent about *ius*, right; (c)
   sharing advantage. Each excludes something (a market crowd; a gang that
   agrees on everything but right; allies who share nothing). Then the
   origin: men associate not from weakness but from "a certain social
   spirit which nature has implanted" — Aristotle's political animal
   (`<Ref>`, one clause) set against Polybius's herd that gathers from
   weakness and against Glaucon's bargain (`<Ref>` both, one clause each).
   Because: if the commonwealth exists for right, the question "does it need
   justice?" is answered in the definition — and the definition becomes the
   target. Mark clause (b): it is the clause Augustine will test.
3. **What Cicero adds to the cycle and the mixture.** One section, recall
   first: every people needs a deliberating head, which sits with one, the
   chosen, or all — Polybius's three forms and the cycle (`<Ref>`
   `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`, one clause; do not re-teach). Then only
   the additions, three of them. (i) *The cycle has branches*: power is
   "snatched" like a ball, from tyrants by aristocrats **or** the people,
   from them by a faction **or** a tyrant — not Polybius's fixed order — and
   a statesman who foresees the motion can steer it. (ii) *Each simple form
   has a flaw even when it works*: too little share in counsel under a king,
   "like slavery" for the many under the best men, and under the people an
   equality that is itself unfair, "the most unequal equality", because it
   gives no degree to merit; if a simple form must be chosen, kingship
   (state it; it surprises readers and is in the text). (iii) *The mixture
   as justice among orders*: magistrates "enough power", the leading men
   "enough influence", the people "enough liberty" (II.xxxiii.57); Servius's
   assembly in which all vote but "the greatest number should not have the
   greatest power" (the reader has the 89 of 193 from the Polybius lesson,
   now with Cicero's approval); Cato's thesis that Rome was built by many
   men over many generations (Polybius's "knowledge gained in disaster",
   recalled); and the end of Day One: concord is a harmony of unlike orders
   that "cannot exist without justice". Because: the whole construction now
   hangs on justice being real — which is what Day Two puts on trial.
4. **The case against justice, and why the garden must hear it.** Carneades
   (c. 214–129 BC, head of Plato's Academy, by then a school of sceptics who
   argued both sides and suspended judgement) at Rome in 155 BC; Philus
   agrees to report his case although he disowns it. Because: a case must be
   heard at full strength before it is answered — the course's own rule.
   Then the case in two halves, each at full strength:
   - *Variety* (III.8–10): if justice were natural it would be the same
     everywhere, like heat and cold; instead Egypt worships a bull, Carthage
     sacrificed men, and Rome forbids the peoples beyond the Alps to grow
     olives and vines "so that our own … may be the more valuable" — quote
     only the Roman item; it is the sting. Even inside Rome justice moves: the
     Voconian law (169 BC) made unjust what had been just.
   - *Folly* (III.13–29): "not nature … but weakness is the mother of
     justice", a pact of mutual fear (Glaucon's bargain in Roman dress,
     `<Ref>` one clause). Wisdom bids a people expand, justice bids it give
     back; Rome took the wise course and would be again "a miserable
     poverty-stricken village" if it returned what it took. The pirate to Alexander: the same wickedness
     makes you harass the whole world. Three cases that make the reader
     choose: the seller who discloses that the house is unhealthy is a fool,
     if he hides it unjust but wise; two shipwrecked men and one plank; the
     Numantine treaty (137 BC: Mancinus kept faith and was handed over to the
     enemy, Pompeius broke his and was not). "No people would be so foolish as not to prefer to be unjust masters
     rather than just slaves." State in one sentence that this is the
     Athenian thesis at Melos (`<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-justice`) argued
     by a philosopher about Rome itself, inside Scipio's garden.
5. **Why a reply from consequences fails.** The obvious reply: justice pays
   (honest traders keep customers; Rome's good faith won allies). Philus has
   blocked it with the two reputations: the just man thought wicked is
   tortured and banished, the wicked man thought just is honoured (III.17) —
   Glaucon's two statues (`<Ref>` `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`). Any defence
   from payoff concedes that justice is valued for its results, which is
   Philus's thesis. Because: the reply must say what justice *is*, not what it
   earns. Short section; it is the hinge.
6. **Laelius's reply: two things called law.** Say once that Laelius's speech
   survives in fragments and that the lesson completes it from Cicero's own
   On the Laws, in his own voice. First the distinction: "law" names the
   statutes a people votes (the Twelve Tables, the praetor's edict — "a small
   and narrow corner") and "the highest reason, implanted in Nature, which
   commands what ought to be done and forbids the opposite" (Laws I.18).
   Then the chain (Laws I.23): reason shared by gods and men → right reason →
   law → justice → one commonwealth of gods and men. Then the sentence
   (Rep. III.33, `<Quote>`), parsed clause by clause as an Example: reason,
   not will; nature, not enactment; one for Rome and Athens, not many;
   God as author, not the people. Because: only a law of this kind can be the
   standard by which statutes are judged — and the reader already judges
   statutes, as link 7 shows.
7. **How the reply meets the two halves.** Against *variety*: men are more
   alike than any two things in nature, "a single definition will apply to
   all" (Laws I.29–30); variety is the corruption of custom by "bad habits
   and false beliefs", not evidence that nature is silent — variety of
   opinion about justice is not variety of justice. Against *folly*, the
   reductio (Laws I.42–44, II.5.11–13): if votes or utility made justice, the
   votes of fools could make robbery, adultery and forged wills just; the
   Thirty's decrees and the law that made Sulla dictator were enacted and
   unjust (the reader called the Thirty unjust in the Plato lesson before any
   definition told them to — `<Ref>`); and a deadly enactment is no more a
   law "than the rules a band of robbers might pass in their assembly" or a
   quack's poison a prescription. Here the lesson's one `<KeyFact>`, which
   must earn its box (L15) by its forward consequence: an unjust statute is
   not a law — the sentence a theologian will build a hierarchy of laws on
   and an impeachment will cite against an empire (forward clause, slugs
   `aquinas-law-and-kingship`, `burke-against-hastings`). Then conscience
   (Laws I.40–41): the Furies as remorse; the man alone in a desert with a
   rich, helpless traveller — the ring rerun (`<Ref>`), answered not by
   detection but by what he is.
8. **Three consequences — and the same premise pointing two ways.** Tie back
   to the definition: *iuris consensu* now means agreement about *true*
   right, not any agreed rule. (a) *A tyrant's city is no commonwealth*
   (III.43–45): Syracuse under Dionysius was magnificent and "no
   commonwealth"; nor Athens under the Thirty, nor Rome under the decemvirs;
   nor the mob, "that monster which falsely assumes the name of a people".
   And Cicero's own verdict on his time (V.1–2): the republic as a painting
   whose colours have faded; "we retain only the form of the commonwealth, but have long since lost
   its substance". A commonwealth can cease to exist while its name
   survives. Contrast with Polybius in one sentence (`<Ref>`): Polybius gave
   every constitution a natural decay; Laelius says a state should be founded
   to last for ever and that its death is not natural but a punishment
   (III.34). (b) *Just war* (III.34–35; Laws II.14.34): only for defence or
   redress, declared, after reparation has been demanded, with good faith
   kept — introduce `just-war` here — and the Roman gloss, "our people by
   defending their allies have gained dominion over the whole world". (c)
   *Empire as rule over those better off ruled* (III.36–37): nature gives
   dominion to the best "to the great advantage of what is weak" — God over
   man, mind over body, reason over lust; the mind rules the body as a king
   his subjects and lust as a master his slaves; there is "a kind of unjust
   slavery" only when the ruled could govern themselves. Keep this at full
   strength: it is Aristotle's natural slavery (`<Ref>`, one clause) doing
   the work of justifying an empire, and the reader should feel that the
   premise that condemns the tyrant licenses the conqueror.
9. **OBJECTION, at full strength, from both sides.** (a) *Philus's side,
   still standing:* Laelius was asked for evidence that such a law exists and
   answered with a definition and the feeling of conscience. Cicero himself
   belonged to the sceptical Academy, asks its members in the Laws not to
   attack the argument lest they "play too great havoc with it", and says the
   doctrine seems to him only usually right — so the author may be holding
   Laelius's teaching as the one a decent commonwealth needs rather than one
   he can prove (state as the course's own reading; Holton and Strauss in
   References). (b) *From a reader who grants natural law:* "right reason"
   at Rome concluded that Rome should rule — a law the same at Rome and
   Athens that happens to put Rome on top. The reply available in the text:
   the standard condemns Rome too — the Numantine case cuts both ways, and
   the Laws name Sulla's enacted law unjust — so it is not Rome's interest,
   even when Rome's advocates use it. Say what the exchange settles, as a
   fact about the arguments. On (a): Laelius did not answer Philus's demand
   for evidence — he offered a definition and the testimony of conscience —
   so the existence of such a law is not established in this lesson. But one
   half of Philus's case is answered: variety of customs is variety of
   opinion about justice, and does not by itself prove that justice is
   convention (the course states this as its own result). On (b): the reply
   holds — the standard did condemn Roman acts (Numantia, Sulla's law), so
   it is not simply Rome's interest. Then the point the course states
   against Laelius himself: the title to empire in (c) above, rule over
   those better off ruled, inherits natural slavery's failure (`<Ref>`
   `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`): no criterion, stated
   in advance, picks out the peoples who are better off ruled, and in
   Rome's case the mark was conquest. Whether natural law exists is carried
   forward, not closed (Hands off 4).
10. **One law, many gods** (hand-off paragraph, not a section of its own).
    Laws II gives the best commonwealth a religious code: citizens must be
    persuaded the gods watch, because oaths and treaties depend on it and an
    association of citizens becomes "sacred" when the gods are its judges and
    witnesses; no one may have "new gods or alien gods, unless recognized by
    the State"; ancestral rites are kept; virtues may be deified, vices never.
    One sentence beside Polybius's religion as a check on the multitude
    (`<Ref>`): Cicero gives the same institution a reason inside natural law.
    State the tension and hand it on: one law for all nations, each city its
    own gods (judged on the documents of 384 in `altar-of-victory`). A sect that refuses the city's gods because its God is the only
    one will test it (forward clause: `constantine-and-representation`).
11. **★ The Dream of Scipio** (optional starred section; the core path must
    read correctly without it). Philus's strongest point was that justice
    does not pay here. The Republic ends with the elder Scipio, in a dream,
    showing his grandson that those who have "preserved, aided, or enlarged
    their fatherland" have a place in heaven, that nothing pleases God more
    than "assemblies … of men associated in justice, which are called
    States", and that from the stars Rome's empire is "only a small island"
    and fame cannot cross the Caucasus or the Ganges. Because: the reward
    Philus showed to be missing on earth is moved off it — Cicero's answer to
    the ring, and the one part of the work read without a break through the
    Middle Ages (through Macrobius's commentary), while the rest lay under a
    Psalms commentary until 1820.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The definition parsed** (Rep. I.39; lines 2524–2545). Quote the two
   sentences; a three-row Example: multitude / agreement about right /
   partnership of advantage, each with the counter-case it excludes; the
   "social spirit" sentence as the fourth row (origin), set against
   Polybius's herd and Glaucon's bargain.
2. **The ball against the cycle** (Rep. I.44.68; line 3969). Draw Polybius's
   fixed ring (recalled) beside Cicero's ball with two exits at the tyrant
   and two at the aristocracy/people; the reader states what the branches
   change (prediction gives way to steering).
3. **Carneades in Rome, 155 BC** (Plutarch, Cato 22; Lactantius V.14; Rep.
   III.8). Four sentences as the scene; then Philus taking up the brief he
   disowns.
4. **The olive and the vine** (Rep. III.9; ~7433). One quoted sentence: Rome
   forbidding the Transalpine peoples to plant olives and vines to raise the
   value of its own. Rome is a case in the argument against Rome.
5. **The plank, the seller and the treaty** (Rep. III.28–29; 7853–7906). Work
   as a set: in each case what Philus says the "wise" man does and what the
   "just" man does; the reader extracts the conventionalist's definition of
   wisdom. Then the Numantine treaty read twice — by Philus (justice does not
   pay: Mancinus handed over) and by Laelius (faith is owed anyway): one case,
   two readings.
6. **Laelius's law parsed** (Rep. III.33; 7966–7988). Quote the first
   sentence and the Rome/Athens/God sentence; a four-row parse against the
   statute: reason vs will; nature vs enactment; one vs many; God vs the
   people.
7. **The robbers' assembly** (Laws II.5.11–13; 14260–14275). Quintus concedes
   step by step: a thing whose absence makes a state no state is a good; a
   state without law is no state; so law is a good; so a deadly enactment,
   which is no good, is no law. Recreate the mini-dialogue as an Example; it
   is the course's first "an unjust law is no law".
8. **The ladder of rule turned into an empire** (Rep. III.36–37; 8050–8119).
   God / man; mind / body (as king over subjects); reason / lust (as master
   over slaves); Rome / provinces — and the one concession, "unjust slavery"
   where men could rule themselves. Keyes notes the passage survives because
   Augustine quoted it; the same ladder is redrawn in `augustine-two-cities`.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Terms a newcomer will not know — anchor at first mention, at most three
  per section:** res publica (literally "the public thing"; translate once,
  then "commonwealth"); populus; *ius* (right; the root of "justice" in
  Latin); consilium (the deliberating body); the Academy (Plato's school at
  Athens, by Carneades's time sceptical); sceptic (one who argues both sides
  and suspends judgement); Stoics (the Greek school holding that the cosmos
  is governed by reason and that living by nature is the good — Laelius's
  doctrine is Stoic; one clause); Epicurean (Atticus, in the Laws, holds that
  the gods do not govern — hence the joke when he concedes it); the Twelve
  Tables and the praetor's edict (Rome's written code of the 450s BC and the
  yearly rules of the magistrate who ran the courts); Voconian law (169 BC,
  limiting what women could inherit); Numantia (a hill town in Spain that
  defeated Roman armies; the treaty 137 BC; destroyed by Scipio in 133);
  Sulla (dictator 82–79 BC; the law that made him dictator is Cicero's
  example of enacted injustice); the decemvirs (the board of ten that wrote
  the Twelve Tables and would not step down, 451–449 BC); Dionysius of
  Syracuse (tyrant of the Greek city in Sicily, c. 400 BC); provinces
  (conquered territory under a Roman governor); fetial priests (who declared
  war formally) only if used; *lex* / *nomos* (Cicero's etymologies — say
  once or not at all). Do not re-anchor Scipio Aemilianus, the Gracchi,
  consuls, senate, tribunes or the centuriate assembly: the Polybius lesson
  did; `<Ref>` it with a clause.
- **The text is fragments.** Rep. III, V and VI survive largely as quotations
  in Lactantius, Augustine and Nonius (the Dream through Macrobius); Book I
  has lacunae; the Laws stop in Book III. Say so once, up front (the
  palimpsest is the one-sentence story of how we have it); never stitch a
  lacuna silently; Keyes's bracketed restorations are conjectures.
- **Numbering.** Keyes prints chapter numerals (Rep. III "XXII", "XXIII",
  "XXV") beside the standard section numbers; cite standard numbers in the
  body and References (III.33 = Keyes XXII; III.34–35 = XXIII; III.36–37 =
  XXV). The summary's "III.22 natural-law passage" is Keyes's XXII = III.33.
- **"An unjust statute is no law"** is stated in Cicero's own voice in Laws
  II.5–6 (and in Laws I.42–44); the surviving fragments of Laelius in Rep. III
  do not contain the sentence. The course.yaml key idea attributes it to
  Laelius; the lesson should attribute it to Cicero in the Laws, after
  presenting Laelius's definition of true law from which it follows.
- **Do not re-teach** the cycle, the mixture or the Roman institutions
  (Polybius lesson), Glaucon's bargain and the two statues (Plato lesson), or
  natural slavery (Aristotle lesson): each a `<Ref>` plus one clause. The
  lesson's new things are the definition, the ball's branches, Carneades's
  Roman form of the case, the definition of law above statute, and its three
  consequences.
- **The egalitarian passage** (Laws I.28–30) is often read as a charter of
  human equality. Present the text, then the dispute as the course's own:
  its argument is about kinship and shared reason, and the same author ranks
  men as fit or unfit to rule (III.36–37 in this lesson). Say what the text
  shows: the passage establishes one definition of man and shared reason,
  not equal fitness to rule, and Cicero holds both; exercise 4 works it.
- **Keep the imperial justification at full strength** (course.yaml notes):
  it is the argument Augustine will grant and turn and Vitoria will reject.
  Present the strongest version, then the strongest objection, then the
  course's finding on the argument (link 9): it inherits natural slavery's
  failure, because nothing picks out in advance who is better off ruled. No
  label for Cicero or for Rome; the finding is about the step.
- **Kingship as best simple form** surprises readers who expect a republican
  to prefer the people. Keep it; it is in the text, and later monarchists
  quote it.
- **Civil religion** is a hand-off paragraph here; `constantine-and-
  representation` and `altar-of-victory` read Laws II.7–8 against real cases.
- **★ The Dream** is optional by construction: no exercise and no later
  lesson may depend on it (L2b).
- **Numbers:** anchors — 155 BC (Carneades), 129 BC (the dialogue), 137/133
  (Numantia). No keeper numbers beyond dates; the 89 of 193 is recalled, not
  re-derived.
- **Names only in References:** Keyes (translator), Holton (Strauss–Cropsey),
  Strauss, Adamson, Plutarch (as the source of the 155 anecdote — or name him
  as an object: "Plutarch's life of Cato tells…" is a citation; prefer the
  plain statement), Mai (may be named as the object of the palimpsest story).
  Material and objects named freely: Cicero, Scipio, Laelius, Philus,
  Carneades, Cato, Polybius, Lactantius and Augustine as transmitters,
  Macrobius in the ★ section.
- **Dossier thinness:** no Lactantius, Plutarch or Stoic primary text is
  staged; the 155 embassy and the Stoic background are one clause each. The
  Strauss–Cropsey chapter is OCR and was not summarised: grep it before
  relying on a page.
- **Do not moralise** Cicero's politics or the Gracchan crisis; the lesson
  reads an argument, not a career.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (the one warm-up).* Write Scipio's definition from memory and
   name the clause each of these fails: a crowd at the games; a pirate crew
   with a written code; Rome and Carthage under a treaty.
2. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill on Book III.* Most of Philus's and
   Laelius's speeches reach us as quotations in two Christian writers arguing
   against pagan Rome (Lactantius; Augustine). Metadata first; predict what
   such transmitters would keep and drop; then: what can the surviving Book
   III be USED to establish about what Cicero argued, and what must stay
   uncertain (for example, the balance between the two speeches)?
3. *Boundary probe.* Remove the fourth clause of Laelius's law — a natural
   law with no God as author or judge. Does the robbers'-assembly argument
   still work, and if so what is doing the work? (Solution: the reductio runs
   on the definition of law as reason and on the reader's own judgement; the
   God clause supplies the sanction — conscience, oaths — not the
   distinction. Forward clause to `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`,
   where laws of nature without a sword are "but words".)
4. *Claim to refute.* "Laws I.28–30 shows that Cicero held all men equal and
   therefore condemned slavery and empire." Name the failing step with
   Rep. III.36–37 in hand; state what the likeness passage establishes (one
   definition of man, shared reason, capacity for virtue) and what it does not
   (equal fitness to rule).
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* Laelius says a state should be founded to
   last for ever and that its death is a punishment, not a natural end
   (III.34); Scipio says a tyrant's city is no commonwealth (III.43). Derive
   what Cicero must say happened to Rome under the decemvirs and under
   Sulla — did the commonwealth die or pause? — and then set the answer
   against Polybius's natural decay (`<Ref>` `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`,
   VI.57): which of the two accounts can explain V.1–2's "we retain only the form … but have long since lost its
   substance", and how?
6. *Counter-argument stem.* Someone claims: "Natural law is the ideology of
   the strongest state — a law 'the same at Rome and at Athens' that happens
   to make Rome the ruler." Name two facts the claim fails to account for
   (the Numantine judgement against Rome; the Laws naming Sulla's law unjust)
   and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that the
   standard never in practice condemned a Roman act — testable against the
   text).
7. *Paired sources — corroboration (E3 reach-back).* Set Philus's "weakness
   is the mother of justice" and "no state would rather be a just slave than
   an unjust master" beside Glaucon's bargain (`<Ref>`
   `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`) and the Athenians at Melos (`<Ref>`
   `thucydides-power-and-justice`). List what is the same, what Philus adds
   (Rome itself as the example; the plank; empire), and which of the three
   is hardest for Laelius's reply to meet.
8. **FORWARD (E7)** → `augustine-two-cities`. Take Scipio's definition and
   Laelius's law together: a people is bound by agreement about right, and
   right is what agrees with true law, whose author is God. Did Rome, which
   worshipped gods that Laelius's argument does not make the authors of law,
   ever have "agreement about right" in this sense? Write the syllogism out.
   The solution says: this is the argument of City of God XIX.21; a bishop
   runs it to the conclusion that Rome never was a commonwealth — and then
   changes the definition.

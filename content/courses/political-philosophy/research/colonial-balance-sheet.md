# Research brief: colonial-balance-sheet (The colonial balance sheet: the Case for Colonialism and its critics)

status: planned
module: empire-and-guilt
type: core
goal: Evaluate the argument that colonialism was on balance beneficial and legitimate, the arguments against it, and the standards of evidence each side uses, as a contested claim with its history

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `burke-against-hastings`: empire as a trust under law,
  geographical morality rejected ("the laws of morality are the same
  everywhere" — the premise a cost–benefit accounting must either accept or
  relativise), and Burke's own ledger of Bengal with no counterfactual in it.
  From `locke-and-mill-on-empire`: tutelage and Mill's test — a despotism over
  "barbarians" is legitimate only "provided the end be their improvement, and
  the means justified by actually effecting that end" — which is an empirical
  test and so invites exactly the accounting this lesson examines. From
  `vitoria-right-to-the-americas`: "for their benefit, not Spanish profit", and
  the requerimiento's void consent (the ancestor of the "subjective legitimacy"
  dispute). NEW backward Refs from the heredity module, which now precedes this
  one: `geography-or-institutions` — the institutional account of colonial
  rule (`inclusive-and-extractive-institutions`: where Europeans settled they
  built institutions they would live under; where they extracted, they built
  institutions to extract, and those outlasted them; the reversal of fortune —
  the colonies richest in 1500 are among the poorest now); and
  `national-data-audited` — the settler-mortality table under that account,
  audited: a strong association, a causal estimate that rests on recoded
  entries, and the point the reader was asked to reach in its forward exercise,
  that a table comparing colonies with colonies cannot say what colonial rule
  did compared with no colonial rule. That lesson also ran the sourcing drill on
  datasets, which this lesson's "lazy citations" charge and the next-but-one
  audit lesson both use. From module 1 onward: the sourcing drill.
- **Contributes:** A contested empirical claim laid out as an argument with its
  history: the thesis as its author made it (three failures of the anti-colonial
  critique; a method of counterfactual cost–benefit; a test of legitimacy by
  revealed behaviour; a proposal), the strongest replies from the critics' own
  texts, what the two sides measure differently, and the controversy's record
  as data about how such claims are adjudicated — and then a verdict on each of
  the Case's three charges (see VERDICT). Nothing moralised, in either
  direction; the same burden of a counterfactual on both sides.
- **Hands off:** Decided here: the "no counterfactual" charge holds against the
  polemical catalogues and fails against the critical literature; the
  never-colonised comparators prove nothing; behaviour shows graded legitimacy,
  not consent (each strong). Whether colonial rule was a net benefit or a net
  harm overall is not decided here; the module verdict in
  `auditing-the-case-for-colonialism` weighs the ledger. Two problems pass on.
  Even if a ledger could be balanced,
  does a debt — or a credit — pass to the descendants of those who incurred
  it? `inherited-guilt-and-reparations` takes that. And both sides' numbers
  rest on cited studies that can be read: `auditing-the-case-for-colonialism`
  reads three of them.
- **Spine step:** "The West judges its own conquests: Vitoria's titles, Locke's waste land, Burke's impeachment of Hastings, Mill's tutelage, the colonial balance sheet, and whether guilt is inherited"

## VERDICT

Binding: research/verdicts/REGISTER.md, module 14, `colonial-balance-sheet`.

- **Claim judged.** The Case for Colonialism's three charges: (a) "the critics
  never ran a cost–benefit against a counterfactual"; (b) "the never-colonised
  comparators (China, Ethiopia, Liberia, Libya, Saudi Arabia, Thailand, Haiti,
  Guatemala) show colonialism was no handicap"; (c) "behaviour reveals that
  the colonised treated colonial rule as legitimate".
- **Verdict.** (a) **Holds against the polemical catalogues; fails as a
  description of the critical literature.** Grade: **strong** on each half.
  (b) **Fails.** Grade: **strong**. (c) **Fails as proof of consent**; it shows
  legitimacy was graded. Grade: **strong**.
- **What decides it.** (a) A catalogue of railways or of massacres is not a
  balance, and the polemics on both sides are catalogues. But the critical
  literature has run counterfactual designs since 2001: Acemoglu, Johnson &
  Robinson — cited in the Case's own note 4 (`research/texts/gilley/case-for-colonialism-original-AQ2018.txt`:
  "they do not control for the presence or absence of colonialism (for
  instance in a highly cited study by Acemoglu and colleagues). [4]"); Nunn on
  the slave trades (`research/texts/audit-right-shading/nunn-2007-slave-trades-mpra-wp.txt`);
  Heldring & Robinson, NBER WP 18566 (2012), a review against stated
  counterfactuals (`research/texts/verification-2026-11/heldring-robinson-2012-nber-w18566.txt`),
  which the Response itself concedes "measures up well to the highest standards
  of scientific inquiry" (`research/texts/gilley/response-to-my-critics-AQ2022.txt`). (b) The
  comparators are not comparable (Ethiopia occupied 1936–41; Liberia a settler
  state; Haiti paying an indemnity to France; Thailand a buffer under unequal
  treaties), survivorship selects them (the places left uncolonised were the
  hardest to take), and the Case concedes the counterfactual is unobservable —
  which removes the comparators' power to prove anything. (c) Compliance under
  power is not consent (Vitoria on the requerimiento); but low force relative
  to population, collaborators and Sarawak show acceptance varied, so
  legitimacy was graded, not nil.
- **Strongest objection.** On (a): the cited designs compare colonies with
  colonies (settler mortality, kinds of rule), so they do not supply the
  counterfactual of no colony — the Case's own point in note 4, which
  `national-data-audited` also reached. So the charge stands against the
  literature too.
- **Why it does not overturn the verdict.** The charge as made is that the
  critics "never" ran a cost–benefit against a counterfactual. Heldring &
  Robinson state counterfactuals and weigh mechanisms against them, and Nunn
  estimates an effect of the slave trades against their absence. That their
  counterfactuals are disputable is a point about the quality of the designs,
  not a proof that none were run. Feyrer & Sacerdote (NBER w12546) and
  Easterly & Levine (NBER w18162), designs whose results favour the Case's
  side, show the same literature is not one-directional.
- **What would overturn it.** For (a), showing that the cited designs state no
  counterfactual at all; for (b), comparators matched on the conditions that
  made them hard to colonise; for (c), evidence of consent given by those
  ruled, under conditions in which refusal was possible.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim. Develop it: the problem here is not
"was colonialism good or bad" — the module's last lesson weighs that and finds
it open across colonies — but how anyone could know. The obvious attempt on each side is a catalogue: railways,
hospitals, the end of the slave trade; Amritsar, the Herero, Mau Mau, the Congo.
Both fail the same way: a list of goods and a list of harms is not a balance
until someone says what each is worth, and against what alternative. Anchor the
article in one sentence (a professor of political science at Portland State; a
"Viewpoint" essay in a development-studies journal, Third World Quarterly,
September 2017) and leave the controversy for link 7.

1. **The thesis, unadorned.** State it as its author restated it in 2022: with
   controls, a feasible counterfactual and multi-dimensional weighted costs and
   benefits, many or most episodes of Western colonialism were a net benefit to
   the colonised and were often locally legitimate; and some of its forms should
   return, with consent. Then the scope move, which decides much of the dispute:
   "colonialism" means the British, French, German, Belgian, Dutch and
   Portuguese colonies from the early nineteenth to the mid-twentieth century —
   the second of two phases of European empire, dated from 1824 — so the
   conquest of the Americas (Vitoria's subject, `<Ref>`), the Atlantic slave
   trade, and Leopold II's Congo Free State (1885–1908, the king's private
   estate, not a Belgian colony) fall outside it. Give both sides at strength:
   the critics' charge that excluding the Congo is convenient; the author's
   reply that scholars should know the difference between a private estate and
   a state, and that the Free State is "the best argument for colonialism"
   because the Belgian colony that replaced it ended the worst of it. Because:
   what the claim names is the first thing a balance sheet has to settle.

2. **Failure one: no cost–benefit accounting, and the counterfactual.**
   Question: how would you score a colonial regime? The test the article adopts
   (Abernethy's, quoted): on balance a positive effect on training for
   self-government, material well-being, labour allocation, mobility,
   cross-cultural communication and dignity, "compared to the situation that
   would likely have obtained absent European rule" — morally indefensible
   where it did not. Define **cost–benefit of empire** and **counterfactual
   development** here. The counterfactual problem: compared to what? Three
   baselines — the pre-colonial state, a better colonialism, the post-colonial
   record — and the never-colonised comparators (China, Ethiopia, Liberia,
   Libya, Saudi Arabia, Thailand, Haiti, Guatemala): "If colonial subjection
   caused poor performance, then today's Ethiopia would be the economic miracle
   of Africa." The critics' reply at full strength, from their texts: the
   comparators are not comparable — Ethiopia was invaded and occupied 1936–41,
   Liberia was a settler state of freed Americans, Haiti was a slave colony
   that paid an indemnity to France for most of a century, Thailand a buffer
   under unequal treaties; survivorship — the places left uncolonised were the
   ones hardest to take; and the counterfactual is unobservable for both sides
   equally. The epistemic virtues the article demands (unbiased case
   selection; coherence; falsifiability) and the "too disruptive and not
   disruptive enough" charge — critics blame colonialism both for destroying
   institutions and for freezing them — with Taylor's reply that the two
   complaints are about different things (what was provided and how it was
   used). Then the third ledger the reader already holds (`<Ref>`
   geography-or-institutions; `<Ref>` national-data-audited, one clause each):
   the institutional account does not ask whether colonialism helped or harmed
   but which colonialism — settlement colonies left institutions their settlers
   lived under, extraction colonies left institutions built to extract, and the
   effect runs in opposite directions. Quote the article's own sentence that
   such studies "do not control for the presence or absence of colonialism" —
   both sides of this dispute agree that a comparison of colonies with
   colonies cannot supply the counterfactual of no colony, which is the point
   the previous module's audit ended on. Because: a balance needs a baseline,
   and the dispute is largely about which.

3. **Failure two: subjective legitimacy.** Question: can the governed settle
   it? The article: ask whether those subject to it treated it as rightful,
   read from behaviour — rapid spread with little force relative to population
   and territory; collaborators outnumbering resisters ("indigenous pull");
   Sarawak, where the Sultan of Brunei installed James Brooke as rajah in 1841
   and preferred Brooke rule into the 1940s; a governor of the Gold Coast
   noting the people could have "pushed us into the sea"; Lumumba's 1962
   autobiography praising Belgian rule; Achebe's memoir; the young man in
   Kinshasa in 2010: "When are the Belgians coming back?" Define **subjective
   legitimacy**. The critics' replies at full strength: compliance is not
   consent — recall Vitoria's requerimiento (`<Ref>`: consent given by a crowd
   surrounded by armed men is void) and the punitive expeditions that made
   "little force" sufficient; the provenance of the Lumumba text (written
   1956–57 as a petition for advancement, published after his death by others —
   Taylor's doubt, which the Response answers with Hoskyns's review);
   selection among native voices; and the reply the article itself names and
   dismisses — "false consciousness" — which the lesson states fairly as the
   claim that preferences formed under rule do not measure the rule. The
   author's counter: defining colonialism as illegitimate alien rule forecloses
   the question by fiat; legitimacy is graded and empirical. Because: whether
   acceptance under power counts as legitimacy is a question the module has
   met before (Vitoria, Burke on "princes' panegyrics as evidence of terror").

4. **Failure three, and the costs of anti-colonialism.** The charge that
   anti-colonial thought was advocacy, not falsifiable scholarship; then the
   case study: Guinea-Bissau, where Cabral's movement set out to "reduce to
   ash" the colonial state, the war killed about fifteen thousand of six
   hundred thousand people, rice output fell from its late-colonial peak to
   under half by 1980, and the gain in life expectancy slowed (bind every
   number in its sentence; verify each against the text before using; two
   keepers at most). The critics' replies: a hard case chosen (Portugal's was
   the last and most violent decolonisation; the war is the cost of the
   refusal to leave, not of leaving); post-colonial failure as colonial legacy
   (Taylor) against "everything is rooted in antecedents tells us nothing about
   causation" (Gilley); the author's own concession that Heldring and
   Robinson's study meets scientific standards and finds a negative effect of
   colonialism on African development, and that Lange's work is "top-rate" —
   show the concessions; they are what distinguish an argument from a
   polemic. Because: the costs side of the ledger also has a counterfactual —
   what independence would have cost had it come later or otherwise — and the
   article's own standard applies to it.

5. **What the two sides measure differently.** The lesson's KEEPER section.
   Absolute levels against comparative trends (life expectancy rising for
   everyone in the twentieth century; the article's standard is the rate
   against the regional or world trendline — the critics answer that a people
   does not live on a trendline). Incommensurables: Brandon and Sarkar's
   question — how many railways balance one torture? — and the Response's
   answer that this is what cost–benefit analysis exists to decide, with
   weights stated and defended. Scope: six powers from 1824. And the "lazy
   citations" charge made both ways — each side says the other cites studies it
   has not read, which is a claim the reader can test (hand-off to the audit
   lesson, one sentence). Because: much of what looks like a dispute about facts
   is a dispute about the measure, and the measure is a judgment the data
   cannot make.

6. **What a conservative's ledger looked like, and Mill's test.** Two
   backward Refs, one paragraph: Burke's accounting — misery to Bengal, the
   delinquents of India in the Commons, "To obtain empire is common; to govern
   it well has been rare", the star from the West — a ledger with no
   counterfactual and no comparison, which judges by the trust and the law;
   Mill's test — a despotism justified "by actually effecting" improvement,
   else "selfish usurpers" — which is the 2017 method in 1861 words. Where
   would each put the 2017 article's evidence? Burke would ask whether the
   people's own laws and property were kept; Mill whether the training for
   self-government was real. The reader sees that the balance-sheet method is
   not new and that its two ancestors in the module disagree about what to
   count.

7. **The controversy as data.** Reported as fact, with the sources named in
   References and the two partisan accounts (the NAS Editor's Note and the
   author's own Introduction) flagged as such: a desk acceptance at one journal
   withdrawn for fear of backlash; rejection from a TWQ special issue; run as a
   Viewpoint after double-blind review; publication 8 September 2017; two
   petitions with thousands of signatures demanding retraction and the
   editor's dismissal; fifteen of thirty-four editorial-board members resign
   (19 September); the publisher defends the review process; the article is
   withdrawn after what the publisher's notice calls "serious and credible
   threats of personal violence" to the editor; reprint in 2018; the Response
   in 2022; professional penalties listed by the author. What it shows about
   adjudication, stated as the course's own observation and not as a verdict:
   a withdrawn argument is neither refuted nor vindicated; the dispute moved
   from evidence to permissibility; both frames for why — the charge that the
   academy is a "culture of repudiation" that cannot hear the case, and the
   critics' charge that the article was advocacy wearing peer review's clothes
   — are themselves the two "failures" each side attributes to the other. The
   seam: the reader expects a scholarly dispute to end in a reply; the record
   is that it ended in a withdrawal and a reply five years later in another
   journal.

8. **The proposal, briefly.** The normative claim is
   separable from the empirical: colonial governance borrowed "as an attic to
   ransack"; shared sovereignty (RAMSI in the Solomons, GEMAP in Liberia) called
   by its name; charter cities and the Galinhas thought experiment — a
   ninety-year lease to Portugal at a dollar a year, "A hundred years of
   disaster is enough." Consent as the precondition the author himself calls
   "a tall order". Vitoria's condition (`<Ref>`) and Mill's test return as the
   questions the proposal has to answer. One paragraph; the weighing follows.

9. **The weighing — the Case is right about the catalogues, wrong about the
   literature, and its other two proofs fail.** The lesson's last body section
   reaches the verdict (see VERDICT) on each charge in turn, with the same
   burden on both sides. (a) Grant the charge against the polemical catalogues
   — both sides' lists of goods and harms are not balances. Then show the
   critical literature: Acemoglu, Johnson & Robinson cited in the Case's own
   note 4; Nunn's slave-trade estimate; Heldring & Robinson's review against
   stated counterfactuals, conceded in the Response. Set the strongest
   objection (colony-against-colony designs do not give the no-colony
   counterfactual) and answer it: that is a dispute about the designs'
   quality, not evidence that none were run. Strong on each half. (b) The
   comparators: not comparable, selected by survival, and the Case concedes the
   counterfactual is unobservable. Strong. (c) Behaviour: compliance under
   power is not consent; acceptance varied, so legitimacy was graded. Strong.
   Then say what is not decided here: the net ledger itself, which the module's
   last lesson weighs with designs on both sides (the island and settlement
   studies; Heldring & Robinson; Nunn); and what data cannot decide: the
   weights and the scope.

Headings fall out as: "What the claim names"; "Compared to what?"; "Did the

**Conclusion.** States the verdicts in plain words with their grades: the
charge that no one weighed colonial rule against a counterfactual is true of
the catalogues on both sides and false of the critical literature, which has run

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** Third World Quarterly (a development-studies
  journal, founded 1979); "Viewpoint" (an opinion essay category); withdrawal
  vs retraction (a retraction is for error or misconduct; a withdrawal removes
  the text without a finding); the National Association of Scholars (a
  conservative academic association, publisher of Academic Questions — the
  Editor's Note is its voice); counterfactual; revealed preference; false
  consciousness; the Congo Free State (1885–1908) vs the Belgian Congo
  (1908–60); Sarawak (north Borneo); Gold Coast (Ghana); Guinea-Bissau
  (Portuguese Guinea; war 1963–74; independence 1974); Cabral; Mau Mau (the
  Kenyan insurgency, 1952–60); the Herero war (German South-West Africa,
  1904–08); Amritsar / Jallianwala Bagh (13 April 1919; 379 killed by the
  official count); Rodney (a Guyanese historian, 1972); RAMSI, GEMAP, CICIG
  (shared-sovereignty missions in the Solomon Islands, Liberia, Guatemala);
  charter cities (Romer, 2009). Three per section at most.
- **Both dossier texts are one side's.** The Editor's Note and the author's
  Introduction tell the controversy as the author's camp tells it; the
  critics' pieces are not in the dossier. The lesson's fairness depends on the
  writer obtaining Kendhammer, Khan, Klein, Taylor, Robinson and the board
  letter and stating their arguments from them. Where a critic is only known
  from the Response, say so.
- **Separate skeleton from invective.** The Response calls the field a "cult",
  answers a "Holocaust denial" charge in kind, and ridicules; the original
  flourishes ("a backwater of druid worshippers"). Quote the skeleton (the
  method, the standard, the concessions); leave the invective out or quote one
  line as evidence of register, never as argument. The same for the critics'
  rhetoric.
- **Both sides' slips are shown, neither as decisive.** The author's: the
  Lefebvre wording he concedes to Taylor; Sèbe's objection to being cited; the
  AER citation (vol. 95, 2000 for a 2001 paper in vol. 91); the inconsistent
  Frankema–van Waijenburg citations (Response notes 40 and 47 — authors
  reversed, year 2011 vs 2012, an SSRN DOI in one). The critics': the Congo
  Free State as "Belgian Congo under Leopold"; Vansina's population loss
  traced to Buell. These feed the audit lesson; here one sentence each.
- **Numbers:** two or three keepers — 15 of 34 board members; 379 at Amritsar;
  Guinea-Bissau's deaths per population or its rice. Every number bound in its
  sentence. The summary's Guinea-Bissau figures (182,000 t; 0.73 vs 0.3 years
  per year) must be verified against the text before use; if the text does
  not carry them, do not use them.
- **No settling, no moralising, no "some believe".** The tone rule names this
  lesson's subject by name. The course's own sentences state what each side
  claims, what evidence each gives, and what each must concede, and then gives
  the verdict on each of the Case's three charges as the register records it,
  with the same burden of proof on both sides. The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Do not re-teach** Burke's trust or Mill's test (one Ref clause each), nor
  the institutional account, the reversal of fortune or the settler-mortality
  audit (`<Ref>` geography-or-institutions, `<Ref>` national-data-audited — one
  clause each); do not anticipate Waldron or Murray beyond the hand-off
  sentence; do not
  pre-run the audit of the three studies — name the "lazy citations both ways"
  charge and stop.

  is now the clean full text (summary index 75); the earlier djvu file and the

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (E3, the one warm-up).** State in one line each how Vitoria
   ("for their benefit, not Spanish profit"), Burke (a trust under their own
   laws) and Mill ("actually effecting" improvement) would grade a colonial
   regime, and which of the three the 2017 method most resembles.
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill on the NAS Editor's Note.** Metadata
   (a sympathetic reprint's preface, 2018); predict what it will stress and
   omit; records (two petitions; fifteen of thirty-four resignations;
   publisher's defence) / argues (that the article was sound and the response
   illegitimate) / omits (the content of the critics' objections; who made the
   threats); use: which of its facts are corroborated by the critics' own
   letter (the resignations) and which rest on a single source (the threats —
   the publisher's notice).
3. **Paired corroboration.** The Editor's Note, the author's 2022 Introduction
   and the fifteen members' resignation letter on why the article was
   withdrawn: list agreements and disagreements, then judge what can be
   established about the withdrawal's cause.
4. **Boundary probe.** A colony with no plausible comparator and no
   pre-colonial record (the article's own admission that pre-colonial
   histories "almost by definition" show weak institutions). What is left of
   the counterfactual method when the baseline cannot be observed — and does
   the same gap disable the critics' "legacy" claim? Solution: both sides'
   causal claims need the baseline; what survives on each side is the
   descriptive record (what was built, what was destroyed) and the
   distributional question (for whom).
5. **Claim to refute.** "The article was withdrawn because its claims were
   shown to be false." Name the step that fails (the publisher's stated reason;
   withdrawal vs retraction) and state what a refutation would have had to
   contain.
6. **Derivation the lesson skipped.** Run the "too disruptive and not
   disruptive enough" dilemma on one specific pair from the critics' texts

   apply Taylor's provision/use distinction and say whether it dissolves the
   dilemma or only relocates it.
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `auditing-the-case-for-colonialism`.** "Lumumba praised
   Belgian rule in his 1962 autobiography." Before accepting or rejecting the

   the next lesson applies exactly this drill to three cited studies, with a
   `<Ref>`.
8. **Synthesis — claim–evidence–reasoning both ways.** Write the balance-sheet
   entry for Guinea-Bissau as the article would and as its critics would (one
   paragraph each, evidence named), then state in two sentences what remains a
   judgment the data cannot make (the weight of a war against a trend in life
   expectancy; the baseline for a decolonisation that came otherwise).
9. **Attack the verdict.** The lesson finds that the "no counterfactual"
   charge fails against the critical literature (strong). State the
   strongest objection in its best form: every design the lesson cites
   compares colonies with colonies, or pre-1824 slave exports with their

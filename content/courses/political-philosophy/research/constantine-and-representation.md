# Research brief: constantine-and-representation (Constantine and the struggle for representation)

status: planned
module: rome-and-christianity
type: core
goal: Analyze how the Roman Empire and the Church fought over who represented the truth of society, from the imperial cult to Eusebius and Augustine

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Cicero's civil religion — a city must have gods, may admit
  no new or alien ones, deifies its virtues and (by the Caesars' time) its
  rulers — standing beside a natural law that is one for all nations
  (`cicero-natural-law`, Laws II); Scipio's commonwealth as the property of a
  people (`cicero-mixed-constitution`); and from `hellenization-of-christianity`
  a Church that put "an irremovable landmark between God and Caesar", then
  grew Roman forms of its own, with Logos Christology as its concept of God
  (`logos-christology`). The reader has no Voegelin and no theory of
  representation beyond the everyday electoral sense.
- **Contributes:** One move: a society exists in history by being
  *represented* — someone acts and the acts count as the society's — and it
  represents, beyond itself, a *truth* about the order of things; Rome's
  compact answer (the city makes its gods; the emperor is at once the
  people's representative and the gods' chief priest) is broken by a sect
  whose God is the only one, so that for a century two bodies, Empire and
  Church, fight over which of them represents the truth of the same society;
  Constantine's settlement, Eusebius's theology of the one emperor for the
  one God, and the Trinity's defeat of that theology are the stages. Three
  terms are introduced and defined — existential representation, civil
  theology, political theology — and the Soviet constitution of 1936 is the
  modern test that shows why the elemental (electoral) sense is not enough.
- **Hands off:** If the emperor cannot represent the one God because God is
  three, and the Church represents a truth that is "not of this world", who
  represents the truth of Roman society after 325? Augustine's answer — no
  earthly city is just; the heavenly city uses the earthly peace — is the
  next lesson; the Altar of Victory (384) is the module's test case; and the
  vocabulary returns in `schmitt-political-theology` (Voegelin's "end of
  political theology" as the standing objection to Schmitt) and
  `voegelin-gnosticism`, which only *recalls* what this lesson defines
  (course.yaml line 1243).
- **Spine step:** "A Jewish movement becomes Europe's religion by being remade
  in Greek concepts and Roman forms; Constantine's empire and the Church fight
  over who represents the truth; Augustine denies that any earthly city can be
  just"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: an empire that deified its
emperors and tolerated every god so long as it was worshipped alongside them
met a sect that refused the sacrifice and claimed its God was the only one;
within a century the emperor presided over the sect's councils; the lesson
asks what each side gained and gave up, and introduces the idea that a society
exists by being represented.

1. **Why would a tolerant empire kill people over a sacrifice?** The obvious
   attempt: intolerance, or the Christians' stubbornness. Neither explains
   why *this* refusal and not the Jews' or the Epicureans'. The reader
   already has Cicero's law (`<Ref>` `cicero-natural-law`): a city has its
   gods and admits no new ones unless the state recognizes them, because
   oaths, treaties and "the association of citizens" become sacred when the
   gods are members of it. Rome's gods were not a belief the city held but
   part of what the city *was*. Anchors: the imperial cult (sacrifice to the
   emperor's genius and to deified predecessors, from Augustus on); the test
   of loyalty under Decius (250) — a certificate of sacrifice (libellus) for
   every inhabitant; Diocletian's Great Persecution (303–311). So the
   question becomes: what is it about a city's gods that makes refusing them
   an attack on the city? The lesson needs a tool to answer that.
2. **The tool, first half — a society exists by being represented.** The
   obvious meaning of "representation" is elections; the reader should be
   let to try it and see it fail on the test case. The 1936 Soviet
   constitution guaranteed universal, equal, direct and secret suffrage;
   Western critics said the voter had no choice; Communists said the party
   monopoly was exactly what made the system truly representative; and the
   whole range of opinions — no parties, one party, two, two as factions of
   one — shows that counting parties does not reach the question (Voegelin
   I.3). But one thing is beyond dispute: the Soviet government's commands
   were obeyed and its armies fed — it represented Soviet society "as a
   political society in form for action in history" (I.4). Define
   **existential representation**: a society *articulates* itself —
   produces rulers whose acts "are not imputed to their own persons but to
   the society as a whole" — and so exists as a unit that can act; the
   elemental (electoral) type is one special way of articulating, found only
   in the Greco-Roman and Western worlds (I.9). The agent/representative
   distinction (a UN delegate acts under instructions; his government acts by
   position). One illustration from Voegelin's medieval material — the
   Lombards "had to have a king" to be in form for action, or Fortescue's
   headless people that erects a head — to show that articulation precedes
   institutions. Because: a society that has not produced a representative
   cannot act in history at all, whatever its constitution says; and a
   representative who stops representing is replaced whatever his legal
   title (Hauriou). Name Voegelin once here as the author of the tool.
3. **The tool, second half — a society also represents a truth.** Once
   articulated, every society claims to stand for the right order of things:
   the Persian king on the Behistun rock is the representative of Truth
   against the Lie; the Mongol Khan writes to the Pope that the Order of God
   has given him the world; the early empires understood themselves as small
   copies of the cosmos (II.2). Call this *transcendental* representation and
   name three types of the truth represented (III.1): **cosmological** (the
   society as analogue of the cosmos — Egypt, Babylon, Persia);
   **anthropological** (the discovery, in Athens, that the order of society
   is measured by the order of the soul — the reader has this from Plato:
   "the city is man written large", `<Ref>` `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-
   king`, one clause); **soteriological** (Christianity's addition: God
   reaches toward man — grace). Define each in a sentence of plain English;
   the Greek labels are Voegelin's and need not be repeated after their
   first use. Then **civil theology**: Varro's category (c. 47 BC) for the
   gods a city institutes as the condition of its own existence, beside the
   poets' gods and the philosophers' — the Roman form of transcendental
   representation (III.2). Because: now the Christian refusal can be named —
   it denied that any city's gods could be true, i.e. it denied Rome's
   transcendental representation while leaving its existential
   representation (the emperor's commands) intact. That split is the lesson.
4. **Rome's compactness — why the Romans could not see the problem.** The
   obvious expectation: a philosophical culture should have had Plato's
   doubt about its gods. Rome did not. Varro: "as the painter is prior to
   the painting… so are the cities prior to the institutions of the
   cities" — the city makes its gods, and that does not make them false
   (III.4). Cicero's Scipio treats Greek philosophy as "foreign learning",
   a spice on ancestral custom (`<Ref>` `cicero-mixed-constitution`: Scipio
   refusing to build a city in speech). The imperial cult grew out of this
   compactness, not out of megalomania: the principate itself came from the
   patron–client bond — Octavian's oath of 32 BC bound the West to him as
   clients to a patron — so the emperor was the existential representative
   of the "empire people" first and the gods' chief priest (pontifex
   maximus) as a consequence (III.5). Augustine will later see only
   flattery here (XVIII.24: the Caesars deified "not through error, but in
   flattery"); Varro saw a city representing itself. The lesson states both
   and does not decide; it uses Rome as the case where the two layers of
   representation were *one*.
5. **Why the empire's own gods were weakening — syncretism and the edicts.**
   The seam: from the compact Roman answer one would expect a confident
   state cult; the record shows emperors shopping for a god strong enough.
   Elagabalus marrying a Vestal to unite Baal and Tanit; Aurelian's Sol
   Invictus (274); Licinius praying to "the Highest, Holy God" before battle
   (III.6). Then the turn of 311–313 read as the Romans themselves would
   have read it: the Edict of Galerius (311) notes that persecuted
   Christians neither sacrifice to the old gods nor worship their own God
   properly — so a powerful God is going unworshipped and may take revenge;
   let them worship, and let them pray for the emperor. "The good, solid
   Roman do-ut-des principle" — I give that you may give. The "Edict of
   Milan" (313) speaks of *divinitas*, a word vague enough for pagans and
   monotheists at once — and the same hand that chose it chose, at Nicaea,
   the "sublimely meaningless homo-ousios". Because: Constantine's settlement
   was not a conversion of the empire's self-understanding but the inclusion
   of a strong new God in the imperial system of divinity — Rome's civil
   theology absorbing, as it had absorbed Magna Mater (`<Ref>`
   `hellenization-of-christianity`, one clause). Anchors: Galerius (eastern
   emperor, dying, 311); Licinius and Constantine (co-emperors; Constantine
   sole emperor from 324); Milan; Nicaea (325, the first general council,
   convened and attended by the emperor).
6. **OBJECTION at full strength — Celsus: a universal religion dissolves the
   empire.** The pagan philosopher Celsus (c. 178) understood what the
   Romans of 311 did not: the Christians say one cannot serve two masters,
   and that is "the language of sedition". Nothing is taken from God by
   honouring his many servants; to single out one God and honour him alone
   "introduces factiousness into the divine kingdom"; and since each region
   has its superintending spirit and national cult, "who wishes to destroy
   the national cult wants to destroy the national cultures" — an attack on
   the construction of the Roman empire itself. Could Asiatics, Europeans,
   Libyans, Greeks and barbarians agree in one law? "Anyone who thinks this
   possible knows nothing" (III.7). Give Celsus the whole paragraph: it is
   the strongest statement of why civil theology is not a superstition but a
   condition of a plural empire's existence — and it is the ancestor of
   every later argument that a universal creed cannot hold a particular
   people together (forward clause to module 10, no names). Origen's reply
   (c. 248): it not only can happen, it will. Then the lesson's own
   observation: both were right — Christianity did dissolve the gods that
   held the ethnic cultures of the empire, and it did become the one nomos
   of the Mediterranean. That is **de-divinization**: polytheism dying "from
   experiential atrophy", society reordered around a destination beyond it
   (IV.1 definition; use the word once, defined — it is reinforced later in
   `voegelin-gnosticism`, where it is a concept of its own; here it is a
   description).
7. **Eusebius makes the emperor Christ's representative — political
   theology.** Now the Church's side of the bargain. Eusebius of Caesarea
   (bishop, historian, Constantine's panegyrist): the appearance of Christ
   coincided with Augustus's pacification of the world; the apostles could
   roam an empire that had broken the independent nations; the pax Romana
   fulfilled the prophets' peace; and Constantine completed Augustus — "the
   one basileus on earth represents the one God, the one King in Heaven, the
   one Nomos and Logos" (Tricennial Oration, 336; III.8). Define **political
   theology** here: the claim that a political order's authority rests on or
   mirrors a theological structure — one God in heaven, one emperor on
   earth. Point out what has happened: the Church, which had refused the
   emperor's cult, now has a bishop who makes the emperor the image of its
   God — Rome's cosmological representation returned in Christian words. And
   Harnack's sentence from last lesson (`<Ref>`): the emperor was "at once a
   Christian Emperor and an oriental despot; the more conscientious he was,
   the more intolerant he was bound to be". What each side gained and gave
   up, stated as a ledger: the empire gained a God strong enough and a
   universal cult; it gave up the gods that made Rome Rome. The Church
   gained the sword and the schools; it gave up the "landmark between God
   and Caesar" its martyrs had set.
8. **Why the Trinity ended it — or did it?** The seam again: from Eusebius
   one would expect a Christian Byzantium ruled by God's one image. The
   record shows the theology collapsing under the Church's own doctrine. The
   emperors and court bishops leaned Arian (Arius: the Son is created,
   subordinate — a tidy monotheism that fits one emperor under one God);
   Athanasius and the West carried the Trinity; Gregory of Nazianzus: the
   Christian "divine monarchy" is a triunity that "has no analogue in
   creation", so no one man can image it. The joke that proves it: in the
   680s the army asked Constantine IV to crown his two brothers co-emperors
   so that earth would mirror the Trinity, and the two "got their noses cut
   off" (III.8). Augustine on "He maketh wars to cease": "that we see not
   yet accomplished; hitherto we have wars" — the pax Romana is not the
   peace of God. "This is the end of political theology in orthodox
   Christianity" — stated as the course's own thesis, with the question mark
   the course.yaml puts on it: a theology that ends in 381 and returns
   whenever a ruler needs it (Byzantium's emperors, Charlemagne, the "Third
   Rome" — forward clause only). The reader is left with the open problem:
   if the emperor cannot represent God's truth, and the Church represents a
   truth not of this world, what truth does the Roman *society* now
   represent? The answer the next lesson gives has two cities in it.
9. **Close:** what the reader can now do — use the three terms on a case;
   say why the Soviet constitution and the Edict of Milan are instances of
   the same distinction (an institution's letter vs the truth a society
   claims to stand for); and name the open problem.

Headings that fall out: "Why a tolerant empire killed over a sacrifice"; "A
society exists by being represented: the Soviet test"; "A society represents
a truth: three kinds, and the city's own gods"; "Rome's compactness: the city
is prior to its gods"; "Shopping for a strong god: 311 and 313"; "Celsus: one
God dissolves the empire"; "Eusebius: one God, one emperor"; "The Trinity
ends political theology (or does it)"; conclusion.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The Soviet constitution test** (I.3–I.4; lines 1704–1800). The 1936
   text's suffrage clauses; the two verdicts; the four-term series of
   opinions about parties; then the thing nobody disputes (commands obeyed,
   armies fed). Worked as the lesson's first `<Example>`: elemental vs
   existential on one object. The reader should be able to run the same
   test on any constitution.
2. **Agent vs representative** (I.4, 1843–1852): the UN delegate and his
   government. One paragraph, one diagram.
3. **The Lombards / Fortescue** (I.6–I.7): "to be articulate for action was
   to have a king" — the Alans and Suebi kept kingship "though they had no
   need for it"; Fortescue's headless people. Pick one as the illustration of
   articulation preceding institutions.
4. **Varro's painter** (III.4, 3615): "as the painter is prior to the
   painting… so are the cities prior to the institutions of the cities" — and
   Augustine's retort that "true religion is not instituted by some
   terrestrial city". Two sentences, two whole theories of religion. Set
   beside Cicero Laws II.8 ("no new gods unless recognized by the State").
5. **The Edict of Galerius read as do-ut-des** (III.6, 4016–4036). Paraphrase
   the edict's reasoning step by step (the Christians were persecuted → they
   stopped worshipping their God → a powerful God is unworshipped → he may
   avenge it → let them worship and pray for us). Then *divinitas* in 313 as
   a deliberately ambiguous word. A model of reading an administrative text
   for the theology inside it.
6. **Celsus's paragraph** (III.7, 4056–4092): the five steps from "two
   masters" to "an attack on the construction of the imperium Romanum", and
   the "one nomos" exchange with Origen. Build it as an argument map.
7. **Eusebius's Tricennial formula** (III.8, 4160–4170): one basileus / one
   God / one King / one Nomos and Logos — drawn as a mirror. Then the
   Arian/Trinitarian break and Gregory's "no analogue in creation".
8. **The noses of Constantine IV's brothers** (III.8, 4195–4203): the one
   anecdote that shows what an imperial Trinity would have required.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know — anchor at first mention:** imperial
  cult; genius (an emperor's guardian spirit, to which incense was offered);
  pontifex maximus (chief priest of Rome's state religion, a title the

  Licinius, Constantine (the co-emperors of 311–324; Constantine sole ruler
  324–337); Milan 313 (a letter of Licinius, conventionally "Edict of

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (warm-up, the only one).* Define the three terms in one
   sentence each and attach one Roman instance to each (e.g. the oath of 32
   BC; the altar to Fever / the imperial cult; Eusebius's Tricennial
   formula).
2. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill on the Edict of Galerius (311).*
   Metadata: a dying eastern emperor who had persecuted, writing to his
   subjects; purpose; audience. Predict before reading what such a text will
   admit and omit; then read (Lactantius, De mort. pers. 34, PD); then
   records / argues / omits; then: what can it be USED to establish — a
   change of belief, or a change in the imperial system of divinity? Name
   the clause that decides it (the order to pray for the emperor).
3. *Boundary probe.* Set existential representation to zero: a society whose
   constitution is in force but whose commands are not obeyed and whose army
   cannot be fed (the Western empire in the 470s; or any case the reader
   prefers). Does it still "represent a truth"? What does the tool predict
   will happen (Hauriou: an existential representative replaces the
   constitutional one), and does the record of 476 fit?
4. *Claim to refute.* "Constantine converted the empire to Christianity in
   313." Name the failing steps using *divinitas*, the do-ut-des reasoning,
   the Sol coinage and the date of the Theodosian laws (391); state what 313
   did do in the lesson's terms (admit a God to the imperial system without
   changing the system).
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* Celsus says a universal creed destroys
   the national cultures that the empire is built from; Origen says the one
   nomos will come to pass. Derive what each must predict about the empire's
   *existential* representation after a Christian victory: does a society
   that has lost its gods lose its capacity to act? Then test against
   Eusebius (the empire acquired a new truth to represent) and against
   Augustine's "hitherto we have wars". The solution shows the two layers
   can come apart — which is the lesson's thesis derived rather than stated.
6. *Synthesis — counter-argument stem.* Someone claims: "Political theology
   did not end with the Trinity; Byzantium was ruled by God's image for a
   thousand years and Charlemagne took Augustine's chapters on the happy
   emperor as his guidebook." Name two facts the claim fails to account for
   (Gregory's "no analogue"; the noses) and state what would have to be true
   for it to survive (that the theology continued in practice without its
   doctrinal warrant — which is a claim about symbols outliving arguments,
   and may well be true). Leave it open; it is the question mark in the
   heading.
7. **FORWARD (E7)** → `augustine-two-cities`. Take Varro's sentence ("the
   cities are prior to the institutions of the cities") and Scipio's
   definition of a people (`<Ref>` `cicero-mixed-constitution`: bound by
   agreement about right). If the city makes its gods, and justice means
   giving each his due *including the true God* (`<Ref>` `cicero-natural-law`:
   God the author of law), can a city that makes its own gods ever be a
   people in Scipio's sense? Write the inference. The solution says: the
   next lesson's bishop runs exactly this inference and concludes that Rome
   was never a republic — and then has to say what Rome was instead.
8. *Document reasoning — second-order concept: significance (E3
   reach-back).* Significant to whom? For Cicero's Laws II (the city's gods
   as the glue of oaths and treaties), for Celsus, for Eusebius and for a
   Christian martyr of 250, state in one sentence each what the refusal to
   sacrifice *meant* — and what each would have called the person who
   refused.

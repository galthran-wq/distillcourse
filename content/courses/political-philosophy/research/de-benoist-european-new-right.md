# Research brief: de-benoist-european-new-right (De Benoist and the European New Right: ethnopluralism and metapolitics)

status: planned
module: after-liberalism
type: core
goal: Analyze the European New Right's programme — metapolitics, ethnopluralism, the critique of liberalism and of the 'ideology of the Same' — as set out in the 1999 Manifesto, and evaluate it against both liberal and nationalist objections

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `evola-revolt-against-the-modern-world`: `primordial-tradition`,
  `regression-of-castes`, Evola's refusal of the nation, the Church and the bourgeoisie alike,
  his "empire" as a supranational unity bound by fides rather than a centralising state (Revolt
  ch. 12), and the problem his book left: no regime embodied Tradition, so his readers had to
  choose between withdrawal and a long work on ideas (the Evola lesson's FORWARD exercise lands
  the reader here). From `moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich`: `conservative-revolution`, "the
  Third Party" above left and right, "Every people has its own socialism", "Liberalism is the
  death of nations" (Germany's Third Empire, ch. III title), the turn to Russia, Germanic
  democracy of kin and assembly against "parliamentism". From `herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation`:
  `volk`, `plurality-of-cultures` — each people has its centre of happiness in itself "as every
  sphere its centre of gravity" — and Herder's refusal to rank peoples. From the course: Maistre's
  "I have never met man" (`maistre-and-bonald`, `no-such-thing-as-man`); Schmitt's
  `friend-enemy-distinction`, `pluriverse-of-states` and the `secularization-thesis`
  (`schmitt-concept-of-the-political`, `schmitt-political-theology`); Bodin's and Hobbes's
  indivisible sovereign (`bodin-sovereignty`, `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`); Gobineau's and
  Chamberlain's ranking of races (`gobineau-inequality-of-races`, `chamberlain-and-grant`); Barrès
  and Maurras on France first (`barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism`); the postwar
  `anti-fascist-consensus` (`the-right-after-1945`); Rawls's priority of the right over the good
  (`rawls-theory-of-justice`); the replacement argument (`powell-raspail-and-camus`). From the
  module: Deneen's two liberalisms and the pincer (`deneen-post-liberalism`); Hazony's
  `order-of-national-states` and his verdict that EU "subsidiarity" is "a euphemism for empire"
  (`hazony-virtue-of-nationalism`); Vermeule's top-down subsidiarity "released in a state of
  exception" (`vermeule-common-good-constitutionalism`; that lesson's FORWARD exercise asked the
  reader to write the bottom-up definition).
- **Contributes:** One move: the fourth post-liberal answer, and the only one that is NEITHER
  nationalist NOR universalist. Where Hazony defends the national state and Vermeule a classical
  law of universal reason, the 1999 Manifesto rejects both the nation-state and universalism and
  defends PEOPLES: each has a right to remain different, and that right "has significance only in
  terms of its generality" — I may claim my difference only if I defend yours. The lesson
  introduces `metapolitics` (win the culture before seeking power) and `ethnopluralism` (the right
  to difference, against both assimilation and hierarchy), reconstructs the diagnosis (modernity as
  five processes rooted in Christianity; liberalism, not communism, as "the main enemy"), the
  anthropology (man "as such does not exist"), and the politics (a federal Europe of regions and
  peoples built from the bottom up, democracy as equality of MEMBERS, restriction of immigration
  plus a communitarian model for those already present) — and then asks whether the programme holds
  together, against the liberal objection (difference without rank is still separation), the
  nationalist objection (a federal Europe is an empire), and the objection from its own lineage.
- **Hands off:** The manifesto rejects the "Europe of Nations" and the Jacobin nation-state; the
  module's application (`hungarys-fundamental-law`) reads a constitution that does the opposite — a
  national state that defines its people by a thousand-year history, a Christian heritage and a
  nation extending beyond its borders, inside a union it contests. The reader will judge which of
  the four post-liberal theories (Deneen, Hazony, Vermeule, de Benoist) the text actually embodies.
  The synthesis (`the-argument-assembled`) places the right to difference as the last answer to
  "what holds a people together" and the question whether it is still a right-wing answer.
- **Spine step:** "After liberalism: Deneen says it failed by succeeding, Hazony defends the order
  of national states, Vermeule the common good, de Benoist the right to difference — Hungary's
  constitution as the case — and the whole argument is assembled"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): in 1968 young French writers on the right concluded that elections
were lost before they were held, in the culture; thirty years later their founder publishes a
manifesto rejecting nationalism, racism, liberalism and globalisation at once, and defending the
right of every people to remain different; what does such a programme rest on, and does it hold
together? Anchor (L11): Alain de Benoist (b. 1943), journalist and essayist, who began as a
student activist in the campaign to keep Algeria French (Algeria's war of independence,
1954–62); GRECE founded in 1968, the year of the May student revolt in Paris — the anchor event;
the press named it the "New Right" (Nouvelle Droite) in 1979, a name it never chose; Charles
Champetier, then editor of its magazine Éléments, co-author. The newcomer's problem, sharpened by
`<Ref the-right-after-1945>` (`anti-fascist-consensus`): every word of the old right had been
tied to the regimes defeated in 1945; how does a right with no party, no electorate and a vocabulary
it cannot use act at all?

1. **Why work on ideas instead of winning elections?** Obvious attempt: found a party. The
   movement's answer, borrowed from an enemy: the Italian Communist leader Antonio Gramsci
   (1891–1937), jailed by Mussolini, had argued in his prison notebooks that a class rules first by
   the consent organised in schools, press, churches and books — "hegemony" — and only then by
   force; so the left must win the culture before it can hold the state. GRECE read Gramsci from the
   right (Fawcett's "Gramscian toil"; Vu de droite, 1977): `metapolitics`. Then the 1999 text's own
   definition (l.106–117) and its disclaimer — "neither a 'strategy' to impose intellectual
   hegemony, nor an attempt to discredit" — and the reader is asked to hold both: the founders'
   Gramscian strategy and the manifesto's claim that metapolitics is only the premise that ideas
   move history. Ref `<Ref evola-revolt-against-the-modern-world>` in one clause: Evola's readers
   had already been told the way back was blocked; withdrawal into "testimony" is the seed.
   Because: if politics follows culture, a defeated right that cannot win votes can still change
   what voters take for granted.
2. **What is wrong with the modern world?** Obvious attempt (the right's usual answer): communism,
   secularism, decadence of morals. The manifesto: modernity is five converging processes —
   individualisation, massification, desacralisation, rationalisation, universalisation (l.161–170)
   — and its source is Christianity: individual salvation → individualism, equal souls →
   egalitarianism, providential history → progress, revealed truth for all → universalism
   (l.171–189). Ref `<Ref schmitt-political-theology>` (`secularization-thesis`) for "secularised
   theological concepts". Say the seam: one expects a defence of the Christian West from a
   movement of the right; this one blames Christianity for the West's universalism — Fawcett notes
   it was "new in turning its back on Christian conservatism". Nietzsche link (see below).
3. **Why is liberalism, not communism, "the main enemy"?** Obvious attempt (the Cold War right):
   communism is the enemy, liberalism the ally. The manifesto: liberalism and Marxism are twins of
   the Enlightenment (the list at l.299–309) and liberalism "has only realised more effectively"
   their shared aims; market economy became market society; the welfare state is liberalism's
   complement, not its opposite; and liberalism "denies the specificity of politics" — it decides
   "what is just rather than what is good" (l.337–338). Two backward hooks, one clause each:
   Moeller's "liberalism is the death of nations" (`<Ref moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich>`) and
   Deneen's two liberalisms and pincer (`<Ref deneen-post-liberalism>`); one forward-looking
   contrast: Rawls's priority of the right over the good (`<Ref rawls-theory-of-justice>`) is
   exactly what II.4 inverts ("the 'good' precedes the idea of the 'just'").
4. **If there is no "man in general", what is a human being?** Obvious attempt (the liberal): a
   bearer of universal rights, identical everywhere. The manifesto: "man as such does not exist"
   (l.407–410 — set beside Maistre's "I have never met man", `<Ref maistre-and-bonald>`,
   `no-such-thing-as-man`, and note the difference: Maistre's point is the nation's particular
   constitution, the manifesto's adds that this "does not stem from relativism"); humanity is
   "irreducibly plural"; each culture has its "centre of gravity" — HERDER, quoted in German and
   English (set piece; `<Ref herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation>` for `plurality-of-cultures`); so
   "all attempts to unify them end up destroying them", and universal law is "the basis of all
   totalitarianisms". Anthropology underneath (II.1): neither blank slate nor genetic fate; "Man is
   an heir, but he can dispose of his heritage". Because: if belonging is constitutive, a politics
   that dissolves belongings dissolves persons.
5. **If peoples differ, are some better? The right to difference.** Obvious attempt from the old
   right (`<Ref gobineau-inequality-of-races>`, `<Ref chamberlain-and-grant>`): difference means
   rank. The manifesto's surprise, said as a seam: it defines racism by three postulates —
   qualitative hierarchy of races, individual worth deduced from race, race as the central factor
   in history — and declares "All three are false" (l.919); racism is a doctrine of positivist
   science and social evolution, not mere prejudice. Then the move that defines the movement:
   there are TWO anti-racisms. Universalist anti-racism reduces "the 'Other' to the 'Same'"
   (assimilation) and so "leads to the same conclusions as does the racism it denounces";
   differentialist anti-racism refuses "both exclusion and assimilation: neither apartheid nor the
   melting pot". Introduce `ethnopluralism` here, with the honest note that the manifesto's own
   words are "right to difference" and "differentialist anti-racism" — "ethnopluralism" is the
   movement's wider vocabulary (de Benoist's self-description, per References). Then the
   GENERALITY CLAUSE (III.1, l.900–904) quoted: the right to difference holds only if it is
   everyone's. Because: a right claimed only for oneself is privilege; universalised, it becomes a
   principle of a world of many peoples.
6. **What political form follows? Not the nation-state.** Obvious attempt (the reader's
   expectation after Hazony, `<Ref hazony-virtue-of-nationalism>`): a defence of the national
   state. The manifesto rejects it: Bodin's and Hobbes's indivisible sovereign (`<Ref
   bodin-sovereignty>`, `<Ref hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty>`) destroyed intermediate bodies and led
   to "absolutist monarchy, revolutionary Jacobinism, and, finally, modern totalitarianism"
   (l.552–554); against it Althusius (Johannes Althusius, 1563–1638, a German Calvinist jurist whose
   Politica, 1603, described the commonwealth as a federation of families, guilds, towns and
   provinces — anchor at first mention); the nation-state "too big … too small"; a federal Europe
   built "from the bottom up", "in close continental association with Russia", with subsidiarity
   "the keystone at every level" — authority delegated upward only for what the lower level cannot
   do (l.1111–1114); and explicitly against a "Europe of Nations". Then the world: a
   "pluriversum" of civilisations (`<Ref schmitt-concept-of-the-political>`, `pluriverse-of-states`),
   power as "the ability to resist the influence of others rather than to impose one's own".
   "Empire in the federal sense" (course key idea): the manifesto says the Althusian view "has
   inspired both imperial and federal constructions" (l.559) — the model is the Holy Roman Empire
   as a federation of autonomies, not a conquest state; show the Evola echo (empire as a
   "metapolitical unity", ch. 12) in one clause. Seam: from a book of the right one expects the
   nation; the record shows its enemy is named as "a 'Europe of Nations', a mere assemblage of
   national egos".
7. **Who belongs, and what about the people already here?** Democracy (III.7): popular sovereignty
   since the Greek city and "the ancient German 'freedoms'" (Moeller's Germanic democracy — one
   clause); political equality is equality of MEMBERS, not of men — it "implies the possibility of a
   distinction … between citizens and non-citizens" (`<Ref schmitt-concept-of-the-political>` for
   the friend/enemy, citizen/foreigner criterion; the echo of Schmitt's democratic identity stated
   as the manifesto's own). Immigration (III.3): restriction plus aid to poor countries; blame on
   the industrial nations, not the migrants; for those already settled, "it would be illusory to
   expect their departure en masse" — instead a COMMUNITARIAN model in which ethnocultural identity
   is recognised in the public sphere and citizenship may in time be decoupled from nationality.
   Set against `<Ref powell-raspail-and-camus>` (`replacement-as-argument`): the manifesto shares the
   diagnosis that mass immigration is "an undeniably negative phenomenon" and refuses the remedy of
   removal. Because: once difference is a right, the stranger already inside has it too.
8. **The OBJECTIONS at full strength, and whether it holds together** (one `##`, four `###`).
   - **(a) The liberal objection: separation by another name.** At strength: a doctrine that
     says cultures are destroyed by mixing and that each people should keep its own way, whose
     first policy is immigration restriction, reaches by the vocabulary of difference the
     conclusions the old racism reached by the vocabulary of rank — critics in the 1980s named it
     "differentialist racism" (state as the course's own objection; Taguieff and Balibar in
     References). Second strand: the communitarian model ends common citizenship — equal rights
     for individuals become different statuses for groups (the French republican objection). Third
     (Fawcett): such programmes "pay homage to liberalism by disavowing ethnic prejudice" — the
     disavowal is borrowed from the liberalism they reject. The manifesto's replies from its text:
     the generality clause ("cannot be used to exclude others"); the three postulates rejected;
     support for regional languages and "peoples struggling against Western imperialism"; the
     communitarian model is offered for immigrants already present, not as expulsion. Where the
     lesson leaves it: the manifesto never says what a people may do to a member of another people
     who wants to join it — the generality clause protects groups, not the individual who wants to
     leave his group (contrast Deneen's Amish anecdote, `<Ref deneen-post-liberalism>`, one
     clause).
   - **(b) The nationalist objection: a federal Europe is an empire.** At strength, from Hazony
     (`<Ref hazony-virtue-of-nationalism>`): "who decides?" — in any federation the centre decides
     what the lower levels "cannot" do, so bottom-up subsidiarity becomes top-down in the first
     dispute (Maastricht read against itself, l.1138–1142); "pooled sovereignty" is make-believe; and
     a "Europe of regions" dissolves the nations that actually exist into units no one has died
     for. From the integral nationalists (`<Ref barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism>`): France
     first; the nation is the inheritance one accepts, not a level in a federation. The
     manifesto's replies: the summit's powers are listed and limited (l.1128–1133); "a 'Europe of
     Nations'" cannot prevent future wars; national egos are what produced 1914–45 (III.6). Leave
     the dispute open: it is the dispute the module's case (Hungary inside the EU) puts to a test.
   - **(c) The objection from its lineage.** At strength: the movement's sources are the
     Conservative Revolution and Evola — Moeller's anti-liberalism and "every people has its own
     socialism", Evola's anti-Christian Tradition, the tripartite ranking of wisdom over force over
     wealth (II.6), "paganism" (II.9); a 1981 article already called it the fascism of Evola and
     de Benoist (Sheehan, References). Teach what is fact and what is inference. Fact: the
     founder's own books and later prefaces engage these authors (Vu de droite; the introduction to
     the Arktos Moeller); the manifesto keeps their anti-liberalism, their anti-universalism, the
     "third way" beyond left and right, the empire as a federation of autonomies. Also fact,
     equally load-bearing: the manifesto departs from both — it defends democracy and popular
     sovereignty where Evola called democracy the rule of the lowest caste and Moeller wanted a
     "democracy with a leader"; it calls racism false where Evola kept a "race of the spirit"; it
     defends "total freedom of expression". The inference (a lineage proves the doctrine) is the
     critic's; the manifesto's reply is the "excluded middle" (Introduction): it takes "what seems
     valuable in all currents of thought", Gramsci included. Draw the comparison as a table
     (figure 5) and let the reader judge.
   - **(d) Does it hold together? The strongest internal objection.** A manifesto that calls every
     "absolute, universal, and eternal law" the basis of totalitarianism rests its whole programme
     on one universal right — the right to difference, which "has significance only in terms of
     its generality". Either the right is universal (and the anti-universalism is qualified to "no
     universal CULTURE, one universal rule of coexistence"), or it is not (and it is one people's
     preference). The lesson works this through and concedes: the manifesto's own wording (II.2
     "This observation does not stem from relativism"; "All men have in common their human nature")
     shows it takes the first horn; its anti-universalism is aimed at universal ways of LIFE, not
     at all universal norms. What it then owes, and does not supply, is who enforces the universal
     rule of coexistence among peoples in a world where power is only "the ability to resist" —
     the same "who decides?" Hazony put to the EU. Fawcett's charge that it has "barely a sketch of
     an alternative" is placed here as the liberal verdict, and the manifesto's III.5–III.7 (local
     decision, referendum, participatory democracy) as its answer.

Headings as claims (suggested): "Culture before power"; "Christianity, the religion of the way out of
religion"; "Liberalism, not communism, is the main enemy"; "Man as such does not exist"; "Two
anti-racisms"; "Against the nation-state, for a federal Europe"; "Equality of members, not of men";
"Separation by another name?"; "Empire under another name?"; "A universal right against
universalism".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms and events to anchor at first mention (L11)**, at most three per section: May 1968
  (student revolt and general strike in France); the Algerian war (1954–62); GRECE (spell out and
  translate the acronym once); "New Right" / Nouvelle Droite (a press name of 1979); Éléments
  (GRECE's magazine); Telos (an American journal of the New Left that published the translation in
  1999 — itself a fact worth one clause: a left journal judged the text worth printing); Gramsci
  and "hegemony"; Jacobinism (the centralising republicanism of the French Revolution's Jacobin
  club: one law, one language, citizens with no intermediate bodies — the manifesto's target);
  Althusius; Westphalia (1648); the "New Class"; Polanyi (Karl Polanyi, The Great Transformation,
  1944 — the reader has him from `<Ref deneen-post-liberalism>`, one clause); pluriversum; "Nomos
  of the Earth" (Schmitt, 1950 — one clause).
- **The manifesto does not contain the words "ethnopluralism" or "Gramsci".** The concept names come
  from the movement's wider record (Fawcett; the scholarship in References). Say where each word
  comes from; never put either in the manifesto's mouth. Likewise the concept definition's "each
  people … on its own territory" is stronger than III.3, which accepts settled immigrant
  communities and proposes a communitarian model — teach the gap, do not paper over it.
- **The lineage claim needs care.** course.yaml notes call the lineage from Moeller and Evola "a
  fact of the movement's own self-description". The 1999 manifesto names neither; the dossier
  grounds the lineage through (i) de Benoist's introduction to the Arktos 2012 Moeller (reported in
  `moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich` brief and sources.yaml, text not held), (ii) Fawcett's account,
  (iii) the critics' (Sheehan, cited in the Evola volume). The Arktos back-list at the end of the
  file (Moeller, Evola, Faye) is the publisher's catalogue, not GRECE's self-description. State the
  lineage as documented engagement plus the manifesto's visible echoes (figure 5), and the
  departures with equal weight.
- **The "biological" passages** (II.1 hereditary predispositions; "cognitive ability is the main
  determinant of social status"; III.4 on sex) — quote neutrally, Ref the heredity module
  (`<Ref hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations>`, `<Ref gould-and-harden-on-heredity>`) only if used;
  do not treat them as proof of either a racist subtext or a scientific grounding. They are claims
  the manifesto asserts without evidence; say that, and no more.
- **Do not re-teach** Herder, Evola, Moeller, Maistre, Schmitt, Bodin, Hazony, Deneen: Ref and one
  clause each. The new matter is metapolitics, the two anti-racisms, the generality clause, the
  federal Europe of peoples, membership equality, and the four objections.
- **Real misreading (L13 slot, optional):** "The New Right is a nationalist movement." Correct
  claim: the manifesto rejects the nation-state, the "Europe of Nations" and "chauvinistic
  nationalism" (III.1); misconception stated once; why it fails (III.6 text); replacement model
  (peoples, regions, a federal Europe, a pluriverse of civilisations); discriminating example (its
  stance on Corsican or Breton autonomy vs a French nationalist's — III.1's "regional cultures under
  the threat of extinction"). Only if the writer judges the misreading prevalent among the course's
  readers; otherwise no slot.
- **Later influence** (identitarian movements, Faye, Dugin, the populist right) is contested and
  outside the dossier; at most one sentence, attributed and in References, no list of admirers.
- **Register.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or
  approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label
  may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. This governs "fascist",
  "racist", "far right", "neo-pagan" (all critics' or journalists' words — attribute or omit) and
  equally the manifesto's own labels for its opponents ("thought police", "New Class", Tartuffe),
  which stay inside quotation marks. The reader summary's characterisation of the text as defining
  opponents "by caricature" is a reader's judgement — the lesson shows the claim about liberalism
  (I.3) and the liberal reply, and lets the reader judge.
- **Translation:** the Telos translation is the authorised English; where a key French term matters
  give it once: droit à la différence (right to difference); métapolitique; Nouvelle Droite. The
  French original (Éléments 94) is not in the dossier.
- **Dossier thin:** only the 1999 manifesto is held from de Benoist himself; Vu de droite, the
  Gramsci colloquium, the Moeller introduction and the critics are not. The lesson is therefore a
  reading of ONE text; it should say so in its References note and avoid claims about the
  movement's other writings beyond what Fawcett reports.

1. **Retrieval and chronology (warm-up, E3):** Place in order and date to the decade: Herder's
   "centre of gravity"; Moeller's Third Reich; Evola's Revolt; the founding of GRECE; the
   Maastricht Treaty; the manifesto. For each earlier text, name the one idea the manifesto keeps
   (`<Ref herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation>`, `<Ref moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich>`,
   `<Ref evola-revolt-against-the-modern-world>`).
2. **Document reasoning (corroboration):** Two descriptions of metapolitics — the manifesto's
   (l.106–117: "neither a 'strategy' to impose intellectual hegemony …") and Fawcett's account of
   "years of unsung Gramscian toil" (paraphrased in the exercise; source in References). Metadata
   first (who, when, for whom); where do they agree, where do they conflict, and what can each be
   USED to establish about GRECE's strategy in the 1970s and in 1999?
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "The European New Right is a racist movement in the sense the
   manifesto itself defines." Work it strictly: state the manifesto's three postulates, check the
   text against each (III.2 rejects all three), and conclude what the claim must change to survive
   (move to the critics' "differentialist" sense — and then name the step that sense requires: that
   the right to difference functions to exclude, which III.1 denies in words and III.3 partly
   concedes in policy).
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** Run the generality clause at its degenerate case. A member of people A
   wants to leave A's culture and join people B, and B does not want him. What does the right to
   difference say — whose difference wins, the individual's or each people's? Show from II.2 ("Man
   is an heir, but he can dispose of his heritage") and III.1 that the manifesto's principle protects
   groups and is silent on exit and entry, and state which earlier theory in the course answers
   the case (Carens's open borders, `<Ref carens-open-borders>`; Miller's self-determination,
   `<Ref miller-strangers-in-our-midst>`).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** The manifesto rejects universal law and asserts a
   universal right to difference. Derive the weakest universal norm the manifesto needs to stay
   consistent, and show which sentence of II.2 already concedes it ("All men have in common their
   human nature"). Then state one concrete case the norm must decide and the manifesto does not
   (e.g., a practice of people A that harms its own members).
6. **Synthesis — the nationalist objection (E3, `hazony-virtue-of-nationalism`):** Write Hazony's
   reply to III.6 in his own terms ("who decides?"; subsidiarity as "a euphemism for empire") and
   the manifesto's rejoinder ("a 'Europe of Nations' … cannot prevent future wars"). Which has the
   better account of 1914–45? Use Hazony's reading of the world wars as imperial wars and the
   manifesto's as wars of nation-states.
7. **Research-shaped (E6d):** Design the test that would distinguish the two readings of
   ethnopluralism — "a principle of coexistence among many peoples" vs "separation by another
   name". Name the evidence each predicts in the movement's own policy proposals after 1999
   (stance on regional languages; on settled immigrants' citizenship; on intermarriage; on asylum)
   and the sources you would need (GRECE's later publications; Éléments; the movement's
   collaborators) — and say which of those the course's dossier holds (only the 1999 text).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `hungarys-fundamental-law`:** The manifesto rejects the nation-state and the

   of the EU writes a constitution that opens with "the members of the [national] nation", makes

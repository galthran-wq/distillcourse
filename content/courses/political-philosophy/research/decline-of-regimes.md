# Research brief: decline-of-regimes (The decline of regimes: from aristocracy to tyranny)

status: planned
module: greek-discovery
type: core
goal: Explain Plato's account of how each regime produces the man who destroys it, why the freedom of democracy ends in tyranny, and what the Laws' moderated Sparta adds

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`: the city of three classes ruled by those who know; the city–soul analogy (a regime and a type of soul share one shape); the metals and the rulers' duty to sort births; the FORWARD exercise there asked what happens when one sorting goes wrong. From `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`: Thrasymachus's praise of the tyrant as the happiest man, still unanswered. From `thucydides-power-and-justice`: a real democracy described by its own leader — rule by the many, "we do not feel called upon to be angry with our neighbour for doing what he likes", obedience to law from fear of disgrace (the Funeral Oration); the same assembly voting a massacre and reversing it overnight (Mytilene), Cleon "by far the most powerful with the commons" calling it "the audience of a rhetorician"; the plague, in which "fear of gods or law of man there was none to restrain them"; and the historian's judgment that after Pericles the city was given over to "the whims of the multitude" (II.65). Plato's Book VIII democracy is the second portrait of the city the reader met there, drawn by a hostile hand a generation later. From `greek-miracle`: isonomia, the two equalities and the agora — the democratic city the reader has already seen being built; Sparta's kings and overseers (one clause).
- **Contributes:** One move: a regime is a psychology before it is a constitution, and each regime raises the son who overthrows it. Five regimes, five men, four transitions, each driven by the excess of the regime's own defining good — honour, wealth, freedom — and the last, tyranny, shown from inside as the most enslaved soul. This is the course's first theory of political change and its first answer to Thrasymachus on the tyrant's happiness. Then the second telling (Laws III), which the goal now requires: Persia and Athens as the two "mother forms" ruined by excess of despotism and excess of freedom, and Sparta saved by mixing — two kings, twenty-eight elders of equal weight, ephors like magistrates chosen by lot. This lesson INTRODUCES the concept `mixed-constitution` (concepts.yaml): it must define it here, at full strength, because `platos-laws-second-best-city` is an extra and only reinforces it, and `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, `sparta-as-test-case`, `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome` all build on it.
- **Hands off:** Whether the chain is a law is DECIDED here: as a fixed sequence every city follows it fails (strong), Aristotle's objection from the record holding; the demagogue-to-tyrant mechanism holds on balance for archaic Greece and fails for the democracy Plato knew best, Athens. What passes on is the second telling's remedy: Plato tells the story twice (Republic VIII as a sequence of sons, Laws III as the history of Persia and Athens), and the second telling has a different moral — not "philosophers must rule" but "mix the two mother forms"; whether the mixture keeps a city stable is tested in `sparta-as-test-case` (and `british-franchise`). `platos-laws-second-best-city` (extra) takes the mixing; `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` and `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life` take Aristotle's objection that real regimes do not change in Plato's order; `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome` inherits the cycle (six simple forms decaying in order) and the mixture as Rome's, and `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law` has Scipio translate this book's democracy-to-tyranny passage into Latin (Rep. I.66–68). The democratic man returns as Tocqueville's and Ortega's (`tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`, `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`).
- **Spine step:** "Plato answers 'who should rule' with knowledge: justice is each part doing its own work, so the city should be ruled by those who know the good — and freedom left to itself slides through democracy into tyranny"

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** Plato's chain (timocracy → oligarchy → democracy → tyranny) as a law every city follows, ending in democratic freedom breeding tyranny.
- **Verdict:** split. (a) As a fixed sequence, **fails** — grade **strong**. (b) The demagogue-to-tyrant mechanism: **holds for archaic Greece** — grade **on balance**; it fails for the democracy Plato knew best.
- **What decides it:** (a) Aristotle's objection, Politics V.12 (1316a): regimes change into any other, "a democracy is even more likely to change into an oligarchy than into a monarchy", and tyranny often passes into another tyranny; the chain has no last link and no single cause. (b) Politics V.5 (1305a7–10, staged `texts/aristotle/politics-jowett-ross-1921.txt` l. 6118–6120): "Of old, the demagogue was also a general, and then democracies changed into tyrannies. Most of the ancient tyrants were originally demagogues" — with Peisistratus at Athens and Theagenes at Megara as cases (l. 6144–6147); Aristotle adds that this is "not so now", because orators are not generals, and "instances to the contrary are few and slight" (l. 6110–6115). Athens's democracy was overthrown by oligarchs in 411 (the Four Hundred) and 404 (the Thirty) and ended under Macedon (after Chaironeia, 338, and the Lamian War, 322), never in a tyranny raised by the demos.
- **Strongest objection, and why it does not overturn the verdict:** Plato never meant the chain as a law; the text calls it a "sketch" of types and runs it in the order of the soul (the drafted lesson's "### What kind of sequence it is"), so the verdict judges a claim nobody made. It does not overturn the verdict: the text also states its first step as a law of all that comes into being ("everything which has a beginning has also an end"), the Laws III telling applies the same mechanism to real states (Persia, Athens), and the chain as a law of regimes is what the tradition inherited from the book (the cycle at Rome). The lesson keeps the distinction the objection rests on — read as a psychology of what each regime's honours do to its sons, Book VIII survives, and Aristotle keeps that claim ("education adapted to the constitution"). For (b), the nearest counter-case is classical Syracuse: Dionysius I won the tyranny in 405 by denouncing "Daphnaeus and the rich" (V.5, l. 6148–6151). One case, which Aristotle himself sets against his "few and slight", does not make a body of classical democracies ending in tyranny.
- **What would overturn it:** a body of classical-era democracies that ended in tyrannies raised from the demos.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): a city ruled by the best decays into one ruled by the honour-loving, then the money-loving, then the free, then a tyrant; Plato tells it as fathers and sons; work through the five regimes and the five men and ask whether the chain is a law or a warning. Teach it as Plato's psychology of regimes; then, in the last section, test it as a law and pass the verdict (see VERDICT).

1. **Why would the best city change at all?** The obvious attempt: an external shock — war, plague. Why it fails for Plato: "all political change begins in division of the ruling element"; a united ruling class cannot be moved by anything outside it. The answer: the Muses' joke about the nuptial number — the rulers, for all their wisdom, cannot time every birth; a mistimed birth puts iron where gold should be; the metals mix; the ruling class divides between those who want land and gold and those who want virtue. Because: the city of `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king` depended on sorting births correctly (`<Ref>` the FORWARD exercise), and that dependence is where it breaks. State the method sentence: "states are as the men are; they grow out of human characters" — a regime is a kind of soul written large, so to explain a regime's fall is to explain how it raises its own successor.
2. **Timocracy: the rule of honour — and the man it makes.** The compromise between the two factions: land and houses are parcelled out privately, the former free dependants are made serfs, the spirited soldiers rule, honour is the public good and gold is hoarded in secret. The timocratic man: the son of a quiet, decent father who holds no office and is called a fool by his mother, who sees the servants admire the ambitious — he is pulled between his father's reason and the house's appetite and settles in the middle, at spirit: proud, a lover of command and victory, "losing philosophy tempered with music". Because: a boy raised in a house where honour is what the servants admire learns to love honour. Mark the template the lesson will reuse four times: origin of the regime → its defining good → its characteristic defect → the man, formed by father, mother and servants.
3. **Oligarchy: when honour gives way to wealth.** The hoarded gold becomes the standard; a property qualification for office — "the pilot chosen by wealth is a shipwreck". Defects: two cities in one (rich and poor) living in the same walls; it cannot wage war because it fears to arm the poor and will not pay for mercenaries; everything is for sale, so men lose their function and become drones — stingless paupers and stinged criminals. The oligarchic man: son of a ruined timocrat who saw his father fall, he enthrones gain and makes reason and spirit its slaves — a miser who is honest only where it pays, "two men in one". Because: a regime that measures men by property teaches its sons to measure themselves by it.
4. **Democracy: the poor win, and freedom becomes the good.** The oligarchs breed their own destroyers: they will not curb the spendthrifts (they want to buy their land), so a class of armed, dispossessed, resentful men grows; on campaign the lean sunburnt poor man stands beside the puffing rich one and thinks "men like him are only rich because no one has the courage to despoil them". The poor win, kill or exile the rich, share offices by lot. The regime's character, at full strength and in Plato's own appreciative words: freedom and frankness; every kind of life, like an embroidered robe; a bazaar of constitutions where a founder can shop; no one compelled to rule or be ruled; a man under sentence walks the streets unnoticed; "supremely delightful — for the moment"; it dispenses "a sort of equality to equals and unequals alike" — THE phrase that names which of `greek-miracle`'s two equalities democracy chose. Seam (L10b, one sentence with `<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-justice`): the features are the ones Pericles praised in Thucydides' account — rule by the many, every man living as he likes without his neighbour's anger — now described by a philosopher whose teacher that democracy executed; the reader can hold the two portraits of one city side by side (the paired-source exercise). Because: the defining good of the regime is freedom, and freedom means nothing may rule anything.
5. **The democratic man: the soul with no ruling part.** Necessary vs unnecessary desires; the miser's son, raised without the arts, tastes the drones' honey and his soul becomes a civil war; the false counsellors seize the citadel and rename the vices — insolence "breeding", anarchy "liberty", waste "magnificence", impudence "courage"; he lives from day to day — drink and the flute, then a water-diet, then the gymnasium, then idleness, then "philosophy", then politics, then a soldier, then a businessman — and "declares that all pleasures are equal". Concept `democratic-man`. The lesson's dry seam: from the regime of freedom one would expect the freest soul; the text shows a soul ruled by whichever desire arrives, free of everything except its appetites. Because: if nothing may rule anything, nothing in the soul may rule anything either. Forward links course.yaml asks to make explicit: one clause, placed in the Conclusion or in an exercise solution, never as a roadmap in the body — the democratic man is re-described in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries as the citizen of soft despotism and as the mass-man (`<Ref>` `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`, `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`).
6. **Why freedom ends in tyranny — the lesson's central derivation.** "The insatiable good of democracy is freedom." A democracy "thirsting for freedom" with "evil cup-bearers" drinks the wine of freedom unmixed; any ruler who restrains is called an oligarch; the father fears the son, the teacher the pupil, the old imitate the young, slaves are as free as their masters, "the she-dogs are as good as their mistresses and the asses claim the road" — "the excess of liberty, whether in states or individuals, seems only to pass into excess of slavery". Mechanism, step by step: three classes (the drones who run the assembly, the orderly rich they prey on, the working people who want their share of the rich's estates); the rich defend themselves and are called oligarchs and plotters; the people raise a protector; he tastes kindred blood by false accusation and becomes the wolf of Lycaean Zeus; he asks for a bodyguard "lest the people's friend be lost to them"; he is a tyrant full grown. Then the handbook of tyranny: smiles and debt relief; perpetual war so the people need a leader and are too poor to plot; the purge of the brave, wise and rich — "the reverse of a physician's"; a bodyguard of freed slaves and foreigners; the temple treasures, then "his father's estate", the people; the son beats the father: "out of the smoke into the fire". Because: a soul and a city with no ruling part are ruled by whatever is strongest, and the strongest desire is the one that wants everything.
7. **The tyrant from inside — Thrasymachus answered.** Book IX: every man has lawless desires that wake in dreams; the tyrannical man is one in whom they are awake all day, ruled by a single master passion, eros, with Madness as its bodyguard; he robs his parents, then the temples; his gang makes a tyrant of the worst of them. The slave-owner with fifty slaves carried by a god into a wilderness where no freeman will help him: he would flatter his own slaves and fear for his life — the tyrant's whole life. The tyrant "never knows freedom or friendship"; he cannot travel; he lives shut in like a woman in the house. The three proofs that the just life is happier: by the ranking of the five men (the herald's proclamation); by the three pleasures, which only the philosopher has tasted all of; by the truth of pleasures — the 729 is a joke the lesson states as a joke. Mark the reply to `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`: Thrasymachus called the man who enslaves a city "happy and blessed"; Plato's answer is a portrait, not a proof, and the lesson says which.
8. **The second telling, and Plato's own signals against the predictive reading.** (Aristotle's objection, formerly here, moves to step 9, the weighing.) Plato tells the story again in the Laws (III): the "fountain-head of seditions" is that seven titles to rule (parents, the noble, the elder, masters, the stronger, the wise, the lot) all claim at once; Persia and Athens are the two "mother forms", and each decayed by its own excess — Persia by despotism and a royal education by women and eunuchs (Cyrus's sons, Xerxes), Athens by freedom ("as they led their people into utter servitude, so we led ours into all freedom"), beginning with the theatre audience judging music it had no right to judge (the "theatrocracy" — the reader will hear Cleon's "audience of a rhetorician" in it; one clause, `<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-justice`). The moral is different: not rule by the wise, but mix the two. THE CONCEPT IS INTRODUCED HERE (concepts.yaml `mixed-constitution`): Laws III ll. 815–843 — "do not give too great power to anything", a sail too large, too much food, too much authority; Sparta, alone of the three Dorian kingdoms, survived because a god gave it two kings instead of one, "human wisdom" added twenty-eight elders "equal with the kings in the most important matters", and a "third saviour" added the ephors, "whose power resembles that of magistrates elected by lot" — so that the kingly power was "compounded of the right elements and duly moderated". Define the mixed constitution in the lesson's own words from this passage: a regime that combines a monarchic, an aristocratic and a popular element so that each checks the others and none can rule alone. Then Megillus's wall (Laws IV ll. 389–397): asked which of the regimes Sparta is, he cannot say — tyranny (the ephors), democracy, aristocracy, the oldest monarchy. State plainly that not being nameable is the point of a mixture, and pass on whether a regime nobody can name is a regime at all (the FORWARD exercise; Aristotle answers it in part in `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, and whether a mixed state can stand is decided in `english-revolution-as-test`). Figure 7 carries it. Then Aristotle at full strength: the cycle is not peculiar to the ideal city (any regime can be entered at any point); tyranny changes into tyranny as often as into anything; oligarchy has several causes of change, not one; there are many democracies and oligarchies, not one of each; and his own list runs kingship → oligarchy → tyranny → democracy. The lesson concedes Plato's own signals: as he tells it, the sequence is a story about a type, told in the order that makes the psychology clear; as a theory of what a regime does to the souls raised in it, it is the thing Aristotle keeps (he too says "education adapted to the constitution" is the first preservative).
9. **The weighing: the chain fails as a law; the demagogue's road to tyranny was archaic.** The last body section (see REVISION GAIN 2). Aristotle's V.12 against the fixed order; V.5 for the mechanism in archaic Greece ("most of the ancient tyrants were originally demagogues"; "not so now"); Athens's record (ostracism; 411 and 404 to oligarchs; the end under Macedon). Strongest objection: Plato never meant a law — answered by the text's own law of all that begins and the Laws' application to Persia and Athens; Syracuse 405 as the nearest classical counter-case. The verdict in words: fails as a fixed sequence (strong); the mechanism on balance for archaic Greece, failing for Athens; overturned only by a body of classical democracies that ended in tyrannies raised from the demos. What survives is the psychology. `<Conclusion>`: the verdict in plain words with its grades; then the open problem — if the sequence can be interrupted by mixing, whether a mixture with no nameable ruling part is a regime at all, and who in it is sovereign (`aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`; the Laws' own second-best city is the optional `platos-laws-second-best-city`). One clause may say that the cycle and the mixture go to Rome (`polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`), and that a Roman statesman's dialogue translates the democracy passage into Latin (`cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`) — or leave both for the exercise solutions.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The Muses' speech and the nuptial number** (VIII L1611–1665): the rulers "with all their wisdom" will miss the right season for births; iron mixes with silver, brass with gold; "everything which has a beginning has also an end". Half a page; state the number as Plato's joke (Jowett footnotes it) and do not compute it.
2. **The timocratic son** (VIII L1751–1837): the quiet father who avoids office, the mother who calls him a fool, the servants who admire the pushy — the boy "pulled both ways" settling at spirit. Work it as the lesson's worked `<Example>` of the template (origin → good → defect → man), so the reader can run the same template for oligarchy themselves (exercise 1).
3. **The rich man beside the poor soldier** (VIII L2236–2244): on campaign or on a voyage, the lean sunburnt poor man beside the pale puffing rich one — "men like him are only rich because no one has the courage to despoil them". One paragraph; the moment oligarchy breeds its destroyers.
4. **The embroidered robe and the bazaar of constitutions** (VIII L2269–2345): Plato's generous portrait of democracy — variety, no compulsion to rule or be ruled, the condemned man strolling about "like a hero", the contempt for all the training the city in speech demanded, "supremely delightful … for the moment". Quote the "equality to equals and unequals alike" sentence; it is the lesson's keeper line.
5. **The democratic man's day** (VIII L2505–2551): the renamed vices and the rotation of lives. `<Quote>` the rotation (drink and flute … water … gymnastics … idleness … philosophy … politics … war … business); then `<Task>`: name what is missing from this soul (anything that rules).
6. **The cup-bearers, the asses, and the wolf** (VIII L2608–2689, L2807–2872): the undiluted wine of freedom; authority dissolving step by step down to the animals; the protector who tastes kindred blood and becomes a wolf; the request for a bodyguard. Build as a `<FigureSequence>` (figure 4) and reconstruct the request in Plato's words.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), in chain order:** timocracy (Plato's coinage, "rule of honour" — one clause; also called the Cretan or Spartan type in the text, Ref `sparta-as-test-case`); oligarchy ("rule of the few" — in Plato, rule by a property qualification); the Muses (invoked for a mock-solemn speech); the nuptial number (a joke about a geometrical period — say so); drones (Plato's image for men without function; stingless = paupers, stinged = criminals); the "protector" / champion of the people (prostates); the wolf of Lycaean Zeus (an Arcadian cult legend: whoever tastes human entrails among the sacrifice becomes a wolf — one clause); the tyrant's bodyguard (anchor Peisistratus at Athens in one clause as the historical case Plato's audience would hear — optional); eros as the master passion (not "love" in the modern sense — the desire that wants everything); Lycaean, Persia (the empire Athens fought, 490 and 480), Cyrus, Darius, Xerxes (its kings — founder, rebuilder, invader), Marathon and Salamis (490 land, 480 sea), aidos (reverence/shame), theatrocracy (Plato's word: the audience as ruler); Scipio (Roman general who destroyed Carthage, 146 BC, the speaker in Cicero's dialogue — one clause, only if the Cicero hook is used; the full anchor is in `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`); ephors (five Spartan "overseers" elected yearly), gerousia/elders (recall Sparta from `greek-miracle` in one clause; `sparta-as-test-case` has the full account); mixed constitution (define at Laws III — the concept is introduced here).
- **Psychology, and then the test as a law.** Sections 1–8 teach the chain as Plato tells it, a psychology of regimes; the lesson must never say in its own voice that democracies "become" tyrannies. The last section judges the chain as a law and reaches the register's verdict: fails as a fixed sequence (strong); the demagogue mechanism holds on balance for archaic Greece (Politics V.5) and fails for Athens. Keep the two grades apart; do not let the psychology's survival soften the first, or the first's failure spill onto the psychology. Syracuse (Dionysius I, 405) is mentioned once, as the nearest classical counter-case, not hidden. Do not hint at any modern country; the Laws III passage is Persia and Athens.
- **Plato's praise of democracy is real.** Quote it. A lesson that makes Book VIII a sneer at democracy fails the full-strength rule; the embroidered robe and "supremely delightful" are Plato's words, and the lesson's dry seam is that the praise and the diagnosis sit in one paragraph.
- **Do not re-teach the three parts of the soul or the metals** — `<Ref>` `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king` with one clause at the nuptial-number step and at the democratic man. Do not re-teach the Funeral Oration or Mytilene — one clause each with `<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-justice`, and do not map Plato's "protector" onto Cleon or any named Athenian: Plato names nobody, and the historical tyrant his audience would hear is Peisistratus (optional clause).
- **Register.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Timocracy is not aristocracy.** The ideal city is "aristocracy or monarchy" (Book IV end); timocracy is its first decay. Aristotle will use "aristocracy" for something else (rule of the best, for the common good) — flag the word so `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` can reuse it without confusion.
- **The 729**: a numerological joke; one sentence, no arithmetic (playbook-history rule 4).
- **Thrasymachus is answered by a portrait.** Say so. The three proofs of Book IX are summarised, not carried through; the first (the ranking) and the slave-owner image do the lesson's work.
- **Where the dossier is thin:** nothing missing for Books VIII–IX. The Laws III text is complete. Cicero is complete. The Strauss–Cropsey chapter is the unread secondary.
- **Summary vs primary:** spot-checked L2608–2612 and L2688–2689 match. Shard-4 L numbers are +18769 for the full file; Book VIII's tyrannical man is in shard 5 (IX), not VIII.
- **Numbers:** dates to the decade for Marathon/Salamis (490, 480) only because Laws III's argument is about those battles; no durations of tyrannies (those are Aristotle's, `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`).
- **Nothing is a demo.**

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up, the one mechanical item).** Fill the template (figure 1) for oligarchy from memory: origin, defining good, defect, the man and his household. From `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king` (E3): which of the four virtues of the city is the first to go when the metals mix, and why does Plato say it is that one?
2. **Document reasoning (paired-source corroboration, E3 from `thucydides-power-and-justice`) on the embroidered-robe passage (L2269–2345) and Pericles' constitution paragraph (Funeral Oration, `thucydides-funeral-oration.txt` l. 74–92).** Metadata first for both: Plato, c. 380, two decades after the democracy executed Socrates, a philosopher writing for readers who knew it; Pericles as Thucydides reconstructed him, 431, a leader praising the city over its war dead. What each records (lot or election, free speech, private tolerance, leniency), what each argues (Pericles: freedom in private life coexists with obedience in public through fear of disgrace; Plato: the same freedom is the regime's defining good and its solvent), what each omits (Pericles: the empire's subjects, women — the reader saw both in the oration; Plato: Socrates's trial, never named here). Agreement, disagreement, and the judgment: what the two portraits together can be USED to establish about how Athenian democracy understood itself, and what the plague passage (II.53, "fear of gods or law of man there was none") adds as a third witness from a historian who praised the city's leader.
3. **Claim to refute.** "Plato predicts that every democracy becomes a tyranny." Name the step that fails (the chain runs through a type of soul bred by a type of upbringing, and Plato says the sequence "need not" hold — the cities "are as the men are"; Aristotle's cases show tyranny arising from oligarchy and from other tyrannies) and state what Plato's claim is instead.
4. **Boundary probe.** Set the drones to zero: a democracy with free speech, lot and lenient courts but no class of men without function. Does the mechanism of section 6 start? Which of the three classes is load-bearing for the rise of the protector, and what does that show about what Plato thinks democracy's danger actually is (not freedom as such but freedom plus a class with nothing to do)?
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped: the tyrannical man from the democratic man.** Using only the rule "the son is formed against the father's household" and the democratic man's trait "all pleasures equal", derive what the next son's soul must look like (one desire that will not share) and what the city then needs (the same thing in a man). Then check against Book IX's winged-drone passage. Solution carries the derivation and marks where Plato adds something the rule does not give (eros as the specific master passion).
6. **Paired-source corroboration.** Set Republic VIII's democracy-to-tyranny passage beside Laws III's Athens (reverence, then the theatre judging music, then contempt for law). Where do they agree (freedom's excess), where do they differ (what starts it: a class with nothing to do vs a loss of reverence; what ends it: a tyrant vs — nothing named), and what does the difference show about why the Laws recommends mixing rather than philosopher-kings?
7. **Counter-argument stem (synthesis).** "Someone claims Aristotle's objection refutes Book VIII." Name two facts the claim fails to account for (Plato says explicitly that he is telling the regimes in the order that shows the souls; Aristotle keeps the core thesis that the regime shapes the citizen — "education adapted to the constitution"), and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that Plato meant the order as history). The solution adds that the objection does refute the chain read as a law, which is the lesson's verdict; what it leaves is the psychology.
8. **Attack the verdict (new; before the FORWARD exercise).** The lesson judges that Plato's chain fails as a law (strong) and that the demagogue's road to tyranny holds on balance only for archaic Greece. Make the strongest case against the second half using Syracuse (Dionysius I, 405, V.5), and say what evidence would overturn the verdict. Solution: Syracuse fits the mechanism in the classical era; overturning needs a body of such cases against Aristotle's "few and slight" and Athens's record; the fixed-sequence half is untouched.
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` (core).** The lesson has named the mixed constitution and Megillus's wall: he cannot say which regime Sparta is. Take the objection at full strength: a regime in which no part can be named the ruler has nobody responsible for it, or else is really ruled by whichever part controls the decisive office. State what one would have to define first — what a regime IS, and who counts as ruling — to decide whether a mixture is a regime. The solution says in one sentence that Aristotle starts exactly there (a constitution is "the arrangement of offices, especially the supreme one") and names the mixture "polity", whose sign of success is that it "may be called either a democracy or an oligarchy", `<Ref>` `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`; it may add, in a clause, that the Laws' own second-best city is the optional `platos-laws-second-best-city`.

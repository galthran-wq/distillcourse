# Research brief: deneen-post-liberalism (Deneen's Why Liberalism Failed: the post-liberal critique)

status: planned

type: core
goal: Analyze Deneen's thesis that liberalism failed because it succeeded, and evaluate his answer to whether the West can survive on liberal principles alone

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The reader arrives from the end of the nation module, where `the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways` closed on "a decision made in a week by one government, enforcement that did not follow law, and a crisis ended by a treaty none of the theories contemplates", and with the Böckenförde dilemma from `murray-strange-death-of-europe` (concept `bockenforde-dilemma`) — does the free secular state live on presuppositions it cannot guarantee? — diagnosed there but not explained. From the module after Rawls, the two backward Refs course.yaml names: MacIntyre's verdict that modern moral language is the fragment of a lost tradition and his closing call for "another — doubtless very different — St. Benedict" (`macintyre-after-virtue`, concepts `enlightenment-project`, `practice-and-tradition`), and Lasch's mobile, credentialed elites who have seceded from the places they used to share (`lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`, concepts `revolt-of-the-elites`, `meritocracy-critique`). The whole genealogy Deneen uses, lesson by lesson: Machiavelli's `effectual-truth` (`machiavelli-effectual-truth`); Hobbes's `state-of-nature`, `authorization`, `liberty-as-silence-of-law` (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`, `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`); Filmer's objection that no one consents to being born into a polity (`filmer-patriarcha`); Locke's `consent`, tacit consent and the `labour-theory-of-property` (`locke-property-and-consent`); Hume's "no actual government rests on consent" (`hume-against-the-original-contract`); Burke's `prejudice` and `partnership-of-generations` (`burke-reflections`); Hegel's `ethical-life`; Tocqueville's `individualism-tocqueville` and `soft-despotism`; Mill's `harm-principle` and `individuality-mill`; Maine's "from status to contract" (`maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government`); the Federalist's `extended-republic` and `faction-madison`, now read in full in `federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`, whose objections section pointed forward to this lesson's charge of "political technology"; Jouvenel's Power allied with the common man against the intermediate bodies (`jouvenel-on-power`); Nisbet's war of the state on the `intermediate-associations` and Kirk's fusionist strain (`kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`); Burnham's managers (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`); Oakeshott's `conservatism-as-disposition` and `government-as-umpire`; Strauss's `historicism` and `modern-natural-right`; Scruton's `authority-before-freedom`; Rawls's `original-position` (`rawls-theory-of-justice`).
- **Contributes:** One move: that liberalism's pathologies are its fulfilment, not its betrayal. From two anthropological premises (`liberal-anthropology`) — the human being as a free, unencumbered individual whose bonds are legitimate only if chosen, and nature (including human nature) as material to be mastered — follow the state and market growing together as a pincer on everything between them; the dissolution of culture into `anticulture`; a new aristocracy of the mobile and credentialed (`liberalocracy`); and a citizenry that cannot govern itself. The lesson reconstructs the diagnosis at full strength through thinkers the reader has read, follows Deneen to his answer (no return, no new ideology, practice before theory — concept `post-liberalism`), and meets it from three sides: liberal (the achievements Deneen concedes; the right must accept liberal-democratic ground rules; "antonym substitution"), conservative (the truth in liberalism; a disposition needs no theory), and Strauss's warning about diagnoses from history.
- **Hands off:** Deneen answers "can the West survive on liberal principles alone?" with no, and proposes no political programme above the household and the town. That leaves open what form a post-liberal *polity* would take, and the module's next three lessons are the three answers: `hazony-virtue-of-nationalism` (the national state as the right unit — which Deneen's pincer would count as one arm of liberalism), `vermeule-common-good-constitutionalism` (law reread as reason ordered to the common good — a programme from the top that Deneen's "practice before theory" refuses), and `de-benoist-european-new-right` (culture before power, the right to difference). `hungarys-fundamental-law` reads Deneen's programme against a real constitution; `the-argument-assembled` places post-liberalism as the last link of the chain "what holds a people together" and leaves the Böckenförde question open.
- **Spine step:** "After liberalism: Deneen says it failed by succeeding, Hazony defends the order of national states, Vermeule the common good, de Benoist the right to difference — Hungary's constitution as the case — and the whole argument is assembled"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: liberalism promised freedom from arbitrary power and delivered the largest states and most pervasive markets in history; it promised self-government and delivered citizens who cannot name their representatives — and a Notre Dame professor says this is fulfilment, not betrayal. The newcomer meets it as a puzzle about inference: how can a theory's *success* be the cause of what looks like its *failure*? The lesson must show the mechanism, not assert the paradox.

1. **What did "liberty" mean before liberalism?** The obvious attempt: freedom has always meant doing as one likes within the law. Deneen: for the Greeks, Romans and Christians the reader has read, liberty was *self-rule* — of the soul over its appetites (`<Ref kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king>`: the three parts, one ruling) and of the city by its citizens — achieved by habituation, *paideia*, virtue; the man ruled by appetite was a slave though no one owned him (`<Ref decline-of-regimes>`: the democratic man). Liberalism redefined the word as the absence of external constraint and kept the old vocabulary. Because: a theory that renames the goal while keeping the name colonises the institutions built for the old goal (the palimpsest).
2. **Where did the new liberty come from?** Three revolutions, each a lesson the reader has done: Machiavelli grounded politics on "the low" — on how men are, not how they ought to be (`<Ref machiavelli-effectual-truth>`); Descartes and Hobbes stripped the human being of custom in thought experiments and handed the result to a central state to keep order (`<Ref hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant>`); Bacon turned knowledge into power over nature. None was a liberal. Because: liberalism is what results when Locke builds consent on Hobbes's individual and Bacon's nature — the two anthropological premises.
3. **The two premises.** (i) Anthropological individualism and voluntarism: the human being is by nature free and unencumbered, and every bond — political, familial, religious — is legitimate only if chosen. Hobbes's law as hedges that keep travellers "in the way"; liberty in the silence of the law (`<Ref hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty>`); Locke extending consent even to children's inheritance (`<Ref locke-property-and-consent>`: tacit consent, §§ 119–122). (ii) The separation from nature: nature — and later human nature — is material to be mastered, not a *telos* to be realised (`<Ref aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery>` for the premise it rejects). Because: from (i) every unchosen attachment is a constraint to be dissolved; from (ii) there is no natural limit on what may be re-made. Note Deneen's inversion of the course's left/right axis: first-wave liberals (whom American usage calls conservatives) master external nature; second-wave (Rousseau, Marx, Mill, Dewey) master human nature too.
4. **Why the state and the market grow together.** Attempt: the reader's whole experience of politics says the state and the market are opposites — more of one is less of the other. Deneen: both liberate the individual from the same thing — the intermediate bodies (family, parish, guild, town) that bound him without his consent — and each needs the other to do it. The state dissolves local norms by standardising law; the market dissolves them by making everything exchangeable; the individual who results is "independent and weak" (Tocqueville, `<Ref tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism>`) and turns to the state for what the parish once gave. "Laissez-faire was planned" (Polanyi via Deneen): enclosure, the abolition of guilds, free-trade treaties were state acts. Because: "statism enables individualism, individualism demands statism" — the pincer. Left and right are two liberalisms that won in different arenas (economic deregulation; sexual autonomy) and advanced the same dissolution — MacIntyre's charge that conservatism is liberalism's mirror image (`<Ref macintyre-after-virtue>`), made into a mechanism. The pincer was stated before Deneen from both halves: Jouvenel's Power allied with the common man against the aristocracies that limited it (`<Ref jouvenel-on-power>`), and Nisbet's state at war with the intermediate associations, leaving the uprooted to seek community in the state (`<Ref kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism>`). Deneen's addition is the market as the second jaw — which is why he rejects the fusionism of Kirk's movement, whose market half dissolved what its traditionalist half defended.
5. **Anticulture.** Attempt: liberalism is neutral among cultures and lets a thousand bloom. Deneen: culture stands on three pillars — nature (customs that fit human nature), time (a trust between generations), place (a community one did not choose) — and liberalism's two premises attack all three: nature is mastered; the social contract is timeless (Tocqueville: democracy "breaks the chain and frees each link"; the reader has `partnership-of-generations` as the counter-picture, `<Ref burke-reflections>`); the "state of nature" is placeless (men "like mushrooms"). What replaces culture is a monoculture of law and market — the liturgies of Black Friday and the Super Bowl — which Deneen calls *anticulture*; it is parasitic (the cowbird) because it lives in the nests other cultures built and cannot build its own. Because: a culture is a set of unchosen constraints, and liberalism's first premise makes every unchosen constraint illegitimate. Here the Böckenförde dilemma (`<Ref murray-strange-death-of-europe>`) receives its theory: the presuppositions the state cannot guarantee are the culture its own principles dissolve.
6. **A new aristocracy.** Attempt: whatever else, liberalism abolished rank. Deneen: it founded a new one. Locke's wager — accept inequality for rising prosperity (the American king fed worse than the English day-labourer, `<Ref locke-and-mill-on-empire>`); Mill's experiments in living serve those strong enough to run them, while Burke's prejudice serves the ordinary (`<Ref mill-liberty-and-its-limits>`, `<Ref burke-reflections>`); the result is a *liberalocracy* of the mobile and credentialed who preach autonomy and practise, for their own children, the stable family and the inherited advantage (Belmont and Fishtown — a sociologist's two composite towns, named in References) — a Noble Lie its tellers believe (`<Ref kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king>`). The reader has met this class twice already: Lasch's seceding elites (`<Ref lasch-revolt-of-the-elites>`) and the sorting the cognitive-elite data measured (`<Ref the-cognitive-elite>`). Deneen adds the cause: liberalism "begins as a version of the Rawlsian Original Position" (`<Ref rawls-theory-of-justice>`) — a wager accepted behind a veil by people who expected to be among the winners. Because: a regime of choice rewards those equipped to choose.
7. **Citizens who cannot govern.** Attempt: civic ignorance is a failure of education that civic education can cure. Deneen: it was designed in. The reader has the Federalist's design from its own lesson (`<Ref federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic>`: representation and the `extended-republic` as the cure for faction). Deneen's reading: the design wanted citizens too dispersed to combine — "communication is always checked by distrust" — and Hamilton foresaw local affection yielding to "a much better administration" at the centre (Fed. 17). The constitution is "applied technology of liberal theory": a machine that works without civic virtue, and therefore stops producing it. The counter-reading at full strength, from the same texts: the distrust clause is conditional — it checks combinations formed "where there is a consciousness of unjust or dishonorable purposes" — and Madison's Fed. 46 adds that the federal power can be "advantageously administered" only "within a certain sphere"; the design aims at unjust majorities, not at self-government. The citizen the design produces is the one Tocqueville's tutelary power governs (`<Ref tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism>`: the flock), and when he tires of it he calls for a strongman. Because: self-government is a habit (the township) and the design removed the places it was practised.
8. **After liberalism.** Attempt: then overthrow it, or go back. Deneen refuses both: liberalism's achievements — the end of arbitrary rule, the dignity of persons — are real and rooted in the classical and Christian ideals it drew down; there is no return; and a new ideology would repeat the error (Oakeshott's rationalism, `<Ref oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics>`). Three steps: acknowledge the achievements; outgrow ideology; practice before theory — households, local economies, polis life, communities as "lighthouses and field hospitals", and the "Benedict Option", a 2017 proposal named after the last sentence of `<Ref macintyre-after-virtue>` (the call for "another — doubtless very different — St. Benedict"). Because: culture is a deeper consent across generations than any contract, and it can only be rebuilt by living it.
9. **Three objections, each at full strength.** (a) *Liberal*: the achievements Deneen concedes are the whole case — rights, the rule of law, the end of arbitrary power — and his method is "antonym substitution": he contrasts liberal choice with *moral* choice, when liberals contrast it with the choice an arbitrary authority calls moral; liberal reluctance to enforce morality by law is not a denial of morality; the Benedict Option free-rides on the pluralism it despises; and a right that will not accept liberal-democratic ground rules becomes something else (the Weimar case, `<Ref article-48-weimar>`). (b) *Conservative*: the truth in liberalism (Scruton) — the political order of consent, compromise and rights-as-shields is a real good distinct from the religious order of submission, and the conservative keeps it — and Oakeshott: a conservative never needed liberalism to be a true *theory*; a disposition to prefer the familiar needs no doctrine about human nature, so a refutation of liberal anthropology leaves the disposition untouched. Deneen's counter (the course has it waiting): Oakeshott's umpire state is already the liberal state, and a disposition that keeps playing a game whose rules dissolve its culture conserves nothing. (c) *Strauss*: a diagnosis that says liberalism was the product of its moment and is now exhausted must say from where it speaks — if all political thought is of its time, so is Deneen's (`<Ref strauss-natural-right-and-history>`: "historicism thrives on the fact that it inconsistently exempts itself from its own verdict"). Deneen's reply is that he speaks from classical and Christian natural right, not from history; the open question is whether "practice before theory" can supply a standard at all, or only a habit. The lesson concedes to each objection exactly what Deneen concedes, and closes with the answers side by side: Deneen's no; Scruton's and Oakeshott's "not alone, but with"; the liberal's yes-if-the-rules-hold — and the question Deneen leaves, which the next lesson takes: if not the liberal state, which political unit? Deneen's localism stops below it; `<Ref hazony-virtue-of-nationalism>` answers with the nation.

Headings fall out as claims: "Liberty once meant self-rule"; "Three revolutions that were not liberal"; "Two premises: the unencumbered self and the mastered world"; "The state and the market are a pincer, not opposites"; "Anticulture: what dissolves when every bond must be chosen"; "A regime of choice breeds an aristocracy of choosers"; "Civic ignorance was in the design"; "No return, no new ideology"; "Three replies: the liberal, the conservative, the Straussian".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first mention** (L11): *post-liberal* (define: the position that liberalism's failures follow from its premises and cannot be fixed within them — concepts.yaml); *paideia* (Greek: formation, education of the whole person); *anticulture*, *liberalocracy*, *res idiotica* (Deneen's coinages — Latin *res publica* vs *idiotes*, the private person; define at first use, use sparingly); *voluntarism* (the doctrine that obligation arises only from will/choice); *telos* (end, purpose — the reader has it from Aristotle, Ref); *the Federalist* (set piece 8); *Belmont and Fishtown* (fictional composite towns in a 2012 sociological study of American class divergence — one clause, author in References); *Benedict Option* (a 2017 proposal that Christians withdraw into intentional communities, after St Benedict's monasteries — one clause, author in References); *Rumspringa* (Amish adolescence, when the young may leave before choosing baptism); *Polanyi* (a Hungarian-born economic historian, 1944 — References); *Solzhenitsyn's Harvard speech* (1978 commencement address on the West's legalism and loss of courage); *the Life of Julia* (set piece 6); *epistocracy* (rule by the knowledgeable — a 2016 proposal; author in References); *the cowbird*.
- **The post-liberal position at full strength.** The reader is predisposed to agree with Deneen; the temptation is to under-argue him and over-argue the replies, or the reverse. Each premise gets its mechanism; each reply gets its best case; the lesson decides nothing.
- **Deneen's genealogy is the course's own material; use Refs, not re-exposition.** Every claim about Hobbes, Locke, Mill, Tocqueville, Burke, Machiavelli, Plato is a `<Ref>` plus one clause (L0e). The lesson's new work is the *inference* Deneen draws from them, not the thinkers.
- **Contestable claims flagged as Deneen's** (summary `pedagogy`): the 2008 crisis as a lost local mortgage culture; campus sexual norms as Hobbes in microcosm; the humanities' self-destruction; his reading of the Federalist as intending the displacement of local loyalty; Mill as the strong man's theorist. State as his readings; give the counter-reading where the course already has it (Federalist 10 against majority tyranny; Mill's own limits in `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`).
- **The Federalist is now a lesson of its own** (`federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`) and in the dossier (`texts/federalist-full.txt`): Ref, do not re-teach; quote exactly from the text, not from Deneen. Deneen's "communication is always checked by distrust" drops the clause that limits it to combinations with "unjust or dishonorable purposes" — the lesson gives the full sentence and lets the reader weigh whether the omission changes the argument (Deneen can reply that a design which makes all large combination hard does not sort just from unjust ones). Cross-check his other quotations too (his notes carry small errors — "Considerations on Repressive Government"; the summary flags them).
- **MacIntyre and Lasch are not post-liberals of the right.** MacIntyre writes as a man of the left turned Thomist and rejects conservatism (`<Ref macintyre-after-virtue>`); Lasch is a historian of the left (`<Ref lasch-revolt-of-the-elites>`). Deneen draws on both; the lesson says so in one clause each, so the reader sees that the critique of liberalism the module opens with has sources on both sides.
- **Fawcett is one reading** (self-declared left-liberal); "antonym substitution" is Holmes's (1993) via Fawcett. Both in References; the body says "the liberal reply" and "the charge of antonym substitution".
- **Oakeshott vs Deneen is constructed by the course**, not in either text: Deneen does not discuss Oakeshott in this book (he is in the bibliography via ch. 3 notes), and Oakeshott died in 1990. State the confrontation as the course's, with the texts as its materials.
- **Strauss's warning is the course's application**, not Strauss on Deneen. Make the structure explicit: Deneen is not a historicist (he judges by a natural standard), so the warning bites on "practice before theory" rather than on the diagnosis — can practice supply a *standard*?
- **Dates**: the book (2018) was written before November 2016 (Preface); the strongman passage is a prediction, not a comment. Decade precision elsewhere.
- **Register.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. Deneen's diagnosis is reported as his argument ("Deneen argues"), neither endorsed nor diminished; the module's question is left to the reader with the three answers beside it.
- **Thin spots**: Deneen's constructive proposal (Conclusion) is two pages; do not inflate it — one section of ~250 words. His treatment of *immigration* is nil: the lesson must not pretend he addresses 2015; its link to the preceding module is the Böckenförde question and the "we" (`<Ref the-nation-and-its-defenders>`), stated once. His treatment of the *nation* is thin too (the nation-state appears as liberalism's state arm): do not supply Hazony's answer here — it is the next lesson.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (E3, earlier lessons)*: For each of Deneen's three revolutions, write the one sentence from the earlier lesson that supports his reading and the one that resists it (Machiavelli — `machiavelli-effectual-truth`: the effectual truth / but `machiavelli-discourses-republic`'s civic religion and republican liberty; Hobbes — `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`: the covenant / but the laws of nature bind in conscience; Bacon — not in the course: say so). Then name the earlier lesson that already stated each of Deneen's two jaws without the other (the state's war on intermediate bodies — `jouvenel-on-power`, `kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`; the market's cash-nexus — `carlyle-heroes`, `marx-manifesto`).
2. *Derivation the lesson did not do*: Deneen says the two anthropological premises jointly entail the pincer. Derive the pincer from premise (i) alone — show that voluntarism without the mastery of nature still dissolves unchosen bonds and still hands the residue to the state — and then say what premise (ii) adds (the dissolution of *nature* as a limit, hence the second wave). The solution shows that Deneen's "two waves" are the two premises taken one at a time.
3. *Boundary probe*: A society of perfectly chosen bonds. Run Deneen's thesis on a community in which every attachment — marriage, residence, faith, trade — was in fact freely chosen by each member, with no inheritance and no custom. Show what his theory predicts (anticulture: nothing to transmit, no trust across time) and then identify the one premise in the thought experiment that cannot hold for *children* — which is exactly the point Deneen presses with Locke's heirs and Rumspringa.
4. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill*: Hamilton's sentence in Federalist 17 that the states will keep the people's affection "unless the force of that principle should be destroyed by a much better administration" of the Union, beside Madison's Federalist 46 on the same point ("only within a certain sphere"). Metadata (New York, late 1787 and early 1788; pseudonymous newspaper essays arguing for ratification against the fear of central power). Predict what the sentences are doing in that argument; then read: do they record an intention (Deneen) or offer a reassurance (the ratification context)? Do the same for Fed. 10's "communication is always checked by distrust" with and without its conditional clause. What can the sentences be USED to establish about the design, and what would be needed to establish Deneen's stronger claim?
5. *Claim to refute*: "Deneen's thesis is self-refuting: if every political theory is the product of its age and fails when its age passes, so is his." Name the step that fails (Deneen does not hold that every theory is of its age — he judges liberalism by a standard he takes to be natural, the classical and Christian account of liberty as self-rule — so the Straussian warning bites on his *method of recovery*, not on his diagnosis) and say what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that "practice before theory" is a refusal to state any standard at all).
6. *Synthesis — counter-argument stem*: Someone claims that a conservative must choose between Oakeshott and Deneen: either conservatism is a disposition that needs no theory, or it is a post-liberal doctrine about human nature. Name two facts from the lesson the claim fails to account for (Scruton holds both — a disposition *and* a doctrine of the pre-political "we"; Deneen's own prescription is "practice before theory", i.e. a disposition), and state what would have to be true for the dilemma to be real.
7. *Retrieval and application (E3)*: Apply the pincer to a border. In Deneen's account, who decides who belongs — the local community, the national state, or the individual exercising a right to leave (Jefferson)? Write down which of the three Deneen's premises favour and which they dissolve; then recall who in fact decided Germany's intake in the week of 25–31 August 2015 (`<Ref the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways>`: "a decision made in a week by one government", the local "we" nowhere in it) and say whether the record fits the pincer, or is an exception it cannot classify.
8. **FORWARD (E7)** → `hazony-virtue-of-nationalism`: Deneen's two jaws are the state and the market; his remedy lives below both, in households and towns. Place the national state on his map: is it one of the intermediate bodies the pincer dissolves (a people with its own language, memory and loyalty), or the state jaw itself (standardising law, administration from the centre)? Give the best case for each answer from this lesson. The solution says Deneen's text mostly treats it as the second, and that the next lesson argues the first — that an order of independent national states is the bulwark against universal, imperial liberalism — so the reader arrives with the question that lesson answers.

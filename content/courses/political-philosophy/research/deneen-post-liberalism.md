# Research brief: deneen-post-liberalism (Deneen's Why Liberalism Failed: the post-liberal critique)

status: planned
module: nation-and-immigration
type: core
goal: Analyze Deneen's thesis that liberalism failed because it succeeded, and evaluate his answer to whether the West can survive on liberal principles alone

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The reader arrives with the Böckenförde dilemma from `murray-strange-death-of-europe` — does the free secular state live on presuppositions it cannot guarantee? — and Murray's second concatenation, a loss of faith diagnosed but not explained. They hold the whole genealogy Deneen will use, lesson by lesson: Machiavelli's `effectual-truth` (`machiavelli-effectual-truth`); Hobbes's `state-of-nature`, `authorization` and `liberty-as-silence-of-law` (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`, `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`); Locke's `consent`, tacit consent and the `labour-theory-of-property` (`locke-property-and-consent`); Mill's `harm-principle`, `individuality-mill` and "experiments in living" against the despotism of custom (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`); Tocqueville's `individualism-tocqueville` and `soft-despotism` (`tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`); Burke's `prejudice` (`burke-reflections`); Hegel's `ethical-life` (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`); Rousseau's `general-will`; Plato's `noble-lie` and the Cave; and from the postwar module, Oakeshott's `conservatism-as-disposition` and `rationalism-in-politics`, Strauss's `historicism` and `modern-natural-right`, Scruton's `authority-before-freedom`. They have read Federalist 10 only as cited in `kallipolis` or nowhere — anchor it.
- **Contributes:** One move: that liberalism's pathologies are its fulfilment, not its betrayal. From two anthropological premises — the human being as a free, unencumbered individual whose bonds are legitimate only if chosen, and nature (including human nature) as material to be mastered — follow the state and market growing together as a pincer on everything between them; the dissolution of culture into "anticulture"; a new aristocracy of the mobile and credentialed; and a citizenry that cannot govern itself. The lesson reconstructs the diagnosis at full strength and its genealogy through thinkers the reader has read, follows Deneen to his answer (no return, no new ideology, practice before theory), and then meets it from three sides — liberal (the achievements Deneen concedes; the right must accept liberal-democratic ground rules; "antonym substitution"), conservative (the truth in liberalism; a disposition needs no theory), and Strauss's warning that a historicist diagnosis saws off the branch it sits on.
- **Hands off:** Deneen answers the module's question — can the West survive on liberal principles alone? — with no, and proposes no political programme. The application lesson (`the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways`) takes the module's three membership theories to the record; Deneen's thesis returns there only as the frame for "a decision made in a week by one government". The synthesis (`the-argument-assembled`) places post-liberalism as the last link in the chain "what holds a people together" and leaves the Böckenförde question open.
- **Spine step:** "The live question: what the nation is, who may join it, and whether the West can survive on liberal principles alone — Scruton, Miller and Murray against Carens, Deneen against liberalism itself, and 2015 as the test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: liberalism promised freedom from arbitrary power and delivered the largest states and most pervasive markets in history; it promised self-government and delivered citizens who cannot name their representatives — and a Notre Dame professor says this is fulfilment, not betrayal. The newcomer meets it as a puzzle about inference: how can a theory's *success* be the cause of what looks like its *failure*? The lesson must show the mechanism, not assert the paradox.

1. **What did "liberty" mean before liberalism?** The obvious attempt: freedom has always meant doing as one likes within the law. Deneen: for the Greeks, Romans and Christians the reader has read, liberty was *self-rule* — of the soul over its appetites (`<Ref kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king>`: the three parts, one ruling) and of the city by its citizens — achieved by habituation, *paideia*, virtue; the man ruled by appetite was a slave though no one owned him (`<Ref decline-of-regimes>`: the democratic man). Liberalism redefined the word as the absence of external constraint and kept the old vocabulary. Because: a theory that renames the goal while keeping the name colonises the institutions built for the old goal (the palimpsest).
2. **Where did the new liberty come from?** Three revolutions, each a lesson the reader has done: Machiavelli grounded politics on "the low" — on how men are, not how they ought to be (`<Ref machiavelli-effectual-truth>`); Descartes and Hobbes stripped the human being of custom in thought experiments and handed the result to a central state to keep order (`<Ref hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant>`); Bacon turned knowledge into power over nature. None was a liberal. Because: liberalism is what results when Locke builds consent on Hobbes's individual and Bacon's nature — the two anthropological premises.
3. **The two premises.** (i) Anthropological individualism and voluntarism: the human being is by nature free and unencumbered, and every bond — political, familial, religious — is legitimate only if chosen. Hobbes's law as hedges that keep travellers "in the way"; liberty in the silence of the law (`<Ref hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty>`); Locke extending consent even to children's inheritance (`<Ref locke-property-and-consent>`: tacit consent, §§ 119–122). (ii) The separation from nature: nature — and later human nature — is material to be mastered, not a *telos* to be realised (`<Ref aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery>` for the premise it rejects). Because: from (i) every unchosen attachment is a constraint to be dissolved; from (ii) there is no natural limit on what may be re-made. Note Deneen's inversion of the course's left/right axis: first-wave liberals (today's "conservatives") master external nature; second-wave (Rousseau, Marx, Mill, Dewey) master human nature too.
4. **Why the state and the market grow together.** Attempt: the reader's whole experience of politics says the state and the market are opposites — more of one is less of the other. Deneen: both liberate the individual from the same thing — the intermediate bodies (family, parish, guild, town) that bound him without his consent — and each needs the other to do it. The state dissolves local norms by standardising law; the market dissolves them by making everything exchangeable; the individual who results is "independent and weak" (Tocqueville, `<Ref tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism>`) and turns to the state for what the parish once gave. "Laissez-faire was planned" (Polanyi via Deneen): enclosure, the abolition of guilds, free-trade treaties were state acts. Because: "statism enables individualism, individualism demands statism" — the pincer. Left and right are two liberalisms that won in different arenas (economic deregulation; sexual autonomy) and advanced the same dissolution.
5. **Anticulture.** Attempt: liberalism is neutral among cultures and lets a thousand bloom. Deneen: culture stands on three pillars — nature (customs that fit human nature), time (a trust between generations), place (a community one did not choose) — and liberalism's two premises attack all three: nature is mastered; the social contract is timeless (Tocqueville: democracy "breaks the chain and frees each link"; the reader has `partnership-of-generations` as the counter-picture, `<Ref burke-reflections>`); the "state of nature" is placeless (men "like mushrooms"). What replaces culture is a monoculture of law and market — the liturgies of Black Friday and the Super Bowl — which Deneen calls *anticulture*; it is parasitic (the cowbird) because it lives in the nests other cultures built and cannot build its own. Because: a culture is a set of unchosen constraints, and liberalism's first premise makes every unchosen constraint illegitimate. Here the Böckenförde dilemma (`<Ref murray-strange-death-of-europe>`) receives its theory: the presuppositions the state cannot guarantee are the culture its own principles dissolve.
6. **A new aristocracy.** Attempt: whatever else, liberalism abolished rank. Deneen: it founded a new one. Locke's wager — accept inequality for rising prosperity (the American king fed worse than the English day-labourer, `<Ref locke-and-mill-on-empire>`); Mill's experiments in living serve those strong enough to run them, while Burke's prejudice serves the ordinary (`<Ref mill-liberty-and-its-limits>`, `<Ref burke-reflections>`); the result is a *liberalocracy* of the mobile and credentialed who preach autonomy and practise, for their own children, the stable family and the inherited advantage (Belmont and Fishtown — a sociologist's two fictional towns, named in References) — a Noble Lie its tellers believe (`<Ref kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king>`). Because: a regime of choice rewards those equipped to choose.
7. **Citizens who cannot govern.** Attempt: civic ignorance is a failure of education that civic education can cure. Deneen: it was designed in. Federalist 10 (anchor it) wanted a republic, not a democracy — representation and an "extended sphere" in which "communication is always checked by distrust" — and Hamilton foresaw local affection yielding to "a much better administration" at the centre. The citizen the design produces is the one Tocqueville's tutelary power governs (`<Ref tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism>`: the flock), and when he tires of it he calls for a strongman. Because: self-government is a habit (the township) and the design removed the places it was practised.
8. **After liberalism.** Attempt: then overthrow it, or go back. Deneen refuses both: liberalism's achievements — the end of arbitrary rule, the dignity of persons — are real and rooted in the classical and Christian ideals it drew down; there is no return; and a new ideology would repeat the error (Oakeshott's rationalism, `<Ref oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics>`). Three steps: acknowledge the achievements; outgrow ideology; practice before theory — households, local economies, polis life, communities as "lighthouses and field hospitals". Because: culture is a deeper consent across generations than any contract, and it can only be rebuilt by living it.
9. **Three objections, each at full strength.** (a) *Liberal*: the achievements Deneen concedes are the whole case — rights, the rule of law, the end of arbitrary power — and his method is "antonym substitution": he contrasts liberal choice with *moral* choice, when liberals contrast it with the choice an arbitrary authority calls moral; liberal reluctance to enforce morality by law is not a denial of morality; the Benedict Option free-rides on the pluralism it despises; and a right that will not accept liberal-democratic ground rules becomes something else (the Weimar case, `<Ref article-48-weimar>`). (b) *Conservative*: the truth in liberalism (Scruton) — the political order of consent, compromise and rights-as-shields is a real good distinct from the religious order of submission, and the conservative keeps it — and Oakeshott: a conservative never needed liberalism to be a true *theory*; a disposition to prefer the familiar needs no doctrine about human nature, so a refutation of liberal anthropology leaves the disposition untouched. Deneen's counter (the course has it waiting): Oakeshott's umpire state is already the liberal state, and a disposition that keeps playing a game whose rules dissolve its culture conserves nothing. (c) *Strauss*: a diagnosis that says liberalism was the product of its moment and is now exhausted must say from where it speaks — if all political thought is of its time, so is Deneen's (`<Ref strauss-natural-right-and-history>`: "historicism thrives on the fact that it inconsistently exempts itself from its own verdict"). Deneen's reply is that he speaks from classical and Christian natural right, not from history; the open question is whether "practice before theory" can supply a standard at all, or only a habit. The lesson concedes to each objection exactly what Deneen concedes, and closes on the module's question with the answers side by side: Deneen's no; Scruton's and Oakeshott's "not alone, but with"; the liberal's yes-if-the-rules-hold.

Headings fall out as claims: "Liberty once meant self-rule"; "Three revolutions that were not liberal"; "Two premises: the unencumbered self and the mastered world"; "The state and the market are a pincer, not opposites"; "Anticulture: what dissolves when every bond must be chosen"; "A regime of choice breeds an aristocracy of choosers"; "Civic ignorance was in the design"; "No return, no new ideology"; "Three replies: the liberal, the conservative, the Straussian".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **"Failed because it succeeded"** (Intro, l. 183). 2. **The operating system and the cave with movie-set walls** (Intro): two images for invisibility — an ideology noticed only when it crashes; a cave whose painted walls promise endless vistas. One paragraph; then the Ref to Plato's Cave (`<Ref kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king>`) and Deneen's twist in ch. 6 — a Cave "rendered invisible by artificial lighting".
3. **The three revolutions as three lessons** (ch. 1, l. 276). A table the reader can fill from memory: Machiavelli (the low), Descartes/Hobbes (custom stripped, the state), Bacon (nature mastered) — each with the earlier lesson's slug and the one sentence the reader already knows. The Princeton colleague who, hearing that Amish youth who return choose baptism at rates above 80 per cent, says "We will have to consider ways of freeing them" — the consent double standard in one anecdote: tacit consent to liberalism counts; chosen return to a tradition does not. Then Deneen's inference: culture is itself a deeper consent across generations.

Then Scruton's Antigone (HtbC ch. 6): "Vengeance is mine, saith the Lord; but justice, says the city, is mine" — the truth in liberalism as the city's own justice.
11. **Antonym substitution** (Fawcett l. 13912–13922, stated as the course's own): three liberal contrasts restored — not choice vs the moral choice but choice vs what an arbitrary authority calls moral; not liberty vs authority but liberty vs *unchecked* authority; not self vs community but self vs *unchosen, subordinate* membership. A three-row table; the reader tests Deneen's chapters against it.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first mention** (L11): *post-liberal* (define: the position that liberalism's failures follow from its premises and cannot be fixed within them — concepts.yaml); *paideia* (Greek: formation, education of the whole person); *anticulture*, *liberalocracy*, *res idiotica* (Deneen's coinages — Latin *res publica* vs *idiotes*, the private person; define at first use, use sparingly); *voluntarism* (the doctrine that obligation arises only from will/choice); *telos* (end, purpose — the reader has it from Aristotle, Ref); *the Federalist* (set piece 8); *Belmont and Fishtown* (fictional composite towns in a 2012 sociological study of American class divergence — one clause, author in References); *Benedict Option* (a 2017 proposal that Christians withdraw into intentional communities, after St Benedict's monasteries — one clause, author in References); *Rumspringa* (Amish adolescence, when the young may leave before choosing baptism); *Polanyi* (a Hungarian-born economic historian, 1944 — References); *Solzhenitsyn's Harvard speech* (1978 commencement address on the West's legalism and loss of courage); *the Life of Julia* (set piece 6); *epistocracy* (rule by the knowledgeable — a 2016 proposal; author in References); *the cowbird*.
- **The post-liberal position at full strength.** The reader is predisposed to agree with Deneen; the temptation is to under-argue him and over-argue the replies, or the reverse. Each premise gets its mechanism; each reply gets its best case; the lesson decides nothing.
- **Deneen's genealogy is the course's own material; use Refs, not re-exposition.** Every claim about Hobbes, Locke, Mill, Tocqueville, Burke, Machiavelli, Plato is a `<Ref>` plus one clause (L0e). The lesson's new work is the *inference* Deneen draws from them, not the thinkers.
- **Contestable claims flagged as Deneen's** (summary `pedagogy`): the 2008 crisis as a lost local mortgage culture; campus sexual norms as Hobbes in microcosm; the humanities' self-destruction; his reading of the Federalist as intending the displacement of local loyalty; Mill as the strong man's theorist. - **The Federalist is not in this course's dossier.** Avalon's text is public domain; quote exactly; anchor the essays; and cross-check Deneen's quotations (his notes carry small errors — "Considerations on Repressive Government"; the summary flags them).
- **Fawcett is one reading** (self-declared left-liberal); "antonym substitution" is Holmes's (1993) via Fawcett. Both in References; the body says "the liberal reply" and "the charge of antonym substitution".
- **Oakeshott vs Deneen is constructed by the course**, not in either text: Deneen does not discuss Oakeshott in this book (he is in the bibliography via ch. 3 notes), and Oakeshott died in 1990. State the confrontation as the course's, with the texts as its materials.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (E3, earlier lessons)*: For each of Deneen's three revolutions, write the one sentence from the earlier lesson that supports his reading and the one that resists it (Machiavelli — `machiavelli-effectual-truth`: the effectual truth / but the Discourses' republican liberty; Hobbes — `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`: the covenant / but the laws of nature bind in conscience; Bacon — not in the course: say so).
2. *Derivation the lesson did not do*: Deneen says the two anthropological premises jointly entail the pincer. Derive the pincer from premise (i) alone — show that voluntarism without the mastery of nature still dissolves unchosen bonds and still hands the residue to the state — and then say what premise (ii) adds (the dissolution of *nature* as a limit, hence the second wave). The solution shows that Deneen's "two waves" are the two premises taken one at a time.
3. *Boundary probe*: A society of perfectly chosen bonds. Run Deneen's thesis on a community in which every attachment — marriage, residence, faith, trade — was in fact freely chosen by each member, with no inheritance and no custom. Show what his theory predicts (anticulture: nothing to transmit, no trust across time) and then identify the one premise in the thought experiment that cannot hold for *children* — which is exactly the point Deneen presses with Locke's heirs and Rumspringa.
4. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill*: Hamilton's sentence in Federalist 17 that the states will keep the people's affection "unless… destroyed by a much better administration" at the centre. Metadata (New York, December 1787; a pseudonymous newspaper essay arguing for ratification against the fear of central power). Predict what the sentence is doing in that argument; then read: does it record an intention (Deneen) or offer a reassurance (the ratification context)? What can the sentence be USED to establish about the Founders' design, and what would be needed to establish Deneen's stronger claim?
5. *Claim to refute*: "Deneen's thesis is self-refuting: if every political theory is the product of its age and fails when its age passes, so is his." Name the step that fails (Deneen does not hold that every theory is of its age — he judges liberalism by a standard he takes to be natural, the classical and Christian account of liberty as self-rule — so the Straussian warning bites on his *method of recovery*, not on his diagnosis) and say what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that "practice before theory" is a refusal to state any standard at all).
6. *Synthesis — counter-argument stem*: Someone claims that a conservative must choose between Oakeshott and Deneen: either conservatism is a disposition that needs no theory, or it is a post-liberal doctrine about human nature. Name two facts from the lesson the claim fails to account for (Scruton holds both — a disposition *and* a doctrine of the pre-political "we"; Deneen's own prescription is "practice before theory", i.e. a disposition), and state what would have to be true for the dilemma to be real.
7. **FORWARD (E7)** → `the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways`: Apply the pincer to a border. In Deneen's account, who decides who belongs — the local community, the national state, or the individual exercising a right to leave (Jefferson)? Write down which of the three Deneen's premises favour and which they dissolve, and then ask who in fact decided Germany's membership in the week of 25–31 August 2015. The solution says the reader has reached the application lesson's last section — "a decision made in a week by one government", with the local "we" nowhere in it — and that the lesson will ask whether that is the pincer at work or an exception none of the module's theories contemplates.

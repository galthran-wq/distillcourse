# Research brief: donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship (Donoso Cortés: dictatorship against the discussing class)

status: planned
module: contract-revolution-reaction
type: core
goal: Analyze Donoso Cortés's 1849 argument that when liberal institutions fail, the choice is between the dictatorship of the insurrection and the dictatorship of the government, and his theological diagnosis of liberalism as a discussing class

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `maistre-and-bonald` (the prerequisite): `providence-in-history` (the
  Revolution uses men; it is a punishment), `counter-revolution` ("the contrary of revolution"),
  the executioner as the hidden bond of society, God as the author of sovereignty and of
  punishment, Bonald's parallel between the structure of theology and the structure of the state
  (marriage and sovereignty as bonds a third party makes indissoluble), and the forward paragraph
  on the *decision* — Maistre's "any government is good once it is established" and his wish that
  a question be decided "without delay and without appeal". From `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`
  (the previous lesson): the state as the reconciliation of opposites, the monarch who "dots the
  i", public opinion as deserving respect and contempt, and §5's "fanaticism of destruction" — the
  synthesis Donoso will refuse. From `locke-property-and-consent`: *prerogative*, "the power of
  doing public good without a rule" and sometimes against it (Sects 159–166), and "who shall be
  judge?" From `hume-against-the-original-contract`: "Salus populi suprema lex" and resistance in
  extremity (Of Passive Obedience); the "total dissolution of government" after which "every wise
  man" wishes for a general (OC 14). From `bodin-sovereignty` and `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`:
  `sovereignty` as the power bound by no law of its own making. From
  `machiavelli-discourses-republic`: the Roman dictatorship (Discourses I.34) — conferred, limited
  to six months, unable to change the laws. From `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: Rousseau's
  own chapter on dictatorship is NOT in the reader's lesson; do not assume it.
- **Contributes:** One move, made twice. In the 1849 speech Donoso argues that every legal order
  contains, openly or hidden, a power to suspend itself, because a rule cannot provide for the
  case it was not written for; that the size of the resisting force must match the concentration
  of the attacking one; and that in the Europe of 1848, where the choice between liberty and
  dictatorship no longer exists, the only real choice is between a dictatorship from below and one
  from above — "the dagger" or "the sabre" (`dictatorship-of-the-sabre`). In the Essay of 1851 he
  explains why liberals cannot see this: every political question is a theological one, and
  liberalism, which keeps a God who does not act and a king who does not rule, answers every
  ultimate question by proposing to discuss it — the *discussing class* (`discussing-class`) that
  can rule only in the interval before the people choose between Christ and Barabbas. The lesson's
  centre is the step from "legality cannot decide its own exception" to "someone must decide, and
  the choice is only who".
- **Hands off:** (1) The module's application, `assignats` (next lesson): Burke forecast a general,
  Maistre a Providence no assembly can direct, Donoso a choice between two dictatorships; the
  record of 1790–1804 — the Terror, the Directory's coups, Brumaire, a code made under one ruler —
  is read against all three. (2) The later use of the argument, named here and argued in
  `schmitt-political-theology` (Donoso as the hinge "from legitimacy to dictatorship"; the
  "discussing class"; decision against "everlasting conversation") and tested in
  `article-48-weimar` (an emergency power written into a constitution, used until the constitution
  was gone). (3) The case for discussion Donoso attacks gets its classic defence ten years later —
  "all silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility" (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`,
  the FORWARD exercise). (4) Tocqueville's soft despotism (`tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`)
  is the liberal's version of Donoso's "million eyes and ears" — one clause, forward.
- **Spine step:** "Government by consent (Locke) is doubted by Hume and radicalised by Rousseau's
  general will; when France tries to frame a government from principles, Burke, Maistre and
  Donoso Cortés answer that no generation knows enough, Herder and Fichte put the people before
  the state, and Hegel absorbs both sides into the rational state"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): In January 1849, a year after revolutions broke out in Paris,
Vienna, Berlin and Rome, a Spanish deputy rises in the Cortes to defend the government's
emergency powers. His opponents say that legality is enough to save society. He answers that
when legality suffices, it should be kept, and when it does not, a dictatorship is needed; and
that the only real choice in Europe is now between a dictatorship from below and one from above.
The lesson follows the speech and the book in which he tried to show why liberalism could not see
this.

Anchors (L11) at the opening, no more than three per section: *1848* (February: the Paris crowd
overthrows Louis-Philippe and a republic is proclaimed; March: risings in Vienna and Berlin;
June: the Paris workers' rising crushed in four days of street fighting; November: Pope Pius IX
flees Rome, where a republic will be proclaimed in February 1849 — one sentence, dates to the
month only because the speech's argument is about simultaneity); *the Cortes* (Spain's
parliament; the Congress of Deputies is its lower house); *Donoso Cortés* (a former liberal
lawyer, by 1849 a Catholic deputy of the governing Moderate party) and *the Progressives* (the
liberal opposition; Cortina their leader).

1. **Is legality enough?** The Progressives' principle as Donoso states it: "legality, everything
   for legality, everything by legality, legality always". His inversion: "laws were made for
   society, and not society for laws"; "When legality is enough to save society, legality; when
   it is not enough, dictatorship." Obvious attempt for the liberal: a well-made constitution
   provides for its own emergencies. Donoso's reply turns the attempt into his evidence: every
   order has done exactly that, openly or under another name — Athens had ostracism (anchor: the
   assembly's vote to exile a citizen for ten years without trial), Rome the dictator appointed by
   the Senate's consul (`<Ref>` machiavelli-discourses-republic for I.34), the restored French
   monarchy hid it in article 14 of its Charter (anchor: the king's power to issue "the ordinances
   necessary for the security of the state", which Charles X used in July 1830 and lost his
   throne), and the English Parliament holds it permanently — it can suspend habeas corpus, change
   the dynasty and the religion, everything "except turning a woman into a man". Then the reader's
   own tradition (`<Ref>` locke-property-and-consent): Locke's prerogative is "the power of doing
   public good without a rule", "sometimes even against" the law; Hume's "Salus populi suprema
   lex" (`<Ref>` hume-against-the-original-contract). So the question is not *whether* an order
   contains a power to suspend its rules, but *when* it is used and *who* uses it. Heading as
   claim: *Every legal order hides a dictatorship*.
2. **Why must the response be concentrated?** The theory (l.17–18), built step by step. Society,
   like a body, lives by "invading forces" (disease, subversion) and "resisting forces" (health,
   government, courts). Invading forces have two states: diffuse (crime and vice spread through
   individuals) and concentrated (organised in "political associations" — the clubs and secret
   societies of 1848; anchor in a clause). Resisting forces exist only to repel invading ones and
   "must necessarily be proportioned to their state": diffuse resistance (ordinary courts,
   ordinary law) answers diffuse attack; concentrated attack calls for resistance "concentrated
   into a single hand". The reader should see the argument's form: it is an argument from
   proportion, not from right — and its hidden premise is that a deliberating body cannot act with
   the speed or unity the concentrated attack has. Then the analogy Donoso draws from theology
   (l.21–24): God governs "constitutionally", through the regular laws of nature, and acts
   "dictatorially" when he works a miracle; a party that renounces dictatorship "believes it can
   govern with less means than God". Flag this sentence: a jurist of the next century will build
   his theory of sovereignty on exactly this analogy between the exception and the miracle
   (`<Ref>` schmitt-political-theology, forward, one clause).
3. **Where do revolutions come from?** The speech's diagnosis of 1848, because the dictatorship is
   justified by it. The Progressives' theory: revolutions come from poverty and tyranny ("feed the
   hungry"). Donoso's counter-thesis: "Revolutions are diseases of rich peoples; revolutions are
   diseases of free peoples" — slaves and the starving never made one; revolutions are made by
   those near the top, from "overexcited desires" fed by politicians, on a single formula repeated
   at every level: "And you shall be like the rich … like the nobles … like kings … like Gods" —
   "From Adam, the first rebel, to Proudhon, the last impious". Give the objection where the
   speech itself records it: a deputy shouted "Spartacus!" (l.30) — the slave war of 73–71 BC;
   Donoso's answer, that slaves made "a few civil wars" but no "profound revolutions". Add the
   record against him at full strength, as the course's own (stated, sourced in References): 1848
   followed two years of crop failure and dear bread across Europe (1846–47); so the hunger his
   opponents cited was real. And what survives of his thesis: the leaders of 1848 were lawyers,
   journalists and professors, not the hungry. The providential frame comes from Maistre
   (`<Ref>` maistre-and-bonald — the Revolution as an instrument): "the Republic did not win, the
   Republic was the instrument of victory of a higher power"; four monarchies fell — divine right
   with Louis XVI, glory with Napoleon, heredity with Charles X, prudence with Louis-Philippe —
   and "the last of all possible monarchies" has gone. Seam: if no kind of monarchy can stand,
   legitimacy in Maistre's sense is gone; what remains is the decision. This is where the lesson
   should say that Donoso goes past Maistre: Maistre wanted the king back; Donoso, seeing no kings
   left, wants a dictator.
4. **What is the long trend?** The thermometer law (l.57–66), the speech's set piece. "There are
   but two possible repressions, one internal and the other external; the religious and the
   political"; when one rises the other falls. Step through it as a `<FigureSequence>`: the
   ancient world (no inner restraint, so tyranny and slaves); Christ and his disciples ("the only
   society that ever existed without government"); the apostolic churches with arbitrators, "the
   embryo of government"; the feudal monarchy, "the weakest of all"; the Reformation and absolute
   monarchy with standing armies ("a soldier is a slave in uniform" — a million arms); the police
   (a million eyes); administrative centralisation (a million ears); the telegraph (the privilege
   of being everywhere at once). Then the fork (l.67–72): a religious reaction would bring the
   political thermometer down "to the temperate day of the freedom of the peoples"; without one,
   "a gigantic, colossal, all-encompassing, immense tyrant", for railways and steamships have
   abolished frontiers, the telegraph distances, and "all patriotisms are dead". He thinks the
   reaction possible and improbable. Mark the seam (L10b): from a defender of dictatorship one
   expects praise of the strong state; Donoso describes the strong state as the symptom of a
   disease and dictatorship as a remedy chosen because nothing better is left. Name the
   testability: the law predicts that political control rises as belief falls; exercise 5 asks
   what evidence would test it. One forward clause: a French liberal had described the
   administrative state's "immense and tutelary power" a decade earlier, from the other side
   (`<Ref>` tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism).
5. **Then what is the choice?** The conclusion (l.80–84), quoted whole in the Spanish-checked
   wording. "If it were a matter of choice … between liberty on the one hand and dictatorship on
   the other, there would be no dissent here" — but "Freedom does not in fact exist in Europe";
   constitutional governments are a shell, as Imperial Rome kept consuls, tribunes and Senate:
   "there is one man too many and a Republic is left out"; "legitimate majorities, always defeated
   by turbulent minorities", "responsible ministers who answer for nothing", "inviolable kings who
   are always violated". So the choice is "between the dictatorship of insurrection and the
   dictatorship of the Government"; "from below … from above"; "of the dagger and … of the sabre;
   I choose the dictatorship of the sabre, because it is nobler". Define
   `dictatorship-of-the-sabre` here, and make its structure explicit as a dilemma: if liberty is
   available, choose liberty; liberty is not available; so the only choice is between two
   dictatorships, and the one from above is "less burdensome and less disgraceful". The reader
   should see that everything rests on the second premise. And Donoso's personal disclaimer: "Two
   things are impossible for me: to condemn dictatorship and to exercise it."
6. **Why can legality not decide the case it was not written for?** The analytic core of the goal,
   put in the course's own plain terms. A rule is general and is written in advance; an emergency
   is particular and new; so the rule cannot itself say when the emergency has come, what it
   requires, or when it has passed — someone must judge those three things, and that judgment is
   not an application of the rule. Locke's answer (`<Ref>`): the executive judges, the people
   judge the executive, and where there is no earthly judge, the appeal to heaven — that is, the
   question is left to force and conscience. Hume's answer (`<Ref>`): salus populi, but do not
   preach the exceptions. Donoso's: whoever holds the concentrated resisting force judges, and in
   1849 that is the government. State what is new in Donoso: the liberal tradition treats the
   emergency as an exception inside an order that remains normal; Donoso treats the age itself as
   the emergency (liberty "finished") — so the exception has become the rule. This is the step
   the objection in link 9 attacks.
7. **Why could liberalism not see this? Every political question is theological.** The Essay
   (1851). Book I ch. I: "a great question of theology is always involved in every great political
   question" — quoted with Proudhon's line that provoked it ("It is wonderful how we ever stumble
   on theology in all our political questions"). The set piece: Pilate and Caiaphas (l.1110–1126) —
   Pilate the layman "could not comprehend what the state had to do with religion"; Caiaphas held
   that every new god dethrones Caesar; "The mob instinctively thought with Caiphas." Then the
   classification that diagnoses liberalism (Bk II ch. VIII): who holds sovereignty, in the
   language of the schools — the God who both constitutes and governs (Catholic), the God who
   constituted the world and does not govern it (deist), no God (atheist). Liberalism is the
   deist position in politics: a God who made the world and leaves it to its laws, and on earth
   the sovereignty of reason, "incarnate … in the middle classes" and "the philosophers" — a God
   who cannot act, and (the 1849 speech's phrase) a king who is "inviolable" and "always
   violated". Bonald drew this parallel between theology and the theory of the state first
   (`<Ref>` maistre-and-bonald, one clause).
8. **The discussing class.** Bk I ch. III and Bk II ch. VIII. First the argument against
   discussion as a method of finding truth (l.1720–1790), drawn as a dilemma tree: free discussion
   rests on one true supposition (governments are fallible) and one false one (discussion is
   infallible); infallibility cannot come out of discussion unless it is in those who discuss;
   if human nature is sound, everyone already has the truth and discussion is pointless; if it is
   fallen, everyone is uncertain and discussion cannot reach certainty; so discussion is absurd
   either way — unless there is an authority exempt from the fall (the Church). Then the political
   consequence (l.6608–6640, quoted): liberalism "never says, I affirm, or, I deny, and … ever
   says, I distinguish"; it "confounds by means of discussion all notions"; it rules only "when the
   world does not know whether to go with Barrabas or with Jesus", and "perpetual discussion, being
   as it is, the enemy of action, contradicts human nature", so a day comes when the people "flow
   through the squares and the streets resolutely calling for Barrabas or Jesus, and levelling
   with the dust the chairs of the sophists". Define `discussing-class` here: Donoso's Spanish
   name for the liberal bourgeoisie, "clase discutidora", a class that answers every ultimate
   question by proposing to discuss it. Then the socialist's mockery of the liberal, which Donoso
   writes in the socialist's voice and endorses as logic: "The sovereign is like God — he is one,
   or he does not exist"; "Discussion is the title under which Death travels … incognito".
9. **Socialism as the consistent heresy.** Why Donoso respects the socialists and despises the
   liberals (l.6640–6650; Bk II ch. X; Bk III ch. III–IV): socialism "is strong, only because it is
   a theology; and it is destructive, only because it is a satanic theology"; it "always propose[s]
   a peremptory and decisive solution"; it draws the consequences liberalism's premises contain —
   if men are innocent and evil is in institutions, then monarchy, nobility, property, family and
   nation must go (the chain, Bk III ch. III); Proudhon wants fraternity "provided that I be the
   eldest brother", calls himself an anarchist and hungers for government — every road from the
   denial of sin ends in "an omnipotent government". The Slav forecast: see Sources (not in the
   dossier) — include only if verified from the 1850 speech, as one dated paragraph.
10. **The OBJECTION at full strength — dictatorship as a cure that becomes the disease**, from
    liberal and from Catholic ground, then the reply. (a) By Donoso's own thermometer, the
    instruments of dictatorship — the army, the police, centralisation — are the rising political
    repression he calls the disease; the cure he prescribes is the symptom he diagnosed. (b) Who
    judges that "legality is not enough"? The government that will hold the dictatorship —
    Locke's objection to the absolute sovereign, judge "in his own case" (`<Ref>`), applied to
    the emergency; Hume's warning against preaching the exceptions cuts the same way. (c) The
    Roman dictator was named for six months and could not change the laws (`<Ref>`
    machiavelli-discourses-republic); a dictatorship justified by an age in which "liberty is
    finished" has no term, so it is no longer an exception but a new order. (d) From his own side:
    a French priest objected in the early 1850s that Donoso had made human nature more depraved than the
    Church's dogma allows (Trent teaches a wounded nature, not a worthless one) — so his
    anthropology is a political choice, not theology. (e) The forecast: Spain and Europe in the
    1850s did not fall to a "colossal" tyrant; the twentieth century is another matter, and the
    course does not settle it here. **Reply, from the text:** Donoso never claims dictatorship is
    good in itself; his argument is a dilemma, and to refuse both dictatorships is, on his premises,
    to choose the dagger by default — the Progressives' "legality" did not prevent the risings of
    1848, the government did; and his dictatorship is the existing, legal government's, "from
    above", not an adventurer's. **Where the lesson concedes:** the argument stands or falls on
    the premise that liberty is no longer available — an empirical claim about 1849 that Donoso
    states and does not prove — and on the question who judges the emergency, which he answers by
    pointing at whoever holds force. The later use (one paragraph, forward): a German jurist will
    read the speech as the moment counter-revolutionary thought passed "from legitimacy to
    dictatorship" and will call the liberal answer to "Christ or Barabbas?" a motion to adjourn
    or to appoint a commission of investigation (`<Ref>` schmitt-political-theology); the
    Weimar Republic's emergency article is the case that tests the argument (`<Ref>`
    article-48-weimar). The course states these as forward questions, not verdicts.
11. **Close.** The reader can now reconstruct the speech's three proofs (rational, historical,
    divine), the thermometer law and the final dilemma, and the Essay's two arguments (politics is
    theology; discussion cannot find truth), and say on which premises each rests. Open problem
    for the next lesson: three forecasts made against the Revolution — Burke's general, Maistre's
    Providence, Donoso's two dictatorships — can be checked against the record of 1790–1804
    (`<Ref>` assignats).

Headings as claims (suggested): "Every legal order hides a dictatorship"; "Concentrated attack,
concentrated defence"; "Revolutions are diseases of rich and free peoples"; "Two thermometers";
"The dagger or the sabre"; "A rule cannot decide its own exception"; "Every political question is
theological"; "The class that distinguishes"; "The cure and the disease".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no
  condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and
  evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), at most three per section:** 1848 (see opening); *Cortes*, *Congress of
  Deputies*; *Moderados* (Moderates) and *Progresistas* (Progressives) — Spain's two liberal
  parties of the 1840s, the first in power under General Narváez; *Narváez* (one clause);
  *ostracism*; *Roman dictator* (`<Ref>`); *article 14 of the Charter of 1814*; *Louis-Philippe*
  (the "citizen king" of 1830–48, whose monarchy Donoso calls "of prudence"); *June Days*;
  *Pius IX* and the Roman Republic of 1849 (one clause; Donoso's Roman section is skipped);
  *Proudhon*; *Spartacus*; *habeas corpus* (the writ against detention without trial — one
  clause); *deism* (belief in a God who made the world and does not intervene); *Pilate*,
  *Caiaphas*, *Barabbas* (the Roman governor, the high priest and the prisoner the crowd chose to
  free instead of Jesus — one clause, a reader may not know the Gospel scene).
- **Translation.** The speech's English is an unnamed web rendering whose fidelity is unverified;
  check every quoted sentence against the Spanish and prefer the course's own translation of the
  Spanish. "Sable" is "sabre" in the course's concept (`dictatorship-of-the-sabre`); the staged
  English says "sword" — say once that the original is "sable". The Essay's 1879 English is
  public domain but OCR-damaged: correct obvious slips ("Barrabas", "Caiphas" are the
  translator's spellings — keep them inside quotations, use "Barabbas", "Caiaphas" in prose).
- **"Discussing class" is Donoso's phrase but reaches the dossier through Schmitt's quotation of
  the Spanish.** Use the Essay's own sentences as the evidence; never attribute the "commission
  of investigation" line to Donoso (it is Schmitt's summary).
- **The Slav forecast (course.yaml key idea) is not in the dossier.** See Sources; verify or omit.
- **Do not read Donoso through Schmitt.** The 1849 speech and the 1851 Essay are taught first, in
  their own terms; Schmitt enters only in the forward paragraph of link 10. In particular, do not
  use "decisionism", "the exception", "commissarial" or "sovereign dictatorship" as the lesson's
  terms — they are `schmitt-political-theology`'s; this lesson poses the questions in plain words
  (who judges the emergency; does the dictatorship have a term?).
- **Real misreadings (L13, at most one slot):** the reading that Donoso praises military rule
  for its own sake is the one to refute — no: he puts liberty first where it is available ("who, being
  able to embrace liberty, would kneel before a dictatorship?"), calls the growth of armies and
  police a disease, and wants a religious reaction that would make government almost unnecessary.
  State that and then give the objection in link 10 at full strength.
- **Do not re-teach** Maistre's Providence or executioner, Locke's prerogative, Hume's passive
  obedience or Machiavelli's dictator — each is a `<Ref>` and a clause; the lesson's new matter is
  the speech's argument and the Essay's diagnosis.
- **Do not teach Donoso's theodicy** (Essay Bk II ch. I–VII, Bk III ch. I–II, VII–IX) beyond one
  clause: original sin is the key he uses on history and politics.
- **The speech's Roman and English sections** (l.44–51, 73–78) are skipped beyond a clause; the
  Bulwer quarrel is not needed.
- **Numbers (L12):** none are needed beyond dates (1848 by month where simultaneity is the point;
  4 January 1849; 1851; 1853). "A million arms / eyes / ears" is Donoso's figure of speech, not a
  count.
- **No moralising:** the lesson neither celebrates nor condemns the choice of the sabre; it states
  the dilemma and tests its premises.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval, warm-up.* Give Donoso's three proofs that dictatorship is legitimate (rational,
   historical, divine) with one example each from the speech, and the two instances from earlier
   lessons that a liberal reader would add (Locke's prerogative, `<Ref>`; Hume's salus populi,
   `<Ref>`).
2. *Document reasoning (sourcing drill).* The final paragraph of the speech ("It is a question of
   choosing between the dictatorship that comes from below …"): speaker (a deputy of the governing
   party, a former liberal), date (4 January 1849, after the risings of 1848 and the flight of the
   Pope), audience (the Cortes, and through the press Europe's Catholic readers), purpose (to
   defend the government's emergency powers against the Progressives), what it records (the
   collapse of the July Monarchy, the June Days, Spanish risings), what it argues (a dilemma
   between two dictatorships), what it omits (who decides when the emergency ends; how a
   dictatorship from above returns power), and what it can be USED to establish — the
   counter-revolutionary reading of 1848 and the form of the argument, not the facts of Spain's
   danger.
3. *Boundary probe.* Run Donoso's rule ("when legality is enough … legality; when it is not …
   dictatorship") at the two degenerate cases: (a) an emergency that never ends (his own "Liberty
   is finished"); (b) a government that is itself the invading force. Show that in (a) the rule
   abolishes the legality it was to protect, and that in (b) the rule, on Donoso's own theory of
   forces, licenses the "dictatorship from below". State which of his premises excludes (b) (the
   dictatorship must come "from above", from the existing government) and whether that premise is
   argued.
4. *Plausible false claim to refute.* "Donoso held that dictatorship is the best form of
   government." Name the failing steps: liberty is preferred where available (l.82); the political
   thermometer's rise is the disease (l.57–66); the religious reaction is the remedy he wants
   (l.67–69); "two things are impossible for me: to condemn dictatorship and to exercise it"
   (l.15). Then say what the claim gets right (dictatorship "a legitimate government … a good
   government … in given circumstances", l.17).
5. *Research-shaped task.* Design the test of the thermometer law: what two measures would you
   need (one of religious belief or practice, one of state coercive capacity — police per head,
   standing army, central administration), over what period and which countries, and what result
   would refute the law? Name one confound the design must handle (state capacity grows with
   wealth and railways, which Donoso himself names as causes in l.70). No data required; the answer
   is the design and the refuting result.
6. *Corroboration (E3, `maistre-and-bonald`).* Maistre said the Revolution "leads men more than men
   lead it" and that a counter-revolution must be "the contrary of revolution", not a revolution in
   reverse; Donoso says the 1848 Republic "was the instrument of victory of a higher power" and
   chooses the dictatorship of the sabre. Set two short excerpts side by side, state what each
   concludes about what royalists should *do*, and name the premise that has changed between 1797
   and 1849 (Donoso: "the last of all possible monarchies" has ended — there is no legitimate king
   to restore).
7. *Counter-argument.* Someone claims that Donoso's dilemma refutes itself: by his thermometer, the
   dictatorship he chooses raises the political repression he calls the disease. Name two facts
   the claim must account for (the dictatorship is a holding measure pending a religious reaction,
   l.67–69; the alternative he sees is not liberty but the dagger, l.82–84) and state what would
   have to be true for the claim to survive (that a third path — legality strong enough to resist
   without suspending itself — existed in 1849, which is the Progressives' claim and the premise
   Donoso denies).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`.** Donoso argues that free discussion rests on
   the false supposition that discussion is infallible, and that only an infallible authority can
   end disputes. Turn the dilemma around: if no human authority is infallible — Donoso's own
   premise about fallen nature, applied to the Church's human ministers by a reader who does not
   share his faith — what follows for an authority that silences a question? The reader should
   reach the thought that silencing presupposes the infallibility Donoso denies to discussion.
   The solution says in one sentence that the next module's English liberal makes exactly this
   argument ("all silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility"), with the `<Ref>`.

# Research brief: english-revolution-as-test (The English Revolution read against Hobbes)

status: planned
module: christendom-to-sovereignty
type: core
goal: Apply Hobbes's and Bodin's theories of sovereignty to the documents of 1642–1660 and judge where the record confirms the diagnosis and where it departs

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The whole module. From `bodin-sovereignty`: the marks of sovereignty (law for
  all without consent; war and peace; officers; taxation), the no-mixture argument (a kingdom
  whose two bodies each hold a mark is a war waiting to be fought — Denmark), "no subject may
  judge the sovereign", whoever controls the armed forces is master (IV.1), England classed as
  "a monarchy governed democratically". From `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`: the twelve rights;
  XXIX's "Mixt Government" as three factions; the seditious doctrines (private judgement,
  conscience, divided sovereignty, imitation of the Greeks and Romans — "they say not Regicide
  but Tyrannicide"); "the end of obedience is protection"; obligation ends on conquest. From
  `filmer-patriarcha` (the prerequisite): the royalist theory that was available in 1642 — the
  king a father of fathers, heir to Adam's dominion; no one born free; the king above the laws,
  law "the rule or instrument of the ruler" as the carpenter's rule is the carpenter's;
  Parliament the king's court and its liberties "the liberties of grace from the king";
  mixed monarchy "a mere impossibility or contradiction" (if king, Lords and Commons have a
  voice each, two may bind the king — Patriarcha II.16); no contract between king and people
  can be held broken without an ordinary judge, or the multitude is "judge in his own case"
  (II.17); Edward II and Richard II deposed "more upon wantonness than for any want" (II.18, a
  contemporary historian Filmer quotes and endorses).
  The lesson reads it alongside Hobbes and Bodin as the third theory of sovereignty, the one
  that grounds the king's right in nature rather than in fear or in the marks of the office.
  From `machiavelli-effectual-truth`: armed prophets conquer, new princes by their own arms hold with
  difficulty, and a founder must make institutions that outlive him (Prince VI–VII, XXIV;
  Discourses I.9, III.1). From `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: deposition by the public authority
  that appointed a king, never private tyrannicide. From `sparta-as-test-case` and
  `altar-of-victory`: the method of this kind of lesson — read the artefact against the theory
  and name the departures.
- **Contributes:** The one move (L16, APPLICATION): read four real documents — the Nineteen
  Propositions (June 1642), His Majesty's Answer to them (June 1642), the Charge and Sentence
  against Charles I (January 1649), and the Act of Free and General Pardon, Indemnity and
  Oblivion (1660) — with the war, the regicide, the Protectorate and the Restoration between
  them, against four theories (Hobbes XXIX, Bodin, Filmer's patriarchal right, Machiavelli's
  armed prophet) and one principle (protection and obedience), and judge: the diagnosis of 1642
  is confirmed to the letter (neither side could say where sovereignty lay, and the king's own
  advisers called England a mixture — passing over the patriarchal theory that denied any
  mixture was possible); the regicide confirms Bodin's and Hobbes's warning and the rival theory's
  premise at once (the court claimed the people's authority and was an army's purge); Cromwell
  is the armed prophet who could not found; 1660 confirms protection-and-obedience in its speed
  — and the theory cannot explain what was restored: a limited crown.
- **Hands off:** The rival theory of 1649 — that the people, through their representatives, may
  judge and remove a king who breaks his trust — is the next module's first lesson
  (`locke-property-and-consent`, 1689 as the revolution that succeeded), which answers Filmer by
  name: Patriarcha was printed only in 1680, in the crisis over whether the king's Catholic
  brother could be excluded from the succession, and the Two Treatises were written against it
  — one clause. The parallel between
  1640–60 and 1789–99 that a counter-revolutionary writer drew in 1797 is the forward exercise
  (`maistre-and-bonald`; `burke-reflections` predicted a general). Cromwell as the hero a
  Victorian will vindicate is the spiral to `carlyle-heroes` — one clause.
- **Spine step:** "Christendom builds a law above kings; Machiavelli breaks it; the wars of
  religion force one sovereign power above every faction — Bodin's definition, Hobbes's
  derivation from fear, Filmer's from fatherhood — and the English Revolution tests them"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):**
  - *Artefact:* (i) The Nineteen Propositions sent by the two Houses to the King at York, 2 June
    1642; (ii) His Majesty's Answer to the Nineteen Propositions, 18 June 1642 (drafted by
    Lucius Cary, Viscount Falkland, and Sir John Culpeper); (iii) the Act erecting a High Court
    of Justice (6 Jan 1649), the Charge against the King (20 Jan 1649), the Sentence (27 Jan
    1649), and the King's words at the trial and on the scaffold (30 Jan 1649); (iv) the
    Declaration of Breda (4 April 1660) and the Act of Free and General Pardon, Indemnity and
    Oblivion (29 Aug 1660, 12 Car. II c. 11). Supporting: the Engagement (1650), the Instrument
    of Government (Dec 1653). NONE is in the dossier — see Sources for where to obtain them and
    the licence.
  - *Module results used:* `sovereignty` (Bodin's marks and indivisibility — I.10, II.1),
    `indivisible-sovereignty` (Hobbes XVIII, XXIX "Mixt Government"), `mixed-constitution` (the
    doctrine the Answer states and Hobbes attacks), `patriarchal-divine-right` (Filmer: the
    king a father of fathers, above the laws, Parliament by grace, no mixture, no deposition —
    Patriarcha II.16–18, III.1, III.8, III.12–16), `tyrannicide` (Aquinas I.7: public
    authority; Bodin II.5: never a subject; Hobbes XXIX: "Tyrannicide" as a Greek and Roman word
    for regicide), `armed-prophet` (Prince VI; the founder who must make institutions outlive
    him — Prince VII, Discourses I.9), `protection-and-obedience` (Hobbes XXI l.7268; XX on
    conquest), and Bodin IV.1 (whoever controls the armed forces is master).
  - *Departures from the idealisation the lesson must name:* (1) in 1642 NEITHER side claimed
    Bodinian sovereignty in public — both claimed to defend the ancient constitution and the
    law, so the theory's "sovereign" was a place both sides said was empty of anyone's will;
    the one royalist theory that did claim it, Filmer's, was in manuscript and the king's own
    Answer took the opposite line (England a mixture of three estates) — so the war began with
    the absolutist theory on neither side's banner; (2) the
    Answer's authors held that the mixture WAS the safeguard against tyranny and anarchy, and
    the Restoration settlement of 1660–61 kept the limits of 1641 (no prerogative courts,
    Triennial Act of 1664 in weaker form, Parliament voting the revenue, feudal tenures
    abolished) — a mixed monarchy restored and lasting, which XXIX says cannot stand; (3) the
    regicide court claimed "the people's" authority, but the Commons that erected it had been
    purged by the army a month before (Pride's Purge, Dec 1648) and sat with a fraction of its
    members — "the people" was a fiction for both Hobbes's authorisation and the resistance
    tradition's "inferior magistrates"; (4) the armed prophet refused the crown (1657), could
    not transmit his office (Richard Cromwell, 1658–59), and his new order lasted eighteen
    months beyond his death; (5) 1660's Act of Oblivion pardoned a nation and EXCEPTED the
    regicides by name — the restored sovereign judged the judges, which protection-and-obedience
    does not predict; (6) religion: Hobbes said the sovereign decides doctrine, and the
    Restoration did so (Act of Uniformity 1662) — yet the settlement failed to hold and
    toleration came in 1689, Bodin's politique road rather than Hobbes's.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): in June 1642 Parliament sent nineteen demands; the king's
advisers answered with a defence of England as a mixed monarchy; nine years later Hobbes blamed
exactly that doctrine. Anchor (L11) the module's timeline figure at once (Figure 1) and the five
or six anchors the lesson reuses: 1640 the Long Parliament meets (after eleven years without
one); January 1642 the king leaves London; June 1642 the Propositions and the Answer; August
1642 war (Edgehill in October); 1645 Naseby, 1646 the king surrenders; December 1648 the army
purges the Commons; 30 January 1649 the king is beheaded; 1653–58 Cromwell Lord Protector; May
1660 Charles II returns. The newcomer's problem, stated as the module's: three theories say what
sovereignty is and what happens when it is divided or judged; here is a kingdom that divided it,
judged it, abolished it, replaced it with an armed man, and restored it — eighteen years of
evidence.

1. **What did Parliament demand, and which marks of sovereignty did the demands touch?** The
   sourcing drill first (metadata before text): author — the two Houses, in June 1642, after the
   king had left London and after Parliament had claimed by ORDINANCE (March 1642 — a measure
   without royal assent) to command the militia; audience — the king at York, and the printing
   press; purpose — terms, and a public case. Prediction task: what would such a body demand?
   Then the text: ministers and privy councillors to be approved by Parliament and removable by
   it (Props. 1–3); the great officers and judges likewise (4–6); the militia settled as
   Parliament "shall determine" (9); the forts and the Tower (15); the Church reformed "as both
   Houses … shall advise" (8); the king's children educated and married with Parliament's
   consent (10–11); the laws against Catholics enforced and Catholic peers barred (7, 12–13);
   foreign alliances by Parliament's advice (18). Map each onto Hobbes's twelve rights (Figure
   2): officers (right 10), judicature (8), the sword (9), doctrine (6), succession (XIX
   "artificial eternity"), war and peace (9). Record: of Bodin's marks, every one but law-making
   itself is claimed — and law-making is claimed by implication in the ordinance. The ledger
   shows what the theory predicts: a second sovereign.
2. **What did the king answer? The mixed-monarchy doctrine in the royalist's own words.**
   Metadata: drafted by two moderate royalists (Falkland, Culpeper), who had voted against the
   king in 1641, now his advisers; June 1642; audience — the Houses and the political nation;
   purpose — refuse the terms without claiming absolute power. The text's doctrine (quote
   short): there are three kinds of government, "absolute monarchy, aristocracy and democracy",
   each with "particular conveniences and inconveniences"; England's "experience and wisdom of
   your ancestors hath so moulded this out of a mixture of these as to give to this kingdom …
   the conveniences of all three, without the inconveniences of any one"; the king is one of
   "three estates" (king, Lords, Commons) in law-making; the prerogative — to call and dissolve
   parliaments, make war and peace, choose officers — is the monarchic element, "regulated" by
   law; accepting the Propositions would leave him "but the outside, but the picture, but the
   sign of a king", and power would pass "to Jack Cade or Wat Tyler" (the medieval rebels),
   ending in "a dark equal chaos of confusion". Then the second royalist text, recalled
   (Ref `filmer-patriarcha`, one or two sentences, no re-teaching): Patriarcha II.16 calls the
   Answer's doctrine "a mere impossibility or contradiction" — give king, nobility and people a
   voice each and "the nobility and commons together should have power to make a law to bind
   the king" — and III.12–16 makes Parliament "the king's court" whose liberties are "of grace".
   So in June 1642 royalism had two theories: the patriarchal one, which denied that any mixture
   could exist, and the Answer's, which made the mixture the constitution. The king's advisers
   published the second. Then the test: this is EXACTLY Hobbes XXIX's
   "Mixt Government" — money on one body, command on another, law on three — stated by the
   KING'S side as England's glory. Bodin II.1 (Ref): to call France a mixture was treasonable;
   the Answer calls England one as its defence. Judgement, as the course's own: the diagnosis
   fits — neither party in June 1642 would say where sovereignty lay; both claimed to defend the
   law and the ancient constitution; Hobbes's XVIII sentence (the "opinion received … that these
   Powers were divided") is a description, not a caricature. Departure (1) named here: there was
   no Bodinian sovereign to defend, because no one had claimed the office in public — the one
   theory that claimed it for the king (Filmer's) stayed in manuscript, and the king's own paper
   gave it up. Filmer and Hobbes agree on the verdict (a mixture is no state) from opposite
   premises (fatherhood; fear and covenant), and Bodin from a third (the logic of the marks) —
   Figure 3 shows the three against the Answer.
3. **The regicide: who may judge a king?** Narrative in a paragraph (anchor dates): the war; the
   army's victory; the king's negotiations with every party at once; the army seizes him (1647);
   Pride's Purge (Dec 1648 — soldiers exclude or arrest most of the Commons; the remnant is the
   "Rump"); the Act erecting a High Court (6 Jan 1649, by the Commons alone, the Lords having
   refused). The documents: the Charge — Charles "trusted with a limited power to govern by and
   according to the laws of the land, and not otherwise", had "a wicked design to erect and
   uphold in himself an unlimited and tyrannical power", and "levied war against the present
   Parliament and the people therein represented"; the Sentence — "tyrant, traitor, murderer,
   and a public enemy"; and the king's refusal to plead — "I would know by what power I am
   called hither … by what authority, I mean lawful"; "a king cannot be tried by any superior
   jurisdiction on earth". Three theories read the same scene. BODIN (Ref II.5): a subject never
   judges the sovereign; the Houses are subjects; the court's "authority of the Commons of
   England … in behalf of the people" is the thing he said could not exist. HOBBES (Ref XXIX):
   "they say not Regicide … but Tyrannicide" — the Greek and Roman vocabulary ("tyrant", "public
   enemy") in the Sentence is the imitation he named; and AQUINAS (Ref I.7): the public
   authority that APPOINTED a king may depose him — the Charge's "trusted with a limited power"
   is a claim of exactly that standing. The RIVAL THEORY at full strength, one paragraph: the
   resistance tradition of the 1570s (the Vindiciae — not in the dossier; see Sources) holds
   that the king is bound by a covenant with the people and that the people's inferior
   magistrates may resist and remove him when he breaks it; the Charge is that theory in a
   courtroom. Its 1649 English statement is Milton's Tenure of Kings and Magistrates (a
   people may choose and reject its kings — see Sources; one sentence). The royalist reply that
   the reader already holds (Ref `filmer-patriarcha`, II.17): no contract can be held broken
   without "a lawful trial … by the ordinary judge", or "every man may be both party and judge
   in his own case" — and the High Court of Justice was no ordinary judge; Charles's "by what
   authority, I mean lawful" is the same objection in the dock. Departure (3) named: the "people" who judged were a purged Commons sitting under
   an army — so the record cannot decide between Hobbes ("private judgement in arms") and the
   Vindiciae ("the people's magistrates"), because the people's representative had itself been
   made by the sword; Bodin IV.1 (whoever controls the armed forces is master) is the one
   sentence the record confirms without qualification. Hume's "This is the head of a traitor!"
   and the masked executioner (Ref the Maistre quarry; Hume's words quoted from Hume) as the
   scene — one paragraph, testimony not data.
4. **Cromwell: an armed prophet who could not found.** Metadata on the Instrument of Government
   (Dec 1653): drafted by army officers, "in four days", making Oliver Cromwell Lord Protector
   with a council and a parliament — England's only written constitution. Test against Prince VI
   (Ref): his own arms (the New Model Army he had built), a new order, the belief of the godly
   compelled by victories he read as providences — the armed prophet. Then Machiavelli's own
   further tests: a new prince by his own arms must make institutions that outlive him and
   secure succession (Prince VII — Borgia "provided for everything except being near death
   himself"; XXIV; Discourses I.9 — one man founds, many must maintain); Cromwell dissolved the
   parliaments his own Instrument created, ruled through major-generals (1655–57), refused the
   crown Parliament offered (1657), and left the office to his son Richard, who fell within
   eight months; the Protectorate lasted eighteen months beyond its founder. Aquinas's old woman
   of Syracuse (Ref I.7 [44]) as the test the Protectorate meets: the deposers became the new
   tyranny to many, and worse. The Victorian who will call him the one sincere man of his age is
   a forward clause (`carlyle-heroes`). Judgement: Machiavelli explains the rise exactly and the
   fall by his own rule (no institutions, no heir) — the theory holds; what it does not explain
   is why the armed prophet refused to become a prince.
5. **1660: protection and obedience, and what the theory cannot explain.** The Engagement (1650)
   first: every adult male to subscribe "I do declare and promise that I will be true and
   faithful to the Commonwealth of England, as it is now established, without a King or House of
   Lords" — Hobbes XX–XXI (Ref): a conquered man's submission from fear is valid, obligation
   follows protection; Hobbes himself returned to England in 1651–52 and submitted. Then the
   collapse: Richard falls (1659), the army and the restored Rump quarrel, General Monck marches
   from Scotland with 6,000 men against several times as many and no one fights (Hume via the
   quarry; quote Hume), the Long Parliament readmits its purged members and dissolves itself,
   the new Convention invites the king. The Declaration of Breda (April 1660): "a free and
   general pardon" to all who return to loyalty within forty days, "excepting only such persons
   as shall hereafter be excepted by Parliament"; "liberty to tender consciences"; soldiers'
   arrears paid; land sales left to Parliament. The Act of Oblivion (Aug 1660): pardons all
   treasons since 1 January 1637, forbids for three years any "words of reproach" recalling the
   late differences — and EXCEPTS by name those who sat in judgment on the king (about fifty
   named; thirteen executed, others imprisoned for life; three had fled to New England — the
   aside). Charles II's jest. Read against the theory: the SPEED is Hobbes's — obedience moved
   with protection, and a nation that had sworn to the Commonwealth swore to the king within
   months; Bodin IV.1 — Monck's army decided it. What the theory CANNOT explain (departures 2,
   5, 6): the king came back to the limits of 1641 — the prerogative courts (Star Chamber)
   stayed abolished, the Triennial Act stood (weakened 1664), Parliament voted the revenue,
   feudal tenures were abolished for a fixed excise (1660) — a mixed monarchy, the thing XXIX
   says is three factions, restored and lasting; the Act of Oblivion judged the judges, so the
   restored sovereign used the one power (punishment of those who had claimed to judge a king)
   in a way protection-and-obedience does not predict — it is Bodin's "no subject judges",
   enforced retroactively; and the religious settlement (Act of Uniformity 1662) did what right
   6 prescribes and failed within a generation, so that the toleration of 1689 followed Bodin's
   politique road rather than Hobbes's. The lesson closes on the judgement the module has
   earned: the theory of indivisible sovereignty describes 1642–49 as a diagnosis describes a
   disease, and describes 1660 as a mechanism; it does not describe 1660–89, where a divided
   constitution held — because, the next module will say, the division was not of sovereignty
   but of trust (`locke-property-and-consent`, forward clause).

Headings as claims (precise questions allowed): "Nineteen demands: which marks of sovereignty
did Parliament claim?"; "The king's own advisers call England a mixture"; "By what authority?
The regicide read three ways"; "An armed prophet who could not found"; "1660: obedience follows

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms and names to anchor (L11):** the Long Parliament (met Nov 1640, after eleven years
  without a parliament); ordinance (a parliamentary measure without royal assent — the legal
  novelty of 1642); the Militia Ordinance (March 1642); York (where the king sat in spring
  1642); Falkland and Culpeper; "three estates" (in the Answer: king, Lords, Commons — NOT the
  French three orders of the Bodin lesson: say so); the New Model Army (Parliament's
  professional army from 1645); Naseby (June 1645); Pride's Purge (Dec 1648); the Rump; the High
  Court of Justice; Whitehall; the Commonwealth (1649–53); the Engagement (1650); the
  Protectorate and Lord Protector (1653–59); the Instrument of Government; major-generals; the
  Humble Petition and Advice (1657); Richard Cromwell; General Monck; the Convention Parliament
  (1660); Breda (the Dutch town from which Charles II issued his declaration); Act of Oblivion;
  Star Chamber (the prerogative court abolished 1641); Triennial Act; Act of Uniformity (1662);
  Levellers (the radical democrats of 1647–49); Presbyterians and Independents (the two
  parliamentary religious parties — anchor only if used). Three per section; the timeline figure
  carries the rest.
- **Do not re-teach** XXIX, the twelve rights, Bodin's marks, the armed prophet or Aquinas's I.7
  — each is a `<Ref>` plus a clause; this lesson spends its words on the DOCUMENTS and the
  JUDGEMENT.
- **Do not narrate the war** beyond the anchors: the lesson is about where sovereignty lay, not
  about battles. Edgehill and Naseby appear only on the timeline.
- **The "three estates" trap:** in the Answer the king counts himself one of three estates with
  Lords and Commons; the older usage (clergy, lords, commons) is different, and the Bodin
  lesson's French estates are a third thing. Say it once.
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — 1642, 1649, 1660; "nineteen"; the eighteen years. Monk's 6,000,
  the fifty excepted and thirteen executed, the "365 days" and the "50,000 guineas … 300" all
  live in asides or exercises with their frame — and the 365-days footnote must be CHECKED in
  cof.txt p. 189 before use (ambiguous referent, see Sources). Casualty figures for the war are
  NOT needed and are contested — omit.
- **Hume is a source, not an authority:** the body quotes Hume only inside a `<Quote>` with
  attribution (L8a(a) — a near-contemporary text read as evidence), and Exercise 2 sources him
  (a Scottish historian writing in the 1750s, sympathetic to Charles, hostile to the sects).
  Maistre's parallel is NOT stated in the body — it is the forward exercise; Maistre's name is
  in References only.
- **The rival theory must be at full strength:** one paragraph on the Vindiciae's
  covenant-and-magistrates doctrine, not a "some believed". The lesson may say that this is the
  theory 1689 vindicated, as a forward clause.
- **Register:** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or
  approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label
  may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. For the regicide this means
  the lesson reports what each party claimed (the Sentence's words, the king's words) and what
  the documents can establish.
- **Filmer's dates:** Patriarcha was NOT a published pamphlet of 1642. It was written between
  the late 1620s and c. 1640 (modern editors disagree; the text's own clues — "his late Majesty
  King James", the 1628 Petition of Right speech — put at least part of it after 1628), was
  refused a licence to print in the early 1630s (Sommerville's edition — verify before stating),
  circulated in manuscript, and was printed only in 1680. Call it "the royalist theory written
  before the war" or "the absolutist theory the king's party had to hand in 1642", never "a
  1642 pamphlet". Filmer's own published interventions in the war — The Anarchy of a Limited or
  Mixed Monarchy (1648, against the mixed-monarchy doctrine of Philip Hunton's Treatise of
  Monarchy, 1643), The Freeholders Grand Inquest (1648), Directions for Obedience to Government
  in Dangerous or Doubtful Times (1652, on the Engagement) — are NOT in the dossier: name them in
  References at most, quote nothing from them. II.18's "the late rebellion" (~l.1184) is of
  uncertain reference in this transcription; do not use it to date the text.
- **Contested history taught as argument (course tone):** whether the war was caused by the
  constitutional doctrine (Hobbes), by religion, by finance, or by the Scottish and Irish crises
  is contested — Exercise 6 ranks the causes; the body states Hobbes's causal claim as HIS and
  tests it, never as settled.
- **Dossier gaps (say so in References):** none of the four artefacts, the Engagement, the
  Instrument, Breda, the Vindiciae, Hume's History or Hobbes's Review and Conclusion is in the
  dossier; the lesson's primary quotations come from the public-domain editions named in
  Sources, which the writer must fetch and verify; Milton's Tenure is not in the dossier
  either (the Strauss–Cropsey Milton chapter summarises it); there is
  no modern narrative history in the dossier at all — the writer must keep narrative to the
  anchors and cite Gardiner's documents for every quoted clause.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (the only mechanical one):** order six events and attach each to
   the theory it tests — the Militia Ordinance, the Answer, Pride's Purge, the Sentence, the
   Engagement, the Act of Oblivion — and state, for the Propositions, which of Hobbes's twelve
   rights three named demands deny.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill) — His Majesty's Answer:** author (two moderate
   royalists who had opposed the king in 1641), date (June 1642), audience (the Houses and the
   printing press), purpose (refuse terms without claiming absolute power); what it RECORDS (the
   king's side accepting that he is one of three estates), ARGUES (England as the best mixture),
   OMITS (that the king had tried to arrest five members in January and that the ordinance
   already existed); what it can be USED to establish — that "mixed monarchy" was the official
   ROYALIST doctrine of 1642, not a parliamentary invention — and what it cannot (whether the
   king believed it). Then corroborate with Patriarcha II.16 (a Kentish royalist gentleman,
   written before the war, unpublished until 1680): what does the existence of a royalist text
   denying any mixture establish about the choice the Answer's drafters made, and what can an
   UNPUBLISHED text not establish (that anyone in June 1642 read it)?
3. **Corroboration (paired sources):** Hume's account of the king's last days (ch. lix) and the
   Sentence of 27 January describe the same man. Set two short excerpts side by side; state what
   each takes the king's conduct to be evidence of (constancy / contempt of the court); source
   Hume (a Scottish historian of the 1750s, sympathetic to Charles, hostile to the sects); and
   judge what the two together can establish about the king's view of where authority lay ("by
   what authority, I mean lawful") and what they cannot (whether he was a tyrant).
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** the degenerate case of protection and obedience — Charles I in the
   army's custody (1647–48), unable to protect anyone. By XXI l.7268–7340, were his subjects
   discharged? Hobbes's cases: a captive sovereign whose subjects are not protected; a sovereign
   who has not renounced. Work the text and state the problem: the king refused to renounce, the
   army protected, and the subject had to JUDGE whether the sovereign still protected — the
   private judgement XXIX forbids. The solution says that the Engagement of 1650 is Parliament's
   attempt to settle by oath what the theory left to each man's judgement, and that Hobbes's own
   return in 1651–52 was his personal answer.
5. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "The civil war proves Hobbes right: mixed monarchy caused it, and
   only indivisible sovereignty could end it." Name two facts the claim fails to account for
   (the Restoration restored a limited crown that lasted; Bodin's own classification of England
   as a monarchy GOVERNED democratically makes "mixed" a description of government, not of
   sovereignty — so the Answer and XXIX may not even disagree about where the sword lay in 1641)
   and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that 1660–89 was itself only a
   truce ended by another division — which the next module will test).
6. **Causal ranking (second-order concept):** rank as necessary / enabling / precipitating
   among: the doctrine of divided sovereignty (Hobbes's cause), the religious quarrel over the
   Church (Laud's ceremonies; the Scottish rebellion of 1638), eleven years of government
   without parliament and its finance (1629–40), the Irish rising of 1641, the Militia
   Ordinance. Defend the ranking; then state which cause each THEORIST would rank first (Hobbes:
   doctrine; Bodin: religion — IV.7; Machiavelli: the arms — who held the sword) and what each
   ranking leaves unexplained.
7. **Research-shaped (E6d):** the Act of Oblivion names those excepted from pardon. Using the
   statute (12 Car. II c. 11, §§ 34–41, on British History Online or Wikisource), count how many
   are excepted by name, how many of those were commissioners of the High Court, and check the
   lesson's "about fifty, thirteen executed" against the text and against Hume's account of the
   trials of October 1660; then state what the exceptions establish about the restored
   sovereign's theory of the regicide (Bodin's "no subject judges", applied retroactively) and
   what the ban on "words of reproach" establishes about protection-and-obedience as a policy.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `maistre-and-bonald`:** the sequence of 1640–60 as this lesson has fixed it
   — a parliament summoned for money; a quarrel over religion and the sword; a purge; a king
   tried and executed by a body claiming the people's authority; sects; confiscations; an army
   and a general who writes a constitution in four days; a restoration by a general without a
   battle; an oblivion that excepts the regicides. Write beside it the French sequence of
   1789–1815 as far as the reader knows it (the opening of the course's next module gives the
   first lines), and state what the parallel would PREDICT for France if it were a law. The
   solution says in one sentence that a Savoyard writer drew exactly this parallel in 1797,
   column by column from Hume, and predicted a restoration by events rather than by a reversed
   act of will — and that `burke-reflections` had already predicted the general — with `<Ref>`s
   to both.

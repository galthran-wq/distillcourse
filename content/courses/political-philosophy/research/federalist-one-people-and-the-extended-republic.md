# Research brief: federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic (The Federalist: one people and the extended republic)

status: planned
module: nation-and-immigration
type: core
goal: Analyze the Federalist's argument that a large republic of representatives controls faction better than a small democracy, and its claim that Americans were already one people, and evaluate both against the critics of 1787 and since

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `hume-against-the-original-contract`: `government-founded-on-opinion`
  ("as Force is always on the side of the governed, the governors have nothing to
  support them but opinion"), consent as rare and never the only foundation, and
  the silk-worms passage — generations do not leave the stage at once, so each
  must "conform themselves to the established constitution" (OC 28). That lesson's
  notes name Madison as its forward Ref. From `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`:
  `mixed-constitution` and `checks-and-balances` — consuls, senate and people, each
  able to block the others (VI.11–18); the cycle in which every simple regime decays;
  the forecast that prosperity turns a mixed regime into "liberty or democracy",
  i.e. mob-rule (VI.57); and the remark that a state "wholly of philosophers" would
  not need religion to restrain the multitude (VI.56). From
  `the-nation-and-its-defenders`: the problem of who "we" are, the
  `pre-political-loyalty` without which there is no politics to disagree about,
  and the point where the conservative and the liberal nationalist part — what the
  bond is made of. From earlier in course order (recall by Ref only):
  `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` (a city has a natural size; the polity rests on
  the middle); `decline-of-regimes` (democracy's slide into tyranny);
  `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will` (Social Contract II.3: no partial societies,
  "But if there are partial societies, it is best to have as many as possible");
  `locke-property-and-consent` (`consent`); `burke-reflections` (prejudice and the
  veneration of inherited institutions); `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`
  (the omnipotence of the majority in America — a later witness who quotes Federalist
  51).
- **Contributes:** The inversion of the oldest axiom of republican theory. Every
  writer had said a republic must be small; Madison derives from the causes of
  faction that it must be large — representation filters, and extent multiplies
  interests until no majority shares a motive to oppress. The lesson rebuilds the
  derivation step by step from Federalist 10, shows how Federalist 51 turns Polybius's
  checks among social orders into checks among offices all drawn from the people
  (the mixed constitution "without estates"), and shows that Hume's foundation —
  government rests on opinion — is kept, not discarded (Federalist 49, 85). Then it
  sets beside this Jay's claim in Federalist 2 that Americans were already one people
  and asks how the two fit: one people in loyalty, many factions in interest.
- **Hands off:** Jay's "same ancestors … same language … same religion" is a
  factual claim about 1787, and the next lessons test it: was the people one, and
  what held a people of many ancestries together afterwards?
  `albions-seed-and-who-are-we` takes it — four British peoples rather than one,
  and a political scientist who opens his account of American identity with Jay's
  very sentence. Two later readings of the extended republic are named only as
  forward Refs: that it dissolves local loyalty by design
  (`deneen-post-liberalism`) and that a nation's unity can be maintained by
  institutions alone (`miller-strangers-in-our-midst`; `the-argument-assembled`).
- **Spine step:** "The live question: what a nation is and who may join it — Scruton and Miller on the pre-political loyalty, America as England's heir from the Federalist to Who Are We?, the prophecies of displacement from Powell to Camus, Carens and Miller on borders, Murray on Europe — and 2015 as the test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "Every political writer from Aristotle to Rousseau had
said a republic must be small … The lesson follows both claims." Anchor at once (L11b),
one sentence each: the Articles of Confederation (1781 — a league of thirteen states
with a Congress that could not tax or raise troops without them); Shays's Rebellion
(1786–87 — indebted farmers in western Massachusetts closing the courts); the
Philadelphia Convention (summer 1787) and its draft, which nine of the thirteen
states' conventions had to ratify; the Federalist — 85 newspaper essays signed
"Publius", October 1787 to May 1788, written by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and
John Jay to win New York's convention. The newcomer's problem: the reader's own
lessons say the small-republic axiom is right (`<Ref>` aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle,
`<Ref>` decline-of-regimes, `<Ref>` rousseau-inequality-and-general-will — a clause
each: citizens who know one another, assemble, share manners; democracies that slide
into tyranny); thirteen states spread along a thousand miles of coast cannot meet in
one place.

1. **Why must a republic be small?** Question: what, exactly, goes wrong in a large
   one? The axiom's reasons, from the texts the reader has met: the citizens cannot
   assemble, cannot know their rulers, and differ in manners and interests; so the
   large state needs a monarch or an army to hold it. Obvious first attempt — the
   one the Anti-Federalists and Montesquieu offered: a confederate republic, a league
   of small republics (No. 9). Fails, in the Federalists' telling, because the league
   of 1781 already exists and cannot act (one clause; do not run Nos. 15–22). So the
   axiom forces a choice the lesson states plainly: many small republics that
   quarrel, or one large state that cannot be a republic. Because: the axiom was a
   claim about faction — that only in a small, similar body is the common good
   visible — and Madison answers it on that ground.

2. **What is faction, and why can its causes not be removed?** Quote the definition
   (No. 10, l.1843): citizens, "whether amounting to a majority or a minority",
   united by passion or interest "adverse to the rights of other citizens, or to the
   permanent and aggregate interests of the community". Two cures. Remove the
   causes: destroy liberty ("Liberty is to faction what air is to fire") or give
   everyone "the same opinions, the same passions, and the same interests". The seam
   (L10b): the reader expects the republican answer the course has taught —
   Plato's city that forms one opinion (`<Ref>` kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king),
   Rousseau's general will with no partial societies (`<Ref>`) — and Madison refuses
   both: reason is fallible and tied to self-love, and men differ in faculties, so
   protecting those faculties — "the first object of government" — produces unequal
   property, "the most common and durable source of factions". "The latent causes of
   faction are thus sown in the nature of man." And no statesman can referee:
   legislators are judges in their own causes, and "Enlightened statesmen will not
   always be at the helm". Answer: control the effects. Because: a cure that
   removes faction removes the liberty or the diversity that a free people is.

3. **Which faction is the danger?** A minority faction is outvoted — "the republican
   principle" handles it. A majority faction uses that same principle to oppress, and
   in a pure democracy (citizens governing in person) nothing stops it: such
   democracies "have ever been spectacles of turbulence and contention … as short in
   their lives as they have been violent in their deaths". Name the period's examples
   as Madison does — "a rage for paper money, for an abolition of debts, for an equal
   division of property" — the debtor majorities of the state legislatures after the
   war (anchor: states printing paper money and staying debt collections in the
   1780s). State the reading both ways in one sentence each: a defence of creditors'
   property against majorities; a defence of any minority's rights, as the definition
   is written. Because: the problem of popular government is a majority that is
   itself the faction.

4. **The two differences of a republic.** Quote l.1992–1996. (a) Representation:
   "to refine and enlarge the public views, by passing them through the medium of a
   chosen body of citizens" — and the risk Madison states himself, "the effect may be
   inverted". Larger districts give a larger pool of fit candidates and make the
   "vicious arts" of election harder. (b) Extent — the core: "Extend the sphere, and
   you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable
   that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of
   other citizens; or if such a common motive exists, it will be more difficult for
   all who feel it to discover their own strength, and to act in unison". Work it as
   an `<Example>` (figure 3): a town with two interests — one of them is a majority;
   a state with five; a union with dozens that cut across one another — a majority
   must now be a coalition of many interests, and a coalition of many can agree
   "seldom … on any other principles than those of justice and the general good"
   (No. 51). Then No. 51's parallel: civil rights are secured as religious liberty
   is, by "the multiplicity of interests … the multiplicity of sects". Recall in a
   clause that Rousseau reached "as many as possible" as his second-best (`<Ref>`).
   Because: the size that made republics impossible is, once representation removes
   the need to assemble, the source of their stability.

5. **Where the argument comes from: Hume's opinion and Hume's large commonwealth.**
   Question: is this a break with the past or a borrowing? The texts show the
   borrowing (`<Ref>` hume-against-the-original-contract, one clause: governors have
   nothing to support them but opinion). No. 49 restates it — "all governments rest on
   opinion" — and draws Hume's conservative conclusion: frequent appeals to the people
   would deprive the government "of that veneration which time bestows on every
   thing", since "a nation of philosophers is as little to be expected as the
   philosophical race of kings wished for by Plato". No. 85 quotes Hume by name: no
   genius by "reason and reflection" can balance a large state; "EXPERIENCE must guide
   their labor; TIME must bring it to perfection". And Hume's "Idea of a Perfect
   Commonwealth" (obtained; quote one sentence) had already argued against "the common
   opinion" that no large state could be a commonwealth. Seam: the reader expects the
   founders of a new republic to be the rationalist "projectors" Burke attacks
   (`<Ref>` burke-reflections); the papers' own words put them closer to Hume and,
   in No. 49, to Burke's "prejudice". The tension the lesson leaves visible: No. 14
   praises Americans for not suffering "a blind veneration for antiquity" — the same
   author, three months apart, asks for innovation in the design and veneration for
   the result. Because: the extended republic is new machinery resting on the oldest
   foundation, opinion.

6. **The mixed constitution without estates.** Question: what became of the checks
   the reader met in Rome (`<Ref>` polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome: consuls, senate,
   people — three social orders, each with powers to block the others)? No. 51 keeps
   the mechanism — "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition"; give each
   department "the necessary constitutional means and personal motives to resist
   encroachments"; "If men were angels…" — and changes what is balanced. No. 39: all
   power must derive "from the great body of the society, not from an inconsiderable
   proportion, or a favored class of it". No. 51: the alternative guardian of
   minorities, "a will in the community independent of the majority" — the
   hereditary element of every mixed constitution — is "at best … a precarious
   security". No. 63: the ancients knew representatives (ephors, tribunes); the
   difference is "THE TOTAL EXCLUSION OF THE PEOPLE, IN THEIR COLLECTIVE CAPACITY" —
   and Carthage's senate cited "according to the testimony of Polybius". The course's
   own statement of the result (the close reading is in References): the American
   republic is "unmixed" (No. 14) — every office drawn from one people; the checks run
   between offices with different terms and constituencies (two-year House, six-year
   Senate, four-year President, judges for good behaviour), not between classes.
   Figure 4. Because: once no hereditary order is allowed, the only materials left to
   balance are offices, terms and ambitions.

7. **"One united people."** Question: if the republic is to hold together by a
   multitude of interests, what holds the multitude together? Quote No. 2 whole
   (l.259–266) and set it in its argument: Jay is answering the proposal to split
   the Union into three or four confederacies; nature (the rivers that "bind it
   together") and history (one war, one Congress, one set of treaties) have made
   one country and one people, "a band of brethren". No. 14 repeats it: "the kindred
   blood which flows in the veins of American citizens, the mingled blood which they
   have shed in defense of their sacred rights". The puzzle, stated as the reader will
   feel it: Madison wants variety, Jay wants sameness — a contradiction? The answer,
   the lesson's synthesis: two levels. A people must be one in what `<Ref>`
   the-nation-and-its-defenders called the pre-political loyalty — enough to accept
   one government and lose elections to one another — and many in interests, so that
   no majority faction forms. Jay's "same religion" and Madison's "multiplicity of
   sects" describe the same Protestant population at the two levels. And Hume's
   foundation needs the first level: a government resting on opinion rests on an
   opinion shared (figure 5). Because: the extended republic does not replace the
   nation; it presupposes one.

8. **The objections — of 1787 and since — at full strength.** (a) Brutus (October
   1787; from his own text): a republic needs citizens whose "manners, sentiments, and
   interests" are similar; extent makes that impossible, makes representatives
   strangers to those they represent, and leaves a distant government obeyed only by
   force — Montesquieu, whom both sides cite, is on his side. Give Madison's reply as
   the text gives it (representation; the double security of No. 51) and the
   concession: Brutus attacks the premise Jay supplies — the extended republic works
   only if the one people exists, and extent may dissolve it. (b) Was the one people
   true? The census of 1790: about 3.9 million, about one in six enslaved — whom
   Madison himself, in No. 54, describes as having "the mixed character of persons
   and of property" and whom Jay's sentence does not include; Native nations outside
   it; among whites, mostly British stock but Germans in Pennsylvania (Franklin's
   complaint of 1751) and Dutch in New York; Jay himself of Huguenot and Dutch
   descent; one religion only at the level of "Protestant". State it as the course's
   finding: Jay's sentence was a strong claim about the English-speaking Protestant
   majority and a political argument, not a census (figure 6). (c) The tyranny of
   opinion (`<Ref>` tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism): forty years
   on, a visitor found the majority's power over opinion unchecked, and quoted No.
   51 to say so. (d) Forward, one paragraph: a post-liberal critic reads No. 10's
   "distrust" as a design for civic privatism (`<Ref>` deneen-post-liberalism) —
   named, not taught. Where the lesson concedes: the extended-republic argument holds
   against majority faction in legislation where interests cut across one another;
   where one cleavage coincides with geography it fails (Exercise 3); and it rests on
   Jay's premise, which the next lesson tests.

Headings fall out as: "Why every republic had been small"; "Faction cannot be cured,
only controlled"; "The majority is the danger"; "Representation and the extended
sphere"; "What the Federalist kept from Hume"; "Checks without estates"; "One people,
many factions"; "Brutus, the census and the later critics".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **The register rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), three per section at most:** Articles of Confederation;
  Shays's Rebellion; the Philadelphia Convention and ratification; "Publius";
  Anti-Federalists (opponents of ratification — not a party, a family of objections);
  Montesquieu (the French author of *The Spirit of the Laws*, 1748, whose
  small-republic maxim both sides cite — one clause); "confederate republic";
  "faction" (Madison's sense, not party); "pure democracy" vs "republic" in Madison's
  definitions (the reader's word "democracy" is Madison's "republic" — say so); "the
  sphere"; "department" (= branch); the three-fifths clause (one clause, at No. 54).
  Do not introduce the federal/national analysis of No. 39 beyond its definition of a
  republic.
- **Real misreadings (L13), one slot at most:** "the Constitution copies the mixed
  British or Roman constitution" — refute from Nos. 39, 51, 63, 14 (unmixed; no
  hereditary element; checks among offices). A second, handled in a clause: "Madison
  defended democracy" — he defined his republic against it.
- **Do not re-teach** Hume's opinion theory, Polybius's mixture and cycle, Rousseau's
  general will, Burke's prejudice, Scruton's and Miller's nation — one `<Ref>` clause
  each. Do not pre-run Fischer's four folkways or Huntington's core culture
  (`albions-seed-and-who-are-we`): the census and Jay's sentence only; or Deneen's
  reading (`deneen-post-liberalism`): one paragraph naming it.
- **Hume as source: show, don't assert.** No. 85 names Hume; No. 49 restates FP 1;
  the perfect-commonwealth essay states the large-republic idea first. That Madison
  took Federalist 10 from Hume is a scholar's thesis — say "probably", put the
  scholar in References.
- **Dossier gaps:** Hume's "Idea of a Perfect Commonwealth", "Of the Independency of
  Parliament" and "Of Parties in General" are NOT staged (index 91 holds FP and OC
  only) — obtain from davidhume.org (PD) and verify every quoted sentence. Brutus is
  not staged — obtain. The ancestry shares for 1790 are estimates; use ranges and
  cite them; the census figures are public record.
- **Numbers (L12):** 85 essays; 1787–88; nine of thirteen states; about 3.9 million in
  1790, about one in six enslaved (the keeper). Everything else to the decade.
- **No moralising either way** on Jay's omission of the enslaved or on Madison's
  property examples: state what the texts include and exclude, and let the
  objections carry the weight.
- **Spelling:** quote Gutenberg's text as printed ("adversed" in No. 10's definition
  is the Gutenberg reading of "adverse" — check against the 1788 McLean edition and
  prefer it if they differ).

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (E3; the one warm-up).** Name Polybius's three parts of the Roman
   constitution and one check each holds on another (`<Ref>`
   polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome); then name the American office that No. 51's design
   puts in each position, and the element that has no American counterpart.
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill on No. 2.** Metadata (John Jay — Secretary
   for Foreign Affairs of the Confederation; a pseudonymous essay in a New York paper,
   31 October 1787; written to defeat the plan of several confederacies). Predict what
   such a text will stress and omit; then read the sentence; records / argues / omits
   (the enslaved, Native nations, non-British settlers, the divisions among
   Protestants); use: what it can establish (how a leading Federalist wished New
   Yorkers to see themselves) and what it cannot (the composition of the population).
3. **Boundary probe (E6a).** Run Madison's argument on a republic where one interest
   coincides with a region and is held by a majority there (slavery by the 1850s will
   do). Does extending the sphere still prevent a majority faction, or does it
   multiply the faction's weight? Solution: the argument assumes interests that cut
   across one another; when one cleavage aligns with geography, extent organises
   rather than dilutes it — state the hidden premise as a condition of No. 10.
4. **Claim to refute (E6b).** "The American Constitution is a mixed constitution on the
   Roman model." Name the steps that fail (No. 39's definition of a republic; No. 51's
   rejection of "a will … independent of the majority"; No. 63's "total exclusion";
   No. 14's "unmixed") and state what was kept (checks between parts, ambition
   against ambition).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c).** From Hume's "government founded on
   opinion" (`<Ref>`) and No. 10's argument, derive why No. 49 rejects frequent
   conventions to correct the constitution; then state how No. 14's praise of
   Americans for refusing "a blind veneration for antiquity" can be squared with it
   (innovate once, by reflection and choice; then let time confer veneration).
6. **Corroboration (paired sources).** Brutus I (October 1787) and No. 10 (November
   1787) on extent and similarity: two short excerpts; list where they agree (faction
   is the disease; Montesquieu's maxim is the issue), where they disagree (whether
   extent cures or causes it), and judge which premise decides the dispute (whether a
   people can stay one in loyalty while many in interest).
7. **Research-shaped task (E6d).** "Jay's one people was true in 1787." Design the check:
   which sources would you use (the 1790 census; surname-based ancestry estimates;
   church and language records), what each can and cannot establish (the census records
   status, not ancestry; surnames under-count Germans and Irish who anglicised), and
   what result would make each of Jay's six elements true or false. State the
   conclusion the sources can support.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `albions-seed-and-who-are-we`.** Suppose, as the ancestry estimates
   suggest, most white Americans of 1790 descended from people of the British Isles.
   Does "same ancestors" then mean one way of life? From what earlier lessons say about
   the English Revolution (`<Ref>` english-revolution-as-test), predict two ways a
   Puritan from East Anglia and a royalist's servant from the south-west of England
   might have differed in religion, rank and idea of liberty. The solution says the
   next lesson finds four such British peoples in four regions of America, and opens
   with a political scientist who quotes Jay's very sentence to define the American
   core, with a `<Ref>`.

# Research brief: filmer-patriarcha (Filmer's Patriarcha: the natural power of kings)

status: planned
module: christendom-to-sovereignty
type: core
goal: Analyze Filmer's argument that political power is fatherly power inherited from Adam and that natural liberty is a dangerous fiction, and locate the premises Locke would attack

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty` (the prerequisite): the most absolute
  sovereign in the tradition — twelve rights, no breach of covenant possible, no accusation or
  punishment by a subject, indivisible, not subject to the civil laws, a "mixt government" is
  three factions — built on natural equality and the consent of men who covenant "every man with
  every man"; and Leviathan XX's claim that a parent's dominion comes from the child's consent,
  not from generation (the FORWARD exercise of that lesson lands on its reversal). From
  `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`: the state of nature as a condition of equals with no
  common superior; Hobbes's admission that it was "never generally so". From
  `bodin-sovereignty`: sovereignty as absolute and perpetual, marked by law-making, war, peace,
  pardon and judgement of life and death; the family as "a true image of the commonwealth" and
  the father's power of life and death that Bodin wanted restored (I.2–4); commonwealths begun
  "in force and violence", Nimrod the first to subject others (I.6); no subject may judge even a
  tyrant (II.5); no mixed state. From `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: law's author is "the whole
  people or … him who has care of the community" (q.90 a.3) and a multitude that appointed its
  king may depose him (De Regno I.7 [49]) — the scholastic doctrine Filmer's first sentence
  names as the source of the error ("since the time that school divinity began to flourish").
  From `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`: household → village → city; the
  household's relations of rule as natural; natural slavery and its admitted difficulty. From
  `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`: the origin of rule in a herd following the strongest, "as
  among bulls … and cocks" (VI.5), and Rome's mixture. From `machiavelli-discourses-republic`:
  Rome's tumults as the source of its liberty — the verdict Filmer reverses on the same
  evidence. The reader has NOT met Locke, Bellarmine, Suárez, Buchanan, the Exclusion Crisis or
  any seventeenth-century English institution beyond what `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`
  anchored (Coke, the Long Parliament in one clause).
- **Contributes:** The one move: deny the premise every party had granted — that men are born
  free and equal — and rebuild the whole of politics on the one relation of rule no one can
  claim to have consented to: a father's over the child he begot. Fatherly power and royal
  power differ only in extent; Adam's dominion passed to the patriarchs, was divided among
  families at Babel, and descends to kings as heirs of the first fathers; so no people ever had
  the right to choose a ruler, no majority can bind the rest, no mixture of king and people can
  exist, no subject can judge the king, and the king is above the laws he makes. The lesson
  rebuilds the case link by link at full strength and then marks, as the opening promises, the
  exact premises an opponent has to break: that Adam's dominion was political, that it was
  heritable, that its heir can be identified, and that "every authority, however acquired, is
  a father's" does not collapse right into possession.
- **Hands off:** The lesson decides Filmer's case (see VERDICT): it fails, established, at the
  step it needs most — no living king can be shown to be Adam's heir, and the fallback ("however
  acquired") collapses right into possession; his strongest point, that no one consents to being
  born into a polity, stands and is carried on. What passes on: (i) The test, next lesson (`english-revolution-as-test`): in June 1642 the
  king's own advisers defended England as a mixture of three estates — the doctrine Patriarcha
  II.16 calls "a mere impossibility or contradiction"; the war will show which royalism the
  king's party actually fought for. (ii) The reply, next module (`locke-property-and-consent`):
  the Two Treatises were written against Patriarcha after its printing in 1680; the First
  Treatise's refutation is reported there, the Second Treatise answers "no man is born free"
  with "we are born free, as we are born rational". This lesson names the steps Locke breaks; it
  does NOT give Locke's positive theory. (iii) The sceptic's reply to both sides
  (`hume-against-the-original-contract`): God is the author of all government in the same sense
  that he is the author of "a Borgia or an Angria", so divine right proves too much — and no
  actual government rests on consent either. (iv) A forward clause only: the problem Filmer
  makes vivid — consent given by one generation cannot bind the next — is answered without Adam
  by the conservative doctrine of inheritance as a partnership of generations
  (`burke-reflections`).
- **Spine step:** "Christendom builds a law above kings; Machiavelli breaks it; the wars of
  religion force one sovereign power above every faction — Bodin's definition, Hobbes's
  derivation from fear, Filmer's from fatherhood — and the English Revolution tests them"

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** political power is fatherly power inherited from Adam, so the king rules as
  Adam's heir and no people ever had the right to choose or remove a ruler.
- **Verdict — grade: established** (for the inheritance step). The argument fails at the step
  it needs most: no living king can be shown to be Adam's heir — Filmer himself grants that "the
  knowledge who should be that one man be quite lost" (I.9) — and the fallback, that every
  authority "whether he come to the crown by usurpation, or by election … or by any other way
  whatsoever" is "the only right and natural authority of a supreme father" (I.10), collapses
  right into possession: it can no longer tell the rightful king from whoever holds the crown,
  the distinction the royalist needed against rebels. Filmer's strongest point stands and is
  carried on: no one consents to being born into a polity, and no people has been shown
  choosing its first ruler.
- **What decides it:** Filmer's own text — I.9 and I.10 read together (the failure is internal
  and textual, which is why the grade is established, not merely strong). Corroborating
  readers, forward only: Locke, Second Treatise §1 (the four points; "make Sir Robert
  intelligible … where he treats of usurpation"); Hume on God as author of "a Borgia or an
  Angria".
- **Strongest objection:** Filmer never needed the heir to be known: I.9 says power then goes to
  the heads of families, who confer it, and the man so set up "is substituted properly by God";
  any settled succession is "reputed" the heir's. It does not overturn the verdict, because this
  fallback is I.10's "however acquired" in other words — a title that any successful holder
  acquires — so the theory survives only by ceasing to distinguish right from possession, which
  is the thing it was written to do.
- **What would overturn it:** no conceivable evidence, since the heir cannot be identified. (A
  genealogy tracing a reigning king's line to Adam would be needed, and none can exist.)

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): if every man is born free, as the Jesuits and the Calvinist
pamphleteers said, a king rules by the people's leave and the people may take it back; an
English country gentleman answers that no one has ever been born free — everyone is born a son,
and a king is a father of fathers — and asks where in all of history a people ever met to choose
a ruler. Anchor at once (L11): Sir Robert Filmer (c. 1588–1653), a landowner of East Sutton in
Kent and a royalist; Patriarcha, written between the late 1620s and c. 1640, circulated in
manuscript and printed only in 1680, twenty-seven years after his death. One sentence on why the
date matters: it was printed in a crisis (1679–81) over whether Parliament could exclude the
king's Catholic brother from the succession, and it was answered at once — the answer is the
next module's first lesson (forward clause only). The newcomer's problem, tied to the previous
lesson (`<Ref>` `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`): Hobbes built absolute power from men born equal
and free who covenant; every resistance writer the reader has met (Aquinas's multitude that
appointed, De Regno I.7 — Ref; Calvin's magistrates set "to moderate the licentiousness of
princes" — Ref `bodin-sovereignty`) started from the same premise. A royalist who grants it has
already granted that the people once had the power to give — and so, perhaps, to take back.
What if the premise is false?

1. **What exactly is the doctrine, and why attack the premise rather than the conclusion?**
   The obvious royalist move — the one Filmer says every royalist before him made — was to
   accept natural liberty and deny that it licenses deposition. Why Filmer thinks it fails: the
   resistance writers' "perilous conclusion" (that "the people or multitude have power to punish
   or deprive the prince if he transgress the laws") follows "as a necessary consequence" from
   natural freedom and the people's original choice; grant the premise and you have granted the
   power. Answer: attack the premise — "if they did but confute this first erroneous principle
   the whole fabric of this vast engine of popular sedition would drop down of itself" (I.1).
   Then give the doctrine at FULL STRENGTH in its best spokesman's words, as Filmer himself
   does (I.2): Bellarmine — power is in the multitude by the law of nature, since among equals
   "there is left no reason why … one rather than another should bear rule"; the multitude must
   give it to one or a few; "if there be a lawful cause, the multitude may change the kingdom".
   Filmer's three replies, each one sentence: then God instituted democracy and every monarchy
   is a human device; then the people's natural power is only the power to give power away;
   and "who shall judge of this lawful cause? If the multitude — for I see nobody else can —
   then this is a pestilent and dangerous conclusion". Seam (L10b): the reader met the last
   question with Aquinas (Ref) — the scholastic answer was "the public authority that appointed
   him"; Filmer's point is that this authority is the multitude judging its own cause.
2. **If not from the multitude, where does power come from?** Obvious attempt: from God, as
   Bellarmine says, by way of the people. Filmer turns Bellarmine against himself (I.3): the
   cardinal concedes that "if many men had been together created out of the earth, they all
   ought to have been princes over their posterity" — so creation itself made the first man
   prince of those born from him. Answer: political power IS fatherly power. Adam's dominion was
   "as large and ample as the absolutest dominion of any monarch", and the proof is that the
   patriarchs exercised the marks of sovereignty the reader knows from Bodin (Ref
   `bodin-sovereignty`): Judah sentences Tamar to be burned (life and death), Abraham commands
   318 men of his own household (war), Abraham makes a league with Abimelech under oath (peace).
   Father and king "all one, without any difference at all but only in the latitude or extent";
   the commandment that orders obedience to kings is "Honour thy father" (I.10). The "because"
   the reader must see: a son's subjection is the one relation of rule nobody claims to have
   consented to — it is there at birth.
3. **How did one father's power become the world's kingdoms?** Obvious objection: kings today
   are plainly not their subjects' fathers ("It may seem absurd", I.8 — Filmer raises it
   himself). The answer in three steps. (a) The descent: Noah divided the world among three sons;
   at Babel the seventy-two nations were "distinct families, which had fathers for rulers over
   them", tongues distributed "after their families" (Gen. 10) — so even the confusion of
   languages preserved fatherly rule (I.5). Nimrod, lawful king of his own family, seized other
   fathers' rights and became "the author and first founder of monarchy" by usurpation (I.6) —
   say the seam: Bodin (Ref) also made Nimrod the first to rule by force, but made force the
   origin of every commonwealth; Filmer makes it one deviation from a fatherly order already in
   place. (b) The evidence that kingdoms grew from families: the "heaps of kings" — nine kings
   in one corner of Asia, Joshua's thirty-one in Canaan, four in Kent when Caesar landed (I.7).
   (c) The inheritance: kings "either are, or are to be reputed, the next heirs to those first
   progenitors" and succeed to "the right of that fatherhood"; so "many a child, by succeeding a
   king, hath the right of a father over many a greyheaded multitude" (I.8). Then the hard case,
   which Filmer states himself (I.9): what if the heir is unknown? "An heir there always is. If
   Adam himself were still living, and now ready to die, it is certain that there is one man,
   and but one in the world, who is next heir, although the knowledge who should be that one man
   be quite lost." Failing knowledge, power goes not to the people but to the heads of families,
   who may confer it — and the man so set up holds it from God, not from them. Mark this step:
   the lesson returns to it in link 8.
4. **Is a kingdom really a family? The philosophers' objection.** Obvious objection, from the
   reader's own Aristotle (Ref `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`): household and
   city differ in kind, and Suárez says Adam had "only economical power, but not political".
   Filmer's replies (II.1–4): Aristotle's apparent egalitarian sentence reports what "seems to
   some" — the Latin translation dropped the word "some"; Aristotle's own Politics I says "the
   eldest in every house is king", and Plato called a commonwealth "a large family"; Adam lived
   930 years and saw "seven or eight descents", ruling a multitude "far bigger than many
   commonwealths", and "some mean cottage or tent did serve him to keep his court in" — so
   economical and political power "differ no otherwise than a little commonweal differs from a
   great one". Roman patria potestas as the historical model: the Twelve Tables let a father sell
   his son three times; Cassius threw his tribune son from the Tarpeian Rock for proposing a land
   law while "the magistrates and people [stood] thereat amazed and not daring to resist". Two
   seams the reader must see. (i) Filmer rejects Polybius's origin story (Ref
   `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`): men herding "like … bulls … and cocks" until the strongest
   rule is "the ignorance of the Creation" — rule began in generation, not in strength. (ii)
   Filmer, the champion of natural hierarchy, REJECTS Aristotle's natural slavery: rule by the
   wise over the strong is "a dangerous and uncertain rule … for if a man prove both wise and
   strong, what will Aristotle have done with him?" — and nature "aims at perfection in all her
   works". His hierarchy is of birth order, not of souls: say so, because a newcomer will expect
   the opposite.
5. **The demand for the historical instance.** This is the section the opening promises. The
   obvious defence of popular choice: the people chose their rulers at some first meeting, by
   majority, with the absent voting by proxy or consenting afterwards. Filmer's dilemma, one
   horn at a time (II.5–7), each as a short step the reader can check: (a) if God gave power to
   the whole multitude of mankind, then no single commonwealth can elect a ruler "but by a mere
   usurpation upon the privilege of the whole world" — and if any multitude may divide itself at
   will, "a gap is opened for every petty factious multitude to raise a new commonwealth"; (b)
   "Was a general meeting of a whole kingdom ever known for the election of a prince? Is there
   any example of it ever found in the whole world?"; (c) MAJORITY: where men are assembled by a
   human power, that power may set the rule that a majority binds; but natural liberty cannot be
   overruled by any inferior power — "no one man nor a multitude can give away the natural right
   of another"; (d) PROXY: "I ask but one example out of the history of the whole world"; (e)
   TACIT ASSENT proves too much: if silent acceptance counts as election, "every prince that
   comes to a crown, either by succession, conquest, or usurpation, may be said to be elected by
   the people, which inference is too ridiculous". Then the two consequences course.yaml asks
   for, DERIVED here from (c) as the course's own steps (Filmer stated them outright only in a
   later tract — Sources): who counts as "the people" — in Scripture "all the people" means "only
   all the fathers" (II.10); and if natural right cannot be given away by another, a consent
   given by one generation cannot bind infants or those born later, so the people's consent
   would have to be renewed every time a man comes of age — which no state has ever done. Seam:
   Hobbes's covenant (Ref) needs exactly such a moment — every man covenanting with every man —
   and Hobbes himself said the state of nature was "never generally so"; Filmer's demand falls on
   the covenant as hard as on Bellarmine's election.
6. **Then why not let the people at least share in rule?** Obvious attempt: a mixed state —
   king, nobles and people — the arrangement the reader met in Rome (Ref
   `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`) and that Machiavelli made the source of Roman liberty (Ref
   `machiavelli-discourses-republic`). Filmer's reply has two parts. Experience (II.11–15):
   Rome's free centuries had no settled form — consuls, tribunes, decemvirs, dictators in turn —
   and poured "an ocean of blood within Italy"; Rome "began her empire under kings, and did
   perfect it under emperors"; in danger it named a dictator, "a king for a time"; Sulla's
   proscriptions alone killed more citizens than all the bad emperors together; a tyrant has a
   selfish interest in his subjects' lives and goods and fears revenge, while in a crowd no one
   answers for the wrong — "There is no tyranny to be compared to the tyranny of a multitude".
   Logic (II.16): a mixture is "a mere impossibility or contradiction" — give king, nobles and
   people a voice each and "any two of these voices should have power to overrule the third", so
   Lords and Commons could make a law binding the king, "which was never yet seen in any
   kingdom"; a king who admits the people as companions "leaves to be a king". Seam: the
   conclusion is Bodin's and Hobbes's (Ref) — no mixed state — reached from fatherhood instead of
   from the marks of sovereignty or the covenant. And deposition (II.17): a contract cannot be
   held broken "except that first a lawful trial be had by the ordinary judge … or else every man
   may be both party and judge in his own case". The reader should notice that this is the
   charge the next module's author will throw back at absolute kings — keep it to one clause.
7. **Is the king under the law?** Obvious attempt: the coronation oath and the common law bind
   him. Filmer's answer (III): kings came before laws, so laws cannot have made kings; a law is
   "the command of a superior power", and the law no more governs a kingdom than "the carpenter's
   rule builds a house"; "for every custom there was a time when it was no custom", so the common law
   was once the unwritten command of kings; laws were invented to keep "the multitude in order",
   not to bridle kings; the oath binds the king only to the "upright" laws, and he judges which
   those are; Parliament is "the king's court", its liberties "the liberties of grace from the
   king, and not the liberties of nature to the people"; Magna Carta itself begins "we, of our
   mere free will, have granted". Then what DOES bind him — this is where a newcomer misreads
   divine right: the "natural law of a father" to preserve his family; "the safety of the
   kingdom" as his chief law; he rules by the laws "at his good will and for good example"; and
   the subject owes "patient obedience" even to a tyrant — "not so that it is right for kings to
   do injury, but it is right for them to go unpunished by the people if they do it". Seam with
   the reader's Hobbes (Ref): the sovereign "is not Subject to the Civill Lawes" (XXVI) and
   cannot be punished (XVIII) — the same rights; Hobbes derives them from authorisation, Filmer
   from paternity.
8. **THE WEIGHING — "The heir cannot be found, and 'however acquired' is possession"** (verdict
   **established** — see VERDICT). Where the argument is strongest and where it is weakest — the
   OBJECTION at full strength.
   STRONGEST, stated as the course's own and conceded: no one consents to being born into a
   family or a polity; the subjection of children is natural and the first obedience anyone
   learns; and the demand for an instance of a people choosing its first ruler has never been
   met from the record. The best reply available is that "government is every where antecedent
   to records" — states are founded before anyone writes their history — which explains the
   silence without filling it; and the consent theorist who answers him will himself concede
   that the first governments commonly began in the father, adding only that grown sons
   consented to it (forward clause, `locke-property-and-consent`). WEAKEST, in four steps an
   opponent must break, each of them visible in Filmer's own text: (1) that Adam's power over his
   sons was POLITICAL and not a parent's temporary care (and why the father's and not the
   mother's — Hobbes's "the Dominion is in the Mother", Ref, one clause); (2) that it was
   HERITABLE as a whole by one heir; (3) that the heir can be IDENTIFIED — Filmer grants that
   "the knowledge who should be that one man be quite lost" (I.9); and (4) the step that undoes
   the others: I.10 says that whether a king "come to the crown by usurpation, or by election …
   or by any other way whatsoever", his authority is "the only right and natural authority of a
   supreme father". If the usurper's power is a father's, the theory can no longer tell the
   rightful king from whoever holds the crown — the very distinction the royalist needed against
   rebels. State the verdict as the course's own: the inheritance step fails, established, by
   Filmer's own I.9 and I.10 — the strongest defence (the heads of families confer, God
   substitutes) is "however acquired" restated; nothing could overturn it, because the heir
   cannot be identified. The strongest point (birth, not consent) stands. Name the two replies
   the course will take up later: the next module's first author
   makes exactly these four points (Second Treatise §1) and dares his reader to "make Sir Robert
   intelligible … where he treats of usurpation"; a Scottish sceptic will say God authorises "a
   Borgia or an Angria" as much as a Trajan (`hume-against-the-original-contract`). The lesson
   ends by marking, on a map of the argument (Figure 7), which premise each later reply breaks.

`<Conclusion>`: states the verdict in plain words with its grade — Filmer's case fails,
established, at the heir: no living king can be shown to be Adam's heir, and "however acquired"
makes any possessor a father; what stands is his demand that a consent theory show when anyone
consented to the polity he was born into. Then the hand-off: in June 1642 the king's own
advisers defended the very mixture Filmer called a contradiction, and the next lesson reads the
war against all three theories of sovereignty (`english-revolution-as-test`).

Headings as claims: "Attack the premise, not the conclusion"; "Creation made the first man a
king: Bellarmine's own concession"; "From Adam to Babel to the next heir"; "A kingdom is a
family, larger"; "Where did a people ever meet to choose a king?"; "No mixture, no deposition:
the tyranny of a multitude"; "The king above the laws, and the law of a father above the king";
"Strongest at birth, fatal at the heir: 'however acquired' is possession".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms and names to anchor at first use (L11), at most three per section:** "school
  divinity" (the theology of the medieval universities — Ref `aquinas-law-and-kingship`);
  Jesuits (a Catholic order founded 1540, the main controversialists against Protestant kings);
  "the Geneva discipline" (Calvin's church order — Calvinism); Robert Bellarmine (Italian Jesuit
  cardinal, d. 1621); Francisco Suárez (Spanish Jesuit, d. 1617, who wrote against James I's oath
  of allegiance); George Buchanan (Scottish humanist, tutor of James VI, whose De Jure Regni apud
  Scotos, 1579, defended the deposition of Mary Queen of Scots); Robert Parsons (English Jesuit
  who wrote under the name "Doleman", 1594); patria potestas (the Roman father's legal power,
  including life and death); escheat (the return of land to the lord when no heir exists —
  Filmer's metaphor for a crown without a known heir); Babel (Genesis 11 — the tower and the
  confusion of languages); Nimrod ("a mighty hunter before the Lord", Genesis 10); Tarpeian Rock
  (the cliff in Rome from which traitors were thrown); Sulla (Roman general and dictator, 82–79
  BC, whose proscription lists licensed the killing of his enemies); Codrus (legendary last king
  of Athens); Bracton (thirteenth-century English judge and legal writer); Ulpian (Roman jurist,
  d. 228); Chancery (the king's court of equity); the coronation oath; "Le roy le veult" (the
  Norman-French formula of royal assent to a bill, still used); the Petition of Right (1628);
  the Exclusion Crisis (1679–81, one clause). Do NOT introduce Laslett's dating debate, the
  manuscript tradition, or Filmer's other tracts in the body.
- **Do not re-teach** Hobbes's covenant and rights, Bodin's marks, Aquinas's tyranny chapter,
  Aristotle's household or Polybius's cycle — each a `<Ref>` plus a clause (L0e). Locke's and
  Hume's replies are FORWARD clauses only; this lesson must not teach consent theory (course.yaml
  notes: "natural liberty and popular consent appear here only as the thesis Filmer attacks").
- **The real misreadings (L13) — one slot, one Remark:** (a) SLOT: "divine right means the king
  may rightly do anything." Refute from III.2 ("not so that it is right for kings to do injury,
  but it is right for them to go unpunished by the people"), III.1 and III.6 (the natural law of
  a father; the safety of the kingdom), and III.3 ("We must obey where the commandment of God is
  not hindered"); the doctrine is about who may PUNISH a king, not about what is right for him to
  do. (b) REMARK, not a slot: "Filmer is a straw man no one believed, kept alive only as Locke's
  target." The evidence against it is in Locke's own Preface — "the pulpit, of late years,
  publicly owned his doctrine, and made it the current divinity of the times" — and in the fact
  that a refutation had to be written at all. State it once.
- **Filmer and Hobbes are not one position:** they agree on the sovereign's rights and disagree
  on everything underneath (equality, the state of nature, covenant, the origin of paternal
  dominion). Do not let the body call Filmer "Hobbes with Adam" or Hobbes "Filmer without God".
- **Patriarchal ≠ Aristotelian:** Filmer rejects natural slavery as a criterion (II.1) — the
  hierarchy is of generation and inheritance. A writer who presents Filmer as "natural hierarchy
  restated" has misread him (Exercise 7 makes the reader find this).
- **Mothers and wives:** Filmer's theory gives dominion to the father alone and does not argue
  for it; Hobbes (XX) and Locke (§52) both raise the mother. Report this as the gap in the
  argument it is (step (1) of link 8), in one clause, without commentary in the course's voice.
- **Dates (L12):** the keepers are 1680 (printed), the 1620s–30s (written), 1653 (death), and —
  as a forward clause — 1689/90. Say "written before the civil war" rather than any exact year:
  modern editors disagree, and the text's own clues (James I "his late Majesty"; the 1628
  Petition of Right speech) only bound it from below. Do NOT call Filmer a baronet even though
  the 1680 title page says "Bart." — he was knighted (1619); verify against a reference work
  before giving any biographical detail beyond Kent, royalist, d. 1653. Sulla's numbers, Rome's
  480 years, Adam's 930 years, the 72 nations are Filmer's premises — quote them as his, framed
  in the same sentence, or leave them out.
- **Spelling and translation:** the transcription modernises the 1680 spelling; quote as it
  stands. Filmer's Greek tags are garbled in the transcription (II.1, II.17) — give the English
  only.
- **Dossier thin:** only Patriarcha itself; no Filmer secondary at all (Strauss–Cropsey has no
  chapter; Smith gives one sentence); Bellarmine and Suárez only as Filmer quotes them; the
  First Treatise absent (Second Treatise §1 summarises it); the later tracts (Anarchy, 1648;
  Observations, 1652) absent — so the infants-and-generations argument (course.yaml key idea 3)
  is grounded here only as a derivation from II.6's principle, which the brief marks as the
  course's own step. The biography rests on the title page and the text's internal dates.
- **Register:** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right. (Locke's "glib nonsense" may
  appear only as Locke's words, attributed, in a forward clause — never as the course's
  summary of Filmer.)

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (the only mechanical one):** place in order and attach each to
   its author's foundation of sovereignty — Bodin's Six Books (1576), Bellarmine's doctrine of
   power in the multitude (1580s–90s), Patriarcha written (before the civil war), Leviathan
   (1651), Patriarcha printed (1680) — and for each say in one line whether it holds that men are
   born free (`bodin-sovereignty`, `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` recalled).
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill) — Patriarcha I.1, the paragraph on the "common
   opinion":** author (a Kentish royalist gentleman), date (written before 1642, unpublished),
   audience (a manuscript circle — and, after 1680, every reader of a Tory publisher in a
   succession crisis), purpose (to remove the premise of the resistance writers); what it
   RECORDS (that by Filmer's time the doctrine of natural liberty was held by Jesuits and
   Calvinists alike and granted even by royalists), what it ARGUES, what it OMITS (any royalist
   who had already denied the premise); then the key question — how does the 1680 printing change
   what the same text can be USED to establish (about the 1630s vs about the Tory argument of
   1680)?
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** the escheat case (I.9) — the royal line dies out and no one knows
   who Adam's heir is. Follow Filmer's own rule: power goes to "the princes and independent heads
   of families", who may "consent" to confer it on one man, who then holds it "not … as a
   donative from the people, but as being substituted properly by God". Decide whether this is
   an election under another name, and name exactly what distinguishes it from Bellarmine's
   multitude (who votes: fathers, not all; what they give: a testimony of God's choice, not a
   grant). The solution shows the theory survives the case only by restricting "the people" to
   heads of families — the point II.10 makes about "all the people".
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "On Filmer's theory a king may rightly do whatever he pleases with
   his subjects." Name the steps the claim drops — the natural law of a father and the safety of
   the kingdom as the king's chief law (III.1, III.6), "not so that it is right for kings to do
   injury, but it is right for them to go unpunished by the people" (III.2), obedience only
   "where the commandment of God is not hindered" (III.3) — and state what the claim gets right
   (no subject may punish, resist or judge him; II.17, III.7).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** start from II.6 — "no one man nor a multitude can
   give away the natural right of another" — and derive, in three steps, what it implies for a
   consent given by the founders of a commonwealth and a child born forty years later; then say
   what a consent theorist must add to escape the conclusion (a consent each person gives on
   coming of age, express or tacit). The solution notes that Filmer drew this conclusion himself
   in a tract of 1648 (not read here) and that the consent theorist's answer — tacit consent by
   inheriting land and living under the law — is tested in `hume-against-the-original-contract`
   (Ref, one clause).
6. **Corroboration (E3 from `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`):** Leviathan XX ("not so derived from
   the Generation … but from the Childs Consent"; "If there be no Contract, the Dominion is in
   the Mother"; "a great Family … is … a little Monarchy") and Patriarcha I.4 and II.4 (the
   subjection of children "the fountain of all regal authority"; "I know no means by the law of
   nature" by which sons become free). Set two short excerpts side by side, state what the two
   AGREE on (a family is a little monarchy; the sovereign's rights) and the single premise that
   divides them (whether generation by itself gives dominion), and judge which account can
   explain an orphan raised by strangers (Hobbes: dominion goes to whoever preserves the child)
   and which cannot without adding something.
7. **Synthesis / counter-argument (E3 from `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery` and
   `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`):** "Filmer only restates the ancients: Aristotle's natural
   hierarchy and Polybius's first kings." Name two facts the claim fails to account for — Filmer
   rejects Aristotle's natural-slavery criterion ("if a man prove both wise and strong, what will
   Aristotle have done with him?") and calls Polybius's herd origin "the ignorance of the
   Creation"; his only ancient support is Aristotle's "the eldest in every house is king" and
   Plato's large family — and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that
   rule by birth order and rule by superior nature are one principle).
8. **Attack the verdict.** The lesson concludes, established, that Filmer's case fails because
   the heir cannot be identified and "however acquired" collapses into possession. State the
   strongest objection in its best form — a royalist could drop Adam's genealogy and keep the
   structure: any settled succession is "reputed" the heir's by God's providence, so the theory
   needs only a known line, not a known Adam — and say what evidence or argument would be
   needed to overturn the verdict (a criterion, stated in advance, that tells a "reputed" heir
   from a successful usurper without appeal to consent or possession). The solution shows that
   every candidate criterion is either consent (Bellarmine's premise) or prescription
   (possession lasting long enough), so the verdict stands.
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `locke-property-and-consent`:** take I.9 ("an heir there always is … although
   the knowledge who should be that one man be quite lost") and I.10 (every authority, "whether
   he come to the crown by usurpation, or by election … or by any other way whatsoever", is "the
   only right and natural authority of a supreme father"). Show that, once the heir is unknown,
   I.10 alone decides who rules — and derive what it then implies about a successful rebel who
   seizes the crown. Then state what a philosopher who rejects P1 (Adam's dominion) must supply
   instead if he is to tell a lawful ruler from a usurper. The solution says in one sentence that
   the next module's first author makes exactly this objection — challenging his readers to make
   Filmer "intelligible … where he treats of usurpation" — and puts consent in the place of
   fatherhood, with a `<Ref>`.

# Research brief: geography-or-institutions (Geography or institutions: Diamond and Acemoglu–Robinson on the gap between nations)

status: planned
module: heredity-and-civilization
type: core
goal: Analyze Diamond's geographical explanation and Acemoglu and Robinson's institutional explanation of the inequality between nations, and evaluate each against the other and against the hereditarian and cultural accounts

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From the module so far: the hereditarian account of the gap between nations
  (`hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`: national IQ correlated with GDP, IQ → wealth and wealth → IQ,
  and Lynn and Vanhanen's own concession that markets and democracy are a "necessary second factor");
  ancient DNA's record of mixture and migration (`reich-ancient-dna-and-the-indo-europeans`); and the
  objections, ending on the open problem that within-population genetics does not, on present
  evidence, settle gaps between populations (`gould-and-harden-on-heredity`, whose forward exercise
  already met Nogales). From `why-the-west`: culture as cause — Weber's Protestant ethic
  (`weber-only-in-the-west`), Landes's fragmentation and "culture makes all the difference", including
  Zheng He's fleets (`landes-european-exceptionalism`), Henrich's Church and kinship
  (`henrich-church-and-kinship`), Pomeranz's coal and colonies (`pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis`).
  From the civilizations module: Toynbee's refusal of race and of environment as causes and his
  "challenge and response" (`toynbee-challenge-and-response`). Older anchors: Aristotle's climate
  argument for the Greeks' position between cold Europe and Asia (Politics VII.7; Ref
  `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`); the English settlement of 1688 and the Revolution
  before it (`english-revolution-as-test`); Michels's iron law of oligarchy (`michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`),
  which Acemoglu and Robinson borrow; the Gobineau–Grant racial answer (`gobineau-inequality-of-races`,
  `chamberlain-and-grant`) as the answer Diamond's book is written against.
- **Contributes:** The one move: two explanations that leave genes out divide the problem between them
  — one explains why Eurasians held the guns, germs and steel in 1500 (environment working over ten
  thousand years), the other why neighbours with the same environment and ancestry are rich and poor
  today (the rules a society lives under) — and each attacks the other at its strongest point. The
  lesson supplies the distinction that makes the attack intelligible (proximate and ultimate causes)
  and then weighs all four explanations the module and the previous one have built — genes, culture,
  geography, institutions — on the same cases, showing which cases discriminate between which
  explanations and which discriminate between none.
- **Hands off:** Each explanation, when it moves from cases to the whole world, rests on one
  cross-country table: the hereditarian on national IQ estimates, the institutional on the death
  rates of European settlers used to predict which colonies got which institutions. Whether those
  tables can carry the weight is the module's application, `national-data-audited`. The colonial
  origins of extractive institutions return in the empire module (`colonial-balance-sheet`,
  `auditing-the-case-for-colonialism`).
- **Spine step:** "Heredity as an explanation of the gaps between peoples, at full strength (The Bell Curve, national IQ, ancient DNA), met by its objections (Gould, Harden) and by the rival explanations from geography and institutions — and the key datasets audited"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "Why did Spanish soldiers capture the Inca emperor at Cajamarca in
1532, and not Inca soldiers the king of Spain? And why is one half of the town of Nogales, on the
Arizona side of a fence, three times richer than the other half in Mexico, with the same people,
climate and food? Two explanations of the inequality between nations take one question each. The
lesson follows both, and the attack each makes on the other."

Anchors (L11): Jared Diamond (b. 1937), physiologist and ecologist who worked in New Guinea from the
1960s; Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997), with an afterword of 2017 answering the institutionalists. Daron
Acemoglu (b. 1967) and James A. Robinson (b. 1960), economist and political scientist; Why Nations
Fail (2012). Cajamarca: a town in the northern Andes of Peru (locator map). Nogales: a town on the
Arizona–Sonora border, split when the United States bought the strip in 1853 (the Gadsden Purchase).

1. **Four candidate causes, two depths.** The newcomer's problem: the course has now met four
   answers to "why are some peoples richer and stronger?" — genes (this module), culture (Weber,
   Landes, Henrich; Ref), geography (Aristotle's climate, VII.7, quoted in one line; Montesquieu in a
   clause), institutions (the rules — property, law, who governs — a society lives under). They
   cannot be weighed until it is clear that a cause can be near or deep. First attempt: list the
   advantages of the winners. Why it fails: listing the means of victory (guns, germs) leaves the
   question why one side had them. Answer, derived at Cajamarca (link 2): proximate vs ultimate causes
   (concept introduced) — the proximate cause is the immediate mechanism, the ultimate cause what put
   the mechanism on one side. Seam: the distinction is Diamond's tool, and the institutionalists will
   say he applies it only in one direction.
2. **Cajamarca: the proximate causes.** The scene (eyewitness sentence; Diamond ch. 3): 168
   Spaniards, an army some 500 times larger, the emperor captured, no Spaniard killed. First attempts:
   an accident of one afternoon; or the superior nature of the Spaniards — the answer of Gobineau and
   Grant (Ref). Diamond against accident: outcomes across five centuries and continents were not close
   ("51/49"), so they need causes as general as they are. The proximate list, each with its mechanism:
   steel and horses; smallpox, which had already killed the emperor's father and heir and started the
   civil war Atahuallpa had just won; ships; a centralized state that fell with its head; writing,
   which gave Pizarro accounts of earlier conquests and gave Atahuallpa none. Seam: every item on the
   list is something Eurasians had and Americans lacked; the question is why.
3. **The ultimate causes: food production and the axes.** Diamond's chain, built link by link
   (FigureSequence): farming yields many more people per acre → stored surplus → full-time soldiers,
   chiefs, scribes, smiths → states, writing, metal; livestock → crowd diseases to which herders became
   resistant. Why did farming start early in western Eurasia? Because the wild material was there:
   large-seeded wild grasses concentrated in the Mediterranean-climate zone; of the big mammals ever
   domesticated, almost all were Eurasian (the "Anna Karenina" condition: an animal must pass every
   test — diet, growth, breeding in captivity, temper, herd structure — to be domesticable). Why did
   it spread? Eurasia's long axis runs east–west, so a crop moved along one latitude with the same day
   length and seasons; the Americas and Africa run north–south, and crops and animals stalled at each
   change of climate. The thesis sentence quoted (l.307). Against race (link 2's second attempt):
   Diamond states the strongest racial argument himself — Europeans built an industrial state in
   Australia within a century where Aborigines had lived 40,000 years, "two successive experiments" —
   and answers that the colonists imported everything (crops, animals, metal, writing, germs) from
   Eurasia; the experiment tests imports, not peoples.
4. **The limit inside Eurasia: why Europe and not China?** Diamond's answer moves from soil to
   politics: China's smooth coastline and river cores allowed early unification, so one decision of
   one court ended the treasure fleets in 1433; Europe's peninsulas and mountains kept it divided, so
   Columbus could try prince after prince (Ref `landes-european-exceptionalism` for the fragmentation
   thesis — recall, do not re-teach). Seam (L10b): from a geographical theory one expects causes in
   crops and climate; at its edge the cause is a political structure. That is where the second book
   begins.
5. **Nogales: same people, same place, different rules.** The case: household income about $30,000 on
   the Arizona side and about a third of that on the Sonora side; schooling, health, roads, safety all
   differ; the inhabitants share ancestors, food and music. Hold each explanation fixed: geography (the
   same valley), culture (the same, by their own account), ancestry (the same) — the border remains.
   The two Koreas: one people, one language, one history until 1945, now one of the richest and one of
   the poorest countries (night-light image). Acemoglu and Robinson's answer, defined: inclusive economic
   institutions (secure property for the many, impartial law, open entry) vs extractive ones (designed to
   take from many for few — the Andean labour draft, North Korea); economic institutions are chosen by
   politics, and political institutions are inclusive when power is both centralized (there is a state)
   and pluralistic (no one group holds it). Why do rulers choose poverty? Because growth through new
   firms and technologies ("creative destruction") threatens their power — poor countries are poor not
   by ignorance but because the rules serve those who make them. How do institutions change? Small
   differences drift apart; a critical juncture (concept introduced), such as the Black Death, which
   in western Europe raised wages and dissolved serfdom and in eastern Europe produced a second serfdom
   from the same shock (the labour-dues series, figure). England's turn to inclusive institutions came
   with 1688 (Ref `english-revolution-as-test` for 1642–60; one clause on 1688). Seam: the theory now
   turns on its rivals.
6. **The institutionalists' attack on geography (and culture).** Three arguments at full strength. (a)
   The reversal of fortune: in 1500 the richest parts of the Americas were the tropical Aztec and Inca
   lands, and the poorest the temperate north; today the order is reversed — the land did not change,
   the colonial rules did (extraction where dense populations could be put to forced labour; incentives
   where settlers could not be coerced). (b) Spain and Peru: a Spaniard of 1532 was probably less than
   twice as rich as an Inca subject; today more than six times — the gap opened after the crops, animals
   and technologies were shared, which Diamond's theory says should have closed it. (c) Diamond's
   theory is about continents; most of today's inequality is within them (England vs Moldova; China and
   India, with the richest endowments, hold most of the world's poor). And (d) on the origin of farming
   itself: at Natufian sites hierarchy and settled life came before farming, so institutions came first.
   Against culture (Ref Weber, Henrich): trust and cooperation are mostly outcomes of institutions, not
   causes — Mexicans report less trust than Americans because their courts and police fail them.
7. **Diamond's counter-attack (2017).** At full strength: (a) institutions are "not wrong… just not
   the full answer" — geography acts directly today: Africa's ten temperate countries are richer than
   its thirty-eight tropical ones, and landlocked countries poorer than coastal ones (the reader can
   check); (b) the institutional theory confuses proximate with ultimate causes — institutions do not
   "drop randomly out of the sky"; the length of a country's history of farming and of the state
   accounts for about half the explained variance in income across countries; (c) 1688: Zambia could
   not have had a glorious revolution in 1688, having no king to overthrow and no parliament, because
   farming reached it about 2,000 years ago and Britain 5,500 years ago (the marriage-therapist
   analogy: the slap is not the cause of the divorce). Seam: each book explains its own question and
   fails on the other's. Neither mentions genes; the hereditarian account has its own attack on both.
8. **The other two explanations on the same ground (genes, culture).** At full strength, briefly,
   each with its evidence and the evidence against:
   - **Hereditarian, against geography:** Lynn and Vanhanen's objections to Diamond (Africa had
     domesticable plants and animals — sorghum, millet, yams, guinea fowl; China and western Eurasia
     were cut off by the Himalayas and the Gobi as surely as by an ocean, so the east–west axis did not
     carry inventions — printing was reinvented, not borrowed); against institutions as the whole
     story: "people, not places" — the reversal of fortune largely tracks who lives in the former
     colonies now (when a country's history is counted by its people's ancestors rather than its
     territory, the reversal mostly disappears; 2010 paper, References) — which leaves open whether what
     the settlers carried was culture, skills, institutions or genes. A hereditarian synthesis also
     makes geography and genes complements: farming, which geography placed, changed what was selected
     for (named in References; no adjudication).
   - **Hereditarian concessions, on the same cases:** Lynn and Vanhanen treat the two Koreas as an
     institutional natural experiment and call high national IQ "necessary, but not sufficient"; their
     own data put China and Japan far below the IQ prediction in 1820 and 1900, which they attribute to
     autocracy. So the hereditarian account, in its authors' own hands, needs an institutional term.
   - **Cultural:** Henrich's arrow runs from kinship and the Church's marriage rules to psychology to
     institutions (Ref) — the opposite of Acemoglu and Robinson's "trust is an outcome"; the human-
     capital objection (2004 paper) — schooling and skills come first, good institutions follow; Landes's
     "culture makes all the difference" (Ref).
   - **The weighing table (the lesson's synthesis; Figure 7).** Cases as rows: Cajamarca 1532; Europe vs
     China 1400–1800; Nogales; the two Koreas; Spain vs Peru, 1532 and today; the reversal of fortune in
     the Americas. Columns: genes, culture, geography, institutions — each cell says what the explanation
     predicts and whether the record fits, fails, or is silent, and who argues it. What the table shows:
     same-population comparisons (Nogales, Korea) rule out geography and ancestry as causes OF THOSE
     GAPS and isolate institutions — but they cannot test a genetic difference between populations,
     because they hold ancestry fixed by construction; Cajamarca is decided at the proximate level and
     contested at the ultimate; Europe vs China is claimed by all four (coastline, sea bans and court
     politics, kinship and culture; and East Asian national IQs at or above Europe's predict the
     opposite of what happened before 1900, so the hereditarian must invoke institutions there); the
     reversal of fortune is claimed by institutions and by "people, not places". No case cleanly tests
     genes between nations. The course passes no verdict.
9. **Close (Conclusion).** The reader can now: distinguish proximate from ultimate causes; state each
   book's mechanism and its attack on the other; and say which case discriminates between which
   explanations. Open: at world scale every explanation rests on one table — national IQ estimates for
   the hereditarian, settler death rates for the institutionalist — and the next lesson takes both
   tables back to their sources.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms (L11c):** proximate/ultimate cause; domestication (selective breeding in captivity, vs
  taming); the Fertile Crescent (arc from the Levant through south-eastern Turkey to Iraq and western
  Iran — locator); crowd diseases; institutions (define plainly: the rules, formal and informal, that
  govern property, exchange and power); inclusive/extractive; pluralism; centralization; creative
  destruction; critical juncture; institutional drift; serfdom and the "second serfdom"; encomienda and
  mita (one clause each: grants of native labour to Spaniards; the Andean rotating labour draft for the
  silver mines); the Gadsden Purchase; the 38th parallel; the Natufians (settled foragers of the Levant,
  c. 12,500–9,500 BC); the Glorious Revolution (1688–89: James II replaced by William and Mary, Parliament
  gains control of taxation — one clause, Ref the English Revolution lesson for the earlier crisis).
- **The two books answer different questions.** Diamond explains the state of the continents in 1500;
  Acemoglu and Robinson explain differences today, especially within continents. Teach the attack each
  makes as an argument about scope as well as about mechanism — and say where each concedes ground
  (Diamond: institutions explain Nogales; Acemoglu and Robinson: Diamond's thesis "is a powerful
  approach to the puzzle on which he focuses").
- **What Nogales can and cannot show.** It isolates institutions by holding ancestry and geography
  fixed; therefore it cannot be evidence against a genetic difference between populations of different
  ancestry. Do not write "Nogales refutes the hereditarian hypothesis"; do not write the reverse either.
  Why Nations Fail does not discuss genetic explanations at all (grep finds no treatment); do not
  attribute to it an argument it does not make.
- **Diamond's own hereditarian-in-reverse claim.** His Prologue argues New Guineans are "probably
  genetically superior" in mental ability, from impression and a selection story. Report it exactly; it
  is a claim of the same form as the ones he rejects and is untested; it is a fair exercise (4), not a
  gotcha.
- **Labels in the sources.** Diamond calls racial explanations "loathsome" and "racist"; Lynn and
  Vanhanen call Diamond's argument "obviously wrong"; Acemoglu and Robinson head their chapter on the rivals "Theories That Don't Work".
  Register rule: The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving
  labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only
  inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Colonialism's double edge.** Acemoglu and Robinson make colonial rule the author of extractive
  institutions in some places and of inclusive ones in settler colonies. Both halves stay; the moral
  ledger is the empire module's (`colonial-balance-sheet`, forward Ref in one clause at most).
- **Numbers:** three keepers (Cajamarca odds, Nogales ratio, Spain/Peru ratio). Diamond's "about half
  of the explained variance" is an estimate from regressions he does not set up in the book; if used,
  say what it means in one sentence (of the part of income differences the model explains, about half
  goes with the length of farming/state history) or leave it out (L0g).
- **Do not re-teach:** fragmentation and Zheng He (Landes), the Protestant ethic (Weber), kinship
  (Henrich), coal and colonies (Pomeranz), national IQ (hereditarian-case), the iron law (Michels).
  Each a Ref and a clause.
- **Dossier thin spots:** the 2001 and 2002 colonial-origins papers, the 2010 population-flows paper,
  the 2004 human-capital paper and the 2012 review are outside the dossier, named above for the writer
  to verify; Why Nations Fail's maps and photo plates are captions only (no images). No sourced images
  in the pool.

# Research brief: hazony-virtue-of-nationalism (Hazony's Virtue of Nationalism: the order of national states against empire)

status: planned
module: after-liberalism
type: core
goal: Analyze Hazony's argument that an order of independent national states is better than any universal order, including liberal internationalism, and evaluate it against the cosmopolitan and the liberal-nationalist alternatives

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** (1) From the lesson just before, `deneen-post-liberalism`: `post-liberalism` and `liberal-anthropology` — Deneen's charge that liberalism's premise of the unencumbered individual dissolves every unchosen bond. Hazony makes the same charge against Locke (ch. IV) but draws a different conclusion: not local practice, but a world order. (2) The backward anchor, Schmitt's `pluriverse-of-states` (`schmitt-concept-of-the-political`): the political presupposes many states; a world state would end politics; whoever fights in the name of "humanity" is claiming a universal concept against an enemy. Hazony's `imperialism-of-universal-doctrine` is the same structure in moral rather than existential terms, and he never cites Schmitt — the lesson can show the convergence and the difference. (3) The nation as the nineteenth century argued it: Renan's `daily-plebiscite` and his refusal of annexation ("Tu m'appartiens, je te prends" — no nation may say this to a province, `renan-what-is-a-nation`); Herder's `volk` and `plurality-of-cultures` (`herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation` — Hazony quotes Herder against multinational states); the racial nation — `teutonic-thesis`, `nordicism` (`chamberlain-and-grant`); and Moeller's `third-reich-idea`, an empire claimed on grounds of race and speech (`moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich`). (4) The contract and its critics: Hobbes's and Locke's `state-of-nature` and `consent` (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`, `locke-property-and-consent`); Hume's `original-contract` refuted from history (`hume-against-the-original-contract`); Bodin's `sovereignty` (`bodin-sovereignty`), a word Hazony avoids. (5) The previous module on the nation: Scruton's `oikophilia` and `pre-political-loyalty`, and Scruton's line between the nation and nationalism (`the-nation-and-its-defenders`); Miller's self-determination as an interest, not a right (`miller-strangers-in-our-midst`); the Federalist's extended republic (`federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`), which Hazony reads as a national state and whose federal principle he says the centre always revokes; the Visegrád refusal of the 2015 quotas (`the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways`). (6) The universal empire the course has already met: Rome and the Church as one order of law for all peoples (`constantine-and-representation`, `augustine-two-cities`), Cicero's single law for all nations (`cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`), and the wars of religion that produced the sovereign state (`bodin-sovereignty`).
- **Contributes:** One move: to shift the question from "how should a state be governed?" to "how should the world be ordered?" — and to answer that the best order known is one of independent national states, because every alternative is either anarchy (tribes and clans with no standing government) or empire (one authority over many nations), and liberal internationalism is an empire with a universal doctrine. The lesson reconstructs the argument from its foundation (mutual loyalty, which scales from family to nation and is not biological kinship), through the nation as tribes united by language or religion and a shared history of common defence, the national state as the midpoint between anarchy and empire, its five virtues, the claims that a federation of nations is an empire and that there is no neutral (civic) state, to the denial of any universal right of national independence and the seven principles of the order. Then the objections at full strength: the record of wars made in the name of nations; the minority inside the national state; the line between nation and empire, which the argument needs and may draw by stipulation (Hitler, the German Reich, Kant); the reply that pooled sovereignty enlarges a middling nation's freedom; and the liberal-nationalist alternative, which accepts the national state and refuses the reclassification of liberal institutions as empire.
- **Hands off:** Hazony's order rests on two principles — a moral minimum binding every government and each nation's freedom to interpret it. He says little about what the moral minimum requires *inside* a nation or who decides it. The next lesson (`vermeule-common-good-constitutionalism`) supplies a full answer: law as an ordinance of reason for the common good, interpreted by the classical legal tradition — and Hazony's own taxonomy (ch. VII) would place a theory that leads with a universal moral law in his "neo-Catholic" camp, whose sympathy for international enforcement he distrusts. The reader carries that tension forward. What this lesson decides and hands on: Hazony's charge that a federation of nations is an empire is split — it fails as a charge of rule without consent or exit, and holds, on balance, as a charge of compulsory jurisdiction under one legal order; whether that makes the EU an "empire" turns on his stipulated definition and stays open as a definitional question. His claim that there is no neutral, purely civic state holds (strong), and the module verdict (`hungarys-fundamental-law`) uses it. De Benoist (`de-benoist-european-new-right`) rejects the nation-state for empire in a federal sense, the position Hazony says cannot exist; Hungary's 2011 constitution (`hungarys-fundamental-law`) is a national state's own statement of the principles, read against Hazony and his critics.
- **Spine step:** "After liberalism: Deneen says it failed by succeeding, Hazony defends the order of national states, Vermeule the common good, de Benoist the right to difference — Hungary's constitution as the case — and the whole argument is assembled"

## VERDICT

Register, module 16, lesson verdict. Two claims are judged; grades are fixed.

- **Claim (a): a federation of nations, the EU above all, is an empire.** Hazony's test is his own definition — imperialism is "any project to bring many nations under one authority and one doctrine" — together with the "who decides?" cascade (ch. XV: compulsory jurisdiction over independent states "simply is" rule over them). The verdict is split, as the evidence is:
  - *As rule without consent or exit*, the charge **fails — strong.** Members joined by treaty and may leave: Article 50 TEU was invoked by the UK (notification 29 March 2017) and used (exit 31 January 2020); and a member state's own constitution can authorise the Union's competences — Hungary's Fundamental Law, Article E, does exactly that.
  - *As compulsory jurisdiction under one legal order over member nations*, Hazony's actual criterion, the charge **holds — on balance.** The primacy of EU law over national law, the Court of Justice's compulsory jurisdiction, infringement proceedings, and the rule-of-law conditionality mechanism under which the Council withheld funds from Hungary in December 2022 [secondary: the amount, commonly reported as about €6.3 billion, is not used as decisive evidence]. Why only on balance: the Union's jurisdiction is conferred and bounded by treaty, members can and did exit, and a national court (Germany's, 2009) asserts a reserved constitutional identity — the cascade's "officials decide" branch is real but not total.
  - *Whether that makes the EU an "empire"* depends on Hazony's stipulated definition. That is a question of definition, not of evidence, and the lesson leaves it open as such: what would settle it is agreement on whether "empire" requires rule without exit, which no record can supply.
- **Claim (b): there is no neutral, purely civic state.** **Holds — strong.** Every state chooses an official language, public holidays, a public history and naturalisation terms; the liberal-nationalist alternative (Miller, Scruton) concedes this, and constitutional patriotism is itself taught inside a national tradition (ch. XVI). The module verdict (`hungarys-fundamental-law`) and the course verdict use this finding.
- **Strongest objection to the verdict.** From the liberal side: the EU is a treaty organisation of consenting democracies, and calling its court "rule" erases the difference between delegated, revocable authority and conquest — so the on-balance half concedes too much. Answer: the verdict keeps that difference — it is exactly why the first half fails strong. The second half concedes only what the record shows: within membership, the Union's court binds national governments and parliaments, and the Council can withhold funds; that is compulsory jurisdiction in Hazony's sense, whatever name it is given. From Hazony's side: exit is a fiction, since leaving is costly, so the first half fails. Answer: the UK left, by the article's procedure, within four years; costly exit is still exit, and empires do not provide for it.
- **What would overturn it:** for (a), first half — a member prevented from leaving, or bound to a competence it never ratified; for (a), second half — EU jurisdiction that member states could in practice disregard without legal consequence; for (b) — a state with no official language, calendar, public history or naturalisation terms.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: after 1945 nationalism was held responsible for two world wars, and the European Union, international courts and global markets were built to tame it; an Israeli political philosopher argues in 2018 that this gets history backwards — the wars were made by empires, and the liberal order that replaced them is one more empire, with a universal doctrine it imposes on peoples who never chose it. The newcomer meets it as a reversal of something they took for settled; the lesson's task is to see what the reversal rests on and whether it holds.

1. **Which question is being answered?** The obvious frame: political philosophy asks who should rule within a state — the question the course began with. Hazony: that question presupposes a prior one — how the world is divided into states at all (ch. VIII: philosophy of government vs philosophy of political order). Three answers are possible: anarchy (clans and tribes with no standing government), empire (one authority over many peoples), or an order of national states. Because: a theory of good government inside a state is silent about whether that state should exist, and the post-1945 order answered the silent question without arguing it. Seam: from `deneen-post-liberalism` the reader has a critique of liberalism from inside a society; Hazony's is from outside — about the order *between* societies.
2. **What holds any political body together?** Attempt: consent — individuals agree and form a state (`<Ref locke-property-and-consent>`). Hazony: the stork story (ch. X) — no state was ever born that way; free states are formed when tribes already bound by loyalty unite against a common danger, despotic ones by conquest. The reader has heard this from Hume (`<Ref hume-against-the-original-contract>`: governments founded "on usurpation or conquest, or both"). What actually binds is **mutual loyalty**: the self extends to include others, so their suffering is one's own; it scales from family to clan to tribe to nation "in much the way that the force of gravitation" gathers matter, and it is not biological kinship — families adopt, nations adopt whole tribes (ch. IX). Because: a body whose members would not suffer for one another cannot defend itself or keep its laws without fear or bribery. The seam must say so: from a nationalist one expects blood; the material denies it in so many words.
3. **What is a nation, then?** Hazony's definition (chs. I, XIII): a number of tribes with a shared heritage — usually a common language or religion — and a history of joining against common enemies; a national state is such a nation under one standing government independent of all others. Set beside the reader's earlier answers in one paragraph: not Renan's daily consent (`<Ref renan-what-is-a-nation>`), since loyalty is formed in childhood and is not chosen; not the racial nation (`<Ref chamberlain-and-grant>`), since outsiders join — Ruth, Jethro; nearest to Herder's `volk` (`<Ref herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation>`) but with the stress on shared defence rather than language. Because: the nation is the largest body in which mutual loyalty still holds among people who will never meet.
4. **Why the national state and not empire?** Here is the core argument (ch. XII–XIV). Empire's case is put first and fairly, as Hazony puts it: the ancient empires really did bring peace and an end to famine ("None hungered in my years"). Against it, five virtues of the national state: violence pushed to borders; disdain for conquest, because its rulers' loyalty is to their own nation, which foreign rule would weaken; collective freedom — and with it, through the balance of power, the freedom of others; competition among nations as a set of experiments (Mill's "plurality of paths"); and individual liberty, which needs the trust that only a cohesive nation provides (Mill on Austria-Hungary). The great wars of devastation, Hazony says, were wars of universal doctrine — the Thirty Years' War, Napoleon's, Hitler's. Because: a power with no natural limit has every reason to keep conquering, and a power whose loyalty is to one nation has a reason to stop.
5. **Why liberal internationalism counts as empire.** Attempt: a federation of free nations, with a court for their disputes, is not an empire — it is how Kant and Wilson hoped to end war. Hazony's "who decides?" cascade (ch. XV): when a state complains against another, who decides whether the complaint is heard, who is right, what the remedy is, and who enforces it? If the federation's officials decide, they rule the nations; if the nations decide, it is voluntary arbitration and no federation at all. A mandate limited to "war and peace" is as wide as the grievances states can have. Because: compulsory jurisdiction over independent states simply is rule over them. Add ch. XVI: the civic or neutral state is also a myth, because loyalty to a constitution is taught inside a national tradition — every free state is national, with a majority nation secure enough to grant minorities their rights. Here the backward anchor enters: Schmitt said the same thing from the other side — the political is a pluriverse, and a world order that claims "humanity" claims a universal concept against an enemy (`<Ref schmitt-concept-of-the-political>`, `pluriverse-of-states`). The lesson marks the difference: Schmitt's plurality rests on enmity, Hazony's on loyalty, and Hazony wants peace among nations where Schmitt wants only the possibility of war kept real. This is where the concept `imperialism-of-universal-doctrine` is introduced, as the answer to the question.
6. **Does every nation have a right to its own state?** Attempt: if national freedom is the good, every people that asks for it must get it (Wilson). Hazony: no (ch. XVII). A good is not a right; there is no lower limit to what can call itself a nation; minting states is like a foolish king minting money; Versailles' breaking up of Austria-Hungary invited Germany east. Independence belongs to nations cohesive and strong enough to hold it — the first of his seven principles (ch. XVIII), followed by non-interference, a monopoly of force, a balance of power, parsimony in making states, protection of minority nations and tribes, and no transfer of powers to universal institutions. Because: an order of national states needs fewer, stronger nations, not as many as can be named. The reader should see the agreement with Miller here (`<Ref miller-strangers-in-our-midst>`: self-determination an interest, not a right).
7. **The first OBJECTION: the record of nationalist wars and the minority.** At full strength, from the cosmopolitan side: nations have made wars of conquest and expulsion — the Balkan wars, the annexation of Alsace-Lorraine in 1871 that provoked Renan's lecture, the partitions and population exchanges of the twentieth century; Hazony himself concedes that "Serbian nationalism" was the immediate cause of the First World War. And the minority inside a national state depends on the "plain and unquestioned" majority's restraint, which is exactly what minorities have had reason to doubt. Hazony's replies, given as his: the decisive wars were ideological and imperial; nationalists have done injustice, but empire is worse (Conclusion); his principles (3) and (6) bind the national state to protect all its tribes, on pain of collapsing into anarchy. What the lesson states about the reply: on the minority, Hazony answers with a principle, not a mechanism — principles (3) and (6) bind the majority but name no one who can hold it to them, so the objection's point (that minorities depend on the majority's restraint) is conceded rather than answered; on the First World War, his own Serbian admission stands unanswered by his re-reading. The general comparison — whether national states or empires made more wars of conquest — is not graded in the course: it would need a count of wars of conquest classified by type of initiator, with the classification fixed before the count, which is exactly the line link 8 tests.
8. **The second OBJECTION: where is the line between nation and empire?** This is the hardest one and the lesson spends its longest section on it. Hazony's argument works only if one can tell a national state from an empire independently of whether one approves of it. Test it on three cases the reader has met. (a) Hitler: Hazony classifies the Third Reich as imperial (its model the Holy Roman Empire; "lord of the earth"). But the regime's own vocabulary was the *Volk* and its race (`<Ref chamberlain-and-grant>`, `nordicism`), and Moeller's *Third Empire*, which gave the regime its name (`<Ref moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich>`), claimed lands "which belonged to us by race and speech" — empire justified on national grounds. If every nation that expands is reclassified as an empire, the claim that national states disdain conquest cannot be tested. (b) Germany 1871–1945: Hazony writes that the German-speaking peoples "have never been constituted as a national state"; the reader can name the state that existed from 1871 and decide which side of the line it falls. (c) Kant: Hazony reads *Perpetual Peace* as a call for a world state; Kant's own text, in the sentences after Hazony's quotation, settles for a federation because the nations will not accept a world republic, and elsewhere rejects universal monarchy as "soulless despotism" — set piece 6. Hazony's reply, at full strength: the federation Kant settles for is either toothless or an empire (his ch. XV), and a Kant scholar states the same dilemma. What the lesson states: in case (a) the classification is drawn after the conduct, so as stated the virtue "national states disdain conquest" cannot fail; in case (c) Hazony's quotation omits Kant's turn away from the world state, though his ch. XV dilemma survives the correction. Because: a classification that decides the outcome of the argument must be drawn before the argument; where Hazony's is not, the claim it protects is not tested by the record he cites. This is why the VERDICT judges the EU charge by his criterion (compulsory jurisdiction) and leaves only the name "empire" open.
9. **The third OBJECTION, and where the liberal nationalist stands.** Two replies from inside the West's own institutions, each at full strength. (a) Pooled sovereignty: a middling nation inside a union gains freedom of manoeuvre against larger powers that it would lack alone (the course's own statement; Fawcett in References) — which is Hazony's balance-of-power virtue turned against his principle (7). (b) The liberal nationalist (Miller, `<Ref miller-strangers-in-our-midst>`) accepts the national state, the bounded people and the absence of a universal right to independence — and denies that liberal institutions are an empire: a nation may adopt liberal principles as part of its own inheritance, which Hazony himself half-concedes (Introduction l. 280: British and American concepts of individual liberty "are themselves the cultural inheritance of certain tribes and nations"; ch. VI l. 553: British and American liberals "tempered" by national tradition). The nation understood as ethnos or as demos (Fawcett's distinction) is the fork. And Scruton (`<Ref the-nation-and-its-defenders>`) defends the nation while keeping "nationalism" as the name of its vice; Hazony takes the name for the virtue. The liberal nationalist's position is the one the VERDICT's claim (b) and Miller's verdict (`<Ref miller-strangers-in-our-midst>`, on balance) leave standing beside Hazony: a bounded, non-neutral nation that holds liberal institutions as its own inheritance.
10. **The weighing: "A union of nations is not rule without consent, but it is compulsory jurisdiction — and no state is neutral."** (The last body section before the Conclusion; L10e.) Take Hazony's charge on his own criterion, not a substitute one. First the half that fails: rule without consent or exit — members joined by treaty; Article 50 used by the UK (notified 29 March 2017, out 31 January 2020); Hungary's own Article E authorises the Union's competences. **Strong.** Then the half that holds: run the "who decides?" cascade (set piece 4) on the Court of Justice — primacy, compulsory jurisdiction, infringement proceedings, the conditionality mechanism applied to Hungary in December 2022 — and show that at each step the Union's organs, not the nations, decide. **On balance**, because the jurisdiction is conferred, bounded and exitable. Then say plainly what is not decided: whether this is "empire" is Hazony's definition, and the record cannot settle a definition. Then claim (b): every state chooses a language, a calendar, a public history and terms of naturalisation, and Miller and Scruton concede it — **strong**. Then the strongest objection to the verdict (VERDICT) and the answer. Close: what Hazony has shown, what he has not, and what is left — his first principle, the moral minimum each nation interprets for itself, leaves open what a nation's law owes its members, which the next lesson takes up.

**Conclusion** (L10): in plain words — Hazony is wrong that the Union rules nations without their consent (strong): they joined by treaty and one has left. He is right, on balance, that membership means submitting to a court and a legal order the nations do not control, which is his test of empire; whether to call that an empire is a choice of words the course leaves to definition. And he is right that no state is neutral (strong). Hand-off: if every state must teach a nation and a law, what does that law owe the people under it? Vermeule's answer is the next lesson.

Headings fall out as claims: "Before 'who rules?': how is the world divided?"; "The stork and the tribe: states are not born by contract"; "A nation is tribes that have fought together"; "Why a national state stops conquering"; "Who decides? Hazony's case that a federation of nations is an empire" (his claim, stated as his); "No right to a state for every nation"; "The minority depends on a principle with no enforcer"; "A line drawn after the conduct cannot be tested"; "Pooled sovereignty and the liberal nation"; "A union of nations is not rule without consent, but it is compulsory jurisdiction — and no state is neutral".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **Two visions and the imperial promise** (ch. I l. 339–343). Open with the two visions in Hazony's words (short quotation), then the imperial case at full strength: Amenemhet I's "None hungered in my years… Men dwelled in peace through that which I wrought" and Hammurabi's "four quarters of the world" — the empire's offer of peace and bread is real. Only then the national alternative. The reader should feel why empire was attractive before hearing why Hazony rejects it.
2. **The stork** (ch. X l. 756–780). The nursery story and its point in three sentences; then the two births of the state (a coalition of tribes; conquest) and Hume's "usurpation or conquest, or both" beside it. Short.
3. **Mutual loyalty and gravitation** (ch. IX l. 701–715). The quarrelling siblings who close ranks at the first hardship; the extended self; the gravitation analogy; and the sentence denying that this is biological kinship ("But this has never been so"), with the adoption of families and tribes. One quotation; the rest paraphrased.
4. **The "who decides?" cascade** (ch. XV l. 1089–1100 ff.). The lesson's worked example. A state complains that its neighbour has violated a treaty. Four questions in order — who decides whether the complaint is heard? who decides who is right? who decides the remedy? who enforces it? — and at each, two branches: the federation's officials or the nations themselves. Every "officials" branch ends in rule over the nations; every "nations" branch ends in voluntary arbitration. Draw as a decision tree (FIGURE PLAN 4) and then test it on a real body the reader can name (the European Court of Justice, whose rulings bind member states).
5. **Hitler as imperialist** (ch. V l. 498–500, with Notes Part One n. 50–52). Give Hazony's evidence as he gives it: the "First Reich" model; Frederick III's motto *Austriae est imperare orbi universo* ("Austria is destined to rule all the world"); Wilhelm II's 1915 order of the day; "lord of the earth"; the extermination as a policy that reached Shanghai. Then the counter-evidence (EXPLANATION PLAN link 8): the regime's racial *Volk* vocabulary and Moeller's "by race and speech" (l. 5913–5918). Both columns; then the lesson's finding — the classification is drawn after the conduct, so on this case the "disdain for conquest" virtue is protected rather than tested.
6. **Kant quoted and Kant read** (ch. XXI l. 1418–1432; Perpetual Peace, Second Definitive Article; Strauss–Cropsey pp. 607–609). The lesson's sourcing drill. Hazony's two quotations ("savages cling to their lawless freedom"; "an international state, which would necessarily continue to grow until it embraced all the people of the earth"), with his inserted "[national]" visible. Then the sentences that follow in Kant (the world republic replaced, because the nations will not accept it, by a federation that averts war) and Kant's "soulless despotism". Then the reply that rescues Hazony's point: a federation either binds or does not, and Kant's own commentator states the dilemma. The point is method — what a quotation with an ellipsis records, argues and omits.
7. **The seven principles as a checklist** (ch. XVIII l. 1265–1301). Run one case through them: the European Union (principle 7 directly; principle 4, the balance of power; principle 2, non-interference). Then the Dutch-protectorate thought experiment (l. 1301): Hazony would accept the Netherlands becoming a German federal state but not transferring powers to the EU — and the reader should say what distinction he draws (a bounded national state vs an unbounded universal one).
8. **The foolish king minting currency** (ch. XVII l. 1232) set beside Renan's "Tu m'appartiens, je te prends" (l. 105): two principles that can collide — no right to independence for every people, and no right to annex a people against its will. What happens to a minority nation that is not "cohesive and strong enough"? The reader takes this into exercise 4.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first mention** (L11): *nationalism* in Hazony's stipulated sense (a theory of world order, not loyalty to one's own nation — say plainly that this is his definition and that ordinary usage, and Scruton's, differ); *globalism* (Hazony's word for the post-Cold-War project; give it as his); *imperialism* (his sense: any project to bring many nations under one authority and one doctrine — not only colonial empire, which the reader met in the empire module); *Westphalia* (the treaties of 1648 ending the Thirty Years' War; Hazony's reading of them as the founding of the national-state order is contested — his own note says the "Westphalian system" label is later); *Holy Roman Empire* / *First Reich*; *Maastricht* (the 1992 treaty founding the European Union) and *subsidiarity* (its principle that the Union acts only where member states cannot); *pooled sovereignty*; *Vattel* (Emer de Vattel, Swiss jurist, *The Law of Nations*, 1758); *Selden*, *Fortescue* (English jurists Hazony claims as his tradition — one clause each or omit); *Adenauer* (West Germany's first chancellor, 1949–63); *Wilsonian self-determination* (Woodrow Wilson's 1918 principle); *constitutional patriotism* (loyalty to a constitution rather than a nation — a German post-war idea; the author goes in References); *Versailles* (1919). Re-anchor *pluriverse*, *Volk*, *daily plebiscite*, *Third Reich idea* with a clause and the Ref (L11d).
- **The definitions are stipulated, and the lesson says so.** Hazony defines nationalism, empire and nation so that his conclusion follows readily; the reconstruction must show the definitions, flag them as his, and then test them (link 8). Do not let "nationalism" in the lesson's own voice slide between Hazony's sense and the ordinary one.
- **Contested historical claims — present as his, with the evidence against.** That the First World War was an imperial war (his own admission about Serbia stands beside it); that Hitler was not a nationalist; that "the German-speaking peoples… have never been constituted as a national state"; that the EU is "a German empire in all but name" and an American protectorate; that liberalism is imperialism; that Westphalia established national states rather than a settlement among princes. Each is a thesis with its evidence and its counter-evidence. None is presented as settled, and none is dismissed.
- **The Israel chapters (XX–XXIV)** are Hazony's own application and his country's case. One clause at most (he wrote as an Israeli and applies the theory to Israel's legitimacy); the course takes no side and does not reconstruct the Auschwitz paradigms. Hazony's "I" — his grandfather's letter, his Torah memory — is part of the material and may be given as testimony, attributed.
- **Do not re-teach** Schmitt's friend and enemy, Renan's plebiscite, Herder's Volk, Locke's consent, Hume on the contract, Moeller's Third Reich or Deneen's liberal anthropology. Each is a clause and a Ref; the lesson's new work is Hazony's argument and its tests.
- **Schmitt is the backward anchor, not a co-author.** Hazony never cites Schmitt. The convergence (no world state; universal concepts used against an enemy) is the course's observation, and the difference (loyalty vs enmity; peace among nations as the goal) must be stated in the same breath.
- **Kant is not in the dossier.** The writer must obtain *Perpetual Peace* (public domain translation) and check the sentences after Hazony's quotation before set piece 6 is written; if they cannot be checked, use only the Strauss–Cropsey chapter's account and say so.
- **Thin spots.** Hazony "engages objections only briefly" (summary 85 pedagogy): the dossier has no sustained critique of this book. The objections in links 7–9 are assembled from Hazony's own admissions, the course's earlier lessons (Renan, Moeller, Chamberlain and Grant, Miller, Scruton), Strauss–Cropsey on Kant and Fawcett's two passages — say in the body that these are the course's objections, not a named critic's. Hazony's account of how minorities fare in a national state (ch. XVI, XVIII) is short; do not invent a fuller one.
- **Copyright**: a sentence or two per quotation; paraphrase the rest. Renan, Hume, Mill, Kant (in a public-domain translation) and Moeller (check the translation's status in its `.meta.yaml`) may be quoted more fully, but only what the lesson parses.
- **Register.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right. This binds in both directions: the words Hazony applies to his opponents ("imperialist", "utopian", "heresy hunts", "fanaticism of the universal") are his and appear as his; the words applied to Hazony's position by its critics appear only as quotations with speaker and date, and the lesson's own voice uses neither.

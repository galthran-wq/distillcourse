# Research brief: hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state (Hegel's Philosophy of Right: ethical life and the rational state)

status: planned
module: contract-revolution-reaction
type: core
goal: Analyze Hegel's argument that freedom is realised only in family, civil society and state, and his critique of the social contract and of the Revolution's negative freedom

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The whole module. From `locke-property-and-consent`: property by
  labour and consent as the ground of government; from
  `hume-against-the-original-contract`: government founded on opinion, the
  denial that any actual state began in a contract, time turning force into
  title — Hegel will agree that the state's origin settles nothing and deny that
  opinion and utility are its ground; from `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`:
  the general will, total alienation, "forced to be free", and the open difficulty
  (a universal assembled from individual wills); from `burke-reflections`: the
  entailed inheritance, the partnership of generations, prejudice as reason men
  inherit, and the forward question whether a standard "in the process" can judge
  the process; from `maistre-and-bonald`: no constitution is made by deliberation,
  Providence uses men, "there is no such thing as man", and the open problem that
  lesson closed on — is there an account on which the state is rational AND not
  designed? From `herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation` (the prerequisite): the
  `volk` as a community of language, custom and memory prior to any state, the
  `plurality-of-cultures`, Fichte's `language-as-nation` and his national
  education, with the state as the instrument of the nation's eternity — Hegel's
  answer is that "a nation does not begin by being a state" (§349) and becomes
  actual and sovereign only as one; Hegel names Fichte three times (Preface,
  §258 Remark, §273 Remark). From `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`: Plato's
  abolition of property and family for the guardians (Hegel's foil at §46 and
  §185); from `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`: the city prior to
  the individual (Hegel's ancestor at §258) and natural slavery (§57); from
  `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`: indivisible sovereignty (§278–279 inherits and
  corrects it); from `aquinas-law-and-kingship` and
  `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`: `natural-law`, which Hegel absorbs into
  the rational actuality of right.
- **Contributes:** One move: freedom is not the absence of restraint (the
  Revolution's) and not obedience to what is given (the reaction's) but a
  structure a person grows into and knows as his own — family, civil society,
  state — *ethical life*; the state is therefore not a contract among
  individuals who were free before it, and not a mere power that happens to
  exist, but the actuality of freedom, which can be understood after the fact
  though it could not be designed in advance. The lesson's centre is the
  derivation of the three spheres from the failures of the two abstractions
  that precede them (abstract right, morality) and the §258 critique of the
  contract.
- **Hands off:** Two objections stated, one argued later, and one refusal. (a)
  Marx's inversion — civil society, not the state, is the real ground, and the
  state is its committee (`marx-manifesto`, next module; one paragraph here, and
  the rabble of §§241–245 as the seam). (b) The historicism charge, stated as the
  course's own with Strauss in References: if world history is the court of
  judgment, right has no standard outside what wins — argued in
  `strauss-natural-right-and-history`. (c) The refusal comes next: a Spanish
  deputy who visited Berlin in 1849 and was horrified by the Hegelians will deny
  that the age's oppositions can be reconciled at all — Catholicism or
  socialism, the dagger or the sabre — and will ask, of a constitution under
  attack, who decides when its rules run out (`donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship`,
  the next lesson; the FORWARD exercise). The module's application `assignats`
  reinforces `rational-state` by asking whether the Revolution's lasting
  institutions are Hegel's "rational actual" or Maistre's "paper".
  `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township` opens the next module from Hegel's civil
  society and the fear of a formless mass. Popper's reading goes in References
  only.
- **Spine step:** "Government by consent (Locke) is doubted by Hume and
  radicalised by Rousseau's general will; when France tries to frame a government
  from principles, Burke, Maistre and Donoso Cortés answer that no generation
  knows enough, Herder and Fichte put the people before the state, and Hegel
  absorbs both sides into the rational state"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: the Revolution promised freedom and
delivered the Terror; the reaction promised order and delivered a police state.
A professor in Berlin claims both sides misunderstood freedom: it is not the
absence of restraint and not obedience to tradition, but a structure a person
grows into — family, the market, the state — and knows as his own. The lesson
rebuilds the structure in his order and asks what it concedes to each side.

1. **What can a philosophy of politics do — and what can it not?** The reader
   has three books that told a state what it ought to be (Plato's city,
   Rousseau's contract, Maistre's theses) and one that said no book can (Burke).
   Hegel's answer in the Preface: philosophy cannot teach the state what it
   ought to be; it comes too late — "the owl of Minerva takes its flight only
   when the shades of night are gathering"; it "paints its grey in grey". Its
   task is to understand the state that exists as rational: "what is rational is
   real; and what is real is rational". Obvious misreading: whatever exists is
   right. Why it fails: Hegel distinguishes actuality from mere existence — "a
   bad state is one which merely exists … a hand which is cut off still looks
   like a hand" (§270 Addition); the rational is what has realised the concept,
   not what happens to stand. So the claim is Maistre's "no assembly can make
   a constitution" (`<Ref>` maistre-and-bonald) with the opposite valence: the
   order that no one designed can nonetheless be *understood*, and understanding
   it is what freedom in it requires. Heading as claim: *Philosophy comes at
   dusk: it explains the state, it does not draw one*.
2. **What is freedom, if not doing what one likes?** §§4–7, the core of the
   Introduction. Will is thought that gives itself existence; freedom is to will
   "as weight is to body". Three moments: (a) the will can abstract from every
   content — pure negative freedom, "the freedom of the void", which "in
   religion is Hindu fanaticism" and "in politics the fanaticism of
   destruction" — the §5 Addition names the Revolution: it had to destroy every
   institution because any determinate order limits the abstract will; (b) the
   will gives itself a content and so limits itself; (c) true freedom is to be
   "with oneself in the other" — to will a determinate content and know it as
   one's own, as in friendship and love (§7 Addition). Then §15: *caprice*
   (Willkür) — choosing among given contents — is "the ordinary idea of freedom"
   and is contradictory, since the content comes from outside; "the ordinary man
   believes he is free when allowed to act capriciously". Set Rousseau's "forced
   to be free" beside this (`<Ref>`): Hegel takes the thought (freedom is
   obedience to a law one gives oneself) and will say Rousseau located the law
   in the wrong place.
3. **Why begin with property?** The method (§32 Addition): the order of the
   concept is not the order of time; truth appears as result, so the exposition
   starts from the most abstract shape of freedom and shows it insufficient.
   Abstract right: the person — "be a person and respect others as persons"
   (§36). The person is free but empty; he gives his freedom an external
   existence by putting his will into a thing — *property*, justified not by
   need but as "the first embodiment of freedom" (§41, §45). Hence: every
   person must own property, not equal amounts (§49 — "equalised wealth would
   soon be undone"); Plato's guardians, denied property, are denied personality
   (§46 Remark, `<Ref>` kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king); slavery is wrong
   because a person cannot be a thing, yet "free by nature" and "slave by
   nature" are both one-sided — the slave's own will acquiesces, and only the
   state makes freedom actual (§57, `<Ref>`
   aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery). Then contract (§§71–75):
   two wills recognise each other over a thing — and here the first blow at the
   module's whole tradition: marriage and the state are not contracts, because
   a contract is between persons over things they can alienate, and "we are
   already citizens by birth" (§75 Addition). Why abstract right fails on its
   own: wrong is possible (§82), and punishment restores right (§§99–101: the
   criminal is honoured as rational by being punished) — but right as mere
   permission says nothing about the good a will should pursue.
4. **Why is morality — the conscience — not enough either?** The reader has
   Kant through Rousseau's "law one prescribes to oneself" and the Idealist
   gloss (`<Ref>`). Morality (§§105–141) is the standpoint of the subjective
   will that counts only what it recognises as its own; its truth is the right
   of the subject (the pivot between antiquity and modernity, §124). Its
   failure: duty for duty's sake is empty — Kant's test of non-contradiction
   presupposes a content ("where there is nothing there can be no
   contradiction", §135 Remark: the absence of property contradicts nothing);
   so conscience, left to itself, "is on the verge of evil" (§139) — it can make
   anything a duty (§140: the end justifies the means; conviction as criterion;
   irony — "I am beyond this law"). Both abstractions collapse into each other
   and into their truth: *ethical life* — "right and morality depend on ethical
   life like ivy on a tree" (§141 Addition). Mark the seam (L10b): the reader
   expects the inward conscience to be the highest court; Hegel says it is the
   most dangerous.
5. **What is ethical life, and why does duty liberate?** §142: "the Idea of
   freedom as the living good" — freedom that has become "an existing world and
   the nature of self-consciousness". The laws and institutions are substance,
   individuals accidents (§145; Antigone: "no one knows whence the laws come");
   their authority is higher than nature's and yet not alien — "a relation
   closer to identity than even faith or trust" (§147). Duty liberates (§149):
   from natural impulse and from "the indeterminate subjectivity" that never
   reaches existence. Custom (Sitte) is "second nature" (§151); the Pythagorean
   asked how to educate a son answered "make him a citizen of a state with good
   laws" (§153 Remark, the Addition against Rousseau's Emile). This is Burke's
   "through just prejudice his duty becomes a part of his nature" (`<Ref>`
   burke-reflections) made into a system: the difference is that Hegel will
   require the second nature to be *rational* and *known*, not merely inherited.
   Define Sittlichkeit at first use and contrast Moralität (§33).
6. **Family, then civil society: why two spheres before the state?** The triad
   (§157). Family: unity by love — "the most tremendous contradiction"
   (§158 Addition); marriage "a contract to transcend the standpoint of
   contract" (§163 — Bonald's conclusion by Hegel's route, `<Ref>`
   maistre-and-bonald, one clause); the child; the family dissolves as children
   found families. Civil society (§§182–256): the sphere of needs, labour and
   exchange where each is his own end and depends on all others — "the external
   state", "the state based on need"; the Addition to §182: the theorists who
   treat the state as a partnership for security of life and property (Hobbes,
   Locke, `<Ref>`s) have described civil society and mistaken it for the state.
   Its achievements: Bildung, political economy's discovery of law in a mass of
   accidents (§189 Addition, like the laws of the solar system), the three
   estates with free choice of station (against Plato and caste, §§201–208),
   codified public law (§§211–216), the police and the Corporation as "second
   family". Its failure, the seam Marx will pull (§§241–245): wealth and poverty
   grow together; below subsistence a *rabble* forms — "poverty plus
   indignation", a class that feels its exclusion as a wrong done to it by
   another class; direct relief violates self-reliance, work relief aggravates
   overproduction; "despite an excess of wealth civil society is not rich
   enough"; so it is driven outward to colonies (§§246–248). State the point
   plainly: civil society cannot integrate itself; that is why the state is
   needed — and why Hegel says the state is "not so much the result as the
   beginning" (§256), the condition under which family and civil society were
   possible all along.
7. **Why the state is not a contract: §258 against Rousseau.** The standing
   objection, argued here. §257–258: the state is "the actuality of the ethical
   Idea", in which objective freedom (the universal will) and subjective freedom
   (the individual who knows and wills it) are one; the individual's "supreme
   duty is to be a member of the state". The Remark: Rousseau's merit — he made
   *will* the principle of the state, not a herd instinct or divine authority;
   his error — he took will "only in the determinate form of the individual
   will" and the universal as "the general will" that "proceeds out of this
   individual will as out of a conscious will"; the union of individuals thus
   becomes a *contract* resting on their arbitrary consent, and when this
   abstraction gained power, "it produced the most frightful spectacle of
   violence and terror" — because an order built from individual wills can be
   unbuilt by them, and an abstract universal destroys every concrete
   institution that limits it (§5 again). The questions of historical origin
   (patriarchy, fear, trust, Locke's consent, Maistre's Providence) are "no
   concern of the Idea of the state": the state's authority does not derive
   from how it came to be. Then the opposite error, Haller: the state as might
   — "the mightier rules, must rule, and will always rule" — "hatred of law is
   the shibboleth" by which the false friends of the people are known. Hegel's
   position between them: the state is rational, not willed; its rationality
   is shown in its structure, not its origin. Hand the reader the Rousseau
   lesson's located difficulty (`<Ref>`): this is the answer to it — the
   general will cannot be "general" if assembled from individual wills; it must
   already be embodied in institutions the individual finds and recognises.
   What Hegel concedes to Rousseau: the principle of subjective freedom — the
   modern state, unlike the ancient, "lets subjectivity reach self-subsistent
   particularity" and returns it to unity (§260); what he concedes to Burke and
   Maistre: the constitution "cannot be given a priori" (§274, Napoleon's
   constitution for Spain), and "who is to frame the constitution?" is a
   meaningless question (§273) — but he refuses their ground: not inheritance,
   not Providence, but the concept. Note that the Remark adds "as Fichte did
   later": the same error, Hegel says, runs through the philosopher of the
   German nation (`<Ref>` herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation) — one clause. And
   the origin questions Hegel dismisses include Hume's (`<Ref>`
   hume-against-the-original-contract): Hegel agrees with Hume that origin
   settles nothing and disagrees that opinion and interest are the ground.
   Then the step the previous lesson requires (one paragraph, §274 and §349):
   Herder's and Fichte's people come before any state; Hegel grants that "a
   nation does not begin by being a state" and that a constitution must match
   the nation's self-consciousness (§274), and denies that a nation without a
   state is free — its independence is "formal only and is not sovereignty"
   (§349). The Volk is the material; the rational state is its actuality. Mark
   the seam (L10b): from a philosopher of the nation-as-mind one expects the
   nation to be the end; Hegel makes the state the end and the nation its
   bearer.
8. **What does the rational state look like, and what guarantees it?** Briefly
   (§§272–320), as the structure that makes the §258 claim concrete:
   constitutional monarchy as the differentiation of the concept — legislature
   (universal), executive (particular), crown (the "I will" that decides); the
   division of powers as moments of one whole, not mutual checks (§272:
   self-subsistent powers destroy the state or unity is restored by force —
   Bodin's and Hobbes's indivisibility, `<Ref>`, rethought); the monarch who
   "has only to say yes and dot the i" (§280 Addition) and why "the sovereignty
   of the people" against the monarch is "a wild idea" (§279 — the people
   without a constitution is "a formless mass"); the Estates as mediation,
   corporate not atomised representation (§§302–311: mass suffrage breeds
   indifference and caucus rule); public opinion deserving respect and contempt
   at once (§§316–318). Patriotism is everyday trust — "when we walk the streets
   at night in safety it does not strike us that this might be otherwise" (§268
   Addition) — not sacrifice. One Remark on religion (§270): the ground of ethics
   but not its authority; church and state united is "oriental despotism".
9. **Where does the state end — and who judges between states?** §§321–340:
   the state is an individual among states; the citizen's duty to sacrifice life
   for it; war is "the ethical moment", not an absolute evil (§324 — one
   paragraph; `<Ref>` maistre-and-bonald, whose starred section set Maistre's
   divine war beside it); states are in a state
   of nature to each other, "there is no Praetor to judge between states"
   (§333), so disputes are settled by war; and the only judge is world history —
   "the world's court of judgment" (§340, Schiller's Weltgeschichte ist das
   Weltgericht). §§341–360: nations as tools of the world mind; the dominant
   nation of an epoch has absolute right (§347: "it is only once that a nation
   can make its hour strike"); the right of heroes to found states and the
   "barbarians" passage (§§350–351 — flag as the course does Aristotle's
   climate theory: stated, not endorsed); the four realms — Oriental (the
   substantial unity, one is free), Greek (beautiful individuality, some are
   free), Roman (abstract universality, the person), Germanic (the
   reconciliation of the religious and the worldly in the rational state).
   Then the OBJECTION, stated as the course's own and argued in the postwar
   module (Strauss in References): if history is the court, right has no
   standard outside what prevails — the historical school that arose against
   the Revolution preferred the local to the universal and so lost the
   "transcendence" that made "the best regime" a standard; Hegel escapes
   relativism only by an "absolute moment" that exempts his own standpoint; and
   the step from Burke's grown constitution to a "standard inherent in the
   process" is the road to this. Give Hegel's reply in his own terms: world
   history is "the necessary development of freedom", not blind might (§342),
   and the judge is reason, not success. Then the second objection, one
   paragraph (`<Ref>` marx-manifesto): Marx keeps Hegel's civil society and its
   rabble and inverts the conclusion — the state is civil society's instrument,
   "a committee for managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie", and
   §245's admission that civil society cannot cure poverty is the seam.
10. **What the structure concedes to each side.** Close by answering the
    opening's question. To the Revolution: the principle of subjective
    freedom, property as personality, law that is public and known, free choice
    of station, careers open — and the diagnosis of its failure as the failure
    of abstraction, not of freedom. To the reaction: the state is not a contract
    and not an instrument; the constitution is not made; custom is second
    nature; the monarch is hereditary; the Estates are corporate — and the
    refusal of its ground (Providence, prejudice) as unknowing. What the reader
    can now do: rebuild the three spheres from the failures that force each, and
    state §258 as the answer to Rousseau, and §349 as the answer to Herder and
    Fichte. What stays open: what happens when the rational constitution's
    normal case breaks down and someone must decide outside its rules (`<Ref>`
    donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship, next); whether a state judged only by history
    has any standard at all (the postwar module); and whether the Revolution's
    own institutions — the departments and the Code — are "rational actual" or
    "paper" (`<Ref>` assignats, the module's application).

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The owl of Minerva and "a hand which is cut off"** (Preface l.300–380; §270
   Addition): the two sentences that fix what "the rational is actual" means and
   does not mean — worked as a two-column refutation of the "whatever exists is
   right" reading (a genuine, prevalent misreading: L13 slot).
2. **The three moments of the will with the Revolution as frame 1** (§§5–7 and
   Additions): negative freedom → self-limitation → being with oneself in the
   other; "the fanaticism of destruction" named at the first moment; friendship
   and love at the third. Then §15 caprice: "the ordinary man believes he is
   free when allowed to act capriciously".
3. **"Be a person and respect others as persons" → property → "equal persons,
   unequal holdings"** (§§36, 41–49): the derivation in four steps, with the
   §46 Remark on Plato's guardians and the §49 Addition that equalised wealth
   "would soon be undone".
4. **§75: why marriage and the state are not contracts** — the Addition's "we
   are already citizens by birth" parsed: a contract is between persons over
   alienable things; neither the family nor the state is a thing one can hold
   or quit at will; Bonald reached the same conclusion from God, Hegel from the
   concept of contract itself (`<Ref>`).
5. **Kant's test has no content** (§135 Remark): the universalisation test run
   on "there should be no property" — no contradiction results; so duty for
   duty's sake cannot say what duty is. Then §140's ladder of what an empty
   conscience can justify (theft for the poor, murder to uproot wickedness,
   conviction, irony).
6. **The rabble** (§§241–245 and Additions, l.6198–6265): "poverty in itself
   does not make men into a rabble; a rabble is created only when there is
   joined to poverty a disposition of mind, an inner indignation against the
   rich, against society"; the dilemma of relief stated as Hegel states it (the
   English poor rate; Scotland leaving the poor to beg); "despite an excess of
   wealth civil society is not rich enough". Mark it as the seam Marx pulls.
7. **§258 Remark reconstructed as an argument map**: Rousseau's merit /
   Rousseau's error / the consequence (contract → arbitrary consent → the
   abstract universal destroying every institution → terror) / origin is no
   concern of the Idea / Haller as the opposite error / Hegel's position. With
   the three contract formulas from the module (`<Ref>`s) as the row Hegel is
   answering.
8. **"When we walk the streets at night in safety"** (§268 Addition): patriotism
   as everyday trust, not heroics — against Rousseau's citizen who assembles and
   Maistre's soldier. Then "the monarch has only to dot the i" (§280 Addition)
   as the picture of a sovereignty that decides without ruling.
9. **The four realms as a table** (§§352–360): principle / form of freedom /
   who is free / what passes to the next — ending on the Germanic realm's
   reconciliation and §347's "only once can a nation make its hour strike"; then
   §340 "the world's court of judgment" with Schiller's line.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or
  idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only
  through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with
  who applied it and when.
- **Do not re-teach Herder or Fichte.** The Volk, the plurality of cultures, the
  Urvolk and national education are recalled with a `<Ref>` and a clause each;
  the lesson's new matter is §274 and §349 — the nation as the state's material,
  not its end.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** *Sittlichkeit / ethical life* (define at §142 — not
  "morality"; the German Sitte = custom, mores) against *Moralität / morality*
  (the inward standpoint of conscience, Kant's); *abstract right*; *the person*
  (in Hegel's sense: the self aware of itself as abstractly free — §35 "at once
  the sublime and the trivial"); *Willkür / caprice*; *the Idea* (concept plus
  its actualisation — §1 Addition's seed and tree); *actuality vs existence*
  (§270 Addition); *civil society / bürgerliche Gesellschaft* (define at §182 —
  the sphere of needs and exchange, "the external state", NOT the modern
  "voluntary associations" sense); *the Corporation* (a trade or professional
  body, §250 — not a company); *the police* (Polizei, §231: public authority
  over welfare and order, broader than constables); *the Estates* (Stände: both
  social classes and the political assembly — §303 Remark notes the double
  sense); *the rabble (Pöbel)*; *Bildung* (education as formation, §187);
  *constitutional monarchy* in Hegel's sense; *Praetor*; *Weltgericht* (Schiller's
  poem "Resignation", 1786: "Die Weltgeschichte ist das Weltgericht"); *Fries*
  (Jakob Friedrich Fries, the Preface's target, a philosopher of feeling
  dismissed from Jena after 1819 — one clause); *Haller* (Karl Ludwig von
  Haller, the Swiss restoration theorist of the Restauration der
  Staatswissenschaft, 1816–34, for whom the state is the natural rule of the
  stronger — one clause); *Antigone* (Sophocles' heroine who buries her brother
  against the king's edict — Hegel's figure for family piety against public law,
  §166); *Berlin 1821* (Hegel was professor at the University of Berlin from
  1818; the Prussian state after the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 — one clause, so
  the Preface's caution is placed).
- **Dates to the decade** (1820s) except 1789/1793 in the §5 Addition.
- **"The march of God in the world" (§258 Addition, Knox) is a mistranslation
  that fuels the totalitarian reading.** The German is "es ist der Gang Gottes
  in der Welt, daß der Staat ist" — "it is the way of God with the world that
  there should be the state". Quote it, say what the German says, and move on;
  this is where Popper's reading lives (References only).
- **"The rational is actual" is not "might is right"**: §270 Addition and the
  Haller Remark are Hegel's own refutation of that reading. One L13 slot.
- **Do not teach the Logic.** The dialectic is shown, not explained: "immanent
  production of determinations" (§31) can be one clause; every transition in the
  lesson is stated as "X fails because Y, so Z" in plain words.
- **Do not re-teach Rousseau, Burke, Maistre or Kant.** Each is recalled with a
  `<Ref>` and a clause; the §258 Remark is argued against the general will the
  reader already has. Kant is not a course lesson: give the one sentence of
  Kant Hegel needs (duty for duty's sake; the universalisation test) at §133 and
  no more.
- **Do not present constitutional monarchy as Hegel's blueprint for all
  states**: §274 says a constitution depends on a nation's self-consciousness;
  the structure of §§272–320 is the concept's articulation, presented as such.
- **The rabble and colonies (§§241–248)** are the seam to Marx and to the
  empire module; state them in Hegel's words, do not argue Marx here.
- **Period matter to flag, not moralise:** the gender passages (§166 Addition:
  women as plants, men as animals; women in government endanger the state) are
  not needed by the argument and stay out; the "barbarians" passage (§351) is
  stated as Hegel's and set beside Aristotle's climate theory with a `<Ref>` —
  the empire module takes it up.
- **"Prefer the Additions as the entry point"** (course.yaml): each section's
  example should come from an Addition (the night streets, the dotted i, the
  cut-off hand, friendship, "uncultured people insist most on their rights",
  §37). Mark each as an Addition in the pointer; the lesson's body need not say
  "Addition".
- **Translation and licence:** Dyde (Preface and Introduction) is public
  domain; Knox (1952) should be treated as copyrighted — short quotations only,
  paraphrase the rest; the full text's OCR has minor errors (check each quotation
  against the line).
- **Names ONLY in References:** Strauss, Hassner (Strauss–Cropsey), Hamilton
  (SEP), Fawcett, Popper, Knox, Dyde, Gans, Nisbet, Wood, Avineri, Taylor,
  Pelczynski, Kojève. Thinkers who may be named: Hegel, Rousseau, Kant, Fichte,
  Haller, Fries, Plato, Aristotle, Burke, Maistre, Marx (forward `<Ref>`),
  Schmitt (forward), Schiller (as the poem's author — a named object),
  Napoleon (as a figure in Hegel's Additions). State the historicism charge as
  "the objection that a state judged only by history has no standard", never
  "Strauss argues".
- **Dossier gaps:** the summary notes shard 6/7 was supplied under another
  label (§§257–297 are present in the full file — verified: §258 at l.6460,
  §273 at l.7416, §279 at l.7634, §274 at l.7519, §349 at l.9129); the
  Strauss–Cropsey Hegel chapter has landed (OCR, l.35558–37006); the
  Phenomenology "cabbage" line is only in Fawcett — verify or omit.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval, warm-up (E3).* Give the three spheres of ethical life and, for
   each, the failure of the sphere before it that makes it necessary (the
   person's right is only permission; conscience is empty; civil society cannot
   integrate itself). Then name which of the module's thinkers Hegel says
   described civil society and called it the state (§182 Addition) and which
   two he answers in §258.
2. *Plausible false claim to refute.* "'What is rational is actual' means that
   Hegel endorses whatever state exists, so he must endorse the Terror." Name
   the failing steps: §270 Addition (actuality ≠ existence); §5 Addition (the
   Terror is the freedom of the void, the failure of abstraction); §258 Remark
   (the Revolution as "abstractions without the Idea"); and the Preface's own
   distinction between understanding and approving.
3. *Boundary probe.* A state with no civil society — Plato's city, where the
   guardians own nothing and the producers have no share in rule (`<Ref>`
   kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king). Apply §46 Remark, §185 Remark and §260:
   what does Hegel say such a state lacks, and why does he call the ancient
   state "universality without freed particularity"? The reader should find that
   the modern state's whole claim is to contain civil society without being
   reduced to it — the thing Marx will deny it does.
4. *Document reasoning (sourcing drill).* The §258 Addition's "march of God"
   sentence: author (Hegel's lecture, recorded by a student — Gans's
   compilation), date (lectures of the 1820s, published 1833 with the text),
   audience (Berlin students under the post-1819 censorship), what it records
   (a spoken gloss), what it argues (the state as the actuality of the ethical
   Idea), what it omits (the German's "Gang Gottes … daß der Staat ist" — "the
   way of God with the world is that there be a state"), and what it can be
   USED to establish — Hegel's theological framing of the state's necessity,
   not a doctrine of state worship; the translation question changes the use.
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* From §§182–184 (civil society's two
   principles) and §§241–245 (the rabble), derive why Hegel must say the state
   is "the beginning, not the result" (§256): if civil society were prior, what
   would stop the rabble from dissolving it, and what in §§250–255 (the
   Corporation) and §§301–311 (the Estates) is doing the work of mediation?
6. *Synthesis / counter-argument (Rousseau answered?).* Someone claims that
   Hegel's state is Rousseau's general will with a monarch added: both say
   freedom is obedience to a law that is one's own, both reject the individual
   as judge. Name two facts the claim fails to account for (§258 Remark: the
   general will is assembled from individual wills by contract, Hegel's
   universal is embodied in institutions prior to any will; §279: the people
   without a constitution is a formless mass, so there is no "sovereign people"
   to will) and state what would have to be true for it to survive (that
   Rousseau's Legislator and civil religion — `<Ref>` — already supply the
   prior institutions, which is the Idealist reading the Rousseau lesson marked
   as a gloss).
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship`.** Hegel's monarch "has
   only to say yes and dot the i" (§280 Addition): he decides, but inside a
   constitution whose laws, ministers and Estates have already done the work.
   Take a case the constitution's laws did not foresee: armed political clubs in
   the capital, the ministers divided, the Estates unable to meet. Using §§272–273
   and §§279–280, say what in Hegel's structure decides, and whether the "I will"
   of the crown can act when the constitution it completes is itself under
   attack. The reader should reach the thought that a rational constitution
   presupposes the normal case, and that someone must decide when the normal case
   has ended. The solution says in one sentence that the next lesson's Spanish
   deputy, who found the Hegelians of Berlin incomprehensible, answers exactly
   this — "when legality is not enough, dictatorship" — with the `<Ref>`.
8. *Retrieval across the module (E3).* One sentence each: what Locke, Hume,
   Rousseau, Burke, Maistre, Fichte and Hegel say the state IS (a trust; a
   government held up by opinion; the sovereign people; a partnership of
   generations; a providential growth; the instrument of a nation's eternity;
   the actuality of the ethical Idea) and what each says it is NOT.

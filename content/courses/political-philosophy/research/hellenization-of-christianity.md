# Research brief: hellenization-of-christianity (The Hellenization of Christianity: Harnack's thesis and Nietzsche's counter-thesis)

status: planned
module: rome-and-christianity
type: core
goal: Explain how a Jewish movement was remade in Greek concepts and Roman forms, and compare Harnack's and Nietzsche's opposite verdicts on the result

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The reader has Cicero's natural law — one law for Rome and
  Athens, God its author, each city nonetheless keeping its own ancestral gods
  (`cicero-natural-law`) — and the Roman commonwealth as a thing defined by
  agreed right (`cicero-mixed-constitution`). They have Plato's Forms only as
  far as the Cave and the Good (`kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`). They
  have NOT met Christianity as a historical object: no Gospels, no Paul, no
  creed, no bishop. If they took the Nietzsche course they have his Jesus/Paul
  reading; the lesson must work for a reader who did not.
- **Contributes:** One move: the Christianity Europe received is shown to be
  a *construction* — a Galilean preaching of the Father and the soul, severed
  from Judaism by Paul, given a body by Greek philosophy (Logos = Christ),
  hardened into creed, law and clergy by the fight with Gnosticism, and then
  housed in the Roman Empire's own forms — and two verdicts on that
  construction are set side by side: the historian's (kernel survived under
  husk) and the philosopher's (the whole thing, kernel included, was a
  foreign body), with a third reading (a Magian religion deformed in
  Classical moulds) to show the dispute is about *what* was Hellenized, not
  whether.
- **Hands off:** If the Church is "the Roman Empire consecrated by the
  Gospel", then two bodies now claim to represent the same society — the
  problem of `constantine-and-representation`. If the Gospel is "not of this
  world" (Harnack's "two provinces", Mark x.42), what is left for the city to
  be — Augustine's question. And the conceptual vocabulary (Logos, substance,
  dogma) is what Spengler will call a pseudomorphosis and Voegelin a
  differentiation; both return in module 7–8.
- **Spine step:** "A Jewish movement becomes Europe's religion by being remade
  in Greek concepts and Roman forms; Constantine's empire and the Church fight
  over who represents the truth; Augustine denies that any earthly city can be
  just"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: Europe's religion was founded by
an Aramaic-speaking Galilean who never wrote a word and whose first followers
expected the world to end; within two hundred years it had a creed in Greek
philosophical terms, bishops in Roman cities and a dispute about the word
"substance"; one historian said the Gospel survived under all this as kernel
under husk, one philosopher said the whole thing was a foreign body in Europe
from the start.

1. **What is the thing we are asking about?** The obvious attempt: define
   Christianity by its doctrines (Trinity, incarnation, Church). Harnack's
   method refuses: to ask "what is Christianity" is to ask it "solely in its
   historical sense" — from Jesus, his first disciples, and everything that
   grew from them — and to separate what is permanent from "the inevitable
   husk of contemporary form" (Lect. I). The child who peels the bulb to find
   the kernel and is left with nothing is Harnack's own warning against the
   method (I, p. 11); state both the method and its risk. Anchors here: Jesus
   of Nazareth, executed c. AD 30 under Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judaea;
   Aramaic, the spoken language of Galilee; the Synoptic Gospels (Mark,
   Matthew, Luke, written c. 70–90, in Greek) as Harnack's sources, the
   fourth Gospel (John) as evidence of impression, not history. The
   contested step inside this link: Harnack's confidence that "Greek lies on
   them like a diaphanous veil" — Greek was spoken in Galilee "much as
   Swedish in Finland", but Plato's influence on Jesus is "absolutely
   impossible to maintain" (II). So the Hellenization question is not
   whether Jesus was Greek (Harnack: no) but what happened after.
2. **What was the Gospel, before anything happened to it?** Three heads,
   each containing the whole (III–IV): the kingdom of God — not the
   apocalyptic drama Jesus shared with his contemporaries but "the kingdom
   is within you", God ruling in the individual soul; God the Father and the
   infinite value of the human soul — against Plato, who praised the knowing
   mind and spoke to the wise, Jesus calls every poor soul (IV, p. 46), the
   first "transvaluation of all values" (Harnack uses Nietzsche's phrase,
   line 1884 — flag for link 7); the higher righteousness — ethics severed
   from ritual, intention over act, love as the one root, "religion the soul
   of morality, morality the body of religion". And what it was NOT: a
   social programme (VI: no reformer, yet "profoundly socialistic and
   profoundly individualistic"), a philosophy, a politics ("Render unto
   Caesar" as two provinces that must not be mixed, VI; "my kingdom is not
   of this world" forbidding "direct and formal interference of religion in
   worldly affairs", VII). Here the reader meets the thing the lesson will
   watch being transformed: a religion without doctrine, law or institution,
   addressed to the soul. Because: the kernel must be named before the husk
   can be seen as husk — and the writer should say plainly that this
   "kernel" is Harnack's selection, a liberal Protestant's, which link 7
   will contest.
3. **Why did it leave Judaism — and what did it lose on the way?** The
   obvious attempt: Jesus's followers could have stayed a Jewish sect. Harnack
   (X): "there is no tougher or more conservative fabric than a properly
   constituted religion; it can only yield to a higher phase by being
   abolished" — Paul alone said "the old is done away". Paul's four
   achievements: redemption as accomplished; the end of law-religion; an
   individual, therefore universal, religion carried from Jewish to
   Greco-Roman ground; a language (spirit/flesh) Greeks could understand.
   Renan's line, used by Harnack: tell a first-century emperor that "the
   little Jew from Antioch" was his best collaborator and he would call you
   mad — "Alexander the Great's work has perished; Paul's has remained"
   (X, lines 4798–4812). And the price, in the same lecture: churches with
   form, law and discipline, to which "the value of that to which they
   minister is insensibly transferred"; Paul's Christology, which with the
   Greeks "inevitably set an entirely new theory in motion"; the Old
   Testament as a permanent door for obsolete principles. "There is no
   progress that has not its dark side" (p. 123). Anchors: Paul of Tarsus
   (a Jew of the Greek-speaking diaspora, Roman citizen, d. c. 64–67 under
   Nero); Antioch (the Syrian city where the Gentile mission began);
   Galatians 3:28 as the one-line charter of universality.
4. **How did a bodiless Gospel get a Greek body?** Severed from the Jewish
   nation, the movement "hovered bodiless over the earth… bodiless and
   seeking a body" (XI, line 5356). Three stages of Hellenic influx: c. 130,
   philosophy; c. 220–230, the mysteries and Greek civilisation; c. 320 and
   after, the old polytheism relabelled as saints. The decisive step: the
   apologists, converts from Platonic-Stoic philosophy, drew the equation
   Logos = Jesus Christ (XI, 5430–5470). Explain Logos for a newcomer first:
   the Greek word for "word/reason", used by the Stoics and Platonists for
   the rational principle ordering the cosmos; John's prologue had already
   called Christ the Word, but as a predicate; the apologists made it the
   *basis*. Because: "Messiah" — a Jewish anointed king — was "entirely
   unintelligible" to Greeks; the Logos made Christ cosmic, explained his
   relation to God, and "the cosmos, reason, and ethics were comprehended as
   one". Harnack's verdict in the same breath: "not an unmixed blessing" —
   the Gospel became a philosophy of religion; the Christological question,
   which Jesus never posed ("the Gospel… has to do with the Father only and
   not with the Son", VIII), became the chief question. Anchor Justin
   Martyr (Rome, c. 150, the first apologist to argue this way; ANF text
   available) and Tertullian's protest (Carthage, c. 200: "What has Athens
   to do with Jerusalem?") as the contemporary dissent.
5. **Why creed, law and clergy — who forced them?** The obvious attempt:
   bishops wanted power. Harnack rejects the "personal ambition" account:
   Gnosticism — the "acute Hellenisation" of the second century, which would
   have dissolved the Gospel into Greek speculation and cut it from the Old
   Testament — forced the Church to fix a rule of faith, a canon, a
   discipline and an office to guard them (XI, 5534–5580): "the conquered
   gave laws to the conquerors" (Victi victoribus legem dederunt). Within
   about 120 years living faith became creed, hope of the kingdom became
   immortality and deification, the Spirit became law, brothers became laity
   under tutelage (XI, pp. 126–127). Then the four costs (XII): creed before
   Christian; intellectualism; the Church as an independent power of grace;
   a message of fear and rigorism over glad tidings. And the achievement
   Harnack sets against the costs, which the course needs: the Church
   "abolished the imperial system of state-idols" — "to place an irremovable
   landmark between religion and politics, between God and Caesar, the
   martyrs shed their blood" (XI, 5252–5258). This is the seam to the next
   lesson; say so in one sentence.
6. **What did the result look like — two Churches, two dresses.** The Greek
   Church: ritual, relics, mysteries, a "natural religion" of the Hellenic
   world, which "would have done battle with the Christians of the first
   century just as it did battle with the worship of Magna Mater" — "not a
   Christian product in Greek dress, but a Greek product in Christian dress"
   (XII, 5890–5908); its dogma, the God-Man and the Trinity, driven by a
   soteriology of deification in which salvation is a quasi-"pharmacological
   process" (XIII, 6201), hence Nicaea's homoousios (325: "of one substance"
   — explain the word; it is the "substance" dispute of the opening). The
   Roman Church: "the old Roman Empire consecrated by the Gospel" — Popes as
   Trajan and Marcus Aurelius, Peter and Paul in place of Romulus and Remus,
   bishops as proconsuls, priests and monks as legions (XIV, 6712–6740);
   salvation as contract, revelation as lex, infallibility as sovereignty.
   Harnack's harshest sentence, "total perversion" (7000), should be quoted
   with his own qualification: the Church also educated the Romano-Germanic
   nations and kept religion independent of the state; and Augustine's sin
   and grace run through it, making it "at once Caesarian and Augustinian"
   (6861–6945). Then the thesis in one line: Hellenization gave the Gospel
   its concepts, Romanization its forms; the kernel — Father, soul, love —
   "a red thread in the centre of the web" (XVI, 7956) — survived both.
7. **OBJECTION at full strength — Nietzsche's counter-thesis, by `<Ref>`.**
   Set up the point of contact exactly and nothing more. Both agree: the
   Christianity Europe received is not Jesus's; Paul made the break; the
   Greek element remade it; Jesus and his religion were a "transvaluation of
   all values" (Harnack borrows the phrase for praise, lines 1884, 2892;
   Nietzsche coined it for the accusation — `<Ref>`
   `nietzsche/slave-revolt-and-ressentiment`). They disagree on three
   things. (a) *What the kernel was.* For Harnack: eternal life in time,
   the infinite value of every soul, love — a religion above the tension of
   this world and the next. For Nietzsche (`<Ref>` `nietzsche/jesus-and-paul`):
   Jesus is a type without ressentiment, a "new way of life, not a new
   faith", admirable and decadent at once — and the valuation of every soul
   as infinitely valuable is the slave revolt's own coin, the weak lying
   their weakness into merit. (b) *Who the villain is.* Harnack has none —
   "no progress without its dark side"; the Greeks gave a body the Gospel
   needed. Nietzsche names Paul, the "dysangelist", who turned a way of life
   into guilt, judgment, resurrection and "equal rights for all". (c)
   *Whether the Greek element is husk or substance.* Harnack: husk — the
   Greek Church is Greek religion with the Gospel absorbed. Nietzsche: the
   Greek element (Plato's other world, "Christianity is Platonism for the
   people") is itself the disease, so Hellenization did not disguise the
   Gospel, it completed a decadence already present in the kernel; and the
   "Rome against Judea" of Genealogy I.16 reads Rome's conversion as the
   defeat of noble Europe by a foreign valuation, not as Rome absorbing a
   Greek product. Harnack had pre-answered one of these (Lect. I, pp.
   13–14: the Gospel is not "a religion of the despairing", distress is
   occasion not cause — the Fichte analogy) and had in 1900 seen that
   Nietzsche's ideas were being "exhibited in their special affinity with
   the Gospel" (line 127). The lesson states where each would say the other
   begs the question: Harnack's kernel is selected by a liberal Protestant's
   taste; Nietzsche's "slave revolt" is a psychology no document records.
   Then the reader is left to weigh — no verdict from the course.
8. **The third reading — what if neither Greek nor Jewish is the right
   frame?** Spengler (`<Quote>`, short): a pseudomorphosis is "an older alien
   Culture [lying] so massively over the land that a young Culture, born in
   this land, cannot get its breath… All that wells up from the depths of the
   young soul is cast in the old moulds" (vol. 2 ch. VII). Define the word
   from mineralogy. On this reading Christianity is neither a Jewish
   movement Hellenized nor a Greek product in Christian dress but the first
   expression of a *new* Culture — Aramaic, "Magian", born between Sinai and
   the Zagros from c. 300 BC, with its own prime symbol (the world-cavern)
   and its own idea of the soul — forced to grow inside Classical forms:
   Greek concepts, Roman law, the basilica. "The Pseudomorphosis began with
   Actium; there it should have been Antony who won" (9088). Jesus before
   Pilate is "the world of facts and the world of truths face to face"
   (10361); Paul made the faith urban and Western; Marcion made the canon.
   So the Christological disputes Harnack calls Greek intellectualism are,
   for Spengler, a Magian problem (how the one God's substance relates to the
   man) answered in Greek words that could not fit it. Why this is the third
   view and not a compromise: it inverts Harnack's phrase — not a Greek
   product in Christian dress but a Magian substance misshapen by a Greek
   mould — and it agrees with Nietzsche that the result was foreign to
   Europe while denying that it was Jewish in origin. Flag its weaknesses
   (a unitary "Arabian Culture" is Spengler's construction; the Actium
   counterfactual is unfalsifiable) and leave it as a reading, not a result.
9. **Close:** what the reader can now do — name the three operations (Paul's
   breach, Logos = Christ, Gnosticism's forcing of creed and office) and the
   Roman housing; state the three verdicts as answers to "what was the
   kernel" and "should it have survived"; and see the problem the next lesson
   inherits: a Church that has put "an irremovable landmark between God and
   Caesar" now stands inside an Empire that is itself a religion.

Headings that fall out: "What are we asking about? Kernel and husk"; "The
Gospel before its history: three heads"; "Why it had to leave Judaism, and

   A table, exactly as Harnack lists it.
6. **Logos = Christ, worked as a problem** (XI, 5430–5470): the Greek
   convert's difficulty ("Messiah" means nothing to him) → the available
   concept (Logos: the cosmos's reason) → the equation → what it buys (cosmic
   significance, relation to God, unity of cosmos-reason-ethics) → what it
   costs (the Gospel becomes a philosophy; the Son displaces the Father).
   Quote John 1:1 and 1:14 in a `<Quote>`.
7. **The Roman Church as the Roman Empire — the correspondence table** (XIV,
   6712–6740): Popes / Trajan and Marcus Aurelius; Peter and Paul / Romulus
   and Remus; bishops / proconsuls; priests and monks / legions; Jesuits /
   imperial guard; Pontifex Maximus kept. Then Harnack's own concessions
   beside it.
8. **Jesus before Pilate as Spengler reads it** (vol. 2 lines 10360–10400):
   "What is truth?" — "the one word that is race-pure in the whole Greek
   Testament" — against "What is actuality?"; "My kingdom is not of this

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know — anchor at first mention:** Gospel (the
  "good news" Jesus preached, and the four books about him); Synoptic
  Gospels (Mark, Matthew, Luke, which "see together"; John stands apart);
  Aramaic (the Semitic language of Palestine; Hebrew's cousin); Messiah (the
  anointed king Jewish hope expected; Greek *Christos* translates it);
  apocalyptic (the expectation that God will end the present age, soon);
  Pharisees (the lay teachers of the Law whom the Gospels set against Jesus;
  Harnack's foil, to be used with care); Paul (Saul of Tarsus, d. c. 64–67);
  Gentile (non-Jew); Logos; Stoa/Stoics and Platonists (one clause each);
  apologist (a second-century defender of Christianity to educated pagans;
  Justin, c. 150); Gnosticism (second-century movements teaching salvation by
  secret knowledge, a lesser creator-god, contempt for the material world and
  the Old Testament); rule of faith; canon (the list of authoritative books);
  bishop; clergy/laity; creed; dogma; Nicaea (325, the council Constantine
  convened); homoousios ("of one substance" — the Greek word that split the
  Church; explain *substance* here as what a thing is, so that "same
  substance as the Father" means Christ is God as the Father is); Trinity;
  deification (salvation as sharing God's immortal nature); Magna Mater (the
  Anatolian mother-goddess cult Rome adopted in 204 BC); Pontifex Maximus
  (Rome's chief priest; a title the emperors held and the popes took);
  Marcion (c. 144, excommunicated at Rome; rejected the Old Testament and
  assembled the first Christian canon); pseudomorphosis (define from
  mineralogy); Magian (Spengler's name for the "Arabian" Culture — Persian,
  Jewish, Christian, Islamic — whose prime symbol is the cavern); Actium
  (31 BC, Octavian defeats Antony and Cleopatra; the Principate follows).
  No more than three new named terms per section (L11c): the lesson is
  term-dense, so sections must be short and many.
- **Do NOT re-teach Nietzsche.** Every claim about his position is a `<Ref>`
  plus a clause. The lesson reconstructs only the *point of contact* (what
  both agree on; the three disagreements). A reader without the Nietzsche
  course must still be able to follow: give each disagreement one sentence
  of Nietzsche's view, attributed, and the Ref for the argument behind it.
- **Face the tension directly** (course.yaml note): the two agree Europe's
  Christianity was Greek-made; they disagree on what the kernel was and
  whether it should have survived. Do not soften either; do not adjudicate.
- **Harnack is a 1900 liberal Protestant** (summary 47 pedagogy): the
  "kernel" is his selection, Jewish forms and Catholic ritual are "husk" by
  his taste, some of his history is dated (Synoptic sourcing, demons as
  nervous illness). Present the thesis as *his*, with his own concessions
  (monasticism, Augustine, the martyrs' landmark), and say once that the
  kernel/husk cut is itself a judgment. Never present "the Gospel was X" as
  settled history.
- **Harnack's Pharisee foil** is a stock of nineteenth-century Protestant
  anti-Judaism; use his structural claim (law-religion vs inward religion)
  without the caricature, and do not moralise either way.
- **Spengler's vol. 2** carries race, blood and antisemitism-adjacent
  passages (summary 11 pedagogy); use only the pseudomorphosis definition,
  Actium, Pilate, Paul and Marcion, and frame "Magian" as his construction.
  Atkinson is PD in the US only — short quotes.

- **Dossier thinness:** no New Testament, no Justin, no Tertullian, no Nicene

- **Names only in References:** Saunders (translator), Atkinson (translator),

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (warm-up, the only one).* List Harnack's three operations (the
   breach, the Logos, the Gnostic forcing) with a date to the decade and the
   institution each produced; then name the one thing he says survived them.
2. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill.* Lecture XI, pp. 132–134 (Logos =
   Christ). Metadata: a Berlin professor of church history, 1899–1900,
   lecturing to six hundred students of all faculties, liberal Protestant.
   Predict what such a speaker will count as gain and as loss; then read the
   passage; then: what can it be USED to establish — about second-century
   doctrine, or about 1900 Protestantism's view of it? Name one claim in it
   that is a value judgment dressed as history.
3. *Boundary probe.* Run Harnack's method to its limit: peel away the
   apocalyptic (husk), the Jewish Law (husk), the Greek dogma (husk), the
   Roman office (husk). Is what remains — "eternal life in the midst of
   time" — a *historical* finding or a definition chosen in advance? State
   Harnack's own defence (the bulb warning; "a life again and again kindled
   afresh") and where it leaves the method.
4. *Claim to refute.* "Harnack and Nietzsche disagree about the history:
   Harnack thinks Christianity was Hellenized, Nietzsche thinks it stayed
   Jewish." Name the failing step: both accept the Greek remaking (Nietzsche:
   "Platonism for the people"); the disagreement is over the kernel's value
   and over whether the Greek element disguised or completed it. Use the two
   Nietzsche Refs.
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* Harnack calls the Greek Church "a Greek
   product in Christian dress" and the Roman Church "the Roman Empire
   consecrated by the Gospel". Derive what he must therefore say about the
   Reformation (the third dress), using only his own principle that each form
   has a dark side — then check against Lecture XVI's costs (State-churches,
   moral laxity, loss of monasticism). The point: his method predicts its own
   result.
6. *Synthesis — corroboration across three sources.* Harnack (XI), Nietzsche
   (Antichrist 42–43 via `<Ref>` `nietzsche/jesus-and-paul`) and Spengler
   (vol. 2 ch. VII) all make Paul the turning point. Set the three
   descriptions of what Paul did side by side (two sentences each) and state
   what they agree on as *fact* and what each adds as *valuation*. Then: what
   single document would distinguish Harnack's Paul (deliverer from the Law)
   from Nietzsche's (inventor of guilt)? The solution says: none the dossier
   has — Paul's letters support both readings, which is why the dispute is
   about weighting.
7. **FORWARD (E7)** → `constantine-and-representation`. Harnack says the
   martyrs set "an irremovable landmark between God and Caesar" and that the
   Christian emperor was "at once a Christian Emperor and an oriental
   despot… the more conscientious, the more intolerant". Take the two
   sentences together and state the contradiction they imply: a religion that
   refused the emperor's cult now has the emperor as its guardian. Who, after
   313, *represents* the truth of Roman society — the emperor, the bishops,
   or neither? The solution says the next lesson gives this question a name
   and a tool.
8. *Document reasoning — second-order concept: change and continuity (E3
   reach-back).* Cicero's Laws II.8 forbade "new gods or alien gods unless
   recognized by the State" (`<Ref>` `cicero-natural-law`). Using Harnack's
   three stages, say at which stage, if any, Christianity stopped being an
   "alien god" in Cicero's sense — and what that says about which side
   changed, the city or the sect.

# Research brief: henrich-church-and-kinship (Henrich's WEIRD thesis: the Church, the family and the Western mind)

status: planned
module: why-the-west
type: core
goal: Analyze Henrich's argument that the medieval Church's marriage and family programme dissolved intensive kinship and produced the psychology behind Western institutions, and evaluate its evidence

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `landes-european-exceptionalism` (`european-fragmentation`,
  `invention-of-invention`): rival authorities and the right of exit kept inventions from being
  suppressed, but Landes's answer to *why Europeans kept trying* was "the market" and "culture",
  left undefined. From `murray-human-accomplishment`: the count of significant figures — Europe
  after 1400, a compact core that leaves out southern Italy, Iberia and the east — and Murray's
  claim that Christianity supplied the individual *autonomy* great work needs, though it was "not
  individualistic" in its early centuries; a cause without a mechanism. From
  `weber-only-in-the-west` (`protestant-ethic`): a religious origin for a Western disposition,
  located in the Reformation. From `augustine-two-cities` (course.yaml notes): the Church's claim to
  judge what the earthly city may not — here, marriage; Augustine himself argued in *City of God*
  XV.16 that marrying outside one's kin binds more people together. From
  `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government` (concept `status-to-contract`): Maine's formula that
  progressive societies move from relations fixed by birth and family to relations made by
  agreement — a description in search of a cause. From `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`
  (`civil-society`): the sphere of individuals bound by interest rather than kinship. From
  `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township` and `kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`: the art of
  voluntary association, and the conservative concern that the modern state destroys the
  intermediate bodies (family, church, guild).
- **Contributes:** The one move: the Western difference is located not in religion's doctrine
  (Weber, Murray) nor in politics alone (Landes) but in the *family*. Between about 500 and 1200 the
  Western Church forbade cousin marriage out to sixth cousins, marriage to in-laws, polygyny and
  arranged unions without consent, and pushed couples into their own households; this dissolved
  the clans and lineages that elsewhere organized life, and a population raised outside them
  developed an unusual psychology — individualist, analytic, guilt- rather than shame-driven,
  trusting of strangers and attentive to intentions — which built voluntary associations
  (charter towns, guilds, universities, monastic orders) and later representative government,
  impersonal law and science. The lesson starts from the 2010 finding that Western subjects are
  psychological outliers, follows Henrich's chain link by link, and tests each link against his
  evidence and the strongest objections, saying for each whether Henrich answers it. The lesson
  grades its own evidence (strongest for present-day kinship ↔ psychology correlations, weaker
  for dosage → kinship in the Middle Ages, weakest for the links to science and industry); it does
  not pass a module verdict.
- **Hands off:** Whether kinship — or fragmentation, a religious ethic, or high-medieval
  institutions — caused the West's lead is judged in `the-borrowing-ledger`: each is partly
  supported, none decisive, the cause **open**, with what would settle it (within-Europe designs
  separating the timing of kin-dissolution, state competition and literacy); the genetic
  candidate staged here against Henrich (Clark) is graded there **open**, its England-specific
  form failing **on balance**. (i) If a
  distinctive European psychology and its institutions were in place by 1500, Europe's material
  lead should be visible long before 1800; the claim that it was not — that the most advanced
  regions of China and England were close around 1750 and that the gap opened with coal and New
  World land — is `pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis` (FORWARD exercise);
  there too the author who first linked the Church's marriage rules to the dissolution of the
  European family is read for his larger thesis. (ii) Henrich explicitly rejects a genetic
  account of the psychological differences (ch. 14); the hereditarian argument at full strength,
  and its objections, are `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations` and `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`.
  (iii) Henrich's finding that children of immigrants keep their parents' origin psychology into
  the second generation bears on the nation module (`miller-strangers-in-our-midst`); not developed
  here.
- **Spine step:** "Why the West? Weber, Landes, Murray and Henrich argue for Western exceptionalism
  from religion, fragmentation, accomplishment and kinship; Pomeranz and Goody answer that the West
  was one link in a long chain of exchange; the borrowing ledger tests both"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): psychologists find Western university students unusual — more
individualistic, more trusting of strangers, more guilty than ashamed, more analytic; where did the
difference come from? A Harvard anthropologist proposes the medieval Church's rules on whom a
Christian could marry; the lesson follows the chain from incest rules to impersonal markets and
tests each link. Anchor (L11): Joseph Henrich (b. 1968), anthropologist at Harvard who works on
cultural evolution with economic games run in small-scale societies; co-author of the 2010 paper
and author of *The WEIRDest People in the World* (2020).

1. **What exactly is unusual, and is it real?** The 2010 finding stated as its authors stated it:
   behavioural science drew 96 percent of its subjects from countries with 12 percent of the world's
   population, mostly American undergraduates, and assumed the results were human; where
   comparisons exist, these subjects sit at the extreme. Worked case (`<Example>` with FIGURE 1):
   the Müller-Lyer lines — American students need one line about a fifth longer before they judge
   the two equal; San foragers of the Kalahari see them as equal. Then the economic games: offered
   a sum to split with an anonymous stranger, U.S. adults offer close to half and reject low offers;
   in many small-scale societies offers are lower and rejections rare. Define WEIRD (Western,
   Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) and `weird-psychology` here. Two things to fix at
   once (L13 — the real misreadings): (a) the 2010 paper is about *sampling*, "takes no position
   regarding the substantive origins" and disclaims any moral judgment in the acronym; (b) it does
   not say all psychology varies — it lists documented universals (emotion recognition in faces,
   a number sense). Seam: the paper left the origin open; the 2020 book answers it — that is the
   lesson's problem.
2. **What does a non-WEIRD social world look like?** Obvious attempt: "traditional" or "collectivist"
   as a vague contrast. Henrich's specific answer: **intensive kinship** — clans tracing descent
   through one line, cousin marriage that keeps property and alliances inside the lineage,
   polygyny, marriages arranged by elders, shared liability (a man obliged to avenge a second
   cousin, blood-money owed by a whole kin group), ancestor cults, land held by the lineage.
   Define `intensive-kinship` from the Ethnographic Atlas traits; state the rarity of the Western
   family: of the societies recorded, about half have none of the five WEIRD kinship traits and
   under one in a hundred have all five (Table 5.1). Then the psychological claim with its "because":
   in such a world your safety, marriage and land depend on the group, so conformity, deference to
   elders, loyalty to in-group over stranger, and shame (public standing) pay; where relationships
   are chosen, a reputation for impartial fairness and a stable personal conscience (guilt) pay.
   Answer: kinship institutions select a psychology; this is the mechanism Weber, Landes and Murray
   lacked. Recall Maine's "from status to contract" in one clause (`<Ref
   maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government>`): Maine described the movement; Henrich proposes what
   moved it.
3. **Who broke the clans in Europe, and how?** Obvious attempt: cities and markets dissolved kinship,
   then states and industry finished it (the usual modernization order). Henrich's reversal
   (l. 1591): in Europe "the historical order was reversed" — the family changed first, between
   about 400 and 1200, by the Church's **Marriage and Family Program** (define
   `marriage-and-family-program`). The nine elements (list once, as Henrich's, then group them by
   what each does to a lineage): bans on marrying blood kin (out to sixth cousins by the 11th
   century) and in-laws; no polygyny or concubines; spiritual kinship (godparents) with its own
   incest bar; adoption discouraged; consent of bride and groom required; new couples in their own
   household; individual property and inheritance by will. Then the scenes: Gregory I answering the
   missionary to Kent around 600 that cousins and a dead brother's widow are forbidden though Roman
   law allowed them; the 786 papal commission finding "incest" and polygyny among baptized
   Anglo-Saxons and the Church's weapon — children of unlawful unions declared illegitimate and cut
   from inheritance. Primary text (`<Quote>`, Dods): Augustine, *City of God* XV.16 — relationships
   should "be distributed among several" so as "to bind together the greatest number"; recall
   Augustine with one clause (`<Ref augustine-two-cities>`: the bishop who denied any earthly city
   true justice; here, the same author on why the Church should govern marriage). Why the Church did
   it — Henrich's answer, which he does not claim was anyone's plan: lineages were the Church's
   rivals for loyalty and land; a lineage that cannot adopt, take a second wife or marry a cousin
   dies out more often, and its land passes by will — often to the Church (about a third of western
   Europe's farmland by 900). Answer: the programme dissolved the clans "from the middle strata
   outward"; by about 1000 the Carolingian manors show small nuclear households.
4. **Can the effect be measured?** Obvious attempt: compare Christians and non-Christians today.
   Why it fails: the Orthodox East is Christian too, and modern religion is not the treatment.
   Henrich's design (FigureSequence, FIGURE 4): (a) measure kinship intensity per population
   (KII, cousin marriage); (b) measure **Church dosage** — years under a bishop of the *Western*
   Church between 500 and 1500, from the founding of bishoprics — and carry it to today's
   populations by where their ancestors lived; (c) relate dosage to kinship and kinship to the
   psychological measures of link 1. Results: Western plus Orthodox dosage explains about 40
   percent of the international variation in kinship intensity and 62 percent in cousin marriage;
   the Orthodox Church — which allowed cousins to marry until 692 and never banned past third
   cousins ("MFP-light") — has *no* effect on cousin marriage; a millennium of exposure goes with
   about 20 percentile points less conformity in the Asch task and 30 points less willingness to
   lie for a friend. The point of the Orthodox comparison: Christianity as a creed and Roman
   institutions are present in both; only the marriage rules differ — so the effect is attributed
   to the rules.
5. **Inside Europe: the Italian test.** Problem: cross-country comparisons hide too much — Henrich
   says so himself (l. 2445). Sharper design: compare regions *within* a country. Southern Italy was
   Christian early but outside the Carolingian realm, under Byzantine and Muslim rule, and under
   papal bishops only after the Norman conquest (11th–12th c.). Prediction: more cousin marriage and
   the kin-based psychology in the south. Record: 20th-century dispensations for cousin marriage
   below 0.4 percent in the north, over 4 percent at the toe of the boot; across regions of France,
   Spain, Italy and Turkey dosage explains most of the variation in cousin marriage; in Italy,
   cousin marriage tracks lower voluntary blood donation, cash rather than cheques, loans from
   family. Children of immigrants carry their parents' origin pattern into the second generation.
   Spiral (one sentence, `<Ref murray-human-accomplishment>`): the region Murray's count left
   outside Europe's core is the region Henrich's map places outside the Church's early reach — a
   convergence the reader should register and not over-read (the synthesis exercise works it).
6. **From families to institutions.** Obvious attempt: weaker clans just mean more isolated
   individuals. Henrich: people freed from lineages formed *voluntary associations* — monastic
   orders (Cluny 910, the Cistercians 1098), guilds, chartered towns with their own law (Magdeburg
   Law copied by eighty-odd cities; town charters limiting a father's liability for a son's crime),
   universities (Bologna, Paris) — whose members had abstract rights and duties as individuals; the
   longer a city's exposure to the Church, the larger it grew and the likelier it was to get a
   representative government (ch. 9). Canon law put intention at the centre of guilt and consent at
   the centre of marriage and of corporations ("What concerns everyone ought to be considered and
   approved by everyone"). Then the political-philosophy payoff (ch. 12, quoted briefly): Locke and
   Rousseau "began to understand society as a voluntary association — specifically, as a
   corporation"; the Enlightenment thinkers "were just the intellectuals and writers on the scene"
   when this psychology reached the nobility (`<Ref locke-property-and-consent>`,
   `<Ref rousseau-inequality-and-general-will>`). Seam (L10b — a real reversal the reader will
   feel): the course has treated the social contract as an argument; Henrich treats it as a
   symptom. Set against `<Ref hume-against-the-original-contract>` and `<Ref burke-reflections>`
   in one sentence each: Hume and Burke also denied that contracts founded governments, and
   Henrich's claim that people's theories of their institutions are made after the fact ("like water
   to fish", ch. 2) is close to Burke's — though Henrich draws no conservative conclusion from it.
   Answer: the institutions the course calls Western — representation, individual rights, impersonal
   law — are, on this account, products of a psychology, not of reasoning alone. Define
   `marriage-and-family-program`'s downstream effects in FIGURE 5 (the whole chain).
7. **The OBJECTIONS at full strength, and what the thesis survives.** In order of severity, each
   from its side:
   - *Reverse causation and hidden causes (methodological).* Prosperous, urban, commercial regions
     might both get bishops early and develop individualism for other reasons. Henrich's replies:
     dozens of controls (geography, fertility, latitude, waterways, disease, colonization), "no
     lightning-bolt factor", within-country comparisons, the Orthodox contrast, immigrants' children.
     His own concession stands: cross-country correlation is "not a great way to unearth 'what
     causes what'", and bishopric founding is a crude proxy for enforcement.
   - *Timing (historical).* The thesis needs low cousin marriage to *follow* the bans. Henrich's
     own endnote: Roman aristocrats already married cousins rarely under the early empire, and he
     found no data for the lower classes; Augustine reports cousin marriage "not frequent" in his
     day "though the law allows it" — before the bans spread. And there is no direct count of
     medieval cousin marriage; the effect is inferred from later dispensations, kinship
     vocabulary (the change to modern terms — "in-law" = in canon law — by c. 700 in Romance
     languages, c. 1100 in German and English) and household censuses. If the Western family's
     low cousin marriage predates the programme, the Church may have codified a pattern rather
     than created it.
   - *Scope and measures (empirical).* Many psychological measures come from student samples —
     the very population the 2010 paper warned about; the Ethnographic Atlas records norms, not
     behaviour, and is thin for Europe; several findings (priming, ch. 11) are single studies Henrich
     himself flags as speculative. State that the evidence is graded: strongest for kinship ↔
     psychology correlations, weaker for dosage → kinship within the Middle Ages, weakest for the
     later links to science and the Industrial Revolution.
   - *Two readings of the same history (normative, from the right).* What Henrich describes as the
     dissolution of intensive kinship is what Nisbet called the loss of the intermediate
     associations that stand between the individual and the state (`<Ref
     kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism>`), and what a defender of the clan, the lineage and
     the inherited obligation would count as a loss. Henrich's book records costs too (ch. 14:
     impersonal institutions transplanted into kin-based societies misfire; a WEIRD sense of self
     can lack meaning), so he does not dispute the description; he answers the objection only by
     declining to rank. The lesson says so: the two readings agree on the facts, and whether the
     change was a loss or a gain is a question of value that the evidence in this lesson does not
     settle.
   - *The genetic rival: Clark (required — round 2, author's review).* State it at full
     strength, as a contender for the same explanandum, not as a footnote. Henrich himself
     cites it (ch. 9 n.27: "Clark suggests that these psychological changes are likely due to
     genetic changes, but he leaves open the possibility that it's culture"). Clark's case:
     in English wills 1250–1800 the richest left more than twice as many surviving children as
     the poorest (*A Farewell to Alms* ch. 6, l.38–39), so the descendants of the prosperous
     spread through the population, carrying patience, literacy and application "probably
     mostly culturally, but also potentially genetically" (ch. 9, l.33); falling interest rates
     (the evidence Henrich borrows for rising patience, l.3510) are part of his record; and in
     2023 he showed that status in 422,374 related English people, 1600–2022, persists with a
     correlation falling by only 0.79 a generation, a pattern fitting additive genetic
     inheritance with assortative mating (*PNAS* 2023, l.24–34). Cochran & Harpending (2009)
     add the general claim that selection accelerated after agriculture (Hawks et al. 2007).
     Then what each side has: Henrich's answer (ch. 14, l.4232–4256) — cultural change is fast
     relative to selection over centuries; the institutions and the new psychology grew in
     towns that were "genetic death traps" (the urban graveyard: life expectancy up to 50%
     lower, populations kept up by immigration) and in celibate monasteries, so selection, if
     anything, ran against the traits; and twentieth-century selection ran against
     education-associated alleles while schooling rose. The record beyond Henrich: the
     rich-fertility advantage was not English but European (Allen 2008, l.1310–1315); Clark
     concedes "no proof here that additive genetic transmission causes social status" (2023,
     l.84) and in 2008 that "to conclude that the mechanism was genetic changes we need further
     information"; his own surname persistence is as high in China, Japan and Korea (2014,
     l.414–415); ancient DNA finds selection on alleles associated with test scores and
     schooling in West Eurasia, "largely driven by selection before ~2000 BP" (Akbari et al.
     2026, l.177). Whether Henrich answered it — a fact about the argument: he answered the
     urban form (the traits concentrated in towns) and showed a mechanism fast enough; he did
     not answer the rural-elite form Clark actually proposes (the rich were often landed and
     rural), and his graveyard argument does not touch Clark's 2023 persistence data, which
     post-date the book. Neither side has the test that would decide between them (dated
     polygenic scores for English, continental and Chinese lineages 1000–1800). The weighing
     of the five candidate causes is the module's closing lesson's (`<Ref the-borrowing-ledger>`,
     one clause): there the genetic candidate is graded **open**, its England-specific form
     failing **on balance**.
   - *Exceptionalism without superiority (from both sides).* Henrich's West is exceptional but not
     "better": its psychology is a historical accident of one Church's marriage policy, nothing in
     it is fixed or innate (ch. 14 on genes), and the same programme run by a modern state (the
     PRC's Marriage Law of 1950, Meiji and South Korean civil codes) produced similar shifts in
     decades. The exceptionalist can claim the institutions; the rival thesis can claim the
     contingency. State this as the hinge to the next lesson, without reproach either way.
   Where the lesson stands, objection by objection — whether Henrich answered it is a fact about
   the argument, and the lesson states it: *reverse causation* is answered in part (controls,
   within-country comparisons, the Orthodox contrast, immigrants' children) but not closed, by his
   own concession that cross-country correlation is a weak guide to cause; *timing* is not
   answered — his own endnote and Augustine leave open that low cousin marriage predates the
   bans, and there is no direct medieval count; *scope and measures* he partly concedes, which is
   why the evidence is graded. Henrich supplies the mechanism Landes's "culture" and Murray's
   "autonomy" lacked, with more evidence than either; his strongest links are the present-day
   correlations and the regional comparisons, his weakest the medieval timing. Whether kinship
   was *the* cause of the West's lead is not decided here: the module's closing lesson weighs it
   with fragmentation, Weber's ethic and high-medieval institutions and finds each partly
   supported and the cause **open** (`<Ref the-borrowing-ledger>`, one clause). The genetic rival is answered in part:
   Henrich shows culture fast enough and the urban selection running the wrong way, and leaves
   Clark's rural-elite fertility and his later persistence data unanswered.

   a bishop of the 410s–420s, arguing from Genesis; it records a custom and argues a principle; it

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **The label rule.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **"WEIRD" is an acronym, not a judgment.** The authors say so (endnote 1). The lesson uses it as
  a technical term, defines it once, and never plays on the word.
- **The 2010 paper vs the 2020 book.** The paper documents the outlier status and *refuses* to say
  where it came from; the book is Henrich's alone and proposes the origin. Never attribute the
  Church thesis to the 2010 paper or its co-authors.
- **"No one expected" (the opening).** The link between the Church's marriage rules and the
  dissolution of the European family was argued before Henrich (an anthropologist in 1983, economic
  historians after him — Henrich says he is "following the lead" of them, n. 10). Henrich's addition
  is the *psychological* link and the quantitative tests. The body should say this in one clause,
  without names (References carry them); the next lesson reads that anthropologist for a different
  thesis.
- **Proxy, not enforcement.** "Church dosage" is years since a bishopric under the Western Church was
  founded nearby — not a measure of how strictly the bans were obeyed. Say so when the measure is
  introduced.
- **Percentages of variance explained** (40, 62, 75 percent) must be framed in plain words the first
  time: "across countries, differences in Church exposure line up with about two-fifths of the
  differences in kinship intensity". Do not stack them; two keepers at most.
- **Ch. 8 (polygyny, testosterone, crime)** is the book's most biologically loaded chapter and is
  not needed for the chain; at most one sentence (monogamy for all men, "the same sexual rules for
  king and peasant" — Henrich quoting a historian) or nothing. Do not import the testosterone
  studies.
- **Immigrants' children (ch. 7)** — if mentioned, state exactly what is reported (the parents'
  origin pattern persists into the second generation, controlling for income and education) and
  nothing beyond it; no policy inference in the course's voice. Forward Ref only.
- **Genes:** Henrich's position (ch. 14) is that cultural evolution, not genetic selection,
  explains the differences, and that any genetic push may run the other way. Report it as his
  position; the hereditarian argument and its critics have their own lessons (`<Ref
  hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations>`, forward), where the question is weighed with its evidence;
  this lesson reports Henrich's position and passes no judgment on it.
- **Borrowings (if the collective-brain material enters):** keep the graded statements — paper
  via the Arab world (secure); gunpowder most likely via Mongol-era contact, no document of the
  transfer, Europeans plausibly unaware of its origin; Gutenberg most probably an independent
  invention (Henrich himself describes Gutenberg's press as a European recombination of the screw
  press, block printing and metal casting — consistent). The systems reply at full strength; the
  rival thesis never as a reproach. One sentence, `<Ref the-borrowing-ledger>` forward.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), max three per section:** clan / lineage / segmentary lineage; levirate
  (marrying a dead brother's widow) and sororate (marrying a dead wife's sister); affinal kin
  (in-laws); neolocal residence; godparent / spiritual kinship; dispensation (a Church licence to
  marry within a forbidden degree); bishopric; Carolingian Empire (the Frankish realm of
  Charlemagne, crowned emperor 800, map at c. 814); Fourth Lateran Council (Rome, 1215); Great
  Schism (1054, the formal split of the Western and Eastern Churches); Ethnographic Atlas (a coded
  database of about 1,300 societies' customs, recorded mostly around 1900 — Henrich's KII draws on
  it and related datasets covering 7,000+ groups); Asch conformity task; San (foragers of the
  Kalahari). Places: Kent, Wittenberg, Magdeburg, Bologna, Sicily — anchored at first mention.
- **Do not re-teach** Weber (one clause + Ref), Landes's fragmentation (one clause), Augustine's two
  cities (one clause), Maine's formula (one clause), Tocqueville's associations (one clause).
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — 96 percent of subjects from 12 percent of the world; bans out to sixth
  cousins (2,730 cousins excluded); cousin marriage 0.4 vs 4 percent, north vs south Italy. The rest
  in words or figures.
- **Dossier coverage:** both texts full. The underlying 2019 *Science* paper, Goody 1983 and Hajnal
  1965 are not in the dossier (References only, verified). Henrich's figures are absent from the
  extraction (captions only): describe, do not redraw, any published chart; the course's own
  figures below are schematic or built from numbers stated in the prose.

   different rules should not show the cousin-marriage effect — Henrich's Orthodox and Italian

6. **Synthesis (E3, spaced):** Murray's European core (`<Ref murray-human-accomplishment>`)

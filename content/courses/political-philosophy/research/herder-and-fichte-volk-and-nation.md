# Research brief: herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation (Herder and Fichte: the Volk, its language and the nation)

status: planned
module: contract-revolution-reaction
type: core
goal: Analyze Herder's account of peoples as distinct cultures each with its own measure, and Fichte's turn of it into a call for German national education, and evaluate what each makes of humanity and of rank among peoples

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `burke-reflections`: `entailed-inheritance` (liberty as an estate handed
  down), `prejudice` ("the general bank and capital of nations and of ages"), the
  `little-platoon`, and the charge that a people who design a society from principles get
  Terror and then a general. Burke's inheritance is a *constitution's*: the estate is the realm,
  the heirs are its orders. The open question this lesson answers: inheritance of what — a state
  or a people? From `maistre-and-bonald`: `no-such-thing-as-man` ("I have seen Frenchmen,
  Italians, Russians… but man I have never met") — only historically formed peoples exist; no
  constitution can be written for man in general. Maistre gets there through Providence and the
  throne; Herder got there first, through language and the people, without the throne. From
  `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: the general will of a sovereign people, and the
  Legislator who must "change human nature" — Fichte's national education is the nearest thing
  in the course to Rousseau's Legislator acting on a whole generation. From
  `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery` (optional, one clause): the claim that some
  peoples are by nature fit to rule others — the claim Herder rejects by name for "chosen
  peoples" and Fichte half-revives. Each one `<Ref>` and a clause; none re-taught.
- **Contributes:** One move, in two steps. (1) Herder relocates the subject of history and of
  inheritance from the state to the *Volk* — a community of language, custom and memory that
  precedes any state — and denies that peoples can be ranked on one ladder: each "bears in itself
  the standard of its perfection". (2) Thirty years later, under occupation, Fichte keeps the
  Volk and its language and makes three changes: language becomes the criterion of a nation and
  of its right to govern itself; one language is "living at the root" and the others "dead"; and
  the state becomes the instrument by which a nation educates itself into an earthly eternity.
  The lesson locates the exact step at which plurality becomes rank, puts to it the strongest
  objection — Herder's own humanitarianism — and Fichte's strongest reply, and judges the ranking
  (see `## VERDICT`: it fails, strong).
- **Hands off:** The lesson decides that Fichte's ranking of living over dead languages fails
  (strong): his reply answers the charges of racial nationalism and conquest, not the charge of
  rank. What passes on: (i) `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state` (next; prerequisite): Fichte puts the
  nation above the state and makes the state its means; Hegel answers that the state is "the
  actuality of the ethical Idea", the place where a people's ethical life becomes rational —
  absorbing both Burke's inheritance and the Volk. (ii) The criterion question — does a language,
  a descent, a memory or a will make a nation? — is not decided here; it passes to
  `renan-what-is-a-nation` (the daily plebiscite against language and race) and
  `barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism` (the nation accepted, not chosen). (iii) Herder's
  "no races, only peoples" is the baseline against which `gobineau-inequality-of-races` and
  `chamberlain-and-grant` will make descent the criterion. (iv) Herder's plurality of cultures is
  the ancestor of two later defences of a world of nations: `hazony-virtue-of-nationalism` and
  `de-benoist-european-new-right` (ethnopluralism, the right to difference). Name these only in
  the Conclusion's last sentence and in the FORWARD exercise; do not preview their content.
- **Spine step:** "Government by consent (Locke) is doubted by Hume and radicalised by
  Rousseau's general will; when France tries to frame a government from principles, Burke,
  Maistre and Donoso Cortés answer that no generation knows enough, Herder and Fichte put the
  people before the state, and Hegel absorbs both sides into the rational state"

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** Fichte's step from Herder's plurality of peoples to rank: German is a living
  original language, and the neo-Latin languages are "dead at the root" (Addresses, para 55),
  so the Germans hold "the seed of human perfection" (para 231).
- **Verdict:** the ranking fails. Grade: **strong**.
- **What decides it:** (1) Fichte's reply (descent disclaimed, para 45; membership open to anyone
  who believes in freedom, para 107; universal monarchy and remoulding others rejected, paras
  204, 206) answers the charges of racial nationalism and of conquest. It does not answer the
  charge of rank: para 55 withdraws its own disclaimer ("between life and death there is no
  comparison") and para 231 makes one nation the bearer of mankind's hope. (2) The criterion
  "living / dead at the root" has no stated test: Fichte names no observation that would show a
  speaker thinking more "livingly". (3) His own example cuts against it. *Humanität*, his
  specimen of a dead, laundered foreign word (para 53), is the word Herder made the end of
  history (Ideas XV.III). And *Menschlichkeit*, the "living" German word he offers in its place,
  was itself formed in the sixteenth century as a translation of Latin *hūmānitās* (Pfeifer,
  *Etymologisches Wörterbuch des Deutschen*, via DWDS: "16. Jh., Übersetzung von lat. hūmānitās").
  By his own test the living word is a calque on the dead one.
- **Strongest objection:** Fichte's claim is about history, not about words: a people that kept
  its language continuously keeps its images for invisible things transparent, whatever the
  origin of a given word, so one calque proves nothing; and the ranking is the rhetoric of a
  conquered people's self-defence, not a programme. **Why it does not overturn the verdict:**
  continuity of a language is a fact; that continuity makes its speakers think more "livingly"
  is the claim, and Fichte offers no test of it beyond his examples, of which the chosen one
  fails. The defensive context explains the rhetoric and is granted (Fichte's reply holds against
  the conquest charge), but para 231 claims more than defence needs.
- **What would overturn it:** a measurable sense in which speakers of a continuously inherited
  language think more "livingly" than speakers of an adopted one.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

**Opening (course.yaml, verbatim):** the philosophers of the 1770s judged every past age by how
close it came to their own century; a Lutheran pastor objects that each people had its own centre
of happiness, language and way of thinking; thirty years later, in a Berlin occupied by Napoleon, a
philosopher tells his audience the Germans alone still speak a living original language, and that
this gives them a mission; what changed on the way.

**Anchor (L11), immediately after the opening, one sentence each.** Johann Gottfried Herder
(1744–1803): East Prussian, student of Kant at Königsberg, teacher and preacher in Riga
(1764–69), court preacher at Bückeburg, a small German principality, when he wrote the 1774 book
(printed anonymously by a Riga publisher), then head of the Lutheran clergy at Weimar, where he wrote
the Ideas (1784–91). (The opening's "pastor in Riga" is loose — he had left Riga by 1769; the anchor
sentence states the facts without contradicting the opening; see Pitfalls.) Johann Gottlieb Fichte
(1762–1814): the most famous philosopher in Germany after Kant, dismissed from Jena in 1799 over a
charge of atheism; after Napoleon crushed Prussia at Jena (October 1806) and dictated the Peace of
Tilsit (July 1807, Prussia halved), he gave fourteen Sunday lectures in Berlin in the winter of
1807–08, with French troops in the city and the censor reading each text. The newcomer's problem:
`burke-reflections` (Ref) said a people inherits its liberty like an estate. Inherits it *as
what* — as the subjects of a constitution, or as a people with its own language whatever state it
lives under? And if each people has its own measure, can any people be better than another?

1. **Can one age judge another by its own standard?** Obvious attempt (the Enlightenment
   historians Herder argues with — Voltaire, Hume, Iselin, named as his targets, as he names
   them): yes; history is a ladder, and the present stands on the top rung; earlier ages are
   childhood and darkness. Herder's first answer turns the ladder's own image against it: the ages
   of life (part1 l.636–648) — the patriarchal Orient the child, Egypt the boy at his school-bench,
   Greece the youth, Rome the man; "the boy loathed the child in its swaddling clothes, the youth
   hated the boy's school-prison", yet each age was the condition of the next and none is a failed
   attempt at another. Why the ladder fails: each age formed only what its time, climate, need and
   fate gave occasion for — "every human perfection is national, of its century, and, looked at
   most precisely, individual" (l.1606). And its virtues and vices grow from the same root: the
   noble Spartan and the abused Helot belong to one order (l.1760; part2 l.2922 "humanity is no
   vessel of perfection"). Answer: each nation "has its centre of happiness within itself, as
   every sphere has its centre of gravity" (l.1852) — so the question "which people was happiest?"
   has no answer. Because: happiness is a fit between a soul and what it strove for, not a quantity
   on one scale ("who can compare?" l.1838). Define **`plurality-of-cultures`** here.
2. **Does that make Herder a relativist who denies progress?** Real misreading (L13): it does
   not. Herder keeps a "path of God over the nations" and a progress of the whole "even if no
   individual gained" — the tree whose crown cannot ask to be the root (Zusätze); no age can be
   revived ("you Ptolemies could not make Egypt again!" part2 l.1458). And in the Ideas he names a
   universal end: Humanität, "reason and equity in all conditions" (XV.III). The distinction the
   lesson needs, stated as the course's own: *pluralism* — many ways of life, each with real values
   an outsider can understand and recognise as values without adopting them — is not *relativism*,
   where nothing can be said across the line. Herder is the first; the tension between his
   incommensurable "standards of perfection" and his one Humanität is real and is used again in
   link 8.
3. **What does prejudice do for a people?** Seam (L10b): from the Enlightenment one expects
   "prejudice" to be the enemy; Herder defends it twenty years before Burke — "Prejudice is good,
   in its time, for it makes happy. It presses peoples together toward their centre" (part1
   l.1920–1925); "the age of longing for foreign lands… is already sickness". Then his satire of
   his own century (part2 l.695–705): "With us, thank God, all national characters are
   extinguished!… we have no fatherland… but we are friends of mankind and citizens of the world."
   The comparison with `burke-reflections` (Ref, one clause: prejudice as the bank of nations and
   ages): Burke's prejudice keeps a *constitution* in being; Herder's keeps a *people* in being.
   That is the answer to the lesson's first problem — the unit of inheritance is the people.
4. **What makes a people one people?** Obvious attempts: blood, or climate. Herder rejects both
   as sufficient. Climate "does not force, but incline" (VII.III); "race" is rejected as a word —
   "Race refers to a difference of origin, which in this case does not exist… every nation is one
   people, having its own national form, as well as its own language" (VII.I p.166), "neither four
   or five races". What does bind: tradition received "like his mother's milk" (VIII.II), and above
   all language — "A nation has no idea, for which its language has no word" (IX.II); "the genius
   of a people is no where more displayed than in the physiognomy of their language". Define
   **`volk`** here: a people as a community of language, custom, memory and character prior to any
   state. Then the political corollary (IX.IV hint 2): "a nation is as much a natural plant as a
   family, only with more branches"; the most natural state is "one nation, with one national
   character"; empires that glue peoples together are "machines of state", Daniel's beast, a Trojan
   horse "formed of clay". The "because": a state that does not grow from a people has no life of
   its own and is held together only by force.
5. **What follows for rule and for empire?** Herder draws three conclusions, all against power
   that overrides peoples. (a) Against the claim that man "needs a master" (IX.IV hint 1 — reversed:
   "the man who needs a master is a mere animal") and against the "right of blood" of hereditary
   rule (p.245). (b) Against the state as the end of history: "the state gives us nothing but
   instruments of art, and these may rob us of ourselves" (VIII.V); Providence separated nations by
   language "that the work of subjugating despotism might be rendered more difficult". (c) Against
   European empire: Europe "compelling all the nations of the Earth to be happy in her way"
   (VIII.V); the slave trade — "this brands you as robbers, as stealers of men" (VII.II); "neither
   the pongo nor the gibbon is thy brother: the american and the negro are" (VII.I). And against
   rootless cosmopolitanism: "the deluged heart of the idle cosmopolite is a hut for no one".
   Herder's people stand *before* the state, and the state is judged by whether it serves them.
   (The reading note about Herder's own period ethnic judgments goes here, as a `<Remark>` — see
   Pitfalls.)
6. **Thirty years on: what does a conquered people have left?** New problem, Fichte's. Prussia's
   army and state have collapsed in one campaign. First Address: states held their subjects by
   fear and hope; self-seeking dissolved the bond until men fought "under foreign colours against
   their mother-country"; the conqueror now holds fear and hope and will use them in his own
   interest. What bond can a people keep that the conqueror cannot control? Fichte's answer: moral
   love, produced by "a total change of the existing system of education" — a "new self", given to
   every German without distinction of rank. Seam with `burke-reflections` (Ref): Fichte agrees
   with Burke's diagnosis of 1789 — "the State in accordance with reason cannot be built up by
   artificial measures from whatever material may be at hand" (para 80) — and draws the opposite
   remedy: not to keep the inheritance, but first to make the material: "the nation must first be
   trained and educated up to it".
7. **Why the Germans, and what is a nation?** Fichte's criterion, derived in his order (Fourth
   Address). The Germans and the French, English and others are branches of one Teutonic stock.
   Not place: changing one's sky "is quite unimportant" (para 45). Not descent: all branches
   intermarried, with Slavs at home as with Gauls abroad, so none could show "a greater purity of
   descent" (para 45). Language: the Germans kept theirs; the others took up Latin — "men are
   formed by language far more than language is formed by men" (para 46), and a people's
   knowledge "expresses itself out of the mouth of the people" (para 47). A language continuously
   spoken stays alive "at the root": its words for invisible things are still images the speaker
   can see through; an adopted one moves "on the surface only" (para 55). The worked example (para
   53): "the three notorious words, Humanity, Popularity, and Liberality" — to a German they are
   noise and launder vices; *Menschlichkeit* would be understood at once. Define
   **`language-as-nation`** here, with Fichte's two corollaries: a separate language makes a
   separate nation "which has the right to… govern itself", and a people that has ceased to
   govern itself "is equally bound to give up its language" (para 191); the first and natural
   boundaries are "internal boundaries" of speech, before rivers and mountains (para 198). Then
   the nation as eternal (Eighth Address): a people is "the totality of men… continually creating
   themselves… out of themselves" under a "special law of divine development" (para 114); a
   noble-minded man wants his work to last on earth, and only his people's continuance can
   carry it (para 115); so love of fatherland must "govern the State itself" (para 118), and the
   state's ordinary aims — peace, property, well-being — are its means. The education that
   serves this: it "completely destroys freedom of will" and produces "strict necessity in the
   decisions of the will" (para 14); children are "entirely separated" from parents (para 138);
   the state may compel parents as it conscripts soldiers (para 170).
8. **THE WEIGHING. Heading as the verdict: "Fichte's ranking of languages fails his own test"
   (or "Plurality becomes rank, and the rank has no test").** The last body section: it sets the
   ranking against Herder's objection, Fichte's best reply and the evidence, and reaches the
   verdict of `## VERDICT` (strong). **Where does plurality become rank? The OBJECTION at full
   strength, from Herder.** Locate the
   step precisely, as a `<FigureSequence>` (Figure 5): Herder — every people its own centre and
   standard; Fichte keeps language as the mark of a people (continuity); then says one language is
   alive and the rest dead at the root (the step); then that this people holds "the seed of human
   perfection", and if it perishes "every hope of the whole human race" perishes with it (para 231).
   The disclaimer in para 55 ("not… intrinsic value") is withdrawn in the same breath: "between
   life and death there is no comparison". The objection, in Herder's own words, written before
   Fichte spoke: to esteem the Germans "God's chosen people in Europe… would be to display the base
   pride of a barbarian" (XVI.VI); each nation "bears in itself the standard of its perfection,
   totally independent of all comparison with that of others" (XV.III). Note the irony the lesson
   should state flatly: the word Fichte picks as his example of a dead, laundered foreign word,
   *Humanität*, is the word Herder made the end of history — and *Menschlichkeit*, the living word
   he sets against it, was formed in the sixteenth century as a translation of Latin *hūmānitās*
   (Pfeifer via DWDS). This is evidence, used in the verdict below. A second objection, from
   the liberal side, one sentence: Constant, of the Fichtean circle in 1804, that their "Spartan
   ideals" amid modern commerce would "begin Robespierre all over again" if they came to power
   (quoted as Constant's; source in References).
   **Fichte's reply at full strength** (the lesson gives it its full weight before judging): his
   nation is not a race — descent is disclaimed (para 45) and membership is open to anyone who
   "believes in spirituality and in the freedom of this spirituality… wherever he may have been
   born and whatever language he speaks" (para 107); he rejects universal monarchy and any people's
   right to remould others in its image (paras 204, 206); his argument is the defence of a conquered
   people, not a programme of conquest; and Herder himself grounded rule in nations and condemned
   multinational empires, which is Fichte's para 198 in other words. **Where the lesson concedes
   and where it stops:** Fichte's reply holds against the charge of racial nationalism and of
   conquest; it does not answer the charge of rank, because para 107 makes "German" a name for a
   philosophy — those who believe otherwise are "non-German… and it is to be wished that he would
   separate himself from us completely" — and para 231 makes one nation the bearer of mankind's
   hope. And Herder has his own unsettled tension (link 2): a universal Humanität that lets him
   condemn the slave trade sits beside incommensurable standards. **The verdict, in the course's
   voice:** the ranking fails (strong). Fichte's reply answers racial nationalism and conquest, not
   rank; "living / dead at the root" has no stated test; and his own example cuts against it
   (*Menschlichkeit* is a calque on *hūmānitās*). State the strongest objection — the claim is
   about a language's continuous history, not about the origin of single words — and why it does
   not save the ranking (continuity is a fact; that it makes thought more "living" is the
   unsupported step). Say what would overturn the verdict: a measurable sense in which speakers of
   a continuously inherited language think more "livingly". Then the wider result: Herder's
   pluralism needs some universal standard to condemn anything; Fichte supplied one and gave it to
   one people.
9. **Close (Conclusion).** First the verdict in plain words with its grade: Fichte's step from
   many peoples to one living people fails — strong — because the rank has no test and his own
   example refutes it; his reply holds only against the charges of race and conquest. Then what
   the reader can now do: say what Herder means by a Volk and why
   peoples cannot be ranked; reconstruct Fichte's criterion (language), his nation-above-state and
   his national education; point to the step at which plurality becomes rank. What stays open: if
   the nation stands above the state, what is the state? — `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`
   (next). One closing sentence on reception, as fact: the Addresses were invoked by the
   liberators of 1813, by the first president of the Weimar Republic in 1919 for a democracy of
   "equality of all humanity" (translators' Introduction, p. xxii — the phrase at l.626), and by the
   nationalism of the twentieth century. Whether language, descent or will makes a nation is the
   question of the crowd-race-nation module (Ref `renan-what-is-a-nation`).

Headings as claims (suggested): "Every age has its own centre of gravity"; "Pluralism is not
relativism"; "Prejudice holds a people together"; "A people is made by its language, not its
blood"; "A people before the state"; "A conquered nation must make itself anew"; "Living and dead
languages"; "The nation as earthly eternity"; "Fichte's ranking of languages fails his own test"
(the weighing).

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The ages of life** (AEP part1 l.636–648): the four stages with what each despised in the
   last; then the reversal: "eben ihr Haß zeigt Entwickelung… Stufen der Leiter" (their hatred
   itself shows development). The image that looks like the Enlightenment ladder and is used to
   abolish it as a standard.
2. **The centre of gravity** (part1 l.1838–1853): the shepherd, the merchant, the conqueror — "the
   laurel wreath or the sight of the blessed herd… is nothing; everything lies in the soul that
   needed it" — then the sphere sentence in German and English.
3. **"Prejudice makes happy"** (part1 l.1915–1937) set beside Burke's "general bank and capital
   of nations and of ages" (Ref): two defences of prejudice, one of a constitution, one of a people.
4. **"Every nation is one people"** (Ideas VII.I p.166) read whole with the pongo-and-gibbon
   sentence: the earliest text in the course that rejects "race" by argument (a difference of
   origin that does not exist; complexions that run into one another).
5. **The Trojan horse** (Ideas IX.IV p.249–250): nation as a plant with branches; empire as
   Daniel's composite beast and a Trojan horse "formed of clay" — the political conclusion of the
   Volk in one image.
6. **The two branches** (Addresses paras 44–46): stay or migrate, keep or change language — and
   Fichte's own elimination of place and descent, so the reader sees language left standing alone.
7. **"Humanity, Popularity, Liberality"** (para 53): Fichte's showpiece of a dead word laundering
   vice, then the irony with Herder's Humanität (link 8).
8. **The two Herder sentences against para 231** (XVI.VI p.489 "base pride of a barbarian"; XV.III
   p.452 "independent of all comparison") — the objection made decades before the claim.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), no more than three per section:** *Volk* (German "people"; keep the
  German word once, then "people"); *Humanität* (Herder's "humanity" as the end of human nature:
  reason and equity — not "humanitarianism"); *Bildung* (formation, education — in the 1774 title);
  *Urvolk/Ursprache* — the translation says "original people", "original language"; give the German
  once at para 114; "neo-Latin peoples" (Fichte's term for French, Italians, Spaniards — the
  Romance-language peoples); "Teutonic" (Fichte's word for the Germanic family, not modern
  "Germany"); Jena and Auerstedt (October 1806, the twin battles in Thuringia where Napoleon
  destroyed the Prussian army in one day); Tilsit (July 1807, the peace that took half Prussia's
  territory); the Wends (para 190: a Slavic people of Lusatia whose language survived "only in serf
  hovels"); Pestalozzi (Swiss educator, one clause, if para 133ff is mentioned); the War of
  Liberation (1813, the German rising against Napoleon).
- **Places (L11a):** Riga (Baltic port, then in the Russian Empire, German-speaking town), Bückeburg,
  Weimar, Jena, Berlin, Königsberg — on the locator figure (Figure 3) at first mention.
- **The opening's "pastor in Riga".** The 1774 book was written at Bückeburg; Herder had left Riga
  in 1769. The opening must be used verbatim (L3); the anchor sentence gives the correct facts in
  passing ("a Riga pastor until 1769, by then court preacher at Bückeburg"). A course.yaml fix is
  recommended (see final report), not made here.
- **Real misreadings (L13), one slot each, no strawman:** (a) "Herder is a relativist / an
  enemy of progress" — refute from the Zusätze and Ideas XV (link 2). (b) "Fichte's nation is
  defined by blood/race" — refute from paras 45–46 and 107; then show what the reply does not
  cover (link 8). (c) A third, handled in one sentence not a slot: Herder is often read backwards
  through later German nationalism; his texts against German chosenness (XVI.VI) and against
  empire are the record.
- **Herder's period judgments.** The same Ideas that reject race contain a hereditary "negro form"
  (VII.IV), a remark placing Africans near the ape (XV.I), and Jews called "parasitical plants"
  (XVI.V) beside the forecast that no one will ask whether a man is Jew or Christian. State them in
  a `<Remark>` at link 5, quoted with book and chapter, without either suppressing them or using
  them to discount the argument; the lesson's point is that Herder's anti-race argument and these
  sentences sit in one book.
- **Fichte's text is rhetoric delivered as lectures;** many claims (the history of languages,
  Luther, the medieval cities) are asserted, not argued — say so once (Fichte himself admits it of
  experience, Fifth Address para 58). The lesson reconstructs the argument where there is one
  (paras 44–55, 80, 107, 114–118, 191, 198) and does not try to make the rest an argument.
- **The education programme is not decoration.** "Destroys freedom of will" (para 14) and
  separating children from parents (para 138) are Fichte's words; quote them, place them beside
  Rousseau's Legislator (Ref `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`, one clause), and do not
  either soften or dramatise them.
- **Do not re-teach** Burke's prejudice or inheritance, Maistre's "no such thing as man",
  Rousseau's Legislator — one clause and a `<Ref>` each.
- **Do not preview** Renan, Barrès, Gobineau, Hazony or de Benoist beyond a name in the closing
  sentence and in the FORWARD exercise. Do not present later uses of Fichte (1813, 1919, the
  twentieth century) as his meaning or as a verdict on him; they are reception, one sentence.
- **L8a — names.** May be named in the body: Herder, Fichte, Burke, Maistre, Rousseau, Hegel, Kant
  (as Herder's unnamed target, one clause), Voltaire, Hume, Montesquieu (as Herder's targets),
  Constant (one quotation), Napoleon, Luther. References only: Berlin, Hausheer, Mali, Wokler,
  Beiser, Gossman, Momigliano, Forster, Jones and Turnbull (translators), Churchill (translator),
  Suphan (editor). The pluralism/relativism distinction and the "wounded pride" reading of German
  nationalism, if used, are stated as the course's own.
- **Numbers (L12):** dates only — 1774, 1784–91, 1806, 1807–08, 1813 — and "fourteen lectures".
  Keeper facts: the two dates (1774, 1807–08) and the one sentence each (the centre of gravity;
  "formed by language far more than language is formed by men").
- **Dossier thin:** no English text of the 1774 book (the writer translates; renderings are given
  above); Kant's Proposition 6 and his review of the Ideas are not in the dossier; Herder's Treatise
  on the Origin of Language (1772) and his Letters for the Advancement of Humanity are not in the
  dossier and are not needed; no Strauss–Cropsey chapter for either author.
- **Translation quality.** Churchill (1800) is archaic and OCR'd with long-s as "f": quote with
  modern s, keep the 1800 wording otherwise, gloss once that the translation is of 1800. Jones and
  Turnbull's paragraph numbers are Fichte's; cite by paragraph.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (the one warm-up):** put in order and date to the decade — Herder's
   1774 book, Burke's Reflections, the battle of Jena, the Addresses, the War of Liberation — and
   attach to each of three sentences its author: "each nation has its centre of happiness within
   itself"; "men are formed by language far more than language is formed by men"; prejudice as
   "the general bank and capital of nations and of ages" (Ref `burke-reflections`).
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill on para 191** ("wherever a separate language is found…
   where a people has ceased to govern itself, it is equally bound to give up its language"):
   metadata first (a Prussian professor, winter 1807–08, a Berlin audience under French
   occupation, a text passed by a censor); predict, then read; what it records (the conqueror's
   language policy he expects), argues (language gives a right of self-government), omits (the
   Wends of para 190, the Poles under Prussian rule); what it can and cannot be used to establish.
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Herder taught that peoples are races and that the Germans were
   destined to lead them." Name the two passages that break it (VII.I p.166; XVI.VI p.489) and the
   step that fails (race as difference of origin; chosenness as "the base pride of a barbarian"),
   and say what later doctrine the claim is reading back into him.
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** Fichte gives two criteria of "German" — continuous language (para 46)
   and belief in freedom and endless improvement (para 107). Construct the two degenerate cases (a
   German speaker who believes in "the round dance"; a French speaker who believes in freedom), say
   where each belongs by each criterion, and decide which criterion governs para 191's right of
   self-government. The solution shows that para 107 makes the nation a community of belief and
   para 191 a community of speech, and that the argument for self-government uses only the second.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** from Herder's thesis that each nation "bears in itself
   the standard of its perfection, totally independent of all comparison" (XV.III), derive whether
   he can condemn the slave trade (VII.II). Then find the standard he actually uses (Humanität,
   "reason and equity in all conditions"; "they are men, like thee") and state what that does to
   the thesis — a bounded pluralism, not an unbounded one.
6. **Corroboration (E3, earlier lessons):** set Burke on 1789 (no generation can design a
   society; keep the inheritance — Ref `burke-reflections`) beside Fichte's para 80 ("the nation
   must first be trained and educated up to it"), and Maistre's "man I have never met" (Ref
   `maistre-and-bonald`) beside Herder's "every nation is one people". For each pair: where they
   agree, where they split, and which split matters for what the state is allowed to do to a
   people.
7. **Synthesis — counter-argument:** "Fichte's nationalism is the defence of a conquered people
   and cannot be a doctrine of rank." Name two facts the claim must account for (para 55's "between
   life and death there is no comparison"; para 231's "seed of human perfection") and two that
   support it (paras 45 and 107 disclaiming descent; paras 204 and 206 against universal monarchy
   and remoulding others), then state what would have to be true for it to survive.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `renan-what-is-a-nation`:** apply paras 191 and 198 to Alsace after 1871 — a
   German-speaking province annexed by the new German Empire whose people, by most accounts,
   wanted to stay French. Which state does Fichte's rule assign it to, and what does it make of the
   inhabitants' wish? The solution states the result (language assigns Alsace to Germany; the
   people's will is not a criterion in paras 191/198) and says in one sentence that this is the case
   a French scholar's 1882 lecture answers by making a nation a matter of shared memory and present
   consent, with a `<Ref>` to `renan-what-is-a-nation`.
9. **Attack the verdict.** The lesson judged that Fichte's ranking of living over dead languages
   fails (strong). State the strongest objection in its best form — Fichte's claim concerns a
   language's unbroken history and the transparency of its images for abstract things, so the
   origin of one word (*Menschlichkeit* as a sixteenth-century calque on *hūmānitās*) proves
   nothing; and German, unlike French, did keep its own stock of roots for such words — then say
   what evidence would be needed to overturn the verdict. The model answer grants the
   distinction between a word and a language, then asks what observation would show that a
   speaker "sees through" his words to images more than another speaker does, notes that Fichte
   names none, and states the overturning evidence: a measurable difference in some cognitive or
   linguistic performance between speakers of continuously inherited and adopted languages, of
   the kind Fichte's thesis predicts.

# Research brief: hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations (The hereditarian case: The Bell Curve on group differences and IQ and the Wealth of Nations)

status: planned
module: heredity-and-civilization
type: core
goal: Analyze at full strength the argument that average differences in measured intelligence between groups and nations are partly genetic and explain part of the gaps in wealth between them, with its evidence

Register rule (binding on the lesson): The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.

This lesson states the hereditarian case as its authors make it, with the evidence each book cites, and neither endorses nor dismisses it. The objections are the next lessons' material (`reich-ancient-dna-and-the-indo-europeans`, `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`, `geography-or-institutions`, `national-data-audited`): this lesson names them in one section and develops none. Where the authors themselves raise a difficulty and answer it (the within/between point, the Flynn effect, China's lag, the outliers), the lesson gives the difficulty and their answer, because that is part of the case at full strength.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `the-cognitive-elite`: the general factor g (the common factor behind the
  positive correlations among mental tests, its interpretation contested), heritability (a share
  of variation within a given population in given environments, not a measure of fixity), the
  AFQT and the NLSY79 survey, the standard deviation, and The Bell Curve's class argument — with
  the race chapters (13–14) explicitly held for this lesson. From `gobineau-inequality-of-races`
  and `chamberlain-and-grant`: the racial theory of history (civilizations rise and fall with the
  hereditary qualities of their builders), Nordicism, the 1924 Immigration Act, and the
  objections of their own day (Tocqueville's letters; Boas's head-form study). From
  `the-borrowing-ledger` and the why-the-west module: two families of answers to the gap between
  the West and the rest (exceptionalist: culture, fragmentation, kinship; rival: late divergence,
  coal and colonies, a chain of exchange) — both of which leave the peoples' own capacities
  equal; and the fact that China led in three of the four techniques of the ledger until c. 1450.
- **Contributes:** The one move: the third family of answers at its strongest. Two books, two
  scales. (1) Within the United States (The Bell Curve, chs. 13–14, 1994): the average gap in
  measured intelligence between groups is real on the tests and not explained by test bias;
  socioeconomic status explains part of it; heritability within groups does not by itself make
  the gap genetic, but several lines of indirect evidence (the size of the environmental
  difference a purely environmental account would need, Spearman's hypothesis, the pattern
  across income levels, the transracial adoption data) make a partly genetic cause, in the
  authors' words, "highly likely", while they stay "resolutely agnostic" on the mix; and with IQ
  held equal several group gaps in outcomes shrink or vanish. (2) Across nations (IQ and the
  Wealth of Nations, 2002): average measured intelligence assembled for 81 countries from
  published test samples (and estimated for 104 more) correlates with income per head at about
  .7, has done so since the earliest data, and on the authors' account causes part of the gap,
  partly through genes, with markets and democracy as the main other factor. The lesson
  introduces `hereditarian-hypothesis` and `national-iq`, and the Flynn effect as a fact both
  books discuss.
- **Hands off:** The objections, in the order the module takes them: what ancient DNA shows about
  population history and the "pure race" (`reich-ancient-dna-and-the-indo-europeans`); the
  within/between inference, the reification of g, the history of the measures, and the genetics
  of individual differences that do not transfer to groups (`gould-and-harden-on-heredity`); the
  rival explanations of national wealth from geography and institutions
  (`geography-or-institutions`); and the audit of the national-IQ table itself, entry by entry
  (`national-data-audited`). The `<Conclusion>` names these as the open questions, in one
  paragraph, without previewing their answers.
- **Spine step:** "Heredity as an explanation of the gaps between peoples, at full strength (The Bell Curve, national IQ, ancient DNA), met by its objections (Gould, Harden) and by the rival explanations from geography and institutions — and the key datasets audited"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): average scores differ between groups within and between
countries and track income; everyone agrees on that much; the dispute is why; two books argued
that part of the answer is genetic — one in 1994 about groups within the United States, one in
2002 ranking 81 nations; the lesson reconstructs both at their strongest, with the evidence each
cites, before the next lessons put the objections. Anchors (L11): Richard Herrnstein (Harvard
psychologist, d. 1994) and Charles Murray (political scientist — met in `the-cognitive-elite`,
and in `murray-human-accomplishment`, a different book by the same author: one clause to prevent
confusion); Richard Lynn (British psychologist) and Tatu Vanhanen (Finnish political scientist,
author of democratization indices). The newcomer's problem, set from the previous module: the
exceptionalist and rival theses both explained the West's distinction while holding the peoples'
capacities equal; a third family of answers, as old as Gobineau (Ref), denies that premise. What
distinguishes its modern form is that it claims to measure.

1. **What exactly is claimed?** Obvious attempt: "some groups are smarter by nature". Why it is
   too loose: it mixes four claims of very different status. Separate them, as the books do:
   (i) average scores on standardised tests differ between groups (a measurement); (ii) the tests
   measure the same thing in each group and predict outcomes equally well (no predictive bias);
   (iii) part of the average difference has a genetic cause (the contested inference); (iv) the
   difference explains part of the gaps in income between groups and nations (a causal claim
   about society). Define `hereditarian-hypothesis` here as (iii) + (iv) — the word "partly" is
   load-bearing. A figure fixes the four rungs; every later section says which rung it is on.
2. **Rung (i): how big is the gap, and in whose numbers?** Recall the SD and percentiles (Ref
   `the-cognitive-elite`). The book's figures: black–white gap about one SD, stable for decades
   (156 studies averaging 1.08 SD; the NLSY AFQT 1.21 SD); East Asian Americans a few points above
   white, more on visuospatial tasks; Ashkenazi Jews half to one SD above; Latinos half to one
   SD below; men and women equal in mean, men more variable. Two qualifications the authors insist
   on, and the lesson keeps: individuals differ far more than group means ("If all the ethnic
   differences in intelligence evaporated overnight, most of the intellectual variation in America
   would endure"); and the gap had narrowed by two or three points in twenty years before
   stalling in the mid-1980s. KEEPER: "about one SD; individual variation far larger than group".
3. **Rung (ii): is it the test?** Obvious attempt: the tests are culturally biased. The book's
   three tests of bias (ch. 13; App. 5): external — a biased test would underpredict the group's
   later performance in school or work, and it does not (if anything it overpredicts); internal —
   items rank in the same order of difficulty in both groups, and the gap is larger on abstract,
   "culturally neutral" items than on information items; motivational — the gap is larger on
   backward than forward digit span and on reaction time than movement time, tasks that differ in
   mental load, not in effort. Answer, as the authors state it: whatever the gap is, it is not a
   bias of the tests in the predictive sense. (They concede that a "uniform background bias" —
   one that depresses everything equally — cannot be tested this way.)
4. **Is it poverty?** Obvious attempt: the gap is socioeconomic. Their evidence: controlling for
   parental SES shrinks it by about a third; but SES is itself partly an effect of ability; and
   the gap is no smaller — in standard units somewhat larger — among the better-off. Answer: SES
   accounts for part, not most.
5. **Rung (iii): from heritability to a genetic gap — the step the authors themselves guard.**
   Recall heritability (Ref `the-cognitive-elite`). The authors state the obstacle in their own
   words: that a trait is heritable in individuals "does not mean that group differences in that
   trait are also genetic in origin" — genetically identical seed corn planted in Iowa and in the
   Mojave. The lesson must make this point theirs (it is the opening of `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`
   from the other side). Their case is therefore indirect, four arguments:
   (a) *The size of the environment a purely environmental account needs.* With heritability .6
   within groups, a 1 SD gap would require the average black environment to be 1.58 SD worse than
   the average white — at the 6th percentile of white environments — and the average East Asian
   environment at the 63rd; the authors call environmental differences of that size and pattern
   implausible, and note that the gap does not narrow with SES. Show the arithmetic once (a gap of
   1 SD divided by the square root of the non-genetic share, 1/√0.4 ≈ 1.58) in an `<Example>`,
   then state in one sentence the assumption it rests on: that the environmental causes of the
   between-group gap act on the same scale as the environmental differences within groups. Do
   not develop the objection; mark it as the next lesson's first question.
   (b) *Spearman's hypothesis.* The more a test measures g, the larger the black–white gap — in
   the book's account confirmed in every large data set examined, including reaction-time tasks;
   since g is the most heritable part of test performance, the pattern "undercuts" accounts that
   blame bad tests (the authors' word; they say it does not by itself demand a genetic
   explanation).
   (c) *Adoption.* The Minnesota Transracial Adoption Study: children adopted into white
   middle- and upper-middle-class families ranked by ancestry at both testings — at about age 7,
   adopted white 112, mixed 109, black 97 (the families' own children 117) — the black and mixed
   adoptees well above the black average, which the book reads as "a considerable impact of the
   home setting"; in adolescence 106, 99, 89 (own children 109), the 89 "slightly above the
   national black mean but not above the black mean for the North Central United States", and a
   seventeen-point gap between black and white adoptees (l.3210–3216, verified). The authors read the
   results as consistent with a mixed genetic and environmental cause — "which seems to us the
   most plausible conclusion" — as the study's own authors did; they note the debate over the data
   is unsettled.
   (d) *Africa.* African samples (from Lynn) below African Americans, which the authors take to
   cut against a purely American-history explanation — a datum the lesson reports as theirs and
   leaves to `national-data-audited`.
   Then the conclusion, quoted: "It seems highly likely to us that both genes and the environment
   have something to do with racial differences. What might the mix be? We are resolutely agnostic
   on that issue" (l.3227). And the thought experiment (l.3233–3247): if the gap were known to be
   wholly genetic, nothing in the treatment of individuals should change — "an IQ of 120 is an IQ
   of 120"; and environmental causes are not necessarily easier to change ("environments are
   heritable too"). KEEPER: "highly likely both; agnostic on the mix".
6. **The Flynn effect — a fact the case itself must handle.** Problem the authors raise
   themselves under "Arguments Against a Genetic Explanation": average scores on IQ tests have
   risen in many countries by up to a point a year over some spans, so that whites "today may
   differ in IQ from whites … two generations ago as much as whites today differ from blacks
   today", and changes that fast must be environmental (l.3198–3200). Define the Flynn effect
   here (named after the political scientist who documented it; name in References). Their
   answers: there is "no reason to believe that white and Asian means can be made to stand still
   while the Flynn effect works its magic"; the rise may be in test-taking skills rather than g;
   it is concentrated in the lower half of the distribution (l.3202–3204). They also concede, in
   the same section, that "the instability of test scores across generations should caution
   against taking the current ethnic differences as etched in stone" (l.3208) — quote it: it is
   the case's own boundary. Lynn and Vanhanen meet
   the same fact twice: they correct every old national sample for it (≈2–3 points per decade,
   App. 1), and they attribute the rise to better nutrition before age two, ruling out schooling
   because the gains appear in two- and four-year-olds (pp. 185–186). Seam: the effect is the
   strongest evidence that large shifts in measured intelligence can be environmental; both books
   concede the shift and dispute what it implies for a gap between groups.
7. **Rung (iv) at the scale of nations: national IQ.** Problem: how would one measure the average
   intelligence of a country? Lynn and Vanhanen's method (ch. 6; App. 1): collect published
   studies that gave a standard test (mostly Raven's Progressive Matrices) to a sample in a
   country; convert each score to a British scale (mean 100, SD 15) using the test's norms;
   correct for the years between norming and testing (the Flynn correction); average studies with
   equal weight — 81 countries this way; for 104 more, estimate from neighbours or from the
   country's ethnic composition (Afghanistan as the mean of India and Iran). Define `national-iq`
   here. Then their validation: two independent measures for 45 countries correlate .94; national
   IQ correlates .88 with the 1999 TIMSS maths scores. Then the main result: across 81 countries
   national IQ correlates with income per head at .54 in 1820 (24 countries) and about .72 in
   1997–98 (Spearman .83), "29 percent … to 52 percent of the variance"; on the larger set that
   includes the 104 estimated countries the correlations are somewhat lower (around .6 —
   check the exact figure in ch. 8 before stating it).
   The authors state their test in advance (weak or negative correlations "would falsify the
   hypothesis") and their causal assumption openly (p. 87). KEEPERS: "81 measured, 104
   estimated"; "r ≈ .7, about half the variance". One worked `<Example>` reads one row of their
   scatter: a country, its national IQ, its predicted and actual income, the residual.
8. **What the case says the correlation means — and what else it admits.** The causal story
   (chs. 3–5, 9): within countries, IQ predicts education, earnings and job performance;
   aggregate those and a nation's average predicts its productivity; feedback runs both ways
   (wealth improves nutrition and health, which raise the next generation's scores); and,
   in their final chapter, the national differences are "quite probably" partly genetic,
   evolved over tens of thousands of years (pp. 193–194). What the case concedes: 50–60% of the
   variation in income is due to other factors; IQ is "a necessary, but not a sufficient,
   condition"; market economies and democracy add 10–12 points of explained variance; socialist
   economies sit far below the line. Then the set piece that ties the module to this lesson — L&V
   pp. 174–176: China, by their table a high-IQ country, led the world in technique for two
   thousand years and then stalled; their answer is markets: one empire could impose controls,
   competing European states could not (Ref `landes-european-exceptionalism`,
   `the-borrowing-ledger`). The lesson states the consequence plainly: at the national scale, the
   hereditarian case needs the institutional factor to explain its largest anomaly.
9. **What is new here and what is old.** One section, in the dry register, comparing with
   Gobineau and Grant (Ref): new — standardised measurement on normed scales, published samples
   that others can re-examine, correlation and regression with stated falsification conditions,
   explicit agnosticism about the mix (The Bell Curve), individual treatment as the stated policy
   consequence; shared — the explanatory form (inherited capacities of peoples explain the rank of
   their civilizations), and in Lynn and Vanhanen an account of civilizational origins ("some 5,000
   years ago when the first civilizations were developed by…") and a climatic-selection story that
   echo the nineteenth-century theories in shape. State both lists as facts, without verdict.
10. **The objections, named only.** A short closing section (before the `<Conclusion>`): (a) the
    inference from within-group heritability to a between-group cause, and whether g is one thing
    — `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`; (b) what population genetics now shows about the history of
    the groups being compared — `reich-ancient-dna-and-the-indo-europeans`; (c) whether geography
    and institutions explain the national gaps better — `geography-or-institutions`; (d) whether
    the national-IQ samples can carry the table — `national-data-audited`; (e) whether the US gap
    has narrowed since 1994 — disputed in the literature, taken up with (a). One sentence each, no
    evidence previewed.

Headings as claims: "Four claims, not one"; "The gap on the tests: about one standard deviation";

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** name the four rungs of the hereditarian case and, for each,
   one piece of evidence the books cite for it.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** the Bell Curve's "resolutely agnostic" paragraph
   (l.3227). Metadata (two authors, 1994, a book for general readers, chapter 13 of 22); predict
   what a book with this thesis would say about genes; then what the passage records, argues,
   omits; what it can be used to establish about the authors' position and what it cannot (the
   state of the evidence).
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "If intelligence is 60 percent heritable, a group difference in
   intelligence must be 60 percent genetic." Name the step that fails, using the authors' own
   seed-corn sentence, and state what extra evidence they bring because the step fails.
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** reproduce the companion values of note 76 — the
   environmental gap needed for a 1-SD difference at h = .4 and h = .8 — and say which direction
   the argument moves as heritability falls and why.
5. **Boundary probe (E6a):** in Lynn and Vanhanen's ch. 7, the correlation between national IQ
   and income falls as the sample is restricted to higher-IQ countries (≥ 97: about zero). State
   what the authors conclude from this (threshold; economic system), and what the case would have
   to show if the same pattern held across the whole range.
6. **Corroboration (paired sources):** The Bell Curve on the Flynn effect (l.3198–3204) and Lynn
   and Vanhanen on its cause (pp. 185–186). Where do they agree (the rise is environmental and
   real on the tests), where do they differ (whether it is a rise in g; whether nutrition explains
   it), and what each reading implies for a gap between groups.
7. **Synthesis / argument (spaced review, E3):** compare the hereditarian account of China's lag
   (L&V pp. 174–176) with the exceptionalist account in `landes-european-exceptionalism` and the
   ledger in `the-borrowing-ledger`. Write a claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph on what the two
   accounts share and what the hereditarian account adds.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`:** the 1.58-SD argument assumes that whatever
   environmental factor separates the groups acts on the same scale as the environmental
   differences inside each group. Construct a hypothetical factor that would violate the
   assumption (one that affects every member of one group and no member of the other) and say
   what it would do to the computation. The solution says in one sentence that this is the
   within/between objection the next-but-one lesson develops, and that the Flynn effect is its
   main real-world example, with a `<Ref>`.

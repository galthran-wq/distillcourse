# Research brief: hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations (The hereditarian case: The Bell Curve, Facing Reality and IQ and the Wealth of Nations)

status: planned
module: heredity-and-civilization
type: core
goal: Analyze at full strength, as its proponents make it, the case that part of the average differences in measured intelligence between groups and between nations is genetic and explains part of their differences in income, and identify which premise each line of its evidence rests on

Register rule (binding on the lesson): The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.

This lesson states the hereditarian case as its authors make it, with the evidence each of them cites and the
wording each of them chose. It reconstructs; it does not judge the case's lines here, because each line is
judged in the lesson that tests it, and the module's verdict on the whole is given in `national-data-audited`.
Where the authors meet an objection, the lesson says whether their answer meets it (a fact about the argument).
It is the third lesson of a module organised
by the four premises of the hereditarian argument, laid out in `heritability-within-and-between-groups`:
(1) the tests measure a real general ability that matters; (2) it is heritable within groups; (3) groups differ
in mean on a test that measures the same thing in both; (4) therefore the gap is partly genetic. Lessons 1–2
taught premises 1–2 and the logic of the step 3→4; this lesson shows the case its proponents built on that
skeleton, and tags every line of its evidence with the premise it leans on and the later lesson that tests it
(`us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`, `adoption-studies-across-groups`, `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`,
`national-data-audited`; `gould-and-harden-on-heredity` for the political claims). It previews no evidence
those lessons own. Where the authors themselves raise a difficulty and answer it (the within/between point,
the narrowing of the gap, the rise of scores across generations, China's lag), the difficulty and their
answer are part of the case at full strength and stay.

**What changed from the pre-restructure brief** (this file replaces it): g, heritability, predictive bias,
invariance, the seed corn/two pots and the default hypothesis are no longer taught here — they are recalled by
`<Ref>` to lessons 1–2 and `the-cognitive-elite`. The Flynn effect is no longer defined here: it appears only as
the authors discuss it, with a forward `<Ref>` to `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`, which owns it. Added:

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `what-intelligence-tests-measure`: g and the hierarchical model, predictive validity,
  test bias in the predictive sense (a test is biased against a group if it under-predicts that group's later
  performance; in the main US data it does not), measurement invariance and what it does not show (why latent
  means differ), the reification objection. From `heritability-within-and-between-groups`: heritability by age
  (about .4 at nine to near .8 in adulthood, Western samples), SNP heritability and the twin–DNA shortfall, the
  four-premise argument with its hidden premise, two pots of seed (the within/between point, granted by both
  sides), Jensen's default hypothesis (concept `default-hypothesis`: the causes between groups are of the same
  kind and in the same proportion as within them), the 2024 proof that within-group data cannot partition a
  between-group difference, equal heritabilities across US groups (not evidence that the gap is genetic), and
  the map of objections by premise. From `the-cognitive-elite`: The Bell Curve Parts I–II, the NLSY and AFQT,
  the SD and percentiles, the authors' heritability of .6. From `chamberlain-and-grant` and
  `gobineau-inequality-of-races`: the racial theory of history, Nordicism, the 1924 Act, the army tests' use
  in the 1920s (named only; `gould-and-harden-on-heredity` develops it), Boas. From `the-borrowing-ledger` and
  `landes-european-exceptionalism`: China's early lead (graded) and European fragmentation.
- **Contributes:** The one move: the modern hereditarian case at full strength, as three groups of authors
  built it on the four premises, with each line of evidence tagged by premise and by the later lesson that
  tests it. (1) Within the United States, *The Bell Curve* chs. 13–14 (1994): a gap of about 1 SD on the tests,
  not a predictive bias, shrunk by about a third by SES control (an ambiguous control, by the authors' own
  argument), and an indirect case for a partly genetic cause — the 1.58-SD environment a purely environmental
  account would need at h = .6, the gap not narrowing with SES, Spearman's hypothesis, the transracial adoption
  data, African samples — ending "resolutely agnostic" on the mix; with IQ held equal, several outcome gaps
  shrink or reverse. Murray's *Facing Reality* (2021) restates premise 3 with 2010s data (0.85 SD) and the
  absence of predictive bias, sets causes explicitly aside, and frames the "two truths" politically.
  (2) Rushton and Jensen (2005): ten categories of evidence scored for a "50% genetic–50% environmental" model
  against "culture-only", the default hypothesis applied to raise it to 80%, and a reply putting the fit at
  "50% … to 80%". (3) Across nations, Lynn and Vanhanen (2002) and Lynn and Becker (2019): national IQ assembled
  for 81 countries and estimated for 104 more, correlated with income per head at about .6–.7, read as partly
  causal and "quite probably" partly genetic, with markets as the main other factor and China's lag as the
  hardest case. Introduces `national-iq` and `spearmans-hypothesis`; reinforces `hereditarian-hypothesis`,
  `default-hypothesis`, `g-factor`, `heritability`, `measurement-invariance`, `racial-theory-of-history`,
  `great-divergence`.
- **Hands off:** The case's empirical lines to the lessons that test them, in order: the size and trend of the
  US gap, the SES control and the rise of scores across generations (`us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`, next
  — and the case's own authors disagree with one another on whether the gap narrowed, which the FORWARD exercise
  sets up); the adoption data (`adoption-studies-across-groups`); admixture and the genetic correlates of
  national IQ (`admixture-and-polygenic-scores`); what follows politically from the same facts, including the
  authors' own policy conclusions ("an IQ of 120 is an IQ of 120", the "two truths", the distributional model,
  "impossible to eradicate") (`gould-and-harden-on-heredity`); the national-IQ table, the African samples and
  the 2025 meta-analysis audited entry by entry (`national-data-audited`). The `<Conclusion>` names these in one
  paragraph without previewing their answers. Where each line is decided (register): the step from
  within-group heritability, `heritability-within-and-between-groups` (already decided: invalid); bias and
  invariance, `what-intelligence-tests-measure` (already decided: the tests are not biased in the main US data);
  size, trend and SES, `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`; adoption, `adoption-studies-across-groups`;
  admixture and polygenic scores, `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`; China's lag and the institutional term,
  `geography-or-institutions`; the national table and the module's question itself (the genetic share of the
  gap), `national-data-audited`. Lessons 1–2 are already behind the reader, so the lesson may say in one clause
  each what they decided.
- **Spine step:** "Heredity as an explanation of the gaps between peoples, tested premise by premise — what the tests measure, what heritability can and cannot imply, the hereditarian case at full strength, then the US gap, adoption, admixture and polygenic scores on the step from a real gap to a genetic one — then what follows politically whatever the answer, what ancient DNA shows, the rival explanations from geography and institutions, and the key datasets audited"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): scores differ between groups within and between countries and track income;
several researchers argued part of the explanation is genetic — 1994 (US groups), 2005 (ten kinds of
evidence), 2002 (81 nations against their wealth); none claims to have measured a genetic difference directly;
each assembles indirect evidence; the lesson reconstructs the case as its authors make it. The newcomer's
problem after lessons 1–2: they now know what premises 1–2 can and cannot carry, and that the step to 4 needs
evidence beyond within-group heritability. What evidence did the people who believe the step is warranted
actually bring, and how strong did they themselves say it was? Anchors (L11, one clause each): Richard
Herrnstein (Harvard psychologist, died 1994, the year the book appeared) and Charles Murray (political
scientist; met in `the-cognitive-elite`; author of a different book in `murray-human-accomplishment` — one
clause to prevent confusion; not Douglas Murray); J. Philippe Rushton (Canadian psychologist) and Arthur Jensen
(Berkeley educational psychologist whose 1969 article started the modern dispute; the default hypothesis is
his, Ref lesson 2); Richard Lynn (British psychologist), Tatu Vanhanen (Finnish political scientist, author of
democratisation indices), David Becker (German psychologist, co-author of the 2019 revision).

Running device: the four-premise skeleton from lesson 2 (Ref, one sentence) with a column for "the later
lesson that tests it". Every section ends by placing its evidence on that skeleton (L10e bearing).

1. **What the case claims, and in what words.** Problem: "hereditarians say the gap is genetic" is how the case
   is usually reported. Obvious attempt: take that at face value. Why it fails: the authors' own claims differ
   in strength, and the differences are the content — The Bell Curve: "highly likely" both, "resolutely
   agnostic" on the mix (l.3227); Rushton and Jensen: a 50/50 model judged "provisionally true", raised by the
   default hypothesis to 80/20, reply "50% … to 80%" (pp. 271, 279, 334–335); Lynn and Vanhanen: "quite
   probable that they are at least partly genetic" (pp. 193–194); *Facing Reality*: causes set aside (l.886,
   l.492). Answer: a ladder of wording, from agnostic to 80%, all built on the same premises. Concept recalled:
   `hereditarian-hypothesis` (Ref lesson 2) — "partly" is the load-bearing word. KEEPER: none here; the figure
   of claims is Figure 1.
2. **Premise 3 as the case states it: the measured gap.** Problem: before arguing about causes, how big do the
   authors say the gap is? Their figures, as theirs (dossier §2 for the status): about 1 SD in 1994 (156 studies;
   NLSY AFQT 1.21 SD — black mean at the white 11th percentile, l.2960–2965); 1.1 SD in 2005 ("not … a matter of
   empirical dispute", p. 236); 0.85 SD in 2010s data, mostly achievement tests, read as "conservative"
   (FR l.766), i.e. about 103 vs 91. The authors' own qualifications, kept: individuals differ far more than
   group means (l.2903; R&J p. 238; FR l.812: "millions of Africans and Latins have higher cognitive ability
   than millions of Europeans and Asians"); and the tails — why the authors say a mean gap matters socially
   (FR l.814: the proportion at 140 is 96%). Then the measurement half: all three say the tests do not
   under-predict (Ref `what-intelligence-tests-measure`, one clause; FR quotes the APA task force, l.849–855).
   Tag: premise 3 and the measurement half of 1 → tested in lesson 1 (invariance, already done) and lesson 4
   (size and trend). One sentence that the number has moved in the authors' own hands (1.2 → 1.1 → 0.85 SD)
   and lesson 4 asks why; do not explain. KEEPER: "about 1 SD in 1994; 0.85 SD in 2010s data, on the authors'
   own figures".
3. **Is it poverty? The authors' SES argument.** Problem: the obvious explanation for a gap between groups of
   unequal income is the income. Their evidence: in the NLSY, adding parental SES shrinks the gap from 1.21 to
   .76 SD — 37% of it (l.3046). Their reading of what remains: SES is "also a result of cognitive ability"
   (l.3048), so controlling for it removes part of the very thing measured — the sprinters-on-college-teams
   analogy (l.3050). And the pattern they set against a pure SES account: the gap in SD units is no smaller,
   if anything larger, among the better-off (l.3140). Tag: premise 3→4 → lesson 4 (it owns the residual gap
   after SES, `ses-residual-gap`, and the ambiguity in both directions). One sentence that the opposite reading
   (measured SES is a crude proxy that under-controls) is lesson 4's.
4. **The environment a purely environmental account would need (the 1.58-SD argument).** Problem: lesson 2
   showed that within-group heritability cannot by itself say how much of a gap is genetic. The authors grant
   this — in their own words, with the seed-corn image (Ref lesson 2 / `the-cognitive-elite`; one clause, no
   re-telling). Then their move: suppose the whole 1-SD gap were environmental; with h = .6 within groups, how
   far apart would the groups' environments have to be? The `<Example>` (note 76, l.7591): IQ variance
   15² = 225; environmental share .4 → 90; environmental SD √90 ≈ 9.49 points; a 15-point gap needs
   15/9.49 ≈ 1.58 environmental SDs → the black mean environment at the white 6th percentile; and, for a
   3-point East Asian advantage, .32 SD → the 63rd percentile. Their verdict: differences of "this magnitude
   and pattern are implausible" (l.3140) — especially as some groups who met discrimination score above
   average. Then the assumption, stated in one sentence, not developed: the calculation treats the
   environmental cause of the between-group gap as acting on the same scale as the environmental differences
   inside each group — the default hypothesis in quantitative form (Ref lesson 2). Tag: premises 2 and 4 via
   the hidden premise → lesson 2 has already shown why it is the crux; lesson 4 brings the one real case of a
   gap-sized environmental shift. KEEPER: "1.58 SD, the 6th percentile, at h = .6".
5. **Spearman's hypothesis.** Problem: if the gap were an artefact of culturally loaded items, where would it be
   largest? Obvious expectation: on information and vocabulary items. The authors' answer: the reverse — the
   gap is larger the more a subtest measures g (l.3158), on matched-IQ profiles (l.3160–3162), in "13 out of
   13" data sets (l.3164), and on reaction-time tasks (l.3166); deaf children's lower scores do not follow g
   loadings, so the pattern is not universal to every group difference. Define `spearmans-hypothesis`
   (introduced here): the B–W gap on a test grows with that test's g loading. What the authors claim from it
   (their word "undercuts" for test-bias accounts) and what they say it does not do (it does not by itself
   demand a genetic cause); Rushton and Jensen go further and score it "novel prediction confirmed" (Table 5,
   §4; and §5: the gap larger on more heritable subtests). Tag: premises 1 and 3, offered for 4 → lesson 4
   (the pattern of the cohort gains, which differ in structure, is the test both sides use). Dossier
   constraint: the lesson never states that Spearman's pattern shows the gap is genetic.
6. **Families: regression to the mean and adoption.** Problem: what would family data show if part of the gap
   were genetic? Rushton and Jensen's two predictions: (a) regression — children of high-scoring parents score
   closer to their own group's mean, so siblings of black and white children matched at 120 average about 100
   and 110 (p. 263); (b) adoption — children of different ancestry reared in the same kind of homes should keep
   part of the gap. The Bell Curve's reading of the Minnesota Transracial Adoption Study (named object): the
   adopted children's scores at about 7 and 17 (l.3210–3214, as figures in a small table, not in prose), "a
   considerable impact of the home setting" at 7, a seventeen-point gap at 17, and the authors' preference for
   a mixed explanation. Lynn and Vanhanen state the adoption argument more strongly ("show no improvement",
   p. 194). Tag: (a) premises 2 and 4 via the default hypothesis → lesson 2's logic (the regression argument
   assumes the group means it regresses toward are partly genetic — one sentence, as a question for the
   reader, not an argument); (b) premise 4 → `adoption-studies-across-groups`, which owns the study, its
   follow-up, its confounds and its authors' own reading. The lesson states only what the case claims the
   study shows; it does not give the original authors' conclusion (see Pitfalls: that is lesson 5's, and the
   Bell Curve's report of it is contested).
7. **Ancestry, brain size and the world pattern: the rest of the ten.** Problem: the 2005 review claims
   strength from many independent lines ("consilience", p. 278). The remaining categories, in one table
   (Figure 4), each stated as the authors state it and tagged: the worldwide three-way pattern (§3) and the
   African samples (Bell Curve l.3060–3066) — premise 3 at world scale → `national-data-audited`; racial
   admixture (§8: mixed-race adoptees intermediate; lighter-skinned scoring slightly higher; the 85-vs-70
   argument from ~20% European ancestry) — premise 4 → `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`; brain size (§6) —
   premise 4; not tested by any lesson of this module (say so in a clause; the dossier has no section on it);
   the race–behavior matrix and human origins (§§10–11) — outside this module (one clause each, no detail);
   environmental variables (§12: none identified that closes the gap; the secular rise judged to sit apart from
   g, p. 269–271) — premise 4 → lesson 4. Then the scorecard itself: ++/+/0/−, 17 against −7 (p. 271–273),
   with the authors' own caveat ("these scores reflect our particular evaluation … we invite them to assign
   their scores"). Bearing: the "consilience" claim adds strength only if the lines are independent and each
   holds; which ones hold is what lessons 4–6 and 10 test.
8. **From 50% to 80%: the default hypothesis applied.** Problem: the 2005 review's headline model is 50/50, but
   the reply says "50% … to 80%". Where does 80 come from? Answer: §14 (p. 279) — since the review could not
   identify the environmental half, the authors apply the default hypothesis: the gap is caused by the same
   factors in the same proportion as individual differences, and adult heritability is about .8, so about 80%
   genetic. Show this as a two-line derivation: (within-group h² in adulthood ≈ .8) + (default hypothesis: the
   between-group proportion equals the within-group one) → 80/20. Bearing, without verdict: the 80% figure is
   exactly as strong as the hidden premise lesson 2 isolated (Ref; the 2024 proof shows within-group data
   cannot establish it — one clause, recalled, not re-argued). Dossier constraint: "50–80% genetic" is stated
   only as the authors' claim, in their words, never as a finding. KEEPER: "50% → 80% → '50 … to 80%': the
   authors' claims, the last resting on the default hypothesis".
9. **The rise of scores across generations, as the case meets it.** Problem the authors raise themselves
   (Bell Curve, "Arguments against a genetic explanation"): average scores in many countries rose across
   generations, by as much as a point a year over some spans, so that whites today may differ from whites two
   generations ago as much as whites from blacks today, and so fast a change must be environmental (l.3200).
   This is the Flynn effect — named here as the authors name it, with a forward `<Ref>` to
   `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment` where it is defined and its evidence weighed; no rates, no studies
   here. Their answers: other groups do not stand still (l.3202); the rise may be in narrow test skills, not
   in intelligence (l.3204); Rushton and Jensen add that the gains form a separate cluster from g and the gap
   (p. 269–271) and score §12 neutral (p. 277). Lynn and Vanhanen meet it twice: they correct old samples for
   it, and they attribute it to infant nutrition (pp. 185–186); Lynn and Becker forecast that developing
   nations' scores will keep rising through nutrition, health and education, narrowing the national gaps
   without closing them (p. 335). Then the case's own boundary, quoted: the instability of scores "should
   caution against taking the current ethnic differences as etched in stone" (l.3208). Tag: premise 4 → lesson
   4. Seam: the authors concede environments can move scores a gap-sized amount; they dispute that this tells
   us what made the gap.
10. **National IQ.** Problem: how would one measure, and compare, the average intelligence of whole countries?
    Lynn and Vanhanen's method (ch. 6; App. 1) as a procedure derived from its constraints: few countries have
    representative samples → use any published sample given a standard test, mostly Raven's Progressive
    Matrices (one clause: a non-verbal pattern-completion test); tests have different norms → convert to a
    British scale (mean 100, SD 15); old samples were tested on old norms → correct for the rise across
    generations (≈2–3 points per decade); some countries have several studies → average them (Japan's ten
    estimates → 105, App. 1); 104 countries have none → estimate from neighbours or ethnic composition. Define
    `national-iq` (introduced here). Their validity checks: two independent measures for 45 countries
    correlate .94; TIMSS 1999 maths .88. The 2019 revision (Lynn and Becker): the same data reissued as a
    documented dataset, each sample coded representative, normative, random or selected (l.428–435), in answer
    to criticism of "unrepresentative, small or incomparable samples" (l.320). Tag: premise 3 at world scale
    (are the national means real and comparable?) → `national-data-audited`, which audits entries; this lesson
    gives the method only and says so in a clause. Dossier constraint: no national value (above all "about 70"
    for sub-Saharan Africa) is stated as a measurement in the course's voice; values appear only as the
    authors' figures, in a figure, attributed.
11. **What the correlation with income is said to show.** Problem: a correlation between national IQ and
    income per head could run either way. Their result: across the 81 measured countries r rises from about .54
    in 1820 to .72 in 1997–98 (about half the variance; Spearman .83) (p. 107); with the 104 estimated countries
    included, about .63 (Pearson) (p. 158); later revisions .64 and .71, and .82 for an updated measure built
    from school tests (Lynn and Becker, pp. 5–9). One sentence on r and r² (the share of variance; .7 → about
    half). Their test stated in advance (p. 86: weak or negative correlations "would falsify the hypothesis")
    and their causal assumption stated openly (pp. 86–87). Their mechanism: individual IQ predicts earnings;
    nations are aggregates; feedback runs both ways (pp. 56–57). Their concessions, kept: IQ "a necessary, but
    not a sufficient, condition"; 50–60% of the variation is due to other factors; economic freedom and
    democracy add 10–12 points of explained variance (p. 158); the correlation vanishes among countries above
    IQ 97 (p. 106). Tag: the association → premise 3 at world scale and lesson 10; the cause → lessons 9
    (`geography-or-institutions`) and 10. KEEPER: "r about .6–.7; about half the variance at most; markets and
    democracy the other named factor".
12. **China, the case's hardest case.** Problem: by their own table China is a high-IQ country, yet it stalled
    after leading in technique. Their own words: "One of the most perplexing problems for our theory" (p. 174).
    Their answer: China "failed to develop a market economy", one empire against many competing European states
    (p. 176) — the fragmentation thesis (Ref `landes-european-exceptionalism`; `the-borrowing-ledger` for the
    graded record of the Chinese firsts they list). Consequence, stated plainly: at the national scale the

13. **The genetic step at the scale of nations.** Their three arguments (pp. 193–194: brain size; adoption;

14. **What the case asks of policy — named, not argued.** One short section so that the reader knows the case

    group-level conclusions from the same facts, which is `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`'s problem. No

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know, and where each is anchored (L11; at most three new per section):**

  invariance, two pots, the default hypothesis, the NLSY/AFQT. New here — Spearman's hypothesis (section 5);
  g loading (one clause: how strongly a subtest correlates with g); subtest profile (section 5); regression to
  the mean (section 6, one sentence with the dice image the authors use, p. 263); transracial adoption study
  (one clause; defined in lesson 5); racial admixture (one clause: mixture of ancestries in one person,
  measured today from DNA — lesson 6 owns the method); brain size (no definition needed); the secular rise of
  scores (named "the Flynn effect", described in one clause, defined in lesson 4); national IQ (section 10);
  Raven's Progressive Matrices (one clause); r and r² (section 11, one sentence); income per head at
  purchasing-power parity (one clause); TIMSS (one clause: an international maths and science test of
  schoolchildren); the research-programme vocabulary of the 2005 review ("progressive", "novel prediction",
  "culture-only") — use the authors' terms in quotation marks, explained in a clause each, not as theory of
  science.
- **Group labels.** Use the sources' census categories inside their claims (black, white, East Asian, Latino;
  The Bell Curve writes "ethnic" because "race is such a difficult concept to employ in the American context",
  l.2905; *Facing Reality* writes "European, African, Latin, Asian" in its four factual chapters, l.464–494,
  and returns to the usual labels in ch. 7). Say once that these are the authors' categories; the course adds
  none and uses no capitalisation scheme of its own beyond the house style.
- **The register rule in both directions.** No label in the course's voice for the authors or their critics.
  Labels that may appear only as quotations with who and when: Lynn and Becker quote critics calling national
  IQs "virtually meaningless" (2004) and "technically inadequate … and meaningless" (2006), and allies (2008);
  Rushton and Jensen quote Urbach's "pseudo-scientific maneuvers" (1974) against culture-only salvage
  attempts (p. 279) and title their reply with "moralistic fallacy" (2005); the Bell Curve Afterword's "Never
  has such a moderate book attracted such an immoderate response" (Michael Ledeen's line, adopted by Murray,
  1995). Each, if used, is quoted and attributed with its date, and the lesson adds one plain sentence that the
  reception is not this lesson's subject. Murray's "new Red Guards" (FR ch. 7) is not quoted. Funding and
  journal provenance (Pioneer Fund, *Mankind Quarterly*, Ulster Institute) are not arguments about evidence
  and do not appear here; if anywhere, they are metadata in lesson 10's sourcing drill — and the same rule
  applies to the critics' provenance.
- **Report the case at its own strength; judge it where it is tested.** The authors' guarded wording ("partly", "highly likely", "agnostic",
  "provisionally true", "quite probable", "necessary but not sufficient", "perhaps to 80%") is reported
  exactly — never strengthened (none claims to have measured a genetic difference; the opening says so) and
  never weakened (they do argue from evidence and say what would falsify them). Where the authors differ in
  confidence, the difference is content.
- **Real misreading (L13), one slot:** "The Bell Curve says the gap is genetic" / "*Facing Reality* argues the
  gap is genetic." The first book says both genes and environment are "highly likely" involved and declines an
  estimate (l.3227); the second sets causes aside (l.886, l.492). Refutation schema; the discriminating example
  is the side-by-side wording of Figure 1. A second, in prose: "national IQ is a measurement of each country"
  — for 104 of 185 countries it is an estimate from neighbours, as the authors state.

  - The Bell Curve reports (l.3214) that Scarr and Weinberg "continue to argue" for a mixed gene–environment
    source; the dossier (§6) records the 1992 follow-up's authors concluding that the social environment
    "maintains a dominant role", and their 1994 reply that the balance, "although not conclusive, favors a
    predominantly environmental etiology". The lesson reports only the Bell Curve's reading as the Bell
    Curve's, and leaves the original authors' reading to `adoption-studies-across-groups` (one forward clause:
    "the study's own authors read it differently; lesson 5").
  - Lynn and Vanhanen's 2002 correlation is .72 (81 measured countries, average of six Pearson correlations,
    1997–98) in ch. 7, about .63 for all 185 in ch. 8 (p. 158), .62 for 185 with real GDP 1998 as cited by
    Lynn and Becker (p. 5), and .66/.68 in Lynn and Becker's ch. 3 (p. 218). Not a contradiction of substance:
    different income measures, years and samples. The lesson gives "about .6 to .7" in prose and one labelled
    pair in a figure.
  - Lynn and Becker's dataset codes four sample types (representative, normative, random, selected), not three
    as the dossier §7 has it.
  - Rushton and Jensen's reply says the gap has not narrowed in "nearly 100 years" (p. 328); The Bell Curve

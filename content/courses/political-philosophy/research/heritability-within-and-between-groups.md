## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** "intelligence is highly heritable within groups, so the gap between them is
  probably genetic", including its variant "equal heritabilities in both groups show it".
- **Verdict:** the inference is **invalid**. Grade: **established**. Jensen's default hypothesis
  survives as a hypothesis, not as a default: it carries its own burden of proof like its rival.
- **What decides it:** within-group data cannot partition a between-group difference. Between-group
  heritability h²B = δG²/(δG + δE)² is defined without reference to within-group heritability, places
  no constraint from it, can exceed 1, and fits a genetic difference in either direction (Schraiber &
  Edge 2024, l.120–148, l.402–405). In simulations, lowering every member of one group by a constant
  leaves its heritability unchanged, because a constant changes no correlation (Sorjonen & Melin
  l.14–33, l.81–97). Lewontin's seed-corn case is granted by both sides (Bell Curve ch. 13; Warne
  2021; Rushton & Jensen disown the confusion). Dossier §1 and §8, "Claims the lessons must NOT make".
- **Strongest objection:** human groups are not random handfuls of the same seed; they have different
  recent ancestries, so some genetic difference is to be expected and between-group heritability is
  "likely" above zero (Warne l.~355–375); and the uniform environment the simulations assume is just
  the "Factor X" no one has found (R&J p. 250). It does not overturn the verdict. Expecting *some*
  genetic difference says nothing about its direction or size for this trait, and that is what the
  inference claimed to deliver; and the Factor-X reply bears on whether H is plausible, not on whether
  heritability is evidence for it, since equal heritability is what a uniform cause would leave
  behind too. The objection moves the dispute to the genomic and environmental designs of the
  following lessons, which is the verdict's own conclusion.
- **What would overturn it:** an error in the derivation of h²B, or a method that uses within-group
  data alone and constrains the size and sign of δG without measuring environments. Neither exists.
- **What the verdict does not reach:** whether the gap is partly genetic. That is **open** (module
  verdict, `national-data-audited`); the lesson forbids "heritability is irrelevant" with the same
  force, since heritability is premise 2.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): identical twins separated in infancy resemble each other as adults
almost as closely as those raised together; twin and family studies put the share of differences in
measured intelligence that goes with genetic differences at about half in childhood and more in
adulthood, in the Western countries where they were done; the tempting next step from individuals to
groups; whether it holds depends on what the share means, how it is measured, and what else would have
to be true.

The newcomer's problem, from the previous lesson (one sentence, `<Ref>` to
`what-intelligence-tests-measure`): the groups differ on the same abilities measured the same way; the
question is why, and the first evidence anyone reaches for is the evidence that intelligence runs in
families. Anchor: twin studies from the 1920s, the large twin registries and the separated-twin
studies of the 1980s–90s, DNA chips from the late 2000s. Jensen's 1969 article (anchor: it argued that
compensatory schooling had failed and raised a genetic explanation of the US gap) is the start of the
modern dispute.

1. **What the share is, and how twins estimate it.** Problem: genes and homes come together — parents
   give their children both — so a resemblance between parents and children cannot by itself say which
   did the work. Obvious attempt: compare relatives who share more genes. Why it is not enough:
   relatives who share more genes usually share more of their surroundings too. The answer: find
   comparisons where nature or society pulls the two apart. (a) Identical (MZ) twins share all their
   genes, fraternal (DZ) twins on average half, and both pairs share a home at the same age; if MZ
   pairs correlate more, the extra resemblance is put down to the extra shared genes — roughly
   heritability ≈ 2 × (rMZ − rDZ), with the rest of the MZ resemblance put down to the shared home
   (worked `<Example>` in the exercises, not in the body, to keep the formula from becoming the
   point). (b) MZ twins raised apart share genes and not homes: in five adult studies their scores
   correlated .68–.78 (Neisser l.~547) — the opening's twins. (c) Unrelated children raised in one
   adoptive home share a home and not genes: by adolescence their scores correlate about zero. Recall
   the definition by `<Ref>` to `the-cognitive-elite` (a population ratio, not a share of a person,
   not a measure of changeability) — one clause, not re-taught. Then the finding with its scope, from
   the dossier's Status: heritability of g rises with age — about .4 at nine, .55 at twelve, .66 at
   seventeen, near .8 by the late teens and twenties, the shared home's share falling to about a tenth
   — in Western samples (figure 2). Why it rises is a fair question for the reader, answered in one
   sentence as the field's leading conjecture (people increasingly choose and shape their own
   surroundings as they age; Warne l.~130–135, Neisser on active gene–environment correlation l.~536–
   544), labelled a conjecture. Bearing: premise 2, within the populations studied, is as well
   established as anything in the dispute (dossier §1: "settled in direction and rough size for
   Western samples").
2. **What could make the share wrong, or local.** Problem: every design above rests on an assumption
   the reader should be able to state. (a) *Equal environments.* The twin comparison assumes MZ pairs
   are not treated more alike than DZ pairs in ways that matter for the trait; if they are, part of the
   extra MZ resemblance is environmental and heritability is overstated (dossier §1, Haworth's
   limitation). State the reply: the separated-twin and adoption designs do not rest on this
   assumption and give figures of the same order. No number on how large the bias is — the dossier
   has none. (b) *Range.* A heritability is relative to the spread of environments in the sample;
   adoption and twin samples hold few of the poorest families, so their figures may not hold for a
   population with more deprivation (Neisser l.~552–560; the-cognitive-elite's "changes with
   conditions", recalled). (c) *It changes with conditions — measured.* In US studies heritability is
   lower among children from poorer homes; in Western Europe and Australia the interaction is zero or
   reversed (dossier §1, Tucker-Drob & Bates [abstract]); one US twin study found almost no genetic
   variance at the bottom of the SES scale and almost all at the top (Nisbett 2012 l.~180–200,
   [secondary]). Harden's garden twist as the intuition (rich soil, taller and more unequal plants) —
   one sentence, the example the course's own. (d) *Across groups.* Does it differ between US groups?
   A 2020 meta-analysis of 16 samples (about 85,000 White, 37,000 Black, 18,000 Hispanic people) found
   it moderate to high and about the same in all three (.58, .60, .73 — dossier §8; Pesta Table 3).
   That result is the bridge to section 5 and must be stated without its interpretation here. Bearing:
   premise 2 holds in the US groups; its size depends on the population and its range of
   environments, which already tells the reader a heritability is not a constant of nature.
3. **The DNA check: SNP heritability and what it leaves.** Problem: twin estimates infer genes
   without looking at any; reading DNA should check them. Define `snp-heritability`: the share of
   variation in a trait accounted for by the hundreds of thousands of common variants read on a DNA
   chip, estimated from how far genetically more similar *unrelated* people are more similar in the
   trait. Finding: about .15–.25 for cognition, against .5–.8 from adult twins (dossier §1). Three
   things this gap can mean, none yet decided: variants the chip does not tag (rare ones), effects
   that are not additive, and twin estimates inflated by the assumption of section 2 (dossier ll.105–
   108: "unresolved"). Then the within-family check, derived from a problem: a variant can go with
   higher scores across families because it is commoner in families that are better off in other ways
   (population stratification), or because parents who carry it shape the home (genetic nurture: the
   variants a parent did *not* pass on still predict the child's education, at about 30% of the
   effect of those passed on — dossier §1, Kong [abstract]). Siblings solve it: each receives a random
   half of each parent's DNA, so differences between siblings in variants are not confounded by
   family. The findings disagree and the lesson says so: in one 2022 study of 178,076 siblings the
   association of genetic scores with cognitive ability shrank by 22% within sibships (interval 6–37%)
   and SNP heritability fell from .24 to .14, on a cognition sample of about 27,600 with wide
   intervals; a 2025 family study found no such reduction (.186 population, .188 direct); both agree
   the shrinkage is much larger for educational attainment (about half) than for cognition (dossier
   §1; §9). Scores built from such variants predict roughly 5–10% of cognitive variance in
   European-ancestry samples — one clause only, describing them as "scores summing the small effects of
   very many variants", with a forward `<Ref>` to `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`, where they are
   built and tested. State the Status: "DNA-based and within-family estimates are lower and still
   moving." Bearing: direct DNA evidence confirms that genes contribute to differences in cognition
   within populations, at a smaller share than twins suggest — and none of it, by itself, bears on
   between-group differences (the Status's last sentence), which is the next section's problem.
4. **The argument laid out.** Problem: the opening's "tempting next step" sounds like one step; written
   out, it is several, and only then can one see where it can fail. The course's own numbering
   (figure 4, KEEPER):
   - P1. The tests measure a general ability that predicts outcomes (`what-intelligence-tests-measure`).
   - P2. Within each group, differences in that ability are substantially heritable (sections 1–3).
   - P3. The groups differ in their mean on a measure that is invariant across them (the lesson
     before; size in `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`).
   - C. Therefore part of the difference between the groups is genetic.
   Define `hereditarian-hypothesis` as C in its careful form: part of the average difference in
   measured intelligence between groups is genetic in origin. Then test validity the way logic does:
   can P1–P3 all be true and C false? The two gardens (Lewontin's, the course's own example; figure
   5): one bag of genetically mixed seed, half sown in rich soil and half in rocky, parched ground.
   Within each garden the differences in height are almost all genetic — the soil is uniform — so P2
   holds at nearly 1; the gardens differ in mean height, and a ruler measures height the same way in
   both (P3); yet the whole difference between gardens is the soil. (Gould's villages are the same
   case with human height: 95% heritable in each, four inches apart from famine — one sentence.) The
   argument is invalid as stated. Recall by `<Ref>` that the Bell Curve's own authors make this point
   with seed corn in Iowa and the Mojave (`the-cognitive-elite`, one clause), and that Rushton and
   Jensen grant it in their opening pages ("no necessary implication", p. 239) — so both sides of the
   dispute grant it. Define `within-between-fallacy`: inferring C from P2 (and P3) alone.
   What makes it valid? The premise the gardens violate: that whatever causes differences between the
   groups is of the same kind, and in the same proportion, as whatever causes differences within them.
   Name it H, the hidden premise. With H added, C follows — and so does a number: if the causes
   between groups are the within-group causes in their within-group proportions, the genetic share of
   the gap equals the within-group heritability. That is how the strongest published estimate was
   produced: a reply of 2005 raised its authors' model from "50% genetic" to "80% genetic–20%
   environmental", on the explicit ground that by adulthood genetic and environmental factors "carry
   the same weight in causing group differences as they do in causing individual differences" (reply
   p. 328) — adult heritability, about .8, carried across by H. The reader sees that such a figure is
   H applied, not a measurement (dossier "must NOT make": "the gap is X% genetic"). Bearing, and the
   section's central sentence: heritability is not irrelevant — without P2 there is nothing for H to
   carry across (if heritability within groups were zero, H would make the gap wholly environmental) —
   but it is not enough; the dispute is about H.
5. **The default hypothesis at full strength.** Problem: H is not an arbitrary assumption to its
   proponents; why would anyone hold it? Define `default-hypothesis`: Jensen's proposal (named in his
   1998 book; stated without the name in a 2005 review) that differences between groups are aggregated
   individual differences — produced by the same genetic and environmental factors, in the same
   proportions, as differences within them. The case, in three steps the reader can weigh:
   (a) *Parsimony.* Groups are made of individuals; if within each group a set of causes — genes,
   schooling, nutrition — produces the differences, the economical hypothesis is that the same causes
   produce the differences between the groups' averages, unless some cause acting on one group only
   can be named (R&J p. 239: "plausibility", with "independent evidence … needed"). Gould quotes this
   form himself — "isn't it more likely all the same…" (l.~7737) — and denies it in the next sentence;
   both sentences go in, the reply first.
   (b) *No special factor found.* If a cause acted on one group only — an "X factor", in the authors'
   term — it should leave traces: different correlations between home background and achievement,
   different factor structures, different heritabilities. Rushton and Jensen report none: equal
   covariance matrices of background and outcomes across groups, the same factor structure on the
   military battery, similar heritabilities in Black and White twins — "There was no 'Factor X'
   specific to race" (pp. 249–250); the 2020 meta-analysis (section 2) adds equal heritabilities in
   larger samples; the previous lesson's invariance results (`<Ref>`) are read the same way by Warne
   (Evidence 2).
   (c) *The size of the environment needed.* Under the default hypothesis the environmental
   difference between groups needed to produce a gap is measured against the environmental spread
   within them. The higher the within-group heritability, the smaller that spread, so the larger, in
   its units, the environmental difference must be if the gap is to be wholly environmental: for a
   gap of one standard deviation, about 1.4 environmental SDs at heritability .5 and about 2.2 at .8
   (Warne Table 1; figure 8). Family SES differences between Black and White children in four federal
   samples are about two-thirds of an SD (Warne Table 2) — far short. The Bell Curve made the same
   argument with its own numbers in 1994; that instance is the next lesson's (`<Ref>` forward). The
   authors' challenge, quoted: environmental explanations "have to be formulated rather than simply
   assumed" (l.3142).
   Bearing: the default hypothesis is a substantive empirical claim with arguments behind it — not a
   confusion of within and between, which its authors explicitly disown.
6. **The 2024 proof and the simulations, at full strength.** Problem: does within-group heritability at
   least make H more probable, as (a) says? Two results answer, each derived at the reader's level.
   (a) *The proof.* Between-group heritability has a definition of its own: for two groups, the share
   of the squared difference in means that the genetic difference accounts for, h²B = δG²/(δG + δE)²
   (δG the difference in the groups' average genetic values, δE in their average environments;
   Schraiber & Edge l.141–148). Nothing in it refers to heritability within the groups; it can be
   derived "without reference to within-group heritability". Three consequences, each a sentence:
   knowing within-group heritability, even exactly, places no constraint on it; it can exceed 1, when
   genes and environment push in opposite directions; and the same value fits a genetic difference in
   either direction (δG = ±δ√h²B; worked `<Example>`, set piece 5). The older formula that seemed to
   tie h²B to h²W does so only through a quantity (the share of genetic variance that lies between the
   groups) that cannot be estimated without controlling environments — the tie is a definition, not
   a prediction (l.120–138). Their analogy, for the reader who has met averages: a variance can be
   written using an average, yet knowing the average tells you nothing about the variance (l.402–405).
   The same holds for any method that uses within-group data in the aggregate (l.8–22; dossier §1).
   (b) *The simulations.* A sharper form of the hereditarians' own test: if a group's lower mean were
   environmental, shouldn't its heritability be lower (the prediction tested by the 2020 meta-analysis
   — section 2(d))? Recent simulations built twin pairs, lowered every member of one group by 10, 20
   or 30 points, and re-estimated heritability: it came out identical in both groups in every
   condition (Sorjonen & Melin l.81–97; figure 7). The reason is elementary and the reader can check
   it: heritability is computed from correlations between relatives, and adding or subtracting a
   constant to everyone's score leaves a correlation unchanged (l.14–33). Their everyday analogy
   (calories and BMI) in one sentence. So equal heritability across groups — the finding of section 2
   — is what an environmental gap of this kind predicts as well. They state the symmetry themselves:
   a genetic gap is equally compatible with equal heritability; what fails is the use of equal
   heritability as evidence either way (l.155–174). The 2020 meta-analysts had said the same of their
   own result (Pesta l.~680–695) — say so: the point is common ground.
   (c) *Lewontin's chemist* (Schraiber & Edge l.480–491), as the bridge to measured environments: a
   chemist surveys the two gardens' soils, finds a large nitrate difference, corrects it, and the gap
   only partly closes — and the remainder is called genetic, though the cause was a trace of zinc no
   one measured. Bearing: a residual left after measured environments are accounted for does not show
   a genetic cause — nor, by itself, an unmeasured environmental one (forward `<Ref>` to
   `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`, where measured family circumstances leave a residual).
   Bearing of the section: within-group heritability, equal or not, high or not, gives no information
   about the genetic share of a between-group difference (dossier §1 Status, last sentence; §9).
7. **What the hereditarian side can still say.** Problem: does the proof end the argument? It ends one
   argument — "heritability makes a genetic gap probable" — and leaves the argument its proponents
   actually make. Three replies, each at full strength, each followed by the objection to it.
   (a) *Heritability was never the whole case.* It is premise 2; the case rests on H, and the evidence
   offered for H is of other kinds — invariance and equal factor structure, the pattern of the gap
   across tests, adoption, admixture, regression to the mean (the ten categories of 2005; Warne's five
   lines, of which within-group heritability is only one and is offered jointly with SES data). Warne
   (2021) himself calls the data "not fully conclusive" and the degree "not known". The lesson lists
   these lines without their evidence and hands them to the next lessons (figure 4).
   (b) *The simulated environment is an X factor.* The simulations lower every member of one group by
   exactly the same amount and touch no one in the other — an environment that varies between groups
   and not at all within them. That is precisely the kind of cause the default hypothesis says has not
   been found (R&J p. 250), and it must be named, not assumed (Bell Curve l.3142). Objection: equal
   covariance structures and equal heritabilities — the evidence that "no Factor X" was found — are
   what such a uniform cause would leave behind too, since it shifts means without changing
   correlations (the simulations' own mechanism); so those findings cannot exclude it. Whether
   causes of that kind exist at the scale of a population is an empirical question with a candidate
   on record — the rise in whole populations' scores over the twentieth century — which the
   hereditarian side answers by arguing that rise differs in kind from the group gap (forward `<Ref>`
   to `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`; no evidence previewed).
   (c) *Groups are not random handfuls of seed.* Warne: Lewontin's gardens hold genetically
   *identical* bags of seed; human groups have different recent ancestries, so some genetic
   difference between them is to be expected and between-group heritability is "likely" above zero
   (l.~355–375). Objection: the proof's point is that the quantity this needs (how much genetic
   variance lies between groups, and in which direction for this trait) is not given by within-group
   data; expecting *some* genetic difference says nothing about its direction or size for a given
   trait (δG = ±…). Expecting differences is a claim about evolution and population history, tested
   by the genomic designs of `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`, not by heritability.
   The settlement the lesson states (and the dossier's): within-group heritability is evidence for
   premise 2 and no evidence about H; H is neither established nor refuted by it; the gap's genetic
   share has not been estimated by any design (dossier "Overall answer" 3; "must NOT make", both
   lists). Gould's "not tied by rising degrees of probability" is right about heritability alone;
   whether H is true is the question the module's remaining evidence lessons take up.
8. **The weighing: heritability within groups cannot show that the gap is genetic.** (Heading as a
   claim; it can carry the map as its second half.) Set the inference at full strength (Jensen's
   default hypothesis, the 2020 meta-analysts' equal-heritability test) against the proof and the
   simulations, and against the strongest objection (VERDICT: different ancestries make some genetic
   difference likely; the uniform environment is an unfound Factor X). Reach the verdict in the
   course's voice: the inference is invalid, **established**; the default hypothesis stands as a
   hypothesis, not a default, and the evidence that could decide it is genomic and environmental,
   not heritability. Then the map. Problem: with the premises numbered, every
   objection in the dispute can be placed on the premise it attacks — and the reader can see which
   are settled, which open. The table (figure 4, built row by row here, KEEPER):
   | Premise | Objection | Status (dossier) | Where tested |

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Warm-up (the one computation, unfamiliar):** In a twin sample, identical twins' scores correlate
   .86 and fraternal twins' .60. Estimate heritability, the shared-home share and the rest (h² ≈ 2 ×
   .26 = .52; c² ≈ .34; e² ≈ .14). Then: if identical twins were treated more alike than fraternal
   twins in ways that raise their resemblance, which of the three estimates would be too high? Spaced
   retrieval (E3): from `what-intelligence-tests-measure`, state in one sentence what invariance
   between two groups does not show.
2. **Claim to refute (hereditarian direction, E6b):** "Heritability of intelligence is about .6 among
   Black Americans and about .6 among White Americans; if the gap between them were environmental,
   the disadvantaged group's heritability would be lower; so the gap is probably genetic." Name the
   step that fails (adding a constant to every member's score leaves every correlation, hence every
   heritability, unchanged), cite what the meta-analysts said of their own finding, and state the
   symmetric conclusion the simulations' authors draw.
3. **Claim to refute (environmental direction, E6b):** "Since within-group heritability tells us nothing
   about differences between groups, twin studies are irrelevant to the dispute about the gap, and the
   gap is environmental." Name the two steps that fail: heritability is premise 2 (under the default
   hypothesis it fixes the genetic share; without it H has nothing to carry), and "uninformative about
   the between-group share" is not "the between-group share is zero" (the proof's own scope; the task
   force's "no adequate explanation").
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** Using h²B = δG²/(δG + δE)² for a gap δ = 1: (a) find two
   different pairs (δG, δE) giving h²B = .5 with opposite signs of δG; (b) find a pair giving h²B > 1
   and say what situation it describes; (c) explain why neither computation needed the within-group
   heritability — and what extra information would have to be measured to fix δG.
5. **Boundary probe (E6a):** Under the default hypothesis's arithmetic, the environmental difference
   needed for a wholly environmental 1-SD gap is d·√[(1 − h²B)/(1 − h²W)]. Let h²W → 1. What happens to
   the required difference, and how does the two-gardens case — heritability within each garden near
   1, the whole gap environmental — escape it? Name the assumption the formula's unit (the
   within-group spread of environments) builds in, and say which premise of section 4 it is.
6. **Corroboration (paired sources, playbook-history):** Two short excerpts — R&J 2005 p. 239 ("no
   necessary implication … However, within-groups evidence does imply the plausibility…") and Gould
   1996 ("isn't it more likely all the same …" — "The answer is still 'no'"). Metadata first (two
   hereditarian psychologists in a review for a law-and-policy journal; a palaeontologist in a revised
   critique). Where do they agree (the logical point), where exactly do they disagree (whether
   plausibility transfers), and what kind of evidence — not heritability — would decide between them?
   Write the judgment as a claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph.
7. **Research-shaped task (E6d):** Design two studies that would test the default hypothesis instead
   of assuming it — one that could support it, one that could refute it — and for each, say whether it
   controls the environment (a common garden), varies ancestry with environment held fixed, or
   identifies specific environmental causes and measures their size (the chemist's task done
   properly). Assign each design to the later lesson of the module that examines it (adoption;
   admixture within families; measured environments). The solution sketches: adoption at birth with
   placement independent of ancestry (`adoption-studies-across-groups`); sibling-difference admixture
   regression (`admixture-and-polygenic-scores`); measured family circumstances and their residual
   (`us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`:** Adult heritability within a country is about
   .8. Two samples of that country's conscripts, born forty years apart, differ in mean score by one
   standard deviation; the population's genes cannot have changed appreciably in forty years. (a)
   Under the default hypothesis's arithmetic, how large an environmental difference (in within-group
   environmental SDs) would a wholly environmental 1-SD difference need? (b) Since it must be wholly
   environmental here, what does that say about whether causes acting on a whole population at once —
   X factors — exist? (c) What check would a defender of the default hypothesis run to argue that this
   difference is not the same kind as a gap between groups? The solution computes about 2.2 SDs,
   concludes that population-wide environmental shifts of a gap's size have to exist if the scenario
   is real, names the check as invariance across cohorts (`<Ref>` back to
   `what-intelligence-tests-measure`), and says in one sentence that the next-but-one lesson measures
   exactly this rise and what each side makes of it (`<Ref>` to `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`).

9. **Attack the verdict:** The lesson holds as **established** that within-group heritability cannot
   show a between-group difference to be genetic. State the strongest reply in its best form: groups
   of different recent ancestry should differ genetically in many traits, so with within-group
   heritability high, a genetic share above zero is the reasonable prior, and the burden lies on
   whoever names the Factor X. Then say what evidence would be needed to overturn the verdict itself
   (not merely to make H more plausible): a method using within-group data alone that fixes the sign
   and size of δG. The solution shows that the reply argues for a prior on H, which the verdict
   leaves untouched, and that only genomic designs with controlled environments (`<Ref>`
   `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`) could move H.

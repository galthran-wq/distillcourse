# Research brief: hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty (Hobbes on the rights of the sovereign and the liberty of subjects)

status: planned
module: christendom-to-sovereignty
type: core
goal: Analyze the powers Hobbes gives the sovereign, the liberties he leaves the subject, and his diagnosis of why commonwealths dissolve

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`: the derivation to the covenant of
  every man with every man; author and actor; the sovereign receives the right without being a
  party; justice as keeping covenants; the concession that the derivation assumes fear outweighs
  pride in most men. From `bodin-sovereignty`: sovereignty absolute and perpetual, marked by
  law-making, indivisible, no mixed state, no subject judges the sovereign — but bound by God
  and nature, fundamental laws, covenants and property. From `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: the
  sovereign exempt from the coercive but not the directive force of law; an unjust law is
  violence. From `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` and `cicero-mixed-constitution`: the ancient
  liberty of sharing in rule; the mixed constitution. From `machiavelli-discourses-republic`:
  conflict as the source of liberty — the thesis XXIX turns into a disease.
- **Contributes:** The one move: from "the sovereign is not a party and every subject is his
  author" derive the twelve rights (he cannot breach, forfeit, be accused or punished; he judges
  doctrine, makes property, judges, makes war, appoints, rewards, ranks), show why they are
  indivisible (so that "mixed monarchy" is three factions), and then show what the SUBJECT keeps
  — liberty wherever the law is silent, the rights no covenant can transfer, and release from
  obligation the moment protection ends — and read XXIX's catalogue of seditious doctrines as
  the diagnosis of England's war. The lesson closes on the paradox: the most absolute sovereign
  in the tradition, built from equality and consent, leaving a private liberty the ancients
  never had.
- **Hands off:** Open problems: (i) the test — does the record of 1642–1660 behave as XXIX says?
  `english-revolution-as-test`. (ii) The objection the next module opens with: an absolute
  sovereign is "judge in his own cause" and so still in the state of nature with his subjects
  (`locke-property-and-consent`). (iii) Two forward frames, one clause each: the reading of
  Hobbes as a moralist of individuality — the whole edifice protects the individual will from
  other wills (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`), and "protection and obedience" restated as
  the cogito of the state (`schmitt-concept-of-the-political`).
- **Spine step:** "Wars of religion force a new answer: one sovereign power above every faction,
  bound by no law of its own making — Bodin's definition, Hobbes's derivation from fear, and the
  English Revolution as the test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): once a people has authorised one man or assembly, what may he
do and what may a subject refuse? Recall in one sentence with `<Ref>` the covenant formula and
"the sovereign is not a party". The newcomer's problem: Bodin (Ref) bound his sovereign by God,
nature, fundamental laws, covenants and property, and still left no judge of whether the bounds
were kept; Hobbes has to say what, if anything, a sovereign made by authorisation can be bound
by — and whether the subject who authorised him has anything left.

1. **Can the sovereign break the covenant?** Obvious attempt: he was made by covenant, so he is
   bound by it (Bodin's covenants bind both parties; the resistance tracts, Ref). Hobbes (XVIII
   cons. 1–2): no — the covenant was of every man WITH EVERY MAN; the sovereign was party to
   none; before the institution the multitude was not one person able to covenant, and after it
   any covenant with him would be with himself; so "there can happen no breach of Covenant on
   the part of the Soveraigne", and he cannot FORFEIT. Then (cons. 3–5): the dissenter is bound
   by the majority he joined; "every Subject is … Author of all the Actions and Judgements of
   the Soveraigne", so nothing he does "can be … injury to any of his Subjects" — "he that
   complaineth of injury from his Soveraigne, complaineth of that whereof he himselfe is
   Author"; he "may commit Iniquity, but not Injustice"; and no sovereign "can justly be put to
   death". David and Uriah (XXI l.7024): David sinned against God, did no injustice to Uriah,
   whose sovereign he was. Seam with Aquinas (Ref): "unjust law is violence" has become "no law
   can be unjust" — because justice is keeping covenants and the sovereign made none.
2. **Then what may he do? The twelve rights as a table.** Each right derived from "the end of
   the institution is the peace and defence of them all": 6 judge of doctrines, "a Doctrine
   repugnant to Peace, can no more be True, than Peace and Concord can be against the Law of
   Nature" — because actions follow opinions; 7 the rules of propriety, "Mine and Thine" —
   property is the sovereign's creation (XXIV: it excludes other subjects, "not … their
   Soveraign"); 8 judicature; 9 war and peace, and the sword to that end; 10 choosing
   counsellors, ministers, magistrates, officers; 11 reward and punishment; 12 honour and titles
   — because men's valuations of themselves breed "Quarrells, Factions, and at last Warre".
   Present as the table the module notes ask for (Figure 1), grouped: constitutive (1–5, what
   the covenant's form entails) and executive (6–12, what the end requires). XXVI adds what law
   is: "the Legislator in all Common-wealths, is only the Soveraign", he "is not Subject to the
   Civill Lawes" (Aquinas's exemption from coercive force, Ref, made total), custom is law by
   his silence, interpretation is his, precedent binds no one, and Coke's "artificial perfection
   of reason" of the lawyers is the reason of "our Artificiall Man the Common-wealth". XXVIII:
   punishment is not a right the subjects gave (none can give up the right to defend himself)
   but the sovereign's own natural right to everything, left untouched.
3. **Can the rights be divided?** Obvious attempt: England's own constitution — King, Lords and
   Commons sharing power (the reader will read the 1642 Answer in the next lesson). Hobbes
   (XVIII l.6028–6060): "These Rights Are Indivisible … incommunicable, and inseparable"; "a
   Kingdome divided in it selfe cannot stand"; "if there had not first been an opinion received
   of the greatest part of England, that these Powers were divided between the King, and the
   Lords, and the House of Commons, the people had never been divided, and fallen into this
   Civill Warre". Then XIX: there are only three forms — one, some, all — and "Tyranny, and
   Oligarchy" are "the same Formes misliked"; "mixed" and "limited" monarchy reduce to one of
   the three on inspection (who holds the sword?); monarchy is preferred for CONVENIENCE not
   power (the monarch's private interest coincides with the public; counsel heard in secret;
   constancy), and the infant-king objection answered. Bodin (Ref): the same conclusion by a
   different road — Bodin from the logic of the marks, Hobbes from the logic of authorisation.
   The hurt of sovereign power is "scarce sensible" beside civil war; men see the first through
   "multiplying glasses" (their passions) and lack the "prospective glasses" (moral and civil
   science) to see the second (l.6127).
4. **Then is the subject a slave? Liberty defined.** Obvious attempt: under such a sovereign
   there is no liberty. Hobbes (XXI): "Liberty, or FREEDOME, signifieth (properly) the absence
   of Opposition" — external impediments to motion; FEAR and liberty are consistent (a man who
   throws his goods overboard in a storm does it "very willingly"; a man who pays a debt from
   fear of prison acts freely); liberty and NECESSITY are consistent (water runs freely and
   necessarily down the channel); civil laws are "Artificiall Chains" fastened by the subjects
   themselves "at one end, to the lips of that Man … and at the other end to their own Ears" —
   weak in their nature, held by the danger of breaking them. So the subject's liberty is in
   "the laws praetermitted" (l.6987–6999): "the Liberty to buy, and sell, and otherwise contract
   with one another; to choose their own aboad, their own diet, their own trade of life, and
   institute their children as they themselves think fit" — "The Greatest Liberty Of Subjects,
   Dependeth On The Silence Of The Law" (l.7218). Then the attack on ANCIENT liberty
   (l.7069–7100, Ref `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, `cicero-mixed-constitution`): the
   liberty the Greek and Latin authors praise is the commonwealth's, not the man's — "There is
   written on the Turrets of the city of Luca in great characters at this day, the word
   LIBERTAS; yet no man can thence inferre, that a particular man has more Libertie, or
   Immunitie from the service of the Commonwealth there, than in Constantinople"; men "have
   gotten a habit (under a false shew of Liberty,) of favouring tumults" from reading them, and
   "never any thing so deerly bought, as these Western parts have bought the learning of the
   Greek and Latine tongues". Say the seam: the reader expects, from Machiavelli (Ref), that
   liberty lives in sharing rule and in tumult; Hobbes relocates it in immunity from service and
   calls the tumult a disease.
5. **What may the subject refuse?** From the words of the covenant, "I Authorise, or take upon
   me, all his actions" (l.7159): no obligation arises to kill, wound or maim oneself, to
   abstain from food or air, to accuse oneself, or — unless the refusal "frustrates the end for
   which the Soveraignty was ordained" — to kill another: "'Tis one thing to say 'Kill me, or my
   fellow, if you please'; another thing to say, 'I will kill my selfe, or my fellow'". The
   soldier commanded to fight may "without Injustice refuse" when he "substituteth a sufficient
   Souldier in his place", and "allowance to be made for naturall timorousnesse" — but running
   away is not cowardice when the commonwealth's own existence is at stake, and the sovereign
   may punish refusal with death. Then the LIMIT of obligation (l.7268): "The Obligation of
   Subjects to the Soveraign is understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power
   lasteth, by which he is able to protect them … The end of Obedience is Protection" — a
   captive is discharged, a banished man is no subject during banishment, a sovereign who casts
   off the government or becomes another's subject releases his subjects (l.7301–7340). Hobbes's
   own return to England in 1651–52 and submission to the new Council of State is the
   biographical instance (one sentence, References for the source).
6. **Why do commonwealths dissolve? XXIX read as diagnosis.** Obvious attempt: by conquest.
   Hobbes: a commonwealth dies of internal disease only through "Imperfect Institution" — a
   sovereign who accepted less than absolute power (l.10602) "like the children of Peleus" who
   were persuaded to be made young by being hacked to pieces. Then the "poyson of seditious
   doctrines" (l.10648–10900), each named with the right it attacks (Figure 5): that every
   private man is judge of good and evil (→ right 6 and XXVI: the civil law is the measure);
   that whatever is against conscience is sin (→ the subject's conscience is the law, since he
   authorised it); that faith and sanctity come by inspiration, not study; that the sovereign is
   subject to civil law (→ XXVI: a regress — a new sovereign over him, and over him another);
   that every private man has an ABSOLUTE property excluding the sovereign (→ right 7); that the
   sovereign power may be DIVIDED (l.10752 → right 13 "indivisible"); the imitation of neighbour
   nations (l.10765 — the Dutch) and of the Greeks and Romans (l.10790: "they say not Regicide …
   but Tyrannicide"; "Tyrannophobia, or feare of being strongly governed"); the spiritual power
   set against the civil — "three Soules in a man", two kingdoms in one, and "Mixt Government"
   (l.10879–10905): when levying money depends on one assembly, command on one man, and
   law-making on the consent of a third, "such government, is not government, but division of
   the Common-wealth into three Factions, and call it mixt Monarchy; yet the truth is, that it
   is not one independent Common-wealth, but three independent Factions; nor one Representative
   Person, but three" — "I have seen a man, that had another man growing out of his side".
   Lesser diseases: want of money (the "nutritive faculty" starved), monopolies, "the Popularity
   of a potent Subject", great towns, "the Liberty of Disputing against absolute Power".
   Dissolution by conquest ends obligation (link 5 again). This is the chapter the next lesson
   tests — the writer does NOT narrate 1642–60 here; one clause names what the application
   lesson reads.
7. **The OBJECTION at full strength: absolutism or the first liberalism?** State the paradox as
   the course's own (noticed in Hobbes's own time — References): the most complete sovereign in
   the tradition — judge of doctrine, owner of the rules of property, legislator subject to no
   law — built from equality, consent and the right of self-preservation, and leaving the
   subject a private liberty "where the law is silent" that no ancient republic recognised. Two
   readings, each at strength. (a) Hobbes as the theorist of absolutism: rights 6–7, XXVI, "no
   law can be unjust", the sovereign's control of universities and doctrine — the omnipotent
   state that admits no public truth but peace (the frame from
   `constantine-and-representation`'s "representation" vocabulary, Ref). (b) Hobbes as a
   moralist of individuality: every move in Parts I–II starts from the individual will and ends
   by protecting it from other wills; the sovereign is an umpire of a race whose runners want to
   be "foremost"; the retained rights, the silence of the law, "the end of obedience is
   protection", XXX's hedges ("not to stop Travellers, but to keep them in the way"), the "good
   law" that is "needful … and perspicuous" as distinct from a merely just one — a state that
   exists so that each may pursue his own felicity; and the moral cost that reading names: the
   whole arrangement is written for "the tame man", who trades felicity for security, while
   Hobbes himself knew the proud man who "knows how to belong to himself" and called justice "a
   Nobleness or Gallantness of courage (rarely found)" (XV) — and did not build on him because
   such men are rare. The lesson concedes that both readings quote Hobbes correctly, states
   where they divide (whether "peace" is the end or the means), and hands the question forward:
   the next module's first author will say the absolute sovereign is "judge in his own cause"
   and so still in the state of nature (`locke-property-and-consent`); a twentieth-century
   jurist will restate "the end of obedience is protection" as the first truth of every state
   (`schmitt-concept-of-the-political`); and the postwar reading of Hobbes as the first theorist
   of RIGHTS before duties (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`) will make this chapter the
   hinge between ancient and modern.

Headings as claims: "The sovereign made no covenant, so he can break none"; "Twelve rights, one
end: peace and defence"; "Indivisible: a mixed monarchy is three factions"; "Liberty is where
the law is silent — not where men share rule"; "What no covenant transfers, and when obedience
ends"; "The seditious doctrines: a diagnosis of England"; "Absolutism from consent: the paradox
stated".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **Why the sovereign is not a party** (XVIII cons. 1–2, l.~5750–5800): the two-step argument
   (no one person to covenant with before; covenant with himself after) and the consequence — a
   subject who alleges breach "is in the condition of warre" and the "pretence of Covenant with
   God" is "so evident a lye".
2. **"He that complaineth … complaineth of that whereof he himselfe is Author"** (cons. 4) with
   David and Uriah (XXI l.7024–7030): iniquity to God, no injustice to Uriah. The sentence the
   whole chapter turns on.
3. **The twelve rights** (XVIII) as the table of Figure 1, each row with its one-line "because"
   from the end of the institution and the 1640s claim that denied it (reserved for the next
   lesson's Exercise; here only Coke's law and a Presbyterian assembly as the two examples
   Hobbes himself names).
4. **Multiplying glasses and prospective glasses** (XVIII l.6127–6130): passions magnify the
   sovereign's exactions; "Morall and Civill Science" would show the civil war behind them.
5. **Goods overboard; water in the channel; artificial chains; Lucca** (XXI): four images in
   sequence, each defining liberty against one confusion (fear, necessity, law, republican
   self-rule).
6. **"Kill me, or my fellow" vs "I will kill my selfe"** (XXI l.7159–7165) with the substituted
   soldier (l.7183–7186): the exact scope of what authorisation does not reach.
7. **"The end of Obedience is Protection"** (XXI l.7268–7340): the sentence and its four cases
   (captive; abdication; banishment; the sovereign who becomes another's subject) — the
   Hobbesian instance being Hobbes's own return in 1651–52.
8. **The man with another man growing out of his side** (XXIX l.10879–10905): the three
   faculties (nutritive — money; motive — command; rational — law) on three bodies; "three
   Persons, and three Soveraigns"; paired with "Tyrannophobia" (l.10823) and "they say not
   Regicide … but Tyrannicide" (l.10806).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** "propriety" (property); "praetermitted" (passed over — what the law
  does not regulate); "iniquity" vs "injustice" (sin against God's law / breach of covenant);
  "artificial eternity" (succession); Coke (Sir Edward Coke, 1552–1634, the common lawyer whose
  Institutes made the common law "the artificial perfection of reason"); Lucca (a small Tuscan
  republic); Constantinople (the Ottoman capital — Hobbes's example of a monarchy); the
  Presbyterians and "the spiritual power" (the clergy claiming a jurisdiction over conscience
  independent of the king — the "two kingdoms"); the Dutch ("Low Countries") as the neighbour
  imitated; "the children of Peleus" (Medea's trick — the daughters cut their father to pieces
  to make him young); the Council of State (the executive of the English Commonwealth after
  1649). Three per section.
- **Do not narrate the English civil war here** — one clause per XXIX item; the next lesson
  reads the documents. Do not re-teach the covenant, author/actor, or Bodin's marks — `<Ref>`
  plus a clause.
- **The real misreading (L13):** "Hobbes gives the sovereign unlimited power over the subject's
  life." Refute from XXI's retained rights, the substituted soldier, and "the end of obedience
  is protection"; add the honest qualification that the sovereign may still punish refusal with
  death (the right is a liberty of the subject, not a limit on the sovereign). One slot.
- **"No law can be unjust" vs "good law":** keep XXX's distinction (just ≠ good; a good law is
  "needful … and perspicuous"; hedges) or the absolutist reading swallows the text.
- **The paradox** is argued from Hobbes's own sentences; the three scholars' readings
  (individuality; rights before duties; protection as the cogito of the state) are stated as
  readings with their names in References only. Do not resolve the paradox for the reader; hand
  it forward.
- **Coke may be named** in body as the object Hobbes attacks (L8a(b)); Oakeshott, Schmitt,
  Strauss, Voegelin, Smith may not.
- **Numbers (L12):** none beyond the chapter numbers and 1651–52; the "twelve" rights and
  "nineteen" laws are the keepers.
- **Spelling:** 1651 orthography inside quotations; gloss once.
- **No moralising:** neither "Hobbes the totalitarian" nor "Hobbes the liberal" as the lesson's
  verdict.
- **Dossier thin:** Behemoth (Hobbes's own history of the war) is NOT in the dossier — do not
  cite; De Cive neither; the Strauss–Cropsey chapter file is empty; Oakeshott's essay is the
  only secondary on the liberty chapters and is copyrighted.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (the only mechanical one):** for each of three claims Hobbes names, say
   which of the twelve rights it denies and where XXIX classes it — a common lawyer's "the law
   is the artificial reason of the judges" (right 8 / XXVI); a clergy claiming jurisdiction over
   conscience independent of the king (6; two kingdoms); a subject's "my property is mine
   against the sovereign" (7; absolute property).
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** the Lucca passage (XXI l.7069–7075). Author (an
   English exile in Paris, about to return home under a republic), date, audience (English
   readers steeped in Greek and Roman authors), what it RECORDS (that LIBERTAS was carved on
   Lucca's towers), argues (that the word names the commonwealth's liberty, not the subject's),
   OMITS (that Lucca's citizens elected the officers who commanded their service); what it can
   be USED to establish about the republican vocabulary of 1651 and what it cannot (whether a
   Luccan WAS freer than an Ottoman subject).
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** the degenerate case — a sovereign who can no longer protect (a king
   in his enemies' custody; a city under occupation). By XXI l.7268–7340, exactly when does the
   subject's obligation end, and WHO judges that the moment has come? Show that the only
   available judge is the subject — and that this reintroduces the "private judgement" XXIX
   names as the first seditious doctrine. The solution states the tension plainly and says the
   next lesson watches England meet it in 1646–51.
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Hobbes's sovereign may do anything whatever to a subject, and the
   subject must comply." Name the steps that fail (retained rights; the substituted soldier;
   "iniquity" is still possible and God punishes it; obedience ends with protection) and state
   what the claim gets right (no subject may JUSTLY resist, and refusal may be punished with
   death).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** derive XXVI's "the sovereign is not subject to the
   civil laws" from XVIII alone (he who can make and repeal a law can free himself from it when
   he pleases, so he was free before), then run it backwards: what would a sovereign bound by
   civil law require (a judge above him with power to execute — a new sovereign), and why does
   Hobbes call this a regress "to the Confusion, and Dissolution of the Common-wealth" (XXIX)?
   Compare in one sentence with Aquinas's "exempt from coercive, subject to directive force"
   (`aquinas-law-and-kingship`, Ref): which half Hobbes keeps.
6. **Corroboration (E3 from `bodin-sovereignty`):** Bodin I.8 (the sovereign bound by covenants
   with subjects and by their property) and Hobbes XVIII cons. 1–2 and XXIV (no covenant with
   the sovereign; property excludes other subjects, not him) describe the same office. Set two
   short excerpts side by side, state the premise that differs (whether the sovereign is a party
   to any agreement), and judge which account can explain a subject's taxation without consent
   (Bodin: a breach; Hobbes: an act the subject authored).
7. **Synthesis / counter-argument:** "Hobbes is the first liberal: his state exists to protect
   individual liberty." Name two facts the claim must account for (right 6 — the sovereign
   judges which doctrines may be taught; XXVI — no law can be unjust) and state what would have
   to be true for it to survive (that "peace" in Hobbes is only a means to each man's felicity,
   never an end — the dividing line the lesson named in link 7).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `english-revolution-as-test`:** take XXIX's "Mixt Government" (money on one
   assembly, command on one man, law on the consent of a third) and write, in two or three
   sentences, what a KING'S adviser would have to assert to defend England as a mixture of three
   estates that is nonetheless one commonwealth — and name which of Hobbes's twelve rights that
   defence must concede to Parliament. The solution says in one sentence that the king's
   advisers wrote exactly such a defence in June 1642 and that the next lesson reads it against
   XXIX, with a `<Ref>`.

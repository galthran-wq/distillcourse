# Research brief: hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant (Hobbes: from the state of nature to the covenant)

status: planned
module: christendom-to-sovereignty
type: core
goal: Derive Hobbes's argument from natural equality through the war of all against all to the covenant that institutes a sovereign

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `bodin-sovereignty`: the definition (absolute and perpetual, marked by
  making law for all), the no-mixture argument, no subject judges the sovereign — and the open
  problem: Bodin's sovereign holds his power from God and the father-model and commonwealths
  began in force, so why would equals ever submit, and what turns force into obligation? From
  `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`: justice as the interest of the stronger; Glaucon's story that
  justice arose by a compact among the weak and is kept only from fear. From
  `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`: Carneades's case that justice is convention and the wise man who does
  not seize his advantage is a fool. From `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`: man
  as political animal, the natural master and slave — the two theses Hobbes denies by name. From
  `machiavelli-effectual-truth`: men as they are; fear more reliable than love. From
  `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: natural law as a hierarchy descending from God's reason — the
  model Hobbes keeps the name of and empties.
- **Contributes:** The one move: Hobbes concedes the realist's premises — no natural "right
  reason", good and evil as names for appetite, justice as a convention that begins with a
  compact — and derives from three plain facts (rough equality, scarce objects of desire, mutual
  fear) that men without a common power are at war, and from the passions and reason of such men
  the laws of nature and the one act, a covenant of every man with every man authorising a
  representative, that ends the war. The lesson rebuilds the derivation link by link, as a
  `<FigureSequence>`.
- **Hands off:** Open problems: (i) what the authorised sovereign may do and what the subject
  keeps — `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty` (XVIII–XXI, XXVI, XXIX); (ii) whether the derivation's
  foundation holds — fear of violent death is only "commonly" the strongest passion (the man led
  to execution fights; the soldier substitutes another) — stated here as the course's own
  objection and carried into the next lesson's reading of Hobbes as a moralist of individuality;
  (iii) the forward objections named only as such: Locke's "judge in his own cause"
  (`locke-property-and-consent`), Rousseau's "he read society into nature"
  (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will` — where the course judges the empirical premise
  against the archaeological and ethnographic record: the state lowers lethal violence, so
  Hobbes's point holds there, while the solitary natural man fails), and the thesis that with Hobbes fear of violent
  death takes the place of the natural end — the frame of `strauss-natural-right-and-history`.
  The nearest forward objection comes from the royalist side, two lessons on
  (`filmer-patriarcha`): grant every right the covenant gives the sovereign, deny that men were
  ever born equal and unbound — every man is born a son, under command. One clause.
- **Spine step:** "Christendom builds a law above kings; Machiavelli breaks it; the wars of
  religion force one sovereign power above every faction — Bodin's definition, Hobbes's
  derivation from fear, Filmer's from fatherhood — and the English Revolution tests them"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): take away every judge, policeman and law, and leave people as
they are; from three plain facts to war, and from war to the one arrangement that ends it.
Anchor (L11): Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679 — born, he said, when his mother took fright at the
Spanish Armada, so that "fear and I were born twins"), tutor to the Cavendish family, who fled
to Paris in 1640 as England moved toward civil war and published Leviathan in London in 1651,
two years after the king's execution; the title is the sea-monster of the Book of Job, "king of
all the children of pride". The newcomer's problem: Bodin (Ref) told us what sovereignty IS and
that it comes from God through the father; Hobbes must show why men who are equals, fear each
other and acknowledge no superior would MAKE such a power — and he must do it after Thrasymachus
and Carneades (Ref) have said justice is only the stronger's interest or a convention, premises
he will not deny.

1. **Can reason settle a dispute by itself?** Obvious attempt: yes — natural law is "right
   reason" (Cicero, Aquinas, Ref). Why Hobbes says it fails: reason is "nothing but Reckoning
   (that is, Adding and Substracting) of the Consequences of generall names agreed upon" (V);
   names of virtues and vices are "inconstant" — what one calls prudence another calls fear
   (IV); "whatsoever is the object of any mans Appetite … that is it, which he for his part
   calleth Good" (VI) — there is no good or evil in the nature of things; so when men differ
   "the parties must by their own accord set up for right Reason the Reason of some Arbitrator",
   as players who want their own suit to be trumps must agree on a trump (V l.1280–1295).
   Answer: even if there WERE agreed laws of nature, men would need a judge of their application
   — the sovereign is first of all the arbitrator the dispute requires. Seam: Hobbes has granted
   the realists' premise (Ref Thrasymachus, Carneades) and will build the state on it rather
   than refute it.
2. **What is man like, before any law?** Obvious attempt: naturally social (Aristotle's
   political animal, Ref) and naturally ranked (natural master and slave). Hobbes denies both,
   with reasons: men are EQUAL in the only sense that matters — "the weakest has strength enough
   to kill the strongest, either by secret machination, or by confederacy with others", and in
   prudence, since experience is equally distributed in time (XIII l.3950); and the inequality
   we see "has bin introduced by the Lawes civill" (XV, against Aristotle by name). Felicity is
   not rest but "continuall progresse of the desire"; there is no highest good, only "a
   perpetuall and restlesse desire of Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death" (XI) —
   because to secure what one has one must acquire more. Power is "present means to obtain some
   future apparent Good" (X) and a man's worth is his price. Define the two passions that run
   the argument: PRIDE (glory — the desire to be valued above others) and FEAR (above all of
   violent, shameful death at another man's hands).
3. **Derive the war.** Obvious attempt: men are wicked. Hobbes: "the desires and other passions
   of man are in themselves no sin" — the war follows from the situation, not from vice. The
   chain (XIII), one link at a time: equality of ability → "equality of hope in the attaining of
   our Ends" → two men desire the same thing which both cannot enjoy → they become enemies →
   each, from DIFFIDENCE (distrust), anticipates — strikes first "by force, or wiles, to master
   the persons of all men he can" — and the modest man who would be content with his share must
   do the same or be invaded → and some take pleasure in conquest and others demand to be
   valued, so GLORY adds a third cause; hence "three principall causes of quarrel": competition
   (gain), diffidence (safety), glory (reputation). The condition is WAR, "consisteth not in
   Battell onely … but in a tract of time, wherein the Will to contend by Battell is
   sufficiently known" — as foul weather is not a shower but an inclination over many days. In
   it there is no industry, agriculture, navigation, building, knowledge, arts, letters,
   society, "and which is worst of all, continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the
   life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short" (l.4043). Then the DARE
   (l.4051–4057): the reader who arms himself for a journey, locks his doors, locks his chests
   at home though he knows there are laws — "Does he not there as much accuse mankind by his
   actions, as I do by my words?" And the status of the claim: "never generally so, over all the
   world" — but the savage people of America, and sovereign states toward one another "in the
   state and posture of Gladiators" (l.4068–4079). One forward clause, no data here: whether
   life without a state really is more violent is an empirical question the course takes up
   with Rousseau (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`), where the record of rates of
   violent death in bands, tribes, chiefdoms and states is weighed (Gómez et al. 2016; Fry &
   Söderberg 2013). Consequence: where there is no common power
   there is no law, no justice, no property — "Force, and Fraud, are in warre the two Cardinall
   vertues". Say the seam: from Glaucon's compact (Ref) one expects Hobbes to conclude that the
   just man is a fool; he will conclude the opposite.
4. **Is there a way out that stays inside nature?** Obvious attempt: men agree to keep the
   peace. Why it fails as stated: in nature every man has a RIGHT to everything (XIV — right of
   nature = the liberty to use one's power for one's preservation), so an agreement is one more
   thing he may break. Answer: the LAWS OF NATURE as "theorems" of reason about what conduces to
   peace — first law (l.4186) "to seek Peace, and follow it" and, failing that, to use all helps
   of war; second law: be willing, when others are too, to lay down the right to all things and
   be content with as much liberty against others as one allows them — the golden rule in
   Hobbes's dress. Then the mechanics: transferring right; CONTRACT and COVENANT (a contract
   where one side performs later, on trust); the rights that CANNOT be laid down — to resist
   death, wounds, chains — because no one lays down a right except for some good to himself, and
   a covenant not to defend one's life is void; covenants extorted by fear are valid (ransom
   promised to a thief binds — l.~4440); and the crux — "Covenants of Mutuall Trust, When
   Invalid" (l.4421): in nature, where either party may break faith, a covenant to perform first
   is void "upon any reasonable suspition", because he who performs first "does but betray
   himselfe to his enemy"; under a common power it is valid. Draw the 2×2 (each performs / each
   defects) as the course's own device: in nature the rational move is to defect first; with a
   sword over both, to perform. Oaths add nothing (XIV end).
5. **Is justice anything but convention?** Third law (XV): "that men performe their Covenants
   made"; injustice is "no other than the not Performance of Covenant" — so justice and property
   BEGIN with the commonwealth (Thrasymachus's and Glaucon's premise granted). Then the FOOLE
   (l.4703): "there is no such thing as Justice" — every man's conservation is in his own hands,
   so breaking a covenant when it profits is reason; Hobbes's reply (l.4703–4790): no one can
   survive without confederates, and he who declares he will break faith when it profits "cannot
   be received into any Society … but by the errour of them that receive him"; the rebel who
   succeeds has still acted against reason, since he "teacheth others to do the like"; "Justice
   therefore, that is to say, Keeping of Covenant, is a Rule of Reason" (l.4777). Laws 4–19
   compressed to their rule: "Do not that to another, which thou wouldest not have done to thy
   selfe"; they bind in foro interno always, in foro externo only where there is security — a
   man who kept them alone "should but make himselfe a prey"; they are "but Conclusions, or
   Theoremes" and only LAWS as the word of God. Seam: the Aquinas descent (Ref) is kept in name
   — "law of nature" — and emptied of its source: these laws descend from the fear of death, not
   from God's plan.
6. **How can many wills become one?** The missing piece is XVI: "A PERSON, is he whose words or
   actions are considered, either as his own, or as representing the words or actions of an
   other" (l.5264) — persona as the stage mask (l.5283); ACTOR and AUTHOR; a covenant made by an
   actor with authority binds the author; "a Multitude of men, are made One Person, when they
   are by one man, or one Person, Represented … For it is the Unity of the Representer, not the
   Unity of the Represented, that maketh the Person One" — each man is author of all the
   representer does; the majority's voice counts as all. Then XVII: why the laws of nature fail
   without a sword — "Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words" (l.5531); why a few men, or a
   temporary alliance, or a multitude directed by many judgments will not do; why men cannot
   live as bees and ants do (six reasons, l.5610 — competition for honour, private good differs
   from common, each thinks himself wiser, the art of words, "Man is then most troublesome when
   he is most at ease", their agreement is natural and ours artificial). THE COVENANT FORMULA
   (l.5676) quoted whole and parsed word by word: "I Authorise and give up my Right of Governing
   my selfe, to this Man, or to this Assembly of men, on this condition, that thou give up thy
   Right to him, and Authorise all his Actions in like manner" — a covenant of every man WITH
   EVERY MAN, not with the sovereign; the sovereign RECEIVES the right without being a party
   (the consequence the next lesson draws); "the Generation of that great LEVIATHAN … that
   Mortall God, to which wee owe under the Immortall God, our peace and defence" (l.5681); the
   definition of a commonwealth; institution (fear of one another) vs acquisition (fear of the
   one submitted to) — both by covenant, both from fear.
7. **The OBJECTION at full strength, and where the lesson concedes.** The derivation rests on
   fear of violent death being the strongest passion. The objection the course states as its own
   (sources in References): it is only "commonly" so — the man led to execution fights his
   guards (Hobbes's own example in XIV: a covenant not to resist is void); the soldier Hobbes
   excuses who "substituteth a sufficient Souldier in his place" and the "naturall
   timorousnesse" he allows (XXI l.7183–7186) mean the commonwealth's defence rests on men the
   derivation does not describe — those who prefer honour or a cause to life; and the pride the
   Leviathan is built to humble ("king of all the children of pride") is the passion the
   derivation has to assume is weaker than fear in every subject. Hobbes's reply, from the text:
   the sovereign may punish refusal with death (XXI), and glory-seekers are the few — the
   commonwealth is built for the many. Say plainly whether the reply answers the objection: it
   answers it for the many and not for the few, and Hobbes says so himself. The lesson concedes:
   the argument needs an empirical premise about the distribution of courage, and states that the next lesson's reading of
   Hobbes — as a moralist who knew the proud man and wrote for the tame one — turns on exactly
   this.

Headings as claims: "Reason is reckoning, so disputes need an arbitrator"; "Men are equal in the
one way that matters: each can kill"; "Equality → hope → diffidence → war"; "The laws of nature
are theorems of peace, not commands"; "Justice is keeping covenants — and the Foole is wrong";
"A multitude becomes one person by the unity of the representer"; "What the derivation assumes
about fear".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The trump suit and the arbitrator** (V l.1280–1295): men demand right reason as judge "as
   if they would have all things determined by no mans reason but their own"; "bearing all the
   trump" — the image carries the whole case for a sovereign judge. Pair with VI's "calleth
   Good" (l.1614).
2. **The equality passage and the dare** (XIII l.3950–3960, l.4051–4057): quoted and parsed;
   then the "accuse mankind" sentence — the reader's own locks as evidence. Note (L9) this is
   Hobbes's argument, not an invented persona.
3. **"Solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short"** (l.4043) read WITH its list — no industry,
   agriculture, navigation, building, letters, society — so the reader sees that the famous five
   words are the tail of a ledger of what peace buys.
4. **The ransom to the thief** (XIV): a covenant made from fear binds — "I covenant to pay a
   ransome … to a Theefe; I am bound to pay it" — the sentence on which commonwealth by
   acquisition and the Engagement of 1650 (next lessons) will hang.
5. **The Foole answered** (XV l.4703–4790): his argument stated at strength (the successful
   usurper; Coke on the attainted heir), the two replies (confederates; teaching others), the
   concession ("except by the errour of them that receive him").
6. **Actor, author, mask** (XVI l.5264–5290): persona on the stage; Cicero "I bear three
   persons"; the multitude made one "by the Unity of the Representer" — then Moses: "Hoc dicit
   Moses" vs "Hoc dicit Dominus" as the test of speaking in one's own person or another's.
7. **Bees and ants: six reasons** (XVII l.5610–5670): rebuilt as a table — what insects have,
   what men have instead — ending in "their agreement is Naturall; that of men, is by Covenant
   only, which is Artificiall".
8. **The covenant formula** (XVII l.5676–5690): typeset whole in a `<Quote>`, then dissected —
   "I authorise" (XVI: author and actor), "give up my right of governing myself" (XIV:
   transfer), "to this man or assembly" (not a party), "on this condition that thou" (every man
   with every man), "authorise all his actions in like manner" (the consequence the next lesson
   states). Then "Mortall God".

  God" — Hobbes's reworking); "Mortal God"; "institution" vs "acquisition". No more than three
  per section.
- **Do not re-teach** Thrasymachus, Glaucon's ring, Carneades, Aristotle's political
  animal/natural slave, Bodin's definition — each is one clause plus `<Ref>`. The lesson's new
  matter is the DERIVATION; the backward links are what make the reader expect the opposite
  conclusion.
- **The real misreading (L13):** "Hobbes says men are naturally wicked / selfish." Refute from
  XIII ("the desires … are in themselves no sin"), from the situational structure of the
  argument (the modest man must anticipate too), and from the Foole's answer (breaking covenant
  is against reason, not only against morals). One slot; no second strawman. A second real
  one, handled in a Remark not a slot: "the state of nature is Hobbes's account of prehistory" —
  he says it was "never generally so" and gives America and inter-state relations as the cases
  where it obtains.
- **Spelling:** quote the 1651 orthography as printed ("poore", "Soveraign") and gloss once that
  it is the original; do not modernise inside quotation marks.
- **The prisoner's-dilemma 2×2** is the course's own device for XIV's "covenants of mutual
  trust"; do not name game theory or its authors in the body, and do not claim Hobbes drew it.
- **Smith's lectures** supply the paradox (absolutism from equality and consent) and the courage
  objection; strip the 2006 hooks (Yale dorms, firemen, Iraq) and the film clip — the objection
  is stated from Hobbes's own text (XIV void covenant; XXI substitution).
- **Numbers (L12):** none needed beyond 1588, 1640, 1649, 1651 and the "nineteen" laws; do not
  inventory laws 4–19 in body prose — a folded table.
- **Register:** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right. The paradox (absolute power
  from equality and consent) is stated as the question the next lesson answers.
- **Dossier thin:** De Cive and the Elements of Law are not in the dossier; Aubrey's Brief Lives

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (the only mechanical one):** name the three causes of quarrel and, for
   each, the passion it answers to and the good it seeks (XIII); then say which of the three
   Glaucon's shepherd (Ref `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`) exemplifies.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** the "accuse mankind" passage (XIII l.4051–4057).
   Author (an English royalist exile in Paris), date (1651, two years after the regicide),
   audience (English readers who have just lived through nine years of war), what it RECORDS (a
   1651 Englishman's habits — arms on a journey, locked chests), what it argues, what it OMITS
   (that the locks are fewer where the laws are stronger); what it can be USED to establish
   about security in mid-century England and what it cannot (human nature).
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** remove equality — suppose one man (or one group) so much stronger
   that no confederacy could kill him (Aristotle's natural master, Ref). Does the war still
   follow? Work the derivation: without equality of hope there is no diffidence among equals,
   but the strong man's own fear of confederacy remains; state what Hobbes says in XV about such
   inequality and what regime the case describes (commonwealth by acquisition, XX). The solution
   shows that the equality premise is what makes the covenant necessary rather than mere
   submission.
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Hobbes holds that men are naturally wicked, so they must be
   chained." Name the step that fails (passions are no sin; the modest man must anticipate too;
   the Foole is answered by reason, not by a moral law), and state what Hobbes actually blames
   (the absence of a common power).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** run the Foole's argument BACKWARDS: under what
   condition does breaking a covenant become rational on Hobbes's own account? (When there is no
   security that the other will perform — XIV's void covenant; the laws bind in foro interno
   only.) Then state whether that condition licenses the Foole's usurpation in a functioning
   commonwealth, and why not (XV: confederates; teaching others).
6. **Corroboration (E3 from `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`):** Glaucon's story of the origin of
   justice (Rep. II — the weak compact not to do or suffer injustice) and Hobbes XIV–XV tell the
   same story. Set two short excerpts side by side, state what each takes the compact to show
   (that justice is a second-best the strong would never choose / that keeping it is a rule of
   reason for everyone), and name the premise that differs (whether any man is strong enough to
   be safe alone — XIII's equality).
7. **Synthesis / counter-argument:** "A commonwealth founded on fear of death cannot defend
   itself: its subjects will not fight." Name two facts the claim must account for (the
   sovereign's right to punish refusal with death, XXI; commonwealth by acquisition — conquered
   men covenant from fear and are bound, XX) and state what would have to be true for it to
   survive (that courage cannot be bought or compelled — the empirical premise the lesson named
   in link 7).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`:** the covenant formula makes every subject
   the AUTHOR of the sovereign's acts and makes the sovereign no party to the covenant. Derive
   what follows for a subject who says the sovereign has "broken the covenant" and for a subject
   who says the sovereign has "injured" him — using only XVI's author/actor and XVII's formula.
   The solution says in one sentence that the next lesson lists these as the first consequences
   of institution (the sovereign cannot breach a covenant he did not make; to accuse him is to
   accuse oneself), with a `<Ref>`.

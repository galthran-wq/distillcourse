# Research brief: hume-against-the-original-contract (Hume against the original contract: government founded on opinion)

status: planned
module: contract-revolution-reaction
type: core
goal: Analyze Hume's argument that no actual government rests on consent, that all rest on opinion of interest and of right, and evaluate what this leaves of Locke's case

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `locke-property-and-consent` (the previous lesson): consent as the only
  ground of legitimate government (`consent`); express vs tacit consent, with tacit consent
  reaching anyone who "barely travels freely on the highway" (Second Treatise Sect. 119) and
  binding only while he enjoys the protection (Sects 121–122); Locke's own answer to "no
  historical instances" (Sects 100–112); the right of resistance and the appeal to heaven; the
  1688 frame (James II's flight, the Convention, the Declaration of Right). From
  `filmer-patriarcha`: `patriarchal-divine-right` — no one is born free, kings are fathers of
  fathers — and Filmer's demand "where, in all of history, did a people meet to choose a
  ruler?", which Hume will repeat for the opposite purpose. From
  `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` and `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`: `covenant`, the third
  law (keep covenants), the commonwealth by acquisition (conquered men covenant from fear and are
  bound) and protection-and-obedience. From `english-revolution-as-test`: 1649, the Protectorate
  as sovereignty without legitimacy, 1660. From `machiavelli-discourses-republic`: Rome's
  emperors and the praetorian guard only as names the reader has met; not needed beyond a clause.
- **Contributes:** One move. Hume grants the contract theorist everything he can (that
  government at its very first origin must have begun in some consent; that consent, where it
  exists, is "the best and most sacred" foundation) and shows that it is neither the actual nor
  the necessary ground of any existing government: governments began in conquest and
  usurpation; tacit consent by residence is a fiction; generations cannot each consent; and the
  duty to obey stands on exactly the same footing as the duty to keep promises — the general
  interest of society — so resolving one into the other gains nothing. In its place: all
  government rests on *opinion* — of public interest, of right to power, of right to property —
  and time and custom turn what began in force into what men take as right. The lesson's centre
  is the replacement of a historical-juridical question (who agreed?) by an
  explanatory-utilitarian one (why do the many obey the few, and why should they?).
- **Hands off:** The lesson decides that consent is not the ground of any actual government
  (strong: the record, the ship, the promise regress). Three things pass on. (1) What survives of
  consent is a hypothetical test, a standard to measure a government against; that use is
  carried to `rawls-theory-of-justice`. The normative gap Hume does leave — he explains why men
  obey and appeals to "general opinion" as the standard in morals, but gives no test for when
  opinion is wrong — is the problem Rousseau answers the opposite way in the next lesson
  (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`): keep consent and make it real, by assembly. (2) The
  conservative inheritance: "time mellows into legality governments violent in their
  commencement" is Burke's sentence (`burke-reflections`), and the dread of a "total dissolution
  of government" that makes "every wise man" wish for a general is the forecast Burke makes of
  France; Maistre will build his English parallel out of Hume's History (Considerations ch. 11,
  `maistre-and-bonald`). (3) Madison's "all governments rest on opinion" (Federalist 49) and his
  debt to Hume's essays (`federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`), forward only.
- **Spine step:** "Government by consent (Locke) is doubted by Hume and radicalised by Rousseau's
  general will; when France tries to frame a government from principles, Burke, Maistre and
  Donoso Cortés answer that no generation knows enough, Herder and Fichte put the people before
  the state, and Hegel absorbs both sides into the rational state"

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** legitimate government rests on the consent of the governed, given at the
  origin (the original contract) or tacitly, by residence.
- **Verdict:** the claim fails as an account of any actual government. Grade: **strong**.
- **What decides it:** (1) As history the original contract fails: the governments Hume surveys
  began in conquest and usurpation (OC 15–19: Athens, the Achaeans, the two Henrys, "conquest
  or usurpation, that is, in plain terms, force"; and 1688 itself, where "the majority of seven
  hundred" determined the change "for near ten millions").
  (2) Tacit consent by residence fails: the poor peasant who knows no foreign language and lives
  from day to day is as free to leave as the passenger carried aboard asleep is free to leave the
  ship (OC 24). (3) Hume's further point holds: allegiance and promise-keeping rest on the same
  ground, the general interest of society, so deriving the duty to obey from a promise "gains
  nothing" (OC 36–37). What survives is consent as a hypothetical test, a standard a government
  could be measured against, not a fact about how any government began. That use is carried to
  `rawls-theory-of-justice`.
- **Strongest objection:** Hume answers a question Locke did not ask. Locke asked when obedience
  is owed, not how governments began; consent is a standard of legitimacy, and Hume himself calls
  it "the best and most sacred" foundation, so he shows only that most governments fall short of
  it. **Why it does not overturn the verdict:** the verdict judges consent as the ground of
  existing governments, and on that the objection concedes Hume's case. A standard that nearly
  every actual government fails cannot be what makes any of them legitimate; and the ground
  Locke needs to make consent bind (the general interest) grounds obedience without it. The
  objection is right that Hume leaves the normative question (when is opinion wrong?) without a
  test; the lesson says so.
- **What would overturn it:** a body of states founded and kept by consent that individuals
  actually gave. Ratification of the American constitution in 1787–88 is the nearest case, and it
  bound non-voters and later generations who gave none.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): Locke said a government is legitimate only if the people
consented to it, and that a person who stays in a country has consented tacitly. A Scottish
philosopher sixty years later asks where any such consent can be found: most governments began
in conquest or usurpation, and a poor peasant who knows no foreign language and lives on daily
wages is as free to leave his country as a man carried aboard a ship asleep is free to leave it
by jumping into the sea. If consent is not the foundation, the question is what is. The lesson
follows his answer.

Anchor (L11) at first mention: David Hume (1711–76), Edinburgh philosopher and historian, whose
Essays appeared from 1741; "Of the Original Contract" was published in 1748, two years after the
last Jacobite rising (1745–46: the exiled Stuart claimant's army marched from Scotland to Derby
before retreating), in a Britain where the Whigs — the party of 1688 and of the Hanoverian
succession — held contract theory as their creed and the Tories — the party of crown and church,
some of them loyal at heart to the exiled Stuarts (Jacobites) — held some form of divine right.
Define Whig, Tory and Jacobite here, once; `burke-reflections` recalls them with a `<Ref>`.

1. **How do the few govern the many at all?** The reader has two answers: Hobbes's sword
   (`<Ref>` hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant — "covenants, without the sword, are but words")
   and Locke's consent (`<Ref>` locke-property-and-consent). Obvious attempt: force. Why it fails
   (FP 1): "Force is always on the side of the governed" — the sultan of Egypt can drive his
   subjects like beasts, but he cannot drive his own guards; he must lead his mamelukes "like
   men, by their opinion". The sword is held by men who obey; so even the most military
   government rests on what its soldiers believe. Answer: "It is therefore, on opinion only that
   government is founded." Anchor *soldan* (sultan), *mamelukes* (the slave-soldier corps who
   ruled Egypt) and *praetorians* (the Roman emperor's guard, which made and unmade emperors —
   OC 41–42 will show it) in one clause each. Heading as claim: *Force is always on the side of
   the governed*.
2. **What kind of opinion?** FP 2–6. Two kinds, three objects: opinion of *interest* (that
   government in general is advantageous, and that this one is as good as any that could
   "easily be settled" — the second clause is the conservative hinge: the comparison is with
   what is reachable, not with the ideal); opinion of *right to power* ("Antiquity always begets
   the opinion of right"); opinion of *right to property* (Harrington's thesis that property is
   the foundation of all government, "carrying the matter too far"). Then the derivation the
   lesson must show step by step: self-interest, fear and affection are *secondary*, because
   each presupposes the primary — a man expects rewards only from an authority already
   established; a tyrant is feared only because others obey him, since "as a single man, his
   bodily force can reach but a small way"; virtue in a ruler counts only once he is "invested
   with a public character". Define `government-founded-on-opinion` here. Seam: this is a claim
   about every government, despotisms included, so it cannot by itself say which government is
   legitimate; that is the next question.
3. **What is true in each party's theory?** OC 1–3. Both parties built "a philosophical or
   speculative system" to cover a practical scheme. Divine right first, because the reader has
   just met it (`<Ref>` filmer-patriarcha): Hume grants that God is the author of all government,
   since he is the author of everything — and so the claim proves too much: "A constable,
   therefore, no less than a king, acts by a divine commission"; Providence gave power to Titus
   and Trajan and equally to Cesare Borgia and Angria (anchor: Kanhoji Angre, the Maratha admiral
   the British called a pirate, d. 1729). Note the difference from Locke's reply to Filmer: Locke
   denied that Adam's title descended; Hume grants the divine origin and empties it.
4. **Was there ever an original contract?** OC 4–6. The concession, at full strength: men are
   "nearly equal" in body and mind (Hobbes's premise, `<Ref>`), so no one could first subject a
   multitude except by their consent; that compact "was not written on parchment, nor yet on
   leaves or barks of trees", but traced "in the nature of man". Then the limit: that consent was
   "very imperfect" — a chieftain "who had probably acquired his influence during the continuance
   of war", each exercise of authority "particular", producing "an habitual … and therefore
   precarious, acquiescence" (the Origin of Government essay tells the same story: the chieftain,
   the revenue, the soldiers, "Habit soon consolidates"). What the "philosophers" add (OC 6) is
   the claim the lesson tests: that governments *now* rest on a conditional promise each subject
   has given, and that its breach frees him — Locke's trust and right of resistance in Hume's
   words. Define `original-contract` here.
5. **Does the record show consent?** OC 7–19. Hume's method is to "look abroad into the world".
   The evidence, in four groups, each a short quotation: (a) belief — men everywhere think
   themselves born subjects, as born sons; preach consent "in most parts of the world" and you
   are imprisoned "as seditious" or confined "as delirious"; an act every subject is supposed to
   have performed has left no memory anywhere; (b) origins — "almost all the governments, which
   exist at present … have been founded originally, either on usurpation or conquest, or both";
   the usurper's art is that his enemies cannot learn "their number or force", so each obeys for
   fear of the others; (c) elections — a few great men deciding for all, or a mob following a
   ringleader "not known, perhaps, to a dozen among them"; and the sentence the module will
   return to: a total dissolution of government is the most terrible event, and "every wise man,
   then, wishes to see, at the head of a powerful and obedient army, a general" (OC 14); (d) the
   best cases — 1688 itself, "only the majority of seven hundred, who determined that change for
   near ten millions" (one keeper number, framed: the two Houses of the Convention against the
   population of England and Scotland); Athens, where not a tenth of those bound ever voted;
   Henry IV and Henry VII, who held their crowns by parliamentary election and would not admit
   it. Locke's reply at full strength (`<Ref>`, Sects 100–112): history is silent about the
   state of nature as it is silent about every people's infancy, and where we see beginnings
   (Rome, Venice, the peoples of America) we see agreement. Hume's answer: the objection is not
   that records are missing but that the records we have show force. Then the concession that
   frames the rest (OC 20): consent is "surely the best and most sacred" foundation where it has
   place; it has "very seldom had place in any degree, and never almost in its full extent";
   "therefore some other foundation of government must also be admitted". Mark the seam (L10b):
   from a critic of contract one expects a defence of conquest; Hume keeps consent as the best
   title and denies only that it is the only one.
6. **Does staying in a country count as consenting?** OC 23–27, the lesson's set piece. Locke's
   claim at its real strength (`<Ref>`, Sects 119–122 — tacit consent binds only while one enjoys
   the protection, and makes no one a member), so that Hume is put against the real claim and not
   a straw one. Hume's first step: implied consent exists only where a man "imagines, that the
   matter depends on his choice", and men born under a government believe the opposite. Second,
   the ship (OC 24), quoted whole and parsed: "Can we seriously say, that a poor peasant or
   artizan has a free choice to leave his country, when he knows no foreign language or manners,
   and lives from day to day, by the small wages which he acquires? We may as well assert, that a
   man, by remaining in a vessel, freely consents to the dominion of the master; though he was
   carried on board while asleep, and must leap into the ocean, and perish, the moment he leaves
   her." Three conditions the passage names (language, manners, daily wages) and the one the
   image adds (he did not choose to board). Third, the boundary cases: a prince may forbid
   emigration, as Tiberius and the old Muscovite tsars did — would he thereby lose every
   subject's allegiance? (OC 25); colonists who leave are still claimed (OC 26); the one real
   tacit consent, the naturalised foreigner, gives an allegiance "much less expected or depended
   on" than the native's (OC 27). Then the generational point (OC 28): were generations replaced
   at once, "as is the case with silk-worms and butterflies", a new race might choose; but
   "human society is in perpetual flux", so "the new brood should conform themselves to the
   established constitution". Read OC 28's qualification aloud: some innovation is "happy" when
   "the enlightened genius of the age" directs it "to the side of reason, liberty, and justice";
   what no individual may make is *violent* innovation. And the usurper/restored-prince case
   (OC 29): the people consent to the restored prince *because* they think him already their
   sovereign — consent follows the opinion of right; it does not create it.
7. **Then why are we bound to obey?** OC 32–37, the "more philosophical refutation". Two kinds
   of duty (OC 33–34): those we feel before any reflection (love of children, gratitude, pity) and
   those we perform "entirely from a sense of obligation", because we see society cannot subsist
   without them — justice and fidelity to promises. Allegiance belongs to the second kind (OC 35).
   The argument in three steps (draw it): the contract theorist says we must obey because we
   promised; ask why we must keep promises; the only answer is that society cannot subsist
   without it; but that answer "would immediately, without any circuit, have accounted for our
   obligation to allegiance" (OC 37) — "we gain nothing by resolving the one into the other"
   (OC 36). Add the Origin of Government formula: "OBEDIENCE is a new duty which must be invented
   to support that of JUSTICE", and the deflating image — the whole apparatus of kings,
   parliaments, fleets and armies exists for "the support of the twelve judges". Tie back to
   Hobbes (`<Ref>`): Hobbes grounded keeping covenants in reason and answered the Foole by
   self-interest; by Hume's argument that ground makes the covenant itself redundant as a ground
   of obedience. Define *artificial virtue* (Hume's term for a duty that rests on a human
   convention serving the general interest) once, in a clause; do not teach the Treatise.
8. **To whom is allegiance owed, and how does force become right?** OC 38–45 and FP 3. "Our
   present sovereign, who inherits, in a direct line" admits no reply, though the family's first
   title was usurpation — as every property in land, traced back, "must, in some period, have
   been founded on fraud and injustice", and "there is no virtue or moral duty, but what may,
   with facility, be refined away" by "a false philosophy". The titles in order — long
   possession, present possession, succession, positive law, conquest — and the hard cases
   where none decides: Edward III against Philip of Valois, settled only by "an appeal to heaven,
   that is, by war and violence" (Locke's phrase, `<Ref>`, turned into a description); the
   Roman succession of 192–193, told as a sequence (Commodus murdered, Pertinax proclaimed and
   killed, the empire "set … formally to sale" and bought by Julian, three generals proclaimed by
   three armies, Severus prevailing) — and Gordian, "a boy of fourteen", made emperor "because
   there was no other at hand". The lesson draws the rule: time converts force into title by
   converting fear into the opinion of right; in a despotism a broken succession is a
   catastrophe, in a free government "much less dangerous" (OC 44), which is Hume's one
   concession to 1688. Then Passive Obedience: resistance is lawful "in extraordinary
   emergencies" — "Salus populi suprema Lex"; no reader of the risings against Nero or Philip II
   wishes them to fail — but obedience "ought chiefly to be inculcated", and a preacher who
   studies the exceptions is "preposterous". This is where Hume and Locke converge on the
   practical question (both permit resistance in extremity) and diverge on what to teach.
9. **THE WEIGHING. Heading as the verdict: "No actual government rests on consent" (or "Consent
   founded none of the governments Hume surveys").** This step sets the claim against its
   strongest objection and the evidence and reaches the verdict of the `## VERDICT` section above
   (strong). Hume's own closing tests (OC 46–48): in morals
   "there is really no other standard" than general opinion, so a theory that issues in
   "paradoxes, repugnant to the common sentiments of mankind" is refuted — and Locke's two
   paradoxes are named (absolute monarchy no civil government at all, Sect. 90; no tax without
   the consent of the taxed or their representatives, Sects 138–140); the Crito gives "a tory
   consequence of passive obedience on a whig foundation of the original contract"; "if scarce
   any man, till very lately, ever imagined that government was founded on compact, it is
   certain, that it cannot, in general, have any such foundation". **The OBJECTION, at full
   strength (enter here, from the liberal side, stated as the course's own):** Hume answers a
   question Locke did not ask. Locke asked when obedience is *owed*; Hume shows why obedience is
   *given* and what men *believe*. "General opinion" as the standard in morals cannot be the last
   word, because Hume himself praises innovations that run "to the side of reason, liberty, and
   justice" (OC 28) — by a standard opinion did not supply — and because on his test the
   "paradox" of no taxation without consent, held in "this single kingdom", would have been
   refuted by the practice of every other; within forty years it was the creed of two
   revolutions. Locke's defender adds: consent is a *standard of legitimacy*, and Hume concedes
   it is "the best and most sacred" — so what he shows is that most governments fall short of
   it, not that the standard is wrong. **Where the lesson concedes and replies:** it concedes
   the normative gap (Hume gives no test for when opinion should change except utility, and
   utility is itself contested); it replies with what survives: (i) a theory that makes nearly
   every actual government illegitimate cannot guide obedience in any of them; (ii) the ground
   Locke needs for consent to bind — the general interest — grounds obedience without it; (iii)
   Hume's practical conclusions (resist only in extremity; keep the established government;
   reform by degrees) are close to Locke's own (Sects 223–230: people are slow to stir), so the
   quarrel is over what the doctrine teaches, not over what men may do. The reading that Hume
   attacks Locke from inside Locke's own aim — an order that works with men's ordinary passions —
   is stated as the course's own (Strauss–Cropsey in References). **The verdict, stated in the
   course's voice:** as history the original contract fails, and tacit consent by residence
   fails; Hume's point that allegiance needs no promise to rest on holds; what survives is
   consent as a hypothetical test. Strong. Name the test that would overturn it: a body of states
   founded and kept by consent individuals actually gave — and say why the nearest case,
   ratification in 1787–88, is not one (it bound non-voters and later generations).
10. **Where the argument goes.** One paragraph, three hand-offs: the next lesson takes the
    opposite way out — keep consent and make it actual, by an assembled people
    (`<Ref>` rousseau-inequality-and-general-will); Burke will say that "prescription … mellows
    into legality governments that were violent in their commencement" and forecast the general
    Hume's "wise man" wished for (`<Ref>` burke-reflections); Madison will write that "all
    governments rest on opinion" (`<Ref>` federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic). In
    the `<Conclusion>`: first the verdict in plain words with its grade — no actual government
    rests on consent, as origin or by residence, and this is strong; consent survives only as a
    test a government could be measured against, which is what Rawls will make of it (`<Ref>`
    rawls-theory-of-justice). Then the reader can state the opinion theory, rebuild the four
    arguments against the original contract (record, tacit consent, generations, the promise
    regress), and say what Hume concedes to consent. Then the hand-off to Rousseau.

Headings as claims (suggested): "Force is always on the side of the governed"; "Interest, right
to power, right to property"; "Both parties are right, not in the sense they intend"; "The
record shows conquest, not compact"; "A man carried aboard asleep"; "Why keep promises? The same
reason we obey"; "Time turns force into title"; "No actual government rests on consent" (the
weighing, step 9).

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The soldan and his mamelukes (FP 1)** — quoted whole; then the derivation as a small
   step-through: one ruler, a ring of guards, the multitude; remove the guards' opinion and the
   ruler has "his bodily force" alone. The reader sees that "force is always on the side of the
   governed" is a claim about numbers, not about virtue.
2. **Primary and secondary opinions (FP 2–6)** — rebuilt as a table: what each opinion is about,
   what it presupposes, Hume's example; the tyrant sentence ("his bodily force can reach but a
   small way") as the proof that fear is secondary.
3. **"A constable … acts by a divine commission" (OC 3)** — Titus and Trajan beside Borgia and
   Angria: the reductio of divine right in four sentences, set against Filmer's Adam (`<Ref>`).
4. **Seven hundred for ten millions (OC 15)** — quoted with its two questions ("was the matter
   left, in the least, to their choice? … How otherwise could the matter have ever been brought
   to any issue?"); the sourcing drill in exercise 2.
5. **The ship (OC 24)** — quoted whole, parsed condition by condition, set beside Locke's
   highway (Sect. 119) in a two-panel figure where one element changes (whether leaving is
   possible). Then OC 25–27's three boundary cases (emigration forbidden; colonists claimed; the
   naturalised foreigner).
6. **Silkworms and butterflies (OC 28)** — the thought experiment of a generation that leaves
   the stage at once, and the inference to "perpetual flux"; draw it (the layout
   `burke-reflections` reuses for the partnership of generations).
7. **The promise regress (OC 33–37)** — three steps drawn as a chain with a short-circuit arrow:
   allegiance → promise → why keep promises? → general interest; and the direct arrow allegiance
   → general interest. Pair with "OBEDIENCE is a new duty which must be invented to support that
   of JUSTICE" and "the support of the twelve judges".
8. **The empire for sale (OC 41–43)** — Commodus to Severus as a timeline with spans (months,
   not years), ending on Gordian: what decides a title when no rule does.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), at most three per section:** Whig, Tory, Jacobite (once, at the
  opening anchor — `burke-reflections` will `<Ref>` back); *soldan*, *mamelukes*,
  *praetorians* (FP 1); *Harrington* (James Harrington, whose Oceana, 1656, made the balance of
  landed property the foundation of every government — one clause); *Borgia* (Cesare Borgia,
  `<Ref>` machiavelli-effectual-truth if the reader met him there; otherwise one clause) and
  *Angria*; *the Convention* of 1689 (`<Ref>` locke-property-and-consent for 1688); *house of
  Lancaster / white rose* (the fifteenth-century dynasty and the York party's emblem, OC 31 —
  one clause or omit); *Commodus, Pertinax, Julian, Severus, Gordian* (inside the timeline
  figure, not in prose); *the Crito* (Plato's dialogue in which Socrates, condemned, refuses to
  escape prison because he has tacitly agreed to obey Athens's laws); *allegiance*, *fidelity*
  (Hume's word for promise-keeping), *artificial virtue*; *salus populi suprema lex* ("the
  safety of the people is the supreme law", a Roman maxim — Cicero, Laws III, `<Ref>`
  cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law if useful); *fiat justitia ruat caelum* ("let justice be
  done though the heavens fall").
- **The real misreading (L13, one slot):** "Hume says might makes right." Refute from FP 1
  (force is on the side of the governed — rulers survive only by opinion), from OC 20 (consent
  the best title where it exists), from OC 44 and Passive Obedience (resistance in extremity is
  right; altering the succession in a free state is legitimate), and from OC 22 (it is *time*
  and the opinion of right it produces, not force, that makes the title). A second,
  smaller one in a clause: Hume is not a Tory partisan — the essay's first move is to call both
  parties' systems half right.
- **Hume's concessions must be in the lesson.** He grants an original consent at the first
  origin (OC 4) and that consent is "the best and most sacred" foundation (OC 20). A lesson that
  leaves these out is a strawman of Hume, and the objection section depends on them.
- **Put Hume against Locke's real claim.** Locke limits tacit consent to "the duration of such
  enjoyment" and says it makes no one a member (Sects 121–122). The ship still bites, because the
  question is whether staying is chosen; say so precisely.
- **"General opinion" is restricted by Hume** to "morals, as well as criticism", against the
  speculative sciences (OC 46). Do not report it as "majority opinion settles everything".
- **Do not re-teach** Locke's consent, Filmer's patriarchy, Hobbes's covenant or the Crito's
  argument at length: each is a `<Ref>` and a clause. The lesson's new matter is the opinion
  theory, the four arguments, and the promise regress.
- **Do not teach Hume's epistemology or the Treatise's account of justice** beyond the one
  clause defining "artificial virtue"; the Enquiry's "Of Justice" in Muller is background only.
- **Spelling:** quote the eighteenth-century orthography as printed in the staged text
  ("partizans", "chuse", "prætorian"); gloss once.
- **Numbers (L12):** keeper numbers are 700 / ten millions (OC 15, framed as Hume frames it)
  and "not a tenth" (OC 16). Dates: 1741, 1748 and 1688 exact (the essay's occasion); the rest
  to the decade; the Roman succession inside the figure.
- **No moralising:** the course does not call Hume a conservative or a liberal in its own voice;
  "the conservative reading of Hume" may be named as a reading, with Muller in References.
- **Dossier coverage:** both essays are staged whole and are short — the lesson can quote whole
  sentences. "Of the Origin of Government" and "Of Passive Obedience" are present only as
  Muller's excerpts (enough for the lesson). Not in the dossier and not needed: the Treatise
  (Book III), "Idea of a Perfect Commonwealth" (Madison's lesson needs it, not this one). The
  Strauss–Cropsey chapter has landed (OCR; quotations checked at l.26880–27000).

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval, warm-up (the only mechanical one).* Name Hume's three primary opinions and three
   secondary principles, and for one secondary principle give Hume's reason it presupposes the
   primary (FP 5–6).
2. *Document reasoning (sourcing drill).* OC 15, "only the majority of seven hundred, who
   determined that change for near ten millions": author (a Scottish philosopher with a
   reputation for Tory sympathies), date (1748, sixty years after 1688 and two after the last
   Jacobite rising), audience (a Britain whose ruling Whigs held contract theory as orthodoxy),
   what it records (the Convention's vote; that most subjects acquiesced), what it argues (1688
   was no express consent), what it omits (Locke's own claim that the people act only when the
   government is dissolved, and that representatives may consent for the represented, Sects
   140, 149), and what it can be USED to establish: that the Revolution settlement was not a
   Lockean express consent — not that Locke's standard is false.
3. *Boundary probe.* Take the one case Hume calls "the truest tacit consent", the naturalised
   foreigner (OC 27), and a second: a company of settlers who sign a written compact before
   landing (the 1620 Mayflower Compact may be anchored in one clause). Does Hume's argument deny
   that either consented? Work through OC 20, 23, 26–28: the reader should find that Hume
   admits real consent at the boundary and denies only that it reaches the second generation —
   so the generational argument (OC 28), not the record, carries the weight there.
4. *Plausible false claim to refute.* "Hume holds that might makes right: whoever seizes power
   and keeps it is legitimate." Name the failing steps (FP 1: rulers hold power only through the
   opinion of the governed; OC 20: consent the best title; OC 22: what makes the title is time
   and the opinion of right it produces; Passive Obedience: resistance lawful in extremity; OC
   44: altering the succession in a free state is no catastrophe) and state what Hume actually
   says about a fresh usurpation (obeyed "from fear and necessity", not yet from allegiance).
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* Apply Hume's promise regress (OC 36–37) to Hobbes's
   covenant (`<Ref>` hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant, hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty). Hobbes
   answered the Foole by showing that keeping covenants serves each man's preservation. Derive
   what Hume's argument says follows for the covenant as a *ground* of obedience, and then say
   what Hobbes's covenant still does that Hume's argument does not touch (it authorises a
   representative and settles who the sovereign is — the "to whom" question of OC 38).
6. *Synthesis / counter-argument.* "Hume's standard — general opinion in morals — would have
   made slavery right wherever it was generally approved." Name two facts the claim must account
   for (OC 46 confines the test to theories that contradict "all nations and all ages"; OC 28
   praises innovations toward "reason, liberty, and justice"; FP 3–4 the opinions of right are
   themselves subject to argument) and state what would have to be true for the claim to
   survive (that Hume has no standard but opinion — which the lesson's objection section
   concedes in part). The model answer argues the warrant both ways.
7. *Retrieval and corroboration (E3, `filmer-patriarcha`).* Filmer asked where in history a
   people ever met to choose a ruler and said tacit assent "proves too much" (Patriarcha ch. II
   sec 5–7); Hume asks the same and says the same of tacit consent. Set two short excerpts side
   by side, state what each concludes from the absence of consent (paternal title descended from
   Adam / time, interest and the opinion of right), and name the premise that differs (OC 3: Hume
   grants God authors all government and concludes that this sanctifies none in particular).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `burke-reflections`.** Hume says that a "total dissolution of government" is
   the most terrible event, that every wise man then wishes for "a general, who may speedily
   seize the prize" (OC 14), and that violent innovations "no individual is entitled to make"
   (OC 28). Suppose a great kingdom deliberately dissolves its government and summons an
   assembly to frame a new one from principles. Using only OC 14, 21–22 and 28, predict what
   Hume's argument says will follow, and in what order. The solution says in one sentence that
   a Whig member of Parliament makes exactly this forecast about France in 1790 — first
   disorder, then a general — with the `<Ref>`.
9. **Attack the verdict.** The lesson judged that no actual government rests on consent
   (strong). State the strongest objection in its best form — consent is a standard of
   legitimacy, not a history, and Hume concedes it is "the best and most sacred" foundation —
   and then say exactly what evidence would overturn the verdict. The model answer: the
   objection changes the question rather than answering it; to overturn the verdict one would
   need states founded and kept by consent individuals actually gave, including those born
   later; the reader tests the American ratification of 1787–88 against that requirement and
   says why it falls short (non-voters, later generations), and what a case that met it would
   have to look like (e.g. a polity in which each adult affirms membership, with a real option to
   refuse at no ruinous cost).

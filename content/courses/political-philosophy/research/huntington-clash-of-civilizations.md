# Research brief: huntington-clash-of-civilizations (Huntington's clash of civilizations)

status: planned
module: civilizations
type: core
goal: Analyze Huntington's hypothesis that cultural fault lines will replace ideology as the source of conflict, and lay out the main objections to it

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `toynbee-challenge-and-response`: the unit "civilization" and
  Toynbee's list of about twenty-one, of which some six or seven are living — the
  list Huntington takes over by name; the five tokens that relate them; and the
  method question (a unit built for the history of growth). From
  `spengler-decline-of-the-west`: Culture and Civilization, and Spengler's claim
  that Cultures do not influence each other — against which Huntington's whole
  hypothesis is about interaction. From `schmitt-concept-of-the-political`: the
  friend–enemy distinction as the criterion of the political, the pluriverse of
  states, "whoever invokes humanity wants to cheat" — the backward Ref the
  hypothesis invites (Huntington's "universal civilization is a Western idea" is
  Schmitt's argument in another vocabulary). From `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`:
  the nation as a project (Renan's plebiscite), which Huntington's "torn countries"
  test. From `hellenization-of-christianity` and `altar-of-victory`: the 1500
  boundary of Western Christianity rests on a history the reader has (the Greek
  and Latin churches). The reader also has the Cold War only as general knowledge;
  the lesson anchors it (L11).
- **Contributes:** The module's third answer: civilizations are many, and the thing
  that matters about them after 1989 is not their life-course but their borders —
  "the fault lines between civilizations will be the battle lines of the future".
  The lesson reconstructs the 1993 hypothesis exactly as stated (a hypothesis,
  "descriptive, not advocacy"), its definition of a civilization, the six reasons,
  the fault lines, the kin-country syndrome, torn countries and "the West versus
  the rest"; then it develops the three main objections as arguments, each at full
  strength.
- **Hands off:** Two problems stay open. The hypothesis is a prediction about
  conflict after 1993, so it can be checked — `testing-the-clash` reproduces one
  count from the published tests and reads Bosnia for the kin-country syndrome.
  And the measure: Huntington measures a civilization by power — indices of
  military, economic and institutional control — so `what-measures-greatness` sets
  that measure beside Spengler's duration and Toynbee's ideas-taken-up. A third,
  deferred: Huntington's account of the WEST'S OWN identity — the Anglo-Protestant
  core culture of *Who Are We?* (2004) — was not obtained by the reading swarm and
  is handled in `the-nation-and-its-defenders` as reported by others, with the gap
  stated there and here.
- **Spine step:** "The question widens from the state to the civilization: Spengler's
  organisms, Toynbee's challenge and response, Huntington's fault lines — three
  measures of greatness and three accounts of decline, tested on the record"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): when the Cold War ended the question was what
would organise world conflict next; a Harvard political scientist proposed in 1993
that it would be culture, along the borders of seven or eight civilizations. The
newcomer's problem: in 1993, with the Soviet Union gone, what is a reasonable
expectation about who will fight whom — and how would one even state such a
hypothesis so that it could be right or wrong? The chain:

1. **The problem of 1993: what replaces ideology.** Question: for forty years the
   line of conflict was the Iron Curtain, between two ideologies; it is gone — what
   now? Anchor the Cold War in one sentence (L11: 1947–1989, the United States and
   the Soviet Union, nuclear-armed, divided Europe at the Iron Curtain). Attempts
   Huntington names and rejects: the end of history (one liberal world), the
   return of nation-state rivalry, tribalism versus globalism. Why they fail, on
   his reading: each misses "a crucial, indeed a central, aspect" — that the next
   conflicts will run between cultures. Answer: the sequence princes → nations →
   ideologies → civilizations, all the earlier ones being "Western civil wars";
   after 1989 "international politics moves out of its Western phase". The because:
   the great wars of the past two centuries were fought within one civilization
   over its own quarrels; the actors now include peoples who were objects. Seam from
   Toynbee (Ref): the unit is his; the use is new — a unit built to explain growth
   is turned to explain conflict (Exercise 8 of the last lesson).
2. **What a civilization is, for this purpose.** Question: to say conflicts will run
   "between civilizations" one must be able to say which civilization a group
   belongs to. Attempt: a civilization is a region, or a religion, or a race. Why
   each fails for Huntington: a region contains several (Europe east and west of the
   1500 line), a religion is only the most important of several elements, and race
   is explicitly not it ("people of the same race can be deeply divided by
   civilization"; Toynbee's refutation stands — Ref). Answer: "the highest cultural
   grouping of people and the broadest level of cultural identity people have short
   of that which distinguishes humans from other species", fixed by objective
   elements AND subjective self-identification — the Rome-resident ladder (Roman →
   Italian → Catholic → Christian → European → Westerner), the Ibo who is an Ibo in
   Lagos, a Nigerian in London, an African in New York. The list: Western,
   Confucian, Japanese, Islamic, Hindu, Slavic-Orthodox, Latin American, "possibly
   African". The because: this is Toynbee's list of living civilizations with
   twentieth-century adjustments (Latin America and Africa added; the Far Eastern
   split into Confucian and Japanese). Flag at once (pitfalls) that the definition
   makes membership partly a matter of self-identification — which is what lets
   boundaries "change" and torn countries exist, and what the tests in the next
   lesson must fix one way or the other.
3. **Why they will clash: the six reasons, derived.** Question: why should
   difference produce conflict — Europe's nations differed in language for
   centuries? Each reason is derived as the answer to a sub-question, not listed:
   (1) what differences? — the basic ones, religion first, on God and man,
   individual and group, rights and duties, liberty and authority — "the product of
   centuries", not soon to disappear; (2) why now? — the world is smaller, so
   interaction raises consciousness of difference (the Ibo; North Africans in France
   vs Polish Catholics); (3) why culture rather than class? — modernization uproots
   local identity and religion fills the gap ("la revanche de Dieu"); (4) why against
   the West? — the West at its peak provokes a return to roots, and the elites that
   were once Westernized now de-Westernize while the publics do the opposite; (5) why
   cannot it be bargained away? — cultural traits are less mutable than political or
   economic ones: "Which side are you on?" could be answered by changing sides; "What
   are you?" cannot — "a person can be half-French and half-Arab … It is more
   difficult to be half-Catholic and half-Muslim"; (6) why does it show in economics
   too? — regional blocs form where culture is shared (the trade-share figures in a
   table, not prose). Then the two levels, micro and macro. The because, stated once:
   the hypothesis is not that difference causes war but that AFTER ideology, the
   remaining salient difference is cultural and the remaining salient border is
   civilizational.
4. **Where the lines run: the fault lines.** Question: can the hypothesis be drawn on
   a map? Answer: the Velvet Curtain — the eastern boundary of Western Christianity
   in 1500, from Finland through Belarus and Ukraine, around Transylvania, "almost
   exactly along the line now separating Croatia and Slovenia from the rest of
   Yugoslavia", which is also the Habsburg–Ottoman border (Ref `altar-of-victory` /
   `hellenization-of-christianity` for the Latin–Greek division in one clause); the
   West–Islam line "for 1,300 years" from Tours to the Gulf; Islam's southern border
   in Africa; Islam's northern border with Orthodoxy (Bosnia, Serbs and Albanians,
   Armenians and Azeris, the Caucasus); Hindu–Muslim; China; Japan–US as economic.
   The sentence to quote and then examine: "Islam has bloody borders". The because:
   where the micro-level claim is testable it is here — adjacent groups on these
   lines fight over territory. The lesson states, in the dry register, that the map
   is built from history (1500) and from 1993's current conflicts, and that the next
   lesson asks whether the second was selected by the first.
5. **How states take sides: the kin-country syndrome and the torn countries.**
   Question: if states remain the actors, how does a civilization act? Answer:
   states rally to kin — the Gulf War's coalition shrinking to "almost only the West
   and Kuwait"; Turkey and Russia over Armenia–Azerbaijan; Germany and the Vatican
   recognising Croatia and Slovenia, Iran arming the Bosnian Muslims against the
   embargo, Saudi money, Russian nationalists for the Serbs; "a world of clashing
   civilizations … is inevitably a world of double standards"; the Spanish Civil War
   parallel; and the corollary Huntington himself draws — conflicts WITHIN a
   civilization will be "less intense and less likely to expand", so "the
   likelihood of violence between Ukrainians and Russians should be low". Then the
   torn countries: a state whose elite wants one civilization and whose history is
   another — Turkey (Özal), Mexico (the adviser's "we could never say so publicly"),
   Russia (Atlanticists vs Eurasianists) — and the three conditions for switching.
   Seam (Ref `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`): Ortega's nation as a plebiscite on a
   future would make switching a matter of will; Huntington's third condition (the
   host must be willing) says it is not only up to the switcher. The because: this is
   where the hypothesis says what it predicts — and the next lesson reads Bosnia and
   Russia–Ukraine against exactly these paragraphs.
6. **The West versus the rest, and why "universal civilization" is a Western idea.**
   Question: if civilizations are equal units, why does the hypothesis single out
   the West? Answer: because the West is at "an extraordinary peak of power" and
   runs the institutions — the Security Council, the IMF — that speak for "the world
   community" (Major's slip from "the West" to "the world community"); Western ideas
   (individualism, liberalism, human rights, the rule of law, separation of church
   and state) "often have little resonance" elsewhere; so "the very notion of a
   'universal civilization' is a Western idea", and non-Western states respond by
   isolation, bandwagoning or balancing ("modernize but not Westernize"). Seam to
   Schmitt (Ref `schmitt-concept-of-the-political`): the reader has heard this before
   — "whoever invokes humanity wishes to cheat"; the pluriverse against the world
   state. State the kinship and the difference in two sentences: Schmitt's enemy is
   chosen by a decision; Huntington's by inheritance — which is what reason (5)
   claims and what the objections attack.
7. **The disclaimer: what kind of claim it is.** Question (the module's recurring
   one): is this a theory, a prediction or a programme? Answer in Huntington's own
   words — "descriptive hypotheses as to what the future may be like", "not to
   advocate"; the list of what the article "does not argue" (identities will not
   replace all others; nation states will not disappear; groups within a civilization
   will fight). The because: a hypothesis stated this way is checkable, and the
   author then draws policy from it ("unite the West", "limit the expansion of the
   military strength of Confucian and Islamic states") — so the lesson notes, dry,
   that the policy half assumes the descriptive half and would make it more likely
   if followed. Mark this as the course's own observation, not Said's.
8. **The objections, as arguments.** Where they enter: after the whole hypothesis
   stands. Three, developed, with their authors in References only. (a) The
   "clash of ignorance": the units are not units — "Islam" and "the West" each
   contain more quarrel than they contain unity; the hypothesis lumps a billion
   people under one label and reads the label as a motive; the Gulf War was one Arab
   state invading another and the coalition that stopped it was mostly Arab. Reply
   for Huntington: reason (2) — labels harden under contact — and his own
   disclaimer that intra-civilizational conflict continues. (b) Identity and
   violence: a person has many identities (the Ibo ladder is Huntington's own
   example), and which one counts is chosen, not inherited; to say "What are you?"
   has one answer is to make the choice for people — and the "singular affiliation"
   view is what the organisers of communal violence want believed. Reply for
   Huntington: self-identification is in his definition; his claim is that at the
   civilizational level the choice is constrained ("Russians cannot become
   Estonians"). (c) The realist critique: states still fight states and ally with
   whoever serves them — Iran and Syria with the Gulf coalition, Christian Armenia
   with Russia (Huntington's own cases), Muslim Turkey in NATO; "civilizations do
   not control states, states control civilizations"; the Velvet Curtain is drawn
   where Habsburg and Ottoman armies happened to stop. Reply for Huntington: the
   kin-country syndrome was "limited, but growing"; the macro level IS states
   competing, with culture as the new basis of alignment. Where the lesson
   concedes: the hypothesis's own unit is soft at the edges (the "possibly African";
   torn countries), and softness at the edges is where the fault lines are. Where it
   holds: the hypothesis predicts, so it can be checked — which is the next lesson.

Headings as claims or questions: "After ideology: what organises conflict?" / "A
civilization is the broadest identity a person will fight for" / "Six reasons the
differences will matter" / "The fault lines: a map drawn from 1500" / "Kin-countries
and torn countries: how states take sides" / "Why 'universal civilization' is a
Western idea" / "A hypothesis, not a programme" / "Three objections: ignorance,
identity, states".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first mention (L11):** *the Cold War* and *the Iron Curtain*
  (one sentence each, link 1); *Foreign Affairs* (the New York policy journal of the
  Council on Foreign Relations); *the Gulf War* (1990–91: Iraq's invasion of Kuwait
  and the US-led coalition that expelled it); *Bosnia-Herzegovina* and *the Yugoslav
  wars* (1991–95: the break-up of Yugoslavia into Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia, Serbia
  and Montenegro, Macedonia; Bosnia's three peoples — Muslim Bosniaks, Orthodox
  Serbs, Catholic Croats); *Nagorno-Karabakh* (Armenian–Azeri war 1988–94);
  *Transylvania*; *Tours 732*; *the Peace of Westphalia* (1648); *the end of history*
  (Fukuyama's 1989 thesis, one clause — References for the essay); *Confucian* as a
  civilization name (Huntington's label for China and its cultural neighbours — he
  later wrote "Sinic"); *Slavic-Orthodox*; *kin-country* (Greenway's phrase);
  *bandwagoning* and *balancing* (international-relations jargon: joining the strong
  vs combining against them — define); *Atlanticist / Eurasianist*; *Özal* (Turkish
  president 1989–93); *Mahbubani* (Singaporean diplomat — only if the "West and the
  Rest" phrase is attributed; otherwise the phrase is Huntington's in use).

  Huntington's later account of what holds the United States together is treated in

  war (Said's charge) or a neutral observer; both are verdicts the lesson leaves
  open.
- **Civilization vs race:** Huntington's definition is cultural and he says so;
  reason (5)'s "Russians cannot become Estonians" is about culture and language,
  not blood. The hereditarian account is NOT Huntington's; do not conflate — it is
  taught in `what-measures-greatness`, where his fifth reason is used as the modern
  "immutability" claim that the critics read as essentialism.
- **The six reasons are not six separate causes:** the summary's crispness tempts a
  list; derive each as a sub-answer (link 3) or the section is a recap (L0b).
- **The trade-share figures** (Europe 51→59 etc.) are the article's only data; bind
  them (per cent of each region's trade that is intraregional, 1980 and 1989) and
  put them in a table; do not let prose carry six numbers.
- **Toynbee's list vs Huntington's:** Toynbee's living civilizations in the 1930s
  were Western, Orthodox (Byzantine and Russian), Islamic, Hindu, Far Eastern (with
  Japan as an offshoot); Huntington adds Latin American and "possibly African" and
  splits Confucian from Japanese. Say "adjusted", not "copied".
- **Objections are developed as arguments, authors in References (L8a).** Said,
  Sen, Ajami, Walt, Fukuyama, Kepel, Pye, Lind, Palmer, Wallace, Greenway, Horowitz,

  Huntington quotes them, the quotation is his evidence and may be given as "an

  exception: Huntington himself is the author of the material and is named.
- **No misconception slot:** the common misreading ("Huntington wanted a clash") is
  addressed by the disclaimer set piece (link 7) as a reading of the text, not as a
  "some believe" strawman.
- **Numbers:** keeper numbers are three — seven or eight civilizations, 1500 (the

- **Dossier thinness:** the article is complete; the objections are not in the

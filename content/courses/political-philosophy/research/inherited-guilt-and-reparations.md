# Research brief: inherited-guilt-and-reparations (Inherited guilt and the supersession of historic injustice)

status: planned
module: empire-and-guilt
type: core
goal: Analyze Waldron's argument that historic injustices can be superseded by changed circumstances and the opposed case for inherited guilt and reparation, and evaluate whether guilt can pass between generations

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `locke-property-and-consent`: the labour theory and its
  two limits — "enough and as good" is the proviso Waldron turns into
  circumstance-sensitivity, and the labour "mixed" with the land is the image
  he rejects. From `vitoria-right-to-the-americas`: dominium — the Indians owned,
  so by the course's own earlier standard the taking was unjust; the question
  here is what follows now. From `burke-reflections`: the partnership "between
  those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born" and
  liberty as an entailed inheritance — the course's own counter-premise (if the
  living inherit the glory, do they inherit the debt?); and the sentence from
  the Reflections that corporate bodies are immortal "for the good of the
  members, not for their punishment". From `colonial-balance-sheet`: the
  cost–benefit of empire — whatever the ledger says, who is owed and who owes.
  From Locke ch. XVI (met in `locke-and-mill-on-empire`): the unjust
  conqueror gets no title and conquered descendants "may always shake off" the
  yoke — a Lockean seed of the reparations case.
- **Contributes:** Three answers to one question — what does justice now
  require of a wrong done then — reconstructed at full strength: counterfactual
  rectification and why it cannot be carried out; continuing injustice and the
  fading of entitlements; supersession, where a claim lapses "without ever
  having been wrong". Then a second question kept separate from the first:
  whether guilt, as distinct from a claim, passes between generations — the
  "tyranny of guilt" case and the partnership-of-generations reply.
- **Hands off:** Two open ends. Whether a particular remedy — admission to a
  country as reparation — is the right form of repair is `miller-strangers-in-
  our-midst` ch. 7 (restoration in place vs compensation by admission; the
  Gurkhas); the forward exercise lands there. And every reparations
  calculation rests on empirical premises about what colonialism did —
  `auditing-the-case-for-colonialism` reads three of the studies such claims
  cite.
- **Spine step:** "The West judges its own conquests: Vitoria's titles, Locke's waste land, Burke's impeachment of Hastings, Mill's tutelage, the colonial balance sheet, and whether guilt is inherited"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim. Develop it: three questions that
are usually run together must be pulled apart. Was the taking unjust? For the
cases the module has read, the course's own earlier results say yes (Vitoria's
dominium; Burke's trust; `<Ref>` one clause each). What does justice now require
— the land back, its value paid, or nothing? And who owes it — can a wrong pass
to people who did not do it? The newcomer's attempt is the simplest rule:
return what was stolen. The lesson's first set piece tests it.

1. **Why judge the past at all?** Question: what is the practical point of
   saying an act two centuries ago was unjust? Attempt: none — bygones. Fails:
   a moral judgment is universalizable — to condemn the 1865 seizure is to
   prescribe for acts of that type now; the past is where moral imagination is
   trained; and memory sustains the identity of those whose ancestors were
   wronged while guarding against the comfortable stories their beneficiaries
   tell (that the land fell to them by virtue). Hence **symbolic reparation**
   has value on its own: the 1988 payments to surviving Japanese-American
   internees ($20,000 each, with an apology, for internment in 1942–45 — bind
   the number) were "a gift to someone you stood up", an acknowledgment that
   costs something. Because: acknowledgment is owed to persons who are still
   here, whatever follows about property.

2. **Rectification by counterfactual.** Question: if the wrong is to be
   corrected, corrected toward what? The principle (Nozick, 1974, in
   References: holdings are just if acquired and transferred justly;
   otherwise they must be rectified toward what would have obtained without
   the injustice). Define **rectification**. The method: actual history
   E→F→G→H against the counterfactual E+→F+→G+→H+, and move the present from H
   toward H+ (the desert water hole as the first example: you seize the only
   hole as I am about to drink). Then six reasons it cannot be run, each a
   short paragraph: (a) free choices are indeterminate — the aunt's will: my
   best evidence says Amnesty, she leaves everything to a dogs' home; a
   prediction has no normative authority, unlike a trustee's guess, which is
   authorised; (b) the rational-choice floor (no one voluntarily worsens
   themselves) gives only broad conclusions; (c) contagion — the fraudulent
   sale changes prices for the honest neighbour's land too, so every title is
   touched; (d) non-identity — people who exist only because of the injustice;
   (e) the reckless gambler who lost his land by a voluntary wager asks why
   descendants of the wronged are insulated from the consequences of choices
   when he is not; (f) a contractarian counterfactual is holistic — rectifying
   one taking means asking what distribution would be just overall, and then
   why the nineteenth-century baseline rather than any other. Because: the
   method needs a fact about what would have happened, and there is none.

3. **Continuing injustice and the fading of entitlements.** Question: is the
   counterfactual needed at all? Not if the wrong is still going on: the
   stolen car model — "at 9:30 A.M. on September 5, my car was stolen" — the
   thief still has it, I am still here, give it back "without further ado", no
   counterfactual required. The model fits an enduring tribe and an enduring
   piece of land. But: "what if she is never caught?" — the thief's
   grandchildren hold the car as an heirloom; do entitlements fade? The law
   says so (prescription, adverse possession, limitation periods) for
   pragmatic reasons (evidence, settled expectations) and, Waldron argues, for
   a reason of principle: the Lockean ground — my labour embedded in the land
   like a jewel (`<Ref>` locke-property-and-consent) — is incoherent and would
   also forbid ever selling the land; the better ground is that the resource
   has become the centre of the owner's plans and life, which is exactly what
   fades when, after decades, the dispossessed have built lives around other
   things — the yearning to have it back is not the original ground. The
   moral-hazard objection (wrongdoers would wait out the clock) and the reply
   (we cannot pretend a long-lost resource is still central merely to deter).
   The exception the argument itself names: sacred and burial land, where the
   community's identity is bound to the place. Because: an entitlement rests on
   a relation between a person and a thing, and relations change.

4. **Supersession.** Question: can a claim lapse without ever having been
   wrong? The thesis: justice is circumstance-sensitive — for almost any
   entitlement there are circumstances C1 in which it is barely justified and
   C2 in which it is not, with a tipping point between (Locke's own proviso,
   `<Ref>`: appropriation is just where "enough and as good" is left — a
   condition that can stop holding). The oasis acquired in plenty carries a
   right to exclude while water is plentiful and a duty to share when it is
   scarce. Then the savanna (figure 2, four frames): groups appropriate water
   holes with enough for all; Q, from greed, intrudes on P's hole — an
   injustice; drought dries every hole but P's; now justice requires P to share
   — and Q's presence, which was wrong, is what justice now requires. Quote the
   definition. Define **supersession**. The moral-hazard reply again, and why
   it holds here: P would have had to share after the drought even had Q never
   intruded, so Q gains nothing from the wrong. The two limits: supersession is
   possible, not guaranteed — some injustices remain "crying"; and it applies
   only where an honest attempt at just arrangements for the future is being
   made, and takes priority only over reparation that runs contrary to
   prospective justice (which often coincides with reparation, because past
   injustice has present effects). The conclusion drawn for the settler
   societies: the cost of restoring exclusive aboriginal entitlements is far
   higher now than in 1800 — populations have grown and "most of the
   descendants of the colonists, unlike their ancestors, have nowhere else to
   go"; "We were here first" is no more persuasive for being said on behalf of
   the sympathetic side. Because: justice is weighed by what happens if it is
   not done now, not by what might have happened then.

5. **The case FOR reparations, at full strength.** Question: does the
   analysis leave the reparations case standing anywhere? Yes, and the case
   must be stated from its own texts (Coates and Boxill in References; the
   positions as the course's own reconstruction). (a) Slavery in the United
   States is not the only wrong, and not the most recent: redlining under
   federal mortgage insurance from the 1930s and the contract-selling schemes
   of the 1950s–60s (Clyde Ross in Chicago) were done to people who are alive
   — this is Waldron's own continuing-injustice model, not the counterfactual
   one; the racial wealth gap is the present effect that, by Waldron's second
   qualification, makes reparation and prospective justice coincide. (b) The
   Lockean argument: the enslaved were owed wages and their liberty; an unpaid
   debt is part of an estate and passes to heirs as any other asset does — the
   claim is inherited as property is, which requires no inheritance of guilt.
   (c) Acknowledgment: the symbolic value from link 1, which the counterfactual
   problems do not touch. The replies Waldron's side gives: which model is in
   play decides everything (a continuing wrong against the living needs no
   counterfactual; slavery itself needs one and meets the non-identity
   problem); the baseline and the holism problem return for any monetary
   calculation; and who pays — a taxpayer who arrived in 1990 inherits no
   estate from a slaveholder. Leave the reader with the strongest version of
   each.

6. **Hereditary guilt — the "tyranny of guilt" case.** Question: separate
   from any claim, does guilt pass? The restrictionist argument (Murray and
   Bruckner in References; stated as the course's own reconstruction): if
   historical wrongs demand present atonement, "what is the statute of
   limitations and to whom else may it apply?" — why Europe and not Turkey for
   the Ottoman record, not the Gulf, not the Mongols' heirs; the concept
   requires "a hereditary stain of complicity" passing down the generations,
   and the one historical precedent — the Christian attribution of guilt to
   Jews, formally lifted only in 1965 — is one "the modern age views as morally
   repugnant"; and guilt is enjoyed — "a moral intoxicant… From being a nobody
   one becomes a somebody" — the theatre director who marched in Banjul in
   chains and a "So Sorry" T-shirt in 2006 for a sixteenth-century ancestor;
   perhaps "a mono-generational phenomenon". Add Scruton's condition as a
   step: forgiveness requires the wrongdoer's acknowledgment, penitence and
   atonement — acts only the wrongdoer can perform; a descendant can regret
   and repair, not confess. Define **hereditary guilt** here as the question,
   not the answer. The distinction that does the work: liability (a debt or
   duty attached to an estate, a corporation, a state — which passes, as
   Boxill's estate does and as a nation's treaties and debts do) against guilt
   (attached to a will — which does not). Burke's sentence: corporate bodies
   are immortal "for the good of the members, not for their punishment".
   Because: "who owes" is a question about standing, not about feeling, and
   the two sides often talk past each other by answering different questions.

7. **The course's own counter-premise: if we inherit the glory, do we inherit
   the debt?** The seam: from a conservative one expects the "tyranny of
   guilt" conclusion; but the conservative premise is Burke's — society is a
   partnership of the dead, the living and the unborn, liberty an entailed
   inheritance, "in what we retain we are never wholly obsolete" (`<Ref>`
   burke-reflections). A people that inherits its constitution, its
   cathedrals and its victories as an estate inherits the estate's
   encumbrances; the liberal premise (consent; individual responsibility; the
   person as the unit) is the one that cuts against inherited anything, debt
   included. So the lines do not fall where the reader expects: the partnership
   of generations supports liability without guilt; the individualist premise
   denies liability and is embarrassed by the inheritance of benefits it
   accepts. The lesson's close lays the three positions side by side —
   supersession with its qualifications; the reparations case in its
   continuing-injustice and estate forms; the tyranny-of-guilt case with the
   liability/guilt distinction applied — and states what each must concede.
   The reader weighs.

Headings fall out as: "Why judge the past at all"; "What would have happened:
rectification and its six problems"; "The stolen car: continuing injustice and
fading"; "Supersession: a claim can lapse without ever having been wrong"; "The
case for reparations"; "Can guilt be inherited?"; "If we inherit the glory, do
we inherit the debt?"

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** rectification; entitlement theory (acquisition,
  transfer, rectification); counterfactual; adverse possession, prescription,
  statute of limitations (the legal doctrines by which long possession or
  delay extinguishes a claim); non-identity (a person who would not exist but
  for the wrong cannot be made better off by its not happening — Parfit in
  References); Maori land confiscations (New Zealand, after the wars of the
  1860s; 1865 is Waldron's date); the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) and the
  Tribunal (1975); Japanese-American internment (1942–45; the 1988 Act);
  redlining (the federal mortgage-insurance maps of the 1930s that marked
  Black neighbourhoods ineligible); contract selling; H.R. 40; CARICOM; Sorry
  Day and the 2008 Australian apology (Rudd); the 1965 declaration (Nostra
  aetate); Banjul (the capital of the Gambia). Three per section at most.
- **Keep the three questions apart.** Was it unjust / what is owed / who owes.
  Most of the public argument runs them together; the lesson's clarity depends
  on not doing so. "Liability vs guilt" is the course's own distinction for
  the third question — state it as such.
- **Waldron's cases are land; slavery is different.** His examples are Maori,
  Aboriginal and Native American land and the internment; the reparations
  debate the opening names includes slavery, where there is no land to return
  and the non-identity problem is sharpest, and colonial rule, where the
  "estate" is a state. Say so when the lesson moves from his cases to those.
- **The reparations case must come from its own texts.** Coates and Boxill are
  not in the dossier; the writer obtains them (the Atlantic essay is online;
  Boxill is a journal article). If unobtained, the lesson states the case from
  the public record as a reconstruction and says so in References. Never only
  as Murray or Waldron present it.
- **Murray's framing is rhetoric.** "Masochists and sadists", "the guilt
  industry", the Hawkins vignette as ridicule: the course takes the argument
  (statute of limitations; the one precedent; guilt as enjoyed), not the
  register; the summary flags the Hauken passage as "the book's most
  provocative rhetorical moves" — do not use it. Equally, the reparations case
  is not presented as "guilt" by the course: its own authors frame it as debt
  and acknowledgment.
- **No moralising, no verdict.** The tone rule's "no moralizing in either
  direction" applies with full force; the lesson ends with three positions and
  their concessions.
- **Numbers:** $20,000 (1988) bound; dates 1865, 1942–45, 1965, 1988, 1992,
  2006, 2014. No wealth-gap figures unless the writer has the source and binds
  it (Coates gives several; one at most, with its base).
- **Do not re-teach** Locke's labour theory or proviso, Vitoria's dominium,
  Burke's partnership — one `<Ref>` clause each. Do not teach Miller ch. 7 —
  the forward exercise only.
- **Mis-staged file:** `research/texts/murray/strange-death-of-europe.txt` is
  not Murray. Use the djvu file; line pointers above are to it.
- **Nozick, Parfit, Bruckner, Murray, Scruton, Miller, Kukathas, Sher,
  Thompson: References only.** Waldron is the material and may be named as the
  author of the argument under reconstruction ("Waldron argues") — course.yaml
  names him in the goal; the concept definitions call supersession "Waldron's
  thesis".

2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill on the Civil Liberties Act of 1988
   (or, if obtained, Coates's 2014 essay).** Metadata (a statute signed by

   use: what it can be used to establish about which model of reparation a

3. **Boundary probe.** The savanna with the drought caused by Q (Q dammed the
   river). Does the moral-hazard reply still hold? Solution: no — the reply
   requires that P would have had to share even absent the wrong; a change of
   circumstances produced by the wrongdoer does not supersede. State the
   general condition this adds to the definition.
4. **Claim to refute.** "Waldron holds that time heals injustice." Name the
   step that fails (not time but changed circumstances; "possible, not
   guaranteed"; the two qualifications) and the sentence that shows it ("remains
   a crying injustice").

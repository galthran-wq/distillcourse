# Research brief: jouvenel-on-power (Jouvenel's On Power: the minotaur and the growth of the state)

status: planned
module: postwar-conservatism
type: core
goal: Analyze Jouvenel's natural history of Power — its growth through war, its alliance with the common man against the aristocracy, and democracy as the regime of its greatest extent — and judge it against the record of state spending since 1870

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `the-right-after-1945`: the `anti-fascist-consensus` and the problem of what the
  right could still say after 1945 — Jouvenel is one of the men who rebuilt in a new register. From
  `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism` (prerequisite): `soft-despotism`, the
  "immense tutelary power" that grows out of `equality-of-conditions`, and the counterweights
  (associations, local liberties, independent courts). From `bodin-sovereignty`: `sovereignty` as
  the power bound by no law of its own making. From `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`: the subject as
  author of all the sovereign's acts, so that the sovereign cannot injure him. From
  `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: the `general-will`, the people that cannot wrong itself.
  From `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: an unjust law is no law, the king under a law he did not make.
  From `burke-reflections`: the `little-platoon`; from `maistre-and-bonald`: Bonald's nobility as a
  limit set to the king. From `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`: Taine on the Jacobin state
  (Jouvenel quotes him on conscription). From `mosca-ruling-class` and
  `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`: `ruling-class`, `political-formula` and the
  `iron-law-of-oligarchy` — Jouvenel restates the last as a "law of the elite" for the state
  apparatus. From `article-48-weimar` and `the-right-in-1933`: Hitler came to power through the
  machinery of a republic. Each is a `<Ref>` and one clause.
- **Contributes:** The one move: the history of the state written by its EXTENT, not by its FORM.
  Measured by what it takes (soldiers, taxes, laws, officials), Power has grown almost without
  interruption from the twelfth century to 1945, and it grew fastest under the regimes that claimed
  to hand it to the people — because it grows at the expense of the social authorities that limit
  it (aristocracies, Church, corporations, family), allied with the common man those authorities
  ruled, and because a Power that speaks as the general will meets no one who regards it as alien.
  Total war is the visible product of that growth. The lesson rebuilds the argument from the
  medieval king's forty-day army to the "social protectorate", then puts the strongest objection to
  it: that the growth was asked for by the people it served — and judges (see `## VERDICT`): the
  growth is established, the law of near-uninterrupted growth fails after about 1990 (on balance),
  and whether Power or the voters drove it is open.
- **Hands off:** (i) Jouvenel's remedy is "intermediate social forces" that "enframe, protect and
  control the life of man, thereby obviating the intervention of Power" (XIX.10–11): which forces,
  and can they be rebuilt once the state has absorbed their functions? `kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`
  takes it up — Nisbet's `intermediate-associations` and his "new laissez faire" are a sociologist's
  version of the same thesis, written eight years later. (ii) If the state grows by employing ever
  more administrators, who rules the apparatus? `burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`
  answers: a managerial class. (iii) The "new rights of man" that require a state to guarantee them
  (XVIII.9) — `udhr-read-against-the-right` reads Articles 22–25 of the 1948 Declaration with this
  lesson's concept.
- **Spine step:** "After 1945 fascism has discredited the right, and it rebuilds in new registers:
  Jouvenel's history of Power, Oakeshott's disposition, Strauss's natural right, Voegelin's diagnosis
  of Gnosticism, Kirk's and Nisbet's American conservatism, Burnham's managers — tested on the
  Declaration of 1948"

## VERDICT

(From `research/verdicts/REGISTER.md`, Module 9, lesson verdict. Binding: carry each sub-claim and
its grade exactly.)

- **Claim judged:** Power has grown in extent almost without interruption, fastest under the regimes
  that claim to give power to the people. The register splits it into four parts.
  - **(a) The state's extent grew enormously across the democratising era. Established.** Government
    spending averaged about 10.8% of output around 1870 and 41.9% in 1980 across fourteen industrial
    countries (Tanzi 2005, Table 1, from Tanzi & Schuknecht 2000, Table I.1). Quote this one row
    (the "Average" row) and no other figure for the long series. Precision for the writer: the table
    lists fourteen countries, but only eleven have a figure for about 1870 (Canada, Ireland and New
    Zealand are blank), so write "the industrial countries in the standard series" rather than
    "fourteen" when quoting the 1870 average.
  - **(b) As a law of near-uninterrupted growth, the thesis fails after about 1990. On balance.**
    Several democracies cut government spending as a share of GDP for a decade or more with no war:
    Canada 53.3% (1992) to 38.5% (2007), New Zealand 53.0% (1991) to 36.2% (2004), the Netherlands
    56.4% (1995) to about 45% (2001–07) (IMF *Public Finances in Modern History*, via OWID). Caveats
    the lesson states with the numbers: a share can fall because GDP grows; coverage changes in some
    series (Sweden's 1993 jump is a series break and is not used); spending is one measure of extent,
    not the whole of it (conscription, regulation and law are others).
  - **(c) Whether Power's appetite or voters' demand drove the growth. Open.** The spending series
    cannot tell the two apart. No sentence may say that the democrat's objection "confirms the
    mechanism". What would settle it: cases where the franchise widened and spending did not follow,
    or the reverse.
  - **(d) Whether the "protected subject" is less free than the subject of the old intermediate
    powers. Open** — a question of value, left to argument; the lesson states both sides' best case
    and says the course's evidence cannot settle it.
- **Strongest objection to the verdict:** from Jouvenel's side, the post-1990 cuts are shares of a
  growing GDP, not absolute retreats, and spending is not extent — regulation and law grew while the
  share fell, so (b) mistakes a ratio for the thing. It does not overturn (b): Jouvenel's own measure
  is a ratio (Power's resources ÷ society's resources, ch. I translator's note), so a falling share
  over a decade with no war is a reversal on his own terms; the objection is the reason (b) is graded
  on balance, not strong, and the lesson says spending is one measure among several.
- **What would overturn it:** for (b), evidence that the Canadian, New Zealand and Dutch falls are
  artefacts of coverage, or that extent measured in conscription, regulation and law rose through
  the same years enough to outweigh them; for (c), the franchise/spending cases above.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "A medieval king could not conscript his subjects, tax them
without their consent or tell them what to believe; in 1940 democratic governments did all three to
every adult. A French political writer, exiled in Switzerland in 1943, asks how the state that began
as a king's household came to command more than any king ever did — and why the regimes that
claimed to give power to the people were the ones under which it grew fastest. The lesson follows
his answer."

Anchor (L11): Bertrand de Jouvenel (1903–87), French journalist and political writer; wrote On
Power between 1942 and 1945, published in Geneva in 1945, English translation 1948. One sentence,
no adjective, on his past, because the module's question is what the right kept after 1945 (Ref
`the-right-after-1945`): before the war he had moved on the authoritarian right, and in his
eighties he sued a historian who called his early thinking fascist and won token damages (Fawcett,
App. C — state no more than this; the dossier has no further detail). The book's subject is
therefore also its author's: a man who had seen the 1930s up close asks what made the regimes of
1940 possible.

1. **Why did war become total?** Obvious attempt: an aggressor's wickedness, or modern technology.
   Jouvenel's answer from the record (Introduction): the "area of warfare" — the share of society's
   forces sucked into war — rose with each war for nearly a thousand years, because it follows the
   controls Power has over men and money. Work the series in front of the reader: a vassal owed
   forty days; the king could not tax "on any account" and borrowed a tenth from the Church; the
   Hundred Years' War left a permanent tax (the taille) and a standing army; Prussia's cantons
   (1733) and the French levée en masse (23 August 1793) laid hands on bodies; conscription and
   universal suffrage arrived as "twins" (Taine, Ref); 3 million under arms at the end of the
   Napoleonic wars, five times that number killed or mutilated in 1914–18. Answer: total war is the
   product of an apparatus built over centuries — "we are our own Huns". Then the thought
   experiment: had Hitler succeeded Maria Theresa, could he have forged so many weapons of tyranny?
   He "must have found them ready prepared" (Ref `article-48-weimar`). Seam: so the question is not
   who holds Power but how much of society it holds.
2. **How should a state be measured?** Obvious attempt: by its form — one, few, many (Ref
   `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`). Why it fails: the form
   changed from Capetian king to Republic while the series in link 1 rose without a break. Answer
   (ch. I, translator's note): Power's EXTENT = resources at Power's disposal ÷ resources in
   society; history of Power by extent, not by form. Draw the curve. Seam: this changes what counts
   as a revolution — a change of form that leaves the curve rising is no revolution in Power.
3. **Why do men obey?** Obvious attempts: force; or partnership — the people obeys itself (Ref
   `locke-property-and-consent`, `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`). Why each fails: republican
   Rome had a few lictors and no police; modern democracies, where men supposedly obey themselves,
   have the largest police forces ever. Answer: obedience rests first on habit, then on belief —
   "creed, credence, and credit" — in Power's strength, legitimacy and beneficence (Ref
   `hume-against-the-original-contract`: government founded on opinion; `mosca-ruling-class`: the
   political formula). Consequence: Power expands by credit, so whatever raises belief in its
   legitimacy or beneficence raises its extent.
4. **Do the theories of sovereignty limit Power?** Obvious attempt: divine right and popular
   sovereignty are opposites — one subjects the people, the other frees it. Jouvenel: they "stem in
   reality from the same trunk" — the idea that "somewhere there is a right to which all other rights
   must yield" (ch. II). Medieval kingship was not sovereign in that sense: bound by the law of the
   land and by a Church that crowned him as "a sharer in our ministry" (Ref `aquinas-law-and-kingship`;
   Canossa, 1077, with one clause of what happened); divine right was a seventeenth-century fragment
   torn from a doctrine that had bound kings. Popular sovereignty, from Hobbes's covenant (Ref
   `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`: no law can be unjust, the subject is author of it) to Rousseau's
   general will, transferred an unlimited right to whoever speaks for the people (Ref
   `bodin-sovereignty`). The general claim (end of ch. III): theories conceived to restrain Power end
   by serving it, and the reverse never happens — "some mysterious force of attraction". Seam: from
   Rousseau one expects popular sovereignty to be the end of the king's power; Jouvenel shows it was
   the king's power inherited whole.
5. **Against whom does Power grow?** Obvious attempt: against the people. Jouvenel's answer (chs.
   IX–X): against the notables, WITH the people. A central Power can grow only by taking from the
   social authorities that already command men — clan heads, lords, guilds, the Church, later the
   great employers — so its natural ally is their dependants: "The passion for absolutism is,
   inevitably, in conspiracy with the passion for equality." The one-act play: Doge Falieri and the
   plebeian Bertuccio (l.2709). Kings raised plebeian officials ("statocrats", who hold authority
   only from office) against aristocrats (who hold it in their own right); the Ottoman sultan staffed
   army and ministry with converted Christian slaves as the limiting case. Subjects are complicit
   because each advance is presented, often truly, as the suppression of a petty tyranny. Define
   `alliance-of-power-and-the-common-man` here. Then the fork: aristocracies that resisted by
   constitutional means (England — Magna Carta, 1689, the annual Mutiny Act: why there is a Royal
   Navy but no "Royal Army") left Power ringed with defences; the French nobility resisted by riot
   and lost the Third Estate. Two democracies followed: England's extended aristocratic liberties;
   France seized an unrestricted monarchy (Ref `maistre-and-bonald` for Bonald's nobility as a
   limit; `burke-reflections` for the little platoon; `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township` for
   the aristocratic bodies that democracy loses). Define `growth-of-power` here as the result of
   links 1–5.
6. **Why should democracy accelerate it?** Obvious attempt: under democracy Power belongs to the
   people, so it cannot be turned against them. Jouvenel's three reasons: (a) Power is "masked" —
   it claims to be only the instrument of the general will (Ref `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`),
   so no one sees it as alien; (b) "when everyone is potentially a minister, no one is concerned to
   cut down an office to which he aspires one day himself" — the opposition wants the machine, not
   its reduction (the socialists, who called the state an instrument of oppression and planned to
   seize it); (c) democracy was conceived as the sovereignty of law and triumphed as the
   sovereignty of the people, so law becomes whatever the majority wills — "ambulatory law" — and
   the last limit disappears; voters are "citizens for a day", subjects for four years; parties
   become machines and elections plebiscites. Revolutions confirm it: Cromwell, Napoleon, Stalin
   each ruled more than the king he replaced (ch. XII). Conclusion, quoted: "Democracy, then, in the
   centralizing, pattern-making, absolutist shape which we have given to it is… the time of
   tyranny's incubation." Read the qualifier aloud — "in the… shape which we have given to it" —
   it is the hinge of the objection in link 8.
7. **What does Power become when it takes over security?** Obvious attempt: social insurance is a
   service, not a power. Jouvenel (chs. XVII–XVIII): security is the primary need, liberty a
   secondary one; in an age of insecurity a society shows "a marked predisposition to be governed";
   the guarantee of outcomes requires control of conduct. The parallel: Tiberius Gracchus tried to
   make citizens owners, Gaius made the state their provider (the corn dole); Theodore Roosevelt
   fought the trusts to keep citizens independent, Franklin built a structure of Power on the votes
   of the economically weak — "the free citizen passed a milestone on his way to becoming a
   protected subject". The Minotaur returns as protector — "poor-box, housing authority, and god".
   Seam to `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`: Tocqueville's tutelary power was
   mild; Jouvenel's differs by one thing — the same apparatus that provides can mobilize (link 1).
8. **The remedy, and the OBJECTION at full strength.** Jouvenel's remedy (chs. XV–XVI, XIX):
   makeweights — social authorities strong in their own right (Montesquieu's intermediate powers);
   a law older than the state that judges can enforce against officials (the English judicial
   regime; Marshall in 1803 putting a "bit" in the legislature's mouth); intermediate social forces
   that make Power's intervention unnecessary. Then the objection, stated as the course's own, from
   two directions:
   - **From the left / the democrat:** what Jouvenel calls Power's growth was demanded by the people
     it served — pensions, schooling, unemployment insurance, the eight-hour day were won by voters
     and unions, often against the "makeweights" Jouvenel would restore; the aristocracies he calls
     buffers also ruled their dependants (Bertuccio's wound is real), so the alliance of the central
     state with the common man was an alliance against a real domination. Jouvenel concedes the
     first half himself (l.5210, "a marked predisposition to be governed") and answers only that
     consent does not change what Power can later do with what it holds.
   - **From the sceptical liberal (Brogan's preface, References):** the intermediate bodies were not
     only destroyed; many died of their own faults (the parlements became a selfish caste), and
     acceptance of the strong state came from fatigue with disorder, not from a theory. And the
     test case runs against the "law": Britain and the United States greatly enlarged the state's
     extent after 1945 without incubating tyranny — which Jouvenel's own exception (England's
     surviving defences, XIII.6, XV.6) can absorb only by making the outcome depend on situations,
     not on the growth itself.
   Jouvenel's later work (Sovereignty, 1955; practical studies of housing, planning, pollution)
   shows the author himself moving toward the liberal reading; one sentence, from Fawcett App. C.
   The predictive claim — that democracy as such incubates tyranny — is a conditional ("in the shape
   which we have given to it"), and the lesson states the condition. This section states the
   objections; the judgement is link 9.
9. **The weighing: Power's extent grew with democracy, but not as a law, and who drove it is open.**
   Set Jouvenel's thesis against the objections of link 8 and the numbers, and reach the four-part
   verdict of `## VERDICT`, one paragraph each, claim → evidence → bearing (L10e). (a) The growth is
   real and large: the industrial countries of the standard series averaged about 10.8% of output in government
   spending around 1870 and 41.9% in 1980 — **established**. (b) As a law of near-uninterrupted
   growth it fails after about 1990: Canada, New Zealand and the Netherlands each cut spending's share
   of GDP by eleven to seventeen points over a decade or more with no war (figures in `## VERDICT`),
   and on Jouvenel's own measure — a ratio — that is a reversal; state the caveats (GDP growth, series
   coverage, spending as one measure of extent) — **on balance**. (c) Whether Power's appetite or
   voters' demand drove the growth is **open**: the series cannot tell the two apart, so neither
   Jouvenel's mechanism nor the democrat's objection wins; say what would settle it (cases where the
   franchise widened and spending did not follow, or the reverse). Do not write that the objection
   "confirms the mechanism". (d) Whether the protected subject is less free than the subject of the
   old intermediate powers is **open** — a question of value the evidence cannot decide; give each
   side's best sentence and stop. Then the strongest objection to (b) — that a share of a growing GDP
   is not a retreat of Power, and that regulation grew while spending fell — and why it lowers (b) to
   on balance without overturning it. Close the section with what would overturn the verdict.

**Conclusion** (one or two short paragraphs, before Exercises): the verdict in plain words with its
grades — the growth of the state's extent across the democratising era is established; Jouvenel's
law of near-uninterrupted growth fails after about 1990, on balance; whether Power or the voters drove
the growth is open, and so is whether the protected subject is less free. Then the hand-off: if
intermediate social forces are the remedy, which ones, and can they be rebuilt once the state has
absorbed their functions — Ref `kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`.

Headings as claims (suggested; nine body links is too many for 45 minutes — merge links 2–3 into
one section and links 6–7 into one if the draft runs long): "War grew with the controls Power held";
"Measure the state by what it takes, not by its form"; "Men obey from habit and belief"; "Divine
right and popular sovereignty grow from one trunk"; "Power grows against the notables, with the
common man"; "Democracy masks Power and recruits its rivals"; "The protector state"; "What limits
Power: makeweights, and the objection that the people asked for it"; "The state's growth is
established; Jouvenel's law is not, and its cause is open".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule:** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), no more than three per section:** "Power" with a capital (Jouvenel's term
  for the governing apparatus and the men who run it — say so at first use, and that the
  translation keeps the capital); "extent" (the ratio); "Minotaur" (the Cretan monster fed with
  Athenian youths — Jouvenel's image of a state fed with a society's men; one clause); taille; levée
  en masse (the Convention's decree of 23 August 1793 requisitioning all unmarried men 18–25); the
  Fronde (1648–53 revolt against the regency of the boy Louis XIV); Canossa (1077 — the emperor Henry
  IV waiting in the snow for the pope's absolution); "statocrat"; "makeweight" (Huntington's word for
  Montesquieu's intermediate powers — a weight on the other pan of the scale); parlements (the French
  royal law courts, not parliaments); Sieyès (the abbé whose 1789 pamphlet claimed the Third Estate
  was the nation); Constant (Benjamin Constant, French liberal, 1767–1830); Proudhon (French
  socialist and anarchist, 1809–65); the Gracchi (two brothers, tribunes of Rome in 133 and 123 BC).
- **Do not re-teach** Tocqueville's soft despotism, Rousseau's general will, Bodin's sovereignty,
  Hobbes's authorisation, Mosca's political formula — each is a clause plus `<Ref>`. The lesson's
  new matter is the MEASURE (extent) and the MECHANISM (alliance with the common man; the mask).
- **The real misreading (L13):** "Jouvenel is a libertarian who opposes all government." He calls
  Power's duality irreducible — "neither angel nor brute" (VI.15–17); it "established internal order,
  protected the weak against the strong" (l.395); he condemns unlimited Power wherever it rests
  (Constant, XV.5), not Power. One slot; no other strawman.
- **A second distinction to keep clean:** Jouvenel is not saying democracy and totalitarianism are
  the same; he says the apparatus a democracy builds is inherited by whoever takes it, and that a
  majority Power is the hardest to limit because opposition can limit only a minority (XV.7). State
  the conditional, not the slogan.
- **Dated material:** chs. IV–V rest on nineteenth-century ethnology (Frazer, Morgan, Bachofen),
  which Jouvenel himself hedges; use at most one sentence (the war-leader vs priest-king duality) and
  no "primitive" anecdotes. Written 1942–45: the remarks on Franklin Roosevelt and on Germany are
  wartime polemic — present the Gracchi/Roosevelt parallel as Jouvenel's argument, and give the
  reader the counter-case (the New Deal did not end elections).
- **Translation:** Huntington translates via the French and quotes canonical authors from French
  versions; quote Rousseau or Hobbes from the course's own editions (their lessons), not from
  Jouvenel's renderings.
- **Opening facts the dossier does not ground:** "exiled in Switzerland in 1943" is not stated in any
  dossier text (On Power was published in Geneva in 1945; the 1943 flight is from biographies).
  The writer must verify against a standard biography before the lesson states the date; if
  unverified, say "in Switzerland, where he wrote the book's last chapters" only if that too is
  verified, otherwise keep the opening (it is fixed) and add no further detail. "Tell them what to
  believe" in the opening corresponds to Jouvenel's account of wartime propaganda (Introduction
  l.296–300) and of the state's monopoly of education (l.487), not to a claim that democracies
  abolished freedom of opinion — make the referent explicit in the first section.
- **Biography thin:** the dossier gives only Fawcett's appendix lines on Jouvenel's prewar politics;
  do not add party names or episodes from memory without a source, and keep it to one sentence.
- **Numbers (L12):** keeper numbers as in set piece 2; everything else in words or in the figure.
  Do not compute a numerical "extent" for any state — Jouvenel gives no data for the ratio; the curve
  is schematic and the caption says so.
- **Balance:** the objection in link 8 must be put at the same strength as the thesis — the welfare
  state's defenders and the sceptical liberal both get their best case. The register rule above applies.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (the only mechanical one):** order on a timeline and give one clause each:
   forty-day service, the taille, Prussia's cantons, the levée en masse, Taine's twins, Hitler's
   appointment (Ref `article-48-weimar`) — and say which ones raised the extent of Power by men,
   which by money.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** the "Hitler and Maria Theresa" passage (l.491–496).
   Author (a French writer, 1942–45, with a prewar past on the authoritarian right — Ref
   `the-right-after-1945`), audience, purpose; what it RECORDS (nothing — it is a counterfactual),
   what it ARGUES, what it OMITS (the Nazi party's own creations: the SS, the camps); what the
   passage can be USED to establish about the Weimar state and what it cannot.
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** take a state whose extent rose sharply with no loss of liberty — Britain
   1939–51 (conscription, rationing, the National Health Service, then free elections that removed
   the wartime government in 1945 and the next in 1951). Does Jouvenel's law fail at this case, or
   does his own England exception (ringed Power, judicial regime) cover it? The solution names the
   condition in link 6 and says what the case shows about "incubation".
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Jouvenel says democracy is a form of tyranny." Name the step that
   fails (the qualifier "in the centralizing, pattern-making, absolutist shape which we have given to
   it"; incubation is not identity; England vs France) and state what he does claim.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** from link 5's alliance, derive what Jouvenel must predict
   about an aristocracy that tries to GOVERN rather than to limit (Saint-Simon's plan to staff the
   ministries with dukes, X.6), and check the derivation against his verdict that the aristocracy's
   role was "not to govern, but to act as buffer-stop to the government".
6. **Corroboration (E3, two earlier lessons):** set Tocqueville's tutelary power (Ref
   `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`) beside Jouvenel's Minotaur as protector
   (XVIII.12). Where do they agree (equality and centralization travel together; the counterweights),
   where do they part (mildness vs mobilization for war), and which fact of 1914–45 explains the
   difference?
7. **Synthesis / counter-argument:** "The growth of the state since 1800 was demanded by voters, so
   it is not Power's growth but the people's choice." Name two facts the claim must account for
   (taxes and conscription made permanent after wars the voters did not choose to prolong — VII.4;
   "everyone is potentially a minister") and state what would have to be true for the claim to
   survive (that the people can later take back what it granted — the reversibility Jouvenel denies).
   The solution ends on the verdict: the post-1990 cuts in Canada, New Zealand and the Netherlands
   are cases of a people taking back part of what it granted; who drove the growth stays open.
8. **Attack the verdict.** The lesson judged Jouvenel's law of near-uninterrupted growth to fail after
   about 1990, on balance. State the strongest objection in its best form: a falling share of a
   growing GDP is not a retreat of Power, and in the same years the state's reach by regulation and
   law grew. Then say what evidence would overturn the verdict: name the measure of extent other than
   spending you would need (pages of regulation, share of employment set by public law, conscription
   or its absence), the countries and years you would compare, and the result that would show
   Power's extent rising through the 1990s in Canada, New Zealand and the Netherlands.
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`:** Jouvenel ends by saying that
   intermediate social forces "enframe, protect and control the life of man, thereby obviating the
   intervention of Power". Name three such forces from this lesson (the family, the Church, the
   guild or union), and derive what happens to each one's hold on its members once the state takes
   over its function (schooling, welfare, arbitration). The solution says in one sentence that the
   next lesson's sociologist builds his whole account of modern loneliness and of totalitarianism on
   this derivation, with a `<Ref>`.

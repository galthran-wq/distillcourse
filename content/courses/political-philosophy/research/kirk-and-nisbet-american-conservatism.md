# Research brief: kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism (Kirk and Nisbet: the conservative mind and the quest for community in America)

status: planned
module: postwar-conservatism
type: core
goal: Analyze how Kirk's canons of conservative thought and Nisbet's account of the lost intermediate associations founded postwar American conservatism, and evaluate the movement's founding tension between tradition and the free market

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township` (prerequisite): the `art-of-association`
  and the township — the American answer to equality, which both books claim as theirs. From
  `voegelin-gnosticism` (prerequisite): ideology as a political religion that "immanentizes" the
  Christian end — Kirk's Foreword borrows exactly this. From `what-conservatism-is`:
  `conservatism-as-disposition`, `positional-ideology`, `epistemic-conservatism` and the rival
  definitions — Kirk's canons are a fifth kind, a canon by example. From
  `oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`: the three political illusions (Kirk quotes them). From
  `burke-reflections`: `prejudice`, the `little-platoon`, the partnership of the dead, the living and
  the unborn, `natural-aristocracy`. From `jouvenel-on-power`: `growth-of-power` and the
  `alliance-of-power-and-the-common-man` against the social authorities — Nisbet's thesis is the
  same history told through the functions of groups. From `bodin-sovereignty`,
  `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`, `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: the three sovereigns
  Nisbet ranks as the stages of the absolute state. From
  `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`: `soft-despotism` — Nisbet calls the 1840
  passage the best analysis of totalitarianism. From `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`: the mass-man.
  From `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government` and `stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity`: status to
  contract, and two of Kirk's own chapters' subjects. Each a `<Ref>` and one clause.
- **Contributes:** The one move: in a country whose founding was a revolution and whose public
  philosophy was liberal, two books of 1953 supply a conservatism from two opposite ends — Kirk a
  pedigree (a line of ideas from Burke through John Adams, Randolph and Calhoun, held together by six
  canons), Nisbet a diagnosis (modern loneliness is the result of the state's absorption of the
  functions of family, church, guild and locality; totalitarianism is the community offered to the
  uprooted). Both name the same enemy — the centralised state and the abstract individual it faces —
  and both leave open whether the free market, which the movement they founded would defend, is the
  ally of the intermediate associations or another solvent of them.
- **Hands off:** (i) The third founding figure of the National Review circle answers a question
  both books raise and neither answers: if the planned state is growing, WHO runs it? Kirk himself,
  in his last chapter, calls the coming order what "James Burnham called the 'managerial
  revolution'" — `burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`. (ii) Kirk's canons are applied to a
  document in `udhr-read-against-the-right` (Kirk's own objection to the Declaration's "right to
  marry"). (iii) Later: `scruton-meaning-of-conservatism` (authority before freedom; autonomous
  institutions), `lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`, `deneen-post-liberalism` (market and state as twin
  solvents of the communities Nisbet defends), `the-nation-and-its-defenders`.
- **Spine step:** "After 1945 fascism has discredited the right, and it rebuilds in new registers:
  Jouvenel's history of Power, Oakeshott's disposition, Strauss's natural right, Voegelin's diagnosis
  of Gnosticism, Kirk's and Nisbet's American conservatism, Burnham's managers — tested on the
  Declaration of 1948"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "In 1953 the United States had won a world war, was the richest
nation in history and had no conservative intellectual tradition anyone in its universities took
seriously. Two young scholars publish that year a genealogy of conservative thought from Burke to
T. S. Eliot and a sociology of why modern men feel alone among millions. The lesson reads the two
books as the founding of a movement and asks what kind of conservatism a country founded by a
revolution could have."

Anchors (L11): Russell Kirk (1918–94), son of a Michigan railway engineer, history instructor at
Michigan State, who wrote the book while studying at St Andrews; Robert Nisbet (1913–96), sociologist
at the University of California. The opening's "from Burke to T. S. Eliot" is the subtitle of later editions; the 1953
subtitle ran "from Burke to Santayana", and the final chapter on Eliot and the pages on Nisbet were
added later (see Pitfalls) — say so in one clause where the lesson first quotes ch. XIII.

1. **Was there anything to conserve?** The problem, stated twice. In 1950 a leading critic wrote
   that liberalism was "not only the dominant but even the sole intellectual tradition" in the United
   States, and that conservative impulses showed themselves only "in action or in irritable mental
   gestures which seek to resemble ideas" (Kirk l.13743–13750; `<Quote>`). The deeper form of the
   problem (Hartz's, stated as the course's own and held for link 7): a country founded by a
   revolution, with no king, no established Church, no nobility — no ancien régime — was liberal at
   birth; conservatism there could only be the defence of a liberal founding. Seam: two books in one
   year answered from opposite ends — one with a pedigree of ideas, one with a diagnosis of a social
   condition.
2. **What makes a conservative, if not a doctrine?** Obvious attempt: a list of policies, or the
   defence of whatever exists (Ref `what-conservatism-is`: `positional-ideology`). Kirk refuses both:
   conservatism is "not a fixed and immutable body of dogmata", its essence "preservation of the
   ancient moral traditions of humanity". But he gives SIX CANONS (l.979–1009) — define
   `canons-of-conservatism` here and work each one against a lesson the reader already has: (1) a
   transcendent order, politics as at bottom a moral and religious problem (Ref
   `aquinas-law-and-kingship`); (2) affection for "the proliferating variety and mystery" of life
   against "narrowing uniformity, egalitarianism, and utilitarian aims"; (3) "civilized society
   requires orders and classes" — "if natural distinctions are effaced among men, oligarchs fill the
   vacuum" (Ref `burke-reflections`: `natural-aristocracy`; `mosca-ruling-class` for the oligarchs);
   (4) "separate property from private possession, and Leviathan becomes master of all" (Ref
   `jouvenel-on-power`); (5) prescription and distrust of "sophisters, calculators, and economists"
   (Burke's phrase; Ref `burke-reflections`: `prejudice`); (6) "change may not be salutary reform";
   prudence as the statesman's chief virtue. Against them, four radical premises: perfectibility,
   contempt for tradition, political levelling, economic levelling. Kirk then calls conservatism "the
   negation of ideology" — Ref `voegelin-gnosticism` for ideology as political religion, and
   `oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics` for the illusions Kirk quotes. Seam: a definition by canons
   can be tested by what it excludes — the next link.
3. **Whose tradition, and does America have one?** Kirk's genealogy: Burke as founder; Hobbes,
   Filmer, Bolingbroke excluded, Hegel only "a Tory by chance", the Continental reactionaries left
   out (Ref `maistre-and-bonald`, `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state` — the reader can see why each
   fails a canon); in America, John Adams rather than Hamilton (Adams against perfectibility and
   against the single all-powerful assembly; "an aristocrat is a citizen who commands two votes or
   more" — aristocracy cannot be abolished, only checked and used); Randolph ("Change is not
   reform") and Calhoun (the concurrent majority — a veto for each major interest, so that "the rich
   and the poor" cannot simply outvote each other); Tocqueville as the one Continental admitted (Ref
   `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`). The answer to link 1: the American
   Revolution "was not an innovating upheaval, but a conservative restoration of colonial
   prerogatives" — the rights of Englishmen held by prescription (l.2750–2760). So America has a
   conservative line because its founding conserved. Seam: the Southern half of the line raises the
   hardest question about the canon — handled in link 7, not here.
4. **Why do modern men feel alone among millions?** Nisbet's problem (ch. 1): "a specter is
   haunting the modern mind, the specter of insecurity". Obvious attempts: industry, the city,
   technology, capitalism, "social disorganization". Why they fail (ch. 3): the great organizations
   are not disorganized; what failed is the small primary group's hold on its members. The mechanism
   (the lesson's central derivation, worked on one institution): a group keeps psychological and
   symbolic authority only while it performs functions its members need — the family that produced,
   educated, nursed and buried; once school, welfare office, factory and hospital take those
   functions, the family is asked to supply affection and meaning with none of the functions that
   made it matter — "the family is now the accident of the worker rather than his essence". The
   discriminating example: caste in India survived twenty-five centuries of religious assault and
   began to dissolve once courts, schools and factories took over its functions (l.839–849). Define
   `intermediate-associations` here.
5. **Who took the functions?** Nisbet's thesis (ch. 5): "the single most decisive influence upon
   Western social organization has been the rise and development of the centralized territorial
   State" — a "permanent revolution". The real conflict of modern history is "not… between State and
   individual, but between State and social group"; the state liberated the individual from guild,
   Church and kin, and the same act concentrated power in itself. The stages (ch. 6) are recalled, not
   taught: Bodin's sovereign who still exempts the family, Hobbes's state with no room for lesser
   bodies ("wormes in the entrayles"), Rousseau's general will that tolerates no partial society — one
   clause and a `<Ref>` each. The list of what every modern state claims (conscription, taxation,
   education, the family, opinion) "communists, fascists, and democrats" alike (l.1210) as the
   reader's own checklist. Say the seam to `jouvenel-on-power`: the same history, but where Jouvenel
   made war and the alliance with the common man the engine, Nisbet makes the absorption of FUNCTIONS
   the engine — and so locates the damage not in the state's size but in the members' loneliness.
6. **What is totalitarianism, then?** Obvious attempts (ch. 8 lists and rejects them): racism,
   poverty, capitalism, reaction, irrationality. Nisbet: two elements — the masses and the ideology of
   the political community. A mass is qualitative, not quantitative: India's millions are no mass
   while their groups hold; a mass is people "ground down… into mere particles of social dust"
   (l.1890). The totalitarian party offers the uprooted a community (the Party as "family, school,
   church, barracks"), and where masses do not yet exist it must create them by destroying village,
   church, family and union (l.1895–1900). Define `quest-for-community` here: the uprooted seek
   community, and the state, the only group left, offers it. Ref `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses` (the
   mass-man as a type) and `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism` (Nisbet's chosen text);
   the difference from both: Nisbet's mass is made by institutions, not by temperament or by equality
   alone.
7. **The remedy, the founding tension, and the OBJECTIONS at full strength.**
   - *Nisbet's remedy (ch. 11):* "Freedom… lies in the interstices of authority" — a plurality of
     authorities, each tight enough to give security, with the member always able to move to another;
     a "new laissez faire" whose unit is the group, not the individual; a pluralist rather than a
     monolithic state. Kirk endorses it (ch. XIII, l.14119–14138).
   - *Weaver, as reported (one paragraph, ★ if it runs longer):* the third book usually named with
     these two, Ideas Have Consequences (1948), traced the same disorder to an idea — fourteenth-century
     nominalism — rather than to an institution; the lesson says plainly that it reports the book's
     thesis as others summarise it (in References: "not read for this course"), and gives the
     objection that Ockham may not have held the view blamed on him.
   - *The founding tension — tradition and the market:* the movement that formed around these books
     after 1955 (Buckley's National Review) joined traditionalists, free-market liberals and
     anticommunists; Hayek told the 1957 Mont Pelerin meeting why he was "not a conservative", Kirk
     answered him on the spot, and the attempt to hold the strands together got the name "fusion".
     The strongest statement of the strain is inside Nisbet's own book (ch. 10 sec. 4): the free
     market "never existed at all save in the imaginations of the rationalists"; it rested on family,
     locality and property "which had nothing whatsoever to do with the essence of capitalism", and,
     in the words he borrows, capitalism "is bound to destroy" those props — while he grants "the
     abstract superiority" of economic freedom. Kirk's canons 2 and 4 pull the same two ways
     (variety against utilitarian aims; property as freedom), and his ch. VII calls the equation of
     conservatism with the defence of accumulation an American error.
   - *Objection 1 (the liberal-consensus reply, Hartz's, stated as the course's own):* with no ancien
     régime, the "conservative restoration" of 1776 conserved English liberties that were already
     liberal; Kirk's American line is liberalism under another name or a tradition imported from
     England. Kirk's reply, from his text: the rights of Englishmen were held by prescription, not by
     abstract right — the difference between Burke's and Paine's account of the same liberties (Ref
     `burke-reflections`).
   - *Objection 2 (from the left and from the record — localism):* the intermediate bodies Nisbet
     defends also governed their members, and in the American South the locality and the state
     legislature enforced segregation; the central state that broke those authorities in the 1950s
     and 60s freed individuals from them. Kirk's own line runs through Randolph and Calhoun, and
     Kirk's own page concedes "Human slavery is bad ground for conservatives to make a stand upon"
     while proposing "to keep clear" of the controversy (l.4935–4938). Nisbet's reply is his own
     condition: freedom in the interstices requires a real exit from any one authority — which a
     slave or a segregated citizen did not have. The lesson states the condition and lets the reader
     weigh whether it saves the thesis.
   - *Objection 3 (from the record — the prediction):* civic membership in America rose to a peak in
     the 1950s–60s and fell from about 1970 (Carroll's essay, with sourced data in References) — after
     Nisbet wrote; the decline came both with a larger state and with television, the car and the
     suburb, so the evidence fits Nisbet's mechanism and its rivals; the critics' rival cause is
     technology and the market, not the state.
   - *Objection 4 (the definitional one):* Kirk's tests are "generously loose" — with a few
     substitutions a market liberal passes them (Fawcett, References); his canon prefers "literary
     evocation over social and political analysis" (Muller, References). Reply: Kirk says himself the
     canons are not a doctrine, and judges by thinkers, not by tests.
   Where the lesson concedes and holds: the books founded a movement (reception, 1953–57, is a fact);
   the thesis that the state's absorption of functions weakens groups is supported by the caste and
   family cases; whether its remedy is compatible with the free market the movement championed is the
   open question the lesson hands on.

Headings as claims (suggested): "A tradition denied"; "Six canons, not a doctrine"; "A revolution
that conserved"; "Groups hold their members by their functions"; "The state against the group";
"The masses are made"; "Freedom in the interstices — and the market".

   "If natural distinctions are effaced among men, oligarchs fill the vacuum" set beside Mosca
   (Ref) — the reader sees a conservative canon restate an elite-theory law.
3. **"Commands two votes"** (Kirk ch. III l.3403–3425): Adams's definition of an aristocrat, worked
   on a town meeting of a hundred men — the man whose son-in-law or debtor votes as he does is an
   aristocrat whether the law says so or not.
4. **The family's functions removed one by one** (Nisbet ch. 3 l.863–921): production, education,
   care of the sick and old, welfare, recreation — each transferred to an outside institution; then
   "the accident of the worker rather than his essence" (l.877).
5. **Caste in India** (Nisbet l.839–849): what twenty-five centuries of religious assault failed to
   do, a few decades of courts, schools and factories did — the discriminating case for "function
   before symbol".
6. **The list of what a modern state claims** (Nisbet l.1210): monopoly of force, war and peace,
   conscription, taxation, property, crime, education, the family, personal habits, opinion — and
   "no real difference in the size of the claim between communists, fascists, and democrats".
7. **The free market's props** (Nisbet l.2251–2270): the passage that states the movement's tension
   inside its founding text.
8. **Mont Pelerin, 1957** (Regnery l.459–480): Hayek's paper, Kirk's extemporaneous reply — the
   founding tension as a scene (dated, with the society defined in a clause: an international
   association of free-market economists and writers founded in Switzerland in 1947).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule:** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or
  approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may
  appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Edition trap (real, and easy to miss):** the dossier's Kirk is the 7th edition (1986). The 1953
  book was subtitled "from Burke to Santayana"; ch. XIII as we have it (Eliot, the pages on Nisbet,
  the "managerial revolution" passage, the 1980s remarks) was written or revised later. Never present
  a ch. XIII sentence as "Kirk in 1953". The course.yaml opening says "from Burke to T. S. Eliot" —
  the writer keeps the opening verbatim and corrects the date at first quotation (one clause).
- **Terms to anchor (L11), at most three per section:** "canon"; "prescription" (title by long
  possession — recall from `burke-reflections`); Southern Agrarians (a group of Vanderbilt writers who
  in 1930 defended the agrarian South against industrialism); John Adams (second president, 1797–1801;
  author of a defence of the American state constitutions); John Randolph of Roanoke (Virginia
  congressman, 1773–1833); John C. Calhoun (South Carolina senator and vice-president, 1782–1850;
  defender of slavery and of a state's right to nullify federal law — say both, plainly); "concurrent
  majority"; National Review (Buckley's magazine, founded 1955); Mont Pelerin Society; "fusion";
  "intermediate association"; "mass" (Nisbet's qualitative sense); nominalism (one sentence: the view
  that general terms name no real common natures, only collections of particulars).
- **Do not re-teach** Bodin, Hobbes, Rousseau (Nisbet's ch. 6 is a tempting re-run), Tocqueville's
  soft despotism, Burke's prejudice and little platoons, Oakeshott's rationalism, Voegelin's
  immanentization — each is a clause and a `<Ref>`. The new matter is the CANON (as a method of
  definition) and the FUNCTION mechanism.
- **The real misreading (L13):** "Nisbet is a nostalgic who wants the medieval village back." He
  denies it in the 1970 preface (l.296: "any lament for the old, any nostalgia for the village,
  parish…") and his remedy is new associations created under a new laissez faire, not restored old
  ones. One slot.
- **Slavery and race (Kirk ch. V, VII, X):** state facts plainly (Calhoun defended slavery; Kirk
  separates his constitutional ideas from it and says so), quote Kirk's own two sentences, and let
  the objection in link 7 carry the argument. Do not reproduce the Forrest anecdote (l.4929–4933) or
  the antisemitic remarks Kirk quotes from Henry Adams (ch. X). No adjectives about either side.
- **Weaver:** see Sources. The lesson must say in References that the book was not read for the
  course and that the account follows the named reports. No direct quotation of Weaver.
- **Fusionism is thin in the dossier:** only Fawcett's three lines (l.11386–11389), Regnery's Mont
  Pelerin account and the reception notes; Frank Meyer's own texts are not here. Keep "fusion" to a
  defined word and a dated scene; do not reconstruct Meyer's argument. Flagged for the human.
- **Hartz is reported, not read:** state his thesis in one sentence from Fawcett and argue with it;
  do not attribute further claims to him.
- **Numbers (L12):** none needed beyond dates (1948, 1950, 1953, 1955, 1957) and the caste example's
  "twenty-five centuries". If the civic-membership decline is used, give it in words ("peaked in the
  1950s and 60s, fell from about 1970") unless the writer obtains the underlying series with its
  source.
- **Balance:** Kirk's and Nisbet's case at full strength first (a reader should feel the force of the
  family example), then the objections from the liberal-consensus side, from the civil-rights record
  and from the data. The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (the only mechanical one):** for each of Kirk's six canons, name the earlier
   course thinker whose argument it compresses and the lesson (e.g. canon 5 → Burke's prejudice;
   canon 3 → Burke's natural aristocracy or Mosca's organized minority — the reader must choose and
   say why).
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** Kirk's sentence on slavery (l.4935–4938). Author, date
   (1953, repeated in 1986), audience, purpose; what it records, what it argues ("keep clear… of that
   partisan controversy"), what it omits; what the passage can be USED to establish about how the
   founding text handled the Southern half of its canon — and what it cannot establish about Calhoun.
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** Nisbet's "freedom lies in the interstices of authority" holds "so long
   as man has even the theoretical possibility of removing himself" from an authority (l.2531). Apply
   it to a county in which the school board, the church and the employer all enforce the same racial
   rule. What does the thesis say at this boundary, and which actor does it license to intervene? The
   solution shows that the exit condition does the work and that it implies a role for an outside
   (possibly central) authority.
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Postwar American conservatism was from the start the defence of free
   enterprise." Name two passages that break it (Nisbet ch. 10 sec. 4 on the market's precapitalist
   props; Kirk ch. VII on the "Hamilton legend" and canon 2 against "utilitarian aims") and state what
   the founders defended instead.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** from Nisbet's function mechanism (link 4), derive what he
   must predict for a church that hands its schools, hospitals and charities to the state but keeps
   its liturgy — and compare the prediction with the caste case.
6. **Corroboration (E3, `jouvenel-on-power`):** set Jouvenel's alliance of Power with the common man
   beside Nisbet's "State versus social group". Two short excerpts; where they agree (the state grows
   by absorbing rival authorities), where they differ (engine: war and the levelling alliance vs the
   transfer of functions; harm: tyranny and total war vs loneliness and the mass), and which one the
   caste example supports better.
7. **Research-shaped task (E6d):** design the comparison that would distinguish Nisbet's cause (the
   state's absorption of functions) from his critics' (technology and the market) in the decline of
   American civic membership after about 1970: which countries or states to compare, what to measure,
   what result would favour each account.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `udhr-read-against-the-right`:** Kirk objects to the 1948 Declaration that "if a
   man has a right to marry, some woman must have the duty" (ch. II, l.2083–2095). Using canon 5 and
   Burke's real rights (Ref), state the general principle behind the objection (every right names a
   bearer of a correlative duty) and apply it to one more article of the Declaration of your choice.
   The solution says in one sentence that the module's last lesson reads the Declaration article by
   article with exactly this test, with a `<Ref>`.

# Research brief: landes-european-exceptionalism (Landes's Wealth and Poverty of Nations: fragmentation, invention and culture)

status: planned
module: why-the-west
type: core
goal: Analyze Landes's account of why Europe pulled ahead — political fragmentation, property rights, the invention of invention, culture — and evaluate it against the cases he contrasts

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `weber-only-in-the-west`: the module's question in Weber's form (why rational
  science, law, bureaucracy and the capitalist firm appeared together "only in the West"), the
  `protestant-ethic` and `rationalization` — and Weber's own disclaimer of a single cause, which
  leaves open what *else* made Europe different. Landes calls himself Weber's heir ("Here Max
  Weber was right on", ch. 29) and treats the Protestant ethic as one input among several (ch. 12),
  so the lesson inherits Weber as a partial answer to be widened. From `greek-miracle`: the polis
  as power "in the middle" against the palace kingdom — Landes opens his political chapter with
  the same contrast (Greek free city against "oriental despotism"). From `bodin-sovereignty` and
  `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` (concept `sovereignty`): sovereignty was invented to end
  Europe's division; Landes's thesis is that the division itself, which Bodin and Hobbes wanted
  overcome inside each state, was Europe's luck *between* states. From `burke-reflections` and
  `locke-property-and-consent`: property secure from the ruler as the condition of industry
  (Landes quotes Burke: "a law against property is a law against industry"). From
  `what-measures-greatness`: the three measures of a civilization's greatness; Landes's is
  wealth and the capacity to make and spread techniques.
- **Contributes:** The one move: Europe's lead is explained not by what Europe *had* (it was
  poorer, smaller and later than China around 1000–1400) but by *who could stop whom*. A
  continent of competing rulers gave subjects a right of exit, forced rulers to bargain with towns
  and merchants and to respect property, and left no single authority able to suppress a new
  technique — so inventions were not only made but kept, improved and spread ("the invention of
  invention"). Where one authority could say no, as in Ming China over ocean voyages and the
  Ottoman world over printing, it did. Landes adds, last and most contested, that "culture makes
  all the difference". The lesson rebuilds the chain, tests it on Landes's own contrasts (Zheng
  He; the clock in China and Islam; printing; Japan as a "miniature of medieval Europe"; India),
  and puts the strongest objections to it.
- **Hands off:** (i) Landes asserts Europe's accomplishment but does not measure it; the attempt
  to count it is `murray-human-accomplishment` (FORWARD exercise). (ii) Fragmentation explains why
  innovations were not *suppressed*; it does not explain why so many Europeans *tried* — Landes's
  answer is "the market" and "culture", which he leaves undefined; a mechanism for that culture is
  `henrich-church-and-kinship`. (iii) The objection that Europe's lead came late (after 1800) and
  rested on coal and colonies, and that "the invention of invention" claims for Europe what was a
  Eurasian inheritance, is stated here as an objection and developed in
  `pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis`. (iv) The borrowings Landes mentions in ch. 4 (paper,
  printing, gunpowder) are graded item by item in `the-borrowing-ledger`. (v) Landes's "law of
  imperialism" and his balance sheet of empire are taken up in `colonial-balance-sheet`; his
  geography chapter is answered in `geography-or-institutions`.
- **Spine step:** "Why the West? Weber, Landes, Murray and Henrich argue for Western exceptionalism
  from religion, fragmentation, accomplishment and kinship; Pomeranz and Goody answer that the West
  was one link in a long chain of exchange; the borrowing ledger tests both"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): Chinese fleets larger than anything Europe could build reach
East Africa, are given up within a century, while small Portuguese ships round Africa; an
economic historian says the difference lay less in what each had than in who could stop whom.
Anchor (L11) at first mention: David Landes (1924–2013), Harvard economic historian, author of a
1969 history of European industrialization; *The Wealth and Poverty of Nations* (1998), a book for
the general reader that asks why a few nations became rich. Zheng He: a Muslim eunuch admiral of
the Ming emperor Yongle; seven voyages 1405–1433 into the Indian Ocean, the later ones to the
coast of East Africa. Vasco da Gama: Portuguese captain who sailed round the Cape of Good Hope to
Calicut in India, 1497–99. Place both on a locator figure (FIGURE 1).

1. **What has to be explained?** Problem as the newcomer meets it: around 1000 no observer would
   have bet on Europe — Landes: the probability of European dominance was "somewhere around
   zero"; five centuries later "close to one" (ch. 3 opening). China had more people, more iron
   (Landes: pig-iron output in the late 11th century not matched by Britain for seven centuries —
   summary gems ch. 4), paper, printing, the compass and gunpowder. Obvious attempt: Europe had
   more resources or more talent. Why it fails: the inventory of *what each had* favours China
   — that is the point of the opening's fleet comparison. Answer the lesson pursues: look at what
   happened to inventions and ventures *after* they were made. Recall Weber in one clause
   (`<Ref weber-only-in-the-west>`): a religious ethic made some Europeans work and save
   methodically; Landes accepts it and asks what kept such men from being stopped. State the
   three answers Landes rejects (Introduction l. 592–596) as the field he enters: good against
   bad, villains against victims, Europe as free rider. Seam: Landes will argue from politics
   before culture.
2. **Is it geography?** Obvious attempt: climate and soil decide. Landes's partial yes: geography
   is "unfair" — heat and disease weigh on the tropics; Europe's even, year-round rain let
   farming families work their own plots without a canal authority, whereas along great rivers
   "the master of food was master of people" (ch. 2) — the Joseph-and-Pharaoh story as allegory
   (Genesis 47: money, then cattle, then land, then persons given for grain). Why it is not the
   answer: "it would be a mistake to see geography as destiny" (ch. 1); geography works through
   the political order it favours. Answer: geography sets the odds of centralised power; the
   decisive variable is the power itself. One paragraph and a two-column figure; do not argue
   the hydraulic thesis (flag in Pitfalls that it is contested).
3. **Why did division help rather than hurt?** Obvious attempt (and the reader's likely
   intuition, and the lesson of `bodin-sovereignty` and `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`):
   division means war and insecurity; unity brings peace and prosperity — Rome's dream, the
   *pax Romana*. Landes's reversal: "Europe's great good fortune lay in the fall of Rome." The
   mechanism, link by link: (a) after Rome "the arm of authority was short" — rulers nominally
   elected, "human rather than divine"; (b) a ruler who wants revenue from new land, crops and
   trade must *attract* settlers, townsmen and merchants with franchises, because they could go
   elsewhere — "they had to persuade them to come" (contrast: Chinese rulers moved tens of
   thousands of peasants by order); (c) the commune — a self-governing town with its own law —
   becomes a hole in the net of serfdom (*Stadtluft macht frei*, "city air makes one free"; the
   count of Flanders driven out of the Bruges market when he tried to seize a runaway serf);
   (d) property held against the prince (Samuel's "Whose ox have I taken?", Burke's sentence;
   `<Ref burke-reflections>`, `<Ref locke-property-and-consent>`), whereas the despot's
   subjects held what they had "at his pleasure" — the people of Balkh told that war "was not
   their affair; their duty was to pay and obey whoever ruled them"; (e) Church against crown —
   "where authority is divided, dissent flourishes". Answer, Landes's sentence: "Political
   rivalry and the right of exit made all the difference." Seam (L10b — a real reversal): the
   course taught that sovereignty was invented to *end* Europe's division; Landes argues that
   the division sovereignty failed to end at the level of the continent is what made Europe rich.
   Define `european-fragmentation` here, as the answer just derived.
4. **What does fragmentation do to an invention?** Obvious attempt: inventions spread because they
   are useful. Why it fails: Landes's China — clocks reverted to water clocks, hemp spinning never
   moved to cotton, coke smelting fell out of use; and the Ming ban on maritime trade. Useful
   things can be suppressed when one authority finds them threatening or costly. Work the four
   examples as a single comparison (table, FIGURE 4): eyeglasses (the 1306 witness; doubled
   skilled working life; led to instruments of precision), the clock (equal hours, a public
   time owned by towns, productivity measured; in China an imperial secret, in Islam no public
   clocks lest the muezzin lose authority), printing (no one could "put a lid on" it in Europe;
   refused in the Ottoman lands, "Islam's greatest mistake"), gunpowder (a borrowing — see the
   graded statement in Pitfalls — turned into corned powder and bronze cannon by bell-founders).
   Answer: in a fragmented continent a ruler who banned a technique handed it to his rivals;
   so techniques once made were kept and improved. That is the political half of "the invention
   of invention".
5. **Why did Europeans keep trying?** Obvious attempt: the political half suffices. Why not: a
   technique that cannot be suppressed must still be invented, and Landes's China *had*
   inventors. His answer has two layers. First, values he calls Judaeo-Christian — manual work
   honoured (Noah builds the ark himself), nature subordinated to man, time as a line rather than
   a circle — which he ranks second. Then: "In the last analysis, however, I would stress the
   market. Enterprise was free in Europe. Innovation worked and paid" — success bred imitation.
   Second layer (ch. 14): from the 16th–17th c. Europe *institutionalised* discovery: autonomy of
   inquiry, a common adversarial language of proof across rival states ("unity in disunity"),
   and "the routinization of research and its diffusion" — academies (the Lincei, Rome 1603),
   correspondence networks, journals, priority fights. Define `invention-of-invention` here:
   Landes's phrase for sustained, cumulative making *and* spreading of techniques, first by
   craftsmen and markets, then by institutions of science.
6. **Test the thesis on Landes's own contrasts.** Each case set as a question with what the thesis
   predicts:
   - *Zheng He (the opening).* Predicts: a single authority will end a costly venture when its
     priorities change, and no rival will take it up. Record: the fleets end in the 1430s; by 1500
     two-masted ships are capital offences; in 1477 the logs disappear; no Chinese ship reaches
     Europe until 1851. Landes's causes: no target ("not an appetizer but a dessert"), no profit
     calculus, no private stake — whereas Gama's voyage was a crown venture with merchants
     behind it and a rival crown (Spain) to race. Landes himself says the result was
     "overdetermined": fiscal strain, the northern frontier, court faction. Concede in the body:
     several of those causes are not fragmentation.
   - *Printing in the Ottoman lands.* Predicts: a unified religious-political authority can refuse
     a technique for centuries. Record as Landes states it; the evidence against is in Pitfalls
     (Jewish and Christian presses in Istanbul from the 1490s; a Muslim-run press only from 1727;
     the existence of a formal ban is debated).
   - *Japan, the boundary case.* Landes: Tokugawa Japan was "a rough miniature of medieval Europe"
     — one shogunate over some 250 semi-autonomous domains — and it learned fast once opened.
     This *supports* the thesis outside Europe; it also shows the thesis is not about Europeans
     as such.
   - *India, the hard case.* Fragmented for long periods, commercial, the world's best cotton
     industry in 1700 — and no mechanization. Landes's answer is not fragmentation but labour
     cost and the merchant's distance from production (`cui bono`). State plainly that this case
     shows fragmentation is not sufficient; it needs the market and the "culture" of link 5.
   Answer: the thesis is strongest as a claim about *suppression* (what a single authority can
   stop) and weakest as a claim about *initiative* (why people try), where Landes falls back on
   the market and on culture.
7. **"Culture makes all the difference" — and the OBJECTIONS at full strength.** Landes's
   conclusion (ch. 29) quoted with its two qualifications he gives himself: culture is not race
   and is not immutable ("a sulfuric odor of race and inheritance, an air of immutability" — he
   says this is a mistaken fear), and "culture does not stand alone". Then three objections, each
   from its own side, each met by Landes's reply where he gives one:
   - *Selection of cases (methodological).* Each contrast picks a European success against a
     non-European failure; Europe's own suppressions (the Index, Galileo, the 1559 Spanish ban on
     study abroad — Landes's ch. 12 — and Portugal's Inquisition) are moved into a separate story
     about the Catholic south. If a European authority could stop science in Spain, fragmentation
     protected Europe only because other European states did not follow. Landes's reply: that is
     the thesis — the Huguenot or the Galilean had somewhere to go. The objection's residue: the
     thesis explains Europe-wide outcomes better than any single country's.
   - *Timing and inheritance (the rival thesis, stated without reproach and Ref'd forward).* If
     Europe was ahead from 1000 or 1500, its wealth should show it by 1700; historians who compare
     the most advanced regions of China and England around 1750 find them close, and argue that
     the gap opened after 1800 with coal and New World land; and an anthropologist argues that
     calling the cumulative making of techniques a European "invention" claims for one part of
     Eurasia what the whole continent shared (`<Ref pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis>`; no
     names in the body). Landes's reply in advance: aggregate income estimates miss quality and
     new goods (ch. 13 box: the price of light), and the record of who copied whom runs one way
     after 1500 ("unlike China, Europe was a learner … This was no common stream", ch. 21).
   - *The West's violence (moral and causal).* Landes's own "law of imperialism" (ch. 5): marked
     disparity of power, private access to arms and equality of groups cannot coexist; the same
     fragmentation that freed enterprise freed private predation abroad — "This was a bellicose
     society." The objection: Europe's lead was partly taken, not made. Landes's position: the
     law is universal (empires are ancient and everywhere, ch. 25), and the gains from empire were
     smaller than the gains from knowledge (Portugal, ch. 9: trade in commodities small "compared
     to trade in ideas"). Forward `<Ref colonial-balance-sheet>`.
   Where the lesson stands (in the dry register, L9): the fragmentation-and-exit mechanism is the
   best-supported part of the argument and the one Landes's critics largely accept; "culture" is
   the least specified. The next lessons try to measure the outcome (Murray) and to give culture
   a mechanism (Henrich).

Headings as claims (suggested): "Around 1000 nobody would have bet on Europe"; "Geography sets
the odds, not the outcome"; "Division forced rulers to bargain"; "An invention a ruler banned went
to his rival"; "The market and the academies kept Europeans trying"; "Four cases: Zheng He,
printing, Japan, India"; "What 'culture' has to carry".

3. **"Treat them badly, and they might go elsewhere"** (ch. 3, l. 1070) set against the Qianlong

4. **The spectacles sermon of 1306** (ch. 4, l. ~1205): "It is not twenty years since there was

   with him" — with Landes's arithmetic (presbyopia at about forty; twenty further working
   years; skilled working life "more than doubled"). The arithmetic is the argument (playbook
   numbers rule 4); state the assumption it rests on (a craftsman surviving to sixty).
5. **The clock as a public instrument** (ch. 4, l. ~1240–1272): equal hours kept by towns, the
   Church resisting for a century; China's astronomical clocks reserved to the emperor; Busbecq's
   1560 report that the Ottomans feared public clocks would diminish the muezzins' authority.
   One technique, three political orders.
6. **"No one was trying. Why try?"** (ch. 4, l. 1358) with the list it closes — state monopolies
   of salt, iron, tea, alcohol, foreign trade, and education — then Landes's own ranking of the
   religious values and "the market" (l. 1372–1378).
7. **The law of imperialism** (ch. 5, l. 1481) — quoted whole (one sentence) and parsed as three
   conditions; the reader is asked which of the three medieval Europe lacked (a central
   restraint on private access to arms).
8. **"Culture makes all the difference"** (ch. 29, l. 8833–8837) — with Landes's own examples
   (overseas Chinese, Indians in East Africa, Lebanese in West Africa, Jews and Calvinists in
   Europe) and his puzzle: the same people unproductive at home and enterprising abroad — which
   he answers with "bad government" at home. Note for the writer: this puzzle itself is evidence
   *for* the political half of the thesis and against culture standing alone.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **The label rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning
  or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a
  label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Landes's register is polemical** ("goodthink", "Europhobia", "anti-intellectual", critics
  "zealous in their political correctness", ch. 2, l. 956). These are his words about his
  opponents; quote one if the argument needs it, attributed, dated 1998; never adopt the tone.
  The same holds for his sharpest generalisations about Islam, China, the Aztecs, Latin American
  Catholicism: present them as his claims with the evidence he gives.
- **Borrowings — graded, never one flat line.** Paper reached Europe from China through the Arab
  world (secure: mills at Samarkand, Baghdad, Damascus, Játiva in Spain). Gunpowder most likely
  came through contact in the Mongol era — Landes's "probably … early fourteenth, possibly the
  late thirteenth century"; no document records the transfer, and Europeans plausibly did not
  know its origin. Gutenberg's press (1450s) was most probably an independent invention — Landes
  says only that Europe "came to printing centuries after China", not that it took it from
  China. Give the pro-Western reply at full strength, in Landes's terms: everyone borrowed;
  Europe turned what it borrowed into self-sustaining systems (corned powder and cast bronze
  cannon; print run by competing presses in rival states). One sentence each here; the ledger
  itself is `<Ref the-borrowing-ledger>` (forward). The rival thesis is never phrased as a
  reproach to Europe, nor the exceptionalist reply as a reproach to the others.
- **Zheng He numbers are contested.** 317 ships and 28,000 men are the Ming record's figures and
  are widely accepted; the 400-foot length of the treasure ships is the traditional figure and is
  disputed by naval historians (a study arguing that ships of that size were implausible: S. K.
  Church, "Zheng He: An Investigation into the Plausibility of 450-ft Treasure Ships", *Monumenta
  Serica* 53, 2005 — not in the dossier; verify before citing; References only). Say "by the
  traditional figure". Also: the opening's "in 1400 … within a century they were burned" compresses
  the record — the first voyage sailed in 1405, Africa was reached on voyages after 1417, the last
  voyage was 1433; what was burned (or hidden) in 1477 was the logs; in 1525 officials were told to
  destroy ocean-going ships. The body gives the exact sequence once.
- **The Ottoman "printing ban" is debated.** Landes gives no date or document. The usual story (an
  edict of Bayezid II in 1485, renewed 1515) rests on later European reports; Jewish refugees from
  Spain printed in Istanbul from the 1490s, Armenians and Greeks later; the first Ottoman Muslim
  press (İbrahim Müteferrika, licensed 1727) printed a few dozen titles. A recent study asks
  whether a formal ban existed at all (K. A. Schwartz, "Did Ottoman Sultans Ban Print?", *Book
  History* 20, 2017 — not in the dossier; verify; References only). The lesson states the
  argument with this caveat: the *absence* of Muslim printing for 250 years is not disputed; its
  cause (law, religion, scribes' interests, low demand) is.
- **The hydraulic thesis** (Wittfogel's "oriental despotism") is contested among historians of
  China; Landes defends it (l. 956–959). Present it as his position in one sentence; do not let
  link 2 rest on it.
- **"Fragmentation" is not "feudal anarchy".** The thesis is about competing *authorities* and the
  subject's ability to move, not about disorder; Landes's communes are islands of order.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), max three per section:** Ming dynasty (1368–1644) and the move of the
  capital to Beijing (1420s); eunuch (a castrated court servant; the admirals' faction against
  the Confucian officials); Confucian mandarinate (scholar-officials chosen by examination);
  commune (self-governing chartered town); *Stadtluft macht frei* (German, "city air makes [one]
  free"); seigneur (local lord); Domesday (England's land survey of 1086); presbyopia; Ottoman
  Empire (Turkish empire centred on Istanbul from 1453); Tokugawa shogunate (1603–1868) and *han*
  (domains); Busbecq (Habsburg ambassador to Istanbul, letter of 1560); Ibn Khaldun (North African
  historian, 1332–1406) — named only as the source Landes quotes.
- **Do not re-teach** Weber's Protestant ethic (one clause + `<Ref weber-only-in-the-west>`), the
  polis (`<Ref greek-miracle>`), sovereignty (`<Ref bodin-sovereignty>`), Burke on property.
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — the probability "around zero" in 1000 and "close to one" by 1500
  (Landes's figure of speech, quote it as such); 317 ships vs four; spectacles doubling a
  craftsman's working life. Others to words. The 400:1 income gap may open the problem if framed
  ("richest to poorest country, against about 5:1 two and a half centuries earlier").
- **The Greek sentence "Where there are kings, there must be the greatest cowards"** (l. 1119) is
  from the Hippocratic *Airs, Waters, Places*, quoted by Landes; if used, say so in the
  attribution, and note it is a climatic-political argument the course met in a different form
  (the Greek contrast with Persia).
- **Dossier coverage:** full book; Landes's endnotes give his sources (Balazs, Elvin, Needham,
  Cipolla). No published critique of Landes is in the dossier apart from the Goody and Pomeranz
  remarks cited above; the objections in link 7 are built from those, from Landes's own
  concessions, and from his own cases. Do not invent named critics.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (the only warm-up):** place in order and date to the decade:
   Domesday survey; the 1306 spectacles sermon; Zheng He's first voyage; the Ming capital moved to
   Beijing; Gama at Calicut; the Lincei founded in Rome; and, from earlier lessons, Bodin's *Six
   Books* (`<Ref bodin-sovereignty>`). Then say which of these Landes uses as evidence of
   fragmentation and which as evidence of its absence.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** the 1477 report on the voyage logs ("deceitful
   exaggerations of bizarre things far removed from the testimony of people's eyes and ears",
   with "betel, bamboo staves, grape-wine, pomegranates and ostrich eggs"). Metadata first (a
   Ming official of the Ministry of War, 1477, answering a eunuch's request to revive the
   voyages; reported in a later Chinese source, quoted by Landes). Records / argues / omits; what
   it can be used to establish (a faction's grounds for ending the voyages) and what it cannot
   (that China lacked curiosity, Landes's inference).
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** Landes's thesis needs rival states *and* a common culture through
   which techniques and people move (ch. 14's "unity in disunity"). Take the degenerate case of
   many rival states with no common language of learning and no movement between them (the
   Warring States of China before 221 BC, or the Greek poleis at war — `<Ref greek-miracle>`).
   Does the thesis predict cumulative invention? Solution: no — exit and competition protect an
   invention only if it can travel; the second condition is what Latin, the Church and the
   correspondence networks supplied, and it is the condition the next lessons try to explain.
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Landes argues that Europeans were more inventive by nature." Name
   the steps of his argument that contradict it (fragmentation and exit; the market; culture
   explicitly "not immutable" and not race; China's inventors stopped, not lacking; Japan as a
   miniature Europe that learned fast) and state what he does claim.
5. **Derivation the lesson did not do (E6c):** from the law of imperialism (three conditions that
   cannot coexist), derive what Landes must predict about (a) a medieval Europe with a single
   emperor able to disarm private lords and (b) Ming China facing the Portuguese in the Indian
   Ocean. Then state the tension: the same condition (no central restraint) that the
   fragmentation thesis praises is the one the law of imperialism blames.
6. **Synthesis — causal ranking (playbook-history):** rank Landes's causes for the end of the
   Ming voyages — no commercial target, fiscal strain, court faction, the northern frontier, a
   single authority — as necessary / enabling / precipitating, and defend the ranking. Which
   ranking would make the case evidence *for* the fragmentation thesis, and which would make it
   neutral?
7. **Counter-argument (E3, spaced):** "Bodin and Hobbes were wrong: Europe needed more division,
   not a sovereign." Name two facts the claim fails to account for (the religious wars that the
   sovereign was invented to end; Landes's argument is about division *between* states, which
   each sovereign state's internal order made safe for property — `<Ref bodin-sovereignty>`,
   `<Ref hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant>`) and state what would have to be true for it to
   survive.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `murray-human-accomplishment`:** if Landes is right that exit and competing
   authorities protected innovators, where in Europe, and in which centuries, should the people
   remembered for great work in science and the arts cluster — and where should they be scarce?
   Write two predictions (by region and by political regime). The solution says in one sentence
   that the next lesson's count of significant figures tests both — four-fifths of Europe's come
   from a compact core of the north-west and centre, and the despotic peripheries are thin — with
   a `<Ref murray-human-accomplishment>`, and notes that the count also finds the regime effect
   hard to separate from country, which is the limit of the test.

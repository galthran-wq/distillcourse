# Research brief: lasch-revolt-of-the-elites (Lasch's Revolt of the Elites: meritocracy and the betrayal of democracy)

status: planned
module: equality-after-rawls
type: core
goal: Analyze Lasch's argument that the danger to democracy now comes from mobile meritocratic elites rather than from the masses, and evaluate it against Ortega's original thesis

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Ortega's mass-man — a type of any class who "feels himself just like everybody", demands rights without obligations, shows "radical ingratitude" to what made his ease possible, and is closed to any appeal above himself; nobility as obligation (noblesse oblige); and Ortega's own admission that the specialist scientist is the prototype of the mass-man (`ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`, concepts `mass-man`, `noblesse-oblige`). The elite theorists: every society is ruled by an organised minority, and the circulation of elites renews it (`mosca-ruling-class`, `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`, concepts `ruling-class`, `circulation-of-elites`); Burnham's managers as the new ruling class (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`). Earlier ideals of rule by the able: Burke's natural aristocracy (`burke-reflections`), Carlyle's aristocracy of talent (`carlyle-heroes`). Tocqueville's township and art of association (`tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`); Nisbet's intermediate associations (`kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`). From this module: Rawls's natural lottery — no one deserves his talents (`rawls-theory-of-justice`); envy and self-respect (`ressentiment-and-envy`); Scruton's allegiance and the stratified society of many dignified walks of life (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`); MacIntyre's manager whose expertise is a moral fiction and his verdict that every modern political tradition is exhausted (`macintyre-after-virtue`).
- **Contributes:** One move: Ortega defined the mass-man by an attitude, not by a class — and that is exactly what lets Lasch relocate him. The traits Ortega gave the masses (no sense of obligation, no gratitude to the past, belief in limitless possibility, contempt for what is not itself) now fit the mobile, credentialed elites who run money, information and the universities and who have withdrawn from the schools, neighbourhoods and services they used to share. Meritocracy produces them: it is "a parody of democracy", an aristocracy of talent without noblesse oblige, and it replaces the older democratic ideal — the wide diffusion of property and competence — with the narrow ideal of social mobility. Concepts `revolt-of-the-elites`, `meritocracy-critique`.
- **Hands off:** The test. Lasch's evidence is a handful of statistics from the late 1980s and much observation; whether a society is in fact sorting its able into a separate class is an empirical question, and a book published the same year claimed to answer it with a survey of twelve thousand young Americans — `the-cognitive-elite`, the module's application, reads it against Lasch and Rawls. Forward also to `deneen-post-liberalism` (liberalocracy — the credentialed aristocracy), which treats Lasch as a backward Ref.
- **Spine step:** "Rawls makes equality the measure of justice; the right answers with the charge of ressentiment and envy, Scruton's authority, MacIntyre's lost tradition and Lasch's revolt of the elites — and the data on cognitive stratification test both sides"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): Ortega in 1930 — Europe's crisis is the masses' revolt against the minorities who should lead them; an American historian of the left in 1994 turns it around — the people with no loyalty to place, no obligation, no gratitude are now the educated elites, who have stopped sharing schools, neighbourhoods and services; the lesson follows the inversion and asks what evidence would decide between the two.

1. **Why Ortega's thesis could be turned around at all.** Problem: a diagnosis aimed at "the masses" seems to be about the lower classes; how could the same diagnosis fit their opposite? Recall Ortega in a clause each (`ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`, `<Ref>`): the mass-man is defined by an attitude — he feels "just like everybody" and is content, demands rights without obligations, takes civilisation for nature, and is closed to correction. Ortega said this type can be found in any class, and named the specialist scientist — the "learned ignoramus", master of a tiny corner and ignorant of the rest — as its prototype. Answer: because the type is psychological, its location is an empirical question, and Lasch claims the location has moved. Anchor Lasch in two sentences: an American historian (1932–1994) of the left, author of books against the therapeutic culture of the 1970s; this last book, published after his death in 1994, is the one the right has read most. Then MacIntyre's manager as the bridge (`macintyre-after-virtue`, one clause): a class whose authority rests on credentials.
2. **Who the new elites are.** Problem: "elite" is a vague word; whom exactly does Lasch mean? Attempt: the rich. Lasch's answer: not old money — which, rooted for generations in one city, funded its libraries and hospitals and held, with Horace Mann in 1846, that "all are bound, as by an oath, to transmit those benefits … to posterity" — but the professional and managerial class that produces and manipulates information: finance, the media, the universities, the foundations. They are defined by a way of life, not by an ideology: mobile (success "has never been so closely associated with mobility"), international in market and loyalty, married to one another (two $60,000 lawyers make $120,000; two $20,000 clerks make $40,000 — the frame: assortative mating doubles the gap between households without any change in pay), and at home "only in transit". A description from the period (Reich's "symbolic analysts", the top fifth of the workforce, against routine production workers and in-person servers, the other 80 per cent) supplies the category; state it as the course's own with the name in References. Bush and the scanner (l. 222) is the scene: one sentence, as Lasch's own emblem.
3. **Which of Ortega's traits fit them.** The comparison, trait by trait (figure 1): rights without obligations → obligation "depersonalized", exercised through the state, its cost falling on the lower-middle and working classes; radical ingratitude → no sense of debt to the past or to the places that formed them; limitless possibility → the cult of fitness and permanent youth, against the "highly developed sense of limits" Lasch finds among ordinary people; hatred of what is not itself → contempt for "Middle America" as backward, reactionary, dowdy. And the withdrawal: private schools, private police, private refuse collection, gated communities — public services abandoned to those who cannot leave; "It is a question whether they think of themselves as Americans at all". Concept `revolt-of-the-elites`. Bearing on the lesson's question: Ortega's minorities were defined by the demands they made on themselves; Lasch says the new minority makes demands only on others.
4. **Why meritocracy produces this.** Problem: the new elites were selected by merit — by examinations and degrees open to anyone — so why should they behave worse than an aristocracy of birth? Attempt (the obvious one, and the one the elites hold): selection by merit makes the elite legitimate and open; a poor boy can rise. Lasch's answer, in three steps. (a) "Meritocracy is a parody of democracy": opportunities to rise "are no substitute for a general diffusion of the means of civilization" — a society can be highly mobile and deeply stratified, because mobility only changes who fills the top places. (b) Mobility legitimates the hierarchy: an elite that believes it earned its place owes nothing to those below; "the circulation of elites strengthens the principle of hierarchy" — the reader has Pareto's circulation of elites (`michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`, `<Ref>`): the same mechanism, now read as a defect. (c) An aristocracy of talent is "a contradiction in terms": it keeps aristocratic snobbery and drops noblesse oblige (`ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`; and Carlyle's aristocracy of talent and Burke's natural aristocracy, `<Ref>` one clause each — both assumed the able would govern for the whole; Lasch says the able selected by examination have no such bond). The 1958 satire supplies the sharpest line: "For the first time in human history the inferior man has no ready buttress for his self-regard" — those left behind cannot blame birth or luck. Connect to `ressentiment-and-envy` in one sentence: a society that tells the unsuccessful their place is deserved removes the excuse that made low status bearable — the condition the envy theorists and Rawls both said breeds resentment. Concept `meritocracy-critique`.
5. **The older ideal: the democratization of competence.** Problem: if not mobility, what should equality mean? Lasch's history (ch. 3): "social mobility" entered American vocabulary late — around the closing of the frontier (1890) and fully only in the 1940s, when a permanent class of wage-earners could no longer be denied. The older ideal was Lincoln's (1859): a society in which "a large majority are neither hirers nor hired" — men who own their tools and land. Hence: democracy requires the wide distribution of property and of competence — the word meaning both a holding and the skill to manage it; self-governing communities, not individuals, are its units (`tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`, `<Ref>`); and money must be limited, not only fenced off from other spheres — "When money talks, everybody else is condemned to listen". The central choice, from the land-grant colleges of 1862: raise the general level of competence and virtue, or merely recruit the elite more widely; America chose the second. One paragraph from ch. 9 if space allows: democracy needs argument, not information; the public is ill-informed because it is excluded from debate.
6. **A man of the left whom the right has read.** Problem: the reader may already have filed the book as conservative. State both readings as the text supports them. The right's: elites who despise patriotism, family, religion and limits; the lower middle class as the guardian of common sense; respect earned by meeting common standards ("Compassion has become the human face of contempt"); the therapeutic state. The left's: the critique is of capital and the market as much as of culture — "a moral condemnation of great wealth must inform any defense of the free market", wealth itself must be limited, the global market and the mobility of capital dissolve local loyalties; populism means small property, not big business. Lasch's own position (ch. 5): populism against both the market and the welfare bureaucracy — a "third way" that neither party represents. Both readings are partial; the course says so and passes no verdict.
7. **The thesis judged.** (a) **The elite as a moving target**: who exactly is in it — the top fifth by income, the holders of graduate degrees, the "information" professions, the cultural left? Lasch's categories shift between chapters, and his evidence is sparse (late-1980s statistics, observation, quotation): a thesis about a class needs a definition of the class that a count could check. (b) **Ortega's reply** (stated as the course's own on Ortega's behalf): the mass-man's defining act was direct action outside the law; Lasch's elites act through law, markets and courts — so the inversion keeps Ortega's psychology and drops his politics. Lasch's partial answer: Ortega himself located the type among specialists. (c) **Burnham's frame** (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`): the managers were a ruling class by control of production; Lasch's class rules by credentials and culture — is it a ruling class at all, or only a privileged one? (d) **The liberal-egalitarian convergence**: Rawls also rejects the meritocratic society — "Equality of opportunity means an equal chance to leave the less fortunate behind" (§17, `rawls-theory-of-justice`) — but on the ground that talent is undeserved, and his remedy is the difference principle; Lasch's ground is that a democracy needs competent, property-holding citizens, and his remedy is diffusion, not transfer. He answers Rawls that "People in the Rawlsian republic do not love other men and women: they love humankind instead." (e) **The libertarian reply**: mobility is freedom, and leaving a failing public school or a dangerous street is self-protection, not betrayal; Lasch's reply is that the exit of those with the means removes the voices that would have repaired the service. (f) **What evidence would decide between Ortega and Lasch** — the question the opening sets. Derive the observable consequences of each: if Lasch is right, the able are increasingly sorted by education into a few institutions, marry one another, live apart, and withdraw from shared services, while civic attachment and a sense of limits are higher among the less educated; if Ortega is right, it is the many who reject standards and obligations. Most of these can be counted. Hand off: the same year, a book claimed to have counted the first four.

Headings as claims (suggested): "A type defined by attitude can change class"; "The new elites: mobile, credentialed, married to each other"; "Ortega's traits, relocated"; "Meritocracy as a parody of democracy"; "Before mobility: property and competence for all"; "A man of the left whom the right has read"; "What would decide between Ortega and Lasch".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Lasch is a man of the left whom the right has read** (course.yaml note): present both readings from the text (link 6). Do not let the lesson become a culture-war catalogue; the book's chapters on race politics in New York (ch. 7), shame (ch. 11) and religion (chs. 12–13) are not needed and invite exactly that.
- **"Populism" in Lasch's sense** — the nineteenth-century American tradition of small proprietors and producers (the People's Party of the 1890s is its namesake) — is not the 2010s sense. Define it at link 6 in his sense and say the word has since moved.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** "meritocracy" (rule or selection by measured ability and achievement; the word coined in a British satire of 1958 that imagined a society sorted by intelligence tests); "symbolic analysts" (one clause); "assortative mating" (marrying someone like oneself in income or education — define at link 2; the next lesson uses it); "mud-sill" (Lincoln's term for the theory that every society needs a permanent laboring class at its base — the phrase was a Southern senator's of 1858); "Homestead Act" and "Morrill Act" (1862: free western land for settlers; federal land to found agricultural and mechanical colleges); "the closing of the frontier" (the 1890 census's announcement that there was no longer a continuous line of unsettled land); "noblesse oblige" (recall from `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`); "third place" (only if ch. 6 is used).
- **Real misreading to prevent:** that Lasch opposes excellence or standards. The opposite: he attacks the elites for abandoning common standards and demands that respect be earned by meeting them (ch. 4: "Double standards mean second-class citizenship"). His target is the restriction of excellence to a selected class, not excellence.
- **Do not re-teach** Ortega (a `<Ref>` and the clauses the comparison needs), Pareto's circulation of elites, Burnham's managers, Rawls's natural lottery and §17, MacIntyre's manager, Tocqueville's townships.
- **Numbers:** two keeper numbers — the 80 per cent outside the "symbolic" class (frame: the categories Lasch says are losing wealth and status) and the $120,000 / $40,000 household arithmetic. Lasch's other statistics (wage declines 1973–87, turnout) are late-1980s and optional; if used, bind each in its sentence. Dates to the decade except 1859 (Lincoln's speech), 1862 (the Acts) and 1958 (the satire), where the sequence is the argument.
- **Where the dossier is thin:** Lasch's sources (Young, Reich, Kaus, Tawney) are known only through his quotations — say so in References and do not characterise those books beyond what he quotes. The book has no footnote bodies in the extract; Lasch's bibliographic essay (ll. ~1680–2020) annotates his sources and can be used to check a citation.
- **Lasch is not a data book.** The lesson must not imply that Lasch measured anything; the measuring is the next lesson's (the hand-off).
- **L8a:** see the names list above.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (warm-up):** Put in order, with a decade each: Ortega's Revolt of the Masses; Burnham's Managerial Revolution; the 1958 satire that coined "meritocracy"; Rawls's Theory of Justice; MacIntyre's After Virtue; Lasch's Revolt of the Elites. For each, one clause: who rules, or who should, on that author's account.
2. **Claim to refute:** "Lasch's revolt of the elites is the conservative case against the left's cultural elite." Name the parts of the book the claim cannot account for (limit wealth itself; the market and mobile capital as solvents; populism as small property against big business; the rejection of the welfare bureaucracy AND of the free-market right), and say what part of the claim is true (his attack on the therapeutic culture and on elite contempt for "Middle America").
3. **Boundary probe:** Imagine perfect meritocracy — every position filled by the most able, mobility at its theoretical maximum, no inherited advantage. Would Lasch's objection vanish? Show from his own premises that it would not (mobility is consistent with entrenched hierarchy; selection legitimates the elite and removes the buttress of the unsuccessful's self-regard; the diffusion of competence is untouched), and name the one point at which the objection WOULD weaken (if obligation to the community were itself a criterion of selection).
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** Rawls (§17) and Lasch both reject the "meritocratic society". Derive each rejection from its premises (Rawls: talents are undeserved, so inequalities they produce must benefit the least advantaged; Lasch: democracy needs widely distributed property and competence, and an elite without obligation) and state one policy on which they would agree and one on which they would split (e.g. a large cash transfer financed by the able: Rawls for, Lasch doubtful — transfer is not diffusion of competence).

# Research brief: locke-and-mill-on-empire (Locke's waste land and Mill's tutelage: the liberal justification of empire)

status: planned
module: empire-and-guilt
type: core
goal: Analyze how Locke's theory of property and Mill's theory of progress each justified rule over peoples who had not consented, and evaluate the thesis that the exclusion follows from liberal premises rather than contradicting them

  against the "contradiction" thesis without deciding for the reader.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim. Develop it: two sentences by two
men whose names stand for consent and liberty; the reader's natural first
answer is that each made an exception to his principle when his country
profited (both men were, as a matter of fact, employed by the enterprises
concerned: Locke by the proprietors of Carolina and the Board of Trade, Mill
for thirty-five years at the East India Company — state as fact, not as
gotcha). The lesson takes that answer seriously at the end. First it reads the
passages, because the question "lapse or consequence" cannot be settled
without knowing what the theories say.

1. **What did Locke mean by "waste"?** Question: how can a theory that begins
   "the earth was given to men in common" end with America belonging to
   whoever fences it? Attempt: Locke simply assumed Europeans could take what
   they found. Fails: Sect. 30 gives the Indian ownership of the deer he kills —
   the labour rule applies to him. What Locke denies is ownership of the
   forest. Recall the labour theory (`<Ref>` locke-property-and-consent, one
   clause) and add the two "facts" the empire passages rest on: nature is
   nearly worthless (Sect. 43: nine-tenths, "in most things ninety-nine
   hundredths", of value is labour) and unimproved land yields almost nothing
   (Sect. 37: a thousand acres of America against ten in Devonshire; Sect. 41:
   "a king of a large and fruitful territory there feeds, lodges, and is clad
   worse than a day-labourer in England"). Then Sect. 45: where a people has
   not "joined with the rest of mankind in the consent of the use of their
   common money", their lands "lie waste" and are "still in common" — that is,
   open to appropriation by anyone's labour without anyone's consent (Sects
   28–29). Sect. 49: "in the beginning all the world was America". Because:
   property is derived from labour that adds value, not from occupation,
   hunting or jurisdiction — so a hunting people has property in its game and
   none in its ground. Compare Vitoria (`<Ref>`): discovery gave nothing
   because the land had owners; Locke's criterion of ownership makes the same
   land unowned. Figure 2.

2. **Locke's own counter-text.** Question: does Locke then license conquest?
   No — ch. XVI: conquest is no origin of government; an unjust conqueror has
   the title of a robber with a dagger at your throat; a just conqueror gains
   despotic power only over those who fought him, nothing over their children
   and nothing over their land ("damages seldom amount to the value of land").
   The reader must hold both: Locke forbids taking a people's country by war
   and permits taking "waste" by labour. Which America is the real one depends
   on whether the Indians had "political society in his sense" — a settled
   law, a judge, a power to execute. The course's own thesis (Mehta ch. 4 in
   References): because Locke makes legitimacy and identity the same act — the
   unanimous contract — a people without that contract has no territory; the
   four implications (no collective identity before contract, no identity from
   place, no account of nations, no account of boundaries: why the Channel and
   not the Rhine?). This is where the inherited "waste" argument becomes an
   imperial one — not in Locke's America passages, which are about property,
   but in the step from property to territory.

3. **The gate: a universal capacity and the conditions of its exercise.**
   Question: if "all men are by nature free, equal and independent" (Sect. 4,
   recall), who is in? Attempt: everyone. Fails: Sects 57–60 — children,
   lunatics and idiots are not under the law of reason and cannot consent; a
   man is free at twenty-one when he "understands the laws of England". And how
   does a child come to reason? Locke's own answer in Some Thoughts Concerning
   Education (1693): by habit, awe, a tutor, "good company" — breeding "not to
   be learnt, nor taught by books"; a prince, a nobleman and a gentleman's son
   are to be bred differently. So reason, the universal capacity, has thick
   social conditions of exercise. Define the course's own term here —
   **strategies of exclusion**: the space between the capacities every human
   has and the conditions under which they count politically is where a
   theorist can raise the price of admission without ever denying the universal
   premise. The contrast that makes it visible: a conservative excludes openly
   by birth and inheritance (Burke, next lesson), a patriarchalist by literal
   descent (Filmer); a liberal excludes by a condition that is in principle
   satisfiable later — which is why the exclusion can be indefinite and still
   consistent. Figure 1. Because: the exclusion is not written into the
   anthropology; it is written into the pedagogy.

4. **The nineteenth century turns the gate into a scale.** Anchor the Company
   in one sentence (a trading company chartered in 1600 that became, after
   Plassey in 1757 and the grant of Bengal's revenues in 1765, the government
   of most of India until the Crown took over in 1858). James Mill, who never
   went to India, wrote its History (1817), became the Company's Examiner (its
   chief correspondence officer), and his History was the mandatory text for
   its officials: the "scale of civilization" is "an object of the highest
   practical importance" to a governor; the Hindus stand at its "earliest
   steps", compared with "a band of roving Tartars", Druids with Brahmins;
   India's past is "inscrutable" — the limit put in the object, not the
   inquirer. Then the two strategies named in course.yaml's concepts:
   **inscrutability** (a people that cannot be understood cannot meet the
   conditions) and **civilizational infantilism** (a people as a child, Mill's
   "nonage"). Macaulay's Minute (1835): educate "a class of persons, Indian in
   blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in
   intellect" — and Bentinck to James Mill on leaving for India: "I shall not
   be Governor General. It is you that will be Governor General." Because: once
   the condition of inclusion is a collective stage rather than an individual
   age, a whole people can be a child, and the tutor is a state.

5. **Mill's own reasons at FULL STRENGTH.** This section is the lesson's centre
   and precedes any critique. The problem Mill is solving in 1861: Britain in
   fact rules India (the Company abolished in 1858 after the rebellion of 1857);
   how is that rule to be a good and not an evil to the governed? (a) Backward
   peoples: there are conditions "in which a vigorous despotism is in itself the
   best mode of government for training the people"; a native despotism yields
   a good despot only as "a rare and transitory accident"; a more civilized
   ruling people "ought to be able to supply it constantly" — to do "all that
   could be done by a succession of absolute monarchs… qualified by their genius
   to anticipate all that experience has taught to the more advanced nation";
   this is "the highest moral trust which can devolve upon a nation", and
   rulers who do not even aim at it "are selfish usurpers, on a par in
   criminality" with any conqueror. (b) Why the obvious remedy fails: the
   "superficial" answer is a minister responsible to Parliament; but "to govern
   one country under responsibility to the people of another… is despotism";
   "the only choice the case admits is a choice of despotisms", and "the
   despotism of twenty millions" who "neither hear, nor see, nor know any thing
   about their subjects" is likely worse than that of those who do; "such a
   thing as government of one people by another does not and can not exist" —
   one people may keep another as "a human-cattle farm", but it cannot attend
   to their good. The dangers he names are British: settlers, who want the
   natives' land and labour and have friends in Parliament; missionaries who
   want the Bible in state schools; native oppressors with advocates — "the
   silent myriads obtain none". (c) The remedy: a permanent delegated body, not
   a party minister; agents trained from youth and chosen by competition; "a
   choice of imperfections". The Company "suggested the true theory of the
   government of a semi-barbarous dependency by a civilized country, and after
   having done this, to perish". Then the clause from On Liberty read as it
   stands: the sentence "the sole end… is self-protection" is followed at once
   by "any member of a civilized community", and the next paragraph says what
   "hardly needs saying" — the doctrine is for "human beings in the maturity of
   their faculties", not for "backward states of society in which the race
   itself may be considered as in its nonage", where "Despotism is a legitimate
   mode of government… provided the end be their improvement" and until then
   "there is nothing for them but implicit obedience to an Akbar or a
   Charlemagne". Because: for Mill, liberty is a secondary principle valid at a
   stage; utility is the ground; representative institutions are for the ripe.
   Define **tutelage** here as Mill's own concept, not as an accusation.

6. **Lapse or consequence? Two theses, stated so the reader can test them.**
   The **contradiction thesis**: empire contradicts consent,
   representative government and equal citizenship — witness the hardening
   after 1857, and the Ilbert Bill of 1883 (a measure to let Indian judges try
   Europeans, defeated by a settler outcry), when Stephen wrote to The Times
   that the Raj rests "not on consent, but on conquest" and that its rulers
   "shrink from the open… assertion" of superiority; and Stephen's gunpowder
   (recalled with `<Ref>` stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity, one clause — the
   reader has met the man and his argument that coercion is the normal
   instrument of rule; quote only the one sentence, from the 1883 article): "A
   barrel of gunpowder may be harmless or may explode, but you cannot educate
   it into household fuel by exploding little bits of it" — a liberal saying
   the pedagogy is a pretence. The **strategies thesis**, the course's
   own: Mill's liberty is secondary to utility and keyed to a stage, so
   excluding India is not an inconsistency; the thick conditions that excluded
   India are the same ones that excluded the propertyless and women at home and
   later included them; the exclusion is deferred, never denied — which is why
   it can last. And a third, the **discontinuity thesis** (name it in a clause):
   that liberal imperialism is a nineteenth-century turn, not a Lockean seed —
   Hobbes and Locke do not ground individualism in history, the Mills do. The
   seam states the expectation (universal premises include) and the record
   (they deferred). Mark where the lesson concedes: the strategies reading
   cannot say what Stephen can — that the deferral was known to be
   indefinite; and the contradiction reading cannot say why the unfamiliar was
   always read as incomplete rather than as different.

7. **The ethical alternative, one paragraph.** Gandhi, Hind Swaraj (1909):
   civilization is "that mode of conduct which points to man the path of duty"
   — available at any stage of history, so the developmental argument has no
   purchase; and the fact that answers Mill's test from outside the theory:
   independent India gave the vote in 1950 to an electorate most of whom could
   not read. State as the counterpoint; do not adjudicate.

8. **The objection inside Mill, carried to the end.** OL IV on the Mormons: "I
   am not aware that any community has a right to force another to be
   civilised" — set beside OL I's barbarians. Reply: the Mormons are dissenters
   within a civilized community; "the whole East" has, for Mill, "properly
   speaking, no history" because custom rules it (OL III). So the clause does
   the sorting, and the question of the lesson is whether a sorting of that
   kind is a lapse in a liberal theory or the place where a liberal theory
   decides who it is for. Leave the reader with the two passages and the two
   theses.

Headings fall out as: "What Locke meant by waste"; "Conquest gives no title,
labour does"; "A universal capacity and the price of admission"; "The scale of
civilization: India as a child"; "Mill's case for a good despotism"; "Why
responsibility to Parliament is the worst despotism"; "Lapse or consequence?";

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), at first mention and no more than three per
  section:** East India Company (as above); Examiner (the Company's chief
  correspondence officer — both Mills held the post); Governor-General;
  Plassey (1757, Clive's victory that made the Company the power in Bengal);
  the 1765 grant of Bengal's revenues (the next lesson calls it the dewanny —
  do not introduce the word here); the rebellion of 1857 and the Crown's
  takeover of 1858; the Durham Report (1839, on responsible government for
  Canada — Mill's model for colonies "of European race"); the Ilbert Bill
  (1883); Haileybury (the Company's college, 1806–58); Anglicists and
  Orientalists (the 1830s dispute over whether to fund English or Sanskrit and
  Arabic learning that Macaulay's Minute decided); "nonage" (minority, the age
  before legal adulthood); Akbar (Mughal emperor, r. 1556–1605); Charlemagne.
- **"Race" in Mill means "the race itself in its nonage" — a people at a
  stage, not a biological category.** Mehta notes race is conspicuously absent
  from the liberal writings on India (index 42 key_results ch. 1 pp. 15–16).
  Do not import it; Macaulay's "blood and colour" is the one place it surfaces,
  and there as something education is to work behind.
- **Mehta's name appears only in References**, as do Macpherson, Pateman, Dunn,
  Hartz, Said, Pitts, Tully, Armitage. "Strategies of exclusion" and
  "civilizational infantilism" are stated as the course's terms (they are the

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (E3, the one warm-up).** State in one line each how Vitoria's
   discovery title and Locke's "waste" argument decide whether the American
   land had owners, and name the premise that differs (ownership by reason and
   jurisdiction vs ownership by value-adding labour).
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill on Macaulay's Minute (1835).**
   Metadata: a member of the Governor-General's council, writing to decide how
   a fixed education grant is spent, against the Orientalist members; predict
   what it will claim and omit; read the "blood and colour" paragraph; records
   / argues / omits (the Indians who were to be educated are not asked); what
   it can be USED to establish — the content of the tutelage programme, not its
   reception.
3. **Boundary probe.** A people in Locke's state of nature that uses money and
   encloses fields but has no "settled standing rules" and no common judge
   (Sects 124–126). Does Sect. 45 still make its land waste? Does ch. XVI still
   forbid taking it by conquest? Solution: the "waste" argument lapses (Sect.
   45's condition is money, not government), the territorial step (no
   political society → no territory) is the only imperial argument left, and it
   is the one Locke never explicitly makes.
4. **Claim to refute.** "Mill wanted India governed from Westminster like any
   other part of the kingdom." Name the step that fails (RG XVIII l.285–307,
   "a choice of despotisms") and what Mill proposed instead.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped.** Mill's own test: a despotism over
   barbarians is legitimate only if "the means [are] justified by actually
   effecting" their improvement, and rulers who do not aim at it are "selfish
   usurpers". Apply the test to Mill's own evidence of 1857 (OL II fn 10: the
   demand to teach the Bible in state schools; RG XVIII on settlers). State the
   corollary Mill does not draw and the reply he would give (the remedy is a
   better delegated body, not withdrawal).
6. **Synthesis — causal ranking.** Which did more to make the exclusion
   possible: the universal anthropology (which made inclusion a promise), the
   pedagogy of conditions (which made the promise deferrable), or the
   nineteenth-century scale of civilization (which made a people a child)?
   Rank as necessary / enabling / precipitating and defend with one passage
   each.
7. **Counter-argument stem.** "Mill's clause is a lapse: nothing in the harm
   principle requires it." Name two facts the claim must account for (Mill's
   statement that liberty is a secondary principle grounded in utility; the OL
   III passage on custom and "the whole East") and what would have to be true
   for the claim to survive (that Mill's utility could not distinguish stages of
   society — which he denies).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `burke-against-hastings`.** Mill calls rule over India "the
   highest moral trust". A Whig member of Parliament had used the same word in
   1788 while prosecuting the man who had governed Bengal, and his first charge
   was that the governor claimed "the constitution of Asia is despotism".
   Predict, from what you know of Burke's Reflections, whether he would accept
   Mill's "vigorous despotism… for training the people" as the content of the
   trust, and why not. The solution points to empire-as-trust and geographical
   morality with a `<Ref>` to the next lesson.

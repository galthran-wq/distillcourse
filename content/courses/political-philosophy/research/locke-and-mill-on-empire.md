## VERDICT

Binding: research/verdicts/REGISTER.md, module 14, `locke-and-mill-on-empire`.

- **Claim judged.** Locke's "waste" land (Second Treatise ch. V) and Mill's
  tutelage of "backward states of society" (On Liberty ch. I; Representative
  Government ch. XVIII): lapses from their principles, or consequences of
  them?
- **Verdict.** **Consequences.** Grade: **strong**.
- **What decides it.** Both exclusions follow from conditions written into the
  principles themselves. Locke's: property is created by value-adding labour
  (Sects 37, 40–43), and land not improved and not brought into "the consent
  of the use of their common money" lies "waste" and "in common" (Sect. 45) —
  the labour criterion applied to land, with no extra premise. Mill's: the
  exclusion stands in On Liberty itself, in the paragraph after the harm
  principle — the doctrine is for "human beings in the maturity of their
  faculties", not for "backward states of society", where "Despotism is a
  legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end
  be their improvement" — and liberty is a secondary principle grounded in
  utility and keyed to a stage. A lapse would be an exception the principle
  forbids; these are applications the principle states.
- **Strongest objection.** The contradiction thesis: empire contradicts
  consent, representative government and equal citizenship, and Locke's own
  ch. XVI forbids conquest; the exclusions are a betrayal, visible in the
  liberal who said so plainly (Stephen's gunpowder, 1883) and in Mill's own
  Mormon passage ("no community has a right to force another to be
  civilised").
- **Why it does not overturn the verdict.** Ch. XVI forbids taking a people's
  country by war; it does not touch the labour criterion that makes unimproved
  land unowned. The Mormon passage concerns dissenters inside a "civilized
  community"; the clause that sorts communities is Mill's, in the same book.
  Stephen shows that the deferral was indefinite in practice, not that it was
  inconsistent with the theory. Where a principle names its own exclusion, the
  exclusion is part of the principle.
- **What would overturn it.** A reading on which Locke's labour criterion does
  not apply to land without an added premise, or on which Mill's stage clause
  can be removed from On Liberty without changing what the harm principle
  covers.

**Second verdict (author's review, round 2; the register entry is extended to
carry it).**

- **Objection (author's review) — required, stated at FULL STRENGTH at step
  8a.** Slavery as a "positive good", in the author's words in substance: a
  master cannot abuse his slave because it is against his interest, as one
  would not break one's own car or abuse one's own horse; the abuse of slaves
  is propaganda; being a slave to a superior culture benefits the slave. Its
  best authors: Calhoun in the Senate, 6 February 1837 ("I hold it to be a
  good … Never before has the black race of Central Africa … attained a
  condition so civilized and so improved, not only physically, but morally and
  intellectually"; "a good — a positive good"; the old and sick slave "under
  the kind superintending care of his master and mistress" against "the
  pauper in the poorhouse"); Fitzhugh, *Sociology for the South* (1854) ch. V
  ("He is but a grown up child, and must be governed as a child"; slavery
  "relieves him from a far more cruel slavery in Africa … christianizes,
  protects, supports and civilizes him") and *Cannibals All!* (1857) ("The
  negro slaves of the South are the happiest, and, in some sense, the freest
  people in the world"; the free labourer "is more of a slave than the
  negro"; the slave's security lies "in the selfish interest of the master and
  in his domestic affection"; "The law of self-interest secures kind and
  humane treatment to Southern slaves").
- **Claim judged.** The three empirical premises of the defence: (i) the
  owner's interest protects the slave; (ii) the abuse is propaganda; (iii) the
  slave is benefited by the rule of a superior culture.
- **Verdict.** **Fails** against the record. Grade: **strong**. Its one true
  part is stated as such: where a replacement cost more than the loss, owners
  kept their slaves alive and the population grew — the United States after
  1808.
- **What decides it.** (i) The interest argument protects the asset, not the
  person, and only where replacement is dear. Fitzhugh concedes the condition
  himself: in Cuba "the most efficient cause of cruelty and neglect, is the
  African slave trade, which makes it cheaper to buy than to rear slaves"
  (*Cannibals All!* ch. VIII). The record follows the condition exactly: in
  the British Caribbean few colonies ever reached positive natural increase,
  numbers were held up by imports, and when the trade ended in 1807 the slave
  population fell from 775,000 to 665,000 by 1834 (Jamaica from 347,000 in
  1810 to 311,070 in 1834); fewer than 800,000 slaves lived in the British
  West Indies in 1810 against some 1.7 million Africans imported; in the
  United States, where import was banned from 1808, the black population of
  1807 was already over three times the number of Africans imported (Higman
  1986 and Engerman's comment, NBER). And the interest forbids only what
  destroys value: whipping to extract labour and the sale of a spouse or child
  realise value, so the argument cannot forbid them. (ii) "Propaganda" fails
  on the defence's own best modern evidence: Fogel and Engerman, who argued
  that slaves were materially well treated, counted on Bennet Barrow's
  Louisiana plantation "an average of 0.7 whippings per year" per hand; their
  critics, rereading the diary, counted 175 whippings, not 160, and 1.19 per
  hand, a whipping witnessed by the men about every six days (Gutman & Sutch
  in David et al., *Reckoning with Slavery*, 1976, p. 59; both via Vedder
  2024 `[secondary]`). Low or high, the count is of routine labour discipline,
  not of excess. The Middle Passage: of 12.5 million Africans shipped, 10.7
  million landed (Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database, via Gates `[secondary]`)
  — about one in seven died at sea in ships whose owners' interest was to land
  them alive. The interregional trade moved upwards of a million people from
  the Upper to the Lower South, 1790–1860 (Tadman `[secondary]`), and each
  sale that parted a family was the owner's interest exercised. (iii) The
  benefit claim fails on revealed behaviour (the same test the module's
  colonial balance sheet later applies to colonial rule): flight was common enough that Congress passed a
  Fugitive Slave Act in 1850 to recover it, and France's richest colony,
  Saint-Domingue, rose in 1791 and became Haiti in 1804. And it
  fails Mill's test: tutelage is legitimate only if it "actually effect[s]"
  improvement toward self-rule, and the positive-good doctrine makes the
  wardship hereditary and permanent — "a grown up child", governed as a child
  for ever — so by Mill's standard it is the "selfish usurpation" he names.
- **Strongest objection.** The U.S. record vindicates the interest argument:
  natural increase, a diet and housing Fogel and Engerman found comparable to
  free labourers', a population that grew from under 400,000 landed Africans
  to about four million by 1860; and the critics' whipping recount is one
  plantation, as Vedder notes ("Who knows whether Barrow was typical or
  atypical?").
- **Why it does not overturn the verdict.** Granted, and stated in the
  lesson: that is the true part, and it is exactly what the replacement-cost
  logic predicts — survival protected where buying was dear. It does not
  rescue the defence's three claims: survival and growth are compatible with
  routine whipping and with sale away from family, which served the interest;
  and the Caribbean record, where buying was cheap, shows what the same
  interest did when the condition failed. One plantation's count cannot prove
  abuse general; it does refute "propaganda", since it comes from the
  defenders' own chosen evidence.
- **What would overturn it.** Evidence that where buying was cheaper than
  rearing slave populations still grew by natural increase; or that enslaved
  people, where flight or purchase of freedom was open, chose to remain.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim. Develop it: two sentences by two
men whose names stand for consent and liberty; the reader's natural first
answer is that each made an exception to his principle when his country
profited (both men were, as a matter of fact, employed by the enterprises
concerned: Locke by the proprietors of Carolina and the Board of Trade, Mill
for thirty-five years at the East India Company — state as fact, not as
gotcha). The lesson takes that answer seriously at the end. First it reads the
passages, because the question "lapse or consequence" cannot be settled
without knowing what the theories say.

1. **What did Locke mean by "waste"?** Question: how can a theory that begins
   "the earth was given to men in common" end with America belonging to
   whoever fences it? Attempt: Locke simply assumed Europeans could take what
   they found. Fails: Sect. 30 gives the Indian ownership of the deer he kills —
   the labour rule applies to him. What Locke denies is ownership of the
   forest. Recall the labour theory (`<Ref>` locke-property-and-consent, one
   clause) and add the two "facts" the empire passages rest on: nature is
   nearly worthless (Sect. 43: nine-tenths, "in most things ninety-nine
   hundredths", of value is labour) and unimproved land yields almost nothing
   (Sect. 37: a thousand acres of America against ten in Devonshire; Sect. 41:
   "a king of a large and fruitful territory there feeds, lodges, and is clad
   worse than a day-labourer in England"). Then Sect. 45: where a people has
   not "joined with the rest of mankind in the consent of the use of their
   common money", their lands "lie waste" and are "still in common" — that is,
   open to appropriation by anyone's labour without anyone's consent (Sects
   28–29). Sect. 49: "in the beginning all the world was America". Because:
   property is derived from labour that adds value, not from occupation,
   hunting or jurisdiction — so a hunting people has property in its game and
   none in its ground. Compare Vitoria (`<Ref>`): discovery gave nothing
   because the land had owners; Locke's criterion of ownership makes the same
   land unowned. Figure 2.

2. **Locke's own counter-text.** Question: does Locke then license conquest?
   No — ch. XVI: conquest is no origin of government; an unjust conqueror has
   the title of a robber with a dagger at your throat; a just conqueror gains
   despotic power only over those who fought him, nothing over their children
   and nothing over their land ("damages seldom amount to the value of land").
   The reader must hold both: Locke forbids taking a people's country by war
   and permits taking "waste" by labour. Which America is the real one depends
   on whether the Indians had "political society in his sense" — a settled
   law, a judge, a power to execute. The course's own thesis (Mehta ch. 4 in
   References): because Locke makes legitimacy and identity the same act — the
   unanimous contract — a people without that contract has no territory; the
   four implications (no collective identity before contract, no identity from
   place, no account of nations, no account of boundaries: why the Channel and
   not the Rhine?). This is where the inherited "waste" argument becomes an
   imperial one — not in Locke's America passages, which are about property,
   but in the step from property to territory.

3. **The gate: a universal capacity and the conditions of its exercise.**
   Question: if "all men are by nature free, equal and independent" (Sect. 4,
   recall), who is in? Attempt: everyone. Fails: Sects 57–60 — children,
   lunatics and idiots are not under the law of reason and cannot consent; a
   man is free at twenty-one when he "understands the laws of England". And how
   does a child come to reason? Locke's own answer in Some Thoughts Concerning
   Education (1693): by habit, awe, a tutor, "good company" — breeding "not to
   be learnt, nor taught by books"; a prince, a nobleman and a gentleman's son
   are to be bred differently. So reason, the universal capacity, has thick
   social conditions of exercise. Define the course's own term here —
   **strategies of exclusion**: the space between the capacities every human
   has and the conditions under which they count politically is where a
   theorist can raise the price of admission without ever denying the universal
   premise. The contrast that makes it visible: a conservative excludes openly
   by birth and inheritance (Burke, next lesson), a patriarchalist by literal
   descent (Filmer); a liberal excludes by a condition that is in principle
   satisfiable later — which is why the exclusion can be indefinite and still
   consistent. Figure 1. Because: the exclusion is not written into the
   anthropology; it is written into the pedagogy.

4. **The nineteenth century turns the gate into a scale.** Anchor the Company
   in one sentence (a trading company chartered in 1600 that became, after
   Plassey in 1757 and the grant of Bengal's revenues in 1765, the government
   of most of India until the Crown took over in 1858). James Mill, who never
   went to India, wrote its History (1817), became the Company's Examiner (its
   chief correspondence officer), and his History was the mandatory text for
   its officials: the "scale of civilization" is "an object of the highest
   practical importance" to a governor; the Hindus stand at its "earliest
   steps", compared with "a band of roving Tartars", Druids with Brahmins;
   India's past is "inscrutable" — the limit put in the object, not the
   inquirer. Then the two strategies named in course.yaml's concepts:
   **inscrutability** (a people that cannot be understood cannot meet the
   conditions) and **civilizational infantilism** (a people as a child, Mill's
   "nonage"). Macaulay's Minute (1835): educate "a class of persons, Indian in
   blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in
   intellect" — and Bentinck to James Mill on leaving for India: "I shall not
   be Governor General. It is you that will be Governor General." Because: once
   the condition of inclusion is a collective stage rather than an individual
   age, a whole people can be a child, and the tutor is a state.

5. **Mill's own reasons at FULL STRENGTH.** This section is the lesson's centre
   and precedes any critique. The problem Mill is solving in 1861: Britain in
   fact rules India (the Company abolished in 1858 after the rebellion of 1857);
   how is that rule to be a good and not an evil to the governed? (a) Backward
   peoples: there are conditions "in which a vigorous despotism is in itself the
   best mode of government for training the people"; a native despotism yields
   a good despot only as "a rare and transitory accident"; a more civilized
   ruling people "ought to be able to supply it constantly" — to do "all that
   could be done by a succession of absolute monarchs… qualified by their genius
   to anticipate all that experience has taught to the more advanced nation";
   this is "the highest moral trust which can devolve upon a nation", and
   rulers who do not even aim at it "are selfish usurpers, on a par in
   criminality" with any conqueror. (b) Why the obvious remedy fails: the
   "superficial" answer is a minister responsible to Parliament; but "to govern
   one country under responsibility to the people of another… is despotism";
   "the only choice the case admits is a choice of despotisms", and "the
   despotism of twenty millions" who "neither hear, nor see, nor know any thing
   about their subjects" is likely worse than that of those who do; "such a
   thing as government of one people by another does not and can not exist" —
   one people may keep another as "a human-cattle farm", but it cannot attend
   to their good. The dangers he names are British: settlers, who want the
   natives' land and labour and have friends in Parliament; missionaries who
   want the Bible in state schools; native oppressors with advocates — "the
   silent myriads obtain none". (c) The remedy: a permanent delegated body, not
   a party minister; agents trained from youth and chosen by competition; "a
   choice of imperfections". The Company "suggested the true theory of the
   government of a semi-barbarous dependency by a civilized country, and after
   having done this, to perish". Then the clause from On Liberty read as it
   stands: the sentence "the sole end… is self-protection" is followed at once
   by "any member of a civilized community", and the next paragraph says what
   "hardly needs saying" — the doctrine is for "human beings in the maturity of
   their faculties", not for "backward states of society in which the race
   itself may be considered as in its nonage", where "Despotism is a legitimate
   mode of government… provided the end be their improvement" and until then
   "there is nothing for them but implicit obedience to an Akbar or a
   Charlemagne". Because: for Mill, liberty is a secondary principle valid at a
   stage; utility is the ground; representative institutions are for the ripe.
   Define **tutelage** here as Mill's own concept, not as an accusation.

6. **Lapse or consequence? Two theses, stated so the reader can test them.**
   The **contradiction thesis**: empire contradicts consent,
   representative government and equal citizenship — witness the hardening
   after 1857, and the Ilbert Bill of 1883 (a measure to let Indian judges try
   Europeans, defeated by a settler outcry), when Stephen wrote to The Times
   that the Raj rests "not on consent, but on conquest" and that its rulers
   "shrink from the open… assertion" of superiority; and Stephen's gunpowder
   (recalled with `<Ref>` stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity, one clause — the
   reader has met the man and his argument that coercion is the normal
   instrument of rule; quote only the one sentence, from the 1883 article): "A
   barrel of gunpowder may be harmless or may explode, but you cannot educate
   it into household fuel by exploding little bits of it" — a liberal saying
   the pedagogy is a pretence. The **strategies thesis**, the course's
   own: Mill's liberty is secondary to utility and keyed to a stage, so
   excluding India is not an inconsistency; the thick conditions that excluded
   India are the same ones that excluded the propertyless and women at home and
   later included them; the exclusion is deferred, never denied — which is why
   it can last. And a third, the **discontinuity thesis** (name it in a clause):
   that liberal imperialism is a nineteenth-century turn, not a Lockean seed —
   Hobbes and Locke do not ground individualism in history, the Mills do. The
   seam states the expectation (universal premises include) and the record
   (they deferred). Mark where the lesson concedes: the strategies reading
   cannot say what Stephen can — that the deferral was known to be
   indefinite; and the contradiction reading cannot say why the unfamiliar was
   always read as incomplete rather than as different.

7. **The ethical alternative, one paragraph.** Gandhi, Hind Swaraj (1909):
   civilization is "that mode of conduct which points to man the path of duty"
   — available at any stage of history, so the developmental argument has no
   purchase; and the fact that answers Mill's test from outside the theory:
   independent India gave the vote in 1950 to an electorate most of whom could
   not read. State as the counterpoint; say in one clause that it bears on
   whether Mill's premise about stages was true, not on whether the exclusion
   followed from it — the question the next section decides.

8. **The objection inside Mill, carried to the end.** OL IV on the Mormons: "I
   am not aware that any community has a right to force another to be
   civilised" — set beside OL I's barbarians. Reply: the Mormons are dissenters
   within a civilized community; "the whole East" has, for Mill, "properly
   speaking, no history" because custom rules it (OL III). So the clause does
   the sorting, and the question of the lesson is whether a sorting of that
   kind is a lapse in a liberal theory or the place where a liberal theory
   decides who it is for. This is the objection in its best form; the
   weighing is the next section.

8a. **Objection (author's review): slavery as a positive good — the harder
   tutelage.** Required; state it at FULL STRENGTH before any reply. Seam:
   Mill's despotism is temporary and ends in self-government; the reader
   should meet the version of "rule for their benefit" that removed the end
   date, written in the same decades. Calhoun, 1837 (anchor in a clause:
   senator for South Carolina, speaking against receiving abolition
   petitions): slavery is "a good — a positive good"; "Never before has the
   black race of Central Africa … attained a condition so civilized and so
   improved"; the old and sick slave cared for by "master and mistress"
   against the pauper in the poorhouse; every civilised society lives on the
   labour of some part of it, and the South's way is the honest one. Fitzhugh
   (anchor: a Virginia lawyer and pamphleteer): the slave is "a grown up
   child"; slavery "christianizes, protects, supports and civilizes him"; the
   free labourer of the North and of England is a "slave without a master",
   worse off because no one's interest is bound to his survival; "The law of
   self-interest secures kind and humane treatment". Then the author's
   objection in its modern form, in the lesson's words: an owner does not
   wreck his own car or lame his own horse, so stories of systematic abuse
   are propaganda, and to be ruled by a superior civilisation is a gain to the
   ruled. Give it its strongest evidence too: Fogel and Engerman's finding
   that diet and housing compared well with free workers', and the U.S.
   slave population's natural growth. Then the link to the lesson: this is
   Mill's tutelage with the time limit removed, and Mill's own clause supplies
   the test — "provided the end be their improvement, and the means justified
   by actually effecting that end" — which makes the defence an empirical
   claim that can be checked. Fitzhugh's debt to Carlyle in one clause
   (`<Ref>` carlyle-heroes: Gurth's brass collar against "liberty to die by
   starvation"). 250–350 words; the reply is the weighing's part (b).

9. **The weighing — the exclusions are consequences of the principles, not
   lapses; the positive-good defence fails against the record.** The lesson's last body section reaches the verdict (see VERDICT).
   Set the contradiction thesis at full strength (ch. XVI against conquest; the
   Mormon passage; Stephen's gunpowder; the hardening after 1857) against the
   test that decides it: is the exclusion an exception the principle forbids,
   or an application the principle states? Locke: the labour criterion,
   applied to land, makes unimproved land unowned (Sect. 45) — no premise is
   added. Mill: the stage clause is in On Liberty's own statement of the harm
   principle, and liberty is secondary to utility. So both are consequences.
   Grade: **strong**. Then what the verdict does not say: it does not say the
   premises were true (whether hunting peoples owned their ground, whether
   peoples have "stages" — Gandhi's counterpoint and the 1950 franchise bear on
   that), and it does not judge the men; it judges where the exclusion sits in
   the argument. What would overturn it: a reading on which either exclusion
   needs a premise the principle does not contain.
   Part (b), the author's-review objection weighed (see VERDICT, second
   verdict), 500–700 words with every number bound in its sentence. First the
   logic of the property interest: it protects what would lower the asset's
   value, and only where a replacement costs more than the loss; it does not
   forbid what raises the value — the whip that gets more work, the sale that
   realises a price. Fitzhugh states the condition himself (Cuba: "cheaper to
   buy than to rear"). Then the record, cheap replacement against dear: the
   British Caribbean (natural decrease; 775,000 to 665,000 slaves between
   1807 and 1834 once imports stopped; Jamaica 347,000 to 311,070) against
   the United States after 1808 (natural increase; a black population in 1807
   already over three times the Africans imported). Concede the U.S. growth
   as the defence's true part, and say why it is what the logic predicts.
   Then the abuse: Barrow's plantation — 0.7 whippings per hand a year on
   Fogel and Engerman's count, 1.19 on their critics', a whipping before the
   men's eyes about every six days — and the Middle Passage, about one in
   seven dead at sea (12.5 million shipped, 10.7 million landed); and the
   interregional trade that moved upwards of a million people. Then the
   benefit: revealed behaviour (the 1850 Fugitive Slave Act; Saint-Domingue
   1791, Haiti 1804) and Mill's test (a wardship made hereditary and
   permanent effects no improvement toward self-rule). Verdict: the defence
   fails (strong); its one true part stated. Calhoun's two forecasts in one
   sentence, judged by one standard: that agitation would split the Union in
   blood — borne out; that slavery was the most stable foundation — refuted
   by the same war. What would overturn it: natural increase where buying was
   cheaper than rearing, or the enslaved choosing to stay where flight or
   self-purchase was open.
   To make room (est_minutes stays 45): step 4 shrinks to one paragraph
   (James Mill's "scale of civilization" and Macaulay's "blood and colour"
   sentence; Bentinck's line goes), and step 7 (Gandhi) to two sentences.

Headings fall out as: "What Locke meant by waste"; "Conquest gives no title,
labour does"; "A universal capacity and the price of admission"; "The scale of
civilization: India as a child"; "Mill's case for a good despotism"; "Why
responsibility to Parliament is the worst despotism"; "Lapse or consequence?";
"Civilization as conduct"; "The Mormons and the barbarians"; "Slavery as a
positive good: the tutelage without an end"; "The exclusions follow from the
principles; the positive good fails its own test".

**Conclusion.** States the verdict in plain words with its grade: Locke's waste
land and Mill's tutelage are not lapses from their principles but consequences
of conditions those principles state — the labour criterion applied to land,
and the stage clause in On Liberty itself (strong). The defence of slavery as
a positive good fails against the record (strong): the owner's interest kept
slaves alive where they were dear to replace, as in the United States after
1808, and let them die where they were cheap, as in the Caribbean sugar
colonies; it never forbade the whip or the sale, which served it; and those
it claimed to benefit fled and, in Saint-Domingue, rose. The verdict concerns the
argument, not the truth of its premises about other peoples, which the lesson
has shown were contested from outside. Then the hand-off: Mill called the rule
"the highest moral trust"; `burke-against-hastings` follows a conservative who
used the same word to prosecute the man who ruled Bengal.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), at first mention and no more than three per
  section:** East India Company (as above); Examiner (the Company's chief
  correspondence officer — both Mills held the post); Governor-General;
  Plassey (1757, Clive's victory that made the Company the power in Bengal);
  the 1765 grant of Bengal's revenues (the next lesson calls it the dewanny —
  do not introduce the word here); the rebellion of 1857 and the Crown's
  takeover of 1858; the Durham Report (1839, on responsible government for
  Canada — Mill's model for colonies "of European race"); the Ilbert Bill
  (1883); Haileybury (the Company's college, 1806–58); Anglicists and
  Orientalists (the 1830s dispute over whether to fund English or Sanskrit and
  Arabic learning that Macaulay's Minute decided); "nonage" (minority, the age
  before legal adulthood); Akbar (Mughal emperor, r. 1556–1605); Charlemagne.
- **"Race" in Mill means "the race itself in its nonage" — a people at a
  stage, not a biological category.** Mehta notes race is conspicuously absent
  from the liberal writings on India (index 42 key_results ch. 1 pp. 15–16).
  Do not import it; Macaulay's "blood and colour" is the one place it surfaces,
  and there as something education is to work behind.
- **Mehta's name appears only in References**, as do Macpherson, Pateman, Dunn,
  Hartz, Said, Pitts, Tully, Armitage. "Strategies of exclusion" and
  "civilizational infantilism" are stated as the course's terms (they are the
  concept ids). "Terra nullius" is Mehta's gloss and a later legal term; Locke
  says "waste" — use Locke's word.
- **Fairness to Mill is the lesson's test.** RG XVIII argues against the
  simplest imperial arrangement, names settlers and missionaries as the
  dangers to Indians, calls the rule despotism without euphemism, and wants
  the agents trained and examined. Give that before any critique; a lesson
  that opens with "Mill justified despotism" has already lost the newcomer
  test. Likewise Locke: Sect. 30 and ch. XVI must appear before the "waste"
  argument is called imperial.
- **Both Mills worked for the Company — state once, as fact.** Mill's 1858
  Memorandum defending the Company is not in the dossier; mention only as the
  context of RG XVIII's last paragraph.
- **Do not re-teach** the labour theory, the two limits, consent, the harm
  principle, or Stephen's reply to Mill and his India passages (`<Ref>`
  stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity) — one `<Ref>` clause each.
  Burke's "schoolboys without tutors" is a forward pointer, not a set piece
  here.
- **Where the dossier is thin:** James Mill's History and Macaulay's Minute are
  known only through Mehta's quotations — both are PD and must be quoted from
  their own texts; Stephen's gunpowder passage likewise — its source is "The
  Foundations of the Government of India" (1883), not in the dossier; obtain it.
  Gandhi via Mehta pp. 113–114 — PD, obtain. The "discontinuity"
  reading (Pitts) is not in the dossier beyond Mehta's refutation of it; name
  it fairly in a clause and cite Pitts in References.
- **Numbers:** thirty-five years at the Company, 1600/1757/1765/1858, 1835,
  1857, 1883, 1950. The 9/10–99/100 figure is Locke's and stays in his
  sentence. No GDP figures. The only population and mortality figures are
  those of step 8a/9(b) (Higman; the slave-trade totals; Barrow's whippings),
  each bound in its sentence with its source, and each marked `[secondary]` in
  the VERDICT read in the original before use.
- **Register:** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right. The lesson's conclusion is a question held open with
  the two theses stated.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (E3, the one warm-up).** State in one line each how Vitoria's
   discovery title and Locke's "waste" argument decide whether the American
   land had owners, and name the premise that differs (ownership by reason and
   jurisdiction vs ownership by value-adding labour).
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill on Macaulay's Minute (1835).**
   Metadata: a member of the Governor-General's council, writing to decide how
   a fixed education grant is spent, against the Orientalist members; predict
   what it will claim and omit; read the "blood and colour" paragraph; records
   / argues / omits (the Indians who were to be educated are not asked); what
   it can be USED to establish — the content of the tutelage programme, not its
   reception.
3. **Boundary probe.** A people in Locke's state of nature that uses money and
   encloses fields but has no "settled standing rules" and no common judge
   (Sects 124–126). Does Sect. 45 still make its land waste? Does ch. XVI still
   forbid taking it by conquest? Solution: the "waste" argument lapses (Sect.
   45's condition is money, not government), the territorial step (no
   political society → no territory) is the only imperial argument left, and it
   is the one Locke never explicitly makes.
4. **Claim to refute.** "Mill wanted India governed from Westminster like any
   other part of the kingdom." Name the step that fails (RG XVIII l.285–307,
   "a choice of despotisms") and what Mill proposed instead.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped.** Mill's own test: a despotism over
   barbarians is legitimate only if "the means [are] justified by actually
   effecting" their improvement, and rulers who do not aim at it are "selfish
   usurpers". Apply the test to Mill's own evidence of 1857 (OL II fn 10: the
   demand to teach the Bible in state schools; RG XVIII on settlers). State the
   corollary Mill does not draw and the reply he would give (the remedy is a
   better delegated body, not withdrawal).
6. **Synthesis — causal ranking.** Which did more to make the exclusion
   possible: the universal anthropology (which made inclusion a promise), the
   pedagogy of conditions (which made the promise deferrable), or the
   nineteenth-century scale of civilization (which made a people a child)?
   Rank as necessary / enabling / precipitating and defend with one passage
   each.
7. **Counter-argument stem.** "Mill's clause is a lapse: nothing in the harm
   principle requires it." Name two facts the claim must account for (Mill's
   statement that liberty is a secondary principle grounded in utility; the OL
   III passage on custom and "the whole East") and what would have to be true
   for the claim to survive (that Mill's utility could not distinguish stages of
   society — which he denies).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `burke-against-hastings`.** Mill calls rule over India "the
   highest moral trust". A Whig member of Parliament had used the same word in
   1788 while prosecuting the man who had governed Bengal, and his first charge
   was that the governor claimed "the constitution of Asia is despotism".
   Predict, from what you know of Burke's Reflections, whether he would accept
   Mill's "vigorous despotism… for training the people" as the content of the
   trust, and why not. The solution points to empire-as-trust and geographical
   morality with a `<Ref>` to the next lesson.
9. **Attack the verdict.** The lesson concludes that the exclusions are
   consequences of the principles (strong). Build the strongest case that they
   are lapses: take Locke's ch. XVI on conquest and Sect. 4's "all men…
   free, equal and independent", and Mill's Mormon passage and his own
   "selfish usurpers" test, and argue that each theory contains a principle
   that, applied consistently, forbids the exclusion it also states. Then say
   what evidence would be needed to overturn the verdict: a passage showing
   that Locke's labour criterion was not meant to apply to land held by
   hunting peoples, or that Mill's stage clause is detachable from the harm
   principle without changing what it covers. The solution weighs the case
   and says why a theory containing both a universal premise and its own
   stated gate is not thereby self-contradictory.
10. **Attack the verdict — the master's interest (author's review).** State
   the strongest form of the claim that an owner's interest protects his
   slaves as it protects his horse, with its best evidence (U.S. natural
   increase; Fogel and Engerman on diet and housing; Barrow's 0.7 whippings
   per hand). Then: (a) state the condition under which the argument holds,
   using Fitzhugh's own sentence on Cuba; (b) predict from that condition what
   happened to slave populations in the Caribbean before and after 1807 and in

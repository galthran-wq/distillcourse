# Research brief: locke-property-and-consent (Locke: property by labour and government by consent)

status: planned
module: contract-revolution-reaction
type: core
goal: Analyze Locke's derivation of property from labour and of legitimate government from consent, and his case for a right of resistance

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The reader arrives from `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` and
  `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty` with the whole Hobbesian apparatus: a state of
  nature that is a war of all against all, laws of nature that are "but words"
  without a sword, a covenant of every man with every man that authorises one
  sovereign, and a sovereign who cannot be accused of injustice and is bound by
  no law of his own making (`sovereignty`, `covenant`, `authorization`,
  `liberty-as-silence-of-law`, `protection-and-obedience`). From
  `filmer-patriarcha` (the prerequisite): `patriarchal-divine-right` — political
  power is fatherly power descended from Adam, no one is born free, the king is
  above the laws — and the two points that lesson marked for an opponent to
  break: Filmer's strongest (no one consents to being born into a polity; where
  in history did a people ever meet to choose a ruler? how does a majority bind
  infants and the absent?) and his weakest (which living king is Adam's heir?).
  From `english-revolution-as-test` (the previous lesson): 1642–1660, the regicide,
  the Protectorate and the Restoration, so 1688 is the next English date and
  "who judges the king?" is a live question. From
  `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law` and `aquinas-law-and-kingship`:
  `natural-law` as a law above statutes and the question whether an unjust law
  binds.
- **Contributes:** One move: Locke keeps Hobbes's starting point (free and equal
  men with no common judge), refuses Filmer's (men are born subjects), and
  changes one premise — the state of nature is already under a law that reason
  can read — and from that single change derives property before government,
  government by consent and limited by trust, and a right to resist a ruler who
  breaks the trust. The First Treatise's demolition of Filmer is reported in one
  section; the Second Treatise is the lesson. Its centre is the derivation and
  the objections Locke himself raises and answers ("it will be said… I answer").
- **Hands off:** This lesson reconstructs; it judges none of the three claims,
  and names where each is decided. (1) Consent: Locke's "tacit consent" reaches
  anyone who "barely travels freely on the highway"; the reader will feel it
  proves too much, and Locke's own answer to "no historical instances" (Sects
  100–112) is thin — the lesson says so as a fact about the argument (he answers
  that history is silent about infancy, which explains the silence and does not
  supply the instances). It is decided in the next lesson,
  `hume-against-the-original-contract`: consent as the ground of actual
  government fails, strong (the sleeping passenger, conquest and usurpation, the
  promise regress), and survives as a hypothetical test. (2) Property: Locke
  makes "this is mine" the first natural act; Rousseau makes it the first crime;
  `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will` (two lessons on) judges the empirical
  part — inequality rises with property, storage and settlement (strong) — and
  the America passages are judged in `locke-and-mill-on-empire`. (3) The right
  of resistance is the thing Burke (`burke-reflections`) will say England never
  claimed in 1688 and France is about to misuse. The reading of Locke as Hobbes made respectable (link 8) is
  argued again in `strauss-natural-right-and-history`; the America passages are
  held for `locke-and-mill-on-empire`; prerogative ("the power of doing public
  good without a rule", link 7) returns as the liberal tradition's own emergency
  power in `donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship`.
- **Spine step:** "Government by consent (Locke) is doubted by Hume and
  radicalised by Rousseau's general will; when France tries to frame a government
  from principles, Burke, Maistre and Donoso Cortés answer that no generation
  knows enough, Herder and Fichte put the people before the state, and Hegel
  absorbs both sides into the rational state"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: men in a world without government
are already under a law, already own things, already may punish wrongdoers —
so why set up a government, and what could they have agreed to give it? Locke
answers against Hobbes on every point and ends by justifying the overthrow of a
king.

0. **Whom is Locke answering?** One section, before the derivation. The reader
   has Filmer (`<Ref>` filmer-patriarcha, one clause: political power is a
   father's power descended from Adam; no one is born free). Locke's Preface says
   the book justifies William III "by the consent of the people, the only one of
   all lawful governments", and Ch. I Sect. 1 reports the First Treatise's result
   in four steps (Adam had no such power; his heirs had no right to it; no law
   fixes the heir; the eldest line is lost) — so "he that will not give just
   occasion to think that all government in the world is the product only of
   force and violence … must of necessity find out another rise of government".
   State the burden plainly: having denied Filmer's title, Locke must show what
   title any government has, without falling back on force. Then Sect. 2–3: the
   magistrate's power distinguished from a father's, a master's, a husband's, a
   lord's — "political power" defined. Ch. VI (Sects 52–76) answers Filmer's
   strongest point in one paragraph: the power is "parental" (the mother shares
   it), it ends when the child reaches reason, and where fathers became princes
   it was "by the express or tacit consent of the children" (Sect. 74). Mark the
   seam (L10b): from a man who has just destroyed the patriarchal title one
   expects him to deny Filmer's history too; Locke concedes the history (fathers
   often became kings) and denies that it was fatherhood that made them so.
   Heading as claim: *Adam's heir cannot be found, so another title is needed*.
1. **What changes if the state of nature has a law?** The reader has Hobbes
   (`<Ref>` hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant): no common power, no law, so
   war. Obvious attempt: keep Hobbes's picture and only soften the sovereign.
   Why it fails: if there is no law before government, the sovereign's law is
   the only law, and nothing he does can be unjust — exactly Hobbes's result
   (`<Ref>` hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty). Locke's answer: the state of nature
   is "a state of liberty, yet it is not a state of licence" (Sect. 6); the law
   of nature is reason, which teaches that no one ought to harm another in life,
   health, liberty or possessions — because men are "all the workmanship of one
   omnipotent and infinitely wise Maker" and so his property, not one another's.
   Freedom under law before any lawgiver on earth. Heading as claim: *Freedom
   without licence: a law before any legislator*.
2. **If there is a law but no judge, who enforces it?** Obvious attempt: no
   one, so the law is "but words" (Hobbes's exact objection). Locke's answer:
   "the execution of the law of nature is, in that state, put into every man's
   hands" (Sect. 7) — anyone may punish a transgressor, proportionately, for
   reparation and restraint; a murderer "hath declared war against all mankind"
   and may be destroyed "as a lion or a tiger" (Sect. 11). Locke's own objection
   (Sect. 13): "it is unreasonable for men to be judges in their own cases";
   civil government is indeed the remedy — but "absolute monarchs are but men",
   and a single judge in his own case over millions is worse than the state of
   nature. This is the judge-in-own-cause objection that turns Hobbes's cure
   into a disease; mark it as the lesson's first reversal (L10b seam: from
   Hobbes one expects the absolute sovereign to end the state of nature; Locke
   says the absolute sovereign is still in it, Sects 90–93).
3. **How can anything be mine before there is a government to say so?**
   Hobbes: property is the sovereign's creation (`<Ref>`
   hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty). Locke's problem: God gave the earth to men in
   common; how does a part become one man's without everyone's consent? Obvious
   attempt: by agreement — but then "man had starved, notwithstanding the plenty
   God had given him" (Sect. 28). Answer: every man has a property in his own
   person, hence in his labour; whatever he removes from the common state and
   "mixed his labour with" is his (Sect. 27). Worked example: the acorns —
   "when did they begin to be his? when he digested? or when he eat? or when he
   boiled? or when he brought them home? or when he picked them up?" (Sect.
   28). Then the two limits that keep the argument from licensing greed: take
   only what you can use before it spoils (Sect. 31), and only where "enough and
   as good" is left for others (Sect. 27, 33 — the man who drinks from a river).
   Land follows the same rule: as much as a man tills (Sect. 32). The value
   argument (Sects 40–43: labour puts nine-tenths, "ninety-nine hundredths", of
   the value into things; the loaf of bread) is why enclosure does not rob the
   commons but "gives ninety acres to mankind" (Sect. 37).
4. **Then how did anyone come to own more than he can use?** The limits seem
   to forbid great estates; yet they exist. Answer (Sects 45–50): money.
   Durable things — gold, silver, a diamond — can be hoarded without spoiling;
   "the invention of money" lets a man enlarge his possessions by consent of
   those who take his coin; "in the beginning all the world was America" (Sect.
   49) — before money no one would enlarge his land, since surplus would rot.
   So unequal possession arises by tacit consent, before and outside government
   (Sect. 50). The spoilage limit survives only as a ban on waste; the "enough
   and as good" limit quietly lapses. Flag here (one sentence) that the America
   passages are read again in the empire module. Strongest reading to state as
   the course's own (Strauss in References): this is the step where a doctrine
   of limits becomes a doctrine of unlimited acquisition — the terrors of the
   law of nature "no longer strike the covetous but the waster".
5. **If men already have law, property and the right to punish, why would they
   ever set up a government?** The question of the opening. Answer (Ch. IX,
   Sects 123–127): three "inconveniences" — no settled, known law (men are
   biased in their own cases); no known and indifferent judge; no power to
   execute a sentence against the strong. So men give up two powers, and only
   two: to do what they think fit for their preservation (now regulated by law)
   and to punish (now wholly given to the executive). What they cannot give up
   is anything more than they had: "nobody can transfer to another more power
   than he has in himself" (Sect. 135) — hence the legislative can never be
   arbitrary over life and property, must rule by promulgated standing laws,
   cannot take property without consent (so taxation needs the majority or its
   representatives, Sect. 140), cannot delegate law-making (Sect. 141). The
   sergeant may order the soldier to the mouth of a cannon but cannot take a
   penny from his pocket (Sect. 139): absolute power is limited by its end.
   Heading: *What the legislative may never do: nemo dat quod non habet*.
6. **How does one man's agreement bind the rest — and how does anyone agree
   who was born under a government?** Locke's two objections (Ch. VIII). First
   (Sects 96–99): once men incorporate, the body must move "whither the greater
   force carries it", so majority rule is entailed by consent itself, else the
   compact would be like "Cato's coming into the theatre, only to go out again"
   (Sect. 98). Second, the hard one (Sects 113–122): no one living agreed. A
   father cannot bind his son's liberty (Sect. 116); a child is born "a subject
   of no country" (Sect. 118); so consent must be given by each — expressly (a
   compact that makes a member) or tacitly, by enjoying any possession under the
   government, "or barely travelling freely on the highway" (Sect. 119), which
   binds only for the duration of the enjoyment and makes no one a member.
   Filmer's two objections (`<Ref>` filmer-patriarcha) are the ones Locke answers
   here by name (Sect. 100): no historical instance, and all men born under
   government. His replies: history is silent about every people's infancy as
   about every man's, and where beginnings are seen — Rome, Venice, the peoples of
   America — they are agreements (Sects 101–112); a son is "born a subject of no
   country" until he consents (Sect. 118). State the weak point plainly and stop:
   the highway makes consent so cheap that it can hardly be refused, and the
   historical reply rests on absence of evidence. One sentence hands it on: a
   Scottish philosopher sixty years later will ask where any such consent can be
   found (`<Ref>` hume-against-the-original-contract). Do not argue Hume here; the
   next lesson does. Locke's partial defence to keep: tacit consent is limited by
   design (Sects 121–122), and express consent — the naturalised member — is real.
7. **Who judges when the ruler breaks the agreement?** The Hobbesian wall: no
   subject may judge the sovereign (`<Ref>` bodin-sovereignty,
   hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty). Locke's answer in steps: the legislative is
   supreme but "only a fiduciary power to act for certain ends" (Sect. 149);
   the executive's discretion, prerogative, is "nothing but the power of doing
   public good without a rule" (Sect. 166) and is lawful only for that end;
   tyranny is "the exercise of power beyond right" in any form, one or many
   (Sects 199–201 — Locke quotes James I against absolutism); where law ends
   tyranny begins (Sect. 202). When the legislative is altered, or the prince
   hinders it, or delivers the people to a foreign power, or rules by will
   (Sects 214–219), or when legislators "endeavour to take away and destroy the
   property of the people" (Sect. 222), the trust is forfeited and power
   "devolves to the people". Who shall be judge? "The people shall be judge" —
   the principal who deputed the trustee (Sect. 240); and where no earthly judge
   exists, each man judges in his own conscience and "appeals to heaven" (Sects
   21, 168, 241–242), as Jephtha did. Objection Locke raises (Sects 223–230):
   this "lays a ferment for frequent rebellion". Reply: people are slow to stir;
   "a long train of abuses" is needed (Sect. 225, the ship bound for Algiers,
   Sect. 210); and the doctrine is "the best fence against rebellion", because
   rulers who use force against law are the true rebels (Sect. 226). The
   Polyphemus reductio (Sect. 228): telling men to bear a tyrant is Ulysses
   preaching passive obedience in the Cyclops's den. Set this against Filmer's
   "no deposition; the multitude would be judge in its own cause" (`<Ref>`
   filmer-patriarcha) — Locke's answer is that the ruler who uses force against
   law has already made himself the party in a state of war.
8. **Is this Hobbes in sheep's clothing?** Close by putting the reading the
   course states as its own (Strauss, Smith in References) against the text:
   Locke keeps Hobbes's foundation (self-preservation, equality, no common
   judge) and reaches the opposite constitution — limited government, majority,
   separation of legislative from executive, a right to resist — because he
   adds a law before the sovereign and a property before the state. Whether
   that law is really a law (it has no earthly promulgator and, Locke admits,
   no sanction but each man's hand) is the question Strauss presses:
   strip the theology and what remains is Hobbes's right of self-preservation
   plus a theory of acquisition. The lesson states both readings, says which
   sections each rests on (Sects 6, 13, 135 for the law reading; Sects 7, 11,
   45–50, 123 for the Hobbesian reading), and then says plainly what Locke did
   and did not answer: to Hobbes's "law without a sword is but words" he has an
   answer (every man holds the executive power of the law of nature, Sects 7–8,
   and the defect of that sanction is what government remedies, Sect. 13); to
   "does the law survive without its theological ground?" he has none in the
   Treatise, because his argument never drops the ground (Sect. 6, men as God's
   workmanship) — the objection is not answered, it is not met. Which reading of
   Locke's intent is right is not decided here; the reading is taken up again in
   `strauss-natural-right-and-history`.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The acorn question (Sect. 28)** quoted whole and worked as a decision
   tree: digestion / eating / boiling / carrying home / picking up — Locke's
   answer is the first act of labour, because any later point would make
   property depend on another's consent. Pair with the water in the pitcher,
   the grass my horse has bit, the turfs my servant has cut (Sects 28–29).
2. **The two limits and their lapse** (Sects 27, 31, 33, 36–38, 45–50): the
   river no one is harmed by drinking from; plums that rot in a week against
   nuts that last a year against "a sparkling pebble or a diamond" (Sect. 46);
   the island of a hundred families with no money, where no one would enlarge
   his land (Sect. 48); "in the beginning all the world was America" (Sect. 49).
   Run it as a step-through: each frame adds one thing (durable good, exchange,
   money, tacit consent) and shows which limit still binds.
3. **"Absolute monarchs are but men" (Sect. 13) and the polecats and lions
   (Sect. 93)** — the anti-Hobbes core in two sentences: men "take care to
   avoid what mischiefs may be done them by polecats or foxes, but are content,
   nay, think it safety, to be devoured by lions". Place beside Hobbes's own
   defence that the sovereign's injustice is less to be feared than the state of
   nature (`<Ref>` hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty).
4. **The three defects and the two powers surrendered (Sects 123–131) as a
   table**: defect → remedy → power given up → power retained. The nemo dat
   chain (Sect. 135) and the sergeant's penny (Sect. 139) complete it: what the
   people never had, the legislative cannot have.
5. **The highway and its limits**: Locke's tacit consent (Sect. 119, "barely
   travelling freely on the highway") quoted whole, then Sects 121–122 (binding
   only "during such enjoyment"; the naturalised member as the only express
   consent) — so the reader sees how little Locke claims for it before the next
   lesson tests even that. The comparison with Hume's ship belongs to
   `hume-against-the-original-contract`.
6. **The ship to Algiers (Sect. 210)**: the captain who steers for Algiers
   despite contrary winds — how a "long train of actions" reveals a design
   without any single act being conclusive. Use it to derive the threshold for
   resistance (Sects 207–210, 225): the highwayman with a twelve-penny purse
   may be killed, the man who withholds a hundred pounds may not, because one
   leaves time to appeal to law.
7. **Who shall be judge? (Sects 240–242) and Jephtha (Sect. 21)**: the appeal
   to heaven parsed — not "God will intervene" but "there is no earthly judge,
   so each answers for his judgment to God". Contrast Bodin's and Hobbes's
   refusal to let any subject judge the sovereign (`<Ref>` bodin-sovereignty).
8. **Polyphemus's den (Sect. 228)**: Ulysses preaching passive obedience; the
   lamb yielding its throat to the wolf as "an admirable peace". Pair with
   "bid them first be slaves, and then take care of their liberty" (Sect. 220).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Terms to anchor at first mention (L11):** *state of nature* (recall with
  `<Ref>` to Hobbes, one clause); *law of nature* (distinguish from Hobbes's
  "laws of nature" as theorems of peace — Locke's binds as God's law known by
  reason; recall Cicero/Aquinas with `<Ref>` cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law); *executive
  power of the law of nature* (define: every man's right to punish breaches in
  the state of nature); *property* — in Locke's wide sense "lives, liberties and
  estates" (Sect. 123) as well as goods; say so at first use or Ch. IX reads as
  a defence of the rich; *legislative/executive* (in Locke the legislature and
  the executive power, not the modern "branches"); *prerogative* (the crown's
  discretion to act without or against law for the public good); *trust*
  (fiduciary, a legal term: a power held for another's benefit); *appeal to
  heaven*; *Filmer* (`<Ref>` filmer-patriarcha — recall, do not re-anchor;
  the First Treatise is "the foregoing discourse" of Sect. 1); *1688* (James II fled, the Convention Parliament declared
  the throne vacant and offered it to William and Mary with a Declaration of
  Right — one sentence); *Jephtha* (Judges 11, a judge of Israel who, with no
  arbiter between him and the Ammonites, left the cause to "the Lord the
  Judge"); *Hume* — not anchored here beyond the one forward sentence; the
  next lesson introduces him.
- **Dates to the decade** (1680s–90s) except 1688/1690, where contingency is
  the point (the Treatise was drafted before the revolution it is read as
  justifying — the Preface says the middle was lost; do not present it as a
  pamphlet written after the fact without saying the drafting is disputed).
- **Do not re-teach Hobbes or Filmer.** The reader has the covenant and the
  twelve rights, and the whole of Patriarcha; recall each with a `<Ref>` and one
  clause. The lesson's energy goes to the differences. Do not argue Hume's
  objection either — one forward sentence (link 6).
- **Do not teach the America passages here** beyond Sect. 49 and the one-line
  flag; `locke-and-mill-on-empire` reads Sects 36–43 and Ch. VIII Sects
  102–108 as the "waste land" argument.
- **"Property" and "labour theory of value".** Locke's nine-tenths /
  ninety-nine-hundredths (Sects 40, 43) is a claim about where value comes
  from, used to justify enclosure; it is not Marx's theory and the lesson
  should not call it a precursor in the body (Marx is `marx-manifesto`, where
  `labour-theory-of-property` is reinforced).
- **The religious ground.** Locke's law of nature rests on men being God's
  workmanship (Sect. 6) and on an afterlife for its sanction; the "Hobbes in
  sheep's clothing" reading turns on whether the argument survives without
  that ground. Present both readings as readings; this lesson does not decide
  between them, but it does say that the second objection (does the law survive
  without God?) is one Locke does not answer (link 8).
  Strauss's esoteric-writing thesis (ch. V.B pp. 206–209) is NOT to be taught —
  it needs its own apparatus; one sentence in References at most.
- **Tacit consent is weak and Locke knows it** — he limits it to "the duration
  of the enjoyment" and says it makes no one a member (Sects 121–122). Do not
  strawman him as claiming the highway makes citizens; state the real claim,
  so that the next lesson can put Hume against it.
- **"Appeal to heaven" is not a prediction of divine intervention**; it names
  the absence of an earthly judge. A common misreading worth one refuting
  sentence, not a L13 slot.
- **Period prejudice:** Locke's remarks on American peoples, on slaves as
  captives of a just war (Sects 23–24, 85), and on servants, are stated as his
  positions where they bear on the argument (slavery as continued state of
  war is part of the "no self-enslavement" derivation), never moralised either
  way; the empire module takes them up.
- **Names that go ONLY in References:** Strauss, Smith, Muller, Goldwin
  (Strauss–Cropsey), Macpherson (if the "possessive individualism" reading is
  mentioned), Laslett, Dunn, Tully. Filmer may be named in the body as the
  primary interlocutor (`<Ref>`); Hume only in the forward sentence. State the Strauss thesis as the course's own: "the
  charge that Locke's law of nature is Hobbes's right of self-preservation
  under another name".
- **Dossier gap:** the First Treatise is not in the dossier (Second Treatise Ch.
  I Sect. 1 and Ch. VI carry what the lesson needs). The Strauss–Cropsey Locke
  chapter has landed (OCR, l.23136–24894). Filmer is staged in full (index 90).

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval, warm-up (E3 — earlier lessons).* List the one premise Locke
   changes in Hobbes's state of nature and name three conclusions that change
   with it (property before government; a limited legislative; a right to
   resist); state for each the section that carries it.
2. *Boundary probe.* A state of nature in which there is "enough and as good"
   of everything forever and nothing ever spoils: does any of Locke's three
   "inconveniences" (Ch. IX) remain, and would men still have reason to leave
   it? The reader must find that the impartial-judge defect survives abundance
   (men are still partial in their own cases), so government is not only about
   scarcity.
3. *Document reasoning (sourcing drill, E3 — `filmer-patriarcha`).* Second
   Treatise Ch. I Sect. 1 (l.139–170), Locke's four-point summary of his refutation
   of Filmer: author (an exile returned with William of Orange), date (published
   1689/90, the Treatises probably drafted in the early 1680s during the
   Exclusion Crisis — say the drafting date is disputed), audience (readers of
   the Revolution settlement, among whom Filmer's Patriarcha, published 1680, was
   the Tory text), what it records (the shape of the First Treatise's argument),
   what it argues (no living king can claim Adam's title), what it omits
   (Filmer's strongest point — that no one consents to being born into a polity —
   which Sect. 1 does not touch and Ch. VIII must), and what it can be USED to
   establish: that Locke needed a new title for government, not that he had
   found one.
4. *Plausible false claim to refute.* "Locke's labour theory shows that
   whoever works a thing owns it, so a tenant who farms land for twenty years
   owns it." Name the failing step: Locke's title is first appropriation from
   the common (Sects 27, 32) and money-mediated consent (Sect. 50), not labour
   on another's property; the servant's turfs are the master's (Sect. 28).
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* From Sects 135 and 138–140 derive why
   Locke must say that taxation requires consent of the majority or its
   representatives rather than of each taxpayer, and why this does not
   contradict "no taking of property without consent" (the incorporation of
   Sect. 96–99 does the work).
6. *Synthesis / counter-argument.* Someone claims that Locke's right of
   resistance is "a right of revolution for every discontented minority". Name
   two facts the claim fails to account for (the threshold of Sects 207–210,
   225; the principal is "the body of the people", Sect. 242) and state what
   would have to be true for the claim to survive (that a minority may judge
   for the whole — which Locke denies in Sect. 243 but which his "each man
   judges in his conscience" of Sect. 241 sits uneasily beside).
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `hume-against-the-original-contract`.** Locke says a man who
   enjoys any possession under a government, "or barely travel[s] freely on the
   highway", gives tacit consent, binding while the enjoyment lasts (Sects
   119–122). Test the claim on a person for whom leaving is not a real option:
   a labourer who speaks no other language and lives on daily wages, in a
   country whose ruler forbids emigration. Is his staying a choice, and if not,
   what is left of the consent? The reader should reach the thought that tacit
   consent presupposes a free exit the poor rarely have. The solution says in one
   sentence that the next lesson's Scottish philosopher makes exactly this
   objection with a man carried aboard a ship asleep, with the `<Ref>`.

# Research brief: machiavelli-discourses-republic (Machiavelli's Discourses: conflict, religion and the free republic)

status: planned
module: christendom-to-sovereignty
type: core
goal: Analyze Machiavelli's republican teaching — that class conflict produced Roman liberty and that religion is a civic instrument — and reconcile it with The Prince

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `machiavelli-effectual-truth`: the standard (how men actually keep power),
  virtù and fortune, the two humours of Prince IX and the open question "was Machiavelli a
  Machiavellian?" From `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`: the cycle of regimes (anacyclosis —
  kingship, tyranny, aristocracy, oligarchy, democracy, mob-rule and back to one man), Lycurgus's
  Sparta as a mixture designed by reason and Rome's as a mixture reached through trial and
  disaster (course.yaml's phrase for VI.10), and Rome's balance of consuls, senate and people, each able to block the others.
  From `decline-of-regimes`: Plato's version of the decline, fathers and sons. From
  `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`: Scipio's verdict that Rome, built by many generations,
  beats any one founder's design. From `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`: stasis as
  the disease of cities, born of rival conceptions of equality. From `augustine-two-cities`: the
  earthly city's peace is a truce of lusts; pagan virtue is splendid vice; the charge (already
  flagged there as the objection to carry) that Christianity made men weak. From
  `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: Augustine's corrupt electorate (q.97 a.1) — a people's condition
  decides what regime it can bear.
- **Contributes:** The one move: read Rome's four free centuries as the product of the quarrel
  between nobles and people that every earlier writer (Aristotle, Cicero, Augustine — the reader
  has them) called a disease, and read religion as the cement that let a quarrelling city hold
  together — then show that this is the SAME standard as The Prince applied to a different
  question (how a city stays free, not how a man holds it), so that the two books answer each
  other rather than contradict.
- **Hands off:** This lesson decides one dispute (see VERDICT): on the record of the Republic,
  the quarrel of nobles and people made Rome free while it ran through institutions, and
  destroyed it once it left them (after 133) — so the classical view of faction, and the
  royalist reading of the same centuries as an "ocean of blood" (Filmer, Patriarcha II.11–12,
  taught three lessons on), fit only the last century. What passes on is the structural
  question: Machiavelli's Rome is a mixture (senate, consuls, tribunes) kept free by tension; the
  rest of this module denies that any such thing exists — Bodin says sovereignty is indivisible
  and Rome was a popular state governed aristocratically; Hobbes says "mixed government" is
  three factions. `bodin-sovereignty` takes it, and the module verdict decides it in
  `english-revolution-as-test`. The "founder" theme (I.9, III.1)
  is inherited much later by `carlyle-heroes` and `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses` — forward clause
  only.
- **Spine step:** "Christendom builds a law above kings; Machiavelli breaks it; the wars of
  religion force one sovereign power above every faction — Bodin's definition, Hobbes's
  derivation from fear, Filmer's from fatherhood — and the English Revolution tests them"

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** Discourses I.4 — the quarrel of nobles and people was the source of Roman
  liberty — against the classical view of faction as the disease of cities (Aristotle, Politics
  V; Cicero; Augustine) and the royalist reading of the same centuries as an "ocean of blood"
  (Filmer, Patriarcha II.11–12).
- **Verdict — grade: on balance.** Conflict channelled through institutions (the tribunes, public
  accusation) produced the laws of liberty at little cost: "eight or ten" exiles and very few
  deaths from the expulsion of the Tarquins to the Gracchi (I.4). Conflict outside institutions
  destroyed the Republic, as Machiavelli himself concedes (I.37: the agrarian law, revived by
  the Gracchi, "finally overthrew the liberty of Rome"; the factions "resorted to arms and
  bloodshed" and each set up a chief). Filmer's reading fits only the century after 133.
- **What decides it:** Machiavelli's own two chapters read together — I.4 (the cost of the
  quarrel before the Gracchi, l.1041–1152) and I.37 (its cost after, l.3720–3732) — and Filmer's
  own list (Patriarcha II.11–12, filmer text l.845–864): before 133 he names only "seditions
  about usury, about marriages, and about magistracy"; every bloodshed he names (the Gracchan,
  Apuleian and Drusian seditions, the Social War, Marius and Sulla, Caesar and Pompey, the
  triumvirs) falls after 133. The two readers agree on the facts and differ on the period they
  generalise from.
- **Strongest objection:** "eight or ten" exiles is Machiavelli's premise, not a modern count;
  Livy's early books are late, patriotic and partly legendary, so the low cost of the conflict
  of the orders may be a story Rome told about itself, and the quarrel that ended in civil war
  after 133 may be the same quarrel, merely grown up. This is why the grade is on balance, not
  strong. It does not overturn the verdict, because Filmer's own list, written to show the
  bloodshed, finds none before the Gracchi, and because I.37 identifies what changed: the
  quarrel left the tribunate and the courts and went to arms.
- **What would overturn it:** evidence of large-scale bloodshed in the conflict of the orders
  before 133.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): the same man wrote three books on how Rome stayed free for four
hundred years and found the cause in quarrels every other writer called a disease. Anchor (L11):
the Discourses on the First Ten Books of Livy (written c. 1513–19, printed 1531), a commentary
on the Roman historian Livy (d. AD 17) chapter by chapter, dedicated not to a prince but to two
young Florentine republicans; "free for four hundred years" is from the expulsion of the kings
(traditionally 509 BC) to the civil wars of the first century BC. The newcomer's problem: the
reader has just learned the prince's rules; why does the same standard, applied to a republic,
produce a theory of freedom?

1. **How does a city get a good constitution?** Obvious attempt: a wise founder gives it one
   (Lycurgus for Sparta — the reader has Sparta, Ref `sparta-as-test-case`). Why it fails for
   Rome: Rome had no Lycurgus; its kings were expelled and it had only consuls and a senate —
   two of the three elements. Answer (I.2): the six forms slide each into its contrary — the
   cycle the reader met with Polybius (Ref `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`, one clause; I.2
   follows Histories VI almost line for line, unnamed) — so a wise legislator mixes all three;
   Rome reached the mixture "by chance", because the quarrels of nobles and people forced the
   creation of the tribunes (I.3) — a mixture made by conflict, not design. Say the seam: from
   Polybius one expects the mixture to be the cure for discord; Machiavelli makes discord the
   cause of the mixture. Anchor the Roman
   offices at first mention: consuls (two annual chief magistrates), senate (council of former
   magistrates), tribunes of the people (officers created c. 494 BC with power to veto and to
   protect plebeians).
2. **Isn't conflict the disease that kills cities?** Obvious attempt: Aristotle's (Ref
   `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`) — stasis is what a lawgiver prevents. Machiavelli's
   reversal (I.4), argued: (a) "those who condemn the quarrels between the nobles and the
   commons … do not perceive that in every republic there are two humours" and that "all
   legislation favourable to liberty is brought about by the clash between them"; (b) the
   empirical premise — in three hundred years of tumult Rome banished "no more than eight or ten
   of her citizens" and put very few to death; (c) the mechanism — a people that demonstrates in
   the streets, shuts its shops and secedes is venting an ambition that otherwise explodes; the
   tribunes are the outlet. Then I.5: who should guard liberty — the people, who desire not to
   be dominated, rather than the nobles, who desire to dominate; I.6: the trade-off — a quiet
   Venice or Sparta that arms few and cannot expand, or a tumultuous Rome that arms all and
   conquers; "no inconvenience can be removed without another appearing" — there is no stable
   mean, only a choice. I.7–8: legal accusation (the tribunes' power to impeach — Coriolanus)
   lets the humours discharge in court; calumny (accusation without proof — Valori, Soderini in
   Florence) poisons them.
3. **What held a quarrelling city together?** Obvious attempt: its laws. Fails: laws need men
   who fear to break them. Answer (I.11–15): religion — Numa, Rome's second king, pretended
   converse with a nymph to give his laws divine authority; "the balance must turn in favour of
   Numa" over Romulus; Romans "feared more to break an oath than the laws"; Scipio's sword-point
   oath after Cannae; rulers should support religion "even if they judge it false" (I.12); the
   auspices were bent to the general's need (Papirius) but open contempt for them was punished
   (Appius Pulcher throwing the sacred chickens into the sea). Then the hinge: II.2 — states
   grow only under free government because the common good is pursued, and "our religion" has
   taught men to bear injuries rather than avenge them, placing the highest good "in humility,
   lowliness, and contempt for the things of this world", so that the world has become "a prey
   to the wicked"; the Church, by its temporal ambitions, has kept Italy divided (I.12 — move
   the papal court to Switzerland and watch the Swiss corrupt). Here the OBJECTION at full
   strength (Ref `augustine-two-cities`): Augustine would say Machiavelli has described the
   earthly city exactly — a peace that is a truce of lusts, virtue that serves glory — and
   praised it; the "weakness" Machiavelli deplores is the pilgrim's refusal to make Rome's glory
   his god. Machiavelli's own hedge is the reply the lesson records: he blames those who have
   "interpreted our religion to accord with indolence" (l.5962), not the religion — and in III.1
   he praises Francis and Dominic for renewing it. The lesson concedes the hedge is not an
   argument and says so.
4. **Why do free cities die, and can they be saved?** Obvious attempt: a bad ruler. Fails: a
   people long under a prince, suddenly freed, is a caged animal that cannot feed itself (I.16);
   where "gentlemen" (men living idle on estates and castles) exist, no republic is possible,
   and where equality prevails, no princedom (I.55 — Naples/Rome vs Germany's tax-by-oath into a
   chest); good customs need good laws and laws need good customs, so institutions good at first
   become ruinous in corruption (I.18 — open candidacy). Answer (III.1): all mixed bodies decay;
   survival requires a RETURN TO BEGINNINGS, by an outside blow (the Gauls' sack of Rome, 390
   BC) or by inner foresight — exemplary punishment "every ten years" at most (the sons of
   Brutus, Manlius Capitolinus); the Church survived by Francis and Dominic doing the same.
   Hence "slay the sons of Brutus" (III.3) — a new freedom must kill its enemies — and Soderini
   (Machiavelli's own chief, 1502–12), who "from not knowing how to resemble Brutus, lost power
   and fame". The dictatorship (I.34) as the legal emergency power that saved Rome because it
   was conferred, limited to six months and could not change the laws; the Decemvirate (I.35,
   40) as the usurped version that nearly destroyed it.
5. **Who judges better, a people or a prince?** Obvious attempt (every earlier author, Ref): the
   many are fickle. Answer (I.58): the charge rests on Livy's "the nature of the multitude is to
   serve humbly or rule insolently" — but a people bound by laws is wiser and more constant than
   a prince; "the prince who can do what he pleases is a madman, and the people which can do as
   it pleases is never wise"; a people errs in generalities and judges truly in particulars
   (I.47); it is deceived by a false show of advantage (I.53) but recovers; a republic outlasts
   a prince because its diversity of citizens lets it change with the times where a man cannot
   (III.9 — Fabius then Scipio).
6. **Reconcile the two books.** The question the previous lesson left open is answered as the
   course's own: the SAME standard — the effectual truth — asked of a different thing. The
   Prince asks how one man holds a state he has newly taken; the Discourses ask how a people
   keeps a state free for centuries; both assume all men bad (I.3), both make the people the
   safer foundation (Prince IX; Discourses I.5, I.58), both rank founders and reformers above
   all (Prince VI; Discourses I.9–10), and the Discourses say when a prince is needed — to found
   (I.9: "to give new institutions … there must be one man") and to re-found a corrupt city,
   where "a free government cannot be maintained" and a quasi-monarchical hand is required
   (I.18, I.55). The reading on which The Prince warns a people against princes (the republican
   reading of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries — References) is stated and weighed: it
   fits the humours and I.58, it does not fit the Dedication.
7. **THE WEIGHING — "Conflict inside institutions made Rome free; conflict outside them
   destroyed it".** Set I.4 against its strongest objection and the evidence and reach the
   verdict (see VERDICT; grade **on balance**). The two rival readings of the same centuries at
   full strength: the classical one (faction is the disease — Ref Aristotle, one clause) and
   the royalist one, quoted in two sentences from a later reader (Filmer, Patriarcha II.11–12:
   Rome's tumults "shed an ocean of blood within Italy and the streets of Rome"; forward `<Ref>`).
   Then the evidence, dated: before 133 BC — the secessions, the tribunes, the laws on debt,
   intermarriage and office, and by Machiavelli's count "eight or ten" exiles (I.4); after 133 —
   Tiberius Gracchus killed by senators, the agrarian law fought with arms, Marius and Sulla, and
   Machiavelli's own admission (I.37) that the revived quarrel "finally overthrew the liberty of
   Rome" once the factions went "regardless of all civil restraints" to arms and each set up a
   chief. Filmer's own list confirms the split: before the Gracchi he names seditions, not
   blood. Then the objection: the count is Machiavelli's, from Livy, who is late and patriotic —
   which is why the grade is on balance; and it does not overturn the verdict, because the
   hostile witness finds no bloodshed before 133 either. The section states the result as a
   claim about the argument: Machiavelli answered the classical objection for conflict that
   runs through institutions, and conceded it for conflict that leaves them; Filmer's reading
   is right about the last century and wrong about the first three. What would overturn it:
   evidence of large-scale bloodshed in the conflict of the orders before 133.

`<Conclusion>`: states the verdict in plain words with its grade — on balance, Machiavelli was
right that the quarrel of nobles and people made Rome free while the tribunes and the courts
carried it, and his own I.37 shows it destroyed Rome once it left them; the "ocean of blood"
belongs to the century after 133. Then the hand-off: the Discourses praise Rome's mixture; the
next lesson's author will say there is no such thing — Rome was a popular state and the quarrel
of nobles and people was not a mixture but a question of who made law, answered by the people
(`bodin-sovereignty`).

Headings as claims: "Rome's constitution was made by quarrel, not by a lawgiver"; "Tumults
produced the laws of liberty: eight exiles in three hundred years"; "Numa did more than Romulus:
religion as the fear that holds laws up"; "Christianity made the world weak — and Machiavelli
blames the interpreters"; "Corruption, gentlemen and the return to beginnings"; "A people is
wiser and more constant than a prince"; "One standard, two books"; "Conflict inside
institutions made Rome free; conflict outside them destroyed it".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **I.4 reconstructed as an argument** (l.1041–1152): the thesis, the two humours, the
   empirical premise (eight or ten exiles), the mechanism (venting), the institution (tribunes);
   then I.6's trade-off table (Venice/Sparta vs Rome) so the reader sees that Machiavelli does
   not claim tumult is costless — he claims the alternative costs more if you want to expand.
2. **The caged animal** (I.16 l.2270): a people used to a prince freed "like a wild animal
   which, though destined by nature to roam at large … has been reared in a cage"; it is caught
   by the first comer. The passage answers "why not simply free a people".
3. **Numa and the nymph; Scipio's oath; the sacred chickens** (I.11–14): three short scenes —
   Numa's pretence, Scipio forcing the young nobles to swear not to abandon Italy after Cannae
   (216 BC), Appius Pulcher drowning the chickens (249 BC) and losing his fleet — read for the
   single rule: use religion, never show contempt for it.
4. **The Christianity passage** (II.2 l.5931–5966) quoted in two or three sentences and parsed
   clause by clause: what the old religion honoured (captains of armies, rulers of cities), what
   ours honours (the humble and contemplative), the consequence (the world a prey to the
   wicked), the hedge (interpretation). Set against Augustine XIX.25 in one sentence each.
5. **The Curia in Switzerland** (I.12): the thought experiment — carry the Roman court to the
   Swiss, "the only people who now live as the ancients did" in religion and arms, and see their
   customs corrupted in a short time. Shows the method of the counterfactual case.
6. **Return to beginnings** (III.1 l.9347–9530): the Gauls' sack as a blow that re-made Rome;
   the list of exemplary punishments; "every ten years"; Francis and Dominic. Then III.3 "slay
   the sons of Brutus" and Soderini — Machiavelli judging his own chief.
7. **"The prince who can do what he pleases is a madman"** (I.58 l.5387): the sentence and the
   argument around it — the comparison is between a people AND a prince both bound by laws, or
   both unbound; the Livy proof-text answered.
8. **Germany's tax by oath** (I.55): citizens assess themselves and drop the coin in a chest
   unseen — the picture of an uncorrupted people, and the definition of "gentlemen" that
   follows.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms and names to anchor (L11):** Livy (Roman historian, d. AD 17; his first ten books
  cover Rome to 293 BC); tribunes of the people; consuls; senate; the Decemvirate (ten men given
  full power to write laws, 451–449 BC, who tried to keep it); dictator (a Roman emergency
  magistrate, six months, named by the consuls); Numa (second king of Rome, traditionally
  715–673 BC); Cannae (216 BC, Hannibal's destruction of a Roman army); auspices (omens read
  from birds before public acts); Coriolanus; Manlius Capitolinus; the Gauls' sack (390 BC);
  Soderini (Piero Soderini, head of Florence's republic 1502–12); the Corcyra massacre (427 BC,
  from Thucydides); "humours" (Machiavelli's medical word for the two dispositions); "gentlemen"
  (in Machiavelli's sense only: idle landholders with castles); Francis and Dominic (founders of
  the two great thirteenth-century orders — the reader met a Dominican in Aquinas). Keep to
  three per section.
- **Do not re-teach** the regime cycle or Rome's balance of parts (Ref `decline-of-regimes`,
  `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`), Aristotle's stasis, Augustine's two cities, or The Prince's
  rules — one clause each.
- **The real misreading (L13):** "Machiavelli was a democrat / the Discourses are a democratic
  text." Refute from I.55 (a corrupt people needs a prince), III.3 (kill the enemies of the new
  freedom), I.5–6 (the choice is between expansion and quiet, not for or against the people),
  and the Dedication of The Prince. State it once; no second strawman.
- **Numbers:** "eight or ten" exiles in "three hundred years" is Machiavelli's own claim —
  present it as his premise, bound as he binds it, and say in a Remark that it is an argument's
  premise, not a modern count. Sparta's "800 years" (I.2) likewise. Rome's "280,000 vs 20,000"
  (II.3) only if the expansion trade-off needs it; otherwise cut.
- **Thomson's 1883 translation** is Victorian and occasionally archaic ("commons" for plebs,
  "princedom"); quote it as it stands, gloss once.
- **The II.2 hedge** must be reported fairly: Machiavelli blames the interpretation, praises
  renewal (III.1), and uses religion instrumentally (I.12) — the lesson states this as a tension
  in the text, not as a secret atheism to be unmasked.
- **Register:** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Dossier thin:** no secondary beyond Smith L11, Strauss's pages and the Strauss–Cropsey
  chapter (OCR); the Florentine Histories, where Machiavelli condemns Florence's factions as the bad
  kind, are NOT in the dossier — the writer must not cite them, and should say in References
  that the Florence/Rome contrast (I.7–8) is the dossier's only evidence that not all conflict
  is good.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (the only mechanical one):** three Roman institutions — the tribunes, the
   dictatorship, the censors' auspices — each placed in Machiavelli's account: what humour or
   need created it, which chapter, and whether it is an outlet, an emergency power, or an
   instrument of belief.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** the II.2 Christianity passage. Author (a dismissed
   Florentine official writing c. 1517 under a Medici pope), audience (two young republicans),
   what it records (nothing — it is argument), argues, omits (that Christian Europe had just
   produced the armies that overran Italy); what it can be USED to establish about
   sixteenth-century views of religion's civic function, and what it cannot (the author's
   private belief).
3. **Corroboration (E3 from `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life` and
   `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`):** Aristotle's Politics V on faction and Discourses I.4
   describe the same kind of event — the poor against the rich — and reach opposite verdicts.
   Lay the two passages side by side, state what each takes the event to be evidence of, and
   name the premise that differs (whether a city's end is quiet or growth). Then add Polybius
   VI.10 (l.344–352: Rome reached its mixture "through many struggles and difficulties, and by
   continually adopting reforms from knowledge gained in disaster") and say which of the two Machiavelli has rewritten, and in which
   clause.
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** a city with zero conflict — Machiavelli's own Venice or Sparta
   (I.6). State what his theory predicts for it (it cannot expand; it must disarm or exclude its
   people) and what he says happened when it was struck (Venice after Agnadello/Vailà 1509,
   III.31; Sparta after Leuctra). The solution shows the theory does not say "tumult good, quiet
   bad" but "each has its price, and the price of quiet is paid at the first defeat".
5. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "The Discourses show Machiavelli was a democrat." Name the steps
   that fail (I.55, III.3, I.18's quasi-monarchical remedy) and state what the text does hold
   (the people as the safer guardian of liberty, under laws).
6. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** apply "return to beginnings" (III.1) to the one
   institution Machiavelli applies it to that is not a state — the Church renewed by Francis and
   Dominic — and derive what this implies for his verdict on Christianity in II.2 (that the
   religion is renewable to its "beginnings", and that those beginnings are poverty and example,
   not arms). Then state the tension with I.12.
7. **Research-shaped (E6d):** Machiavelli's claim that Rome's tumults cost "no more than eight
   or ten" exiles in three hundred years. Using only Livy's episodes the lesson has named
   (Coriolanus, the sons of Brutus, Manlius Capitolinus, the Decemvirate), design the test that
   would check the claim, say what would count as an exile versus an execution, and state why
   the claim's work in the argument survives even if the count is low.
8. **Attack the verdict.** The lesson concludes, on balance, that the quarrel of nobles and
   people made Rome free while it ran through institutions and destroyed it only after 133.
   State the strongest objection in its best form — the low cost before 133 rests on Livy, a
   late and patriotic source, and the quarrel after 133 was the same quarrel, so the "two
   periods" are one disease seen early and late — and say what evidence would be needed to
   overturn the verdict: large-scale bloodshed in the conflict of the orders before 133
   (killings in the secessions, mass exiles, battles between citizens), from a source
   independent of Livy. The solution names what the record has (the stories of Spurius Cassius
   and Manlius Capitolinus — single executions) and why that is not "an ocean".
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `bodin-sovereignty`:** take Rome's constitution exactly as Machiavelli
   describes it — the people confirm or annul laws, the senate counsels, the consuls execute,
   the tribunes veto — and answer one question: who, in the end, MAKES law for all? Then say
   whether "a mixture of three" is still the right description once that question is answered.
   The solution says in one sentence that the next lesson's author asks exactly this and
   reclassifies Rome as a popular state governed aristocratically, with a `<Ref>`.

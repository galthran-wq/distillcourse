# Research brief: machiavelli-effectual-truth (Machiavelli's Prince: the effectual truth of things)

status: planned
module: christendom-to-sovereignty
type: core
goal: Analyze Machiavelli's argument that a prince must learn how not to be good, and the theory of virtù and fortune that supports it

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: a thousand years of advice to rulers in one
  form — the "mirror for princes" that tells a king to rule justly, and Aquinas's argument that
  tyranny "does not pay" because fear is a weak support and friendship a strong one (De Regno
  I.11). From `augustine-two-cities`: pagan virtue as splendid vice because it serves glory, and
  the earthly city driven by libido dominandi. From `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`: justice as the
  interest of the stronger and the unjust man who seems just. From
  `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`: Carneades's "the wise man who does not seize an advantage
  is a fool", and Rome's empire as the great case of taking what was not its own. From
  `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`: the one who has seen must be compelled to rule.
- **Contributes:** The one move: Machiavelli replaces "how a prince ought to live" with "how men
  actually keep power" (Prince XV) and from that standard derives rules that invert the
  mirror-for-princes — armed prophets, cruelty well used, feared rather than loved, the fox and
  the lion — backed by cases from the Italian wars he watched. The lesson reconstructs the rules
  WITH their examples and puts to them the strongest objection the reader already owns:
  Aquinas's "tyranny does not pay" and Augustine's "splendid vices" read backward.
- **Hands off:** The dispute with Aquinas is settled inside this lesson as a fact about the
  argument: it largely dissolves into Machiavelli's own rule (never be hated), which concedes
  Aquinas's mechanism and disputes only whether fear can be had without hatred. What passes on:
  if the prince must rest on the people (IX) and the people's end is "more decent" (they want
  only not to be oppressed), what regime does Machiavelli's own analysis favour — and was
  Machiavelli a Machiavellian? `machiavelli-discourses-republic` takes it and answers it (one
  standard, two books), and judges his republican thesis on conflict and liberty. The standard itself (effectual truth) is reused there and attacked in `bodin-sovereignty`
  (treaties, fear vs affection) — forward hooks, one clause each.
- **Spine step:** "Christendom builds a law above kings; Machiavelli breaks it; the wars of
  religion force one sovereign power above every faction — Bodin's definition, Hobbes's
  derivation from fear, Filmer's from fatherhood — and the English Revolution tests them"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): a former diplomat out of office writes a short book for the
family that has just taken his city. Anchor (L11): Niccolò Machiavelli (1469–1527), secretary to
Florence's republic from 1498 until the Medici returned in 1512, arrested and tortured in 1513,
retired to his farm at San Casciano; the book is The Prince (written 1513, printed 1532),
dedicated to Lorenzo de' Medici; the "Italian wars" are the French and Spanish invasions from
1494 that made and unmade princes every few years. The newcomer's problem: every book a prince
was handed (Aquinas's De Regno among them, Ref) told him to be just, generous, merciful and
faithful; Machiavelli says a prince who follows that advice "among so many who are not good" is
ruined (XV). Either the advice was wrong or the standard was.

1. **What is different about a NEW prince?** Obvious attempt: ruling is ruling. Fails: a
   hereditary prince "need only not break ancestral custom" (II); a new one faces men who
   changed rulers hoping to improve and must injure the very people who helped him (III).
   Answer: the whole book is about new princes (I's grid, then II's dismissal of the hereditary
   case); the remedies — reside, plant colonies, weaken the strong, never make another powerful
   — are read off Louis XII's five errors in Italy (III) and the Turk/France contrast (IV: a
   state of one master and servants is hard to enter and easy to hold; a state of king and
   barons the reverse). The physician and the hectic fever (III): evils foreseen are easily
   cured; "war is not to be avoided, but is only put off to the advantage of others".
2. **Can a founder succeed by his own excellence?** Obvious attempt: yes, the greatest men
   (Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, Theseus) founded by virtue. Machiavelli's correction (VI): fortune
   gave them only OPPORTUNITY — a people enslaved, scattered, exposed — and "without that
   opportunity their powers of mind would have been extinguished". Then the innovator's problem:
   enemies in all who profited from the old order, lukewarm defenders who fear the enemies and
   do not believe in the new until they have seen it. Hence "all armed prophets have conquered,
   and the unarmed ones have been destroyed" — Savonarola (the Dominican who ruled Florence by
   preaching 1494–98 and was burned when the people stopped believing) as the unarmed case. Seam
   with `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`: Plato's founder must be compelled to rule and
   persuades with a noble lie; Machiavelli's founder compels belief with arms. Define virtù
   here, from the cases: energy, foresight and daring in the use of the occasion — not moral
   virtue; the archer who aims above the mark (VI l.1048–1053).
3. **What is "cruelty well used"?** Obvious attempt: cruelty is simply vice. The cases
   (VII–VIII): Cesare Borgia (son of Pope Alexander VI, carving a state in the Romagna 1499–1503
   on his father's arms) sends Ramiro d'Orco to pacify the province by terror, then has him cut
   in two in the piazza at Cesena with a block and a bloody knife, leaving the people "satisfied
   and dismayed" — the cruelty was his instrument, the blame displaced onto the instrument
   (l.1284–1304). Agathocles of Syracuse (crimes win empire, not glory) and Oliverotto of Fermo
   (massacres his uncle and the town's leading men at a banquet, is strangled a year later at
   Sinigaglia by Borgia). The rule (VIII l.1543–1545): cruelties "well used" are done at one
   blow for security and converted to the subjects' benefit; cruelties that grow with time
   cannot be forgiven. "Injuries ought to be done all at one time … benefits ought to be given
   little by little." Borgia's one error: letting Julius II be elected — "whoever believes that
   new benefits will cause great personages to forget old injuries is deceived".
4. **Why must a prince learn how not to be good?** The standard (XV): "it appears to me more
   appropriate to follow up the real truth of the matter than the imagination of it"; many have
   imagined republics and principalities never seen; "how one lives is so far distant from how
   one ought to live" that he who abandons what is done for what ought to be done "learns his
   ruin rather than his preservation" — so a prince "must learn how not to be good, and to use
   this knowledge and not use it, according to the necessity of the case". Then the ledger of
   paired qualities and the rule for which to risk: liberality seen is ruin, so accept the name
   of meanness (XVI); cruelty well used is "more merciful" than clemency that lets disorder
   breed murders (XVII — Borgia vs the Florentines who let Pistoia be destroyed); "safer to be
   feared than loved, when of the two either must be dispensed with" BECAUSE men are ungrateful,
   fickle, false — "love is preserved by the link of obligation which, owing to the baseness of
   men, is broken at every opportunity; but fear preserves you by a dread of punishment which
   never fails" — yet never hated: keep hands off property and women; Hannibal's "inhuman
   cruelty" held a mixed army together where Scipio's lenity let his legions mutiny. XVIII: law
   is for men, force for beasts, and a prince must know both; among beasts "the fox and the
   lion", since the lion cannot escape snares and the fox cannot fight wolves; faith need not be
   kept "when the reasons that caused him to pledge it exist no longer" — but one must be "a
   great pretender and dissembler", since "men are so simple"; Alexander VI "never did what he
   said, Cesare never said what he did"; "men judge more by the eye than by the hand"; appear
   merciful, faithful, humane, religious — "one judges by the result".
5. **The OBJECTION at full strength, from the two lessons before.** Augustine (Ref
   `augustine-two-cities`, XIX.25): a virtue that serves glory is a vice in splendid dress — and
   Machiavelli has not denied it; he has praised it. Aquinas (Ref `aquinas-law-and-kingship`, De
   Regno I.11–12): tyranny does not pay — fear is water under pressure that bursts out with
   greater force; the tyrant forfeits friendship, the one worldly good "nothing seems worthy to
   be preferred to"; the tyrant's rule is short. The seam says so: from Aquinas one expects
   Machiavelli's prince to fall; Machiavelli's reply is that Aquinas has mis-described the
   alternative — the choice is not fear vs friendship but fear-without-hatred vs a love men
   break "at every opportunity" (XVII), and the remedy for hatred is not virtue but keeping off
   property and women and resting on the people (XIX–XX: "the best possible fortress is not to
   be hated"). So the disagreement narrows to an empirical claim: whether fear without hatred is
   a stable state — and on the mechanism it largely dissolves: Machiavelli's own rule ("not to
   be hated") grants Aquinas that a hated ruler falls, and the two differ only on whether fear
   can be kept apart from hatred. The lesson says so in the course's voice: Machiavelli answers
   Aquinas's prudential objection with his own rule, in Aquinas's terms; he does not answer
   Augustine's — he accepts the description (virtue serving glory) and changes the standard by
   which it would count as a charge. And he concedes what Augustine would press: Agathocles got
   empire "but not glory" (VIII) — the ledger has a column he does not fill.
6. **People or nobles?** IX: every city has two humours — the people desire not to be commanded
   or oppressed, the great desire to command and oppress; a prince raised by the nobles has
   equals who cannot be satisfied, one raised by the people has subjects who ask only not to be
   oppressed — so build on the people, and "he who builds on the people builds on the mud" holds
   only for a private citizen who expects the people to rescue him (l.1638–1649). XIX's Roman
   emperors (Marcus to Maximinus) as the natural experiment: those who satisfied soldiers
   against the people fell with the soldiers. This is where "was Machiavelli a Machiavellian?"
   is asked as the course's own question — the people's end is "more decent"; the prince needs
   them; so what regime does the analysis favour? The next lesson answers it from the
   Discourses (one standard applied to two questions); hand off to it.
7. **What of fortune?** XXV: fortune arbiter of half our actions; a river in flood that men can
   dyke in quiet times; success is the FIT between a man's method and the times (Julius II's
   impetuosity suited his times; a cautious man would have failed), and since men cannot change
   their natures, the prince who prospers is the one whose times do not change; better impetuous
   than cautious, "because fortune is a woman". XXIV: the Italian princes lost their states by
   bad arms, a hostile people, and not securing the nobles — "only those defences are good …
   which depend on yourself and your own valour". XXVI closes with the call to free Italy and
   Petrarch's "Virtù contro al Furore". The lesson's conclusion names what the book has done: it
   has kept the vocabulary of virtue and changed its referent, and it has asked of every rule
   not "is it just" but "does it hold" — the standard Bodin and Hobbes will keep while rejecting
   most of the rules.

Headings as claims: "A new prince cannot keep what he took by being good"; "Opportunity plus
virtù: why armed prophets conquer"; "Cruelty well used is done once"; "The effectual truth:
learn how not to be good"; "Feared, not hated: the reply to 'tyranny does not pay'"; "Build on
the people, not the great"; "Fortune is a river: dykes, timing and the fit to the times".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The Vettori letter (13 Dec 1513; Intro l.271–298):** the day's work on the farm, the
   tavern, then at evening "I enter the ancient courts of ancient men … I am not ashamed to
   speak with them" — four hours "and I feel no boredom, I forget every trouble"; and the whale
   that overturns the ship unless given a cask (l.339–341). Anchors the man and the method
   (history as a quarry of cases).
2. **The landscape painter (Dedication l.526–533):** to know the people one must be a prince, to
   know princes one must be of the people — the standpoint the whole book claims.
3. **Louis XII's five errors (III):** destroyed the weak, strengthened a strong power, brought
   in a foreigner, did not reside, did not plant colonies — worked as a checklist against the
   rules the chapter states; close with the Rouen exchange ("the Italians did not understand
   war; the French did not understand statecraft", l.878–883).
4. **Armed and unarmed prophets (VI l.1098–1130):** the innovator's coalition problem, then the
   sentence, then Savonarola — give his dates and what happened in the piazza in 1498.
5. **Ramiro d'Orco at Cesena (VII l.1284–1304):** told step by step — the province full of
   robbery, a cruel man given full power, the province pacified and hating him, the public court
   set up, the body in two pieces with block and knife, "satisfied and dismayed". Then the
   generalisation of VIII (injuries at once, benefits little by little) as the rule the case
   instantiates.
6. **Chapter XV quoted and parsed (l.2274–2285):** three sentences — the real truth vs the
   imagination; how one lives vs how one ought; "learn how not to be good" — each read for what
   it denies (the mirror-for-princes genre, Ref Aquinas; Plato's city in speech, Ref).
7. **Loved or feared (XVII l.2439–2456):** the catalogue of baseness quoted in one sentence; the
   obligation-vs-dread contrast; the two exceptions (property, women); Hannibal vs Scipio.
   Paired against De Regno I.11 [77–81] as the objection.
8. **The river and the dikes (XXV l.3461–3550):** the flood image; the "fit" argument with
   Julius II at Bologna (1506); "fortune is a woman" reported as what the text says, not
   endorsed or apologised for.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms and names to anchor (L11):** Medici (the banking family ruling Florence 1434–94 and
  again from 1512); Savonarola (Dominican preacher, ruled Florence 1494–98, burned 1498); Cesare
  Borgia (1475–1507, son of Pope Alexander VI, duke of Valentinois — "Valentino"); the Romagna
  (the papal province east of Florence he conquered 1499–1502); Cesena, Sinigaglia (the ambush
  of Dec 1502 where he strangled his captains); Agathocles (tyrant of Syracuse, d. 289 BC);
  Oliverotto of Fermo; Louis XII of France (invaded Italy 1499); Julius II (pope 1503–13, the
  "warrior pope"); Ferdinand of Aragon; Hannibal and Scipio (the Second Punic War, 218–201 BC);
  the Turk (the Ottoman sultan — one master, all others his servants); mercenaries / auxiliaries
  (hired captains vs a foreign prince's lent troops); condottieri; the Italian wars (1494
  onward); virtù (NOT "virtue" — leave it in Italian after defining it from the cases); fortuna;
  "principality" (a state ruled by one; Marriott's "princedom"). At most three new names per
  section (L11c).
- **Do not re-teach** Thrasymachus, Carneades, Augustine's splendid vices or Aquinas's tyranny
  chapters — Ref each with one clause. The objection in link 5 is a RECALL of De Regno I.11,
  which the Aquinas lesson set up as its set piece 8.
- **The real misreading (L13):** "Machiavelli says the end justifies the means." The sentence
  does not occur in The Prince; Discourses I.9 says the END EXCUSES the deed — of a FOUNDER who
  then does not keep the power — and Prince XVIII says "one judges by the result". Refute by
  showing what the slogan omits: VIII's distinction between empire and glory, the "well used"
  test, and XIX's claim that hatred ruins. Do not add a second strawman.
- **Marriott's translation** is Edwardian and occasionally softens; "verità effettuale" is
  rendered "the real truth of the matter" (l.2276) — the lesson may give the Italian once and
  use "effectual truth" thereafter (the concept id). Marriott's Introduction is "mildly
  apologetic" (summary): quote it only for the Vettori letter and biography.
- **Seductive details to CUT** (L12a): the Testina-edition censorship footnote (l.2592–2606),
  the Frederick the Great "King Chance" footnote, the arrest-and-torture footnote beyond one
  clause, the Third Man film clip from the lectures. Keep the Italian-wars chronology to the
  decade and to the four or five names above.
- **Register:** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right. The body states the
  arguments; the charge that the book teaches evil openly appears only as a charge, attributed
  (Strauss, 1958, References), never as the course's verdict.
- **Fortune as a woman (XXV):** report the sentence and its argumentative work (impetuosity
  preferred); do not editorialise, do not omit.
- **Dossier thin:** no Machiavelli secondary beyond Smith's two lectures, Strauss's pages in
  Natural Right and History and his Strauss–Cropsey chapter (OCR). The Florentine Histories and
  the Art of War are not in the dossier — do not cite them.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (the only mechanical one):** classify three cases on the Chapter I grid
   and name the rule of III–IV that applies — Louis XII in Milan (1499), Cesare Borgia in the
   Romagna, the Ottoman sultan in a conquered province.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** the Dedication to Lorenzo de' Medici. Author (a
   dismissed republican secretary), date (1513, after arrest), audience (the family that
   dismissed him), purpose; what it records, argues (the landscape-painter claim to knowledge),
   omits (that the author served the republic the Medici overthrew); what it can be USED to
   establish about The Prince's intent and what it cannot settle (sincere counsel, job
   application, or warning — the reading the next lesson's question turns on).
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** take "feared but not hated" to its limit — a prince feared by
   everyone and hated by everyone (Agathocles; Oliverotto). Using VIII and XIX, state exactly
   when "cruelty well used" becomes cruelty badly used, and what Machiavelli's own cases say
   happened to each (Agathocles died in power without glory; Oliverotto was strangled within a
   year). The solution shows the distinction is about timing and conversion to benefit, not
   quantity.
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Machiavelli says the end justifies the means." Locate what the
   text actually says (Discourses I.9 on founders; Prince XVIII "one judges by the result"), and
   name the two steps the slogan drops (glory vs mere empire, VIII; hatred as ruin, XIX).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** run Aquinas's De Regno I.11 argument (the tyrant
   forfeits friendship, fear is water under pressure) against Prince XVII–XX and state the
   single empirical proposition on which the two disagree (whether fear-without-hatred is
   stable). Then say which of Machiavelli's own cases would count as evidence for Aquinas
   (Borgia's fall in 1503 within months of his father's death, VII l.1361–1365).
6. **Synthesis / counter-argument (E3 from `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`):** "Machiavelli's prince
   is Thrasymachus's ruler: justice is what serves him." Name two facts the claim fails to
   account for (the prince must satisfy the people, IX; glory is withheld from the criminal,
   VIII) and what would have to be true for it to survive.
7. **Research-shaped (E6d):** Machiavelli's "general rule which never or rarely fails" (III):
   whoever makes another powerful is ruined. Test it against the two cases he gives (Louis XII
   and the Church/Spain) and one he does not (Borgia and Julius II, VII): design the comparison
   that would distinguish "never fails" from "fails whenever the beneficiary outlives his
   benefactor".
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `machiavelli-discourses-republic`:** from Chapter IX alone — the two
   humours, the people's end "more decent", the prince who must rest on them — derive what
   regime Machiavelli's analysis favours when the question is not how to hold a city but how to
   keep it free for centuries. State the regime and the mechanism that would have to keep the
   great from oppressing the people. The solution says in one sentence that this is the
   Discourses' tribunate and its thesis that conflict guards liberty, with a `<Ref>`.

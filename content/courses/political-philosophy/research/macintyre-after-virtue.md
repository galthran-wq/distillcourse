# Research brief: macintyre-after-virtue (MacIntyre's After Virtue: the failure of the Enlightenment project)

status: planned
module: equality-after-rawls
type: core
goal: Analyze MacIntyre's argument that modern moral language is the fragment of a lost tradition, that the Enlightenment's attempt to justify morality had to fail, and that the choice is Nietzsche or Aristotle

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From the module: Rawls's theory, built from a hypothetical choice by parties who know nothing of their own good (`rawls-theory-of-justice`); the envy debate, which ended with no agreed way to tell a motive from a principle (`ressentiment-and-envy`); Scruton's reply that the liberal standpoint is one no actual self can occupy, and his account of tradition as unspoken social knowledge guarded by silence about its myths (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`). From the postwar module: Strauss's thesis that the fact–value distinction and historicism destroy natural right, and that the modern break replaced the natural end with fear of death and rights before duties (`strauss-natural-right-and-history` — concepts `fact-value-distinction`, `classic-natural-right`); Burnham's managers as a new ruling class (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`); the Declaration of 1948, which lists rights and gives no ground for them (`udhr-read-against-the-right`). From the Greek module: Aristotle's nature as end — the acorn and the oak, man as political animal (`aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`), the best life as virtuous activity and the city as existing for it (`aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`, concept `common-good`); Thrasymachus (`thrasymachus-and-the-ring`). From the contract module: Hume's allegiance grounded in the general interest, not in promise (`hume-against-the-original-contract`); Burke's tradition and prejudice (`burke-reflections`). Nietzsche from the Nietzsche course by `<Ref>`.
- **Contributes:** One move: the interminable disagreement the reader has watched all module — Rawls against Nozick, the egalitarian against the envy charge, the liberal against Scruton — is not a normal feature of moral life but the symptom of a history. Modern moral words are fragments of a scheme that made sense only with a conception of the human telos; the Enlightenment tried to keep the rules and drop the telos, and had to fail; so the honest choices are Nietzsche (morality is will in disguise) or Aristotle (recover the virtues in practices, the narrative unity of a life and a living tradition). MacIntyre argues this from the left and rejects conservatism as liberalism's mirror image, which makes him a critic of liberalism from outside the right as well as an ally of parts of it.
- **Hands off:** (1) MacIntyre's manager — a character whose authority rests on an expertise he says does not exist — and his verdict that every modern political tradition is exhausted lead into `lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`, where an American historian of the left says the danger now comes from the credentialed elites themselves. (2) The Benedict passage (local communities to carry civility through "the new dark ages") is the backward Ref for `deneen-post-liberalism`'s Benedict Option; the classical legal tradition for `vermeule-common-good-constitutionalism`. (3) Whether a tradition can rationally defeat its rivals without neutral standards (the relativism objection) stays open: MacIntyre's own answer is in the 1984 Postscript and is given here only in outline.
- **Spine step:** "Rawls makes equality the measure of justice; the right answers with the charge of ressentiment and envy, Scruton's authority, MacIntyre's lost tradition and Lasch's revolt of the elites — and the data on cognitive stratification test both sides"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): two people argue about abortion, war or taxation; each argument valid, each starting from premises the other rejects, no agreed way to weigh them, so the debate never ends; a Scottish philosopher in 1981 says this is not normal but what moral language looks like after a catastrophe; the lesson follows his diagnosis, his history and his choice between two ways out.

1. **Why the debate never ends.** The problem as a newcomer meets it, with the module's own case: the reader has just watched Rawls's need-based justice and Nozick's entitlement (`rawls-theory-of-justice`), the egalitarian and the envy charge (`ressentiment-and-envy`), the liberal and Scruton (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`) — each side valid, none moved. Work one of MacIntyre's three paired debates (ch. 2: the equality-vs-liberty pair on health and education is closest to the module; the just-war trio is the cleanest; pick one and lay its premises out). Three features, each derived from the example: the premises are incommensurable (no common measure to weigh a right against a need); yet each speaker uses impersonal language — "it is unjust", not "I don't want it"; and the premises have different historical origins (Locke, Kant, Aquinas, Rousseau, Smith — several already met in the course). Obvious attempt: this is just what moral disagreement is — people hold different values, and moral words express them. That attempt has a name, concept `emotivism`: moral judgments express attitudes ("This is good" ≈ "I approve of this; do so as well") and are neither true nor false. Why MacIntyre refuses it as a theory of meaning: it cannot say which attitude "approve" names without going in a circle, and it erases the difference between "because I wish it" and "because it is your duty". Answer: emotivism is false as a theory of what moral words mean and true as a description of how they are now used — we speak as if appealing to an impersonal standard and have none to appeal to. Because: if the words had always worked this way, their "prestige" — the reason anyone says "that is wrong" rather than "I dislike that" — would be inexplicable.
2. **The disquieting suggestion: a catastrophe no one noticed.** Problem: if moral words once had a standard and lost it, why does nobody see the loss? Attempt: philosophers would notice. MacIntyre's fable (ch. 1, two quoted sentences): science destroyed, then revived from fragments — people say "neutrino" and "atomic weight", argue, and none of their arguments can be settled. Analysis of the present language would never reveal the disorder, because it describes the language as it now is. Answer: only a history can — three stages: a flourishing scheme, a catastrophe, a damaged restoration. This is why the book argues by history and not by analysis, and why it says its own hypothesis, if it seemed plausible at first, would be false. Anchor the author in one sentence here: a Scottish philosopher, once a Marxist writing in the dissident New Reasoner of the late 1950s, who wanted a moral ground from which to reject Stalinism without simply re-adopting the liberalism Marxism had criticised (Preface).
3. **What an emotivist society looks like: the characters.** Problem: a theory of moral language needs a social setting; who lives as if emotivism were true? Answer: "characters" — social roles in which a type of person and a moral philosophy fuse, as the Public School Headmaster once did. Modernity has three: the Rich Aesthete (ends are whatever pleases), the Manager (ends are given; he only matches means to them efficiently) and the Therapist (ends are given; he adjusts people to them). Manager and therapist claim to settle no questions of value — only of effectiveness. Because MacIntyre accepts Weber's account of bureaucratic reason (ends are values on which reason is silent — the reader has Weber's fact–value distinction from `strauss-natural-right-and-history`, `<Ref>`), and then denies its premise: the manager's claim to effectiveness rests on law-like knowledge of society that social science does not have (ch. 8: its best findings hold "characteristically and for the most part"; "No one is or could be in charge"). So managerial expertise is a third "moral fiction" beside rights and utility. Connect in one clause to Burnham's managers (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`): Burnham said managers were the new rulers; MacIntyre says their title to rule is a fiction. Name the two realms: the organisational (ends given) and the personal (values chosen, no rational resolution) — "bureaucratic individualism", whose quarrel between individualism and collectivism hides agreement that only these two modes exist.
4. **The Enlightenment project and why each version undoes the others.** Problem: when did the standard go? Concept `enlightenment-project`: the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century attempt to give inherited moral rules an independent rational justification. Three attempts, each anchored in one sentence for a reader who has met none of these men: Kierkegaard (a Danish writer of the 1840s): morality is a matter of radical choice — but a principle adopted for no reason has no authority over the one who adopted it. Kant (a Prussian philosopher of the 1780s, whose test is: act only on a rule you could will everyone to follow): reason alone grounds morality — but the test passes "Persecute all those who hold false religious beliefs" and "Always eat mussels on Mondays in March"; it can rule out little and rule in anything consistent. Diderot and Hume: morality rests on the passions — but desire cannot arbitrate among desires (Diderot's own Rameau's Nephew asks why the parasite should not prey on his patrons), and Hume's appeal to "sympathy" is a bridge with nothing under it. Answer: each rests its case on the failure of the other two, so their criticisms of one another, taken together, are the failure of all three. This is the turn of the lesson: the reader should now ask WHY all three failed.
5. **Why the project had to fail: the deleted telos.** The obvious attempt: they failed because they were not clever enough; a better argument will succeed (Rawls's is the twentieth century's best candidate). MacIntyre's answer: the failure was structural. Draw the classical scheme as three terms — man as he happens to be; man as he could be if he realised his end (telos); and the moral precepts that move him from the first to the second (the reader has nature-as-end from `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`: the oak in the acorn, `<Ref>`). The precepts were designed to correct untutored human nature toward its end. Delete the end — as the new science and Protestant theology did — and you are left with precepts whose whole point was to correct the very nature you are now trying to derive them from. Hence every derivation from human nature as it is (desire, reason, choice) must fail. Then the is/ought corollary, which the reader met in `strauss-natural-right-and-history` as Weber's fact–value distinction: "no 'ought' from 'is'" is not a timeless law of logic. "He is a sea-captain" entails "he ought to do what a sea-captain ought to do"; "this watch is grossly inaccurate" entails "this is a bad watch" — because "watch", "farmer", "sea-captain" are functional concepts. Once "man" was a functional concept too (defined by a role and an end), "he is a man" carried an "ought". The principle became true only when that concept was lost — so its proclamation was a historical event. Consequence (ch. 6): without a telos, "rights" and "utility" are fictions that claim an impersonal criterion and supply none — "there are no such rights, and belief in them is one with belief in witches and in unicorns", because every attempt to give reasons for them has failed. The reader has the 1948 Declaration, which lists rights and gives no reason (`udhr-read-against-the-right`, one clause); note that MacIntyre dates it 1949 (see Pitfalls).
6. **Nietzsche or Aristotle: the choice that is left.** Problem: if the Enlightenment justifications are gone, what remains? The taboo parable (ch. 9): Cook's sailors in the 1770s found Polynesians unable to say why a forbidden thing was forbidden; in 1819 King Kamehameha II of Hawaii abolished the taboos and met little resistance — rules cut off from the beliefs that made them intelligible look arbitrary, and fall at a push. Nietzsche's achievement was to see that modern moral talk is in this state: appeals to objective standards are the will in disguise (`<Ref>` `nietzsche/genealogy-as-method`, `nietzsche/will-to-power-as-psychology`; one clause each, no re-teaching). So the disjunction: either follow the Enlightenment's collapse to Nietzsche's diagnosis, or hold that the project "should never have been commenced" — "There is no third alternative", and in particular none from Hume, Kant or Mill. If a pre-modern view is to be vindicated, it will be Aristotle's. MacIntyre then judges Nietzsche: his great man, who makes his own values, has no practices, no shared goods, no story but his own — a moral solipsist, the last stage of the individualism he despised, not its exit (ch. 18; `<Ref>` `nietzsche/overman-and-last-man`).
7. **The virtues restored: practice, a life, a tradition.** Problem: Aristotle's telos rested on a "metaphysical biology" few now accept (MacIntyre's own phrase), and on a city-state that no longer exists, and he defended slavery (the reader knows this: `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`). How can his account be restated without them? Three stages, each answering a defect of the one before (concept `practice-and-tradition`): (a) **Practice.** The chess-and-candy child (ch. 14): paid in sweets to play chess, he has every reason to cheat; only when he finds the goods internal to chess — analytical skill, strategic imagination — does cheating defeat his own purpose. Internal goods can be had only by doing the practice well and are recognised only by those who do it; external goods (money, status, power) can be had by many routes and are competed for. A practice is defined (l. 1342; quote the first clause, paraphrase the rest): football, architecture, farming, physics, the making and sustaining of a family, politics in Aristotle's sense — not tic-tac-toe, not bricklaying. A virtue is a quality without which the goods internal to practices cannot be achieved: justice, courage and truthfulness are needed by any practice. And institutions — chess clubs, hospitals, universities — house practices but pursue external goods, so they corrupt the practices they sustain unless the virtues resist. Set this against Scruton's autonomous institution in one sentence (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`): Scruton protects the institution; MacIntyre protects the practice from the institution. (b) **The narrative unity of a life.** Practices conflict (Gauguin's painting vs his family), so a life needs a good that orders them: "I can only answer the question 'What am I to do?' if I can answer the prior question 'Of what story or stories do I find myself a part?'" — the good life for man is "the life spent in seeking for the good life for man". (c) **Tradition.** The story of a life is embedded in the stories of communities: I inherit debts and obligations as son, citizen, member of a nation — the detached self who says "I never owned any slaves" is refusing his own story. A living tradition is "an historically extended, socially embodied argument" about the goods that constitute it — and here MacIntyre turns on the conservatives: Burke contrasted tradition with reason and stability with conflict; "Traditions, when vital, embody continuities of conflict. Indeed when a tradition becomes Burkean, it is always dying or dead." (Burke: `burke-reflections`, `<Ref>`.)
8. **Justice, the modern state, and St Benedict — and the objections.** Problem: what does this mean for the module's question, the justice of distribution? Ch. 17's A and B: A (a shopkeeper or a policeman saving for a house) holds that what he has justly acquired is his; B (a social worker, or someone with inherited money) holds that need gives a claim to redistribution. Rawls articulates B, Nozick A; both are internally coherent and logically incompatible, and both picture society as strangers shipwrecked on an island who must agree on rules. Neither has a place for desert — which the ordinary A and B both appeal to — because desert needs a community with a shared good (Rawls: "No one deserves his greater natural capacity" — `rawls-theory-of-justice`, `<Ref>`). Consequence: "Modern politics is civil war carried on by other means" (the Bakke case — a 1978 US Supreme Court ruling that forbade fixed racial quotas in university admission but allowed race to count — as a truce, not a verdict); patriotism as a virtue is no longer possible "because we lack in the fullest sense a patria"; every modern political tradition — liberal, conservative, radical, socialist — is exhausted. The ending: the Roman parallel — men and women of good will who stopped shoring up the empire and built local communities to carry civility through the dark ages; "the barbarians are not waiting beyond the frontiers; they have already been governing us"; "We are waiting not for a Godot, but for another—doubtless very different—St. Benedict" (St Benedict: c. 480–547, founder of monasteries under a Rule at Monte Cassino; Godot: the character in Beckett's 1953 play who never arrives). Then the objections, each at full strength: **(i) From the liberal**: interminable disagreement is not a symptom but the permanent condition of free people with plural goods (MacIntyre himself credits Weber and Berlin with the heterogeneity of goods in ch. 11); liberalism is the arrangement that lets them live together without a shared telos, and Rawls's later work (Political Liberalism, 1993 — not in the dossier; References only) starts exactly there. MacIntyre's reply: the "neutral" arrangement is itself one tradition (liberal individualism) passing as no tradition. **(ii) Relativism**: if every standard is internal to a tradition, how can one tradition be better? MacIntyre's reply (Postscript, outline only): a tradition wins when it can explain, by its rivals' own standards, why they failed and it did not — as Newton explained what Galileo could not; "the best theory so far". **(iii) From the right** — Strauss's warning (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`): to ground the virtues in history and tradition rather than nature is historicism, which Strauss said ends in Nietzsche anyway; MacIntyre's later turn to Aquinas and "metaphysical biology" (Prologue) is his concession that the account needed a nature after all. And Scruton's (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`): a tradition that is always arguing about its goods is a seminar, not a home; authority comes before argument. **(iv) Nostalgia**: when was the coherent scheme ever in force — in Homer's war-bands, Aristotle's slave-holding city, the medieval kingdom? MacIntyre concedes the defects and says the claim is about intelligibility, not about a golden age. Close: the reader can now explain why the module's debates do not end, and has met a critic of liberalism who is no conservative; the next lesson's author, also of the left, turns the critique from moral language to the class that speaks it.

Headings as claims (suggested): "Valid arguments that never end"; "A catastrophe that analysis cannot see"; "The manager, the therapist and the aesthete"; "Three justifications that refute each other"; "The deleted telos"; "Nietzsche or Aristotle"; "Practices, lives and traditions"; "Justice among strangers, and St Benedict".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **MacIntyre is not a conservative, and says so.** course.yaml's note: present him as a man of the left turned Thomist, a critic of liberalism from outside the right. The Preface (Marxism, the New Reasoner) and the 3rd-ed. Prologue (l. 176: conservatism "a mirror image of the liberalism that it professedly opposes"; l. 174: never a communitarian) are the evidence; l. 1539 (modern conservatives conserve "only older rather than later versions of liberal individualism") is the charge the reader carries back to Scruton. Do not enlist him for the right; do not hide that the right has read him (Deneen, forward).
- **The Declaration's date.** MacIntyre writes "the United Nations declaration on human rights of 1949" (l. 640); the Universal Declaration was adopted on 10 December 1948, as the reader knows from `udhr-read-against-the-right`. Quote around the date or note the slip in one clause; do not reproduce it as the course's own.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (spans the course):** Take the three arguments of one of MacIntyre's paired debates (or the A-and-B dispute of ch. 17) and, for each premise, name the earlier lesson in this course where the reader met its source — Aquinas's just war (`aquinas-law-and-kingship` / `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`), Locke's property (`locke-property-and-consent`), Rawls's need-based principle (`rawls-theory-of-justice`), and so on. Then state in one sentence what MacIntyre infers from the variety of origins.
2. **Boundary probe:** "He is a sea-captain, therefore he ought to do what a sea-captain ought to do" is valid; "He is a man, therefore he ought to …" is not, today. Find the exact condition under which the second becomes valid (a functional concept of "man": a role and an end), say which earlier thinker in the course supplied such a concept (Aristotle's man as political animal with speech about the just), and say why, on MacIntyre's account, the "no ought from is" principle is true for us and was not for that thinker.
3. **Claim to refute:** "MacIntyre is a conservative who wants to restore Christian tradition against liberalism." Break it with three pieces of evidence the lesson gave (the Preface's Marxism; the Prologue's "mirror image" of liberalism; the "Burkean … dying or dead" sentence and the charge that modern conservatives conserve older liberalism), then say what part of the claim survives (his later Thomism; the right's use of the Benedict passage).
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** Apply the practice/institution distinction to an institution the course has defended — Scruton's university or school as an autonomous institution (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`). Derive what each author would protect, from whom, and the one case where their advice diverges (an institution whose members dispute its purpose: Scruton defers to the established form; MacIntyre requires the argument to continue).
5. **Sourcing drill:** The "no such rights … witches and unicorns" passage (ch. 6). Metadata first (1981; a philosopher arguing that the Enlightenment justifications failed; an academic audience); prediction: what will he say about the 1948 Declaration? Records / argues / omits — note the date given as 1949 and what the Declaration's own Art. 29 (duties to the community, read in `udhr-read-against-the-right`) does to his claim that it gives no reasons. Close: what can the passage be USED to establish (that the Declaration does not argue its foundations) and what can it not (that the rights listed are not observed or enforceable)?
6. **Synthesis (claim–evidence–reasoning):** Strauss and MacIntyre both say the modern "no ought from is" is a product of a lost conception of nature or end. Argue where they part (Strauss: return to nature and the best regime; MacIntyre: practices and a living tradition — history) and judge Strauss's objection that MacIntyre's position is historicism, using MacIntyre's own later concession (the Prologue's "metaphysical biology").
7. **Counter-argument stem:** "Someone claims MacIntyre is refuted by the stability of liberal democracies: if modern politics were 'civil war carried on by other means', they would have collapsed." Name two facts the claim fails to account for (MacIntyre's point is about the absence of moral consensus, not of order — the Bakke ruling as a truce; his A and B agree while the economy grows) and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that stable agreement exists on the grounds of justice, not merely on procedures — which the module's debates call into doubt).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`:** MacIntyre says the manager's authority rests on a claim to expertise — law-like knowledge of how to get results — that social science cannot back. Suppose that is true. What then is the title to rule of the credentialed class whose members were selected for that expertise, and what would such a class owe to the people it manages? The solution shows that, once expertise is a fiction, the class's standing rests only on having been selected — on "merit" as a sorting, not as a competence — and says the next lesson's author argues that such an elite has stopped acknowledging any obligation to the rest of the nation (`<Ref>` to `lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`).

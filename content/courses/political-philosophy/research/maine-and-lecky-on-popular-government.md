# Research brief: maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government (Maine and Lecky on popular government: the fragility of democracy)

status: planned
module: democracy-and-its-critics
type: core
goal: Analyze Maine's and Lecky's case that popular government is historically rare, unstable and hostile to the conditions of progress, and evaluate it against the record they cite

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`: equality of conditions as a "providential fact" that no one can stop (`equality-of-conditions`) — the claim Maine attacks first, by name. From `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`: the omnipotent majority (`tyranny-of-the-majority`), and Tocqueville's lawyers, courts and forms as the counterweights — which Maine and Lecky turn into a programme (second chambers, written constitutions, a supreme court). From `stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity`: `coercion-by-opinion`; parliament as "counting heads instead of breaking heads"; and Stephen's passage on universal suffrage ("The strongest man in one form or another will always rule … In a pure democracy, the ruling men will be the Wire-pullers and their friends"), which Maine quotes as the core of his own argument; Stephen's reply "Nothing" to "what do you propose instead?". From `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`: the harm principle, which Lecky accepts for the criminal law (the reader should see a critic of democracy who is a Millian on liberty). From `marx-manifesto`: the "battle of democracy" and the graduated income tax as measure 2 — the programme Lecky's taxation chapter answers. From `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`: `anacyclosis` — Maine's France, overthrown by mob, army and invasion in turn, is the cycle restated as a record. From `decline-of-regimes`: Plato's democracy that ends in tyranny. From `hume-against-the-original-contract`: `government-founded-on-opinion` (the few rule the many by opinion) — Maine's wire-puller is its electoral form; and Hume's 1742 forecast for the French monarchy, which Maine uses as his warning about forecasts. From `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: the sovereign people, which Maine calls the French king turned upside down. Each is a `<Ref>` and one clause.
- **Contributes:** The module's sceptical forecast. Where Tocqueville said democracy was coming and asked how to make it bearable, Maine and Lecky deny the premise that it has come to stay: popular government, as a form with a record, has been rare and short-lived; a multitude cannot will, so "the will of the people" is the opinion of a few adopted by many; universal suffrage is not radical but "a mischievous form of Conservatism" that would have stopped the innovations the modern world rests on; and a democracy can tax the few at the will of the many. Their positive teaching is institutional: popular government survives only where a constitution settled beforehand puts property, contract and the constitution itself out of a bare majority's reach — the American case. The lesson reconstructs the case, then tests it on the cases they themselves cite.
- **Hands off:** (i) `british-franchise` (next lesson, the module's application) decides their forecasts on the British record — right about where power moved (Cabinet, party machine, graduated taxation; **strong**), wrong for Britain that popular government would break (**strong**), with the mechanism split between pressure from below and party competition (**on balance**) — taking in turn Lecky's mechanism of franchise extension (party competition, not pressure from below — the rival of Tocqueville's mechanism), Maine's prediction that power would pass to the Cabinet and the party committee, Lecky's prediction of confiscatory graduated taxation and of a House of Commons that within fifty years would lose the power to make and unmake ministries. Where the British exception does not reach, the fragility forecast is judged at `article-48-weimar`. (ii) The crowd that Maine says has become "the organ of definite opinions" is the subject of `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`, which has this lesson as a prerequisite. (iii) The wire-puller as a law of every regime, not a vice of democracy, is `mosca-ruling-class` (prerequisite: this lesson) and `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`; the forward exercise lands there. (iv) Lecky's "plutocracy" as democracy's corruption returns in `spengler-decline-of-the-west`; his charge that graduated taxation is a majority's weapon returns as the charge of envy in `ressentiment-and-envy`; Maine's reading of the American Constitution as the British constitution of 1760–87 is picked up in `federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`.
- **Spine step:** "Democracy arrives as a fact and is judged: Tocqueville foresees soft despotism, Carlyle demands heroes, Mill defends liberty and Stephen answers him, Marx predicts class war, Maine and Lecky call popular government fragile — and the British franchise tests who was right"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): by the 1880s the vote in Britain was being widened again and almost everyone in public life said democracy was the direction of history; a legal historian answers that popular governments have been rare, short-lived and usually ended in military rule, and that the mass of mankind has always been hostile to the innovations that made the modern world; ten years later an Irish historian adds that democracy taxes the few at the will of the many; the lesson tests their claims against the cases they used.

Anchors (L11), in the first section: the Third Reform Act of 1884 (it gave rural householders the vote that town householders had had since 1867 — one clause; the full series is `british-franchise`'s); Henry Sumner Maine (1822–88), Cambridge professor of civil law, author of *Ancient Law* (1861), legal member of the Viceroy's council in India 1862–69 — the post Stephen took over from him (Ref `stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity`); *Popular Government* (1885), four essays, three first printed in the *Quarterly Review*. W. E. H. Lecky (1838–1903), Dublin-born historian of rationalism, of European morals and of eighteenth-century England, from 1895 member for Dublin University, a Liberal who broke with Gladstone over Irish Home Rule in 1886; *Democracy and Liberty* (1896), written while Parliament was rewriting Irish land tenure, which he read as confiscation. State at once what the two share with Stephen: all three were liberals of Mill's generation, lawyers or historians, who turned against the democratic conclusion of liberalism without turning against liberty.

1. **Is democracy the direction of history?** The obvious answer, which the reader has from two sources: Tocqueville's "providential fact" (Ref `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`) and Stephen's "Nothing … the whole stream of human affairs is setting with irresistible force in that direction" (Ref). Maine's first move is to deny that this is a fact at all. (a) It is a belief with a birthplace: "the view that Democracy is irresistible is of French origin … mainly spread over the world by the book of De Tocqueville", formed by men who watched 1830 bring back the Revolution's language after everyone thought it dead. (b) The longer record runs the other way: from Augustus to the founding of the United States "the tendency of the ages" was toward monarchy. (c) In politics the strongest causes are "the timidity, the listlessness, and the superficiality" of most minds, so a belief in inevitability helps make what it predicts. Then the method, which is the lesson's method too: treat democracy as "simply and solely a form of government … by the Many", one of the old three (Ref `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome` for the six forms), with a record like any other, and read the record "neither for congratulation nor for lamentation, but simply as materials for opinion". The "because": a form of government is judged by what it has done, and the claim that it must come is a forecast, so it can be checked. Seam (state it): Maine himself warns how forecasts about regimes fail — set piece 1, Hume (Ref `hume-against-the-original-contract`) in 1742 calling the French monarchy a "government of laws, not of men" eleven years before Chesterfield saw "all the symptoms … previous to great changes and revolutions" — and the lesson holds him to that warning in link 8.
2. **What has popular government done since 1789?** The record as Maine counts it (Essay I pp. 13–20): France overthrown three times by the mob (1792, 1830, 1848), three times by the army (1797, 1799, 1851), three times by invasion (1814, 1815, 1870), with "forty-four years of liberty and thirty-seven of stern dictatorship"; Spain, from the first constitution of 1812, "forty military risings of a serious nature", nine "perfectly successful", and a king who keeps his throne by changing ministers whenever "the army is in earnest"; the German and Austrian assemblies of 1848 dispersed by armies; Spanish America, where of Bolivia's fourteen presidents "thirteen have died assassinated or in exile" (Lecky adds Mexico: forty-eight forms of government in thirty-two years). Durable successes: Britain, the United States, a few small states with old habits of freedom. The answer: popular government, "since its reintroduction into the world, has proved itself to be extremely fragile". Seam to the backward link, one clause: this is the cycle of regimes (Ref `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`) told as a nineteenth-century ledger — mob rule, then the one man — and Plato's democracy that breeds its tyrant (Ref `decline-of-regimes`).
3. **Why is it fragile?** Maine gives three causes and one structure. The causes (Essay I pp. 21–28): the mass army, because obedience is the soldier's virtue and censure the democrat's right, and a soldier values his share of power in the regiment more than his share in the constituency; the city mob, now "the organ of definite opinions" and armed with dynamite (anchor in a clause: the Irish-American dynamite campaign in British cities, 1881–85, and the bomb that killed Tsar Alexander II in 1881); the Irreconcileables — parties that hold political creeds as religion — and nationalism. The structure (pp. 28–34) is the lesson's centre. "Political liberty, said Hobbes, is political power"; in a wide democracy that power is "minced into morsels", each man's share almost infinitesimal; a share that small is not worth using, so in England it would be sold "if the law permitted it", in America it is sold "in spite of the law"; and someone gathers the fragments. Here Maine quotes Stephen (Ref): "The man who can sweep the greatest number of fragments of political power into one heap will govern the rest … In a pure democracy, the ruling men will be the Wire-pullers and their friends." Define *wire-puller* (the party organiser who manages nominations and votes out of sight — an American term, as Maine says). Party, which the wire-puller works through, is "a survival of primitive combativeness" — men who split on the colour of a cow, or cheer Oxford against Cambridge. The "because": dividing power equally does not make men equal; it makes the man who can collect the pieces stronger than any of them. Say what this does to Tocqueville's fear (Ref `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`): the majority is not an omnipotent master but a crowd with a manager.
4. **Can a people will at all?** The obvious picture, built into the phrases "the will of the people", "public opinion", "vox populi, vox Dei": the multitude makes up its mind as one man does. Maine's question (Essay II pp. 88–89): "in what sense can a multitude exercise volition?" Agreement among many is possible only on the simplest questions; on the complex questions of politics "the common determination of a multitude is a chimerical assumption". So "what is called the will of the people really consists in their adopting the opinion of one person or a few persons". The worked analogy: popular government and popular justice were once the same thing — the ancient assembly-court decided by applause — and the jury is that court tamed, twelve men with a judge to state the law and sum up (Ref Tocqueville's jury as a school, which this reading inverts). Representation is the antidote; the caucus and the doctrine of the delegate are the counter-drug that neutralises it (Maine's toxicologists' triad, p. 94). The consequence Maine draws for Britain, stated as his forecast: the House of Commons is too large to deliberate (obstruction is "the familiar disease of large governing bodies"), so power passes to the Cabinet, and from the Cabinet to party committees chosen "for the adroit management of local political business". Inverted monarchy (Essay II pp. 59–61, 76–78): the Demos is flattered as kings were, by the same kind of courtier. Ref Rousseau's sovereign people in one clause (Maine: the French king turned upside down).
5. **Will democracy confiscate, or stand still?** The reader's expectation — the propertied fear of 1832 and 1867, and Marx's hope (Ref `marx-manifesto`: "to win the battle of democracy", measure 2) — is that a mass electorate will be radical. Maine's answer reverses it: universal suffrage stamps on law "the average opinion of an entire community", and its tendency is "a mischievous form of Conservatism", whose ideal is the Church's rule of what has been believed always, everywhere, by all. THE COUNTERFACTUAL (set piece 4): it would have prohibited the spinning-jenny and the power-loom, forbidden the threshing-machine, prevented the Gregorian calendar, restored the Stuarts, and joined the mobs that burned Lord Mansfield's house in 1780 and Joseph Priestley's in 1791. Popular education will not cure it — it "stereotypes average opinion". The ground under this is the work of Maine's whole career: progress is rare. Define `status-to-contract` here, from Stephen's quotation of it (Ref): the progressive societies have moved from relations fixed by birth, family, tribe or conquest to relations made by agreement — and Maine's gloss that such societies are the exception: "The immobility of society is the rule; its mobility is the exception"; the toleration of change is "not much more than a century old" on the Continent and half a century in Britain. Hence "History … is a sound aristocrat": "the progress of mankind has hitherto been effected by the rise and fall of aristocracies", and even Athenian democracy was an aristocracy of citizens living on "a thousand subject cities". State the argument's form plainly: if progress is the work of the few against the instinct of the many, a government by the many's average opinion will stop it.
6. **Then what does a safe popular government look like?** Maine does not ask for a king. His economic warning first (Essay I pp. 43–52): wealth is not a granary to be shared out but a process kept going by motives, and a majority that takes the stores is the mutinous crew who feast and will not sail to port; there are only two ways to make men produce — competition, or a task enforced by the prison or the lash. Then the one success: the United States, which "sustained the credit of Republics". Why it works, on Maine's account (Essay IV): it is the British constitution of 1760–87 with king and lords replaced, not a new invention; its Senate rests on "an historical principle" (equal states), the only thing that "can be successfully opposed to the principle of making all public powers the mere reflection of the average opinion of the multitude"; Article V makes amendment slow; the courts strike down unconstitutional laws; and the contracts clause, "the bulwark of American individualism against democratic impatience and Socialistic fantasy". Answer: democracy survives where a constitution settled beforehand puts property, contract and the constitution itself out of reach of an ordinary majority. Ref forward in one clause (`federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic` reads the framers themselves). Seam: Tocqueville's counterweights (lawyers, courts, forms) have become a design brief.
7. **Lecky (1896): what has the franchise done to liberty and to property?** Lecky starts from a change of theory, not of institutions: the "complete displacement of the centre of power" without "any change in the external framework". Under the old theory the vote was "a right conferred by legislation on grounds of expediency", Parliament "essentially a machine for taxing", so taxpayers and interests were represented in proportion; under the new, borrowed from Rousseau, numbers decide. The divide is "whether … the world should be governed by its ignorance or by its intelligence" (Lecky quotes Maine's counterfactual here — show the borrowing). Three theses, each as the answer to a question. (a) *Is democracy liberty?* No: "A despotism resting on a plebiscite is quite as natural a form of democracy as a republic" (France: the coup of December 1851 ratified by some eight million votes), and "Equality is the idol of democracy" — reachable only by "stringent repression" of unequal capacities, since "whenever natural forces have unrestricted play, inequality is certain to ensue" (Ref Tocqueville's "equality is their idol" — Lecky draws the conclusion Tocqueville feared). (b) *Who pays?* "The suffrage is a spear as well as a shield" (an American lawyer Lecky quotes); "the first and most vital rule of all good government is, that those who vote taxes should contribute … to paying them"; graduated taxation "realises most completely the supreme danger of democracy, … one class imposes on another burdens which it is not asked to share". Anchor: the graduated death duties of the 1894 budget, two years before the book. (c) *What becomes of parliaments?* Caucus, delegate, log-rolling — small groups forming "a political syndicate" to pass what the nation does not want; both parties outbid each other; and the franchise itself was widened not by demand but by "a competition for power and popularity between rival factions" — "the chief motives of all our recent degradations of the suffrage". The forecast (pp. 248–249): such parliaments "will lose the power of making and unmaking ministries", a strong executive "independently of their fluctuations" will follow, "in fifty years' time" the Commons will not hold the power it holds now. His remedies: diverse franchises (university seats, plural votes — the square mile of the City against a square mile of Stepney), a strong reformed upper house, the referendum as a court of appeal on constitutional questions, and — his envy of America — a written constitution guarded by a supreme court. Mark what Lecky concedes, because the course's balance needs it: he accepts Mill's harm principle for the criminal law; he says "democratic evils are corrected by democratic remedies"; he grants that a popular vote would not have ratified the Toleration Act or Catholic Emancipation "but democracy has been crowned king"; he grades Tocqueville as right on some things and wrong on the Union.
8. **What does the record they cite show, and what does it not? — the strongest OBJECTION here, at full strength.** Take their own cases one at a time (key idea 5). *France:* Maine's count of forty-four against thirty-seven years leaves out "the anomalous period from 1870 to 1885" — the republic he was living under, which went on to last until 1940, longer than any French regime since 1789; Lecky's thirty-two ministries in twenty-three years shows ministries falling inside a regime that did not fall. Two claims are mixed: instability of governments and fragility of the regime. *Spain and Spanish America:* the risings Maine counts were made by armies, under franchises that were narrow most of the time; on his own first cause, the variable may be the army's habit of interference, not the breadth of the vote. *Lecky's successful parliaments* (Belgium, the Netherlands, Italy, Austria) were all elected on a high suffrage — and so was Spain's; the comparison is not controlled. *Switzerland:* both read the referendum's vetoes as the voters' conservatism (Lecky: about 130 federal laws 1874–91, 16 put to the vote, 11 rejected); the same country is their example of durable free government, so the referendum's record can be read as stability as easily as stagnation. *The United States:* both concede the success and explain it by anti-majoritarian design — which is an argument for constitutions, not against wide suffrage. Then the objection proper, in three parts, each at full strength. (i) **Symmetry**, from Lecky's own pages: his rule that one class must not vote burdens on another it does not share condemns the restricted parliaments he praises — his catalogue of repealed taxes on salt, candles, soap, corn and sugar is a list of burdens propertied electorates laid on the poor; the "supreme danger" ran the other way before 1832. (ii) **Prejudice in every class**: Maine's own thesis is that immobility is the rule for mankind, not for the poor; the unreformed Parliament held off Catholic Emancipation until 1829, and Lecky concedes a popular vote would have done the same — so the counterfactual shows that innovation needed protection from majorities of any size, which is again an argument for constitutions. (iii) **Participation educates** (Ref Tocqueville's township and Mill — the capacity Maine measures is the capacity of men who have never governed; Lecky's own evidence that British town governments since 1835 were honest is evidence that it can be learned). Maine's and Lecky's replies, from their texts: popular prejudice is stronger and harder to correct than the prejudice of a class, because no one stands outside a majority to correct it (Maine, Essay II pp. 65–68); a constitutional mistake, unlike a legal one, cannot be undone (Essay III pp. 167–170); and the remedy they ask for is balance, not exclusion (Lecky's diverse franchises). Where the case holds and where it gives way, stated as a judgment the reader can check, not a score: their institutional teaching (entrenched constitutions, second chambers, the protection of contract) is untouched by the objection, and much of it was adopted by the democracies that followed; their inference from the electorate's ignorance to the fragility of democracy is weakest where their own cases are read whole — the count is selected and the comparisons are not controlled — though it is not refuted by that, only left unproved; their forecasts for Britain are dated and testable, and the next lesson decides them. And one finding the lesson states in the course's voice: on the record they cite, their own cases count against military politics more than against a wide franchise. Maine's French count leaves out the Third Republic (1870–1940); Lecky's thirty-two ministries fell inside a regime that did not fall; the Spanish and Spanish-American risings were made by armies under narrow franchises. The institutional teaching does not need the inference from the electorate's ignorance, and stands without it. Hold Maine to his own warning from link 1: he wrote of Hume's forecast that Hume "was unquestionably wrong".

Headings as claims: "Inevitability is a forecast, and forecasts can be checked"; "A ledger of risings: France, Spain, Spanish America"; "Power minced into morsels: the wire-puller"; "A multitude cannot will"; "Universal suffrage as a conservative force"; "What a safe republic needs: the American case"; "Lecky: a spear as well as a shield"; "Their own cases count against armies, not against the franchise".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **The register rule.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Taught at full strength first.** Links 1–7 reconstruct Maine and Lecky as they would want, including their concessions; the objection enters at link 8 and is put with the same force, and their replies follow it. The reader must leave able to state the case for property qualifications and second chambers as Lecky states it.
- **Terms and names to anchor (L11):** popular government (Maine's sense: rulers as agents of the community) vs democracy (the form: government by the Many); the Third Reform Act 1884 (one clause); the Quarterly Review (the Tory quarterly where three of Maine's essays first appeared); wire-puller and caucus (the party manager and the closed meeting of party managers that chooses candidates — anchor with Birmingham's Liberal "caucus" of the late 1870s in a clause); Irreconcileables (parties refusing the regime itself — Maine's examples include Irish nationalists); the mandate (the doctrine that an election authorises specific measures); obstruction (the Irish members' tactic of the late 1870s–80s of prolonging debate to stop the Commons' business); the referendum and the Swiss *Landsgemeinde* (one clause); plebiscite (a vote ratifying an act already done — Louis Napoleon 1851, 1870); graduated (progressive) taxation and the 1894 death duties; the Liberal Unionists (Liberals who left Gladstone over Irish Home Rule in 1886); the Irish land Acts 1870 and 1881 (fixed tenure and judicial rents — Lecky's "confiscation"); the Gregorian calendar (adopted in Britain in 1752, eleven days dropped); the Gordon riots (1780) and Priestley riots (1791); Luddites and the Swing riots; Vincent of Lérins (a fifth-century monk whose rule for orthodoxy was what has been believed always, everywhere, by all); the contracts clause (Art. I §10 of the US Constitution: no state may impair the obligation of contracts) and Article V (amendment by two-thirds of Congress and three-quarters of the states). No more than three new terms per section: the anchors above are spread over seven sections.
- **The calendar riots.** Do not state that the 1752 calendar reform caused riots with crowds shouting "Give us our eleven days". The slogan is on a banner in Hogarth's *An Election Entertainment* (1755), an Oxfordshire election print, and no riot against the calendar is documented; the story grew later. Maine's claim is a counterfactual about what universal suffrage "would have" done, not a report of riots — say so, and let the reader see that his weakest item is the one he presents as most obvious. This is a real misconception (L13), worth one refutation slot.
- **"History is a sound aristocrat."** Maine attributes it to "Strauss" — David Friedrich Strauss (1808–74), author of the *Life of Jesus*, not Leo Strauss, whom the course meets in `strauss-natural-right-and-history`. In the body, say "a German theologian Maine quotes"; do not write the surname.
- **Maine's "Hobbes".** "Political liberty … is political power" and the "desolate freedom of the wild ass" are Maine's report of Hobbes; the reader has Hobbes's own definition — liberty as the silence of the law (Ref `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`) — and Leviathan XXI's remark that the liberty the ancients praised was the liberty of the commonwealth, not of private men. Flag the difference in a clause; do not let Maine's paraphrase stand as Hobbes's words. Writer: check whether "wild ass" is in Leviathan; if not, attribute it to Maine.
- **"Status to contract" is background, not the lesson.** It is introduced (course.yaml) and must be defined, but in one paragraph at link 5, as the ground of Maine's claim that progress is rare. Do not teach *Ancient Law*'s patria potestas, legal fictions or the history of wills. Ancient Law is outside the dossier; quote the sentence via Stephen or from a fetched edition, verified.
- **Do not re-teach** Tocqueville's providential fact or tyranny of the majority, Stephen's coercion by opinion and "counting heads", Polybius's cycle, Hume's opinion, Rousseau's sovereign people, Mill's harm principle: one clause and a `<Ref>` each. The new material is Maine's and Lecky's argument and the record.
- **Do not harvest the next lesson.** The British forecasts (Cabinet, fifty years, graduated taxation, party competition) are stated and dated here and tested in `british-franchise`. Do not lay the Reform Acts' series in this lesson.
- **Contested empirical claims.** Whether wide suffrage leads to confiscatory taxation, whether mass electorates resist innovation, whether the American design or American conditions explain American stability: each is an argument with evidence on both sides. State Maine's and Lecky's evidence, the counter-evidence from their own cases, and stop.
- **Period prejudice in the texts.** Lecky's chapters carry period judgements about the Irish, Catholics, Jews, Black voters and the poor, and Maine's about "Indian blood" and races. Quote none of it unless the argument needs it; where a passage is used, quote it accurately and frame it as what a writer of the 1880s–90s held, without commentary in either direction.
- **Two-way wording check.** Neither "prophets of doom" nor "clear-eyed realists"; neither "elitist" nor "prescient" in the course's voice. "Maine argues", "Lecky counts".
- **Numbers (L12).** Keepers: France's 44 / 37 years (and the fifteen years the count leaves out); Spain's 40 risings, 9 successful; Lecky's Swiss 130 / 16 / 11. Bolivia's 13 of 14 and Mexico's 48 in 32 may enter once as Maine's and Lecky's own figures, bound in the sentence. French debt, American municipal debt, Irish rents, Giffen's wage figures: out of the body (Aside at most).
- **Dossier notes.** Both main texts are full; OCR is noisy (Lecky's has doubled spaces and errors such as "3880" for 1880) — verify every quotation against the page image or a second edition before quoting. *Ancient Law*, Hume's "Of Civil Liberty", the Hogarth print and the secondary works are outside the dossier. No Strauss–Cropsey chapter covers Maine or Lecky.
- **L8a.** Feaver, Burrow, Collini, and the scholars the primary texts quote: References only or by role.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up, the only mechanical one).** Name Maine's three causes of the fragility of popular government and, for each, one case from his record; then name the structural argument that is not on the list (power minced into morsels) and whom Maine quotes for it (Stephen — Ref).
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill.** Maine's French count ("forty-four years of liberty and thirty-seven of stern dictatorship", "putting aside the anomalous period from 1870 to 1885"): author (a lawyer-historian writing in the Tory *Quarterly Review*), date (1883–85), audience (British readers during the 1884 franchise debate), purpose; what it records, what it argues, what it omits (the regime under which it was written), and what it can and cannot be used to establish about France and about popular government. Solution: recompute the share of years with 1870–85 counted as liberty, state how the conclusion moves, and say why the choice to exclude is itself the argument.
3. **Claim to refute.** "Maine shows that the common people have always opposed new inventions." Name the step that fails: the claim is a counterfactual about universal suffrage, not a report; its items differ in evidence (Luddites and Swing riots documented; the calendar riot not); and his own Essay III says immobility is the rule for mankind, not for one class. State what the passage can establish (that some innovations met popular violence) and what it cannot.
4. **Boundary probe.** Shrink the electorate to its pre-1832 size and run Maine's average-opinion argument on it: does a body of a few hundred thousand propertied voters stamp its average opinion on law? Use one case the lesson gave (Catholic Emancipation held off until 1829; Lecky's concession that a popular vote would have done the same). Say which premise of Maine's argument depends on the electorate's size and which on the nature of any majority, and what institutional conclusion survives the probe (protection of innovation by constitution, not by narrowing the vote).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped.** Run Lecky's "cardinal rule" — those who vote taxes should contribute appreciably to paying them — on the unreformed electorate and on the electorate of 1832–67, using his own catalogue of repealed taxes on salt, candles, soap, corn and sugar. Derive what the rule implies about those electorates, and state whether Lecky's remedy (diverse franchises, a second chamber) answers the problem in both directions or only in one.
6. **Corroboration (E3: Tocqueville, Stephen).** Three passages on inevitability: Tocqueville's "providential fact" (Ref `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`), Stephen's "Nothing … we need not sing Hallelujah to the river god" (Ref `stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity`), Maine's "the view that Democracy is irresistible is of French origin". State what each takes to be inevitable, what each therefore recommends (guide it; go with the stream without praising it; doubt it and build barriers), and which of the three Lecky's 1896 position is closest to ("this conception of government will necessarily … dominate", but its "evils" can be "mitigated").
7. **Research-shaped task.** Maine and Lecky read the Swiss federal referendum's vetoes as evidence that mass electorates negate innovation. Design the test that would distinguish "voters reject change as such" from "voters reject badly made laws": what you would count in the Swiss Federal Chancellery's list of federal votes since 1874, against what baseline (e.g. the share of parliament's laws challenged at all; the fate of popular initiatives that propose change; whether rejected measures returned and passed), and what result would embarrass each reading. Model answer names the counts and the warrant; no answer is assumed.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `mosca-ruling-class`.** Put three passages together: Hume's "the few govern the many" by opinion (Ref `hume-against-the-original-contract`), Stephen's "under all circumstances the rank and file are directed by leaders of one kind or another", and Maine's wire-puller. State the general claim they share once the word "democracy" is removed, and say what it would do to the old question whether one, few or many should rule.

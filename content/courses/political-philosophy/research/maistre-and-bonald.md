# Research brief: maistre-and-bonald (Maistre and Bonald: Providence, the executioner and the critique of constitution-making)

status: planned
module: contract-revolution-reaction
type: core
goal: Analyze the counter-revolutionary case that no constitution can be made by deliberation and that authority rests on something reason cannot reach, and weigh the charge that it leads to fascism

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `burke-reflections`: the epistemic case — no generation
  knows enough to design a society; prejudice as the general bank of ages; the
  entailed inheritance; 1688 as a "revolution not made but prevented"; the
  forecast of the gallows and the general; and the last exercise's question —
  if no constitution is designed, how did the English come by theirs? From
  `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: the general will, the Legislator who
  speaks for the gods, the state of nature Maistre will deny ever existed. From
  `bodin-sovereignty` and `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`: `sovereignty` as the
  power bound by no positive law and the sovereign who cannot be judged — the
  concept Maistre will say is "perfectly synonymous" with infallibility. From
  `augustine-two-cities`: Providence in history and the earthly peace; from
  `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: God as author of punishment (Summa I q.49 a.2,
  which Maistre cites). From `english-revolution-as-test`: 1649, Cromwell,
  Monk and 1660 — the parallel Maistre builds his prophecy on, out of Hume's
  History of England (Considerations ch. 11). From
  `hume-against-the-original-contract`: government founded on opinion and the
  denial that any actual government began in consent — Maistre's Seventh
  Dialogue says sovereigns "command effectively … only within the circle of
  things acknowledged by opinion, and they are not the ones who trace the circle
  of opinion" (spd.txt l.12699–12700), Hume's thesis in a theologian's mouth. For
  the starred section: from `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`, sovereigns
  "in the state and posture of Gladiators" toward one another; from
  `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`, `just-war`; from `augustine-two-cities`,
  the earthly peace and sacrifice.
- **Contributes:** One move, in two voices. Maistre: the Revolution is not a
  crime men committed but a punishment God is carrying out through men who think
  they lead it; from this follow rules — no nation gives itself a constitution,
  there is no such thing as "man", written constitutions declare rights they
  did not create, and the hidden bond of every society is the executioner.
  Bonald, in the register of system rather than paradox: society precedes the
  individual, the family is its model, and marriage — like sovereignty — is a
  bond the parties cannot dissolve because a third party (the child; God) is
  always present. Then the charge: that this is the first draft of fascism — put
  at full strength and answered. A starred section (it absorbs what had been planned as
  a separate extra lesson on war) extends the executioner's argument to war — the Seventh
  Dialogue's soldier and executioner, the earth as an altar, war as a divine law
  of expiation (`war-as-expiation`) — with Hegel's and Schmitt's accounts named
  as later comparisons. The core path reads correctly without it.
- **Hands off:** (1) The next lesson, `herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation`, takes
  up "I have seen Frenchmen, Italians, Russians … but man I have never met" from
  the other side: if only peoples exist, what makes a people — its language,
  customs and memory (Herder), or a mission (Fichte)? (2) The forward reading of
  the counter-revolution as a doctrine of the *decision* — authority that answers
  nothing and decides "without delay and without appeal" — is taken a step
  further by a Spanish deputy in 1849, who, finding no legitimate king left to
  restore, asks for a dictatorship (`donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship`, later this
  module), and is theorised by a twentieth-century jurist against liberalism's
  "everlasting conversation" (`schmitt-political-theology`); one paragraph here.
  (3) The problem that stays open for `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`: if
  Burke grounds order in inherited reason and Maistre in a Providence reason
  cannot reach, is there an account on which the state is rational AND not
  designed? (4) Maistre's count of laws and his "three constitutions in five
  years" are tested in `assignats`. (5) The starred section's comparisons point
  forward to Hegel's §§321–340 (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`) and to the
  "real possibility of killing" (`schmitt-concept-of-the-political`). The
  conservative/reactionary distinction (Lilla) is seeded here and taught in
  `what-conservatism-is`.
- **Spine step:** "Government by consent (Locke) is doubted by Hume and
  radicalised by Rousseau's general will; when France tries to frame a government
  from principles, Burke, Maistre and Donoso Cortés answer that no generation
  knows enough, Herder and Fichte put the people before the state, and Hegel
  absorbs both sides into the rational state"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: a Savoyard exile watching the
Revolution from Lausanne concludes that it is not a crime men committed but a
punishment God is carrying out through men who think they are leading it; from
that he draws rules — no nation can give itself a constitution, there is no such
thing as "man" in general, the executioner is the hidden bond of society. The
lesson reconstructs the argument he and his ally Bonald made, then puts to it the
most famous charge against it.

1. **Who is leading the Revolution?** The reader has Burke's answer: men of
   letters and moneyed men, acting from ignorance armed (`<Ref>`
   burke-reflections). Maistre's starting observation: the men who seem to lead
   are mediocrities — Robespierre, Collot, Barère — who "made no mistakes" the
   way Vaucanson's mechanical flutist "never hit a false note"; Mirabeau, the
   one man of genius, is "king of the market hall", master of a crowd and of
   nothing. Obvious attempt: the Revolution is a conspiracy. Why it fails: no
   conspirator got what he wanted; each is destroyed by the next. Maistre's
   answer: "the Revolution leads men more than men lead it" — the leaders are
   "simple instruments", and their very mediocrity is evidence of a superior
   hand. The frame: free beings on a "supple chain" from the throne of the
   Eternal Geometer, like a watch whose springs vary and still keeps time;
   ordinarily the chain is slack, in revolutions it shortens. Define
   *Providence in history* here, as Maistre uses it: not that God approves, but
   that God uses. Heading: *The Revolution uses men: the argument from
   mediocrity*.
2. **If God is using the Revolution, what for?** Obvious answer: nothing — a
   providential evil is a contradiction. Maistre's: punishment, which is for the
   sake of regeneration. France had a "magistracy" over Europe (the most
   Christian king, the language, the proselytising spirit) and abused it; "the
   innocent are far fewer than commonly imagined", and the philosopher who
   justified evil by its end is among the guilty. The decisive step is the
   regicide (ch. 2): Louis XVI walked to death through 60,000 armed men "who had
   not a shot for Santerre" — a national crime, with the whole nation as
   accomplice; set against Charles I, whose executioner hid his face and whose
   people showed concern (`<Ref>` english-revolution-as-test). Then the
   unusual inference: the revolutionaries had to fall by their own accomplices,
   because a restored king could not have punished them — "what could French
   magistrates have done with three or four hundred Damienses?" (anchor Damiens:
   the would-be regicide quartered alive over four hours in 1757). And the
   paradox the lesson must give at full strength: "only Jacobinism could have
   saved France and the monarchy" — the Terror kept France whole for the king
   who would inherit it (the royalist army's imagined speech, pp. 38–40). A
   reaction "must always be equal to the action", so a violent counter-revolution
   is rejected. This is Maistre's realism, and the first evidence against the
   stock portrait.
3. **Can a nation give itself a constitution?** The question Burke's argument
   left open (`<Ref>` burke-reflections, the last exercise). Burke said no
   generation knows enough; the English constitution grew. Maistre takes the
   step Burke would not: no constitution whatever is made by deliberation. The
   thirteen theses of ch. 6, compressed to the load-bearing five: (i) no
   constitution results from deliberation; the rights of the people are never
   written, and written laws only declare rights that existed before; (ii)
   "circumstances do everything, and men are part of the circumstances"; (iii)
   the more that is written, the weaker the institution (Sparta wrote nothing);
   (iv) no nation can give itself liberty if it is not already free — "liberty
   has always been the gift of kings"; (v) legislators are founders — kings or
   high nobles who found polity and religion together — never scholars. The
   evidence is inductive: the die thrown a billion times that never shows "large
   republic" (ch. 4); the English constitution is no model but "the result of an
   infinity of circumstances". The quarrel with Burke stated plainly: Burke
   defended 1688 and the Act of Settlement as a wise necessity; Maistre says a
   settlement is paper, and England owes its liberty to accidents no one
   designed — so Burke conceded too much when he let Price's question be
   answered from statutes at all. Then the sentence everything turns on: "The
   Constitution of 1795, like its predecessors, is made for man. But there is
   no such thing as man in the world. In my lifetime I have seen Frenchmen,
   Italians, Russians, etc.; thanks to Montesquieu, I even know that one can be
   Persian; but as for man, I declare that I have never met him in my life; if
   he exists, he is unknown to me." A constitution is the solution to a problem
   — given the population, mores, religion, geography, wealth, find the laws
   that suit it — and "a constitution made for all nations is made for none".
   State the objection the course owns (Muller fn 20; Berlin XI): the argument
   is circular — what has lasted is divine, and what is divine will last — and
   let it stand until link 7.
4. **What then holds a society together, if not consent and not a written
   law?** The reader has two answers already: Hume's opinion (`<Ref>`
   hume-against-the-original-contract — force is always on the side of the
   governed) and Hobbes's sword (`<Ref>`). Maistre accepts Hume's denial of
   consent and gives it a theological ground (the Seventh Dialogue's "circle of
   opinion" no sovereign traces is one clause here). Against the obvious
   attempt — the sword — Maistre agrees with Hobbes and goes further: not the
   sword as threat but the man who wields it against the
   condemned. The First Dialogue's frame (a boat on the Neva, three men, the
   question why the wicked prosper), then the set piece: the executioner. "So
   who is this inexplicable being who, when there are so many … honourable
   professions … has chosen that of torturing and putting to death his own
   kind?" The house other men move away from; the breaking on the wheel told in
   the present tense; "No one can break men on the wheel better than I"; the
   gold coins thrown from afar; "Is this a man? Yes." Then the pivot: "all
   greatness, all power, all subordination rests on the executioner; he is both
   the horror and the bond of human association. Remove this incomprehensible
   agent from the world, and in a moment order gives way to chaos, thrones fall,
   and society disappears." God is the author of sovereignty and of punishment,
   "the two poles" on which the earth is suspended. Make the argument's
   structure visible: (a) evil exists and acts constantly, so it must be
   constantly repressed (the Laws of Manu: "Punishment governs all mankind");
   (b) repression needs a man who will do it; (c) no motive in human nature
   explains him, so his existence is a "fiat" of creative power; (d) therefore
   the order we live in rests on something reason cannot ground and honour
   cannot approach. This is the goal's "authority rests on something reason
   cannot reach" in its most concrete form. Pair with "things are quiet" = "the
   executioners are resting" (ch. 10) so the reader sees the thread from
   Considerations to Dialogues.
5. **Bonald: the same conclusion by system.** Where Maistre argues by paradox,
   his ally argues by parallel: the obligations owed to God, to kings and to
   fathers have one structure. Anchor Bonald (Louis de Bonald, 1754–1840, an
   émigré officer whose Théorie du pouvoir appeared in 1796, later a deputy
   under the Restoration). Society precedes the individual: a man is born into a
   family, a language, a religion he did not choose, and "man" apart from them is
   the abstraction Maistre never met. The family is the model of every society:
   power (the father), minister (the mother), subject (the child). From this the
   one argument the dossier lets the lesson reconstruct in full — On Divorce
   (1801): marriage is not a contract between two, because its end is "the
   production of children" and the child is a third party who cannot consent to
   its dissolution; domestic society is "not a commercial association", and
   exit is not symmetric (the man leaves with what he had, the woman does not);
   natural affections are not instincts but learned in the family; a state that
   permits divorce is "accomplice" to the breaking of the first social bond —
   and the same reasoning dissolves the social contract: if the bond that makes
   society cannot be unmade by the parties, no bond that can be unmade by the
   parties made society. Religion is the state's foundation for the same reason
   marriage is a sacrament: a bond needs a party above the two who made it.
   One sentence on consequences: Bonald got divorce abolished in France in
   1816 (the law stood until 1884) — VERIFY, not in the dossier. Then the
   difference the course states as its own (Berlin II; Muller headnote):
   Bonald "a man of clear mind and narrow vision" who tried to restore the old
   world as a system; Maistre understood the old world was dying.
6. **How does a counter-revolution happen, then?** If no nation chooses its
   government, neither can royalists choose to restore one. Maistre's answer
   (ch. 9–10): the people "count for nothing" in revolutions; four or five
   persons give France a king; the courier scenario — a rumour at Bordeaux or
   Lyons that Paris has acknowledged the king, officers suspect each other, pay
   stops, a governor hands over his keys and is made a marshal, "there is only
   one cry" — a cascade, not a decision. "They always accept, they never
   choose." Hence the counter-revolution "will be not a contrary revolution, but
   the contrary of revolution": destroyers need many vices, restorers draw on
   nature; the surgeon's hands set the joint and the pain stops. Monk's 6,000
   men and 1660 are the precedent (`<Ref>` english-revolution-as-test). Then the
   realism: Maistre says the Restoration must come after peace, must not avenge
   (anarchy is needed for revenge; order excludes it), must keep the sales of
   Church lands (the buyers' interest is the new regime's best guarantee) — and
   after 1814 he judged that the Restoration would not last and that the
   Revolution could no more be undone than the lake bottled. Define
   *counter-revolution* here in his sense.
7. **The charge: is this the first draft of fascism?** Put at full strength,
   stated as the course's own ("the charge, made most forcefully in the
   mid-twentieth century"; Berlin in References). Its parts: (a) Maistre
   inverts the Enlightenment point by point — faith for reason, hierarchy for
   equality, sin for perfectibility, war for harmony — and does so as a total
   believer, "a Jacobin in reverse"; (b) he makes a virtue of irrationality:
   "religion is superior to reason not because it returns more convincing
   answers but because it returns no answer at all … it commands"; only what
   cannot be defended (hereditary monarchy, marriage) cannot be refuted and so
   endures; (c) his picture of society is the executioner's — order rests on
   terror, men want to immolate themselves and to dominate as much as to be
   free; (d) his list of enemies — Protestants, Jansenists, deists, Freemasons,
   Jews, scientists, lawyers, journalists, intellectuals, "la secte" — is the
   first precise draft of what the counter-revolutionary movement would attack;
   (e) the chain society → state → sovereignty → infallibility → God and the
   demand that individual reason be "annihilated" in the national reason like a
   river in the ocean; (f) the formula "we shall shoot you down in order to
   punish you for not wanting what you do want". Verdict: the doctrine of
   violence, self-immolation, the national soul and hatred of critical
   intellectuals is "the heart of all totalitarian doctrines"; Maistre is "a
   remarkable, and terrifying, prophet of our day".
8. **The replies, and where the lesson concedes.** (i) On the logic: the
   anti-contract argument is correct — a promise presupposes a society in which
   promises bind, so society is not made by a promise (the charge's own author
   concedes this); and the psychological realism (men desire to submit and to
   dominate) has proved "at times a better guide to human conduct than the faith
   of believers in reason" — also conceded. (ii) On the genealogy: the strongest
   version — Maistre as a *source* of fascism — is unsupported; his influence
   ran to nineteenth-century ultramontane Catholics through Du Pape and to
   Maurras's Action française, not to Rome or Berlin; "fascism" is left
   undefined; and fascism as the worship of will and nation is man-centred,
   where Maistre's whole system is God-centred and anti-nationalist in its
   ultramontanism — the charge is "theologically unmusical". (iii) On the man:
   Maistre was a realist who said the Restoration could not last, called
   militarism "la bâtonocratie", wanted to meet Napoleon and was suspected at
   Turin as a "royalist Jacobin" — not a nostalgic. (iv) What survives: that he
   "glorifies war, declares it mysterious and divine" is substantially right
   (one sentence here; the starred section below reads the text for readers who
   want it); and that his method is a lawyer's
   brief — the circle of link 3 — is right. The course's own position: the
   charge is best read as a warning about where the executioner's argument goes
   when its theology is removed and its sociology kept; the reader is left to
   weigh. Seed the distinction `what-conservatism-is` will teach: the reactionary
   reads history as a rupture, as the revolutionary does; Burke does not (one
   sentence, Lilla in References).
9. **Where this leads.** One paragraph, in the core path (it must not depend on
   the starred section). Three hand-offs: (i) "There is no such thing as man" —
   only Frenchmen, Italians, Russians — raises the question what makes a people
   one, which a Lutheran pastor had answered with language and custom and a Berlin
   philosopher will turn into a nation's mission (`<Ref>`
   herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation, the next lesson). (ii) The decision: the
   counter-revolutionaries' real discovery, a later reader will say, was
   authority that decides — sovereignty and infallibility "perfectly synonymous",
   any government good once established, a question decided "without delay and
   without appeal"; a Spanish deputy of 1849, seeing no legitimate king left,
   will draw the conclusion Maistre did not — dictatorship (`<Ref>`
   donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship) — and a jurist of the next century will set it
   against the liberal "everlasting conversation" (`<Ref>`
   schmitt-political-theology). (iii) The open problem: Burke grounds order in
   reason men inherit, Maistre in a Providence reason cannot reach; a professor in
   Berlin will claim the state is rational without being designed (`<Ref>`
   hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state).
10. **★ War is divine: the Seventh Dialogue.** A `## ★` section after link 8's
   replies and before the Conclusion (L2b): nothing in the core path, the
   Conclusion or the exercises outside the ★-marked one may depend on it.
   Problem (from the executioner the reader has just met): society lets two men
   kill by law, and honours the one who kills more and more innocently — why?
   The chain, compressed to six steps, each with its "because":
   (a) *Why do men march at all?* "Kings order, you march" fails, because
   sovereigns command only within "the circle of things acknowledged by opinion"
   (Peter the Great could send thousands to die and nearly faced revolt over
   beards; `<Ref>` hume-against-the-original-contract for opinion) — so the
   puzzle is war's *facility*, not its possibility.
   (b) *The stranger.* Told of the two legal killers, a visitor from another
   world would honour the executioner and shun the soldier; we do the reverse;
   the two "touch … as in a circle 1° touches 360°". The soldier's honour has
   rules (he may execute only soldiers, with soldiers' weapons, for military
   crimes) — so honour is a code about *what kind* of killing is clean, and "the
   most honoured thing in the world has always been the right to shed innocent
   blood innocently". Glory is a fact that must be explained.
   (c) *Is war a relic of a state of nature nations have not yet left?* The
   contract theorists' answer (`<Ref>` hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant:
   sovereigns as gladiators; `<Ref>` rousseau-inequality-and-general-will). Two
   replies: there never was a state of nature — Rousseau's own frontispiece shows
   the Hottentot going back to his fellows, "society is as old as man"; and every
   argument against a society of nations works equally against a society of
   individuals, so if nations never contracted, something stronger than interest
   keeps them apart — "an occult and terrible law demanding human blood". Seam:
   from the contract story one expects war to recede with civilisation; Maistre
   (Considerations ch. 3) counts France at war forty years in ninety-six, by his
   own count, and says the flow of blood is roughly constant.
   (d) *What is this law?* The widening lens — violence through plants and
   animals to man, who "kills … to kill", until "man himself is charged with
   slaughtering man"; the earth "an immense altar on which every living thing
   must be immolated without end". Then the link to the executioner's theodicy
   (link 4): human justice punishes too few and the age has abolished atonement,
   so the debt is collected in war — "if human justice struck all criminals there
   would be no war". Define *expiation* and *reversibility* (the innocent suffer
   for the guilty — Considerations ch. 3 pp. 61–62; `<Ref>` augustine-two-cities
   for sacrifice and the earthly peace) and the course's concept
   `war-as-expiation`.
   (e) *In what sense "divine"?* "War is therefore divine in itself, since it is a
   law of the world" — divine as a *law of the world*, not as approved by God (the
   First Dialogue's distinction: God authors the evil that punishes, not the evil
   that defiles). The seven senses as a compact table (figure); the last — the
   force that decides it — carried by "what is a lost battle? … It is a battle one
   believes one has lost": if victory is moral, not arithmetical, war is the one
   human activity whose outcome cannot be engineered. One clause: a Russian
   novelist read these pages and gave the thought to his generals (Tolstoy in
   References).
   (f) *Two later accounts on which war is no accident*, as a three-column ledger
   (why war exists / what it does for the state / who judges / what would end
   it), each in two sentences and each a forward `<Ref>`: Hegel — war "the
   ethical moment", "the winds preserve the sea from the foulness of calm", no
   Praetor between states, world history the judge (`<Ref>`
   hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state); Schmitt — the political as the grouping of
   friend and enemy with "the real possibility of killing", the enemy public, not
   private (`<Ref>` schmitt-concept-of-the-political). Where they agree with
   Maistre: war is necessary, not accidental; where they differ: Hegel's
   necessity is the state's self-knowledge, Schmitt's a criterion, and neither
   gives a theodicy.
   Close the ★ section on what the argument costs: Maistre's idealised
   old-regime war (pp. 214–215) set against the Revolutionary wars he was living
   through (Lebrun's corrections); and the charge revisited in one sentence — on
   war the reply concedes that Maistre "glorifies war, declares it mysterious and
   divine", and keeps the hedge that he condemned conquest and "the rule of the
   stick" and never praised war for the nation's sake: his altar is God's, not
   the nation's.

   mistakes because they decide nothing. Reconstruct the argument from
   mediocrity in three steps.
2. **Two kings, two crowds (ch. 2, pp. 34–35):** Charles I's executioner hiding

   Then Damiens (pp. 36–37) and the question what a restored king could have
   done with "three or four hundred Damienses" — the derivation that the

3. **The royalist army's speech (ch. 2, pp. 38–40):** the thought experiment in
   full — why a royalist should fight for the Committee of Public Safety to

   the monarchy". The course's best evidence for Maistre's realism against the
   stock portrait.
4. **The thirteen theses as a table (ch. 6, pp. 91–96)**, compressed to five
   rows with the thesis, Maistre's example (Sparta; the Romans; England) and

   whole and parsed: the constitution as a problem with given parameters.
5. **The die and the sixteen thousand years (ch. 4, pp. 66, 70–71):** the
   inductive argument (four thousand years of throws, no "large republic") and
   the arithmetic reductio (15 million eligible, 250 replaced a year, each
   sovereign once in sixteen thousand years — "sovereigns condemned to die
   without having reigned"). Bound the numbers in the sentence; they are
   Maistre's, and the point is rhetorical structure, not demography.
6. **The executioner (First Dialogue, pp. 19–21; spd.txt l.2517–2603)** —
   reconstructed step by step as in link 4, with the four-step argument drawn

   soldier complains that bullets hit only honest men) so the reader sees that
   the executioner is an answer to theodicy before it is politics, and with
   "things are quiet" (ch. 10, p. 142).
7. **Bonald's three-party marriage (On Divorce ch. 4, Muller l.2156 ff.):**
   father, mother, child — the child represented by the state (hence the state

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or
  idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only
  through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with
  who applied it and when.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** *Savoy* (an Alpine duchy ruled by the king of
  Sardinia from Turin, French-speaking, annexed by France in 1792 — Maistre was
  a senator of its high court; anchor at the opening's "Savoyard"); *Lausanne*
  (Swiss city on Lake Geneva where he wrote the Considerations in exile);
  *St Petersburg* (he was Sardinia's envoy there 1803–17; the Dialogues are set
  on the Neva in 1809); *the Terror* (the Convention's rule by the Committee of
  Public Safety, 1793–94; Robespierre; the guillotine); *Thermidor* (July 1794,
  the fall of Robespierre); *the Directory* and *the Constitution of 1795* (the
  third revolutionary constitution, two councils, five directors — Maistre's
  target in ch. 6–7); *Vendémiaire* (October 1795, Bonaparte's grapeshot
  against the Paris royalist rising — "you can be sure you want it", p. 125);
  *Jacobin*; *Damiens*; *Santerre*; *Monk* (`<Ref>` english-revolution-as-test);
  *ultramontane* (holding the Pope's authority above national churches and
  kings — anchor when Du Pape is mentioned in the reply); *Du Pape* (1819,
  Maistre's book on papal infallibility, his most influential in his century);
  *Action française* and *Maurras* (the French nationalist movement founded in
  1899 and its leader — one clause, only in the reply); *Vaucanson* (the
  eighteenth-century maker of mechanical automata — a flute-player, a duck);
  *Laws of Manu* (the ancient Hindu law code, read by Maistre in Sir William
  Jones's translation); *theodicy* (define: the attempt to justify God's
  goodness given evil — the Dialogues' subject); *Providence* in Maistre's
  sense; *counter-revolution* in Maistre's sense.
- **Dates:** 1793 (regicide), 1797 (Considerations), 1821 (Dialogues published)
  exact — contingency is the point (the Considerations predicted; the Dialogues
  were written after Napoleon's fall); the rest to the decade.
- **THE DOSSIER IS THIN ON BONALD.** Exactly three places carry him: Muller's
  On Divorce excerpts (conservatism-anthology.txt l.2110–2270, with the headnote
  l.2116); Berlin II pp. 100–102 (crooked-timber.txt l.4040–4125); Schmitt PT
  ch. 4 l.3033–3089. Nothing from Théorie du pouvoir politique et religieux
  (1796) or the Législation primitive (1802) is cached. The lesson therefore
  gives Bonald ONE section (link 5), built on On Divorce, and states the four
  points the course.yaml names (society precedes the individual; the family as
  model; against divorce and contract; religion as the state's foundation) with
  the first and fourth as summary claims and the second and third reconstructed
  from the text. Do not invent Bonald quotations beyond the excerpts; the
  "power–minister–subject" triad is standard Bonald but is not in the cached
  excerpts — state it as his doctrine without quotation marks. The 1816 divorce
  law is public record but not in the dossier — verify before stating.
- **The starred section (war) is skippable by construction (L2b).** Mark it `## ★`;
  place it after the replies and before the Conclusion; the Conclusion, the
  forward paragraph (link 9) and every exercise except the one marked ★ must read
  correctly without it. The core path keeps only one sentence on war (link 8
  reply iv). Chapter 3 of the Considerations belongs to the ★ section too.
- **★ "War is divine" means "a law of the world", not "approved by God"** —
  state this at first use, or the reader takes the title as blasphemy or as
  cheerleading. Maistre is not a militarist (he condemns conquest and
  "bâtonocratie"); say it once without using it to soften the altar passage.
- **★ The idealised eighteenth-century war (pp. 214–215) is a claim to test**, not
  history the course adopts; Maistre's casualty and war-year figures are his,
  bound as his. The momentum "formula" is an analogy — no arithmetic (E2).
- **★ Hegel and Schmitt enter only as a two-sentence ledger each**, forward
  `<Ref>`s; do not teach §§321–340 or the friend–enemy criterion here.
- **★ Terms (L11):** *the Seventh Dialogue* (the seventh evening, set in 1809);
  *the Abbé de Saint-Pierre* (Charles-Irénée Castel, whose plan for perpetual
  peace in Europe, 1713, Rousseau abridged); *expiation*; *reversibility*; *Te
  Deum* (the hymn sung for victories); *the geese of the Capitol* (Rome, 390 BC,
  saved when geese woke the garrison); *Peter the Great's beards* (his 1698
  decree); *Praetor* (the Roman judge between citizens — there is none between
  states). At most three per paragraph.
- **The 15,479 laws and "three constitutions in five years" are the application
  lesson's artefact**; one sentence here, no table.
- **The stock portrait is a target, not the lesson.** The course does not teach
  "Pope, King and Hangman"; it teaches the arguments and then the charge. Equally,
  the course does not adopt the charge: the reader weighs. No moralising either
  way; Maistre's realism (ch. 2, ch. 10, Berlin IX) is evidence, not
  exculpation.
- **The executioner is theodicy before it is politics.** Its home is the
  question "why do the wicked prosper?" and the answer that punishment is a
  divine law; the political reading (order rests on terror) is the charge's
  reading — give Maistre's own frame first.
- **"There is no such thing as man" is not relativism.** Maistre holds a
  universal truth (the Church) and denies a universal politics; say so, or the
  reader will file him with the historicists the Strauss lesson later attacks.
- **Maistre vs Burke is a real quarrel, not a continuum.** The Act of
  Settlement, the contract, the praise of the English constitution (Berlin VI
  pp. 128–129; Considerations ch. 4 and 6); Maistre admired Burke and
  disagreed. State both.
- **Translation and licence.** Lebrun's translations are copyrighted; keep
  quotations to a sentence or two each and paraphrase the set pieces in the
  lesson's own words with the pointer. Lebrun's notes correct Maistre (the army
  oath, casualty figures): use them silently as facts, name Lebrun in
  References.
- **Period matter:** the First Dialogue's passages on disease as punishment of
  vice and the Senator on heredity are not needed and are left out; the Calas
  passage (Maistre doubting a famous miscarriage of justice) is in the text and
  may be quoted as evidence of the method's cost.
- **Names ONLY in References:** Berlin, Garrard, Lebrun, Muller, Davidson
  (Bonald's translator), Lilla, Fawcett, McMahon, Cioran, Faguet, Sainte-Beuve,

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval, warm-up (E3).* State in one clause each: Burke's reason no
   generation can design a constitution, Maistre's reason no nation can give
   itself one, and the step Maistre takes that Burke does not (the English
   constitution as accident; written law as paper). Name the texts (S1
   l.980–1119; Considerations ch. 6).
2. *Boundary probe.* Apply Maistre's thesis that no durable institution results
   from deliberation to the one case he names and dismisses — the United States
   (ch. 7 pp. 107–109: not a tabula rasa; borrowed English elements; the
   Washington prophecy). What would Maistre have to say about a constitution
   that was deliberated, written, and lasted two centuries? The reader should
   find that his four points are an escape hatch (any success is credited to
   inherited elements), and that the Washington prophecy is a falsified
   prediction of the kind his own method invites.
3. *Document reasoning (sourcing drill).* The executioner passage: author (a
   Savoyard envoy in Russia), date (written c. 1809, published 1821), audience
   (a Catholic European readership after Napoleon), what it records (an
   eighteenth-century execution by breaking on the wheel — a real procedure,
   abolished in France in 1791), what it argues (order rests on punishment, which
   rests on God), what it omits (any case where punishment itself is the crime —
   the Calas passage that follows tries to close this), and what it can be USED
   to establish: Maistre's doctrine of authority, and the mental world of a man
   who in youth belonged to a confraternity that attended executions — not a
   fact about societies.
4. *Plausible false claim to refute.* "Maistre wanted the Revolution undone and
   the old regime restored exactly." Name the failing steps: "the contrary of
   revolution, not a contrary revolution" (ch. 10); the Jacobin paradox (ch. 2);
   keep the sales of Church lands and refuse vengeance (ch. 10); the lake cannot
   be bottled (1814). Then say what the claim gets right (he wanted the throne
   and the altar back).
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped (Bonald).* Run Bonald's divorce argument on
   the social contract explicitly: list the premises (a bond with a third party
   present cannot be dissolved by two; society's "third party" is God/the unborn;
   the contract theorists make society a two-party bond) and derive his
   conclusion; then test it against Burke's partnership of the living, the dead
   and the unborn (`<Ref>` burke-reflections) — same conclusion, is it the same
   argument? (No: Burke's third parties are generations, Bonald's is God; one is
   epistemic, the other theological.)
6. *Synthesis / counter-argument (the charge).* Someone claims that the
   executioner passage shows Maistre to be a theorist of terror in the
   twentieth-century sense. Name two facts the claim fails to account for (the
   passage is an answer to the problem of evil, not a programme; Maistre rejects
   a violent counter-revolution and vengeance, ch. 2 and 10) and state what
   would have to be true for it to survive (that the sociology — order rests on
   punishment men cannot rationally approve — can be detached from the theology
   and still be held; which is what the charge's author says happened).
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation`.** Maistre says the
   Constitution of 1795 "is made for man", that "there is no such thing as man",
   and that he has met only Frenchmen, Italians, Russians. Take him at his word
   and ask what makes a Frenchman a Frenchman rather than a man: list what a
   constitution "made for the French" would have to know (Maistre's own list —
   population, mores, religion, geography) and decide which of these a people
   could carry with it if it lost its state. The reader should reach the thought
   that language, custom and memory make a people prior to any constitution. The
   solution says in one sentence that the next lesson's Lutheran pastor built a
   philosophy of history on exactly that, and that a Berlin philosopher turned it
   into a nation's mission, with the `<Ref>`.
8. *★ Boundary probe (the starred section).* Maistre's own counterfactual: "if
   human justice struck all criminals there would be no war" (Seventh Dialogue p.
   217). Suppose it did. Does the altar passage (pp. 216–217) still hold? The
   reader should find that the "universal law of violent death" is argued from
   nature, not from unpunished crime, so the Dialogue gives war two independent
   grounds and the counterfactual removes only one — and say which of the two a
   later account that calls war "the ethical moment" of the state shares (`<Ref>`
   hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state, forward; the solution names §324).

# Research brief: michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites (Michels and Pareto: the iron law of oligarchy and the circulation of elites)

status: planned
module: crisis-of-liberal-europe
type: core
goal: Analyze Michels's iron law of oligarchy and Pareto's theory of the circulation of elites, and evaluate whether democracy is possible on their account

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `mosca-ruling-class` (prerequisite, the previous lesson):
  the ruling class (`ruling-class`) — a hundred in concert beat a thousand
  alone; the political formula (`political-formula`) — the principle by which a
  minority justifies its rule in terms the ruled believe; juridical defense and
  the balance of social forces; "the representative has himself elected"; the
  aristocratic and democratic tendencies; and the open question with which that
  lesson ended: does an organization FOUNDED on the rule of the many escape the
  law? Further back: Marx's class struggle and the party that will abolish
  class rule (`marx-manifesto`, `class-struggle`); Le Bon's crowd —
  suggestible, moved by images and leaders (`taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`,
  `crowd-psychology`); Carlyle's hero-worship (`carlyle-heroes`) — Michels
  cites Carlyle on "the chief of talkers"; Rousseau's refusal of representation
  — "the people of England … is free only during the election of members of
  parliament" (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`); Machiavelli's lion and
  fox (`machiavelli-effectual-truth`, Prince XVIII) — Pareto's image; the
  Polybian cycle as the oldest account of regimes decaying in a fixed order
  (`polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`).
- **Contributes:** Two moves that complete elite theory. Michels tests Mosca's
  law on its hardest case — the German Social Democratic Party, built to
  realise the rule of the many — and finds that organization itself, through
  size, delegation, expertise and the members' own needs, produces a stable
  leadership that escapes control: "Who says organization, says oligarchy."
  Pareto adds what makes elites rise and fall: men act mostly from constant
  non-logical sentiments (residues) and justify themselves afterwards with
  variable theories (derivations); a governing elite that loses the will to use
  force passes "from the lions to the foxes" and is replaced from below —
  "history is a graveyard of aristocracies". The reader leaves able to state the
  iron law and its causes, the residue/derivation distinction, the circulation
  of elites, and the strongest answer to "is democracy possible?" that survives
  them: competition among elites, which the voters decide.
- **Hands off:** The lesson decides the iron law: a strong tendency inside
  mass organisations, not an iron law, and not decisive between parties
  (strong); Pareto's residues and success-defined elite cannot be falsified.
  The Weimar record of the SPD's toleration (`article-48-weimar`) uses that
  verdict. Two problems pass on. (i) If what moves men is sentiment and
  not theory, and if a class needs a belief to fight, then the belief a
  revolutionary class needs is not a programme it can check but an image it can
  live by — `sorel-reflections-on-violence` (next module; Sorel is cited by
  Pareto at §2193 n., and the concept `circulation-of-elites` is reinforced
  there). (ii) The next lesson in this module, `mann-reflections`, leaves "who
  rules?" for another question that elite theory does not ask: what politics
  itself does to a people's inner life — say the turn in one sentence, no more.
  Forward, one clause each: Burnham's managerial class as the new ruling elite
  (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`); Lasch's meritocratic elite
  that no longer renews itself from below (`lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`).
- **Spine step:** "Liberal Europe breaks: elite theorists show that a minority
  always rules, Mann sets Kultur against Zivilisation, Spengler foretells the
  West's winter, Ortega diagnoses the mass-man, Schmitt names the political as
  friend and enemy and the sovereign as he who decides the exception — and
  Weimar is the case"

## VERDICT

Lesson verdict (REGISTER.md, Module 7). The brief carries it exactly as graded
there.

- **Claim judged:** "Who says organisation says oligarchy" as an iron law, and
  Pareto's circulation of elites.
- **Verdict:** the law **holds as a strong tendency inside mass
  organisations; fails as an iron law; does not decide outcomes between
  parties**. Pareto's residues and his success-defined elite cannot be
  falsified. Grade: **strong**.
- **What decides it:** Michels's own continuity data (the survival count of
  SPD congress delegates and officials, Part Two ch. I) show a tendency, not a
  law. The International Typographical Union is a counter-case: two organised
  internal parties alternated in office for decades (Lipset, Trow & Coleman,
  *Union Democracy*, 1956). The competitive answer (Schumpeter's method, via
  Muller) concedes oligarchy inside each party and denies that it settles the
  contest between them. Pareto's residues are named after the conduct they
  explain, and his elite is defined by success, so no conduct and no outcome
  can count against either.
- **Strongest objection:** the ITU is one union, unusual in its craft and its
  printers' culture — its own students treat it as a deviant case whose
  conditions are rare (bind the wording to *Union Democracy*); so
  the law stands as a law with one freak. Why it does not overturn the
  verdict: a law in Michels's sense ("iron") admits no exception; one
  well-documented counter-case is enough to turn it into a tendency, and the
  rarity of the conditions is what "strong tendency" already concedes. Nor does
  the objection touch the second half: even where every party is an
  oligarchy, voters decide between them.
- **What would overturn it:** a large, long-lived organisation with
  competitive internal parties whose leadership nonetheless proves
  irremovable.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "If any party could practise democracy, it
should be the German Social Democrats of 1910: their whole programme was rule by
the many, their members were workers who distrusted bosses, and their statutes
made every office elective. A young sociologist who had been a member studies
the party and finds a permanent leadership, a salaried bureaucracy and a
congress that obeys. An Italian economist, meanwhile, argues that history is a
graveyard of aristocracies. The lesson follows both and asks what is left of the
idea that the many can rule."

Anchors (L11): the SPD — the Social Democratic Party of Germany, founded 1875
from Lassalle's and Marx's followers, illegal under Bismarck's anti-socialist
laws until 1890, by 1910 the largest party in Europe with its own press, unions,
schools and treasury (the reader has the Manifesto, Ref `marx-manifesto`; one
clause); Robert Michels (1876–1936), German-born, a party member who left
Germany for an Italian university (check the details listed in Sources before
stating more); Vilfredo Pareto (1848–1923), engineer turned economist at
Lausanne, whose *Treatise of General Sociology* appeared in 1916. Hold Pareto's
anchor until link 5. The newcomer's problem: Mosca (Ref) showed that every
STATE is ruled by an organized minority; the SPD is the test that could break
the law — a body whose formula, statutes and members all point the other way.

1. **Why can't the members rule the party directly?** Obvious attempt: they
   can — meet, debate, vote. Michels (Part One A ch. II): numbers forbid it.
   A party of tens of thousands in one city (Berlin's SPD had over 90,000
   members) cannot assemble, deliberate or act quickly; even the devices that
   divide a people into small sections only push the problem up a level. So
   the members delegate; and the delegates, once there are hundreds of them,
   delegate again. A fighting party adds speed and discipline: "democracy is
   not for home consumption, but is rather an article made for export" (l.2308).
   Answer: "Organization implies the tendency to oligarchy" (l.1768) — and the
   weak cannot do without organization, which is their only weapon (ch. I).
   Ref `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: Rousseau had refused
   representation on exactly these grounds (free only at the election); Michels
   grants him the diagnosis and denies him the remedy, since a large body
   cannot do without delegates. Heading: "Numbers forbid direct rule".
2. **Why can't the members control their delegates?** Obvious attempt:
   elections, recall, short terms. Michels's chain (Part One C, Part Two ch. I),
   one link at a time: the work of a large party is specialised (finance, press,
   parliament, negotiation); those who do it learn it and the members do not;
   so the leader becomes indispensable — "The leader's principal source of power
   is found in his indispensability" (l.4428); the members, who work all day,
   cannot judge the work — "The incompetence of the masses … constitutes the
   most solid foundation of the power of the leaders" (l.4448); so elections
   return the same men. The record Michels gives (l.4817): of 200 delegates to
   the SPD's congress at Cologne in 1893, 60 were still in the front rank in
   1910; of 311 at the Italian socialists' Reggio Emilia congress, 102 — bind:
   about a third of a party's leading men still leading after seventeen years,
   where a German minister lasted on average a little over four (summary gem,
   Part Two ch. I p. 100 — verify). The leaders even print the ballot: the
   congress receives a paper bearing the names of the retiring committee
   (l.4953–4967) — "corriger la démocratie". Rotation, tried, is abandoned
   because it removes the competent (the Cologne chamber of commerce,
   l.4875–4882). Heading: "Expertise makes leaders irremovable". KEEPER number:
   the survival count.
3. **Do the members WANT to control them?** The reader expects workers who
   distrust bosses (the opening). Michels's psychological case (Part One B):
   most members do not attend — "An electoral right exists, but no electoral
   duty" (l.2757), so "Effective power is here in inverse ratio to the number of
   those who exercise it" (l.2772); the mass needs guidance and is grateful to
   those who suffered prison for it (ch. VI, l.3140); it venerates its leaders
   (Lassalle's progress through the Rhineland in 1864; children named after
   Lassalle and Marx — gems). Ref `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`: Le
   Bon's crowd seeks a leader; Michels finds the same in an organized party, not
   a street crowd. Ref `carlyle-heroes`: Michels quotes Carlyle on the orator as
   "chief of talkers". The leaders' side (Part Three): power changes those who
   hold it; the former worker becomes a salaried official with an interest in
   his post; "The revolutionaries of to-day become the reactionaries of
   to-morrow" (l.9142). Seam (say the reversal): from a party of men who
   distrust bosses one expects the tightest control; the members' own needs
   loosen it.
4. **Is this a law, and has it exceptions?** The formulation (Part Six ch. IV,
   l.19176): "It is organization which gives birth to the dominion of the
   elected over the electors … Who says organization, says oligarchy" — causes
   in order: technical indispensability first, then the leaders' interests, the
   members' gratitude and passivity. The phrase "iron law" occurs only as a
   chapter title (l.18012); the Preface calls the law "beyond good and evil" and
   disclaims any charge of hypocrisy (l.263–269). Michels tests every remedy
   (Part Five): the referendum (little used, easily steered, and Bonaparte's
   instrument); leaders who renounce their class; syndicalism (it too rests on
   representation); anarchism (it refuses the party and keeps the love of
   power) — each reproduces leadership. The application to the party's own goal:
   "The socialists might conquer, but not socialism, which would perish in the
   moment of its adherents' triumph" (l.18735) — the dictatorship of the
   proletariat would be the dictatorship of its leaders (Ref `mosca-ruling-class`,
   l.3334, the same forecast from the other side). Michels's own test case, in
   the 1915 English edition (Part Six ch. III): in August 1914 the SPD's
   Reichstag deputies voted for war credits; Michels reads the vote as the
   organization's interest — its treasury and its legal existence — deciding
   against its internationalist programme. Present this as Michels's
   interpretation (exercise 3 shows what the vote can and cannot establish). What Michels allows
   against the law (the exceptions he admits): the "ideological tendency of
   democracy towards criticism and control" as one of "two regulative
   principles" (l.19470), and the waves that "break ever on the same shoal" but
   "are ever renewed" (l.19523). Heading: "'Who says organization says
   oligarchy' — the law and the remedies that fail".
5. **Pareto: why do ruling minorities think they rule by right?** Turn to the
   second author with one plain sentence: Mosca called the belief that justifies
   a minority a formula; an engineer-economist asks what moves men to hold such
   beliefs at all. Anchor Pareto here. Obvious attempt: they hold them because
   they are argued for. Pareto's answer (Vol. II §§845–868): most conduct is
   non-logical; it springs from constant sentiments (RESIDUES) and is justified
   after the fact by variable theories (DERIVATIONS). The worked analysis is his
   own (§863): baptism is explained as the removal of original sin; pagans used
   lustral water for purification; other rites use blood or fire — the water
   varies, the explanation varies, what stays is the sentiment that lost
   integrity can be restored by a rite. Two classes carry the political theory
   (§888–889, §991): Class I, the instinct for COMBINATIONS (inventing,
   scheming, adapting — the progressive element); Class II, the PERSISTENCE OF
   AGGREGATES (attachment to family, nation, church, the "cement"). Applied to
   the reader's own world (§§932–936, §1073): democracy, progress and universal
   suffrage function in his age as deities did in others; "socialism is a
   religion" is false of its derivations and true of its residues — with
   Pareto's own disclaimer (§936) that he is not arguing universal suffrage is
   harmful, only that its halo is not an experimental fact. Ref
   `mosca-ruling-class`: this is Mosca's formula with a psychology underneath it
   (the systematisation an editor of both points out — Livingston, References).
   Heading: "Residues and derivations: why the theory comes after the
   sentiment".
6. **Why do elites fall?** Obvious attempt: they are overthrown by the
   oppressed, as Marx says. Pareto (Vol. III §§2026–2059): define the elite
   without praise or blame — give everyone a grade in his branch of activity, as
   in a school examination (§2027: the best lawyer 10, the lawyer without
   clients 1, the clever swindler 8 or 9); the elite is those with the highest
   grades; the GOVERNING elite those of them who play a part in government
   (§2032–2034). In fact membership goes by labels (titles, inheritance) that
   drift away from capacity (§§2035–2040). Hence circulation: the capable rise
   from below and the incapable decay above; "Aristocracies do not last …
   History is a graveyard of aristocracies" (§2053); the governing elite "flows
   on like a river", with floods (§2056); when superior elements pile up below
   and decadent ones above, and the upper stratum shrinks from force, there is
   revolution, led by defectors from above (§§2057–2058); concessions only
   postpone it (Louis XVI, §2059). Ref `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`: the
   oldest cycle of regimes decays in a fixed order of constitutions; Pareto's
   decays in the composition of the ruling stratum under any constitution.
   Michels's reserve (l.8788, l.18068): what happens is less a circulation than
   a "réunion des élites" — the old leaders absorb the new.
7. **Lions and foxes: what decay looks like.** The image is Machiavelli's (Ref
   `machiavelli-effectual-truth`, Prince XVIII — the prince must be both);
   Pareto turns it into a sociology (Vol. IV §2178): a governing class that will
   not use force, from humanitarian sentiment or from calculation, turns to
   "diplomacy, fraud, corruption — governmental authority passes, in a word,
   from the lions to the foxes"; it recruits foxes and blackballs lions; "The
   man who has bursts of rebellion, and does not know how to crook his spine …
   is the worst of leaders"; Class I residues grow in the elite and Class II
   decay, while the subject class keeps its faith and its readiness for force.
   The danger point (§2179): "A humanitarian aristocracy that is closed or
   stiffly exclusive represents the maximum of insecurity"; an elite that
   absorbs the able men of the subject class is almost unassailable. The live
   case Pareto adds (§2480): the Italian "Red Week" of June 1914, a government
   that would neither concede nor fire — "the fox always reasons — but not the
   lion; and that is the chief reason why the lion kills the fox in the end".
   The S and R pair (§§2233–2236) as the economic face of foxes and lions —
   speculators and rentiers — in an Aside at most. Mark Pareto's claim of
   neutrality (§§2174–2175: "is force beneficial?" has no general answer;
   §2235: the analysis is "not condemnation or praise") and let the reader see,
   in the same pages, the contempt for "humanitarianism" (§2471–2474) — the
   tension is part of the record, stated without a label.
8. **The weighing: what is left of the idea that the many can rule?** Three
   answers at full strength, then the verdict in the course's voice. (a) *Michels's own:* democracy is "the
   least of evils" (l.19490); the treasure in the field (l.19399) — the sons
   dig for a treasure that is not there and improve the soil; the search for
   democracy weakens oligarchy even if it never finds its object. (b) *The
   competitive answer, stated as the course's own:* grant that a minority
   governs every party and every state; democracy is then not rule BY the many
   but the many's choice AMONG competing minorities — the method in which
   leaders "acquire the power to decide by means of a competitive struggle for
   the people's vote" (Muller l.3857; author in References). Ref
   `mosca-ruling-class` (the representative system lets many organized forces
   compete). What it concedes to Michels: inside each party the law holds. What
   it denies him: that the law decides the outcome between parties. The
   empirical counter-case to Michels's law inside an organization (course's
   own; References): a printers' union in North America in which two organized
   internal parties alternated in office for decades. (c) *The objection to both
   authors:* Michels generalises from one kind of body (the mass party of the
   1900s) and slides between "a minority leads" and "a minority escapes
   control"; Pareto's residues are named after the conduct they explain, so no
   conduct can count against them, and his "elite" is circular (those with the
   highest grades are those who succeed). **The verdict, argued:** Michels's
   own continuity data show a tendency, not a law; the printers' union is a
   counter-case; the competitive answer concedes oligarchy inside each party
   and denies that it settles the contest between them — so the law holds as
   a strong tendency inside mass organisations, fails as an iron law, and does
   not decide outcomes between parties; Pareto's residues and success-defined
   elite cannot be falsified (**strong**). The strongest objection at full
   strength: the printers' union is one freak case, whose own students called
   its conditions rare. Answer: an iron law admits no exception, and "rare
   conditions" is what "strong tendency" already concedes. Say what would
   overturn the verdict: a large, long-lived organisation with competitive
   internal parties whose leadership nonetheless proved irremovable. Then the
   reception, reported as
   dated fact once each and only after the writer has checked them (see
   Sources): Pareto welcomed Mussolini's government in its first months and
   died in 1923; Michels joined the Fascist party and praised the leader as the
   embodiment of the masses' will. The lesson puts the question — does a
   theory that democracy is impossible incline its holders to welcome its
   end? — and states the fact about the argument: the verdict just reached
   means the theory, read correctly, does not say democracy is impossible, so
   the step to welcoming its end needs a premise the theory does not supply.
   (`the-right-in-1933` decides the parallel question for three later
   thinkers — forward Ref, one clause.)

Headings as claims: "Numbers forbid direct rule"; "Expertise makes leaders
irremovable"; "The members' own needs loosen their control"; "'Who says
organization says oligarchy'"; "Residues and derivations: the theory comes after
the sentiment"; "History is a graveyard of aristocracies"; "From the lions to the
foxes"; "Oligarchy is a strong tendency, not an iron law, and it does not
decide between parties" (the weighing; states the verdict).

**Conclusion.** State the verdict in plain words with its grade: inside a mass
organisation a minority tends to entrench itself — Michels's own figures show
it — but the law is not iron (the printers' union), and it does not decide the
contest between parties, which the voters decide; Pareto's residues and his
success-defined elite cannot be falsified (strong). Then the hand-off: if
what moves men is sentiment, a revolutionary class needs an image, not a
programme (`sorel-reflections-on-violence`); and the next lesson turns from
who rules to what politics does to a people's inner life (`mann-reflections`).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register (exact rule).** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), at most three per section:** SPD (link 0 anchor);
  party congress / Parteitag (link 2: the annual assembly of delegates that
  elected the executive); executive committee (link 2); oligarchy (link 1 —
  recall Aristotle's sense, rule of the few, Ref `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`;
  Michels's sense is rule of the few INSIDE a democratic body); syndicalism
  (link 4: the labour movement that put the union and the general strike above
  parliament — Ref forward `sorel-reflections-on-violence`); Bonapartism and
  plebiscite (link 4: rule by a leader confirmed by popular vote, after Napoleon
  III; the plebiscite as a yes/no vote on a man or measure); residue,
  derivation (link 5 — Pareto insists the words mean only "the constant part"
  and "the justifying part", §868); elite, governing elite (link 6 — the
  everyday word carries praise; Pareto's does not); Class I / Class II residues
  (link 5, named in words, not numbers, in the body: "the instinct for
  combinations" and "the persistence of aggregates"); Red Week (link 7: the
  insurrectionary strikes in the Marches and Romagna, June 1914). People:
  Lassalle (link 3: founder of the first German workers' party, 1863, d. 1864);
  Bebel (link 2 if used: the SPD's leader for four decades); Walras only if
  Pareto's chair is mentioned.
- **"Iron law" is not Michels's refrain.** The phrase is a chapter title once;
  the law is stated as "Who says organization, says oligarchy". The course.yaml
  title uses the received name; the lesson says once where it comes from.
- **Do not overstate the Pareto reading.** The lesson's Pareto rests on the
  sections listed under Sources; it says so in References ("Vol. II §§842–1088;
  Vol. III §§2025–2059; Vol. IV §§2170–2236, 2455–2480"), and states anything
  from biography or later readers as an account, not as his text. Course.yaml
  ("from accounts of the Treatise") is satisfied by the References note plus the
  secondary sources named.
- **Machiavelli's lion and fox are not re-taught** — one clause and a `<Ref>`;
  Pareto's use differs (a sociology of governing classes, not advice to a
  prince), and the lesson says that difference in one sentence.
- **Mosca is not re-taught.** Ruling class, formula and the hundred/thousand are
  recalled with a `<Ref>` and a clause.
- **Period remarks.** Michels has national-character and racial generalizations
  (e.g. on Jews among socialist leaders, Part Four ch. II); Pareto has colonial
  and anti-humanitarian remarks (§§1043–1051, §§2471–2474). The lesson does not
  use them as examples. Where Pareto's anti-humanitarianism is part of his
  argument (link 7), it is reported as his and bound to his text.
- **The real misreading (L13), if the writer wants a slot:** "Michels proved
  that democracy is a fraud." He disclaims the charge of hypocrisy (Preface,
  l.269), calls democracy the least of evils (l.19490), and ends on the treasure
  fable. One slot, at link 8.
- **Numbers (L12):** keeper: the survival count (link 2). Others in words. Do
  not quote the 1906/1913 union treasury figures or the Munich membership table
  (OCR scrambled — summary figures note).
- **Dates:** 1911 (German), 1915 (English, with the war chapter), 1916
  (Trattato), 1935 (English); August 1914 (war credits) and June 1914 (Red
  Week) to the month, because the authors date their tests.
- **Dossier thin:** both authors' biographies and their relations to Fascism
  are outside the dossier (see Sources); Pareto's derivations proper and Vol. I
  are unread; *Union Democracy* is outside the dossier.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up, E3):** State Mosca's hundred-and-thousand
   argument (Ref `mosca-ruling-class`) and name the three groups of causes
   Michels gives for oligarchy in a party (technical, psychological,
   intellectual), with one example of each from the lesson.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** The survival count (Part Two ch. I,
   l.4817). Metadata: a former member writing in 1911 about a party he had left,
   using congress records he compiled himself ("cannot claim absolute scientific
   precision"). Predict what such an author would want the figures to show; then
   records / argues / omits (it omits why the delegates stayed — re-election by
   satisfied members would produce the same count). What can the count be USED
   to establish (stability of a leading group), and what can it not (that the
   group escaped the members' control)?
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "The SPD's vote for war credits in August 1914
   proves Michels's law." Name the step that fails (the vote is consistent with
   the law, but also with the members' own patriotism or fear of invasion; the
   law predicts the leaders' interest prevails when it CONFLICTS with the
   members' wishes, so one must show the conflict) and state what evidence would
   be needed (the members' views in July–August 1914 against the deputies'
   vote). Michels's own reading (Part Six ch. III) is the material.
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** Run Michels's chain at its limits. (i) A party of
   forty members who all know the work: which links fail (size; expertise)? (ii)
   An organization with two organized internal parties that compete for every
   office (the printers' union of link 8): which link does competition cut, and
   which does it leave? The solution shows the law depends on there being ONE
   organized minority inside the body — the condition the competitive answer
   removes.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** Apply Pareto's §863 method to the
   oath — swearing on the Bible in an English court, on the gods in Athens, on a
   constitution at a modern inauguration. Separate the variable explanations
   (derivations) from the constant sentiment (residue), and say which of the two
   residue classes it belongs to. Then say what Pareto's method predicts if the
   explanation is abolished but the sentiment is not.
6. **Synthesis (corroboration across lessons):** Mosca's "aristocratic and
   democratic tendencies" (Ref `mosca-ruling-class`), Pareto's "circulation of
   elites", and Michels's "réunion des élites" describe the same movement. Lay
   out where they agree (ruling groups are renewed from below) and the exact
   point where each parts from the others (balance of tendencies; replacement
   with floods; absorption without replacement). Which of the three does the
   SPD record of links 2–4 support, and why?
7. **Counter-argument stem:** "If every party is an oligarchy, democracy is
   impossible." Name two facts the claim fails to account for (competition
   between oligarchies decided by voters; Michels's own two regulative principles
   and his choice of democracy as the least of evils), and state what would have
   to be true for the claim to survive (that the competing elites collude — a
   "réunion des élites" between parties as well as within them).
8. **Attack the verdict:** The lesson concludes that Michels's law is a strong
   tendency, not an iron law, because of the printers' union. State the
   strongest objection in its best form: one craft union with a unique
   culture is an exception that confirms the rule; every mass party and
   every large union studied since has an entrenched leadership, and the
   competitive answer only moves the oligarchy up a level (a cartel of party
   leaderships). Then say what evidence would be needed to overturn the
   verdict: for the first half, a large, long-lived organisation with
   competitive internal parties whose leadership nonetheless proved
   irremovable; for the second, evidence that competing party leaderships
   collude so that elections do not change who governs. Solution: the first
   would restore the "iron"; the second is exercise 7's condition, and the
   record of governments removed by elections does not show it.
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `sorel-reflections-on-violence`:** Pareto says men act from
   residues and justify themselves with derivations, and that a subject class
   wins when it keeps the faith and the readiness for force its rulers have lost.
   Suppose you led a workers' movement and accepted both claims. What would you
   give your followers to hold them together — a programme they can check
   against results, or something else? Describe what it would have to be like
   (not refutable by a failed forecast; an image of a decisive struggle). The
   solution says the reader has described what the next module's first author
   calls a myth, with a `<Ref>`.

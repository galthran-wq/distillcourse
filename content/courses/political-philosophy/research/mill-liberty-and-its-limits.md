# Research brief: mill-liberty-and-its-limits (Mill's On Liberty: the harm principle and its limits)

status: planned
module: democracy-and-its-critics
type: core
goal: Analyze Mill's harm principle and his case for liberty of thought and individuality, and evaluate the two strongest conservative replies

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`: `tyranny-of-the-majority` as a power over opinion ("the body is left free, and the soul is enslaved") — the thing Mill names "social tyranny" and tries to bound with a rule. From `carlyle-heroes`: `hero-worship` and the demand that the many revere the able — the position Mill's chapter on individuality answers. From `locke-property-and-consent`: `consent` as the ground of legitimate government — which Mill explicitly declines to use (OL IV: "society is not founded on a contract"). From `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`: liberty as the silence of the law — the definition Mill widens to include the silence of opinion. Each a `<Ref>` and one clause.
- **Contributes:** The liberal position taught at full strength, as the rest of the course argues against it: one simple principle for when a society may coerce one of its members; the four-ground defence of free discussion; individuality as a component of well-being; the applications where Mill himself draws the line — and then the two strongest conservative replies (Stephen, Arnold) and the later one (Scruton), each at full strength, with Mill's answer where he has one. The reader leaves able to say where the rule holds and where it gives way.
- **Hands off:** Mill's own exclusion — "backward states of society", "despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians" — is the `civilized-community-clause`, planted here and harvested in `locke-and-mill-on-empire` (the empire module), where Representative Government ch. XVIII is read. `marx-manifesto` takes the next position. `british-franchise` tests Stephen's "parliament as disguised compulsion" and Arnold's "doing as one likes" against 1867. Later: Scruton's `scruton-meaning-of-conservatism` develops ch. 4 in its own place; Deneen's `deneen-post-liberalism` treats Mill as one of liberalism's two revolutions.
- **Spine step:** "Democracy arrives as a fact and is judged: Tocqueville foresees soft despotism, Carlyle demands heroes, Mill defends liberty, Marx predicts class war — and the British franchise tests who was right"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: a whole society against one man; one simple rule for when a society may coerce; the hardest cases — God, Mormon polygamy, the Sabbath; the rule, its defence, its limits, and the replies that said it cannot do its work.

1. **Why is a new rule needed at all — was the problem not solved by making rulers elected?** The old problem was a ruler "in a necessarily antagonistic position to the people" — the "king of the vultures" the flock must guard against; the remedy was limits and then election. But once "the people" rule, "the people who exercise the power are not always the same people with those over whom it is exercised": self-government is "the government of each by all the rest". Ref Tocqueville for the name — the tyranny of the majority — and for the discovery Mill extends: society "practises a social tyranny more formidable than many kinds of political oppression", which "penetrates much more deeply into the details of life". The "because": law stops at the body; opinion reaches the soul. So the question is not who rules but *where any rule stops* — and Mill's charge is that no one has ever asked it: what passes for moral reasoning is "the likings and dislikings of society", and most moralities are class moralities (Spartans and Helots, planters and slaves, men and women).
2. **The one very simple principle.** Quote it whole (ll. 310–331): self-protection is the sole warrant; a man's own good "is not a sufficient warrant"; "over himself… the individual is sovereign". Then the three regions it protects (conscience and expression; tastes and pursuits; combination) and the ground Mill chooses — not natural right ("I forego any advantage… from the idea of abstract right") but utility "in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being". Then the exclusions, in Mill's own words and at full strength: children; and "backward states of society in which the race itself may be considered as in its nonage" — "Despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end be their improvement… an Akbar or a Charlemagne". Define the `civilized-community-clause` here; say in one sentence that the empire module reads what Mill did with it in India (Ref `locke-and-mill-on-empire`); do not argue it here.
3. **Why may no opinion be silenced — not even one against the whole of mankind?** The obvious attempt: silence what is false and dangerous. Mill's dilemma: the opinion is true, false, or partly true, and silencing loses in every case. (1) It may be true: "all silencing of discussion is an assumption of infallibility" — and the same causes "make him a Churchman in London" that "would have made him a Buddhist or a Confucian in Pekin". Reply to the "but we must act on our beliefs" objection: we may, because our beliefs have survived "a standing invitation to the whole world to prove them unfounded" — the devil's advocate at a canonisation; Newtonian physics is certain because open to challenge. Reply to "silence the useless, not the false": the usefulness of an opinion is itself an opinion. Then the cases — Socrates condemned by a tribunal that honestly found him guilty; Marcus Aurelius persecuting Christianity from duty, "one of the most tragical facts in all history"; the roll-call of crushed reformations (Arnold of Brescia, Dolcino, Savonarola, the Albigeois, the Lollards, the Hussites): "persecution has usually succeeded" — truth has no inherent power. And England 1857: Pooley sentenced in Cornwall for words on a gate; the atheist who cannot testify. Social intolerance "kills no one, roots out no opinions, but induces men to disguise them" — the chilling effect, whose greatest harm falls not on heretics but on the timid majority. (2) Even if false, it keeps truth alive: a creed uncontested becomes "dead dogma", held in "the deep slumber of a decided opinion"; "he who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that" (Cicero studying his adversary); not one Christian in a thousand tests his conduct by the Gospels. (3) Partly true: Rousseau's paradoxes "exploded like bombshells" in the complacent eighteenth century; the standing antagonisms (order/progress, property/equality, liberty/discipline) each half right. Close with the four grounds as Mill lists them (1814–1838) and the one limit — none on manner by law; the "real morality of public discussion" is stating the opponent's case fairly.
4. **Does the same hold for actions — and what is individuality for?** No: "no one pretends that actions should be as free as opinions"; the corn-dealer who starves the poor may be said so in print but not before his house to an excited mob. Within that limit, "experiments of living" deserve the same freedom, because individuality "is the same thing with development" — Humboldt's two requisites, freedom and variety of situations. Why: a man who follows custom as custom exercises no faculty; "human nature is not a machine… but a tree, which requires to grow… according to the tendency of the inward forces". Strong impulses are energy; the age's danger is deficiency of individuality, not excess; the ascendant power is mediocrity — "collective mediocrity". Genius "the salt of the earth", which "can only breathe freely in an atmosphere of freedom" — and here Mill's reply to Carlyle (Ref `carlyle-heroes`): "hero-worship which applauds the strong man of genius for forcibly seizing on the government of the world" is exactly wrong; "all that genius can claim is freedom to point out the way". Eccentricity is a service. Then the long view: the despotism of custom is why "the greater part of the world has, properly speaking, no history" — China, which perfected one pattern and stopped; Europe progressed by plurality of paths and is losing it through assimilation (Tocqueville cited by Mill — name him, he is primary) — "Europe… may become another China".
5. **Where does Mill himself draw the line?** Ch. IV–V, the ladder of cases from far to near: the Muslim who would ban pork, Spain banning Protestant worship, Puritans banning amusements, the New England sumptuary spirit, the trade unions' moral police, the Maine Law (prohibition of alcohol, Maine 1851), the Sabbath, the Mormons — each a case where the persecutor's logic is turned back on the reader's own side. The tools: self-regarding vs other-regarding conduct; natural penalties (distaste, avoidance, warning) vs punishment; "definite damage, or a definite risk of damage" takes a case out of liberty (the drunk soldier on duty, the father who starves his children — not the merely drunk man); "a man's duty to himself is not socially obligatory unless it is also a duty to others"; the asymmetry — "there is no parity between the feeling of a person for his own opinion, and the feeling of another who is offended at his holding it", the thief and the owner. Then ch. V's refinements: harm to others is necessary, not sufficient (competition harms losers); poisons sold with "preappointed evidence" rather than banned; the unsafe bridge (stop him if the danger is certain, warn if probable — "liberty consists in doing what one desires, and he does not desire to fall into the river"); "whatever it is permitted to do, it must be permitted to advise to do" (instigators left open); the slavery contract — "it is not freedom, to be allowed to alienate his freedom"; education required but not provided; bureaucracy and "pedantocracy"; "the worth of a State… is the worth of the individuals composing it". Note the one case Mill marks as a tension himself: he condemns polygamy and denies that any community "has a right to force another to be civilised" — against ch. III's China and ch. I's barbarians.
6. **OBJECTION 1 — Stephen (1873), at full strength.** The principle, stripped, says: no appeal to fear except for self-protection. But all morality and all religion appeal to fear and hope; a Day-of-Judgment defence of "I pleased myself and hurt nobody else" would be no defence. The line between social and legal sanction does not exist — Mill's own "natural penalties" are coercion by opinion, and coercion by opinion is how every society has ever been held together; "every great change… was established by coercion". Parliament itself is coercion disguised: the minority yields because it would lose the fight — "counting heads instead of breaking heads". Mill's "capacity for discussion" test fails twice: men are ignorant, and most are "bad and indifferent", so free discussion among them does not tend to truth. Hence "Who is left at liberty to do what?" — liberty is a negation; the question is always *which* restraint, and society is rightly an educator whose law is "graduated" to its morals. The headnote fact matters: Stephen wrote it on the ship home from three years as the law member in India (1869–72) — the man who applied Mill's exclusion clause and found it the rule, not the exception. Mill's answer, from his text: the infallibility argument is untouched (Stephen concedes discussion among the capable); and the asymmetry argument — the owner's loss and the thief's gain are not symmetrical, so "the majority's distaste" is not an injury. Where it gives way: Stephen is right that "natural penalties" are coercion; Mill never gives a criterion for when opinion's pressure becomes tyranny, only examples.
7. **OBJECTION 2 — Arnold (1869), at full strength.** Anchor Hyde Park, July 1866: a Reform League meeting banned, the park railings torn down, the government helpless — the scene Arnold wrote from. The Englishman's one notion of liberty is "doing as one likes"; while "feudal habits of subordination" held, it did no harm; now that deference is dying, "doing as one likes" tends to anarchy, and England has no "idea of the State" to put in its place. The three classes each fail as a seat of authority: the aristocracy, "children of the established fact", cannot think; the middle class mistakes "machinery" (coal, railways, the deceased wife's sister bill, free trade) for ends; the working class has learned from the other two to do as it likes. Hence the State, not as the majority's instrument but as "the organ of our collective best self, of our national right reason" — the ordinary self against the best self — and culture as the pursuit of perfection that alone makes a people fit to be free: "revolution by due course of law". Mill's answer: the "best self" is someone's self; who certifies it is the question the harm principle was written to avoid, and ch. V already accepts a state that *requires* culture (education) without *providing* it. Where it gives way: Mill has no account of what makes a people's "likings" worth protecting — Arnold has.
8. **OBJECTION 3 — the later conservative reply (Scruton ch. 4).** Harm is a moral concept: "harmful" cannot be read off from a fact without a judgment about what matters, so the principle borrows the morality it claims to displace — the move from "wrong because harmful" to "harmful because wrong". Law is "the concrete image of civil life", not a fence around private spheres; a law that forbids only harm "deprives itself of authority". And the onus: Mill puts the burden on society to justify every restraint; a conservative puts it on the reformer to show the restraint can be removed harmlessly — "he who shifts the onus will have to shift everything" (one clause from the Philosophical Appendix). Mill's answer is in ch. IV: he agrees harm is judged, and gives "definite damage" and the asymmetry as the criteria. Where it gives way: the Mormon case shows Mill applying a judgment ("I am not aware that any community has a right to force another to be civilised") his own ch. I clause denies.
9. **Verdict as judgment, not score.** Hold: the infallibility and corrigibility arguments (no reply in the dossier touches them); the asymmetry; the slavery contract. Give way: the self/other-regarding line (Stephen); the source of the "likings" worth protecting (Arnold); the definition of harm (Scruton). Open: the civilised-community clause, which Stephen says is the rule and Mill says is the exception — handed to the empire module. Say what the reader can now do and that the next position (Marx) will deny that *any* of this is the question.

Headings as claims: "Elected rulers do not end the problem of coercion"; "One simple principle, and whom it excludes"; "Silencing an opinion loses in every case"; "Individuality is development, not eccentricity"; "The hard cases: where Mill himself draws the line"; "Stephen: coercion by opinion is how societies work"; "Arnold: doing as one likes, and the State as best self"; "Harm is a moral word"; "What holds and what gives way".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **Mankind minus one** (II, 541–544) — quote and parse: "If all mankind minus one were of one opinion, and only one person were of the contrary opinion, mankind would be no more justified in silencing that one person, than he, if he had the power, would be justified in silencing mankind." Then the infallibility chain built step by step (figure 2).
2. **Marcus Aurelius** (II, 861–917): the best of pagan rulers, persecuting from duty; Mill's point that a persecutor's sincerity is no defence — if the argument licenses Marcus, it licenses every persecutor. Pair with Socrates and Christ in one paragraph.
3. **Pooley, Cornwall, 1857** (II, 995–1035 and fnn.): twenty-one months for words written on a gate; the rule that an unbeliever cannot testify, which "is suicidal" (it leaves atheists free to lie). Sourcing drill in the body: what Mill's footnote records, what it omits (the pardon), what he uses it to establish (that intolerance survives in England, not only in history).
4. **The corn-dealer** (III, 1993–2005): "corn-dealers are starvers of the poor" permitted in print, punished when "delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer". The clean boundary between opinion and instigation.
5. **The tree and the machine** (III, 2126–2130) and **the Chinese lady's foot** (2495–2497): the two images of individuality and its suppression; the "better to be a Pericles than either" line as the ideal.
6. **The Alliance Secretary's "social rights"** (IV, 3200–3278): the United Kingdom Alliance's claim that selling drink "invades my social rights" by weakening society; Mill's reductio — a right that "every person in the community should act in every respect exactly as he ought" is "the only right that every man has to violate every other". Reconstruct the demolition in three steps.
7. **The unsafe bridge and the poison register** (V, 3516–3563): restraint without violation of liberty when the danger is certain; "preappointed evidence" (the buyer signs a register) as the design that preserves liberty while deterring the poisoner. The lesson's worked `<Example>` of the principle in operation.
8. **Stephen's "counting heads"** and **Arnold's Hyde Park railings**: the two objections each anchored in one scene — a judge on a ship home from Calcutta writing that parliament is a disguised fight; a school inspector watching a crowd pull down the Hyde Park railings and asking what "doing as one likes" means without deference.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Taught fairly, at full strength, first.** The lesson is the liberal position the course argues against; it must be reconstructed as Mill would want before any objection enters (links 1–5), and the objections then enter at full strength too. No "some say". The dry register: "Stephen argues", "Arnold argues", never "critics claim".
- **Not natural rights.** Mill grounds liberty in utility "in the largest sense"; a reader who has Locke will assume rights. Say the ground once, plainly, and use it when Stephen (a fellow utilitarian) attacks — the quarrel is inside utilitarianism.
- **The civilised-community clause** is a quotation, not a gloss: use Mill's words (ll. 345–347), define the concept, point forward, and do not argue it here. Do not moralise it; do not excuse it. The Sepoy footnote (II fn. 10, ll. 1950–1975 — toleration "among Christians only" demanded in 1857) is the only other India material permitted, and only in a clause.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** Humboldt (Wilhelm von, Prussian scholar-statesman, 1792 essay on the limits of state action — Mill's epigraph); Comte (one clause: the French positivist whose system Mill calls a despotism); Calvinism (the "pagan self-assertion vs Christian self-denial" contrast); Pericles, Alcibiades, Knox (one clause each — Athens's statesman, its brilliant traitor, Scotland's reformer); Marcus Aurelius; the Locrian law; Pooley; the Maine Law (1851, the first US state prohibition); Sabbatarianism (the campaign to close Sunday trade and travel); the Mormons (Utah, polygamy, 1850s); the United Kingdom Alliance (the prohibition lobby, 1853); the deceased wife's sister bill (a decades-long campaign to legalise such marriages — Arnold's instance of "machinery"); Hyde Park 1866 (date, Reform League, railings); James Fitzjames Stephen (judge, legal member of the Viceroy's council 1869–72, brother of Leslie); Matthew Arnold (poet, inspector of schools 1851–86); Wolfenden/Devlin (one clause if Scruton's example is used: the 1957 report decriminalising private homosexual acts and the judge who answered that law enforces morals).

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up).** State the four grounds for freedom of opinion (OL II, 1814–1838) and, for each, the one example from the lesson that illustrates it. Closed book.
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill.** Stephen's passage on parliament as "counting heads instead of breaking heads": author, date (1873), where written (the ship from India), audience (the English reading public after the 1867 Reform Act), purpose; what it records (an observation about how minorities obey), what it argues (that the franchise does not reduce coercion), what it omits (why heads are counted *instead* — that a society which can count has agreed not to break); what it can be used to establish and what it cannot.
3. **Boundary probe.** Set the "civilised community" to zero: apply the harm principle, as Mill states it, to the Mormons of Utah in 1859 (a community Mill calls the product of "a retrograde step in civilisation"). Does ch. I's clause license a "civilizade", as some Englishmen demanded, or does ch. IV's "no community has a right to force another to be civilised" forbid it? Show that Mill's text gives both answers and name what would have to be fixed to make it give one. **FORWARD (E7)** → the solution says in one sentence, with a `<Ref>`, that `locke-and-mill-on-empire` reads how Mill resolved it for India, and that the resolution is the empire module's question.
4. **Claim to refute.** "Mill grounds liberty on a natural right to be left alone." Name the failing step with his words (ll. 359–366; ch. IV's "society is not founded on a contract"; the duties of positive action — testifying, common defence, saving a life) and say what difference the ground makes when Stephen attacks (both are utilitarians; the dispute is about which arrangement serves "the permanent interests of man as a progressive being").
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped.** Run the infallibility argument on the harm principle itself: a society that forbids *all* coercion of self-regarding conduct assumes the principle true against the whole weight of received morality; by Mill's own rule it must stay open to refutation. What institutional form does that take in ch. V (the Poor Law Board case: dispersed power, centralised information; the state as teacher of its own critics), and does Stephen's "society as educator" differ from it in kind or in degree?
6. **Counter-argument stem (synthesis).** "After 1866 England needed Arnold's State, not Mill's liberty." Name two facts the claim must account for (the railings fell without anarchy following; the 1867 Act that followed widened "doing as one likes" and the state did not collapse — the reader will meet the record in `british-franchise`), and state what would have to be true for Arnold to be right (that the deference Arnold saw dying was replaced by culture rather than by nothing — his own test).
7. **E3 — earlier lessons.** Hobbes's liberty as "the silence of the law" (Ref `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`), Carlyle's liberty as being "made to walk the right path" (Ref `carlyle-heroes`), and Mill's liberty as the sovereignty of the individual over self-regarding conduct: for each, say who decides where it stops, and which of the three Stephen's "Who is left at liberty to do what?" is aimed at.

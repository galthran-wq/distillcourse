# Research brief: moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich (Moeller van den Bruck's Third Reich: the conservative revolution)

status: planned
module: conservative-revolution
type: core
goal: Analyze Moeller van den Bruck's argument for a conservative revolution against liberalism, his distinction between conservative and reactionary, and the idea of a Third Reich, and evaluate what became of it

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `junger-storm-of-steel-and-the-worker` (the direct prerequisite):
  the front generation's verdict that 1918 was a defeat of the bourgeois, not of the
  soldier; a right that welcomes the end of estate, peasant and Bürger and so has
  nothing left to conserve — the problem this lesson's author answers by definition.
  From `sorel-reflections-on-violence`: the political myth and Sorel's own pointer that
  "national traditions" decide where it takes hold; contempt for parliamentary
  bargaining. From `spengler-decline-of-the-west`: Culture and Civilization, Caesarism,
  Spengler's "socialism" of duty — Moeller shares the diagnosis and refuses the fatalism
  (he places Germany not in a Civilization's winter but among the "young nations"). From
  `mann-reflections`: Kultur against Zivilisation and the German as unpolitical — the
  opening's "a cultural critic" belongs to the same camp; Moeller wants the Germans made
  political at last. From `burke-reflections` and `maistre-and-bonald`: the
  counter-revolutionary's two attitudes to a revolution — inheritance to be guarded, and
  (Maistre) "counter-revolution is not a revolution in reverse" — which Moeller
  re-sorts into reactionary and conservative. From `marx-manifesto`: base and
  superstructure, the proletariat, the International — each inverted here. From
  `herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation`: every people its own measure, and Fichte's turn of
  it into a German mission — Moeller's "every people has its own socialism" stands on
  both. From `article-48-weimar`: the republic's end in 1930–33, which this lesson
  precedes in date (1923) and the reader already knows the outcome of.
- **Contributes:** The concept lesson for the module: it introduces
  `conservative-revolution` and `third-reich-idea`. Moeller's one move is to make
  "conservative" mean the opposite of "reactionary": after 1918 the old order is gone and
  cannot be restored; the conservative must therefore win the revolution that the
  liberals of 1918 left unfinished, and create something worth conserving — a
  corporative "German socialism", a "democracy with a leader, not parliamentism", an
  empire beyond party. The reader leaves able to reconstruct the four antitheses
  (conservative/reactionary, conservative/liberal, German socialism/Marx, nation/party)
  and to say why an idea its author called "misty, indeterminate, charged with feeling"
  could be taken as the name of a state he did not foresee.
- **Hands off:** (i) This lesson decides that a revolution cannot be kept conservative
  by the intentions of those who call for it (**strong**): the book names no regime,
  no programme and no leader, and its author died in 1925, so a movement he disdained
  took its name. What passes on is the wider question — whether the radical right's
  doctrines gave its thinkers any ground to refuse the movement in power — decided in
  `the-right-in-1933`. (ii) Moeller's conservatism still rests on
  history and the nation; `evola-revolt-against-the-modern-world` asks for a standard
  that condemns all of history at once. (iii) The three-age shape of the idea — first,
  second, third empire — is traced to a medieval prophecy of a third age in
  `voegelin-gnosticism` (postwar module), which treats it as a pattern of modern mass
  movements. (iv) The definitions themselves — conservative, reactionary, radical
  conservative — are tested on the whole tradition in `what-conservatism-is`.
- **Spine step:** "The radical right turns from conserving to revolution — Sorel's myth,
  Jünger's front and Worker, Moeller's Third Reich, Evola's Tradition — and in 1933 its
  thinkers have to choose: Schmitt and Heidegger join, Spengler refuses"

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** a revolution can produce what is worth conserving and stay
  conservative — Moeller's "conservative revolution", with the Third Reich as its idea.
- **Verdict:** fails. Grade: **strong**.
- **What decides it:** (1) The argument: a conservatism that creates its values has
  given up the reasons conservatism gave for conserving — that inherited institutions
  carry more knowledge than any designer and that change should be slow because its
  consequences are unknown (Ref `burke-reflections`). Moeller concedes it: conservative
  ends may be reached "by revolutionary means" (l.4270); "a conservative's function is
  to create values which are worth conserving" (l.4833). (2) The record: his own
  warning that the Third Reich idea was "misty, indeterminate, charged with feeling"
  and "might well be the most fatal of all the illusions" (Prefatory Letter, l.64–70)
  was borne out. The idea had no institution, no programme and no leader — "the Third
  Party" is a name, not a mechanism — so after his death (1925) and the reissues of
  1930–31 a movement he disdained took its name as the name of its state (Stern, *The
  Politics of Cultural Despair*, 1961, Part III, for the 1922 meeting and his
  judgement of Hitler — as reported, verify before stating detail).
- **Strongest objection:** the authors of an idea are not answerable for its theft;
  Moeller's distinction between the conservative and the reactionary is real and was
  used by later writers on both sides, and he showed no National Socialist intention.
  This is conceded and does not overturn the verdict, which is about the claim, not
  the man: the claim was that the revolution could be kept conservative, and the book
  supplies nothing that could keep it so. Moeller named the danger himself.
- **What would overturn it:** a mechanism in the book that holds the "Third Party" to
  conservative ends against a mass movement — an institution, a rule of selection, a
  check — rather than the intentions of those who call for the revolution.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): 1922; a four-year-old republic paying reparations,
governed by parties most of the right regard as agents of defeat; a cultural critic who
had translated Dostoevsky argues that the right cannot win by restoring the Kaiser, that
only a revolution can produce something worth conserving, gives the new order an old
name — a third empire — and writes a book on why liberalism is the death of nations.

Anchors (L11), section 1, one per sentence and each with a what-happened: 9 November
1918 (the Kaiser abdicates, a republic is proclaimed in Berlin, two days before the
armistice); the Treaty of Versailles (1919: territory, reparations, the war-guilt
article); Weimar (the town whose National Assembly wrote the constitution — Ref
`article-48-weimar` for the constitution itself); "fulfilment policy" (the governments'
strategy of paying reparations to show they could not be paid); the occupation of the
Ruhr by French and Belgian troops (January 1923) and the hyperinflation of that year (Ref
or one clause; the book itself mentions "the morning's dollar level" as "the substitute
for morning prayer", II.4). Arthur Moeller van den Bruck (1876–1925): left school without
a diploma, lived as a writer, supervised the first complete German edition of
Dostoevsky, and after 1918 belonged to a Berlin circle of young conservatives, the June
Club (its details are not in the dossier — verify in the References' accounts); he took his own life in 1925. The "Reich" defined at first use: the
German word for empire or realm; the First Reich is the medieval Holy Roman Empire (to
1806), the Second the empire Bismarck founded in 1871 (to 1918).

1. **Why can the right not simply restore the old order?** Obvious attempt (the
   monarchist parties of 1922): bring back the Kaiser, the flag, the army as they were.
   Moeller's answer, I.1: "A war may be lost … The worst peace is never final. But a
   Revolution must be won." The Second Reich did not fall to its enemies alone; it had
   exhausted itself — Bismarck built a state the Germans were not equal to, an age of
   "all-pervading amateurishness" (I.2, VI.3). And 9 November was a revolution, whether
   the right liked it or not: "A Policy may be reversed: History cannot" (VI). So the task
   is not to undo 1918 but to finish it: the revolution was made "western-parliamentary",
   on the English and French model; it must be made national (I.6). Answer: the
   right must become revolutionary because there is nothing intact to conserve. Seam:
   Jünger (Ref) welcomed the end of the old order; Moeller turns the same fact into a
   programme.
2. **What, then, does "conservative" mean? The conservative against the reactionary.**
   The newcomer's definition: a conservative wants to keep things as they are or were.
   Moeller splits it: the REACTIONARY "demands emphatically the restoration of the status
   quo ante" (l.3952), sees 1918 as a political accident to be reversed, and "toys with
   fate … by a coup d'état" (l.4390); the conservative sees a historical event with a
   spiritual cause and "asks: what is worth conserving?" (l.4518). The definitions to
   quote: "The reactionary creates nothing. The revolutionary only destroys … The
   conservative creates by giving to phenomena a form in which they can endure" (l.4370);
   "a conservative's function is to create values which are worth conserving" (l.4833);
   "a thing can only be conserved by being incessantly re-won" (l.4811); "We live in order
   to bequeath" (l.4453). Answer: concept `conservative-revolution` — conservative ends
   reached "by revolutionary means" (l.4270). Backward link: Maistre's "counter-revolution
   is not a revolution in reverse" (Ref `maistre-and-bonald`) and Burke's inheritance
   (Ref `burke-reflections`) — Moeller keeps the language of inheritance and drops what
   Burke built on it (link 7).
3. **Why is liberalism the enemy, not socialism?** Expectation: a man of the right in
   1922 fights the communists first. Moeller's chapter III: liberalism is "the death of
   nations" because it has no principle — "the principle of liberalism is to have no fixed
   principle and to contend that this is in itself a principle" (l.1569); it turns ideals
   into tactics, "the ideal sanctifies the interpretation" (l.1755); it lives on man's
   weakness where conservatism lives on his strength (l.1883); it is not a party but an
   atmosphere in all parties, producing the professional party leader; "liberal thought is
   disintegrated conservative thought" (l.4337). The left at least has convictions —
   "The Left has reason. The Right has understanding" (l.4728) — and the young of left and
   right can meet against "THE ENEMY", the liberal (III.10). Answer: the enemy is the
   middle, because the middle is what dissolves a nation into interests. Link to
   Schmitt's charge that liberalism replaces politics with discussion and trade (Ref
   `schmitt-concept-of-the-political`, one clause) and to Sorel's contempt for bargaining
   (Ref).
4. **A German socialism against Marx.** Problem: the right needs the workers, and the
   workers' party is Marxist. Moeller inverts Marx point by point. (a) Base and
   superstructure reversed: "The primary laws of history are political laws; economic
   laws are secondary" (l.940) — power, law and state found the economy, not the
   reverse. (b) Men "set themselves only such tasks as they cannot fulfil" (l.653) —
   against Marx's "only such tasks as they can". (c) The proletarian is made by
   consciousness, not by the machine (l.3531): the 1919 meeting — "Ninety out of every
   hundred of us are proletarians!" / "But they don't want to be!" (l.3535); "there is
   always a proletariat" (l.3560). (d) Management-value against surplus value (l.3465).
   (e) "Every people has its own socialism" (l.1421): the German one corporative —
   organisation by trade and profession, as in Stein's reforms and the guilds — and the
   true proletariat is the nation itself, "a proletarian nation" with "twenty million"
   too many (l.1246, l.3635). Answer: socialism inverted — the class question dissolved
   into the national question. The "because": if politics comes first, the class war is a
   question of the nation's place among nations. Backward Ref `marx-manifesto`; one
   clause to Spengler's socialism of duty (Ref).
5. **Democracy without parliament.** Expectation: an anti-liberal is an anti-democrat.
   Moeller claims the word: "Democracy exists where the people take a share in
   determining their own fate" (IV, l.2444) — a democracy older than parliament, the
   Germanic assembly of kin, against "a mechanical counting of votes" (IV.4, Rousseau's
   general will against the will of all, Ref `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`);
   "Germany has proved TOO GOOD for parliaments" (l.2821); "a democracy with a
   leader—not parliamentism" (l.2868); "a people must first be nationalized before it is
   democratized" (l.4663). Answer: democracy as national self-assertion under a leader,
   representation by estates and professions. The problem the lesson states: who decides
   what the people's will is, if not a count? The text's answer — leaders "whose party
   is Germany", trusted rather than elected (VII.9) — names who decides but gives no
   way to tell the true leader from a claimant; the lesson says Moeller did not answer
   the question, and link 7 shows what that gap cost.
6. **The Third Reich as an idea.** The name: "an old German conception … It arose when
   our First Empire fell; it was early quickened by the thought of a millennium" (Letter,
   l.50–54) — a third empire that is not a restoration of the first or second but a new
   creation that continues them (VI.6, VIII); "the Third Party wills the Third Empire"
   (l.5490); "There is only ONE EMPIRE, as there is only ONE CHURCH" (l.6017). Define the
   concept `third-reich-idea`. Then Moeller's own warning, quoted whole: "the thought of
   the Third Empire is a philosophical idea … misty, indeterminate, charged with feeling
   … might well be the most fatal of all the illusions to which they have ever yielded …
   Germany might perish of her Third Empire dream" (l.64–70). The schema's lineage in one
   sentence (the course's own statement): a third age after two, as in the twelfth-century
   prophecy of Joachim of Fiore and in the Russian idea of Moscow as the "Third Rome",
   which Moeller met while editing Dostoevsky; forward Ref `voegelin-gnosticism`.
7. **Weighing — "A revolution cannot be kept conservative by the intentions of those
   who call for it."** The section sets the claim against its strongest objection and
   the evidence and reaches the verdict (see VERDICT). Three objections, each stated as
   the course's own. (a) From the conservative side (Ref `burke-reflections`;
   `what-conservatism-is` will name it): a conservatism that creates its values has
   discarded the reasons conservatism gave for conserving — that inherited institutions
   carry more knowledge than any designer, that change should be slow because
   consequences are unknown. Moeller's conservative is a designer with an older name;
   his own sentence that conservative goals may be reached "by revolutionary means"
   (l.4270) concedes the point. (b) From the liberal side: the book argues by antithesis
   and label — liberal/conservative, reason/understanding, patriot/nationalist — where an
   argument would need evidence (that the parliaments of 1919–22 failed for the reasons
   given and not for reparations, inflation and armed insurrection from left and right).
   A further liberal point: Moeller's own reactionary — the man of the coup d'état —
   describes the putschists of 1920 and 1923 more exactly than anything in the book
   describes the parliamentarians it attacks. (c) The record: Moeller's idea had no
   institution, no programme, no leader — by his own account a mood ("charged with
   feeling"). Moeller died in May 1925; the second edition appeared in 1930 and the third
   in 1931 as the Nazi movement rose; the movement took his title as the name of its
   state. The lesson states (as reported, see Pitfalls) that Moeller met Hitler in 1922
   and judged him unfavourably, and that the idea's vagueness, which Moeller himself had
   named as its danger, is what made it transferable. The thinker's difficulty, stated as
   his: to beat the republic he needed a mass movement; to stay conservative he needed
   it to want what he wanted; nothing in "the Third Party" could make it do so. Where the
   lesson concedes: Moeller's distinction between the conservative and the reactionary
   is a real distinction, used by later writers on both sides (`what-conservatism-is`),
   and the book shows no National Socialist intention; the authors of an idea are not
   answerable for its theft. The weighing: the concession is about the man, the verdict
   about the claim. The claim was that the revolution could be kept conservative, and
   nothing in the book could keep it so — Moeller named the danger himself. Verdict, in
   the course's voice: the conservative revolution, as argued here, fails (**strong**).
   State what would overturn it: a mechanism in the book that holds the "Third Party"
   to conservative ends against a mass movement.

Conclusion: state the verdict in plain words with its grade — a revolution that
creates what it would conserve has given up the reasons for conserving, and Moeller's
idea, by his own warning misty and without institution, programme or leader, was taken
by a movement he disdained; the claim fails (strong). Then the hand-off: Moeller's
conservatism still rests on history and the nation, and
`evola-revolt-against-the-modern-world` asks for a standard that condemns all of history
at once; whether these doctrines gave their holders any ground to refuse the movement in
power is decided in `the-right-in-1933`.

Headings as claims (suggested): "A war may be lost; a revolution must be won"; "The
reactionary restores, the conservative creates"; "Liberalism as the principle of no
principle"; "Every people its own socialism"; "A democracy with a leader"; "The third
empire as an idea"; "A revolution cannot be kept conservative by intentions".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **"A war may be lost … But a Revolution must be won"** (I.1, l.120–124): the four
   sentences quoted and parsed — what kind of defeat is reversible (military, diplomatic),
   what kind is not (a people's own failure to remake itself).
2. **The reactionary, the revolutionary, the conservative** (VI.5, l.4370; VII.6, l.4833;
   VI.2, l.3952; l.4390): three one-line definitions laid side by side as a table (FIGURE
   1), then tested on two cases from earlier lessons: Maistre (Ref) and Jünger (Ref) —
   which column does each fall in by Moeller's own definitions?
3. **"Ninety out of every hundred … But they don't want to be!"** (V.5, l.3535): the 1919
   meeting as Moeller tells it; worked as an argument: what the interruption is meant to
   prove (proletarian is a consciousness, so the class war can be dissolved by a national
   one) and what it does not prove.
4. **"Every people has its own socialism"** (II.7, l.1421–1430): the tour — Russian
   socialism made a red army, the French, the English — and the German corporative
   answer. Pair with the inverted base/superstructure (FIGURE 2).
5. **"Germany has proved TOO GOOD for parliaments" / "a democracy with a leader—not
   parliamentism"** (IV.4–5, l.2821, l.2868): the definition of democracy as a people's
   share in its fate, against the counting of votes.
6. **The author's warning about his own idea** (Letter, l.64–70): quoted whole, with the
   next paragraph's "it must quit the world of dreams and step into the political world".
   The lesson's pivot: the author states the danger and the demand in consecutive
   paragraphs.
7. **The flags** (VIII.1, l.5500–5520): red (Soviet), black-red-gold (1848 and the
   republic), black-white-red (the Second Reich), and "the black flag of need" — a
   compact image of the book's claim to stand above all parties. FIGURE 4.
8. **The three ages and the three empires** (Letter l.50–54; Voegelin l.4377–4492): the
   course's own two-row figure (FIGURE 3), stated with care as lineage of a schema, not
   as proof of intent.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **The register rule.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right. (In particular, the critic's phrase "annihilation by labels" may appear only
  as a quotation with its author and date — and since the author is a scholar, L8a sends
  him to References: state the objection in the course's words, link 7 b.)
- **"Third Reich" / "Third Empire" / "Das dritte Reich".** The 1934 translation renders
  the title "Germany's Third Empire"; the lesson's title uses "Third Reich". Say once that
  Reich means empire or realm, that the English translators chose "Empire", and quote the
  translation as printed.
- **Do not read the book backwards from 1933.** It names no leader, no party, no racial
  programme of state; its "leader" is a function, its "Third Party" a spirit across
  parties. Equally do not read it as innocent: it carries antisemitic portraits (the
  Pariser Platz scene, I.3 l.358–362; Marx, II.7 l.1426 "A homeless man" and V l.3369–3390;
  III.3–4 Freemasonry passage l.1690–1725), stab-in-the-back blame for 1918 (I.3–5), an
  expansionist population argument ("twenty million too many", "a proletarian nation",
  II.6), and racial idiom (VIII.6: "The shadow of Africa falls across Europe"). Present
  these as part of the document, quoted only where the argument uses them (the population
  argument does, link 4 e; the Marx portrait does as the form his anti-Marxism takes),
  neither hidden nor amplified. Fawcett notes the book's echoes in Nazi speeches; the
  lesson states the echoes it can show from the text (Third Reich, national socialism,
  leader, proletarian nation, the liberal as enemy) and says that the echo is in the
  words.
- **The 1922 Hitler meeting** — course.yaml says Moeller's contempt "is attested in the
  secondary literature". The dossier has only Voegelin's "no National Socialist
  intentions"; Stern (1961) and Klemperer (1957) are the attestations, not in hand. The
  lesson may state "Moeller met Hitler at his club in 1922 and, by the accounts of those
  present, thought little of him" only after the writer verifies it in Stern; otherwise it
  states only Voegelin's finding as the course's own ("the book shows no intention of
  the kind"), with Voegelin in References. Never quote a remark not verified.
- **Moeller's suicide (May 1925)** after a breakdown: a fact stated once, plainly, with
  the date — it bears on the record (the idea outlived its author and was edited for a
  new readership: 2nd ed. 1930, 3rd 1931). No speculation about motive.
- **"Conservative Revolution" as a label** was fixed in 1949 (Mohler, via Fawcett); the
  phrase occurs in the 1920s (Hofmannsthal used it in 1927 — not in the dossier, do not
  state unless verified) but the grouping of Moeller, Jünger, Spengler under it is
  postwar. The concept definition in concepts.yaml says so ("the name was fixed after
  1945"); state it once here, where the concept is introduced.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), ≤3 per section:** Reich; Weimar; Versailles and reparations;
  fulfilment policy; the Ruhr occupation; the June Club; Heinrich von Gleichen; Stein
  (Prussian reformer of 1807–08 who abolished serfdom and created town self-government —
  one clause); Stahl (Prussian conservative jurist of the 1840s–50s — one clause);
  corporatism / estates (representation by trade and profession, not by territory and
  party — define at link 4); "proletarian nation".
- **Spengler and the "young nations".** The key_idea "the young peoples against the old"
  rests mostly on Moeller's 1919 book (not in the dossier); in Germany's Third Empire the
  theme appears as "young nations" (II.2 l.796, II.6 l.1477) and Germany as "a proletarian
  nation"; and Moeller corrects Spengler by name: the creative focus is "the centre, not
  the west … and not the east, as Spengler too rashly anticipated" (VIII, l.5554). Use
  that sentence for the backward Ref to `spengler-decline-of-the-west`.
- **Do not re-teach** Marx's base/superstructure, Rousseau's general will, Burke's
  inheritance, Maistre's counter-revolution, Schmitt's critique of liberalism, Sorel's
  myth, Jünger's Worker — one clause plus `<Ref>` each.
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers: the dates 1918, 1922/23, 1925, 1930, 1933; "twenty million
  too many" only as the book's own claim inside the population argument, framed as such
  (the population of Germany was about 60 million — the book's "60 million on land for 40
  million"). No other figures.
- **Dossier thin on:** biography (Fawcett's paragraph and glossary only), the June Club,
  the Hitler meeting, the regime's later use and distance, Moeller's other books. The
  argument is fully grounded in the 1934 translation.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** define, in Moeller's own terms, the reactionary, the
   revolutionary, the liberal and the conservative, one sentence each, and give the
   quotation that carries each definition.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** the Prefatory Letter's warning (l.64–70).
   Author (a Berlin writer of the young-conservative circles), date (the letter is dated
   December 1922; the book appeared in 1923), addressee (Heinrich von Gleichen, the friend
   and political ally to whom the book is addressed), purpose. What does the passage record, argue and omit
   (any institution that would turn the idea into "concrete reality")? What can it be
   used to establish about Moeller's intentions, and what about the idea's later use?
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Moeller was a reactionary who wanted the Kaiser back."
   Name the passages that break it (I.1, VI.1–2, VII.9: "a monarchy must be won, not
   restored"; the reactionary as "the inner danger") and state what kind of anti-republican
   he was instead.
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** apply Moeller's definition — "the conservative creates by
   giving to phenomena a form in which they can endure" — to the Bolshevik state of 1923,
   which the book itself (VI.4) treats as a case of conservatism in Russia. Does the
   definition admit it? What does that show about a definition of conservatism that
   leaves out what is conserved?
5. **Retrieval across lessons (E3):** set Burke's "partnership … between those who are
   living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born" (Ref `burke-reflections`)
   beside "We live in order to bequeath". Name the shared premise and the step Moeller
   adds that Burke refused (the conservative as creator after a break).
6. **Derivation the lesson did not do (E6c):** from "the primary laws of history are
   political laws" and "every people has its own socialism", derive what Moeller must say
   about the international class struggle, and check the derivation against V.6 ("a
   proletarian nation") and II.6 (population). Then state where the derivation leads
   in foreign policy, in the book's own words.
7. **Counter-argument (synthesis):** "The Nazis took nothing from Moeller but a title."
   Name two things beyond the title the text shares with the movement that took it (the
   liberal as the enemy; national socialism against Marx; the leader against parliament;
   the proletarian nation) and two the text lacks (a racial programme of state; a party);
   state what would have to be true for the claim to survive, and what for its opposite
   ("Moeller was the movement's thinker") to survive.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `voegelin-gnosticism`:** the Letter says the idea of a third empire
   "arose when our First Empire fell" and "was early quickened by the thought of a
   millennium". Using FIGURE 3, state what a history divided into three ages, the third
   being final and fulfilling, promises that a history of "reforms" does not, and name
   one other modern political movement of the course (Ref `marx-manifesto`) that shares
   the shape. The solution says in one sentence that a postwar lesson reads this shape —
   the immanent "third age" — as the mark of modern mass movements, with a `<Ref>`.
9. **Attack the verdict (E6b):** the lesson judged that a revolution cannot be kept
   conservative by the intentions of those who call for it (strong). State the
   strongest objection in its best form — that Moeller cannot be judged by a theft
   after his death, that his conservative/reactionary distinction is sound, and that
   any idea can be taken by a movement its author disdains — and say what evidence
   would be needed to overturn the verdict: a passage in the book that gives the "Third
   Party" a mechanism (institution, selection rule, check) holding it to conservative
   ends, or a conservative revolution elsewhere that stayed conservative because of such
   a mechanism.

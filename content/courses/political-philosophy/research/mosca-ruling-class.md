# Research brief: mosca-ruling-class (Mosca's ruling class: the organized minority and the political formula)

status: planned
module: crisis-of-liberal-europe
type: core
goal: Analyze Mosca's thesis that every society is ruled by an organized minority justified by a political formula, and evaluate what it does to the classical question of the best regime

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The classical question in the form the reader learned it:
  Aristotle's six regimes, sorted by the number of rulers and by whether they
  rule for the common good (`aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, concept
  `six-regimes`); Polybius's mixed constitution, in which each part can block
  the others (`polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`, `mixed-constitution`,
  `checks-and-balances`); Machiavelli's effectual truth, how power is actually
  kept (`machiavelli-effectual-truth`). Hume's paradox that "the many are
  governed by the few" because "Force is always on the side of the governed",
  so that government rests on opinion alone
  (`hume-against-the-original-contract`, `government-founded-on-opinion`) —
  the backward anchor for the political formula. Rousseau's popular
  sovereignty and Marx's ruling class as the owning class
  (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`, `marx-manifesto`, `class-struggle`)
  — the two doctrines Mosca writes against. Maine's and Lecky's fragility of
  popular government and Maine's party "wirepuller"
  (`maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government`, prerequisite). From the module
  just finished: Taine's Revolution as the conquest of France by a Jacobin
  minority (`taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`) — Mosca generalises
  Taine's method; Gobineau's and Chamberlain's race as the cause of a
  civilization's rise and fall (`gobineau-inequality-of-races`,
  `chamberlain-and-grant`) — Mosca rejects race as an explanation of
  political organization in his first chapter; and the Dreyfus Affair, "a case
  decided in the end by evidence, courts and a parliamentary republic"
  (`dreyfus-affair-as-test`), the last lesson the reader read.
- **Contributes:** The module's first move. The classical question "one, few
  or many?" is badly put, because in every regime an organized minority
  rules: a hundred who act together beat a thousand who act alone. Mosca puts
  three things in its place — the political formula (the principle by which
  a minority justifies its rule in terms the ruled believe), juridical
  defense (the degree to which law restrains the powerful) as the measure of
  a good regime, and the balance of social forces as what produces it — and
  ends, in 1923, by defending the representative system he had attacked in
  1884. The reader leaves able to restate "who should rule?" as "which
  minority, under what formula, checked by which other forces?".
- **Hands off:** Two problems stay open. (i) Mosca studied states. Whether an
  organization FOUNDED on the principle that the many should rule — a
  democratic party with elective offices — escapes the law, and why ruling
  classes rise and fall, is `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites` (next).
  (ii) Mosca's formula is a belief the ruled need; a writer twenty years later,
  knowing nothing of Mosca, calls the binding image of a class a myth —
  `sorel-reflections-on-violence` (next module). Forward, one clause each:
  Burnham's managers as a new ruling class
  (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`); Lasch's meritocratic elite
  (`lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`).
- **Spine step:** "Liberal Europe breaks: elite theorists show that a minority
  always rules, Mann sets Kultur against Zivilisation, Spengler foretells the
  West's winter, Ortega diagnoses the mass-man, Schmitt names the political as
  friend and enemy and the sovereign as he who decides the exception — and
  Weimar is the case"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "Aristotle sorted regimes by whether one, few
or many rule. A Sicilian law professor in 1896 says the question is badly put:
in every society that has ever existed — monarchy, republic, democracy — a small
organized minority rules a large disorganized majority, and a hundred men acting
together will always beat a thousand who act alone. If that is true, the old
classification sorts nothing. The lesson follows what he puts in its place."

Anchor (L11), in the first section: Gaetano Mosca (1858–1941), born in Palermo,
taught constitutional law (Palermo, Turin, later Rome); his first book (1884)
attacked parliamentary government; the *Elements of Political Science* appeared
in 1896 and was enlarged in 1923; he sat as a deputy from 1908 and was made a
senator for life in 1919 (Livingston l.230 says 1918 — check, give the year
the writer can confirm). The newcomer's problem: the reader has the six regimes
(Ref) and has just watched a republic survive the Dreyfus Affair through courts
and an elected Chamber (Ref `dreyfus-affair-as-test`); Mosca claims that in that
republic, as in the Tsar's empire, a minority decided — and that this is no
scandal but a law.

1. **Does "one, few or many?" sort anything?** Obvious attempt: yes — the six
   regimes (Ref `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, one clause: number of
   rulers × the end they rule for). Why Mosca says it fails (Ch. II §1–2): no
   head of state governs alone — he needs a class behind him to collect taxes,
   command troops and judge; no people governs as a whole — it acts through
   those who organize it. So the labels sort the TITLE of power, not its
   holders, and two regimes with the same label differ more than two with
   different ones (Italy and France against England, l.802; Russia and Turkey).
   Even Aristotle's democracy "was simply an aristocracy of fairly broad
   membership" (l.804). Answer: "two classes of people appear" in every society
   (l.784). Seam (L10b, say the reversal): from a critic who calls democracy a
   rule of the few one expects contempt for elections; Mosca's argument is about
   every regime at once, the Tsar's included. Heading: "The six regimes sort
   titles, not rulers".
2. **Why must the minority win?** Obvious attempts: the few are stronger, or
   richer, or better, or they conspire. Mosca's answer is arithmetic of
   organization (Ch. II §3, l.808): a hundred who act "uniformly in concert"
   beat a thousand who are "not in accord and can therefore be dealt with one by
   one", and the hundred concert easily BECAUSE they are a hundred; so the
   larger the community, the smaller the ruling share and the harder for the
   majority to organize against it. Then what admits a man to the minority
   (§4–6): whatever a society most values at the time — valour among warriors
   (Poland's armed horsemen become nobles, the peasants serfs: Casimir's "sticks
   and stones", l.820), then land, then wealth once the state protects property
   better than private force ("mailed fist … pounds and pence", l.838), priestly
   learning, legal knowledge. Every ruling class tends to make itself hereditary
   in fact before it does in law (§7), and the qualities it credits to its blood
   come largely from upbringing. Seam to the last module (Ref
   `gobineau-inequality-of-races`): Gobineau explained a civilization by the
   blood of its founding race; Mosca, writing forty years later, rejects race
   and climate as explanations of political organization in his first chapter
   (Ch. I §10–14) and puts organization in their place. Worked case: the
   arithmetic run on a city of 10,000 and a state of 10,000,000 (the ruling
   share falls as the denominator grows — figure 2).
3. **If a minority always rules, why does the majority obey it?** Obvious
   attempt: force, or fraud. The reader already has a better answer (Ref
   `hume-against-the-original-contract`): force is always on the side of the
   governed, so government rests on opinion — quote Hume's one sentence. Mosca
   adds a name and a mechanism (Ch. III §1, l.926–940): every ruling class
   rests its power on a "political formula" — the divine right of kings, the
   Son of Heaven, the will of the people — a principle drawn from the beliefs of
   the society it rules. Two corrections the newcomer needs at once: the
   formula is not a deliberate deception (Livingston's gloss, l.234: men defer
   more readily to an abstract principle than to another man's will — mark it as
   the editor's wording); and it need not be true to work: Spencer's quip about
   the two "great superstitions" is answered by "a universal illusion" that
   binds (l.934). Consequence the lesson must draw: popular sovereignty is a
   formula like the others — universal suffrage was granted so that the minority
   that held control could "govern with a clear conscience" (XVII, l.3960). And
   a formula that has not sunk into the lower classes leaves two peoples in one
   state, separated by a "silken thread" of manners (Ch. IV §3–5, l.1226, 1272).
   Heading: "The political formula: why the many obey the few".
4. **If every regime is a minority's rule, is any regime better than
   another?** This is the lesson's centre and the goal's second half. Obvious
   inference: no — if all are oligarchies, the old question is dead and only
   power is left. Mosca refuses it (Ch. V). The measure is JURIDICAL DEFENSE —
   "respect for law, government by law" (l.1350), the mechanisms that discipline
   the powerful; Guicciardini's liberty, "a prevalence of law and public decrees
   over the appetites of particular men" (l.1386). What produces it: not the
   number of rulers but the number of ORGANIZED SOCIAL FORCES that share power
   and check one another — land, money, the army, the clergy, learning, labour.
   Despotism is the absolute dominance of one principle or one force (§6); the
   old preference for mixed government (Ref `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`) was
   right for a reason its authors did not state — a constitution's parts
   restrain each other only if each embodies a real social force, which is why
   Montesquieu's paper separation of powers fails where it does not (§7). The
   requisites (§8–9): a temporal power with nothing sacred about it (separation
   of church and state); wealth and arms not in the same hands; a large middle
   class economically independent of the state. The test case Mosca runs on it
   (Ch. XI §3, XIV §8): a state that owns all production makes its officials
   arbiters of every fortune — one force absorbs the rest — and "never have the
   many … ruled the few": the dictatorship of the proletariat "could never be
   anything more than the dictatorship of a very restricted class" (l.3334).
   Answer the goal states: the classical question survives, transformed — not
   "how many rule?" but "which minority, under what formula, checked by which
   other forces?". KeyFact candidate (it carries the synthesis): that sentence.
5. **What do elections do, then?** Obvious attempt: the voters choose their
   representative. Mosca (Ch. VI §1, l.1554): "the representative has himself
   elected by the voters … his friends have him elected"; the voters choose among
   two or three names that organized minorities put before them. Ref
   `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government` (the party and the wirepuller): the
   reader has met the observation; Mosca makes it general. But — the reversal —
   he does not conclude that elections are worthless: they force many organized
   minorities to compete and to discuss in public, and a small independent
   minority in an assembly can expose a bureaucracy. The danger is the opposite
   case: an assembly that concentrates all authority gives "the irresponsible
   and anonymous tyranny of those who win in the elections" (l.1576). Heading:
   "The representative has himself elected — and why that still matters".
6. **Is the ruling class fixed?** Obvious answer from link 2: it becomes
   hereditary. Mosca's fuller answer (Ch. II §8; Ch. XV): two tendencies always
   at work — the ARISTOCRATIC tendency to keep power among the descendants of
   those who hold it, and the DEMOCRATIC tendency to replenish the ruling class
   from below — and, on a separate axis, two PRINCIPLES of transmitting
   authority, autocratic (from above) and liberal (from below). The axes are
   independent: imperial China was autocratic yet recruited officials by
   examination; Venice and the Polish republic were elective yet closed to all
   but hereditary nobles (l.3348–3362). Who gets in is decided by those already
   in — "one cannot enter paradise over the veto of the saints" (l.3412). A
   ruling class declines when it can no longer render the service that raised
   it and stops renewing itself; a moderate democratic tendency is therefore
   CONSERVATIVE, because it refreshes the class (XV §5). And peoples do not die
   of old age: "this idea of the aging of races seems to us the product of a
   wholly specious analogy between the life of an individual and the life of a
   community … each new generation has all the vigor of youth" (Ch. I,
   l.666–668) — a sentence the reader will need again in
   `spengler-decline-of-the-west` (forward Ref, one clause, no more).
7. **What did the theorist of minority rule recommend?** The reader now
   expects a programme against parliament; Mosca's first book (1884) was one.
   The 1923 Part II reverses the expectation (Ch. XVII §2–6): the representative
   system has embraced the largest societies at the highest levels of
   civilization because it lets the most social forces into the ruling class
   and sets them to check each other; the three "radical solutions" on offer
   after 1918 — dictatorship of the proletariat, bureaucratic Caesarism,
   syndicalism — are regressions to the dominance of one force; universal
   suffrage was a mistake that cannot be undone; "the old house is threatening
   to fall before the materials for building the new one are ready" (l.4108);
   and the author, looking back on his book of fifty years before, urges the
   rising generation "to restore and conserve the political system which it
   inherited from its fathers" (l.4110). The editor's observation (Livingston
   l.350; state it as a dated fact, not as his verdict): the regimes that ended
   Italian and German parliamentary government did not arise from universal
   suffrage's demagogy. If the writer has verified it: Mosca's December 1925
   Senate speech in defence of parliamentary government against the Fascist law
   on the head of government's powers. Heading: "The elitist as a defender of
   the representative system".
8. **The objections, at full strength.** (a) *From the democratic side — the
   thesis is true of everything, so it explains nothing.* Mosca concedes the
   first half in his own words (Ch. XII §3, l.2876): to say a minority rules
   everywhere "is to dismiss the old guides without supplying new ones"; only a
   comparative study of how ruling classes are formed earns the claim its keep.
   And the democrat adds what Mosca's measure leaves out: whether the minority
   can be REMOVED by the many without violence — a criterion on which regimes
   differ sharply and which "every regime is an oligarchy" hides. Mosca's reply
   is in his own link 5: elections matter because they force minorities to
   compete; the democrat's rejoinder is that this concedes the point. Leave the
   exchange open; Michels and Pareto (next) and the competitive theory of
   democracy take it up. (b) *From the left — the ruling class is the owning
   class, and the formula is its ideology* (Ref `marx-manifesto`: "the ruling
   ideas of each age have ever been the ideas of its ruling class"). Mosca's
   reply (Ch. XVI §3, XI §6): political change without economic change (the
   Roman Empire's conversion, the medieval communes, absolutism); the economic
   factor one cause among several; exploiters and exploited in every stratum.
   Mark the convergence before the split: both say the beliefs that justify rule
   serve the rulers; they part on whether the need for a formula belongs to
   human nature (Mosca) or to class society (Marx), and so on whether a
   classless society would need none. (c) *From inside — a formula its own
   theorist does not believe.* If popular sovereignty is a formula and the ruled
   need it, what is a ruling class to do once it has read Mosca? His answer: the
   ruling class must "become conscious of itself" and raise its competence
   (XVII §6) while the formula keeps the ruled loyal; the difficulty — a class
   that sees through the principle it asks others to believe — is stated and left
   for Pareto, who builds his theory on it (next lesson). No verdict.

Headings as claims: "The six regimes sort titles, not rulers"; "A hundred in
concert beat a thousand alone"; "The political formula: why the many obey the
few"; "Juridical defense: the best regime is the best-checked minority"; "The
representative has himself elected — and why that still matters"; "Principles,
tendencies, and the circulation of rulers"; "The elitist as a defender of the
representative system"; "What the thesis cannot sort".

   paradox quoted whole (public domain), Mosca's "political formula" sentence
   and Spencer's two superstitions quoted short — the formula as Hume's
   "opinion" given a content and a function.
4. **"His friends have him elected"** (Ch. VI §1, l.1554): parse it as a
   three-step funnel — the electorate; the organized minorities that put names
   forward; the two or three names among which the voters choose. Then the
   anonymous-tyranny sentence (l.1576) as the other limit.
5. **Juridical defense on one case: Guicciardini's liberty and the Aragonese
   oath** (Ch. V §5, l.1386; Ch. XIV §5, l.3202–3204): "a prevalence of law and
   public decrees over the appetites of particular men"; then the barons of
   Aragon naming their king "on the following conditions … And otherwise not" —
   an organized force that checks the crown, which is what juridical defense
   rests on. Two short quotations; one paragraph.
6. **Principles against tendencies** (Ch. XV §1, l.3348–3362): the 2×2
   (autocratic/liberal × aristocratic/democratic) filled with Mosca's own cases
   — imperial China (autocratic, open by examination), Venice and Poland
   (liberal in form, closed in recruitment), and the reader places the Roman
   Republic and the French Third Republic as an exercise.
7. **The critic turned defender** (Ch. XVII §6, l.4108–4110): the "old house"
   sentence and the retrospect on "a book of his youth", quoted short, set
   against the 1884 title (*Teorica dei governi e governo parlamentare* — the
   theory of governments and parliamentary government). The reader sees the
   same theory serve two verdicts.
8. **"Never have the many … ruled the few"** (Ch. XIV §8, l.3334): the sentence
   and its application to the dictatorship of the proletariat — the theory's one
   explicit forecast about a regime that did not yet exist when the 1923 text was
   written (the Soviet state existed; its development is the reader's check).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register (exact rule).** The course passes no verdict of its own on any
  author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice.
  Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only
  inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Terms to anchor at first use (L11), at most three per section:** ruling
  class / political class (Mosca uses both; "political class", *classe
  politica*, is his Italian term — say so once, link 1); organized minority
  (link 2); political formula (link 3); social type (Mosca's term for a people
  united by beliefs and habits — only if the writer uses Ch. IV, link 3);
  juridical defense (link 4 — explain that "juridical" means "of law", and that
  the phrase is Mosca's, translated literally; *difesa giuridica*); social
  forces (link 4: any activity with political weight — land, money, arms,
  religion, learning, labour); principles vs tendencies (link 6); Guicciardini
  (link 4: Florentine historian and statesman, early sixteenth century, a
  contemporary of Machiavelli); Spencer (link 3: the English evolutionary
  philosopher, 1820–1903); syndicalism (link 7: the labour movement that sought
  power through unions and the general strike rather than parliament — Ref
  forward to `sorel-reflections-on-violence`, one clause); dictatorship of the
  proletariat (link 4: Marx's phrase for the workers' rule after a revolution —
  not in the Manifesto but in his later writings; Ref `marx-manifesto` for the
  class-rule thesis it extends). Places: Palermo/Sicily (link 1 anchor sentence);
  Aragon (set piece 5: the medieval kingdom in north-east Spain).
- **Do not re-teach** the six regimes, Polybius's mixture, Hume's opinion, Marx's
  class struggle, Taine's Jacobin minority, Gobineau's race: each is a `<Ref>`
  and a clause. The lesson's new matter is Mosca's three concepts and the
  argument that links them.
- **Livingston is not Mosca.** The 1939 Introduction is a long editorial essay
  ("Mosca's law", the reading of Part II as "a bona fide return"). Quote
  Mosca's chapters for Mosca's claims; use Livingston for biography and
  framing, with his name in References only.
- **The real misreading (L13):** "Mosca is an enemy of democracy who wants the
  few to rule." The text refutes it in two places: his claim is descriptive and
  covers every regime (Ch. II); his recommendation in 1923 is the
  representative system (Ch. XVII). Equally do not make him a democrat: he
  calls universal suffrage a mistake and Rousseau's popular sovereignty a
  formula. One slot, built on link 7.
- **Period judgments.** The text carries dated racial, colonial and class
  remarks (summary pedagogy note) — e.g. the anecdote reported in Ch. I §11.
  The lesson does not use them as examples and does not need them; where Mosca's
  rejection of race theories is the point (link 2), quote that rejection, not
  his asides.
- **"Elite" is Pareto's word, not Mosca's.** Keep "ruling class" / "political
  class" here; the next lesson introduces Pareto's elite and Livingston's note
  (Intro IX) that Mosca's ruling class is narrower than Pareto's elite or
  Marx's class.
- **Numbers (L12):** none needed beyond dates (1884, 1896, 1923, 1939 English)
  and the hundred and the thousand. Mosca's Roman citizen counts (Ch. XIV) are
  not this lesson's.
- **Dossier thin:** the 1925 Senate speech, Mosca's 1884 *Teorica* and any
  biography beyond Livingston's paragraph are not in the dossier (see
  Sources). The Burnham framing is not in the dossier.
- **Do not prejudge the forecast in set piece 8.** Report the sentence and its
  date; the reader compares it with the record in an exercise; the course does
  not score it.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up, E3):** Name Aristotle's six regimes (Ref
   `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`), then state in one sentence each Mosca's
   two reasons why the classification "sorts nothing" (no ruler governs alone;
   the same label covers regimes that differ more than regimes with different
   labels).
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill) — corroboration:** Hume's sentence
   ("Force is always on the side of the governed … on opinion only that
   government is founded", 1741) and Mosca's political-formula passage (Ch. III
   §1, 1896). Metadata first (a Scottish essayist under a settled monarchy; a
   Sicilian law professor in a young parliamentary kingdom). Where do they
   agree (force alone does not rule)? Where does Mosca go further (the opinion
   has a content — a principle — and a function — it binds even when false)?
   What can either passage be USED to establish about how a real government
   kept obedience, and what can neither establish without other evidence?
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "If every regime is ruled by a minority, then
   there is no difference between a constitutional monarchy and a despotism."
   Name the step that fails (juridical defense varies with the number and
   balance of organized social forces, Ch. V §6–9; Mosca's whole measure of a
   good regime is this difference) and state what DOES survive of the claim
   (that the number of formal rulers is not the difference).
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** Run the hundred-and-thousand argument at its
   limits. (i) A community of thirty people who all know each other: does the
   arithmetic still give a ruling minority, and why does Mosca say political
   problems arise only in large aggregates (Ch. I §20)? (ii) A majority that is
   itself organized — a mass party with a hundred thousand members. Does
   organization then favour the many? Say what Mosca's premise ("easier … simply
   because they are a hundred") predicts will happen inside that party. The
   solution names the next lesson's question.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** From (a) every ruling class rests
   on a formula drawn from the beliefs of the ruled, (b) a formula must sink
   into the lower strata or the state holds two peoples, and (c) Mosca's own
   claim that the representative formula is popular sovereignty — derive what he
   must predict for a state in which the educated classes stop believing in
   popular sovereignty while the mass still believes in it, and the reverse.
   Then say which of the two describes Mosca's own position as a writer.
6. **Research-shaped (E6d):** Place on the principles × tendencies grid,
   defending each placement from what earlier lessons established: the Roman
   Republic of Polybius (Ref `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`), the French Third
   Republic of the Dreyfus Affair (Ref `dreyfus-affair-as-test`), and the
   British state after the franchise reforms (Ref `british-franchise`). For one
   of them, name the evidence that would move it to another cell.
7. **Synthesis / counter-argument stem:** "Mosca's political formula is Marx's
   ideology under another name." Name two facts the claim fails to account for
   (Mosca holds the need for a formula to be a constant of human nature, so a
   classless society would need one too; he holds that a formula can be "a
   universal illusion" that binds rulers as well as ruled), and state what
   would have to be true for the claim to survive (that the beliefs of the ruled
   are produced by the rulers' class position rather than drawn from the ruled's
   own religion and sentiments). Ref `marx-manifesto`.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`:** Take the best
   case for escaping Mosca's law: an organization whose formula IS the rule of
   the many — every office elective, every member an equal, a programme of
   popular sovereignty. Using only links 2 and 5 (the arithmetic of
   organization; the representative who has himself elected), predict what will
   happen to its leadership over twenty years: who will hold office, how long,
   and how the members' formal power to remove them will be used. The solution
   says the reader has written, from Mosca's premises, the thesis the next
   lesson tests on the largest such party in Europe, with a `<Ref>`.

# Research brief: murray-human-accomplishment (Charles Murray's Human Accomplishment: measuring greatness in the arts and sciences)

status: planned
module: why-the-west
type: core
goal: Analyze Murray's method of counting significant figures across 2,800 years of arts and sciences and his finding that accomplishment concentrated in Europe, and evaluate what the method can and cannot measure

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `what-measures-greatness` (concept `measures-of-greatness`): the three rival
  measures — power, duration, ideas others take up — and the open question whether any of them can
  be measured rather than asserted; that lesson pointed here for "ideas others take up, measured
  by count". From `landes-european-exceptionalism` (`european-fragmentation`,
  `invention-of-invention`): the claim that Europe made and kept more inventions because no
  single authority could stop them — asserted, illustrated by contrasts, never counted. From
  `weber-only-in-the-west` (`protestant-ethic`): a religious cause for Europe's difference, which
  Murray will reopen in his last part. From `the-cognitive-elite` and `rawls-theory-of-justice`:
  the reader has met Charles Murray as co-author of *The Bell Curve* (1994), and Rawls's
  "Aristotelian principle" (people enjoy exercising their realized capacities, the more the
  greater the complexity), which Murray takes as the mainspring of accomplishment.
- **Contributes:** The one move: the claim that one civilization contributed more to the arts and
  sciences than another is turned from an assertion into a count with stated rules — every
  serious history, encyclopaedia and chronology of a field, in several languages, is read for
  whom it mentions, and a person counts as "significant" if at least half of the qualified
  sources name him. The count is reliable (independent sources agree), lopsided (from 1400 to 1950
  almost everything comes from Europe, and within Europe from a compact core), and — once divided
  by population — shows Europe's *rate* falling from the mid-19th century. The lesson builds the
  count step by step, reproduces one piece of it, lets Murray's tests for Eurocentric bias run at
  full strength, and then states what the method cannot see: what its sources' canon leaves out,
  what "firsts" reward, and everything it excludes by design — governance, commerce, the
  subject of this course. Then it judges the finding: European dominance of the sciences after
  1400 holds and is not an artefact of Eurocentric sources (**strong**); for the arts the same
  claim holds only **on balance**; the count says nothing about causes.
- **Hands off:** The lesson decides what the count shows (strong for the sciences after 1400; on
  balance for the arts) and passes on what it cannot: *why*. The cause is judged in
  `the-borrowing-ledger` (open). (i) The count says *where* and *when*, not *why*. Murray's own explanation —
  purpose, autonomy, the transcendental goods, and Christianity as "one of the most important"
  variables — names a cause without a mechanism; a mechanism for Christianity's effect on
  individual autonomy is `henrich-church-and-kinship` (FORWARD exercise). (ii) The objection that
  a "first" in a field records the culture that systematized a discovery rather than the one that
  made the parts of it, and that the sources share a European canon, is the opening of
  `pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis` and is tested item by item in `the-borrowing-ledger`.
  (iii) Murray's chapter on women and Jews, and his hypothesis about group differences in measured
  intelligence, are not taught here (see Pitfalls); that argument is the next module's
  (`hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`).
- **Spine step:** "Why the West? Weber, Landes, Murray and Henrich argue for Western exceptionalism
  from religion, fragmentation, accomplishment and kinship; Pomeranz and Goody answer that the West
  was one link in a long chain of exchange; the borrowing ledger tests both"

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged.** Accomplishment in the sciences after 1400 is overwhelmingly European, and
  the finding is not an artefact of Eurocentric sources; the same for the arts.
- **Verdict.** Holds for the sciences — **strong** (sciences after 1400). Weaker for the arts —
  **on balance**. Says nothing about causes.
- **What decides it.** (1) The sciences rest on checkable "firsts": who first did what is a
  question historians of every culture can check, and specialists in Chinese, Indian and Arabic
  science are among the sources (ch. 11, l. 1617–1620). (2) The source most carefully corrected
  with specialists in non-Western science, the *Dictionary of Scientific Biography*, gives the
  same proportions as the other sources — 81 against 76 percent European, 94 against 91 with
  North America (l. 1641–1655). (3) Murray's Challenge (l. 1656–1670) — any rule applied evenly
  that admits a dozen non-Europeans admits hundreds of Europeans — is unmet in the dossier: no
  source the course has names omitted non-European firsts at a scale that changes the shares.
  (4) For the arts there is no test outside expert consensus; Murray concedes "no equivalently
  hard target" (l. 1662), so the canon objection keeps more force there. (5) His causal account
  (purpose, autonomy, transcendental goods, Christianity) has no mechanism and is labelled by him
  "more speculative" — so the count decides where and when, not why.
- **Strongest objection to the verdict.** The sources share one canon, and "firsts" credit a
  discovery to the culture that systematized or named it (paper, the compass, numerals), so the
  count measures European historiography as much as European science; before 1400 the
  sources on Islamic and Chinese work are thinnest. Why it does not overturn the verdict for the
  sciences after 1400: the corrected source agrees with the rest, the borrowings the objection
  names are pre-1400 techniques rather than the post-1400 run of scientific firsts, and the
  Challenge stands unanswered. It does limit the verdict: before 1400 source bias would bite,
  and the verdict is not extended there; for the arts it keeps the grade at on balance.
- **What would overturn it.** A documented list of omitted non-European scientific firsts after
  1400, at a scale that changes the shares.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): to say one civilization contributed more sounds like a prejudice;
an American social scientist tries to make it a count; the tables are lopsided; the lesson
follows how the count is built, what it shows, what it assumes. Anchor (L11): Charles Murray (b.
1943), American political scientist, co-author of the book the reader met in
`<Ref the-cognitive-elite>`; *Human Accomplishment* (2003) covers the arts and sciences from 800
BC to 1950. Disambiguate in one clause: not the British author of a later lesson
(`murray-strange-death-of-europe`).

1. **Can greatness be anything but opinion?** The newcomer's objection, stated as the opening's:
   "Rembrandt was greater than Hopper" is taste; "the Greeks contributed more than the Aztecs" is
   prejudice. Obvious attempt (the relativist's): no ranking is possible. Murray's reply, in
   two steps: (a) refusing to rank is itself a sweeping judgment — to notice a difference is to
   have an opinion about it (ch. 5); (b) in science "important" can be defined by consequence
   (what later work depended on), and in the arts by the convergence of many independent expert
   judgments — Hume's argument that the joint verdict of qualified critics over time is a standard
   of taste (`<Ref hume-against-the-original-contract>` for who Hume is; the essay itself is not
   in the course, say so in a clause). Answer: Murray does not measure excellence; he measures
   *eminence* — how much attention the experts of a field give a person — and argues that
   eminence tracks excellence where the experts are many, independent and of several nations.
   Seam: the reader should now ask how attention can be counted.
2. **How do you count attention?** Obvious attempt: take one good encyclopaedia and list who is in
   it. Why it fails: any one source carries its author's nation (British histories over-weight
   Constable and Turner; French histories of philosophy French thinkers) and its era's fashion.
   Murray's construction, step by step (FigureSequence, FIGURE 1): (a) for each of twelve fields
   gather every serious history, chronology and biographical dictionary, in several languages;
   (b) list everyone named anywhere — the roster; (c) keep as "qualifying" sources only those
   broad enough to cover at least 18 percent of the people named in two or more sources (9–16 per
   field); (d) a person is a **significant figure** if at least half of the qualifying sources
   that cover his time and place name him; (e) for those, the **index score** is the average share
   of each source's attention he receives (index pages, columns, plates), rescaled so the top
   person in the field scores 100 and the lowest 1. Define `accomplishment-inventory` here as the
   answer. Check the reader with Murray's own example: Chopin's raw score of 1.06 means that across
   16 histories of music Chopin received on average about one-hundredth of the attention given to
   all significant composers. Literature is scored only from sources *not* in the author's own
   language (so the English histories do not vote for Thackeray).
3. **Is the count stable?** Obvious objection: change the books, change the list. Murray's tests:
   independent art histories correlate .75–.85; split the sources of any field in two, score each
   half, correlate — at or above .9 in 13 of 20 inventories, median .93; the 50 percent rule
   chosen because it gives the same population whichever dozen sources one uses (Murray: "your
   set of orphans and my set of orphans" would fall in the same places and times). The cutoff at
   1950 avoids judging the recent, and the bias toward recent centuries (Spengler's line about the
   moon looking larger than Jupiter, quoted by Murray) works *against* Murray's later claim of
   decline. Answer: the count is reliable — the sources agree. Mark the open point for link 7:
   agreement among sources can mean they are right, or that they share a canon.
4. **What the count shows.** Three findings, each tied to its rule:
   - *Few names dominate* (ch. 6): in every field eminence is not a bell curve but a steep
     hyperbola — 71 percent of 479 Western artists in the bottom tenth of the scale (FIGURE 2).
     Why it matters here: the shape means a civilization's standing rests on a few hundred people,
     so small changes in who counts move little.
   - *Europe after 1400* (ch. 11): 4,002 significant figures in all; half appear only after 1800;
     in the 1890s 81 percent of new entrants were European; in the sciences 97 percent of figures
     and events belong to Europe and North America (Europe alone 82 percent of figures).
   - *A compact core* (ch. 13): 80 percent of Europe's significant figures grew up inside a polygon
     from Naples and Marseilles to Glasgow, the tip of Denmark and Breslau; outside it lie Russia,
     Iberia, the Balkans, Scandinavia, Ireland — and the southern quarter of Italy (FIGURE 3).
   Seam to `<Ref landes-european-exceptionalism>` in one sentence: Landes's fragmentation thesis
   predicted a pattern of this kind; Murray's regressions (ch. 16) find that the political regime
   *type* explains nothing while de facto "freedom of action" does — 17th-century Germany's 300
   principalities as "alternatives a few miles away" — but that the despotism effect is hard to
   separate from country. That is a partial confirmation, and Murray calls it no more.
5. **Divide by population.** Obvious inference: if the counts rise steeply to 1950, accomplishment
   was growing. Why that fails: so was population. The rate — significant figures per 10 million
   people (France 1850–70: 54 figures in about 36 million, a rate of about 15) — falls in the West
   from the mid-19th century, and the classical Greek rate dwarfs every later one (ch. 14, ch. 21).
   Murray's warning in his own words: "A rate does not measure the quantity of fine work." The
   rate is an index of a culture's density of excellence, not of how much was done. Answer:
   Europe's dominance is a fact about counts after 1400; its *intensity* peaked before 1850. Murray
   tests three artefacts (talent cannot grow with population; markets saturate; recent decades are
   undercounted) and keeps the decline as real in the arts, benign in the sciences ("you only
   discover America once"). Seam: a lopsided count invites the charge that the counters were
   lopsided — Murray's own next chapter.
6. **Is the count Eurocentric? Murray's tests at full strength.** The hypothesis stated as Murray
   states it: Western historians find history made by people like themselves. His four answers:
   (a) the arts and philosophy inventories for China, India, Japan and the Arab world are
   separate, so non-Western figures compete only within their tradition — which *inflates* them
   (a U.S.-only art inventory would hold about 90 Americans where the Western one holds 27); (b)
   in the sciences "first" is a fact checkable by historians of every culture, and specialists in
   Chinese, Indian and Arabic science agree on who discovered what; (c) the most carefully
   corrected source, the *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* (edited over two decades with
   specialists in non-Western science), gives the same proportions as the other sources (81
   against 76 percent European); (d) **the Challenge**: add non-European entries under any rule you
   like, provided the rule counts only firsts and is applied evenly — every relaxation that admits
   a dozen non-Europeans admits hundreds of Europeans. And his concessions (Codicil): 48 percent of
   the 226 highest-scoring figures lived before 1400, many of them in China and India; "the West"
   was Europe, not America, until 1950.
7. **What the method cannot see — the OBJECTIONS at full strength.** Each stated as an argument and
   met by Murray's reply where he gives one:
   - *The canon.* The sources agree because they copy one canon; agreement measures consensus, not
     excellence (Murray states the charge himself, l. 829–832). His reply: the canon was formed by
     several nations' experts over a century, and anyone who disputes it must name the omitted
     figures. Residue: for the arts the reply is weaker than for the sciences, since there is no
     test outside expert judgment — Murray concedes "no equivalently hard target" (l. 1662).
   - *What "firsts" reward.* The scientific inventories credit a discovery once, to the place
     where it first appears in the record; a technique developed over centuries in several places
     (paper, the compass, numerals, gunpowder) enters where it was systematized or named. Murray's
     own Codicil concedes the technology inventory "encourages misinterpretation" for the
     pre-800 BC package. This is the hinge to the rival thesis — `<Ref
     pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis>` and `<Ref the-borrowing-ledger>` — stated here without
     reproach: it asks *what* is being counted, not whether the counter was fair.
   - *What is excluded by design.* Governance, commerce and the social sciences are left out for
     lack of reliable sources; Murray nevertheless nominates, outside the inventories, the
     contractual government, consent of the governed, natural rights (Locke) and Smith's
     positive-sum exchange — then stops: "one person's meta-invention is another's meta-mistake".
     And political thinkers are "secondary figures" in philosophy sources (Hobbes, Locke,
     Rousseau depressed; Cicero and Machiavelli outside the top twenty). For this course the
     consequence is direct: the subject the reader has been studying since the first lesson is the
     one the method cannot count — because, by Murray's own account, there is no agreement on
     what counts as an advance. Tie to `<Ref what-measures-greatness>` (the "ideas others take up"
     measure measured, but only where the experts agree what an idea is worth).
   - *Cause vs count.* Murray's explanations (Part 4): talent is always present; what varies is an
     environment that gives the most able a *purpose* and a belief in their own *autonomy*, a rich
     organizing structure and a live sense of the true, the beautiful and the good — and
     Christianity, which "transmuted" the Greek foundation, as a principal source of both. He
     labels this part "more speculative, and definitely more opinionated". The objection: the
     count cannot test these causes; Murray agrees and leaves them as hypotheses. Hand-off: the
     next lesson offers a mechanism for the Christian part.
8. **The weighing: in the sciences after 1400 the lopsided count is real; in the arts it is
   probable; on causes it is silent.** Set the claim — European dominance is a fact, not an
   artefact of the counters — against its strongest objection at full strength (one canon;
   "firsts" credit the systematizer; thin sources before 1400) and the evidence of the VERDICT
   section: the corrected *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* gives the same shares (81 against
   76 percent European); in the sciences the firsts are checkable across cultures; the Challenge
   is unmet. State the verdict: for the sciences after 1400 the finding holds — **strong**; for
   the arts it holds **on balance**, because there the only test is expert consensus, which is
   what the canon objection questions; before 1400 the verdict is not extended, because that is
   where source bias would bite; and the count establishes nothing about causes — Murray's
   Christianity is a hypothesis without a mechanism. What would overturn it: a documented list
   of omitted non-European firsts after 1400, at a scale that changes the shares.

**Conclusion** (the `<Conclusion>` block): states the verdict in plain words with its grades —
the count is a reliable record of expert attention; in the sciences after 1400 it shows a
European dominance that survives the tests for Eurocentric bias (strong); in the arts the same
holds on balance; it says nothing about why. Then the hand-off: Murray names Christianity as a
cause without a mechanism, and the next lesson proposes one (`henrich-church-and-kinship`).

Headings as claims (suggested): "Ranking is unavoidable; the question is how"; "A significant

sciences' count is real; the arts' is probable; the causes are not counted".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **The label rule.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **The author's other book.** The reader met Charles Murray in `the-cognitive-elite`. Do not let
  that colour this lesson in either direction: one neutral clause of identification, nothing
  about reputation or controversy. Ch. 12 (women 2.2 percent of the 4,002; the rise of Jewish
  significant figures after emancipation, with Murray's hypotheses about environment and measured
  intelligence) is OUT of the body: it fails the deletion test for this goal and its causal
  argument belongs to `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`. If an exercise or Remark mentions that
  the inventories can be split by sex or by group, it says only that Murray does so and Refs
  forward.
- **Eminence is not excellence.** Murray's claim is that eminence among experts *tracks*
  excellence; the lesson must keep the two words apart throughout. "Significant figure" is a
  technical term with a threshold; never use it as praise.
- **"Index score" is relative within one inventory.** Scores are not comparable across
  inventories (Murray's "frog relative to the pond": Ibsen 24th in the West would top Norway).
  Never compare a Chinese painter's score with a Western painter's.
- **Counts vs rates.** The steep rise of the count after 1400 and the fall of the rate after 1850
  are both true; L13 refutation slot only if the writer judges the misreading real ("Murray shows
  Western culture is growing / collapsing"). One slot at most.
- **The extraction lacks every table and chart.** Do not cite a number the prose does not give;
  do not "recreate" a published chart from memory. Where a figure needs data the text lacks, draw
  a schematic and say "schematic" in the alt text.
- **Copyright:** quote a sentence or two; most of Murray's argument is paraphrased.
- **Borrowings (if the lesson touches them in link 7):** paper reached Europe from China through
  the Arab world (secure); gunpowder most likely through Mongol-era contact, with no document of
  the transfer and Europeans plausibly unaware of its origin; Gutenberg's press most probably an
  independent invention. The pro-Western reply at full strength — everyone borrowed; Europe turned
  borrowings into self-sustaining systems — is close to Murray's own "firsts" rule and should be
  stated as such. One sentence, `<Ref the-borrowing-ledger>`. The rival thesis is never phrased as
  a reproach.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), max three per section:** inventory; significant figure; qualifying
  source; index score; Lotka curve (a distribution in which a few account for most of the total);
  reliability (split-half correlation, defined in one sentence as "how far two halves of the
  sources agree"); rate per 10 million; the *Dictionary of Scientific Biography* (a 16-volume
  reference work, 1970–90); "meta-invention" (Murray's term for a cognitive tool such as formal
  logic or linear perspective); -800 = 800 BC (Murray's notation; convert in the body).
- **Do not re-teach** Rawls's Aristotelian principle (one clause + `<Ref rawls-theory-of-justice>`),
  Landes's fragmentation (`<Ref landes-european-exceptionalism>`), Weber's ethic (`<Ref
  weber-only-in-the-west>`), Spengler and Toynbee on greatness (`<Ref what-measures-greatness>`).
- **No moralising either way:** neither "Murray proves Western superiority" nor "Murray's lists are
  biased": the lesson sets his tests against their limits and reaches the graded verdict of link
  8 (sciences after 1400 strong, arts on balance, causes not counted) — a finding about a count,
  not a ranking of peoples — and the exercise lets the reader run one.
- **Dossier coverage:** full book; no published critique of *Human Accomplishment* in the dossier.
  The objections in link 7 are the ones Murray states and answers himself plus the rival thesis's
  question about "firsts" (developed next lesson). Do not invent named critics.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Research-shaped task (E6d) — reproduce one inventory count.** The lesson prints a grid the
   writer compiles and verifies from real books: rows = ten thinkers from this course (suggested:
   Plato, Aristotle, Cicero, Augustine, Aquinas, Machiavelli, Bodin, Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Burke,
   Maistre, Hegel, Tocqueville — choose ten, include at least two the writer expects to fall below
   the bar); columns = five of Murray's actual Western-philosophy sources that the writer can
   check (Russell 1945, Kenny 1994, Copleston 1975, Tarnas 1991, Smart 1999 — Appendix 3, l.
   2963–2969) plus Strauss–Cropsey 1987 as a political-philosophy source; cell = named in the index
   (yes/no). Task: (a) apply the 50 percent rule and list the significant figures; (b) state the
   de facto threshold with six sources and with five (3 of 6 = 50 percent; 3 of 5 = 60 percent —
   Appendix 2's small-number artefact); (c) recompute without Strauss–Cropsey and say whose status
   changes; (d) compare with Murray's remark that political thinkers are "secondary figures in
   some of the sources". The solution shows the full count and states what it can establish
   (agreement among these histories) and cannot (excellence). WRITER: the grid must come from the
   real indexes; record the editions in References; if an index cannot be checked, drop that
   source rather than guess.
2. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Murray's count shows that Europeans were the most creative people in
   history at every period." Name the steps that fail: the count runs from 800 BC and 48 percent
   of the top 226 lived before 1400, many in China and India; the rate falls after 1850; the
   sciences count firsts, not creativity; scores are not comparable across inventories.
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** apply Murray's method to governance. Design a "governance inventory"
   (sources: histories of political thought and constitutional histories in several languages)
   and say at which of the five construction steps it breaks — Murray's own reason (no face
   validity or reliability), and the deeper one he gives in ch. 10: what counts as an advance in
   government depends on one's view of what government is for ("meta-mistake"). Use one example
   from the course where two schools would score the same innovation in opposite directions
   (e.g. universal suffrage: `<Ref maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government>` vs
   `<Ref mill-liberty-and-its-limits>`).
4. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill, paired):** Sivin's sentence that the historical
   discoveries of the last generation leave "no basis for the old myths" of an exclusively European
   science, and Landes's reply ("This was no common stream"), both as quoted by Murray (l.
   1621–1626). Metadata, what each claims as fact and what as evaluation, and Murray's method for
   judging between them (compare the rhetoric with the indexes). Then: what would a historian on
   Sivin's side have to show to meet Murray's Challenge?
5. **Synthesis (E3, spaced):** Landes predicted that exit and competing authorities protected
   innovators (`<Ref landes-european-exceptionalism>`). Using Murray's ch. 16 results — regime type
   explains nothing, de facto freedom of action does, the despotism effect fragile once countries
   are compared with themselves — write a claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph on how far the count
   confirms Landes, and name the one result that would have refuted him.
6. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** Murray says separate non-Western arts inventories
   *inflate* the non-West. Derive why from the 50 percent rule (a figure need only stand out among
   his own tradition's sources) and estimate the direction of the change if the Western arts were
   split into national inventories (Murray: about 90 Americans instead of 27; British, French and
   German literature would add hundreds). State which way this moves the 74 percent Western share.
7. **Attack the verdict (aimed at the course):** The course grades European dominance of the
   sciences after 1400 strong and the arts on balance. State the strongest objection to the
   sciences verdict in its best form (one canon; "firsts" credit the systematizer, not the
   maker of the parts; the corrected *Dictionary* was edited in the same Western tradition),
   then say what evidence would overturn the verdict and what would only lower it. Solution:
   overturn — a documented list of omitted non-European scientific firsts after 1400, found by a
   rule Murray's Challenge allows (firsts only, applied evenly to Europe), at a scale that moves
   the 76–81 percent European share; lower to on balance — a showing that the corrected
   *Dictionary*'s specialists drew on the same canon as the others. Say why pre-1400 borrowings
   (paper, numerals) do not touch the verdict as stated (Ref `the-borrowing-ledger`).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `henrich-church-and-kinship`:** Murray names Christianity as the source of the
   individual *autonomy* he thinks great work requires, but by his own account Christianity "as
   practiced was not individualistic" in its early centuries and its impetus "took more than a
   thousand years" (l. 2454). What would have to change in ordinary
   Christians' *lives* — not their theology — between 500 and 1500 for Murray's claim to hold, and
   what evidence could show it? The solution says in one sentence that the next lesson proposes
   exactly such a change (the Church's rules on marriage and the family dissolving the clans) and

   notes that Murray's European core excludes southern Italy — a detail the next lesson returns
   to.

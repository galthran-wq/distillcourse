# Research brief: national-data-audited (Two datasets audited: national IQ and settler mortality)

status: planned
module: heredity-and-civilization
type: core
goal: Apply the sourcing drill to the key dataset of the hereditarian account (national IQ estimates) and of the institutional account (settler mortality), and judge what each dataset can support

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`: `national-iq` (81 national
  averages measured, 104 more estimated, 185 in all; Britain set at 100), the
  correlation with GDP per head the book reports (rising to about .7 by the 1990s),
  the two-way causal story the authors tell (IQ → income and income → IQ), the
  Flynn effect introduced as a fact the hereditarian case itself discusses, and
  `hereditarian-hypothesis`. From `reich-ancient-dna-and-the-indo-europeans`: that
  populations are mixtures, so a "national" average is an average over a mixture.
  From `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`: the `within-between-fallacy` (heritability
  inside groups does not settle the cause of a gap between them), the Flynn effect
  as environmental change of the size of the gaps at issue, and the hereditarian
  replies. From `geography-or-institutions`: `inclusive-and-extractive-institutions`,
  `critical-juncture`, the reversal of fortune in the colonies, Nogales and the two
  Koreas, Diamond's proximate and ultimate causes — and the institutional account's
  claim that colonial institutions, not geography, explain the gap. That lesson
  states the account; this one opens the table under its statistical claim. From
  `the-borrowing-ledger` (the previous module's application): tracing a claim of
  transfer back to the record that would establish it. From the course's standing
  method (`docs/playbook-history.md`): the sourcing drill (metadata → prediction →
  records/argues/omits → use, not bias → corroboration), so far run on documents.
- **Contributes:** The drill turned on a dataset. A table is a set of claims
  ("Ghana: 71"; "Sierra Leone: so many deaths per thousand"), each with its own
  provenance; the lesson asks of every entry the same five questions — who
  measured, whom, when, how many, how the gaps were filled — and of the table as a
  whole one more: what statistical step carries it to its conclusion. Applied with
  the same standard to the hereditarian table and the institutional table, the
  drill yields what each can carry: the national-IQ table supports a strong
  association between measured cognitive skills and national income (which better
  data corroborate) and does not establish the levels of its weakest entries or
  any cause; the settler-mortality table supports the claim that colonies where
  Europeans died had worse institutions and are poorer today, and its causal use
  stands or falls with entries a critic recoded. The lesson introduces two
  statistical tools, each only where the audit needs it: what a correlation and
  its square measure (and how one entry can move them), and what an instrument is.
- **Hands off:** The module's question stays open: no cross-country table settles
  why nations differ, and the genetic part of the hereditarian hypothesis is not
  tested by either table at all. The institutional account leaves a second
  question the empire module takes: if colonial institutions shaped today's gap,
  were they a wrong, a benefit, or both — `vitoria-right-to-the-americas` opens the
  West's judgment of its own conquests; `colonial-balance-sheet` returns to the
  institutional ledger (colonies compared with colonies, never with no
  colonialism); `auditing-the-case-for-colonialism` reuses this lesson's drill,
  correlation and instrument on three cited studies.
- **Spine step:** "Heredity as an explanation of the gaps between peoples, at full strength (The Bell Curve, national IQ, ancient DNA), met by its objections (Gould, Harden) and by the rival explanations from geography and institutions — and the key datasets audited"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):**
  - *Artefacts read.* (1) Lynn and Vanhanen, *IQ and the Wealth of Nations* (2002):
    Table 6.1 (81 measured national IQs), Table 6.5 and its rule (104 estimated
    ones), Appendix 1 ("The Calculation of National Intelligence Levels",
    pp. 197–225: the per-country derivations), and the ch. 7 correlations. (2)
    Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson, "The Colonial Origins of Comparative
    Development" (2001): the settler-mortality series and its data appendix, the
    causal chain and the instrumental-variable estimate. (3) The critics'
    re-examinations and the replies: on national IQ, the 2010 systematic review of
    African samples and the authors' reply; on settler mortality, the 2012 comment
    that recoded the series and the authors' reply in the same issue.
  - *Module results used.* `national-iq` and `hereditarian-hypothesis` (what the
    first table is for); `within-between-fallacy` and the Flynn effect (why its
    levels and its causal reading are contested); `inclusive-and-extractive-institutions`
    and `critical-juncture` (what the second table is for — colonisation as the
    juncture, settlement or extraction as the fork); Diamond's
    proximate/ultimate distinction (the geographic objection to the second
    table: settler mortality is itself a fact of geography).
  - *Departures from the idealisation the lesson must name.* The ideal dataset is a
    random sample of each nation (or each colony's settlers), measured once with
    one instrument at one date, with no missing entries, analysed by a method
    that can tell cause from association. The national-IQ table departs: samples of
    convenience (48 children; 22 skilled workers; immigrants tested abroad), a
    dozen tests over sixty years harmonised by an assumed rate of secular gain,
    a floor value for samples below the test's range, equal weights for unequal
    samples, discretionary corrections in both directions, and more than half the
    countries estimated from neighbours; the analysis is a correlation of country
    means with the direction of cause assumed. The settler-mortality table
    departs: deaths of soldiers, bishops and sailors rather than settlers, in
    barracks or on campaign, in different decades, with many colonies assigned
    another place's rate; its analysis is an instrument whose validity rests on an
    untestable assumption (that mortality then affects income now only through
    institutions). Both tables were made auditable by their authors, who published
    the derivations; both critiques used that publication; both drew replies.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "Both of the big explanations of why nations differ
rest on one table. …" Develop it in one paragraph before any answer: the reader has
met both accounts at full strength (`<Ref>` hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations;
`<Ref>` geography-or-institutions, one clause each — the 81 averages set against
GDP; the colonies where Europeans died and institutions that extracted). Each account
is an argument; each argument's evidence is a table; a table is a list of claims,
and each claim has a source that can be opened. The newcomer's two attempts both
fail: to trust the table that supports the account one already prefers, or to trust
both because both were published by academic presses and journals. The lesson's
question is narrower and answerable: what can each table carry?

1. **What does one ask of a table?** Question: the drill was built for a letter or a
   speech; what are its questions for a column of numbers? Obvious attempt: check
   the arithmetic. Fails: the arithmetic is usually right; what decides is what
   stands behind each cell. The answer, the drill for a dataset (figure 1): for
   every entry — who measured, whom, when, how many, and was it measured at all
   or filled in; for the table — what comparison it supports and what statistical
   step carries it to the conclusion. Metadata first, for both: a British
   psychologist and a Finnish political scientist, a monograph series on human
   evolution and intelligence (Praeger, 2002), one author's acknowledgment of
   support from a foundation that funds hereditarian research (state the fact as
   the book states it, l.466); three economists, the profession's leading journal,
   peer-reviewed (2001), building on Curtin's histories of soldiers' deaths. The
   prediction step as a Task: from this metadata alone, what would you expect each
   table to be good at and weak at? Because: provenance does not decide truth, but
   it tells the reader where to look first ("use, not bias").

2. **One national average, built in front of the reader.** Question: what is "Ghana:
   71"? The reader has met the method in outline (`<Ref>`
   hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations, one clause: every sample scored against the
   British norm, adjusted for the rise in scores over time). Build one entry as a
   `<FigureSequence>` (figure 2): two studies — 225 adults in Accra on one test
   (1981), 1,639 adolescents on another, described as representative (c. 1990) —
   each converted to the British scale (80; 62), averaged "giving equal weight to
   each study" → 71. Then the questions the entry raises, each answered from the
   appendix: the two numbers are eighteen points apart, and the larger, more
   representative sample counts no more than the smaller one; the result describes
   neither sample. Because: an average of two disagreeing estimates is a
   convention, not a measurement, and the table does not show which entries are
   built this way.

3. **Tracing entries: what the appendix records and omits.** Question: is Ghana
   typical? Trace four more entries (set pieces 1–4), each with records / omits:
   Equatorial Guinea (one study of 48 children, c. 1984 → 59, the lowest value in
   the table; the appendix omits where they were tested, how chosen, their
   schooling — and the critics' report about the sample, only if verified);
   Ethiopia (250 adolescents who had recently emigrated to Israel — omits time since
   arrival, schooling, familiarity with the test); Sierra Leone (22 skilled workers
   and 60 adults in the 1960s, both below the test's range, both "assigned" 64 —
   and, unlike the Congo and Guinea floor samples, given no adjustment for the
   decades between test and norms; the book's own rule applied consistently would
   raise the figure — Exercise 3 computes by how much); Japan (ten separate
   estimates from 99 to 110, one raised by 5 because its American comparison group
   came from Minneapolis — adjustments run upward as well as downward). Then the
   104 countries with no measurement at all: the neighbour rule (Afghanistan = the
   mean of India and Iran) and "rounded toward 100". Figure 3: 185 dots, measured
   against estimated, by region — the reader sees where the estimates
   concentrate. Because: the table's precision is uneven, and its weakest entries
   are concentrated in the region where the hereditarian claim is strongest.

4. **The critics' re-examination and the authors' reply — at full strength, both.**
   The OBJECTION enters here, stated as the course's own reconstruction (names in
   References): a systematic search of the published literature for African
   samples, with inclusion rules fixed in advance, found many more samples than
   the compilation used and an average about ten points higher (≈80 against ≈70 on
   the British scale — verify); the floor value, the uniform Flynn adjustment
   (gains in rural Kenya were much faster than in Britain — verify), and
   unsystematic selection all push estimates down; so the levels of the
   sub-Saharan entries are not established. The REPLY, at full strength: the
   higher averages come from selected samples (urban, schooled, university
   students), which overstate the population; the authors rate sample quality and
   keep their estimate near 70; and independent of any African sample, the
   ordering is corroborated: the two-measure reliability of .939 across 45
   countries, national IQ against TIMSS mathematics r = .88 (N = 38), and later
   large international student assessments (one clause, References) that
   show the same ordering and the same association with income. Where the lesson
   concedes, to each side: the critics have shown that the low African levels are
   uncertain by about ten points and rest on choices; the defenders have shown
   that the cross-national association between measured skills and income does not
   depend on those entries. Because: an audit of entries attacks levels; an
   association can survive the loss of its worst entries.

5. **From the table to the claim: what a correlation carries.** Question: the
   table's purpose is a correlation with income; what does a correlation of .7
   say? (If `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations` already set up r — check its
   ledger `research/taught/hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations.md` — Ref it and add only
   what the audit needs.) The correlation coefficient: how closely country points
   cluster round a straight line, from 0 (no line) to 1 (all on it); its square,
   the share of the spread in incomes that the line accounts for (the book's own
   "29 percent … 52 percent"). Then the audit's two tools on the book's own
   numbers (figures 4, 5): leverage — in 1950 one oil state's income drags the
   correlation from .57 to .26, which is why the authors also report a rank
   correlation that one extreme point cannot move; range restriction — among
   countries at 97 and above the correlation is −.02, read by a critic as "no
   relation where the data are good" and by the authors as a threshold near 90 plus
   socialism. And the step no correlation makes: the direction of cause. The book
   states it: "national IQ is used as the independent variable" — assumed, while
   the authors themselves describe income raising IQ through nutrition, health and
   schooling. Because: the same r is produced by IQ → income, income → IQ, or a
   third cause of both; a table of country means cannot tell them apart.

6. **The institutional table: why it needed an instrument.** Seam (L10b): from the
   national-IQ table one expects the institutional table to be a second
   correlation, open to the same objection; it was built to escape exactly that
   objection. Question: institutions and income are correlated; rich countries can
   also afford good institutions; how could anyone show which way the arrow runs?
   Obvious attempt: control for other variables. Fails: nothing controls for the
   reverse arrow. The answer — an instrument, defined for the newcomer in two
   sentences: a fact from the past that moved institutions and can reach today's
   income by no road except through them; then compare incomes across places the
   instrument sorted. The chain (figure 6, `<FigureSequence>`): where European
   soldiers and bishops died at high rates, Europeans did not settle → they set up
   institutions to extract rather than to live under (`<Ref>`
   geography-or-institutions, one clause: extractive and inclusive) → early
   institutions persisted → today's protection against expropriation → income in
   1995. Keep the one keeper magnitude, if verified: the estimate attributes most
   of the difference between, say, Nigeria and Chile to institutions. Because: an
   instrument turns a correlation into a causal estimate — if, and only if, its
   one assumption (no other road) holds and its numbers are sound.

7. **Tracing the mortality entries, and the critic's recoding.** Question: what is
   "Sierra Leone: so many deaths per thousand"? Trace three kinds of entry (set
   pieces 5–6): an own-territory garrison series (British troops at Freetown in the
   early nineteenth century, from the War Office returns behind Curtin's tables —
   the sourced figure); a value from soldiers on campaign (an expedition dies at
   rates a garrison does not; verify which entries); a colony with no figure of its
   own, assigned a neighbour's or a comparable place's rate; and a Latin American
   value from bishops (older, better-housed men than soldiers). The OBJECTION,
   from the 2012 comment (names in References): more than half the 64 colonies
   carry a rate measured elsewhere; campaign and garrison rates are mixed; keep
   only own-territory rates and allow for campaign sources, and the instrument's
   link to institutions weakens until the estimate's confidence interval has no
   upper bound — the data no longer identify the effect. The REPLY, at full
   strength, from the same issue: the assignments follow disease environments that
   do not stop at borders; discarding them throws away information; capping the
   extreme values or using alternative mortality data leaves the result standing.
   And the second line of objection, to the "no other road" assumption: the
   disease environment may lower income directly; settlers brought skills and
   schooling as well as institutions; settler mortality is a fact of geography,
   which is the rival account's ultimate cause (`<Ref>` geography-or-institutions,
   proximate and ultimate — one clause). Because: an instrument is only as good as
   its first link and its exclusion; the audit tests the first, argument tests the
   second.

8. **Symmetry, and what survives.** The same five questions, set side by side
   (figure 1 completed, a table-figure): both tables rest on old, small,
   unrepresentative records; both fill gaps from neighbours; both published every
   derivation, which is what made the audits possible; both drew a re-examination
   that changed the entries and a reply that defended the conclusion. The
   difference in what is at stake: the national-IQ correlation survives the loss
   of its weakest entries because better data show the same association, but it
   never claimed to identify a cause; the settler-mortality table was the causal
   identification, so a successful recoding removes the causal estimate while
   leaving the correlation, and leaves the institutional claim to rest on its other
   evidence (`<Ref>` geography-or-institutions: Nogales, the Koreas). What no
   cross-country table can show: cause from country means alone (ecological
   comparison, reverse arrows, confounders that all move together — health,
   schooling, institutions, geography, culture — and fewer than two hundred
   observations); and nothing in the national-IQ table bears on whether the
   differences it lists are genetic (`<Ref>` gould-and-harden-on-heredity: the
   within/between gap is untouched). The lesson's verdict is about the tables, not
   the accounts. Because: the drill decides what a dataset can carry; it does not
   decide between explanations.

Headings fall out as: "A table is a list of claims"; "How one national average is
built"; "Forty-eight children and twenty-two workers"; "The re-examination and the
reply"; "What a correlation of .7 carries"; "Why the institutional table needed an
instrument"; "Garrisons, campaigns and borrowed rates"; "The same questions to both

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **The register rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Symmetry is the lesson's test.** Same questions, same depth, same order of
  presentation (entries → critique → reply → what survives) for both tables. Do not
  give one table three traced entries and the other one. Do not present the
  institutional table as the "scientific" one and the IQ table as the "biased"
  one, or the reverse; the lesson's finding is that each is strong and weak in
  different places.
- **Use, not bias, on provenance.** The Pioneer Fund acknowledgment and the
  monograph series are metadata (who paid, who published); the AER and its
  referees are metadata too. Neither is evidence about any entry. The closing
  question for every source is what its provenance changes about what it can be
  USED to establish (playbook-history drill step 4) — never "is it biased?".
- **Terms to anchor (L11), three per section at most:** correlation coefficient
  (r) and its square; outlier / leverage; rank correlation (one clause: correlates
  orders, not values, so one extreme point cannot dominate); range restriction;
  imputation (a value estimated rather than measured); floor (a score below the
  lowest point a test's norms can express); the Flynn effect (`<Ref>` — never
  re-taught); Raven's Progressive Matrices (a non-verbal reasoning test of pattern
  completion — define once; the figure is our own schematic, never a real item:
  the test is copyrighted); WISC-R (a Wechsler intelligence scale for children);
  instrument / instrumental variable and the exclusion restriction ("no other
  road"); first stage (how strongly the instrument moves the cause); mortality "per
  1,000 mean strength" (deaths per year per thousand men on the rolls);
  expropriation risk (an index of how secure property is from seizure by the
  state, 1985–95); garrison vs campaign; Curtin (References only). Places: Freetown
  (capital of Sierra Leone, West Africa — the British base from 1808); Equatorial
  Guinea (a small Spanish colony on the Gulf of Guinea, independent 1968).
- **Real misreadings (L13), one slot at most:** "a published dataset is a
  measurement" — refute with Ghana and the 104 estimates; or "the critique showed
  institutions do not matter / IQ does not matter" — the recoding removes an
  identification, it does not prove a zero; the review lowers confidence in
  levels, it does not erase the association. Choose one for the slot; the other
  is Exercise 4.
- **Do not re-teach:** the hereditarian argument, the Flynn effect, the
  within/between fallacy, inclusive/extractive institutions, the reversal of
  fortune, Diamond's ultimate causes — a `<Ref>` clause each. This lesson's new
  matter is the audit and the two statistical tools.
- **Verify before stating.** Every number from the four external papers (28/36 of
  64; ≈82/≈80 vs ≈68–71; the Nigeria–Chile magnitude; specific mortality values;
  Kenya's Flynn gain; the Equatorial Guinea sample's origin) is given here as
  commonly reported and must be checked in the paper. Where the writer cannot
  obtain a paper, the lesson says so in References and states only what the
  dossier supports — an audit that cites unread papers fails its own test.
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — 81 measured / 104 estimated (of 185); about four
  in ten colonies with their own mortality figure; the correlation of about .7
  and what its square means. Everything else is a traced entry inside its source
  inquiry, bound in its sentence, or lives in a figure.
- **No re-run of regressions.** The lesson does not recompute anyone's estimates
  (no data in the dossier, and the point is provenance); the one computation is
  Exercise 3's consistency check, inside the source inquiry.
- **Thin dossier, by design:** the settler-mortality paper, the comment, both
  replies and both African-sample papers are outside the dossier; the brief gives
  full citations. The lesson cannot be written from the dossier alone (the
  course.yaml note says so). The book's last ~5% (bibliography tail) was not read.
- **Copyright:** the book and all papers are in copyright — a sentence each at
  most; entries and values are facts and may be tabulated. Redraw any scatter from
  published values in house style, never trace the book's or the paper's figures.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (E3; the one warm-up).** State the four links of the
   settler-mortality argument, and name for each the concept of an earlier lesson
   it uses (`critical-juncture`; `inclusive-and-extractive-institutions`; the
   reversal of fortune; Diamond's proximate and ultimate causes for the objection
   to the last link).
2. **Document reasoning — the sourcing drill on one appendix entry.** The Ethiopia
   entry (250 fifteen-year-old Ethiopian immigrants to Israel, tested c. 1989, 65 →
   63). Metadata first; predict what such a sample can and cannot represent;
   records / argues / omits (time since arrival, schooling, language of testing,
   how chosen); use: what the entry can establish (the scores of that group at that
   time) and what it cannot (Ethiopia's population average).
3. **Derivation inside the source inquiry (computation rule 4).** Apply the book's
   own time adjustment, as used for Congo (Brazzaville) and Guinea, to the two
   Sierra Leone floor samples (c. 1964–65 against the 1979 British or 1993 American
   norms, at the book's 2 points per decade). State the figure the rule gives, then
   the interpretation (a few points, one direction), the uncertainty (the floor
   hides the sample's true position; 82 people in all), and what corroborating
   source would settle it.
4. **Claim to refute (E6b).** "The 2012 recoding showed that institutions do not
   explain income." Name the step that fails (a weakened instrument leaves the
   effect unidentified by this route; it does not estimate a zero), state what the
   recoding does show, and name the evidence for institutions that it leaves
   untouched (`<Ref>` geography-or-institutions).
5. **Boundary probe (E6a).** Suppose every colony without its own mortality figure
   had been assigned the same single value. What happens to the instrument's
   ability to sort those colonies, and to the estimate? Then the realistic case:
   values assigned from neighbours with similar disease environments. Solution:
   with no variation an instrument sorts nothing; assigned values add error that
   may correlate with geography itself — which is why the assignment rule matters
   and why the exclusion objection and the data objection meet at this point.
6. **Research-shaped task (E6d).** Sierra Leone appears in both tables. For each
   entry, list what you would need to open (the 1960s test reports; the War Office
   garrison returns), the five drill answers each would give, and one test that
   would show whether the entry is representative of what the table uses it for
   (a later, nationally representative sample; a civilian rather than military
   death series). State which entry's weakness matters more to its table's
   conclusion, and why (the IQ table's association does not hang on it; the
   mortality table's identification hangs on entries like it).
7. **Synthesis — claim–evidence–reasoning, both ways.** "The settler-mortality table
   is more trustworthy than the national-IQ table." Argue for it (a causal design;
   peer review; historical records rather than convenience samples) and against it
   (the design concentrates all its weight on the most-recoded entries; the IQ
   association is corroborated by independent large samples), then judge which
   claim each table can carry — not which table is "better".
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `colonial-balance-sheet`.** The settler-mortality table compares
   colonies with colonies. Can it tell you whether colonial rule as such made the
   colonised richer or poorer than they would otherwise have been? Say what
   comparison would be needed and why the table cannot supply it. The solution
   says that the next module's balance-sheet lesson turns exactly on this question
   — "compared to what?" — and that the article it reads makes this point about
   this very paper, with a `<Ref>`.

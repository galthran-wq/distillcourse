# Research brief: nietzsche-on-the-right (Nietzsche's inheritance on the right)

status: planned
module: democracy-and-its-critics
type: extra
goal: Describe what the twentieth-century conservative tradition took from Nietzsche's critique of equality and where it parted from him, with pointers to the Nietzsche course

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From the module so far: Tocqueville's levelled world ("nothing rises and nothing falls", `equality-of-conditions`, `democratic-man`), Carlyle's demand for heroes (`hero-worship`) and Mill's fear of "collective mediocrity" — three critics of equality who kept, respectively, Christianity, the Bible and liberty. From `carlyle-heroes`, the one Nietzsche link already made: his contempt for Carlyle. From the Nietzsche course (an optional prerequisite of this course): everything about Nietzsche's own position, by `<Ref>` only.
- **Contributes:** A reception history, not a doctrine. The lesson introduces one concept, `aristocratic-radicalism` — the label under which the right received Nietzsche — and follows five readers (Mann, Spengler, Ortega, Strauss, Scruton), asking of each the same two questions: what did he take from the critique of equality, and what did he refuse. The finding the lesson earns: each took the diagnosis (herd, levelling, nihilism) and refused the cure (new values beyond good and evil), substituting his own — Kultur, Caesarism, the select minority, classical natural right, Anglican piety.
- **Hands off:** This is a starred chapter; the core path survives without it. It seeds, without being required by, `mann-reflections` (the Dreigestirn), `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses` (noblesse oblige), `spengler-decline-of-the-west` (Caesarism), `strauss-natural-right-and-history` (the second crisis), `scruton-meaning-of-conservatism` and `what-conservatism-is` (conservative vs radical conservative vs reactionary — the Introduction to the Muller anthology's line that radical conservatives "reject historical utilitarianism and humility and embrace will"). The limit case, the Nazi appropriation, is a `<Ref>` into the Nietzsche course, not a re-telling.
- **Spine step:** "Democracy arrives as a fact and is judged: Tocqueville foresees soft despotism, Carlyle demands heroes, Mill defends liberty, Marx predicts class war — and the British franchise tests who was right"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: a philosopher who despised nationalists, antisemites and the German state, who within thirty years of his death was quoted by every anti-liberal in Europe; the lesson does not re-teach his argument but follows what five readers took and refused.

1. **How does an enemy of the nation and the state become the right's house author?** The obvious answer — he was misread — is too easy, because the readers in this lesson read him well. The puzzle needs the three claims they all found in him, stated as pointers: that the morality of equality is the morality of the herd, dressed in modern clothes (Ref `nietzsche/herd-morality-in-modern-dress`); that there is an order of rank among human types and a culture is measured by its highest, not its average (Ref `nietzsche/order-of-rank`); and that none of this is conservative — he despised the state, the nation, the party and the Christianity the right wished to conserve (Ref `nietzsche/nietzsche-and-politics`). The label under which the right received this package is *aristocratic radicalism* — a Danish critic's phrase of 1889, which Nietzsche accepted. Define the concept: a critique of equality, democracy and pity from the standpoint of rank, carried out *against* the existing aristocracies and churches as much as against the levellers. The "because" the lesson will test: a critique that attacks both sides can be borrowed by either, piece by piece.
2. **Mann (1918): the critic of Germandom as a German weapon.** What he took: the *Dreigestirn* — Schopenhauer, Nietzsche, Wagner as the three stars under which a German of his generation grew up; Nietzsche's critique of morality read as the twin of Bismarck's fight against ideologues ("one spirit"); and above all the equation Mann builds on Nietzsche's contempt for the French as the "apes" of English modern ideas — democracy is *Politisierung*, the dragging of inward life into the political, and Nietzsche's hatred of Bismarck's Reich is read as a protest against that politicisation, not against Germany. "Demokratie und Nationalismus, das ist eines Ursprungs" (democracy and nationalism have one origin) — so the German *Bürger* is anti-political, and that is his depth. What he refused: Renaissance-Nietzscheanism, the aestheticist politics of the superman, which he calls "impotence to life"; and the politicisation of Nietzsche himself — *Verhunzung*. Mann's own word for what remains is *irony* (one of Nietzsche's "two outcomes"). One clause: in 1922 Mann recanted the whole book and took the republic's side — `mann-reflections` reads both.
3. **Spengler (1918–22): the untimely man made timely.** What he took: will to power as the one theme of nineteenth-century philosophy; rank; and the coming of Caesarism (strong men over a formless mass — defined in a clause) as the destiny of a late civilisation. What he refused — and this is the subtle point: Nietzsche's untimeliness. For Spengler, "transvaluation of all values" is not a philosopher's act but the *mark of a Civilization*, the late, hardened phase every Culture reaches when its morale is no longer "had" but "sought"; Buddhism, Stoicism and Socialism are the same end-phenomenon in three Cultures, and Nietzsche belongs to the chain Schopenhauer–Darwin–Nietzsche–Shaw ("Shaw completing Nietzsche" in the millionaire Undershaft). So the critic of his age becomes its symptom; "Culture the body, Civilization its mummy". Spengler keeps the diagnosis of decline and turns Nietzsche into evidence for it.
4. **Ortega (1930): rank without contempt for the many.** What he took: the mass-man is Nietzsche's herd by another name — the man who feels himself "just like everyone else" and is content; "noble life" is life as discipline, "defined by obligations not rights" — *noblesse oblige* rebuilt on effort, not birth ("to live as one likes is plebeian", Goethe's line); and the "select minority" as what a society lives by. What he refused: the equation of rank with class — the division "is not between upper and lower classes" — and the contempt for liberal democracy: "liberalism is the supreme form of generosity", and Europe "needs to preserve its essential liberalism. This is the condition for superseding it". Ortega names Nietzsche among the three prophets of the mass age (with Hegel and Comte — "view history from far enough not to see Cleopatra's nose") and keeps the prophecy while rejecting the aristocratic politics it seemed to license. `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses` develops.
5. **Strauss (1953): the second crisis.** What he took: the diagnosis that the modern project ends in nihilism — "the contemporary rejection of natural right leads to nihilism, nay, it is identical with nihilism" — and the honesty of Nietzsche as the thinker who saw it (Rousseau the first crisis of modernity, Nietzsche the second). What he refused: the premise. Nietzsche is the source of *radical historicism* — the view that all understanding presupposes a horizon no reason can validate, imposed by fate, so that philosophy in the old sense (knowledge of the whole) is impossible; and Strauss's charge is that this deepens the disease it diagnoses: "Historicism culminated in nihilism. The attempt to make man absolutely at home in this world ended in man's becoming absolutely homeless." The cure is not new values but the return to classical natural right and Socratic "knowledge that one does not know". Strauss also supplies the lesson's rule for the limit case: the *reductio ad Hitlerum* — "a view is not refuted by the fact that it happens to have been shared by Hitler" (ch. II pp. 42–43). `strauss-natural-right-and-history` develops.
6. **Scruton (2002–2014): Arnold against Nietzsche.** What he took: *ressentiment* — the resentment of the weak dressed as morality — as the psychology of egalitarian demands (ch. 4 of How to Be a Conservative, with Scheler and Schoeck); and the diagnosis that a culture which repudiates its past ends in nihilism (ch. 7). What he refused, and chose instead: there are two kinds of loss of faith — Arnold's, on Dover Beach, a loss suffered inside a world still made by faith and mourned there, and Nietzsche's, absolute, celebrated; Scruton takes Arnold's. Religion is membership before it is doctrine, and the loss of it is exile from the community that formed you — "the loss of religion is the loss of loss". So the conservative keeps the critique of levelling and refuses the death of God as liberation; and he answers the perspectivism — "no truths, only interpretations" refutes itself (one clause; Ref `nietzsche/perspectivism` for what Nietzsche actually held, so the lesson does not misstate it). `scruton-meaning-of-conservatism` develops.
7. **The limit case, and the pattern.** The Nazi appropriation (Ref `nietzsche/the-nazi-appropriation`: the sister's archive, the selective quotation, the 1934 visit) is the case where "took" became "took over", and the course's rule for it is Strauss's. The pattern across the five: each kept the diagnosis — herd, levelling, the nihilism at the end of the modern road — and refused the cure, new values beyond good and evil, putting his own in its place: Mann's Kultur and irony, Spengler's Caesarism and second religiousness, Ortega's select minority inside liberalism, Strauss's classical natural right, Scruton's Anglican piety. Say what this makes of the label: aristocratic radicalism was received as aristocratic *conservatism* by men who wanted the rank and not the radicalism. The frame (as the course's own, Lilla in References): the reactionary's story — a happy order, alien ideas, an elite's betrayal, a remnant who remember — fits Spengler and does not fit Ortega or Strauss, and Nietzsche himself told no such story. The question handed on: can one take the critique of equality without taking the rejection of Christianity and the nation? Each of the five answers it differently, and their lessons follow.

Headings as claims (short lesson, 4–5 sections): "A critique that attacked both sides"; "Mann: the anti-political German"; "Spengler and Ortega: the prophet as symptom, the noble as effort"; "Strauss: the second crisis"; "Scruton: Arnold's loss, not Nietzsche's"; "What each took and what each refused".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **"There it is, the origin of this war"** (Mann, Einkehr 46–47): Nietzsche's line on the French as the "apes, actors and soldiers" of English ideas, and Mann's gloss — the Western ideas of 1789 against German inwardness — as the clearest case of a borrowing that reverses the lender's intent (Nietzsche despised the Reich that fought the war). Translate the phrase yourself; two clauses.
2. **The Dreigestirn** (Einkehr 34–45): the three masters and what each supplied — Schopenhauer the pessimism, Wagner the art, Nietzsche the psychology — as a small table.
3. **"Shaw completing Nietzsche"** (Spengler ch. X): the chain Schopenhauer–Darwin–Nietzsche–Shaw and the millionaire Undershaft of Major Barbara (1905) as the will to power's bourgeois form; Spengler's 1819–1905 chronology of the century's one theme. The set piece shows how a reader can honour a thinker by demoting him to evidence.
4. **Cleopatra's nose** (Ortega ch. VI): the three prophets and the distance from which history shows its shape; then **"to live as one likes is plebeian"** (ch. VII) beside Mill's "doing as one likes" and Arnold's phrase (Ref `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`) — three thinkers, one phrase, three valuations.
5. **"Absolutely homeless"** (Strauss ch. I p. 18) and **the absolute moment** (pp. 26–29): the sentence and the argument it compresses (historicism exempts itself from its own verdict; radical historicism charges the exemption "to reality"). Two paragraphs; `strauss-natural-right-and-history` does the rest.
6. **Dover Beach against the madman** (Scruton ch. 13): Arnold's "Sea of Faith… retreating" (1867, public domain — quote four lines) against the death of God as announced in Nietzsche (Ref `nietzsche/death-of-god` — do not quote the madman passage here; the Ref carries it). The two losses as a table: where it happens (inside faith / after it), what is felt (grief / exhilaration), what follows (conservation / transvaluation).
7. **The took/refused ledger**: the lesson's one `<Remark>` — five rows, two columns — stated after the five readings, not before.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up).** For each of the five readers, name in one phrase what he took and what he refused (the ledger), closed book; then say which two kept liberal democracy (Ortega; Mann after 1922) and which kept Christianity (Scruton; Strauss as the "Jerusalem" he would not dissolve).
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill. FORWARD (E7).** Mann's sentence "Demokratie und Nationalismus, das ist eines Ursprungs" (1918): author, date (written 1914–18, in a war Germany was losing), audience, purpose; what it records (a German novelist's self-explanation), what it argues (that the democratic West is the nationalist party), what it omits (that the author would reverse himself in 1922); what it can be used to establish (how the Nietzschean critique of "modern ideas" was turned to a national cause) and what it cannot (Nietzsche's view of the war he did not live to see). The solution says in one sentence, with a `<Ref>`, that `mann-reflections` reads the whole book and the reversal.
3. **Claim to refute.** "Strauss is a Nietzschean of the right." Name the failing step: Strauss takes the diagnosis (modern natural right ends in nihilism) and refuses the premise (radical historicism) and the cure (new values); his answer is classical natural right — a return Nietzsche would have called a herd morality in Greek dress. Use the "absolutely homeless" sentence to locate the disagreement.
4. **Boundary probe.** Keep the critique of equality and set Christianity to one (retained): what must give — the death of God, the transvaluation, the contempt for pity — and whose position results (Scruton's, and Tocqueville's "liberty cannot" govern without faith, Ref). Now set Christianity to zero and keep rank: whose position results (Spengler's Caesarism with its "second religiousness" at the end) and what, by Spengler's own account, returns anyway. The exercise shows that the five readings are not five tastes but points on one axis.
5. **Synthesis — claim–evidence–reasoning.** "Aristocratic radicalism was received as aristocratic conservatism." Argue for or against with three pieces of evidence from the lesson (Mann's anti-politics; Ortega's liberalism; Scruton's piety), and state the warrant that connects them (each kept rank and dropped radicalism against the existing order).
6. **E3 — earlier lessons.** Carlyle's hero (Ref `carlyle-heroes`) and Nietzsche's higher type (Ref `nietzsche/higher-type`) both answer a levelled age with a rank of persons. State why Nietzsche despised Carlyle (Ref `nietzsche/skirmishes-of-an-untimely-man`) and which of the five readers in this lesson stands closest to Carlyle rather than to Nietzsche (Spengler's Caesar; or Mann's Bismarck — defend either).

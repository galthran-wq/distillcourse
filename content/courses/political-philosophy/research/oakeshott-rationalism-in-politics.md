# Research brief: oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics (Oakeshott: rationalism in politics and the conservative disposition)

status: planned
module: postwar-conservatism
type: core
goal: Analyze Oakeshott's distinction between technical and practical knowledge and his account of politics as the pursuit of intimations, and evaluate the conservative disposition it supports

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The four candidate definitions and the one left untried: that conservatism is a disposition, not a doctrine (`what-conservatism-is`, concept `conservatism-as-disposition` introduced there as a label, filled in here). The epistemic argument and its self-application problem — "there are no general prescriptions" is one; "a plan to resist all planning" belongs to the planners' style — which Exercise 8 of the previous lesson left the reader unable to escape. Burke's prejudice as collected reason (`burke-reflections`); Hegel's rational state (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`); Hobbes's sovereign as the keeper of rules, flagged forward from `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty` as the place where Oakeshott's reading would return; Machiavelli's Prince as a handbook for a new ruler (`machiavelli-effectual-truth`); Locke's Second Treatise and the Rights of Man read in France and America as principles to be put into practice (`locke-property-and-consent`, `burke-reflections`); the English Revolution's documents (`english-revolution-as-test`).
- **Contributes:** One move: the distinction between knowledge that can be written down and knowledge that exists only in use, which turns every ideology — the Rights of Man, Locke's treatise, the Communist Manifesto — from the parent of a politics into its abridgment, and so dissolves the self-application problem by denying that conservatism is a doctrine at all. From it follow politics as the pursuit of intimations and government as umpire.
- **Hands off:** Two problems. (1) If a tradition is the only standard, how does it criticise itself, and what does a government that "commands for" no truth say to a society whose tradition intimates cannibalism? That is Strauss's opening (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`). (2) Whether an umpire state with no shared purpose is already the liberal state — the charge Deneen will make (`deneen-post-liberalism`) and Scruton half-concedes when he asks for authority and allegiance rather than rules (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`). The Rights of Man as abridgment returns as one of five critiques in `udhr-read-against-the-right`.
- **Spine step:** "After 1945 liberalism is the orthodoxy, and the right answers it in four registers: Oakeshott's disposition, Strauss's natural right, Voegelin's diagnosis of Gnosticism, Scruton's authority before freedom"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: the cookery book cannot teach anyone to cook; the same is said of ideologies and of the politician who thinks a felt need plus a plan is enough.

1. **Who is the Rationalist, and why is he hard to object to?** State the character before the doctrine, since the newcomer will recognise himself: he stands for independence of mind on all occasions; he is sceptical of every authority except "reason" and optimistic that reason will suffice; he is individualist and egalitarian — any mind trained in the method is as good as any other; he treats every situation as a problem with a solution and every custom as an obstacle; he prefers the perfect to the best-in-the-circumstances and the uniform to the local. Politics is engineering: identify a felt need, design the remedy, apply it. The catalogue of his projects is the obvious attempt's self-portrait — a world convention, the Rights of Man, open diplomacy, the single tax, the Beveridge Report, the Education Act of 1944 (anchor both: Britain's wartime blueprints for a welfare state and universal secondary schooling), a World State, the Gaelic revival. Why this is a problem rather than good sense: the list contains causes the reader will like and causes he will not, left and right together, and the one thing they share is a method. The lesson's question becomes: what is wrong with the method?
2. **Two kinds of knowledge, and the illusion that one of them is the whole.** Attempt: knowledge is what can be written down — rules, principles, directions; whoever has the book has the knowledge. Test it on cooking (the recipe), on wheelwrighting (Duke Huan's wheelwright: the right pace of the stroke "cannot get into the hand unless it comes from the heart", and books are "the lees and scum of bygone men"), on medicine, on chess (knowing the rules vs knowing how to play), on science (a hypothesis is formed only by someone who is already a scientist). In every activity two kinds of knowledge are inseparable: technical knowledge, formulable in rules, learnable from a book, and which therefore looks certain; and practical knowledge, which exists only in use, cannot be written, and is imparted by apprenticeship and acquired by practice. Rationalism is the assertion that there is no knowledge but technical knowledge — the "sovereignty of technique" — and the "because" is a craving for certainty: only technical knowledge looks certain, so only it counts. The illusion's root: technique is never learned in an empty mind; it reorganises knowledge already there; the self-made man was never literally self-made. Anchor the history in one paragraph: Bacon's method of the 1620s (rules so mechanical they "place all wits nearly on a level"), Descartes's rules for the direction of the mind, and Pascal's objection within the same century that the mind follows its technique "tacitly, naturally and without art". Vulgarised, the dream becomes "Your mind and how to use it" — a 1920s correspondence course.
3. **An ideology is the stepchild of a politics, not its parent.** Attempt: an ideology — a system of ends and principles premeditated independently of any politics — guides political activity; the Rights of Man of 4 August 1789 (anchor: the National Assembly's declaration in the first summer of the Revolution, `burke-reflections`) looks exactly like that: "a system of rights and duties, a scheme of ends — justice, freedom, equality, security, property, and the rest — ready and waiting to be put into practice for the first time". Why it fails: "For the first time? Not a bit of it." Here, "abstracted and abridged", are the common-law rights of Englishmen, the gift "of centuries of the day-to-day attending to the arrangements of an historic society"; Locke's Second Treatise, read in America and France as a preface to political activity, "has all the marks of a postscript". Because: a cookery book is "an abstract of somebody's knowledge of how to cook"; the book speaks only to those who already know what kind of thing to expect from it; so "political activity comes first and a political ideology follows after". The decisive instance is freedom itself: the freedom of an Englishman is not an idea exemplified by habeas corpus, "it is, at that point, the availability of that procedure" (anchor habeas corpus: the writ that forces a jailer to produce the prisoner before a judge, English since the 1600s). Consequences: the politics of the book is the politics of the politically inexperienced — a new ruler (Machiavelli wrote a crib for him, `machiavelli-effectual-truth`, and his followers forgot the limits), a new class (Locke for the Whigs, Bentham and Godwin for the middle class, Marx and Engels for a proletariat "with nothing but its technique to lose", `locke-property-and-consent`, `marx-manifesto`), a new society (the United States, whose founders were Rationalists by circumstance: no inherited arrangements to attend to, so a Declaration). And the export of democracy: abridge the English manner of politics into "democracy" and ship it, when the only method that works is the one "by which the workmen travel with the tools" — a sentence the UDHR lesson will reuse. Why the error is self-sealing: the Rationalist cannot be corrected by more rationalism; a plan to resist all planning (the 1944 Road to Serfdom, used by Churchill in the 1945 election he lost) belongs to the same style; "first he turns out the light and then complains that he cannot see". This is where the previous lesson's self-application problem dissolves rather than being answered: the epistemic argument fails as a doctrine because it was never a doctrine; stated as one it becomes a crib.
4. **If not ideology, what guides politics? The pursuit of intimations.** The reader will object that something must guide it. Attempt one: pure empiricism — do what the moment demands, no premeditation ("conservons le chaos"). Why it fails: there is no such thing as activity without an idea of what to do; it is "merely impossible". Attempt two, already refuted: empiricism guided by an ideology. The answer: politics is the amendment of existing arrangements by exploring and pursuing what is intimated in them — a tradition of behaviour is not a fixed code but a web of arrangements some of which have gone further than others, and political argument is "the convincing exposure of a sympathy, present but not yet followed up". Worked case, the lesson's `<Example>`: the enfranchisement of women. Not natural right, not justice as an abstract principle, not utility — "the only cogent reason" was that in most other respects women had already been enfranchised (property, education, the professions), so the vote completed an arrangement the tradition had already intimated. (Tie back: the 1918 Act the previous lesson's reply used; `british-franchise`.) Hence politics is "a conversation, not an argument"; an ideology may be used as a scientist uses a hypothesis, a distorting mirror that reveals hidden passages, but not as a criterion; a crisis is met by the tradition's unimpaired resources (an unexpected visitor is dealt with by knowing how to cook, not by a tin-opener); and the image: a boundless and bottomless sea, "neither harbour for shelter nor floor for anchorage, neither starting-place nor appointed destination", where the enterprise is to keep afloat on an even keel. A tradition's two properties: "Everything is temporary, but nothing is arbitrary"; and it is learned like a native language, by use, not grammar. Then the lesson's strongest objection, from the critics Oakeshott answered in his own terminal note and from the SEP: a tradition cannot criticise itself. How do you tell an intimation from a hunch? When two traditions run together in one society (the two Englands of 1840), which one's intimations count? If the only standard is the whole situation, a reformer and a reactionary can each claim to be following it. Reply, at full strength: standards do exist in the whole situation — a barrister in the Court of Appeal argues from the whole body of case law and his argument is good or bad; the cricket "throw" debate of the 1950s was settled by appeal to what the game had become, and settled correctly; principles are abridgments of such manners, useful but secondary. Concede plainly what the reply does not do: it says where standards live, not how a dispute between two readings of them is decided, and it offers no court above the tradition.
5. **The conservative disposition: what it is and where it is appropriate.** Now the previous lesson's fifth candidate, in Oakeshott's own words. To be conservative is to use and enjoy what is available, valuing the present for its familiarity — to prefer the familiar to the unknown, the tried to the untried, the actual to the possible, the limited to the unbounded, the near to the distant, the sufficient to the superabundant. It is not a creed about human nature or God or history. Two distinctions do the work. Change is suffered, innovation designed; the disposition is not against change (identity is "an unbroken rehearsal of contingencies" — the Masai moved to a new reserve named its hills and rivers with the old names) but asks of every innovation five things: the onus of proof is on the innovator; prefer change that resembles growth; prefer a remedy for a specific defect to a vision of perfection; go slowly; choose the occasion — because "there is no such thing as an unqualified improvement". Then: where is the disposition appropriate? Not everywhere — our character is the opposite, lovers of novelty, and the disposition is irrelevant to projects we judge by their results. It is the condition of activities enjoyed for their own sake (friendship: "all the difference in the world between the death of a friend and the retirement of one's tailor"; fishing; patriotism) and appropriate to tools and rules wherever stability outranks improvement — a rule in operation is not to be innovated while in use; "players may do anything except invent new rules"; "the more eager each side is to win, the more valuable is an inflexible set of rules". Here "agreed error is superior to controversial truth" is stated and the socialist rejoinder put at full strength: say "enjoy what is available" to a Manchester factory worker of the 1840s (`carlyle-heroes`). The reply is the next link: the disposition is about government, not about life.
6. **Government as umpire: the derivation and its cost.** The question: why should the disposition fit government in particular? Attempt (the rival): government is the imposition of a dream — "an encounter of dreams", each party with a vision of the perfect society (Apollo and Daphne: "what if it were properly arranged"). Oakeshott's derivation starts not from metaphysics, religion or human nature but from what governing is in a society of people who each pursue their own ends and collide: the provision and custody of general rules of conduct, which concern activities only in their propensity to collide. The ruler is umpire or chairman, not leader, tutor or manager; the rules are tools, not plans; and the "because" of the ban on dreams is loyalty — a government that "commands for truth" cannot hold the loyalty of those who think its truth error, and "the conjunction of dreaming and ruling generates tyranny". Government is a moderator — a "governor" in the engineer's sense, injecting restraint, irony and scepticism; "we keep a government to do our scepticism for us". What legal innovation is legitimate: modification should reflect, never impose, change in activities and beliefs; do not legislate for hypothetical situations; distrust extraordinary powers (the reader has Article 48, `article-48-weimar`) and "social justice" (forward to Scruton). Sextus Empiricus's Persians, who suspended all law for five days after a king's death to remind themselves why they had it, is the one anecdote. Consistency: this makes a man conservative about government and radical about almost everything else — the lineage is Montaigne, Hobbes, Hume, not Burke or Bentham — and politics is an activity for those past the "shadow line", not the young. Recall the Hobbes essay (`hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty` flagged it): Oakeshott reads Hobbes as a moralist of individuality, the sovereign as keeper of the rules of a game played by proud men, and that is the Hobbes the disposition descends from.
7. **What the disposition cannot do, and who said so.** Three objections, each at full strength, then the hand-off. (a) Hayek: a position that only brakes has no direction; "why I am not a conservative" — a disposition cannot say where to go. Oakeshott's reply is that government is not meant to go anywhere; and his counter-charge is that Hayek turned the insight into a crib. The reader judges whether "knowing where to go next in the exploration of an already existing traditional kind of society" is a direction or the absence of one. (b) The epistemic objection carried over: a disposition that cannot criticise its tradition has no answer when the tradition is bad; the Court of Appeal reply shows that standards exist, not that they are right. (c) The charge the course will meet in its last module: the umpire state is already the liberal state — rules under which each pursues his own ends, with no shared purpose, is Hobbes's Leviathan minus the fear; a politics that is only custody of rules dissolves the little platoon as surely as the Rationalist's plan does, because it cannot name anything the players are playing for. Oakeshott would say the shared purposes belong to the players, not the umpire. Hand off: if the umpire "commands for" no truth, what does he say to a society whose intimations run toward cannibalism? That question — whether anything is right by nature — opens the next lesson.

Headings as claims: "The Rationalist: a character before a doctrine"; "Two kinds of knowledge, and the one that looks certain"; "The Rights of Man were a cookery book"; "Politics as the pursuit of intimations: the enfranchisement of women"; "A disposition, not a creed — and where it fits"; "Government as umpire: dreaming and ruling generate tyranny"; "What the disposition cannot say".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** "Rationalist" — Oakeshott's capitalised type, NOT the philosopher's rationalism (Descartes vs empiricism); say so in the first paragraph, since the reader met "rational conservatism" as Hegel's kind in the SEP-derived taxonomy of the previous lesson. The 1947 essay, the 1951 LSE inaugural, the 1956 essay and the 1962 collection are the lesson's four dates; give them once with the Beveridge/1944/1945 anchors so the reader sees the essay as written against Labour's postwar planning and against Hayek at the same time.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** Six items — a recipe, a surgeon's steadiness mid-operation, the Highway Code, a barrister's sense of which precedent will persuade, the rules of chess, knowing when a sauce has thickened — sorted technical / practical, with the test applied ("can it be written down so that an ignorant man could begin?").
2. **Boundary probe:** A society with no tradition to intimate anything — the Rationalist's "new society" (America 1776, Oakeshott's own case) or a refugee people founding a colony (the Lombards and the Trojan band, which Voegelin will use two lessons on). What does "the pursuit of intimations" say when there is nothing to pursue? Solution: Oakeshott's own answer (the pioneers brought a tradition with them and were Rationalists only "by circumstance"), and what it costs — the method presupposes an inheritance, so it cannot be a general account of founding; name the limit.
3. **Claim to refute:** "Oakeshott's view forbids reform." Name the failing step with his own cases: women's enfranchisement; legal innovation that reflects a change already made in activities and beliefs; "change" suffered vs "innovation" designed. Then say what the view DOES forbid and why (innovation that imposes; legislating for hypothetical situations).
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** Apply the five conclusions on innovation (OBC s.1) to one measure the course has met — the 1832 Reform Act (`british-franchise`) or the assignats (`assignats`) — and decide which conclusions it passes and fails; state in one sentence what the five add to Burke's "disposition to preserve and ability to improve".
5. **Sourcing drill:** Hamilton's remark that the Americans need not search "old parchments and musty records" for their rights (as Oakeshott uses it, RiP s.4 pp. 26–28): author, date (1775), audience (Loyalist pamphleteers), what it records, argues, omits — and what it can be USED to establish about the American founding as rationalist, against what it cannot (that the founders had no inherited arrangements: they had the common law, which is Oakeshott's own point in PE).
6. **Synthesis:** Oakeshott puts Burke with Bentham, and himself with Montaigne, Hobbes and Hume (OBC s.4). Both Burke and Oakeshott hold the epistemic argument of the previous lesson. State the difference in one claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph (Burke grounds the disposition in religion, natural law and inheritance; Oakeshott in a view of governing alone) and judge which can answer Broad's self-application problem.
7. **Counter-argument stem:** "Someone claims the umpire state is already the liberal state: rules without a shared purpose dissolve the little platoon as surely as a plan does." Name two facts the claim must account for (the disposition's ban on "commanding for truth" was meant to protect, not dissolve, the players' own purposes; Oakeshott's Hobbes is a moralist of individuality, not a liberal of rights) and what would have to be true for it to survive (that purposes do not survive without a government that names them) — flagged forward to `deneen-post-liberalism` and `scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `strauss-natural-right-and-history`:** Write the umpire's rules for a society whose tradition intimates that defeated enemies are eaten. The umpire "commands for" no truth; the disposition prefers the familiar. Does anything in this lesson let the umpire refuse? The solution says no — Oakeshott's standards live inside the tradition — and that the next lesson opens with exactly this case and asks whether anything is right by nature.

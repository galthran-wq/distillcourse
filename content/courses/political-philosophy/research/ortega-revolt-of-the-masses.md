# Research brief: ortega-revolt-of-the-masses (Ortega y Gasset: the mass-man and the revolt of the masses)

status: planned
module: crisis-of-liberal-europe
type: core
goal: Analyze Ortega's diagnosis of the mass-man and his account of why a civilisation that produced him cannot be run by him

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `spengler-decline-of-the-west` (prerequisite, the previous
  lesson): Civilization as the late stage of every Culture, the world-city and
  the formless "Fourth Estate", and the claim that the West's technique and
  machines will run on through its winter — the two theses Ortega attacks by name
  (ch. IX, XIV.2), so his anti-Spengler passages are now BACKWARD Refs, not
  forward hooks. From the crowd-race-nation module: Le Bon's crowd — the man in
  a crowd is not the man alone; suggestible, moved by images and leaders
  (`taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`, concept `crowd-psychology`) — the
  predecessor Ortega never names, and whose "crowd" his "mass" is NOT; and
  Renan's nation as "a daily plebiscite" (`renan-what-is-a-nation`, concept
  `daily-plebiscite`), which Ortega quotes and turns toward the future. From
  this module: Mosca's ruling class and Michels's party oligarchy
  (`mosca-ruling-class`, `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`) — a
  "minority" defined by organization and power, against which Ortega's minority
  (those who demand much of themselves) must be distinguished at first use;
  Mann's Bürger/Literat/Philister typology and his "Masse" left undefined in the
  right-hand column (`mann-reflections`). Further back: Plato's democratic man,
  ruled by whichever desire arrives (`decline-of-regimes`); Hume's "on opinion
  only … government is founded" (`hume-against-the-original-contract`, concept
  `government-founded-on-opinion`) — Ortega cites Hume for his own rule-by-opinion
  thesis (XIV.1); Tocqueville's soft despotism and the "flock of timid and
  industrious animals" (`tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`);
  Carlyle's demand for heroes (`carlyle-heroes`); Marx's class struggle
  (`marx-manifesto`), which this lesson's left objection will use.
- **Contributes:** One move: a diagnosis of the crisis made by a LIBERAL, in
  which the dangerous new type is not a class but a kind of person — the man
  who feels "just like everybody", demands rights without duties, and is
  closed to appeal — and in which the remedy is not force (rule rests on
  opinion) but minorities who demand of themselves and a nation re-defined as
  a project. The reader leaves able to define mass and minority as Ortega
  does, to say why a scientist can be a mass-man and a labourer not, why the
  State is "the greatest danger", and why rule is never founded on force.
- **Hands off:** Ortega's claim that rule is public opinion and that liberalism
  is "sharing existence with the enemy" is exactly what the next lesson denies:
  `schmitt-concept-of-the-political` will say the political is the real
  possibility of killing the enemy, and that a people which lets others name
  its enemy is no longer politically free. Forward, one clause each: "direct
  action" and the syndicalist general strike, which Ortega names as barbarism,
  are given their own defence at full strength in `sorel-reflections-on-violence`
  (next module); the nation as project (XIV.6–8) is inherited by
  `the-nation-and-its-defenders`; and Ortega's thesis is turned upside down in
  `lasch-revolt-of-the-elites` (equality module) — the people without loyalty to
  place, obligation or gratitude to the past are, sixty years on, the educated
  elites, not the masses. The FORWARD exercise targets Schmitt; Lasch's
  inversion is a forward clause in the closing and may be a second exercise's
  solution note. The lesson carries no verdict (REGISTER.md: diagnosis,
  carried to Schmitt and Weimar); the module question it feeds — can a
  liberal parliamentary democracy govern and defend itself? — is decided in
  `article-48-weimar`.
- **Spine step:** "Liberal Europe breaks: elite theorists show that a minority
  always rules, Mann sets Kultur against Zivilisation, Spengler foretells the
  West's winter, Ortega diagnoses the mass-man, Schmitt names the political as
  friend and enemy and the sovereign as he who decides the exception — and
  Weimar is the case"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (verbatim from course.yaml): everywhere is crowded — cafés,
trains, beaches, theatres — and the crowd, which used to stay in the
background, now occupies the places and functions minorities held; a Spanish
philosopher in 1930 says this visible fact is the key to Europe's crisis: a
new type of man has appeared, satisfied with himself, demanding rights
without duties, and he is not a social class.

1. **What does a full café prove?** The obvious answer: more people (Europe's
   population did roughly treble between 1800 and 1914 — Ortega's one
   statistic, ch. V, from Sombart: about 180 million to about 460 million, a
   figure he says "should make our hair stand on end"). It fails to explain
   what Ortega noticed: not that there are more people but that the crowd is
   now in the FRONT — "the multitude has suddenly become visible, installing
   itself in the preferential positions in society" (ch. I). The answer: a
   change in the relation between two kinds of men that every society
   contains. Define them as he does (ch. I, ~145–230): the minority is "those
   who demand much of themselves", the mass "those who demand nothing
   special of themselves… 'just like everybody'". Then the two corrections a
   newcomer needs at once: the division is NOT between upper and lower
   classes ("within every social class there are mass and genuine minority",
   the "great vehicle" and "lesser vehicle" of Buddhism); and the humble
   mediocre man who knows his limits is not mass — the mass-man is the one
   who feels no lack. Two seams the newcomer needs, one sentence each: (i) this
   is not Le Bon's crowd (Ref `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`) — the
   crowd is people assembled and transformed for an hour, the mass-man is a type
   who is the same alone at home; (ii) Ortega's "minority" is not Mosca's ruling
   minority (Ref `mosca-ruling-class`) — Mosca's is defined by organization and
   power, Ortega's by what a man demands of himself, so that a ruling minority
   can be made of mass-men. Heading: "The crowd has moved to the footlights".

2. **What has he done once in front?** Hyperdemocracy (ch. I end, ~256–303):
   old democracy was tempered by liberalism and law, the mass delegated;
   now it acts directly and imposes its tastes by material pressure — "to be
   different is to be indecent". Two headings of the revolt (ch. II): the mass
   takes over functions once reserved to minorities, and it is indocile —
   refuses to be led. The rise of the historic level: the rights of man have
   passed from a juridical ideal to the psychological state of the average
   man; what was once a demand is now assumed, like air. Ref
   `decline-of-regimes` for Plato's democratic man (the type Ortega re-stages)
   and `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism` for the tyranny
   of opinion. The seam here must break an expectation: Ortega calls his view
   "radically aristocratic" and in the same chapter says Versailles was NOT an
   aristocracy and that the nobles accepted the guillotine "as the tumour
   accepts the lancet" — aristocracy means a demanding minority, not a
   hereditary caste.

3. **Where did he come from?** From the nineteenth century's own success:
   liberal democracy plus experimental science plus industrialism (ch. VI)
   made a world of ease and gave him three convictions — that life is easy and
   abundant; that he is complete and need appeal to no one outside himself;
   that he may intervene in everything by direct action (the three-feature
   psychology, ch. XI, ~2619–2725). He is the spoilt child and the heir: the
   hereditary aristocrat wearing an ancestor's giant armour is the same type
   in an older uniform (~2675–2705). The spoilt child's error is to take
   civilisation for nature — the mob that wrecks the bakeries while looking
   for bread (ch. VI), the man who expects cars to be cheaper in five years
   as he expects the sun to rise. The "because": a man formed by a world of
   solved problems never learns that the solutions had to be made and must be
   kept; he is "radically ungrateful". Heading: "The spoilt child of the
   nineteenth century".

4. **What exactly is wrong with him — snobbery, or something that can be
   defined?** This is where the lesson earns its concept. Noble life is life
   as discipline, defined by obligations, not rights: "Noblesse oblige"; "To
   live as one likes is plebeian" (Goethe, ch. VII, l.1606); noble means
   "known", by effort, and the Chinese ennobled the ancestors through the
   son's deeds ("is not was"). Rights: private rights are UPHELD (they must be
   maintained by the one who holds them); common rights are merely HELD — the
   mass-man confuses them. Mass is inertia, not multitude. Hermetism (ch.
   VIII): the mass-man "does not want to give reasons or to be right"; his
   ideas are "appetites in words"; culture is the existence of standards one
   can appeal to, barbarism the absence of standards to which appeal can be
   made — so direct action, the inversion of civilisation's reduction of
   force to ultima ratio into prima and unica ratio, is "the Magna Charta of
   barbarism" (Syndicalism and Fascism named as the examples). Then the
   definition that makes Ortega a liberal and not a Carlyle: liberal
   democracy is "the loftiest endeavour towards common life… Liberalism is
   the supreme form of generosity; it is the right which the majority
   concedes to minorities… the determination to share existence with the
   enemy; more than that, with an enemy which is weak" (l.2012–2030). Ref
   `carlyle-heroes`: Carlyle wants reverence for superiors; Ortega wants
   minorities to demand of themselves and the mass to let them. Ref
   `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`: the harm principle is one form of this
   generosity. Heading: "Nobility is obligation; barbarism is the absence of
   standards".

5. **Isn't the scientist proof against the diagnosis?** The newcomer's
   natural defence of modern Europe: whatever the café crowd is, the men who
   made the aspirin and the motor-car are not mass. Ortega's answer is his
   boldest chapter (XII): the scientist is the PROTOTYPE of the mass-man.
   Science was constituted by encyclopaedic minds (Galileo, Newton) and is
   advanced by specialised ones; specialisation lets a mediocre man do good
   work in a cell of knowledge and leaves him "a learned ignoramus" (l.3078),
   petulantly sure outside his field, ignorant of the conditions for
   science's own survival. Ch. IX adds the sharper point against Spengler:
   technique is not self-sustaining — it is "a precipitate of superfluous,
   unpractical activities"; cut pure science and the machines run on for a
   generation (Weyl's remark, Note 13: ten or twelve physicists dead and
   physics is lost) — Ref `spengler-decline-of-the-west`: the
   reader has Spengler's claim that the Machine is the Civilization's last work
   and runs on through its winter; Ortega calls him "far too optimistic"
   (~2193–2205) because technique cannot outlive the vocation that feeds it. One
   clause of reminder, then Ortega's argument. The Central African in the
   motor-car taking aspirin
   (~2317–2323) is the test: enjoying civilisation's products does not imply
   understanding or willing its principles. This also answers the charge that
   the diagnosis is contempt for the poor: the labourer who knows his limits
   is not mass, the professor who does not is.

6. **What does the mass-man do with the State?** Ch. XIII. The State is the
   bourgeoisie's technical masterpiece; after 1848 it is so strong that
   revolutions give way to coups d'état. The mass-man, who treats
   civilisation as nature, treats the State as his own ("L'État c'est moi" —
   a mistake) and demands it intervene in everything; Statism is direct
   action institutionalised. The danger is that State intervention absorbs
   spontaneous social action: "the skeleton eats up the flesh around it. The
   scaffolding becomes the owner and tenant of the house" (l.3379); Rome's
   bureaucratisation under the Antonines, militarisation under the Severi,
   foreigners first staffing the army and then taking the State (~3354–3380).
   Ref `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`: this is the
   tutelary power with the mass as its client. Ref `hegel-ethical-life-and-
   the-state` for contrast: Ortega's State is an instrument, never an end.
   The objection from the LEFT enters here at full strength: a "psychological
   type found in every class" cannot explain who owns the State. Marx's
   Manifesto (Ref `marx-manifesto`) says the State is a committee for
   managing the affairs of the bourgeoisie; Ortega's own ch. XIII says the
   State was built by the bourgeoisie and later XIV.9 calls Fascism a petit-
   bourgeois movement and says Bolshevism won in Russia "because there were
   no bourgeois" — so the class categories are doing work in his own text
   that "mass" cannot do. Ortega's reply (ch. I and XI): the type cuts across
   class and the heir-aristocrat is its purest case; class explains who has
   power, not why power is used without standards. Say what the reply
   achieves, as a fact about the argument: it answers the charge that "mass"
   is a class in disguise (the scientist and the heir are real cases of the
   type outside the masses), but it concedes the objection's narrower point —
   for who owns and uses the State, his own text falls back on class terms,
   so "mass" explains the manner of power, not its distribution.

7. **If not force, what holds a society and who rules it?** Ch. XIV.1 — the
   premise the whole module will fight over. "Rule is the normal exercise of
   authority, and is always based on public opinion… Never has anyone ruled
   on this earth by basing his rule essentially on any other thing than
   public opinion" (l.3515–3519); Hume cited (Ref
   `hume-against-the-original-contract`: the reader has Hume's "on opinion only
   … government is founded"; Ortega borrows it, and Mosca's political formula,
   Ref `mosca-ruling-class`, was the same thesis given a content); Napoleon in
   Spain was
   aggression, not rule; Talleyrand: "You can do everything with bayonets,
   Sire, except sit on them" (l.3539). Force is the apparatus one has because
   one already commands. Europe ruled the world for three centuries by
   opinion; now it is unsure it rules, and the rest unsure they are ruled —
   the class when the master leaves (XIV.2). Decadence, Ortega insists, is a
   commonplace people take "as they would take a tram" (XIV.2, with the
   explicit warning not to think of Spengler, l.3703–3705) — what is really
   happening is that European life has outgrown the national State. Ref
   `spengler-decline-of-the-west` here, one clause: the reader has just read
   the book Ortega is warning against, so the seam can SAY the reversal — from a
   critic of the mass one expects a prophet of decline; Ortega denies the
   decline and diagnoses a mismatch of scale: economic,
   intellectual and political capacity exceed the frontiers; Parliaments have
   lost prestige because they have lost TASKS, not because their machinery is
   defective (XIV.5, "Not effective for what?"). Heading: "Rule is opinion,
   so the question is who has an opinion".

8. **The cure: a nation is a project.** XIV.6–8. The polis was an empty space
   enclosed, a refusal of the countryside — the State begins as a work of
   imagination; "The State is a plan of action and a programme of
   collaboration"; a nation is not blood, language or natural frontiers —
   those are effects and later consolidations, obstacles at the start ("the
   State has always been the great dragoman"); the ancient State divided
   governors from governed, the national State makes every subject a
   participant and is therefore democratic at root. Renan's "daily
   plebiscite" (Ref `renan-what-is-a-nation`: the reader has the lecture —
   memory and present consent, against race and language; one clause, no
   re-teaching) is quoted as Ortega quotes it (l.4990–5002) and turned round: the plebiscite is
   on a FUTURE — "In defending the nation we are defending our to-morrows,
   not our yesterdays" (l.5030–5040); a nation is "always either in the
   making, or in the unmaking. Tertium non datur". Hence the proposal: Europe
   as a great national State, the only enterprise big enough to give the
   masses something to do and to outbid the fervour of the Communist five-
   year plan (XIV.9); nationalism is "the effort opposite to the
   nation-creating principle". The coda (XV): Europe has no moral code; the
   mass-man's scheme is all rights and no obligations; "youth" has become a
   form of blackmail; "amorality does not exist" — refusal of norms is
   immorality that keeps the empty form. Hands off: this is where the
   objection from Schmitt is announced and carried to the next lesson — a
   nation defined by a programme and a rule founded on opinion have, on his
   account, no answer to the man who names it his enemy. Ortega's book does
   not answer it: it treats Fascism and Bolshevism as symptoms of the
   mass-man, not as organised enemies a liberal State must decide how to
   resist;
   whether a liberal parliamentary democracy can defend itself is decided on
   the record in `article-48-weimar`.

Headings as claims: "The crowd has moved to the footlights"; "Hyperdemocracy:
to be different is indecent"; "The spoilt child of the nineteenth century";
"Nobility is obligation; barbarism is the absence of standards"; "The
scientist as mass-man: the barbarism of specialisation"; "The State is the
greatest danger — and the left's objection"; "Rule is opinion, decadence is a
commonplace"; "A nation is a plebiscite on tomorrow".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The full theatre** (ch. I opening, l.80–140): Ortega's own surprise at
   his surprise ("to wonder is to begin to understand"), the stage metaphor —
   the chorus has walked to the footlights; Mallarmé's quip that a small
   audience "stressed the absence of the multitude". Tell it in four sentences
   as the lesson's first scene; it IS the opening's visible fact.
2. **Mass and minority defined, with the two corrections** (ch. I, ~145–230):
   quote the definition in one sentence each, then the "great vehicle / lesser
   vehicle" division and the humble man who is not mass. Work a case: a
   village carpenter who knows what he cannot do (minority by Ortega's
   criterion) and a professor who pronounces on politics (mass).
3. **The bakeries and the air** (ch. VI): the mob that wrecks the bakery
   looking for bread; the air nobody thanks; cars expected cheaper like the
   sunrise. Three images, one claim: civilisation mistaken for nature.
4. **Noblesse oblige, held vs upheld** (ch. VII, l.1600–1660): the etymology
   (noble = known, by deeds), the Chinese reversal, Goethe's "to live as one
   likes is plebeian", the heir in his ancestor's armour (ch. XI). Reconstruct
   the distinction between rights one must maintain and rights one merely
   possesses, then apply it to the rights of man as "psychological state".
5. **The ladder of force** (ch. VIII): reason → ultima ratio (force as last
   resort, the achievement of civilisation) → direct action as prima/unica
   ratio. Quote "the Magna Charta of barbarism" and the generosity passage
   (l.2012–2030) side by side: the latter is the lesson's keeper quotation.
6. **The Central African in the motor-car** (ch. IX ~2317–2323) and **the
   learned ignoramus** (ch. XII l.3068–3081): two tests of the same thing —
   whether using a civilisation's products means understanding it. Flag the
   first for its period vocabulary (see Pitfalls) and keep the point, not the
   example's dress, if the writer prefers Weyl's twelve physicists (Note 13).
7. **The prickly pear** (ch. X ~2376–2385): one homesick emigrant's potted
   cactus in the 1840s now covers Australia at a square kilometre a year —
   civilisation needs constant defence against the jungle. One paragraph.
8. **Bayonets and the janissaries** (XIV.1 l.3515–3545): "there is no ruling
   with janissaries"; Talleyrand to Napoleon. Then **Renan re-read** (XIV.8
   l.4990–5040): quote Renan's definition as Ortega quotes it, then Ortega's
   turn to the future; show the "Tertium non datur".
9. **Rome's skeleton** (ch. XIII ~3354–3380): Antonines bureaucratise, Severi
   militarise, foreigners staff the army and take the State; "the scaffolding
   becomes the owner and tenant of the house". The one historical analogy the
   lesson works, because it carries the State chapter.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know, and where to anchor them.** Mass and
  minority (link 1 — defined before used; the English "mass" carries class
  connotations Ortega's "masa" does not, say so). Hyperdemocracy (link 2).
  Noblesse oblige (link 4 — a French tag meaning "nobility obliges", that
  rank carries duties; Ortega reverses it: duties make the rank). Ultima
  ratio, prima ratio (link 4, Latin: last / first resort). Direct action
  (link 4: the syndicalist term for strikes and violence that bypass
  parliament; anchor with the date, the 1900s–1920s). Syndicalism, Fascism
  (link 4: half a sentence each — the French and Italian labour movement
  that trusted the general strike over the ballot; Mussolini's movement, in
  power from 1922). Bolshevism (link 6: Lenin's party, in power since 1917).
  Hermetism (link 4: sealed, closed to appeal). Noluntas (link 3: Ortega's
  coinage, the will-not; negative freedom). Technicism (link 5). Polis (link
  8: Ref `greek-miracle`, one clause). Renan's "daily plebiscite" (link 8: NOT
  anchored afresh — Ref `renan-what-is-a-nation` with one clause of reminder). Rathenau (link 3: German industrialist
  and minister, murdered 1922 — the reader met the murder in
  `mann-reflections`; one clause and a Ref). Sombart (link 1: German
  economist whose population figures Ortega cites — name only as Ortega's
  source if the number is used; otherwise state the number as the course's
  own and put Sombart in References). Talleyrand (link 7: Napoleon's foreign
  minister). Antonines, Severi (link 6: Roman imperial dynasties of the 2nd
  and 3rd centuries; one clause each).
- **The two numbers.** Europe's population ~180 million (1800) → ~460
  million (1914) is Ortega's only statistic and the lesson's one keeper
  number; bind it in the sentence (a continent, a century, about two and a
  half times — Ortega says "triplicated", which is the 1914 figure against the
  long pre-1800 plateau; say which). Weyl's "ten or twelve physicists" is a
  rhetorical count; report it as Ortega's quotation, not as a fact.
- **Period vocabulary.** The 1932 translation carries "inferior races" (ch.
  XI) and the "Central African" of ch. IX. The lesson does not launder and
  does not moralise: it reports the example as Ortega gives it, in one
  clause notes the vocabulary is his and 1930's, and uses the POINT (products
  vs principles), for which Weyl's physicists or the aspirin alone suffice.
  Hereditarian claims are not part of Ortega's argument — he explicitly makes
  the type cross-class and cross-national — so there is no contested
  empirical claim to carry here beyond the population figure.
- **Do not make Ortega a Carlyle or a Nietzsche.** He is a liberal: the
  generosity passage is load-bearing and must appear before any reader
  could file him with the anti-democrats. Equally, do not soften "radically
  aristocratic" — he says it and means a demanding minority. The lesson's
  balance is Ortega's own.
- **The left objection must be at full strength**, with Marx's definition of
  the State as its text (Ref), and Ortega's own ch. XIII and XIV.9 as the
  evidence that class is doing work he does not credit. No strawman "some
  believe".
- **Spengler is NOT re-taught here.** He is the previous lesson. The
  anti-Spengler passages (ch. IX ~2193–2205; XIV.2 l.3700–3716) state two of
  Ortega's theses — technique is not self-sustaining, decadence is a
  commonplace — each with a `<Ref>` and one clause recalling what Spengler
  claimed. One paragraph each at most. The civilizations module's later test
  of the organism theory is a forward clause at most.
- **Le Bon and Renan are not re-taught either.** Le Bon's crowd is the
  predecessor Ortega does not name — one clause and a Ref to separate the two
  concepts (link 1); Renan's plebiscite is recalled with a Ref (link 8).
- **Register (exact rule).** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Scholars live in References only:** Muller (Schumpeter's text may be
  quoted one sentence as a primary voice in an Aside), Lilla, Sombart (unless
  named as Ortega's cited source), Hume may be named — Ortega cites him and he
  has his own lesson (`hume-against-the-original-contract`; Ref it at link 7).
- **Where the dossier is thin:** the Spanish original is not in the dossier;
  Ortega's earlier España invertebrada (1922) and the 1937 English preface
  (the "Epilogue for Englishmen", with its reflections on pacifism) are not
  in the dossier — the latter would sharpen the Schmitt hand-off and the
  writer may cite it in References from any post-1937 edition. Renan's 1882
  lecture IS now in the dossier (French, `research/texts/renan/`), taught in
  `renan-what-is-a-nation`; cite it in References with that lesson's
  translation.
- **Where summary and text may disagree:** the summary's chapter line
  numbers for chs. IX–XIII are "shard 2 numbering" — they match the full
  file as checked (l.2317 aspirin, l.3078 learned ignoramus, l.3379 skeleton,
  l.3539 bayonets, l.4995 Renan). Verify the "triplicated" claim against the
  text of ch. V before asserting it.
- **No real misconception slot** beyond the one above (the reading of the
  book as an aristocrat's complaint about the poor); do not invent "some think the masses are the poor" unless the
  writer finds the reading documented — Ortega himself anticipates it (ch. I,
  II), which is enough to refute it from the text.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** State Ortega's definition of mass and
   minority and give the two corrections he insists on (not a social class;
   the humble man is not mass). Then recall from `decline-of-regimes` Plato's
   democratic man and say in one sentence what Ortega adds (E3: self-
   satisfaction and indocility, not merely licence). Then say in one sentence
   how the mass-man differs from Le Bon's crowd (Ref
   `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`).
2. **Document reasoning / sourcing drill:** the generosity passage (ch. VIII,
   l.2012–2030) with metadata (Madrid 1930, a philosopher writing in a
   dictatorship's last year for a newspaper public; the Primo de Rivera
   regime fell in January 1930). Predict what a liberal in that situation
   would want to say about majorities; read; records / argues / omits; what
   can the passage be USED to establish about Ortega's politics, and what can
   it not establish about Spanish liberalism in general?
3. **Claim to refute:** "Ortega's 'mass' is a polite word for the working
   class, and his book is an aristocrat's complaint about the poor." Name the
   failing step (the definition is explicitly psychological and cross-class;
   the scientist is the prototype; the hereditary aristocrat is the heir-man;
   Versailles was not an aristocracy) — and then state what remains of the
   left objection AFTER the refutation (that class explains who owns the
   State, which "mass" cannot).
4. **Boundary probe:** Run Ortega's claim that rule rests on public opinion,
   never on force, on the limiting case he himself names — rule with
   janissaries — and on a modern one: an occupying army. By his criterion
   neither is "rule". What does the criterion then classify as rule, and what
   does it leave unexplained (how opinion is changed by force over time)? The
   solution notes that the next lesson's author will answer that the
   decision on the enemy, not opinion, is the political fact.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** From Ortega's premises — (a) technique
   is a precipitate of pure science, (b) pure science is improbable and
   depends on a vocation the mass-man despises, (c) the mass-man will not
   uphold what he merely holds — derive his prediction for the sciences
   under hyperdemocracy, and compare it with his own observation in ch. IX
   that scientific vocations were already declining. Then say which premise
   the record since 1930 has tested most severely.
6. **Causal ranking (synthesis):** Rank Ortega's three nineteenth-century
   principles — liberal democracy, experimental science, industrialism — as
   necessary / enabling / precipitating causes of the mass-man, and defend
   the ranking from the text (ch. V–VI, XII). Then state what the ranking
   implies for his remedy (if democracy is only enabling, why keep it? — his
   answer in ch. X: preserve liberalism as the condition for superseding it).
7. **Corroboration (document reasoning):** Two short excerpts — Ortega's
   hermetism (ch. VIII: ideas as "appetites in words") and the Schumpeter
   sentence that the citizen "drops down to a lower level of mental
   performance as soon as he enters the political field" (1942; the Aside
   supplies it). Same diagnosis by two methods (a philosopher's observation,
   an economist's theory of the voter). Where do they agree, where part
   (Schumpeter keeps democracy as competition for leadership; Ortega wants
   minorities followed)? What does the agreement of two unrelated observers
   establish, and what does it not?
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `schmitt-concept-of-the-political`:** Ortega defines
   liberalism as "the determination to share existence with the enemy; more
   than that, with an enemy which is weak". Take the case where the enemy
   is not weak and does not want to share existence — a party that says it
   will use the ballot once to abolish the ballot. Using only Ortega's
   premises (rule is opinion; civilisation is the reduction of force to
   ultima ratio; liberalism is the majority's generosity), say what a
   liberal may do. The solution shows the reader has hit the wall the next
   lesson's author builds his criterion on — that the political begins where
   the possibility of such an enemy is real — and Refs
   `schmitt-concept-of-the-political`.

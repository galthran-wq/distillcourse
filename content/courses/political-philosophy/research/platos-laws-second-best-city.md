# Research brief: platos-laws-second-best-city (Plato's Laws: the second-best city)

status: planned

goal: Analyze how the Laws replaces the philosopher king with law, preambles and property limits, and what the change concedes

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `decline-of-regimes`: the regime cycle; the second telling in Laws III (Persia and Athens as the two mother forms; Sparta saved by two kings, elders and ephors; the FORWARD exercise left the reader unable to classify Sparta). From `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`: the philosopher king, the noble lie, the community of wives and property, the Ship of State. From `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`: "justice is the interest of the stronger", still being answered.
- **Contributes:** One move: what a city can do when there is no one who knows the good. Plato's own answer, late in life — rulers as "servants of the law"; a law that persuades before it compels (the preamble, the free doctor); a property regime that caps wealth at four times the poorest lot; a mixed constitution learned from Sparta; and, in Book X, the first full statement of the materialist thesis ("nature and chance before art; justice is convention; the highest right is might") met by the argument that soul is prior to body. The lesson reads it as Plato's second thoughts and says exactly what each device concedes to the Republic's critics.
- **Hands off:** This is an `extra` (starred) chapter; no core lesson depends on it. The core path continues at `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`, where Aristotle attacks the Republic's unity premise — and whose Politics II.6 is already this lesson's standing objection (a property limit without a population limit). `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` names polity and carries Laws IV's charge that a mixture is "parties, not polities"; `sparta-as-test-case` tests Laws III's Sparta against Aristotle's; `cicero-mixed-constitution` inherits the mixed constitution; `aquinas-law-and-kingship` inherits law as "the distribution of mind".
- **Spine step:** "Plato answers 'who should rule' with knowledge: justice is each part doing its own work, so the city should be ruled by those who know the good — and freedom left to itself slides through democracy into tyranny"

Note: an extra chapter may run shorter (course.yaml est_minutes 30). The core path must read correctly without it; nothing after it may depend on it.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): three old men walk to the cave of Zeus on Crete and plan a real colony; they cannot have philosopher kings, so they must say what a city can do with ordinary men — how much land each may hold, how laws should be worded to persuade before they compel, what to do with a citizen who says there are no gods; read the plan as Plato's second thoughts. Every device is derived from the one missing thing: no one in this city knows the good.

1. **What changes when the philosopher is removed?** The obvious attempt: keep the Republic's institutions and find the next-best rulers. Why it fails: the Republic's institutions (the lie, the communal guardians, the compulsion to rule) all presuppose a ruler who knows; without him the lie is just a lie and the compulsion is just force. The answer, from the Laws' own three-tier ranking (V ll. 443–479): the first-best city is the one where "friends have all things in common" — the Republic, kept as a pattern; the second-best is this one, where men have private households and lots and the law rules the rulers; the third is left unsaid. The draughts image: withdrawing the stone from the holy line — settling for less. Because: a city without a knower must be governed by something that does not depend on anyone's knowledge, and that thing is written law.
2. **Rulers as servants of the law — and the charge that a "regime" may not be one.** Laws IV (ll. 402–408, 521–545): regimes named after their ruling part — democracy, oligarchy, aristocracy — are "parties, not polities"; their notions of justice are "unmeaning". Mark the return of Thrasymachus (`<Ref>` `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`): "justice is the interest of the stronger" is rejected a second time (ll. 462–470), now by the device of making no part the stronger. Offices go to the most obedient to the laws; the rulers are "servants of the law"; "the state in which the law is above the rulers has salvation". The age of Cronos: no human nature can hold supreme power without hubris, so demigods ruled men as men rule flocks — and the nearest imitation is to obey law, which is "the distribution of mind" (nous → nomos). Because: if no man may be trusted with unchecked power, the ruler must be the thing that cannot want anything. Here the seam reverses the Republic: there the best city was the one whose rulers were most reluctant to rule; here it is the one whose rulers are most obedient.
3. **The first mixed constitution: what Sparta had that saved it.** Laws III (ll. 725–843): seven titles to rule — parents, the noble, the elder, masters, the stronger (Pindar's "according to nature"), the wise ("the greatest of all": the rule of law over willing subjects), the lot — all claim at once, and that is "the fountain-head of seditions". "Do not give too great power to anything": a sail too large, too much food, too much authority. Sparta survived where Argos and Messene fell because a god gave it two kings instead of one, then twenty-eight elders with weight equal to the kings, then the Ephors, like magistrates chosen by lot: the kingly power "compounded of the right elements and duly moderated" preserved itself and all the rest. Then the two mother forms — Persia (monarchy) and Athens (democracy) — and the thesis that a good city must have both "in measure" for freedom, friendship and wisdom. Concept `mixed-constitution`. Then Megillus's confession (IV ll. 389–397): asked what Sparta's government is, he cannot say — tyranny (the Ephors), democracy, aristocracy, the oldest monarchy. Because: a regime in which no element can name itself the ruler is the only regime none of the seven claimants can call "the stronger's interest".
4. **The preamble: a law that persuades before it compels.** Laws IV (ll. 697–862): a poet may say contradictory things; a legislator must give one rule and define "moderate" exactly. But must he only threaten? Two kinds of doctor: the slave doctor who "prescribes what mere experience suggests, as if he had exact knowledge, and when he has given his orders, like a tyrant, rushes off"; the free doctor who "carries his enquiries far back, enters into discourse with the patient and with his friends … and will not prescribe until he has first convinced him". The marriage law in simple form (marry between thirty and thirty-five or be fined and dishonoured) and in double form (first the reason — a man shares in immortality by leaving children — then the command). Every law = prelude + command. Concept `preamble-to-law`. Mark what this concedes to the Republic's critics: the Republic told the many a story (`<Ref>` `noble-lie`); the Laws tells them the reason. Because: a citizen who is not a natural slave is owed an account, and a law that gives one is obeyed by free men rather than endured by servants.
5. **Land, lots and the cap on wealth: the arithmetic of a city of equals-by-measure.** Laws V (ll. 385–675): no land redistribution or debt cancellation — "superhuman folly" to create enmities; the city is built "on freedom from avarice and justice as on a rock"; 5040 citizen-landholders, because the number has fifty-nine divisors including all of 1 to 10 (the one number the course computes, playbook-history rule 4: it IS the argument — a city that must be divided into tribes, ranks and messes needs a number that divides); lots inalienable and indivisible, one heir each, the rest sent to colonies; no private gold or silver, a token coin for internal use; no dowries, no deposits at interest; why the very rich cannot be good — a man who both acquires justly and spends justly cannot double his estate, so a doubled estate shows unjust acquisition or stinginess (ll. 591–605); four property classes; the lot is the floor, four times the lot the ceiling; surplus goes to the state and the gods, and the informer is paid half. Because: if wealth cannot be abolished (the first-best city) it must be bounded, and the bound must be stated as a ratio to the poorest, not a sum.
6. **The STRONGEST OBJECTION — Aristotle, Politics II.6, at full strength.** The territory for 5000 idle warriors and their households would have to be "as large as Babylon"; the lot is fixed but the number of children is not, so some must fall into poverty, and "poverty is the parent of revolution and crime" — "fix the property, fix the children"; the regime is "a polity of the hoplites" with elections biased toward the rich; and if the best constitution is a blend, the Laws' blend of monarchy and democracy is poorer than a blend of more elements. Plus Phaleas's sharpening: equalise desires, not possessions, for "two obols were once enough" and desire is never satisfied. The lesson concedes: the Laws does impose a procreation limit by exposure and colonies (ll. 481–549) but never squares it with the lot — the reply is thin, and the course says so. Reply, as Plato's: the "city of wax" exchange (ll. 706–730) — the legislator is a painter who must first draw the self-consistent pattern and only then concede to what is feasible; the paradigm is not refuted by its own impracticability. Because: the Laws is already the concession; Aristotle's charge is that it did not concede enough.
7. **Book X: the materialist's "might is right" and the priority of soul.** A citizen says there are no gods, or that they do not care, or that they can be bribed. Persuade him first (the young atheist addressed gently, ll. 164–173). His doctrine, at full strength (ll. 191–253): everything is by nature, chance or art; the elements are nature, the gods are made by art and convention, justice too, "the highest right is might" — and the young who hear it live by it, and faction follows. Thrasymachus and Glaucon's genealogies given a cosmology (`<Ref>` `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`). Plato's reply is cosmological too: the materialist has the order wrong — soul is prior to body, because only self-moving motion can begin a change ("if everything were at rest, only self-motion could start it", ll. 492–506); soul is the self-mover, so thought, art and law are "by nature" and the elements come after; the orderly circular motions of the heavens are moved by the best soul, so "all things are full of gods". The lesson states the argument's shape and marks its load-bearing step (that what moves itself must be soul) without adjudicating. Because: the only answer to "justice is convention" that Plato has left, once the knower is gone, is that nature itself is ruled by mind — and that is a theology.
8. **What the change concedes.** Close by listing, as derived not asserted: knowledge → law; the lie → the preamble; communism → bounded property; the philosopher's compulsion → the ruler's obedience; "the good" → "God is the measure" (IV ll. 583–586). The `<Conclusion>` says what the reader can now do (state what each device replaces and what it concedes) and what stays open: whether a mixture is a regime at all (Aristotle's polity and Bodin's later rejection, `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, `bodin-sovereignty`) and whether a law can rule without someone to interpret it (`aquinas-law-and-kingship`). The core reader, who skips this chapter, meets these in the Aristotle lessons directly.

Headings that fall out: "Second best: the pattern kept, the knower removed" · "Rulers as servants of the law; parties are not polities" · "Seven titles to rule, and how Sparta was saved from them" · "The free doctor: a law that gives its reasons" · "5040 lots and a ceiling of four: bounding wealth that cannot be abolished" · "Aristotle's charge: property limited, births not" · "Might is right, answered by the priority of soul" · "What the second-best city concedes".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), in chain order:** the Athenian Stranger (nameless; the main speaker — one clause that the dialogue is the only one of Plato's without Socrates), Cleinias of Crete and Megillus of Sparta (an old Cretan commissioned to found a colony; an old Spartan), Cnossus and the cave of Zeus (Crete's old capital and a cult site on Mount Ida — one clause), Magnesia (the colony's name — use once if at all), Dorians (the Greek people of Sparta, Argos, Messene — anchor at Laws III's "three Dorian kingdoms"), Argos and Messene (the two Dorian kingdoms that fell), Ephors (five annually elected Spartan "overseers"), gerousia (the twenty-eight elders plus the kings — Laws III says twenty-eight), Pindar (Theban poet, c. 500, when his "stronger by nature" line is quoted), Cronos (the god who ruled before Zeus; the age of Cronos is a golden age), nous/nomos (mind/law — the pun once, in parentheses), stadia (eighty stadia ≈ 15 km, one clause), obol (a small coin — Aristotle's "two obols were once enough"), Phaleas of Chalcedon (a theorist Aristotle reports, one clause), Babylon (as a byword for a vast territory — Aristotle's comparison).
- **Starred chapter discipline (L2b, C2):** no core lesson may require this one. The writer must not introduce a concept the core path needs; `mixed-constitution` is listed as introduced in `decline-of-regimes` in concepts.yaml and only reinforced here — so this lesson deepens it, never first-defines it. `preamble-to-law` is introduced here and used again only in `aquinas-law-and-kingship` (which Refs this chapter as optional) and `cicero-natural-law` (Keyes's note that Cicero's Laws II borrows the proem from Plato, Zaleucus and Charondas) — check that neither core lesson depends on it.
- **The second-best city is still Plato's.** The lesson must not read the Laws as a recantation: the first-best city is kept as the pattern (V ll. 460–479), the lie survives in a milder form (the preambles are persuasion, but Book X still legislates belief), and Book X's penal law on impiety is as severe as anything in the Republic. State the continuities as plainly as the concessions.
- **The legislator's prayer for a young tyrant** (IV ll. 252–311) is the passage most likely to be misread. It says the fastest route to a good polity is a young, temperate, lucky tyrant who happens to meet a great legislator — not that tyranny is good. Give it one paragraph and say exactly what it claims (ease of reform, not legitimacy); it is the Laws' version of the Ship of State's "one convert-ruler is enough" (`<Ref>` `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`).
- **Numbers (playbook-history rule 4):** 5040 and the ratio of four are the lesson's keeper numbers because the argument is about commensurability and bounds; eighty stadia and the twenty-eight elders are bound in their sentences; nothing else. Do not compute the divisors in prose beyond naming fifty-nine and "all of one to ten".
- **Book X is the lesson's hardest section; keep it to the political point.** The ten motions, the soul of the sun and the penal code are summarised in one figure and two paragraphs; the lesson needs only (a) the materialist doctrine as the cosmological form of Thrasymachus, (b) the reversal of the order of causes, (c) the move from "the good" to "God is the measure". Do not teach Plato's theology.
- **Do not re-teach Laws III's Persia and Athens** — `decline-of-regimes` did; one clause with a `<Ref>`. Do not re-teach Sparta's institutions beyond Laws III's three checks — `sparta-as-test-case` has the full account (and must not depend on this chapter; it uses Laws III directly).
- **Summary vs primary:** the Laws III and IV pointers were spot-checked and match. The staged Book X stops at the private-shrine law (meta.yaml) — the summary's X.13–14 is the end of the dossier's text. Jowett has no Stephanus numbers; the writer adding them (e.g. 739a for the three cities, 720a–e for the doctors, 889a–890a for the materialists) puts them in References.
- **Thin in the dossier:** Laws I–II, VI–IX, XI–XII are unread; the nocturnal council (XII) is therefore not available and the lesson should not describe it beyond one clause with "later books". The Strauss–Cropsey chapter is unread.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up, the one mechanical item).** List the five Republic devices and what the Laws puts in each one's place (knowledge → law; the lie → the preamble; communism → bounded lots; compulsion → obedience; the good → God as measure). From `decline-of-regimes` (E3): which two "mother forms" does Laws III say must be mixed, and which city stands for each?
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill) on the "servants of the law" passage (IV ll. 531–545).** Author (Plato, old, c. 350s), speaker (a nameless Athenian), dramatic audience (a Cretan and a Spartan planning a real colony); what it records (nothing), what it argues (that obedience, not knowledge, qualifies for office), what it omits (who writes the law the rulers serve — the legislator is outside the city he founds). Closing: what can the passage be USED to establish about how far Plato had moved from the Republic, and what does the speaker's anonymity change?
3. **Claim to refute.** "The Laws abandons the philosopher king." Name the step that fails (the first-best city is kept as the pattern; the legislator's prayer still wants wisdom paired with power; Book X still legislates what citizens may believe) and state the claim that survives (that the Laws governs the actual city by law because the knower cannot be counted on).
4. **Boundary probe: the wealth cap at its limits.** Set the ceiling to one (no one may hold more than the lot) and then to infinity (no ceiling). Derive from the Laws' own arguments what each city becomes (the first-best city without its communism of use — or a city that cannot pay its liturgies; an oligarchy by Plato's Book VIII mechanism) and say why Plato chose four. The solution says plainly that Plato gives no argument for four rather than three or five — the ratio is a judgment, and the lesson should say so.
5. **Production in the discipline's method: write a preamble.** Take the Laws' simple law against selling one's lot (V ll. 481–549) and write its double form — the reason first, in the Athenian's register, then the command. "Possible answers": the solution gives one preamble built from the Laws' own reasons (the lot is the city's, not the man's; the land is a god's allotment) and marks which reasons a modern reader would and would not accept.
6. **Counter-argument stem (synthesis).** "Someone claims Aristotle's population objection is decisive against the Laws." Name two facts the claim must account for (the Laws does name colonies and limits on procreation; Aristotle's own best city in Politics VII also fixes the citizen number and relies on exposure) and state what would have to be true for it to be decisive (that no city has ever held a fixed lot-number for a generation — then test against Sparta's oliganthropia in `sparta-as-test-case`).
7. **Paired-source corroboration.** Set the materialist doctrine of Laws X (ll. 191–253) beside Thrasymachus's regime table (`thrasymachus-and-the-ring`) and Glaucon's contract. Where do the three agree (justice is made, not found), where does Book X go further (it gives the making a physics), and what judgment follows about why Plato's final answer is cosmological rather than political?
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` (core).** The Laws says a regime named after its ruling part is "a party, not a polity", and that Sparta is good because no one can name its ruling part. State the strongest objection to this you can — that a regime nobody can name has no one responsible for it, or that every "mixture" is in fact ruled by whoever controls the decisive office. The solution says in one sentence that the reader has stated the question Aristotle answers by defining a constitution as "the arrangement of offices, especially the supreme one" — and that Bodin will later say a mixed state is no state at all, `<Ref>` both lessons.

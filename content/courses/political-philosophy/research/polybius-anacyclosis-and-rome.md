# Research brief: polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome (Polybius: the cycle of constitutions and Rome's mixed government)

status: planned
module: rome-and-christianity
type: core
goal: Explain Polybius's cycle of constitutions and his argument that Rome's mixture of consuls, senate and people arrested it, and judge where the argument fits Roman practice

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `decline-of-regimes`: Plato's chain of regimes (each
  regime breeding the man who destroys it, fathers and sons), the democratic
  man, and the mixed constitution as Laws III's moderated Sparta (concepts
  `regime-cycle`, `mixed-constitution`). From `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`:
  the six regimes sorted by number and by end (`six-regimes`), polity as a
  blend of oligarchy and democracy held by the middle class. From
  `sparta-as-test-case`: the Spartan constitution (two kings, gerousia,
  ephors, assembly), its stability and its failure as an imperial power
  (`spartan-constitution`). From `thucydides-power-and-justice`: the Athenian
  assembly that votes a massacre and reverses itself overnight (Mytilene), and
  Pericles's funeral speech as an account of what holds a city together. From
  `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`: the city built in speech. From
  `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`: Glaucon's story that justice is a bargain of the
  weak. The reader has met no Roman institution, no Roman date and no Roman
  author. `platos-laws-second-best-city` is `extra`: never assume it was read.
- **Contributes:** One move. A Greek who watched Rome conquer his world asks
  what made it possible and answers with a constitution, not an army: every
  simple regime decays by a natural, generational mechanism into its
  perversion and provokes the next (the anacyclosis); a constitution that
  gives each of the three ruling principles its own institution, each able to
  block the others, removes the opportunity for any one to grow into its
  vice — and Rome reached that mixture by trial and disaster where Sparta's
  lawgiver had reached it by reason. Where Aristotle's mixture blended
  classes, Polybius's distributes powers: the first account of checks and
  balances. The mixture only delays the cycle; Polybius himself forecasts how
  Rome will fall.
- **Hands off:** (1) Polybius's test of a constitution is success, above all
  in conquest (VI.50): nothing in the checks says whether Rome deserved its
  empire. A friend of the same Roman circle will define the commonwealth by
  agreement about right and then face the speech that Rome grew great by
  injustice — `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`. (2) Polybius's cycle is a
  law of nature with a fixed order; Cicero's will be a ball snatched in more
  than one direction, and Cicero will deny that a commonwealth has any natural
  death at all (Rep. III.34). (3) Three later uses, one forward clause each,
  no names in the body where the slug carries them: Machiavelli copies the
  cycle almost word for word in Discourses I.2 (`machiavelli-discourses-republic`);
  Bodin denies that power divided among three bodies is a state at all
  (`bodin-sovereignty`); the Federalist rebuilds checks and balances without
  estates and cites Polybius on Carthage in No. 63
  (`federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`). The growth–zenith–decay
  curve of VI.51 returns in `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government` and
  `spengler-decline-of-the-west` (both reinforce `anacyclosis`).
  What this lesson decides and does not pass on: the cycle as a natural law
  with a fixed order fails, and the VI.57 forecast is borne out in outline
  (both **strong**); the next lesson inherits the question Polybius never
  asked, whether the commonwealth that won was just.
- **Spine step:** "Rome turns Greek theory into practice: Polybius explains its
  rise by a mixed constitution that arrests the cycle of regimes, and Cicero
  defines the commonwealth as a people bound by agreed right under a natural
  law above every statute"

## VERDICT

Two claims are judged (research/verdicts/REGISTER.md, module 2, lesson
verdicts).

- **(a) The cycle of regimes as a natural law with a fixed order (VI.4–9) —
  fails. Grade: strong.** Decided by the cases the course has already given
  the reader. Athens went from Solon's reform to democracy, was overthrown
  by oligarchs in 411 and 404, and ended under Macedon, never passing
  through Polybius's order; Sparta kept its mixture for centuries and ended
  by defeat (Leuctra, 371), not by a station of the cycle; Rome reached its
  mixture, on Polybius's own account, by trial and disaster rather than by
  the cycle's sequence. The theory survives only if "stage" is read loosely,
  and read loosely it no longer predicts an order. Polybius's own hedge
  (ch. 9: the stage, if not the date) concedes the date and still claims the
  order, which is the part that fails.
- **(b) The forecast of VI.57, that Rome's mixture would decay through wealth,
  rivalry for office and a flattered people — borne out in outline. Grade:
  strong.** Decided by the record after Polybius wrote: a tribune (Tiberius
  Gracchus) carried a land law over the senate in 133 and was killed by
  senators; a century of rivalry for office followed (Marius from 107, Sulla
  82, Caesar 49) and ended in one man's rule (Augustus, 27). The route
  differs from the forecast: generals with armies loyal to them ended the
  republic, not mob-rule. The forecast names the causes; it misnames the
  last station.
- **Strongest objection:** the late Republic fell to armies, not to the
  internal decay Polybius describes, so his forecast hit by accident: the
  decisive cause (soldiers who owed their land to their general) is nowhere
  in VI.57. It does not overturn the verdict, because the armies were the
  instruments of the rivalry for office the forecast names: each general
  marched to win or keep office, and the land question that made the
  soldiers his was the question of 133. It narrows the hit to the causes,
  which is why the verdict says "in outline".
- **What would overturn it:** for (b), an account on which the late
  Republic's collapse owed nothing to rivalry for office; for (a), a body of
  ancient constitutions that passed through the six forms in Polybius's
  order.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: in 167 BC a Greek cavalry
commander is taken to Rome as a hostage, one of a thousand; he has watched the
Greek cities lose their freedom to a city that had nearly been destroyed by
Hannibal, and he wants to know how Rome did it so fast; his answer is the way
it is governed, not its legions; the lesson follows his account of how each
simple regime decays into the next and why he thinks Rome found a way out.

1. **Why look at a constitution to explain a conquest?** The obvious answers
   are the legions, luck, or Roman character. Polybius takes the question in
   the form a historian can test (ch. 1): the speed is "without precedent",
   and the cause of every policy, success or failure, is the constitution,
   "from this as from a fountain-head". His method: judge a constitution as
   you judge a man — in sudden success or catastrophe. Hence he describes
   Rome "at the period of … Cannae" (ch. 11), its worst day. Anchors (L11):
   Polybius of Megalopolis, the hostage of 167, Scipio Aemilianus's
   companion; Hannibal (the Carthaginian general who invaded Italy in 218);
   Cannae (216, south-east Italy, the worst defeat in Rome's history); the
   fifty-three years (220–167, from the eve of the Hannibalic war to the end
   of the Macedonian kingdom). A locator figure here. Because: if the
   constitution is the cause, a reader must first see what a constitution
   *does* over time — which is the cycle.
2. **Three forms, or six?** The reader arrives with Plato's chain and
   Aristotle's six (`<Ref>` `decline-of-regimes`, `aristotle-regimes-and-the-
   middle`, one clause each). Polybius's first move (ch. 3–4): the common list
   of three is wrong twice — not complete (one-man rule by fear is not
   kingship; the rule of a few rich is not aristocracy; a crowd doing what it
   likes is not democracy) and not the best (the best mixes all three). Then
   the criterion that separates each good form from its twin: consent and
   reason (king/despot), merit (aristocrats/oligarchs), law and piety
   (democracy/mob-rule). Make the reader notice the third: for Polybius
   "democracy" is defined by habits — reverence for gods, parents, elders,
   laws — not by who votes. Because: the transitions in link 3 are all
   failures of exactly these criteria. A table figure.
3. **Why does each form turn into the next?** The obvious answer — bad men
   seize power — is not Polybius's. His engine is *generational forgetting*
   (ch. 5–9). Walk the cycle once, as a step-through: (a) catastrophe leaves
   a herd and the strongest leads — despotism, the limit of whose power is
   physical strength; (b) the notion of duty is born (ch. 6): onlookers see a
   son injure the parents who raised him, reason that the same could happen
   to them, and are indignant; the leader who rewards and punishes as they
   feel is obeyed "from conviction of its utility" — a king; (c) his heirs,
   born to security, want different clothes, food and women — a tyrant; (d)
   the noblest conspire, the people follow, and the liberators rule well —
   aristocracy; (e) their sons, with "no experience of misfortunes" — an
   oligarchy; (f) the people, trusting no one, rule themselves and value
   equality while anyone remembers oligarchy — democracy; (g) the
   grandchildren, the rich among them, buy the people, who learn to live off
   others' property — mob-rule, massacre, redivision of land, and a master
   again. Every step has the same "because": the generation that suffered
   the last form's vice founds the next, and the generation that did not
   suffer it lets it decay. Contrast with Plato in one sentence (Plato's
   engine is the son's reaction against the father's character; Polybius's
   is the loss of the founders' experience) and with Glaucon in one sentence
   (`<Ref>` `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`: Glaucon's justice is a bargain struck
   from fear; Polybius's begins in sympathy and in reasoning about one's own
   case). Then the claim to predictive power (ch. 9): one may err on the
   dates but "will rarely be entirely mistaken as to the stage". That is a
   strong claim; flag it for the exercises.
4. **How can a cycle driven by nature be stopped?** If each simple form
   breeds its own vice "as rust is the natural dissolvent of iron" (ch. 10),
   no simple form can last, however good its founders. The answer is not a
   better simple form but a mixture in which "each power being checked by the
   others, no one part should turn the scale" — the ship "sailing close to
   the wind", held steady by opposing forces. Lycurgus's Sparta is the proof
   case (`<Ref>` `sparta-as-test-case`, one clause: kings, gerousia, ephors):
   the kings kept from insolence by fear of the people, the people from
   contempt of the kings by fear of the elders, the elders throwing their
   weight to whichever side was in the right. Then the sentence the lesson
   turns on: Lycurgus reached the mixture "by the light of reason";
   the Romans "through many struggles and difficulties, and by continually
   adopting reforms from knowledge gained in disaster". Because: a mixture
   designed by one wise man is possible but rare; one learned by a people
   from its own disasters is the version a historian can explain. (Burke will
   make this a principle; no name in the body.)
5. **Rome in three views — and why the mixture is new.** Ch. 11–14 as the
   observer paradox: look at the consuls and Rome is a monarchy (command,
   levies, punishment in the field, money); at the senate and it is an
   aristocracy (treasury, crimes in Italy, embassies — what most Greeks and
   kings saw); at the people and it is a democracy (the "sole fountain of
   honour and of punishment", elections, laws, war and peace, treaties,
   capital trials). "Not even a native" could say which. Then the move that
   makes Polybius more than a describer (ch. 15–17): each part *depends* on
   the others. The consul in the field needs the senate for grain, clothes,
   pay, a second year and a triumph, and the people for treaties and his
   accounting; the senate needs the people for death sentences and for any
   law touching senators, and stops dead if one tribune vetoes; the people
   need the senate, which controls the public contracts nearly every citizen
   has a stake in and supplies the jurors, and fear the consul under whom
   they will serve. Because: no part can act at the others' expense without
   being blocked, so no part can turn into its vice. Here state the contrast
   with Aristotle as the course's own: Aristotle's polity blends two classes
   and is held by the middle; Polybius gives each ruling *principle* an
   institution and makes them block one another — the first account of
   checks and balances (`checks-and-balances`; a before/after figure). One
   forward clause: the Federalist will rebuild the machine without kings or
   nobles.
6. **What the constitution cannot do alone: customs.** The seam: from link 5
   one would expect Polybius to rest his case on the machine; he does not.
   Ch. 47 makes customs and laws the "two things fundamental to every state",
   and the test of both is the private and public character they produce.
   Three Roman customs, each answering a question the reader already has:
   - *Who fights?* Citizens "for country and children", not mercenaries
     (ch. 52) — and no office before ten campaigns (ch. 19).
   - *What makes a man serve?* The funeral (ch. 53–54): the body propped on
     the Rostra, the son's speech, the ancestors' masks worn by men of the
     same build in the robes of the offices they held, seated on ivory chairs.
     Set it beside Pericles's funeral speech (`<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-
     justice`): Athens praises the city and names no one; Rome parades the
     named dead of one family and their offices. Two answers to "what holds a
     people together"; leave the comparison to the reader in one sentence.
   - *What keeps officials honest?* Religion (ch. 56), which Polybius treats as
     a device: a "scrupulous fear of the gods … keeps the Roman commonwealth
     together"; a state of philosophers would not need it; the multitude does.
     The Greek treasurer with ten clerks and twenty witnesses vs the Roman
     who keeps his oath. This is the course's first statement of religion as
     a civic instrument: plant it (it becomes Varro's civil theology in
     `constantine-and-representation` and Symmachus's case in
     `altar-of-victory`).
   Then the counter-cases that prove the rule (ch. 43–50): Thebes rose and
   fell with two men; Athens is "a ship without a commander", wrecked in
   harbour after riding out storms (`<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-justice`: the
   Mytilene vote and its reversal); Plato's Republic is barred from the
   contest, "a statue" beside "living and breathing men" (`<Ref>`
   `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`, one clause); Sparta is perfect for
   freedom and useless for empire — iron money cannot pay a fleet, so it
   ended "humbly at Persian doors" (the reader has this record from
   `sparta-as-test-case`; add only Polybius's verdict). And the set piece that
   closes the book (ch. 58): after Cannae the senate refuses to ransom eight
   thousand prisoners and returns the oath-cheating envoy in chains — the
   machine and the customs at work on the worst day.
7. **THE WEIGHING — "The cycle fails as a law; the forecast of decay holds
   in outline."** First the objections at full strength, then the verdict.
   Two objections to the mixture, the second Polybius's own.
   (a) *The mixture is not balanced.* Ch. 51 credits the Hannibalic victory to
   a senate "at the height of its power" against a Carthage where the people
   had become supreme — so the best moment of the mixture is a tilt toward
   the few. And "the people" voted in blocks weighted by wealth: in the
   centuriate assembly the knights and the first class held 89 of 193 votes
   (Cicero, Rep. II.xxii; say once, and that Cicero approved). Assemblies
   met only when a magistrate summoned them and voted on what he proposed.
   On this reading Rome is an aristocracy with popular forms — the argument
   is the course's own; Holton (Strauss–Cropsey) in References. Polybius's
   reply is in his own text: honour and punishment, the fountain of all
   order, are the people's (ch. 14), and the tribunes can stop the senate
   from meeting (ch. 16). Let both stand.
   (b) *The mixture only delays.* Ch. 57: the same constitution will decay
   by the internal route — wealth, extravagance, office-seeking, a people
   "cheated by some … flattered by others" that demands everything, a regime
   that calls itself "liberty or democracy" and is mob-rule. The record: a
   generation after Polybius wrote, a tribune (Tiberius Gracchus, 133)
   carried a land law over the senate and was killed by senators — the
   tribunate, which Polybius mentions once as a veto, became the lever of
   the conflict his checks were supposed to absorb. The lesson decides it:
   the forecast is borne out in outline (**strong**). One sentence of dates
   carries the sequel — Marius from 107, Sulla 82, Caesar 49, Augustus 27 —
   a century of rivalry for office ending in one man's rule; then say where
   the forecast missed: generals with armies loyal to them ended the
   republic, not mob-rule. Set the strongest objection (the armies, not the
   decay, did it) against the answer (the armies were the instruments of the
   rivalry for office, and the soldiers' land was the question of 133). No
   narrative of the Gracchi or the civil wars.
   (b′) *The cycle as law, judged.* Run Polybius's ch. 9 claim against the
   three cases the reader holds (Athens, Sparta, Rome; exercise 6 rehearses
   it): none follows his order, and the claim survives only with "stage"
   read loosely, at which point it predicts no order. Verdict in the
   course's voice: as a natural law with a fixed order the cycle fails
   (**strong**); the generational engine (each generation forgets the vice
   its fathers suffered) remains a usable account of why a simple regime
   decays, which is what the mixture was built against.
   (c) A forward clause only: later writers will deny that power shared by
   three "sovereign" bodies (ch. 11's own word) can be a state, because when
   they deadlock no one decides (`bodin-sovereignty`).
8. **Close** (`<Conclusion>`): the reader can now run the cycle with its
   generational engine, state why a mixture of blocking powers arrests it,
   say how Polybius's mixture differs from Aristotle's, and name the two
   limits — the tilt toward the senate and the forecast of decay. Then the
   verdict in plain words, with its grades: the cycle does not hold as a law
   of nature, since no city the course has examined followed its order
   (strong); Polybius's forecast of how Rome's mixture would decay held in
   outline, wrong only about the last station, an army's general rather than
   a mob (strong). Hand-off, one sentence: Polybius judged the constitution by what it won;
   whether a commonwealth must also be just — and whether Rome was — is the
   question the next lesson's Roman puts in the same circle of friends
   (`cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`).

Headings that fall out (questions or noun phrases, the writer's choice):
"A conquest explained by a constitution"; "Six forms, not three"; "How each
form breeds the next"; "Rust in iron: why no simple form lasts"; "Rome seen
three ways"; "Each part needs the others"; "Customs: the army, the funeral,
the oath"; "The cycle fails as a law; the forecast of decay holds in outline". About 30–35% of the

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The cycle as a step-through** (ch. 5–9, lines 110–301). Seven frames,
   one transition each, each labelled with Polybius's trigger in his words
   (the strongest leads; ingratitude observed; heirs "gave the rein to their
   appetites"; the noblest conspire; sons with "no experience of
   misfortunes"; grandchildren buying office; "a master and a despot"). The
   reader should be able to restate the engine in one sentence after it.
2. **The birth of duty** (ch. 6, lines 151–174). Quote the core in two short
   pieces: onlookers "reason on the likelihood of the same occurring to each
   of themselves"; "Hence arises a notion in every breast of the meaning and
   theory of duty, which is in fact the beginning and end of justice." Work
   it as an Example: premise (only man reasons about the future), case (the
   ungrateful son), inference (indignation as prudence by proxy), result
   (duty; then honour and disgrace; then kingship by consent). Set Glaucon's
   bargain beside it in one sentence.
3. **Rust and woodworm; the ship close to the wind** (ch. 10, lines 317–332).
   Two images, two claims: decay comes from inside each form; stability comes
   from opposed forces held in tension, not from the absence of force.
4. **The observer paradox** (ch. 11–14, lines 393–493): "not even a native"
   could say what Rome is. A three-panel figure; then the Example the reader
   produces: classify five powers (levy troops, control the treasury, try a
   capital case, declare war, receive embassies) by element.
5. **A consul's year, worked** (ch. 15–17, lines 494–582). Follow one consul
   from election to the end of office and mark each point at which another
   part could stop him: the people elect; the senate votes supplies and may
   replace him after a year; the senate grants or refuses the triumph; the
   people ratify his treaty and hear his accounts. Then the same for the
   senate (death sentences, the tribune's veto) and for the people (contracts,
   jurors). This is the lesson's main worked example; the checks figure is
   built from it.
6. **The funeral with the masks** (ch. 53–54, lines 1662–1727), told in four
   sentences, then Polybius's reason: the fame of brave men "never allowed to
   die", and young men who "shrink from no exertion for the general welfare".
   Paired with Pericles's speech (Ref) as two answers to what holds a people
   together.
7. **The treasurer and the ten clerks** (ch. 56, lines 1770–1790) beside "a
   state wholly of philosophers". Religion as an instrument, stated by a
   Greek admirer without disapproval: the reader must see that this is a
   claim about function, and that Polybius does not say the gods are real.
8. **The Cannae prisoners** (ch. 58, lines 1832–1880): eight thousand men,
   three minae each, the envoy who went back into the camp "under the belief
   that by means of this return he had kept his promise" and was sent back in
   chains. The book's own demonstration, chosen by its author "as a specimen
   of the skill of a good artist".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Terms a newcomer will not know — anchor at first mention (L11), no more
  than three per section:** anacyclosis (Greek "cycling round"; Polybius's own word at VI.9.10,
  which Shuckburgh renders "the regular cycle of constitutional
  revolutions", line 296; define once, then use "the cycle"); ochlocracy / mob-rule
  (Shuckburgh's "mob-rule"; keep his English); despotism vs tyranny (the
  first is rule by strength before any idea of justice; the second is a king
  gone bad — Polybius keeps them apart); consul (one of two chief magistrates
  elected yearly, commanding the armies); senate (a council of about three
  hundred former magistrates, sitting for life); tribune of the plebs (an
  officer of the common people whose veto could stop any act, including a
  meeting of the senate — the reader has never met one); censor (magistrate
  who let the public contracts and revised the citizen lists); quaestor
  (treasury officer); triumph (the victory procession the senate could grant
  or refuse); Rostra (the speakers' platform in the Forum, named for the
  ships' beaks fixed to it); imagines (the wax ancestor masks); fustuarium
  (death by cudgelling at the hands of one's comrades) and decimation (the
  lot falling on one man in ten); Achaean League (the federation of
  Peloponnesian cities Polybius served); Pydna (168, Macedon's defeat);
  Lycurgus (the legendary Spartan lawgiver — the reader has him from
  `sparta-as-test-case`); talent and minae (sums of money: one sentence of
  scale or none). The Gracchi and the sequel to 133 (Marius, Sulla, Caesar,
  Augustus) only in link 7: one sentence of dates, no narrative.
- **Polybius is the author of the material**: "Polybius argues" is allowed.
  Names that go only in References: Shuckburgh (translator), Holton and
  Berns (Strauss–Cropsey), Smith, Panaetius (unless Rep. I.34 is quoted, where
  he is a character in Cicero's text). Machiavelli, Cicero, Plato, Aristotle,
  Thucydides, Pericles and Lycurgus are material or objects and may be named.
- **Do not re-teach** Plato's chain, Aristotle's six regimes or polity, or the
  Spartan institutions: each is a `<Ref>` plus a clause. The new things are
  the generational engine, the blocking powers, and customs as the second
  foundation.
- **Do not teach Cicero here.** Rep. I.34, II.27 and II.22 are used as
  evidence (Polybius in Scipio's circle; the centuriate weights). The
  definition of a people, the ball, Carneades — all next lesson.
- **Shuckburgh's sidenotes** ("The origin of the social compact", "Origin of
  morality") are the translator's headings, not Polybius's words; "social
  compact" in particular is not his concept. Never quote a sidenote as text.
- **Polybius describes Rome "at the epoch of Cannae" (216) and writes in the
  150s–140s**, adding that the powers "with slight modifications, still are"
  (ch. 11). He is describing a past state of the constitution from inside
  the Roman elite's circle, for Greek readers. Use this in the sourcing
  exercise; do not hide it.
- **The ethnic remark in ch. 52** — "Italians as a nation are by nature
  superior to Phoenicians and Libyans both in physical strength and courage"
  (lines 1647–1650) — is in the text and in the very chapter this lesson uses.
  Do not delete it from a quotation that runs through it; if quoted, note in
  one clause that Polybius immediately adds that "their habits also do much"
  and spends the following chapters on institutions, so his explanation of
  Rome's victory does not rest on nature. No comment in either direction
  beyond that; the course's heredity module takes such claims up as
  arguments.
- **Religion as a device** (ch. 56) is Polybius's functional claim, not a
  claim that the Romans were cynics. Do not attribute disbelief to the
  Romans; attribute the functional reading to Polybius.
- **"First account of checks and balances"** (course.yaml key result): fair
  as a claim about an explicit theory of mutually blocking institutions.
  Sparta's balance in Plato's Laws III is a precursor; say that the reader
  has seen a mixture before (`decline-of-regimes`) and that what is new is
  the mechanism of mutual dependence.
- **The opening's arithmetic.** Polybius's "fifty-three years" run from about
  220 to 167 (the end of the Macedonian kingdom); Hannibal invaded in 218 and
  Cannae was 216. The opening (verbatim, unchanged) says the city had been
  nearly destroyed "fifty-three years earlier"; in the body give the span
  220–167 and Cannae 216 without contradicting the opening's sense (see the
  final report's note on this sentence).
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — 167 (the hostages), 216 (Cannae, the day the
  constitution is described at), the fifty-three years. 89 of 193 centuries
  appears once, framed, in the objection. Everything else to the decade.
- **Dossier thinness:** Book VI only; no other Polybius book is staged, so his
  life and his presence at Carthage in 146 are general knowledge held to half
  a sentence each. No modern study of the Roman constitution is in the
  dossier: institutional glosses stay at the level Polybius himself gives.
  Strauss–Cropsey has no Polybius chapter.
- **Not a lesson on the Roman army.** A third of Book VI is the legion and the
  camp. Use ch. 19, 37–38 and 52 only, as customs that shape character.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (the one warm-up).* Write the seven stations of the cycle in
   order and, beside each arrow, the generation that makes the change and
   what it has forgotten.
2. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill on ch. 11–18.* Metadata first: a Greek
   hostage of the Roman elite, friend of Scipio Aemilianus, writing in the
   150s–140s for Greek readers, describing the constitution as it stood in
   216. Predict what such a writer will stress and omit; then read ch. 11's
   "not even a native" and ch. 16's single mention of the tribunes. What can
   the passage be USED to establish — how the powers were formally
   distributed, or how they were used? Name one thing it cannot establish
   (e.g. how often the people overrode the senate) and what source would.
3. *Boundary probe.* Polybius's engine is generational forgetting. Imagine a
   founding generation that never dies (or a people that keeps its memory of
   each vice perfectly, as the funeral speeches try to). Which transitions
   still happen, which stop, and what does that show about what the mixture
   actually removes — the motive, or the opportunity?
4. *Claim to refute.* "Polybius's mixed constitution is Aristotle's polity
   with a king added." Name the step that fails (`<Ref>`
   `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`): what each mixes (classes vs
   principles), what holds it (a middle class vs mutual dependence), how
   many institutions carry it; and give one Roman power from ch. 15–17 that
   has no counterpart in a polity.
5. *Paired sources — corroboration (E3 reach-back).* Polybius calls the
   Athenian demos "a ship without a commander", wrecked in harbour (ch. 44).
   Set this beside the Mytilene debate (`<Ref>` `thucydides-power-and-
   justice`): the assembly votes a massacre and reverses it the next day.
   Does the episode support Polybius's image or tell against it (an
   assembly correcting itself in time)? State what each reading must assume.
6. *Research-shaped task.* Polybius claims (ch. 9) that his cycle predicts
   the stage of a constitution, if not the date. Design a test using cases
   the course has given you (Athens from Solon to the Thirty; Sparta; Rome to
   the Gracchi): what would count as a confirming case, what as a
   falsifying one, and which of the three cases fits worst? The solution
   shows that the claim survives only if "stage" is read loosely, and says
   what that costs the theory.
7. *Causal ranking.* For the Roman recovery after Cannae, rank as necessary /
   enabling / precipitating three causes Polybius gives — the mutual
   dependence of the parts (ch. 18), the citizen army (ch. 52), the oath and
   the senate's refusal to ransom (ch. 56, 58). Defend the ranking from the
   text; the solution notes that Polybius's own closing illustration is a
   custom, not a check.
8. *Attack the verdict.* The lesson judges Polybius's forecast of VI.57
   borne out in outline. State the strongest objection in its best form —
   that the republic was ended by armies loyal to their generals, a cause
   VI.57 never names, so the "hit" is a coincidence of outcome — and say
   what evidence would be needed to overturn the verdict: an account of
   133–27 on which the collapse owed nothing to rivalry for office (for
   example, generals who seized power with no office at stake). The
   solution grants the objection its force on the route and shows why it
   leaves the causes standing.
9. **FORWARD (E7)** → `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`. Polybius's test of
   a constitution is what it achieves, and by that test Rome beats Sparta
   because it can conquer (ch. 50). Construct the strongest objection a
   philosopher could make to that test using only Rome's record as Polybius
   gives it, and say what a definition of a commonwealth would have to
   contain for the objection to count against Rome. The solution says: the
   next lesson's Roman defines the commonwealth by agreement about right —
   and then has to hear a Greek's speech that Rome grew great by injustice.

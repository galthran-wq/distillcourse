# Research brief: pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis (Pomeranz and Goody: the great divergence and the theft of history)

status: planned
module: why-the-west
type: core
goal: Analyze the rival thesis that Europe's lead came late, from coal and colonies (Pomeranz), and that the story of Western uniqueness misreads a long Eurasian exchange (Goody), and evaluate it against the exceptionalist case

Register rule (binding on the lesson): The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.

The rival thesis is stated as its authors state it: an account of when and how Europe diverged, and a claim about how the West's history was written. It is never phrased as a charge against Europe or against the reader ("Europe stole", "the West owes", "denial"), and the exceptionalist reply is given at the same strength.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `weber-only-in-the-west`: the list of what existed "only in the West" (rational
  science, the Press and periodicals, double-entry capitalism, the trained official) and the
  Protestant ethic as its cause — a claim that the difference is old and cultural. From
  `landes-european-exceptionalism`: European fragmentation (exit, bargaining rulers, property
  secure from the prince), the invention of invention, Zheng He and the Ottoman printing ban as
  test cases, "culture makes all the difference". From `murray-human-accomplishment`: the
  accomplishment inventory, Europe's ~97 percent share of significant figures in the scientific
  inventories, and the 1400–1950 concentration. From `henrich-church-and-kinship`: the Church's
  Marriage and Family Program from c. 500 and a WEIRD psychology in place by c. 1500 — a cause
  that, if right, acts centuries before 1800. From `greek-miracle`: the Greeks themselves credited
  Egypt and Babylon (Goody's antiquity argument builds on that seam). From
  `what-measures-greatness`: the three measures of greatness and the question "one civilization or
  many".
- **Contributes:** The one move: the rival thesis at full strength, in two parts that support each
  other. (1) A dating claim with evidence: the most advanced regions of Eurasia (the Yangzi delta,
  England and the Netherlands, the Kinai and Kanto plains of Japan) were alike in markets,
  living standards and ecological strain as late as c. 1750; the gap opened after 1800 and was
  made by two windfalls — coal sited beside British industry, and the land of the New World —
  so a late, contingent divergence needs no ancient cultural cause. (2) A historiographical claim:
  the categories in which the exceptionalist story is told (antiquity, feudalism, capitalism, the
  free city, democracy, even romantic love) were defined with Europe in view and then found
  missing elsewhere; looked for without that frame, each has Eurasian parallels, and leads
  alternate. Then the exceptionalist reply, from the module's own earlier lessons and from the
  economic historians who re-measured the numbers.
- **Hands off:** The concrete record the two sides argue over in the abstract — which techniques
  actually moved across Eurasia, with what evidence, and what Europe did with them — is
  `the-borrowing-ledger` (the module's application). The colonial half of Pomeranz's thesis (the
  New World as "ghost acres", the slave plantation as a new kind of periphery) is a forward Ref to
  `colonial-balance-sheet`, where the colonial ledger is weighed. Whether nations differ for
  reasons of heredity, geography or institutions is the next module (`hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`,
  `geography-or-institutions`).
- **Spine step:** "Why the West? Weber, Landes, Murray and Henrich argue for Western exceptionalism from religion, fragmentation, accomplishment and kinship; Pomeranz and Goody answer that the West was one link in a long chain of exchange; the borrowing ledger tests both"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): if Europe had been ahead since the Greeks or the Reformation,
its lead should show in the numbers by 1700; a historian of China compares the Yangzi delta
with England around 1750 and finds them close; an English anthropologist adds that most of
what Europeans called their unique heritage had parallels across Eurasia. Anchors at first
mention (L11): the Yangzi delta (the lower Yangzi around Suzhou, Hangzhou and Nanjing — some 31
to 37 million people c. 1750, more than any European state; a locator sketch); the Qing dynasty
(ruling China 1644–1912); Kenneth Pomeranz (historian of China, book 2000); Jack Goody
(Cambridge social anthropologist who did fieldwork among the LoDagaa of northern Ghana, book
2006). The newcomer's problem, stated from the preceding four lessons: every exceptionalist
answer so far (Weber's ethic, Landes's fragmentation, Murray's counts, Henrich's kinship) puts
the cause centuries before the industrial take-off; a cause that old predicts a lead that old.
Is there one?

1. **Where would an early lead show, and what should be compared?** Obvious attempt: compare
   Europe with China. Why it fails: a continent against an empire averages London with the
   Russian steppe and Suzhou with Gansu. Pomeranz's answer is reciprocal comparison of like
   cores — England against the Yangzi delta — and asking each question both ways: not only why
   the delta was not England, but "Why wasn't England the Yangzi Delta?" (Intro p. 13). Because
   the units are comparable, a difference found is a difference, not an artefact of the map.
2. **What do the measures show c. 1750?** Obvious expectation (from Landes, Ref): Europe richer,
   healthier, freer. The record as Pomeranz assembles it: life expectancy in Japanese villages
   and the Qing imperial lineage at or above English and French figures; calories per adult male
   in China comparable to English labourers; Asian families controlling fertility as Europeans
   did; Chinese sugar consumption c. 1750 likely above continental Europe's, cotton cloth per
   head in the delta comparable to Britain's in 1800 (ch. 1 pp. 36–41; ch. 3 pp. 117–142).
   Answer: on these measures the cores were alike. KEEPER: "close until about 1750–1800". Say the
   seam (L10b): from four exceptionalist lessons one expects a visible gap by 1700; on these
   measures there is none.
3. **Then Europe's institutions — were its markets freer?** Obvious attempt: yes — property safe
   from the prince, free labour, mobile land (Landes, Ref). Pomeranz's reversal (ch. 2 p. 70):
   eighteenth-century China came closer to the textbook market than western Europe. Because:
   about 93% of Chinese land was freely alienable while about half of English land was tied in
   family settlements and much of continental land in common-field obligations; bound labour was
   negligible in the delta by 1700 while serfdom and seigneurial dues survived in Europe; Qing
   policy aimed at many competing grain buyers. Concede what he concedes: migration toward
   capital was easier in Europe; Europe's genuine institutional edge lay in armed, chartered,
   monopolistic long-distance trade (ch. 4) — an advantage in conquest and colonisation, not in
   home markets.
4. **If everyone was alike, why did anyone break out?** The constraint: all four of Malthus's
   necessities — food, fibre, fuel, building material — come from land, so every core c. 1800 was
   pressing on its land (deforestation in France, the Danish "labour-intensive" path, Lingnan's
   falling forest; ch. 5). Labour and capital could substitute for land only weakly before
   fossil fuel. The way out had to relieve land itself. Two windfalls did, in Britain only:
   (a) coal that lay beside navigable water, skilled artisans and a firewood-short market, where
   steam pumps made deep mining pay (China's lay in the north-west, far from the delta); (b) the
   New World, whose plantations and forests supplied cotton, sugar and timber that would have
   needed 25–30 million acres of British land c. 1830 — more than the country's 17 million arable
   acres. Define *ghost acres* here. KEEPER: "ghost acres exceeded Britain's arable". The
   cotton famine of 1861–65 is the check: when American cotton stopped, no substitute filled the
   gap (ch. 6 pp. 277–278). Answer: the great divergence (define the concept `great-divergence`
   here, with its post-1800 date) — Europe as a "fortunate freak" (p. 206), a none-too-unusual
   economy until a conjuncture of coal and colonies let it escape a constraint everyone shared.
   Note the colonial side is the empire module's question (`colonial-balance-sheet`, forward
   Ref) and do not weigh its morality here.
5. **What follows for the exceptionalist chain?** Pomeranz does not deny European science, but
   holds that knowledge without the resource relief would have been swallowed by population
   growth (p. 68), and that elite culture and "capitalism" separate western Europe from China and
   Japan no better than from each other (Part 2 intro pp. 112–113). Answer: if the divergence is
   late and contingent, causes placed in 500 or 1517 are not needed to explain it — they might
   still be true, but this gap is not their evidence.
6. **Goody: is the question itself framed fairly?** Problem: the exceptionalist story is told in
   a sequence — antiquity, feudalism, Renaissance, capitalism — and asks why others did not pass
   through it. Goody's claim (Intro; ch. 1): the sequence was defined from European history and
   then used as the yardstick, so others are found lacking by construction — "We had it later,
   therefore we had it earlier" (p. 81). Work three cases, one each, each as problem → test →
   finding: (a) democracy — the Greeks invented the word and the written theory, but assemblies
   and elected magistrates existed elsewhere (Carthage's annual suffetes; acephalous peoples
   without chiefs) (ch. 2); (b) the free city and the university — towns elsewhere needed no
   lord's charter; higher schools in Constantinople (863) and the madrasa paralleled the
   university point for point (ch. 8); (c) "Asiatic despotism" — the Ottoman state had a
   standing paid army two centuries before the Habsburgs (ch. 4). Then the positive model:
   Eurasia shared a Bronze Age base (plough, cities, writing), developed in parallel, exchanged
   continuously, and leads alternated; China led in many techniques until c. 1600 (the Needham
   table, Ref forward to `the-borrowing-ledger`); "the east did not need the same rebirth since it
   did not have the same death" (p. 296). Define `theft-of-history` here — stating that it is
   Goody's own term for a way of writing history, not a claim about goods taken. One plain
   sentence also: Goody warns against being "eurocentric about ethnocentricity" — every
   civilization tells its history from its own centre, so the point is method, not blame.
7. **What does the rival thesis concede?** Make this a section of its own; it is the thesis's
   honest boundary and where the reader can compare the two sides. Goody: the West "obviously
   displayed" superiority "in some spheres since the nineteenth century"; the Greeks did invent
   the word and the theory; the alphabet was a literacy advantage. Pomeranz: Europe may have led
   in some technologies by 1750, above all instruments; Britain's coal know-how was real; armed
   trade was a European institutional edge. So the rival thesis denies a permanent,
   culture-given lead, not any lead at all: the West as one link — at times the strongest — in a
   long chain of exchange.
8. **OBJECTION at full strength (the exceptionalist reply), and what each side answers.** Three
   strands, each stated as the course's own argument (names in References):
   (a) *The numbers.* Later reconstructions of wages and national accounts (Broadberry–Gupta
   2006; Allen et al. 2011; Broadberry–Guan–Li 2018) find silver and grain wages in the delta
   well below England's and the Netherlands' by the 1700s, and China's output per head behind
   theirs earlier still — so the gap opens before coal and colonies. Pomeranz's reply (JAS 2002
   exchange; his own appendices): wage series compare unlike workers and miss women's textile
   earnings, which on his estimates out-earned male farm labour (App. E). The disagreement is
   about data, and the lesson shows where each side's numbers come from: Pomeranz's from 1930s
   farm surveys, household budgets and deliberately conservative estimates; his critics' from
   price and wage series in archives.
   (b) *Why only Britain could use the windfalls.* Spain and Portugal had the New World first;
   China had coal; neither industrialised. A windfall is used only by a society that can turn it
   into machines — which returns the question to knowledge and its institutions: the invention
   of invention and the fragmentation that let no one stop it (Ref `landes-european-exceptionalism`),
   the concentration of scientific figures in Europe from 1400 (Ref `murray-human-accomplishment`
   — the ~97 percent share and the challenge to name the missing non-European "firsts"), a
   psychology in place by 1500 (Ref `henrich-church-and-kinship`). The lead, on this reply, was
   in knowledge long before it was in income.
   (c) *Parallels are not equivalents.* That other societies had assemblies, schools or merchants
   does not show they had the same institution: Goody's own authority on the madrasa stresses
   that the university was a self-governing corporation and the madrasa a charitable trust tied
   to its founder's terms; political philosophy itself — argued, written, transmitted theory of
   who should rule — is the Greek difference Goody concedes (Ref `greek-miracle`). Goody's
   answer: such differences are technical, not categorical, and selecting the one that later
   mattered is the teleology he names. The lesson does not decide; it states what evidence would
   decide each strand (wage series that compare like workers; a case where coal or colonies
   without the knowledge institutions produced industry; a comparison of institutions by function
   rather than name).
   Seam to close: both sides agree Europe borrowed heavily before 1500 — Landes's own sentence
   "Europe was a learner" (via Murray ch. 11) — so the dispute moves to the record of particular
   borrowings: `the-borrowing-ledger`.

Headings as claims: "Compare like with like: England and the Yangzi delta"; "In 1750 the cores
were alike"; "China's markets were nearer the textbook than Europe's"; "Every core was running
out of land"; "Coal and the New World: the ghost acres"; "The categories were drawn with
Europe in view"; "What the rival thesis concedes"; "The exceptionalist reply: numbers, knowledge,
institutions".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), at most three per section:** Yangzi delta (Jiangnan) and Lingnan
  (Guangdong–Guangxi) with a locator; Qing; Kinai/Kanto (only if used — prefer "Japan's central
  plains"); "core region" vs "periphery" (define by example); "proto-industry" (rural cottage
  manufacture for distant markets — only if used); Malthus (English economist, 1798, population
  outruns food) — a clause; "ghost acres"; "neoclassical market" (one sentence: a market where
  land, labour and goods are freely bought and sold); "family settlement"/entail (a legal
  arrangement that kept an estate in the family and off the market — define in a clause; do not
  Ref Burke's `entailed-inheritance`, which is his metaphor for the constitution, not the land
  law); the Lancashire cotton famine (1861–65, the American Civil War
  blockade); "feudalism", "antiquity" as period labels; suffetes (Carthage's two annually elected
  chief magistrates); Janissaries (the Ottoman sultan's standing infantry from the 14th c.);
  madrasa (Islamic college endowed as a charitable trust, *waqf*); "teleology" (reading history
  as aimed at its outcome) — define in plain words, then use sparingly.
- **Do not re-teach** Weber, Landes, Murray, Henrich: one clause and a `<Ref>` each, at the point
  where the rival thesis meets them. The module's earlier lessons supply the exceptionalist side;
  this lesson's new matter is the rival thesis and the exchange.
- **The phrasing rule.** "Theft" is Goody's metaphor for a way of writing history; the lesson
  says so at first use and never extends it ("Europe stole", "appropriated from") in its own
  voice. The rival thesis is a claim about evidence and dating, not an accusation; the
  exceptionalist reply is a claim about evidence and causes, not a defence of honour. The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Present the contested as contested.** The dating of the divergence is an open empirical
  dispute (the 2006–2018 wage and GDP reconstructions vs Pomeranz). Do not state "the gap opened
  after 1800" as settled; state whose evidence says so and whose says otherwise.
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — "alike until c. 1750–1800"; "ghost acres 25–30 million vs ~17
  million arable (c. 1830)"; "about 93% of Chinese land freely saleable vs about half of
  English". Life-expectancy and calorie series go into one figure, not prose. Do not quote
  Pomeranz's tael budgets in the body (App. E is exercise material).
- **Real misreadings:** (1) "Pomeranz says Europe got rich by plunder." He argues the New
  World mattered as ecological relief — land and energy — not as profit or treasure (Intro
  p. 23); the slave plantation enters as the institution that made that periphery keep
  expanding. State it accurately, once. (2) "Goody says Europe contributed nothing." He concedes
  the nineteenth-century superiority and the Greek theory; his claim is about periodisation and
  permanence. One `<Remark>` handles both (L13 schema for (2) only if the writer judges it a
  prevalent misreading; otherwise plain prose).
- **Where the dossier is thin:** Goody's ch. 9 and parts of ch. 10 carry 2003–05 opinion on
  Iraq and Guantanamo (pp. 244–247) — period argument, not material for this lesson; leave out.
  The exceptionalist numbers that answer Pomeranz (2006–2018) are not in the dossier; the
  writer must obtain the papers named above before stating their dates. Goody's evidence is
  often second-hand and asserted (summary 83 `pedagogy`); quote him where he argues, not where he
  asserts.
- **Copyright:** both books in copyright — a sentence at most per quotation, paraphrase the rest.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (the one warm-up):** place on one timeline the dates each
   exceptionalist cause in the module acts (Henrich's Church program from c. 500; the Reformation
   1517; Landes's medieval invention of invention; Murray's 1400–1950 concentration) and the
   date Pomeranz gives for the divergence; state which causes the late date makes unnecessary
   and which it leaves possible.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** Pomeranz's sugar estimate for China c. 1750
   (ch. 3 pp. 120–123: built from cash-crop acreage × share in cane × yield, then divided by
   population). Metadata first; what the estimate records, argues, omits; what it can be used to
   establish (that Chinese sugar consumption c. 1750 was probably no lower than continental Europe's) and
   what it cannot (a precise per-head figure); how his stated rule of biasing assumptions against
   his own thesis changes its use.
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Pomeranz shows that Europe grew rich from colonial plunder." Name
   the step that fails (he values the New World as land and energy, not treasure; the silver
   flowed largely to China) and state what his argument would need instead for "plunder" to be
   right.
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** remove one windfall. Using ch. 1 p. 68 and ch. 6, say what Pomeranz
   predicts for Britain with coal but no New World, and with the New World but no coal, and which
   real case each counterfactual resembles (Denmark's labour-intensive path; Spain). The solution
   shows that the thesis needs both, and that this is exactly where the exceptionalist "only
   Britain could use them" reply bites.
5. **Corroboration (paired sources):** Goody on the madrasa and the university (ch. 8 pp. 227–232:
   fifteen parallels, and the corporation/trust distinction he reports) against Murray's challenge
   (ch. 11) to name non-European "firsts" with the same microscope. State where they agree (both
   accept the institutions existed), where they disagree (whether the difference is categorical),

6. **Research-shaped task (E6d):** design the test that would separate the two datings: which

7. **Synthesis / counter-argument:** "The rival thesis concedes everything that matters, because

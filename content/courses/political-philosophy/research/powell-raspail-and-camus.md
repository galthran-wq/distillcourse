# Research brief: powell-raspail-and-camus (Powell, Raspail and Camus: prophecies of displacement)

status: planned
module: nation-and-immigration
type: core
goal: Analyze Powell's 1968 speech, Raspail's Camp of the Saints and Renaud Camus's 'great replacement' as arguments about immigration and the future of European peoples, and test their predictions against the demographic record

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `albions-seed-and-who-are-we`: Huntington's distinction between
  "immigration with or without assimilation", his six differentia of a concentrated,
  continuous migration, and the claim that a culture, not a creed, holds a people; from
  `america-and-the-english-decline`: the four premises of the assembled decline thesis, one of
  which — immigration nobody chose — this lesson isolates and checks (the previous lesson's
  FORWARD exercise posed Powell's forecast). From `the-nation-and-its-defenders`: the
  pre-political "we" (`pre-political-loyalty`) and `oikophilia`. From `renan-what-is-a-nation`
  and `barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism`: the nation as daily plebiscite against the
  nation as "the earth and the dead" (`daily-plebiscite`) — Raspail's *Français de souche* and
  Camus's "deep murmur" stand on Barrès's side. From `chamberlain-and-grant`: Grant's 1916
  forecast that the "native American" stock was being displaced — "a replacement pure and
  simple" — and `nordicism`: the argument form's earlier career. From
  `burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`: guilt and liberalism as the ideology that
  reconciles the West to retreat (Raspail's novel dramatises the same claim nine years later).
  From `the-right-after-1945`: why, after 1945, any argument about peoples and descent met the
  memory of the camps — the frame of the reactions to all three texts.
- **Contributes:** One move: three prophecies of displacement — a speech (1968), a novel
  (1973) and a polemic (2011–2018) — each reconstructed as an argument with premises and a
  forecast (`prophecy-of-displacement`), and each forecast checked against the record that
  now exists: the British and French censuses and statistical series, the 1985 Figaro
  projection, the 2015 crossings. The lesson separates in each text four kinds of claim — the
  demographic (checkable), the causal or intentional (checkable only by evidence of intent),
  the normative (whether the change is a loss, and to whom), and the rhetorical — and teaches
  the reader what a demographic projection can and cannot establish. `replacement-as-argument`
  is introduced here: the claim taught as an argument with its history, its evidence and the
  evidence against it, and then judged claim by claim — the descriptive claim and the
  intentional claim each get their own verdict (## VERDICT).
- **Hands off:** (i) `carens-open-borders` (next): the opposite starting point — the
  individual's freedom to move, from which a people's wish to remain itself gives no right to
  exclude. Raspail's own dilemma (refusing is racism, accepting is the end of identity) is the
  hinge. (ii) `miller-strangers-in-our-midst`: a liberal right to control membership without
  any premise about descent. (iii) `murray-strange-death-of-europe`: Murray recalls this
  lesson's material (he quotes the Figaro cover and names Camus's term as "an experienced
  fear") and adds his own premise, the "tiredness" of the receiving civilization; his lesson
  reads the projections again for what they do and do not show. (iv)
  `the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways`: the year Raspail's million arrived, by another route and
  with another outcome; it now carries the module's four-part verdict (procedure, security,
  principle, integration). (v) `de-benoist-european-new-right`: the "right to difference" as a
  different answer to the same fear. What this lesson decides and what passes on: every
  checkable forecast of the three texts is judged here (## VERDICT); Powell's consent claim —
  that the people were "never consulted" — is open here and is decided in
  `murray-strange-death-of-europe` ("never chosen": not intended or forecast in scale, strong;
  but chosen in its instruments, strong).

## VERDICT

The lesson judges each prophecy claim by claim, in its own voice, with these grades.

**Powell (1968).**
- *His numbers:* **right in direction and roughly in size, later than forecast. Strong.** About
  4.6 million non-White in the UK in 2001, against his 5–7 million for 2000; England and Wales
  18.3 percent not White in 2021. The category mismatch (his "Commonwealth immigrants and their
  descendants" against the census's self-assigned ethnic group) is stated beside the figures.
- *His bloodshed and domination ("the whip hand", the Tiber):* **not borne out in the form he
  gave them. Strong.** The record of ethnic disorder (1981, 1985, 2001) is stated beside this
  verdict, not left out of it.
- *His consent claim* ("a decision by default, on which they were never consulted"): **open
  here**, carried to the Murray lesson, where "never chosen" is decided.

**Raspail (1973).**
- The scale arrived in 2015 by another route (about a million crossings in a year, in small
  boats, mostly to Greece), and for months the core states did not refuse — the nearest the
  record came to his scenario.
- *The strong form* ("the West will not refuse"): **failed. Strong.** Australia's turn-backs
  from 2013, the Hungarian fence in September 2015, the EU–Turkey statement of March 2016.
- *The 2045–50 claim* (half of under-55s in the urban zones of extra-European origin):
  **pending** — not yet checkable; the trajectory is stated from TeO2.
- *The 1985 Figaro projection* understated migration and still got the religious outcome wrong
  (no Muslim majority among the 2016 lycée sample).

**Camus (2011–2018).**
- *Descriptive claim* (the composition of the population is changing): **on balance.** True on
  any definition, by an amount that depends on how mixed descent is counted.
- *Intentional claim, as a plot:* **unsupported. Strong.** His own text rejects a plot
  ("I have never imagined that a group of people with evil intentions congregated one day"),
  and the UN's 2000 report is a projection exercise, not a plan.
- *Intentional claim, as "wanted by part of the governing class":* **unsupported. On balance.**
  One contested insider account (Neather, *Evening Standard*, October 2009, later disowned by
  its author) [secondary] sits against the documented forecasting errors — the 2004 forecast of
  5,000–13,000 a year, the missed "tens of thousands" target (judged in the Murray lesson) —
  which point to drift, not design.

**Strongest objection to the verdicts:** from one side, that "right in direction" lets Powell
off — his speech was a forecast of conflict and domination, and that forecast failed; from the
other, that "unsupported" lets the governing class off — Neather's account is an insider's, and
"drift" is what a policy no one would defend in public looks like from outside. Neither
overturns the verdicts. The first is already the second Powell item: the number forecast and
the consequence forecast are graded separately, and the consequence forecast fails (strong).
The second needs evidence of intent; one account later disowned does not outweigh the record
of governments surprised by their own numbers, which is why the grade is on balance and not
strong. **Would be overturned by:** for Powell's consequences, sustained communal violence on
the scale he invoked; for the intentional claim, documents of policy showing that the scale of
immigration was intended and concealed; for Raspail's strong form, a case in which states with
the means to refuse a mass arrival declined to do so for years rather than months.
- **Spine step:** "The live question: what a nation is and who may join it — Scruton and
  Miller on the pre-political loyalty, America as England's heir from the Federalist to Who
  Are We?, the prophecies of displacement from Powell to Camus, Carens and Miller on borders,
  Murray on Europe — and 2015 as the test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "In April 1968 a Conservative shadow minister tells a
Birmingham audience that a constituent has told him that in fifteen or twenty years the black
man will have the whip hand over the white man, and that he sees the Tiber foaming with much
blood. He is dismissed from the shadow cabinet the next day; London dockers march in his
support. Five years later a French novelist imagines a million people from the Ganges landing
on the Riviera and a France that will not stop them; in 2011 a French writer gives the fear a
name. Each made a prediction that can now be checked. The lesson sets out each argument and
checks it."

The newcomer's problem: three texts, two countries, forty years, one fear. A reader who has
just seen the immigration premise isolated (`<Ref>` america-and-the-english-decline) needs a
method before the cases: in each text, which sentences are forecasts that the record can
check, which are claims about causes or intentions, which are judgments of value, and which
are rhetoric? The lesson applies that four-way split to each text in turn and ends with what
a demographic projection can say.

1. **What did Powell argue, as an argument?** Anchor first (L11): Enoch Powell (1912–98),
   classical scholar, MP for Wolverhampton South West, shadow defence secretary; the setting —
   a Labour government's Race Relations Bill (extending the 1965 Act's ban on discrimination to
   housing and employment) due for its second reading three days later, and the Commonwealth
   Immigrants Act passed weeks earlier to restrict the entry of British-passport holders from
   Kenya. Then rebuild the speech as premises → conclusion: (P1) the statesman must foresee
   evils "not demonstrable until they have occurred"; (P2) on official figures the immigrant
   and immigrant-descended population will reach 3.5 million in 15–20 years and 5–7 million,
   about a tenth, by 2000, concentrated in particular towns; (P3) "numbers are of the essence":
   1 percent and 10 percent are different things; (P4) integration means becoming
   "indistinguishable", which he judges improbable where there are "marked physical
   differences, especially of colour", and organised communal interests are growing against
   it; (P5) the existing population was never consulted and finds itself "strangers in their
   own country"; (C) stop settlement immigration, promote voluntary re-emigration, keep equality
   before the law but no anti-discrimination law on private conduct. The rhetoric is separate:
   the constituent's "whip hand", the widow's letter, the Virgil line. Answer: a forecast (P2),
   two empirical-causal claims (P3, P4), a democratic-consent claim (P5), a policy (C), and a
   frame (the duty to foresee) that makes every critic a man who confuses predicting with
   causing.
2. **How was it received — and what does the reception establish?** Dismissed by Heath the
   next day; The Times: "an evil speech"; dockers and meat porters marched; Gallup: 74 percent
   agreed, 69 percent thought the dismissal wrong; Powell's own definition of a "racialist"
   (one who believes in the inherent inferiority of one race and acts on it — he denied being
   one); Benn's "Dachau and Belsen"; claims that he won votes for the Conservatives in 1970. The
   seam: the reception records what people *believed* about the speech; it does not settle
   whether its forecast came true. Then the sourcing drill on the widow (set piece 2): a letter
   from an unnamed constituent, relayed without verification, identified in 2007 and found
   wrong in several details. Answer: the rhetoric's evidence was weak; the forecast stands or
   falls on other evidence.
3. **Check Powell's forecast against the census.** Obvious attempt: compare 5–7 million with
   the 2001 census. Why it is not straightforward: Powell's category ("Commonwealth immigrants
   and their descendants") is not the census's (self-assigned ethnic group, first asked in
   1991); his figure includes white Commonwealth migrants and excludes non-Commonwealth ones; a
   grandchild of mixed parentage may or may not count. Answer, with the published figures
   bound in their sentences (verify; FIGURE PLAN 3): the 1991 census found about 3 million
   people of ethnic-minority groups in Great Britain (about 5.5 percent), close to his
   15–20-year figure; 2001 found about 4.6 million non-White (about 8 percent), below his 5–7
   million and "one-tenth" for 2000; the shares then kept rising (England and Wales: about 14
   percent in 2011, 18 percent in 2021). His native-born-majority claim for 1985 is checked
   against the 1991 country-of-birth tables. "American proportions": the non-White share of
   England and Wales passed the black share of the United States (about 12–13 percent) around
   2011 — but Powell meant a pattern of conflict, not a percentage. The violence forecast: the
   record has urban disorders with an ethnic dimension (1981, 1985, 2001) and no civil
   conflict; Powell himself later said he had foreseen only the prospect of conflict, and a
   Labour opponent read the Virgil line as foreboding, not prediction. Two readings of the
   record, both reported: a British writer in 2017 (`<Ref>` murray-strange-death-of-europe)
   judged that Powell had "understated the case", because "White British" became a minority
   in London; his critics, that the forecast of bloodshed and domination failed. Answer: the
   number forecast was right in direction and roughly in size, with a lag; the consequence
   forecasts (domination, bloodshed) were not borne out in the form he gave them; the consent
   claim (P5) is a claim about the political process, open here and decided in the Murray
   lesson.
4. **Raspail: a novel as an argument.** Anchor: Jean Raspail (1925–2020), travel writer and
   novelist; the book written in 1971–72, published January 1973; the title from Revelation 20
   (the nations "compass the camp of the saints"). The plot in five sentences (set piece 4):
   a hundred ships from the Ganges carrying a million people; Western governments, churches,
   press and public opinion that will not use force against the unarmed; an army that deserts;
   France yields — "La France a cédé. L'irrémédiable est accompli" ("France has given in. The
   irreparable is done"); the last defenders bombed by their own air force; Switzerland holds,
   then signs. Raspail's own statement of the theme (2011 preface, l.213–215): they inspire
   "une immense pitié", they are "faibles … désarmés", they have "la puissance du nombre"; who
   would obey an order to fire? Reconstruct: (P1) numbers will press on a rich, ageing West;
   (P2) the West cannot bring itself to use force against the weak — the obstacle is moral, not
   material: pity and remorse (the consul's "la haïssable pitié", l.590); (P3) the dominant
   opinion ("Big Other") punishes anyone who says so; (C) submersion. Raspail states the
   dilemma himself, at full strength (1985 preface, via the 2017 account, l.3537–3543): no one
   wishes to renounce his dignity "by acquiescing to racism", yet all peoples have "the sacred
   right to preserve their differences and identities". Report, without label, what the novel
   contains: its depiction of the migrants in animal and excremental imagery, and the
   epilogue's definition of racism as the incompatibility of races sharing one milieu
   (summary, ch. L). One clause each: `<Ref>` nietzsche/against-pity (the critique of pity the
   novel's argument echoes) and `<Ref>` burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west (guilt
   and the ideology of retreat).
5. **Check Raspail's forecasts.** Three different claims. (a) *A million arriving at once*: in
   2015 a little over a million people crossed the Mediterranean to Europe (UNHCR, verify) —
   not in a hundred ships on one night but in thousands of small boats over a year, mostly to
   Greece, not France; Raspail in 2011 said the one thing he got wrong was "the numbers …
   Currently there is no fleet with a million people" (l.~3556–3559). (b) *A West that will not
   refuse*: in the record, states did refuse — Australia's turn-back policy from 2013, the
   Hungarian fence (September 2015), the EU–Turkey statement (March 2016) that cut the Aegean
   route (`<Ref>` the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways, forward, one clause). The novel's strongest
   premise, P2, failed in its strong form; whether it holds in a weaker form is the Murray
   lesson's question (and the 2015 lesson's, which judges what the months of non-refusal
   showed). (c) *The 2045–50 forecast* (preface l.217): half of under-55s in the urban
   zones of "extra-European origin" — not yet checkable; the trajectory is, from the 2019–20
   French survey of origins (TeO2): the writer states what share of the young adult
   population has an immigrant parent or grandparent of non-European origin, and how many of
   those also have a native parent (verify). Then the 2001 landing near the villa as a
   sourcing exercise (Raspail: about a thousand Kurds, fifty metres from the house; the 2017
   account: 1,500 people, 50 metres) — two witnesses to one event, a discrepancy to weigh.
6. **The 1985 Figaro projection: input and output.** Raspail and a demographer published a
   cover story asking whether France would still be French in thirty years, the cover showing
   Marianne veiled (26 October 1985); ministers denounced it (Dufoix: "reminiscent of the
   wildest Nazi theories"; Lang; Fabius); the ministry's objection was that it assumed
   immigrant fertility would stay high and native fertility low. The projection assumed net
   migration of 59,000 a year. The later record: figures of 193,000 (2006) and 235,000 (2013)
   are often set against it (l.3532–3533) — the writer must establish whether these are net
   migration or gross entries of immigrants (see Sources: INSEE); either way, migration
   exceeded the assumption. The headline prediction — Islam the leading religion of France by
   2015 — did not happen: a 2016 poll of French lycée pupils found 33.2 percent identifying as
   Christian and 25.5 as Muslim (l.3600–3604; a poll of school pupils, not of the population;
   the same poll found religion "important" to 83 percent of Muslim pupils and 22 percent of
   Catholic ones). Answer: the input (migration) was understated and the output (the religious
   majority) still wrong — so the projection's mechanism was mis-specified, which is a
   different failure from "it was alarmist" or "it was vindicated". The worked example of the
   lesson (set piece 6).
7. **Camus: the name, the definition, and two readings.** Anchor: Renaud Camus (b. 1946),
   novelist and diarist; Le Grand Remplacement (2011); You Will Not Replace Us! (2018, written
   in English). His definition: the "change of people and civilisation" in France and Europe
   by mass migration since the mid-1970s; he concedes the word is "not absolutely apt"
   because no one is killed or expelled, and frames the identity question with Lichtenberg's
   knife (handle replaced, then blade: is it the same knife? — `<Ref>` to the Ship of Theseus
   only if the Murray lesson has run; otherwise state the puzzle once). His frame: replacement
   is the "central gesture" of modernity — things, culture and finally peoples swapped for
   cheaper interchangeable substitutes ("Global Replacism"); his cast: "replacists" (who "want
   the change"), "replacers", "replacees"; his programme: "remigration". His epistemology:
   "Such numbers? What numbers? … I don't know … And I don't care" — lived experience outranks
   statistics, and "The Great Replacement is not a theory, it is a ghastly fact". Then the two
   readings the lesson must keep apart (FIGURE PLAN 6): (i) *descriptive* — the composition
   of the population is changing; this is measurable (INSEE, TeO2, ONS) and partly true on any
   definition, with the size depending on how descendants of mixed parentage are counted;
   (ii) *intentional* — the change is wanted and organised by an elite. On (ii) Camus's own text
   is double: he rejects the boardroom plot ("I have never imagined that a group of people with
   evil intentions congregated one day … it is more evil than that … enormous, bizarre and
   complex processes", l.4415–4430) yet assigns agency to "replacists" who "want" the change.
   The commonly offered documentary proof, the UN's 2000 report on "replacement migration", is
   a projection exercise that computes the flows needed to offset ageing and states its own
   purpose (the writer quotes its stated purpose and conclusion). The weaker form of (ii) —
   that part of the governing class wanted the change — rests on one insider account (a former
   speechwriter's *Evening Standard* article of October 2009, later disowned; [secondary]),
   which the lesson sets against the documented forecasting errors (the Murray lesson's 2004
   forecast and the missed net-migration target). Answer: (i) is a demographic claim to be
   checked number by number; (ii) needs evidence of intent, which the texts do not supply and
   the author declines to seek; link 10 gives the verdict on each.
8. **The argument's history and its uses.** The form — differential fertility plus immigration
   equals the replacement of a native people — is older than the term: Grant in 1916 called
   the change in American stock "a replacement pure and simple" (`<Ref>`
   chamberlain-and-grant, `nordicism`). **The strongest OBJECTION enters here, from history and
   at full strength:** the groups Grant said were replacing the "native American" — southern
   and eastern Europeans, Catholics and Jews — became within three generations what a later
   lesson's author called the "triple melting pot" of white Americans (`<Ref>`
   albions-seed-and-who-are-we, Huntington ch. 11); the forecast of 1916 failed as a forecast
   of replacement. **The reply, also at full strength, from Huntington and Camus:** earlier
   waves were European and Christian, dispersed, and interrupted by forty years of restriction
   (Huntington's nine facilitators; Camus pp. 24, 33–35); the present migration lacks those
   conditions, so the precedent does not transfer. Then the record of the term since 2011,
   reported as dated facts without comment (Sources: Christchurch 2019, El Paso 2019, Buffalo
   2022; French campaigning 2021–22; polls with wording) — and the fact that writers who accept
   the descriptive claim (the 2017 British writer among them) separate it from the
   intentional one.
9. **What a demographic projection can and cannot say.** Obvious attempt: a projection is a
   prediction. It is a conditional: given fertility, mortality, migration and a rule for
   classifying people, here is the population at a date. Work one example (FIGURE PLAN 7): the
   2017 projection of Europe's Muslim share to 2050 under zero, medium and high migration
   (about 7, 11 and 14 percent from about 5 percent in 2016 — verify); the same exercise for one
   country; and the "2060s" date for the end of a "White British" majority, which moves with the
   migration assumption and with how children of mixed parentage are counted. The
   demographers' standard objections, as the course's own: immigrant fertility tends to
   converge toward the host country's within a generation or two; identity categories are
   self-assigned and change (mixed parentage grows fastest); error widens with the horizon. The
   reply: the direction of change is not in doubt even where the date is, and a state that
   forbids ethnic statistics (France) leaves the question unmeasured. **The objection from
   integration data** (key idea 5): language acquisition, intermarriage, the growing "Mixed"
   group in Britain and the share of French third-generation descendants with one native
   parent (TeO2) — evidence that the categories the prophecies count are dissolving; the
   counter-evidence the prophecies' defenders cite — religious practice that persists across
   generations (the 2016 lycée figures), residential concentration (Huntington's six
   differentia applied to Europe). Answer: a projection can establish the arithmetic
   consequence of its assumptions; it cannot establish its assumptions, and it cannot by itself
   establish that a change of composition is a "replacement" of a people — that depends on
   whether "a people" is defined by descent (Barrès, Raspail's *souche*), by culture
   (Huntington) or by will (Renan) (`<Ref>` renan-what-is-a-nation,
   barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism).
10. **The weighing: the numbers came, the bloodshed and the paralysis did not, and the plot
    is unsupported.** Set each prophecy's claims against the record and against the strongest
    objection, as in ## VERDICT, and give each grade in the course's voice. Powell — numbers
    right in direction and roughly in size, later than forecast (**strong**); bloodshed and
    domination not borne out in the form he gave them (**strong**), with the disorders of 1981,
    1985 and 2001 stated beside the verdict; consent **open**, to the Murray lesson. Raspail —
    the scale arrived in 2015 by another route, and for months the core states did not refuse;
    the strong form failed (**strong**); the 2045 claim **pending**; the 1985 projection's input
    too low and its output still wrong. Camus — the descriptive claim **on balance**
    (definition-dependent); the plot **unsupported, strong**; "wanted by part of the governing
    class" **unsupported, on balance** (Neather against drift). The objections from both sides
    (that "right in direction" flatters Powell; that "unsupported" flatters the governing
    class) each get two sentences and the reason they do not overturn the grades.

    **Conclusion.** The verdicts in plain words with their grades (as above), then the hand-off:
    the question every one of the three raises and none answers by itself — may a people decide
    who joins it, and on what ground? — is the next lesson's starting point, from the opposite
    side; it is decided two lessons on, in `miller-strangers-in-our-midst`.

Headings as claims (suggested): "Powell's speech as an argument"; "What the reception shows,
and what it does not"; "Powell's numbers against the census"; "Raspail: the obstacle is pity";

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **No label in the course's voice (course.yaml notes; co-contested).** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right. Concretely: The Times's "evil speech" (1968), Benn's
  comparison, the ministers' 1985 words, the summary's own characterisations of the novel and
  the polemic — all are quoted with author and date or omitted; none becomes the course's
  description. The rule applies in both directions.
- **Replacement is taught as an argument, then judged claim by claim.** The lesson does not
  use "replacement" in its own voice as a description; it states what was claimed, by whom,
  with what numbers, and what the numbers did, and then gives the register's verdicts: the
  composition is changing by a definition-dependent amount (on balance); the plot is
  unsupported (strong); the "wanted by part of the governing class" form is unsupported (on
  balance). Every projection is
  given with publisher, year, variant/assumption and horizon.
- **Category traps (the commonest real error, L13):** (a) Powell's "Commonwealth immigrants
  and their descendants" ≠ census ethnic group ≠ foreign-born; (b) net migration ≠ gross
  entries of immigrants (the Figaro check); (c) "of extra-European origin" (Raspail) ≠
  immigrant ≠ Muslim; (d) self-assigned identity changes between censuses (Huntington's
  census-form experiment, `<Ref>` albions-seed-and-who-are-we). Each comparison in the lesson
  names its two quantities.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), no more than three per section:** *shadow cabinet*; *Commonwealth*
  and *Commonwealth citizen* (the 1948 British Nationality Act's status that gave the right of
  entry until 1962); *Race Relations Bill/Act*; *Wolverhampton* (an industrial town in the West
  Midlands, Powell's constituency); *the Sibyl* and the *Aeneid* (Virgil's epic; the line is a
  prophecy to Aeneas of war in Italy); *Riviera / Boulouris* (the French Mediterranean coast;
  a village near Saint-Raphaël); *Français de souche* ("French of the stock" — literally "of
  the root-stock"); *loi Pleven* (1972 French law against incitement to racial hatred);
  *INSEE*, *ONS*, *UNHCR*; *remigration* (Camus's word for organised return); *Lichtenberg's
  knife* (an eighteenth-century German writer's puzzle — say so in a clause; References for
  the source).
- **French quotations:** give the English, with the French beside the key phrase: "la
  haïssable pitié" ("hateful pity"); "la puissance du nombre" ("the power of numbers"); "La
  France a cédé. L'irrémédiable est accompli" ("France has given in. The irreparable is
  done"); "Français de souche"; "submersion". Camus's book is in English already.
- **Murray is a source here, not the subject.** Use his reporting for facts (and check them:
  the 59,000/193,000/235,000 comparison; the 1,500 vs Raspail's "un millier"); quote his
  verdict on Powell once, attributed, as one reading; do not reconstruct his argument.
- **The Hungary and Germany figures of 2015** belong to the 2015 lesson; here only the total
  crossings and the closure of the route, in one sentence.
- **The violence after 2011:** facts with dates and sources only; no inference about any
  author's responsibility beyond what is documented; Camus's own statements only as found.
- **Do not re-teach:** Grant and the 1924 Act (`<Ref>` chamberlain-and-grant), Huntington's
  assimilation chapter (`<Ref>` albions-seed-and-who-are-we), Renan and Barrès (`<Ref>`),
  Burnham on guilt (`<Ref>`), Nietzsche on pity (`<Ref>`). Do not run into Carens's argument
  or Murray's concatenations.
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — Powell's 3.5m / 5–7m against the census; the Figaro's 59,000 and
  its failed 2015 prediction; Raspail's million against 2015's crossings. Everything else in
  figures. Exact days only where contingency is the point (20–23 April 1968).
- **Copyright:** Powell's speech, Raspail and Camus are in copyright — quote a sentence or two
  at most from each passage; paraphrase otherwise. Virgil, Revelation and Hansard may be quoted.
- **Dossier thin:** all census, INSEE, TeO2, UNHCR, Pew, UN and Coleman figures are outside
  the dossier and marked (verify) above; Hansard's record of the Registrar General's figure is
  not in the dossier; the Figaro article itself is not (only Murray's and the New York Times's
  accounts of it). If a figure cannot be verified, the lesson states the comparison
  qualitatively and says so.

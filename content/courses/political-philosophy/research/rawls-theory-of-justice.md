# Research brief: rawls-theory-of-justice (Rawls's Theory of Justice: the original position and the difference principle)

status: planned
module: equality-after-rawls
type: core
goal: Analyze Rawls's argument from the original position to the two principles of justice, including the claim that natural talents are morally arbitrary, and evaluate the conservative and libertarian objections

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The contract tradition in three versions and its critic: Hobbes's covenant of every man with every man, made from fear, which authorises a sovereign (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`); Locke's consent, express or tacit, as the only title to political power (`locke-property-and-consent`); Hume's reply that no actual government rests on consent and that allegiance rests on opinion and the general interest (`hume-against-the-original-contract`, concept `original-contract`); Rousseau's general will — each obeys only himself because the law is one he would will as a member of the whole — and his rich man's fraudulent contract (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`). The equality question as the course has posed it so far: Aristotle's natural hierarchy and its admitted difficulty (`aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`); Burke's "equal rights, but not to equal things" and his natural aristocracy (`burke-reflections`); Tocqueville's equality of conditions (`tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`); Marx's class war (`marx-manifesto`). From the module just closed: the postwar right's critiques of abstract rights and of the planning state — Oakeshott's Rationalist (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`), Jouvenel's Power that grows by promising security (`jouvenel-on-power`), Burnham's managers (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`) — and the Universal Declaration of 1948, which listed social rights and declined to say why anyone has them (`udhr-read-against-the-right`).
- **Contributes:** One move: the reconstruction, at full strength, of the argument that justice is what free and equal persons would choose for their society's basic structure if none of them knew which place in it he would occupy — and that this choice yields equal basic liberties first and, second, inequalities only where they benefit the least advantaged, because talents and starting places are no one's desert. The lesson rebuilds the argument as its defenders would want it (the hypothetical contract, the veil, maximin, the lexical order, the natural lottery, pure procedural justice) and then puts three objections to it — the libertarian's (entitlement), the Humean's (a hypothetical contract binds no one) and the conservative's (distributed by whom? and what of desert?) — each with Rawls's reply.
- **Hands off:** (1) The parties are stipulated to be free of envy (§25); whether a society built on the two principles will in fact breed the envy its critics say drove the demand for equality is the next lesson's question, which Rawls answers in §§80–81 (`ressentiment-and-envy`). (2) The conservative objection is stated here only in its question form; Scruton's full reply — first-person vs third-person justification, authority before choice — is read against the Rawls the reader now has (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`). (3) Whether the morally arbitrary talents are in fact sorting a society into classes is the module's application (`the-cognitive-elite`); whether desert can be restored as the ground of justice is MacIntyre's argument (`macintyre-after-virtue`); Lasch's attack on meritocracy picks up Rawls's own rejection of it (`lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`). (4) If birthplace is as arbitrary as talent, the veil extends to citizenship — the open-borders argument (`carens-open-borders`). (5) If talents are partly genetic, the natural lottery becomes a genetic lottery: a behaviour geneticist argues from Rawls's premise that genetic inequality calls for compensation, and Murray reads the same facts the other way — the heredity module's political lesson (`gould-and-harden-on-heredity`), where the value premise this lesson isolates (no one deserves his natural assets) is the one that decides between them.
- **Spine step:** "Rawls makes equality the measure of justice; the right answers with the charge of ressentiment and envy, Scruton's authority, MacIntyre's lost tradition and Lasch's revolt of the elites — and the data on cognitive stratification test both sides"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): a society choosing its basic rules with no one knowing whether he will be born rich or poor, clever or slow, healthy or sick; the 1971 answer; half a century as the main statement of egalitarian liberalism; rebuild it and put the strongest objections. Anchor (L11): John Rawls (1921–2002), Harvard professor of philosophy, who worked on the book for two decades; it appeared in 1971 amid the American arguments over civil rights and Vietnam (the book's sections on civil disobedience belong to that decade — one clause, no more). The newcomer's problem, in the course's terms: the reader has three contracts (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau) and one objection to all of them (Hume: no one ever signed). He also has, from the last module, a Declaration that lists social rights "on condition no one asks why" (`udhr-read-against-the-right`). Rawls offers a "why" for an egalitarian list without claiming anyone ever agreed to anything.

1. **What is justice a virtue of?** Obvious attempt: of persons and their acts — the just man pays his debts (the reader's Cephalus, `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`). Why it is not enough: two people can each act fairly within the rules and the rules themselves can still settle, before either is born, that one will start with an estate and the other with nothing. Answer: "Justice is the first virtue of social institutions, as truth is of systems of thought" (§1); its subject is the basic structure — constitution, property, markets, family — because its effects are "so profound and present from the start" (§2). Its "because": a theory that is elegant but false is rejected; institutions that are efficient but unjust must be reformed — no welfare of society as a whole overrides a person's inviolability. Mark the seam to the module: this is a claim about institutions, not a claim about who rules; the classical question "who should rule?" becomes "by what rules should the society be arranged?".
2. **How can rules be chosen fairly if everyone knows where he stands?** Obvious attempt: ask people. Why it fails: the rich man will call welfare taxes unjust and the poor man will call them just (§4 pp. 18–19); every vote is a bargain between known interests, and its outcome reflects bargaining strength — the reader has Thrasymachus's "interest of the stronger" (`thrasymachus-and-the-ring`) and Rousseau's rich man whose contract was a fraud (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`). Answer: remove the knowledge that makes bias possible. The original position: parties who do not know their class, their natural assets and abilities, their intelligence and strength, their conception of the good, even their psychological propensities (§3, §24); they know general facts of economics and psychology. They are rational and mutually disinterested (not egoists: they take no interest in one another's interests, §25) and they want more rather than less of the primary goods — rights and liberties, opportunities and powers, income and wealth, and the social bases of self-respect (§15) — because whatever else one wants, these help. Since "no one is able to design principles to favor his particular condition, the principles of justice are the result of a fair agreement" (§3) — hence "justice as fairness". Then the status of the device, stated before Hume can be invoked: "a purely hypothetical situation", "not … an actual historical state of affairs" (§3). Seam (L10b): the reader expects a contract to be an event; this one is a test anyone can apply "at any time" by reasoning within its limits (§24).
3. **What would they choose, and why that and not average welfare?** Obvious attempt: since you do not know who you will be, maximise the average — any person's expected share (the utilitarian rule; anchor in one clause: the doctrine, from Bentham, that the right act or law maximises the sum or average of satisfaction). Rawls's answer, in two steps. First, the benchmark is equal division; an inequality is accepted only as an "intelligent investment" that leaves everyone, and in particular the worst off, better than equality would (§26). Second, the maximin rule: rank alternatives by their worst outcome — choose "a society in which his enemy is to assign him his place" (§26 p. 152). Work the gain-and-loss table (the lesson's first `<Example>`, figure 2): three decisions, three circumstances; maximin picks the third row (worst case +5 against −7 and −8) though the second has the highest single gain; then say why maximin is not a general rule of prudence (one does not refuse to cross a street) but is rational under three conditions that the original position has "to the fullest possible degree": no basis for estimating probabilities; a guaranteed minimum one would be content with; alternatives whose worst outcomes one could hardly accept — slavery, persecution (§26 pp. 154–156). Add the further grounds (§29): the agreement is final and must survive "the strains of commitment" in the worst place; a public conception must support each citizen's self-respect; utilitarianism "does not take seriously the distinction between persons" (§5 p. 27) — it lets one person's loss be outweighed by another's gain, as if society were one man trading present for future pleasure. The objection enters here at full strength (the decision theorist's): behind a veil that hides who you will be, each place is equally likely (probability 1/N), and a rational chooser under known equal probabilities maximises expected value — the average, not the minimum. Rawls's reply: the parties have no basis for equiprobability (insufficient reason is "a poor basis", §28), and principles chosen once for a whole life for one's descendants too must be ones one can honour in the worst case. Let the reader feel that this is the argument's softest joint, and say Rawls himself later conceded it (1986: the case for the difference principle "less evident", Höffe appendix p. 216) — the strong argument is for the first principle.
4. **The two principles, and why the first comes first.** State the 1971 first statement (§11, quoted) and the final statement (§46) in a figure, not in prose: (1) each person an equal right to the most extensive system of equal basic liberties compatible with a similar system for all — political liberty, speech and assembly, conscience and thought, the person and personal property, freedom from arbitrary arrest; (2) social and economic inequalities arranged (a) to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged and (b) attached to offices open to all under fair equality of opportunity. Then the lexical (serial) order, defined by the dictionary analogy (no word with "b" in second place precedes "ab…"): liberty cannot be traded for economic gain (§11 pp. 62–63; §39) — the reader can test it on the Declaration: Rawls's first-principle list is roughly UDHR Arts. 3–21, his second principle a ground for Arts. 22–27 that ranks them below the liberties. The "because" for the priority: once a society is beyond bare want, a party who risked his conscience or his vote for more income would be gambling what he cannot afford to lose (§33's gamble argument; §82). Who are "the least advantaged"? Not the unlucky individual but a representative position — e.g. the unskilled worker, or those below half the median income (§16 pp. 97–98).
5. **Why the second principle and not "careers open to talents"? The natural lottery.** This is the step the goal names and the lesson's centre (FigureSequence 4). Obvious attempt, the one most readers hold: a society is just if positions are open to all and each gets what the market pays for his talent and effort — the "system of natural liberty" (§12). Rawls's ladder: (a) natural liberty lets shares be settled by prior distributions of wealth, which are the residue of social accident; (b) so add fair equality of opportunity — similar life chances for the similarly gifted and willing ("liberal equality"); (c) but then shares are still "decided by the outcome of the natural lottery; and this outcome is arbitrary from a moral perspective", and even "the willingness to make an effort, to try, and so to be deserving in the ordinary sense is itself dependent upon happy family and social circumstances" (§12 p. 74); (d) so: "No one deserves his greater natural capacity nor merits a more favorable starting place in society. But it does not follow that one should eliminate these distinctions" (§17 p. 102) — the difference principle treats the distribution of talents "as a common asset", so that the gifted may gain from their good fortune "only on terms that improve the situation of those who have lost out". Then the two sentences that keep the argument from being levelling: "The natural distribution is neither just nor unjust … What is just and unjust is the way that institutions deal with these facts", and the difference principle is not redress — it does not try to make everyone start the race level (§17 pp. 100–102). Place the fourth cell of the 2×2 the conservative reader will notice: "natural aristocracy" — open positions in law, with the advantages of the gifted limited to what furthers the good of the poorer (§12 p. 74). Rawls rejects it as unstable because the social contingencies it ignores are as arbitrary as the natural ones it corrects; the reader has a natural aristocracy from Burke and Jefferson's day (`burke-reflections`, concept `natural-aristocracy`) and should see how close and how far the two are. Close the link with what the principle permits: large inequalities (entrepreneurs' expectations rising, provided the unskilled worker's rise too, §13 p. 78) — it is not strict equality.
6. **Who does the distributing? Pure procedural justice.** The conservative question, stated as the course's own and attributed forward (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`): the general conception speaks of goods "distributed equally unless …" — distributed by whom? It seems to presuppose a state that holds the social product and hands it out. Rawls's answer at full strength (§14, §47): the difference principle is not a rule for allocating a given stock of goods to known people — "The conception of the two principles does not interpret the primary problem of distributive justice as one of allocative justice" (§14 p. 88). It is a standard for designing background institutions (competitive markets, full employment, wide dispersal of property, a social minimum, education); inside such a structure, whatever distribution results from people's free dealings is just, as the outcome of fair bets is fair (pure procedural justice: cake division is perfect procedural justice, a criminal trial imperfect, fair gambling pure). Legitimate expectations formed under just rules are entitlements the community must honour (§48). Then concede what the reply does not dissolve: the background institutions include a transfer branch and a distribution branch that tax inheritance and gifts (§43); the state is not named in the general conception, but it is in the institutions, and the reader decides whether "designing a structure" is a different act from "distributing" (Exercise 6).
7. **The libertarian objection: entitlement and Wilt Chamberlain.** At full strength (Nozick, 1974 — anchor: a Harvard colleague's reply three years later; text not in the dossier, verify): justice in holdings is historical — a holding is just if it was justly acquired or justly transferred; any "patterned" principle ("to each according to …") must be continually enforced against free exchanges. The example: start from any distribution you like, D1 (Rawls's); a million people each freely pay a quarter to watch a famous basketball player; he ends with a fortune and the distribution is D2; every step was voluntary and from a just start; by what right is D2 unjust? And on the natural lottery: whether or not talents are arbitrary from a moral point of view, their owners are entitled to them and to what flows from them; treating them as a common asset makes persons resources for one another — the very failure to "take seriously the distinction between persons" Rawls charged utilitarianism with. Rawls's replies, from his text: (a) the basic structure is the subject precisely because many free transactions, each fair, can cumulatively undermine background fairness (§2, §43's estate taxes) — so the example shows why the structure must be regulated, not that it must not be; (b) talents are not confiscated: their holders keep them and gain from them, on terms (§17), and the principle is chosen by the talented themselves behind the veil. The kidney-donation reductio (the course's own statement of a commentator's objection): behind a veil that hid one's health, would the parties also agree to redistribute organs? Rawls's priority of liberty (integrity of the person is a basic liberty) is the reply, and the reader judges whether it is principled or ad hoc.
8. **The Humean objection: a hypothetical contract binds no one.** Seam (L10b): from `hume-against-the-original-contract` the reader expects this to be fatal. It is not where expected: Rawls agrees with Hume about actual consent — "There is, I believe, no political obligation, strictly speaking, for citizens generally" (§18 p. 114); citizens are bound by a natural duty to support just institutions that apply to them (§19, §51), not by any promise. So the objection must be restated: if no one agreed, why should the original position's verdict bind me? The stronger form (stated as the course's own): a hypothetical agreement is not a weaker agreement but no agreement; the device can only re-describe the convictions built into it, and a veil adjustable "so that we get the desired solution" (§24's own phrase) is circular. Rawls's reply: the original position is not the foundation but a "device of representation"; justification is the "mutual support of many considerations" — reflective equilibrium, going back and forth between the description of the initial situation and our considered judgments (that religious persecution and racial discrimination are unjust) until they fit (§4, §9, §87: "mere proof is not justification"). The concession the reader should see clearly, because the next three lessons press it: the method starts from "our" considered judgments — the judgments of a liberal democratic culture. That is where the conservative's deeper objection will enter: whose judgments, and formed in what tradition (recall Oakeshott's abridgment, `oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`, in one clause; the full charge is Scruton's and MacIntyre's).
9. **What the argument establishes, and what it leaves to the critics of equality.** Not that everyone has agreed, nor that equality is natural; that IF one accepts that the basic structure must be justifiable to each person as free and equal, and that no one deserves his starting place, THEN equal liberties and the difference principle are hard to refuse — with the difference principle the weaker link (by Rawls's own later admission). Hand off in one sentence: the parties were stipulated to feel no envy (§25); critics on the right had said for half a century that the demand for equality IS envy — and Rawls answered them in the book's last part.

Headings as claims: "Justice is a virtue of institutions before it is a virtue of persons"; "To choose fairly, forget who you are"; "Under the veil, guard the worst place"; "Liberty first, then the difference principle"; "No one deserves his talents — and no one need give them up"; "No one distributes: pure procedural justice"; "The entitlement objection"; "Hume's objection, and why Rawls half-concedes it".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The rich man and the poor man** (§4 pp. 18–19): each would call the welfare tax just or unjust according to his place; the veil removes exactly that knowledge. Three sentences; it is the newcomer's way into the device.
2. **The gain-and-loss table** (§26 p. 153, l.6975–6990): the 3×3 in hundreds of dollars (d1: −7, 8, 12; d2: −8, 7, 14; d3: 5, 6, 8); maximin picks d3 (worst case +5). CAUTION, checked: under EQUAL probabilities d3 also has the highest expected value (d1 = 13/3 ≈ 4.3, d2 = 13/3 ≈ 4.3, d3 = 19/3 ≈ 6.3), so the table shows divergence only if c1 is unlikely — e.g. probabilities 0.1/0.45/0.45 give d1 = 8.3, d2 = 8.65, d3 = 6.8, and the expected-value chooser takes d2 with its −8 risk. State the probabilities in the same sentence as the result. This is the only computation in the lesson and it IS the argument (playbook-history numbers rule 4): maximin refuses to trade the worst case for a likely gain.
3. **"A society in which his enemy is to assign him his place"** (§26 p. 152) with Rawls's own caveat in the next sentences: the parties "do not … assume that their initial place in society is decided by a malevolent opponent … an absence of information is not misinformation" (l.6942–6945). Quote both; the caveat is what keeps the image from being a caricature.
4. **The ladder of the natural lottery** (§12 pp. 72–75; §17 pp. 100–104): natural liberty → liberal equality → democratic equality, each rung forced by the arbitrariness the previous one left; then the two §17 sentences ("No one deserves …"; "The natural distribution is neither just nor unjust …"). The lesson's second `<Example>`, as a FigureSequence.
5. **Cake, trial, wager** (§14 pp. 85–86): perfect procedural justice (the divider takes the last slice), imperfect (a trial can convict the innocent), pure (fair bets: any outcome of fair bets is fair — but "an outcome is not just merely because it could have come from fair bets" if the bets were never actually made) — the image that answers "distributed by whom?".
6. **Wilt Chamberlain** (Nozick 1974 pp. 160–164; verify): D1, the quarter in the box, D2 — told in four sentences and drawn; then Rawls's reply from §2 and §43.
7. **The billion dollars and the penny** (§26 p. 157, l.7136–7140): "it seems extraordinary that the justice of increasing the expectations of the better placed by a billion dollars, say, should turn on whether the prospects of the least favored increase or decrease by a penny" — Rawls raises the objection against himself; his reply (the feasible set is restricted by the other principles and by chain connection, so such cases do not arise). One paragraph; it shows Rawls arguing at full strength against himself.
8. **Rawls's 1986 concession** (Höffe appendix p. 216): the case for the difference principle "less evident and unlikely ever to have the force of the argument for the two prior principles". One sentence; used at link 3 and link 9.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), no more than three per section:** "basic structure" (link 1); "original position", "veil of ignorance", "primary goods" (link 2 — the three new terms of that section; "mutually disinterested" glossed in a clause, not a fourth term); "utilitarianism" (one clause, link 3 — the reader met Bentham via Mill and Kirk's lesson, `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`); "maximin" (link 3); "lexical order" (link 4, with the dictionary picture); "least advantaged" as a representative position (link 4); "natural lottery", "fair equality of opportunity" (link 5); "pure procedural justice" (link 6); "entitlement", "patterned principle" (link 7); "reflective equilibrium" (link 8). Pareto efficiency only if used, with Rawls's serfdom example (§12 p. 71) in the same sentence.
- **Do not re-teach** Hobbes's covenant, Locke's consent, Hume's ship, Rousseau's general will: each is a clause and a `<Ref>`. The point of recalling them is the contrast — Rawls's contract is hypothetical, about institutions, and needs no consent.
- **The real misreadings (L13):** (a) "Rawls wants equal outcomes." Refute from §26 (equal division is only the benchmark), §13 (entrepreneurs' larger expectations permitted), §17 ("it does not follow that one should eliminate these distinctions") and §81 (strict egalitarianism is the one form of equality Rawls concedes may spring from envy — flag forward, do not develop). (b) "The original position is an imagined historical event / a claim about human nature." Refute from §3 ("purely hypothetical", "not … an actual historical state of affairs") and §24 (anyone can enter it at any time). One refutation slot (a); (b) in a sentence.
- **The goal says "the conservative and libertarian objections":** both must be at full strength and both must get Rawls's reply. The conservative objections are three: "distributed by whom?" (link 6), the desert/natural-aristocracy objection (links 5 and 9 — the common-sense view that the hard worker deserves what he earns; Rawls's §48 reply; MacIntyre forward), and the Humean (link 8). Do not let the lesson present the conservative case only through Scruton's later lesson.
- **Contested points presented as contested:** whether maximin is rational (decision-theoretic objection); whether talents can be a "common asset" without making persons means (libertarian objection); whether reflective equilibrium is circular. The course decides none of them.
- **Edition:** quote the 1971 text as staged; do not import 1999 wordings (e.g. "fully adequate scheme" belongs to the 1982/1993 revisions, not to TJ). If the writer quotes the first principle, use §11 (1971) or §46 (1971 final statement, l.13172 ff.).
- **Copyright:** TJ, Höffe, Nozick, Scruton, MacIntyre are in copyright — a sentence each, paraphrase otherwise.
- **Dossier thin:** Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia is not in the dossier (reconstructed from MacIntyre ch. 17 and Carens pp. 252–254; the Wilt Chamberlain example must be checked against the book); Harsanyi's average-utility papers are not in the dossier (stated from Höffe ch.1 §1.4, ch.5 §5.5–5.6). Political Liberalism (1993) is out of scope — at most one clause that Rawls later recast the theory as "political, not metaphysical", with References.
- **Numbers (L12):** keeper numbers: none beyond the gain-and-loss table and dates (1971; 1974 for the reply). "Half the median income" (§16) only if the least-advantaged definition is used, bound in the same sentence.
- **Register:** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Do not moralise either way:** neither "the most important political philosopher of the century" nor "the philosopher of the welfare bureaucracy". The reader came to the course from the right; the lesson's job is to make him able to state Rawls's argument as well as Rawls's students would, before he judges it.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** State the two principles in their final form and the priority rules; then place four contract theorists on one line — Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Rawls — by what each takes the contract to be (an event / a tacit fact / a will / a hypothetical test) and what it is meant to justify (a sovereign / limited government / the general will / principles for the basic structure).
2. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** Using the §26 table, find the probabilities over c1–c3 at which an expected-value chooser switches from d3 to d2, and state in one sentence what this shows about the role of the claim that the parties "have no basis" for probabilities.
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** A society with no inequalities of natural talent at all — everyone equally gifted, equally healthy. Which parts of Rawls's argument lose their work (the natural lottery; fair equality of opportunity becomes formal equality) and which still bind (the veil still hides class and conception of the good; the first principle still has priority)? The solution shows the difference principle is then driven by social contingency alone, so the natural-lottery argument is sufficient but not necessary for it.
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Rawls holds that the talented should be held back so that everyone ends equal." Name the failing steps with his own sentences (§17 "it does not follow that one should eliminate these distinctions"; §13 entrepreneurs' expectations; §26 equal division only the benchmark) and state what the difference principle does require.
5. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** §17's "No one deserves his greater natural capacity nor merits a more favorable starting place in society" — author, date (1971), audience (Anglo-American moral philosophers arguing against utilitarianism, and an American public in the decade of civil-rights legislation), purpose; what the sentence argues and what it omits (that desert might be earned by effort — answered in §12 p. 74, which the reader should find); what it can be USED to establish about the book's egalitarianism and what it cannot (that Rawls thinks talent worthless — §17's "common asset" says the opposite).
6. **Synthesis (claim–evidence–reasoning):** "Distributed by whom?" Argue, with §14 (allocative vs pure procedural justice) and §43 (the transfer and distribution branches), whether Rawls's theory presupposes a distributing state; judge which reading the text better supports and what the conservative would have to show to win (that the background institutions are themselves an allocation).
7. **Counter-argument stem (E3, recall `hume-against-the-original-contract`):** "Hume refuted Rawls in advance: no one ever made this contract." Name two facts the claim must account for (Rawls's "purely hypothetical" §3; his denial of political obligation for citizens generally, §18, and reliance on natural duty, §19) and state what would have to be true for a version of the objection to survive (that a hypothetical choice cannot justify without already assuming the convictions it is meant to ground — the circularity charge, which reflective equilibrium accepts rather than escapes).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `ressentiment-and-envy`:** §25 stipulates that the parties are not envious. Suppose instead each party knew he was envious and that his envy grew with the gap between himself and those above him. Would he still choose the difference principle, which permits large inequalities if the worst-off gain — or strict equality, even at a cost to himself? Work it through. The solution says that an envious chooser would accept losses to narrow gaps, so the stipulation does real work; that Rawls therefore split his argument into two steps and returned in §§80–81 to ask whether a just society would breed envy anyway; and that critics on the right had said the demand for equality was envy all along — the next lesson, `<Ref>` `ressentiment-and-envy`.

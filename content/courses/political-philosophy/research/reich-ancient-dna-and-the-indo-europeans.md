## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `chamberlain-and-grant`: Chamberlain's Teutonic thesis (race made by breeding,
  the Teuton as maker of Europe, "Though it were proved that there never was an Aryan race in the
  past, yet we desire that in the future there may be one") and Grant's Nordicism (three European
  races sorted by skull index and colouring; the Nordic as the race that carried "Aryan speech" to
  Italy, Greece, Persia and India as a thin conquering layer; a Nordic homeland in the forests and
  grasslands of eastern Europe; mixture reverting to "the lower type"). That lesson's last key idea
  named "what ancient DNA would later show about migration" as the forward test: this lesson is it.
  From `gobineau-inequality-of-races`: the Aryan as the civilizing type; degeneration by mixture,
  mixture as both the engine and the end of civilization. From `renan-what-is-a-nation`: the
  argument that every European people is of mixed blood, so race cannot found a nation. From
  the module so far: heritability, twin and SNP-based (`heritability-within-and-between-groups`),
  with the within/between point and the default hypothesis; the hereditarian case at full strength
  (`hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`: the measured gaps, the Bell Curve's declared agnosticism on the
  mix); the US gap's size and movement and the Flynn effect (`us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`);
  admixture regression, polygenic scores, their shrinkage within families, their loss of accuracy across
  ancestries, and the 2019 finding that the height-selection signals in Europeans were inflated by
  population structure (`admixture-and-polygenic-scores`: `polygenic-index`, `pgs-portability`); and,
  immediately before, Harden's refusal of group inferences on present evidence and Murray's and Harden's
  opposite political uses of the same genetics (`gould-and-harden-on-heredity`). Harden answers Reich by
  name: the reader has met her answer before meeting the argument it answers, and this lesson puts the two
  together.
- **Contributes:** The one move: a new kind of evidence — the genomes of the dead — settles questions
  the nineteenth-century race theorists and the twentieth-century archaeologists argued from skulls,
  pots and words, and it settles them in a way that fits neither side's expectation. People moved, in
  mass migrations that replaced most of the earlier ancestry of whole regions (against the postwar
  "pots, not people" consensus); but every moving people was itself a recent mixture (against every
  doctrine of an old, pure, isolated race). The lesson separates sharply what this evidence has
  established (the migrations, the mixtures, their dates) from the argument its leading investigator
  builds on it about cognitive and behavioural differences between populations, which is an
  inference, contested from both sides, and taught as such.
- **Hands off:** Reich concludes that average genetic differences between populations in cognitive
  traits are likely to be found, direction unknown, and that they should not change how individuals
  are treated; the module's own lessons have shown why no current design can yet find them (Ref
  `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`). What this lesson decides: old pure races and "pots, not people"
  are both refuted (**established**); Reich's inference to cognitive differences is **open**, with the
  design that would settle it named (`admixture-and-polygenic-scores`, section 10). The question this
  lesson passes on is the one the module turns to
  next: even granting that peoples have moved, mixed and been selected, does anything inherited explain
  the gaps between NATIONS — or do two explanations that never mention genes, geography and
  institutions, explain them better (`geography-or-institutions`)? The steppe herders' expansion is
  itself the bridge: why one population spread over others is a question Diamond answers with horses,
  wheels and germs.
- **Spine step:** "Heredity as an explanation of the gaps between peoples, tested premise by premise — what the tests measure, what heritability can and cannot imply, the hereditarian case at full strength, then the US gap, adoption, admixture and polygenic scores on the step from a real gap to a genetic one — then what follows politically whatever the answer, what ancient DNA shows, the rival explanations from geography and institutions, and the key datasets audited"

## VERDICT

- **Claims judged:** (i) two opposite views of Europe's past: that peoples descend from old, pure races
  formed in isolation (Grant, Chamberlain, Kossinna); and that cultures spread as ideas, "pots, not
  people", without mass movement (the postwar consensus). (ii) Reich's inference that average
  differences between populations in cognitive traits are likely to be found.
- **Verdicts.** (i) **Both refuted.** Mass migrations replaced most of the earlier ancestry of whole
  regions (about three-quarters steppe-related ancestry in Corded Ware Germany; about 90% of Britain's
  earlier ancestry gone after the Beaker period; Reich l.1002–1004, l.1030), and every migrating people
  was itself a recent mixture (the Yamnaya formed about 5,000 years ago from a northern and a southern
  people; the northern European look is a Bronze Age composite). Grade: **established**. Iberia, where
  Beaker pots spread without new people, shows both processes occurred, which refutes "pots, not
  people" as a rule, not as a possibility. (ii) Reich's inference: **open**.
- **What decides (i):** the ancient-genome record as Reich reports it (the passages listed under Sources) and the
  primary papers the brief cites; the test of the race theories in section 7.
- **Strongest objection to (i):** the record supports parts of the old picture (an eastern homeland
  near Grant's grasslands; a male-led spread; Reich's own "uncomfortable support", l.1058), so the race
  theories were partly right. It does not overturn the verdict: what was confirmed is migration and
  dominance by a mixed population; what is refuted is purity and fixity, which is what the theories
  claimed as their foundation.
- **Why (ii) is open, and what would settle it:** the premise that selection had time to act is supported
  (and the 2026 ancient-genome study shows selection on cognition-linked scores is detectable within one
  region); the step to present differences between populations runs through the within/between gap and
  through scores that do not travel across ancestries (`heritability-within-and-between-groups`,
  `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`). The strongest objection to "open", from the hereditarian side
  (the measured phenotypic gaps already point one way), fails because those gaps do not show their cause
  (module verdict); from the other side ("will be found" is not evidence), it fails because no evidence
  excludes a genetic share either. What would settle it: within-family genome-wide studies of cognition in
  large non-European samples, scores validated within families in each ancestry, then compared; and the
  sibling admixture design.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "For most of the twentieth century archaeologists held that pots,
not people, moved: new styles and languages spread by imitation, and the inhabitants of a place
were largely the descendants of its first settlers. After 2010 it became possible to read the
genomes of people dead for eight thousand years. The results showed that the people of a place
almost never descend only from those who lived there before. The lesson follows what ancient DNA
showed about Europe and India, and the conclusion its leading investigator drew about differences
between human populations."

Anchor (L11): David Reich (b. 1974), a geneticist at Harvard Medical School whose laboratory produced
a large share of the world's ancient genomes after 2015; the book (2018) is his own account, and
argues for conclusions of his own. The newcomer's problem, in plain words: before writing, how can
anyone know who lived where and who descends from whom? Until 2010 there were three kinds of
evidence — the shapes of bones, the styles of objects, the family relations of languages — and each
infers people from things. The nineteenth-century race theorists (`chamberlain-and-grant`, Ref)
built a story of a fair, long-skulled northern race carrying the Indo-European languages across
Eurasia from skulls, colouring and words; after 1945 archaeology answered that objects and words
spread without people. Both were inferences. The lesson asks what happens when the people
themselves can be read.

1. **Can living people's genes recover the past?** First attempt: sample people alive today, map
   how gene frequencies change across a continent, read the gradients as migrations — the leading
   synthesis of the 1990s read a south-east-to-north-west gradient in European blood groups as the
   spread of farmers from Anatolia. Why it fails: gradients of the same shape arise without any
   migration, and the method assumes that the people of a place descend from its first settlers —
   the very assumption in question. Answer: read the dead. Explain only what the reader needs:
   a genome is a text of about three billion letters in which rare copying errors (mutations)
   accumulate; two populations that separated long ago differ in which variants they carry and how
   often; ancient bones (the dense inner-ear bone above all) keep fragments of that text for
   thousands of years, and after about 2015 hundreds of ancient genomes could be read cheaply. Three
   tools, each defined in one sentence and drawn: a similarity map (each person a dot, near dots
   genetically similar — the "time-lapse" of ancient samples moving across the map of the living);
   a test of whether population A shares more variants with C than B does (mixture leaves this
   asymmetry); and dating a mixture by the length of the inherited chunks, which halve on average
   each generation as chromosomes recombine (FigureSequence). Then define ancestry as a genetic
   quantity: the share of one's genome descending from a given ancient population, which is not
   the same as one's family tree — beyond eight or so generations some genealogical ancestors left
   no DNA at all (Fig. 4 idea). Seam: with these tools the question "who were the first farmers of
   Europe?" has an answer that can be checked.
2. **Did farming spread by people or by ideas?** First attempt (the postwar view): local hunters
   adopted crops and herds from their neighbours. The record: the earliest farmers from Greece to
   Germany, Spain and Britain carried at least nine-tenths of their ancestry from a population
   related to Anatolian farmers; the Alpine Iceman's closest living relatives are Sardinians, the
   islanders least touched by later movements; farmers and hunter-gatherers lived side by side for
   two thousand years before mixing much. Answer: farming came into Europe with migrants. Seam (L10b,
   a real reversal): from the postwar consensus one expects continuity of population with change of
   culture; the record shows the population changing.
3. **What arrived after the farmers?** The anomaly: northern Europeans today carry an ancestry that
   neither the farmers nor the western hunter-gatherers had — predicted statistically as a "ghost"
   population of north Eurasia, then found in a 24,000-year-old Siberian boy. Where did it come
   from? Answer, in four facts: (a) about 5,000 years ago (c. 3000 BC) the Yamnaya, herders of the
   Pontic–Caspian steppe north of the Black and Caspian seas, spread over the steppe with the wheel,
   the wagon and the horse, living on the move and burying their dead in mounds (kurgans); (b) they
   were themselves a recent, roughly equal mixture of eastern European hunter-gatherers and a people
   from the south related to the early farmers of Armenia and Iran; (c) from about 4,900 years ago
   the Corded Ware culture (named for pots marked with twine) spread from Switzerland to Russia, and
   its people in Germany drew about three-quarters of their ancestry from Yamnaya-related groups;
   (d) a few centuries later the Bell Beaker culture spread across western Europe by imitation in
   Iberia but by migration into Britain, where within a few centuries about nine-tenths of the
   ancestry of Stonehenge's builders was replaced. So the same pottery can travel with or without
   people, and DNA tells which. Why could thinly spread herders displace dense farmers? State the
   two candidate answers as candidates: an unfilled niche (northern forests turned to grassland) and
   disease (plague bacteria found in steppe skeletons of the period) — "a possible answer", in
   Reich's own hedge. "Replacement" here is a genetic measure (whose descendants make up the later
   population), not a description of how — violence, disease, or differences in survival — and the
   lesson says so.
4. **Kossinna, the Nazis and the reversal (★-able set piece, but keep it in the core: it is the
   political stake).** Early twentieth-century archaeology (Kossinna) equated an archaeological
   culture with a people, placed the origin of the Germans in the Corded Ware culture, and claimed
   eastern territories on that basis; the Nazi state used the method; postwar archaeology reacted
   with deep suspicion of any migration. The DNA result vindicated migration — but against
   Kossinna's content: the Corded Ware people came from the east, from the Russian steppe, and had
   not lived in central Europe before. The 2015 episode (co-authors resigning over the comparison
   until the paper stressed that difference) shows the political pressure on the finding from both
   directions.
5. **Where did the Indo-European languages come from?** The puzzle: Sanskrit, Greek, Latin and the
   Germanic, Celtic, Slavic and Iranian languages descend from one ancestor (William Jones's 1786
   sentence, quoted). Two hypotheses: farmers from Anatolia carried them ~9,000 years ago (a
   language shift on a continental scale needs a mass migration, and that one was known); or
   herders from the steppe carried them later (every surviving branch except the extinct Anatolian
   languages shares words for wheel, axle and harness pole, and wheeled wagons appear only about
   6,000 years ago). DNA cannot record what anyone spoke; it can show who moved. Since the
   steppe-related migration reached every region where Indo-European languages were spoken, the
   steppe hypothesis is strengthened for most branches — "theory is always trumped by data" is
   Reich's phrase for the Anatolian hypothesis's loss of its best argument. What remains open: the
   first homeland of the parent language (Reich's 2018 guess was south of the Caucasus; the brief
   flags later revision — say "unsettled"). Grade the claims: migration (established), language
   carried by it (probable, inferred), homeland (open).
6. **India: invasion, mixture and caste.** The Rig Veda (anchor: hymns composed in an early form of
   Sanskrit in north-west India in the second millennium BC) has the arya fighting the dasa. Since
   the nineteenth century an "Aryan invasion" was read into it — by European scholars, by colonial
   administrators, and later rejected by Hindu nationalists (Hindutva: the ideology that Indian
   civilisation owes nothing essential to outsiders). The genetic record: every group in mainland
   India is a mixture of two ancestral populations as different as Europeans and East Asians, given
   neutral names (Ancestral North Indians, Ancestral South Indians) in a negotiation the lesson
   reports as a case of science naming under political pressure; the West-Eurasian–related share
   runs from about 20 to 80 percent; the mixing happened about 4,000–2,000 years ago, after the fall
   of the Indus cities; ANI ancestry is higher in groups of higher traditional rank and came more
   through men than women; and the endogamous groups (jatis) have been closed for thousands of years
   — one group (the Vysya) could not have absorbed even 1 percent outsiders per generation without
   erasing its signal. Two conclusions, both against a popular belief: caste endogamy is ancient,
   not a colonial creation; and about half of the ancestry of Indians today came from migrations
   from Iran and the steppe. India and Europe show the same two-step history (Fig. 18 idea): farmers
   from the Near East, then steppe herders who probably brought Indo-European speech, with Brahmin
   groups — custodians of the Sanskrit texts — carrying extra steppe ancestry.
7. **What the evidence does to the race theories — the test (the lesson's centre of gravity).**
   Put Grant's and Chamberlain's claims (Ref) to the new record one at a time, as a scorecard, with
   the seam said plainly: the evidence supports part of what the theorists claimed and refutes the
   part they cared about most.
   - Supported, in part: mass migrations of herders from the east carried Indo-European speech into
     Europe and India (Grant placed his Nordic homeland in "the forest region of eastern Germany,
     Poland and Russia, together with the grasslands which stretched from the Ukraine eastward into
     the steppes south of the Ural" — the grasslands half is close to the Yamnaya region); the
     spread was led by men and left more male than female ancestry (Grant's "thin layer of ruling
     classes" has a genetic echo, and Reich himself calls the finding "uncomfortable support" for some
     of the old ideas, l.1058).
   - Refuted: a race formed "in isolation" in the north and fixed "in Neolithic times if not
     earlier" (the Yamnaya formed about 5,000 years ago out of a northern and a southern people);
     "all the blondness in the world is derived from this source" (the earliest known blond-hair
     variant is in a Siberian of 17,000 years ago; light skin came with Anatolian farmers; the first
     European hunter-gatherers had blue eyes and dark skin — the northern European look is a Bronze
     Age composite of three sources); purity of descent anywhere (every population sampled,
     including those that spread the languages, was a recent mixture); the German homeland of the
     Corded Ware (it came from the steppe).
   - Untouched: Chamberlain's own fallback — "Though it were proved that there never was an Aryan
     race in the past, yet we desire that in the future there may be one" — is a programme, not a
     historical claim; no finding about the past can refute a will about the future; it can only
     remove the historical warrant he also claimed (Exercise 5). Gobineau's degeneration by mixture
     (Ref): if every population, the creative ones included, was a mixture throughout, mixture
     cannot be what separates rising from falling civilisations. Renan's premise that every European
     people is mixed (Ref) is confirmed.
   - Undecided by this evidence: anything about the mental qualities of the migrants, or why they
     prevailed. Seam to link 8: the same scientist who demolished purity draws a further conclusion
     that the race theorists' critics did not expect.
8. **The weighing: pure races and "pots, not people" are both refuted; Reich's inference is open.**
   (Heading as a claim; the reconstruction follows.) First state verdict (i) in the course's voice, from
   section 7: **established**, with the strongest objection (the old picture partly confirmed) and why it
   fails. Then Reich's argument, labelled contested. Reconstruct in
   steps, at full strength. (a) The consensus since the 1940s (race a social category) was hardened
   in 1972 by the finding that about 85 percent of human genetic variation lies within populations,
   with the conclusion that racial classification has no genetic significance; Reich calls what
   followed an "orthodoxy" that academics defended by "obfuscat[ing]". (b) "Ancestry" is a precise
   measure, not a euphemism. (c) Traits shaped by many genes can still differ on average between
   populations: selection on height within a few thousand years; a predictor built from 74 variants
   in people of European ancestry separates those likely to finish twelve years of school (96 vs 37
   percent between top and bottom twentieths); in Iceland the predictor changed measurably within a
   century. (d) Populations separated tens of thousands of years ago, enough time for selection, so
   "it seems a bad bet" that no average differences in cognitive or behavioural traits exist. (e)
   But the direction is unknown: the serial-founder picture of old separated races is refuted, today's
   populations formed largely in the last 5,000 years, so "our expectations are most likely wrong";
   the sprinting example — the over-representation of West African–descended runners among elite
   sprinters is predicted equally by a higher mean or by a wider spread (West Africans carry about a
   third more genetic diversity), and the two hypotheses predict opposite things at the other tail.
   (f) The moral conclusion: commit to equal rights regardless, as societies already do for average
   differences between the sexes. Reich rejects both the "orthodoxy" and the scientists who say they
   know the differences and that they match old stereotypes (he applies a label to them in 2018;
   quote it with attribution or not at all).
   - **The newest datum on step (c) (dossier §5, abstract only).** A 2026 study of about 15,800
     ancient West Eurasian genomes reports directional change, over ten thousand years, in combinations
     of variants that today predict higher scores on cognitive tests — evidence that selection on such
     scores within one region is detectable. State its own limits in the same paragraph: the authors
     say it is unclear how these variants related to what was adaptive in the past; it compares ancient
     and modern West Eurasians, not present-day continental populations; and it does not test the
     neutrality assumption behind the upper bound of `admixture-and-polygenic-scores` (Ref), only shows
     that the assumption can fail. It supports Reich's premise that selection had time to act; it says
     nothing about the direction or size of any present difference between populations.
   - **OBJECTION (from the egalitarian side), at full strength:** steps (c)–(d) move from findings
     about individuals within one ancestry group to averages between groups — the step whose hidden
     premise `heritability-within-and-between-groups` (Ref) laid out; predictors built in Europeans lose
     most of their power in other ancestries (`pgs-portability`, Ref `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`);
     one strand of (c), the selection-on-height evidence, was weakened in 2019 when the signals were
     shown to be inflated by uncorrected population structure (Ref, same lesson — recalled in a clause,
     not re-taught); and a prediction that differences "will be found" is not evidence that they exist
     (the 2018 open letter; Harden, whose answer to Reich by name the reader met in
     `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`, Ref).
   - **OBJECTION (from the hereditarian side), at full strength:** step (e) is itself a prior
     asserted without evidence — the measured phenotypic gaps of the previous lesson (Ref
     `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`) are data, not stereotypes; if differences are likely and the
     tests already show gaps, Reich owes a reason why the observed direction is uninformative; and
     his position (differences likely, causes undetermined) is close to the agnosticism for which
     The Bell Curve was attacked; and the 2026 selection result shows that the neutrality on which
     upper bounds of a genetic difference rest cannot simply be assumed.
   - **Reply / state of play:** Reich's answer to both is that high-powered studies will decide and
     that the evidence for cognitive traits does not yet exist. The course's verdict: **open**, as a
     finding: neither objection meets its burden (the observed gaps do not show their cause; "will be
     found" is not evidence, and nor is its denial), and the lesson names what would settle it
     (VERDICT). Dossier constraint: the lesson states no claim that ancient DNA or polygenic scores show
     a difference in genetic potential between present populations, and none that they show there is
     none ("Claims the lessons must NOT make", both lists). Close (`<Conclusion>`): first the verdicts in
     plain words: the idea of old pure races and the idea that cultures spread without people both fail
     on the ancient genomes (**established**); whether populations differ genetically in cognitive traits
     is **open**, and the lesson says what study would settle it. Then what the reader can now explain (the
     two migrations, the mixtures, the caste record, which race-theory claims the record supports and
     refutes, where Reich's inference stands) and the open problem handed to `geography-or-institutions`
     — whether the gaps between nations need anything inherited to explain them.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The Iceman and Sardinia** (l.946–948): a 5,300-year-old man found on an Alpine glacier in 1991,
   whose teeth show he grew up in a nearby valley, has as his closest living relatives the people of
   Sardinia, not of the Alps. Work it as a worked example of reading a population history from one
   genome: why an island population preserves the first farmers' ancestry.
2. **Corded Ware and the Yamnaya** (l.1002–1004): "about three-quarters" steppe-related ancestry in
   Germany; "a westward extension of the Yamnaya". Keeper number 1.
3. **Stonehenge's builders replaced** (l.1030; Fig. 16 caption l.1034): before the Beaker period no
   British sample had steppe ancestry; after it, every one did; ~90% of the earlier ancestry gone.
   Pair with Iberia, where the same pots spread without new people: the worked example for "pots
   and people" both being possible. Keeper number 2.
4. **The Kossinna letter** (l.1008–1010): a German co-author's 2015 letter, "We must(!)
   avoid…being compared with the so called 'siedlungsarchäologische Methode' from Gustaf
   Kossinna!", the resignations, and the paper rewritten to stress that Corded Ware came from the
   east. Quote the letter's one line (it is quoted in the book; short).
5. **William Jones, Calcutta, 1786** (l.1044; public domain): "…no philologer could examine them all
   three, without believing them to have sprung from some common source, which, perhaps, no longer
   exists." The anchor for what "Indo-European" means.
6. **Diwali night in Hyderabad** (l.1149–1151): the joint team agrees on the names ANI/ASI, making no
   claim about homeland or migration, and the sentence "No group in India can claim genetic purity."
   A document-reasoning piece: what the naming choice tells about the pressure on the finding.
7. **Grant's Nordic fatherland** (Grant Part II ch. IX, quoted above; public domain) set beside
   Reich l.929 on pigmentation — the scorecard's worked example (link 7).
8. **Chamberlain's footnote** (vol. I p. 266 n.): "Though it were proved that there never was an
   Aryan race in the past, yet we desire that in the future there may be one. That is the decisive
   standpoint for men for action." The worked example of a claim evidence cannot reach.
9. **The sprinting thought experiment** (l.1842): mean shift vs wider spread, each giving about a
   hundredfold enrichment beyond the extreme percentile; the two predict opposite things at the
   bottom tail. Reich's own example of why "direction unknown" is not evasion.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to define at first use (L11c — no more than three per section; spread them):** genome,
  mutation, ancestry (genetic) vs genealogy; mitochondrial DNA (inherited only from the mother) and
  Y chromosome (only from the father) — needed for the sex-biased finding; "ghost population";
  admixture/mixture; principal components / similarity map (call it a map of genetic similarity;
  name PCA once); steppe (the grassland belt from Hungary to Mongolia; the Pontic–Caspian part north
  of the Black and Caspian seas — locator figure); Yamnaya, kurgan, Corded Ware, Bell Beaker (each
  named after an object — say so: archaeological cultures are named for things, which is the whole
  problem); Anatolia (Asian Turkey); Indo-European; Sanskrit; Rig Veda; Hittite (the earliest
  attested Indo-European language, central Anatolia, second millennium BC); varna (the four ranked
  classes of the scriptures) vs jati (thousands of endogamous groups); endogamy; bottleneck; Brahmin;
  Hindutva; Indus Valley Civilisation (cities of c. 2600–1900 BC in today's Pakistan and north-west
  India); Kossinna.
- **Dating convention:** Reich writes "years ago"; concepts.yaml uses BC. Pick BC/AD in prose and
  give "about 5,000 years ago" once as the bridge (Yamnaya c. 3000 BC; Corded Ware from c. 2900 BC;
  Beaker Britain c. 2500 BC; first farmers c. 6800 BC onward; ANI–ASI mixture c. 2000 BC–AD 1).
  Genetic dates depend on assumed mutation and recombination rates — say once that they carry error
  bars and that the order of events is firmer than the dates.
- **"Replacement":** in this lesson a genetic share, not a mechanism. Never imply the herders killed
  the farmers; Reich does not know how it happened. The course later teaches a political argument
  that uses the same word (`powell-raspail-and-camus`, concept `replacement-as-argument`); do NOT
  forward-Ref it from here or let the meanings blur.
- **Findings vs inference.** The migration and mixture results are taught as findings, in the
  declarative register. The argument about cognitive traits (link 8) is taught as an argument, each
  step marked as Reich's, each objection at full strength. Do not let the authority of the first half
  pass to the second, in either direction (neither "the man who disproved race science now says…"
  nor "even the geneticist who…").
- **"Uncomfortable support":** do not suppress the part of the record that agrees with Grant (male-led
  migration from the east carrying the languages). Balance requires stating it; Reich states it.
- **Chamberlain is not refuted by DNA, Grant largely is.** Keep the distinction (link 7): a historical
  claim vs a programme.
- **What the book is and is not.** A first-person scientific memoir from 2018. The field moved fast:
  the Indus genome (2019), the homeland question (2022–25), the height-selection correction (2019).
  The writer states the book's claims as of 2018 and adds a later result only after verifying it,
  in one sentence, with the paper in References.
- **Sampling skew:** by late 2017 about 90% of published ancient genomes were West Eurasian (summary,
  ch. 12). Say once that the European story is the best documented, which is why the lesson is about
  Europe and India.
- **Labels.** Reich applies condemning labels to named scientists, to Nazi ideology and to Hindutva;
  the open letter and the critics apply labels to Reich. Register rule: The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Do not re-teach:** Grant's three-race scheme, Chamberlain's thesis, Gobineau's ten civilizations,
  heritability, the Bell Curve's argument, the within/between point and the default hypothesis
  (`heritability-within-and-between-groups`), polygenic scores, their portability and the 2019
  height-selection correction (`admixture-and-polygenic-scores`), Harden's position
  (`gould-and-harden-on-heredity`) — each recalled with a `<Ref>` and a clause.
- **Dossier thin spots:** Reich's book has no images in the text file (captions only, l.1964–2014
  credit the sources); the 2019–2025 papers are outside the dossier. Ch. 10's sex-bias material is
  rich but optional.
- **Copyright:** Reich is in copyright — one sentence per quotation at most; Grant, Chamberlain and
  Jones are public domain.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (warm-up):** order and date to the millennium the first farmers in
   Europe, the Yamnaya, the Corded Ware, the Beaker arrival in Britain, the ANI–ASI mixture, and place
   Grant's book (1916) and Jones's sentence (1786) against them; say which of these Grant could have
   known and which he dated (his Nordic descent "between 2000 and 1500 B.C.").
2. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Ancient DNA has proved that the Yamnaya spoke the first Indo-European
   language." Name the step that fails (DNA records movement, not speech; the inference to language is
   probable for most branches and the homeland is open) and state what it does establish.
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** Beaker pots in Iberia and in Britain — same objects, different ancestry.
   State under what conditions an archaeologist may infer people from pots, and what the Iberian case
   shows when that condition fails.
4. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill on Grant (Part II ch. IX, the "Nordic fatherland" passage,
   quoted):** metadata first (New York lawyer and conservationist, 1916, a book arguing for
   immigration restriction); predict what he must claim; then records / argues / omits; then sort each
   claim into supported / refuted / not reachable by the genetic record, citing the fact that decides
   it. Solution uses link 7.
5. **Derivation the lesson did not do (E6c):** show why the finding that no ancient population was
   pure leaves Chamberlain's "we desire that in the future there may be one" standing as a programme,
   and name the kind of argument (moral, not historical) that would have to answer it; connect to
   `chamberlain-and-grant` (Ref).
6. **Research-shaped (E6d):** design the study that would tell Reich's two sprinting hypotheses apart
   (higher mean vs wider spread) for any trait: what you would measure, in whom, and which tail
   discriminates; then name the obstacle that arises when the trait is cognitive and the predictor
   was built in one ancestry group (Ref `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`: `pgs-portability`).
7. **Synthesis, spaced (E3):** Renan argued in 1882 (Ref `renan-what-is-a-nation`) that because every
   European people is of mixed blood, race cannot be the basis of a nation. Does the genetic record
   vindicate his premise, his conclusion, both or neither? Write a claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph;
   the solution separates the empirical premise (confirmed) from the inference to what a nation is
   (not decidable by genetics) and notes Barrès's answer that a nation is accepted, not chosen.
9. **Attack the verdict:** The lesson holds as **established** that the ancient genomes refute the old
   race theories. State the strongest case against that verdict: Grant placed the homeland near the
   steppe, the spread was male-led and left a ruling layer, and Reich himself calls this "uncomfortable
   support". Then say what evidence would be needed to overturn the verdict. The solution separates what
   the theories claimed as foundation (purity, fixity since the Neolithic, a northern homeland) from what
   was confirmed, and names the overturning evidence: a population sampled across millennia without
   admixture, whose descendants carried the languages.
10. **FORWARD (E7) → `geography-or-institutions`:** the Yamnaya-related herders, a thinly spread
   people, replaced most of the ancestry of central and north-western Europe within a few centuries. A
   race theorist of 1916 would have explained it by the conquerors' inborn qualities. List the other
   kinds of cause the lesson's own evidence points to (the wheel, the wagon and the horse; plague
   bacteria in steppe skeletons; an open grassland niche) and sort them into immediate means and deeper
   conditions that put those means on one side. The solution states that the next lesson makes exactly
   this distinction — proximate and ultimate causes — the basis of an explanation of why Eurasians
   conquered the Americas, with a `<Ref>`.

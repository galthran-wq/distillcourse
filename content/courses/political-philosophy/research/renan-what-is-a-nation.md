# Research brief: renan-what-is-a-nation (Renan's What Is a Nation?: the daily plebiscite and the racial writings)

status: planned
module: crowd-race-nation
type: core
goal: Analyze Renan's argument that a nation is a soul made of shared memory and present consent rather than race, language or religion, and set it against his own racial hierarchy of 1871

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation`: `volk` (a people as a community of
  language, custom and memory prior to any state), `plurality-of-cultures`, and Fichte's
  `language-as-nation` — a living original language makes a people one and gives it a mission;
  "where a separate language is found, there a separate nation exists" (Twelfth Address).
  That thesis is exactly the one the 1870 annexation of Alsace invoked, and the lecture
  refutes it by name-free counterexample. From `gobineau-inequality-of-races` and
  `chamberlain-and-grant`: `racial-theory-of-history` — blood as the cause of a people's
  worth and its decline — in its 1853 form and its later Teutonic/Nordic forms. From
  `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`: Taine's diagnosis of 1789 as abstract reasoning
  about man in general (`esprit-classique`), which makes "the nation exists by itself" a
  suspect inheritance of the Revolution. From `burke-reflections` (one clause): the
  partnership of the dead, the living and the unborn. From `maistre-and-bonald`
  (`no-such-thing-as-man`): only historically formed peoples exist.
- **Contributes:** The one move: a definition of the nation by ELIMINATION. Every material
  criterion on offer in 1882 — dynasty, race, language, religion, interest, geography — is
  tested against a nation that exists without it (Switzerland, the United States, France
  itself) or a group that has it and is no nation (England and America, Spain and Spanish
  America, the Zollverein); what survives is a "spiritual principle" of two parts, a shared
  past of memories (much of it forgotten or misremembered) and the present will to go on
  living together — "a daily plebiscite". The lesson then reads this lecture against the
  same author's 1871 book, where races are ranked as masters and workers, and asks whether
  the consent principle and the hierarchy can be held together, and on what terms.
- **Hands off:** (i) The objection from inheritance — that a nation is not chosen but
  received, and that consent is a philosopher's abstraction — is stated here at full
  strength (Renan's own 1869 text supplies it) and becomes the next lesson's doctrine:
  `barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism` (`la-terre-et-les-morts`; Barrès's rewriting of
  Renan's own formula with "an ancient cemetery" in place of "a legacy of memories"). (ii)
  The plebiscite's problem with people who did not choose — the minority that votes no, the
  colonised who were never asked, the Jew whose belonging another man denies — is carried
  into `dreyfus-affair-as-test`. (iii) Forward Ref: `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses` (summary
  index 32, ch. XIV.8) re-reads the daily plebiscite as a vote on a common FUTURE rather
  than on a shared past (`nation-as-project`). (iv) Later: `the-nation-and-its-defenders`
  (Scruton's and Miller's pre-political loyalty) and `hazony-virtue-of-nationalism`.
- **Spine step:** "The late nineteenth century looks beneath institutions for what moves
  peoples: the crowd (Taine, Le Bon), the race (Gobineau, Chamberlain, Grant), the nation as
  will or as inheritance (Renan, Barrès, Maurras) — and the Dreyfus Affair puts them to the
  test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "In 1871 Germany annexed Alsace on the ground that its
people spoke German and were German by descent; the Alsatians wanted to stay French. Eleven
years later, at the Sorbonne, a scholar of Semitic languages asks what a nation is, if not
race, language, religion, interest or geography — and his answer must account for the
Swiss with four languages and the Alsatians with the 'wrong' one. The same man had written,
after the defeat, that some races are made to rule and others to work. The lesson follows
the lecture and then reads it against the earlier book."

(NB the opening says "four languages"; the lecture says Switzerland has "three languages,
two religions, three or four races" (l.57) and the 1871 letter "trois langues". Romansh was
made a national language only in 1938. The writer should state "three languages" from the
text when quoting and leave the opening as fixed — or see the course.yaml recommendation in
the final report.)

Anchors (L11): Ernest Renan (1823–1892), Breton, trained for the priesthood, left the
seminary, philologist of Hebrew and the Semitic languages, author of a Life of Jesus (1863)
that Catholic opinion received as a scandal — one clause, no more. The Franco-Prussian War
(1870–71: France defeated at Sedan, the emperor captured, Paris besieged; the Treaty of
Frankfurt, May 1871, ceded Alsace and part of Lorraine to the new German Empire). The
Sorbonne (the University of Paris's faculty building). David Friedrich Strauss (German
theologian, author of a Life of Jesus, with whom Renan exchanged open letters in 1870–71 —
NOT Leo Strauss of `strauss-natural-right-and-history`; say so in a clause).

The newcomer's problem: two peoples claim the same province, one by the language and
descent of its inhabitants, the other by their wish. Which claim is a right? To answer, one
must say what a nation is.

1. **Is a nation simply what a dynasty assembled?** Obvious attempt (the older diplomats'
   answer): a nation is the provinces a royal house gathered by war, marriage and treaty;
   it ends with the dynasty. Renan grants the history — "the borders of France in 1789 had
   nothing natural or necessary about them"; the Capetian house added them piece by piece
   (l.61). Why it fails: Switzerland and the United States have no dynasty; France cut off
   its king's head in 1793 and remained a nation (l.63, the operation compared to keeping a
   body alive after removing its brain and heart). Answer: "besides dynastic right there is
   national right" (l.65) — on what criterion? This is the question the lecture exists to
   answer.
2. **Race?** Obvious attempt, and the German case of 1870: the German family has the right
   to take back the scattered members of Germandom "even when those members do not ask to
   rejoin" — the right of the race over the province is stronger than the inhabitants'
   right over themselves (l.67). Renan's replies, in order: (a) history — Rome and
   Christianity drove race out of government for centuries; the barbarian kingdoms and the
   Treaty of Verdun (843) drew lines with no thought of race; "France is Celtic, Iberian,
   Germanic; Germany is Germanic, Celtic and Slav" (l.71–73); (b) fact — "there is no pure
   race", and "the noblest countries … are those where the blood is most mixed" (l.75); (c)
   a distinction the reader needs: the philologist's "race" (a group that shares a language
   and history) and the anthropologist's (descent by blood, skull shapes) do not coincide —
   "race, as we historians understand it, is something that is made and unmade" (l.77;
   Jersey and the Norman coast, identical in the eleventh century, different now); (d)
   prudence — the argument from race will be turned on its users: the Slavs will read the
   Slavonic village names of Saxony (l.79), and "the frontiers of states would follow the
   fluctuations of science" (l.81). Seam: the reader arrives from `gobineau-inequality-of-races`
   and `chamberlain-and-grant` (Ref) expecting race to be the strongest candidate; Renan
   does not deny that races exist or differ — he denies that politics may be built on them.
   Hold that distinction; link 7 needs it.
3. **Language?** Obvious attempt — Fichte's (Ref `herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation`, one
   clause): a living language makes a people one. Renan: "Language invites union; it does
   not force it" (La langue invite à se réunir; elle n'y force pas, l.83). Counterexamples
   both ways: the United States and England, Spanish America and Spain share a language and
   are not one nation; Switzerland, "made by the assent of its different parts", has three
   (l.83). Languages are "historical formations that tell little about the blood of those
   who speak them" — Prussia spoke Slav a few centuries ago, Wales speaks English, Egypt
   Arabic (l.85). And "before French culture, German culture, Italian culture, there is
   human culture" (l.87). Answer: language is evidence of history, not a title to a
   province.
4. **Religion, interests, geography?** Compressed (one paragraph each, they fall fast):
   religion was the city's own cult in Athens — swearing at the altar of Aglauros was
   swearing to die for the city (l.89, footnote l.126) — but today "one can be French,
   English, German, while being Catholic, Protestant, Jewish, or practising no cult" (l.91);
   interests make commercial treaties, and "a Zollverein is not a fatherland" (l.93: the
   German customs union of 1834, anchor in a clause); natural frontiers are "the most
   arbitrary and most fatal" doctrine, since mountains separate but rivers join, and with
   it "one justifies every violence" (l.95). Close the elimination with the lecture's own
   tally (l.97) — and make the reader see what kind of argument this has been: each
   candidate is refuted by ONE nation that exists without it or ONE group that has it and is
   not a nation. That is the method; the figure (Figure 2) shows it.
5. **Then what is left?** The positive definition (l.101–105), quoted and parsed: "A nation
   is a soul, a spiritual principle. Two things, which in truth are one, constitute this
   soul … One is in the past, the other in the present. One is the common possession of a
   rich legacy of memories; the other is present consent, the desire to live together, the
   will to continue to make the most of the heritage received undivided." Then why the past
   matters ("man does not improvise himself"; the cult of ancestors is "the most legitimate
   of all"); why suffering binds more than glory ("in national memories, mourning is worth
   more than triumph, for it imposes duties", l.103); and the summary: "a great solidarity,
   constituted by the feeling of the sacrifices one has made and of those one is disposed
   to make again" — "The existence of a nation is (pardon the metaphor) a daily plebiscite"
   (un plébiscite de tous les jours, l.105). Define plebiscite at first use (a direct
   popular vote on a single question) and say why Renan asks pardon: in 1882 the word meant
   the plebiscites by which Napoleon III had ratified his coup and his empire (1851, 1852,
   1870) — present this as the likely reason, not as Renan's stated one. The consequence he
   draws at once: "a nation has no more right than a king to say to a province: 'You
   belong to me, I take you'"; "a province, for us, is its inhabitants"; "the wish of
   nations is, in the end, the only legitimate criterion" (l.105). This is the answer to
   the opening: Alsace's wish settles it. Supply the 1871 sentence that says so outright:
   "Alsace is German in language and race; but it does not wish to be part of the German
   state; that settles the question" (Nouvelle lettre, p. 198).
6. **What the definition needs that sounds like its opposite: forgetting.** Obvious
   expectation: a nation built on memory needs good history. Renan: "Forgetting, and I
   would even say historical error, are an essential factor in the creation of a nation,
   and so the progress of historical studies is often a danger for nationality" (l.53);
   "unity is always made brutally" — northern and southern France were joined by "an
   extermination and a terror continued for nearly a century" (the Albigensian crusade,
   13th c.); "every French citizen must have forgotten Saint Bartholomew [the 1572
   massacre of Protestants in Paris], the massacres of the Midi in the thirteenth
   century"; no French family can prove Frankish descent (l.55). Answer: the "legacy of
   memories" is a selected, partly forgotten legacy — `shared-forgetting`. The seam must
   say the reversal plainly: one expects a doctrine of memory to praise the historian; this
   one fears him. This is also where the definition is most exposed: if memory is selected,
   who selects? (hand to link 8's objection).
7. **The second Renan.** The problem the reader now has: the man who said "man is the
   slave neither of his race nor of his language" (l.111) wrote in 1871 that "the conquest
   of a country of inferior race by a superior race, which settles there to govern it, has
   nothing shocking about it", that "nature has made a race of workers, the Chinese race …;
   a race of labourers of the soil, the negro …; a race of masters and soldiers, the
   European race", and "Regere imperio populos, that is our vocation" (RIM pp. 92–94; the
   Latin is Virgil's "to rule the peoples by command", Aeneid VI — gloss once). Quote it as
   the record, at the length needed to see its argument (colonisation as an outlet for a
   warrior class that would otherwise turn socialist), not as a gotcha. Three readings,
   each stated at strength, the reader left to weigh:
   (a) **Inconsistency** — consent cannot be a principle for Alsatians and not for
       Algerians; the lecture's universal premise ("reason, justice, the true, the
       beautiful, which are the same for all", l.79) condemns the 1871 hierarchy.
   (b) **Two domains, one doctrine** — the reconciliation Renan's own text offers in the
       Nouvelle lettre (p. 197): "we reject as a fundamental error of fact the equality of
       human individuals and the equality of races; the higher parts of humanity must
       dominate the lower … But the European nations as history has made them are the peers
       of a great senate in which every member is inviolable." Race is real for the scholar
       and ranks peoples; among the historic nations of Europe it has no political
       application, and the plebiscite governs; outside that senate, conquest is
       permitted. The 1882 sentence "the study of race is capital for the scholar … It has
       no application in politics" (l.77) is consistent with this. On this reading the
       lecture never retracts 1871 because it does not need to: its domain is Europe.
   (c) **Development** — between 1871 and 1882 the consent principle hardened from one
       argument among several (race, language and history still appear beside it in 1871)
       into the whole definition; the 1882 text is the mature view and its universal
       premises are what later readers used against the colonial passages.
   The course does not choose; it requires the reader to state what each reading must
   explain (Exercise 4). Keep it as the record: Renan never published a retraction of
   the 1871 passages.
8. **The OBJECTION at full strength, and where the lesson concedes.** The strongest
   objection comes from the right and Renan wrote it himself, in 1869 (RIM p. 303): if the
   nation is the present will of its citizens, how can one deny the right of secession?
   "The truth is that a nation is something other than the collection of the units that
   compose it, that it cannot depend on a vote, that it is in its own way an idea, an
   abstract thing, superior to particular wills." Three sharpened forms: (i) secession and
   crumbling — Renan concedes the point in 1882 ("no principle should be pushed to excess",
   "nations are not eternal … a European confederation will probably replace them", l.107);
   (ii) the plebiscite is never held — the Alsatians were not consulted in 1871, and Renan
   could only predict that a vote "would give an immense majority for France" (Lettre p.
   181); a "daily" plebiscite is a metaphor for a feeling, not a procedure, so it cannot
   settle a disputed border as he says it should; (iii) the inheritance objection — a man
   is born into a nation as into a family and does not consent to it; the "legacy" half of
   the definition is not chosen, and Renan's own "the ancestors made us what we are" (l.101)
   concedes it. From the left and liberal side: (iv) who counts as the "inhabitants" who
   are consulted — the 1871 text excludes the colonised from the senate of peers, so the
   principle's universality is in question (link 7 (a)). Where the lesson concedes: the
   definition is a criterion of LEGITIMACY (a province may not be taken against its will),
   not a procedure that founds nations; Renan himself presents it as a "modest empirical
   solution" that the "transcendents of politics" will laugh at and that the future may
   vindicate — "the way to be right in the future is, at certain hours, to know how to
   resign oneself to being out of fashion" (l.111). Hand-off: objection (iii) is the next
   lesson's starting point.

Headings as claims (suggested): "Dynasties made the map, but nations survived them"; "Race
is made and unmade, so it cannot draw borders"; "Language invites union; it does not force
it"; "What survives the elimination: memory and consent"; "A nation must forget"; "The
racial hierarchy of 1871"; "What a daily plebiscite cannot do".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The elimination, worked** (l.61–97): a five-row worked `<Example>` the reader can audit
   — for each candidate, the claim, the counterexample, the sentence of the lecture. Then
   the method named in one line: a necessary condition is refuted by one case without it,
   a sufficient one by one case with it and no nation.
2. **Jersey and the Norman coast** (l.77): identical in origin, seven hundred years apart
   under different crowns, now "entirely dissimilar" — the concrete case of race "made and
   unmade".
3. **"Tâter le crâne"** (l.79): "one has no right to go through the world feeling people's
   skulls and then taking them by the throat, saying: 'You are our blood; you belong to
   us!'" — and the prediction that the Slavs would read the village names of Saxony. Pair
   with the 1871 place-name reductio (Vienna, Worms, Mainz are Gaulish names, p. 202).
4. **The definition parsed** (l.101–105): typeset whole in a `<Quote>` (French and English),
   then each clause tied to what it answers: "soul, spiritual principle" (against every
   material criterion of Section II); "legacy of memories" (past; against improvisation);
   "present consent" (present; against race's "primordial right"); "daily plebiscite"
   (renewal; against the once-for-all founding act).
5. **The Spartan chant** (l.101): "We are what you were; we shall be what you are" — "in
   its simplicity the abridged hymn of every fatherland". Read the chant's two tenses as
   the two halves of the definition.
6. **Saint Bartholomew must be forgotten** (l.53–55): the shared-forgetting passage with
   its examples; the reader sees that the historian, the nation's chronicler, is also its
   danger.
7. **The Alsace sentence and the racial passage side by side** (Nouvelle lettre p. 198;

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms and anchors (L11), with where:** Alsace and Lorraine (opening; a locator map,
  Figure 1); the Treaty of Frankfurt (1871) and the Franco-Prussian War (opening); the
  Sorbonne (opening); dynasty/Capetians (link 1: the French royal house from 987); the
  Treaty of Verdun (843, the division of Charlemagne's empire among his grandsons — link 2);
  philologist vs anthropologist (link 2: define both senses of "race"; "brachycephalic /
  dolichocephalic" = round- and long-headed, the skull-index classification of the day,
  one clause); Zollverein (link 4); natural frontiers (link 4: the doctrine that a nation's
  borders are fixed by rivers and mountains — the Rhine for France); plebiscite (link 5,
  with the Bonapartist connotation); Saint Bartholomew's Day massacre (1572) and the
  Albigensian crusade (13th c.) (link 6); "Regere imperio populos" (link 7, Virgil); D. F.
  Strauss vs Leo Strauss (where the 1870–71 letters first appear). No more than three new
  terms per section — the religion/interest/geography section is the danger point.
- **Chronology trap:** the lesson follows `chamberlain-and-grant` in course order, but
  Chamberlain (1899) and Grant (1916) are LATER than the lecture. Renan answers the race
  argument in its 1870 German form and Gobineau's (1853); the reader may recall the later
  forms with a Ref, but the text must never say Renan replies to Chamberlain or Grant.
- **"Switzerland with four languages"** (the opening): the lecture says three. Do not
  correct the fixed opening in the body; quote the text accurately where it is quoted.
- **The lecture's own racial vocabulary:** l.77 speaks of "Aryan" and "Semitic" groups as
  the philologist's races and of the "zoological" origins of mankind. Gloss once that these
  were the categories of comparative philology in the 1880s; neither adopt nor sneer.
- **"Soul" is not mysticism:** the "spiritual principle" is defined by two observable
  things (memories held in common, a will to continue); say so, since the word misleads.
- **Not a gotcha:** course.yaml notes require the racial writings be "taught as part of the
  record, not as a gotcha". The three readings in link 7 are each stated at strength; no
  verdict in the course's voice. The course passes
  no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own
  voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside
  a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Do not re-teach** Fichte's living language, Herder's Volk, Gobineau's blood, Burke's
  partnership, Maistre's "no such thing as man" — each a clause and a `<Ref>`.
- **Do not overclaim the plebiscite's influence:** it became a commonplace of
  self-determination (1919 plebiscites in Schleswig and elsewhere) — if mentioned at all,
  as a fact in one clause without asserting direct descent from the lecture.
- **Translation:** the course renders the French itself; keep "une âme, un principe
  spirituel", "plébiscite de tous les jours", "Tu m'appartiens, je te prends", "L'oubli, et
  je dirai même l'erreur historique", "un Zollverein n'est pas une patrie", "cela tranche
  la question" in French beside the English at first quotation. Copyright: none (Renan d.
  1892).
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers are the dates 1871 and 1882; at most "Switzerland: three
  languages, two religions, three or four races" (l.57). No population figures.
- **OCR:** RIM quotations must be checked against a clean edition (Wikisource has La
  Réforme intellectuelle et morale; Gallica the 1871 volume). The summary itself warns the
  OCR digits are unreliable.
- **Dossier thin:** Renan's philological work on the Semitic languages and his
  correspondence with Gobineau are not in the dossier; do not characterise either beyond
  one clause.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up):** list the six candidates Renan eliminates and, for each, the
   one counterexample from the lecture; then name which candidate Fichte had defended
   (Ref `herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation`) and which Gobineau had (Ref
   `gobineau-inequality-of-races`).
2. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Renan held that race does not exist." Name the passage that
   breaks it (l.77: race is "capital for the scholar"; it is "made and unmade"; it has "no
   application in politics") and state precisely what he denied (that race confers a
   political right over a population).
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** a province votes, in a genuine plebiscite, 55 to 45 to leave.
   What does the 1882 definition imply for the 45? For the whole if the vote is repeated
   every year with different results? Use l.105–107 (the plebiscite; "no principle should
   be pushed to excess"; nations not eternal) and the 1869 secession sentence (p. 303).
   The solution shows that the definition gives a criterion of legitimacy, not a decision
   procedure, and names the case it cannot settle.
4. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill, corroboration):** two excerpts from the same 1871
   volume — the Alsace sentence (p. 198) and the racial passage (pp. 92–94). Metadata first
   (author, date, audience: French readers after defeat; the Strauss letter addressed to a
   German public). Predict, read, then answer: what does each record, argue, omit? Which
   of the three readings (inconsistency / two domains / development) does the "great
   senate" sentence (p. 197) support, and what fact would each reading need that the
   volume does not supply?
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** from Renan's premises alone, derive the rule
   for natural frontiers (l.95) — show that "the will of men" as the "fundamental right"
   makes any frontier argument from rivers or mountains void except as a concession to
   necessity, and identify the concession he does allow (strategic reasons, "within
   limits").
6. **Synthesis / counter-argument (E3, Burke):** "Renan's nation is Burke's partnership of
   the dead, the living and the unborn under another name." Using `burke-reflections` (Ref)
   and l.101–105, name what the two share (the legacy; the cult of ancestors) and the one
   element Renan adds that Burke's partnership does not need (present consent, renewable),
   and say which half of Renan's definition a Burkean would accept.
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`:** Renan's two components sit in the past
   (memories) and the present (consent). Rewrite the definition so that the plebiscite is a
   vote on something in the FUTURE, and say what a nation so defined could do that Renan's
   cannot (unite peoples with no shared past — e.g. Europe). The solution states in one
   sentence, with a `<Ref>`, that Ortega makes exactly this move in 1930 and calls it a
   "plebiscite on the future" (`nation-as-project`).

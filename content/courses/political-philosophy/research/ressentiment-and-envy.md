# Research brief: ressentiment-and-envy (Ressentiment and envy: Scheler and Schoeck on equality, and Rawls's reply)

status: planned
module: equality-after-rawls
type: core
goal: Analyze the argument that the demand for equality is driven by ressentiment and envy, in Scheler's and Schoeck's versions, and evaluate Rawls's reply that a just society produces no excusable envy

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `rawls-theory-of-justice`: the original position and the veil; the parties stipulated to be "not subject to envy" (§25); the two principles, the difference principle as permitting large inequalities that benefit the least advantaged; the natural lottery ("no one deserves his greater natural capacity"); self-respect among the primary goods; and the forward exercise that showed an envious chooser would trade gains for narrower gaps. From earlier modules: Rousseau's amour-propre — the self-love that lives on comparison and is born with society (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`); Tocqueville's observation that the more equal conditions become, the more each remaining inequality offends (`tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`, `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`); Marx's class struggle, where the poor's claim against the rich is a claim of justice (`marx-manifesto`, concept `class-struggle`); Mosca's and Pareto's elites, and the Conservative Revolution's contempt for the "masses" (`michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`, `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`); and from `the-right-after-1945` what the right kept and gave up from Nietzsche's critique of equality (concept `aristocratic-radicalism`). Nietzsche's own slave revolt in morals is held in the Nietzsche course and only recalled.
- **Contributes:** One move: the charge that egalitarianism is driven by a motive it will not admit — ressentiment (Scheler: impotent hatred that ends by falsifying values) or envy (Schoeck: a universal emotion that wants the other dispossessed) — reconstructed at full strength, and set against Rawls's reply, which does not deny that envy exists but distinguishes envy from resentment (a moral feeling that cites an injustice), argues that a just basic structure removes the conditions of excusable envy, and asks the accuser to show which premise of the theory envy supplied. The lesson then weighs the two and decides: as an argument against any principle, the envy charge fails (strong — both sides concede the logic); as a claim about motive, envy is a real but the weakest of three measured motives behind support for redistribution (on balance — four-country survey evidence and a British replication).
- **Hands off:** (0) Decided here: the envy charge cannot defeat a principle, and envy is a real but minor motive for redistribution. What passes on is the question the charge cannot decide — whether unequal talent makes unequal outcomes unjust — which the module's verdict reaches in `the-cognitive-elite`. (1) Scruton reuses the vocabulary — ressentiment, the zero-sum fallacy — and adds the conservative's positive alternative: authority, allegiance and institutions a person does not choose (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`). (2) If talents are unequal and a meritocracy sorts people by them, Rawls's second condition (visible, humiliating disparity) and the old remark that "apparent justice may be more difficult to bear than injustice" become empirical questions about a real society — the module's application (`the-cognitive-elite`). (3) Lasch inverts the charge: the resentment that matters now is the elites' contempt for the people they left behind (`lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`).
- **Spine step:** "Rawls makes equality the measure of justice; the right answers with the charge of ressentiment and envy, Scruton's authority, MacIntyre's lost tradition and Lasch's revolt of the elites — and the data on cognitive stratification test both sides"

## VERDICT

Lesson verdict, two parts (register, module 10).

- **(a) Claim judged:** egalitarian principles are envy institutionalised — offered as an argument against any such principle. **Verdict: fails. Grade: strong.** Decided by the concessions of both sides: Schoeck says that showing envy as a motive "neither refutes nor proves" a cause (ch. 12, pp. 193–199); Scheler exempts the "genuine core" of justice from his charge (the Rathenau note, l.5683–5687); Rawls's parties are stipulated non-envious and every condition of the original position has a justification that does not mention envy (§§80–81), so the accuser must first show "that the form of equality objected to is indeed unjust" (§81). A motive, however base, is a fact about the person who holds a principle, not about whether the principle is true. Strongest objection: if a principle could only have been adopted from envy — if no non-envious reason for it exists — the motive charge reaches the principle through its genealogy. It does not overturn the verdict for the difference principle, because Rawls gives non-envious reasons (impartial choice, reciprocity, self-respect) that the accuser must defeat on their own terms; the genealogical form reaches at most strict equality, which Rawls himself says "conceivably derives from this propensity" (§81) and rejects. Would be overturned by: a showing that some condition of the original position can be justified only by envy.
- **(b) Claim judged:** envy is a real motive behind support for redistribution. **Verdict: holds, as the weakest of three. Grade: on balance.** Decided by survey evidence. In thirteen studies with 6,024 participants in the US, UK, India and Israel, dispositional envy predicts support for redistribution independently of compassion and expected self-interest; a taste for fairness (procedural or distributional) has an unreliable and far smaller effect (Sznycer et al. 2017). Compassion is two to three times stronger (standardized β 0.28–0.39 against envy's 0.10–0.14; self-interest 0.18–0.30). Envy did not differ by US party, and compassion alone predicted personally aiding the poor. A British replication (Lin & Bates 2021) confirms compassion, self-interest and malicious envy (β 0.13–0.15 against compassion's 0.40), with dispositional envy non-significant until split into benign and malicious forms, and again no effect of procedural fairness. This supports "envy is one motive"; it does not support "egalitarianism is envy". Strongest objection: from the egalitarian side, self-report scales of envy measure a disposition, not the reason a respondent supports a policy, and a β of 0.10 is small; from the accuser's side, envy is the one motive no one confesses, so self-reports must understate it. The first lowers the grade to on balance rather than strong; the second cannot raise it, because an unmeasurable motive cannot carry a finding. Would be overturned by: designs that measure envy without self-report (e.g. costly choices to reduce others' payoffs) and find no link to support for redistribution — or, in the other direction, find envy the leading predictor.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): a society that taxes the rich more — justice, or a feeling it would not admit; Nietzsche on Christianity; a German philosopher in 1912 and a German sociologist in 1966 on egalitarianism, the second with evidence from many cultures; Rawls's answer in two sections of his own book. Anchors (L11): Max Scheler (1874–1928), German philosopher of the phenomenological school (one clause: the method of describing an experience as it is lived before explaining it), writing in the Germany of 1912, before the war; Helmut Schoeck (1922–1993), Austrian-born sociologist who taught in America and Germany, whose book appeared in 1966 in the decade of the Great Society and of Britain's comprehensive schools; Rawls's §§80–81 (1971), which cite both by name in their footnotes. The newcomer's problem is a problem of evidence: the charge is about a motive, and motives are not in the text of a law.

1. **Why the charge needs a vocabulary first.** Obvious attempt: ask whether the egalitarian is envious — and he denies it. Why that fails: envy is the one motive no one confesses ("did ever anybody seriously confess to envy?" — Melville, as Schoeck quotes him, ch. 10 p. 138); a denial is no evidence. So the first task is to separate the feelings the charge runs together. Build the table (figure 1) from Rawls §80 and Schoeck ch. 2, both of whom draw on Kant and Aristotle: ENVY — hostility to another's greater good though it takes nothing from me; I would accept a loss to narrow the gap. JEALOUSY — defending what I have against a rival. GRUDGINGNESS and SPITE — the better-off's wish that those below stay there, even at a cost to himself (Rawls's "converses of envy"). EMULATION — the wish to have what the other has, by rising. And RESENTMENT, the one moral feeling of the set: I resent having less because I think the other's advantage comes from an injustice — and so I must be ready to say which. Name the hinge in a sentence: the whole quarrel is whether the demand for equality is resentment (a claim of justice) or envy dressed as one. Ressentiment (the French word, kept because English "resentment" means something else) enters at link 3.
2. **The charge's ancestry, recalled.** Nietzsche had made the charge against Christianity: the weak, unable to act, take "imaginary revenge" by inverting values — what the strong are becomes "evil", what the weak are becomes "good" (`<Ref>` `nietzsche/slave-revolt-and-ressentiment`); and he extended it to democracy and socialism as "herd morality in modern dress" (`<Ref>` `nietzsche/herd-morality-in-modern-dress`). One paragraph, no quotation beyond a phrase; the reader who has not taken the Nietzsche course gets the claim in one sentence. Seam: from Nietzsche the reader expects a German conservative of 1912 to repeat the charge against Christianity. Scheler does the opposite.
3. **Scheler: what ressentiment is, and the law of where it grows.** The phenomenon first (link 3a): "a self-poisoning of the mind" caused by "the systematic repression" of revenge, hatred, malice, envy, the impulse to detract, spite — under felt impotence. Its conditions: if the affect is acted out (the servant who swears in the antechamber) or truly overcome (forgiveness), there is no ressentiment; it needs delay and powerlessness. The strongest source is existential envy: "I can forgive everything, but not that you are … that I am not you". Its work is a falsification in three stages, from the sour grapes (the fox says the grapes are sour, not that sweetness is bad) to devaluing the other, to falsifying the world, to inverting the tables of value themselves — yet the true values remain "transparently present" behind the false ones (which is how ressentiment can be diagnosed at all). Then the sociological law (link 3b), the lesson's first KEEPER claim: ressentiment spreads with the gap between a group's formal status and its factual power — slight in a caste society (no one compares himself with the lord) and slight in a democracy equal in property too; strongest "in a society like ours", where formal equality publicly recognised goes "hand in hand with wide factual differences in power, property, and education" (figure 2). The "because": formal equality gives each the right to compare himself with everyone; factual inequality denies him the means to close the gap; comparison without power is the recipe.
4. **Scheler's charge against equality — and its limits, in his own notes.** The charge at full strength: the modern rule that moral value lies only in what a person has acquired by his own effort is a ressentiment device — it devalues natural excellence and grace, which the less gifted cannot acquire, in favour of what "everyone, even the least gifted, can do"; and the doctrine of equality is "an achievement of ressentiment": "Nobody demands equality if he feels he has the strength or grace to triumph … Only he who is afraid of losing demands equality as a general principle"; "men can only be equal in their least valuable characteristics". Justice itself is redefined: the older justice treated equals alike and unequals unequally (suum cuique — to each his own; "no one can justly be tried except by his equals"); the modern treats all alike and calls any rule for a group "unjust discrimination". Then, before the reader concludes Scheler says justice is envy, his own three limits (they are part of the argument at full strength): "The idea of 'justice' as such demands no equality — it only demands equivalent conduct when the circumstances have equal value", and the claim that justice is based on envy "applies only to the falsification of the idea of 'justice' … and not to its genuine core"; "democracy" in his essay means a scale of values, not any actual political democracy, "which may very well represent an aristocratic scale of values"; and he later said he "went too far" about humanitarian love. Place Scheler's break with Nietzsche in one paragraph: genuine Christian love stoops to the weak from overflow, not from envy; modern humanitarianism (the love of "mankind" as a sum, each counting for one) is where ressentiment lives — `<Ref>` `nietzsche/jesus-and-paul` and `nietzsche/against-pity` for the position Scheler is correcting. Seam to link 5: Scheler's is a phenomenology of one modern type and a law about one modern structure; it offers no evidence beyond observation and literature. Schoeck promised evidence.
5. **Schoeck: envy as universal, and egalitarian policy as appeasement.** The thesis: envy "lies at the core of man's life as a social being" and appears "as soon as two individuals become capable of mutual comparison"; every culture condemns it; and every culture has had to manage it — the evidence is ethnographic (pick ONE case: the Lovedu, among whom the comparative forms of speech are avoided and a bright child is suspected of witchcraft; or the Dobu, for whom the stock of yams is fixed so that a man with more must have stolen them by magic — the fixed-pie belief). Three consequences at full strength. (a) The growth thesis: "the more both private individuals and the custodians of political power … are able to act as though there were no such thing as envy, the greater will be the rate of economic growth and the number of innovations" — societies grow where custom, religion (Christianity's God cannot envy; luck and fate defuse comparison) and opinion agree to ignore the envious. (b) The proportionality thesis: envy is not proportional to the size of an inequality — small gaps between near-equals provoke more than vast unattainable ones (Aristotle: we envy our peers) — so reducing inequality cannot be expected to reduce envy; the envious man "falls back on whatever difference is nearest". (c) Hence the "basic error": not the use of envy in politics, which every party practises, but the promise of a society with nothing left to envy — a programme "by definition insoluble"; policy made to appease envy allocates badly (the one number: in 1962 the American income-tax brackets above 65% yielded about 0.6% of the tax's revenue, kept, Schoeck argues, as a symbol). Then his own limit, which the lesson must state because it governs the weighing: showing that envy is a motive "neither refutes nor proves" a cause (ch. 12) — Schoeck claims to have explained the appeal of egalitarian programmes, not to have shown them unjust. And the honest flag: the summary and the course note record his thesis as close to unfalsifiable, since any demand for equality can be read as envy — hold that for link 8, where Rawls makes the same point.
6. **Rawls's reply, step one: the parties are not envious, and why that is not a dodge.** Seam: from the critics one expects Rawls to deny envy or to call it a reactionary invention; he does neither. He grants that envy exists and is collectively harmful, and that "the appeal to justice is often a mask for envy" (§81 p. 540). His structure (§80, figure 4): the argument is in two steps. First, the principles are chosen by parties assumed free of envy — not because people are, but because a choice that depended on how envious its makers happened to be would be "arbitrary", and because envy "is generally regarded as something to be avoided and feared". Second, the principles are checked: will a society that satisfies them in fact breed so much envy that it becomes "unworkable"? If yes, reconsider; if no, the first step stands. Then the reply to the conservative thesis itself (§81, citing Schoeck by name in a footnote): to show the principles spring from envy one must show that some condition of the original position arises from envy — but each has a justification that never mentions it (generality, publicity, the veil). The form of equality that might spring from envy is strict equality of all primary goods — which the difference principle rejects; it permits inequalities that benefit the worst off.
7. **Rawls's reply, step two: excusable envy and the three conditions.** The concept the lesson introduces (`excusable-envy`; note Rawls defines it at the end of §80): where a person's lesser position is so great "as to wound his self-respect", and it would be unreasonable to expect him to feel otherwise, his envy is excusable — "we can resent being made envious". The question becomes whether a just basic structure produces it. Rawls's psychological root: envy grows from "a lack of self-confidence in our own worth combined with a sense of impotence" — with a footnote to Nietzsche and to Scheler (the reader should notice that Rawls takes their psychology as true). Three conditions of hostile envy, each met by an institution (figure 5): (i) lack of self-worth — met by equal citizenship and by a public conception in which "no one supposes that those who have a larger share are more deserving from a moral point of view"; (ii) painful visibility of the gap — met by smaller disparities than history has known, by many associations that are "noncomparing groups", and by the better-off's not making "an ostentatious display"; (iii) no constructive alternative — met by the opportunities of a just society. Conclusion: "the principles of justice are not likely to arouse excusable general envy … to a troublesome extent". Then the examples that turn the charge around: the poor peasants who believe wealth is fixed — "What is said to be envy may in fact be resentment", and on their belief equal shares would be just (Schoeck's Dobu, read by Rawls); Freud's nursery, where the children's claim to equal parental love is called envy turned into justice — Rawls: one could as well call it resentment at unfair treatment; and the countercharge — "it is mere grudgingness when those better circumstanced reject the claims of the less advantaged".
8. **The two at full strength against each other: where they collide.** The strongest objection to Rawls, from Schoeck and Scheler together, stated as the course's own: Rawls's second condition relies on reducing and hiding disparities, but if envy is strongest among near-equals and does not shrink with the gap (Schoeck ch. 6), and if ressentiment is strongest exactly where formal equality meets real difference (Scheler's law — and the well-ordered society is a society of formal equality with permitted differences), then the Rawlsian society may be the very structure that breeds it; and a society that sorts people fairly by talent removes the last excuse of the unsuccessful — "apparent justice may be more difficult to bear than injustice" (Glass, quoted by Schoeck ch. 14 p. 243). Rawls's first condition has an answer to this: he denies that anyone deserves his advantages — the denial of desert is itself a protection of the less favoured's self-respect, and Rawls rejects meritocracy for that reason (§17, `rawls-theory-of-justice`). Then the sharpest turn: Scheler named as a ressentiment device the rule that moral value lies only in what the individual acquires by effort, because it devalues natural gifts; Rawls goes further and says that even effort is undeserved (§12). Is the natural lottery the most complete form of the devaluation Scheler diagnosed — or, as Rawls would answer, a refusal to rank persons by gifts at all, which leaves the gifted their gifts and their gains ("a common asset") and only denies that the gifts are a moral title? State what the texts settle: Rawls's §§80–81 do not address Scheler's ch. V.1 point, so the objection stands unanswered in Rawls's text; Rawls's nearest answer is §17's distinction between a gift and a title to what it earns, which the lesson quotes and does not credit as a reply he made to Scheler. Whether the denial of desert is right is not a question about envy; it passes to the module's verdict (`the-cognitive-elite`) and to `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`.
9. **What evidence could tell motive from principle.** Both sides concede the decisive point: Schoeck that a motive "neither refutes nor proves" a cause; Rawls that the charge and the countercharge (envy / grudgingness) "call for careful argument" and cannot "be given credence without first examining the conceptions of justice sincerely held". So the motive charge cannot settle justice; it can only raise a question of sincerity and one of stability. Sincerity: Rawls's test — is the principle applied to cases where its holder "would suffer a loss from their being followed"? Stability: an empirical question — do societies that adopt the difference principle's kind of arrangements show rising or falling envy? Neither text answers it with data; name what would (Exercise 6). The standing objection to the charge, at full strength: it can be turned against any claim — the poor's demand is envy, the rich man's refusal is grudgingness — and a charge that fits every case explains none (Schoeck's own "politically neutral"). The standing objection to the reply: Rawls's well-ordered society is an ideal, and the envy that matters is the envy of actual societies far from it. Close the section with its bearing: the charge has been reduced to two testable questions, sincerity and motive.
10. **The weighing: envy is a real motive, and it decides nothing about justice** (the VERDICT, 600–900 words). Seam: link 9 showed the charge cannot settle justice by logic; the question left is whether it is even true of motive, and that one has been measured. (i) The logical verdict first, in the course's voice: as an argument against a principle the charge fails — **strong** — because both sides concede that a motive neither refutes nor proves a cause, and Rawls's principles have non-envious justifications the accuser has not defeated; the genealogical form of the charge reaches only strict equality, which Rawls rejects. (ii) The evidence on motive: surveys in four countries (US, UK, India, Israel; 6,024 participants) in which dispositional envy predicts support for redistribution alongside compassion and self-interest, while a taste for fairness does not reliably; compassion two to three times as strong; envy no different between American parties; compassion alone predicting personal aid to the poor; a British replication that finds the same three motives, envy only in its malicious form. State the coefficients once, in a small table (figure 8), with what a β of 0.1 means in a sentence. (iii) The strongest objection on each side: self-report measures a disposition, not a reason (lowers the grade); envy is unconfessed, so self-report understates it (cannot raise a grade by what is unmeasured). (iv) Verdict: envy is a real motive behind support for redistribution, the weakest of the three measured — **on balance**; this supports "envy is one motive", not "egalitarianism is envy". Note in one sentence what the finding does to Scheler and Schoeck: their psychology is partly borne out, their charge against the principle is not; and what it does to Rawls: his reply holds, and his concession that "the appeal to justice is often a mask for envy" is borne out in part.

Conclusion (`<Conclusion>`, L10c): the reader can now tell envy from resentment and ressentiment, state Scheler's law and Schoeck's growth thesis, and give Rawls's two-step reply. The verdict in plain words: the envy charge cannot defeat a principle of justice — strong, because its own authors concede that a motive neither proves nor refutes a cause; and envy is a real motive behind support for redistribution but the weakest of three, behind compassion and self-interest — on balance, on survey evidence from four countries and a British replication. Hand off: the conservative's positive answer is not a diagnosis of the egalitarian's motive but an account of what a person owes to institutions he did not choose — the next lesson.

Headings as claims: "Envy, jealousy, spite, resentment: the charge needs a vocabulary"; "Ressentiment grows where formal equality meets real inequality"; "Scheler's charge against equality, and the core of justice he exempts"; "Schoeck: envy is universal and cannot be appeased"; "Rawls: choose without envy, then test for it"; "Excusable envy and the three conditions a just society removes"; "Where the charge and the reply collide"; "Motive does not settle justice"; "Envy is a real motive for redistribution, the weakest of three".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** "ressentiment" (French, kept untranslated because English "resentment" is Rawls's moral feeling — the two must never be conflated; say so at link 1 and again at link 3); "phenomenology" (one clause, link 3); "suum cuique" (to each his own); "excusable envy" (link 7); "well-ordered society" (recall from `rawls-theory-of-justice`: a society effectively regulated by a public conception of justice everyone accepts); "noncomparing groups"; "Lovedu"/"Dobu" (a people of the northern Transvaal, South Africa; islanders off New Guinea — one relative clause each, only the one case used); "Great Society" (Johnson's 1960s programme — only if the date anchor is used). Max three new terms per section.
- **The concept-id definition in concepts.yaml says "§81" for excusable envy; the text defines it at the end of §80 (p. 534).** Cite §80 in the lesson.
- **Do not re-teach Nietzsche** (L0e; course note): the slave revolt is one paragraph with a `<Ref>`. No Genealogy block quotation (Scheler quotes it at length; the lesson does not).
- **Do not re-teach Rawls:** the original position, the two principles, the natural lottery are recalled with a clause and `<Ref>` `rawls-theory-of-justice`. The new Rawls material here is only §§80–81.
- **Balance:** the charge and the reply both at full strength before the weighing; each author's own self-limitations included (Scheler's Rathenau note, democracy note, recantation on humanitarian love; Schoeck's "neither refutes nor proves"; Rawls's "the appeal to justice is often a mask for envy" and his admission that particular envy is "endemic to human life"). The verdict (link 10) is on the argument and on measured motive in populations; it never says that any person, party or voter is envious.
- **Period material in Scheler (summary caution):** ch. I contains sweeping claims about women, "old maids", Jews and race. Do not quote them; if the writer must mention them, say once that the essay carries prejudices of its period which the lesson does not use, and why they are irrelevant to the argument reconstructed. Schoeck's ethnography is dated (1930s–60s fieldwork) and selective; present it as his evidence, with "as he reports it", and do not generalise from it in the course's voice.
- **Contested empirical claims:** that envy is universal, that it is not proportional to inequality, that growth follows from ignoring it — Schoeck's theses, taught as arguments with his evidence and with what would test them (Exercise 6); the course grades none of these three. The one empirical claim it grades is that envy is a motive behind support for redistribution (VERDICT (b), on balance), on survey evidence, not ethnography.
- **Schoeck access:** partial — the notes and back matter (from envy.txt l.16367) were not summarised; say so in References. Do not cite a Schoeck note without checking it in the file.
- **Edition:** Rawls is quoted from the staged 1971 edition (§§80–81 pp. 530–541); the 1999 revision did not change these sections materially, but cite §§.
- **Copyright:** all three books are in copyright — a sentence or two per quotation; the lesson parses short passages.
- **Numbers (L12):** one keeper number at most — Schoeck's 1962 top-bracket figure, bound in its sentence ("brackets above 65% yielded about 0.6% of income-tax revenue in 1962, by his count") or omitted. Dates: 1887 (Genealogy, via Ref), 1912, 1966, 1971.
- **Register:** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right. This applies with special force here: neither "envy" nor "grudgingness" may be applied by the course to any actual person or party; they appear only as the authors' charges.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and classification (the one warm-up):** Classify five feelings by the table — a worker who wants a colleague's promotion revoked though it costs him nothing to keep it; a pensioner angry because a rule exempted his neighbour unfairly; a rich man who opposes a school subsidy he does not pay for; a student who works harder after seeing a friend's prize; a clerk who mocks his employer's tastes and calls his own poverty nobler — each with the word in the description that decided it (envy, resentment, grudgingness/spite, emulation, ressentiment).
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** Scheler's "Nobody demands equality if he feels he has the strength or grace to triumph" (1912/15) read WITH his Rathenau note. Author, date (pre-1914 Germany, an empire with a three-class franchise in Prussia — recall Weimar's predecessor in one clause), audience, purpose; what the passage argues and what the note withdraws; what it can be USED to establish (that Scheler distinguished a "genuine core" of justice from its ressentiment form) and what it cannot (that every demand for equality is ressentiment).
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** Apply Scheler's law to its two degenerate cells — a caste society and a democracy equal in property as well as rights. Why does the law predict little ressentiment in both, and what does that imply for anyone who wants to reduce it (abolish formal equality, or abolish factual inequality)? Then say which of these the well-ordered society of `rawls-theory-of-justice` is — neither — and what Rawls relies on instead (the denial of desert; noncomparing groups).
4. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Rawls ignored the problem of envy." Name the passages that refute it (§25's stipulation, §80's two-step argument, §81's three conditions and footnotes to Scheler and Schoeck), and then state what a critic COULD say instead (that the second step is answered by an ideal society, not an actual one).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** Rawls says the poor peasants who believe the stock of wealth is fixed are right, on that belief, to demand equal shares — "what is said to be envy may in fact be resentment". Derive the condition under which the same demand becomes envy on Rawls's own definitions (when wealth is not fixed and the demand would make everyone, the demanders included, worse off), and state which empirical fact therefore decides between Schoeck's reading of Dobu and Rawls's.
6. **Research-shaped task (E6d):** Design the evidence that would tell motive from principle for one real policy (e.g. a top income-tax rate): Rawls's sincerity test (do supporters apply it where they lose?), Schoeck's revenue test (does the rate raise money or only symbolise?), and Scheler's structural test (does support rise where formal equality and factual inequality coexist?). Say what result would count against each author.
7. **Synthesis (claim–evidence–reasoning, E3 with `rawls-theory-of-justice`):** Is Rawls's natural lottery — "even the willingness to make an effort … is itself dependent upon happy family and social circumstances" — the completion of the devaluation Scheler diagnosed in the rule that only self-acquired qualities have value, or its refusal? Argue both with the two texts (Scheler ch. V.1; Rawls §§12, 17, 48) and judge.
8. **Attack the verdict (claim–evidence–reasoning):** The lesson finds envy a real but the weakest of three motives behind support for redistribution (on balance). State the strongest objection in its best form — from either side: that self-report envy scales measure a disposition and not why anyone supports a policy (so the finding is too strong), or that envy is the one motive no one confesses (so the finding is too weak). Then say what evidence would overturn the verdict in each direction. The solution: a behavioural design — costly choices that lower others' payoffs at a cost to oneself, the "Envy option" structure of Sznycer's allocation task — linked to policy support in a population sample; no link would remove envy from the list, a link larger than compassion's would put it first; and neither result would touch verdict (a), since a motive, however large, does not decide whether a principle is just.
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `the-cognitive-elite`:** Rawls's second condition says envy is weakened when disparities are small and not painfully visible; the critic's reply says a society that sorts people fairly by ability makes inequality look deserved and so harder to bear. Suppose a country's ablest young people increasingly attend the same few universities, marry one another and live in the same districts. Which of Rawls's three conditions does this affect, in which direction, and what data would show it? The solution says the module's application reads exactly such a claim — American data on sorting by measured ability, published in 1994 — against Rawls's natural lottery and Lasch's secession of the successful, `<Ref>` `the-cognitive-elite`.

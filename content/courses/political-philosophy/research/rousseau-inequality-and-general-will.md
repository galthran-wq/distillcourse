# Research brief: rousseau-inequality-and-general-will (Rousseau: the origin of inequality and the general will)

status: planned
module: contract-revolution-reaction
type: core
goal: Analyze Rousseau's account of how society made men unequal and his solution — a sovereign people obeying only the general will — and locate its hardest step

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `locke-property-and-consent`: a state of nature already
  under law, property by labour with limits that money dissolves ("in the
  beginning all the world was America"), consent (express and tacit) as the only
  ground of government, and the open problem the last exercise set — that the
  first "this is mine" needs others to believe it. From Hobbes
  (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`): natural men as fearful, competitive,
  proud; sovereignty as absolute and indivisible; the covenant of each with
  each. From `machiavelli-discourses-republic`: `civil-religion` (Numa's
  cults). From `bodin-sovereignty`: `sovereignty` as law-making bound by no
  positive law. From the Plato module: the Legislator will recall
  `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`'s noble lie and `platos-laws-second-best-city`'s
  preambles.
- **Contributes:** One move with two halves. First, Rousseau turns the state of
  nature against Hobbes and Locke alike: both "read society into nature" —
  natural man is solitary, healthy, pitying, without reason or vice; inequality,
  property and domination are made by society, and the first contract was the
  rich man's fraud. Second, having shown that society corrupts, he designs the
  one society that would not: each gives himself wholly to all, so that he
  obeys only the general will, which is his own will as a citizen — and may be
  "forced to be free". The lesson's centre is the hardest step: how total
  alienation can be freedom, and what the general will is if it is not the sum
  of what people want.
- **Hands off:** Three objections are put in this lesson and one is left to the
  next: (a) Maistre's Hottentot — society is as old as man, the state of nature
  is a fiction (held for `maistre-and-bonald` and `war-is-divine`, stated here
  in one paragraph); (b) Constant's ancient against modern liberty — a sovereign
  people is as dangerous as any tyrant, and moderns want security, not
  participation (put at full strength here); (c) Hegel's §258 — the general will
  built from individual wills is "caprice" and ended in the Terror (stated here,
  argued in `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`). The problem that stays open is
  the one Burke takes up: if a people may frame a government for itself from
  principles, what happens when it tries — `burke-reflections` opens with Price
  claiming exactly that right in 1789.
- **Spine step:** "Government by consent (Locke) and the general will (Rousseau)
  make a revolution thinkable; Burke, Maistre and Bonald answer that no
  generation knows enough to design a society, and Hegel absorbs both sides into
  the rational state"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: the first man who fenced a plot and
said "this is mine", and found people simple enough to believe him, founded
civil society — and with it every crime and misery that followed; yet the same
author wrote the founding text of modern democracy. How did the man who thought
society corrupted men come to design a society in which each obeys only himself?

1. **What was man before society, and how could anyone know?** Hobbes and Locke
   both describe natural man and reach opposite politics (`<Ref>` both lessons,
   one clause each). Rousseau's charge: both "read society into nature" — Hobbes's
   natural man is proud, fearful, competitive, but pride needs someone to compare
   oneself with, fear needs foresight, competition needs scarcity felt as such;
   each is a social product. Obvious attempt: look at savages. Rousseau's
   method instead: "let us begin by laying facts aside" — a hypothetical
   history, like the geometers' conjectures, that asks what must be stripped
   away (the statue of Glaucus, worn by the sea until it looks like a beast).
   What remains: a solitary animal, healthy, fearless, with two pre-rational
   principles — self-love (amour de soi, care for one's own preservation) and
   pity (repugnance at another's suffering) — and two capacities that make him
   human: free agency and *perfectibility*, the power to become other than he is,
   "the source of all his misfortunes". No reason, so no vice; "a thinking man
   is a depraved animal". Heading: *Natural man is not wicked: he is not yet
   anything*.
2. **If natural man is good and self-sufficient, how did inequality begin?** The
   natural inequalities (strength, age, health) are slight and cannot be used
   — a stronger man cannot enslave a weaker who can simply walk away (the
   escaped captive). So moral inequality needs dependence, and dependence needs
   a history. The stages (Second Part): the first huts and families; a "nascent
   society" that gathers to sing and dance round a tree, where "each one began
   to look at the others and to wish to be looked at himself" — public esteem
   is the first step to inequality and the birth of *amour-propre*, the
   comparative self-love that wants to be preferred (define against amour de
   soi here, in two sentences); then metallurgy and agriculture — "iron and
   corn … civilised men, and ruined humanity" — which require division of labour
   and give land a value; then property from labour (Locke's own argument,
   `<Ref>`, turned into an accusation) and justice to protect it; then the gap
   between being and seeming, and a state of war. Heading: *Inequality has a
   history: esteem, then iron and corn, then "this is mine"*.
3. **Why did the poor agree to laws that protected the rich?** The hardest
   historical question, and Rousseau's set piece: the rich man, unable to defend
   what he has against the many, invents "the profoundest plan that ever entered
   the mind of man" — he proposes rules that will protect everyone's possessions
   equally, so that the poor guard the rich man's estate thinking they guard
   their own. "All ran headlong to their chains, in hopes of securing their
   liberty." This is Locke's consent (`<Ref>`) rewritten as fraud: a contract
   made among unequals ratifies inequality. Then the three stages of inequality
   (property and law: rich/poor; magistracy: strong/weak; arbitrary power:
   master/slave) until despotism "closes the circle" and all are equal again
   because all are nothing — and the sultan's overthrow is as lawful as his
   rule. Mark here (L10b seam) that the reader expects a return to the forest;
   Rousseau refuses it ("What, then, is to be done?") — perfectibility cannot be
   undone, so the only remedy is a society that does not corrupt.
4. **What would a legitimate society have to do?** SC I.1: "Man is born free;
   and everywhere he is in chains … What can make it legitimate?" Obvious
   grounds dismissed in I.2–I.5: the family (paternal authority ends with
   childhood), the right of the strongest (force creates no right — "the pistol
   is also a power"), slavery contracts (null), conquest. The problem (I.6):
   "find a form of association which will defend and protect with the whole
   common force the person and goods of each associate, and in which each,
   while uniting himself with all, may still obey himself alone, and remain as
   free as before." The answer: *total* alienation of each with all his rights
   to the whole community — total, because if anyone kept a right there would
   be no common judge of it and the state of nature would continue; and
   harmless, because "each, in giving himself to all, gives himself to nobody".
   Set the formula beside Hobbes's covenant (`<Ref>`): the same act (each with
   each), but the beneficiary is not a third party but the people itself, which
   therefore cannot be bound to anyone and cannot alienate what it is.
5. **What is the general will, if not what most people want?** The decisive
   distinction (II.3): the *will of all* is the sum of private wills; the
   *general will* is what remains "when the pluses and minuses cancel" — what
   each wills as a member of the whole rather than as a private person. Hence it
   is "always right" (it aims at the common good by definition) but "the people
   is never corrupted, but it is often deceived" — the vote can miss it;
   factions corrupt it, because a party's will is general for its members and
   particular for the state (the nested wills of the Political Economy). Law
   (II.6) is the general will deciding on a general object — it names no
   persons; a decree about a person is an act of magistracy, not law. The
   Legislator (II.7): since a people cannot see the good it needs before it has
   the laws that would make it see, someone outside the sovereignty must
   propose laws it then enacts — "It would take gods to give men laws", so
   Lycurgus abdicates and Moses speaks for heaven. Recall Plato's noble lie and
   the preamble (`<Ref>` kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king,
   platos-laws-second-best-city) in one clause: persuasion without power.
6. **The hardest step: how can "forced to be free" be freedom?** SC I.7: whoever
   refuses to obey the general will "will be forced to be free" — Rousseau calls
   this "the key to the working of the political machine". The obvious reading:
   a licence for tyranny. Rousseau's own argument: every citizen has two
   wills, a private one that may run against the common interest and the will
   he has as a citizen; the law he voted for (or could have voted for) is his
   own; when he is coerced into obeying it, he is held to his own will against
   his momentary appetite — "obedience to a law which we prescribe to ourselves
   is liberty" (I.8). The test case is IV.2: the outvoted citizen "was mistaken"
   about the general will; had his opinion won, he would have achieved "the
   opposite of what was my will". **Locate the difficulty here, as the goal
   requires:** (i) this works only if the majority vote really tracks the
   general will, which II.3 says it may not when factions exist — so the
   doctrine's safety depends on conditions (small state, equality, no parties,
   no representation, III.15: "the moment a people allows itself to be
   represented, it is no longer free") that no modern state meets; (ii) the
   Idealist gloss (the "real will" forced against the "actual will") is a
   reading, not the text, and the course names it as such with Cole in
   References; (iii) the step from "I am a member of the sovereign" to "the
   sovereign's decision is my will" is exactly what Hegel will call caprice
   dressed as universality. **OBJECTION (b) enters here at full strength —
   Constant (1819):** the liberty of the ancients was a share in collective
   power bought by the complete subjection of the individual to the community;
   the liberty of the moderns is security in private enjoyment under law, with
   participation only as its guarantee; Rousseau "transported into modern times
   a power which belonged to another age", and "the sovereignty of the people
   is not a principle of liberty" — a people with unlimited power is as dangerous
   as any tyrant; what matters is not who holds power but how far it reaches.
   Give Constant his second half: he also says moderns who give up political
   liberty for private peace are "no less absurd", so his quarrel is with
   unlimited sovereignty, not with self-government. The lesson concedes that
   Rousseau's own conditions (II.9–II.11, III.4: "were there a people of gods,
   their government would be democratic") already admit the point.
7. **Why does the republic need a religion, and what does that concede?** IV.8:
   religion of man (true but anti-social — "a society of true Christians would
   not be a society of men"), of the citizen (Numa's — `<Ref>`
   machiavelli-discourses-republic — social but false and intolerant), of the
   priest (two heads, Rome). Rousseau's answer: a civil profession of faith,
   fixed by the sovereign "not exactly as religious dogmas but as social
   sentiments" — a few positive dogmas (God, afterlife, the sanctity of the
   contract and the laws) and one negative dogma, no intolerance; whoever does
   not believe may be banished, whoever lies about believing put to death. The
   paradox to show, not moralise: a doctrine of consent ends by prescribing
   belief; and the Legislator's borrowed divine voice (II.7) plus the civil
   religion are what make a people able to will the laws it needs — the
   solution works only if the question that produced it is not asked again
   (the course's own statement of the Strauss point).
8. **Closing: the two Rousseaus and the two objections held over.** State
   plainly what the reader can now do: trace the thread from amour de soi to
   amour-propre to the rich man's contract to total alienation to the general
   will, and name the step where the argument bears most weight. Then the
   handoffs: (a) Maistre's reply that there never was a natural man — the
   frontispiece of Rousseau's own Discourse shows a Hottentot returning to his
   fellows, "society is as old as man" (one paragraph, `<Ref>`
   maistre-and-bonald and war-is-divine); (c) Hegel's §258 in one sentence
   (`<Ref>` hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state); and the problem the next lesson
   opens — a people in 1789 claiming the right to frame a government for
   itself (`<Ref>` burke-reflections).

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The statue of Glaucus and "laying facts aside"** (Preface/Exordium,
   l.7957–8277): the method stated in Rousseau's words, then one worked
   subtraction — take foresight away and fear goes; take comparison away and
   pride goes. Shows why Hobbes's "robust child" (l.8992) is a social adult in
   disguise.
2. **The fermentation under the tree** (Second Part, l.9602–9660): people
   gather to sing and dance; "the one who sang or danced best … came to be the
   most highly regarded, and this was the first step towards inequality". From
   this derive amour-propre as a definition the reader has already seen in
   action. Pair with the stag-and-hare hunt (l.9499–9504) for the limits of
   natural cooperation.
3. **"This is mine" and the rich man's speech** (l.9390; l.9880–9958):
   quote the opening sentence whole; then reconstruct the rich man's proposal
   as a four-step argument (I cannot hold what I have by force; the many fear
   each other; propose rules protecting every possession; the poor will guard
   my estate as their own) and end on "All ran headlong to their chains". Put
   Locke's Sect. 50 (unequal possession by tacit consent) beside it.
4. **The three stages of inequality and the closed circle** (l.10270–10417) as
   a ladder: property/law (rich–poor) → magistracy (strong–weak) → arbitrary
   power (master–slave) → despotism, "the equality of nothingness", where
   overthrowing the sultan is as lawful as his rule.
5. **SC I.6 parsed clause by clause**: "defend and protect with the whole common
   force" / "the person and goods of each" / "each, uniting with all" / "obey
   himself alone" / "as free as before" — each clause a constraint, then the
   formula of total alienation as the only solution satisfying all five, and
   "each, in giving himself to all, gives himself to nobody" as the proof that
   it costs nothing. Beside it, Hobbes's covenant formula (`<Ref>`) with the one
   changed term (the beneficiary).
6. **Will of all vs general will as arithmetic** (II.3; Political Economy
   l.11034–11058): private wills as vectors with a common component; the sum of
   the differences; what a faction does to the remainder. The nested wills
   (individual / corporation / state / world — a priest good in his order, bad
   in the city) as the structure behind "no partial societies, or many and
   equal".
7. **"Forced to be free" and the outvoted citizen** (I.7, l.2234; IV.2):
   quote both sentences; reconstruct the two-wills argument; then state the
   three difficulties (conditions, the Idealist gloss, Hegel's caprice) as the
   lesson's located hardest step. Cole's own admission that IV.2 contradicts
   II.3 is the course's evidence that the difficulty is real, stated as the
   course's own.
8. **"The people of England regards itself as free; it is grossly mistaken"
   (III.15)**: the attack on representation, with "the word finance is a slavish
   word" — the republican freedom of service against Hobbes's liberty as the
   silence of the law (`<Ref>` hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty). This is where
   Constant's objection bites.
9. **The civil religion (IV.8)** as a 3×2 table: religion of man / citizen /
   priest against social unity and truth; then the profession of faith with its
   one negative dogma and the sanction for lying before the law.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** *amour de soi* vs *amour-propre* (define both at
  first use, in the fermentation scene, not before); *perfectibility*; *the
  general will* (never "the majority's will" — define at II.3, not at I.6 where
  the word first appears; at I.6 say "a term the next section defines");
  *Sovereign / State / government* in Rousseau's senses (the people as active
  = Sovereign, as passive = State; government is an intermediate body, a
  commission not a contract, III.1, III.16); *Legislator* (Rousseau's capital-L
  founder, not a member of a legislature); *Geneva* (Rousseau's native republic,
  a small Calvinist city-state, the model behind the small-state conditions —
  one clause); *Lycurgus*, *Moses*, *Numa* (one clause each; Numa by `<Ref>` to
  Machiavelli); *Hottentot* (the Khoikhoi of the Cape, in 18th-century usage;
  Rousseau's own note tells of one raised by the Dutch who returned to his
  people — Maistre reads the engraving against the book); *Constant* (Benjamin
  Constant, 1767–1830, Swiss-French liberal who served and then opposed
  Napoleon; the 1819 lecture was given in Paris under the restored monarchy).
- **Dates to the decade:** 1750s for the Discourses, 1760s for the Contract and
  Emile; 1762 only if the double condemnation (Paris and Geneva) is mentioned.
- **Cole's Introduction is not Rousseau.** The summary's key_results l.28–34
  are Cole's theses (the "real will", Kantian autonomy, "liberty-fund"). Any
  sentence of the lesson that rests on them must be marked as a reading. The
  primary text wins (brief rule 9).
- **Do not read the general will as totalitarian in the body and do not deny
  the difficulty either.** Put Rousseau's own argument first, then the
  conditions he sets, then the three difficulties, then Constant. "Totalitarian
  democracy" (Talmon) is a later label — References only, if at all.
- **Do not re-teach Locke's property argument**; one `<Ref>` and the clause
  "property by labour, limited by spoilage and enough-and-as-good, limits that
  money dissolved" suffices. Rousseau borrows the labour title (Second Part) and
  then accuses it.
- **Hegel §258 is stated, not argued, here.** One paragraph, with the forward
  `<Ref>`; the Hegel lesson does the work.
- **Maistre's Hottentot is held for `maistre-and-bonald`/`war-is-divine`**: one
  paragraph here, no more; do not teach Providence or the executioner.
- **The Second Discourse is not a call to return to the forest** — Rousseau says
  so (Note 9 / Appendix, "What, then, is to be done?"); refute this real and
  prevalent misreading in one sentence where the Second Part ends (a genuine
  L13 slot, small).
- **Natural goodness ≠ moral goodness.** "Man is naturally good" means
  pre-moral, not virtuous — Strauss's "subhuman" is the sharp statement; the
  lesson should say "good as an animal is good: harmless" in its own words.
- **Period matter:** Rousseau's remarks on women (Dedication, Emile) and on
  "savages" are stated where they bear on the argument and nowhere else.
- **Names ONLY in References:** Cole, Strauss, Bloom (Strauss–Cropsey), Smith,
  Gossman, Talmon, Berlin (for "two concepts"), Holmes, Kelly. Constant,
  Hobbes, Locke, Hegel, Maistre, Mandeville, Grotius, Pufendorf, Montesquieu,
  Voltaire may be named as thinkers/interlocutors. State scholars' theses as
  the course's own ("the reading that Rousseau's general will replaces natural
  law as the test of right").
- **Dossier gaps:** Constant's lecture is present only through Gossman; the
  Strauss–Cropsey chapter is unavailable (empty file); Rousseau's note on the
  Hottentot may be truncated in the Gutenberg Cole text — check, and if absent
  rely on Maistre's description (spd.txt l.12938 ff.) and say the frontispiece
  is Rousseau's.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval, warm-up (E3).* Place in order, with one clause each: amour de
   soi, the dance round the tree, iron and corn, "this is mine", the rich man's
   contract, despotism. Then name which of these Locke's Sect. 27–50 (`<Ref>`)
   treats as legitimate.
2. *Boundary probe.* A people of one: Rousseau says the sovereign cannot be
   represented and acts only when assembled. Apply the formula of I.6 to a
   state of a single citizen, then to a state of two. What does "forced to be
   free" mean when the outvoted citizen is half the sovereign? The reader should
   find that the general will needs a plurality large enough for private
   differences to cancel (II.3) and small enough to assemble (III.15) — and that
   Rousseau's own band is narrow.
3. *Plausible false claim to refute.* "The general will is whatever the
   majority votes for." Name the failing step with II.3 (the people can be
   deceived; factions), II.6 (law has a general object), IV.2 (the majority is a
   judgment of conformity to the general will, which can be wrong) — and say
   what work the Legislator and the ban on partial societies are doing.
4. *Document reasoning (sourcing drill).* "The people of England regards itself
   as free; it is grossly mistaken" (III.15): author, date (1762, a Genevan
   writing under French censorship), audience, what it records about English
   parliamentary practice, what it argues (will cannot be represented), what it
   omits (the Lockean case that representation is how a large people consents,
   `<Ref>` Sect. 140), and what it can be USED to establish — Rousseau's
   standard of freedom, not a fact about Englishmen.
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* Rousseau says the compact alone requires
   unanimity and all else may go by majority (IV.2), and that residence implies
   consent. Derive from I.6–I.7 why unanimity is needed once and only once, and
   then test whether "residence implies consent" is any stronger than Locke's
   highway (`<Ref>` Sect. 119) that the previous lesson's Hume objection broke.
6. *Synthesis / counter-argument (Constant).* Someone claims that Rousseau's
   conditions (small state, no representation, no factions, civil religion)
   show he described a city of the ancient type and so his theory has nothing
   to say to a modern state. Name two facts the claim fails to account for
   (Rousseau's own III.4 and II.9–II.11 admit the conditions; the general
   will is offered as a standard of legitimacy, not a constitution), and state
   what would have to be true for it to survive (that legitimacy requires
   participation and not only limits on power — which is exactly what Constant
   denies and then half-concedes).
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `burke-reflections`.** In November 1789 a London preacher
   will tell his congregation that 1688 gave the English the right "to frame a
   government for ourselves". Using Rousseau's Legislator (II.7) and his
   remark that "a people once accustomed to masters cannot do without them"
   (II.8: "liberty may be gained, but can never be recovered"), state what
   Rousseau himself would require before a people could frame a government —
   and notice that he never says a people can deliberate one into existence.
   The solution says in one sentence that the next lesson opens with a Whig who
   denies the preacher's right on different grounds, with the `<Ref>`.

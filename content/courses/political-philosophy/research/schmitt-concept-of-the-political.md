# Research brief: schmitt-concept-of-the-political (Schmitt's Concept of the Political: friend and enemy)

status: planned
module: crisis-of-liberal-europe
type: core
goal: Analyze Schmitt's criterion of the political as the friend–enemy distinction and his charge that liberalism depoliticises by disguise

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Bodin's sovereignty as the power that makes law and is bound
  by none, and its price — no subject may judge the sovereign
  (`bodin-sovereignty`); Hobbes's state of nature as a disposition to war and
  his protection–obedience principle (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`,
  `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`); Cicero's and Aquinas's just war
  (`cicero-natural-law`, `aquinas-law-and-kingship`); Mill's harm principle as
  the liberal rule for coercion (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`); Ortega's
  mass-man and his two liberal premises — rule rests on opinion, liberalism is
  sharing existence with the enemy (`ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`); Mann's
  "democracy = politicisation" (`mann-reflections`). From `thrasymachus-and-
  the-ring` the reader has the realist challenge the tradition keeps answering;
  Schmitt is its twentieth-century form and the lesson should say so in one
  clause.
- **Contributes:** One move: a CRITERION for the political — not a subject
  matter (the state) and not a tone of life (Mann) but the grouping of men into
  friends and enemies with the real possibility of killing — and the
  consequence Schmitt draws from it, that liberalism has no politics of its own
  and hides the grouping under economics and ethics. The reader leaves able to
  state the criterion, distinguish hostis from inimicus, explain why "humanity"
  cannot wage war, and say what the political anthropology behind every
  political theory is.
- **Hands off:** If the political is the decision on the enemy, who decides,
  and what is law when the decision is taken? That is sovereignty, and
  `schmitt-political-theology` answers: sovereign is he who decides on the
  exception. The 1933 question — what the author of this criterion did when
  the enemy was named by a party — is carried to `article-48-weimar`.
- **Spine step:** "Liberal Europe breaks: Mann sets Kultur against Zivilisation,
  Ortega diagnoses the mass-man, Schmitt names the political as friend and
  enemy and the sovereign as he who decides the exception — and Weimar is the
  case"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (verbatim from course.yaml): what makes a question political rather
than economic, moral or aesthetic? A German jurist in 1927 answers that it is
not the subject matter but the intensity: any opposition becomes political
when it groups men into friends and enemies who might kill each other; from
that he argues that liberalism, which wants politics replaced by discussion
and trade, has never had a politics of its own.

1. **Why not define the political as "what concerns the state"?** The
   obvious answer, and the jurists' (Jellinek, Weber: politics is the state's
   business, or the pursuit of power). Schmitt's objection (I, l.445–621): it
   is a circle — the state is defined as the political entity and the
   political as what concerns the state — and the circle only worked while
   state and society were two things. In the nineteenth century the state was
   "neutral", standing above a society it did not enter; by the 1920s parties,
   unions, churches and the economy had entered the state and the state had
   entered them — the "total state" in which "everything is at least
   potentially political" (I; Burckhardt's gem: the democratic state "should be
   able to do everything, yet allowed to do nothing"). So "political" can no
   longer mean "of the state"; "the concept of the state presupposes the
   concept of the political". Ref `bodin-sovereignty`: the reader knows the
   state as the sovereign's creation; Schmitt is asking what made the sovereign
   necessary. Heading: "The state presupposes the political".

2. **What is the criterion, then?** Schmitt's method: every relatively
   independent domain has an ultimate distinction — good/evil for morals,
   beautiful/ugly for aesthetics, useful/harmful for economics. Is there one
   for the political that is not reducible to these? Yes: friend and enemy
   (II, l.627–690). Three things the newcomer must get at once, from the text:
   it is a CRITERION, not an exhaustive definition; it is independent — the
   enemy "need not be morally evil… aesthetically ugly… an economic
   competitor; it may even seem advantageous to do business with him"; he is
   "the other, the stranger… existentially different and foreign in a
   particularly intense way, so that in extreme cases conflicts with him are
   possible"; and only the participants can judge — no "disinterested third
   party" and no prior norm can decide whether the stranger negates one's
   form of existence. The "because": a norm or a neutral judge would already
   be a political decision in disguise (who appointed the judge?). Heading:
   "Friend and enemy: a criterion, not a definition".

3. **Which enemy — the one I hate?** No. Hostis, not inimicus (III,
   ~723–740): the enemy is PUBLIC, "exists only when, at least potentially,
   one fighting collectivity of people confronts a similar collectivity"; the
   Gospel's "love your enemies" (Matt 5:44) speaks of inimicus, the private
   adversary, and no Christian was ever told to love the Saracens instead of
   defending Europe. Then the hardest sentence: friend, enemy and conflict
   "receive their real meaning precisely because they refer to the real
   possibility of physical killing" (~800–820). The reader will take this as
   warmongering; the text forecloses it (l.820–850): war is "not the goal or
   purpose or even the content of politics, but the precondition, always
   present as a real possibility"; the definition is "neither belligerent nor
   militarist, neither imperialist nor pacifist"; the politician fights all his
   life, the soldier only in exceptional circumstances. A world without the
   possibility of war would be "a world without politics" — not a bad world,
   just not a political one. The pacifist who wages "war against war"
   proves the point: he has an enemy (the warmonger) and will kill him. Ref
   `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`: Hobbes's war as a disposition, not
   only battle, is the ancestor of "real possibility". Heading: "Hostis, not
   inimicus: the enemy is public, and the test is killing".

4. **Where is the political, if not in a domain?** In intensity (IV,
   l.920–1140): any religious, economic, moral or ethnic antagonism becomes
   political the moment it is strong enough to group men by friend and enemy
   — the Reformation wars, the class struggle. Consequence: the political
   entity, whatever it is, is sovereign, because it is the one that decides
   the extreme case; "it exists or it does not exist". Against the pluralists
   (Laski, Cole: the state is one association among many — union, church,
   club), Schmitt's reply: when the union orders its members out of church,
   or Bismarck fights the Catholic Church, which association decided the
   conflict? Whichever one could demand its members' lives. Ref
   `mann-reflections`: Mann's "democracy = politicisation" is a tone; Schmitt's
   intensity is a threshold. Heading: "The political is a degree of intensity,
   not a department".

5. **What does the state do with this?** The jus belli (V, l.1143–1407): the
   right to determine the enemy and fight him, and to demand its members'
   lives — "no norm, no ideal, no programme, however beautiful" can justify
   men killing each other; only the existential defence of one's own form of
   life. Hence the internal enemy: every state declares one in extremity (the
   Athenian decree of Demophantos, 410 BC; the Jacobins; the heretic). Hence
   the attack on JUST WAR (Ref `cicero-natural-law`, `aquinas-law-and-
   kingship`): justice does not belong to the concept of war; a war justified
   by a norm is a war against a criminal, and the Kellogg Pact (1928) outlaws
   not war but particular enemies — the one who "disturbs the peace". And
   hence the formula the course keeps: "protego ergo obligo is the cogito ergo
   sum of the state" (l.1370–1385), Hobbes's protection-and-obedience (Ref
   `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`) raised to the first principle of political
   theory — a people that lets another decide its enemy is protected by that
   other and no longer politically free; "Only a weak people disappears".
   Heading: "Protego ergo obligo".

6. **Could politics end — a world state?** VI (l.1413–1567). The political
   presupposes a plurality: "the political world is a pluriverse, not a
   universe". A world state with no enemy would be an economic-technical
   administration of "gas consumers, bus passengers and tenement
   inhabitants", not a political entity. The set piece: "Humanity as such
   cannot wage war because it has no enemy" (l.1439); whoever fights in the
   name of humanity claims a universal concept against his opponent and so
   denies the opponent humanity — hors-la-loi, hors l'humanité — "In this way
   war can be driven to the utmost inhumanity"; Proudhon's saying, "Whoever
   invokes 'humanity' wishes to cheat" (l.1455). The League of Nations is
   intergovernmental, neither international nor universal; it does not
   abolish war, it legitimises certain wars. Ref `cicero-natural-law` (the
   kinship of all men, one commonwealth) as the tradition this denies.
   Heading: "Whoever invokes humanity wants to cheat".

7. **Why do thinkers disagree about all this?** Political anthropology (VII,
   l.1573–1600): every theory of the state presupposes man as either
   dangerous or harmless, "problematic or unproblematic" — not in a moral
   sense ("evil" may be weakness, stupidity, vitality; "good" may be
   educability, peaceableness). The genuine political theories (Machiavelli,
   Hobbes, Bossuet, Fichte, Maistre, Donoso Cortés, Taine, Hegel) assume the
   first; anthropological optimism would cancel the possibility of an enemy
   and with it politics. The theological parallel: deny original sin and you
   get anarchism; "theologians, priests and politicians are alike superfluous
   in a good world". Hegel's definition of the bourgeois — the man who will
   not leave the sphere of risk-free private life and wants protection
   without danger (~1700–1754) — and of the enemy as "the Foreign in its
   living totality", hatred "undifferentiated, separate from all
   personality"; then Marx and Lenin turning Hegel's enemy into the class
   enemy ("Classes cannot be deceived"). Cromwell on the Spaniard as the
   "providential enemy" (1656; ~1880–1903). Tocqueville's "spectacle
   ridicule et terrible" — aristocrats praising the people's goodness with
   1793 at the door (~1908–1922): inability to tell friend from enemy is the
   symptom of a class's political end. Ref `maistre-and-bonald`,
   `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`, `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`
   (Rousseau's good natural man as the optimist pole). Heading: "Every political
   theory assumes a dangerous man".

8. **The charge against liberalism — and the objections.** VIII (l.1928–2281):
   "there exists no liberal politics, only a liberal critique of politics".
   Liberalism's two poles are ethics and economics, with private property at
   the centre; it cannot justify the state's demand for the sacrifice of life
   from an individual whose freedom is the point; so it translates every
   political concept — the liberal lexicon (l.1990–2015): struggle →
   competition (economics) and discussion (ethics); state → society; people →
   public and consumers; power → propaganda and control; the enemy → "the
   disturber of the peace". It does not abolish the friend–enemy grouping; it
   hides it, and fights wars as "sanctions", "pacifications", "international
   police" (~2267–2274); economic power — credit denial, embargo, starvation
   sanctions under Article 16 of the Covenant — is political power under a
   pacifist vocabulary. Marx wins by following liberalism into the economic
   and showing the one final grouping there (~2065–2160). Ref
   `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`: the harm principle as the liberal rule that
   never asks who decides what harm is. Then the OBJECTIONS, each at full
   strength:
   (a) **Ortega's** (Ref `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`): rule is opinion, not
   force; bayonets cannot be sat on; the political is the normal exercise of
   authority, and Schmitt has defined only its breakdown. Schmitt's reply from
   the text: force is the apparatus, the decision on the enemy is the fact; and
   the Kellogg Pact shows "opinion" too names enemies.
   (b) **The liberal reply, stated as the course's own:** the criterion
   describes WAR and civil war, not politics; most political life — budgets,
   coalitions, courts — never approaches the threshold, and a criterion that
   identifies politics only at its extreme has mistaken the exception for the
   thing (the Brunner objection of 1939 — "the enemy, not the friend, is the
   positive feature" — is the scholarly form; Schmitt's 1963 reply, l.280–288,
   is that a trial begins with a wrong and criminal law with a misdeed, which
   is not a "primacy of crime"). Add the epigram verdict (course's voice):
   "enough truth to distract from its overall falsity".
   (c) **Schmitt's own admission** (1963 Foreword, l.336–352): that a
   "cautious, initial delimitation of a conceptual field has been turned into
   a primitive slogan, a so-called friend-enemy theory, known only by hearsay"
   — the text was "didactic", a framework for "an immense problem", not a
   doctrine; and the Foreword's own later distinction (l.352–437) between
   conventional, real and absolute enemy, which the 1932 text had not drawn.
   The lesson weighs this: a concession about reception, or a retreat from the
   1932 claim that the political is the real possibility of killing?
   (d) **1933**, reported as fact bearing on reception: Schmitt joined the
   NSDAP in May 1933; in August 1934 defended the murder of the SA leadership
   and of his own patron Schleicher in "Der Führer schützt das Recht"; lectured
   in 1936 on German law "in the fight against the Jewish intellect"; was
   sidelined that year; was detained by the Americans after 1945 and never
   taught again. The lesson's question, not its verdict: does a theory that
   says only participants can name the enemy have any way of saying that a
   participant named the wrong one? The strongest reading against Schmitt is
   stated in the course's own voice (it is Strong's): the theory lets the
   enemy define the friend. Strauss's 1932 objection goes to References with
   its thesis stated once in the body as the course's own: affirming the
   political as the condition of seriousness is a moral judgment, so the
   criterion is not value-free.

Headings as claims: "The state presupposes the political"; "Friend and enemy:
a criterion, not a definition"; "Hostis, not inimicus: the enemy is public";
"The political is a degree of intensity, not a department"; "Protego ergo
obligo"; "Whoever invokes humanity wants to cheat"; "Every political theory
assumes a dangerous man"; "Liberalism has no politics, only a critique of

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The grid of autonomous domains** (II, l.627–690): moral good/evil,
   aesthetic beautiful/ugly, economic useful/harmful, political friend/enemy —
   drawn, then tested: Schmitt's own sentence that the enemy may be good,
   beautiful and a profitable trading partner. Work a case the reader knows:
   Athens and Sparta (`sparta-as-test-case`) — mutually admired, trading,
   enemies.
2. **Hostis vs inimicus and Matthew 5:44** (III, ~723–740): the Latin and
   Greek pairs (hostis/inimicus; polemios/echthros), the Gospel verse, and
   Schmitt's gloss that no Christian was asked to love the Saracen instead of
   defending Europe; the Plato note [9] on polemos (war against foreigners) vs
   stasis (civil strife) — Ref `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life` for
   stasis.
3. **"War is the precondition, not the goal"** (III, l.820–850): parse the
   paragraph clause by clause — "not bloody war"; "neither belligerent nor
   militarist, neither imperialist nor pacifist"; Clausewitz "often
   misquoted"; the politician who fights all his life. This is the passage
   that saves the lesson from the misreading in link 3.
4. **The Psephism of Demophantos and the Jacobin sentence** (V, ~1203–1227):
   Athens 410 BC, every citizen swears to kill whoever overthrows the
   democracy; 1793, "between the people and its enemies there is nothing in
   common but the sword". Two internal-enemy declarations two millennia apart,
   used to show the internal hostis is not a modern pathology.
5. **The Kellogg Pact dissected** (V, ~1319–1346): Schmitt's four points — the
   pact outlaws war "as an instrument of national policy", leaves self-defence
   and League wars, lets the signatories decide what counts, and so outlaws
   not war but enemies. Anchor the Pact (Paris, August 1928; the United
   States and France, then most states, renouncing war).
6. **"Humanity cannot wage war"** (VI, l.1439–1475): quote the first sentence
   and Proudhon's saying; then run the inference in three steps (humanity has
   no enemy → a war in humanity's name claims the universal for one side →
   the other side is outside humanity → no limit on what may be done to him).
   Set beside it, as the course's own question, Cicero's "one commonwealth of
   gods and men" (Ref).
7. **The anthropology table** (VII, l.1573–1600 + ~1758–1805): dangerous vs
   harmless man against the theory each implies — Hobbes/Maistre/Donoso
   (authority), Rousseau/anarchists (freedom), liberalism (man educable, state
   suspect), the theology of sin as the parallel column; the animal fables
   (wolf and lamb; Churchill's teeth, claws and horns, October 1928) as the
   folk form.
8. **The liberal lexicon** (VIII, l.1990–2015): a two-column translation table,
   political term → ethical replacement / economic replacement; then the
   pacifist vocabulary list (~2267–2274: executions, sanctions, punitive
   expeditions, pacification, international police). Work one case the
   reader can check: "reparations" vs "tribute" (note [12]).
9. **The 1963 slogan passage and the trial analogy** (Foreword l.280–288,
   336–352): Schmitt in his own words on being misread; the lesson sets the
   1932 killing passage beside the 1963 "didactic framework" and asks the
   reader which is the retreat.

  (link 5: Ref `burke-reflections`/`maistre-and-bonald`, one clause). Original
  sin (link 7: Ref `augustine-two-cities`). Bourgeois in Hegel's sense (link
  7). Versailles Article 231 (link 8, note: the "war guilt" clause, 1919).
  Otto Brunner (link 8 objection: an Austrian historian, 1939). NSDAP (link
  8d: the Nazi party; spell it out once). Three new terms per section (L11c)
  — links 5 and 8 are over budget and need subheadings.
- **The one real misconception to refute (L13):** that Schmitt says politics
  IS war, or glorifies war. He denies it in the text (l.820–850) and the
  lesson refutes the misreading with his sentence, then shows why it arises
  (the "real possibility of killing" is the criterion's test case). Refuting
  the slogan is not defending the theory; the lesson does the first and
  leaves the second to the reader.
- **Do not let the 1933 facts do the arguing, in either direction.** Report
  them dated and once (link 8d), say they bear on reception, and keep the
  objections to the theory separate from the biography. Never "a Nazi
  jurist" as an epithet in body prose; never an apology either. Strong's
  interpretation (Hitler as "miracle", l.698–765) is a reading — References,
  or one clause as "one reading".
- **Scholars live in References only:** Strong, Schwab, Miller (translators
  and editors), Fawcett, Muller, Strauss (1932 Notes), Meier, Bendersky,
  Kennedy, Brunner (though Schmitt names Brunner, so the 1963 Foreword's
  exchange may name him as Schmitt's interlocutor), Wehberg, Laski/Cole/
  Figgis (Schmitt names them; the body may name them as his targets — they
  are the material), Jellinek/Weber/Triepel (same). Hegel, Hobbes, Cromwell,
  Tocqueville, Proudhon, Donoso are Schmitt's witnesses and may be named as
  such.
- **Where summary and text may disagree:** OCR; section numerals; the
  "humanity" section is VI, the liberalism section is VIII though the file
  marks it "VI". Check every quotation against Schwab's translation or the
  German before setting it; Miller's renderings differ from Schwab's in
  places (Schwab: "The specific political distinction to which political
  actions and motives can be reduced is that between friend and enemy").
- **Where the dossier is thin:** Strauss's Notes are absent; the 1932 edition's
  own epilogue is present but the 1933 edition's changes (where Schmitt
  cut Jewish authors and sharpened the text toward the regime) are NOT in the
  dossier — the writer must not assert what changed beyond what Fawcett's
  paragraph says, and should put the point in References (Meier's edition
  and the Schwab introduction document it). Die Diktatur (1921), the 1923
  Crisis of Parliamentary Democracy, Legalität und Legitimität (1932) exist
  here only through Strong, Schwab, Muller and Fawcett; the lesson may name
  them with dates but not quote them.
- **Do not re-teach** Hobbes's covenant, Bodin's marks, Cicero's just war,
  Mill's harm principle, Ortega's rule-by-opinion: Ref and a clause each.
- **Numbers:** none needed. Dates to the year except the Kellogg Pact and the
  1933–34 sequence, where the month carries the argument (May 1933 after
  March's Enabling Act; August 1934 after the June–July killings).
- **Register:** Schmitt argues by epigram; the lesson must not. Every epigram
  the lesson quotes is immediately unpacked into the inference it compresses
  (L0c), and the writer does not borrow his cadence.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** State the criterion in Schmitt's terms and
   list the three features the lesson insisted on (criterion not definition;
   independent of the other distinctions; judged only by participants). Then
   recall from `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty` the protection–obedience
   principle and say in one sentence what Schmitt makes of it (E3).
2. **Document reasoning / sourcing drill:** The 1963 Foreword's "primitive
   slogan" passage (l.336–352), with its metadata (Schmitt, aged 75, West
   Germany 1963, reissuing a 1932 text, barred from teaching since 1945).
   Predict before reading what such a preface would want to say about the
   book's reception; read; records / argues / omits (it omits 1933); what can
   the passage be USED to establish about the theory, and what can it not
   establish about how the text was read in 1932–33?
3. **Claim to refute:** "Schmitt's criterion makes every disagreement
   political and so means that politics is everywhere." Name the failing step
   (the criterion is a threshold of intensity — the real possibility of
   killing — not a property of disagreement; most disputes never cross it; a
   world in which none did would be "a world without politics", which Schmitt
   allows as possible).
4. **Boundary probe:** Set the enemy to zero — a political entity with no
   enemy, internal or external, for a generation. By Schmitt's criterion, is
   it still a state? What does his text say happens to such an entity (it
   becomes administration: gas consumers and bus passengers; or it names an
   enemy). Then the opposite limit: an entity for which EVERYONE is an enemy.
   Why does his own 1963 distinction between real and absolute enemy need
   this case?
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** From (a) only participants can judge
   whether the stranger negates their form of existence, (b) no prior norm
   decides it, and (c) protego ergo obligo — derive what Schmitt must say
   about a court that rules on whether a party is an enemy of the
   constitution (a court is a third party, so either it is itself the
   political entity deciding, or its ruling is not the political decision).
   The solution says this is exactly the question the Weimar Staatsgerichtshof
   faced in October 1932 and Refs `article-48-weimar`.
6. **Counter-argument stem (synthesis):** Someone claims: "The friend–enemy
   criterion is simply Thrasymachus — justice is what the stronger decides."
   Name two facts the claim fails to account for (Schmitt denies that the
   enemy need be unjust or that the decision makes him so — justice is not in
   the concept of war at all; and the criterion is about who is the enemy, not
   who rules), and state what would have to be true for it to survive (that
   "deciding the enemy" and "ruling in one's own interest" are the same act).
   Ref `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`.
7. **Corroboration (document reasoning):** Two short excerpts — Ortega's
   "You can do everything with bayonets, Sire, except sit on them" (rule
   rests on opinion) and Schmitt's "Only a weak people disappears" (a people
   that lets another name its enemy is protected, not free). Both written
   within three years of each other by liberals' critics; do they contradict?
   Lay out what each can be used to establish, where they agree (force alone
   does not rule), and the exact point where they part (whether the decision
   on the enemy or the holding of opinion is the political fact).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `schmitt-political-theology`:** Schmitt says the
   political entity is sovereign because it decides the extreme case — but
   he has not said who, inside the entity, decides, or what the law is while
   the extreme case lasts. Take a constitution that says "in an emergency the
   president may take the necessary measures". Who decides that it is an
   emergency; who decides what is necessary; who decides it has ended; and
   is any of these three decisions derivable from the constitution's text?
   The solution shows the reader has written the first sentence of the next
   lesson — "sovereign is he who decides on the exception" — and Refs
   `schmitt-political-theology`.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Bodin's sovereignty — absolute and perpetual power, the marks
  of sovereignty, the refusal of the mixed state (`bodin-sovereignty`);
  Hobbes's covenant and "autoritas, non veritas facit legem"
  (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`, `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`);
  Locke's prerogative and "who shall be judge" (`locke-property-and-consent`);
  Maistre's executioner, "no such thing as man", the thirteen theses that no
  constitution is made by deliberation (`maistre-and-bonald`); Rousseau's
  general will (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`); the tool
  "political theology" and the Eusebian emperor as God's representative, with
  the sentence that the Trinity ended political theology
  (`constantine-and-representation`); Donoso Cortés's 1849 speech — when
  legality fails, the choice is between the dictatorship of the insurrection
  and the dictatorship of the government, "the dictatorship of the sabre" —
  and his diagnosis of the liberal bourgeoisie as a "discussing class" that
  answers "Christ or Barabbas?" with a committee (`donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship`,
  prerequisite; concepts `dictatorship-of-the-sabre`, `discussing-class`):
  Schmitt's ch. 4 READS Donoso, and the lesson recalls him with a Ref and a
  clause, never re-teaches him; Schmitt's own criterion of the political and
  the sovereignty of whichever grouping decides the extreme case
  (`schmitt-concept-of-the-political`, prerequisite). From this module:
  Pareto's governing class that will not use force and so passes "from the
  lions to the foxes" (`michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`) — the
  sociologist's version of the liberal paralysis Schmitt's ch. 4 describes;
  one clause, optional.
- **Contributes:** One move: the sovereign is defined by the EXCEPTION, not
  the rule — "sovereign is he who decides on the exception" — with two
  consequences Schmitt draws from it: that legal order rests on a decision a
  norm cannot supply (decisionism, against Kelsen and Krabbe), and that the
  modern theory of the state is theology with God removed (the exception is
  the miracle, deism is constitutionalism). The reader leaves able to state
  the definition and its two readings of "decides on", to say why a norm
  cannot apply itself, to run the theology/state table, and to distinguish
  commissarial from sovereign dictatorship.
- **Hands off:** The theory names a constitution's emergency clause as the
  place where the sovereign shows himself; Weimar's Article 48 is Schmitt's
  own example (ch. 1). What the clause did between 1930 and 1933, and whether
  the theory described the collapse or helped produce it, is decided in
  `article-48-weimar` (the module verdict: the diagnosis held; the
  commissarial remedy as practised had no criterion that kept it
  commissarial). This lesson carries no verdict of its own (REGISTER.md); the
  secularisation genealogy gets none anywhere in the course. The secularisation thesis is re-met in
  `voegelin-gnosticism` as the rival reading of the same modernity, and the
  "decision" returns in `strauss-natural-right-and-history` as the thing
   Weber's fact–value distinction leaves to the will. Whether a man who held
   that a wrong decision is still valid could tell, in 1933, which decision to
   resist is `the-right-in-1933` (next module) — a forward clause only.
- **Spine step:** "Liberal Europe breaks: elite theorists show that a minority
  always rules, Mann sets Kultur against Zivilisation, Spengler foretells the
  West's winter, Ortega diagnoses the mass-man, Schmitt names the political as
  friend and enemy and the sovereign as he who decides the exception — and
  Weimar is the case"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (verbatim from course.yaml): every legal order assumes a normal
situation in which its rules can be applied; who decides that the situation is
no longer normal, and what the law then is? Schmitt answers in one sentence —
"sovereign is he who decides on the exception" — and then argues that this
question, which liberal jurisprudence tries to abolish, is the same question
theology asked about miracles.

1. **Who decides that the situation is not normal?** The obvious answer: the
   constitution says — it names an emergency, an officer and a procedure. The
   failure, from the text (ch. 1 l.1479–1531): an exception, by definition,
   is the case the rules did not foresee; it "cannot be circumscribed
   factually"; a constitution can at most say WHO may act, not what he may do,
   because what must be done depends on what has happened. So the person who
   decides whether the exception exists and what to do about it is not
   derived from the order; he is prior to it. That is the sentence: "Sovereign
   is he who decides on the exception" (l.1458) — a "borderline concept", not
   a vague one but one "pertaining to the outermost sphere". The über ambiguity
   is part of the meaning (Strong l.165–220): deciding THAT there is an
   exception and deciding WHAT to do in it cannot be separated, since the
   first is already an act. Everyday instance (course's own, from Strong
   l.433–446): the director who waives the grade requirement for one student
   affirms the rule, shows it is man-made, and judges the case — and nothing
   in the rule told him to. Heading: "The exception cannot be written down,
   so someone must decide it".

2. **Didn't Bodin already settle this?** Ref `bodin-sovereignty` (one
   clause: absolute and perpetual power, bound only by divine and natural
   law). Schmitt's reading (l.1534–1609): the decisive passage is not I.8's
   definition but I.10's marks — above all the retold scenario: if the prince
   must consult senate or people before suspending the law, sovereignty is
   "a play between two parties", and the one who can refuse is sovereign;
   Bodin's own test — whoever can limit the power in time or competence holds
   it — IS the exception test. Pufendorf adds the content: sovereignty is
   deciding what public order is and when it is disturbed. Then Weimar's own
   clause (l.1655–1680): Article 48 lets the president declare the exception
   and lets the Reichstag demand its suspension — liberal control of the
   PRECONDITION; but the CONTENT, "the necessary measures", is unlimited, as
   under Article 14 of the French Charter of 1815, "which made the monarch
   sovereign". The lesson defines Article 48 here in one paragraph (a Reich
   president elected by the people; "public security and order considerably
   disturbed"; the suspension of seven enumerated rights) and leaves its
   history to the next lesson. Heading: "Bodin's marks and Weimar's Article
   48: the content of the exception is never limited".

3. **Norm or decision — which comes first?** The reader expects a legal
   order to be a set of rules; Schmitt says every legal concept has two
   elements, norm and decision, and the second is prior (l.1699–1760). The
   argument: a norm needs a homogeneous normal situation to apply to — "no
   norm applies to chaos" — and whether that situation exists is not itself
   a norm but a decision; so "all law is situational law", and sovereignty is
   "the monopoly to decide", not to coerce or to rule. In the exception "the
   state remains while law recedes": the exception is not anarchy, it is the
   order showing what it rests on; "authority proves that to produce law it
   need not be based on law". Kant's "emergency law is no law" and Anschütz's
   "here is where public law stops" (l.1782) are the liberal confessions that
   the question has been left out. The set piece: "The exception is more
   interesting than the rule. The rule proves nothing; the exception proves
   everything" and Kierkegaard — the exception "thinks the general with
   intense passion" (l.1787–1808). Heading: "No norm applies to chaos: all
   law is situational law".

4. **The jurists who abolished the question.** Kelsen's pure theory (ch. 2
   l.1883–2003): the state IS the legal order, every norm ascribed to a higher
   norm up to a basic norm, sovereignty "radically repressed"; Krabbe
   (l.2003–2250): the sovereignty of LAW, not persons — no one commands, the
   law's own force binds. The refutation (l.2295–2395): "the legal idea cannot
   translate itself"; a rule says how decisions are to be made, never WHO
   makes them (Locke's slip: "the law gives authority" — to whom? l.2376–2395;
   Ref `locke-property-and-consent` for prerogative and "who shall be
   judge"); the decision that applies a norm is not contained in it —
   normatively viewed it "emanates from nothingness"; and a WRONG decision is
   still legally valid, which no norm can explain (the timetable: what
   matters is that the train runs, not which time was printed, l.2314–2320).
   Hobbes is the classic decisionist (l.2398–2462): autoritas, non veritas
   facit legem; the saddler and the rider. Ref `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`
   (the sovereign above the law and Coke's error — one clause). The 1934
   preface's three types (normativist, decisionist, institutional) can be an
   Aside. Heading: "A norm cannot apply itself: against Kelsen and Krabbe".

5. **Why is this theology?** Ch. 3 (l.2466–2495). The claim: "All significant
   concepts of the modern theory of the state are secularized theological
   concepts" — historically (the omnipotent God became the omnipotent
   lawgiver) and STRUCTURALLY; "the exception in jurisprudence is analogous
   to the miracle in theology". The table the lesson builds from the chapter:
   omnipotent God ↔ omnipotent lawgiver; miracle ↔ exception; the Cartesian
   God, one architect of the world ↔ the absolute monarch of the seventeenth
   century (l.2818–2851); deism, a God who made the machine and does not
   intervene ↔ the constitutional state in which "the machine now runs by
   itself" (l.2873–2881) — "the rationalism of the Enlightenment rejected the
   exception in every form"; Rousseau's general will ↔ the philosophes' God
   (Ref `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`); nineteenth-century immanence
   — the identity of ruler and ruled, the organic state, Krabbe's law, Kelsen's
   order ↔ a God dissolved into the world; Tocqueville's people "hovering over
   politics as God over the world" (l.2909–2916). The method (l.2680–2815): a
   SOCIOLOGY OF CONCEPTS, not a causal story in either direction — not "monarchy
   because theism" nor "theism because monarchy" (Engels's predestination-as-
   capitalism reductio, l.2722–2727), but one structure visible in both. Ref
   `constantine-and-representation` to recall the tool: the course has
   already met a political theology (Eusebius's emperor as God's
   representative); Schmitt's thesis is that the modern state kept the
   structure after dropping the God. Heading: "The exception is the miracle:
   secularised theological concepts".

6. **What happened after 1848, and whose side is Schmitt on?** Ch. 3 end and
   ch. 4. The reader has Donoso's 1849 argument from its own lesson (Ref
   `donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship`: legality cannot decide the case it was not
   written for; the dictatorship of the sabre against that of the dagger) —
   recall it in one clause; what is NEW here is Schmitt's use of it. In
   Schmitt's reading, legitimacy collapsed in 1848 — "royalism is no longer
   because there are no kings" — and the decisionist's only answer is
   dictatorship, "the solution that Hobbes also reached" (l.2984–3024). The
   counter-revolutionary philosophers are Schmitt's own tradition (ch. 4
   l.3033–3118): their centre is the decision, "a big either/or" admitting no
   synthesis — against the Romantics' "everlasting conversation"; Bonald's
   traditionalism; Maistre: sovereignty and infallibility "perfectly
   synonymous", "any government is good once it is established", because what
   matters is that a question be decided "without delay and without appeal"
   (Ref `maistre-and-bonald`: the executioner as the bond; the thirteen theses
   become "every order rests on a decision"). The discussing class and "Christ
   or Barabbas?" answered with a commission are Donoso's (Ref
   `donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship`, concept `discussing-class` — recalled, not
   re-explained; l.3225–3230, 3313–3316 are Schmitt quoting him). Schmitt's own
   additions, which ARE this lesson's matter: liberal constitutionalism paralyses
   the king through parliament but keeps him on the throne, as deism excludes God
   but keeps his existence — the theology/state parallel of link 5 applied to
   1830–48; the list of liberal contradictions ending "What did it actually
   want?" (l.3236–3252); and the generalisation "Dictatorship is the opposite of
   discussion" (l.3367). The anthropology (l.3132–3222): the
   anarchist's good people and corruptible magistrate vs Donoso's radicalised
   original sin (Ref, one clause) — and Schmitt's own aside, which is new and
   is the point, that Donoso's exaggeration is a political, not a dogmatic,
   decision. The anarchist paradox: Bakunin must
   "decide against the decision" and becomes "in theory the theologian of the
   antitheological and in practice the dictator of an anti-dictatorship"
   (l.3367–3478). Ref `burke-reflections` for contrast: Burke's inheritance is
   a conversation across generations; Schmitt sides with the Maistre fork
   (`maistre-and-bonald`'s "Maistre vs Burke" is the relevant recall).
   Heading: "Decision against discussion: the counter-revolutionary line".

7. **What kind of dictatorship?** Strong l.223–235, Schwab l.1096–1116: Die
   Diktatur (1921) distinguishes the COMMISSARIAL dictator, who suspends the
   constitution in order to restore the conditions under which it can
   function (Lincoln in the Civil War; the Roman dictator of the Republic —
   appointed by a consul for a named task and at most six months; anchor it in
   one clause, since `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome` taught the consuls, senate
   and people but not the dictatorship, and Machiavelli's defence of it,
   Discourses I.34, was not part of `machiavelli-discourses-republic`'s
   reading), from the SOVEREIGN dictator, who uses the
   crisis to abolish the constitution and create the conditions for a new one
   (the National Convention of 1792; de Gaulle in 1958 as Strong's example).
   Schwab's three questions (l.1026–1055): who decides the exception exists,
   who decides the measures, who decides it has ended — "his sovereign
   slumbers in normal times but suddenly awakens". Article 48 is, on Schmitt's
   own account, commissarial. Hands off to `article-48-weimar`, which decides
   which Weimar's was in the event (on balance: a remedy with no criterion
   that kept it commissarial).

8. **The objections, at full strength.** (a) **The Trinitarian objection**
   (course's own, Ref `constantine-and-representation`): political theology
   ended in orthodox Christianity when the Trinity made the one emperor
   unable to image the one God — Gregory of Nazianzus; Constantine IV's army
   asking for two co-emperors to represent the Trinity, and the noses cut
   off; "the sphere of power is radically de-divinized". If so, what Schmitt
   secularises is not Christian theology but its Eusebian HERESY — the
   structure "one God, one emperor" belonged to the Arian court, and the
   orthodox result was the opposite: the state is temporal and nothing in it
   is a miracle. Say what the 1922 text does with this: it does not answer
   it — the book never considers the Trinitarian break; Schmitt's reply came
   forty-eight years later (1970, Political Theology II, not in the dossier;
   References) and can only be named. The record the reader already has
   (`constantine-and-representation`: Byzantium, Charlemagne) tells against
   the strong form of the thesis. No verdict on the genealogy itself. (b) **Kelsen's reply**, from
   Schmitt's own citation (l.2659–2666): democracy is "political relativism…
   liberated from miracles and dogmas" — and that is its merit; the
   "decision from nothingness" is not an insight but a description of
   arbitrary power, which is what a legal order exists to end (Ref
   `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, rule of law: law is reason without
   appetite). (c) **The internal objection**: if "a wrong decision is still
   valid" and "any government is good once established", the theory cannot
   distinguish the commissarial dictator who saves the constitution from the
   sovereign one who destroys it — the distinction Schmitt himself drew in
   1921 has no criterion inside the 1922 theory. Schmitt does not answer it
   in the book — say so, as a fact about the argument. The next lesson tests
   the gap on the record and finds it real (`article-48-weimar`: on balance,
   the remedy as practised had no criterion that kept it commissarial). (d) **The left's adoption** (Aside; Lilla in
   References): from the 1980s the thesis was taken up by thinkers of the
   left (Taubes, Agamben) as a description of how all legitimacy works —
   which is either a vindication or a sign that the thesis describes any
   order and so explains none.

Headings as claims: "The exception cannot be written down, so someone must
decide it"; "Bodin's marks and Weimar's Article 48"; "No norm applies to
chaos"; "A norm cannot apply itself: against Kelsen and Krabbe"; "The
exception is the miracle"; "Decision against discussion"; "Two
dictatorships"; "Three objections: the Trinity, the relativist, and the
missing criterion".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The first sentence, read three ways** (l.1455–1475 + Strong l.165–220):
   "Sovereign is he who decides on the exception" as (i) who decides THAT it
   is an exception, (ii) who decides WHAT to do, (iii) both inseparably; then
   Schwab's third question (who decides it has ended). Work it on the GPA
   example and on Article 48 in parallel columns.
2. **Bodin retold** (l.1566–1577): the prince, the senate, the suspension of
   law — if he must ask, who is sovereign? Three sentences, then the Ref.
3. **Article 48 as Schmitt reads it** (l.1655–1680): the two halves —
   precondition (controlled) and content (unlimited) — with the French
   Charter of 1815, Article 14, as the comparison he draws. One paragraph; the
   full text waits for the next lesson.
4. **"The rule proves nothing; the exception proves everything"**
   (l.1787–1808): quote the five sentences, then Kierkegaard's passage,
   then unpack the inference: the general is explained only by what it had to
   exclude. Pair with the timetable analogy from ch. 2.
5. **Kelsen's pyramid against Schmitt's question** (l.1883–2003, 2295–2395):
   draw the ascription chain up to the basic norm; then put Schmitt's
   question at every arrow — who decided that THIS case falls under THAT
   norm? — and show that the top of the pyramid is a decision called a norm.
   Locke's slip ("the law gives authority" — to whom?) as the worked case.
6. **The theology/state table** (ch. 3 throughout): God ↔ lawgiver; miracle ↔
   exception; Cartesian God ↔ absolute monarch (Descartes to Mersenne, "God
   established these laws in nature just as a king establishes laws in his
   kingdom", l.2840–2851); deist clockmaker ↔ constitutional state; general
   will ↔ the philosophes' God; immanent God ↔ Kelsen's order. Build it row
   by row as the chapter does, not as a list.
7. **Engels's predestination** (l.2722–2727): the reductio of the materialist
   method — "one could as well derive relativity theory from currency
   relations" — as the paragraph that explains what a sociology of concepts is
   NOT.
8. **Maistre on infallibility, read by Schmitt** (ch. 4 l.3092–3118): the
   quotation and the inference — decided "without delay and without appeal"
   matters more than how. Donoso's Barabbas (l.3313–3316) is recalled in ONE
   clause with a Ref — the set piece belongs to `donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship`.
   Then Schmitt's own generalisation, "Dictatorship is the opposite of
   discussion", and the deism parallel for the July Monarchy.
9. **The Constantine IV anecdote** (Voegelin l.4195–4203) as the course's own
   evidence for the objection: an army asks for two co-emperors to image the
   Trinity; the suggestion "sounds more like a joke"; the noses are cut off.
   Three sentences, with the Gregory of Nazianzus sentence before it.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know, and where to anchor them.** Exception /
  Ausnahmezustand (link 1 — say that the German means "state of exception"
  and is wider than "state of emergency"). Borderline concept / Grenzbegriff
  (link 1). Norm (link 3: a rule stating what ought to be; the jurists' word).
  Decisionism (link 4, defined only after the argument has shown what a
  decision is). Positivism / pure theory of law (link 4: Kelsen, Vienna,
  1920s — the doctrine that law is a system of norms and nothing else).
  Basic norm (link 4). Auctoritatis interpositio (link 4: Latin, "the
  interposition of authority"). Autoritas, non veritas facit legem (link 4:
  Hobbes, Latin Leviathan — "authority, not truth, makes law"). Deism (link
  5: the eighteenth-century belief in a creator who does not intervene).
  Immanence / transcendence (link 5: within the world / beyond it). Sociology
  of concepts (link 5). Legitimacy (link 6: the title by which power is held,
  as distinct from legality — Weimar's word for the fight). Donoso Cortés and
  the "discussing class" (link 6): NOT anchored afresh — he has his own lesson;
  Ref `donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship` with one clause of reminder. Commissarial / sovereign dictatorship (link 7:
  from commissar, one commissioned by another; sovereign, one who commissions
  himself). Katechon only if used (Strong l.780–796; better omitted). Article
  48, Reichstag, Reich president (link 2, one paragraph: the Weimar
  constitution of August 1919; a directly elected president, seven-year
  term, with the emergency power; a parliament that could demand repeal).
  French Charter of 1815, Article 14 (link 2: the restored Bourbon
  constitution's clause letting the king issue ordinances "for the security
  of the state", used in 1830 and the cause of the July Revolution — one
  clause). Kierkegaard (link 3: Danish theologian; Schmitt names only "a
  Protestant theologian" — the lesson may name him, the translator's note
  does). Three new terms per section (L11c): links 4 and 6 are over budget;
  split with subheadings.
- **The one real misconception (L13):** that "political theology" means
  religion in politics, or that Schmitt is making a religious argument. It
  is a STRUCTURAL claim about concepts, and Schmitt insists the sociology of
  concepts is not a causal reduction either way (l.2680–2815). Refute once,
  with the Engels reductio, where the term is introduced.
- **Do not teach Article 48's history here.** One paragraph defines it; the
  counts, the Preussenschlag and 1933 are the next lesson's. The seam at
  link 7 says so.
- **Do not re-teach** Bodin, Hobbes, Maistre, Donoso Cortés (the 1849 speech,
  the sabre and the dagger, the discussing class, Barabbas — all taught in
  `donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship`), Rousseau, Locke's prerogative, Voegelin's
  representation: Ref and one clause each. The lesson's matter is what Schmitt
  DOES with them.
- **Register (exact rule).** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right. `constantine-and-
  representation` already introduced "political theology" as a concept id;
  this lesson REINFORCES it and introduces `secularization-thesis` as the
  specifically Schmittian claim.
- **The Trinitarian objection must be the course's own**, stated with the
  Ref, not "Voegelin argues". It is also contestable — Schmitt's 1970 reply
  exists — and the lesson says the exchange continued, without adjudicating.
- **Scholars live in References only:** Strong, Schwab, Voegelin (as author;
  his tool is already the course's), Peterson, Blumenberg, Taubes, Agamben,
  Lilla, Muller, Bendersky, Kennedy, Meier. Kelsen, Krabbe, Gierke, Preuss,
  Wolzendorff, Anschütz, Laband, Jellinek, Bernatzik are Schmitt's named
  opponents and may be named as such (the material), briefly anchored
  (German and Dutch professors of public law of the 1900s–1920s). Donoso,
  Bonald, Maistre, Hobbes, Bodin, Pufendorf, Descartes, Atger, Boutmy are his
  witnesses.
- **Where summary and text may disagree:** the summary says Schmitt reads
  Bodin "on the exception" in ch. 1; the text's point is narrower — that the
  true marks (I.10), not the definition (I.8), carry sovereignty, and that
  the retold scenario shows it. Say the narrower thing. The translator's note
  on "prägnant" (l.3721–3725) means the famous sentence is better rendered
  "All concise concepts of the modern theory of the state…" — mention in a
  footnote-style Aside or References, not in the body.
- **Where the dossier is thin:** Die Diktatur (1921) is present only through
  Strong and Schwab; Kelsen's and Krabbe's own texts are absent (Kelsen's
  Allgemeine Staatslehre 1925 and Vom Wesen und Wert der Demokratie 1920/29
  — the latter is what Schmitt quotes at l.2659–2666); Peterson 1935 and
  Political Theology II (1970) are absent; Du Pape is absent. The lesson can
  state Kelsen's position only as Schmitt reports it and must say so in
  References. The 1934 preface is present and may be used for the three
  types.
- **Numbers:** none. Dates to the year; 1848 and 1922 (publication, March)
  are the two that matter; the Bodin and Charter dates (1576, 1815) are Refs.
- **Register:** Schmitt's epigrams are unpacked, not echoed (L0c). The
  lesson never adopts "the exception is more interesting than the rule" as
  its own voice.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** State the definition of sovereignty and
   give its two readings of "decides on"; then recall from `bodin-sovereignty`
   Bodin's test for who is sovereign and say in one sentence how Schmitt's
   differs (E3: the marks vs the exception; both ask who can refuse). Then
   recall from `donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship` the choice Donoso put to the
   Cortes in 1849 and say which of the two readings of "decides on" it is
   (deciding WHAT to do, once the exception is granted).
2. **Document reasoning / sourcing drill:** The opening paragraph of ch. 3
   ("All significant concepts…", l.2466–2478) with metadata (Munich/Bonn,
   March 1922, a professor of public law writing for jurists in a republic
   three years old, in a Festschrift for Max Weber). Predict what a jurist in
   that position would want the sentence to do to his colleagues' vocabulary;
   read; records / argues / omits (it omits the Trinity); what can the
   passage be USED to establish about the structure of a concept, and what
   can it not establish about history?
3. **Claim to refute:** "Schmitt's thesis is that modern politics is secretly
   religious, so a secular state is impossible." Name the failing step (the
   thesis is structural — a sociology of concepts, explicitly not a causal
   reduction in either direction; it says the state kept the shape of
   theology's concepts after removing their God, not that it kept the God).
4. **Boundary probe:** Set the exception to zero — a legal order that has
   never met a case it did not foresee, for a century. By Schmitt's theory,
   does it have a sovereign? (His text: the sovereign "slumbers"; the
   question is who WOULD decide.) Then the opposite limit: an order in which
   every case is treated as exceptional. What distinguishes that from the
   anarchy Schmitt says the exception is not? The solution shows that the
   theory needs a normal situation to be the normal one, and that it has no
   way to say how long "exception" may last — the next lesson's question.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** From (a) a wrong decision is still
   legally valid, (b) "any government is good once it is established", and
   (c) the commissarial dictator is distinguished from the sovereign one by
   whether he restores or replaces the constitution — derive whether the 1922
   theory contains any criterion for telling (c)'s two cases apart WHILE the
   exception lasts. The solution shows it does not: the distinction can be
   drawn only afterwards, by the outcome. Ref `article-48-weimar`, where the
   record supplies the outcome.
6. **Counter-argument stem (synthesis):** Someone claims: "Schmitt simply
   restates Hobbes — autoritas, non veritas — with Weimar vocabulary." Name
   two facts the claim fails to account for (Hobbes derives the sovereign
   from the covenant of equals, Schmitt from the exception with no covenant;
   Hobbes's sovereign makes law for the normal case, Schmitt's is visible
   only in the abnormal one), and state what would have to be true for the
   claim to survive (that Hobbes's "the sovereign judges what is necessary
   for peace" is the same act as deciding the exception — which Schmitt
   himself asserts at l.2398–2462 — the solution says the assertion does not
   meet the first fact: Hobbes's sovereign judges within a covenant that made
   him, Schmitt's decision presupposes none). Ref
   `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`.
7. **Corroboration (document reasoning):** Two short excerpts — Schmitt's
   "the exception in jurisprudence is analogous to the miracle in theology"
   and the Gregory of Nazianzus sentence that "the one person of an imperial
   monarch could not represent the triune divinity" (Voegelin's quotation,
   given as the course's own via `constantine-and-representation`). One says
   the modern state kept theology's structure; the other says orthodox
   theology destroyed the structure in the fourth century. Which theology is
   each talking about (Eusebian monotheism vs Trinitarian orthodoxy)? What
   would Schmitt have to show for both to be true (that the modern state
   secularised the Eusebian, not the orthodox, structure — and whether that
   damages his thesis)?
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `article-48-weimar`:** Article 48 lets the Reichstag
   demand the repeal of any emergency decree. Suppose a Reichstag that CAN
   form a majority to refuse every budget and every law, but cannot form a
   majority to demand repeal of any decree — and a president who signs
   whatever his chancellor brings him. Using Schmitt's three questions (who
   decides the exception exists, who the measures, who its end), say who is
   sovereign in that constitution. The solution says the reader has described
   Germany between 1930 and 1932 and that the answer is not any of the three
   organs the constitution names — the wall the next lesson's record puts in
   front of the theory; Ref `article-48-weimar`.

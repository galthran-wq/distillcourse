# Research brief: scruton-meaning-of-conservatism (Scruton's Meaning of Conservatism: authority before freedom)

status: planned
module: equality-after-rawls
type: core
goal: Analyze Scruton's case that conservatism is about authority, allegiance and institutions rather than freedom, and his philosophical reply to contract and Rawlsian liberalism

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The module's two lessons, which set the problem this one answers. From `rawls-theory-of-justice`: the original position and the veil of ignorance, the two principles and their order, the natural lottery ("no one deserves his greater natural capacity"), and the objections already put to Rawls there — Nozick's entitlement, Hume against any hypothetical contract, and the conservative's "distributed by whom?". From `ressentiment-and-envy`: the right's first answer to Rawls — that the demand for equality is driven by ressentiment and envy (Scheler, Schoeck) — and Rawls's reply that a just basic structure produces no excusable envy; the lesson ended on a standoff: no evidence available to either side can tell a motive from a principle, and the envy charge can be turned against any claim. From the postwar module: Oakeshott's disposition, which refuses to be a doctrine and whose government is an umpire that commands for no truth (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`); Strauss's reopened question of natural right and his charge that Burke's prudence slides into historicism (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`); Voegelin's claim that a society must exist as an ordered body before it can represent any truth (`voegelin-gnosticism`); Nisbet's intermediate associations (`kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`); the Declaration of 1948, read article by article against the right, where the reader found the article at which the bearer of the obligation changes kind (`udhr-read-against-the-right`). From earlier modules: Burke's little platoon, the partnership of the dead, the living and the unborn, "the dust and powder of individuality" (`burke-reflections`); Maistre's "no constitution is made by deliberation" (`maistre-and-bonald`); Hume's case that no actual government rests on consent and that allegiance is grounded in the general interest, not in promise (`hume-against-the-original-contract`); Hegel's ethical life, property as the embodiment of freedom and the state as not-a-contract (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`); Tocqueville's art of association and his remedies against soft despotism — "Scruton's autonomous institutions are Tocqueville's remedies" was planted there (`tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`); Locke's consent and Hobbes's covenant (`locke-property-and-consent`, `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`); Mill's harm principle and the reply flagged there as "Scruton's 'morality and law'" (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`); Marx's alienation, flagged in `marx-manifesto` as "Scruton's Meaning ch. 5–6, a conservative reading of alienation".
- **Contributes:** One move: a reply to Rawls that does not depend on impugning anyone's motive. Conservatism is not about freedom but about authority — the authority of family, custom, law and nation that a person did not choose and could not have contracted into — and the only honest defence of such authority is one that speaks to its members from inside (first-person justification) rather than to an observer designing from outside (third-person); the liberal theory of justice, by shifting the onus onto every institution, ends in a self that can either obey reason or act but not both. The Philosophical Appendix is read against the position the reader already holds from `rawls-theory-of-justice`; nothing of Rawls is reconstructed here.
- **Hands off:** Three problems. (1) Scruton's "tradition" is social knowledge that exists only in exercise and is guarded by silence about its myths; the next lesson, `macintyre-after-virtue`, answers from outside the right that a living tradition is an argument, that "when a tradition becomes Burkean, it is always dying or dead", and that modern conservatives conserve only an older liberalism — the reader carries Scruton's account into that charge. (2) What the pre-political "we" is made of — the nation as first-person plural, oikophilia, "to include is to exclude" — is held for `the-nation-and-its-defenders` (How to Be a Conservative chs. 2–3, 7) and answered by Miller and Carens; Rawls's veil returns there as Carens's global original position (`carens-open-borders`). (3) The internal quarrel — does "authority before freedom" return Oakeshott's disposition to the doctrine of human nature he refused? — is left for the reader and for `the-argument-assembled`.
- **Spine step:** "Rawls makes equality the measure of justice; the right answers with the charge of ressentiment and envy, Scruton's authority, MacIntyre's lost tradition and Lasch's revolt of the elites — and the data on cognitive stratification test both sides"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: a young philosopher who watched Paris 1968 from the wrong side; conservatism is about authority, not freedom; he must then say why a reasonable person should accept authority he did not choose; the appendix defends the doctrine against the liberal theory of justice.

1. **A reply that does not depend on motives; dogmatics, not philosophy.** The problem a newcomer meets, from the previous lesson: the charge that egalitarianism is envy and Rawls's reply ended in a standoff, because nothing either side could observe tells a motive from a principle (`ressentiment-and-envy`, one clause). A conservative who wants to answer Rawls needs an argument about the principle itself. Scruton's book (1980, nine years after Rawls's) offers one, and it begins by refusing Rawls's starting point: that politics begins with free individuals asking what they could accept. Attempt: then it is a rival philosophy of justice. Why that misdescribes it: he calls the book "dogmatics" — the articulation of what a tradition's members already believe, as a theologian articulates a creed — and says the philosophy comes after and can only "hint". Because: his second axiom — politics deals with the surface of social consciousness; "the house is white" is a sentence about meaning, and the causal story of how the sentence came to be uttered neither confirms nor refutes it. Anchor the man and the moment: Paris, May 1968, students' barricades and a general strike; a Cambridge research student who concludes the rioters have no idea what they are destroying; the book written in 1979–80, the year Thatcher took office, by an author who then found himself "one of two conservatives at Birkbeck". State the doctrine's first move with its example: conservatism is a stance toward an existing, continuing social order, not a party programme and not a love of freedom. Freedom is the consequence of institutions, not their precondition; the test case is a constraint such as the law of seditious libel — the conservative keeps it until it is shown removable without harm, the liberal removes it unless harm to others is shown (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`). Society, like friendship, is an end as well as a means: impose a programme on it and you destroy the relation. And the first hard saying: power must be accepted as authority, and belief in legitimacy "is a political fact whatever its philosophical truth" — the Mafia and the police differ not in what they can do to you but in whether you think they may.
2. **Authority and allegiance: the bond that precedes choice.** The question the doctrine must answer: why accept an authority one did not choose? Attempt (the contract tradition, which the reader has in three forms — Hobbes, Locke, and Rawls's hypothetical version): because one consented, actually, tacitly, or as one would behind a veil. Why it fails on Scruton's account: a contract presupposes the trust, language and law that make promising possible — a prior social order — so it cannot be the origin of that order (the reader has Hume's version, `hume-against-the-original-contract`, and Hegel's §258, by `<Ref>`); and the obligations that matter most were never chosen — a child cannot be said to have contracted with its parents, yet owes them. Answer, concept `allegiance`: the model of political obligation is the family, not the market. Two kinds of obligation: justice, the obligation of free exchange, chosen; and piety, the honour owed to unchosen bonds — to parents, to the dead, to one's country. Individuality itself is an artefact of social life (Burke's "dust and powder of individuality", `burke-reflections`), so the self that is supposed to consent — or to choose behind a veil — is already formed by what it would consent to. Hence `authority-before-freedom`. Patriotism vs nationalism in one sentence each; the first axiom (no general politics of conservatism; Solon's "For whom? And at what time?"; `maistre-and-bonald` by `<Ref>`). Then tradition as social knowledge: knowledge no one designed, existing only in exercise, an unintended by-product — the market's prices, the common law, a musical tradition Schoenberg could break only because he had mastered it; Oakeshott's practical knowledge (`<Ref>`) given a social home. Close with the law of establishment: every important tradition tends to become part of the state's establishment.
3. **The state as a person, and why a constitution cannot be made.** The problem: if allegiance is to institutions, what is the state — an instrument the governed hire, or something else? Attempt (liberal): the state is a means, a contract-keeper (Oakeshott's umpire is the course's own version — `<Ref>`). Scruton: state and society are one entity under two aspects, as body and soul; the state is an end, like a family, a regiment or a football team. Consequences, each as the answer to a question: Can a constitution be made? No — the American one presupposes the common law ("four hundred volumes of case law"); `oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics` (the cookery book) and `maistre-and-bonald` by `<Ref>`, one clause each. What is a constitution then? The state's tacit self-image, with a style; the conservative guards its essence through change. Does democracy confer legitimacy? No — a choice presupposes legitimate procedures; and democracy, like contract, counts only the living: "Remove the dead from the equation, and you remove the unborn" (`burke-reflections`: the partnership, now given a legal form). What is the nation? "the first-person plural over which the sovereign power presides" (`voegelin-gnosticism`: the ordered body before any truth; `<Ref>`). What are rights? "Rights exist only where there are obligations", and rights without powers are fictions — the reader tested this on the Declaration of 1948 and found the article where the obligor changes from everyone to someone (`udhr-read-against-the-right`, one clause; do not re-run it). Then the English particulars as instances, not dogma: monarchy as the voice of history; the mandate as nonsense; "there is no right to legislate"; "the worse the republic, the more its laws" (Tacitus — the reader counted Maistre's figure of 15,479 laws in `assignats`). The "racism" paragraph of ch. 3 belongs to the nation module; here one sentence that national feeling is the condition of the first-person plural, no more.
4. **Law, property, alienation: what the doctrine says to Mill, to the egalitarian, to the market and to Marx.** To Mill (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`): law covers what sustains the bonds of trust, not only harm to others; "harm" is already a moral concept, so Mill's onus cannot be met without the morality it was meant to exclude; "wrong because harmful" is the liberal's order, "harmful because wrong" the conservative's. To the egalitarian — the reader's Rawls: natural justice is the immediate sense of just dealing between persons — "the natural history of friendship" — and applies to actions and characters, not to states of affairs; "social justice" imagines a single owner distributing wealth, which no one is, and collides with natural justice when the 1968 Rent Act turns a widow's house into her tenant's entitlement (a hired car is not the hirer's). This is the ground of the objection the reader met in `rawls-theory-of-justice` as "distributed by whom?" — `<Ref>`, one clause, no re-teaching; and ch. 7's claim that equality of opportunity is "neither possible nor desirable" is a direct denial of Rawls's fair equality of opportunity, which the reader can now place. To the market liberals: conservatism is not capitalism; economics stands to politics as neurology to friendship; property's sphere is the home (oikos), the gift its highest form (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`, `<Ref>`); the right to property is conditional on allegiance and defeasible by reasons of state; tax income, not wealth; people resent small inequalities near them, not large distant ones (one clause beside Schoeck, `ressentiment-and-envy`). To Marx (`marx-manifesto`): alienation is real and political but stems from the industrial process and the division of labour, not from private property; commodity fetishism is property's pathology, not its essence; liberalism's "freedom" smuggles in one kind of life, the emancipated urban intellectual's.
5. **Autonomous institutions and the stratified society: where the doctrine becomes a politics.** The question: what does a government that is neither an umpire nor a dream-imposer — nor a Rawlsian distributor — actually do? Answer, concept `autonomous-institution`: it protects bodies that have purposes peculiar to themselves, achievable only through them, outliving their members and binding by a bond no member chose — a school, a university, a club, a church, a profession, a regiment, a marriage. "Autonomy" means internal aims, not independence from the state: the state ratifies them (unratified power is the Mafia's) and leaves their ends alone. Worked case, education: it contains its end internally; make it "relevant" and you get Football Studies. Marriage: not a contract — a husband "cannot say 'I did not bargain for this'". Hence the stratified society: many dignified walks of life, each with its own institutions of honour; alienation overcome piecemeal, not by redistribution — Scruton's alternative to the difference principle stated in one sentence. Establishment names the result: power + authority + the process that unites them; separating person from office is how power appears as authority; myth is the artefact by which institutions enter the life of the state. This is Tocqueville's art of association turned into a programme (`tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`), Nisbet's intermediate associations given a legal theory (`kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`) and Burke's little platoon — `<Ref>` each, one clause.
6. **The Philosophical Appendix: first-person and third-person justification, and the veil.** Now the defence, read against the position the reader holds. The liberal's question: why should I obey an authority I did not choose? — and the liberal's method, the onus: every institution must justify itself to the individual, or be changed. Two liberalisms: desire-based (freedom as getting what one wants), which cannot say why freedom matters — the suburb of equal gardens and plastic gnomes is its picture; and autonomy-based (Kant, Rawls, Dworkin), which can, and so makes rights central. Rawls is recalled, not rebuilt: one sentence with a `<Ref>` to `rawls-theory-of-justice` — parties behind the veil, the two principles, the natural lottery — and Scruton's own gloss of what the device is for: "a point of view outside present arrangements, from which they may be surveyed and, where necessary, amended or condemned". Then Scruton's distinction, concept `first-person-vs-third-person-justification`: liberalism generalises the first person (what could I accept?), utilitarianism the third (what does the observer count?); the war-god dancer's reason for dancing is not the anthropologist's account of what the dance does, and the first reason is partly impermeable to the second. The onus-shifting question can be answered only by a first-person reason or by objective natural rights; the liberal has refused the first and (Scruton holds) cannot supply the second; "He who shifts the onus will have to shift everything". The central argument, Kant's dilemma applied to the veil, quoted (ll. 10000–10012) and parsed: the transcendental self — the self considered as a rational agent in general, which is what the parties behind the veil are — can obey reason but cannot act; the empirical self — this person, with these desires and these ties — acts but is deaf to the reasons chosen for no one in particular. Scruton's positive account: the first-person perspective is inescapable and yet "a necessary illusion" that has a third-person justification — institutions produce the self that later questions them; the origin of "ought" is the unchosen debt of love and gratitude (piety); the liberal is the child who turns on the parents who made him able to argue.
7. **The doctrine judged: four objections and the one Scruton concedes.** (a) The Rawlsian reply, which Scruton himself names: reflective equilibrium. Define it from Rawls's own text (§4, §9; one short quotation): the principles chosen behind the veil are tested against our considered judgments and each is revised until the two agree — so the empirical self is not deaf to the veil's reasons but in conversation with them. Scruton calls this liberalism's "unwitting compromise with conservatism"; the reader decides whether it is a compromise or a refutation of the dilemma (an onus that can be answered by considered judgment is not infinite). (b) The noble-lie charge: "belief in legitimacy is a political fact whatever its philosophical truth", myth as the artefact of institutions, and ch. 9's conclusion that "articulate conservatives must recommend silence" — is this Plato's medicinal falsehood (`kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`) and the latent/avowed dilemma of `what-conservatism-is` in modern dress? Scruton's reply: all creeds are myths, liberalism's included, and the test of a myth is what it sustains. (c) The James Mill jibe returned (as the course's own; Fawcett's in References): endurance confers no moral authority, and a unity built on "seeing as" yields at most a civic nation, not the thick one Scruton wants. (d) The internal objection: Oakeshott's government was an umpire because dreaming and ruling generate tyranny; Scruton's state is a person with an essence to guard — has the disposition become the doctrine Oakeshott refused? And Strauss's: a "necessary illusion" with a third-person justification is historicism's self-exemption (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`, `<Ref>`). Where Scruton concedes: rights without powers are fictions, so a conservative who wants rights must want the state that enforces them; and the first-person perspective is "inescapable" — the liberal is not wrong to ask the question, only to think it can be answered from nowhere. Set this against the previous lesson in one sentence: the envy charge said the egalitarian's motive was bad; Scruton's argument says the egalitarian's standpoint is impossible to occupy — a stronger claim, and one that needs no access to anyone's heart. Hand off: the next lesson's author, a man of the left, agrees that the liberal standpoint is empty and denies that Scruton's tradition is the cure.

Headings as claims: "Dogmatics, not philosophy: the doctrine comes first"; "Allegiance: the bond that precedes consent"; "The state is a person, and a constitution cannot be made"; "What the doctrine says to Mill, to the egalitarian, to the market and to Marx"; "Autonomous institutions: the politics of the doctrine"; "The appendix: who is asked to justify, and to whom"; "The doctrine judged".

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and classification (the one warm-up):** Six obligations — repaying a loan; caring for an aged parent; keeping a promise; obeying the law of the country one was born in; a child's duty to learn its mother tongue; paying for goods taken — sorted as justice (chosen, exchange) or piety (unchosen, honour), with the one feature that decided each; then say which kind the contract tradition (Hobbes, Locke, the veil) can explain and which it cannot.
2. **Boundary probe:** A society with no dead — a founding generation (a kibbutz in 1910). On Scruton's account, remove the dead and you remove the unborn: what then grounds trusteeship, and can such a society have a constitution at all, or only a contract? Solution: Scruton's own concession that the US Constitution presupposed the common law the founders brought — the dead came with them; name what a society that truly had none would lack (an essence to guard), and whether that is a refutation or a limit.
3. **Claim to refute:** "Scruton's conservatism is the defence of free markets and private property against the state." Name the failing steps from ch. 5 (conservatism "not capitalism"; property defeasible by reasons of state; the state's relation to citizens parental; economics as neurology to friendship) and say what Scruton DOES defend about property (the home, the gift, the feudal principle) and against whom (both the planner and the market liberal).
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** Run the veil dilemma on Hobbes's covenant (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`): the covenanters choose a sovereign in the state of nature; what binds them after? Solution: Hobbes supplies a first-person motive that survives the choice — fear, and the sword that makes covenants more than words — so his contract escapes horn two at the price of resting on fear rather than reason; Rawls's parties have no motive once the veil lifts except the one reflective equilibrium supplies. State which contract theories the Appendix actually refutes.
5. **Sourcing drill:** The Preface to the third edition (2002): author (the same man twenty-two years on), date, audience (readers after 1989), purpose; what it records ("a young man's book"; Poland, the Falklands, the fall of the USSR), argues (that the 1980 doctrine was vindicated), omits (what the author changed); what it can be USED to establish — how the book's claims shifted between 1980 and 2002 — and what it cannot (that the 1980 text was written with 1989 in view). Prediction before reading: given 2002, what would you expect the preface to retract or harden?
6. **Synthesis (claim–evidence–reasoning):** Oakeshott's government is an umpire that does not command for truth; Scruton's state is a person with an essence, an established church and a stratified society to guard. Argue whether Scruton has returned the disposition to the "dreaming and ruling" Oakeshott said generates tyranny; judge, and say what Scruton would answer (the essence is not a dream but what already exists).
7. **Counter-argument stem (spans two lessons):** "Scruton's argument is the envy charge in philosophical dress: both say the egalitarian cannot mean what he says." Name two facts the claim fails to account for (the envy charge is about a motive and needs evidence of feeling, which `ressentiment-and-envy` showed is unavailable; Scruton's dilemma is about a standpoint and needs no evidence about anyone's feelings; Scruton concedes the liberal's question is inescapable) and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that the "necessary illusion" of the first person is itself a disguised motive — which would turn Scruton's own argument against him).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `macintyre-after-virtue`:** Scruton says tradition is social knowledge that exists only in exercise, is nobody's design, and must be protected by silence about its myths. Take one tradition the course has met — the English common law, or the university as an autonomous institution — and ask: when its members disagree about what the tradition is FOR, does Scruton's account give them any way to settle it other than by deferring to what already exists? The solution shows that it does not, that Scruton's tradition contains practice and authority but no argument, and says the next lesson's author holds that a living tradition is "an historically extended, socially embodied argument" and that a tradition which stops arguing — which becomes "Burkean" — is dying (`<Ref>` to `macintyre-after-virtue`).

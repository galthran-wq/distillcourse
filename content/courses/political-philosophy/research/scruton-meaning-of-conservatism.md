# Research brief: scruton-meaning-of-conservatism (Scruton's Meaning of Conservatism: authority before freedom)

status: planned
module: postwar-conservatism
type: core
goal: Analyze Scruton's case that conservatism is about authority, allegiance and institutions rather than freedom, and his philosophical reply to contract and Rawlsian liberalism

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The module's three answers so far and the problem each left: Oakeshott's disposition, which refuses to be a doctrine and whose umpire commands for no truth (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`); Strauss's reopened question of natural right and his charge that Burke's prudence slides into historicism (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`); Voegelin's claim that a society must exist as an ordered body with a representative before it can represent any truth, and that Hobbes bought that order by abolishing the soul (`voegelin-gnosticism`, Exercise 8). From earlier modules: Burke's little platoon, the partnership of the dead, the living and the unborn, and "the dust and powder of individuality" (`burke-reflections`); Maistre's "no constitution is made by deliberation" (`maistre-and-bonald`); Hegel's ethical life, property as the embodiment of freedom and the state as not-a-contract (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`); Tocqueville's art of association and his remedies against soft despotism — "Scruton's autonomous institutions are Tocqueville's remedies" was planted there (`tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`); Locke's consent and Hobbes's covenant (`locke-property-and-consent`, `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`); Mill's harm principle and the reply flagged there as "Scruton's 'morality and law'" (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`); Marx's alienation, flagged in `marx-manifesto` as "Scruton's Meaning ch. 5–6, a conservative reading of alienation".
- **Contributes:** One move: conservatism is not about freedom but about authority — the authority of family, custom, law and nation that a person did not choose and could not have contracted into — and the only honest defence of such authority is one that speaks to its members from inside (first-person justification) rather than to an observer designing from outside (third-person); the liberal theory of justice, by shifting the onus onto every institution, ends in a self that can either obey reason or act but not both.
- **Hands off:** Two problems. (1) "Rights exist only where there are obligations" and "natural rights are political fictions without the power to enforce them" are tested on a real document in `udhr-read-against-the-right`, where the article at which the obligor changes (Art. 22) is found. (2) What the pre-political "we" is made of — the nation as first-person plural, oikophilia, "to include is to exclude" — is held for `the-nation-and-its-defenders` (How to Be a Conservative chs. 2–3, 7) and answered by Miller and Carens; Rawls returns there as the global original position (`carens-open-borders`). The internal quarrel — does "authority before freedom" return Oakeshott's disposition to the doctrine of human nature he refused? — is left for the reader and for `the-argument-assembled`.
- **Spine step:** "After 1945 liberalism is the orthodoxy, and the right answers it in four registers: Oakeshott's disposition, Strauss's natural right, Voegelin's diagnosis of Gnosticism, Scruton's authority before freedom"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: a young philosopher who watched Paris 1968 from the wrong side; conservatism is about authority, not freedom; he must then say why a reasonable person should accept authority he did not choose; the appendix defends the doctrine against the liberal theory of justice.

1. **Dogmatics, not philosophy: why the book states a doctrine before defending it.** The problem a newcomer meets: the previous three lessons each refused to give conservatism a doctrine — a disposition, a reopened question, a diagnosis — and the first lesson showed every stated doctrine falls to the self-application problem. Scruton's book opens by stating one anyway. Attempt: then it is a philosophy of conservatism. Why that misdescribes it: he calls it "dogmatics" — the articulation of what a tradition's members already believe, as a theologian articulates a creed — and says the philosophy comes after and can only "hint". Because: his second axiom — politics deals with the surface of social consciousness; "the house is white" is a sentence about meaning, and the causal story of how the sentence came to be uttered neither confirms nor refutes it. Anchor the man and the moment: Paris, May 1968, students' barricades and a general strike; a Cambridge research student on the street who concludes the rioters have no idea what they are destroying; the book written in 1979–80, the year Thatcher took office, by an author who then found himself "one of two conservatives at Birkbeck" (Nunzia at the counter was the other). State the doctrine's first move with its example: conservatism is a stance toward an existing, continuing social order, not a party programme and not a love of freedom. Freedom is the consequence of institutions, not their precondition; the test case is a constraint such as the law of seditious libel — the conservative keeps it until it is shown removable without harm, the liberal removes it unless harm to others is shown (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`; Scruton's reply, flagged there, begins here). Society, like friendship, is an end as well as a means: impose a programme on it and you destroy the relation; revolution is "like murdering a sick mother out of impatience to snatch some rumoured infant from her womb". And the first hard saying: power must be accepted as authority, and belief in legitimacy "is a political fact whatever its philosophical truth" — the Mafia and the police differ not in what they can do to you but in whether you think they may.
2. **Authority and allegiance: the bond that precedes choice.** The question the doctrine must answer: why accept an authority one did not choose? Attempt (the whole contract tradition, `locke-property-and-consent`, `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`): because one consented, actually or tacitly or hypothetically. Why it fails on Scruton's account: a contract presupposes the trust, language and law that make promising possible — a prior social order — so it cannot be the origin of that order (the reader has Hume's version, and Hegel's §258, by `<Ref>`); and the obligations that matter most were never chosen — the parents' analogy: a child cannot be said to have contracted with its parents, yet owes them. Answer, concept `allegiance`: the model of political obligation is the family, not the market. Two kinds of obligation: justice, the obligation of free exchange, chosen; and piety, the honour owed to unchosen bonds — to parents, to the dead, to one's country. Individuality itself is an artefact of social life (Burke's "dust and powder of individuality", `burke-reflections`), so the self that is supposed to consent is already formed by what it would consent to. Hence `authority-before-freedom`: institutions a person did not choose give him the standing from which freedom is intelligible. Distinguish patriotism from nationalism in one sentence each — "we stand or fall together", the sentiment that makes civil war unthinkable, vs an ideology of national superiority — and give the first axiom: there is no general politics of conservatism; Solon, asked for the best constitution, answered "For whom? And at what time?" (`maistre-and-bonald`'s "a constitution made for all nations is made for none" by `<Ref>`). Then tradition as social knowledge, tied to the module: knowledge no one designed, existing only in exercise, an unintended by-product — the parallels are the market's prices carrying dispersed information, the common law, a musical tradition that Schoenberg could break only because he had mastered it; this is Oakeshott's practical knowledge (`<Ref>`) given a social, not an individual, home. Close the link with the law of establishment: every important tradition tends to become part of the state's establishment — which is why the state cannot be kept out of the argument.
3. **The state as a person, and why a constitution cannot be made.** The problem: if allegiance is to institutions, what is the state — an instrument the governed hire, or something else? Attempt (liberal): the state is a means, outside society, a contract-keeper (Oakeshott's umpire is the module's own version — `<Ref>`). Scruton: state and society are one entity under two aspects, as body and soul; the split between them is Cartesian dualism surviving in politics; the state is an end, like a family, a regiment or a football team, which no one joins only for what he gets out of it. Consequences, each as the answer to a question: Can a constitution be made? No — the American one presupposes the common law ("four hundred volumes of case law") and a settled order; a written constitution is an abstract formula (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`: the cookery book; `maistre-and-bonald`: the thirteen theses — `<Ref>` both, one clause). What is a constitution then? The state's tacit, reasoned self-image — the rules and customs by which power is exercised, with a style — and the conservative's task is to guard its essence through change. Does democracy confer legitimacy? No — a choice presupposes legitimate procedures for choosing, so the procedures cannot get their legitimacy from the choice; and democracy, like contract, counts only the living: "Remove the dead from the equation, and you remove the unborn" — the testator's will, the school and the hospital as bequests held by temporary trustees (`burke-reflections`: the partnership of the dead, the living and the unborn, now given a legal form). What is the nation? Not race or language but "the first-person plural over which the sovereign power presides" — the "we" that must exist before anyone can disagree (`voegelin-gnosticism`: the ordered body before any truth; `<Ref>`). Then the English particulars as instances, not dogma: monarchy as the voice of history; hereditary peers as a long-term interest; the mandate as nonsense (each voter in the minority on the issue that mattered to him); "there is no right to legislate"; judicial independence against "social justice" legislation; "the worse the republic, the more its laws" (Tacitus — the reader counted Maistre's 15,479 in `assignats`). The "racism" paragraph of ch. 3 belongs to the nation module; here one sentence that national feeling is the condition of the first-person plural, no more.
4. **Law, property, alienation: what the doctrine says to Mill, to Hayek and to Marx.** Three liberal and one Marxist position the reader already holds, each met in a paragraph. To Mill (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`): law covers what sustains the bonds of trust, not only harm to others; "harm" is already a moral concept, so Mill's onus — justify every constraint by harm — cannot be met without the morality it was meant to exclude; "wrong because harmful" is the liberal's order, "harmful because wrong" the conservative's; juries, not statistics, test what a society finds indecent. To the theorists of natural rights and of social justice: natural justice is the immediate sense of just dealing between persons — "the natural history of friendship" — and applies to actions and characters, not to states of affairs; "social justice" imagines a single owner (society) distributing wealth, which no one is, and collides with natural justice when the 1968 Rent Act turns a widow's house into her tenant's entitlement (a hired car is not the hirer's); and here the one phrase from the later book — redistribution raises the question "distributed by whom?", since the state does not hold a common asset but creates rents on taxpayers' earnings. To Hayek and the market liberals: conservatism is not capitalism; economics stands to politics as neurology to friendship; property's sphere is the home (oikos), not the balance sheet — ownership is the first socialising of objects, the gift its highest form (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`: property as the embodiment of freedom, `<Ref>`); the right to property is conditional on allegiance and defeasible by reasons of state; tax income, not wealth, because inheritance duties attack gift, family and continuity; Disraeli's feudal principle — property as duty. To Marx (`marx-manifesto`): alienation is real and political — an alienated society cannot be governed conservatively — but it stems from the industrial process and the division of labour, not from private property, so changing ownership cannot cure it; possession of irreplaceable things as ends (the Dutch interior, the still life) against consumption of replaceable commodities; commodity fetishism is property's pathology, not its essence; and liberalism's "freedom" smuggles in one kind of life, the emancipated urban intellectual's.
5. **Autonomous institutions and the stratified society: where the doctrine becomes a politics.** The question: what does a government that is not an umpire and not a dream-imposer actually do? Answer, concept `autonomous-institution`: it protects bodies that have purposes peculiar to themselves, achievable only through them, outliving their members and binding by a bond no member chose — a school, a university, a club, a church, a profession, a regiment, a marriage. "Autonomy" means internal aims, not independence from the state: the state ratifies them (unratified power is the Mafia's) and leaves their ends alone. The worked case is education: it contains its end internally; make it "relevant" and you get Football Studies (a satire in 1979, a degree by 2000) and Mathematics Studies with a theory of holes. Marriage: not a contract — a husband "cannot say 'I did not bargain for this'" — so the law protects the arrangement as a whole, and legacy and hereditary privilege follow from family love. Hence the stratified society: equality of opportunity is neither possible nor desirable; what a society needs is many dignified walks of life, each with its own institutions of honour (the electrician boy's hobby to his guild), and alienation is overcome piecemeal, not by redistribution. Establishment names the result: power + authority + the process that unites them; separating person from office (the President who becomes "Mr Clinton"; the Crown that carries dignity while the politician carries responsibility) is how power appears as authority; myth is the artefact by which institutions enter the life of the state, and an established church guards against sects. This is Tocqueville's art of association turned into a conservative programme (`tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`: the remedies against soft despotism — `<Ref>`) and Burke's little platoon with a legal theory (`<Ref>`).
6. **The Philosophical Appendix: first-person and third-person justification, and the veil.** Now the defence. The problem stated as the liberal states it: why should I obey an authority I did not choose? — and the liberal's method, the onus: every institution must justify itself to the individual, or be changed. Two liberalisms: desire-based (freedom as getting what one wants), which cannot say why freedom matters — a Brave New World of satisfied desires is its paradigm, the suburb of equal gardens and plastic gnomes its picture; and autonomy-based (Kant, Rawls, Dworkin), which can, and so makes rights central ("rights as trumps"). Here the reconstruction the lesson owes the reader, with the caveat stated in the body: Rawls's theory is here reconstructed from a critic's and a sympathiser's statements of it, since the course does not read him. The position: imagine parties choosing the principles of their society from behind a veil of ignorance about their own place in it — their class, talents, beliefs — so that nothing "arbitrary from a moral point of view" can bias the choice; they would choose equal basic liberties and would permit inequalities only where these benefit the least advantaged (the two principles); the thought experiment is "a point of view outside present arrangements, from which they may be surveyed and, where necessary, amended or condemned" — and its defenders say that is exactly its value: it keeps even the racists of Forsythe County inside the circle of free and equal persons, and reading it as a mere summary of existing values "destroys its critical power". Then Scruton's distinction, concept `first-person-vs-third-person-justification`: liberalism generalises the first person (what could I accept?), utilitarianism the third (what does the observer count?); the war-god dancer's reason for dancing is not the anthropologist's account of what the dance does, and the first reason is partly impermeable to the second — tell the dancer the function and he may stop dancing. The onus-shifting question "Why should I do that?" can be answered only by a first-person reason or by objective natural rights; the liberal has refused the first and (Scruton holds) cannot supply the second; "He who shifts the onus will have to shift everything" — the regress ends in scepticism. The central argument, Kant's dilemma applied to the veil, quoted: "granted that, when choosing from behind the veil of ignorance, I choose the abstract principles of justice, what then binds me to that choice when the veil is removed? Either I am bound, in which case I cannot emerge into the real world of agency; or else I enter that world, in which case these merely 'hypothetical considerations' cannot bind my action." The transcendental self can obey reason but cannot act; the empirical self acts but is deaf to reason. Scruton's positive account: the first-person perspective is inescapable and yet "a necessary illusion" that has a third-person justification — institutions produce the self that later questions them; the origin of "ought" is the unchosen debt of love and gratitude (piety); the liberal is the child who turns on the parents who made him able to argue ("the expiation of the sin of dependence"); monarchy is reasonable from outside and opaque within, and must be.
7. **The doctrine judged: four objections and the one Scruton concedes.** (a) The Rawlsian reply, which Scruton himself names: reflective equilibrium — the principles chosen behind the veil are tested against our considered judgments and revised until the two agree, so the empirical self is not deaf to reason but in conversation with it. Scruton calls this liberalism's "unwitting compromise with conservatism"; the reader decides whether it is a compromise or a refutation of the dilemma (an onus that can be answered by considered judgment is not infinite). (b) The noble-lie charge: "belief in legitimacy is a political fact whatever its philosophical truth", myth as the artefact of institutions, and ch. 9's conclusion that the reasons for sustaining myths cannot be propagated without instilling doubt, so that "articulate conservatives must recommend silence" — is this Plato's medicinal falsehood (`kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`) and the latent/avowed dilemma of `what-conservatism-is` (the prayer paradox) in modern dress? Scruton's reply: all creeds are myths, liberalism's included, and the test of a myth is what it sustains, not whether it is literally true. (c) The James Mill jibe returned (stated as the course's own; Fawcett's in References): endurance confers no moral authority — the Inquisition endured — and a unity built on "seeing as" (seeing the Crown as the voice of history) yields at most a civic nation, not the thick one Scruton wants. (d) The internal objection: Oakeshott's government was an umpire that "commands for" nothing because dreaming and ruling generate tyranny; Scruton's state is a person with an essence to guard, an established church and a stratified society to protect — has the disposition become the doctrine of human nature Oakeshott refused? And Strauss's: a "necessary illusion" with a third-person justification is historicism's self-exemption (the theorist sees through the myth the members must not) — `strauss-natural-right-and-history`, `<Ref>`. Where Scruton concedes: rights without powers are fictions, so a conservative who wants rights must want the state that enforces them; and the first-person perspective is "inescapable" — the liberal is not wrong to ask the question, only to think it can be answered from nowhere. Hand off: a document of 1948 declares thirty rights for every human being without a state to enforce them and without a reason — the next lesson reads it with Scruton's "rights exist only where there are obligations" and the four earlier critics.

Headings as claims: "Dogmatics, not philosophy: the doctrine comes first"; "Allegiance: the bond that precedes consent"; "The state is a person, and a constitution cannot be made"; "What the doctrine says to Mill, to the market and to Marx"; "Autonomous institutions: the politics of the doctrine"; "The appendix: who is asked to justify, and to whom"; "The doctrine judged".

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and classification (the one warm-up):** Six obligations — repaying a loan; caring for an aged parent; keeping a promise; obeying the law of the country one was born in; a child's duty to learn its mother tongue; paying for goods taken — sorted as justice (chosen, exchange) or piety (unchosen, honour), with the one feature that decided each; then say which kind the contract tradition can explain and which it cannot.
2. **Boundary probe:** A society with no dead — a founding generation (the colony Oakeshott's America and Voegelin's Lombards were; a kibbutz in 1910). On Scruton's account, remove the dead and you remove the unborn: what then grounds trusteeship, and can such a society have a constitution at all, or only a contract? Solution: Scruton's own concession that the US Constitution presupposed the common law the founders brought — the dead came with them; name what a society that truly had none would lack (an essence to guard), and whether that is a refutation or a limit.
3. **Claim to refute:** "Scruton's conservatism is the defence of free markets and private property against the state." Name the failing steps from ch. 5 (conservatism "not capitalism"; property defeasible by reasons of state; the state's relation to citizens parental; public service distinguished from nationalised industry; economics as neurology to friendship) and say what Scruton DOES defend about property (the home, the gift, the feudal principle) and against whom (both the planner and the market liberal).
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** Run the veil dilemma on Hobbes's covenant (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`): the covenanters choose a sovereign in the state of nature; what binds them after? Does the dilemma that catches Rawls catch Hobbes? Solution: Hobbes supplies a first-person motive that survives the choice — fear, and the sword that makes covenants more than words — so his contract escapes Scruton's horn two at the price of resting on fear rather than reason; Rawls's parties have no motive once the veil lifts except the one reflective equilibrium supplies. State what this shows about which contract theories the Appendix actually refutes.
5. **Sourcing drill:** The Preface to the third edition (2002): author (the same man twenty-two years on), date, audience (an American Catholic press; readers after 1989), purpose; what it records ("a young man's book"; Poland, the Falklands, the fall of the USSR), argues (that the 1980 doctrine was vindicated), omits (what the author changed — the democracy and natural-rights scepticism is now defended as prudence); what it can be USED to establish — how the book's claims shifted between 1980 and 2002 — and what it cannot (that the 1980 text was written with 1989 in view). Prediction before reading: given 2002 and the press, what would you expect the preface to retract or harden?
6. **Synthesis (claim–evidence–reasoning):** Oakeshott's government is an umpire that is not "leader, tutor or manager" and does not command for truth; Scruton's state is a person with an essence, an established church and a stratified society to guard. Argue whether Scruton has returned the disposition to the "dreaming and ruling" Oakeshott said generates tyranny, using "the state is an end like family, regiment, football team" against "the conjunction of dreaming and ruling"; judge, and say what Scruton would answer (the essence is not a dream but what already exists).
7. **Counter-argument stem:** "Someone claims Scruton's 'recommend silence' is Plato's noble lie: a ruling class that knows the myths are myths and feeds them to the ruled." Name two facts the claim must account for (Scruton says ALL creeds, liberalism's included, are myths, so the asymmetry Plato needs is absent; he distinguishes a myth by what it sustains, not by a hidden truth the rulers hold) and state what would have to be true for it to survive (that the "third-person justification" of the "necessary illusion" is available only to a few — the Straussian reading, `strauss-natural-right-and-history`).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `udhr-read-against-the-right`:** Scruton: "rights exist only where there are obligations", and "rights without powers are political fictions". Take three rights anyone can state — the right to life, the right to work, the right to a nationality — and for each name who bears the obligation and what power enforces it. The solution shows the obligor changes kind between the first and the second (from everyone's duty to refrain, to someone's duty to provide), and says the next lesson reads the 1948 Declaration article by article and finds the exact article where that happens.

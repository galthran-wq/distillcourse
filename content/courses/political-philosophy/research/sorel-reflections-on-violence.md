# Research brief: sorel-reflections-on-violence (Sorel's Reflections on Violence: the myth of the general strike)

status: planned
module: conservative-revolution
type: core
goal: Analyze Sorel's argument that a class is held together by a myth rather than a programme and that violence restores the energy a decadent bourgeois order lacks, and evaluate why both the far left and the far right claimed him

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `marx-manifesto`: class struggle, the bourgeoisie as the class that
  "melts" every fixed bond, the proletariat as the class that overthrows it, and Marx's
  own refusal to write recipes for the future society — Sorel's starting point. From
  `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites` (the direct prerequisite): every party,
  the socialist one first, breeds a salaried leadership that bargains on behalf of the
  led; elites decay when they lose the will to use force ("history is a graveyard of
  aristocracies"). Sorel accepts both diagnoses and asks what can stop them. From
  `mosca-ruling-class`: the political formula — the principle a ruling minority invokes
  so that men obey a principle rather than a man; Sorel's myth is its counterpart for a
  class that wants to overthrow a minority, not to justify one (Ref, one clause). From
  `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`: crowds move by images, not reasons, and (Le
  Bon) crowds are conservative and "will always flock to a Caesar" — Sorel quotes this
  and corrects it (set piece 5). From `dreyfus-affair-as-test`: the Affair as the event
  that put the parliamentary republic and its intellectuals in power; Sorel was a
  Dreyfusard who came to see the victory as the triumph of a political class (Ref, one
  clause; do not re-tell the Affair). From `schmitt-political-theology`: decision against
  "everlasting conversation" — Sorel's contempt for parliamentary bargaining is the
  earlier, left-wing form of the same charge (Ref; Schmitt himself names Sorel as the
  thinker who joined Bergson's philosophy of life to Marx, Political Theology ch. 3).
  From `maistre-and-bonald`: the Church militant and the counter-revolution's distrust of
  optimists — Sorel uses both, from the left.
- **Contributes:** The module's first move. Sorel reverses the question every earlier
  socialist asked ("is the revolution's programme true?") into a question about what
  holds a fighting class together: not a programme the class can check, but a MYTH — a
  whole image of a decisive battle that cannot be refuted because it is not a
  prediction — kept alive by acts of VIOLENCE that mark the line between classes and
  restore the energy a bargaining, pacified bourgeois order has lost. The reader leaves
  able to reconstruct the chain (decadence of social peace → myth versus utopia →
  violence versus force → an ethic of producers born of war) and to say why a doctrine
  built for a workers' strike could be lifted out whole by nationalists.
- **Hands off:** Sorel leaves two problems open, and the module takes both. (i) He
  himself asks why the general strike breeds "sublimity" in some countries and not in
  others, and answers only "here national traditions play a great part" (Ch. VI end,
  p. 251) — the door through which the right takes the myth and swaps the class for the
  nation; `junger-storm-of-steel-and-the-worker` supplies a myth made not in a strike but
  at the front, and `moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich` a national socialism against
  Marx. (ii) An ethic of violence with no stated limit: the lesson ends with the
  objection unrefuted, and `the-right-in-1933` tests what the cult of decision and
  energy did when it met a movement that used both.
- **Spine step:** "The radical right turns from conserving to revolution — Sorel's myth,
  Jünger's front and Worker, Moeller's Third Reich, Evola's Tradition — and in 1933 its
  thinkers have to choose: Schmitt and Heidegger join, Spengler refuses"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): by 1906 French socialists sat in parliament, negotiated
with ministers and won reforms; a retired engineer who admired Marx thought this was the
end of socialism, not its success; what moves masses is not a programme they can check
but an image of a decisive battle they can believe in; he called it a myth.

Anchors (L11), in the first section: Georges Sorel (1847–1922), an engineer of the state
roads-and-bridges service who retired at 45 to read and write; the Third Republic of the
1900s: socialist deputies in the Chamber, one of them (Millerand) a minister since 1899,
the unified Socialist party (1905) led by Jean Jaurès, the great parliamentary orator;
the CGT, the trade-union confederation, whose 1906 congress at Amiens declared the
unions independent of every party and the general strike their weapon; the May Day
strike of 1906 for the eight-hour day. "Syndicalism" defined at first use: unions
(syndicats) as the organ of revolution, not the party. Reflections appeared as articles
in 1906 and as a book in 1908. Three new terms per section at most.

1. **What is wrong with a socialism that wins?** The newcomer's expectation: a workers'
   party that sends deputies, passes labour laws and enters the cabinet is succeeding.
   Sorel's answer, assembled from Ch. I–II: social legislation is "extorted by fear",
   not granted by justice — "the most decisive factor in social politics is the
   cowardice of the Government" (p. 69); a bourgeoisie that buys social peace by
   concession is a class that has lost its nerve (Clemenceau's "everything that lives
   resists", turned against its author, pp. 70–71); and the socialist deputy who
   brokers the concessions becomes a professional of bargaining with an interest in the
   struggle never ending (the Michels result, Ref, one clause). Marx's schema needed a
   conquering, pitiless bourgeoisie facing a united, warlike proletariat (pp. 84–91); a
   revolution made in decadence only inherits decadence (Rome, the barbarians, pp.
   92–98). So the danger is not repression but SOCIAL PEACE: both classes go soft,
   and nothing great is born. Seam (state the reversal): from a Marxist one expects the
   bourgeoisie's weakness to be welcome; Sorel wants it strong.
2. **What then holds a class together, if not its programme?** Obvious attempt: a
   doctrine whose predictions the workers can trust (the "scientific" socialism of
   the parties). Why it fails: predictions are tested and fail; programmes are
   bargained into reforms; "the man who draws up a programme for the future is a
   reactionary" (Marx to Beesly, quoted p. 150). Answer: the MYTH (concept
   `political-myth`) — define from Letter III pp. 22–23 and Ch. IV: men in a great
   movement picture their coming action as a battle in which their cause is certain to
   triumph; the image must be taken whole, never compared item by item with what later
   happens. The tour of examples, quickly: primitive Christianity's apocalypse, the
   Reformation, the soldiers of 1792, Mazzini's Italy, the Church militant. The
   "because": an image grasped whole moves the will before analysis can divide it (the
   one sentence on Bergson: a philosopher of the day who held that intuition grasps
   living wholes that analysis cuts into dead parts).
3. **Myth versus utopia — why a myth cannot be refuted.** Draw the distinction as the
   answer to the obvious objection ("but the general strike will never happen"):
   a utopia is "an intellectual product", a model of a future society that can be
   compared with the present, argued over, refuted, and bargained into piecemeal reform;
   a myth is "not a description of things, but an expression of a determination to act"
   (pp. 32–33). Whether the general strike is "a partial reality, or only a product of
   popular imagination, is of little importance" (p. 137): what matters is whether it
   contains everything socialism expects of the proletariat. Backward link: Mosca's
   political formula (Ref `mosca-ruling-class`) is the ruling minority's principle of
   obedience; Sorel's myth is the insurgent class's principle of combat — the same
   discovery that men act on images, put to opposite uses. **Where the first OBJECTION
   enters (from the liberal and the scientific Marxist at once):** an image immune to
   refutation is immune to correction; a movement that cannot learn it is wrong cannot
   learn anything. Sorel's reply, at full strength (Ch. IV.3, pp. 154–167): every body
   of knowledge has a clear and an obscure region, production lies in the obscure one,
   and the physicist too computes on theories he knows will not last — acting on an
   image one knows to be provisional "is the scientific spirit". The lesson states the
   reply and the residue: the physicist's theory is dropped when experiment refutes it;
   the myth is built so that no experiment can.
4. **Why violence, and what kind?** Obvious reading: Sorel praises bloodshed. Correct
   it from the text (L13 slot — this misreading is real and common): he distinguishes
   FORCE — "the object of force is to impose a certain social order in which the
   minority governs" — from VIOLENCE, which "tends to the destruction of that order" (p.
   195; concept `proletarian-violence`). Bourgeois force is judicial: it punishes,
   proscribes, takes revenge under legal forms — the line runs from the Inquisition
   through royal justice to the Terror's tribunals (Ch. III, Taine's lawyers recalled
   by Ref). Proletarian violence is "purely and simply acts of war", without hatred or
   revenge; "in war the vanquished are not killed" (pp. 121–122); its function is to
   mark the separation of the classes and to make each strike "a reduced facsimile" of
   the final battle (pp. 126–127). It does not need much blood: the early Church's
   martyrs were few — barely two dozen in Roman Africa in forty years, by a church
   historian's count — yet the apocalyptic myth magnified each case into a war (pp.
   209–213). Answer: violence is a means of keeping the myth credible and the class
   apart, not of winning a street fight. The "because": without it the strike becomes a
   negotiation and the myth dies.
5. **Why not take the state?** The obvious socialist goal — conquer the state, then use
   it — is for Sorel the bourgeois plan in red. The political general strike (Belgium,
   Russia 1905) is a lever on parliament run by party chiefs; the proletarian general
   strike is one undivided act aimed at abolishing the state. The dictatorship of the
   proletariat, modelled on the absolute monarchy and 1793, would end in "a wonderful
   system of slavery" (p. 195); politicians treat the proletariat as "food for cannon"
   (p. 190). Demagogues cultivate jealousy and vengeance; the general strike replaces
   them with "a sentiment of the sublime" and "the pride of free men" (pp. 185–187).
   Bridge to `ressentiment-and-envy` later in the course: name nothing, but note the
   theme (the lesson will not borrow the later argument — one clause, no Ref needed).
   Le Bon corrected (set piece 5): crowds are conservative only where there is no idea
   of class war (p. 145).
6. **An ethic born of war: the producer.** The question Ch. VI–VII answer: where does
   morality come from, if not from preaching? Sorel: "lofty moral convictions never
   depend on reasoning … but on a state of war in which men voluntarily participate and
   which finds expression in well-defined myths" (p. 244); where the enemy ceased to be
   formidable, "sublimity has vanished from the ethics of both parties, giving place to
   a morality of extraordinary meanness" (p. 245). The producers' ethic: the artist's
   urge to surpass every model, the exactitude of the soldier of 1794 ("intelligent
   bayonets", p. 285), work done for its own perfection without exact reward; set
   against the consumer's ethic, which Sorel finds in Aristotle's golden mean and in the
   parliamentary socialist. Nietzsche's master morality enters as Sorel's own frame for
   the warrior type (Ref `nietzsche/master-morality`, one clause). Thesis: "It is to
   violence that Socialism owes those high ethical values by means of which it brings
   salvation to the modern world" (p. 295).
7. **Why both extremes claimed him.** State what each side could take without forgery.
   The revolutionary left: the general strike, anti-parliamentarism, the destruction of
   the state, the class as the bearer of morality. The radical right: contempt for
   parliament and bargaining, the diagnosis of bourgeois decadence, the warrior ethic,
   the myth as the engine of mass action — and the open door Sorel leaves himself:
   asked why the myth inspires in some countries and not in others, "here national
   traditions play a great part" (p. 251). Then the record, each item dated, confidence
   graded (see Pitfalls): the Cercle Proudhon (1911), where monarchists of Action
   française (Ref `barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism`) and Sorel's syndicalist
   disciples met; Mussolini, a socialist editor before 1914, later naming Sorel as a
   teacher (a claim Mussolini made); Sorel's own "Plea for Lenin" (1919) and his
   admiration for the Bolsheviks at his death in 1922 — while Lenin had dismissed him in
   1909. Answer: what passed between camps was not a programme (Sorel had forbidden
   himself one) but a FORM — myth plus violence plus contempt for the bargaining class —
   that any group able to picture itself in a decisive war could fill. **Seam:** the
   right's problem with Sorel is its own: it can take the form only by replacing the
   class with the nation, and the nation is a community of classes — the "social peace"
   Sorel despised. The next lessons are attempts to solve exactly that.
8. **The OBJECTION at full strength: an ethic of violence with no account of its
   limits.** State it as the course's own, from both sides. (a) The liberal and
   socialist objection (Jaurès, quoted by Sorel himself, p. 20: "violence is the sign
   of temporary weakness"): legality and votes won the workers what strikes never did,
   and violence invites the state's far greater force. (b) The internal objection:
   Sorel's limits — no hatred, no revenge, no judicial killing of the vanquished, "I
   have a horror of any measure which strikes the vanquished under a judicial disguise"
   (Appendix) — are stated as the character of proletarian violence, but nothing in the
   theory secures them: the myth is irrefutable, the violence is judged only by its
   "remote consequences" for the revolution (Intro to First Publication, pp. 47–49), and
   no one is authorised to say when an act has crossed from war into vendetta. (c) The
   conservative objection, from the course's earlier lessons: Burke's charge that
   abstract principle licenses unlimited means (Ref `burke-reflections`) recoils here on
   a man who rejected abstract principle — the danger lies not in the theory's
   abstraction but in its indifference to evidence. Sorel's reply, as far as the text
   gives one: the violence he describes is the strike, not the barricade; it needs "only
   a few short conflicts"; and the bloodiest men in history were optimists with power,
   not pessimists without it (p. 9). The lesson concedes the reply's force about the
   Terror and states the residue: the theory has no institution that could hold the
   limits it describes — the problem `the-right-in-1933` returns to.

Headings as claims (suggested): "Social peace as decadence"; "A myth is a will, not a
forecast"; "Force imposes, violence breaks"; "Against taking the state"; "Morality from
war: the producer"; "Why the left and the right could both use him"; "What limits the
violence?".

   to call myths"), then the list, then the Church-militant sentence. Parse: the image is
   of a battle; its outcome is certain; it is taken whole.

   versus "the general strike as a myth" (nothing in 1906 can falsify it).
3. **"Of little importance"** (Ch. IV, l.5926, p. 137): the sentence that the general
   strike may be "only a product of popular imagination" — the reader is asked to state
   what kind of claim survives that concession.
4. **Force and violence** (Ch. V, l.8505, p. 195) beside **acts of war** (Ch. III,
   l.5278–5290, pp. 121–122): the definition card, then the claim about what proletarian
   violence is like. This is where the L13 refutation lives.
5. **Le Bon corrected** (Ch. IV, l.6303–6315, p. 145): Le Bon's crowds "conservative",
   flocking "to a Caesar"; Sorel: true "only for societies which lack the conception of
   the class war". Backward Ref to `taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd`; forward
   echo of Spengler's Caesarism (Ref `spengler-decline-of-the-west`, one clause).
6. **The martyrs' arithmetic** (Ch. VI, l.9116–9125, pp. 209–210): a church historian's
   count — before 180 no martyrdom in Roman North Africa, and from 180 to after 220
   "scarcely more than two dozen" — against the Christians' own language of a war of
   persecution. The keeper number: a few dozen cases sufficed to found a myth of
   martyrdom. This is the one number the lesson needs; it carries the claim that a myth
   needs episodes, not statistics.
7. **"National traditions play a great part"** (Ch. VI end, l.10958–10966, p. 251): the
   question Sorel raises and declines to answer, quoted whole — the hinge of link 7 and
   the module.
8. **The Appendix of 1908** (l.12942–13037): "Socialism could not continue to exist
   without an apology for violence"; the horror of judicial vengeance; "the epic of the
   strikes" set against Napoleon's officials and the Grande Armée that "would remain
   poor". Use for the limits objection (link 8).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **The register rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea:
  no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through
  argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it
  and when.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), and where:** syndicalism / syndicat and the CGT (section 1);
  the general strike (section 1 — a simultaneous stoppage of all trades meant to bring
  down the order, not to win a wage); Jaurès and the unified Socialist party (section 1);
  Millerand's ministry (1899 — section 1, one clause); "the bourgeoisie" and "middle
  class" are the same word in Hulme (bourgeoisie) — say so once; utopia (section 3);
  Bergson (section 2, one sentence); Pelloutier and the bourses du travail only if used;
  the Terror and the revolutionary tribunals (Ref `burke-reflections`/`taine-and-le-bon…`,
  one clause); Mazzini (Italian unifier, one clause); Tertullian (a Christian writer of
  Carthage, c. 200); Action française (Ref); Cercle Proudhon (named once, dated); Lenin
  and Mussolini (each a half-sentence of what they were in the year cited).
- **The real misreading (L13): "Sorel advocates bloodshed / terror."** Refute from the
  force/violence distinction, the "acts of war" passage, the horror of judicial
  vengeance, and the martyrs (a myth needs few episodes). Then, NOT as refutation but as
  the objection, show that the text's limits are unsecured (link 8). One slot.
- **A second real confusion:** "myth" in Sorel does not mean "a false story". It is a
  neutral technical term for an image that moves action; Sorel says outright that the
  general strike may never happen. State this at the definition.
- **Sorel's readers — graded confidence (do not flatten into one line):** documented in
  the dossier: Fawcett's report that Mussolini claimed to keep Sorel by his bed, and that
  Sorel ended as an admirer of Lenin; Muller's note that Sternhell calls Sorel "the
  seminal figure" for fascist ideology (a contested thesis — Sternhell's reading is
  disputed by historians who stress Sorel's distance from nationalism; state it as one
  reading, in References). Not in the dossier, to verify before stating: the "Plea for
  Lenin" (1919); Lenin's dismissal (1909); the Cercle Proudhon membership. The
  course.yaml key idea "Lenin's and Mussolini's uses" overstates the Lenin side: Lenin
  did not use Sorel; Sorel admired Lenin. The lesson says so.
- **Period prejudices in the text:** antisemitic asides (Letter III's Herr passage l.2053
  region; Ch. VI p. 222 and p. 239 notes; Ch. VII p. 255) and remarks on women. Neither
  hide nor amplify: the set pieces avoid them; if Sorel's later turn is mentioned (Fawcett:
  "turned anti-Semitic" after 1918), state it as fact with its date.
- **Hulme's translation** is of the 1908 text, with the Appendix; it lacks the 1919
  "Plea for Lenin". Page numbers are Hulme's (inline running heads in the OCR).
- **Dated detail:** the book is thick with 1900s parliamentary names (Viviani, Millerand,
  Waldeck-Rousseau, Combes). Use at most Jaurès and Clemenceau; the rest is
  evidence-budget waste (L12).
- **Do not re-teach** Marx's class struggle, Michels's iron law, Mosca's formula, Le
  Bon's crowd, Nietzsche's master morality, Schmitt's decision — each one clause plus
  `<Ref>`.
- **Do not make Sorel a man of the right.** He is a Marxist syndicalist who wrote for a
  workers' movement; the lesson's question is why his form travelled, and the answer is
  given from what the text contains (link 7). Equally, do not make the right's borrowing
  a misreading: the anti-parliamentary, anti-bourgeois and warrior passages are there.
- **Numbers (L12):** one keeper number (the martyrs, set piece 6) and the dates (1899,
  1905, 1906, 1908, 1911, 1919, 1922). No strike statistics.
- **Dossier thin on:** Sorel's biography (only Fawcett's paragraph and glossary), his
  reception (no Sternhell, no Berlin essay, no Plea for Lenin), the French strike record
  of 1906–08. The argument itself is fully grounded in the Hulme text.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (the only mechanical one):** state Sorel's definitions of myth,
   utopia, force and violence, each in one sentence, and give one example of each from
   the lesson.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** the Appendix sentence "Socialism could not
   continue to exist without an apology for violence" (Le Matin, May 1908). Author (a
   retired engineer and syndicalist theorist), date (1908, the year of the
   Draveil–Villeneuve strikes and their shootings — anchor in the solution), audience (a
   mass Paris daily), purpose. What does it record, argue, omit (the limits Sorel states
   elsewhere)? What can it be used to establish about syndicalism in 1908, and what not
   (what strikers believed)?
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Sorel's myth is a lie told to the workers to make them
   fight." Name the step that fails (a myth is not a description, so it is neither a true
   nor a false report; Sorel says the strike may never happen and that this is "of little
   importance"), then state what IS left of the charge (that a leader can manufacture
   episodes to feed a myth — the martyrs' arithmetic cuts both ways).
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** suppose the bourgeoisie stops conceding and meets every
   strike with full force. Does Sorel's theory predict the myth's death or its
   strengthening? Work it from Ch. I–II (a pitiless bourgeoisie is what Marx's schema
   needs) and from the martyrs (repression magnifies episodes). Then state the case the
   theory cannot handle: force so great that no strike occurs at all.
5. **Retrieval across lessons (E3):** Mosca's political formula (Ref
   `mosca-ruling-class`) and Sorel's myth. Give one feature they share and two in which
   they differ (who uses it; whether it can be argued with; what it is for).
6. **Derivation the lesson did not do (E6c):** from Sorel's own premises — morality is
   born of a war voluntarily joined and expressed in a well-defined myth — derive what
   he must say about the morale of a nation at war in 1914. Then show why this conclusion
   embarrasses a syndicalist who wrote "antipatriotism" into the general-strike idea, and
   which sentence of Ch. VI (p. 251, national traditions) shows he saw the difficulty.
7. **Counter-argument (synthesis):** "Sorel's limits on violence — no hatred, no revenge,
   no judicial killing — make his theory safe." Name two facts the claim fails to account
   for (the myth is immune to refutation; violence is judged only by its remote
   consequences, with no judge named) and state what would have to be true for it to
   survive (an institution, inside the movement, able to say when an act is war and when
   vendetta).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `junger-storm-of-steel-and-the-worker`:** take Sorel's three marks
   of a myth (an image of a decisive battle; certainty of victory; taken whole, not
   checked) and the producer's ethic (surpassing models, exactitude, work without exact
   reward). Which of them could be satisfied by a soldier's experience of the Western
   Front rather than by a strike, and which one would a defeated army have to give up?
   The solution says in one sentence that the next lesson follows an officer who made the
   front itself such an image — and kept the battle while losing the certainty of victory
   ("We might be crushed, but surely we could not be conquered") — with a `<Ref>`.

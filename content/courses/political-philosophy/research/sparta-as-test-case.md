- **Contributes:** One move (L16 application): the module's three theories — the mixed constitution, natural hierarchy, the decline of regimes — are run on one real regime that every thinker in the module admired and faulted, and the lesson judges, case by case, where each fits the record and where the record resists, and then answers the module question (which Greek answer to "who should rule" survives a real city): the mixed constitution of the middle, on balance. - **Spine step:** "Aristotle grounds rule in nature: man is a political animal, hierarchy runs through all things, some are slaves by nature — and he admits the theory's difficulty himself; the stable city is the one the middle governs"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):**
  - *The artefact:* the Spartan regime as it is reported — Aristotle Politics II.9 (the one sustained ancient analysis in the dossier), Plato Laws III (the moderated monarchy) and Republic VIII (timocracy), Vernant's reconstruction of the hoplite city (ch. 4), Thucydides' two dry reports (IV.80, the two thousand helots who claimed their freedom and "no one ever knew how each of them perished"; V.68, "owing to the secrecy of their government the number of the Lacedaemonians was not known") — the reader already knows this witness — and, outside the dossier, Plutarch's Lycurgus (the Great Rhetra, the lots, the messes, the agoge, the krypteia) and Xenophon's Constitution of the Lacedaemonians (the only account by a contemporary admirer). The artefact is the REPORTED regime; the lesson states that the sources are late, admiring or hostile, and that "what is known" is a sourcing problem before it is a political one.
  - *Module results it uses (by concept id / lesson):* `mixed-constitution` (`decline-of-regimes`, Laws III; `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, IV.9 "called either name"); `regime-cycle` (`decline-of-regimes`, timocracy; Aristotle V.12); `natural-slavery` and `greek-barbarian` (`aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`); `polity` and the stability test (`aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`); `stasis` and the single-virtue critique (`aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`, V.2–3, VII.14); `isonomia` and the hoplite city (`greek-miracle`).
  - *Departures from the idealisation the lesson must name:* (1) The mixed constitution of Laws III is three checks on kingship; Aristotle's Sparta shows the ephors as "tyrannical", bribable, chosen "childishly", with the kings courting them — the mixture held, but not as a balance of virtue, as a balance of interest ("the people are contented when they have a share in the highest office"). (2) The "equals" (homoioi) of the hoplite idealisation were a shrinking minority: the land passed "into the hands of a few", two-fifths to women, and the citizen roll fell below a thousand — the city of equals was an oligarchy of fewer and fewer. (3) Natural slavery predicts a relation of common interest between natural master and natural slave; the helots "are always lying in wait for the Spartans' misfortunes", must be managed by terror (the krypteia), and were the kinsmen of free Messenians the Spartans had conquered — slavery by conquest, which Aristotle's own I.6 refuses to justify. (4) Plato's timocracy is a regime of honour decaying toward money; Aristotle's Sparta is a regime trained for one virtue that, having won its empire, "lost the better part of life" — two different decline stories, and the record (the loss of Messenia after Leuctra, 371) fits Aristotle's better. (5) The reader's own idealisation: "Sparta" as a byword for discipline. The record is a few thousand men policing a countryside.

## VERDICT

This lesson carries the MODULE verdict (research/verdicts/REGISTER.md, Module 1). It is binding: state it no more and no less firmly.

- **Claim judged (the module question):** who should rule, and which of the three Greek answers (rule by knowledge, rule by nature, the mixed regime of the middle) survives a test against a real city.
- **Verdict:** of the three, the mixed constitution holds up best against Sparta. Grade: **on balance**.
- **What decides it:**
  - *The mixture.* Sparta lasted for centuries without a tyrant or a change of regime (Thucydides I.18), and Aristotle gives the reason in II.9: "the people are contented when they have a share in the highest office … all the parts of the state must wish that it should exist". What held the city together was a balance of interest, not a balance of virtue (the ephors bribable and "tyrannical", the elders "childishly" elected, the kings distrusted).
  - *Decline.* Aristotle's single-virtue account fits the record better than Plato's sequence from honour to money: the city trained for war won its empire (404), ran short of citizens (*oliganthropia*: "the whole number of Spartan citizens fell below 1000"; "the want of men was their ruin") through the land law and the mess dues, and lost Messenia after Leuctra (371). The gold arrives after 404; the shrinking roll is attested before it.
  - *Rule by nature, in the form the module tested — mastery by nature — fails* (round 2 wording: `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery` now splits "rule by nature" into four parts; Sparta tests only the fourth, that some men are by nature fit to be owned. It does not test, and the lesson must not appear to deny, that people differ in the capacity to lead or that the more capable should hold office among equals; on that, Sparta's yearly ephors chosen from the whole people are part of the mixture that held). The helots were Greeks enslaved by conquest, held down by the *krypteia* and "always lying in wait" (II.9). Aristotle's own I.6 refuses to justify slavery of that kind, and II.9 (with II.5, II.10) treats the helots as a management problem, never as slaves by nature.
  - *Rule by knowledge* has no case in the record to test: no city was ever ruled by philosophers, and Sparta, unwritten and secret, was the opposite of a city of reasoned knowledge.
  - The answer that survives is Aristotle's: give every part a share, and keep the citizen body broad. Sparta's end is the second half of that sentence failing.
- **Strongest objection:** the record is one city, known only through admirers and enemies (the "Spartan mirage"), and Sparta may have held together through fear of the helots rather than through its mixture (the reading that Spartan moderation was "rooted in their fear of a slave revolt", Thucydides VIII.24, 40 — Bolotin in Strauss–Cropsey, paraphrased as one view).
- **Why it does not overturn the verdict:** it weakens "proves" to "fits", which is why the grade is on balance and not strong. It does not reverse the verdict, because Aristotle's own account already names the shrinking citizen body and the helot danger as the causes of the end; fear of the helots explains why the citizens closed ranks, not why kings, elders and people each kept wanting the same regime for four centuries. And the theories are tested against the same reports their authors used.
- **What would overturn it:** evidence that a closed clique held Sparta's offices throughout its stable centuries (so that the ephorate's openness "to the whole people" was nominal and no share held the people).

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): every thinker in this module admired Sparta and every one found something wrong with it; two kings, a council of elders, elected overseers, a citizen body of equals who ate together and trained for war; a subject population many times larger held down by terror; citizen numbers that fell until the city could not field an army; set what is known against what Plato and Aristotle said. The lesson runs as an application (L16): artefact → the module's three theories applied in turn → where each fits and where it does not → the departures named → the forward reading.

1. **What is known, and from whom — the artefact is a sourcing problem first.** The obvious attempt: describe the Spartan constitution. Why it fails as a first step: Sparta wrote nothing down (Vernant: "speech was still rhetrai" — unwritten sayings), kept its numbers secret (Thucydides says so at Mantinea), and is known from an Athenian admirer who lived there (Xenophon, c. 370s), a Macedonian-educated critic who collected cases (Aristotle, c. 330), a Boeotian biographer under Rome half a millennium after the founding (Plutarch, c. AD 100), and a Cretan colony-planner's Spartan interlocutor in a Platonic dialogue. The answer: build the regime from what the sources agree on — two hereditary kings from two houses; a council of twenty-eight elders plus the kings (gerousia) elected for life; five ephors ("overseers") elected annually from all citizens; an assembly (apella) of citizen-equals (homoioi) that voted by shouting and could not debate; each citizen holding a lot (kleros) worked by helots and paying his share to a mess (syssition); boys taken at seven into the agoge; a subject population of helots (the conquered Messenians and Laconians) and free non-citizen perioeci — and mark what they disagree on (the number of lots; whether the krypteia was Lycurgan; whether the "equals" were ever equal in land). Concept `spartan-constitution`. Because: a test of a theory against a record needs the record's provenance stated before the theory touches it (playbook-history, metadata first).
2. **The Great Rhetra read as a document.** Plutarch quotes an oracle-shaped founding law (set piece 1): build the temples, divide the people, establish a gerousia of thirty with the kings, hold assemblies between two landmarks, "and the people must have the deciding voice" — then a rider, attributed to later kings, that if the people "adopt a crooked measure" the elders and kings may dissolve the assembly. Sourcing drill in full: author (Plutarch, citing Aristotle's lost Constitution of the Lacedaemonians), date (the text archaic; the quotation Roman-era), audience, what it records (an assembly with a final voice, and a council that proposes), what it argues (nothing — it commands), what it omits (the ephors, who are not in it — so they are later, as Laws III also says: "your third saviour"). What it can be USED to establish: that the mixed constitution was built in stages, as Laws III says, and that the people's "deciding voice" was from early on hedged by a veto — the first departure from the idealisation. Because: the one surviving text of the regime shows the mixture as a sequence of amendments, not a design.
3. **Theory one — the mixed constitution: fit.** Laws III's idealisation (`<Ref>` `decline-of-regimes`): one king is too strong; a god gave two; "human wisdom" added twenty-eight elders equal in weight; "a third saviour" added the ephors "like magistrates chosen by lot"; so the regime was "compounded of the right elements and duly moderated" and lasted where Argos and Messene fell. Aristotle's test (`<Ref>` `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`): a good mixture "may be called either a democracy or an oligarchy" — and Sparta is called both (election from oligarchy, no property bar from democracy); Megillus could not name it. The record fits: no tyrant, no revolution of the citizen body for three centuries, which no other Greek city matched. Aristotle says WHY it fit, and his reason is not Plato's: "the people are contented when they have a share in the highest office … if a constitution is to be permanent, all the parts must wish that it should exist — the kings because they have honour, the nobles because they sit in the council, the people because all are eligible to the ephoralty." Because: the stability thesis is confirmed, but as a balance of interests, not of virtue — DEPARTURE (1): Aristotle's ephors are "tyrannical", bribable (the Andrian affair), poor men "open to bribes", courted by the kings; the elders are "irresponsible" and chosen "childishly"; the kings are so little trusted that the city sends enemies abroad with them as colleagues. The mixture held because every part had something to lose.
4. **Theory two — hierarchy and natural slavery: the helots as the case the theory cannot classify.** Recall the theory (`<Ref>` `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`): a natural slave apprehends reason without having it; where the relation is natural, master and slave "are friends and have a common interest"; where it rests "merely on law and force the reverse is true"; conquest confers no title. Now the record: the helots were the Messenians, Greeks of the same Dorian stock, conquered in two wars (c. 730 and c. 650) and bound to the land; Aristotle's own words — they "have often risen against their masters" and "are always lying in wait for the Spartans' misfortunes"; "if not kept in hand they are insolent and think they are as good as their masters; if harshly treated they hate and conspire"; the Spartans "have not found out the secret of managing their subject population". Plutarch's krypteia — young men sent into the countryside by night to kill helots — which Plutarch himself refuses to attribute to Lycurgus. The seam, dry: from a theory of natural hierarchy one would expect its admirers to produce the paradigm case; the one Greek regime built entirely on a servile class is the case Aristotle discusses ONLY as a management problem — never once as natural slavery. By his own I.6, Greeks do not call Greeks slaves, "some are slaves everywhere, others nowhere", and conquest proves nothing; so the helots are legal slaves whose natural status is unknown and whose behaviour ("they think they are as good as their masters") is the behaviour of men with free souls. DEPARTURE (3). The STRONGEST reply on Sparta's behalf, at full strength: Xenophon's — the system produced the best soldiers and the most obedient citizens in Greece, and the helots were not chattel but a conquered people on their own land, with their own families and a share of the crop; and Aristotle's own Book VII wants exactly such "spiritless farmers of mixed race" for the best city. The lesson concedes both and states the result: the theory of natural slavery, tested on Sparta, becomes a theory of how to hold down men you have conquered — the thing I.6 said was "merely law and force". The course does not moralise; it reports what the theory does when applied.
5. **Theory three — decline: two stories, one record.** Plato's (`<Ref>` `decline-of-regimes`): Sparta is timocracy, the honour regime, which decays when honour gives way to secret gold — and Aristotle confirms the gold ("wealth is too highly valued"; the citizens "run away from the law into secret indulgence"). Aristotle's own: the constitution "has regard to one part of virtue only, the virtue of the soldier"; so "they were preserved while at war, but fell when they had attained empire; they did not know how to be at leisure" — "like unused iron" (`<Ref>` `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`). The record: Sparta won the Peloponnesian war (404), held an empire for thirty years, lost one battle at Leuctra (371) to Thebes, and within a year had lost Messenia and half its land — "the city sank under a single defeat". Then the mechanism Aristotle names and the lesson must make a figure of: the lots passed "into the hands of a few" by gift and bequest; "nearly two-fifths of the whole country" came to women through heiresses and dowries; men who could not pay their mess dues lost citizenship; so "although the country is able to maintain 1500 cavalry and 30,000 hoplites, the whole number of Spartan citizens fell below 1000" — oliganthropia, "the want of men was their ruin". Keeper numbers (L12c): eight thousand at the time of the Persian wars (Herodotus's Demaratus); about a thousand by Aristotle's day — bound as reports, not a census. DEPARTURE (2): the "city of equals" was an oligarchy of fewer and fewer; DEPARTURE (4): the record fits Aristotle's single-virtue story better than Plato's honour-to-money story, and the lesson says so and why (the gold was a symptom; the land law and the mess dues were the mechanism). Xenophon's ch. 14 — the admirer's own admission that the Spartans "no longer obey" Lycurgus — dated to the 370s, is the contemporary witness.
6. **The women — Aristotle's second mechanism and the test of the household theory.** II.9: "in those states in which the condition of the women is bad, half the city may be regarded as having no laws"; Lycurgus "tried to bring the women under his laws" and failed; they "live in every sort of intemperance and luxury", held two-fifths of the land, and in the Theban invasion "caused more confusion than the enemy". Xenophon's ch. 1, at full strength on the other side: the women were trained like the men because the lawgiver thought childbearing a woman's chief work, and strong mothers made strong sons. The lesson applies the household lesson's ladder (`<Ref>`): Aristotle's theory says the husband rules the wife "constitutionally"; the Spartan record shows the rule failing exactly where the men were away at war — the hierarchy did not hold without the presence of the ruler, which is what a hierarchy "by nature" should not need. Stated as the module's own finding, not as a modern verdict.
7. **The weighing: of the three Greek answers, the mixed regime of the middle survives — on balance.** (Last body section; heading states the verdict as a claim, e.g. "Of the three Greek answers, the mixed regime of the middle survives".) Build the ledger (figure 7) with the lesson's verdicts, not a blank column: mixed constitution / stability — fits, with Aristotle's reason (interest, not virtue); the single-virtue decline — fits the record of 371 and the land law; Plato's timocracy — fits the symptom, misses the mechanism; natural slavery — does not fit: it cannot classify the helots and is never applied to them by its own author; the household hierarchy — untested (the corrupted-case clause leaves it no prediction to break). Then map the tests onto the module question: rule by nature fails by Aristotle's own I.6; rule by knowledge (`<Ref>` `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`) has no case in the record — no city was ruled by it — so the record cannot test it, a finding stated in one sentence; the mixed regime of the middle survives: it held while every part had a share, and it ended when the citizen body shrank. The answer that survives is Aristotle's: give every part a share and keep the citizen body broad. The STRONGEST OBJECTIONS, both at full strength: (a) from the sources' side — every witness is Athenian, Macedonian-trained or Roman-era; Sparta's own voice is a founding oracle and some sayings; "what is known" may be the Athenian mirage of Sparta as much as Sparta; (b) the rival cause — Sparta held together through fear of the helots, not through its mixture (Thucydides IV.80, "their policy at all times having been governed by the necessity of taking precautions against them"). The replies: (a) the theories are tested against the SAME reports their authors used — the test is fair to the theories even if it is not a history of Sparta; (b) fear explains closed ranks against the helots, not why kings, elders and people each kept wanting the same regime for four centuries, and Aristotle's own account names the helot danger and the shrinking roll as the causes of the end. Together they weaken "proves" to "fits": hence on balance, not strong. Say what would overturn it: evidence that a closed clique held Sparta's offices throughout its stable centuries.
8. **`<Conclusion>`.** First paragraph states the verdict in plain words with its grade: of the module's three answers to who should rule, the mixed regime of the middle holds up best against Sparta, on balance — stable while every part had a share, undone when the land law shrank the citizen body; natural slavery fails by its own author's test; rule by knowledge has no case to test; on balance because the record is one city known only from foreigners, and fear of the helots may have done some of the mixture's work. Second paragraph, the hand-off: the regime outlived its purpose (Messenia lost 369; the system kept until 188 BC) — the forward reading as one sentence with `<Ref>` `toynbee-challenge-and-response` (a later historian of civilizations classes Sparta as "arrested", "fast bound in misery and iron", and takes Aristotle's VII.14 as its "epitaph"; no more than a sentence or two, the course's own); then what passes on: the verdict said "keep the citizen body broad", and the next city did — a Greek hostage, who has watched Rome conquer the Greek world, sets Lycurgus's designed mixture beside Rome's grown one and asks why the second could rule an empire and the first could not (`polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`).

Headings that fall out: "What is known of Sparta, and who says so" · "The Great Rhetra: a mixture built in stages" · "The mixed constitution fits — for Aristotle's reason, not Plato's" · "The helots: the case natural slavery never classifies" · "Two stories of decline and the land law that decided it" · "Half the city without laws: the household hierarchy tested" · "Of the three Greek answers, the mixed regime of the middle survives" (the forward reading lives in the Conclusion, not its own section).

Three sentences; it shows the "third saviour" as a check on kings that is religious before it is constitutional.
3. **Aristotle on the helots** (II.9, 1269a36–b12): "lying in wait for their misfortunes"; insolent if loose, conspiring if harsh; "the secret of managing their subject population". Set beside I.6's "merely law and force … the reverse is true" in a two-column figure (figure 4). Then Plutarch 28 on the krypteia and his refusal to attribute it to Lycurgus — quote the refusal.
4. **The land law and the want of men** (II.9, 1270a15–b6): sale shamed, gift and bequest free; heiresses and dowries; two-fifths to women; the country could maintain 1500 cavalry and 30,000 hoplites and the citizens fell below 1000; "the city sank under a single defeat". 5. **"All the parts must wish it to exist"** (II.9, 1270b17–28): kings honoured, nobles in council, people eligible to the ephoralty — Aristotle's reason for the mixture's stability, quoted; set beside Laws III's three saviours as "the same institutions, two explanations" (figure 3).
6. **The women in the Theban invasion** (II.9, 1269b35–1270a8) beside Xenophon ch. 1 (women trained for strong children): the paired-source corroboration in the sourcing drill — agreement (women were trained and were free of the men's discipline), disagreement (the point of it), judgment.
7. **Xenophon's reversal** (Lak. Pol. 14): the admirer who has praised every institution for thirteen chapters writes that the Spartans now "no longer obey" the laws, keep gold at home, seek governorships abroad and are hated. Two sentences quoted; the contemporary witness to the single-virtue decline, dated to the 370s.
8. **The Sparta Museum** (Toynbee IX p. 181): early promise, then a gap where every other Greek collection has its classical masterpieces, then standardised Hellenistic work — the single-track regime "leaps to the eye of any visitor". Two sentences, paraphrased, as the course's own forward image; it is also the cue for the sourced image of a Laconian bronze (figure 9).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), in chain order:** Sparta / Lacedaemon / Laconia (the city, its state's name, its region in the south-east Peloponnese — one clause with the locator sketch), Messenia (the fertile region west of the Taygetus range, conquered c. 730 and c. 650, lost 370/369), helots (the conquered population bound to the land — anchor "serfs, not chattel slaves" in one clause and note the sources' own vagueness), perioeci (recall from the household lesson: free non-citizen "dwellers-around" in their own towns), homoioi ("equals" or "peers" — the full citizens), kleros (the lot), syssitia / the messes, the agoge (the upbringing from seven), the krypteia ("the hidden thing" — Plutarch's "secret service"), gerousia (council of elders), apella (the assembly), ephors ("overseers", five, annual), the two royal houses (Agiads and Eurypontids — one clause), Lycurgus (the legendary lawgiver; the lesson says "legendary" once and then uses the name as the sources do), Theopompus (recall from `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`), the Great Rhetra ("the great saying" — the founding oracle quoted by Plutarch), Dorians (recall from Laws III), Leuctra (371, the Theban victory over Sparta), Thebes (the Boeotian city that won it), Mantinea (418), Xenophon (Athenian soldier and writer, c. 430–354, exiled, friend of King Agesilaus), Plutarch (Greek biographer under Rome, c. AD 46–120), Herodotus and Thucydides (recall — Thucydides was anchored in `thucydides-power-and-justice`, so a `<Ref>` and a clause, not a new anchor; Herodotus in one clause if not yet anchored in the reader's path — he was quoted in `greek-miracle`), oliganthropia (Aristotle's word: "fewness of men" — keep it as the concept word for the citizen collapse), Polynesians / Eskimos / Ottomans (in the forward paragraph only, one clause as "the other arrested civilizations").
- **Metadata first, always (playbook-history).** Every Spartan "fact" in the lesson carries its source in the same sentence or in the figure's caption — not as a citation (L8a forbids naming scholars) but as the primary witness ("Aristotle reports", "Plutarch quotes", "Xenophon, who lived there, says"). The primary authors ARE the material and may be named; Vernant, Toynbee, Lord, Strauss are not and go to References.
- **Do not write a history of Sparta.** The lesson is an application: the artefact is built in one section, and every later section runs a theory on it. QA will fail a chronological narrative of Spartan history; dates enter only as anchors (c. 730/650 Messenian wars; 480 Persian wars; 404; 371) and placed relative to each other.
- **Register.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **No moralising in either direction (course tone).** The krypteia is reported in Plutarch's words with Plutarch's own refusal; Xenophon's praise is reported at full strength; the lesson's judgments are about the THEORIES (fit / does not fit), never about Sparta's goodness. Equally, do not let the lesson "defend" Sparta: the reply on Sparta's behalf in section 4 is Xenophon's and Aristotle's Book VII, named as such.
- **Natural slavery and the helots:** the lesson's finding is that Aristotle NEVER applies the natural-slavery theory to the helots — II.9 treats them as a management problem. The writer must check this against the text (politics-s1 l. 2000–2030 and I.6) and state it as a textual fact, not an inference. The inference (that they are therefore legal slaves whose natural status is unknown, by Aristotle's own criterion) is the course's own and is marked as such.
- **The Laws extra lesson must not be required.** Everything the lesson needs from Laws III (the three saviours; the two mother forms) was taught in `decline-of-regimes` and is recalled from there; `platos-laws-second-best-city` may be Ref'd as optional ("for the Laws' own second-best city, see…") and nothing else.
- **Numbers (L12, playbook-history):** the keeper numbers are three — eight thousand Spartiates c. 480 (Herodotus), under a thousand c. 371 (Aristotle), two-fifths of the land in women's hands (Aristotle) — each bound in its sentence with its witness and its uncertainty. The 9000 lots (Plutarch) and 1500 cavalry / 30,000 hoplites (Aristotle's "able to maintain") live in the figure, not prose. Plutarch's lot-ration (a medimnus of barley a month, etc.) is one clause if used at all. No modern estimates of the helot population — the dossier has none and the ancient sources give none; say "many times larger" as the opening does.
- **Summary vs primary:** II.9 pointers checked against text (l. 2007–2112, 2125–2200). The summary's "~2/5 of land" and "under 1000 citizens" are Aristotle's own figures; Ross's footnote places the "below 1000" at the Theban invasion. Vernant's ch. 4 Sparta pointers (l. 2295–2380) checked. Toynbee's §(3) is in `toynbee-s4.txt` l. 1992–2160 (checked), not where the summary's page numbers alone would send a grep.
- **Thin in the dossier (say so in the lesson's References):** Plutarch and Xenophon are not in `research/texts/`; Herodotus is (Macaulay, `greek-primary/`), and Thucydides IV.80 and V.68 are not in the staged extracts but are in the Gutenberg text named above; the writer fetches the PD translations named above and reads each passage before quoting. Aristotle's own Constitution of the Lacedaemonians is lost (Plutarch cites it) — one clause. The Strauss–Cropsey chapters are unread. No archaeological or modern-historical source is in the dossier; the lesson does not pretend to one.
- **Nothing is a demo.**

Then: which of Aristotle's two names for Sparta (democracy / oligarchy) does the rider support?
3. **Paired-source corroboration on the women.** Aristotle II.9 beside Xenophon Lak. Pol. 1 (and Plutarch 14 if fetched): agreement, disagreement, and the judgment the drill ends in — which account does the land evidence (two-fifths to women) corroborate, and what does each author's position (critic who never lived there; admirer who did) change about what each can establish?
4. **Claim to refute.** "Sparta proves Aristotle's theory of natural slavery: a master race ruled a slave race for three centuries." Name the steps that fail (Aristotle never calls the helots natural slaves; they were Greeks conquered in war, which I.6 says confers no title; their reported behaviour — "they think they are as good as their masters" — is Aristotle's own description of men who are not slaves by nature; the rule required the krypteia, which is "law and force") and state what Sparta does show about the theory (that its author did not apply it where it would have had to be applied).
5. **Boundary probe: the mixture with one part removed.** Remove the ephors (the "third saviour") from figure 2 and run Aristotle's stability test: which part no longer "wishes the regime to exist", and what does Aristotle say the ephoralty did for the people? Then remove the helots instead — the citizens must farm their own lots: which of the regime's institutions (the messes, the agoge, the standing readiness for war) can survive, and what does that show about which part of the "mixed constitution" the mixture actually rested on?
6. **Research-shaped task: the citizen-number series.** Using only the three ancient reports in figure 6, state the fall in Spartiate numbers between c. 480 and c. 371 as a ratio, bind it (reports, not censuses; Herodotus's figure is a speech put in Demaratus's mouth; Aristotle's is "at the Theban invasion" and may be the field army, not the roll), then say which of Aristotle's two mechanisms (land concentration via heiresses; loss of citizenship for unpaid mess dues) the ratio alone can and cannot distinguish, and what further evidence would (the fate of the "inferiors" — men struck from the roll — in the conspiracy of Cinadon, Xenophon Hellenica III.3, if the writer fetches it).
7. **Synthesis — check the ledger (figure 7).** For each of the five rows, write "fits / partly / does not / untested" with one sentence of evidence and one of warrant.

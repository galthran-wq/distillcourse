## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `mill-liberty-and-its-limits` (prerequisite, the lesson just before): the
  `harm-principle` in Mill's words ("the sole end for which mankind are warranted… in interfering
  with the liberty of action of any of their number, is self-protection"), its reach over "the
  moral coercion of public opinion" as well as law, the four grounds of `liberty-of-thought`
  (infallibility, corrigibility, dead dogma, half-truths), `individuality-mill` (the tree, not the
  machine; the despotism of custom), the self-/other-regarding line with "natural penalties" and
  "definite damage", and the `civilized-community-clause` (Akbar, Charlemagne, "barbarians"). That
  lesson ends on the question Stephen answers — whether the rule can do its work. Before writing,
  read `C/research/taught/mill-liberty-and-its-limits.md` (the ledger, once that lesson is
  written): whatever of Stephen it previewed (the old brief planned a full Stephen objection, which
  under the new plan belongs here) is recalled with a `<Ref>` and a clause, not re-taught. From
  `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`: `tyranny-of-the-majority` over opinion —
  the thing Mill feared and Stephen calls the normal and proper work of opinion. From
  `carlyle-heroes`: `hero-worship`, the rule of the able — Stephen's "wise minority" is its
  utilitarian, lawyerly form. From `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`: liberty as "the silence of the
  law" — Stephen's "liberty is a negation" is Hobbes's definition turned into a method; Stephen
  quotes Hobbes. From `burke-reflections` and `maistre-and-bonald`: Stephen reads Burke as "a
  utilitarian of the strongest kind" and treats Maistre as the mirror-image enthusiast of order
  (LEF ch. IV, "Mr. Buckle and De Maistre"); one clause each.
- **Contributes:** The case against the harm principle made from inside utilitarianism, at full
  strength and in its own order: (1) coercion — by law, by opinion, by religion — is the normal
  instrument by which societies form character and change, so a principle that forbids it except
  for self-protection condemns every morality and religion and cannot be applied; (2) liberty,
  equality and fraternity, taken seriously, are respectively a negation, "a big name for a small
  thing", and a sentiment that cannot bear the weight of a creed. The lesson introduces
  `coercion-by-opinion` and then puts Mill's side — through the reply his allies made in 1873 and
  through Mill's own texts — against it, and says where each holds.
- **Hands off:** (i) `marx-manifesto` (next in course order) denies that the quarrel between
  liberty and coercion is the question at all. (ii) `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government`:
  Stephen accepts Maine's "from status to contract" as a fact and denies it proves improvement —
  a rider that lesson takes up; and Stephen's view that universal suffrage only subdivides power
  while leaders rule is the opening of the Victorian case for the fragility of popular government.
  (iii) `british-franchise` tests "counting heads instead of breaking heads" and Stephen's
  "Nothing" (no alternative to universal suffrage, but no hymn to it either) against the Reform
  Acts. (iv) `locke-and-mill-on-empire` reads what Stephen made of India: the Raj resting "not on
  consent, but on conquest" and the gunpowder passage; this lesson names India only as where
  Stephen came from and as his instance that legislation cannot be neutral about religion. (v)
  `ressentiment-and-envy`: Stephen's claim that "equality" is in many cases "a vague expression of
  envy" — one of the earliest statements of the charge Rawls answers. (vi)
  `scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`: law as the image of a society's morality.
- **Spine step:** "Democracy arrives as a fact and is judged: Tocqueville foresees soft despotism,
  Carlyle demands heroes, Mill defends liberty and Stephen answers him, Marx predicts class war,
  Maine and Lecky call popular government fragile — and the British franchise tests who was right"
- **Decides (for itself and for `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`):** the question the Mill lesson

## VERDICT (lesson verdict, from research/verdicts/REGISTER.md; also settles what `mill-liberty-and-its-limits` leaves open)

**Claims judged.** (a) Mill's harm principle as "one very simple principle" that applies itself —
Stephen says it cannot. (b) Stephen's attack on Mill's case for free discussion.

**Verdict (a): Stephen shows the principle cannot apply itself.** Grade: **on balance**. Mill does
state a criterion: penalties for self-regarding faults are legitimate only "in so far as they are
the natural, and, as it were, the spontaneous consequences of the faults themselves, not because
they are purposely inflicted on him for the sake of punishment" (*On Liberty* ch. IV, staged
`research/texts/mill/on-liberty-body.txt` ll. 2822–2826). But that line runs through the motive of
the person who avoids or warns, which no one can observe; so the principle needs a judgment it
cannot itself supply. There is no contradiction between *On Liberty* and *Utilitarianism* ch. V,
as Stephen claims: Mill classes self-regarding faults as "not properly immoralities" (ll.
2857–2859), so the definition of "wrong" as what opinion may punish does not reach them.

**Verdict (b): Stephen does not refute Mill's case for free discussion.** Grade: **strong**. His
method's first question (is the object good?) needs someone entitled to decide which objects are
good for others, and his answer, "the wise minority", is the premise in dispute. His libel case
concerns a particular fact decided under rules both parties accept, not the truth of a creed. His
own limits on the criminal law (the "roughest engine"; the four limits on moral legislation)
concede most of Mill's practice, as Stephen himself says.

**Strongest objection.** To (a): a boundary can be vague and still be followed — Morley's heat and
motion — and courts apply motive tests every day (malice, intent). This does not overturn the
verdict, because a court's motive test is applied by a court, after the fact, under rules of
evidence; Mill's line must be applied by every neighbour who avoids or warns, with no judge, which
is Stephen's point. It is why the grade is on balance and not strong. To (b): Stephen's claim that
men hold opinions by temperament, not argument, is true of most men; but Mill's case rests on the
entitlement to silence, not on how many men reason, and Stephen supplies no non-question-begging
holder of that entitlement.

**What would overturn it.** For (a), a way of applying Mill's line that does not depend on
unobservable motive. For (b), a non-question-begging account of who the "wise minority" are.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

**Opening (course.yaml, verbatim):** Mill drew a line; a man back from codifying the law of India
replies that every religion, morality and government rests on coercion and that most of the
progress Mill admires was made by it; then he takes the Revolution's three words and asks of each
what it would mean if taken seriously.

**Anchor (L11), one or two sentences.** James Fitzjames Stephen (1829–1894): barrister and
journalist, brother of the critic Leslie Stephen, legal member of the Viceroy of India's council
1869–72, where he drafted codes of evidence and criminal procedure; the book was planned in
Calcutta, partly written on the voyage home in 1872, serialised in the Pall Mall Gazette and
published in 1873, the year Mill died; he became a High Court judge in 1879. (The opening's "Indian
civil servant and English judge" compresses this; the anchor states it exactly — see Pitfalls.)
One clause on why the man matters to the argument: he had spent three years applying law to a
society Mill's principle explicitly excluded, and came home convinced that the exclusion described
every society. Stephen is himself a utilitarian: the quarrel is inside one ethical theory, about
whether its own standard licenses coercion (Ref `mill-liberty-and-its-limits` for Mill's ground,
utility "in the largest sense").

1. **What exactly does the principle forbid?** Obvious reading: it limits the criminal law.
   Stephen's first move is to make the reader read Mill's sentence again (LEF p. 6, Mill ch. I):
   it governs "legal penalties, or the moral coercion of public opinion". Then his account of
   motive: men act from hope or from fear; to compel is to act on fear. There are four sanctions —
   the law, the neighbours' disapproval, punishment hereafter, conscience — and the second and
   third bear on vastly more conduct than the first, and almost none of it is self-protection.
   So the principle, applied as written, forbids every morality except "Let every man please
   himself without hurting his neighbour", and makes a day of judgment "fundamentally immoral" —
   "it ought to be a good plea… to say 'I pleased myself and hurt nobody else'". Answer: the
   principle's real target is not the gaol but opinion, and against opinion it proves too much.
   Define **`coercion-by-opinion`** here: the thesis that legal and moral coercion are the normal
   and legitimate instruments by which societies form character and change.
2. **Is coercion bad in itself?** Obvious reply for Mill: coercion has done terrible things.
   Stephen: that shows its abuse, not its nature — Mill must show "that surgery is an art bad in
   itself", not that operations hurt or surgeons blunder. Then the record of coercion that was not
   self-protective and that Mill himself would not undo: established religions spread "in the
   nature of a conquest"; morals maintained by an energetic minority on an indifferent mass; custom
   as "dams, sluices, weirs, and embankments" that turn a flood into a navigable river; the
   Reformation and every great political change carried "ultimately by the fear of revolution".
   Even Parliament: "a mild and disguised form of compulsion. We agree to try strength by counting
   heads instead of breaking heads… The minority gives way not because it is convinced that it is
   wrong, but because it is convinced that it is a minority." (If the Mill lesson quoted this line,
   Ref it and use the marsh instead; `british-franchise` tests it.)
3. **Can discussion do the work instead?** Mill's escape (Ref the `civilized-community-clause`):
   compulsion for a man's own good is for societies in their nonage; in nations "with whom we need
   here concern ourselves" the age of improvement by discussion "has long since arrived". Stephen's
   dilemma: either the clause says only that wisdom does not always license control — a
   commonplace — or it says that in England no adult can be helped by compulsion — "an incredible
   assertion". Then the evidence against the second horn: men are ignorant of exactly the subjects
   Mill would leave to discussion (millions live by Mohammed's law; how many have understood Adam
   Smith?); and there is "an enormous mass of bad and indifferent people" whom liberty does not
   improve — the stagnant marsh told it is free to run to the sea. And the Akbar point: the rulers
   Mill allows to backward peoples ruled by force of character and power, not superior
   enlightenment; the difference between a rough and a civilised society is that force is better
   guided, not that it is absent. Seam (L10b): from Mill one expects India to be the exception that
   proves the rule; Stephen, who had governed there, says it is the rule seen plainly.
4. **Does silencing an opinion assume infallibility — and is eccentricity a service?** Mill's
   first ground (Ref): to silence is to claim infallibility. Stephen's counter-case: A calls B a
   thief, B sues, the jury finds for B — a court has in effect forbidden anyone to say B is a thief,
   and no one's rational confidence that B is honest is thereby destroyed; men act every day on
   moral certainty short of infallibility. Mill's other three grounds hold, Stephen grants, for a
   small class of thinking believers; most men hold opinions by temperament. On individuality:
   character is invigorated by exertion and restraint, not by being let alone — "no theory can
   possibly be right which requires us to believe that such a man as John Knox was a poor
   heartbroken creature with no will of his own"; "Eccentricity is far more often a mark of weakness
   than a mark of strength… Originality consists in thinking for yourself, not in thinking
   differently from other people."
5. **If not one simple principle, then what?** Stephen's positive method (ch. II pp. 48–50, ch.
   IV p. 137): asking whether liberty is good is like asking whether fire is good. Compulsion is
   bad when the object is bad, when the means will not attain it, or when the price is too high —
   otherwise, on utilitarian grounds, it cannot be bad. Worked through his three instances (forced
   murder; a persecution that irritates without deterring; the spring-gun against trespassers),
   then applied by the lesson to two of Mill's own cases (Ref): the poison register and the Maine
   Law. Then the method's limits, which are Stephen's own and are the lesson's L13 slot (see
   Pitfalls): the criminal law is "the roughest engine which society can use", fit only for acts
   definable, provable and worth the damage — "impossible to indict a man for ingratitude or
   perfidy" — and moral legislation must not meddle, must not act on imperfect evidence, must be
   "graduated to the existing level of morals", and must respect privacy. Stephen is not arguing
   that the law should punish every vice; he is arguing that a society may and does press on vice,
   mostly through opinion, and that the right question is always one of object, means and price.
   One instance from India, one sentence, for the claim that legislation cannot be neutral about
   religion: British law there assumed the native religions false (the 1850 Act, schools, suttee)
   while pledging impartiality; "three relations and no more" are possible between legislation and
   religion.
6. **What is liberty, then?** Power precedes liberty: war and conquest decide what nations are and
   what their people shall believe — "Who is to say whether the Rhine or the Vosges is to divide
   France from Germany?… Force… must decide"; "it is only under the protection of a powerful,
   well-organized, and intelligent government that any liberty can exist at all". So "liberty" is a
   negation, and every praise of it must answer: "Who is left at liberty to do what, and what is the
   restraint from which he is liberated?" Recall `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty` (Ref, one clause:
   liberty as the silence of the law). Then the motto: liberty breeds inequality; equality and
   fraternity, as a common stock, leave no room for liberty — the three words pull apart.
7. **Equality: what can it mean?** Four senses (ch. V p. 189): equal subjection to the law;
   impartial administration; equal division of goods; or "a vague expression of envy on the part of
   those who have not against those who have". In justice, equality is only the fit between a law's
   general terms and the cases it covers — the red-haired-thief rule: a law imprisoning all thieves
   except red-haired ones is unequal, and one imprisoning all thieves is equal however unequal the
   thieves. Real inequalities of age, sex, capacity, wealth are never greater than now; force has
   not diminished, it has been stored — "the sun and moon are mere superfluities" because day and
   night are established. Women: Mill's four propositions (The Subjection of Women) and Stephen's
   argument for the husband's authority (the captain and first lieutenant) — given as his argument,
   in two or three sentences, with the date, and set against Mill's case in one sentence (see
   Pitfalls). Universal suffrage subdivides power but does not equalise it; leaders gather the
   fragments; asked what he would put in its place, "Nothing" — the old ways are "haycocks in a
   flood", but "I do not see why as we go with the stream we need sing Hallelujah to the river god".
   Verdict: "Equality, like liberty… a big name for a small thing."
8. **Fraternity: can love of mankind be a religion?** What is wanted from mankind is respect and
   justice, not love; love unreciprocated is a nuisance. "Humanity is only I writ large, and love
   for Humanity generally means zeal for my notions as to what men should be." A creed needs a
   statement of fact behind its feeling; the Religion of Humanity has none. Hence Stephen's last
   thesis: everything turns on whether there is a God and a future state — without them morality
   comes down to inclination; Christian love itself "stops short at the gates of hell". Cross-link
   (one clause): a German contemporary made the same claim from the opposite direction — that
   whoever gives up the Christian faith loses the right to Christian morality (Ref
   `nietzsche/herd-morality-in-modern-dress`). The book ends on the mountain pass in "whirling snow
   and blinding mist": act on probabilities, "dream no dreams, and tell no lies".
9. **The OBJECTION at full strength — Mill's side, through the 1873 reply.** Mill died in May 1873
   without answering; the reply his friends accepted as his came in August from John Morley, and
   Stephen answered it in the 1874 preface — so the exchange can be staged in its own words.
   (a) *Stephen attacks the existence of coercion, not its warrant.* Mill's essay was written to
   show, on utilitarian grounds, that compulsion of self-regarding conduct in developed societies
   is always bad; that opinion in fact coerces is Mill's own premise (the tyranny of the majority,
   Ref), not a refutation. And Mill's text already distinguishes the "natural penalties" of
   another's disapproval — "not to the oppression of his individuality, but in the exercise of
   ours" — from opinion organised to punish (Ref). (b) *The self-regarding line is a working
   classification, not a metaphysical one* — as we keep speaking of heat and motion after learning
   they are one energy; a boundary can be vague and still be followed. (c) *The aristocratic
   argument, turned:* if, as Stephen says, one man in a million can add to what is known in
   religion and morals, liberty is the only arrangement under which that man is sure to be heard;
   Stephen's method would have opinion "discourage, freeze, browbeat all that eccentricity" in which
   he appears. (d) *The test needs a judge.* Stephen's first question — is the object good? —
   presupposes someone entitled to decide it for others; Mill's infallibility argument is about
   exactly that entitlement, and the libel case is answered by the fact that the court decides a
   particular fact under rules both parties accept, not the truth of a creed.
   **Stephen's rejoinders, at the same strength:** the self/other line is like dividing acts into
   those that happen in time and those in space — every act is both; and the real difference is
   stated with complete clarity: both men think the minority wise and the majority foolish, but
   Mill denies that the wise may ever coerce the foolish for their own good and Stephen affirms
   that "under circumstances" they may — "the wise minority are the rightful masters of the foolish
   majority", and to renounce coercion for fear it be turned on oneself is to refuse to ride a
   horse because horses are stronger. Plus his best internal point: Mill's own Utilitarianism
   defines "wrong" as what a person "ought to be punished in some way or other for… if not by law,
   by the opinion of his fellow-creatures" — so Mill's ethics licenses the coercion by opinion his
   Liberty forbids.
10. **The weighing — Mill's line cannot apply itself, but Stephen does not refute free
    discussion.** Set the two claims against the strongest objection to each and reach the
    verdict. (a) Mill does state a criterion, and the lesson quotes it (ch. IV, staged
    ll. 2822–2826): natural penalties are legitimate only as "the natural, and, as it were, the
    spontaneous consequences of the faults themselves, not because they are purposely inflicted on
    him for the sake of punishment". The line runs through the motive of the one who avoids or
    warns — which no one can observe; so Stephen is right that the principle cannot apply itself.
    Objection at full strength (Morley: a vague line can still be followed; courts try motive
    every day); answer: a court applies a motive test after the fact under rules of evidence,
    while Mill's line must be applied by every neighbour with no judge. Verdict: **on balance**.
    Stephen's "best internal point" fails: Mill classes self-regarding faults as "not properly
    immoralities" (ll. 2857–2859), so *Utilitarianism* ch. V's definition of "wrong" does not reach
    them, and the two texts do not contradict each other. (b) Stephen's method needs someone
    entitled to decide which objects are good; his answer — the wise minority — is the premise in
    dispute; the libel case concerns a fact decided under agreed rules, not the truth of a creed;
    his own limits on the criminal law concede most of Mill's practice (he says he opposes Mill's
    theory more than his practice). Verdict: Stephen does not refute Mill's case for free
    discussion, **strong**. State what would overturn each: a way of applying Mill's line that
    does not depend on unobservable motive; a non-question-begging account of who the wise
    minority are.
11. **Close (Conclusion).** States the verdict in plain words with its grades: Stephen shows that
    Mill's harm principle cannot apply itself, because its line runs through a motive no one can
    observe (**on balance**); he does not refute Mill's case for free discussion, because his own
    method needs the wise minority his argument assumes (**strong**). The reader can now
    reconstruct the case that coercion by opinion is how societies form and change, apply
    Stephen's three questions to a case, and say which of Mill's claims survive it. Hand-off:
    Stephen's "Nothing" — he accepts that power is passing to universal suffrage and refuses to
    call it progress; whether popular government can last is the next critics' question (Ref
    `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government`), and the Reform Acts the test (`british-franchise`).

Headings as claims (suggested): "The principle governs opinion, not only law"; "Coercion is how
societies change"; "Discussion cannot do coercion's work"; "Suppression need not claim
infallibility"; "Object, means, price"; "Power precedes liberty"; "Equality is a big name for a
small thing"; "Love of humanity cannot be a creed"; "Mill's reply and Stephen's answer"; "Mill's line cannot
apply itself, but Stephen does not refute free discussion".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **The opening's description of Stephen.** "An Indian civil servant and English judge" compresses
  a career: in 1873 he was a barrister and journalist recently returned from three years as legal
  member of the Viceroy's council (not a member of the Indian Civil Service); he became a judge in
  1879. Use the opening verbatim (L3); the anchor gives the facts. A course.yaml fix is recommended
  in the final report, not made here.
- **Key ideas the dossier does not support.** course.yaml key_ideas name "the 'reeds shaken by the
  wind'" and "the gunpowder passage". Neither phrase occurs in either edition of LEF in the dossier
  (grep of `lef-1882.txt` and `lef-1874.txt`: no "reed" in that sense, no "powder"). The gunpowder
  sentence is quoted in the empire brief from a secondary source, apparently from Stephen's later
  writing on India; it is held for `locke-and-mill-on-empire` in any case. Do not use either here;
  do not invent a source for them.
- **Real misreading (L13), one slot:** "Stephen wanted the criminal law to punish every vice / was
  an advocate of persecution." Refute from ch. IV's conditions for criminal law and four limits,
  and from his approval of Liberal practice (ch. II pp. 99–104) — his quarrel is with the theory.
  No second strawman.
- **Pilate and the persecutors (ch. II pp. 85–99).** Stephen accepts that Pilate did his duty in
  keeping the peace and faults the Roman persecutors for half-measures. If used, quote him exactly,
  give the dilemma Mill posed (condemn Marcus Aurelius or justify Pilate) and Stephen's choice of
  horn, and leave it; it is optional and can be cut for length.
- **Women (ch. V pp. 203–219).** The argument is in the book and in the key_ideas; teach it as an
  argument with its date (1873), its premises (real inequalities of age and sex; a family needs one
  head, as a ship), and Mill's counter-position in one sentence (Subjection of Women: legal
  subordination is a survival of the law of force, unproven by any experiment of equality). The
  register grades nothing here; the lesson states, as a fact about the argument, that the ship
  analogy shows a family may need one head but not that the head must be the husband, which is the
  point Mill disputes, and that Stephen does not answer Mill's "no experiment" claim. No omission.
- **India.** Name it as Stephen's experience and as the religious-neutrality instance (link 5).
  The justification of the Raj, Mill's tutelage, and the gunpowder passage are
  `locke-and-mill-on-empire`'s; one sentence pointing there at most. Stephen's racial remarks
  (ch. IV pp. 177–178) are not needed for the argument; do not quote them.
- **Do not re-teach Mill.** Each Mill doctrine is a clause and a `<Ref>`; where Stephen quotes Mill
  the quotation may stand, since it is the object being argued with.
- **Terms to anchor (L11), at most three per section:** the Religion of Humanity (Auguste Comte's
  proposed worship of mankind, and the phrase Stephen extends to the liberal creed); Positivists
  (Comte's English followers, Harrison among them); Pall Mall Gazette (London evening paper);
  Viceroy of India / the Viceroy's council (the governor-general and his executive council in
  Calcutta); Lord Mayo (Viceroy assassinated by a convict in the Andaman Islands, February 1872);
  Act XXI of 1850 (Caste Disabilities Removal Act — change of religion no longer cost inheritance);
  suttee (the burning of widows, made a crime in 1829); John Knox (Scotland's Calvinist reformer);
  Sadowa and Sedan (1866 and 1870, Prussia's victories over Austria and France); the Maine Law
  (Ref Mill lesson); Utilitarianism (Mill's 1861 essay); John Morley (Liberal journalist, editor of
  the Fortnightly Review, Mill's disciple).
- **L8a — names.** In the body: Stephen, Mill, Morley (as the respondent in a public exchange the
  lesson stages — his article is a primary document of the dispute), Harrison (optional, one
  clause), Comte, Hobbes, Burke, Maistre, Buckle (one clause if the Manichee passage is used),
  Carlyle. References only: Muller, Magid, Strauss–Cropsey, Fawcett, Hart, Devlin, Leslie Stephen
  as biographer.
- **Numbers (L12):** almost none. Keeper facts: 1859 (On Liberty), 1869–72 (India), 1873 (the book;
  Mill's death; Morley's reply). "One man in a million" is Stephen's phrase, not a statistic.
- **Overlap with neighbours.** `british-franchise` uses "counting heads" and the suffrage passage
  as a forecast to test; this lesson reconstructs them as argument and does not test them.
  `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government` takes Maine's status-to-contract; mention Stephen's
  acceptance of it only in that lesson's direction, not here.
- **Dossier thin:** Utilitarianism and The Subjection of Women are only available as Stephen
  quotes them; Morley's article only as Stephen reports it; Mill's private reaction is not in the
  dossier and must not be asserted.
- **OCR:** `lef-1882.txt` has stray marks (">", "^") and broken words in places (e.g. l.1123,
  l.2112); check each quotation against the line and normalise only obvious OCR noise.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** name Stephen's four sanctions and say which of them Mill's
   principle, as Mill words it, covers; then state the three conditions under which Stephen holds
   compulsion bad.
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill on the Dedication** (LEF l.200–240): a judge-to-be writes to
   a colleague in the Government of India that the book was planned in India and that the European
   newspapers of 1870–71 looked "false and poor" from Calcutta. Metadata first (author, 1873,
   audience, purpose); predict; what it records, argues, omits (what happened in Europe in 1870–71 —
   the Franco-Prussian War and the Paris Commune); what the provenance lets the passage establish
   about the book's argument and what it does not.
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Stephen held that the criminal law should punish every vice." Name
   the passages that break it (ch. IV pp. 146–148, the four limits pp. 158–162) and state what he
   did hold about vice (pressure mostly by opinion; law only where definable, provable, worth the
   cost).
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** run Stephen's three-question test on a case where the object itself is
   contested — a Protestant and a Catholic legislature each considering a law favouring its own
   church. Show that questions two and three can be answered and question one cannot be answered
   by the test, and say what Stephen supplies at that point (moral certainty; "three relations and
   no more"; "one of us two must rule") and what Mill's infallibility argument says about it.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** from Mill's own definition in Utilitarianism ch. V
   ("we do not call anything wrong unless we mean to imply that a person ought to be punished in
   some way… if not by law, by the opinion of his fellow-creatures"), derive Stephen's conclusion
   that the definition licenses coercion by opinion of self-regarding wrongs; then construct the
   reply Mill could make from On Liberty ch. IV (self-regarding faults are not "wrongs" in this
   sense; natural penalties are not punishment) and say whether it holds. The solution: it holds —
   Mill calls self-regarding faults "not properly immoralities" (ch. IV, ll. 2857–2859), so there
   is no contradiction; Stephen's stronger point is that the line between natural penalty and
   punishment turns on unobservable motive.
6. **Corroboration (E3, earlier lessons):** set three definitions of liberty side by side —
   Hobbes's "silence of the law" (Ref `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`), Mill's sovereignty of the
   individual over himself (Ref `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`), Stephen's "Who is left at liberty to
   do what…?" — and for each say who decides where liberty stops. Add Stephen's charge that Buckle
   and Maistre (Ref `maistre-and-bonald`) are Manichees with their principles swapped, and say
   whether it applies to Mill.
7. **Synthesis — the 1873 exchange as an argument:** Morley says that if one man in a million can
   add to moral knowledge, liberty is the only way to be sure he is heard; Stephen says the wise
   minority are the rightful masters of the foolish majority. Write a claim–evidence–reasoning
   paragraph deciding which premise each argument needs that the other denies, using the libel case
   and the marsh.
8. **Attack the verdict (E6b):** the lesson concludes that Mill's line cannot apply itself (**on
   balance**) and that Stephen does not refute free discussion (**strong**). Take the first and
   state the strongest objection in its best form — the law applies motive tests every day
   (murder and manslaughter, malice in libel), so a line drawn through motive is no worse than
   much of the criminal law Stephen himself codified — and say what evidence or argument would be
   needed to overturn the verdict: a way of applying Mill's line, by the neighbours who must apply
   it, that does not depend on knowing why they avoid or warn. The solution says why the
   objection lowers the grade to on balance and does not overturn it (a court applies the test,
   after the fact, under rules of evidence; Mill's line has no court).
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `british-franchise`:** Stephen (1873) says universal suffrage only subdivides
   power, that leaders gather the fragments, and that nothing can stop it ("haycocks in a flood").
   Turn these into two forecasts that the British record from 1867 to 1928 could confirm or refute,
   and say what observation would refute each. The solution states the forecasts (franchise
   extension continues; party leaderships and organisations, not voters, hold the effective power)
   and says in one sentence that the module's application lesson lays them against the Reform Acts,
   with a `<Ref>` to `british-franchise`.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The whole course is this lesson's evidence. Natural law as right reason in agreement with nature, Carneades's argument from the variety of customs and Laelius's reply (`cicero-natural-law`); Thrasymachus and the ring (`thrasymachus-and-the-ring`); the cave and the philosopher compelled to rule (`kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`); Aristotle's natural slavery with its internal difficulty and the forward hook planted there ("Strauss's remark on the experience of history") (`aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`); the mixed regime (`aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, `cicero-mixed-constitution`); Aquinas's hierarchy of laws and the hook "Strauss's 'Thomistic natural law' remark" (`aquinas-law-and-kingship`); Machiavelli's effectual truth (`machiavelli-effectual-truth`); Hobbes's derivation from fear and the hook "fear of violent death replaces the telos — the frame for the postwar module" (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`, `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`); Locke's property by labour and the hook "Strauss's reading of Locke as Hobbes made respectable" (`locke-property-and-consent`); Rousseau and the hook "the first crisis of modernity" (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`); Burke's prescription and the hook "Strauss's charge that Burke's historicism opens the road to Hegel" (`burke-reflections`); Hegel's "what is rational is actual" and the hook "Strauss's 'historicism'" (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`). From the module: Oakeshott's tradition as the only standard and the umpire who commands for no truth (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`, Exercise 8), and the epistemic argument's unanswered question of what, if anything, it can ground (`what-conservatism-is`).
- **Contributes:** One move: the claim that the two modern grounds for rejecting a natural standard of right — that all thought is historical, and that science knows facts but not values — each refute themselves, and that the West arrived at them not by discovering anything but by a sequence of lowered aims from Hobbes through Locke and Rousseau to Burke, whose defence of inherited practice is the door through which "History" replaced nature. The lesson leaves the reader with the question reopened, not with a doctrine.
- **Hands off:** Three problems. (1) Whether the reader's own Burke and Oakeshott are, on this reading, the last step to nihilism — the module's internal quarrel, taken up by Scruton's "dogmatics not philosophy" (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`). (2) Another postwar émigré agrees that positivism destroyed political science and dates the rot not to Machiavelli but to a Christian heresy — Voegelin (`voegelin-gnosticism`). (3) A declaration of rights that lists them without a ground is exactly the case this lesson predicts; whether the list can stand without the ground is tested in `udhr-read-against-the-right`. Natural right returns as `classic-natural-right` in `the-argument-assembled`.
- **Spine step:** "After 1945 liberalism is the orthodoxy, and the right answers it in four registers: Oakeshott's disposition, Strauss's natural right, Voegelin's diagnosis of Gnosticism, Scruton's authority before freedom"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: if every idea of justice is of its time, so is that idea; the cannibal; the trace from Hobbes to Burke.

1. **Why anyone needs a natural standard: the cannibal and the tolerant man.** The problem as a newcomer meets it: a modern society that says all right is positive right still calls some laws unjust — by what standard? Attempt: the standard is our society's ideal, embodied in its institutions. Why it fails: every society has an ideal, "cannibal societies no less than civilized ones"; if acceptance by a society justifies a principle, cannibalism is as sound as civilisation, and since our ideal is changing, "nothing except dull and stale habit could prevent us from placidly accepting a change in the direction of cannibalism". The fact that we can ask whether our ideal is good shows "something in man that is not altogether in slavery to his society". The cost of refusing: a social science that is "instrumental and nothing but instrumental… the handmaid of any powers or any interests that be"; wisdom in means, "utter ignorance in the most important respect" — "retail sanity and wholesale madness"; "the contemporary rejection of natural right leads to nihilism — nay, it is identical with nihilism". Then the tolerance paradox, the course's own version of the Oakeshott hand-off: liberal relativism grew from the natural-right tradition's teaching of tolerance; but once tolerance is one value among many and all values are equal, what is admired is resoluteness, and the relativist becomes "a seminary of intolerance" (the reader has Rousseau's "intolerance of tolerance", `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`, and Mill's infallibility argument, `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`, to set beside it). Two cautions the lesson must carry: "A wish is not a fact" — the need for natural right does not prove it exists; and "beware of pursuing a Socratic goal with the means, and the temper, of Thrasymachus" (`thrasymachus-and-the-ring`). Name the dilemma set aside: classic natural right rode on a purposive cosmos that modern physics seems to have refuted on Aristotle's own test, the heavens; the book does not solve this; it treats what social science can clarify — the rejection "in the name of History" and "in the name of Facts and Values". Two links for each.
2. **Historicism: the argument from history and the sentence it exempts.** Attempt (the naive objection): peoples disagree about justice, therefore there is no natural justice — Carneades's variety of customs (`cicero-natural-law`). Why it fails: universal consent was never a condition of natural right ("savages cannot be expected to know it"); that a view is denied somewhere does not show the denial justified; the variety is the incentive for the quest, not its refutation. Historicism proper is different and stronger: all thought belongs to its time; there is no exit from the cave (`kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`) because the "cave" is history itself. Where it came from, in the reader's own material: the historical school arose against the French Revolution — rights of Englishmen against rights of man (`burke-reflections`) — preferring the local and temporal to universal principles that were "potentially revolutionary"; then the preference was generalised: positivism applied to history; and the end is nihilism — "The attempt to make man absolutely at home in this world ended in man's becoming absolutely homeless", since history yields no norm (no principle sanctions every tradition or every "wave of the future"). Why the "experience of history" cannot support it: that a view was abandoned does not show it deserved to be; the claim rests on an unstated philosophic critique of reason and on two beliefs — in progress and in the equal right of all epochs — "dogmatism identifies the goal of thinking with the point where we tire of thinking". The slavery case, promised in the Aristotle lesson: Aristotle "did conceive the injustice of slavery" (he reports and answers the objection, `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`), so the fundamental questions were visible to him; what he could not conceive was a world state, because he held technology must be morally controlled — and unchecked technology makes "universal and perpetual tyranny a serious possibility". Then the sharp edge: historicism asserts, as true for all times, that nothing is true for all times; "historicism thrives on the fact that it inconsistently exempts itself from its own verdict". Hegel's escape — an "absolute moment" when history becomes transparent (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`, "what is rational is actual") — only names the exemption. Radical historicism (Nietzsche, by `<Ref>` only — `nietzsche/perspectivism`, `nietzsche/will-to-truth`) bites the bullet: every understanding presupposes a horizon that cannot be rationally validated; fate imposes it; the historicist insight belongs to a privileged moment and the self-exemption is charged "to reality". Its price is to declare philosophy itself impossible, since philosophy presupposes that the whole is intelligible and that "to be" means "to be always". Where the lesson concedes: Strauss does not refute radical historicism; he shows that "simple experiences of right and wrong" persist, that the fundamental problems keep their identity, and that grasping them as problems is itself a liberation from the horizon — Socratic philosophy as "knowledge that one does not know". The issue is left open; the thesis is only that the "discovery" of history may have been an "invention", made by political philosophy, which links 5–6 will show.
3. **The fact–value distinction: what Weber proved and what he assumed.** Attempt: science establishes facts; values are chosen; therefore a social science must be value-free (Weber — anchor: the German sociologist, d. 1920, whose rule that scholarship must not judge became the orthodoxy of the American social science Strauss addresses; his essays are not in the dossier, his position is reconstructed from Strauss's statement of it and the reader is told so). Why the inference fails — the lesson's cleanest step: Is and Ought may be heterogeneous and the Ought still be knowable; the "therefore" needs a hidden premise, that no knowledge of the Ought is possible; with knowledge of ends, social science would be architectonic. So value-freedom rests not on logic but on a prior nihilism — "every preference, however evil, base or insane, is as legitimate before reason as any other" (and the method of refuting a view by its company is forbidden: "reductio ad Hitlerum" is coined here and ruled out). Show the descent of Weber's own imperative in four steps: "Thou shalt have ideals" → "Follow God or the Devil as you will, but with all your heart" → "Thou shalt live passionately" → "Thou shalt have preferences". One cannot call this nihilism "noble" without already knowing the noble from the base. Then the practice: value judgments are inescapable in social science — a sociology of religion must tell genuine from spurious; a report on a concentration camp that avoided every evaluative word would be "a bitter satire"; putting "cruelty" in quotation marks is "a childish trick"; Weber's own prose is a vocabulary of praise and blame. Where the lesson gives Weber his strength: the decisive value conflicts (the prophet, the Buddha, the noble unbeliever) may indeed be undecidable by reason; and Strauss concedes that the real root is deeper than method — the quarrel between human guidance and divine guidance, philosophy and revelation, neither of which has refuted the other, so that "the mere fact that philosophy and revelation cannot refute each other would constitute the refutation of philosophy by revelation". He steps back from this "awful depth" to the task social science can do: start from the prescientific world in which the idea of nature was first found.
4. **Classic natural right: the discovery of nature and the doctrine of the best regime.** The reader has the texts; this link reframes, not re-teaches. Before philosophy there is no "nature": the Hebrew Bible has no word for it; what there is is the ancestral — the way of the fathers, the divine code; "dogs wag tails, women menstruate, Jews avoid pork, Moslems avoid wine", all equally "ways". Nature is discovered when codes conflict and someone asks which is right: it is a term of distinction (nature vs convention), reached by two tests — what is seen vs what is heard, what grows vs what is made — and it is "the ancestor of all ancestors": philosophy appeals from the ancestral to the older. But discovering nature does not settle whether right is natural: conventionalism (Epicurus, the sophists, Carneades, Thrasymachus — `cicero-natural-law`, `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`) accepts the distinction and puts right on the convention side; its nerve is three steps — right belongs to the city, the city is conventional, so right is conventional. Socrates's answer is not a code but a method and a regime: ask "what is?" of each thing; the good for a being follows from what it is; man is by nature social and speaking, so justice is natural because sociality is; "not everything is permitted" is the natural conscience. The decisive concept is the regime (politeia): a way of life determined by who rules, "the American Constitution is not the American way of life"; classic natural right in its original form IS the doctrine of the best regime — the rule of the wise, which, because the wise are few and consent is needed, is diluted into the rule of gentlemen under law, the mixed regime (`aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, `cicero-mixed-constitution`). Hence the classics are non-egalitarian ("equal rights for unequals are most unjust"), the city is closed, and natural right is "dynamite" for any actual society — the politically good is the quotient of the simply good and the ancestral. Aristotle's one page: all natural right is changeable; Strauss's reading against both the Averroist (it is pedagogic convention) and the Thomist (immutable principles, mutable conclusions): natural right lives in concrete decisions; in extreme situations the exception is as just as the rule; "a universally valid hierarchy of ends but no universally valid rules of action" (the reader has Machiavelli and Schmitt to feel the danger here — `machiavelli-effectual-truth`, `schmitt-political-theology`). Thomistic natural law — the version the reader met in `aquinas-law-and-kingship` — is "practically inseparable from theology", which is why the moderns reacted against it as they did.
5. **The modern break: Hobbes puts a right where a duty was.** Attempt (the usual story): modern natural right begins with Locke, who completes the tradition of Hooker. Strauss: it begins with Hobbes, "that imprudent, impish, and iconoclastic extremist, that first plebeian philosopher", and Locke hides his debt. What Hobbes keeps: that political philosophy is possible and necessary; what he rejects: that man is by nature political. He takes Epicurus's premises (apolitical man, the good is pleasure) to an idealistic political purpose — "political hedonism" — and arrives with "political atheism". The method: "we understand only what we make"; the constructed commonwealth is the island of certainty in a sea of blind causation; man is sovereign because a stranger in the universe. The decisive substitution, promised in `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`: the fear of violent death takes the place of the telos; the good is no longer the perfection of nature but the avoidance of the worst; natural law is deduced from the strongest passion, not from human excellence. Consequence: the fundamental moral fact is a right (self-preservation), duties are conditional on it, the state exists to safeguard rights — "Hobbes is the founder of liberalism"; the state of nature becomes the topic for the first time; since each judges his own means, the "natural right of folly" and consent over wisdom; morality shrinks to peaceableness, vice to pride; institutions replace character ("a nation of devils" — Kant's later formula); power becomes a theme and both power and rights become measurable and morally neutral. Where the derivation fails on its own terms: fear of violent death is only "commonly" the strongest passion — the condemned murderer, the soldier; so the scheme needs the fear of invisible powers dissolved, that is, an enlightened atheistic society (Hobbes "the first of all that fled"). Here put the module's internal objection, from Oakeshott's Hobbes essay: Strauss's "prudential" reading confuses cause with reason — self-preservation is a right, not a duty, and Hobbesian obligation comes from civil law, not fear. The reader has both and judges. Locke, briefly, as Hobbes made respectable: his law of nature needs a lawgiver and otherworldly sanctions reason cannot prove; his "state of nature" is a state of war and penury, so the law is not promulgated and not law; innate right, no innate duty; the same premises yield limited government, majority rule and revolution because they protect self-preservation better. And property: labour, spoilage, then money, which dissolves every limit — "unlimited appropriation… is true charity", the discoverer of quinine did more good than the builders of hospitals; so the end of civil society is dynamic property — "the classic doctrine of the spirit of capitalism", a revolution concealed against both Bible and philosophy; man, not nature, becomes the source of value; "life is the joyless quest for joy". (`locke-property-and-consent`; "a day laborer in England is better off than a king of the Americans" is flagged forward to `locke-and-mill-on-empire`, not developed.)
6. **The crisis: Rousseau takes away nature, Burke hands the standard to History.** Rousseau accepts Hobbes's state of nature and turns it against him: Hobbes "read social man into nature" — pride needs society, so natural man is not proud, not vicious, "good"; but also prerational and premoral, since reason needs language and language society; "man has no nature in the precise sense"; humanity is an accident and reason "the seeing master of its fate". Therefore the crisis (`rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`): a subhuman state of nature yields no norm, and history cannot yield one either unless one already has trans-historical knowledge of the right order. The general will is "a 'realistic' substitute for natural law": a desire is shown just by surviving generalisation; freedom, not reason, becomes man's specific difference — the beginning of the "philosophy of freedom" and of German idealism; and the legislator and civil religion are society's necessary "obfuscation": "the problem posed by political philosophy must be forgotten if the solution to which political philosophy leads is to work". Nation and custom more natural than contract is Rousseau's step toward historicism. Then Burke, where the lesson's seam is sharpest: the reader expects the enemy of the Revolution to be the friend of natural right, and so he meant to be — civil society is the true state of nature, the contract a "partnership in every virtue", restraints among men's rights, legitimacy from prescription (`burke-reflections`). But he states the superiority of practice to theory so emphatically — particular vs universal, attached vs detached, irreversible vs reversible, "in proportion as they are metaphysically true, they are morally and politically false" — that he reverses Aristotle: no conviction that theory is higher; and his best regime is natural in its MANNER of coming to be (grown by accident, directed to many ends) where the classics' was natural in its END (made by design for the highest end). Two ideas then combine into "the discovery of History": the economic principle that the common good arises from activities not aimed at it, and Rousseau's accident; Providence made intelligible is "secularization as temporalization", and a Providence read in events nearly licenses yielding to "a mighty current" — the opposite of Cato's lost cause. So Burke prepares Hegel: the standard is inside the process; political theory becomes understanding of the actual; and link 2's historicism has its pedigree. Objections, at full strength: (a) the reader's own Burke and Oakeshott — the epistemic argument of `what-conservatism-is` was a claim about knowledge, not a denial of a standard; Strauss's charge is that the distinction cannot hold once theory is depreciated; the reader judges whether "metaphysically true, morally and politically false" is a historicist sentence or a prudential one. (b) The historicist reply (Lilla, stated as the course's own): Strauss's own method is historical through and through — he reaches natural right by a genealogy of its loss; his "single tradition from Socrates to Aquinas" ignores Christianity's break; and his teacher's problem (Heidegger's) is answered by moving the rupture from Socrates to Machiavelli, which is a choice of where to stand, not a proof. (c) The esotericism charge: if the classics wrote one thing for the many and another for the few, how can a reader know Strauss's Hobbes is Hobbes rather than Strauss? Strauss's own rules for when caution is legitimate (ch. V.B pp. 206–209) are the reply, and the reader tests them on Locke's missing scriptural politics. Straussian politics after 1968 — References only.
7. **What the book claims and what it leaves open.** Not a proof of natural right ("a wish is not a fact") but three results: the two modern grounds of rejection refute themselves; the loss of natural right was a sequence of lowered aims, each thinker answering the last — fear for the telos, right for duty, freedom for nature, process for standard; and therefore the question is open again, which is the Socratic position. What stays unsolved: the teleology dilemma of the Introduction, and the philosophy–revelation stalemate. Hand off in one sentence: another émigré of the same decade and the same Chicago lecture series agrees that positivism ruined political science and that Weber "saw the promised land" — and locates the rot not in Machiavelli but in a twelfth-century monk.

Headings as claims: "A society that believes nothing is right by nature cannot answer the cannibal"; "Historicism exempts itself from its own verdict"; "Facts and values: the hidden premise in Weber's inference"; "Nature was discovered, and natural right is the best regime"; "Hobbes puts a right where a duty was"; "Rousseau takes nature away; Burke hands the standard to History"; "The question reopened, not answered".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

- **"Do we still hold these truths?"** (Intro pp. 1–2): the Declaration of Independence quoted ("all men are created equal… endowed by their Creator with certain unalienable Rights") and the observation that a nation defeated on the battlefield can impose "the yoke of its own thought" on its conqueror — German historicism in America. One paragraph; the reader has the Declaration from Oakeshott's "new society".
- **The cannibal argument, reconstructed step by step** (Intro p. 3, ll. 257–274): (1) if all right is positive, the only standard is our society's ideal; (2) every society has an ideal; (3) so cannibalism is as sound as civilisation; (4) and since our ideal changes, only habit stops us drifting toward it; (5) but we can ask whether our ideal is good; (6) so something in us is not in slavery to society; (7) so we are able, "and hence obliged", to seek a standard. ## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** "historicism" — defined ONCE at link 2, in the lesson's own words (the doctrine that all thought, about justice included, belongs to its historical situation), and distinguished in the same paragraph from the "variety of customs" argument the reader already knows; "positivism" (the nineteenth-century programme to make the study of man a natural science — the reader will meet Voegelin's fuller version next lesson; one clause here); "nihilism" (Strauss's sense: every preference equally legitimate before reason — not the Nietzsche course's sense, which the reader may have; say so if the course is a prerequisite they took); "Walgreen Lectures" (a Chicago lecture series; both Strauss 1949 and Voegelin 1951 gave them — the anchor that ties the two lessons); "Weber" (above); "Thomist/Averroist" (the two medieval readings of Aristotle: Aquinas's, which ties natural right to God's eternal law — `aquinas-law-and-kingship`; Averroes's, which treats universal rules as pedagogic — a one-clause anchor, since Averroes returns in Voegelin V.3); "telos" (end or purpose — the reader has it from the Aristotle lessons; recall); "esoteric writing" (writing one thing for most readers and another for the few, under persecution or for prudence — anchored at link 6, the first time the course uses it as a method rather than Machiavelli's trick).
- **L8a:** Lilla, Wokler, Hamilton, Mansfield, Berns, Goldwin, Bloom (both as Strauss–Cropsey author and as Closing of the American Mind), Jaffa, Kelsen (n.2), Troeltsch, Acton — References only. Conversely the lesson must say, where it uses the forward hooks planted earlier (natural slavery, Aquinas, Hobbes's fear, Locke "made respectable", Rousseau "first crisis", Burke "road to Hegel"), that this is where they were going — one clause each, so the reader feels the course's seams close.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (the one warm-up):** Place on the four-lowered-aims diagram, with dates and the course lesson that read each, the move each thinker makes — Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Burke — and state in one clause what each replaced (telos → fear; duty → right; nature → freedom; standard above → standard within).
2. **Boundary probe:** A society of complete and unanimous agreement about justice — no Carneades, no dissent, one unchanging tradition. The "variety of customs" argument has nothing to work on; does historicism? Solution: historicism does not need variety (it needs only that the agreement is historical), so the naive and the serious objection come apart exactly here; and natural right does not need agreement either ("universal consent is no condition") — so unanimity settles nothing for either side, which is why Strauss says the attack must be philosophic, not historical.
3. **Claim to refute:** "Strauss proves that natural right exists." Name the failing step in his own words — "a wish is not a fact"; the teleology dilemma left unsolved; the philosophy–revelation stalemate conceded; the Socratic "knowledge that one does not know" — and state what the book does establish (that the two grounds of rejection are self-refuting and that the question is open).
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** Strauss runs the self-exemption test on historicism and on Weber. Run it on Oakeshott's "pursuit of intimations" (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`): is "the only standard is the tradition" itself a standard the tradition supplies, or a trans-historical claim? Solution: it is the latter, so Oakeshott either exempts himself (historicism's move) or holds a standard above the tradition (the move he refuses); say which Oakeshott's Court-of-Appeal reply amounts to.
5. **Sourcing drill:** The cannibal passage (1949 lectures, published 1953): author (a German-Jewish émigré at Chicago), audience (American social scientists trained in value-freedom), purpose; what it records (the state of American social science), argues (the self-refutation), omits (that Aristotle's cosmos is gone — which Strauss himself supplies four pages later); what it can be USED to establish — that the postwar attack on relativism came from within the universities and from the right of a liberal consensus, not from a church — and what it cannot (that natural right exists).
6. **Synthesis (claim–evidence–reasoning):** Is "in proportion as they are metaphysically true, they are morally and politically false" a historicist sentence? Argue both ways with Burke's own text (`burke-reflections`: real vs metaphysical rights; rights "refracted"; "the science of constructing a commonwealth is not to be taught a priori") and the previous two lessons' reading (an epistemic claim about applying principles, not a denial that there are principles); then judge, and say what Strauss would need to show to win.
7. **Counter-argument stem:** "Someone claims Strauss's single classical tradition 'from Socrates to Aquinas' ignores the break Christianity made — a God who commands replaces a nature that is seen." Name two facts in Strauss's own text that the claim must account for (he says Thomistic natural law is "practically inseparable from theology" and nearer the moderns' target than Aristotle; he puts the Bible outside philosophy in ch. III) and state what would have to be true for the charge to survive (that Strauss's classic natural right is Thomas's, which it is not).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `voegelin-gnosticism`:** Strauss writes that making Providence intelligible is "secularization as temporalization" — the Christian end of history pulled into history (ch. VI.B pp. 316–318). Take Hegel's "world history is the court of judgment" (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`) and the Soviet claim to represent the truth of history (`constantine-and-representation`): what would a diagnosis look like that started not from Machiavelli's lowered goal but from this Christian symbol misused? The solution names the next lesson's term — the immanentization of the eschaton — and the monk who first did it.

# Research brief: taine-and-le-bon-revolution-and-crowd (Taine and Le Bon: the Revolution, the Jacobin and the crowd)

status: planned
module: crowd-race-nation
type: core
goal: Analyze Taine's explanation of the Revolution by the 'classical spirit' and the Jacobin, and Le Bon's theory of the crowd built on it, and evaluate both as explanations of mass politics

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `burke-reflections`: the forecast that a people who design a society
  from principles get Terror and then a general; "metaphysical rights" against real ones; men
  reduced to "loose counters"; the "sophisters, economists and calculators" (concepts
  `entailed-inheritance`, `prejudice`). From `maistre-and-bonald`: "man I have never met"
  (`no-such-thing-as-man`) — the claim Taine turns into a genealogy of a habit of mind. From
  `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: the Social Contract's total alienation and the
  general will — what Taine calls the Jacobin's catechism. From
  `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`: the omnipotent majority and
  democratic credulity (`tyranny-of-the-majority`; Le Bon quotes Tocqueville on it). From
  `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government`: popular government as rare, unstable and hostile
  to innovation (`fragility-of-popular-government`); Lecky's case for a property
  qualification — which Le Bon's own theory undercuts. From `assignats` (module 4
  application): the paper money and the maximum, which Taine's Revolution volumes chart.
- **Contributes:** The lesson's one move: the question "what moves a people in revolution?"
  is pushed below institutions twice. Taine answers historically — a privileged class that
  had stopped governing, a habit of abstract deduction (the `esprit-classique`) that the
  whole educated class shared, and, when authority dissolved in 1789, the conquest of the
  state by an organized minority of a recognizable psychological type, the Jacobin. Le Bon,
  reading the same scenes, claims a general law: assembled men under certain conditions form
  a collective mind that is credulous, suggestible and moved by images, affirmation and
  prestige (`crowd-psychology`), and modern politics is the "era of crowds". The reader
  leaves able to state both explanations, to say what each explains that the other does not,
  and to state what the evidence on who was actually in the crowds does to both.
- **Hands off:** (i) Le Bon rests the crowd on a deeper layer, "the spirit of the race", and
  never says what it is — `gobineau-inequality-of-races` takes up the theory that made race
  the cause of history. (ii) The organized minority that rules in the name of the many —
  `mosca-ruling-class` and `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites` generalise Taine's
  Jacobin machine and Le Bon's "the reign of crowds is the reign of committees". (iii) The
  crowd as the subject of a new politics — `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses` (the mass-man, with
  Le Bon as the predecessor Ortega does not name) and `sorel-reflections-on-violence` (the
  myth as an image that moves masses). (iv) `dreyfus-affair-as-test` reads the street and
  press record of 1898 against Le Bon's crowd and Taine's Jacobin.
- **Spine step:** "The late nineteenth century looks beneath institutions for what moves
  peoples: the crowd (Taine, Le Bon), the race (Gobineau, Chamberlain, Grant), the nation as
  will or as inheritance (Renan, Barrès, Maurras) — and the Dreyfus Affair puts them to the
  test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): after the Paris Commune of 1871 burned the Tuileries, a
French critic who had written on English literature turned to how France had got there; why
a country with the most cultivated elite in Europe had produced, eighty years before, a
revolution of massacres; twenty years later a doctor who had studied hypnosis claimed the
general law behind it — a man in a crowd is no longer the man he was alone.

Anchors (L11): the Commune (March–May 1871, after France's defeat by Prussia: Paris governed
itself for ten weeks and was retaken by the army in a week of street fighting; the
Tuileries palace was burned). Hippolyte Taine (1828–1893), critic and historian, best known
before 1871 for a History of English Literature; The Origins of Contemporary France,
1875–93, unfinished at his death. Gustave Le Bon (1841–1931), physician turned popular
writer on psychology, anthropology and politics; The Crowd, 1895. Place the reader on a
short timeline: 1789 (Bastille), 1792–94 (Republic, Terror), 1799 (Napoleon), 1871
(Commune), 1875 (Taine's first volume), 1889 (Boulanger nearly seizes power), 1895 (The
Crowd).

1. **Was it misery?** Obvious attempt: the people rose because they were poor and taxed.
   Taine grants the misery and documents it — of a peasant's 100 francs, about 53 went in
   direct taxes, about 14 to the lord and about 14 to the Church's tithe (Bk 5, Note 5);
   peasants whom foreign travellers took for twice their age. Why it does not suffice:
   misery explains riots, which France had known for centuries; it does not explain why
   the riots of 1789 ended in a new doctrine, a written constitution and a Terror run from
   Paris. The answer needs the classes above the peasant.
2. **Why did the privileged not defend themselves?** Obvious attempt: they were wicked or
   decadent. Taine's answer is structural and is the lesson's first "because": privilege
   began as payment for service — the clergy's teaching and charity, the noble's protection,
   the king's justice — and outlived the service once the nobles left their estates for
   Versailles and royal officials (intendants) governed in their place. "When sovereignty
   becomes transformed into a sinecure it becomes burdensome without being useful, and on
   becoming burdensome without being useful it is overthrown." "The evil is in their
   situation and not in their character." The English squire, who turned his old military
   role into the unpaid magistracy of his county, kept his title to rule; the French noble
   did not. And a class trained for the salon had learned to fence, not to box. Seam (L10b):
   one expects the defenders of an old order to blame its enemies; Taine's first culprit is
   the old order's own top.
3. **Why did the reformers not reform piece by piece, as England did?** Obvious attempt:
   they were bad men. Taine's answer: a habit of mind shared by the whole educated class,
   which he calls the classical spirit — define here (`esprit-classique`): formed over two
   centuries in French letters, it prized the general over the particular, the oratorical
   over the observed, and it worked by the "mathematical method": take a few simple notions,
   set experience aside, deduce. Joined to the new natural science it made a "venomous
   compound" of two ingredients harmless alone (the poison metaphor). Applied to politics it
   produced "Man in general": strip every man of what distinguishes him, call the residue
   the social unit, multiply by twenty-six million, and deduce equal rights and popular
   sovereignty with the certainty of geometry — men "born at twenty-one years of age,
   without relations, without a past, without traditions, without a country". Seam: Burke
   (`<Ref>`) objected to metaphysical rights and Maistre (`<Ref>`) said he had never met
   "man"; Taine's addition is a genealogy — why a whole class came to reason that way, from
   the salon, the pruned vocabulary, the tragedy of generic characters.
4. **What happened when that doctrine met a state that stopped working?** 1789 is, in
   Taine's account, not a revolution but a dissolution: dearth, rumour and the expectation
   of a new order; the army unreliable; authority passing from the king to the Assembly to
   the municipalities to whoever held the street. The Bastille "surrendered before it was
   attacked". Set piece: the death of its governor, Launay, told from the interrogatory of
   the man who cut off his head (below). "The Terror dates from the 14th of July." In a
   band bent on violence each man "descends lower than himself". Answer: the Terror is not
   a later betrayal of 1789; the dissolution of authority in 1789 made it possible.
5. **Who rules when no one rules?** Obvious attempt: the people. Taine's answer: an
   organized minority of a recognizable mental type — the Jacobin. Set piece: the statesman
   and the Jacobin face the same principle (popular sovereignty); the statesman imagines it
   applied in a dozen towns he knows, applies it in part, watches, amends; the Jacobin takes
   it as "an axiom of political geometry" that carries its proof with it. Who became
   Jacobins: the half-educated lower middle class — the attorney, surgeon, journalist,
   curé — like "the shepherd that has just found, in one corner of his hut, a lot of old
   parchments which entitle him to the throne". How they won: clubs linked across France,
   petitions manufactured in Paris and returned from the provinces, moderates who stayed
   home; "four times" the majority would not break with custom and lost; in the end some
   "three hundred thousand Jacobins" ruled a nation of twenty-six million. Seam: from
   Taine's own premise — the people's misery — one would expect the people to rule; his
   record shows a minority ruling in its name. (Forward, one clause: `mosca-ruling-class`.)
6. **Is there a general law under Taine's scenes?** Le Bon's problem. He praises Taine's
   facts and says Taine read them "as a naturalist" and so missed their cause. Definition
   (`crowd-psychology`): under certain conditions — not mere numbers (a thousand people in a
   square are not a crowd, six men can be) — assembled men's "conscious personality
   vanishes. A collective mind is formed", which is not the average of its members but a
   new compound (his chemistry analogy). Three causes: anonymity and the sense of invincible
   power; contagion; suggestibility, which Le Bon likens to hypnosis. Consequence: the
   individual "descends several rungs in the ladder of civilisation" — "a grain of sand
   amid other grains of sand, which the wind stirs up at will". Then the same scene:
   Launay's killer, in Le Bon's retelling, is not a monster but a man obeying "a
   suggestion … all the stronger because of its collective origin", convinced he deserves a
   medal. Answer: the act is the crowd's, which is why the crowd's crimes are "legally but
   not psychologically" crimes.
7. **What moves a crowd, and who moves it?** Images, not arguments: a crowd thinks in
   pictures, cannot tell the imagined from the seen (the Belle Poule's sailors who saw a
   raft of shipwrecked men in floating branches), holds its convictions in religious form
   (the Jacobins as devout as inquisitors). Leaders are men of action "hypnotised" by an
   idea; their means are affirmation "kept free of all reasoning and all proof",
   repetition, contagion; their power is prestige, "the mainspring of all authority", which
   failure or discussion destroys. Hence "The divine right of the masses is about to
   replace the divine right of kings" — "the era of crowds".
8. **Is every assembly a mob?** The turn the reader will not expect (state it as a seam):
   Le Bon classifies juries, electorates and parliaments as crowds too — and draws
   conclusions against the restrictions his contemporaries proposed. On general questions
   "a vote, recorded by forty academicians is no better than that of forty water-carriers",
   so a franchise restricted to the educated would change nothing (against Lecky's property
   qualification, `<Ref>` `maine-and-lecky-on-popular-government`); keep the jury, since
   "Crowds are open to conviction; castes never are"; parliaments are "the best form of
   government mankind has discovered as yet", whose two dangers are spending and the
   growth of an irresponsible administrative caste. Under the crowd lies a slower layer:
   "the spirit of the race", which makes "the inferior characteristics of crowds … less
   accentuated" — the Latin crowd calls on the state, the English on private initiative.
   Name it as the theory's unexplained foundation (hand-off).
9. **The OBJECTIONS at full strength, and what each theory survives.**
   (a) *The historians against Taine.* The charge (stated as the course's own; names in
   References): Taine's transcriptions contain errors, and his archive was searched for
   horrors — he leaves out war and blockade as causes of the famine of 1793–95. The reply:
   the errors are local; the thesis that a disciplined network of clubs, not the nation,
   carried the Revolution has been restated by historians who never shared Taine's horror
   of it. Concede both: Taine's figures are his selection and must be read as a thesis's
   evidence (his own method notes say as much).
   (b) *Who was in the crowd.* The strongest empirical objection, and it falls on both men.
   When later historians read police records and the official list of those honoured for
   taking the Bastille, they found settled artisans, journeymen and shopkeepers with trades
   and addresses, not Taine's "dregs" or Le Bon's barbarians; and revolutionary crowds chose
   their targets — the toll-gates, the grain stores, particular officials — which a mind
   reduced to suggestion would not. Reply available to Le Bon: his claim is about how
   people behave once assembled, not about who they are; the reply concedes that the
   crowd's actions have reasons that the theory calls irrational.
   (c) *The crowd hides interests.* From the left (`<Ref>` `marx-manifesto`): bread prices,
   feudal dues and tax are interests, not suggestions; calling the bearers of an interest
   "a crowd" moves the question from what they wanted to how they felt. Le Bon's own book
   says it was written partly against socialism — state it as his purpose, from his text.
   (d) *The evidence of the law.* Le Bon's support is anecdote and analogy (hypnosis,
   chemistry, microbes); later research on crowds found that people in them act on a shared
   identity and its norms more often than they lose themselves. What survives: contagion,
   slogans, leaders' affirmation and the minority machine are recognised by every later
   student of mass politics — which is why the book became a manual for its readers
   (reception: the 1921 psychoanalytic theory of the group; Mussolini's stated admiration).
   The lesson ends on what each explains: Taine, why a cultivated elite could not stop a
   revolution and how a minority took it over; Le Bon, how masses are moved — and neither,
   why these crowds wanted what they wanted.

Headings as claims, for example: "Privilege outlived the service that paid for it"; "The
classical spirit: man in general, deduced"; "1789 as a dissolution"; "The Jacobin is a
method before he is a party"; "A crowd is a new mind, not a sum"; "Affirmation, repetition,
prestige"; "Juries, voters and parliaments are crowds too"; "Who was in the crowd".

   giving the crowd what it wanted.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), at most three per section:** Commune (1871); Ancien Régime;
  intendant (the king's salaried administrator of a province); tithe; seigneur and feudal
  dues; salon; philosophes; Constituent Assembly (1789–91); Jacobin club (a Paris club with
  affiliated societies across France, which became the organ of the Terror); sans-culottes;
  the Convention (1792–95); the Terror (1793–94); Thermidor (the fall of Robespierre, July
  1794); Brumaire (Napoleon's coup, November 1799); the September massacres (1792: some
  1,100–1,400 prisoners killed in Paris — verify the figure before use); Boulanger (a
  general whose popular movement nearly took power in 1889 — Le Bon's living example of
  prestige); hypnosis and "suggestion" (the 1880s French medical debate between the
  Salpêtrière and Nancy schools — one clause).
- **Chronology inside the module:** Gobineau's Essay (1853) is older than both books here;
  the module orders by problem (crowd, race, nation), not by date. Say "two decades before
  Taine began" in the next lesson, not here; here, only Le Bon's word "race" points ahead.
- **Taine's own triad "race, milieu, moment"** (Introduction to the History of English
  Literature, 1863) — not in the dossier; one clause at most, as the reason his later work
  reaches for collective character.
- **Do not re-teach** Burke's forecast, Maistre's "man", Rousseau's general will,
  Tocqueville's majority, Lecky's qualification — a clause plus `<Ref>` each. The new matter
  is the explanation of 1789 by a habit of mind plus a dissolved authority, and Le Bon's
  law.
- **Real misreading (L13):** "Le Bon was simply an enemy of democracy who wanted the masses
  kept from the vote." Refute from his text (universal suffrage defended on practical
  grounds; forty academicians; jury; parliament "best form"). One slot; no strawman
  elsewhere.
- **Gutenberg's (SR.) notes** in the Taine shards compare the text to Lenin, Stalin,
  Hitler, Mao and the UN. They are a modern editor's, not Taine's; never quote them or let
  them shape the lesson.
- **Period language:** both authors use racial and class similes of their time (Taine's
  "unchained Negroes" in Rev. I `-s2` l.52; Le Bon's crowds likened to women, children and
  "savages"). Do not quote them for colour. Where a reader must see the register (Le Bon's
  race substratum is load-bearing), quote the law in capitals (l.4596) and say what it
  claims.
- **Contested, do not settle:** whether Taine's history is a scholarly work or a polemic is
  itself the historians' dispute — give the charge and the reply. Whether Le Bon's crowd
  exists as he describes it is an empirical question with evidence on both sides — give
  both.
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — Taine's 53-in-100 peasant tax; the "three hundred thousand
  Jacobins" against twenty-six million French (Taine's estimate, framed as his); Le Bon's
  forty-and-forty (rhetorical, not data). Everything else in order-of-magnitude words.
- **Dossier thin:** the Aulard/Cochin exchange, the social history of the crowds, Le Bon's
  later readers, and Taine's 1875 Preface are not in the dossier (see Sources). Le Bon's
  Lois psychologiques de l'évolution des peuples (1894), where the "soul of the race" is
  argued, is not in the dossier; The Crowd's own statements suffice.
- **Register rule:** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no
  condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and
  evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the only mechanical one):** name the two ingredients of Taine's "poison" and
   say why each was harmless alone; then place in order 1789, 1794, 1799, 1871, 1875, 1895
   with one event each, and name which earlier thinker forecast "Terror and then a general"
   (`<Ref>` `burke-reflections`).
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** Desnot's interrogatory as Taine uses it. Metadata
   first (who spoke, to whom, when, under what threat); predict what such a source will
   claim; then: what it records (the sequence of acts, the stated motive), what Taine
   argues from it, what it omits (who else was present, their trades); what it can be used
   to establish and what it cannot (that the crowd was made of "dregs").
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Le Bon's theory of crowds implies that the vote should be
   restricted to the educated." Name the step that fails (forty academicians, l.5408; the
   jury defence) and state what Le Bon proposes to fear instead (spending, the
   administrative caste).
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** a thousand people in a square are not a crowd; six men can be.
   State Le Bon's criterion, then ask: if a large assembly deliberates calmly and votes
   against its leaders, does that count against the law of mental unity or does it show
   the assembly was not a "psychological crowd"? The solution shows how the definition
   protects the law from counter-examples, and what independent mark of a crowd would make
   the law testable.
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** apply Taine's sinecure law to the English
   aristocracy of the nineteenth century: derive from it why a landed class that still
   served as county magistrates could lead the extension of the vote rather than be swept
   away by it (`<Ref>` `british-franchise` — the aristocracy that led the extensions), and
   state what the law predicts for a class that stops serving.
6. **Counter-argument (corroboration):** "The crowds of 1789 were vagrants and criminals."
   Name two facts the claim fails to account for (the trades and addresses in the police
   and honours records; the choice of targets) and state what would have to be true for it
   to survive.
7. **Synthesis:** Burke (`<Ref>`) wrote before the Terror and Taine eighty years after it.
   State one thing Taine could establish that Burke could only predict (the recruitment and
   machine of the Jacobins from the archives), and one claim of Burke's that Taine's
   evidence cannot test (that inherited institutions carry more reason than any
   individual).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `gobineau-inequality-of-races`:** Le Bon's law says the crowd's traits
   are "less accentuated in proportion as the spirit of the race is strong". State what
   "race" must mean for this law to be testable, and what evidence would show one race's
   "spirit" to be stronger than another's. The solution notes that Le Bon never defines it,
   and that the next lesson takes up the theory, forty years older than The Crowd, that
   made race the cause of the rise and fall of civilizations (`<Ref>`).

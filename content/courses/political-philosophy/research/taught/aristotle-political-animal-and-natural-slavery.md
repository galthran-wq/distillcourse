# Taught: aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery

## Problem
Can the ranks of ruler and ruled, master and slave included, be read off nature, against conventionalism (city made, justice agreed, force underneath — the Laws X materialist's doctrine, stated in full in place here; Plato's answer is only named, "from an order of causes", not explained; Aristotle answers from how a city grows, at the household's least natural rank)? Answer reached: Aristotle makes the city natural (grows from natural pairings; is their end; man alone has speech about the just) and rule a feature of every composite, down to the natural slave who "apprehends reason without having it"; he accepts the objection that slavery by capture is force; then his own text removes every mark (body, unseen soul, capture, birth) by which a natural slave could be found. The difficulty is internal. Aristotle does hold some peoples more slavish by nature (III.14, VII.7); what he meant by it is taught as two readings (a mark by peoples vs a tendency), left as an interpretive dispute. VERDICTS (last body section, round 2, five <Verdict> blocks): along Aristotle's own line between political rule (free and equal, in turn) and despotic rule (mastery), "some men are slaves by nature" is four claims — (1) people differ in the capacity to lead, partly by birth: **strong**; (2) the more capable should rule: common ground, no grade; (3) whoever rules is the most capable: fails as a rule, holds where office is contested and removable: **on balance**; (4) some men are by nature fit to be owned: fails, **established**. Outsiders objection ("freedom among equals, slavery for outsiders"): historical premise fails **established**; principle fails **strong**. Hinge (KeyFact): unequal capacity grounds at most office among equals, in turn and removably, never ownership of a man. Chrematistics: acquisition bounded by living well; money as end has no bound; usury most unnatural.

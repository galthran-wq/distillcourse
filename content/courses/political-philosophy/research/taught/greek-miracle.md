# Taught: greek-miracle

## Problem
Why did argument about who should rule arise in the Greek cities (c. 800–500 BC) and not in the palace kingdoms around them? Answer: the palace kingdom left no one entitled to ask; its collapse (c. 1200) left pieces without a centre, peers who would not accept a new king, and (c. 800) a public alphabet; the king's functions became annual offices, power was placed "in the middle" (es meson) of a body of equals, and law became a written public text. Argument about rule is the by-product of that arrangement. Verdict, graded **on balance**: the arrangement made the difference (only explanation present wherever argument appears and absent wherever missing); the special-people account is set aside as untestable, not refuted.

## Explained
- Mycenaean palace kingdom (c. 1600–1200; Mycenae, Pylos, Thebes, Knossos): wanax holds war, cult, land and stores; servants not officials; no private trade/money — "A palace has no one entitled to ask who should rule" / ### What the palace was
- Linear B: ~90 syllable signs + commodity pictures, adapted by Cretan scribes to Greek, deciphered 1952; tablets survive only because the fire baked them; te-re-ta read four ways; Remark "What a storeroom list can establish" (records quantities, not obedience) — ### How we know
- Why the question had no place: local lord = vassal, village elders, commoners murmur; writing ran inward; question not forbidden but had no askers and no forum; palace system common to Egypt/Babylon/Hittites, imported via Crete; Mesopotamia anchored here (Tigris–Euphrates plain, now Iraq) — ### Why the question had no place
- Fall c. 1200: no palace rebuilt, wanax drops out of vocabulary, writing lost ~four centuries; Dark Age (c. 1100–800) — "The palaces fell and were not replaced" / ### What vanished
- Why pieces, not a new whole: villages never depended on the palace; contrast Egypt (Nile coordination → restoration); basileus = provincial lord under wanax → plural leading men; Ithaca picture — ### What survived
- Alphabet from Phoenician traders (c. 800), "Phoenician things", learnable by anyone, used publicly; KeyFact: a script any citizen can learn is the precondition of law "in the middle" (readable by each, quotable against the official); Linear B ran inward, the alphabet outward — ### What came back changed
- Tyrant (sole power taken by force, treated as outrage); agon among nobles; Hesiod WD 25–26 "potter angry with potter" — rivalry presupposes peers — "Sovereignty divided into offices held in turn" / ### Rivalry among peers
- Athens: polemarch added (kings feeble in war), archon (civil command; life → 10 yrs → annual), basileus keeps sacrifices; annual office ⇒ yearly human choice among peers — ### Athens takes the king apart
- Scythian legend (foreign, recorded by Greeks; gold plough, yoke, axe, cup; youngest takes all) used for its shape, not as evidence about Greece: one figure holding farming/war/worship like the wanax; set beside the Athenian royal legend as its opposite (Pandion's sons divide: Erechtheus kingship+war, Butes priesthood); the change is visible in the Athenian one: order from one vs order from parts — ### Two royal legends
- es meson: prizes, spoils, disputes, speaker "in the middle"; agora (meeting place before market), common hearth in the town hall, temples on old citadel, walls round all — "Power in the middle" / ### What "in the middle" meant
- polis defined (before Maeandrius): self-governing body of citizens deciding public affairs in common; "city-state" misleads; the arrangement itself, an empty centre surrounded by equals — ### The polis
- Maeandrius at Samos c. 522 (Hdt 3.142, Greek quoted): objects to Polycrates mastering men homoioi to him; "I place the rule in the middle and proclaim isonomia"; asks a gift from the treasury (no sum given) + priesthood; citizen demands he "give an account of the money" — ### A ruler who used the phrase
- isonomia defined: equal standing under law + equal share in rule among citizens — same subsection
- What a claim in the middle requires (derived from: an order needs obeying, a claim must win over men who owe nothing): speech (vote by hands/shout ⇒ persuade; Persuasion a goddess), publicity (the middle belongs to all ⇒ nothing handled out of sight; Samian demand for an account; Athenian officials' accounts at year end), likeness (equal distance from centre ⇒ no one closer by birth; equal standing under one law + equal right to be heard and vote in assembly, whatever wealth; homoioi; whether it means equal shares of office deferred to the two-equalities section) — "Justice comes down to the agora" / ### What a claim in the middle requires
- Written law: serves all three needs; Hesiod's dike split heaven/"bribe-swallowing lords"; written in the agora → same text for all, quotable, disputable — ### Written law
- Solon c. 594: debt on the body, sixth-part tenants, debt-slavery; archon by agreement; cancels debts on persons, code of law; fr. 36 quoted ("Equal laws I gave / To evil and to good"); refused tyranny (fr. 34 line quoted); boundary stone (horos) figure; worked Example = sourcing drill on the poem; "justice came down to the agora" = became accountable and disputable — ### Solon at Athens
- Anacharsis's spiders' webs (Plutarch Sol. 5 quoted) and Solon's covenant reply — ### The objection from the spider's web
- Four rival explanations (the arrangement stated first, in the section's opening, as the answer the earlier sections traced: no single ruler holding the whole of power; pieces held by peers in the middle; public writing); a sequence of events is not an explanation; each rival tested by a case with the cause but not the effect, because a cause present elsewhere without the result cannot be the difference; four-row table (gods/Mesopotamia; cities/Phoenicia, Etruria; phalanx/Sparta; special people/none, untestable): many quarrelsome human-like gods (Mesopotamia: same gods + sacred king re-enacting the god's victory at New Year; the missing sacred king belongs to the arrangement), many cities (Phoenicia: Tyre, Sidon, Byblos; Etruria, central Italy; thin-record caveat; land shortage from 8th c. answered three ways: colonies overseas, Sparta conquers western neighbours late 8th c. and makes serfs, Athens later by olive-oil export + wider politics), hoplite phalanx from c. 650 (Aristodemus at Plataea 479, Hdt 9.71 quoted; Sparta = phalanx + equality (equal plots, common messes, homoioi) + two hereditary kings checked by five yearly overseers, unwritten law, Fear not Persuasion), special people ("Greek miracle", c. 1900 strong form; objection: postulates no development; same people under palace kings; Sparta/Athens diverge under one pressure; untestable is not refuted: would become a rival if a trait were named in advance and found before the palaces fell) — "Four rival explanations and the case each fails"
- Borrowings conceded: alphabet (Phoenicia), geometry (Egypt, Hdt 2.109 quoted), gnomon/12 hours (Babylon), Thales' eclipse 585; Theogony (Zeus vs Typhoeus, "divided their dignities") ≈ Enuma elish (Marduk/Tiamat); Enuma elish = the poem recited at the New Year rite (rite itself explained once, under human-like gods) — "What the Greeks borrowed" / ### The borrowings conceded
- Continuity reading: Milesian accounts keep the myth's shape — one power is origin of the rest and governs them; Thales (all from water, earth floats on it), Anaximenes (all from air by thickening/thinning; air holds earth up; "as our soul, being air, holds us together and controls us"); claims Anaximander too (undivided original mass, opposites separate like sky and earth in myth) — ### The continuity reading
- Break: Anaximander c. 550 (between Thales and Anaximenes), on what holds the world in place: earth "ruled by nothing", equidistant; elements pay back in turn; Alcmaeon: health = isonomia, disease = monarchia; published in prose, no ritual. Two open questions presented as argued: break vs continuation of myth; cosmos shaped on the polis (for: close fit, Alcmaeon's word; against: no Milesian says so, symmetry is also a geometer's idea) — ### What was not borrowed
- [Section now sits directly after the Solon section, before the rivals] Geometric equality (Solon fr. 5 quoted; four classes 500/300/200/less; equal ratio of share to worth, illustrated with the classes: give the 300-man the 500-man's share and the ratio breaks; named after the mathematicians' geometric proportion; eunomia) vs arithmetic (one man one share whatever his harvest, isonomia; Cleisthenes 508/7: 30 districts coast/inland/city, ten tribes each one of each by lot, why not twelve, Council 500 = 50 × 10, year in ten presiding turns) — "Two equalities the city could not reconcile"
- The two equalities unreconciled: aristocrats hold geometric = justice, democrats arithmetic; both appeal to *isotes*; KeyFact: a city could claim both at once (one law for all, office by merit); which is justice → Plato and Aristotle (anchored as 'the philosophers of the fourth century BC') — end of ### Cleisthenes' equality: one share each
- Equality only among citizens (not women, slaves, foreigners; Sparta's few thousand over a subject population); section ENDS on "equal among whom?" — the seam the Conclusion picks up — ### Equality among whom
- The weighing — "The arrangement made the difference, on balance": five-row case matrix (Greek cities of the agora: complete arrangement, argument yes; Babylon: sacred king, none; Phoenician cities: kings, none known; Etruscan cities: partial in some (elected magistrates replace kings, no public law known), none known; Sparta: partial (divided checked kingship, no written law, no debate), none); only the arrangement present wherever argument appears and absent wherever missing; partial rows narrow the claim to the PUBLIC part (law anyone can read, assembly where anyone can speak against a proposal); circularity charge stated (open assembly looks like the result, a cause undetectable apart from its effect, as with special people) and answered: laws in stone and lists of yearly officials are findable whether or not argument survives — ### Every case at once
- Strongest objection at full strength: survival bias (Phoenician only short inscriptions + Greek/Hebrew reports; Etruscan short texts); Carthage (Tyrian colony, modern Tunisia; Greek account c. 330 BC [Aristotle Pol. II.11, unnamed in body]: magistrates elected for merit, council of elders, assembly where anyone may speak against a proposal; books lost when Rome destroyed it 146 BC); Athens writing about itself (Sparta answered once, in the matrix, not here). Why it fails to overturn: no case of a rival producing argument without the arrangement; Carthage shows the arrangement in place, described >2 centuries after Solon after contact with Sicilian Greeks since 6th c.; Samos and Miletus are not Athens — ### The strongest objection
- Grade on balance (not strong: thin negative cases); overturn unpacked in steps: verdict = arrangement is what Greece had and neighbours lacked → early Phoenician/Etruscan inscriptions of yearly offices and public law remove the contrast → Phoenician silence reads as lost books → many-cities rival loses its test case; contrasted with a Greek counter-case (arrangement, no argument) which leaves the contrast intact and shows only non-sufficiency → lowers to open; stated limit: explains possibility, not intensity — ### The grade
- Conclusion hand-off: Athens built the fullest equality at home and ruled other cities by force (stated only in the Conclusion, not the body); next lesson reads its speeches on its freedom and to a weak island; Anacharsis's objection named as still unanswered, "things turned out more as Anacharsis thought than as Solon wished" — <Conclusion>

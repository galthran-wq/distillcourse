# Taught: thucydides-power-and-justice

## Problem
How could one city (Athens) say three things: the Funeral Oration's free city of equals (431/0), the Mytilene vote to kill a rebel ally's men and the reversal (427), and the Melian envoys' "justice only between equals in power" (416)? And what do the speeches say about who should rule? Answer reached: not hypocrisy or wartime corruption but one principle in two domains: among those who can hold one another to account, speech, law and shares; towards those who cannot, power. Melos states the second half without disguise; the thesis was old (I.76, II.63). Historian's arrangement read two ways; his own causes tell against the "order as verdict" reading. Verdict on the Athenian thesis (between cities), split: as a DESCRIPTION of how cities at war treated those at their mercy, upheld on both sides (Scione, Melos; Plataea 427; Sparta never came for Melos); as a RULE that fairness protects no one, fails, graded **on balance** (404: Corinth and Thebes demanded Melos's fate for Athens; Sparta refused, stating as its own ground that Athens "had done great service"; Lysander restored the Melians; the envoys' own forecast at Melos (V.91: a rival empire like Sparta "is not so terrible to the vanquished" as rebel subjects) was BORNE OUT in 404 — the rival spared Athens, the demand for destruction came from Corinth and Thebes — and is the interest reading's best evidence; the Athenians' fear in 405 refutes nothing, it shows only that they did not trust their own forecast; not strong because appeal and Spartan interest pointed the same way in 404 (as the forecast shows), and at Plataea, where they diverged, interest won). <Verdict grade="on balance"> at the end of ### The grade.

## Explained
- Thucydides: Athenian, general 424, failed to save Amphipolis (Athenian colony, north Aegean coast), exiled twenty years, "present with both parties" (V.26 quoted); history breaks off 411 — ## A speech in this history… / ### The historian
- What a speech is: reconstruction of "what was … demanded … by the various occasions", held to "the general sense" (I.22 quoted); events tested severely; "possession for all time"; Remark "What a speech in this history can establish" (good evidence of thinkable arguments, weak of exact words) — ### What the speeches are
- Peloponnesian War 431–404, ~fifty years after Plataea; Lacedaemon = Sparta's state; Athenian empire: post-479 alliance of islands/Asia Minor coast against Persia → yearly *tribute* in silver, members who left besieged and brought back, "subjects in all but name" (Delian League NOT named); I.23 quoted (growth of Athenian power, Spartan alarm); Lesbos (large island off Asia Minor), Melos (small, southern Aegean) — ### The war and the empire
- Public funeral 431/0: coffins by Cleisthenes' ten tribes, empty bier for the missing, speaker "chosen by the state"; Pericles = leading Athenian, one of ten annually elected generals, re-elected again and again, died 429 — ## The Funeral Oration… / ### A funeral by tribes
- Constitution paragraph II.37 quoted whole; Example "What the constitution paragraph claims": six claims (rule by many/democracy = demos+kratos; equal law = isonomia; office by capacity; poverty no bar; private freedom; public obedience through awe ("fear" = reverence, not dread of punishment) of magistrates and laws written/unwritten); equal law = arithmetic, office by capacity = geometric, worth redefined as capacity not wealth ⇒ both equalities claimed, coherent because in different spheres — ### The constitution in one paragraph
- Citizen who takes no part "useless"; "able to judge … if we cannot originate"; discussion "indispensable preliminary" — ### The citizen who judges
- Two supports: awe of the law makes free citizens obey; love of the city makes them willing to die. Problem posed: dying is more than obeying; obvious answer (Sparta's training, "from their very cradles by a painful discipline") ruled out by Pericles: Athenians "live exactly as we please" yet face danger, courage "not of art but of nature" (II.39); "fear of punishment" NOT claimed; "school of Hellas"; "feed your eyes upon her … till love of her fills your hearts" — ### What holds the city together
- Boundaries inside the speech: empire as proof ("title by merit to rule"; II.41 "whether for evil or for good … imperishable monuments" quoted); widows "least talked of among the men" — ### What the speech says about those outside
- Mytilene: chief city of Lesbos, paid no tribute, own fleet/walls, *oligarchy* (rule of the few, rich families) glossed; revolt 428, Spartan fleet too late, Paches takes it 427, commons armed and surrendered; the *assembly* (every adult male citizen, Pnyx hill west of Acropolis, show of hands); first decree "in the fury of the moment"; "the morrow brought repentance" — ## Mytilene… / ### A rebel ally and a decree
- Cleon ("the most violent man at Athens … most powerful with the commons", quoted as the historian's): four steps (assembly the wrong judge: "bad laws … never changed", "ordinary men … better", "audience of a rhetorician"; deliberate aggression, people share guilt; justice + interest coincide, deterrence; III.39 leniency ⇒ revolts "upon the slightest pretext" ⇒ Athens wins back "a ruined town" with no revenue; "pity, sentiment, and indulgence"; dilemma III.40 quoted "right in rebelling … wrong in ruling") — ### Cleon
- Diodotus (otherwise unknown): III.44 quoted "not in a court of justice … how to make the Mitylenians useful"; four steps (debate needed for uncertain future, suspicion makes best counsellor lie, advisers accountable/assembly not; death does not deter, hope and desire "invisible agents", "impossible to prevent … human nature"; equal punishment ⇒ fight to the end, ruined town, no revenue (Cleon's own picture, cause reversed: severity not leniency ruins towns); commons are Athens's friends vs oligarchs); III.47 quoted "voluntarily to put up with injustice"; reversal (harsh side argues from justice, merciful from interest) carried by section heading + seam, explained at the hinge — ### Diodotus
- Two ships: vote "almost equal", day-and-night start, barley-cakes at the oar, arrives as decree read; III.50: "rather more than a thousand" executed on Cleon's motion, walls down, fleet taken, land in lots to Athenian holders (rent from Lesbians); no KeyFact (keeper numbers live in the mytilene-tree footer) — ### Two ships
- Mytilene had no vote (both speakers judging by what keeps the empire: mytilene-tree caption); who-should-rule answers are about Athens governing itself — ### What the debate settled…
- Melos: Spartan colony, refused alliance, "at first remained neutral", hostile after 426 raid; 416 force; heard before "the magistrates and the few" (Athenian gibe about the multitude); question-and-answer format unique in the work; Melians: "judges in your own cause" — ## Melos… / ### A conference before the few
- V.89 quoted whole; "the Mede" = the Persians; *Athenian thesis* defined (justice a question only between parties equal in power; else strong do what they can, weak suffer what they must) — ### The thesis
- L13 refutation: thesis ≠ "might makes right" (they claim no right; deny the question arises between unequals); grounded in Solon's condition (breaking gains nothing when the other can retaliate ⇒ agreements kept when each can make the other pay) run between unequal cities; discriminating example: closing maxim "moderate towards their inferiors" = policy, not duty — ### What the thesis does not say
- Five Melian replies, three main (fairness = the one argument from Athens's interest; gods and Sparta = the hopes Melos stakes its city on; neutrality and honour lean on those hopes). Replies + answers: fairness as common protection / vengeance (answer: fear subjects not rival; both gain by submission); neutrality (answer: friendship = sign of weakness to subjects; Melian point V.98 stated: attacking a neutral turns all neutrals against Athens; answer: mainland cities give little alarm, islanders and subjects are the danger); honour and hope ("danger's comforter", oracles) — ### The Melians' replies
- Gods ("just men fighting against unjust") → V.105 quoted (law of nature for gods and men, found and left, no exemption); Sparta ("for very shame") → "agreeable honourable … expedient just", ally needs superiority of power, Athens holds the sea; Task (C3 felt win) + visible model answer — ### Gods and Spartans
- End of Melos: "seven hundred years"; "most completely deceived"; no Spartan force; V.116 sentence quoted (men killed, women/children sold, 500 colonists) — ### The end of Melos
- Thesis was old: I.76 quoted (fear, honour, interest; "always been law that the weaker should be subject to the stronger"), praise for those who respect justice more than position compels; Pericles II.63 "a tyranny; to take it perhaps was wrong, but to let it go is unsafe" (430; plague first mentioned here with a gloss, "an epidemic in the crowded city", plus Spartan raids on the farmland) — ## One principle, two domains / ### The thesis was old
- One principle two domains; Mytilene as hinge (inside decides outside; why mercy had to argue from interest); two-domains figure carries the answer (no KeyFact) — ### Equality among those who can answer back
- Section order: … One principle → The objection from compulsion → Does the arrangement judge Athens? → Who should rule (ends on the Plato/Aristotle forward clause) → The thesis describes the war and fails as a rule: the record of 404 (the weighing).
- Arrangement: oration → plague (430; country people crowded inside walls; II.53 "Fear of gods or law of man there was none" quoted; parallel with clause 6 rests on fear-paired-with-law, not one word: the translation says "fear" in both, the Greek has two near-synonyms (II.37 nearer awe, II.53 nearer dread); in the oration fear holds men to the law, under the plague it does not); Melos → Sicilian expedition 415–413 (Syracuse, chief Greek city of Sicily; VI.24 "all alike fell in love", the few "feared to appear unpatriotic"; VII.87 quoted); reading 1 order as verdict; reading 2 historian's own causes (plague from "Ethiopia above Egypt"; Sicily lost by "private cabals" at home, II.65; Athens fought nine more years); both readings rest on text; no speaker refutes the envoys — ## Does the arrangement judge Athens?
- Who-should-rule table (Pericles, Cleon, Diodotus, envoys, Melian magistrates, Thucydides on Pericles II.65, Thucydides on 411 VIII.97); II.65 "nominally a democracy … government by the first citizen", successors → "whims of the multitude" → Sicily; Five Thousand (411, open to all who could furnish armour, abolished pay for office = the daily allowance for council/jury that let poor citizens serve) "best government … fusion of the high and the low"; forward clause: rule by one who knows (Plato), mixture of few and many (Aristotle) — ## Who should rule, according to each speaker
- Objection from compulsion: if honour and interest compel, nothing voluntary, no blame (Cleon's "not involuntary" charge dissolves; Diodotus near it); Athenian reply (prediction not blame; credit for moderation, I.76); concession: consistent as description, inconsistent with language of guilt; whether it is true between cities is testable on Athens as the weak party (the last section); whether it stops at the city wall no war between cities can test — ## The objection from compulsion (own section, between One principle and Does the arrangement…; ### The Athenian reply)
- Arrangement section now ends: no speaker defeats the envoys' argument; the test the thesis meets comes from events after the history breaks off. juxtapositions.svg caption: "the historian's own causes tell against the first".
- The weighing — ## The thesis describes the war and fails as a rule: the record of 404 (last body section, after Who should rule):
  - Thesis split into DESCRIPTION (strong city does what it can) and RULE (appeal "to what is fair and right" worth nothing; fairness protects no one weak); can come apart; one counting case refutes the rule — ### Two claims in one sentence
  - Description upheld on both sides: Scione (north Aegean coast; went over to Sparta 423; retaken 421, men killed, women and children enslaved, V.32 quoted); Melos; Plataea (small town beside the 479 battlefield, Athens's ally; besieged by Sparta and Thebes from 429, taken 427; Plataeans pleaded their service against the Persians; each prisoner asked only whether he had done Sparta "any service in the war"; 200+ Plataeans and 25 Athenians killed; III.68 quoted: "mainly adopted to please the Thebans, who were thought to be useful"); Sparta never came for Melos — ### The description holds on both sides
  - Thebes anchored (largest city of Boeotia, region on Athens's northern border, Sparta's ally); Corinth anchored (trading city on the isthmus joining Peloponnese to rest of Greece); Xenophon anchored (Athenian of the next generation who continued the history from 411); Aegospotami 405 (beach on the Hellespont, strait between Europe and Asia Minor, Athens's grain route; Spartan fleet under Lysander destroys Athens's); Hell. II.2.3 quoted in prose (night, "suffer such treatment as they had visited upon the Melians"); II.2.9 Lysander restores the Melians; 404 Corinth and Thebes demand destruction; II.2.20 quoted ("would not enslave a Greek city which had done great service amid the greatest perils"); "greatest perils" = the Persian invasions; terms: long walls (glossed: joined city to harbour) down, twelve ships, follow Sparta "by land and by sea"; tie to Melian reply 1 ("your fall would be a signal for the heaviest vengeance") and the Athenian answer (V.91, rival empire "not so terrible to the vanquished"): in 405 Athens did not trust its answer, in 404 the answer proved right (rival refused, allies demanded) — ### When Athens was the weak party
  - KeyFact: the service the envoys refused to plead at Melos (defeating the Persians, "specious pretences") is the ground Sparta spared Athens on in 404.
  - Strongest objection: Plataea vs 404 as controlled comparison (same judge, same ground of Persian-war service — pleaded by the Plataeans in 427, stated by Sparta itself in 404, no Athenian plea — same Theban demand; Thebes useful in 427, a rival once the war was won); terms = disarmed counterweight Sparta controlled; Sparta at war with Thebes and Corinth within a decade; interest killed the Plataeans and spared the Athenians; V.91 quoted as the reading's early Athenian witness (a forecast from rulers-and-subjects logic, no fairness, matched by 404) — ### The strongest objection: interest spared Athens (figure service-appeal.svg)
  - Why the verdict stands (thesis claimed "nothing"): TWO reasons — the ground Sparta gave, to allies who wanted the opposite (a ground offered to unwilling hearers must be one they accept); the Melians restored (Sparta "liberator of Greece" since 431 — display works only on onlookers who count Melos a wrong). The V.91 forecast answered: it predicts THAT the rival would spare Athens, not the ground given nor the Melians' restoration; it is why the verdict cannot be firmer than on balance. The Athenians' fear is explicitly NOT a reason on either side: it shows only that they did not trust their own forecast, which the outcome bore out — ### Why the verdict stands
  - Grade on balance; overturned by a contemporary account of the Spartan debate giving the counterweight as the reason and the service appeal as a formula — ### The grade

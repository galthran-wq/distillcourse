# Research brief: testing-the-clash (Testing the clash: Huntington's hypothesis against the conflict record since 1993)

status: planned
module: civilizations
type: core
goal: Evaluate Huntington's hypothesis against the published tests of conflict across civilizational lines since 1993 and against the Yugoslav wars, and state where the record supports, qualifies or refutes it

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `huntington-clash-of-civilizations`: the hypothesis as stated
  ("the fault lines between civilizations will be the battle lines of the future"),
  the definition of a civilization (objective elements plus self-identification), the
  list of seven or eight, the two levels (micro fault-line fighting, macro state
  competition), the kin-country syndrome with Huntington's own three cases (Gulf,
  Caucasus, Bosnia), the corollary that intra-civilizational conflict is "less
  intense and less likely to expand" (Russia–Ukraine), the disclaimer ("descriptive
  hypotheses"), and Exercise 8 there — the reader has already tried to write a
  coding rule and found two states it cannot place. From `what-measures-greatness`:
  that Huntington's is the only one of the three theories with a dated prediction,
  and that the measure (power) and the account of decline (shift in who fights whom)
  are his. From `toynbee-challenge-and-response`: the method question — twenty-one
  cases "at our own peril" — which this lesson answers for Huntington with a dataset
  rather than a sample. From the course's earlier applications (`sparta-as-test-case`,
  `altar-of-victory`, `english-revolution-as-test`, `assignats`, `british-franchise`,
  `dreyfus-affair-as-test`, `article-48-weimar`, `udhr-read-against-the-right`, and
  above all `the-cognitive-elite`, the module just before, which also read a
  published statistical argument against a theory): the habit of reading a real
  artefact against a theory and naming the departures.
- **Contributes:** The module's application (L16): a hypothesis of 1993 checked thirty
  years on. The artefact is twofold — the published statistical tests of the
  hypothesis on dyadic conflict data (Russett–Oneal–Cox 2000; Henderson–Tucker 2001;
  Chiozza 2002, with Huntington's own reply), of which the lesson reproduces the
  simplest count, and the Bosnian war of 1992–95, Huntington's own first case, read
  for the kin-country syndrome with the counter-cases he himself named. The lesson
  states where the record supports (Islam–West as a partial exception; some kin-
  country behaviour in Bosnia), qualifies (more conflict within civilizations than
  across; the alignments that cut across the lines) and refutes (the Russia–Ukraine
  corollary) the hypothesis — and names what the tests measure that the hypothesis
  did not claim.
- **Hands off:** The module ends here, and the next module (why-the-west) opens with
  `weber-only-in-the-west`. The hand-off is the one premise all three of the module's
  authors share and none of them explains: that the West is singled out — Spengler's
  Faustian Culture in its winter, Toynbee's one civilization "not clearly
  disintegrating" that has "Westernised all", Huntington's West "at an extraordinary
  peak of power" whose ideas (individualism, liberalism, constitutionalism, human
  rights, the rule of law, the separation of church and state) "often have
  little resonance" elsewhere. The count has just tested who fights whom; it has said nothing
  about why that list of ideas, and that power, arose in one civilization and not
  another. That question is the next module's, and its first answer — Weber's,
  from a religious ethic — is the next lesson. Two later threads, one clause each in
  the Conclusion at most: the question Huntington's fifth reason raised (identity
  inherited or chosen) returns as the nation and who may join it, with Huntington's
  own answer for the United States in `albions-seed-and-who-are-we` (Who Are We?,
  now read); and the method of this lesson — a theory read against a count and a
  case, departures named — is the method of `the-2015-crisis-read-three-ways`.
- **Spine step:** "The question widens from the state to the civilization: against
  Spengler's organisms, Toynbee's challenge and response and Huntington's fault lines
  — three measures of greatness and three accounts of decline, tested on the record"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):**
  - *The artefact (what real thing is read):* (1) three published tests of the
    hypothesis on interstate conflict data — the papers themselves, their tables,
    their coding of states to civilizations — and the public datasets they used
    (Correlates of War Militarized Interstate Disputes; COW Inter-State Wars), from
    which the lesson reproduces ONE count: the share of interstate conflicts between
    states of different civilizations against the share of state pairs that are
    cross-civilizational, by Huntington's own 1993 list; (2) the Bosnian war 1992–95
    as a record of who recognised, armed, funded, fought beside and bombed whom.
  - *Module results it uses (by concept id / slug):* `civilizational-fault-line`
    (what counts as "across a line"), `kin-country-syndrome` (what third-party
    alignment the hypothesis predicts), `torn-country` (why coding is hard: Turkey,
    Russia, Mexico), `measures-of-greatness` (power as Huntington's measure; a count
    of disputes is not a count of power), `challenge-and-response` by way of the
    method only (Toynbee's small-sample caveat answered by a population of dyads),
    and from the Huntington lesson the disclaimer and the two levels.
  - *Departures the lesson must name:* (a) the tests count the FREQUENCY of
    interstate disputes, each dyad-year weighted equally; the hypothesis claimed the
    "principal", "most dangerous", "most likely to escalate" conflicts — intensity
    and escalation, not frequency; (b) dyadic interstate data exclude intrastate
    fault-line wars (Bosnia itself, Chechnya, Kashmir's insurgency, Sudan) — the
    micro level the hypothesis was most confident about; (c) most of the tests' data
    pre-date 1993, and the post-Cold-War years they contain are few — the hypothesis
    is about the future; (d) the tests must code every state to exactly one
    civilization, where the hypothesis allows self-identification, torn countries and
    "possibly African"; (e) the kin-country syndrome is a claim about THIRD PARTIES
    aligning, which a dyadic dispute count does not test and only the case study
    reaches; (f) the Islam–West partial exception, which some tests find and others
    do not, depends on how "Islam" is coded and whether intrastate and ethnic
    conflicts are included. And on the case side: (g) Bosnia's war included a
    Croat–Muslim war inside the "clash" (1992–94), a Western-brokered Catholic–Muslim
    alliance (Washington, 1994), a Western air war on behalf of a Muslim-led
    government against Orthodox Serbs (1995), and a Russian contingent under NATO
    command (1996) — alignments the kin-country rule does not predict; (h)
    Russia–Ukraine, the intra-civilizational dyad Huntington said "should" stay
    peaceful, did not.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): a hypothesis stated in 1993 can be checked
thirty years later; political scientists did check it, counting wars and disputes by
whether the parties belonged to the same civilization or different ones, and the
Yugoslav wars ran their course; the lesson reproduces the simplest form of the test
and reads the Bosnian case for the kin-country syndrome. The newcomer's problem: how
do you turn "the fault lines will be the battle lines" into something a count can
confirm or refute — and what does it mean when the count and the case disagree? The
chain:

1. **From a sentence to a count: what the hypothesis commits to.** Question: what
   observation would show the hypothesis right or wrong? Attempt: look for
   wars between civilizations after 1993 — there are some, so it is confirmed. Why
   it fails: there were wars between civilizations before 1993 too, and wars within
   them after; a hypothesis about a CHANGE in pattern needs a baseline and a
   comparison. Answer: take the hypothesis's own three commitments — (i) conflicts
   across civilizational lines will be a larger share of conflicts than before;
   (ii) conflicts across lines will be more intense and more likely to spread than
   conflicts within; (iii) third parties will align by civilization (kin-country).
   Then the parts that need fixing before counting: a unit (a pair of states in a
   year; a war; a dispute), a period (before and after 1989/1993), and a rule for
   "across a line" — which is where the reader's own attempt (Exercise 8 of the
   Huntington lesson) left two states unplaced. The because: a hypothesis stated
   "descriptively" (Ref `huntington-clash-of-civilizations`) is checkable exactly to
   the extent that these three can be fixed; Toynbee's "at our own peril" (Ref) was
   about twenty-one cases, and here the cases are thousands of state-pairs per year.
2. **The coding problem: which civilization is a state in?** Question: by Huntington's
   own definition (objective elements plus self-identification), how does one place
   Turkey, Russia, Israel, the Philippines, Ethiopia, Nigeria? Attempt: by majority
   religion. Why it fails: the definition is not religion alone, torn countries exist
   by Huntington's own account, and "possibly African" is not a coding. Answer: every
   test must make the soft boundary hard — the published tests used the map in the
   1996 book; the lesson builds its own table from the 1993 list and shows the
   judgment calls in an Aside. The because, and the first departure named: a count
   needs a partition; the hypothesis offered a gradient. Whatever the count then
   shows, part of it is the partition's doing — which cuts both ways (a result
   against the hypothesis may be an artefact of hard coding; so may one for it).
3. **The reproduced count: shares of conflict across and within lines.** Question
   (the research-shaped task, playbook-history rule 4 — the computation IS the
   argument): of interstate conflicts since 1946 (or disputes since 1950), what share
   had states of different civilizations on opposite sides — and what share WOULD
   have, if civilization did not matter? Attempt: report the first share alone. Why
   it fails: most pairs of states ARE cross-civilizational (seven or eight groups of
   unequal size), so a high share proves nothing; and most fighting is between
   neighbours, who are more often of the same civilization than a random pair. Answer:
   compute the share of conflicts that are cross-civilizational, then the share of
   all state-pairs (and, better, of contiguous pairs) that are cross-civilizational,
   and compare; do it for a Cold War window and a post-1989 window. State the result
   the lesson finds, bound in the sentence (dataset, years, unit, denominator), then
   state what the three papers found with their controls — in the writer's words
   from their tables, each number bound. End, per the playbook, with (a) the
   interpretation, (b) an uncertainty statement (the coding; the small number of
   post-1993 years; the dyad-year unit), and (c) the non-quantitative corroboration
   the next sections supply (Bosnia). The because: this is commitment (i) checked,
   and it is where the published tests converge — conflict within civilizations is
   at least as common as across, and the post-1989 change the hypothesis predicted
   does not appear in interstate dispute frequency.
4. **What the count measures that the hypothesis did not claim.** Question: does the
   count refute the hypothesis? The seam states the expectation and breaks it (L10b):
   from the papers' conclusions one would expect "refuted"; the hypothesis's own text
   resists. Attempt: the papers' verdict as final. Why it is too quick — the
   departures (a)–(f) of the APPLICATION spec, each argued in a sentence or two:
   frequency vs "principal"/"most dangerous"/"most likely to escalate"; interstate
   dyads vs the micro level (Bosnia is not in an interstate dispute dataset as a
   Bosniak–Serb war); the period (most of the data are from the Cold War the
   hypothesis says has ended); the partition (link 2); third-party alignment untested
   by dyads; and the Islam–West exception, which some specifications find and which
   Huntington's "Try Again" claimed as the point. Answer: the count refutes
   commitment (i) as a claim about interstate dispute FREQUENCY and leaves (ii) and
   (iii) to other evidence; the defenders' reply ("the tests miss what was claimed")
   is partly right and partly a retreat to the unmeasurable — the lesson says which
   part is which. The because: the hypothesis's disclaimer ("does not argue … that
   groups within a civilization will not conflict") protects it from the count at
   the cost of making the count's question — is there MORE across than within? —
   the only one it still answers.
5. **Bosnia 1992–95 read for the kin-country syndrome: what Huntington saw in 1993.**
   Question: commitment (iii) needs a case; Huntington gave Bosnia as his first. What
   did he report? Answer (from l. 192–194, told as his evidence): Germany leading the
   Community to recognise Slovenia and Croatia, the Vatican first; Western sympathy
   for the Bosnian Muslims but "relatively little concern … over Croatian attacks on
   Muslims"; Yeltsin's "middle course" under nationalist pressure, Russian
   volunteers with the Serbs; Iran's arms "in violation of the U.N. arms embargo",
   "up to 4,000" volunteers, Saudi money; the Saudi editor's Spanish Civil War
   parallel. Anchor the war (L11): Bosnia's three peoples, the April 1992 outbreak,
   the embargo of September 1991, Sarajevo's siege. The because: this is the
   syndrome at its clearest — each of the three parties had a civilization behind it
   — and the lesson grants it before reading on.
6. **Bosnia 1992–95 read to the end: the alignments that cut across the lines.**
   Question: did the rest of the war follow the rule? Attempt: assume it did, since
   it began that way. Why it fails, dated: (1) the Croat–Bosniak war of October
   1992–February 1994 — two of the three "civilizations" at war with each other
   inside the clash (Mostar, Ahmići) — which Huntington's 1993 text mentions as
   "Croatian attacks on Muslims" and does not weigh; (2) the Washington Agreement of
   March 1994 — the United States brokering a Catholic–Muslim federation and
   alliance against the Serbs; (3) the Iranian arms pipeline through Croatia in
   1994–95 with American acquiescence — a Western state letting an Islamic state arm
   a Muslim government through a Catholic one; (4) Operation Deliberate Force,
   August–September 1995 — Western air power used against Orthodox Serbs on behalf of
   a Muslim-led government, after Srebrenica; (5) Dayton, November 1995, imposed by
   the West on all three; (6) Russia in the Contact Group and a Russian brigade in
   IFOR in 1996 under American tactical control. Answer: the kin-country rule
   predicts the first year's recognitions, arms and volunteers and nothing after
   the Washington Agreement; state interest, Western unity and the shock of
   Srebrenica predict the rest. The because: Huntington's own criterion — "the
   positions of nations and the cleavages between them increasingly were along
   civilizational lines" as the conflict continued — is a claim about the direction
   of change over the war, and the direction ran the other way. Where the lesson
   concedes: the volunteers, Iran's and Saudi Arabia's conduct, and Russian
   nationalist pressure on Yeltsin were real and are what the syndrome names; a
   reader who weights the first year differently may reach a different verdict.
7. **The counter-cases Huntington named himself.** Question: the hypothesis's
   corollary was that violence WITHIN a civilization "should be low" — what became of
   its two chosen examples? Answer: Iraq–Kuwait (August 1990) and Iran–Iraq (1980–88)
   are intra-Islamic wars Huntington mentions in the Gulf section without weighing
   them; and Russia–Ukraine, the case he picked in 1993 to show civilization
   restraining violence ("two Slavic, primarily Orthodox peoples"), ended in the
   annexation of Crimea in 2014 and the invasion of 24 February 2022 — the largest
   interstate war in Europe since 1945, within one of his civilizations. State it in
   two sentences, dated, without commentary on the war itself. The because: a
   corollary the author stated as a test ("If civilization is what counts … should be
   low") is the one place the record gives a clean answer, and the answer is no — or
   rather, that civilization was not "what counts" there; the disclaimer covers
   intra-civilizational fighting in general but was not what the Ukraine sentence
   said.
8. **Where the record supports, qualifies, refutes — and what the module can now
   do.** Answer, as a ledger (FIGURE 7) argued in prose: SUPPORTS — the Islam–West
   pairing shows elevated conflict in some tests and in the intrastate and ethnic
   conflict literature (bound and sourced); the first year of Bosnia shows kin-country
   rallying as described. QUALIFIES — conflict within civilizations is at least as
   frequent as across, so the hypothesis survives only as a claim about intensity
   and escalation, which the dyadic tests do not measure; coding is where tests and
   hypothesis part. REFUTES — the corollary about intra-civilizational restraint, on
   Huntington's own example; and the claim that alignments would run increasingly
   along civilizational lines "as the conflict continued", on his own first case.
   Then the module's close: of the three theories, the one that could be checked was
   checked and gave a mixed answer, with one corollary its author stated as a test contradicted by the record; the two that cannot
   be checked stand or fall by their measures (Ref `what-measures-greatness`). The
   problem that stays open, stated in the Conclusion: the count tested who fights
   whom, and took for granted what all three authors took for granted — that the
   West is the civilization the others react to, at a "peak of power", carrying
   ideas the Rest call Western rather than universal. Why that list of ideas and that
   power arose in one civilization is the question the next module asks, and its
   first answer starts from a religious ethic (Ref `weber-only-in-the-west`, one
   sentence; no Weber content here).

Headings as claims or questions: "What would count as a test?" / "Which civilization
is a state in? The coding problem" / "The count: across the lines and within them" /

  papers' tables, with page references, and every Bosnia date is checked against a
  standard account. The course brief's recollection of the findings (above) is
  approximate and must not be transcribed. If a paper cannot be obtained, the lesson
  says so in References and reproduces the count from the public data alone.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** *dyad* / *dyad-year* (a pair of states, observed in one
  year — the unit of the tests; define at first use); *militarized interstate dispute*
  (a threat, display or use of force between states short of or including war — the
  Correlates of War category); *Correlates of War* (the public dataset project,
  founded 1963); *contiguity* (sharing a border — why it is a control); *controls* /
  *regression* (one clause: the papers ask whether civilizational difference adds
  anything once distance, alliances, power and democracy are accounted for — the
  lesson does not teach regression and does not report coefficients, only
  directions and the authors' stated conclusions); *Bosniak* (the name adopted in
  1993 for Bosnia's Muslim people — use "Bosnian Muslims" where Huntington does and
  say the two are the same); *Srebrenica* (the July 1995 massacre of some 8,000
  Bosniak men and boys by Bosnian Serb forces — one sentence, dated, from the ICTY
  record); *IFOR* (the NATO Implementation Force after Dayton); *Contact Group*;
  *Dayton* (the Ohio air base where the agreement was negotiated); *UNSCR 713*;
  *Operation Deliberate Force*; *the Washington Agreement*; *Crimea* and *Donbas*
  (2014); *Ahmići*, *Mostar* — each anchored at first mention or cut.
- **Numbers (L12, playbook-history):** the count is the one place numbers are the
  argument; keep them in the table and bind every one (dataset, years, unit,
  denominator). In prose, keeper numbers are two or three: the share across vs the
  base rate (as a relation: "about the same as", "fewer than"), and "up to 4,000"
  volunteers bound to Huntington's 1993 report. No coefficients, no p-values, no
  casualty totals beyond Srebrenica's bound figure. Dates to the month where
  contingency is the point (the Washington Agreement before Deliberate Force; 24
  February 2022), to the year otherwise.
- **The departures (a)–(h) are the lesson's content, not a caveat paragraph.** Each
  is argued where it bites (links 2, 4, 6, 7) and listed once in the ledger. A
  lesson that reports "the tests refuted Huntington" and stops is an inventory; one
  that reports "Huntington's defenders say the tests miss the point" and stops is a
  retreat. State which departures are the tests' fault and which the hypothesis's
  (frequency vs intensity: the hypothesis's claim was about intensity, so the tests
  measure the wrong thing — but the hypothesis gave no way to measure intensity;
  coding: both).
- **No moralising on Bosnia or Ukraine, in either direction.** The lesson reads the
  record for alignments; it does not adjudicate the wars. Srebrenica is stated as
  the ICTY found it, once, because it is what changed Western policy in 1995; nothing
  else about atrocities is needed for the argument.
- **Do not credit Huntington with predicting Ukraine or blame him for missing it
  beyond what he wrote.** He wrote that violence "should be low" "if civilization is
  what counts"; the lesson reads that sentence and its disclaimer and stops.
- **Islam–West is a PARTIAL exception, stated as such:** some tests find the pairing
  more dispute-prone; others do not; the ethnic/intrastate literature (Fox) finds
  Islamic groups involved in many conflicts, most of them with other Muslims. The
  lesson gives the finding as the papers give it, bound, and does not generalise to
  "Islam has bloody borders" as proven or refuted.
- **Who Are We?** is read (index 76) and belongs to `albions-seed-and-who-are-we`;
  earlier versions of this brief called it a gap — that is wrong. Not used here.
- **Register rule.**
  The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **L8a:** Russett, Oneal, Cox, Henderson, Tucker, Chiozza, Fox, Tusicisny, Silber,
  Little, Malcolm, Burg, Shoup, Holbrooke — References only. In the body the tests
  are "the published tests", "one study", "a second"; the datasets (Correlates of
  War, MID, UCDP) and documents (UNSCR 713, the Washington Agreement, Dayton, the
  1996 House report) are named objects and may be named. Huntington is named.
- **E2 / playbook-history rule 4:** the count is legitimate because the calculation
  is the historical argument (share vs base rate); it ends in interpretation,
  uncertainty and non-quantitative corroboration. No other arithmetic.
- **Dossier thinness — the lesson's main risk:** the whole artefact is outside the
  dossier. The writer should budget time to obtain the three papers (JSTOR / SAGE /
  Wiley; all are standard and widely held), download MID v5.0 and the state system
  list, and read one Bosnia narrative. If the count cannot be run, the lesson still
  stands on the papers' tables and the Bosnia case; say which was done.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up, E3):** For each of these, say which of the hypothesis's
   three commitments it bears on and whether it supports, qualifies or refutes: the
   share of disputes across lines vs the base rate; the Washington Agreement; the
   invasion of 24 February 2022; the Islam–West pairing in the tests; the 4,000
   volunteers. Solution places each with one sentence of warrant.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** Huntington's "Try Again" reply (2000) —
   the writer quotes one short passage once obtained. Author, date (seven years after
   the hypothesis, in reply to a test), audience (the Journal of Peace Research's
   readers — the testers' own forum), what it records (what the author now says the
   hypothesis claimed), what it argues (the tests measured the wrong period and the
   wrong level), what it omits (any measure of the right level). Closing: how does
   the reply change what the 1993 article can be USED to establish — a theory of
   interstate conflict, or of intrastate and third-party behaviour?
3. **Boundary probe:** Make every state its own civilization; then make all states
   one. Compute what the "share across lines" becomes in each case and what the
   hypothesis would predict; then state the number of civilizations at which the
   test has power at all and why Huntington's seven or eight, with one of them
   holding most of the world's contiguous dyads, is near the edge. Solution: the
   test's power depends on the partition's granularity — the coding problem in its
   degenerate form.
4. **Claim to refute:** "The published tests show that culture does not cause
   conflict." Name the step that fails (the tests measure civilizational
   MEMBERSHIP as a dyadic attribute, not culture; they measure dispute frequency,
   not intensity; they mostly cover the Cold War) and state what the tests do
   establish (no post-1989 rise in cross-civilizational dispute frequency; within
   at least as common as across). The solution gives the exact scope.
5. **Derivation the lesson did not do (research-shaped):** Design the test that
   would distinguish the kin-country syndrome from ordinary alliance politics in
   Bosnia: list the third parties, code each by civilization and by its pre-war
   alliances and interests (Germany's Croatian diaspora and recognition policy;
   Iran's isolation; Russia's position in the Contact Group; the United States'
   interest in NATO), and state what pattern of support over 1991–95 would favour
   each explanation. Then say, from the lesson's dated record, which pattern the
   record shows and in which year it changed. Solution: the first year favours
   kin-country; from 1994 the alliance explanation predicts the record and the
   kin-country rule does not.
6. **Synthesis (counter-argument stem):** Someone claims, with Huntington's
   defenders, that the tests miss the point because the hypothesis was about the
   most dangerous conflicts, not the most frequent. Name two facts the claim fails to
   account for (the article's own frequency language — "the principal conflicts",
   "flash points for crisis and bloodshed", "increasingly … along civilizational
   lines"; the Russia–Ukraine corollary, which is about frequency of violence) and
   state what would have to be true for the defence to stand (an intensity measure
   on which cross-civilizational wars since 1993 rank highest — the reader names what
   that measure would be and what Rwanda 1994, Congo 1998–2003 and Ukraine 2022 do to
   it).
7. **Synthesis (E3 spiral, causal ranking):** Rank, as necessary / enabling /
   precipitating, three explanations of the West's 1995 intervention against the
   Bosnian Serbs — civilizational sympathy for a Muslim people (which the kin-country
   rule says should not exist), Srebrenica as a shock to Western publics, and the
   state interest in NATO's credibility — and defend the ranking from the lesson's
   dated record. Then say which of the module's three measures of greatness (Ref
   `what-measures-greatness`) the intervention counts under, if any.
8. **FORWARD (E7)** → `weber-only-in-the-west`: Huntington lists the ideas he says
   the West has and the Rest resist — individualism, liberalism, constitutionalism,
   human rights, equality, liberty, the rule of law, democracy, free markets, the
   separation of church and state. For each, write down whether you know of an
   earlier or non-Western instance (the reader has some from the course: the Greek
   polis, Polybius's Rome, Chinese examination officials named in passing, the
   Islamic trade world), and then state what, if each has precedents elsewhere,
   could still be peculiar to the West — the things themselves, or the way they were
   combined and made systematic. The solution says in one sentence that the next

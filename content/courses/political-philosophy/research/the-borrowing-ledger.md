# Research brief: the-borrowing-ledger (The borrowing ledger: paper, gunpowder, print and numerals)

status: planned
module: why-the-west
type: core
goal: Apply the module's rival theses to the documented record of what the West borrowed — paper, gunpowder, printing, Hindu-Arabic numerals — grading each transfer by the strength of its evidence, and evaluate the reply that the West turned borrowings into self-sustaining systems

Register rule (binding on the lesson): The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.

Every borrowing is stated with its own grade of confidence, never as one flat line ("Europe took paper, gunpowder and printing from China" is the sentence the lesson exists to take apart). The rival thesis's answer is stated as an argument about evidence and timing, never as a reproach; the pro-Western reply is stated at full strength.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis`: the great divergence dated after
  1800 and the theft-of-history thesis — a Eurasian chain of exchange, leads that alternate,
  Goody's list of eastern techniques Europe received (paper, compass, gunpowder, printing) and
  the Needham table of transmission lags; the exceptionalist reply that the lead was in knowledge
  before it was in income; and both sides' agreement that Europe borrowed before 1500 ("Europe
  was a learner"). From `landes-european-exceptionalism`: the invention of invention (the
  mechanised paper mill, corned powder, the printing that "no one could put a lid on" in a
  fragmented Europe; the Ottoman printing ban) and European fragmentation. From
  `murray-human-accomplishment`: the accomplishment inventory and its "firsts" proviso; numerals
  and proof as meta-inventions; the 1560 burst in mathematics. From `weber-only-in-the-west`:
  "There was printing in China. But a printed literature … and, above all, the Press and
  periodicals, have appeared only in the Occident"; the decimal system invented in India "led to
  no modern arithmetic or book-keeping" there; Luther and the Reformation (1517). From
  `henrich-church-and-kinship`: literacy and Protestant Bible-reading (as Henrich used it).
- **Contributes:** The one move: replace the flat list "Europe took China's inventions" with a
  ledger in which each transfer carries the grade its evidence supports — paper via the Arab
  world (secure: a documented chain of mills), Hindu-Arabic numerals via Baghdad and Pisa
  (secure, and slow to spread), gunpowder (probable: Chinese priority certain, European recipes
  appear a few decades after Mongol-era contact, no document of the passage, Europeans plausibly
  unaware where it came from), Gutenberg's press (most probably an independent invention; at
  most stimulus) — and then test the module's two theses on the ledger: the pro-Western reply
  (everyone borrowed; the West turned borrowings into self-sustaining systems) at full strength,
  then the rival thesis's answer.
- **Hands off:** The module closes; the question it leaves is whether the differences between
  peoples that the ledger records are themselves to be explained by something deeper —
  heredity (`hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`, next module's first lesson), geography or
  institutions (`geography-or-institutions`). One sentence in the `<Conclusion>` says the next
  module takes the gap between nations, not between techniques, as its problem.
- **Spine step:** "Why the West? Weber, Landes, Murray and Henrich argue for Western exceptionalism from religion, fragmentation, accomplishment and kinship; Pomeranz and Goody answer that the West was one link in a long chain of exchange; the borrowing ledger tests both"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):** The artefact read is the documentary record of four transfers,
  assembled as a ledger: for each item, the earliest dated evidence at each station of its route
  (a manuscript, a recipe, a dated object, a statute), the gaps between stations, and the grade
  those gaps allow. Module results it uses: `invention-of-invention` and `european-fragmentation`
  (Landes: what Europe did with paper, powder and print, and why no one could stop it);
  `accomplishment-inventory` (Murray's "firsts" proviso: a borrowing counts to its inventor, not
  its adopter — the ledger applies the same rule); `protestant-ethic`/`rationalization` (Weber's
  "only in the West": the Press, rational arithmetic and book-keeping); `great-divergence` and
  `theft-of-history` (Pomeranz's late date; Goody's chain of exchange and transmission lags).
  New concepts: `graded-transmission`, `borrowing-into-system`. Departures from the idealisation
  the lesson must name: (1) a transfer is rarely one event — "blueprint copying" (a technique
  carried by its makers, as paper by artisans) differs from "idea diffusion" (a rumour that a
  thing can be done, re-invented locally), and absence of a document is not evidence of
  independence; (2) a grade is a judgement on surviving records, which favour literate,
  record-keeping stations (Baghdad, Italian notaries) over oral ones (caravan and steppe); (3)
  "who invented" and "who made it matter" are different ledgers, and each side tends to count
  the one that favours it; (4) the "system" reply is judged with hindsight from the nineteenth
  century — the reader must ask when each system became self-sustaining and whether others had
  comparable ones at that date.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): paper, gunpowder, the compass and printing are often listed
together as Chinese inventions Europe took; the evidence for each transfer is very different —
a chain of mills for paper, a formula appearing a few decades after the Mongol armies with no
document of the passage, almost nothing linking Gutenberg to East Asia; the lesson grades each
claim and asks what the ledger proves. Anchor (L11): the Mongol empire (from the 1206
proclamation of Genghis Khan to its splitting in the 1260s; its armies in Poland and Hungary in
1241; the Pax Mongolica that put Italian merchants and friars on the road to China); the Abbasid
caliphate at Baghdad (750–1258). The newcomer's problem, from the previous lesson: both sides
agree Europe borrowed, and disagree about what that shows. Before deciding, the reader needs to
know what exactly was borrowed — and how one knows.

1. **What would prove a transfer?** Obvious attempt: Chinese priority plus later European use
   equals borrowing. Why it fails: priority shows only that one came first; the same thing can be
   invented twice (the Phaistos disk stamped signs in clay c. 1700 BC and nobody followed — Ref
   nothing, it is new; Diamond ch. 13 is the source, named in References). Answer: three kinds of
   evidence of increasing strength — (i) priority; (ii) a route with dated stations, each closer
   to the receiver; (iii) the carriers — artisans, texts, a borrowed name. Grades follow: secure
   (all three), probable (priority and timing, no carrier), possible stimulus (an idea could have
   travelled, nothing shows it did), independent (different process, no contact evidence). Define
   `graded-transmission` here. Distinguish blueprint copying (the technique carried by its
   makers) from idea diffusion (the knowledge that a thing can be done). KEEPER: the four grades.
2. **Paper: secure.** The chain as a step-through: Chinese paper from the Han (court tradition
   names Cai Lun, 105 AD; older archaeological paper exists); paper in Central Asia before the
   Arabs came; Samarkand paper from the 8th c. (the story of Chinese prisoners taken at the
   Talas River in 751 who taught the craft is told three centuries later — a weak link in the
   telling, not in the chain); Baghdad manufacture by the 790s; Damascus and Egypt; the Maghreb
   and Muslim Spain (Xàtiva's paper praised in the mid-12th c.); the oldest European paper
   manuscripts on Arab paper (11th c.); Italian mills at Fabriano by the 1270s–80s. Carriers: the
   craft, the material, and the words (*ream* from Arabic *rizma*, via Spanish *resma* — verify in
   the OED before use). Grade: secure. Then the European step: water-driven stamping hammers,
   watermarks, gelatine sizing; Europe soon exported paper to the Near East. "Paper had traveled
   nearly halfway around the world, but no culture or civilization on its route had tried to
   mechanize its manufacture" (Landes l.1202, a clause only). Seam: the first item already shows
   both theses' data in one place — the chain (Goody) and the mechanised mill (Landes).
3. **Numerals: secure, and slow.** Problem: Europe counted with Roman numerals and the abacus
   board; why would anyone change? The chain: place value with a zero sign in India (in use by
   the 7th c.; Brahmagupta's rules for zero, 628); a Syrian bishop in 662 praising the Hindu
   method; an Indian astronomical text translated in Baghdad under al-Mansur; al-Khwarizmi's
   arithmetic c. 825 — in Latin *Algoritmi de numero Indorum*, whence "algorithm"; the
   *Codex Vigilanus* (976) with nine numerals and no zero; Leonardo of Pisa's *Liber Abaci*
   (1202), learned at Bugia in North Africa, opening with "the nine Indian figures" and the zero
   "which the Arabs call zephir" (whence *zero* and *cipher*). Grade: secure — the names travel
   with the thing. Then the slowness: Florence's money-changers forbade the new figures in their
   books in 1299 (easier to forge); Roman numerals in many ledgers into the 16th c. KEEPER: "two
   centuries from India to Baghdad, four from Baghdad to general use in Italian ledgers".
4. **Gunpowder: probable, undocumented.** Problem: here the priority is beyond doubt (Chinese
   formulae printed in 1044; fire lances in the 10th–12th c.; metal guns by the 1280s) and the
   timing is suggestive (the Mongols used Chinese gunpowder weapons in their wars with the Jin
   and Song; their armies reached Hungary and Poland in 1241; the first clear European mention —
   Roger Bacon's description of a saltpetre firecracker, 1267 — follows within a generation; the
   first Arabic recipes, c. 1280, call saltpetre "Chinese snow"; the first European guns appear in
   1326). But no document records the passage, no carrier is named, and Europeans did not call it
   Chinese — Francis Bacon in 1620 calls its origin "obscure and inglorious", and a later legend
   credited a German monk, Berthold the Black. Grade: most likely Mongol-era transfer, by the
   steppe or by the Islamic world; undocumented; Europeans plausibly unaware of the origin. State
   the alternative honestly: independent European discovery of saltpetre mixtures cannot be
   excluded, but no scholar the dossier reaches argues it. Then the European step: corned powder
   (16th c.), bell-founding turned to cannon (Landes l.1300–1304).
5. **Printing: most probably independent.** Problem: China printed from wood blocks by the 9th c.
   (the *Diamond Sutra*, 868), invented movable type c. 1040 (Bi Sheng's baked-clay type), and
   Korea cast metal type by the 13th c. (the *Jikji*, 1377); Gutenberg's Mainz press comes c. 1450.
   Obvious attempt: Korean metal type → Gutenberg. Why it is graded low: no document, traveller,
   tool or word links Mainz to Korea or China; Gutenberg's process differs at each step — type
   cast in a hand mould from punched matrices in a lead-tin-antimony alloy, oil-based ink, a screw
   press adapted from wine and oil presses, all fitted to an alphabet of a few dozen letters
   (Diamond l.137, as facts; Diamond's claim that "the idea of movable type" came from China is the
   claim being graded and is not repeated as fact). What did travel: paper (above), and probably
   block-printed images, playing cards and paper money that European travellers to Mongol China
   saw (block printing in Europe from c. 1400). Grade: Gutenberg most probably an independent
   invention, with possible stimulus from block printing; the strongest transmission case (Carter,
   Hobson — References) rests on contact and plausibility, not on a document. KEEPER: the four
   items with their four grades, as one table (figure 1).
   A `<Remark>` handles the compass (named in the opening): Chinese magnetic needle described by
   the 1080s–1110s, European descriptions c. 1190, near-simultaneous and no route attested;
   graded possible transfer or independent — the ledger leaves it open. One paragraph, no more.
6. **The pro-Western reply at full strength: borrowing into system.** Problem: if Europe borrowed
   paper and numerals for certain and powder probably, what was Western about the outcome? The
   reply (define `borrowing-into-system`): everyone borrowed — the Arabs borrowed paper and
   numerals, the Ottomans and Mughals cannon — and borrowing is no mark against anyone (Murray's
   inventory counts a borrowed "first" to its inventor, Ref); what is distinctive is turning a
   borrowed technique into a system that keeps producing new results without being re-imported.
   Three chains, each with its "because":
   (a) *Numerals → measurement and the calculus.* Indian numerals met Greek proof (Murray's two
   meta-inventions, Ref `murray-human-accomplishment`) in commercial Italy: abacus schools,
   double-entry book-keeping (Weber's sentence, quoted), then decimal fractions (Stevin, 1585),
   logarithms (Napier, 1614), algebraic notation, coordinate geometry (Descartes, 1637) and the
   calculus (Newton and Leibniz, 1660s–80s) — and with them proof from measurement: physics
   stated as equations tested against observation (Galileo). Because written place-value
   arithmetic made calculation a thing done on paper, checked and printed, it compounded.
   (b) *Powder → the military revolution.* Corned powder and cast cannon broke medieval walls;
   the answer was the angled bastion (*trace italienne*); bastions needed bigger infantry armies;
   armies needed taxes and credit — the fiscal-military state. Because Europe was fragmented
   (Ref `european-fragmentation`), no ruler could stop and none could afford to stop: every
   improvement was copied and answered, a competition that kept improving the weapon for three
   centuries.
   (c) *Print → the Reformation and the scientific journal.* Luther's theses of 1517 were
   reprinted across Germany within weeks; his pamphlets made him the most printed author of his
   century; the Church could not suppress what dozens of independent cities printed (Landes
   l.1290, Ref; Weber's "Press and periodicals", quoted). Print fixed texts so that errors could be
   corrected cumulatively, and in 1665 the first scientific journals (London's *Philosophical
   Transactions*, Paris's *Journal des sçavans*) turned the correspondence of savants into a
   public, citable, priority-establishing record. Because results were printed, dated and
   answerable, knowledge accumulated without depending on any one patron.
   Then Francis Bacon as the reply's own text: the aphorism on printing, gunpowder and the magnet
   (I.129) and his programme — a method for making inventions and an institution of inventors
   (Salomon's House) — the invention of invention proposed in so many words (Strauss–Cropsey
   frame, References). Answer: on this reply the West's distinctive invention was the system
   itself.
7. **The rival thesis's answer.** Stated as its authors would, never as a reproach, and in three
   parts, each tested against the ledger:
   (a) *Others built systems too.* The Ottomans, Safavids and Mughals reorganised armies and
   states around firearms — a standing paid infantry under the Ottomans from the 14th c., an
   imperial cannon foundry (Goody ch. 4, Ref `pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis`); Ming and Qing
   China kept military parity with Europe in East Asia until the 18th c. (Andrade). China's
   woodblock publishing was large and commercial in the Ming and Qing; the measure "it never
   exploded as in Europe" judges block printing by movable type's yardstick, and movable type was
   ill-suited to thousands of characters (Landes's own footnote, l.9287). Paper's mechanisation
   used water power, which China also applied to spinning and to bellows centuries earlier
   (Pomeranz ch. 1; Landes l.1340s on the hemp-spinning machine).
   (b) *Timing.* The systems the reply names became self-sustaining in output and income only
   in the 18th–19th centuries — which is the late divergence, and on Pomeranz's account it needed
   coal and the New World to escape the land constraint; before that, leads alternated (Goody's
   lags of one to seventeen centuries run the other way).
   (c) *The ledger's own symmetry.* The rival thesis applies the same grading to claims in its
   own favour: the Kerala school's infinite series for π and the sine (14th c.) anticipate parts of
   the calculus, and some argue Jesuits carried them to Europe — no document shows it; graded
   possible, most probably independent, exactly like Gutenberg. Both sides lose a claim to the
   same rule.
   Where each side concedes: the reply concedes that Europe originated none of the four techniques
   on this ledger except (probably) the press; the rival thesis concedes that Europe mechanised
   paper first and that print and powder were taken furthest, fastest, in Europe (Goody's own
   paper-export sentence). The lesson does not decide whether "systems" are the West's
   distinction; it states what would decide it — a comparison of when each society's version of
   a system became self-reinforcing, judged by the same measure.
8. **Application check (L16, the departures).** One short section reading the ledger against the
   module's idealised theses: priority is not transfer; absence of a document is not proof of
   independence; surviving records favour literate stations; "who invented" and "who made it
   matter" are two ledgers. Close by naming which module result each item tests (paper:
   invention of invention; numerals: Murray's meta-inventions and Weber's book-keeping; powder:
   fragmentation; print: fragmentation and the "Press" of Weber's list).

Headings as claims: "What would prove a borrowing"; "Paper: a chain of mills from Samarkand to
Fabriano"; "Numerals: secure, and two centuries in the waiting room"; "Gunpowder: a recipe
without a route"; "Printing: most probably invented twice"; "Borrowing into system: the
pro-Western reply"; "Others built systems too: the rival thesis's answer"; "What the ledger can
and cannot show".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The ledger table** (the lesson's artefact): four rows (paper, numerals, gunpowder, printing)
   × columns: origin and date; stations with earliest dated evidence; carriers named?; European
   development; grade. Built row by row in sections 2–5 and shown whole as figure 1.
2. **The Fabriano watermark / Landes's "halfway around the world"** (l.1202, n.2 l.9269): the
   mechanised mill and the watermark as the European addition — the first point where both
   theses touch the same object.
3. **The *Liber Abaci* opening** (Sigler tr., 1202) quoted in one sentence; the Florentine 1299
   prohibition beside it — a new tool resisted for fraud-prevention reasons, not ignorance.
4. **Bacon's firecracker and Bacon's aphorism** — Roger Bacon (1267) describing a children's toy
   that bursts with noise and flash, and Francis Bacon (1620) calling gunpowder's origin "obscure
   and inglorious": three and a half centuries in which Europeans used the powder without knowing
   whose it was. The "plausibly unaware" grade rests on these two documents and on the Berthold
   legend.
5. **Gutenberg's six components** (Diamond ch. 13 l.137) as a worked `<Example>` of process
   comparison: for each component (paper, type, metal, press, ink, script) say whether it came
   from the east, from Europe's own crafts, or is new — the comparison that grades the press.
6. **Weber's two sentences** (l.834–841, l.1077–1080) — "There was printing in China. But…" and
   the decimal system that "led to no modern arithmetic or book-keeping" in India — the systems
   reply in its first statement, 1920.
7. **The Ottoman counter-case** (Goody ch. 4 pp. 104–106; Landes l.1322): learned "the use (but
   not the making)" of cannon (Landes) vs the Imperial Foundry and the Janissaries (Goody) — one
   pair of short quotations, the reader asked which describes a "system".
8. **The Needham lag table** (Goody Table 5.1): paper 10 centuries, printing 4–6, gunpowder
   4–6 — the rival thesis's numbers, set against the ledger's own dates (do the lags agree with the
   stations?).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), at most three per section:** Mongol empire and the 1241 campaign;
  Abbasid caliphate and Baghdad; al-Andalus (Muslim Iberia); Talas River (751, in today's
  Kazakhstan–Kyrgyzstan border region, a battle between Abbasid and Tang armies); place value and
  zero; the abacus board (counters on lines — Europe's calculator before written arithmetic);
  saltpetre (potassium nitrate, the oxidiser in gunpowder); "corned" powder (wetted and grained so
  it burns fast and evenly); fire lance; woodblock vs movable type vs cast-metal type; hand mould
  and punch-and-matrix; incunabula (books printed before 1501); *trace italienne* (the angled
  bastion fortress); fiscal-military state; the Reformation (Ref `weber-only-in-the-west` for
  Luther — one clause); *Philosophical Transactions*; calculus (one plain sentence: the
  mathematics of rates of change and accumulation — no formulas).
- **The flat line is the target.** Never write "Europe borrowed paper, gunpowder and printing
  from China" or any sentence that gives the four one grade. Each item carries its grade at the
  point it is stated, in the body, in captions and in exercises.
- **Correct the dossier where it errs, without naming it in the body:** Diamond's "idea of
  movable type reached Europe from China" (ungraded assertion) and "Islamic treatise of about
  1100" with gunpowder recipes (not supported); Diamond's Talas story (later report); Murray's
  placing of the numerals in the algebra rather than the arithmetic; Goody's misquotation of
  Francis Bacon's three inventions (printing, not paper). The lesson states the correct facts; the
  brief records the corrections for the writer.
- **Roger Bacon's "recipe".** The course.yaml key idea says "first European recipes (Bacon,
  c. 1267)". The secure statement is "the first European description of gunpowder, in Roger
  Bacon's works of 1267; recipes in Latin by the end of the century (the *Liber Ignium*)". Recommend
  the course.yaml wording be read this way (do not edit course.yaml).
- **Dates:** keep to the decade except where the gap is the argument (1241 → 1267 → 1326 is the
  argument for gunpowder's grade; 1377 vs c. 1450 for print). Verify every station date against
  Bloom, Needham/Partington, Tsien, Ifrah before writing; this brief gives them from the standard
  accounts and flags them for checking.
- **No reproach either way.** The rival thesis is not "Europe stole"; the reply is not "the others
  wasted their gifts". Murray's own caution (l.565) that Western retellings give Chinese
  discoveries "the flavor of accidents" is the guard against the reply's careless form; Goody's
  "eurocentric about ethnocentricity" (Ref the previous lesson) is the guard against the rival
  thesis's careless form. No label for either side in the course's voice.
- **Do not re-teach** Landes's invention of invention, Weber's "only in the West", Murray's
  method, Pomeranz's coal and colonies, Goody's alternation: each a clause and a `<Ref>`.
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — the four grades; "1241 → 1267 → 1326" for powder; "868 / c. 1040 /
  1377 / c. 1450" for print (in the figure, not strung in prose); the lag table in a figure.
- **Where the dossier is thin:** the transfer chains themselves are not in the dossier except as
  summaries in Landes, Diamond, Goody and Murray; every station date comes from the public
  scholarship listed and must be checked there. The Kerala transmission claim is not in the
  dossier at all (Joseph; Plofker). Andrade's military-parity argument is not in the dossier.
- **No lab, no demo.** The ledger is a static table built in prose; a `<FigureSequence>` adds rows.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (the one warm-up):** from memory, give each of the four items its grade and the
   single strongest piece of evidence for that grade.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** Francis Bacon, *Novum Organum* I.129 (1620).
   Metadata (an English Lord Chancellor and natural philosopher, writing to launch a method of
   discovery); predict before reading what he will say about the inventions' origins; then: what
   the passage records (that educated Europeans of 1620 credited these inventions to no one), what
   it argues (that mechanical discoveries changed the world more than empires or sects), what it
   omits (where they came from); what it can be used to establish for the gunpowder grade and what
   it cannot.
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Gutenberg copied Korean metal type." Name the step that fails (no
   documented contact or carrier; a different process at each of the six components) and state
   what single document would change the grade.
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** take the grading rule to its edge — a technique with certain priority
   and a perfect timing match, but no document and no borrowed word (gunpowder). What grade does the
   rule give, why is that grade lower than paper's, and what would happen to the grade if a
   Latin text were found calling saltpetre "Chinese snow"?
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** apply the same rule to a claim in the rival thesis's
   favour — the Kerala infinite series and the calculus — and to one in the reply's favour —
   "the scientific journal had no precedent anywhere". Grade both, and state which earlier
   lesson's concept each grade bears on (`theft-of-history`; `accomplishment-inventory`).
6. **Corroboration (paired sources):** Landes ("the use (but not the making)" of cannon, l.1322)
   and Goody on the Ottoman Imperial Foundry and Janissaries (ch. 4 pp. 104–105). State the
   agreement, the disagreement, and which of the two counts as "borrowing into system" on the
   lesson's definition.
7. **Synthesis / argument:** in a claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph, decide whether the ledger
   supports the exceptionalist reply, the rival thesis, or neither, item by item; the model
   solution shows that the answer differs by item and by date.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`:** both theses in this module explain the
   West's distinction by culture, institutions or circumstance and leave the people's own
   capacities equal. State what kind of evidence an explanation by inherited differences would
   have to bring that the ledger cannot supply (a measure of the populations, not of their
   techniques), and say why China's early lead in three of the four techniques is a problem any
   such explanation must address. The solution says in one sentence that the next module opens
   with exactly that argument — and that its authors answer the China case with markets and
   institutions — with a `<Ref>`.

# Research brief: the-cognitive-elite (The cognitive elite: The Bell Curve's class argument as a test of Rawls and Lasch)

status: planned
module: equality-after-rawls
type: core
goal: Apply Rawls's natural lottery, Lasch's revolt of the elites and the module's account of envy to The Bell Curve's analysis of cognitive stratification in American data, and judge what the data show and where the argument departs from them

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The whole module. From `rawls-theory-of-justice`: the natural lottery — "no one deserves his greater natural capacity" — so that inequalities flowing from talent are just only if they benefit the least advantaged (concepts `natural-lottery`, `difference-principle`), and Rawls's §17 rejection of the "meritocratic society". From `ressentiment-and-envy`: envy and self-respect; Rawls's conditions for excusable envy and his claim that a well-ordered society divides into "noncomparing groups" that blunt it. From `scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`: the stratified society of many dignified walks of life, and the claim that equality of opportunity is neither possible nor desirable. From `macintyre-after-virtue`: the manager's claim to expertise as a moral fiction; desert as the concept modern justice lacks. From `lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`: the mobile, credentialed elite that has seceded from shared institutions; meritocracy as a parody of democracy; assortative mating; and the question the lesson ended on — what evidence would decide between Ortega and Lasch (concepts `revolt-of-the-elites`, `meritocracy-critique`). Earlier: Burke's natural aristocracy (`burke-reflections`), Carlyle's aristocracy of talent (`carlyle-heroes`), the elite theorists' ruling minority and circulation of elites (`mosca-ruling-class`, `michels-and-pareto-oligarchy-and-elites`), Aristotle's claim that some are fitted by nature to rule (`aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`).
- **Contributes:** The module's results cashed out on one real artefact: a 1994 book's measurement of whether America sorts itself by tested intelligence, and of how much tested intelligence predicts adult outcomes once parental background is held fixed. The lesson introduces what is measured — the general factor g and heritability, carefully — reads Parts I and II with their data, and shows that one finding feeds three opposite normative conclusions: Rawls's (talent is undeserved, so compensate), Lasch's (the able have seceded; merit is a legitimating story, so diffuse competence), and the authors' own (equal negative rights and "valued places", not redistribution). It then names where the data stop and the argument goes on without them. Concepts introduced: `g-factor`, `heritability`, `cognitive-stratification`.
- **Hands off:** (1) The book's chapters 13–14 on ethnic and racial differences are not read here; their argument is `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`, in a heredity module organised by the steps of that argument: what the tests measure and whether g is a real ability or a statistical summary (`what-intelligence-tests-measure`), what heritability within groups can and cannot imply about a difference between groups (`heritability-within-and-between-groups`), the US gap and the Flynn effect (`us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`), adoption, admixture and polygenic evidence (`adoption-studies-across-groups`, `admixture-and-polygenic-scores`), and the genetic lottery read as an argument for equality against Murray's opposite reading (`gould-and-harden-on-heredity`). (2) The credentialed aristocracy returns as Deneen's "liberalocracy" in `deneen-post-liberalism`. (3) The next module widens from the class inside one state to whole civilizations (`toynbee-challenge-and-response`).
- **Spine step:** "Rawls makes equality the measure of justice; the right answers with the charge of ressentiment and envy, Scruton's authority, MacIntyre's lost tradition and Lasch's revolt of the elites — and the data on cognitive stratification test both sides"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):**
  - *Artefact read:* R. J. Herrnstein & C. Murray, The Bell Curve: Intelligence and Class Structure in American Life (1994) — Introduction (g, the six conclusions), Part I (chs. 1–4: college, occupation, the economy, heritability and assortative mating), Part II (introduction and chs. 5–12, the NLSY analyses of white Americans; ch. 5 on poverty worked in full), ch. 21 (the "invisible migration", the "secession of the successful", the custodial state), ch. 22 (equality and "a place for everyone"), the 1995 Afterword (the "Rawlsian" expectation; the statistical replies), Appendices 2–4 (the AFQT, the SES index, the regressions).
  - *Module results it uses:* `natural-lottery` and `difference-principle` (Rawls §17, the meritocratic society), `excusable-envy` and Rawls's noncomparing groups (`ressentiment-and-envy`), `revolt-of-the-elites` and `meritocracy-critique` (Lasch; the book's ch. 21 cites the same "secession of the successful"), `natural-aristocracy` (Burke; the authors' ch. 22 invokes Jefferson's "natural aristocracy"), Scruton's stratified society, MacIntyre's missing desert.
  - *Departures from the idealisation the lesson must name:* the test is the AFQT, a test that rises with schooling (by the authors' own estimate about 3 centiles per year of education), taken at ages 15–23 by people with different amounts of schooling; parental background enters as a four-item SES index measured with error and with income missing for part of the sample, so "holding SES constant" holds only the index constant; outcomes are analysed for non-Latino whites only; IQ usually accounts for under 10 per cent of the variation among individuals, so the findings are about group rates, not persons; several Part I claims rest on estimates the authors themselves hedge (occupational IQs "with a grain of salt"; no direct data that assortative mating by intelligence had increased); the causal order between test score, schooling and background is in part, by the authors' own admission, unresolvable; the policy chapters (17–22) are a separate normative argument that the data neither entail nor exclude.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): in 1994 two authors claimed America was sorting itself by intelligence — the ablest to a handful of colleges, marrying one another, working in a few professions, living in a few places, the rest of the country left with fewer of its abler members; a survey that followed twelve thousand young Americans since 1979; if right, Rawls's natural lottery and Lasch's secession of the successful describe the same thing; the lesson reads the class argument and its data, leaving the chapters on race for a later module.

1. **What would have to be measured.** Problem: the claim "a society sorts itself by intelligence" is empty until intelligence is measured, on whom, and against what. Three things the reader needs before any number, in this order. (a) The survey: the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth, begun in 1979 with 12,686 Americans then aged 14 to 22, re-interviewed every year; in 1980 the Defense Department, renorming its enlistment tests, gave its battery to almost all of them — so the survey holds a test score, a family background and fifteen years of adult life for the same people. (b) The test: the Armed Forces Qualification Test (AFQT), a paper test of word knowledge, reading, arithmetic reasoning and mathematics, reported on the IQ scale — mean 100, and a standard deviation of 15. Define standard deviation for this reader by its use: on a bell-shaped distribution about two-thirds of people fall within one SD of the mean, about 95 per cent within two; an IQ of 115 is higher than about 84 per cent of the population, 130 higher than about 98 per cent. (c) The groups: the authors cut the distribution into five "cognitive classes" at the 5th, 25th, 75th and 95th percentiles. Bearing: every later number in the lesson is a rate within one of these five groups, or a prediction from a score on this one test.
2. **What the test is supposed to measure: g.** Problem: a test of vocabulary and arithmetic is not obviously a test of "intelligence". Attempt: intelligence is many separate abilities, and a test measures whichever it happens to contain. The finding that complicates this (Spearman, 1904): people who do well on one kind of mental test tend to do well on every other kind, however different the content — the correlations among mental tests are almost all positive. Spearman's explanation: the tests share a common factor, which he called g ("general intelligence"); his method of extracting it, factor analysis, finds how much of each test's variation is shared with the others. Concept `g-factor`: the common factor extracted from the positive correlations among scores on different mental tests. Then, immediately, the status of the claim, in the authors' own sentence: the evidence for g was "pervasive but circumstantial … Its reality therefore was, and remains, arguable". State the two readings at full strength in a sentence each: g as a real capacity (the authors' classicist position; the AFQT's high correlation with other IQ tests and with job performance) and g as a statistical summary that does not show there is one thing in the head (the objection the heredity module develops — `<Ref>` forward to `what-intelligence-tests-measure`). The lesson needs only this: the AFQT ranks people consistently with other mental tests, and that ranking predicts things; what it is a ranking OF is contested.
3. **Heritability: what it does and does not mean.** Problem: the opening's claim that the able marry one another and pass on their advantage depends on whether test scores run in families, and why. Define concept `heritability` from the authors' own text: the share of the variation of a trait within a given population, in given environments, that is statistically associated with genetic variation — a ratio between 0 and 1. The authors' figure: .6, give or take .2. Three things it does NOT mean, each with its reason: (a) not a fact about a person — "It makes no more sense to talk about the heritability of an individual's IQ than it does to talk about his birthrate"; (b) not fixed — it changes when the conditions producing variation change; (c) not unchangeable — a highly heritable trait can be shifted by a change of environment that affects everyone (state as the course's own; the heredity module develops it). And a fourth, held for later, which the lesson must state and not argue: heritability within a group says nothing by itself about the cause of a difference between groups (`heritability-within-and-between-groups`, forward). Then the worked `<Example>`, which IS the authors' argument: if every child had exactly the same environment, how much variation would remain? Variance is the SD squared: 15² = 225. If heritability is .6, the genetic share is 0.6 × 225 = 135; equalise environments and only that remains; √135 ≈ 11.6. So perfect equality of environment would shrink the spread of scores by about a quarter, not abolish it. And the corollary the authors call "the central irony of egalitarianism": the more equal the environments, the larger the share of what remains that is genetic — heritability RISES as opportunity is equalised. Bearing: Rawls's fair equality of opportunity, fully achieved, would leave the natural lottery as the main remaining source of inequality (`rawls-theory-of-justice`, `<Ref>`).
4. **Part I: the cognitive elite.** Problem: is there evidence that sorting by test score increased? The authors' four channels, each with its evidence and its strength. (a) College: through the twentieth century the chance that a student in the top quarter of ability went to college rose steeply, with the largest change in the 1950s; at Harvard the mean verbal SAT of entering freshmen went from 583 in 1952 to 678 by 1960 — a scale on which 500 was then about average for test-takers (frame in the sentence). (b) Occupation: twelve "high-IQ professions" (law, medicine, engineering, science, university teaching, accounting, architecture, dentistry, management at the top) — the share of the top tenth of ability working in them rose from fewer than one in twelve in 1940 to about one in four or five by 1990; the authors warn that the occupational IQs behind this are estimates, "to be taken with a grain of salt". (c) Marriage: likes marry likes; as the able are concentrated in the same colleges and offices, they marry each other more. The Harvard–Radcliffe calculation (children of the class of 1930 expected at about IQ 114, of 1964 at about 124) is a model with a stipulated propensity, not a measurement; the authors themselves say no study shows assortative mating by intelligence increasing, and lean on rising marriage within the same education level. (d) Place and work: the workplace tour — a factory mixes every level of ability; a law firm does not. Thesis, in their sentence: "Social class remains the vehicle of social life, but intelligence now pulls the train" — and their observation that earlier societies had cognitive screening without cognitive classes, because the clever were scattered among farmers and smiths, or absorbed into aristocracies that lost them to regression to the mean and primogeniture. Concept `cognitive-stratification`. Bearing on Lasch: this is his secession, described with numbers (`lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`, `<Ref>`).
5. **Part II: what test scores predict, holding background fixed.** Problem: if high scorers do better, perhaps it is only because they come from better-off homes. The standard analysis, derived from that objection: for each adult outcome, estimate the probability from the AFQT score and from parental socioeconomic status (SES: an index of the parents' education, income and occupational prestige) together, so that each is "held constant" while the other varies; rerun among those who stopped at high school and among college graduates; non-Latino whites only, so that race cannot be the hidden cause. Work poverty in full as the `<Example>`: a white person born in 1961 whose parents were at the 2nd percentile of SES but who had an IQ of 100 had about an 11 per cent chance of being poor in 1989; one whose parents were of average SES but who scored at the 2nd percentile had about 26 per cent — hence the authors' "better to be born smart than rich". State in the same paragraph their own qualification: the starting line "remains unequal … even among whites", and IQ almost always explains less than a tenth of the variation between individuals, "you cannot predict what a given person will do from his IQ score". The other outcomes (leaving school, unemployment, births outside marriage, welfare, crime) go in one figure as rates by cognitive class; the prose says what they share — a steep rise in every rate from the top class to the bottom, with the SES gradient flatter. Bearing: if this holds, the natural lottery matters for life chances at least as much as the social one.
6. **One finding, three conclusions.** Problem: suppose the findings of links 4–5 are granted. What follows? Three answers, each from a lesson the reader has, each at full strength. (a) **Rawls** (`rawls-theory-of-justice`): talent is undeserved and now decides more; the natural distribution "is neither just nor unjust … What is just and unjust is the way that institutions deal with these facts" — so the more test scores govern outcomes, the stronger the case for arranging institutions so that the able gain only in ways that benefit the least advantaged. The authors saw this. Their ch. 22 opens with "the twin realities that people differ in intelligence for reasons that are not their fault, and that intelligence has a powerful bearing on how well people do in life", grants "economic pragmatism about how to produce compensating benefits for the least advantaged" in phrasing their own note traces to Rawls, and Murray's 1995 Afterword says both authors expected the book to point toward "a Rawlsian egalitarian state". (b) **Lasch** (`lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`): the same sorting is the secession of a class without obligation; the authors' ch. 21 describes it in Lasch's very terms — segregated and "socialized" in the same schools and suburbs, the "secession of the successful", gated communities and private security — and fears it. But the two part on the cause: for Lasch "merit" is the story that legitimates the elite; for Herrnstein and Murray it is a real, measured ability the economy increasingly pays for — "Would we prefer less of a meritocracy? Put that way, no". (c) **The authors' own**: neither redistribution nor Lasch's diffusion, but "an older intellectual tradition" — equal rights among people unequal in ability (they cite Hobbes and Locke, and Jefferson's "natural aristocracy" — recall Burke's, `burke-reflections`), and a society in which everyone can find "a valued place": "You occupy a valued place if other people would miss you if you were gone" — functions returned to neighbourhoods, simpler rules, marriage restored as the institution through which ordinary people find such a place; "Some sort of redistribution is here to stay", in the form of income supplements. Set beside Scruton's stratified society of many dignified walks of life (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`, one clause) — the closest earlier position. Bearing: the data do not choose among (a), (b) and (c); each adds a premise the data do not supply (what is owed for undeserved advantage; what a democracy needs from its citizens; what makes a life dignified).
7. **Envy and self-respect in a sorted society.** Problem: the module asked whether the demand for equality is envy (`ressentiment-and-envy`). What does cognitive sorting do to envy? Two opposite effects, both drawn from the reader's lessons. Rawls said a well-ordered society divides into "noncomparing groups" that reduce the "painful visibility" of differences — and cognitive stratification produces exactly such separation: the elite no longer lives beside the rest. But the 1958 satire Lasch quoted says that a society which sorts by ability leaves "the inferior man" with "no ready buttress for his self-regard": he can no longer blame birth or luck. If outcomes track a test the society calls merit, low status looks deserved — the condition under which, on the envy theorists' account, resentment is hardest to bear and on Rawls's account envy becomes excusable (no self-respect left to the loser). The authors' "valued place" is their answer to the same problem: dignity not from rank but from being needed. Bearing: the class argument turns the module's envy debate from a dispute about motives into a dispute about institutions — what a sorted society must provide so that the unsorted keep their self-respect.
8. **Where the argument departs from the data.** The application's audit, each departure as a problem with its "because". (a) **The test is not schooling-free.** The authors' own appendix estimates that each additional year of education raises the AFQT by about 3 percentiles (0.11 SD); the NLSY members took the test at ages 15–23, with different amounts of schooling already behind them, and schooling depends partly on family background. So part of what Part II calls "IQ" is accumulated schooling, and part of that is background — which tilts the IQ-versus-SES comparison toward IQ. The authors' reply: earlier childhood IQ scores predict outcomes as well, and the education subsamples bound the effect. (b) **"Holding SES constant" holds an index constant.** Background enters as four reported items (parents' education, income, occupation); income was missing for part of the sample; anything about a family the index omits — the school, the neighbourhood, the father's presence, the parents' own abilities and habits — is not held constant, and a background measured with error loses explanatory weight to a variable measured well. The authors' reply (Afterword): they tried alternative constructions and the result did not move; and the unmeasured family variables, once measured, would further reduce the role of SES, not increase it. Both the objection and the reply are stated at full strength; the reader judges. (c) **Group rates, not persons.** IQ typically accounts for under a tenth of the variation between individuals; the large differences are between the rates of the five classes. The R² quarrel (critics said the low R² shows IQ explains little; Murray conceded the R² in logistic regression was misused but said that understates the relationships) is reported in one sentence each. (d) **Direction of cause.** The test score correlates with family background (about .55); the authors list three readings (the test is biased toward advantaged homes; advantaged homes raise scores; able parents pass on ability and status) and say the arrow cannot be fully settled with these data. (e) **Part I's evidence is partly estimated** (occupational IQs; assortative mating inferred from education). (f) **Whites only** in Part II, by design; what holds for other groups is a separate question the book takes up in chapters held for the heredity module. (g) **From fact to policy.** Chapters 17–22 argue normatively; the same data appear in Rawls's argument for compensation, Lasch's for diffusion, and the authors' for valued places — a direct demonstration that no "ought" follows from these "is"s alone (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`, `macintyre-after-virtue`: one clause, no re-teaching). What the data DO show, stated plainly: in this national sample of white Americans, a test score taken in youth predicts the rates of several adult outcomes more steeply than the parental-background index does, and the sorting of high scorers into colleges and professions rose across the mid-century. Close: the reader can now read a dataset against three political theories and say which steps are data and which are premises; the book's chapters on ethnic differences, and the question whether heritability within a group can explain gaps between groups, are taken up in the heredity module. Mention the reception in one sentence, as a fact: the book was among the most attacked works of social science of its decade; Murray's Afterword opens by quoting a colleague at his own institute: "Never … has such a moderate book attracted such an immoderate response" — a quotation, attributed as such, not a verdict of the course.

Headings as claims (suggested): "A survey, a test and five classes"; "The general factor and its contested status"; "What heritability does and does not mean"; "Intelligence pulls the train: the evidence of Part I"; "Born smart or born rich: the evidence of Part II"; "One finding, three conclusions"; "Envy and self-respect in a sorted society"; "Where the argument outruns the data".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Register rule.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **Balance (course rule for contested empirical claims).** The class argument is taught as an argument with its data and the evidence against it — neither endorsed nor dismissed. Every objection at link 8 is followed by the authors' reply where they gave one; every claim of the authors is followed by its strongest objection. Before handing in, the writer rereads for prejudging words in the course's voice ("debunked", "pseudo-", "notorious", "brave", "obviously", "merely", scare quotes round "IQ" or "merit") and removes them.
- **The race chapters are held.** Chapters 13–14 (and the racial paragraphs of chs. 15, 19–21) are `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`'s. The lesson says in one sentence that the book has them and where they are read; it quotes no group means, does not summarise their argument, and does not let the custodial-state forecast (ch. 21) bring them in. Part II is white-only by the authors' design — say why (so that race cannot be the hidden variable), not as a criticism.
- **Heritability misreadings to refute (L13 — documented and prevalent):** (1) "60% heritable means 60% of my IQ comes from my genes" — false; heritability is a population statistic (authors' birthrate analogy). (2) "Heritable means unchangeable" — false; the authors' own caveat (conditions change heritability) plus the course's statement that a trait shared by everyone can shift with a common environment. (3) "Heritable within a group means genetic between groups" — false as an inference; held for the heredity module but must be named here so the reader does not draw it. Use the refutation schema once, at link 3.
- **g is contested, not settled.** Do not write "intelligence is g" in the course's voice. "The AFQT measures g" is the authors' claim; "the AFQT ranks people consistently with other mental tests and predicts outcomes" is the part the lesson can state as fact.
- **Do not confuse the Murrays.** Charles Murray (this book; later Human Accomplishment, `murray-human-accomplishment`) is not Douglas Murray (`murray-strange-death-of-europe`). If Human Accomplishment is mentioned forward, say "the same author".
- **Dates and authorship.** Published in autumn 1994; Herrnstein died in September 1994, as it appeared; the 1995 Afterword is Murray's alone and is a reply to critics — treat it as a later document with its own purpose (Exercise 6).
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** IQ (a score scaled so the population mean is 100 and SD 15); standard deviation (link 1); percentile/centile (the book uses "centile"; use one word); correlation (how closely two measures rise together, from −1 to +1; .55 means strongly but far from perfectly); regression and "holding constant" (link 5, by what it does, not by formula); logistic regression (only as "a method for estimating the probability of a yes/no outcome"); AFQT, ASVAB (the Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery, the test battery the AFQT is drawn from); NLSY; SES; SAT (the college entrance test; its 1950s scale); "high-IQ professions"; assortative mating (recall from `lasch-revolt-of-the-elites`); regression to the mean (children of very able parents tend to be closer to the average than their parents — one clause at link 4); primogeniture (inheritance by the eldest son — recall if met; else one clause); Griggs v. Duke Power (1971 US Supreme Court ruling restricting employment tests with disparate effects — only if ch. 3 is mentioned).
- **Numbers (L12):** keeper numbers — the 12,686 sample (framed: ages 14–22 in 1979, followed into their thirties); heritability .6 ±.2 and the 15 → 11.6 calculation; the 11% vs 26% poverty comparison; the "under a tenth of individual variation" qualifier. Everything else in figures. Do not quote the book's spurious-precision numbers (e.g. 18.37%) in prose.
- **What NOT to do.** Do not re-teach Rawls, Lasch, envy, Scruton: `<Ref>` plus a clause. Do not narrate chs. 6–12 one by one. Do not use the policy chapters' proposals on immigration, affirmative action or welfare (chs. 19–20, 22) beyond the "valued place" frame — they are separate arguments and bring in the held material. Do not present the critics' objections as decisive or the authors' replies as decisive.
- **L8a:** body names Herrnstein, Murray, Rawls, Lasch, Spearman (the method's name); the NLSY and AFQT as named objects. Critics (Gould, Heckman, Goldberger, Kamin) and the 1958 satire's author in References; their objections as the course's own.
- **Copyright.** In copyright: short quotations; numbers redrawn as our own figures with the source credited in the caption's References entry, never a scan of the book's charts.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Warm-up computation (unfamiliar, and the authors' own argument):** If the heritability of a test score in a population is .4, .6 or .8 and its SD is 15, what would the SD be if every child's environment were made identical? (√(0.4×225) ≈ 9.5; √135 ≈ 11.6; √180 ≈ 13.4.) Then say in one sentence what the three answers mean for the hope that equal opportunity removes inequality of measured ability, and why the answer depends on a number the authors give only as a range.
2. **Claim to refute:** "The Bell Curve shows that 60 per cent of each person's intelligence is inherited, so nothing can change it." Name the two steps that fail (heritability is a population ratio, not a share of a person — the authors' birthrate analogy; heritability changes with conditions and does not measure changeability), and say what the authors themselves concede about individual prediction ("you cannot predict what a given person will do from his IQ score").
3. **Boundary probe:** Suppose Rawls's fair equality of opportunity were achieved perfectly: every child the same schooling, health and home. Using the "central irony of egalitarianism", say what happens to heritability and to the share of inequality that is due to the natural lottery, and whether this strengthens or weakens the case for Rawls's difference principle. (Solution: heritability rises toward 1; the remaining inequality is almost all natural lottery; on Rawls's own premise — the natural distribution is neither just nor unjust, institutions must deal with it — the case for the difference principle is strengthened, which is why the authors expected their book to be read as Rawlsian.)
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped (data reasoning):** The comparison "11% vs 26%" holds parental SES at the 2nd percentile in one case and the test score at the 2nd percentile in the other. Using the path diagram, show how each of two departures — schooling raising the test score; family advantages missing from the SES index — would change the comparison if corrected, and in which direction. State what result would leave "better to be born smart than rich" standing.
5. **Research-shaped task:** Design a test that would separate "the AFQT mostly measures accumulated schooling" from "schooling mostly reflects prior ability". Use features the NLSY offers (members tested at different ages and grades in the same year; earlier school IQ scores for some members) or a natural experiment (children whose birthdays put them a school year ahead or behind at the same age); say what pattern of results would favour each reading. (The authors' Appendix 3 uses the first approach; the solution may cite its 3.2-centile estimate.)
6. **Sourcing drill:** The 1995 Afterword's paragraph on the "Rawlsian egalitarian state" (l. 5236). Metadata first: who (Murray alone, after Herrnstein's death), when (a year after publication), for whom (readers of the controversy; first printed in a magazine), purpose (reply to critics). Prediction: what would such a document claim about the book's politics? Records / argues / omits: what can the passage be USED to establish (that the authors saw the egalitarian reading and rejected it for ch. 22's reasons) and what can it not (what the authors expected before the reception, as distinct from what one of them said about it afterwards)?
7. **Synthesis (claim–evidence–reasoning), spanning the module:** Rawls, Lasch and Herrnstein & Murray all agree that a society sorted by measured ability poses a problem of justice or of citizenship. Argue which of the three conclusions follows best from the Part I–II data, then show that none follows from the data alone by naming the premise each adds — and judge, with one piece of evidence, which premise the reader finds best supported.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `heritability-within-and-between-groups`:** The authors estimate the heritability of test scores at about .6 within the white NLSY sample. Suppose two groups differ in their average score. Does a within-group heritability of .6 tell you how much of the between-group difference is genetic? Work it with the authors' own image (ch. 13, l. 3134, where they call the point "so basic, and so commonly misunderstood"): two handfuls of genetically identical seed corn, one planted in Iowa and one in the Mojave Desert — plant height is a heritable trait, yet the whole difference between the two plots is environmental. (The lesson may quote this one passage of ch. 13: it is the authors' statement of a limit, not their argument about groups.) The solution states that within-group heritability does not by itself settle the cause of a between-group difference, and says that the heredity module lays out the premise the step needs and what further evidence would supply it (`<Ref>` to `heritability-within-and-between-groups`), before reading the authors' own case (`hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`).

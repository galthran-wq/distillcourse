# Research brief: the-nation-and-its-defenders (The nation and its defenders: Scruton, Miller and the pre-political loyalty)

status: planned
module: nation-and-immigration
type: core
goal: Analyze the case that a nation is a pre-political loyalty to a place and a way of life, held in different forms by a conservative and a liberal nationalist, and distinguish it from nationalism

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The reader arrives from three directions. (1) The nineteenth-century answers to "what is a nation?": Renan's `daily-plebiscite` and `shared-forgetting` (`renan-what-is-a-nation` — the nation as consent renewed, not race or language), Barrès's `la-terre-et-les-morts` (`barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism` — the nation accepted, not chosen), Herder's and Fichte's `volk` and `language-as-nation` (`herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation`), and Ortega's re-reading of the plebiscite as a vote on a common future (`ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`, `nation-as-project`). (2) The conservative bond: from `scruton-meaning-of-conservatism` (now read after Rawls, in `equality-after-rawls`) they have `allegiance` (the bond to family and nation that precedes choice), `authority-before-freedom` and the critique of the social contract ("Which people?" was not yet asked there — it is asked here); from `burke-reflections` the `little-platoon` and the `partnership-of-generations`; from `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township` the `mores` that sustain a free people; from `huntington-clash-of-civilizations` the `civilizational-fault-line` and the idea that identity scales with the observer. (3) The liberal equipment: they have read Rawls himself — the `original-position`, the veil, the `natural-lottery` (`rawls-theory-of-justice`) — so a liberal who accepts national partiality is a surprise they can measure. From the empire module they have just finished (`auditing-the-case-for-colonialism`) they bring the sourcing drill and the habit of reading a contested claim as an argument with evidence.
- **Contributes:** One move: that a bounded "we" must exist before politics can begin — before anyone can vote, dissent or compromise — and that this premise is held by a conservative (Scruton) and by a liberal of the left (Miller) alike. The lesson builds the shared premise from the coordination problem up, sets it against the two nineteenth-century answers the reader already holds (Renan's chosen nation, Barrès's inherited one — Scruton's unchosen home sits nearer Barrès but keeps Renan's openness to newcomers), then locates exactly where Scruton and Miller part: what the bond is made of (territory, custom, settled home vs. a national identity that is one strand beside economic cooperation and citizenship) and what follows for who may join.
- **Hands off:** The premise is stated but not yet tested on a real people. The next lesson (`federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`) takes the first case: in 1787 John Jay claims Americans are already "one united people" — same ancestors, language, religion and principles of government — while Madison designs a republic that needs no such unity to control faction. Which of Jay's bonds is Scruton's and which Miller's is the question the reader carries in. The fork this lesson locates — culture or institutions — is the axis of `albions-seed-and-who-are-we`, where Huntington's Anglo-Protestant core culture and the American Creed are read in full; the open-borders denial of the premise (`carens-open-borders`), Miller's own controlled-borders case (`miller-strangers-in-our-midst`) and Murray's charge that the "we" no longer exists (`murray-strange-death-of-europe`) come later in the module. The lesson must leave the reader able to state the premise so precisely that each of these lands on something.
- **Spine step:** "The live question: what a nation is and who may join it — Scruton and Miller on the pre-political loyalty, America as England's heir from the Federalist to Who Are We?, the prophecies of displacement from Powell to Camus, Carens and Miller on borders, Murray on Europe — and 2015 as the test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: before anyone can disagree about politics they have to agree on who "we" are — who votes, whose law applies, whose children inherit. The newcomer meets this as a puzzle about democracy itself: a vote presupposes an electorate, a law presupposes a jurisdiction, and neither can be voted into existence by the people it defines.

1. **Why can't the "we" be chosen?** The obvious attempt is the social contract the reader knows from `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` and `locke-property-and-consent`: the people agree, and the agreement makes them a people. It fails on its own first word — "We, the people" (Scruton ch. 2): who is party to the contract, and who decided that? A contract can only be made by people who already recognise each other as the ones entitled to make it. And it cannot bind the dead or the unborn (Burke, `<Ref burke-reflections>`), though they bequeathed the institutions and will inherit the debts. So the answer: membership is prior to agreement — a *pre-political* fact. Because: consent needs a pre-existing "who consents". This is the shared premise; both thinkers will be shown to hold it.
2. **What does a pre-political "we" do?** Attempt: perhaps it is just a feeling, politically inert. Scruton ch. 3: it is what makes opposition possible. Disagreement, dissent and compromise all presuppose that the losers stay — that they accept being governed by people they dislike — and they stay only if they identify with something the winners share. Because: without a shared identity, every lost vote is secession or civil war (Lebanon as his case; `<Ref scruton-meaning-of-conservatism>` for patriotism as the obstacle to civil war). Hence "democracy needs boundaries, and boundaries need the nation state."
3. **Why a nation and not a religion or a race?** Attempt: any shared identity would do. Scruton: a religious "we" binds believers across borders and does not accept that law can be made by men; it endures where secular law adapts; so it does not sit with democratic politics. A racial "we" cannot be joined. The nation — territory, a shared history and customs, national myths, secular law — is the one form of first-person plural that is bounded, adaptable and open to newcomers (ch. 3; ch. 7 on the civic culture that frees membership from religion, race and kin). Because: the bond must be something a stranger can acquire by settling, yet something that makes a *this* rather than an everywhere. This is where **oikophilia** enters, as the answer to "what is the bond made of": love of a home one did not choose, the settled condition that produces a "we" of place.
4. **Chosen or inherited?** The reader already holds two nineteenth-century answers and must see where Scruton's sits between them. Renan's: a nation is "a daily plebiscite", consent renewed (`<Ref renan-what-is-a-nation>`; Ortega made it a vote on a common future, `<Ref ortega-revolt-of-the-masses>`). Barrès's: the nation is the earth and the dead, accepted, not chosen (`<Ref barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism>`). Attempt: Scruton's unchosen home is simply Barrès again. It is not, and the difference is the lesson's: Scruton's home is unchosen by those born into it but *joinable* by settling — the civic culture "has proved permeable" (ch. 7) — whereas Barrès's determinism cannot be acquired. And against Renan: a plebiscite presupposes an electorate, so the daily vote already assumes the "together" it is said to create (link 1 again). Because: the unchosen bond does the work Renan's consent cannot (it fixes who votes), and the permeable bond avoids the closure Barrès's cannot. One paragraph per thinker; each is a Ref and a clause, not a re-teaching.
5. **Is this just nationalism?** The seam must say so: from a conservative defending the nation one expects nationalism; the material refuses it. Nationalism (Sieyès's nation as a single will; the ideology of superiority) treats the nation as an actor above its members; the ordinary nation is the frame within which members disagree (`<Ref scruton-meaning-of-conservatism>`: patriotism vs nationalism). The test of the distinction is Scruton's own concession that national myths are partly made (set piece 4).
6. **Can a liberal hold the same premise?** Here the second thinker enters and the register changes from home to duty. Miller's airport queue: two lines, one for citizens, one for everyone else. The obvious liberal attempt — *strong* cosmopolitanism, identical duties to every human being whatever the relationship — makes the two lines indefensible. Miller shows it is implausible for anyone: the hiker with water owes the dehydrated stranger consideration, not identical treatment with his own child. *Weak* cosmopolitanism — weigh everyone's interests, give reasons for unequal weight — is compatible with strong compatriot partiality. Because: relationships carry obligations (associative obligations) that are not chosen and not universal; to deny them is to deny that friendship or parenthood ground duties.
7. **What makes compatriots a relationship?** Three strands: a cooperative economic scheme, shared citizenship, shared nationality. Attempt: the first two suffice (distributive justice and collective freedom follow from cooperation and citizenship alone). Miller's secession test: what does nationality add that Scandinavia has and a mere customs union lacks? Solidarity across strangers, extension across generations, attachment to a homeland — the things that let a welfare state ask the rich to pay for the poor they will never meet. Evidence leg: diversity and trust (ch. 1 pp. 9–11, ch. 8 pp. 144–145), stated with the OECD conditional and Miller's own caution that the effect is not inevitable. Because: trust is what lets a people accept losing votes and paying taxes; it tracks identification.
8. **Where do they part?** Both: a bounded people, prior to politics, with a right of self-determination. Scruton's bond is a *home* — territory, custom, the inherited settlement; the bond is thick, and exclusion is its law ("to include is to exclude"). Miller's bond is a *national identity* that is one strand among three, defined by a public culture that can be thin, and bounded by human rights owed to everyone; compatriot partiality is permitted, not mandated, and it is answerable to weak cosmopolitanism's demand for reasons. The practical fork: Scruton asks newcomers to join a home (culture); Miller asks them to join a scheme of cooperation whose public culture they help revise (institutions). The practical fork has an American name the reader will meet in two lessons: whether a people is held by a settler culture or by a creed anyone can adopt (Huntington's Anglo-Protestant core against the American Creed, `albions-seed-and-who-are-we` — one sentence, held forward). Mark the **strongest objection**, which enters here from the left-liberal side (the position Carens will hold later in the module): if the "we" is prior to choice, then the ones outside it never chose their exclusion either; a premise about *our* unchosen bonds says nothing yet about what we owe *them*. Scruton's reply (ch. 7): the civic culture is permeable — membership can be acquired. Miller's reply: weak cosmopolitanism — exclusion must be reasoned, and human rights bind. The lesson concedes that neither reply has yet said *why* a people may decide who joins; that is the next two lessons' work.

Headings fall out as claims: "A contract needs a 'we' before it can be signed"; "Shared identity is what lets the losers stay"; "Why the bond is a nation and not a church or a race"; "Unchosen but joinable: between the plebiscite and the earth and the dead"; "Patriotism is not nationalism"; "The airport queue: two lines a liberal can defend"; "Three strands make compatriots"; "Where home and identity part".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **"We, the people" — which people?** (Scruton ch. 2, l. ~886). Work the preamble of a constitution as a logic problem: the sentence that founds the polity names the people it founds. Show the circularity in two sentences and the way out (a pre-contractual plural). Short quotation of Scruton's phrase "presupposes a first-person plural".
2. **The coordination problem to the home** (ch. 2, l. 947–975). Reconstruct Scruton's chain: strangers must cooperate → cooperation needs trust → trust needs a shared "we" → the settled "we" is a place and its ways → oikophilia. Quote only "People settle by acquiring a first-person plural – a place, a community and a…" and gloss the rest.
3. **The Lebanon lesson** (ch. 1 gems; ch. 3): boundaries arise from national identities, not map lines; a confessional "we" endangers democracy because the loser of a vote has another loyalty to retreat to. One paragraph, anchored (Lebanon's civil war 1975–90, a state of confessional communities — Maronite, Sunni, Shia, Druze — sharing offices by sect).
4. **Sieyès and the three national myths** (ch. 3, l. 1168–1203, ~1397). Sieyès's *What Is the Third Estate?* (Paris, January 1789: the pamphlet that declared the commoners *the* nation) as the birth of nationalism-as-ideology; against it, the ordinary nation's myths — glory, sacrifice, emancipation — Scott of the Antarctic (1912; the 1948 film) and the 2012 London Olympics opening ceremony, which Scruton calls, with Plato, noble lies (`<Ref kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king>` for the `noble-lie`). Make the point that a conservative is here *conceding* that national identity is partly made.
5. **The airport queue and the hiker** (Miller ch. 2 pp. 20–24, text l. 778, 887–922). Open the Miller half with the two lines. Then the hiker thought experiments, told in sequence: (i) you have water, a dehydrated stranger appears — you must give some (weak cosmopolitan floor); (ii) two stranded travellers, one your friend — partiality is allowed above the floor; (iii) the stranger then asks for your book — you may refuse but must consider the request (p. 37, with Cicero's "light from the fire" — keep Cicero as a one-clause `<Ref cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law>`).
6. **The secession test** (ch. 2 p. 28): take two strands away and see what is left. A customs union has economic cooperation; the EU has a kind of citizenship; neither has produced the solidarity of a Scandinavian welfare state. What nationality adds is the thing the test isolates. Pair with the OECD finding (ch. 1 pp. 10–11) bound as Miller binds it: a conditional estimate, not a law.
7. **"To include is to exclude"** (ch. 10 l. 4388–4389): quote the one sentence and show the tension between liberty and equality it names — every association that confers advantage on members withholds it from non-members. This is the sentence the open-borders lesson later in the module turns around (Carens: the nation-state is an association whose exclusion confers life chances by birth).
8. **The identity ladder** (Huntington 1993, `<Ref huntington-clash-of-civilizations>`): the Ibo in Lagos, London and New York — identity scales with the observer; used to show that "we" has levels, and that both Scruton and Miller choose the *national* level deliberately, against the civilizational (Huntington) and the global (cosmopolitan).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first mention** (L11): *oikophilia* (Greek *oikos*, household/home; Scruton's coinage, explain at the first use and once); *first-person plural* (the grammatical "we" used as a political term — say so); *pre-political* (existing before and independently of the institutions that govern); *cosmopolitanism* (and the three senses — identity, political, moral — but the lesson needs only moral, strong vs weak); *associative obligations* (define by example before the term); *Sieyès* (Abbé Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès, pamphleteer of 1789); *Lebanon* (see set piece 3); *OECD* (the Paris-based club of rich democracies that compiles comparative statistics); *welfare state* (the twentieth-century arrangement of pensions, health and benefits paid from general taxation — the reader has met it only in `british-franchise`, Ref it); *Renan*, *Barrès*, *Ortega* (each already introduced in his own lesson — re-anchor with a clause and the Ref, L11d); *Who Are We?* (Huntington's 2004 book — one clause naming it, its lesson comes two later).
- **Who Are We? is held for its own lesson.** The book is in the dossier and read in full (index 76), but `albions-seed-and-who-are-we` introduces `anglo-protestant-core` and `american-creed`. Here: one sentence at the culture-vs-institutions fork, no evidence, no quotation, no Ref to a concept not yet introduced. Do not attribute to Huntington 2004 anything from the 1993 article, or the reverse.
- **Renan and Barrès are recalled, not re-taught.** The daily plebiscite and the earth and the dead each get one clause and a Ref; the lesson's own work is to place Scruton's unchosen-but-joinable home between them (EXPLANATION PLAN link 4). Do not reopen Renan's racial writings or Barrès's anti-Dreyfusard politics.
- **Do not re-teach** `allegiance`, `little-platoon`, `partnership-of-generations`, `mores`, `nation-as-project`, `daily-plebiscite`, `la-terre-et-les-morts`, `volk`, `original-position`, `noble-lie`: one clause and a `<Ref>` each (L0e).
- **Do not run ahead into Miller ch. 4** (territorial jurisdiction, the park warden, self-determination, Abizadeh) or ch. 5–8; do not put Carens's attack here; do not read Federalist 2 in the body (it opens the next lesson; only the FORWARD exercise quotes it). The lesson ends with the premise stated and the fork located.
- **Miller is a philosopher of the left** (social democrat, self-described "communitarian"; the book began as a commissioned defence of states' right to close borders — Acknowledgments pp. 211–212). Say so once, plainly: it is the lesson's reversal seam — from a liberal of the left one expects the cosmopolitan line; the record is otherwise.
- **Contested empirical claim**: the diversity–trust–welfare link. Miller himself qualifies it (not inevitable; offset by integration and inclusive identity, ch. 4 pp. 64–65 — cite the caution without importing ch. 4's argument). Quote the OECD figure with its counterfactual frame or not at all. Do not present "diversity lowers trust" as settled; present it as Miller presents it — a cost that belongs in the ledger.
- **Scruton's sweeping assertions** (EU legitimacy, treaties as "dead hands", the culture of repudiation) are his positions; keep them as positions and keep the EU material to one clause (it belongs to Murray's lesson).
- **Thin spots**: Miller's treatment of what national identity is *made of* is brief (ch. 2 pp. 27–30; ch. 8 pp. 141–149 on public culture is fuller but belongs to his own lesson); the writer may borrow one clause from ch. 8 p. 149 (strict cultural neutrality is impossible — language, flag, land use) to make the "institutions vs culture" fork honest. Scruton's HtbC gives no sustained account of how a newcomer *acquires* oikophilia beyond ch. 7's "welcomed into, not beside, the civic culture" — say the account is thin rather than invent one.
- **Register.** The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. The nationalism/patriotism distinction is Scruton's; the lesson reports it and lets the module's later lessons test it, rather than endorsing it.
- **Register**: Scruton's aphorisms tempt quotation; use at most four short quotations from him in the whole lesson.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval and chronology (E3, earlier lessons)*: Put in order and date to the decade Renan's daily plebiscite, Barrès's earth and the dead, Burke's partnership of generations and Scruton's allegiance; state in one sentence each what it contributes to this lesson's premise; and name the one thing this lesson adds that none of them said (the "which people?" question put to the contract itself).
2. *Boundary probe*: A people of one. Run Scruton's argument with a "we" of a single person, then with a "we" of all humanity. Show that in the first case there is nothing to disagree about and in the second nothing to lose a vote to — so the premise requires a bounded plurality, and say why *bounded* (not merely plural) does the work.
3. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill*: Quote Scruton's "democracy needs boundaries, and boundaries need the nation state" (ch. 3) with its metadata (a British philosopher, 2014, writing for a general right-of-centre readership during the eurozone and migration debates). Predict what it will omit; then read: what does the sentence record, argue and omit (it argues from democracy's need, not from culture's worth; it omits any account of how boundaries are drawn or redrawn)? What can it be USED to establish — about Scruton's position, not about democracies?
4. *Claim to refute*: "Miller's associative obligations are just nationalism with footnotes: if compatriots come first, outsiders count for nothing." Name the step that fails (weak cosmopolitanism sets a floor every human being is owed; partiality operates only above it; the hiker must give water), and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that Miller's floor is empty — which the next lessons test).
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped*: Miller's secession test backwards. Take a political unit with all three strands (Denmark) and remove them one at a time in the other order — nationality first, then citizenship, then cooperation — and say at each step which obligations Miller's theory predicts would lapse. The solution shows that his three strands are not symmetrical: nationality is the one that extends obligation across generations.
6. *Synthesis — counter-argument stem*: Someone claims that a conservative and a liberal who agree that the "we" is pre-political must also agree about immigration. Name two facts from the lesson the claim fails to account for (the bond's content differs; Miller's partiality is permitted, not mandated, and answerable to reasons) and state what would have to be true for it to survive.
7. *Boundary probe — the veil at the border*: Stand in Miller's airport hall and suppose, as Rawls's parties do (`<Ref rawls-theory-of-justice>`), that you do not know which line you will be sent to — citizen or "all other passports" — and that your life chances depend on it. Write down which rule for the two lines you would accept before learning your place, and say which premise of this lesson your rule denies. The solution notes that Rawls himself set his device inside "a closed system isolated from other societies" (§2) and that putting birthplace behind the veil points away from the two lines both thinkers in this lesson defend — the move a later lesson in the module makes its whole argument (`carens-open-borders`; not marked FORWARD here).
8. **FORWARD (E7)** → `federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`: In 1787 an American wrote that Providence had given "this one connected country to one united people — a people descended from the same ancestors, speaking the same language, professing the same religion, attached to the same principles of government, very similar in their manners and customs" (Federalist 2, public domain; `research/texts/federalist-nos-1-2-10-39-51.txt` l. 224–227). Sort his five bonds into Scruton's kind (a home and its ways) and Miller's kind (a scheme of cooperation and citizenship), and say which of the five a newcomer could acquire by settling. The solution says the reader has stated the question the next lesson opens with — whether a republic needs a people united by ancestry, language, religion and manners, or only by principles of government — and that the module's American lessons test both answers on the record.

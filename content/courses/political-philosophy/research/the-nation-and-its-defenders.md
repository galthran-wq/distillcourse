# Research brief: the-nation-and-its-defenders (The nation and its defenders: Scruton, Miller and the pre-political loyalty)

status: planned
module: nation-and-immigration
type: core
goal: Analyze the case that a nation is a pre-political loyalty to a place and a way of life, held in different forms by a conservative and a liberal nationalist, and distinguish it from nationalism

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The reader arrives from two directions. From `scruton-meaning-of-conservatism` they have `allegiance` (the bond to family and nation that precedes choice), `authority-before-freedom` and the critique of the social contract ("Which people?" was not yet asked there — it is asked here). From `burke-reflections` they have the `little-platoon` and the `partnership-of-generations`; from `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township` the `mores` that sustain a free people; from `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses` the `nation-as-project` and Renan's daily plebiscite; from `huntington-clash-of-civilizations` the `civilizational-fault-line` and the idea that identity scales with the observer. From the empire module they have just finished (`auditing-the-case-for-colonialism`) they bring the sourcing drill and the habit of reading a contested claim as an argument with evidence. They have met Rawls only as the orthodoxy Scruton answers (Philosophical Appendix) — never his text.
- **Contributes:** One move: that a bounded "we" must exist before politics can begin — before anyone can vote, dissent or compromise — and that this premise is held by a conservative (Scruton) and by a liberal of the left (Miller) alike. The lesson builds the shared premise from the coordination problem up, then locates exactly where the two part: what the bond is made of (territory, custom, settled home vs. a national identity that is one strand beside economic cooperation and citizenship) and what follows for who may join.
- **Hands off:** The premise is stated but not yet attacked. The next lesson (`carens-open-borders`) takes the strongest liberal denial of it — that a bounded "we" with the right to exclude is feudal privilege — at full strength. Miller's reply (`miller-strangers-in-our-midst`) and Murray's charge that the "we" Miller assumes no longer exists (`murray-strange-death-of-europe`) follow. The lesson must leave the reader able to state the premise so precisely that Carens's attack lands on something.
- **Spine step:** "The live question: what the nation is, who may join it, and whether the West can survive on liberal principles alone — Scruton, Miller and Murray against Carens, Deneen against liberalism itself, and 2015 as the test"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: before anyone can disagree about politics they have to agree on who "we" are — who votes, whose law applies, whose children inherit. The newcomer meets this as a puzzle about democracy itself: a vote presupposes an electorate, a law presupposes a jurisdiction, and neither can be voted into existence by the people it defines.

1. **Why can't the "we" be chosen?** The obvious attempt is the social contract the reader knows from `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` and `locke-property-and-consent`: the people agree, and the agreement makes them a people. It fails on its own first word — "We, the people" (Scruton ch. 2): who is party to the contract, and who decided that? A contract can only be made by people who already recognise each other as the ones entitled to make it. And it cannot bind the dead or the unborn (Burke, `<Ref burke-reflections>`), though they bequeathed the institutions and will inherit the debts. So the answer: membership is prior to agreement — a *pre-political* fact. Because: consent needs a pre-existing "who consents". This is the shared premise; both thinkers will be shown to hold it.
2. **What does a pre-political "we" do?** Attempt: perhaps it is just a feeling, politically inert. Scruton ch. 3: it is what makes opposition possible. Disagreement, dissent and compromise all presuppose that the losers stay — that they accept being governed by people they dislike — and they stay only if they identify with something the winners share. Because: without a shared identity, every lost vote is secession or civil war (Lebanon as his case; `<Ref scruton-meaning-of-conservatism>` for patriotism as the obstacle to civil war). Hence "democracy needs boundaries, and boundaries need the nation state."
3. **Why a nation and not a religion or a race?** Attempt: any shared identity would do. Scruton: a religious "we" binds believers across borders and does not accept that law can be made by men; it endures where secular law adapts; so it does not sit with democratic politics. A racial "we" cannot be joined. The nation — territory, a shared history and customs, national myths, secular law — is the one form of first-person plural that is bounded, adaptable and open to newcomers (ch. 3; ch. 7 on the civic culture that frees membership from religion, race and kin). Because: the bond must be something a stranger can acquire by settling, yet something that makes a *this* rather than an everywhere. This is where **oikophilia** enters, as the answer to "what is the bond made of": love of a home one did not choose, the settled condition that produces a "we" of place.
4. **Is this just nationalism?** The seam must say so: from a conservative defending the nation one expects nationalism; the material refuses it. Nationalism (Sieyès's nation as a single will; the ideology of superiority) treats the nation as an actor above its members; the ordinary nation is the frame within which members disagree (`<Ref scruton-meaning-of-conservatism>`: patriotism vs nationalism). Ortega's "nation as project" (`<Ref ortega-revolt-of-the-masses>`, Renan's daily plebiscite) is the test: a nation is renewed by the decision to go on together — which already presupposes a "together".
5. **Can a liberal hold the same premise?** Here the second thinker enters and the register changes from home to duty. Miller's airport queue: two lines, one for citizens, one for everyone else. The obvious liberal attempt — *strong* cosmopolitanism, identical duties to every human being whatever the relationship — makes the two lines indefensible. Miller shows it is implausible for anyone: the hiker with water owes the dehydrated stranger consideration, not identical treatment with his own child. *Weak* cosmopolitanism — weigh everyone's interests, give reasons for unequal weight — is compatible with strong compatriot partiality. Because: relationships carry obligations (associative obligations) that are not chosen and not universal; to deny them is to deny that friendship or parenthood ground duties.
6. **What makes compatriots a relationship?** Three strands: a cooperative economic scheme, shared citizenship, shared nationality. Attempt: the first two suffice (distributive justice and collective freedom follow from cooperation and citizenship alone). Miller's secession test: what does nationality add that Scandinavia has and a mere customs union lacks? Solidarity across strangers, extension across generations, attachment to a homeland — the things that let a welfare state ask the rich to pay for the poor they will never meet. Evidence leg: diversity and trust (ch. 1 pp. 9–11, ch. 8 pp. 144–145), stated with the OECD conditional and Miller's own caution that the effect is not inevitable. Because: trust is what lets a people accept losing votes and paying taxes; it tracks identification.
7. **Where do they part?** Both: a bounded people, prior to politics, with a right of self-determination. Scruton's bond is a *home* — territory, custom, the inherited settlement; the bond is thick, and exclusion is its law ("to include is to exclude"). Miller's bond is a *national identity* that is one strand among three, defined by a public culture that can be thin, and bounded by human rights owed to everyone; compatriot partiality is permitted, not mandated, and it is answerable to weak cosmopolitanism's demand for reasons. The practical fork: Scruton asks newcomers to join a home (culture); Miller asks them to join a scheme of cooperation whose public culture they help revise (institutions). Mark the **strongest objection**, which enters here from the left-liberal side (the position Carens will hold next lesson): if the "we" is prior to choice, then the ones outside it never chose their exclusion either; a premise about *our* unchosen bonds says nothing yet about what we owe *them*. Scruton's reply (ch. 7): the civic culture is permeable — membership can be acquired. Miller's reply: weak cosmopolitanism — exclusion must be reasoned, and human rights bind. The lesson concedes that neither reply has yet said *why* a people may decide who joins; that is the next two lessons' work.

Headings fall out as claims: "A contract needs a 'we' before it can be signed"; "Shared identity is what lets the losers stay"; "Why the bond is a nation and not a church or a race"; "Patriotism is not nationalism"; "The airport queue: two lines a liberal can defend"; "Three strands make compatriots"; "Where home and identity part".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **"We, the people" — which people?** (Scruton ch. 2, l. ~886). Work the preamble of a constitution as a logic problem: the sentence that founds the polity names the people it founds. Show the circularity in two sentences and the way out (a pre-contractual plural). Short quotation of Scruton's phrase "presupposes a first-person plural".
2. **The coordination problem to the home** (ch. 2, l. 947–975). Reconstruct Scruton's chain: strangers must cooperate → cooperation needs trust → trust needs a shared "we" → the settled "we" is a place and its ways → oikophilia. Quote only "People settle by acquiring a first-person plural – a place, a community and a…" and gloss the rest.
3. **The Lebanon lesson** (ch. 1 gems; ch. 3): boundaries arise from national identities, not map lines; a confessional "we" endangers democracy because the loser of a vote has another loyalty to retreat to. One paragraph, anchored (Lebanon's civil war 1975–90, a state of confessional communities — Maronite, Sunni, Shia, Druze — sharing offices by sect).
4. **Sieyès and the three national myths** (ch. 3, l. 1168–1203, ~1397). Sieyès's *What Is the Third Estate?* (Paris, January 1789: the pamphlet that declared the commoners *the* nation) as the birth of nationalism-as-ideology; against it, the ordinary nation's myths — glory, sacrifice, emancipation — Scott of the Antarctic (1912; the 1948 film) and the 2012 London Olympics opening ceremony, which Scruton calls, with Plato, noble lies (`<Ref kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king>` for the `noble-lie`). Make the point that a conservative is here *conceding* that national identity is partly made.
5. **The airport queue and the hiker** (Miller ch. 2 pp. 20–24, text l. 778, 887–922). Open the Miller half with the two lines. Then the hiker thought experiments, told in sequence: (i) you have water, a dehydrated stranger appears — you must give some (weak cosmopolitan floor); (ii) two stranded travellers, one your friend — partiality is allowed above the floor; (iii) the stranger then asks for your book — you may refuse but must consider the request (p. 37, with Cicero's "light from the fire", which the reader met as Grotius's innocent profit only if the Miller lesson's brief brings it in — here keep Cicero as a one-clause `<Ref cicero-natural-law>`).
6. **The secession test** (ch. 2 p. 28): take two strands away and see what is left. A customs union has economic cooperation; the EU has a kind of citizenship; neither has produced the solidarity of a Scandinavian welfare state. What nationality adds is the thing the test isolates. Pair with the OECD finding (ch. 1 pp. 10–11) bound as Miller binds it: a conditional estimate, not a law.
7. **"To include is to exclude"** (ch. 10 l. 4388–4389): quote the one sentence and show the tension between liberty and equality it names — every association that confers advantage on members withholds it from non-members. This is the sentence the next lesson turns around (Carens: the nation-state is an association whose exclusion confers life chances by birth).
8. **The identity ladder** (Huntington 1993, `<Ref huntington-clash-of-civilizations>`): the Ibo in Lagos, London and New York — identity scales with the observer; used to show that "we" has levels, and that both Scruton and Miller choose the *national* level deliberately, against the civilizational (Huntington) and the global (cosmopolitan).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first mention** (L11): *oikophilia* (Greek *oikos*, household/home; Scruton's coinage, explain at the first use and once); *first-person plural* (the grammatical "we" used as a political term — say so); *pre-political* (existing before and independently of the institutions that govern); *cosmopolitanism* (and the three senses — identity, political, moral — but the lesson needs only moral, strong vs weak); *associative obligations* (define by example before the term); *Sieyès* (Abbé Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès, pamphleteer of 1789); *Lebanon* (see set piece 3); *OECD* (the Paris-based club of rich democracies that compiles comparative statistics); *welfare state* (the twentieth-century arrangement of pensions, health and benefits paid from general taxation — the reader has met it only in `british-franchise`, Ref it); *Renan* (Ernest Renan, French historian; the 1882 Sorbonne lecture — one clause, via the Ortega Ref); *Who Are We?* (see below).
- **Who Are We? is not in the dossier.** State in the body that the Anglo-Protestant core-culture thesis is reported as summarised by a restrictionist who quotes it, and mark it in References as not consulted directly. Do not reconstruct its argument beyond what Murray's quotation supports (one sentence: that multiculturalism is "in its essence anti-European civilisation", i.e. that a core culture rather than a creed is what newcomers assimilate to). Do not attribute to Huntington 2004 anything from the 1993 article.
- **Do not re-teach** `allegiance`, `little-platoon`, `partnership-of-generations`, `mores`, `nation-as-project`, `noble-lie`: one clause and a `<Ref>` each (L0e).
- **Do not run ahead into Miller ch. 4** (territorial jurisdiction, the park warden, self-determination, Abizadeh) or ch. 5–8; and do not put Carens's attack here. The lesson ends with the premise stated and the fork located; the attack is the next lesson's opening.
- **Miller is a philosopher of the left** (social democrat, self-described "communitarian"; the book began as a commissioned defence of states' right to close borders — Acknowledgments pp. 211–212). Say so once, plainly: it is the lesson's reversal seam — from a liberal of the left one expects the cosmopolitan line; the record is otherwise.
- **Contested empirical claim**: the diversity–trust–welfare link. Miller himself qualifies it (not inevitable; offset by integration and inclusive identity, ch. 4 pp. 64–65 — cite the caution without importing ch. 4's argument). Quote the OECD figure with its counterfactual frame or not at all. Do not present "diversity lowers trust" as settled; present it as Miller presents it — a cost that belongs in the ledger.
- **Scruton's sweeping assertions** (EU legitimacy, treaties as "dead hands", the culture of repudiation) are his positions; keep them as positions and keep the EU material to one clause (it belongs to Murray's lesson).
- **Thin spots**: Miller's treatment of what national identity is *made of* is brief (ch. 2 pp. 27–30; ch. 8 pp. 141–149 on public culture is fuller but belongs to his own lesson); the writer may borrow one clause from ch. 8 p. 149 (strict cultural neutrality is impossible — language, flag, land use) to make the "institutions vs culture" fork honest. Scruton's HtbC gives no sustained account of how a newcomer *acquires* oikophilia beyond ch. 7's "welcomed into, not beside, the civic culture" — say the account is thin rather than invent one.
- **No moralising in either direction**: the nationalism/patriotism distinction is Scruton's; the lesson reports it and then lets Carens test it, rather than endorsing it.
- **Register**: Scruton's aphorisms tempt quotation; use at most four short quotations from him in the whole lesson.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. *Retrieval (E3, earlier lessons)*: State in one sentence each what Burke's partnership of generations (`burke-reflections`) and Scruton's allegiance (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`) contribute to this lesson's premise, and name the one thing this lesson adds that neither said (the "which people?" question).
2. *Boundary probe*: A people of one. Run Scruton's argument with a "we" of a single person, then with a "we" of all humanity. Show that in the first case there is nothing to disagree about and in the second nothing to lose a vote to — so the premise requires a bounded plurality, and say why *bounded* (not merely plural) does the work.
3. *Document reasoning — sourcing drill*: Quote Scruton's "democracy needs boundaries, and boundaries need the nation state" (ch. 3) with its metadata (a British philosopher, 2014, writing for a general right-of-centre readership during the eurozone and migration debates). Predict what it will omit; then read: what does the sentence record, argue and omit (it argues from democracy's need, not from culture's worth; it omits any account of how boundaries are drawn or redrawn)? What can it be USED to establish — about Scruton's position, not about democracies?
4. *Claim to refute*: "Miller's associative obligations are just nationalism with footnotes: if compatriots come first, outsiders count for nothing." Name the step that fails (weak cosmopolitanism sets a floor every human being is owed; partiality operates only above it; the hiker must give water), and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that Miller's floor is empty — which the next lessons test).
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped*: Miller's secession test backwards. Take a political unit with all three strands (Denmark) and remove them one at a time in the other order — nationality first, then citizenship, then cooperation — and say at each step which obligations Miller's theory predicts would lapse. The solution shows that his three strands are not symmetrical: nationality is the one that extends obligation across generations.
6. *Synthesis — counter-argument stem*: Someone claims that a conservative and a liberal who agree that the "we" is pre-political must also agree about immigration. Name two facts from the lesson the claim fails to account for (the bond's content differs; Miller's partiality is permitted, not mandated, and answerable to reasons) and state what would have to be true for it to survive.
7. **FORWARD (E7)** → `carens-open-borders`: Stand in Miller's airport hall and suppose you do not know which line you will be sent to — citizen or "all other passports" — and that your life chances depend on it. Write down which rule for the two lines you would accept before learning your place. The solution notes that the reader has just built a *global original position*, the device the next lesson names, and that it points away from the two lines both thinkers in this lesson defend.

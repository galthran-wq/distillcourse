# Research brief: the-right-after-1945 (The right after 1945: what fascism did to the conservative tradition)

status: planned
module: postwar-conservatism
type: core
goal: Explain how fascism's defeat discredited the European right for half a century, what its thinkers kept, renamed or abandoned, and evaluate whether the tradition could be separated from what had used it

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The previous lesson's three men and their documents of 1933–34, and its question left open — whether the tradition itself is implicated (`the-right-in-1933`); the whole Conservative Revolution module — Sorel's myth, Jünger's Worker, Moeller's revolution "to create things worth conserving" and his "Third Reich", Evola's Tradition (`sorel-reflections-on-violence`, `junger-storm-of-steel-and-the-worker`, `moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich`, `evola-revolt-against-the-modern-world`; `conservative-revolution`, `third-reich-idea`). From earlier modules, the two thinkers the postwar "origins of fascism" charge fastened on: Burke's inheritance, prejudice and natural aristocracy (`burke-reflections`, `natural-aristocracy`) and Maistre's providence, executioner and counter-revolution (`maistre-and-bonald`, `counter-revolution`); Herder and Fichte's people before the state (`herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation`); Mann's Kultur against Zivilisation and his 1922 reversal (`mann-reflections`); Spengler's Caesarism (`spengler-decline-of-the-west`); Ortega's mass-man and noble life (`ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`); Schmitt's friend and enemy (`schmitt-concept-of-the-political`). From the Nietzsche course (an optional prerequisite): Nietzsche's own politics, by `<Ref>` only.
- **Contributes:** One move: the right's situation in 1945 stated as a problem the right's own thinkers had to solve — which of their words still meant what they meant, and why they were not what the defeated regimes had meant — and the three ways they solved it. The lesson introduces `anti-fascist-consensus` (the settlement that discredited the vocabulary and set the terms of re-entry) and `aristocratic-radicalism` (the form in which the right had received Nietzsche, and which it now had to split). It builds a ledger — kept, renamed, abandoned — and tests the goal's question on two cases: the renaming of the Conservative Revolution (1950) and the charge that fascism descended from Maistre and Burke. The finding the lesson earns: the separation holds at the level of argument (prescription and epistemic modesty are the opposite of will and myth) and fails at the level of words and persons (the same men, the same terms); the right that rebuilt did so by drawing that line in public.
- **Hands off:** The rebuilding itself, register by register, is the module: Jouvenel's natural history of Power, written in Swiss exile in 1943–45 by a man who had himself been on the far right (`jouvenel-on-power`, next); the definitions that separate conservative, reactionary and radical conservative (`what-conservatism-is` — this lesson uses the distinction; that lesson argues it); Oakeshott's disposition, the liberal-conservative exit at its most philosophical (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`); Strauss and Voegelin, the emigration into philosophy (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`, `voegelin-gnosticism`); the American founding (`kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`); Burnham (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`); and the Declaration of 1948 as the settlement's own text (`udhr-read-against-the-right`). Nietzsche's slave revolt in morals, which the dropped extra lesson also carried, is Ref'd from `ressentiment-and-envy`, not here.
- **Spine step:** "After 1945 fascism has discredited the right, and it rebuilds in new registers: Jouvenel's history of Power, Oakeshott's disposition, Strauss's natural right, Voegelin's diagnosis of Gnosticism, Kirk's and Nisbet's American conservatism, Burnham's managers — tested on the Declaration of 1948"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "In 1945 almost every word the European right had used for a century — nation, people, hierarchy, authority, community, blood, soil — had been used by regimes that had just been defeated and whose camps were being opened. A conservative who still believed in some of those things had to say which, and why they were not what Hitler and Mussolini meant. The lesson follows how the right's thinkers met that problem and what it cost them."

The problem as the newcomer feels it: the reader has just watched three of the right's best minds choose in 1933. Now the regimes are gone, and the words are left. The chain:

1. **What did 1945 do to the right's words?** The obvious picture — the victors banned fascism and the right carried on — misses what changed. Set out the settlement in three countries, one paragraph each: Germany (occupation and denazification; the Basic Law of 1949, drafted by German Christian and Social Democrats, opening "Human dignity shall be inviolable" and letting the constitutional court ban parties that would abolish the constitution — the court used it against a neo-Nazi party in 1952); Italy (the 1948 constitution forbids the Fascist party's reorganisation "in any form"); France (the trials of Pétain and Laval; Maurras, who had called the 1940 defeat a "divine surprise", sentenced for life — Ref `barres-and-maurras-integral-nationalism`). And the exception: in Britain and the United States the right had been on the winning side and was not discredited — the discredit was continental. Name the concept here: `anti-fascist-consensus` — the settlement in which fascism's defeat discredited the vocabulary of nation, hierarchy and authority and set the terms of the right's re-entry. Because: the words had been co-signed by the defeated regimes, and the settlement was written by those who had fought them, communists included. The terms of re-entry, read off the settlement: the right could come back as anti-communist, as Christian, as constitutional — and not as anything else. Fawcett's sentence, as the course's own: 1945 was "year zero" for the right.
2. **Which words survived, and in what form?** The lesson's ledger (Figure 1), built here and filled across the lesson. Abandoned: race and "blood and soil" as political categories; the leader-principle; the one-party state; war as a nation's regeneration (Ref `junger-storm-of-steel-and-the-worker`, `front-experience`); public antisemitism. Renamed: *Reich* into *Abendland*, "the West" — a Christian, European unity (the journal *Neues Abendland*, 1946–58); the people (*Volk*) into the nation inside a constitution; hierarchy into "elites" or "natural aristocracy" (Ref `burke-reflections`, `natural-aristocracy`); community into the family, the church and the local association (forward to `kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`); the Conservative Revolution into a movement that ended in 1932 (link 4). Kept: religion, property, the critique of mass society and of equality, anti-communism — the one cause the settlement gave the right. Because: what was kept was what the victors also held or could not do without (property, churches, anti-communism); what was abandoned was what the regimes had made their own; renaming happened where the right believed the thing and could not use the word.
3. **Three ways out.** Each is an answer to the opening's demand ("say which, and why they were not what Hitler meant"), and each has a price. (a) **Christian democracy**: the right's Catholic and Protestant parties fused (the CDU, 1945; Adenauer chancellor from 1949), took over the Christian-social tradition of before 1933 and accepted a liberal constitution — "avoidance of concentration of power" — as the principle. Price: the nation is no longer sacred; reckoning with the recent past is deferred (Adenauer replaced denazification with reparation to victims). (b) **Liberal conservatism**: totalitarianism read as the work of planning and of the claim to know society as a whole — Hayek's *Road to Serfdom* (1944), Popper's *Open Society* (1945), Oakeshott's Rationalism — so that fascism and communism are one error and the right's enemy is the same as the liberal's. Price: hierarchy survives only as a market outcome or a disposition, not as a political principle (Hayek's later "Why I am not a conservative" is the sign — forward to `oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`). (c) **Emigration into philosophy**: two émigrés from Germany and Austria, Strauss and Voegelin, place the catastrophe not in the right but in modernity itself — historicism ending in nihilism, the Gnostic dream of heaven on earth — so that Nazism and communism are two late fruits of one modern root. Price: politics becomes diagnosis; the cure is a return to classical or Christian philosophy that no party can enact (forward to `strauss-natural-right-and-history`, `voegelin-gnosticism`). A `<FigureSequence>` carries the three (Figure 3). Muller's observation, stated as the course's own, closes the link: some of the sharpest postwar conservative criticism came from former radical conservatives who "now saw no plausible alternative to the liberal democratic welfare state" — Arnold Gehlen, party member from 1933, for whom National Socialism became "the god that failed", is the example.
4. **What became of the Conservative Revolution's authors — and of its name?** The four survivors' public lines, in one short paragraph each, recalled from `the-right-in-1933` and its FORWARD exercise. Schmitt: interned, interrogated at Nuremberg, never charged, never taught again, never recanted; his 1963 foreword calls the friend–enemy criterion a "didactic" framework misread as "a primitive slogan … known only by hearsay" — a reframing by the author (dossier text). Heidegger: barred from teaching until 1949–51; republished in 1953 his 1935 phrase about "the inner truth and greatness of this movement" with a parenthesis about technology; kept a public silence about the crimes. Jünger: refused the Allied questionnaire on his past; republished *Der Arbeiter* in 1963 unaltered "for reasons of documentation" and said in 1980 that neither the Nazis nor their opponents could use it (dossier texts). Evola: tried in 1951 and acquitted; turned to *apoliteia*; read by the young right. Then **the renaming**: in 1950 Armin Mohler, Jünger's secretary, published *The Conservative Revolution in Germany 1918–1932*. Read the title as a document: the end-date stops before 1933, and the typology (five groups) separates a "purer" anti-liberal radicalism from Nazism, which becomes a "contamination" or a movement "of an altogether different kind" (Fawcett's reading, stated as the course's own). The reader's concept `conservative-revolution` was fixed in this act (Ref `moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich`). **The thinkers' difficulty, stated as theirs:** to keep the critique of liberalism and disown its 1933 outcome, a radical conservative had to show a line of principle between the Conservative Revolution and National Socialism; a date (1932) is not a principle, and the previous lesson showed two of the movement's own authors crossing the line in a month.
5. **The Nietzsche question: what could the right keep of the critique of equality?** (The reception material of the dropped extra, compressed to one section.) Problem: the right's house critic of equality was a philosopher who despised the state, the nation and antisemitism (Ref `nietzsche/nietzsche-and-politics`) and whose name the regime had borrowed through his sister's archive (Ref `nietzsche/the-nazi-appropriation`). Name the concept: **`aristocratic-radicalism`** — the Danish critic Georg Brandes's phrase of 1889 for Nietzsche's politics, which Nietzsche accepted: a critique of equality, democracy and pity from the standpoint of rank, carried out against the existing aristocracies and churches as much as against the levellers. Because a critique that attacks both sides can be borrowed by either, piece by piece. Then the four readers the course has met, each in two clauses — what he took and what he refused (Figure 4): Mann (1918: the *Dreigestirn*; democracy as the "politicisation" of inward life — "Demokratie und Nationalismus, das ist eines Ursprungs"; refused the aesthete's superman politics; reversed himself in 1922 — Ref `mann-reflections`); Spengler (took will to power and rank; refused Nietzsche's untimeliness — transvaluation becomes the mark of a late Civilization, Nietzsche a symptom in the chain "Schopenhauer–Darwin–Nietzsche–Shaw" — Ref `spengler-decline-of-the-west`); Ortega (took the herd as the mass-man and nobility as obligation; refused rank as class and contempt for liberalism — "liberalism is the supreme form of generosity" — Ref `ortega-revolt-of-the-masses`); Strauss (1953: took the diagnosis that the modern road ends in nihilism — "absolutely homeless"; refused the premise, radical historicism, and the cure, new values; Nietzsche "the second crisis of modernity" — forward Ref `strauss-natural-right-and-history`). The pattern: each kept the diagnosis — herd, levelling, nihilism — and refused the cure. After 1945 that split became a condition of speaking: aristocratic radicalism could be kept only as aristocratic *conservatism* — the rank without the radicalism. State the strongest version of the charge from the right's own side (a postwar reference work in the Strauss school): "his words are singularly easy to abuse". And Strauss's rule for the case, quoted: "A view is not refuted by the fact that it happens to have been shared by Hitler" — the *reductio ad Hitlerum*. The rule protects an argument from guilt by association; it does not supply the argument (Exercise 5).
6. **Did fascism descend from the right? The charge of continuity and the replies.** The charge at full strength, the course's own statement of a postwar literature: fascism had a pedigree, and it ran through the Counter-Enlightenment — Maistre's providence of blood, his executioner, his contempt for reason and for intellectuals made him, on this reading, "the first theorist" of a line ending in Maurras and the fascists, his doctrine of violence and self-immolation "the heart of all totalitarian doctrines" (quote that phrase, attributed in References); Herder's people and Fichte's will led to the *Volk*; Burke's organic nation, read by his German translators, fed Romanticism's (verify the Gentz–Adam Müller link before stating). Recall Maistre by `<Ref>` (`maistre-and-bonald`) — the reader already weighed the fascism charge there; do not re-run it. The replies, each at full strength: (a) **The right's own reply (1953):** totalitarianism is the product of masses torn from community and of revolutionary ideology; its talk of family and tradition was "sham"; "Far from being … a lineal product of nineteenth-century Conservatism, totalitarianism is, in fact, the very opposite of it" (Nisbet, quoted; Kirk repeats it). (b) **The evidence reply:** the strong form of the Maistre charge has no evidence of influence on fascists beyond the Action française; his readers were nineteenth-century Catholics, through his book on the papacy (Garrard's finding, as the course's own). (c) **The symmetry reply:** the same method found the roots of totalitarianism in Plato and Hegel (Popper, 1945) and in Rousseau (the thesis of "totalitarian democracy", 1952) — on the left as much as on the right; a genealogy that reaches everyone indicts no one, and where the rupture is placed — "1933 . . . or 1917, or 1789, or further back" — is a choice the method does not make. (d) **The distinction reply:** conservatism and radical conservatism are different things — the radical conservatives of the previous module shared sensibilities with National Socialism (will, myth, blood and soil); the conservatism of prescription and epistemic modesty did not, and was discarded by them (Muller's distinction, as the course's own; forward to `what-conservatism-is`). Where the lesson concedes: (d) is the strongest reply and the charge's strongest target survives it — the previous module's authors, and two of them in person. The verdict on the goal's question is the reader's, with the evidence laid out: the separation holds at the level of argument (prescription against will) and does not hold at the level of words and persons.
7. **What it cost.** Obscurity and the need to argue in the opponent's terms: an American critic wrote in 1950 that there was no "serious and intelligent" conservatism left to argue with, and Europe agreed (Fawcett's report, as the course's own). The cost Muller names with Burke's phrase, "choice of inheritance": after 1945 every conservative had to say which inheritance he chose, in public, and to whom it belonged. Close by handing on the open problem: a right that had abandoned race, leader and myth and kept religion, property and the critique of the masses still had to say what the state should be — and the first postwar book to answer was written in Swiss exile by a man who had himself been on the far right in the 1930s. Ref `jouvenel-on-power`.

Headings as claims (core, 45 min, 5–7 sections): "Year zero: a settlement written by the victors"; "Kept, renamed, abandoned"; "Three ways back"; "1932 as a boundary: the renaming of the Conservative Revolution"; "Rank without radicalism: the Nietzsche question"; "Did fascism descend from the right?"; "The price of the choice of inheritance".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms and names to anchor (L11):** denazification (the Allied programme of 1945–49/51 to classify and purge former Nazis by questionnaire and tribunal) — link 1; Basic Law (the West German constitution of 1949, called "basic law" because meant to be provisional until reunification) — link 1; Christian democracy (parties uniting Catholics and Protestants on Christian-social principles: the CDU in Germany, the DC in Italy, the MRP in France) — link 3; Adenauer (former Catholic mayor of Cologne, removed by the Nazis in 1933, chancellor 1949–63) — link 3; *Abendland* (literally "evening land", the Christian West as a cultural unity) — link 2; Vichy (the French state of 1940–44 under Pétain, collaborating with Germany) — link 1; Maurras (Ref; "divine surprise" anchored there); MSI (the Italian Social Movement, founded 1946 by veterans of Mussolini's last republic) — link 4, one clause; Mohler (Swiss writer, Jünger's secretary 1949–53) — link 4; Brandes (Danish critic whose 1888 Copenhagen lectures were the first university course on Nietzsche) — link 5; *Dreigestirn*, transvaluation, mass-man — each one clause, Ref'd to their lessons; Gnosticism and historicism — one clause each, forward Ref'd, not defined here beyond a phrase. No more than three new names per section: link 3 and link 4 are the dense ones — use Figures 2 and 3 to carry dates.
- **Do not re-teach** Maistre's executioner, Burke's prescription, Mann's Kultur, Spengler's Civilization, Ortega's mass-man or the Nazi appropriation of Nietzsche. Each is `<Ref>` + a clause. The Nietzsche section is the place the risk is highest: every "Nietzsche held…" is a Ref and one clause, phrased to match the Ref'd lesson's goal (`nietzsche/nietzsche-and-politics`: whether his contempt for state, nationalism and antisemitism plus his praise of rank add up to a politics; `nietzsche/the-nazi-appropriation`: what the sister and the regime forged, compiled or clipped, and the residue of hard passages).
- **Do not pre-teach the module.** Hayek, Popper, Oakeshott, Strauss, Voegelin, Kirk, Nisbet, Jouvenel and Burnham each get a clause here and a lesson later; the lesson names their exits, not their arguments. Strauss's two sentences (the *reductio ad Hitlerum*; "absolutely homeless") are the only Strauss quoted.
- **Britain and America are the exception, not the rule.** The opening's "European right" is meant: say in link 1 that the right in the victor countries was not discredited, and that this is why the postwar American conservative canon (Kirk, 1953) could begin with Burke and end at the Channel, keeping Tocqueville alone of the Continentals. This is itself a renaming — the canon drawn so as to leave Maistre, Bonald and Gentz outside.
- **The continuity charge is a postwar literature, not a settled finding, and it runs both ways.** Present Berlin's Maistre thesis, Popper's Plato and Hegel, the "totalitarian democracy" thesis on Rousseau, and the irrationalism thesis (Lukács, 1954) as arguments with their evidence; present the replies with theirs. Never write "fascism grew out of the counter-revolution" or "conservatism had nothing to do with fascism" in the course's voice. The register rule: The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.
- **The Burke charge is thin in the dossier.** The course.yaml key idea pairs "Maistre and Burke"; the dossier documents the charge against Maistre (Berlin, Garrard) and the right's reply on behalf of conservatism generally (Nisbet, Kirk), but holds no text that makes Burke an origin of fascism. Treat Burke in one sentence: his German reception through Gentz's translation (1793) and the Romantics (verify before stating) against his postwar American canonisation as the first enemy of ideology — and say in References that the Burke side of the charge is not documented in the dossier.
- **Dates where sources diverge:** Mohler's book — Fawcett says 1949 (the dissertation), course.yaml "the 1950 renaming" (the publication). Both right; say "a dissertation of 1949, published in 1950". The 1953 republication of Heidegger's 1935 phrase and the parenthesis: the parenthesis's date is disputed (Heidegger said it was in the 1935 manuscript; the manuscript page is missing) — say "with a parenthesis he later said had been there from the start" (verify in Ott or Wolin), or omit.
- **Schmitt, Heidegger and Jünger after 1945 are reported, not judged.** Schmitt's posthumous *Glossarium* (1991) contains antisemitic entries from 1947 — report in one clause if used, dated, after verifying. Heidegger's Bremen lecture of 1949 setting "the manufacture of corpses in gas chambers" beside motorised agriculture is the most cited postwar sentence of his — if used, quote exactly from a verified edition (GA 79, p. 27) and without comment; it may better be left out. The Black Notebooks belong to the previous lesson.
- **The Nietzsche section is reception, compressed.** It replaces a 20-minute extra with one section of about 400–500 words, one figure and one exercise; the fuller pointers stay in `C/research/nietzsche-on-the-right.md`. Scruton is forward-only (one clause, or omit); Carlyle (`carlyle-heroes`) is optional for one exercise only.
- **Copyright:** Fawcett, Muller, Lilla, Kirk, Nisbet, Strauss, Ortega's translation, Mann's German (Mann d. 1955 — protected to 2025 in the EU and so now free there; the 1983 English translation is in copyright — translate yourself), Schmitt's 1963 foreword, Jünger's prefaces: all short quotations. Public: the Basic Law, the Italian Constitution, Brandes's essay, Spengler's Atkinson translation in the US.
- **Do not moralise in either direction; do not manufacture a happy ending.** The right's recovery after 1945 was swift (Fawcett: back in office by the 1950s) and partial (the unreconciled fringe, the Criticón line of the 1970s, the hard right of the 2010s — one forward clause to `the-argument-assembled` at most). State that the settlement's terms were set by the victors and that the right accepted them; the reader judges whether the acceptance was conviction or necessity, with the evidence of link 4 (the authors) and link 3 (the parties).
- **The human asked that the difficulties right-wing thinkers faced be taught as their own problems.** The lesson's opening states the problem in their voice; links 3, 4 and 5 each state the thinker's difficulty before the objection: the Christian democrat's (how to keep the nation without its sacredness), the liberal conservative's (how to keep hierarchy without a principle of rank), the émigré's (how to diagnose without a party), the radical conservative's (how to draw a line of principle where there was only a date), the Nietzschean's (how to keep rank without the radicalism).
- **Where the dossier is thin — say so in References:** no Mohler, no Heidegger, no postwar Schmitt beyond the 1963 foreword, no Christian democratic text, no Popper or Talmon, no Lukács or Kaufmann; Fawcett, Muller, Kirk and Nisbet carry the settlement and the reply, Berlin and Garrard the Maistre charge, the Jünger and Evola apparatus the authors' postwar lines.
- **L8a:** never "Fawcett shows", "Muller distinguishes", "Berlin argues", "Garrard replies", "Lilla observes". State each thesis as the course's own: "the charge that…", "the reply that…", "the distinction between…". Nisbet, Kirk and Strauss are quoted as the right's own voices of the period (their texts are material, and each has a lesson); Mohler's book is studied as an object.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up; E3).** Closed book, place each of these in the ledger's columns and name the earlier lesson it comes from: Burke's natural aristocracy; Jünger's war as the school of a new man; Moeller's "Third Reich"; Evola's caste; the Church as the "bond" of society (Maistre); Herder's people. Then name which of them the Basic Law of 1949 could accommodate and which it excluded, and by which article.
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill.** "Far from being, as is sometimes absurdly argued, a lineal product of nineteenth-century Conservatism, totalitarianism is, in fact, the very opposite of it." (1953.) Metadata (an American sociologist on the right, writing eight years after the war, in a book on the loss of community); predict, then read: records / argues / omits (omits the Conservative Revolution of the 1920s, which the reader has just studied); what it can establish (how the postwar right understood its own relation to fascism) and what it cannot (whether that understanding is true — for that, the previous module's authors and their documents).
3. **Claim to refute.** "After 1945 the right simply put its old ideas under new names." Break it with the ledger: the abandoned column (race, the leader, the one-party state, war as regeneration) has no renamed counterpart; Christian democracy signed a constitution that made dignity, not the nation, its first article. Then state what is true in the claim (Mohler's periodisation and *Abendland* are renamings), and what further evidence would decide how much of the old vocabulary survived (the programmes of the German Party and the MSI; *Criticón*).
4. **Document reasoning — a title as evidence.** Mohler's book of 1950 is called *The Conservative Revolution in Germany 1918–1932*. What does the end-date do to Schmitt's place in the movement, and to Heidegger's (Ref `the-right-in-1933`)? State the argument the date makes without stating it, and what a reader who wanted to test that argument would compare (Schmitt's texts of 1932 and of 1934).
5. **Boundary probe.** Strauss's rule: "A view is not refuted by the fact that it happens to have been shared by Hitler." Apply it to two views the regime held — that nations are entitled to govern themselves, and that peoples are ranked by blood. Show that the rule protects both from the same fallacy and neither from other objections; find the case where the rule is silent (a view that is wrong for the same reason it was Hitler's), and say what the right needed besides the rule after 1945 (arguments of its own).
6. **Synthesis — claim–evidence–reasoning (the Nietzsche ledger).** "After 1945 aristocratic radicalism survived on the right only as aristocratic conservatism." Argue for or against with three readers from Figure 4 (Mann, Ortega, Strauss), stating the warrant (each kept rank and refused the radicalism against the existing order), and name the one reader who kept the radicalism (Spengler, through Caesarism) and what became of that line in 1933 (Ref `the-right-in-1933`).
7. **Research-shaped task (E3: earlier lessons).** Design the test of the charge that Maistre is an origin of fascism (Ref `maistre-and-bonald`). Define "influence" so that it can be checked; name the evidence (citations of Maistre in fascist and National Socialist texts, editions and translations 1900–1940, his presence in the Action française's canon); say which result would confirm the strong form ("dominant influence"), which the weak form ("affinity"), and which neither — and why the weak form cannot be tested in this way.
8. **FORWARD (E7).** A French writer born in 1903 interviewed Hitler for a Paris newspaper in 1936 and belonged for two years to a party of the far right; in exile in Switzerland in 1943–45 he wrote a natural history of the state's power that found democracy to be the regime under which power grew fastest; forty years later he sued a historian who had called his early thinking fascist, and won token damages. Using the three exits of link 3, classify his postwar position and say what his past makes harder and what it makes easier for his argument to be heard. The solution says, in one sentence with a `<Ref>`, that `jouvenel-on-power` reads the book, *On Power* (1945).

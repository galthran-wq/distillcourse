# Research brief: the-right-in-1933 (1933: Schmitt, Heidegger and Spengler choose)

status: planned
module: conservative-revolution
type: core
goal: Apply the Conservative Revolution's concepts to what three of its leading thinkers did when the Nazis took power — Schmitt's and Heidegger's adherence, Spengler's refusal — and judge what in their earlier work disposed or failed to dispose each to that choice

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The whole Conservative Revolution module, now cashed out: Sorel's myth that moves masses because it is not a prediction (`sorel-reflections-on-violence`, `political-myth`); Jünger's front, total mobilization and the Worker as the new ruling type (`junger-storm-of-steel-and-the-worker`, `front-experience`, `total-mobilization`, `gestalt-of-the-worker`); Moeller's revolution to create what is worth conserving and his "Third Reich" (`moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich`, `conservative-revolution`, `third-reich-idea`); Evola's Tradition and his law that every mass movement is the rule of the lowest caste (`evola-revolt-against-the-modern-world`, `regression-of-castes`). From the previous module: Spengler's Caesarism as the predicted end of the West's democracy (`spengler-decline-of-the-west`, `caesarism`); Schmitt's friend and enemy, the internal enemy and *protego ergo obligo* (`schmitt-concept-of-the-political`, `friend-enemy-distinction`), his sovereign who decides on the exception and his decision "out of nothingness" (`schmitt-political-theology`, `state-of-exception`, `decisionism`); and the record of how the Weimar Republic ended — the presidential decrees, 20 July 1932, the chain of legalities from 30 January to the Enabling Act of 23 March 1933, Schmitt's party membership and the article of 1 August 1934 reported as facts (`article-48-weimar`). The reader arrives knowing WHAT happened in 1933 and that Schmitt joined; this lesson reads the three men's own documents.
- **Contributes:** The module's application (L16). Three documents of 1933–34 — Schmitt's article "The Führer Protects the Law" (1 August 1934), Heidegger's Rectoral Address (27 May 1933) with his student appeal of 3 November 1933, and Spengler's *The Hour of Decision* (August 1933) — read against the concepts the module built, to answer the lesson's question: did the doctrine decide the choice? The finding the lesson earns: each doctrine disposed its author in BOTH directions, and the choice was settled by something the doctrine left open — for Schmitt, the step from "someone must decide" to "the one who has decided is the law"; for Heidegger, the hope that a philosopher could lead a revolution; for Spengler, an aristocrat's contempt for a mass party, which his own morphology did not license. Careerism, fear and chance are named where the record shows them, as departures from the idealisation that thinkers act on their theories.
- **Hands off:** (i) What the three did and said after 1945 — Schmitt's refusal to recant, Heidegger's silence, the fate of Jünger and Evola, the renaming of the movement — and what the whole right had to give up because of 1933: `the-right-after-1945` (next lesson, the first of the postwar module). (ii) The general question this lesson poses through three cases — whether a tradition can be separated from what used it — is that lesson's goal. (iii) Heidegger reappears as the teacher whose question Strauss answered (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`); the lesson introduces him only as far as 1933 requires.
- **Spine step:** "The radical right turns from conserving to revolution — Sorel's myth, Jünger's front and Worker, Moeller's Third Reich, Evola's Tradition — and in 1933 its thinkers have to choose: Schmitt and Heidegger join, Spengler refuses"

H. Beck, August 1933; Eng. *The Hour of Decision*, 1934), its introduction above all. Supporting documents already in the dossier: Schmitt's preface to the second edition of *Political Theology* dated November 1933 (the three types of legal thinking); Strong's foreword and Schwab's introduction (the record); the Heidegger chapter of Strauss–Cropsey (a 1987 account of the rectorate, itself read as evidence in an exercise).
  - **Module results used:** `decisionism`, `state-of-exception`, `friend-enemy-distinction` (Schmitt's article); `caesarism`, `culture-vs-civilization-spengler` (Spengler's refusal); `gestalt-of-the-worker`, `total-mobilization`, `front-experience` (Heidegger's "three services" and his reading of Jünger; Jünger's own refusal); `conservative-revolution`, `third-reich-idea` (the name the regime took and the movement it used); `political-myth` (the movement as myth, not programme); `regression-of-castes` (Evola's verdict that the mass party is the last caste — the one case of the module's doctrines predicting refusal outright).
  - **Departures from the idealisation the lesson must name:** (a) the theories underdetermine the choices — the man whose theory most predicted a Caesar refused him, the man whose chief book had no politics joined; (b) careerism — the rectorship, the posts Schmitt took (Prussian State Councillor, July 1933; leadership of the National Socialist university jurists, November 1933); (c) fear — Schmitt's patron Schleicher was killed on 30 June 1934 and Schmitt himself had reason to fear (Strong's endnote 59); (d) chance — who was rector when (Heidegger's predecessor resigned in April 1933 — verify), who met whom; (e) the regime's own choice — it used Schmitt and then let the SS attack him (1936), let Heidegger go after a year, and did not need Spengler.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "On 1 May 1933 a jurist who had defined the political as friend and enemy and a philosopher who had become rector of Freiburg both joined the Nazi party. A historian whose book had made the decline of the West a household phrase met Hitler, refused the offer of a role, and published a book that autumn which the regime soon banned. All three had been read as prophets of the movement now in power. The lesson reads their documents of 1933–34 against everything the module has built and asks what made the difference."

(Check before writing: the opening says "1 May 1933" for both; Strong says "May 1933", Muller "late April". The party back-dated the admission of the spring 1933 wave to 1 May; both men's membership numbers carry that date. The writer keeps the opening and adds one clause in the body: "the date the party gave the spring's applicants" — verify in Mehring and Ott. Likewise "published a book that autumn": the book appeared in August 1933 — "late summer"; flag to the caller, do not alter the opening.)

The problem as the newcomer feels it: the module has built four doctrines that despised liberal democracy and wanted something to replace it — a myth, a Worker-state, a third empire, a sacred hierarchy. In 1933 a movement took power using their words. The obvious expectation is that their authors joined it in proportion to how much their doctrine resembled it. The record breaks the expectation, and the lesson follows the break. The chain:

1. **What happened in 1933–34, and what did the three men do?** Recall the chain of legalities to 23 March 1933 in a paragraph with `<Ref>` to `article-48-weimar` — do not re-tell it. Add what that lesson did not cover: the spring wave of party admissions (both men's dated 1 May); Heidegger's election as rector (April) and his address (27 May); Spengler's book (August) and his meeting with Hitler (July); the plebiscite on leaving the League of Nations (12 November) for which Heidegger campaigned; Heidegger's resignation (April 1934); the killings of 30 June–2 July 1934 — the SA leadership, and with them Schleicher and others with no tie to the SA — and the one-sentence law of 3 July declaring them "lawful as the state's self-defence"; Schmitt's article (1 August); the SS attack on Schmitt (December 1936); Spengler's death (May 1936). A three-lane timeline carries this (Figure 1). Then the module's own roll-call, the lesson's data: Sorel (d. 1922) and Moeller (d. 1925) were dead, their words taken; Jünger refused a seat in the Reichstag and the regime's academy of poetry and left Berlin; Evola, in Italy, was judged by the SS a "reactionary Roman" (Ref `evola-revolt-against-the-modern-world`); Schmitt and Heidegger joined; Spengler refused. The question: does the doctrine explain the column "what he did"? (Figure 2, the table, starts empty in its third column and is filled across the lesson.)
2. **Schmitt: how did a theory of the decision read a decision?** The setup: the reader knows the decisionist's claims — the sovereign decides on the exception; a decision "emanates from nothingness", no norm can derive or judge it (Ref `schmitt-political-theology`, `decisionism`); whoever protects commands obedience (Ref `schmitt-concept-of-the-political`). Now the artefact: the 1934 article, read in four steps (a `<FigureSequence>`, Figure 3). (a) The setting: the state's self-defence statute of 3 July and Hitler's claim of 13 July to have been, in that hour, "the supreme judge of the German people". (b) Schmitt's argument: the liberal *Rechtsstaat* would have bound the leader with norms in the moment of danger, as Weimar's legalism had disarmed the state; the true leader "is always also judge"; the deed "was itself the highest justice" — the exception decided, and the decision declared to be law. (c) The limit he keeps: acts outside the Führer's commission are crimes (verify). (d) What the article omits: that the statute was retroactive; that among the dead was his own patron. Then the question the lesson poses to the theory, which `schmitt-concept-of-the-political` first raised as a question about naming the enemy (Ref, one clause): **can a decisionist judge a decision?** The theory's own answer is no — there is no norm above the sovereign decision by which to call it wrong. That is the strongest case that the doctrine disposed him: the article is decisionism applied to its hardest case. **Objection at full strength, the reader's own:** in 1932 the same Schmitt argued that a constitution may deny the "equal chance" to parties that would use legality to abolish it, and he advised the government that tried to keep the NSDAP from power (Ref `article-48-weimar`) — the theory had served the opposite cause a year before. So the doctrine did not choose the side; what moved him was the step from "someone must decide" to "the one who has decided is the law" — the Hobbesian clause *autoritas non veritas facit legem* read as accepting whoever holds authority, which Schwab's introduction names as the cause. The reader has met this step before, in Maistre's "any government is good once it is established" (Ref `maistre-and-bonald`) and in Hobbes's protection and obedience, which ends when protection ends (Ref `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`). Mark Schmitt's own revision in the preface of November 1933: decisionism now one of three types of legal thinking, the "institutional" or concrete-order thinking added (dossier, ll. ~1375–1451) — the theory adjusted to a movement that claimed to be an order, not a decision. Name the departures in this link: the posts (State Councillor, July 1933; head of the National Socialist university jurists, November 1933); the antisemitic texts of 1933–36, reported once, dated; and fear — Schleicher killed, Schmitt himself possibly spared by Göring's protection (Strong n. 59). State them as the record; do not rank them against the doctrine for the reader. Close with Strong's thesis stated as the course's own: the enemy defined the friend too easily — the theory had no account of who the friends were.
3. **Heidegger: who is he, and what did a philosopher want with a revolution?** Introduce him for the newcomer: a philosophy professor at Freiburg (in south-western Germany), author of *Being and Time* (1927), the most discussed philosophical book of the decade; it asks what it is to exist as a human being, and has no politics. Give exactly one idea from it, the one that bears on 1933: §74, "historicity" — a person exists authentically when he takes over the possibilities his heritage hands him, and a people likewise has a "destiny" (*Geschick*) it takes over together; "Dasein chooses its hero" (one quotation; anchor Dasein as Heidegger's word for the human being, "being-there"). Then the artefact, the Rectoral Address of 27 May 1933: the university's "self-assertion" — it is to lead the nation's spiritual renewal, not follow the party; "academic freedom" in the old sense "expelled"; students bound by three services — **labour service, military service, knowledge service** — so that knowledge stands beside work and arms; the last line, "All that is great stands in the storm", Heidegger's rendering of Republic 497d. Two readings, both at full strength. (a) The address as an attempt to lead the movement from within — the title says the university asserts itself; the source of the last line is Socrates' warning about the philosopher who takes the city in hand (Ref `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`: the philosopher-king and why the city resists him). Heidegger's own later account, and the standard account of the 1980s, say this; he resigned within a year. (b) The address as adherence — the three services copy the regime's own labour service and conscription; the "earth- and blood-bound strengths" of the people; and the student appeal of 3 November: "The Führer himself and alone is the present and future German reality and its law". The appeal is the document reading (a) has to absorb. Then what disposed him, from his own work: the reading of `gestalt-of-the-worker` — he held a seminar on Jünger's *Der Arbeiter* and took the Worker as the figure of man in the technological age (Ref `junger-storm-of-steel-and-the-worker`); the "three services" are the Worker-state's mobilization (`total-mobilization`) with the philosopher placed at its head. And historicity: his former student Karl Löwith reported that in 1936 Heidegger agreed his partisanship came from that concept (attributed, dated). **The difficulty as Heidegger's own:** a philosophy that says a people must choose its destiny gives no criterion for telling a destiny from a movement that claims to be one — the same gap the reader found in Schmitt's decision. **The objection, from his side:** *Being and Time* contains no word for any party, and its critique of the anonymous "they" (*das Man*) can as easily ground withdrawal from mass politics as entry into it; the decision was his, not the book's. The postwar record — the Black Notebooks' antisemitic passages published in 2014, and his silence after 1945 — is stated in two sentences here as the reason the debate did not end, and handed to the next lesson.
4. **Spengler: why did the prophet of Caesarism refuse the Caesar?** The setup from `spengler-decline-of-the-west`: the West has entered its Civilization; democracy is the rule of money and the press; a Caesarism of personal power will end it, and freedom means "doing the necessary or nothing" (Ref, `caesarism`). From this the reader expects Spengler to welcome a leader who took power from the parties. The artefact: *The Hour of Decision* (August 1933). He did welcome the national revolution — "no one could have longed for it more than I" — and in the next breath denied it was a victory: "the opponents were missing"; the seizure of power came "in a whirl of strength and weakness" and was celebrated with too much "noise"; the real decisions — world wars, world revolutions — lay ahead and would be won by a disciplined few, not by a mass party. On race he turned the Decline's position against the regime's: race is something "one has", a quality of bearing, "not a race to which one belongs" — "ethos, not zoology" (verify), the old scorn for skull-measuring (Ref, Vol. 2 ch. V). What disposed him to refuse: the morphology's contempt for parties ("Estate to Party to Following"), its Caesar who stands above parties, its rejection of race biology, and a Prussian aristocrat's distaste for mass enthusiasm. **The difficulty as Spengler's own, at full strength:** by his own doctrine a Caesar is a fact of the late Civilization to be accepted, not chosen; to refuse this one he needed a criterion for a "true" Caesar that the morphology does not contain — so his refusal rests on taste and on the Prussian ideal of *Preussentum und Sozialismus* (one clause), not on his science. **The limits of the refusal** (course.yaml key idea): he praised the revolution's destruction of Weimar, wanted dictatorship, and the book's second half rests on fear of a "coloured world revolution" — a racial theory of world politics of his own; he did not oppose the regime beyond the book; he died in 1936. State the regime's response exactly as verified (press silence, printing stopped), once.
5. **Did the doctrines decide? The test across the module.** Fill the table's last column (Figure 2) and read it: of the module's living authors in 1933, the one whose theory most predicted a Caesar refused him (Spengler); the one whose book had no politics joined (Heidegger); the theorist of the decision joined after having argued the opposite case the year before (Schmitt); the theorist of the Worker-state refused to serve the state that took his vocabulary and was told by the party paper that he was entering "the zone of head-shots" (Jünger); the theorist of sacred hierarchy was judged by the SS useless and reactionary (Evola). The one doctrine that predicted its author's distance outright is Evola's — his law classes every mass movement as the last caste. Answer the lesson's question in two parts. (a) Each doctrine disposed its author both ways: it supplied reasons to join (contempt for parliaments, the need for a decision, a destiny to be seized, the end of the bourgeois age) and reasons to refuse (aristocratic contempt for masses, a criterion of rank the movement violated). (b) What settled each choice was a premise the doctrine left open — whether established power is legitimate as such (Schmitt), whether a philosopher can lead a revolution (Heidegger), whether this Caesar is the true one (Spengler) — plus the departures (careers, fear, chance, the regime's own use and disuse of them). Introduce here, as the course's own hypothesis, the explanation by "tyrannophilia" — intellectuals drawn to rulers who seem to enact their ideas — and test it on the table: it fits Heidegger's hope and fails Spengler's refusal. Name `third-reich-idea` and `political-myth` in one sentence: the regime took Moeller's name and Sorel's method — the myth that is not a programme — which is why the doctrines' authors could each read it as theirs.
6. **The right's hardest question, stated as the right's.** The course.yaml key result: "what in their earlier work disposed each to the choice is the right's hardest question about itself." Close on its two forms, both in the thinkers' own terms, without a verdict: if a doctrine that rejects liberal norms cannot say which anti-liberal movement to refuse, it needs a norm it does not have (Schmitt's and Heidegger's case); if it can refuse only on grounds of taste and rank, it must say why its taste binds anyone else (Spengler's). The hand-off: after 1945 every right-wing thinker had to answer this in public, and the next lesson follows how.

Headings as claims (application lesson, 45 min, 5–6 sections): "Seven authors, one year"; "Schmitt in 1934: the decision declared law"; "Heidegger in 1933: the university that would lead"; "Spengler in 1933: welcome and refusal"; "What the doctrines decided, and what they left open".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Do not re-tell the Weimar collapse.** The reader has the decree counts, 20 July 1932, the Enabling Act and the bare facts of Schmitt's membership from `article-48-weimar` (prerequisite). One paragraph of recall with `<Ref>`; the new material is the documents and the 1934 purge.
- **Terms and names to anchor (L11):** NSDAP (the Nazi party's initials) — recalled from `article-48-weimar`; SA (the party's brown-shirted street army, led by Ernst Röhm, purged 30 June 1934) — link 1; SS (the party's elite guard under Himmler, which carried out the purge and later attacked Schmitt) — link 1; "Night of the Long Knives" (the name later given to the 30 June–2 July killings) — link 1; Schleicher (general, chancellor December 1932–January 1933, killed on 30 June 1934) — link 2; *Rechtsstaat* (the state bound by law — the liberal ideal Schmitt says disarms it) — link 2; *Deutsche Juristen-Zeitung* (the leading German lawyers' journal) — link 2; Prussian State Councillor (an honorary seat Göring created in 1933) — link 2; Freiburg (university city in Baden, south-west Germany) — link 3; rector (the elected head of a German university, for a one- or two-year term) — link 3; *Being and Time*, Dasein, historicity — link 3, one paragraph, no more Heideggerian vocabulary; Black Notebooks (Heidegger's private philosophical notebooks, 1931 on, published 2014) — link 3; plebiscite of 12 November 1933 (on leaving the League of Nations) — link 1 or 3; *Völkischer Beobachter* (the party's daily) — link 5; Bayreuth (the Wagner festival town where Spengler met Hitler, 1933 — verify) — link 4. No more than three new names per section: Figure 1 carries the dates so the prose need not.
- **Heidegger is new to the course.** Introduce him from zero (link 3); do not assume the reader has heard of him; do not summarise *Being and Time* beyond §74's one idea. The Strauss lesson will meet him again; leave room.
- **Dates where they diverge in the dossier:** Schmitt's entry — Muller "late April", Strong "May", the opening "1 May"; the party back-dated the spring 1933 admissions to 1 May (verify in Mehring) — so all three are reconcilable: applied in April, admitted dated 1 May. Spengler's book — the opening says "that autumn"; it appeared in August. Report the exact months in Figure 1.
- **The purge is where the exact day matters** (contingency is the point): 30 June, 1–2 July, 3 July, 13 July, 1 August 1934. Elsewhere the month.
- **The 1987 Heidegger chapter is evidence, not authority.** Its rector who "resisted Nazification" and would not "dismiss Jewish faculty members" reflects Heidegger's own 1945 account; after Ott (1988) and the Black Notebooks (2014) the record is disputed — e.g. his report on the chemist Hermann Staudinger to the ministry in 1933–34 (verify in Ott). Do not state either picture as settled; the corroboration exercise does the work. Equally, do not let the later documents turn the lesson into a prosecution: the question is what disposed him, and the reading of the address as an attempt to lead from within remains the reading of serious scholars.
- **Lilla misquotes the 1935 phrase** ("of National Socialism"); Heidegger's words are "the inner truth and greatness of this movement" (Strauss–Cropsey l. 43251). Quote the latter, or neither — the phrase belongs to the next lesson's postwar dispute (it was republished in 1953).
- **Spengler's refusal is not a resistance.** He welcomed the end of Weimar, wanted dictatorship, and built the book's second half on a racial picture of world politics ("the coloured world revolution"). The course's register: report his refusal and its limits in the same section, in the same tone; no sentence that makes him either the hero or the hypocrite of the story.
- **The regime's measure against *The Hour of Decision*** is reported in sources variously as a ban, a press blackout, or a halt to printing. Use the verified form only; course.yaml's "soon banned" is to be checked against Koktanek or Felken and stated exactly.
- **Copyright:** Schmitt (d. 1985) and Heidegger (d. 1976) are in copyright in German and English; quote a sentence at a time, three or four from each at most, in the course's own translation from the German where possible, marked so. Spengler's German is public domain; his 1934 English translation's status must be checked. Being and Time: one quotation.
- **No moralising, either way.** The register rule: The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. So "crown jurist" appears, if at all, in quotation marks with Waldemar Gurian and 1934 named; "Nazi philosopher", "opportunist", "collaborator" never appear in the course's voice. Equally, no sentence that excuses ("a man of his time", "naively"). Antisemitic writings are reported, dated, once; their content is named ("writings attacking Jewish influence on German law", 1936), not quoted.
- **The human asked that the difficulties right-wing thinkers faced be taught as their own problems.** Each of links 2–4 states the difficulty in the thinker's own terms before any objection: Schmitt's — a decisionist cannot judge a decision; Heidegger's — a philosophy of destiny cannot tell a destiny from a movement that claims to be one, and a philosopher who would lead the city must first be accepted by it; Spengler's — a prophet of the necessary cannot refuse the necessary except by a standard his science lacks. Link 6 states the right's question in its own voice.
- **Do not make 1933 the measure of the whole tradition.** The question here is what disposed three men; whether the tradition itself is implicated is `the-right-after-1945`'s question. One sentence at the end hands it on; do not answer it here.
- **Where the dossier is thin — say so in References:** *The Reckless Mind* (the course.yaml note's source for Heidegger and Schmitt) is NOT in the dossier; nor is any Heidegger text, nor the 1934 article, nor *The Hour of Decision*, nor any Spengler biography; Fawcett has nothing on Spengler after 1922. The dossier's own anchors are Strong's foreword and Schwab's introduction (index 34), Muller's Schmitt headnote, Fawcett on Schmitt and Jünger, the Strauss–Cropsey Heidegger chapter, the Shipwrecked Mind passages, and Jünger's 1980 recollection. Everything else is obtained as listed and verified before it is quoted.
- **L8a:** the body names Schmitt, Heidegger, Spengler, Jünger, Evola, Moeller, Sorel, Plato (the material) and participants (Hitler, Göring, Himmler, Schleicher, Löwith); never Strong, Schwab, Lilla, Gillespie, Fawcett, Muller, Ott, Krockow, Mehring. ## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (warm-up).** Put in order and attach to Schmitt, Heidegger or Spengler: the Rectoral Address; the meeting at Bayreuth; the statute of 3 July 1934; the article of 1 August 1934; the student appeal; *The Hour of Decision*; the Enabling Act (Ref `article-48-weimar`); the SS attack on Schmitt. Then say which two events fall in the same summer as the address, and why that simultaneity matters for the lesson's question.
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill.** "The true leader is always also judge. Judgeship flows from leadership." Metadata first (author: the best-known constitutional lawyer of the Republic, now a party member and State Councillor; date: 1 August 1934, a month after the killings, among them his patron; venue: the lawyers' journal; purpose). Predict before reading what such a source will claim and omit. Records / argues / omits (omits that the 3 July law was retroactive, and the names of the dead). What it can be used to establish (how the regime's legal claims were argued in public by its jurists; how decisionism was applied to a concrete case) and what it cannot (what Schmitt privately believed, or whether fear moved him — that needs other documents, e.g. the "belly of the fish" poem).
3. **Corroboration — two sources that conflict.** A reference account written in 1987: Heidegger accepted the rectorate "to help preserve [the university's] independence", the address had a "decidedly un-Nazi title", and he resisted the "Nazification of the university". Against it, the student appeal of 3 November 1933: "The Führer himself and alone is the present and future German reality and its law." State what each establishes, where they agree (he hoped the university would lead), where they conflict, what the 1987 account's silence about the appeal establishes about that account, and what further documents would settle the question of his conduct as rector (the rectorate files, the Black Notebooks).
4. **Claim to refute (E3: earlier lessons).** "Decisionism made Schmitt's adherence inevitable." Break it at the step that fails: in 1932 the same theory argued for denying the equal chance to anti-constitutional parties (Ref `article-48-weimar`); the move to "the one who has decided is the law" needs the additional premise that established authority is legitimate because established — Maistre's "any government is good once established" (Ref `maistre-and-bonald`) — which Hobbes himself limited: obedience lasts as long as protection (Ref `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty`). Then state what survives of the claim (the theory supplied no norm by which to refuse).
5. **Boundary probe.** Spengler: "freedom means doing the necessary or nothing", and Caesarism is necessary. Take the degenerate case — the Caesar arrives, and he is a party leader carried by a mass movement. What does the morphology tell Spengler to do? Show that it contains no criterion distinguishing a "true" Caesar from a false one, so that his refusal must rest on something outside it (the Prussian ideal, aristocratic taste, the rejection of race biology), and state what that does to his claim that history is read, not willed.
6. **Derivation the lesson did not do.** From *Being and Time*'s one idea used in the lesson — a people takes over its destiny by choosing among the possibilities its heritage hands down — derive both conclusions a reader could draw in 1933: adherence (the national revolution is that choice) and refusal (a movement of the anonymous "they" is the opposite of an authentic choice). Then say which premise Heidegger would need to add to reach the first, and where the address supplies it (the university's leadership of the people's "spiritual world").
7. **Research-shaped task.** Design the study that would distinguish "doctrine disposed them" from "career, fear and chance disposed them" across the module's seven authors (Figure 2). Name the variables (age and academic position in 1933; prior party contacts; date of joining relative to the risks of not joining; Jewish colleagues and students; what each lost or gained), the documents for each (party file dates, private letters, the Black Notebooks, Spengler's correspondence, the SS dossier on Evola), and which result would count against the doctrinal explanation.
8. **FORWARD (E7).** In 1945 Spengler is dead; Schmitt is interned by the Americans; Heidegger faces a university committee and the French occupation authorities; Jünger is asked to fill in the Allied questionnaire on his past. From the 1933–34 documents alone, predict what line each of the three survivors will take in public after the war — recant, fall silent, re-describe what he did, or refuse to answer — and give the evidence for each prediction.

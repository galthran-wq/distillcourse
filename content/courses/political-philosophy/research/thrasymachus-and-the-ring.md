# Research brief: thrasymachus-and-the-ring (Justice as the interest of the stronger: Republic I–II)

status: planned
module: greek-discovery
type: core
goal: Analyze Thrasymachus's and Glaucon's case that justice is a convention serving the strong, and Socrates's first replies

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `greek-miracle`: the polis as the community that puts power and law "in the middle" and argues about them; written law as a human standard anyone may dispute; Anacharsis's jibe that laws are cobwebs the strong tear through (the FORWARD exercise there asked the reader to state it at full strength). The reader knows no Plato and has met no Socrates.
- **Contributes:** One move: the realist challenge stated at its strongest and shown to be unanswerable by consequences. Thrasymachus says the law IS the interest of the ruling party; Glaucon says justice is a bargain among the weak, kept only from fear of being caught — the ring of Gyges. Socrates's Book I replies (ruler fallibility, the craft argument, pleonexia, the function argument) win the exchange and lose the war: Glaucon shows that any defence of justice by its rewards concedes the point. The reader ends able to state the challenge in their own words and to say why it forces Plato to build a city.
- **Hands off:** The challenge is open. To show justice good in itself, Plato must find what justice IS, and he proposes to read it "in large letters" in a city built from scratch — `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`. Thrasymachus is not refuted here and the course says so; he returns as Carneades (`cicero-natural-law`), as Machiavelli (`machiavelli-effectual-truth`), as the materialist of Laws X (`platos-laws-second-best-city`), and as Schmitt (`schmitt-concept-of-the-political`).
- **Spine step:** "Plato answers 'who should rule' with knowledge: justice is each part doing its own work, so the city should be ruled by those who know the good — and freedom left to itself slides through democracy into tyranny"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): a shepherd finds a ring of invisibility; if he keeps behaving well anyway, is he just or a fool? A young man wants to be shown that justice is good for the one who practises it. The lesson follows the challenge and the first attempts to answer it. The felt win (C3): within ten minutes the reader states the challenge in their own words — "if nobody can see you, why be just?" — and sees why every answer that appeals to reputation or reward has already lost.

1. **Why start from an old man's commonplace?** Cephalus says the best thing about wealth is that it lets you die owing nothing and having deceived no one — justice is telling the truth and paying debts. The obvious attempt: that is a decent working definition. Why it fails: a friend lends you a sword and asks for it back when he has gone mad. One counterexample, and the definition is dead. Answer: the method of the whole book is on display — a definition must survive every case, and a counterexample kills it. Because: justice is being asked for as a thing with a nature, not a list of decent habits. (Two paragraphs; Cephalus leaves "to attend to the sacrifices".)
2. **"Give each his due" — help friends, harm enemies.** Obvious attempt: the inherited morality of the Greek gentleman. Why it fails three ways: (a) in every real use another art does the work (the physician heals; justice guards what is not in use — "the good keeper is the good thief"); (b) we misjudge friends and enemies, so the rule makes us harm the good; (c) harming anyone makes him worse at the human excellence, and justice cannot be what makes men worse. Answer: the saying belongs to "a tyrant-like rich man" — the first hint that conventional morality is somebody's interest dressed up. Because: the three refutations each remove a piece of the definition, and what is left is a question, not a rule.
3. **Thrasymachus: justice is the interest of the stronger.** He bursts in "like a wild beast" and forbids Socrates to answer with "the useful" or "the dutiful". At full strength (quote l. 735–744): every regime — tyranny, democracy, aristocracy — makes laws for its own interest and calls obedience justice; so "everywhere there is one principle of justice, which is the interest of the stronger". Mark where this grows out of `greek-miracle`: Anacharsis said the law was a cobweb; Thrasymachus says the law is a net the strong wove for their own catch. The obvious attempt to dismiss it: rulers make mistakes, so obeying them sometimes hurts them — then justice is NOT their interest. Thrasymachus's escape: the ruler "in the strict sense", like the physician in the strict sense, never errs. Because: he has to concede that ruling is a craft to keep his thesis, and that concession is what Socrates uses.
4. **The craft argument and the shepherd's reply — the heading is a question: "whom does a craft serve?"** Socrates: every craft serves the good of its subject — medicine the body, piloting the sailors — so ruling in the strict sense serves the ruled. Thrasymachus's second speech, at full strength: the shepherd fattens the sheep for his master's table and his own; the just man pays more tax, gets less from every contract, neglects his affairs in office and is hated by his friends for refusing favours; the man who steals a purse is a thief, the man who enslaves a whole city is "happy and blessed" — people blame injustice because they fear suffering it, not because they fear doing it. The lesson concedes that this is the strongest statement of the realist position in the book and that Socrates's reply — pay is a separate art; good men rule only under the penalty of being ruled by someone worse — answers the craft point and not the shepherd. Because: "ruling is a craft" shows what a true ruler would do, not that any ruler does it.
5. **Three more replies, and why they win the exchange.** (a) Pleonexia: the just man does not try to outdo the just, only the unjust; the knower (musician, doctor) does not try to outdo his like; so injustice is like ignorance and justice like knowledge — Thrasymachus blushes "on a hot summer's day". (b) Injustice disables joint action: a robber band needs a remnant of justice to rob at all; a city, an army, a single soul full of injustice cannot act. (c) The function argument: every thing has a work it alone or best does (a pruning-hook prunes); the soul's work is to rule, deliberate and live; justice is the excellence that lets it do that work; so the just live well. Socrates then confesses he has learned nothing of what justice IS. Because: the three replies show that injustice is self-defeating in various ways, and none says what justice is — Book I ends in deliberate failure.
6. **Glaucon restarts the challenge at a level consequences cannot reach — the STRONGEST OBJECTION, from inside the text.** Three classes of goods: wanted for themselves (harmless pleasures), for themselves and their results (health), only for their results (medicine, money-making). The many put justice in the third class. Glaucon wants it shown to belong in the second — good in itself, apart from reward. His genealogy: doing injustice is best, suffering it worst; those who cannot do it without suffering it make a bargain and call the bargain justice — a convention of the weak. Then the ring (set piece 4, told in full): give the just man and the unjust man each a ring of invisibility and "both come at last to the same point". Then the two statues: the perfectly unjust man with a perfect reputation for justice, the perfectly just man thought unjust, "scourged, racked, bound, his eyes burnt out, and finally impaled". Which life is better? Adeimantus adds: everyone who praises justice — parents, poets, priests — praises its wages; nobody has ever praised what it does in the soul. Because: any answer that mentions reputation, reward, gods or punishment has accepted that justice is in the third class; the only move left is to say what justice does inside the one who has it.
7. **Where the lesson concedes and where it replies.** Concede: Thrasymachus's description of actual regimes is not refuted in Book I and is, as description, often true (the course will meet it again in Aristotle's "every regime legislates for its rulers", `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`). Reply, as Plato's: the description assumes it knows what the ruler's interest is, and that is exactly what is in dispute — the craft argument shows that "interest" needs knowledge, and knowledge is what Socrates will build the city to find. The reader is told plainly that this is a promise, not a proof.
8. **Why a city?** Socrates's method: justice in the individual is small print; the same word written large in a city can be read first. The lesson ends on the question the city must answer: what does justice do in a soul such that a man with the ring would still practise it? `<Conclusion>` names `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`.

Headings that fall out: "One counterexample kills a definition" · "Help friends, harm enemies: whose morality is this?" · "Thrasymachus: the law is what the rulers' interest says it is" · "Whom does a craft serve? The physician, the pilot, the shepherd" · "Three ways injustice defeats itself — and what they do not show" · "Glaucon's ring: justice without witnesses" · "Why consequences cannot answer the ring" · "Reading justice in large letters".

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The madman's sword** (Book I, l. 212–224): a friend deposits weapons, goes mad, and asks for them back. Repaying the debt is unjust. Four sentences; the lesson's model of refutation by counterexample, reused in the exercises.
2. **The twelve analogy** (l. 606–661): Thrasymachus demands an answer that is not "the useful, the dutiful, the advantageous"; Socrates: as if you asked what twelve is and forbade 2×6 and 3×4. Half a paragraph; it shows what Thrasymachus is doing — ruling out the answer in advance.
3. **The regime table** (l. 735–744): Thrasymachus's own list — tyranny, democracy, aristocracy — each makes laws in its rulers' interest and calls obedience justice. Render as the lesson's first table (figure 2) and quote the sentence. This is concept `justice-as-advantage-of-the-stronger` and must be exact.
4. **The ring of Gyges, told in full** (Book II, l. 1829–1879): Gyges the shepherd, ancestor of Croesus the Lydian; the storm and earthquake; the hollow bronze horse with doors; the giant corpse wearing only a gold ring; the monthly shepherds' meeting at which he turns the collet inward and vanishes from the conversation; the embassy to court, the queen, the murder, the kingdom. Then the twist: two rings, one for the just and one for the unjust man, and "no man can be imagined to be of such an iron nature that he would stand fast in justice"; whoever did would be "a most wretched idiot", praised to his face by men who fear each other. Reproduce the whole passage in a `<Quote>` (it is public domain, Jowett) and then have the reader restate the claim in one sentence (`<Task>`). This is the felt win and sits within the lesson's first ten minutes of reading — move Cephalus and Polemarchus to two tight paragraphs so the ring arrives early, or open section 1 with the ring and go back to Book I (the opening already does this).
5. **The shepherd and the sheep** (l. 994–1016): Thrasymachus's second speech; then the ledger of the just man's losses — contracts, taxes, office, friends (l. 1017–1049) — and "the man who enslaves a city is called happy". Reconstruct as Thrasymachus's strongest case, in his own words, before any reply.
6. **Why good men rule at all** (l. 1175–1215): pay in money, honour, or the penalty of being ruled by someone worse; a city of good men would quarrel to avoid office as men now quarrel to get it. Carry forward: this is the seed of `philosopher-king` (the reluctant ruler of Republic VII).
7. **The two statues** (Book II, l. 1881–1948): Glaucon "polishes up" two men — the perfectly unjust with a perfect reputation, the perfectly just with none, impaled. Build the 2×2 grid (figure 4) and work the cells: Glaucon's claim is that the many prefer the top-left (be unjust, seem just) and pity the bottom-right.
8. **The robber band** (l. 1556–1570) and **the pruning-hook** (l. 1601–1612): two short worked examples for the Book I replies — a gang needs justice among its members to rob; a tool has a work and an excellence for it.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11), in chain order:** Socrates (Athenian, 469–399, executed by the restored democracy; Plato's teacher and the speaker of these dialogues — one sentence, no biography); Plato (c. 428–348; the Republic written c. 380, a generation after Socrates's death); the Piraeus (Athens's harbour, a few miles from the city); Cephalus (a wealthy old arms-manufacturer, resident alien), Polemarchus (his son), Thrasymachus (a teacher of rhetoric from Chalcedon — anchor "sophist" here: a paid teacher of persuasion), Glaucon and Adeimantus (Plato's brothers); Gyges, Croesus, Lydia (a kingdom in western Anatolia; Croesus its last king, c. 550, a byword for wealth); Simonides, Pindar, Aeschylus when quoted (poets — one clause each); pleonexia (the wish to have more than one's share — define once, keep the Greek because the course reuses it in Vernant's sense from `greek-miracle`); ergon/arete (work and excellence — translate, keep the Greek in parentheses once). Stephanus numbers: the Jowett text has none; if the writer adds them from a standard edition (Thrasymachus 338c, Gyges 359d–360d), they go in References, not prose.
- **Thrasymachus at full strength, and NOT refuted** (course.yaml notes): the lesson must not pretend Book I settles anything — Socrates says himself that he has learned nothing. QA will check that the shepherd speech appears before any reply and is never summarised as "cynical".
- **Do not confuse Thrasymachus with Glaucon.** Thrasymachus: justice is a tool of the rulers (the law serves the strong). Glaucon: justice is a compromise of the weak (the law protects those who cannot dominate). They are two different genealogies and the lesson's figure 3 keeps them apart. Strauss's distinction (vulgar vs philosophic conventionalism) is useful here but is stated as the course's own.
- **The ring is not about secrecy alone.** The point is the convergence of the just and unjust once detection is removed — the concept definition says so. A reading that makes it "people cheat when unwatched" misses the argument about justice's class among goods.
- **The social-contract origin (l. 1813–1828) is Glaucon's report of "what people say"**, not Plato's view and not Hobbes's; the lesson flags it as the first contract theory in the course and Refs forward to `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` in one clause. Do not teach Hobbes.
- **No "Plato the totalitarian" slot here.** Popper belongs to `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king` References. Nothing in Books I–II supports or invites it.
- **No real misconception slot (L13)** beyond the Thrasymachus/Glaucon conflation above, which is a documented student error; do not invent "some believe".
- **Where the summary and primary text agree**: all checked pointers (l. 708, 735–744, 1829–1879) match the text. The summary's shard-1 line numbers are slice lines; the full-file numbers are +8609.
- **Numbers:** none needed except the dramatic dates (Socrates's death 399; the Republic c. 380) and these to the decade. No "500 jurors".
- **Do not re-teach `greek-miracle`.** The agora and written law are recalled in one clause with a `<Ref>` where Thrasymachus enters; Anacharsis's cobweb is recalled in one clause as the reader's own forward exercise.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (warm-up, the one mechanical item).** List the three definitions of justice refuted in Book I and the single counterexample or argument that killed each. From `greek-miracle` (E3): which Greek phrase named the place the law was put so that anyone could dispute it, and how does Thrasymachus's thesis depend on the law being disputable?
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill) on l. 735–744.** Author (Plato, c. 380), speaker (Thrasymachus), dramatic audience (Socrates and the young men), setting (a wealthy arms-dealer's house a generation after the democracy executed Socrates): what does the passage record about Greek regimes, what does it argue, and what does it omit (any claim about what the rulers' interest actually is)? Closing question: what can a speech Plato wrote for an opponent be USED to establish about Greek opinion in 380 — and what can it not?
3. **Claim to refute.** "Socrates refutes Thrasymachus in Book I: the craft argument shows that rulers serve the ruled." Name the step that fails (the craft argument describes the ruler in the strict sense, not any actual ruler; Thrasymachus's shepherd speech is never answered on its own ground) and state what Socrates himself says at the end of Book I.
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped: run the ring backwards.** Suppose a man who, holding the ring, still does no wrong. Glaucon says onlookers would call him an idiot. Derive what Glaucon must believe about the class of goods justice belongs to for that judgment to follow, and show that the same belief makes Adeimantus's complaint about parents and poets inevitable. Solution shows the inference chain from the three classes of goods.
5. **Boundary probe.** Take Glaucon's contract genealogy and set the number of weak parties to one: a single man who cannot dominate, facing many who can. Does any "justice" arise? Then set the strong to one and the weak to many (the tyrant and the city). Which of the two genealogies — Thrasymachus's or Glaucon's — describes each case, and what does that show about whether they are rivals or two halves of one picture?
6. **Synthesis / counter-argument stem.** "Someone claims that Thrasymachus and Anacharsis (from `greek-miracle`) are saying the same thing." Name two facts the claim fails to account for (Anacharsis says the strong tear the law; Thrasymachus says the strong wrote it; Solon's reply about contracts answers Anacharsis and not Thrasymachus) and state what would have to be true for it to survive.
7. **Document reasoning, paired sources.** Set the shepherd speech (l. 994–1049) beside Socrates's "why good men rule" (l. 1175–1215). Where do they agree (office is a burden the just man loses by)? Where do they disagree (whether the loss shows justice is folly or shows what a true ruler is)? Write the evidence-based judgment the sourcing drill ends in.
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`.** Glaucon demands that justice be shown good "in the soul, unseen by gods and men". List what you would need to know about a soul to meet the demand (what its parts are, which should rule, what happens when the wrong one does). The solution says in one sentence that this is the inventory the next lesson builds a city to read off, `<Ref>` to it; and notes that the reader has also just stated why Socrates says good men would have to be compelled to rule — the doorstep of the philosopher-king.

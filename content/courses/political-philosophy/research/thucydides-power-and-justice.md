# Research brief: thucydides-power-and-justice (Thucydides: the Funeral Oration, Mytilene and Melos)

status: planned
module: greek-discovery
type: core
goal: Analyze how Thucydides's speeches set Athens's account of its own freedom against the argument that the strong do what they can, and what each speech claims about who should rule

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `greek-miracle` only (the reader's one earlier lesson): the polis as a
  community that puts power, law and debate "in the middle" (es meson) of men who count one
  another as equals; isonomia; written law as a public standard anyone may dispute; the two
  equalities (geometric, shares by worth; arithmetic, one share each) that the Greek cities
  never reconciled; Cleisthenes' Athens (508/7) with its Council and assembly; Sparta as the
  hoplite city with kings, overseers and unwritten law; the Persian invasion and Plataea (479)
  as the timeline's last anchor; Anacharsis's jibe that written laws are spiders' webs the
  strong tear through, and Solon's reply that men keep agreements when neither side gains by
  breaking them. The reader has met no historian, no Pericles, no empire and no war between
  Greek cities.
- **Contributes:** One move: the same city that described itself as a community of equals who
  decide by speech ruled other cities by force, and its own speakers said so; Thucydides
  records three moments — the Funeral Oration (the city's account of its freedom), the
  Mytilene debate (the assembly deciding, in the open, whether to kill a rebel ally, on
  grounds of justice or of interest) and the Melian dialogue (the envoys' thesis that justice
  is in question only between equals in power, and "the strong do what they can and the weak
  suffer what they must"). The reader learns that these are not three contradictory voices but
  one principle applied to two domains: equality, speech and law hold among those who count as
  equals; between unequal powers, on the envoys' account, there is no common standard to appeal
  to. The concepts `funeral-oration-ideal` and `athenian-thesis` are introduced; `polis`,
  `isonomia`, `es-meson` are reinforced by being shown at work and at their boundary (the city
  wall). C3 felt win: the reader states the Athenians' argument at Melos and the Melians' three
  replies in his own words, with the Athenian answer to each.
- **Hands off:** The Athenian thesis is a claim about relations between cities, where there is
  no common law. It leaves open whether the same thing is true inside a city: whether the law
  "in the middle" is itself only the interest of whoever is stronger there. Thrasymachus says
  it is, to Socrates, in an Athenian house during these same war years —
  `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`, which must read him as the theory of what the Athenians did at
  Melos. The "who should rule" answers recorded here (the many, with office by merit; settled
  law and plain men over clever speakers; an assembly guided by a first citizen; the stronger)
  return as Plato's charge against the assembly (`kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king`, the
  Ship of State; `decline-of-regimes`, the democratic man) and as Aristotle's feast and polity
  (`aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`, which can recall Thucydides' own praise of the
  Five Thousand's "fusion of the high and the low"). The thesis returns at Rome as Carneades's
  case that Rome grew by taking what was not its own (`cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`).
  What this lesson DECIDES: the Athenian thesis between cities. As a description of how cities
  at war behaved it is borne out on both sides; as a claim that fairness protects no one it
  fails on the record of 404 (on balance). What passes on is only the question inside the city.
- **Spine step:** "Political theory began where kingship broke: the Greek polis placed power 'in the middle' of a community of peers, and its own historian recorded both the city's account of its freedom and the argument that the strong do what they can"

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** the Athenian thesis at Melos — justice arises only between equals in power,
  and fairness is worthless to the weak ("the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what
  they must", V.89; "a necessary law of their nature", V.105).
- **Verdict:** split. As a description of how cities at war behaved, upheld: it is borne out on
  both sides. As a claim that fairness protects no one, it fails on the record of 404. Grade:
  **on balance**.
- **What decides it:** the description half — Sparta did not come for Melos (V.116); Sparta
  killed the Plataeans after the siege of 427 (III.68); Athens destroyed Scione and Melos. The
  claim half — the record of 404, after Thucydides breaks off, in Xenophon's *Hellenica* (Brownson
  tr., Perseus section numbers): when the news of Aegospotami reached Athens, the Athenians feared
  they would "suffer such treatment as they had visited upon the Melians" (II.2.3); Lysander
  restored the Melians to their island (II.2.9); and Sparta refused the Corinthian and Theban
  demand to destroy Athens, saying "they would not enslave a Greek city which had done great
  service amid the greatest perils that had befallen Greece" (II.2.19–20). The Melians' first
  reply — fairness is "our common protection", and "your fall would be a signal for the heaviest
  vengeance" — is what the record of 404 bears out: the appeal to a common standard and to past
  service counted when Athens was the weak party.
- **Strongest objection, and why it does not overturn the verdict:** Sparta's restraint can be
  read as interest, not fairness — a ruined Athens would have left Thebes and Corinth stronger,
  so a weakened Athens was a counterweight. On that reading the thesis survives intact: interest
  spared Athens. It does not overturn the verdict because Sparta's stated ground was Athens's
  past service, the very kind of appeal the envoys called worthless, and the Athenians' own fear
  in II.2.3 shows they expected the standard they had denied at Melos to be turned on them. Both
  motives can be present; the thesis said fairness counts for nothing, and in 404 it counted for
  something. That the motive is mixed is why the grade is on balance and not strong.
- **What would overturn it:** a reading of Sparta's restraint in 404 as pure balance-of-power
  calculation that gives no weight to the appeal to Athens's past service.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim): "In 431 BC Athens buries its first war dead and its leading
citizen tells the crowd that their city is a school for all Greece, free and open, governed by
the many. Four years later the same assembly votes to kill every man of a rebel ally and changes
its mind overnight; eleven years after that its envoys tell a small neutral island that justice
is a question only between equals in power. A single historian recorded all three speeches. The
lesson asks how one city could say all three things, and what the speeches show about who should
rule."

Then develop the problem (L10a) in plain words: the reader has just seen (`<Ref>`
`greek-miracle`) how the Greek city put power in the middle of equals and made justice a public,
disputable text. The obvious expectation is that such a city, of all cities, would treat justice
as binding everywhere. The record shows the opposite, and the city's own speakers say why. The
answer the lesson reaches: the three speeches apply one principle to two domains — among those
who count as equals, speech, law and shares; toward those who do not, power — and the Melian
dialogue is the place where that principle is stated without disguise. The felt win (C3) is a
`<Task>` in the Melos section: state the Athenians' argument and the Melians' three replies, with
the answer to each, in your own words.

Section order follows the opening's order (the reader expects it); the historian comes first,
briefly, because every quotation depends on what a Thucydidean speech is.

1. **Who recorded these speeches, and what is a speech in his book?** The obvious attempt: read
   the speeches as transcripts. Why it fails: Thucydides says he could not carry them word for
   word and made each speaker "say what was in my opinion demanded of them by the various
   occasions … adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what they really said"
   (I.22). The answer: anchor Thucydides (L11) — an Athenian who commanded a squadron in 424,
   failed to save Amphipolis (a city on the north Aegean coast), and spent twenty years in exile
   "present with both parties" (V.26); he "lived through the whole" of the war and wrote it as
   "a possession for all time" (I.22). So a speech is the historian's reconstruction of the
   argument the occasion called for: good evidence of what arguments were thinkable and
   answerable in Athens, weaker evidence of anyone's exact words. Because: every later section
   quotes these speeches, and the reader must know from the first what they can be used to
   establish (playbook-history: metadata first). Then the war in three sentences: the
   Peloponnesian War (431–404) between Athens with its empire of tribute-paying allies — mostly
   island and coastal cities that had joined Athens against Persia after 479 (`<Ref>`
   `greek-miracle`'s Plataea anchor) and had become its subjects — and Sparta with its allies;
   "the growth of the power of Athens, and the alarm which this inspired in Lacedaemon, made war
   inevitable" (I.23). Timeline figure here.

2. **What did Athens say held it together? The Funeral Oration.** Set the scene from the text:
   the bones laid out by tribe, the empty bier "for the missing", the speaker "chosen by the
   state" — Pericles (anchor: the leading Athenian statesman of the 440s–430s, general year
   after year; died 429). The obvious expectation for a war funeral: praise of the dead's
   courage. Pericles instead praises the constitution first, because the city is what they died
   for (l. 66–72). The claims, each quoted short and then stated plainly (concept
   `funeral-oration-ideal`):
   (a) WHO RULES — "Its administration favours the many instead of the few; this is why it is
   called a democracy". Mark the seam to `greek-miracle`: this is isonomia in practice; and the
   same paragraph also says office goes by "reputation for capacity … class considerations not
   being allowed to interfere with merit", and poverty does not bar it. Ask the reader (one
   sentence of the lesson's own, not a Task) which of the two equalities each clause invokes:
   equal justice in private disputes is arithmetic, office by merit is geometric — Pericles
   claims Athens has both at once, which `greek-miracle` said no city had reconciled
   (`<Ref id="two-equalities-the-city-could-not-reconcile">`).
   (b) FREEDOM WITH LAW — "we do not feel called upon to be angry with our neighbour for doing
   what he likes", and yet "this ease … does not make us lawless", because fear (respect) of
   magistrates and of laws written and unwritten restrains. Private freedom, public obedience.
   (c) THE CITIZEN — the man who takes no part is "useless", not quiet; ordinary citizens "are
   able to judge at all events if we cannot originate"; discussion is "an indispensable
   preliminary to any wise action" — speech in the middle as the city's method, stated by the
   city.
   (d) THE OPEN CITY AND THE SCHOOL — no expulsions of foreigners; courage "not of art but of
   nature", against Sparta's "painful discipline"; "as a city we are the school of Hellas".
   (e) WHAT HOLDS IT TOGETHER — not calculation: "feed your eyes upon her from day to day, till
   love of her fills your hearts"; the dead "escaped, not from their fear, but from their
   glory".
   Then the boundary of the ideal, from inside the same speech, stated without adjective: the
   power that proves the city's worth is an empire — "we have forced every sea and land to be
   the highway of our daring, and everywhere, whether for evil or for good, have left
   imperishable monuments", and Athens's subjects, he says, cannot "question her title by merit
   to rule" (l. 154–172); the women's virtue is to be "least talked of among the men" (l.
   264–268). Because: the city's account of its freedom is an account of relations among
   citizens; the speech itself shows two other relations, to subjects and to women, governed
   by something else. Keep this as the setup of the lesson's question, not as an accusation.

3. **What does an assembly of equals do with a rebel ally? Mytilene, 427.** The problem as the
   Athenians met it: Mytilene, the chief city of the island of Lesbos (anchor: off the coast of
   Asia Minor), an ally with its own fleet and walls, revolted in 428 and asked Sparta for help;
   Athens besieged and took it. The assembly — the many, deciding by speech — voted "in the fury
   of the moment" to kill "the whole adult male population" and enslave the women and children,
   and sent a ship with the order. "The morrow brought repentance"; a second assembly was
   called. Two speeches, at full strength, in turn:
   CLEON (anchor: "by far the most powerful with the commons"): a democracy "is incapable of
   empire", because citizens who live without fear of one another extend the same ease to
   subjects; "your empire is a despotism"; bad laws kept are better than good laws unsettled;
   ordinary men govern better than clever ones; the assembly has become "the audience of a
   rhetorician"; Mytilene's was "deliberate" aggression, so justice and interest coincide in
   punishing all; and the dilemma he puts to the assembly — "if they were right in rebelling, you must be wrong
   in ruling … or else you must give up your empire and cultivate honesty without danger".
   DIODOTUS (otherwise unknown — say so in a clause): "we are not in a court of justice, but in
   a political assembly; and the question is not justice, but how to make the Mitylenians
   useful to Athens"; the death penalty does not deter, because hope and desire are "invisible
   agents, far stronger than the dangers that are seen"; if surrender and resistance are
   punished alike, every rebel city will fight to the end and Athens will inherit a ruined town
   with no revenue; the common people of every allied city are Athens's friends, and killing
   Mytilene's people would hand them to their oligarchs; better "voluntarily to put up with
   injustice". The reversal the lesson marks (L10b, dry): from a contest between justice and
   interest one would expect the merciful side to argue from justice; here the harsh speaker
   argues from justice and the merciful one from interest alone, and wins. The vote was "almost
   equal"; the second galley, its rowers eating at the oar, arrived as the decree was being
   read. Then the outcome the extract omits (III.50): on Cleon's motion "rather more than a
   thousand" men sent to Athens as ringleaders were killed, the walls pulled down and the land
   given to Athenian settlers. Bearing on the lesson's question (L10e): inside the assembly,
   equals argued in the open and changed their minds — the polis working as `greek-miracle`
   described it; the object of the argument was a city that had no voice in it. And both
   speakers, harsh and mild, agreed that the empire must be kept and that its rule was not
   over equals.
   Who should rule, in this section's terms: Cleon — settled laws and plain men, not clever
   speakers; Diodotus — an assembly that hears advisers out and holds them, not itself,
   accountable. Both answers are about the many governing themselves well; neither asks whether
   the many should govern Mytilene.

4. **What is said when nothing has to be pretended? Melos, 416 — the lesson's centre and the
   felt win.** Anchor: Melos, a small island in the southern Aegean, a colony of Sparta, which
   had stayed out of the war, was raided by Athens (426) and became hostile; in 416 an Athenian
   force landed and sent envoys. Note the detail the text gives first: the Melians hear them not
   before their people but before "the magistrates and the few", and the Athenians remark that
   this is so the many will not be swayed — a small mirror of the question of who should rule.
   The format: no speeches, question and answer. Then the argument in the order the text gives
   it, built as a `<FigureSequence>` (figure 5), one exchange per frame:
   (i) THE THESIS. The Athenians will not claim a right to empire for having beaten the Persians,
   or that Melos wronged them; the Melians should not plead their neutrality or innocence;
   "right, as the world goes, is only in question between equals in power, while the strong do
   what they can and the weak suffer what they must" (V.89). Concept `athenian-thesis`. State
   what it says and what it does NOT say (L13, the real misconception — "might makes right"):
   the envoys do not say their act is just; they say justice is not the question where power is
   unequal, because justice presupposes parties each able to make the other pay — which is
   Solon's condition for keeping agreements (`<Ref>` `greek-miracle`, Anacharsis and Solon) run
   between a strong and a weak city. Drill the refutation schema once: correct claim →
   misconception → why it fails (they reserve "right" for equals; they deny that the question
   arises, not that justice exists) → replacement → discriminating example (their own advice at
   the end: be moderate toward inferiors — a prudential maxim, not a claim of right).
   (ii) MELIAN REPLY 1: fairness as everyone's protection — do not destroy "our common
   protection, the privilege of being allowed in danger to invoke what is fair and right",
   because "your fall would be a signal for the heaviest vengeance". At full strength: this
   answers the Athenians on their own ground, interest, by pointing out that power changes
   hands. Answer: we fear our own subjects more than any rival empire; and we will gain by
   not destroying you, you by submitting before suffering the worst.
   (iii) Neutrality: why not friends of both? Answer: "your friendship will be an argument to
   our subjects of our weakness, and your enmity of our power" — the empire rests on reputation,
   so a free island is a standing advertisement against it.
   (iv) Honour and hope: it would be "baseness and cowardice" not to resist; war's fortune is
   sometimes even. Answer: this is no contest of equals with honour as the prize; hope is
   "danger's comforter", and the weak who turn to "prophecies and oracles" are ruined by it;
   "that idea of disgrace" enslaves men "by the mere influence of a seductive name".
   (v) MELIAN REPLY 2, the gods — "we are just men fighting against unjust". Answer, quoted in
   full (V.105): "Of the gods we believe, and of men we know, that by a necessary law of their
   nature they rule wherever they can … we found it existing before us, and shall leave it to
   exist for ever after us; all we do is to make use of it, knowing that you and everybody else,
   having the same power as we have, would do the same as we do." The thesis is now a law of
   nature, for gods and men alike, and the Athenians claim no exemption from it.
   (vi) MELIAN REPLY 3, Sparta — kinship and shame will bring help. Answer: the Spartans are
   "most conspicuous in considering what is agreeable honourable, and what is expedient just";
   an ally trusts "a decided superiority of power", not goodwill; Athens commands the sea.
   (vii) The close: the Athenians' advice — "those who do not yield to their equals, who keep
   terms with their superiors, and are moderate towards their inferiors, on the whole succeed
   best"; the Melians refuse to take freedom from "a city that has been inhabited these seven
   hundred years"; the Athenians' parting line that they will be "most completely deceived".
   Then the outcome in Thucydides' own one sentence (V.116): the grown men killed, women and
   children sold, five hundred Athenian colonists sent. Spartan help never came.
   THE TASK (C3 felt win), placed right after frame 7: "Before reading on, write the Athenians'
   argument in three sentences of your own, then each Melian reply in one sentence with the
   Athenian answer to it." Give a model answer in the following paragraph (not hidden), so the
   reader can check: the Athenian thesis (justice only between equals; the strong do what they
   can; this is a law of nature for gods and men), and the three replies with their answers
   (common protection → we fear subjects, not rivals; the gods → the gods rule where they can
   too; Sparta → the Spartans call the expedient just). Because: this is where the reader must
   be able to say the argument at full strength in his own words; everything after it builds on
   that.

5. **How could one city say all three things? The one principle in two domains — the lesson's
   answer.** The obvious reading: hypocrisy, or a democracy corrupted by war. Why it fails as the
   whole answer (state it without either label): (a) the Melian thesis is not new; the Athenians
   said the same at Sparta before the war — the empire was taken under "fear, honour, and
   interest", and "it has always been law that the weaker should be subject to the stronger"
   (I.76); Pericles himself told the assembly that "what you hold is … a tyranny; to take it
   perhaps was wrong, but to let it go is unsafe" (II.63); Cleon said "your empire is a
   despotism". The city never claimed that its empire rested on the same equality as its
   assembly. (b) The Funeral Oration's equality is equality among citizens, and the thesis at
   Melos is precisely that justice holds among equals. Draw it as figure 3: three relations —
   citizen to citizen (equal: speech, law, shares, es meson), Athens to allies (unequal:
   tribute, the ship with the decree), Athens to a neutral (unequal: the thesis). The answer:
   the same principle — right among those who can hold each other to account — produces the
   oration inside the wall and Melos outside it; Mytilene is the hinge, where the inside (an
   assembly of equals arguing) decides the outside. Bearing: `greek-miracle` ended with
   "equality among whom?" as a question built into the polis from the start; Thucydides' three
   speeches are that question answered by a city in practice.

6. **Does the historian judge? The arrangement.** Thucydides says almost nothing in his own
   voice, but he places things. The funeral of 431/0 is followed at once by the plague (II.47–54),
   where "fear of gods or law of man there was none to restrain them" (II.53) — the oration's
   "ease … does not make us lawless" undone in a season. Melos (416) is followed at once by the
   Sicilian expedition (415–413): "all alike fell in love with the enterprise", the few who
   opposed it "feared to appear unpatriotic by holding up their hands against it" (VI.24), and
   it ended "most calamitous to the conquered … few out of many returned home" (VII.87). The
   first reading, at full strength: the order is a verdict — the boast of imperishable
   monuments "for evil or for good" answered by a city without law, the thesis that the strong
   do what they can answered by the strong city's ruin. The second reading, at full strength
   (state as the course's own; source in References): the historian refuses that moral himself —
   he gives the plague a natural course, from "the parts of Ethiopia above Egypt" (II.48), and
   says Sicily failed less from miscalculation than from "private cabals" at home after Pericles
   (II.65); and Athens fought on for nine more years. The lesson concedes both are readings of an
   arrangement, not statements, and says that the historian's own causes tell against the first
   (figure 6). Bearing: if the work judges, it judges by the course of events, not by a principle
   stated against the Athenian thesis — no speaker refutes the thesis, and the test it meets
   comes from events after the history breaks off (step 8).

7. **What do the speeches claim about who should rule — and the strongest objection to the
   Athenian thesis.** Collect the answers as a table (figure 7): Pericles — the many, with office
   by merit, held together by love of the city; Cleon — settled laws and ordinary men, not clever
   speakers; Diodotus — an assembly that deliberates and listens to accountable advisers; the
   Athenians at Melos — between cities, the stronger, by a law of nature; the Melian few — not
   the people (they kept the envoys from them); and the historian himself, twice: under Pericles
   "what was nominally a democracy became … government by the first citizen", and his successors,
   "each grasping at supremacy", gave affairs to "the whims of the multitude" (II.65); and the
   one regime he calls the best Athens had in his time, the Five Thousand of 411, because "the
   fusion of the high and the low was effected with judgment" (VIII.97). Then the STRONGEST
   OBJECTION to the Athenian thesis, at full strength (the course's own statement; source in
   References): the thesis says the strong are compelled by nature to rule where they can, and
   the Athenians at Sparta said they were compelled by fear, honour and interest. If honour and
   interest compel, nothing is ever done voluntarily, and nobody can be blamed for anything —
   including the Mytilenians for rebelling, whom Cleon blamed for "deliberate" wrong, and
   including the Melians for resisting. Diodotus came close to saying so ("impossible to prevent
   … human nature doing what it has once set its mind upon"). So the thesis, consistently held,
   dissolves the accusations the empire was enforced with. The reply on the Athenians' side, also
   at full strength: they did not need blame; they needed only to predict, and their predictions
   at Melos were borne out (Sparta did not come; the hopes failed); and they claimed credit for
   speaking plainly and for moderation — "praise is due to all who … respect justice more than
   their position compels them" (I.76). The lesson concedes: the thesis is consistent as a
   description of what powers do and inconsistent with the language of guilt the same city used;
   whether it is true of cities is weighed in step 8; whether it stops at the city wall passes to
   the next lesson.

8. **The weighing: the thesis describes the war and fails as a rule (the record of 404).** See
   VERDICT and REVISION GAIN 1. Description upheld on both sides (Sparta did not come; Plataea,
   III.68; Scione and Melos). The claim that fairness protects no one tested on 404 (Xenophon,
   *Hell.* II.2.3, II.2.9, II.2.19–20): Athens feared Melos's fate, Lysander restored the Melians,
   Sparta refused to destroy a city "which had done great service". Strongest objection: Sparta's
   restraint was interest (a counterweight to Thebes). Why it does not overturn: the stated
   ground was past service, the Athenians' fear shows they expected the standard to be turned on
   them; mixed motive, hence on balance. What would overturn it: a reading of 404 as pure
   balance-of-power with no weight on past service. `<Conclusion>`: the reader can now state the
   Funeral Oration's account of what holds Athens together, the two Mytilene arguments and why
   the merciful one argued from interest, and the Athenian thesis with the Melians' three
   replies; he can say how one city held all three positions (equality among equals, power over
   the rest) and what each speech says about who should rule. The verdict in plain words: as a
   description of cities at war the thesis held on both sides; as a claim that fairness protects
   no one it failed in 404 — on balance, because Sparta's restraint also served its interest.
   Passed on: whether the law "in the middle" of a city is itself just the interest of the
   stronger there — the next lesson, where a teacher of rhetoric says so to Socrates.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms and names to anchor at first mention (L11), no more than three per section:**
  Thucydides (Athenian general, exiled after Amphipolis 424, writing through and after the war);
  the Peloponnesian War (431–404, Athens and its empire against Sparta and its allies — place it
  "about fifty years after Plataea", the last anchor of `greek-miracle`); Lacedaemon (the
  state of which Sparta was the city — the translation uses both; gloss once); the Athenian
  empire / the allies (cities that had joined Athens against Persia after 479 and came to pay
  it tribute and follow its orders — anchor in one clause; do not name the Delian League unless
  needed, and if named, gloss it); tribute (a yearly payment to Athens); Pericles; the
  assembly (all adult male citizens, meeting on the Pnyx hill — one clause, the reader knows
  the agora); Cleon; Diodotus; Mytilene and Lesbos; Paches (the Athenian general at Mytilene —
  name only if needed); oligarchy (rule of the few — the reader met "the few" and "tyrant" in
  `greek-miracle`, not the word "oligarchy"; gloss at Diodotus's argument about the commons);
  Melos (southern Aegean, a Spartan colony); "the Mede" (the Persians — gloss in the quotation's
  aftermath); the plague (430–426, an epidemic in besieged Athens; Pericles died of it, 429);
  Sicily and Syracuse (the large island south of Italy and its chief Greek city);
  Alcibiades and Nicias (only if the Sicily paragraph needs them — one clause each, or leave
  them out); the Five Thousand (411, a regime that limited full citizenship to those who could
  provide armour). Use a locator figure for Lesbos, Melos, Sparta, Athens, Sicily.
- **Dates (L12d):** decade by default; the exact years 431, 427, 416, 415–413, 404 stay because
  the opening and the arrangement argument depend on sequence. Keeper numbers (L12c), three:
  the interval structure (431 → 427 → 416, as the opening gives it); the vote "almost equal"
  with a day and a night between the ships; "rather more than a thousand" killed at Mytilene
  even after the reprieve. Melos's "seven hundred years" and "five hundred colonists" stay
  inside quotations; no army sizes in prose.
- **The speeches are reconstructions** (I.22). Say it in section 1 and never write "Pericles
  said" as if it were a transcript without the reader knowing the status; "Pericles, in
  Thucydides' account, says" once, then the plain name.
- **The real misconception (L13):** "the strong do what they can" quoted as "might makes right".
  One refutation slot, in section 4 (schema above). No second strawman.
- **Do not let the lesson become a history of the war.** Three speeches, the arrangement, and
  the "who should rule" table. Corcyra, Pylos, Plataea's siege, Brasidas, the Hermes affair,
  the Four Hundred: out (a clause at most where an exercise needs one).
- **Do not teach Plato or Thrasymachus here.** The hand-off is one sentence in the Conclusion
  ("the next lesson"); Thrasymachus is not named in the body except in the Conclusion's
  hand-off if the writer wants the name — preferably as "a teacher of rhetoric". Do not
  re-teach `greek-miracle`: es meson, isonomia, the two equalities, Solon's reply are each a
  clause and a `<Ref>`.
- **Balance and register.** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
  In practice: the Athenian thesis is given at full strength as a consistent account of how
  powers behave, with its evidence (the narrative bore out its predictions about Sparta and
  hope) and its strongest objection (compulsion dissolves blame); the Melians' replies are
  given at full strength too (their first argument is from interest, not sentiment). The
  Funeral Oration is neither debunked by the empire nor exempted from it: the lesson states
  both and lets "equality among whom?" do the work. Cleon is not described in the course's
  voice; the historian's "the most violent man at Athens" is quoted as the historian's.
- **Mytilene is not a story of mercy simply.** The reprieve spared the people; the thousand
  were still killed and the land taken (III.50). The lesson says so.
- **Melos's status:** "at first remained neutral", then openly hostile after Athenian raids
  (V.84; the 426 raid is III.91). The opening's "neutral island" is course.yaml's phrasing of the

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and chronology (the one warm-up, E3 from `greek-miracle`):** place the funeral,
   Mytilene, Melos, the Sicilian disaster and Athens's surrender relative to Plataea (479) and
   to each other; then say which of the two equalities from the Greek-cities lesson Pericles
   invokes in "equal justice to all in their private differences" and which in "advancement …
   falls to reputation for capacity", and whether a city can have both. Solution: arithmetic,
   geometric; Pericles claims both by putting them in different spheres (private law vs office).
2. **Document reasoning — the sourcing drill on the Melian dialogue itself (I.22 + V.84–116):**
   metadata first (author an exiled Athenian general; the conference held in private before the
   Melian few; no other record), a prediction before reading (what would you expect such a
   source to give each side?), then records / argues / omits, and USE: what the dialogue can
   establish (that the argument was thinkable and stateable in Athens; how Thucydides wanted the
   decision understood) and what it cannot (the envoys' words). Solution names the format's
   uniqueness as evidence of construction.
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "At Melos the Athenians argued that might makes right." Name the
   step that fails (V.89 reserves "right" for equals in power and denies that the question
   arises between unequals; V.105 claims necessity, not justice; their closing maxim is
   prudence) and state the claim that survives.
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** from the Athenian thesis as stated at Sparta (I.76:
   compelled by fear, honour, interest) and Diodotus's "impossible to prevent … human nature
   doing what it has once set its mind upon", derive what follows for Cleon's charge that
   Mytilene's revolt was "deliberate and wanton aggression"; then state what the Athenians would
   need to add to keep both the thesis and the right to punish. Solution: the thesis dissolves
   voluntary wrongdoing; to punish, they need deterrence (Cleon) — which Diodotus has just
   denied works — or a standard of justice the thesis rejects.
5. **Boundary probe (E6a):** set the powers equal. Athens and Sparta before the war were roughly
   equal; what does the Athenian thesis predict about how they deal with each other ("right …
   is in question between equals")? Check against the record the lesson gives (the Athenians at
   Sparta in I.76 still argued, offered terms, and claimed moderation) and say what the probe
   shows about where the thesis draws its line. Solution: the thesis is not "no justice ever"
   but "justice where each can make the other pay" — Solon's condition.
6. **Paired-source corroboration:** Pericles — "we do not feel called upon to be angry with our
   neighbour for doing what he likes" — beside Cleon — "fears or plots being unknown to you in
   your daily relations with each other, you feel just the same with regard to your allies".
   Where they agree (Athenians live without fear of one another), where they disagree (whether
   that trait fits a ruling city), and the written judgment the drill ends in.
7. **Counter-argument (playbook-history stem):** "Thucydides condemns Athens: he puts the plague
   straight after the Funeral Oration and Sicily straight after Melos." Name two facts the claim
   fails to account for (II.48's natural account of the plague; II.65's account of why Sicily
   failed; VIII.97's praise of an Athenian regime) and state what would have to be true for it
   to survive (that arrangement can carry a judgment the author's explicit causes deny).
8. **Attack the verdict (new; before the FORWARD exercise):** the lesson judges, on balance, that
   the Athenian thesis fails as a claim that fairness protects no one, on the record of 404. Make
   the strongest case that Sparta's restraint was interest alone (Thebes and Corinth in 404), then
   say what evidence would overturn the verdict. Solution: a destroyed Athens would have
   strengthened Thebes and Corinth; to overturn the verdict one needs evidence that Sparta weighed
   only the balance and that the appeal to past service carried no weight (a contemporary account
   of the debate giving the counterweight as the reason); the Athenians' fear in II.2.3 stands
   either way as evidence that they did not believe the thesis about themselves.
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `thrasymachus-and-the-ring`:** The Athenian envoys say justice is in
   question only between equals in power. Apply the thesis INSIDE a city: in Athens's assembly
   the many outnumber the rich; in an oligarchy the rich hold the offices and the arms. On the
   envoys' premise, what would "justice" in each city's laws turn out to be? Solution: the
   interest of whichever part is stronger there, written as law and called just — the thesis a
   teacher of rhetoric puts to Socrates in the next lesson, `<Ref lesson="thrasymachus-and-the-ring">`;
   one sentence notes that the envoys stopped at the city wall and he does not.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Six critiques of abstract rights, each established in its own lesson and here only recalled. Burke: real vs metaphysical rights; "In this partnership all men have equal rights; but not to equal things"; a share in power a matter of convention, not an original right; "the restraints on men, as well as their liberties, are to be reckoned among their rights" (`burke-reflections`). Maistre: "there is no such thing as man"; a constitution made for all nations is made for none (`maistre-and-bonald`). Oakeshott: an ideology is the abridgment of a tradition — the Rights of Man of 1789 as the common-law rights of Englishmen "abstracted and abridged"; freedom as "the availability of that procedure"; the export of democracy as shipping the book without the cook (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`). Strauss: modern natural right puts rights before duties; a society that cannot say why anything is right by nature has "retail sanity and wholesale madness"; "a wish is not a fact" (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`). Kirk: the six canons — a transcendent order, orders and classes with equality only before God and the law, freedom bound to property, prescription, prudent change (`kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`, concept `canons-of-conservatism`). Jouvenel: Power grows by allying with the common man against every intermediate authority and by promising security, so that the "free citizen" becomes a "protected subject" (`jouvenel-on-power`, concepts `growth-of-power`, `alliance-of-power-and-the-common-man`). Also: Voegelin's use of "the Bill of Rights is not a suicide pact" (`voegelin-gnosticism`); Burnham's liberalism as the ideology of Western contraction (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`); the anti-fascist settlement of 1945 of which the Declaration is a founding text (`the-right-after-1945`, concept `anti-fascist-consensus`); Hobbes's self-preservation as the first modern right (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`); Locke's property (`locke-property-and-consent`); Cicero's natural law as the ancient universal (`cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`); the British franchise to 1928 (`british-franchise`).
- **Contributes:** One move (L16): the module's six critiques cashed out on one real deployed document, article by article — which articles each critique hits, which articles the drafters wrote as if they had read the critic, and where the document departs from every idealisation of it (no court, a divided vote, a deliberate silence on foundations, a list that changes kind at Article 22 and says so).
- **Hands off:** (1) The question the Declaration refused — WHY anyone has the social rights of Arts. 22–27 — is answered in 1971 without metaphysics, by a hypothetical choice behind a veil; Rawls's first principle lists roughly the liberties of Arts. 3–21 and ranks them above the social claims, which is a liberal's answer to Jouvenel's charge that the new rights abrogate the old (`rawls-theory-of-justice`, next lesson in course order, first of the equality module). (2) Kirk's warning that rights confused with desires fix on society "a permanent grudge and frustration" is the charge of envy the equality module examines (`ressentiment-and-envy`); Scruton's "rights without powers are political fictions" and his account of rights inflation are read there too (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`). (3) Arts. 13–15 (movement, asylum, nationality) are the ground of the border debate (`carens-open-borders`, `miller-strangers-in-our-midst`); an earlier universal right — to travel, trade and preach — and what it licensed is `vitoria-right-to-the-americas`. (4) Whether a list of rights can hold a society together is the course's closing question (`the-argument-assembled`).
- **Spine step:** "After 1945 fascism has discredited the right, and it rebuilds in new registers: Jouvenel's history of Power, Oakeshott's disposition, Strauss's natural right, Voegelin's diagnosis of Gnosticism, Kirk's and Nisbet's American conservatism, Burnham's managers — tested on the Declaration of 1948"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):** The artefact is the text of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the UN General Assembly at the Palais de Chaillot, Paris, on 10 December 1948 (Resolution 217 A (III)): a Preamble and thirty articles, public and quotable in full (un.org/en/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights). Module results it uses: `no-such-thing-as-man` (Maistre; Arts. 1–2 vs Arts. 15, 21); `rationalism-in-politics` and ideology-as-abridgment (Oakeshott; Arts. 8–11, 21); `classic-natural-right` and `historicism` (Strauss; the Preamble and Art. 1's silence on the ground; Art. 29's one teleological clause); `canons-of-conservatism` (Kirk; transcendent order vs the struck "by God"; rights "married" to duties; the Art. 24 holidays); `growth-of-power` and `alliance-of-power-and-the-common-man` (Jouvenel; Arts. 22–25 as the "new rights of man", Art. 21's popular will, Arts. 8–10's independent tribunals); `natural-law` (Cicero to Strauss; whether "inherent dignity" is a ground); plus Burke's real vs metaphysical rights by `<Ref>`. Departures from the idealisation the lesson must name: (a) a resolution, not a treaty — no court, no sanction, "a common standard of achievement" (the binding Covenants came in 1966); (b) universality voted 48 for, 0 against, 8 abstaining, 2 absent, of 58 member states, the abstainers' reasons being the critics' reasons; (c) the foundation deliberately left unstated; (d) articles of mixed kinds — liberties and immunities to Art. 21, claims from Art. 22, with Art. 22 itself qualifying the claims "in accordance with the organization and resources of each State"; (e) against all six critics, a document that writes in duties and limits (Art. 29), a particular order (Art. 28), a nation to belong to (Art. 15), a people whose will is the basis of authority (Art. 21), the family as "the natural and fundamental group unit" (Art. 16), independent tribunals (Art. 10) and a self-protection clause (Art. 30).

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: fifty-eight states, thirty articles, declining to say why; the remark; the document read article by article with the critics the course has assembled; what it knows that they say it cannot.

1. **The artefact, sourced first: who wrote it, for whom, and why it says nothing about why.** Metadata before the text (playbook-history sourcing drill): a resolution of the General Assembly, Paris, 10 December 1948, three years after the camps were opened and two after the Nuremberg judgments (Art. 11's ban on retroactive penal law is the Nuremberg problem written down; recall the settlement in a clause, `the-right-after-1945`); drafted over eighteen months by a Commission chaired by Franklin Roosevelt's widow, from a first draft by a Canadian lawyer in the Secretariat, restructured by a French jurist who described it as a portico — the Preamble its steps, the articles its columns, Arts. 28–30 the pediment — with a Lebanese Thomist and a Chinese Confucian on the committee; addressed to governments as "a common standard of achievement", not a treaty: no court, no sanction. The vote in one bound sentence: 48 of 58 member states for, none against, 8 abstaining — six of the Soviet bloc, Saudi Arabia, South Africa — and 2 absent. The reader predicts before reading (`<Task>`): given who abstained, which articles would you expect to be contested? Then the fact the opening promised, with its exact provenance: Art. 1's "endowed with reason and conscience" is what remained after "by nature" and "by God" were struck; a UNESCO committee of philosophers found agreement on the list and none on the ground, and the remark — "we agree about the rights but on condition no one asks us why" — was reported by one of them from a UNESCO meeting. That silence is the datum every critique below reads differently.
2. **Burke: the real rights are in the list, and so is the one he would strike.** Recall (`burke-reflections`): the real rights — to justice, to the fruits of one's industry and one's parents' acquisitions, to instruction — are "all the advantages for which civil society is made"; metaphysical rights unrefracted by circumstance are, "in proportion as they are metaphysically true, … morally and politically false". Run the test, worked fully as the lesson's one `<Example>` (the other five critics are then done faster): Arts. 3, 6–12, 17 and 26 are Burke's real rights, the rights of Englishmen written for everyone; Art. 21's "the will of the people shall be the basis of the authority of government … universal and equal suffrage" is the one he would strike — a share in power "a thing to be settled by convention"; Art. 2's "without distinction of any kind" and the Preamble's "equal" read against "equal rights; but not to equal things" (the five shillings and the five hundred pounds). Where the document answers him: Art. 29's duties and limits "determined by law" — restraints among men's rights — and Art. 16(3)'s family as "the natural and fundamental group unit of society". Where it does not: no inheritance, no dead, no unborn — the partnership of generations has no article.
3. **Maistre: "everyone" is the man he never met — and two articles concede it.** Recall (`maistre-and-bonald`). The Declaration's grammatical subject is "everyone", "all human beings", "no one". The strongest form of the charge, tested on the document's own text: Art. 15 ("Everyone has the right to a nationality") and Art. 21 (authority rests on "the will of the people" — a people, with elections it knows how to hold) presuppose the concrete membership the abstract man lacks. And the vote is Maistre's evidence: eight of fifty-eight could not sign "man" — for religion and marriage law (Saudi Arabia, on Arts. 16 and 18), for a racial order (South Africa), for the sovereignty of their states (the Soviet bloc). Where the document answers: it knows it. Art. 15 is an admission — the stateless of the displaced-persons camps of 1948 were the case before the drafters (bound once: "about a million displaced persons still in camps in Germany, Austria and Italy in 1948", verify) — and Art. 28's "social and international order in which the rights … can be fully realized" admits that order does not yet exist. Where it does not: Art. 15 names no one who must grant a nationality.
4. **Oakeshott: the common law abridged and shipped as a poster.** Recall (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`). Arts. 8–11 are habeas corpus, jury trial and the common law's rules of evidence abstracted from the two societies that practised them; Art. 21's "periodic and genuine elections … universal and equal suffrage … secret vote" abridges the British franchise as it stood in 1928 (`british-franchise`). The export: the Declaration printed as a poster and sent round the world (figure 1) — the cookery book without the cook. Where the document answers: the Preamble calls it "a common standard of achievement", a measure rather than a code — Oakeshott's own permitted use of an ideology, as a hypothesis, not a criterion; Arts. 8 and 10 name the institutions ("competent national tribunals", "independent and impartial tribunal") that must do the work — the drafters knew the right was the availability of a procedure. Where it does not: "progressive measures" is ideology as project; an abridgment that does not name the tradition it abridges claims a universality the tradition never had.
5. **Strauss: the list without the ground — preference or prudence?** Recall (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`). The charge at full strength: thirty specific rights and a refusal to say why — "retail sanity and wholesale madness" at the level of states; Art. 3's life, liberty and security first (Hobbes's self-preservation, `<Ref>`), Art. 29's duties twenty-six articles later (rights before duties, exactly the modern pattern); "inherent dignity" asserted, never derived; the agreement "on condition no one asks us why" as Weber's "Thou shalt have preferences" adopted by governments. The reply from Strauss's own classics: natural right in its pure form is "dynamite" for any actual city; a city agrees on practice without agreeing on theory, and must — so the silence can be read as the prudence classical natural right itself prescribes. The document's evidence cuts both ways: Art. 29(1) ("the community in which alone the free and full development of his personality is possible") and Art. 26(2) are its only teleological sentences — a human end stated without a nature to ground it; Art. 30 answers the tolerance paradox — no right to destroy the rights (`voegelin-gnosticism`, Jackson's "suicide pact", `<Ref>`). The reader judges.
6. **Kirk: rights confused with desires — the article where a duty is missing.** Seam (L10b): the reader expects an American conservative of the 1950s, writing in the country that drafted the Declaration, to defend it as the rights of Englishmen generalised. Kirk attacks it by name. Recall the canons in a clause (`kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`) and read his passage: since Paine, "'rights' with desires" confounded — the Declaration's "thirty articles, and a somewhat greater number of 'rights'", many "not rights at all, but merely aspirations"; Art. 24's paid holidays as "the Right to be Idle"; and the test — two conditions "attached to all true rights": the capacity to claim and exercise the right, and "the correspondent duty that is married to every right. If a man has a right to marry, some woman must have the duty of marrying him; if a man has a right to rest, some other person must have the duty of supporting him". His positive list, Burke's as he states it: "equal justice, security of labor and property, the amenities of civilized institutions, and the benefits of orderly society". Now make the test precise with the standard scheme of jural relations (the course's device; Hohfeld in References): a claim correlates with someone's duty; a liberty with others' having no right to stop you; a power with a liability; an immunity with a disability (figure 5). Apply it — and the document answers Kirk on one of his own examples and not on the other: Art. 16's right to marry is a liberty (no one may prevent it) whose correlative is a no-right in others, not a woman's duty — Kirk's example misreads the incident; Art. 24's "periodic holidays with pay" is a claim, and someone — an employer, the state — must pay: there his test bites. Sort the rest: Art. 18 a liberty plus an immunity; Art. 9 an immunity; Art. 21 a power; Arts. 22–27 claims; Art. 28 a claim against "an order", which is nobody. The kind changes at Art. 22 — and the drafters marked it: Art. 22 qualifies every claim that follows "in accordance with the organization and resources of each State". Kirk's further canon, the transcendent order, meets the struck "by God" of Art. 1: the Declaration rests no right on God, which is exactly what a conservative who holds that rights are "linked with man's duties" under a moral order denies a document can do. And his warning about consequence — rights confused with desires fix on the mass of men "a permanent grudge and frustration" — is the charge the next module examines as envy (forward clause only).
7. **Jouvenel: the new rights abrogate the old — and Power is their guarantor.** Seam: Jouvenel wrote before the Declaration existed; his critique is a forecast, and the lesson can test it against the text. Recall Power's growth (`jouvenel-on-power`). The forecast at full strength: the "new rights of men" — Roosevelt's 1944 list of a job, a decent wage, a home, medical care, protection from the fears of old age and unemployment — "are given out as coming to complete those already proclaimed in the eighteenth century. But … they contradict and abrogate them": the old rights made each man master of his actions and left him their consequences; the new guarantee the consequences, and "if the state is to guarantee to a man what the consequences of his actions shall be, it must take control of his activities"; the free citizen becomes "a protected subject". Read Arts. 22–25 against it (they descend visibly from the 1944 list, and the Commission's chair was its author's widow — two facts, one sentence, verify): social security, work and free choice of employment, "protection against unemployment", paid holidays, an adequate standard of living, "security in the event of unemployment, sickness, disability, widowhood, old age". Then Jouvenel's second point, which the reader can apply to Art. 21: a Declaration of the Rights of Man belongs to an individualist philosophy in which rights limit sovereignty; but "democracy" can also mean "the absolutism of a government which draws its title from the masses" — and Art. 21 founds authority on "the will of the people" while Art. 29(2) lets law limit every right for "the general welfare in a democratic society". Where the document answers him: Art. 10's "independent and impartial tribunal" is precisely the "castle" of the rights Jouvenel says the French Revolution destroyed by subordinating the judges; Art. 20(2) ("no one may be compelled to belong to an association") and Art. 17 (property; no arbitrary deprivation) keep the shields of the old rights; and Art. 22's resources clause concedes the new rights are aims of each state's policy, not guarantees. Where it does not: nothing in the Declaration says which kind of right wins when a guarantee of security requires control of activity — the conflict Jouvenel named is left to Art. 29's "general welfare", that is, to the state.
8. **The ledger, and the departures.** Lay the six readings out as a table (figure 6): which articles each critique hits, which articles answer it. Then the L16 departures, a sentence each: a resolution, not a law, until the 1966 Covenants made some of it treaty law for ratifying states; a vote of 48–0 with 8 abstentions whose reasons were the critics' own; a ground deliberately left out; a list that changes kind at Art. 22 and says so; and against all six, a document that writes in duties, limits, nationality, a people, a family, an order, independent courts and a self-protection clause — a document whose drafters had read the objections of 1790 and 1796 and answered what they could. The lesson gives the method and the table and does not hand down a verdict: Burke's real/metaphysical distinction is visible in the document's own structure; Maistre's "no such thing as man" is conceded by Arts. 15 and 21 and confirmed by the abstentions; Oakeshott's abridgment is admitted by Arts. 8 and 10; Strauss's list without a ground is what the drafters chose, for reasons the reader must decide between; Kirk's missing duty is real at Art. 24 and mistaken at Art. 16; Jouvenel's abrogation is marked, and half-answered, by Art. 22's clause. Hand off in one sentence: the question the drafters refused — why anyone has the social rights of Art. 22 — is answered twenty-three years later by a philosopher who also ranks the old liberties first, and who claims no one need have agreed to anything.

Headings as claims: "A list agreed on condition no one asks why: the artefact and its vote"; "Burke: the real rights are in the list, and so is the one he would strike"; "Maistre: 'everyone' is the man he never met, and Articles 15 and 21 admit it"; "Oakeshott: the common law abridged and shipped as a poster"; "Strauss: the list without the ground — preference or prudence?"; "Kirk: where a right has no one bound by it"; "Jouvenel: the new rights and the old"; "The ledger: what the document bears out and what it knows".

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and classification (the one warm-up):** Six articles — 10, 15, 21, 22, 29, 30 — each matched to the critic whose objection it most directly answers, with the clause that does the answering quoted; then each of the six critiques classified as perversity, futility or jeopardy (`what-conservatism-is`).
2. **Boundary probe (E6a):** A stateless person in a displaced-persons camp in 1948 — no state, no employer, no court of his own. Go down the thirty articles with Kirk's test (is there a correspondent duty, and on whom?) and mark which rights he holds, which he holds against no one, and what Art. 15 does for him. Solution: the liberties and immunities (3–12, 18–20) bind everyone around him; the claims (22–27) bind a state he lacks; Art. 15 gives him a right to the one thing that would make the rest real and names no one who owes it.
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "The Universal Declaration is a Lockean document: natural rights to life, liberty and property held by individuals against the state." Name the failing steps from the text (Art. 16(3) protects an institution; Art. 22 makes claims the state must satisfy; Art. 29 states duties and limits; the Preamble appeals to "the conscience of mankind", not nature or God) and say what the Declaration is on the evidence.
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** Complete the ledger (figure 6) by sorting all thirty articles by the four incidents; find the article at which the predominant incident changes (22), state what Art. 28 is in the scheme (a claim with no duty-bearer), and decide whether Kirk's test makes it a right at all.
5. **Sourcing drill:** Jouvenel's "the new rights of man … contradict and abrogate" the old (On Power, ch. XVIII). Prediction first: given a French writer in Swiss exile in 1943–45, reading Roosevelt's 1944 message, what would you expect him to fear? Then author, date, audience, purpose; what it records (the 1944 list), argues (guarantee requires control), omits (that the old rights were also enforced by a state — Art. 10's courts); what it can be USED to establish about the Declaration (that its Arts. 22–25 were contested in principle before they were written) and what it cannot (how any state actually implemented them).
6. **Synthesis (claim–evidence–reasoning):** Is "agreement on the rights on condition no one asks why" Weber's "Thou shalt have preferences" at the level of states, or the classical prudence of a city that agrees on practice without agreeing on theory? Argue both with Strauss's text and with Art. 29(1) and Art. 26(2); judge, and say what evidence from the drafting record would decide it.
7. **Counter-argument stem (E3, `maistre-and-bonald`):** "The Declaration refutes Maistre: fifty-eight nations agreed about 'man' in 1948." Name two facts the claim must account for (the eight abstentions and their reasons; Arts. 15 and 21 presuppose a nation and a people) and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that a list agreed by forty-eight governments is evidence about human nature rather than about forty-eight governments).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `rawls-theory-of-justice`:** The drafters listed the social claims of Arts. 22–27 and refused to say why anyone has them; Kirk said they bind no one; Jouvenel said they abrogate the liberties of Arts. 3–21. Write the requirements an argument for Arts. 22–27 would have to meet to answer all three: it must say why the claims are owed, by whom (Kirk), and why they do not override the liberties (Jouvenel) — without appealing to God or nature (the drafters' silence). The solution says that an American philosopher in 1971 offered exactly such an argument — a hypothetical choice made behind a veil of ignorance, which ranks equal liberties first and permits social inequalities only where they benefit the least advantaged — and that the next lesson rebuilds it, `<Ref>` `rawls-theory-of-justice`.

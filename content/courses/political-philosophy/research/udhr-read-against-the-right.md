# Research brief: udhr-read-against-the-right (The Universal Declaration of Human Rights read against the right)

status: planned
module: postwar-conservatism
type: core
goal: Apply Burke's, Maistre's, Oakeshott's, Strauss's and Scruton's critiques of abstract rights to the text of the 1948 Declaration and judge which critique the document bears out and where the document answers it

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Five critiques of abstract rights, each established in its own lesson and here only recalled: Burke's real vs metaphysical rights, "equal rights but not to equal things", a share in power as convention not original right, restraints among men's rights (`burke-reflections`); Maistre's "there is no such thing as man" and "a constitution made for all nations is made for none" (`maistre-and-bonald`); Oakeshott's ideology as the abridgment of a tradition — the Rights of Man of 1789 as the common-law rights of Englishmen abstracted, freedom as the availability of a procedure, the export of democracy as shipping the book without the cook (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`); Strauss's modern natural right — rights before duties, the list without the ground, "a wish is not a fact", the tolerance paradox, "retail sanity and wholesale madness" (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`); Scruton's "rights exist only where there are obligations", "rights without powers are political fictions", the state as the first-person plural (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`, Exercise 8). Also Voegelin's "the Bill of Rights is not a suicide pact" (`voegelin-gnosticism`), Hobbes's right of self-preservation as the first modern right (`hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`), Locke's property (`locke-property-and-consent`), Cicero's natural law as the ancient universal (`cicero-natural-law`), and the Declaration of Independence from Oakeshott's and Strauss's openings.
- **Contributes:** One move: the module's five theories cashed out on one real deployed document, article by article — which articles each critique hits, which articles the drafters wrote as if they had read the critics, and where the document departs from every idealisation of it (no court, a divided vote, a deliberate silence on foundations, a list that changes kind at Article 22).
- **Hands off:** Three problems to later modules. (1) An earlier universalism — Vitoria's law of nations, with its right to travel, trade and preach that licensed war when refused — is the next lesson (`vitoria-right-to-the-americas`, which follows in course order though its knowledge prerequisites are Aquinas and Aristotle; the question is real: what did the last universal declaration of rights license?). (2) Articles 13–15 (movement, asylum, nationality) are the ground on which Carens and Miller fight (`carens-open-borders`, `miller-strangers-in-our-midst`), and Scruton's full account of rights inflation (How to Be a Conservative ch. 6) belongs to `the-nation-and-its-defenders` and `deneen-post-liberalism`. (3) Whether a list of rights can hold a society together is the course's closing question (`the-argument-assembled`; the Böckenförde dilemma in `murray-strange-death-of-europe`).
- **Spine step:** "After 1945 liberalism is the orthodoxy, and the right answers it in four registers: Oakeshott's disposition, Strauss's natural right, Voegelin's diagnosis of Gnosticism, Scruton's authority before freedom"
- **APPLICATION spec (L16):** The artefact is the text of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the UN General Assembly at the Palais de Chaillot, Paris, on 10 December 1948 (Resolution 217 A (III)): a Preamble and thirty articles, public and quotable in full (un.org/en/about-us/universal-declaration-of-human-rights). The module results it uses: `no-such-thing-as-man` (Maistre; Arts. 1–2 vs Arts. 15, 21), `rationalism-in-politics` and ideology-as-abridgment (Oakeshott; Arts. 8–11, 21), `classic-natural-right` and `historicism` (Strauss; the Preamble and Art. 1's silence on the ground; Art. 29's one teleological clause), `authority-before-freedom` and rights-presuppose-obligations (Scruton; the change of kind at Art. 22; Hohfeld's four incidents), `natural-law` (Cicero to Strauss; whether "inherent dignity" is a ground), plus Burke's real vs metaphysical rights by `<Ref>`. The departures from the idealisation the lesson must name: (a) the Declaration is a resolution, not a treaty — no court, no sanction, "a common standard of achievement" (the binding Covenants came in 1966); (b) its universality was voted 48 for, 0 against, 8 abstaining, 2 absent, of 58 member states, with the abstentions from the Soviet bloc, Saudi Arabia and South Africa for reasons that were themselves the critics' reasons; (c) its foundation was deliberately left unstated after the drafters and a UNESCO philosophers' committee found they could agree on the list and not on the why; (d) its articles are of mixed kinds — liberties and immunities to Art. 21, claims from Art. 22 — and Art. 22 itself qualifies the claims "in accordance with the organization and resources of each State"; (e) and against all five critics: it writes in duties and limits (Art. 29), a particular order (Art. 28), a nation to belong to (Art. 15), a people whose will is the basis of authority (Art. 21), the family as "the natural and fundamental group unit" (Art. 16), and a self-protection clause (Art. 30).

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: fifty-eight states, thirty articles, declining to say why; the drafter's remark; the document read article by article with the five critics; what it knows that they say it cannot.

1. **The artefact, sourced first: who wrote it, for whom, and why it says nothing about why.** Metadata before the text (playbook-history sourcing drill): a resolution of the General Assembly, Paris, 10 December 1948, three years after the camps were opened and two after the Nuremberg judgments (Art. 11's ban on retroactive penal law is the Nuremberg problem written down); drafted over eighteen months by a Commission chaired by the widow of the American president, from a first draft by a Canadian lawyer in the Secretariat, restructured by a French jurist who described the result as a portico — the Preamble its steps, the articles four columns, Arts. 28–30 the pediment — with a Lebanese Thomist and a Chinese Confucian on the committee; addressed to governments as "a common standard of achievement", not a treaty: no court, no sanction. Then the vote, bound in one sentence: 48 of 58 member states for, none against, 8 abstaining — six of the Soviet bloc, Saudi Arabia, South Africa — and 2 absent. The reader predicts before reading (the `<Task>`): given who voted and who abstained, which articles would you expect to be contested? Then the fact the opening promised: the drafters could not agree why anyone has these rights. Art. 1's "endowed with reason and conscience" is what remained after "by nature" and "by God" were both struck; a committee of philosophers consulted by UNESCO in 1947–48 found the same — agreement on the list, none on the ground — and one adviser said they agreed "on condition no one asks us why". That silence is the datum every critique below reads differently. Give the document's own self-description, from the Preamble: "inherent dignity", "barbarous acts which have outraged the conscience of mankind", "a common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations", to be secured "by progressive measures".
2. **Burke: real rights, metaphysical rights, and the one article he would strike.** Recall in one clause (`burke-reflections`): the real rights of men — to justice, to the fruits of their industry and their parents' acquisitions, to instruction, to "do what he can separately do without trespassing upon others" — are "all the advantages for which civil society is made"; the metaphysical rights are those stated in the straight line, unrefracted by circumstance, "and in proportion as they are metaphysically true, they are morally and politically false". Run the test: Arts. 3, 6–12 and 17 (life and security; recognition and equality before the law; remedy, no arbitrary arrest, fair trial, presumption of innocence; privacy; property) are the real rights of Burke's list — the rights of Englishmen written for everyone; Art. 26 (instruction) is his too. Where Burke stops: Art. 21 — "the will of the people shall be the basis of the authority of government… periodic and genuine elections… universal and equal suffrage" — a share in power is "a thing to be settled by convention", not an original right; and Art. 2 and the Preamble's "equal" — "equal rights, but not to equal things". His deeper charge is the method: a declaration states rights a priori where "the science of constructing a commonwealth… is not to be taught a priori"; "the prattling about the rights of men will not be accepted in payment of a biscuit". Where the document answers him: Art. 29's duties and limits "determined by law" — restraints among men's rights — and Art. 16(3)'s family as "the natural and fundamental group unit of society" are the little platoon and the "contrivance for human wants" written into a rights document. Where it does not: no inheritance (Art. 17 is property, not entail), no dead, no unborn — the partnership of generations has no article.
3. **Maistre: "everyone" is the man he never met — and two articles that concede it.** Recall (`maistre-and-bonald`): there is no such thing as man; "a constitution made for all nations is made for none"; the more is written the weaker the institution; Paine's constitution you can put in your pocket. The Declaration's grammatical subject is "everyone", "all human beings", "no one" — thirty times the man of 1795. Test the strongest form of the charge on the document's own text: Art. 15, "Everyone has the right to a nationality", and Art. 21, where authority rests on "the will of the people" — a people, with elections it knows how to hold — presuppose exactly what Maistre says the abstract man lacks, a concrete membership; the universal document cannot state its political rights without a particular society to hold them in. And the vote itself is Maistre's evidence: of fifty-eight nations, eight could not sign "man" — two for his religion and his marriage law (Saudi Arabia on Arts. 16 and 18), one for its racial order (South Africa), six for the sovereignty of their states (the Soviet bloc). Where the document answers: it knows it. Art. 15 is not a description but an admission — the stateless in the displaced-persons camps of 1948 were the case before the drafters, and the right to a nationality says that the abstract rights-bearer needs a nation to be anything at all (Scruton's first-person plural, by `<Ref>`); Art. 28's "social and international order in which the rights… can be fully realized" admits that the order which would make the rights real does not yet exist. Where it does not: Art. 15 names no one who must grant a nationality; the document cannot say which nation, and a right with no obligor is, in Maistre's thirteenth thesis, a weakness written down. Berlin's gloss — no Nature, so no Man — is the course's paraphrase if used (Berlin in References).
4. **Oakeshott: the Declaration as abridgment, and the export by poster.** Recall (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`): the Rights of Man of 1789 were "abstracted and abridged, the common law rights of Englishmen"; freedom is not an idea exemplified in habeas corpus but "the availability of that procedure"; shipping the abridgment abroad is the opposite of the method "by which the workmen travel with the tools". Read Arts. 8–11 as Oakeshott reads 1789: an effective remedy "by the competent national tribunals", no arbitrary arrest, a "fair and public hearing by an independent and impartial tribunal", the presumption of innocence, no retroactive law — this is habeas corpus, jury trial and the common law's rules of evidence abstracted from the two societies that practised them; Art. 21's "periodic and genuine elections… universal and equal suffrage… secret vote" abridges the British franchise as it stood in 1928 and the American as it stood on paper (`british-franchise`). What the practice contained that the list omits is in the list's own words: Art. 8 presupposes tribunals that exist and are competent; Art. 21 presupposes a people that knows how to count votes and lose them. The export: the Declaration printed as a poster and sent round the world (the sourced image: the chair of the Commission holding the Spanish-language poster) — the cookery book without the cook, "democracy" shipped as a recipe. Where the document answers: the Preamble calls it "a common standard of achievement", a measure rather than a code — which is Oakeshott's own permitted use of an ideology, as a hypothesis or "distorting mirror", not a criterion; Art. 8 and Art. 10 name the institutions ("national tribunals") that must do the work, so the drafters knew the right was the availability of a procedure. Where it does not: "progressive measures" (Preamble) is the ideology as a project; and an abridgment that does not name the tradition it abridges claims a universality the tradition never had.
5. **Strauss: the list without the ground — nihilism, or classical prudence?** Recall (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`): to reject natural right is to say all right is positive; the cannibal; "retail sanity and wholesale madness"; "a wish is not a fact"; modern natural right puts a right before every duty and grounds it in fear of violent death; tolerance made one value among others becomes "a seminary of intolerance". The Declaration is the test case Strauss did not live to read: thirty specific rights and a refusal to say why — the retail/wholesale split at the level of states; Art. 3's life, liberty and security first (Hobbes's self-preservation, `<Ref>`), Art. 29's duties twenty-eight articles later (rights before duties, exactly as ch. V.A says of Hobbes); Arts. 22–25's comfortable self-preservation as rights (Locke's end of civil society, `<Ref>`); "inherent dignity" asserted, never derived; and the 1948 agreement "on condition no one asks us why" is Weber's "Thou shalt have preferences" adopted by fifty-eight governments. That is the charge at full strength. Now the reply from Strauss's own classics: natural right in its pure form is "dynamite" for any actual city; the politically good is "the quotient of the simply good and the ancestral"; a city agrees on practice without agreeing on theory, and must — so the drafters' silence can be read as the prudence classical natural right itself prescribes, not as the nihilism Weber licensed; the question is whether the drafters were silent because they thought the ground unknowable (Weber) or because they thought it unsayable in a document of fifty-eight nations (Cicero's exoteric Stoicism, ch. IV). The document's own evidence cuts both ways: Art. 29(1) — "the community in which alone the free and full development of his personality is possible" — and Art. 26(2) — education "directed to the full development of the human personality" — are the only teleological sentences in it, a human end stated without a nature to ground it; Art. 30 is the tolerance paradox answered — no right "to engage in any activity… aimed at the destruction of any of the rights", the Declaration refusing to be a suicide pact (`voegelin-gnosticism`, Jackson, `<Ref>`). The reader judges which reading the text bears.
6. **Scruton: the article where the obligor changes.** Recall (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`): rights exist only where there are obligations; "rights without powers are political fictions"; and the hand-off exercise, which found that the right to life and the right to work bind different kinds of person. Now the document's structure makes the point visible. Rights began as liberties limiting government — natural law plus the common law, 1689, Locke — and the Declaration "begins with a list of freedom rights and then suddenly, at Article 22, begins making radical claims against the state". Quote Art. 22 whole and parse: "entitled to realization, through national effort and international co-operation and in accordance with the organization and resources of each State, of the economic, social and cultural rights indispensable for his dignity". Then Arts. 23–27: work, equal pay, trade unions; rest, "reasonable limitation of working hours and periodic holidays with pay"; a standard of living adequate for health; free elementary education — each a claim that someone must provide, not a freedom from someone's encroachment. State the standard scheme of jural relations as the course's own (the four incidents, each paired with what it imposes on another: a claim with a duty to perform; a liberty with no right to interfere; a power with a liability; an immunity with a disability), and sort: Art. 18 is a liberty plus an immunity (no one may stop you; the state may not alter your status); Art. 9 an immunity; Art. 21 a power; Art. 23 a claim — against whom? An employer? The state? Everyone? Art. 24's paid holidays presuppose an employer, a wage and a labour market; Art. 28 is a claim against "an order" that is nobody. Scruton's thesis: freedom rights "have the best claim to universality" because they fence a sphere of sovereignty anyone can respect by refraining — "if I have a right to sit in a certain room then you cannot expel me from it without wronging me"; a claim right detached from any act or agreement of the obligor "overrides the other's sovereignty" and, since only a state can carry it, expands the state and breeds inflation. He concedes: "I don't say that those benefits are not rights: but… they are not justified in the same way." Where the document answers him: Art. 22's qualifying clause is the drafters' own scheme — they knew the obligor changes and wrote the limit in ("in accordance with the organization and resources of each State"); Art. 29(2) names "the just requirements of morality, public order and the general welfare in a democratic society" — the society Scruton says rights presuppose; Art. 16(3) protects an institution, not a choice; Art. 20(2), "no one may be compelled to belong to an association", protects the autonomous institution from the other side. Where it does not: the claims from Art. 22 require a state, and the Declaration binds none; "the right to a nationality" and "the right to work" name no one who must give either.
7. **The ledger, and the departures.** Lay the five readings out as a table (the figure): which articles each critique hits, which articles answer it. Then the L16 departures, each in a sentence: a resolution, not a law — rights without powers, until the Covenants of 1966 made some of them treaty obligations for the states that ratified; a vote of 48 to 0 with 8 abstentions whose reasons were the critics' own; a ground deliberately left out; a list that changes kind at Art. 22 and says so; and against all five, a document that writes in duties, limits, nationality, a people, a family, an order and a self-protection clause — which is to say, a document whose drafters had read the objections of 1790 and 1796 and answered what they could. Which critique does it bear out? The lesson gives the reader the method and the table and does not hand down a verdict (course tone): Burke's distinction between real and metaphysical rights is visible in its own structure; Maistre's "no such thing as man" is conceded by Arts. 15 and 21 and confirmed by the abstentions; Oakeshott's abridgment is admitted by Arts. 8 and 10; Strauss's "list without a ground" is exactly what the drafters chose, for reasons the reader must decide between; Scruton's change of kind is at Art. 22, and the drafters marked it. What the document knows that the critics say it cannot: that rights need a community, a law, a nation and an order — it says so in Arts. 15, 28 and 29 — and that saying so is not the same as having them. Hand off: the next lesson reads the earlier universal — a Dominican in 1539 asking what rights Spain had in America, and finding one right of all men, to travel, trade and preach, that licensed war when it was refused.

Headings as claims: "A list agreed on condition no one asks why: the artefact and its vote"; "Burke: the real rights are in the list, and so is the one he would strike"; "Maistre: 'everyone' is the man he never met, and Articles 15 and 21 admit it"; "Oakeshott: the common law abridged and shipped as a poster"; "Strauss: the list without the ground — preference or prudence?"; "Scruton: Article 22, where the obligor changes"; "The ledger: what the document bears out and what it knows".

- **The vote and the eight** (UN Yearbook 1948–49; verify): 48–0–8, two absent; the abstainers named with their stated reasons in one sentence each — the Soviet bloc on sovereignty and economic rights, Saudi Arabia on Arts. 16 and 18, South Africa on equality. Drawn as the tally figure; read as Maistre's evidence in link 3 and as the sourcing drill's metadata in link 1.
- **"On condition no one asks us why"** (UNESCO 1948, Introduction; verify wording): the committee of opposed ideologies, the agreed list, the remark — three sentences, then its two readings (link 5). Maritain in References only.
- **Art. 1's residue** (drafting record; verify): "by nature" and "by God" both struck; "endowed with reason and conscience" what remained; the Confucian proposal and the Thomist's as the two grounds that could not both be written. One paragraph; mark every detail as from the public drafting record.
- **Cassin's portico** (verify in Glendon/Morsink): Preamble as steps; four columns — Arts. 1–2 foundations; 3–11 the person; 12–17 the person among others; 18–21 the citizen; 22–27 the worker and member; 28–30 the pediment. Drawn as the structure figure; it is the drafters' own picture of what kind of thing they made, and the lesson's armature for the article-by-article reading.
- **Burke's five shillings and five hundred pounds** (Reflections S1 l. 1981–84): "equal rights, but not to equal things" — the partnership in which the man with five shillings has an equal right to his five shillings; set against Art. 2 and Art. 22. Then "a share in power… a thing to be settled by convention" against Art. 21. The lesson's one `<Example>` of a critique applied to an article, worked fully; the other four are then done faster.
- **Art. 15 read as an admission** (the text; the DP camps of 1948 as the case before the drafters — anchor: about a million displaced persons still in camps in occupied Germany, Austria and Italy in 1948, bound as "the stateless whom the war had left without a country"): "Everyone has the right to a nationality" says the abstract man needs a concrete one; it names no state that must give it.
- **Arts. 8–11 as the common law abridged** (Oakeshott PE s.3 ll. 4743–49 beside the articles): "effective remedy by the competent national tribunals"; "fair and public hearing"; presumption of innocence; no retroactive law — each matched to the English or American practice it abstracts (habeas corpus; jury trial; the 1689 Bill of Rights; the ex post facto clause of the US Constitution). Then the poster.
- **Art. 22 parsed clause by clause** (Scruton HTBC ll. 2608–2630): "as a member of society" / "the right to social security" / "entitled to realization" / "through national effort and international co-operation" / "in accordance with the organization and resources of each State" / "economic, social and cultural rights" / "indispensable for his dignity and the free development of his personality" — each clause annotated: who the obligor is, what the limit is, where the "weight of political philosophy" sits. Then the four incidents sorted on Arts. 9, 18, 21, 23, 24, 28.
- **Art. 29 and Art. 30 as the drafters' replies** (the text): duties "to the community in which alone the free and full development of his personality is possible"; limits "determined by law… in a democratic society"; no right to destroy the rights — read against Burke (restraints), Scruton (the society presupposed), Strauss (the one teleological clause), Voegelin's Jackson (the suicide pact).

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **The artefact is public; the drafting facts must be verified** (none are in the dossier): the vote, the abstainers and their reasons, the Humphrey draft, Cassin's portico, Chang and Malik, the UNESCO committee and the remark — each is standard but the writer checks it against the UN Yearbook, Morsink or Glendon and cites them in References. A wrong abstainer or a misattributed remark is a factual error in a lesson whose whole method is sourcing. Dates: 10 December 1948 is the one exact day the lesson needs (contingency is the point: Human Rights Day); the Covenants are "1966" and named only.
- **Terms to anchor (L11):** "General Assembly" and "resolution" (a vote of all member states; not binding law); "Palais de Chaillot" (the Paris building where the Assembly met in 1948, since the UN had no home yet); "Nuremberg" (the 1945–46 trials of the Nazi leadership — the reader has 1933 and Weimar, not the trials; one clause); "Covenant" (the two 1966 treaties that made some rights binding for ratifying states); "displaced persons" (above); "Thomist" (the reader has Aquinas; one clause); "jural relations" / "the four incidents" (claim, liberty, power, immunity — defined in the lesson's own words at link 6, as "the standard scheme", with Hohfeld in References); "habeas corpus", "ex post facto" (recall and one clause).
- **L8a:** Maritain, Hohfeld, Morsink, Glendon, Cassin (as historian of his own draft), Berlin, Fawcett, Hamilton, Carens, Dworkin — References only. Burke, Maistre, Oakeshott, Strauss, Scruton, Voegelin, Hobbes, Locke, Cicero are the thinkers whose texts are applied and may be named; Roosevelt, Humphrey, Cassin, Malik and Chang are historical actors in the artefact's making and may be named as such (L8a(b): the document is the named object the lesson studies).
- **Do not re-teach any of the five critiques.** Each enters as a `<Ref>` plus one quotation and is then APPLIED; a paragraph that re-derives real vs metaphysical rights or the stepchild thesis is a required fix. The lesson's new work is the application and the table.
- **How to Be a Conservative is used ONLY for ch. 6's rights passage** (ll. 2596–2800); chs. 2–3, 7, 10 are the nation module's. Do not import oikophilia, "to include is to exclude", or the EU material. Scruton's ECHR inflation cases are 2014 examples, not 1948 — use one at most and mark it as his illustration of a later consequence.
- **The document answers its critics — do not let the lesson become either a demolition or a defence.** The course tone requires each critique at full strength and then the text's reply at full strength, with the reader weighing. The table (figure 7) is the device: a cell is filled only where the text itself supplies the hit or the reply.
- **Berlin's "no lady called Nature, so no creature called Man"** is Berlin's gloss on Maistre (VI pp. 133–134), not a Maistre quotation. The reader has Maistre's own sentence (Considerations ch. 6 p. 96); use that, and if Berlin's phrase is used, write it as the course's paraphrase with Berlin in References.
- **Numbers (playbook-history):** 58 member states, 30 articles, 48–0–8 and 2 absent — all bound in one sentence in link 1 and nowhere else; the DP figure only if bound ("about a million still in camps in 1948") and only once; otherwise order-of-magnitude words. Computation: none. Labs: none.
- **The 1789 and 1776 declarations** are recalled from `burke-reflections` and `oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`, not re-told; the timeline figure gives them a place.
- **Contested empirical claims:** none; the one interpretive dispute (why the drafters were silent — unknowable vs unsayable) is presented as a dispute (link 5) and handed to Exercise 6.
- **Do not moralise** about the abstaining states; report their stated reasons as the record gives them and let them be Maistre's evidence.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval and classification (the one warm-up):** Five articles — 15, 21, 22, 29, 30 — each matched to the critic whose objection it most directly answers, with the clause that does the answering quoted; then each of the five critiques classified as perversity, futility or jeopardy (`what-conservatism-is`): which kind of "no" is each saying to a declaration of rights?
2. **Boundary probe:** A stateless person in a displaced-persons camp in 1948 — no state, no employer, no court of his own. Go down the thirty articles with Scruton's test (does an obligor exist?) and mark which rights he holds, which he holds against no one, and what Art. 15 does for him. Solution: the liberties and immunities (3–12, 18–20) bind everyone around him and so exist; the claims (22–27) bind a state he does not have; Art. 15 gives him a right to the one thing that would make the rest real and names no one who owes it — the degenerate case shows which half of the Declaration is Scruton's "fiction" and which half is not.
3. **Claim to refute:** "The Universal Declaration is a Lockean document: a list of natural rights to life, liberty and property held by individuals against the state." Name the failing steps from the text (Art. 16(3) protects an institution; Art. 22 makes claims the state must satisfy; Art. 29 states duties and limits; the Preamble appeals to "the conscience of mankind", not nature or God) and say what the Declaration IS on the evidence (a mixed list — Burke's real rights, Locke's liberties, and welfare claims — with a deliberately missing ground).
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** Complete the ledger (figure 7) for all thirty articles: sort every article by the four incidents and by which critic it bears out or answers; find the article at which the predominant incident changes (the solution shows it is 22, and that 21 — a power — is the hinge Burke would strike); state in Hohfeld's terms what Art. 28 is (a claim with no correlative duty-bearer) and whether that makes it a right at all on Scruton's test.
5. **Sourcing drill:** The UNESCO committee's finding and the remark "we agree about the rights but on condition no one asks us why" (1947–48). Prediction first: given a committee of Thomists, Marxists, liberals and a Confucian asked to ground a list, what would you expect them to agree on? Then: author (a philosophers' committee convened by UNESCO; the remark one member's), date, audience (the Commission; UNESCO's readers), purpose; what it records (agreement on the list), argues (that practical agreement is possible without theoretical agreement), omits (whose list it was — the Commission's draft, not the philosophers'); what it can be USED to establish — that the silence on foundations was a decision, not an oversight — and what it cannot (that the drafters thought no ground existed).
6. **Synthesis (claim–evidence–reasoning):** Is "agreement on the rights on condition no one asks why" Weber's "Thou shalt have preferences" at the level of states, or the classical prudence of a city that agrees on practice without agreeing on theory? Argue both with Strauss's text (Intro "retail sanity"; ch. II the staircase; ch. IV natural right as "dynamite", the politically good as quotient) and with the document's Art. 29(1) and Art. 26(2); judge, and say what evidence from the drafting record would decide it (whether the strikings of "by nature" and "by God" were made because no ground was believed or because none could be shared).
7. **Counter-argument stem:** "Someone claims the Declaration refutes Maistre: fifty-eight nations agreed about 'man' in 1948." Name two facts the claim must account for (eight abstained for reasons that were Maistre's own — religion, marriage, race, sovereignty; and the document itself presupposes a nation and a people in Arts. 15 and 21) and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that a list agreed by forty-eight governments is evidence about human nature rather than about forty-eight governments — the point Strauss's "universal consent is no condition of natural right" cuts both ways).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `vitoria-right-to-the-americas` (and, two modules on, `carens-open-borders`):** Art. 13 gives everyone the right to leave any country and to return to his own, but not to enter another; Art. 14 gives a right to seek asylum. An earlier universal doctrine, four centuries before, asserted a right of all men to travel, trade and preach in any land. Write what such a right would add to Art. 13, and what it would license when refused. The solution names Vitoria's ius communicationis (1539) and the war it justified — the next lesson's uncomfortable end — and notes that the same right, argued from the opposite side, becomes the open-borders case of `carens-open-borders`.

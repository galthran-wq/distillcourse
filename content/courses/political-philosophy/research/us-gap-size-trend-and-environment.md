# Research brief: us-gap-size-trend-and-environment (The US Black–White gap: size, trend and what environment explains)

status: planned
module: heredity-and-civilization
type: core
goal: Evaluate the evidence on the size and trend of the US Black–White gap in measured cognitive scores and on how much of it measured environments account for, and explain what the Flynn effect does and does not show about its cause

Register rule (binding on the lesson): The course passes no verdict of its own on any author or idea: no condemning or approving labels in its own voice. Judgement comes only through argument and evidence; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when.

This lesson is premise 3 of the module's four-premise argument (groups differ in mean on a test that
measures the same thing in both) and the first evidence on the step from 3 to 4 (therefore partly genetic).
It works through numbers: how large the gap is, whether it has moved, how much of it measured family
circumstances account for, and what the rise of scores across generations — the Flynn effect, defined here —
implies. Each position is stated at full strength, then its strongest objection. The lesson ends on the
dossier's status for §§2–3 and on one limit the dossier establishes — the gap as it stands is not wholly
genetic — and it must not slide from that limit into the claim that the gap is environmental, which no
evidence in this lesson shows. Factual authority: `C/research/heredity-evidence-review.md` §§2, 3, 9 and
"Claims the lessons must NOT make".

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `what-intelligence-tests-measure`: predictive validity, predictive bias (none in the main
  US data), measurement invariance (tenable for US Black and White samples in the main datasets; it says
  nothing about why latent means differ). From `heritability-within-and-between-groups`: heritability rising
  with age, two pots of seed (concept `within-between-fallacy`), the default hypothesis (`default-hypothesis`),
  the four premises and the map of objections (this lesson takes the objections to premise 3 and the first
  ones to the step 3→4). From `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`: the case's own figures for the gap (about 1
  SD in 1994, 1.1 SD in 2005, 0.85 SD for the 2010s), its SES argument (the NLSY gap 1.21 → .76 SD with SES,
  "37 percent", and the claim that SES is partly an effect of ability), the 1.58-SD argument and its
  same-scale assumption, Spearman's hypothesis, the authors' answers to the rise of scores across generations,
  the 50% / 80% / "50 … to 80%" claims — and the three incompatible statements about the gap's history that
  its FORWARD exercise set up.
- **Contributes:** The one move: replace "the gap" as a single fixed number with the evidence on its size,
  history and environmental share, read by both sides. Size: about 0.75 SD in recent child norms (WISC-V 11.6
  points), about 1 SD in adult norms (WAIS-IV 14.5), about 0.85 SD in 2010s achievement data, widening with
  age. History: the period/cohort distinction; narrowing of roughly 0.3–0.5 SD between cohorts born early in
  the century and those born around 1970, flat or slightly wider for later cohorts on most series, maths
  widening after 2020, district gaps from near zero to 1.5 SD. Environment: measured parental education and
  income account for a large share of the child gap and leave about 6 points (concept `ses-residual-gap`,
  introduced), with the SES control ambiguous in both directions. The Flynn effect (concept `flynn-effect`,
  introduced): about 3 points per decade, within-family and so environmental in Norway, different in
  measurement structure from the gap; what each side takes from it. Status: dossier §§2–3, and the limit that
  the gap as it stands is not wholly genetic, while no evidence here excludes a genetic share.
- **Hands off:** The residual gap after measured SES is either unmeasured environment or partly genetic; the
  classical design for separating rearing from parentage is adoption — children of different ancestry reared
  by the same kind of family (`adoption-studies-across-groups`, next). Its concept `placement-confound` is
  reached by the FORWARD exercise. Admixture and polygenic designs (`admixture-and-polygenic-scores`) come
  after; the audit of the 2025 meta-analysis used here as a trend source belongs to `national-data-audited`.
- **Spine step:** "Heredity as an explanation of the gaps between peoples, tested premise by premise — what the tests measure, what heritability can and cannot imply, the hereditarian case at full strength, then the US gap, adoption, admixture and polygenic scores on the step from a real gap to a genetic one — then what follows politically whatever the answer, what ancient DNA shows, the rival explanations from geography and institutions, and the key datasets audited"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): American test series since the army tests of 1917 have found Black children
and adults scoring lower on average than White ones; whether the gap has narrowed matters to both sides,
because a population's genetic make-up barely changes within two generations while its circumstances can;
the series differ in tests, ages and birth years and are read differently; meanwhile whole countries' average
scores rose by about as much as the gap; the lesson works through the numbers. The problem as the reader meets
it, from `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations` (Ref, one sentence): the case's own authors gave three
incompatible histories of the gap. Which, if any, do the data support — and what would each history mean for
the step from premise 3 to premise 4?

Group labels: the sources' census categories (Black, White, Hispanic; "non-Hispanic White" where the source
uses it); said once to be the sources' categories.

1. **How big is the gap? Not one number.** Problem: a reader wants "the gap". Obvious attempt: take the
   classic "15 points, one SD". Why it fails: the gap depends on age, test and decade — recent child norms
   (WISC-V: 103.5 vs 91.9, 11.6 points, 0.77 SD; Weiss l.151), adult norms (WAIS-IV: 14.5 points, 0.97 SD;
   l.153), 2010s achievement data (0.85 SD, mostly NAEP; FR l.766), and a pooled estimate over 139 studies
   1918–2017 of 85, i.e. 15 points, with heterogeneity so high (I² = 98%) that the single pooled number hides
   most of the variation (dossier §2; one clause, forward `<Ref>` `national-data-audited`). The age pattern
   (dossier §2): in the 2006 analysis the gap was about 10 points at age 4 and 21 at age 23 in 1972 (Black IQ
   90 → 79 against a White 100), 4.5 and 15.5 in 2002 — wider by about 11 points across those ages in both
   years; another account gives about 5 points at 4 and 17 at 24. Whether that is a loss with age or younger
   cohorts doing better is not determined (Nisbett l.1093–1098) — the seam to section 2. Premise 3's
   measurement half (Ref `what-intelligence-tests-measure`, one clause): in the main US datasets the tests
   measure the same latent abilities in both groups, so these are differences in the same thing. Answer: about
   0.75 SD in children, about 1 SD in adults, 0.85 SD in 2010s school-test data, larger at older ages. KEEPER.
2. **Has it moved? First decide how to line the scores up.** Problem: three histories (lesson 3). Obvious
   attempt: list each test's gap by the year it was given. Why it fails: when the gap widens with age, and
   when a test given in 1980 to adults aged 20–60 mixes people schooled from the 1930s to the 1970s, a
   test-year series averages over generations whose circumstances differed most; the change one wants to see
   is between people born at different times. The concrete device (Murray 2007 l.30–50): the same vocabulary
   survey shows a small narrowing analysed by survey year and a substantial one analysed by birth year. Define
   *period* (by test year) and *cohort* (by birth year) series. Answer: line the series up by birth cohort, and
   read test-year series as what they are. (Concrete numbers enter in section 3, in the figure.)
3. **The cohort series: narrowed, then stopped.** Problem: what do the cohort series show? The evidence
   (Figure 1; dossier §2 for every value, with the full-text additions marked): one battery's three
   standardisations, fitted, put the IQ gap at about 1.3 SD for people born around 1921, 0.9 in 1958, 0.8 in
   1972, and 1.05 for 1981 (Murray 2007 Table 4); the adult Wechsler 1.14 → 0.73 SD from 1920s to 1971–75
   cohorts; the AFQT 1.21 → 0.97 between the two NLSY cohorts; NAEP narrowest for cohorts born 1969–79; after
   that, flat or slightly wider on most series (Stanford–Binet children 0.65 → 0.62; children of NLSY mothers
   slightly wider), with one exception, the WISC (1.08 → 0.78 between cohorts born 1973–83 and 1986–96). The
   2007 norming of the adult Wechsler, read by birth cohort in one cross-section, runs from 19.3 points (born
   1917–42) to 10.0 (born 1988–91) — with age and cohort confounded (Weiss Table 9). Status (dossier §2,
   verbatim in substance): narrowed by roughly 0.3–0.5 SD between cohorts born early in the 20th century and
   those born around 1970 — settled in direction for achievement data, size disputed for IQ (the earliest
   estimates rest on army tests of 1917 and 1944–45 that disagree, 1.16 vs 1.52 SD [secondary], and on
   samples that missed the rural South); flat or slightly wider for later cohorts on most series. KEEPER:
   "narrowed by about a third to a half of an SD across cohorts born before about 1970; little change since".
   Then the period series in the same figure's second panel: the 2006 analysis (Black gain of about 5.5 points
   on Whites, 1972–2002, under age 25; g gap narrowed by about 5 points), the test-year inventory (1.33 SD
   fitted in 1972 → 0.87 in 1987, flat 1983–2019, 0.85 SD in the 2010s), and the 2023 long-term-trend result
   (age-13 maths gap 35 → 42 points since 2020: Black −13, White −6). Bearing: of the three histories from
   lesson 3, "never narrowed" fails against every cohort series; "narrowed, then stalled" survives; the
   2006 period gain and the WISC exception keep the end-date of narrowing in dispute. Dossier constraints in
   both directions here: no "never narrowed", no "is closing" stated of the present.
4. **How each side reads the history.** Problem: what does a gap that narrowed and then stopped imply for the
   step from 3 to 4? Environmental reading at full strength: a population's genes cannot change by a third of
   an SD in two generations, and changes in who is classified or identifies as Black explain only a small
   fraction (Dickens & Flynn l.63–92) — so the narrowing was environmental, "the constancy of the Black-White
   IQ gap is a myth" (their words, quoted with date 2006), and the narrowing reached the measured g gap (5.13
   points; and since every subtest loads on g, gains could not avoid g — Nisbett 2012 l.1078–1092).
   Hereditarian reading at full strength: the 1994 book itself attributed the narrowing to environment
   (l.3088 — Ref lesson 3) and its model always included environment (the 50/50 model "requires" environmental
   effects, R&J p. 279); the narrowing came in the cohorts whose environments changed most (rural South,
   segregated schooling) and stopped for cohorts born after about 1970 despite continued social investment
   (FR l.829–831); the gains do not follow the subtests' g loadings (the correlations are negative — Dickens &
   Flynn l.292 themselves report it; the 2025 meta-analysis gives −.28 and −.38), so they may not be gains in
   g. A trend source both sides cite: the 139-study meta-analysis found no significant change with cohort
   (+0.04 points per birth-year, not significant) — compatible with no change and with a moderate one; one
   clause and a forward `<Ref>` to `national-data-audited`, which audits it. Bearing: the history shows the gap
   as it stood in mid-century cohorts had an environmental component; it does not show whether what remains
   has a genetic one.
5. **Place and the last few years.** Problem: if the remaining gap were fixed by ancestry, how much should it
   vary between places and between years? The evidence (dossier §2, §9): district achievement gaps range from
   near zero to more than 1.5 SD, and local SES, segregation and schooling account for 43–72% of that
   geographic variation (a study's summary, **[abstract]**); the age-13 maths gap widened by 7 scale points in
   three years after 2020. Both are achievement tests, not IQ batteries (say so). Bearing: a gap that varies
   from zero to 1.5 SD with place and moves within three years is not fixed by ancestry as it stands; that is
   all these two facts show.
6. **What measured environment explains: the residual after SES.** Problem: the obvious environmental account
   is family circumstances. The gradient first (Figure 4): in the WISC-V norms, children's mean FSIQ rises from
   about 88 (parents with eight years of school or fewer) to about 111 (a parent with a graduate degree)
   (Weiss l.84–93), and Black and White parents in the norm samples differ in education. The worked
   `<Example>` (WISC-V, Weiss Table 4, l.229–250): with race alone in the regression, the gap is 12.8 points;
   adding parents' education leaves 8.3; adding income (estimated from zip code) leaves 6.7. The paper reports
   this as "78.7% mediated" — computed on the share of score variance the race term explains (.089 → .019),
   not on points; in points the gap fell by about half (12.8 → 6.7). The WISC-IV sample: 10.5 → 7.9 → 6.3
   ("65.4%"). Adults (ages 20–90, own education, occupation, income, region): 14.9 → 11.2 ("38.4%") — measured
   SES accounts for less in adults. Define `ses-residual-gap` (introduced): the part of a group difference
   left after controlling for measured SES — about 6 points of the child WISC gap. KEEPER: "about 6 points
   left in children (about 11 in adults) after parents' education and income".
7. **Why the SES control settles nothing by itself.** Problem: does the 6-point residual measure the genetic
   part, or does the shrinkage measure the environmental part? Neither, and the reason runs both ways. (a) The
   control may over-correct: parents' education is partly a result of parents' ability, which children
   inherit through genes and through the home — so removing it removes part of what is to be explained (the
   1994 book's argument, Ref lesson 3: SES "is also a result of cognitive ability"; the authors of the
   mediation study themselves: their results are "clearly consistent with both hereditary and acculturation
   interpretations", l.93–96). (b) The control may under-correct: years of schooling and a zip-code income are
   "only gross indicators" (l.~357) of the environments that matter; parents' academic expectations, for
   instance, carry much of the SES effect in the same data (Table 10). Answer: the residual is an upper bound on
   nothing and a lower bound on nothing; it is what measured SES leaves. Dossier constraints: "SES explains the
   gap" fails (6 points remain in children, more in adults, and SES is partly downstream of ability); "the
   residual is genetic" is not shown either.
8. **The Flynn effect.** Problem from the opening: whole countries' average scores rose during the century by
   about as much as the gap. Define `flynn-effect` (introduced): the rise of mean scores on intelligence tests
   across birth cohorts, about 3 IQ points per decade in the 20th century across many countries, largest on
   non-verbal reasoning tests and smaller in recent decades (dossier §3 status; the per-test-type rates are
   an abstract-level summary of a 271-sample meta-analysis, cite as such), with a reversal in some countries.
   The name: after the political scientist who documented it most widely (scholar in References). Its
   evidence for an environmental cause: in Norway, brothers born years apart differ by the same amount as
   unrelated men born the same years apart — rise 0.20 points a year within families vs 0.18 across (born
   1962–75), decline 0.33 vs 0.34 (born 1975–91) — so neither the rise nor the fall comes from which families
   had children (Bratsberg l.199–206): environmental, though the study cannot name the factor. Schooling is one
   candidate with quasi-experimental support (1–5 points per extra year, **[abstract]**). Status (dossier §3,
   verbatim in substance): settled that mean scores rose about 3 points per decade and that the Norwegian rise
   and decline are within-family, hence environmental. KEEPER: "about 3 points per decade — a gap-sized change
   in about fifty years — environmental".
9. **What the Flynn effect does and does not show about the gap.** Problem: the 1994 book raised it against
   its own case (Ref lesson 3); which side does it help? Environmental reading at full strength: it is a real
   case of the two pots (Ref `heritability-within-and-between-groups`): scores highly heritable within each
   cohort, yet the difference between cohorts is environmental — so high within-group heritability does not
   make a between-group difference genetic, in fact as well as in logic; and it shows an environment can shift
   a whole population by a gap-sized amount without anyone having identified the factor — the kind of
   "mysterious" environment the 1.58-SD argument called implausible (Ref lesson 3). Hereditarian reading at
   full strength: the cohort gains are not the same kind of difference as the gap — measurement invariance
   fails between cohorts but holds between Black and White Americans in the main data, so "the nature of the
   Flynn effect is qualitatively different from the nature of B–W differences" (Wicherts et al. 2004 — a study
   by researchers who do not hold the hereditarian view; state that it supports this half); the gains sit apart
   from g in the case's own analysis (Ref lesson 3); and other groups rose too, so the gap need not shrink.
   Dossier status (§3): it shows environments can move scores by a gap-sized amount; it does not show what
   causes the gap. And neither side has shown which environmental differences between US Black and White
   children, of what size, produce a latent difference of the observed size (dossier §3). Bearing on the
   default hypothesis (Ref lesson 2): the Flynn effect shows the default can fail between cohorts; it does not
   show it fails between these groups. Dossier constraint: "The Flynn effect proves the gap is environmental"
   is not made.
10. **Where the evidence leaves premise 3 and the step to 4.** Problem: what can now be said, and what cannot?
    The status lines (dossier §2, §3, "Overall answer" 2 and 4), stated plainly: the gap is real on the tests,
    measured the same way in both groups in the main datasets, about 0.75 SD in children, 1 SD in adults, 0.85
    SD in 2010s achievement data; it narrowed across cohorts born before about 1970 and stopped narrowing on
    most series; maths gaps widened after 2020. The limit the evidence establishes: the gap as it stands is not
    wholly genetic, because it narrowed by several points within decades, varies from near zero to 1.5 SD
    across districts, and moved after 2020 — none of which a fixed genetic difference can do. The limit it does
    not establish: that the remainder is environmental — no measured set of environments accounts for all of
    it, and no evidence in this lesson excludes a genetic share. What would settle the trend: one
    representative battery re-normed on the same design each decade and analysed by birth cohort; what would
    settle the environmental share: an identified set of environmental causes whose measured group difference
    times causal effect reproduces the gap on an invariant measure (dossier §§2–3). `<Conclusion>`: the reader
    can now state the gap's size and history with their uncertainties, read an SES control in both
    directions, and say what the Flynn effect does and does not show; the open problem: measured SES leaves a
    residual that is either unmeasured environment or partly genetic, and the oldest design for separating
    the two — raise children of different parentage in the same kind of home — is the next lesson's
    (`adoption-studies-across-groups`).

Headings as claims: "Not one number: the gap by age, test and decade"; "By year of testing or by year of
birth"; "Narrowed, then stopped: the cohort series"; "Two readings of the same history"; "A gap that varies
with place and year"; "About six points left after parents' education and income"; "Why the SES control
settles nothing by itself"; "Three points a decade, within families"; "What the rise does and does not show
about the gap"; "Not wholly genetic as it stands — and what that does not mean".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms a newcomer will not know (L11; ≤ 3 new per section):** standardisation / norm sample (section 1:
  the representative sample a test is scored against when published — one clause); achievement test vs IQ
  battery (section 1: NAEP measures school reading and maths; its scores correlate with g but are not IQ
  scores); pooled estimate and heterogeneity (section 1, one clause each — the 2025 meta-analysis's I² means
  the studies disagree far more than sampling error explains); period vs cohort (section 2, defined);
  fitted value (section 3, one clause: the value a trend line gives for a year, not an observation); mediation
  / "percent mediated" (section 6, defined in the Example); ΔR² (section 6: the extra share of score variance
  a variable explains once the others are in — one clause, only inside the Example); zip-code income estimate
  (one clause); within-family comparison (section 8); measurement invariance (Ref lesson 1, one clause);
  g loading (Ref lesson 3, one clause); district gap (section 5).
- **Do not merge the series.** IQ batteries, the AFQT and NAEP are different instruments; NAEP and the 2023
  result are achievement tests. Figure 1 marks instrument type; prose never averages across them.
- **Do not claim the cohort narrowing for the 1994 book's period figures or vice versa.** The 1994 book's
  ".15 to .25 SD" was a period estimate from school tests (l.3086); the cohort figures are a different
  quantity.
- **The 2006 period gain and the cohort flatline are not a contradiction to be resolved by the lesson.** The
  2006 analysis covers people tested 1972–2002 under age 25 (born roughly 1950–1990) and finds about 5.5
  points; the cohort series find little change for cohorts born after about 1970 except on the WISC. The
  dossier lists the end-date as disputed; the lesson says so and stops.
- **"Percent mediated" is a share of variance.** See the correction in Sources. The lesson's prose uses points
  ("about 6 points left"); the variance figure appears only inside the Example, explained.
- **Nothing in this lesson shows the gap is environmental**, and the writer must not write a sentence that
  implies it (e.g. "since the gap moved, it is environmental"). What moved shows an environmental component;
  the remainder is open.
- **Kirkegaard & Jensen 2025** is a trend source here only; its moderator test vs its discussion's claim is
  `national-data-audited`'s material. The lesson may not compute the moderator's confidence interval or set
  the discussion against the table (that is lesson 10's exercise). One clause: "a 2025 meta-analysis of 139
  studies found no statistically significant change with birth cohort; lesson 10 examines what that test can
  show". Its journal (*Mankind Quarterly*) is metadata for lesson 10's sourcing drill, not an argument here.
- **The army tests.** The 1917 Army Alpha/Beta figure (1.16 SD) and the 1944–45 AGCT (1.52 SD) are known here
  only through a 2007 article citing a 1975 review [secondary]; mention only as "early estimates disagree"
  and Ref `chamberlain-and-grant` for what the 1917 tests were and how they were used.
- **Group membership.** The objection "the Black population's ancestry or self-identification changed" is
  real and must be answered, not skipped: the 2006 analysis estimates it at a small fraction (l.63–92).
- **Register.** Labels in the sources, used only as quotations with who and when if at all: Dickens & Flynn's
  "The constancy of the Black-White IQ gap is a myth" (2006) — an empirical claim in their voice, quotable as
  theirs; Rushton & Jensen's "moralistic fallacy" (2005) — not needed here. The mediation paper's subtitle
  ("The case for environmental and social justice", 2020) is the authors' framing; if mentioned, as theirs.
  No label in the course's voice for any researcher on either side.
- **DOSSIER CONSTRAINTS** (quoted from "Claims the lessons must NOT make"; both directions):
  - Hereditarian direction: "'The gap has never narrowed' — every cohort series shows narrowing before ~1970s
    cohorts." → section 3 rejects it on the evidence and section 4 still gives the hereditarian reading of the
    narrowing at full strength. "'The gap is X% genetic' for any X — including Rushton & Jensen's 50–80% or the
    survey mean of ~49%. No design has estimated it." → the 6-point residual is never called the genetic part.
    "'High heritability within groups implies the gap is probably genetic' …" → section 9 shows a real case
    where it fails between cohorts, and stops there.
  - Environmental direction: "'Science has shown the gap is entirely environmental' / 'genes play no role' —
    the APA task force said no adequate explanation exists." → section 10 states the limit and its converse.
    "'The Flynn effect proves the gap is environmental' — it proves environments can produce gaps of that
    size; it differs in structure from the gap." → section 9. "'SES explains the gap' — measured SES accounts
    for part, leaving ~6 points in children and more in adults; and SES is itself partly downstream of
    ability." → sections 6–7. "'The gap is closing' stated of the present — it stopped narrowing for cohorts
    born after the 1970s on most series and widened in maths after 2020." → section 3. "'Test bias explains
    the gap' — main US datasets are invariant and do not under-predict." → section 1 recalls lesson 1.
  - Status lines to state in substance (verbatim in the brief, paraphrase allowed in the lesson):
    §2 — "the gap is real on the tests; in the main US datasets the tests measure the same latent abilities in
    both groups (moderate–strong evidence; partial non-invariance in one referred urban sample). Size: about
    0.75 SD in recent child norms, about 1 SD in adult norms, about 0.85 SD in 2010s achievement data; it widens
    with age. Trend: narrowed by roughly 0.3–0.5 SD between cohorts born early in the 20th century and those born
    around 1970 (settled in direction for achievement data; size disputed for IQ); flat or slightly wider for
    later cohorts on most series; maths gaps widened after 2020."
    §3 — "settled that mean scores rose about 3 IQ points per decade in the 20th century and that the Norwegian
    rise and decline are within-family, hence environmental (strong). Settled that cohort gains and the US
    Black–White gap differ in measurement structure (moderate; MGCFA comparisons). It shows environments can
    move scores by a gap-sized amount; it does not show what causes the gap."
    "Overall answer" 4 — "The gap is not wholly genetic as it now stands: it narrowed by several points within
    decades, varies from near zero to 1.5 SD across school districts, and moved after 2020. And no evidence
    excludes a genetic share."
  - Reading tags carried into the lesson's figure source notes: Pietschnig & Voracek, Ritchie & Tucker-Drob,
    Reardon et al. **[abstract]**; the army-test figures **[secondary]**; NAEP 2023 [highlights page];
    everything else [full].
- **Numbers (L12):** keepers — "about 0.75 SD in children, 1 SD in adults"; "narrowed by about a third to a half
  of an SD across cohorts born before about 1970, little change since"; "about 6 points left after parents'
  education and income"; "about 3 points per decade". Everything else lives in Figures 1–7; prose gives
  relations ("wider in adults than in children", "about half the point gap").
- **Not this lesson's material:** adoption, Eyferth and the Minnesota study (5); admixture and polygenic

   "parental SES explains four-fifths of the gap" so that it is true.

   the trend that the evidence supports.

## Module 1 — greek-discovery: Who should rule, and which Greek answer (rule by knowledge, rule by nature, or the mixed regime of the middle) survives a test against a real city?

**Module verdict** (in `sparta-as-test-case`). Grade: on balance.
Of the module's three theories, the mixed constitution holds up best against Sparta. Sparta lasted for centuries, and Aristotle gives the reason in II.9: "the people are contented when they have a share in the highest office". The balance that held it was a balance of interest, not of virtue. Aristotle's single-virtue account of decline fits the record better than Plato's sequence from honour to money: the city trained for war won its empire, ran short of citizens (oliganthropia), and lost Messenia after Leuctra in 371. Natural slavery fails the test. The helots were Greeks enslaved by conquest, held down by the krypteia and "always lying in wait". Aristotle's own I.6 refuses to justify slavery of that kind, and in II.9 he treats the helots as a management problem, never as a case of slavery by nature. Rule by knowledge has no case in the record to test. The Greek answer that survives is Aristotle's: give every part a share and keep the citizen body broad.
- Strongest objection to this verdict: the record is one city, known only through admirers and enemies, and Sparta's stability may owe more to the shared fear of the helots than to the mixture. This does not overturn the verdict, because Aristotle's own account already names the shrinking citizen body and the helot danger as the causes of the end. It weakens the stability claim to "fits", not "proves". The verdict would be overturned by evidence that Sparta's offices were controlled by a closed clique throughout its stable centuries.

### Lesson verdicts
- `greek-miracle` — Claim judged: political argument arose in Greece because sovereignty was broken up and power placed "in the middle", not because of a special people, human-like gods, competition between cities or the phalanx. Verdict: the arrangement is the only rival that is present wherever the result appears and absent wherever it is missing, and "a special people" cannot be tested at all, because no trait can be named that existed before the change it is meant to explain. Grade: on balance. Decided by: the lesson's own test cases (Mesopotamia, Phoenicia, Etruria) and its timeline exercise. Would be overturned by: Phoenician or Etruscan inscriptions showing rotating offices and public law, which would turn their silence into an accident of survival. The drafted lesson already reaches this conclusion in its table; the brief should let it say so outright.
- `thucydides-power-and-justice` — Claim judged: the Athenian thesis at Melos, that justice arises only between equals in power and the appeal to fairness is worthless to the weak. Verdict: as a description of how cities at war behaved, the thesis is borne out by the war's record on both sides. Sparta did not come for Melos, Sparta killed the Plataeans (III.68), and Athens destroyed Scione and Melos. As a claim that fairness protects no one, it fails on the record of 404. When the Athenians were besieged, they feared they would "suffer what they had done to the Melians". Lysander restored the Melians to their island, and Sparta refused the Corinthian and Theban demand to destroy Athens. That is the Melians' "common protection" at work, though Sparta's motive can also be read as interest (a counterweight to Thebes). Grade: on balance. Decided by: Thucydides III.68 and V.116, and Xenophon, Hellenica II.2.3, II.2.9 and II.2.19–20. Would be overturned by: a reading of Sparta's restraint in 404 as pure balance-of-power calculation with no element of the appeal to Athens's past service. Xenophon reports that appeal, so the verdict stays "on balance" and not "strong".
- `decline-of-regimes` — Claim judged: Plato's chain (timocracy, oligarchy, democracy, tyranny) as a law every city follows, and its last link, that democratic freedom breeds tyranny. Verdict: as a fixed sequence it fails. Aristotle's objection in V.12 holds: regimes change into any other, and tyranny often passes into another tyranny. The demagogue-to-tyrant mechanism has real support for archaic Greece; Aristotle's V.5 has "most of the ancient tyrants were originally demagogues". It fails for the democracy Plato knew best. Athens's democracy was overthrown twice, in 411 and 404, both times by oligarchs, and it ended under Macedon, never in a tyranny raised by the demos. Grade: strong against the law; on balance for the mechanism in archaic cases. Decided by: Politics V.5 and V.12 (staged in `texts/aristotle/`), and the Athenian dates the course already holds (the Five Thousand in 411, the Thirty in 404). Would be overturned by: a body of classical-era democracies that ended in tyrannies raised from the demos.
- `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery` — Claim judged: some men are slaves by nature, so mastery over them is just. Verdict: the argument fails by Aristotle's own concessions. Nature "often fails" to give the bodies and souls the theory needs, so there is no mark by which a natural slave can be known. War captives may be the wrong men, and the master may have the slavish soul. A justification that cannot pick out its object justifies no actual slavery. What does not fall with it is the premise that people differ in ability. The failure is in the step from a difference in ability to a right of mastery. Grade: established (the failure is internal and textual). Decided by: Politics I.5–6, and the helots in `sparta-as-test-case`. Would be overturned by: a criterion, stated in advance, that separates natural slaves from free men and does not reduce to "whoever was captured". Aristotle has none.

### No verdict
- `thrasymachus-and-the-ring` — the challenge is deliberately left unanswered and carried by the whole Republic and later lessons.
- `kallipolis-and-the-philosopher-king` — reconstruction. Aristotle's "all say mine" objection is strong, and Plato concedes the city is "for gods or sons of gods" (Laws V 739), but the question is carried to the Laws and Aristotle lessons.
- `platos-laws-second-best-city` — extra lesson that reports Plato's own concessions. Nothing core depends on it.
- `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle` — the theory of polity and the middle class. Its stability claim is tested in `sparta-as-test-case`, and later in `british-franchise`.
- `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life` — the theory of stasis, tested in `sparta-as-test-case`. Machiavelli's counter-thesis is judged in `machiavelli-discourses-republic`.

### Sources the briefs must add

Dakyns translation, public domain: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1174. The book and translator are verified; the passage numbers are from memory and should be checked against the text [UNVERIFIED].

## Module 2 — rome-and-christianity: What holds a commonwealth together (its constitution, its justice, or its gods), and what happened to that bond when Rome's gods were replaced?

**Module verdict** (in `altar-of-victory`). Grade: strong.
The documents of 384 bear out the civil-theology account of what held Rome together, and they show that both sides held it. Symmachus argues the ancestral cult is the condition of Rome's existence. Ambrose does not answer that the commonwealth can do without a public worship. He answers that it must have the right one. He reasons do-ut-des himself (the good harvest of 384), and he backs "the emperor serves God" with the threat that the emperor will find no bishop in his church. The Christian victory replaced Rome's god; it did not separate the city from worship. Augustine's later "the heavenly city preserves the diverse laws and customs of the nations" (XIX.17) describes neither party's conduct in 384. Natural law decides nothing in the case, because both sides claim it in opposite senses.
- Strongest objection to this verdict: the record shows bargaining (a boy emperor, a divided court, a usurper in Gaul), so the outcome may reflect power, not either theory. This does not overturn the verdict, because the bargaining was over the cult itself and lasted a decade (391, 393, Prudentius in c. 402). Both sides acted as if the public worship were what the commonwealth turned on. The verdict would be overturned by evidence that the court treated the altar as a pure patronage question, indifferent to doctrine.

### Lesson verdicts
- `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome` — Claim judged: (a) the cycle of regimes is a natural law with a fixed order; (b) Polybius's forecast (VI.57) that Rome's mixture would decay through wealth, rivalry for office and a flattered people. Verdict: (a) fails. The cycle survives only if "stage" is read loosely, and the brief's own Exercise 6 shows that Athens, Sparta and Rome do not follow its order. (b) The forecast is borne out in outline. A tribune carried a land law over the senate in 133 and was killed by senators. A century of rivalry for office and popular leaders followed and ended in one man's rule under Augustus, a return to monarchy as the cycle says. The route differs from the forecast: the republic was ended by generals with armies loyal to them, not by mob-rule. Grade: strong for the outline of (b); strong against (a). Decided by: the record from the Gracchi to 27 BC. Would be overturned by: an account on which the late Republic's collapse owed nothing to rivalry for office and everything to external causes.
- `augustine-two-cities` — Claim judged: (a) the pagan charge of 410, that Rome fell because it abandoned its gods; (b) Augustine's proof that, by Scipio's own definition, Rome was never a republic. Verdict: (a) fails on the record. Rome had been sacked under its old gods (the Gauls, c. 390 BC, Augustine's own example). The Christian eastern empire, with the same religion, lasted until 1453. The political version of the charge, that Christianity weakened civic devotion, is not settled here and is carried to Machiavelli. (b) fails as a refutation of Scipio on Scipio's terms. As the brief's own objection says, Augustine changes the meaning of *ius* from Romans' agreed right to worship of the true God, then finds Rome wanting by the new meaning. The argument holds only for a reader who already grants his theology. Grade: strong for (a); strong for (b). Decided by: the brief's equivocation objection, City of God II–III, and the survival of the eastern empire. Would be overturned by: (a) evidence that the Christianization of the West, unlike the East, removed something the West's defence depended on, which is a different and better argument than the one made in 410.
- `hellenization-of-christianity` — Claim judged: Harnack's kernel (the kingdom within, the Fatherhood of God, the infinite value of the soul) as what the historical Jesus preached, against Nietzsche's counter-thesis. Verdict: the thesis both men share, that the Christianity Europe received was remade in Greek concepts and Roman forms, is established by the evidence the lesson lays out. Harnack's kernel fails as history. The historical Jesus's preaching was eschatological: the kingdom was coming soon, as an act of God. This has been the main line of scholarship since Weiss (1892) and Schweitzer (1906). The brief itself calls Harnack's kernel "his selection". Which kernel, if any, should have survived is a question of value that the course's evidence cannot settle. Grade: established (the shared thesis); strong (against Harnack's kernel as history). Would be overturned by: the non-eschatological reading of Jesus (a minority position in current scholarship) becoming the consensus.

### No verdict
- `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law` — natural law against convention is the course's long arc (Aquinas, Hobbes, Hume, Strauss). Two partial results the brief may state without a lesson verdict: the argument from diversity of customs does not prove that justice is convention, and Laelius's "rule over those better off ruled" inherits natural slavery's failure.
- `constantine-and-representation` — the question mark ("does the Trinity end political theology?") is deliberately carried to `schmitt-political-theology`. Note for that lesson: Byzantium and Charlemagne tell against the strong form of the thesis.

### Sources the briefs must add
- `polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome`: the sequel to 133 BC in outline — Marius and the armies loyal to their generals (107 onward), Sulla (82), Caesar (49), Augustus (27). A standard account such as R. Syme, *The Roman Revolution* (1939), or M. Beard, *SPQR* (2015).
- `augustine-two-cities`: City of God II–III on Rome's disasters under the old gods (Books I–XIII were "not read by the swarm", according to the brief), and the eastern empire's survival to 1453.
- `hellenization-of-christianity`: J. Weiss, *Die Predigt Jesu vom Reiche Gottes* (1892); A. Schweitzer, *Von Reimarus zu Wrede* (1906; Eng. *The Quest of the Historical Jesus*, 1910). Named in References; the fact is stated in the body without the names.

## Module 3 — christendom-to-sovereignty: Must sovereignty be one and undivided, or can a mixed or limited state stand?

**Module verdict** (in `english-revolution-as-test`). Grade: strong.
Bodin and Hobbes were right that a kingdom with no settled answer to "who decides" goes to war. In 1642 king and Parliament each claimed powers that were marks of sovereignty, neither could say where sovereignty lay, and the result was war, regicide and an army's rule. Hobbes's further claim fails on the same record: that a mixed or limited monarchy cannot stand at all (XXIX's "Mixt Government" as three factions). The settlement of 1660–61 restored a crown limited by the reforms of 1641, and after 1689 a monarchy limited by Parliament lasted for centuries. What the record supports is narrower than the theory: there must be a settled last word (king-in-parliament), but that word can be held by a composite body and bound by limits.
- Strongest objection to this verdict: Hobbes allows sovereignty to sit in an assembly, so king-in-parliament after 1689 is a single sovereign, and the theory is vindicated, not refuted. This narrows but does not overturn the verdict. The restored and post-1689 constitutions kept real division (the Crown's executive, Parliament's purse, independent courts), which XXIX calls fatal. The record shows that division can last when a final procedure is agreed. It would be overturned by showing that the limits of 1660 and 1689 were nominal and the Crown or Parliament ruled without check.

### Lesson verdicts
- `machiavelli-discourses-republic` — Claim judged: the quarrel of nobles and people was the source of Roman liberty (I.4), against the classical view that faction is the city's disease, and against Filmer's reading of the same centuries as an "ocean of blood". Verdict: conflict channelled through institutions (tribunes, public accusation) produced Rome's liberty laws at little cost. From the Tarquins to the Gracchi, Machiavelli counts eight or ten exiles and very few deaths (I.4). Conflict outside those institutions destroyed the Republic. Machiavelli concedes this himself: the agrarian law revived by the Gracchi "finally overthrew the liberty of Rome" (I.37). Both sides are right about different kinds of conflict. Filmer's reading fits only the century after 133. Grade: on balance. Decided by: Discourses I.4 and I.37 (staged at `research/texts/machiavelli/discourses.txt`, l.1062–1072 and l.3720–3732), and I.7–8 on accusation against calumny. Would be overturned by: evidence of large-scale bloodshed in the conflict of the orders before 133.
- `filmer-patriarcha` — Claim judged: political power is fatherly power inherited from Adam, so kings rule by natural right. Verdict: the argument fails at the step it needs most. No living king can be shown to be Adam's heir. Filmer's fallback, that every authority "however acquired" counts as a father's, collapses right into possession, so the theory can no longer tell a king from a usurper. Filmer's strongest point stands and is carried on: no one consents to being born into a polity. Grade: established (for the inheritance step). Decided by: the gaps the lesson marks itself, and Locke's First Treatise. Would be overturned by: no conceivable evidence, since the heir cannot be identified.

### No verdict
- `aquinas-law-and-kingship` — reconstruction. The enforcement question (who judges the king?) is carried to Bodin and the English test.
- `machiavelli-effectual-truth` — the dispute with Aquinas ("tyranny does not pay") largely dissolves into Machiavelli's own rule (do not be hated; rest on the people). "Was Machiavelli a Machiavellian?" is carried to the Discourses.
- `bodin-sovereignty` — definition. Its no-mixture thesis is judged in the module verdict.
- `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` — derivation. Its empirical premise about life without a common power is judged in `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`, where the dispute is staged.
- `hobbes-sovereign-and-liberty` — tested in `english-revolution-as-test`.

### Sources the briefs must add
- `machiavelli-discourses-republic`: Discourses I.37 (the agrarian law and the Gracchi). It is staged but not cited in the brief.
- `english-revolution-as-test`: the post-1689 record in outline (the Bill of Rights 1689, the Act of Settlement 1701, annual sessions of Parliament from the Mutiny Act), to show the limited monarchy lasted. A standard account such as J. Hoppit, *A Land of Liberty?* (2000).

## Module 4 — contract-revolution-reaction: Can a people frame a government for itself from principles, or does designed order fail, as Burke and Maistre said it would?

**Module verdict** (in `assignats`). Grade: strong.
Burke's forecasts for the 1790s came true in outline: paper money on confiscated land ended in collapse, the Terror followed, and then a general took power. His argument that no single generation can redesign a whole society at once is borne out by that record. Maistre's stronger thesis fails on the same record: that nothing durable is made by deliberation, and designed law is "paper". The departments, which Burke said would not obey Paris, became the instruments of the most centralised administration in Europe and exist today. The metric system, decreed "for all peoples, all times", is the world's measure. The Civil Code outlasted every regime. The answer is that a people cannot design a whole society at one stroke without paying Burke's price, but designed institutions can last. The ones that did were mostly enacted under one ruler, built on the old monarchy's centralisation, and were not the constitutions.
- Strongest objection to this verdict: the institutions that lasted were Napoleon's, made by a "legislator who says fiat", and Maistre can claim them as proof that only a single will, not an assembly, founds. This narrows the verdict but does not overturn it. The departments (1790) and the metric system (1795) were assembly work, and Maistre's thesis denied deliberate making as such. Separately, the assignat collapsed by a route Burke did not name: war finance and price controls after 1792. The verdict would be overturned if the departments and the metric system turned out to be continuations of designs of the old regime rather than new designs.

### Lesson verdicts
- `hume-against-the-original-contract` — Claim judged: legitimate government rests on the consent of the governed, given at the origin or tacitly by residence. Verdict: as history the original contract fails, because the governments Hume surveys began in conquest and usurpation. Tacit consent by residence also fails: the sleeping passenger cannot be said to consent by staying aboard. Hume's further point holds, that the duty of allegiance rests on the same ground as the duty to keep promises (the general interest), so deriving one from the other gains nothing. What survives for consent theory is consent as a hypothetical test, not a historical event. That version is carried to Rawls. Grade: strong. Decided by: "Of the Original Contract" and Locke's thin answer to "no historical instances" (Second Treatise §§100–112). Would be overturned by: a body of states founded and kept by a consent that individuals actually gave. The ratification of the US constitution in 1787–88 is the nearest case, and it bound non-voters and later generations, as Hume predicts.
- `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will` — Claim judged: natural man is solitary and peaceable, and violence and domination are made by society, so Hobbes "read society into nature". Verdict: the solitary natural man fails. Humans have always lived in groups, which is Maistre's Hottentot objection and Aristotle's political animal. On violence the evidence leans towards Hobbes and away from Rousseau. Archaeology and ethnography record high rates of lethal violence in many societies without states, though the rates vary widely, and among mobile foragers much killing is personal rather than war. Hobbes is wrong about the unit, since the fighting is between groups and not all against all. Grade: established (against the solitary natural man); strong (that lethal violence is common without a state). Decided by: the comparative evidence below. Would be overturned by: a consistent finding that non-state societies have lower rates of violent death than states. The current data do not show this.
- `maistre-and-bonald` — Claim judged: the charge that Maistre's doctrine is a first draft of fascism. Verdict: the strong version fails, the charge that Maistre is a source of fascism. His influence ran to ultramontane Catholics and to the Action française, not to Rome or Berlin. His system is centred on God and anti-nationalist where fascism is centred on man and the nation, and the charge leaves "fascism" undefined. Two narrower points stand. Maistre glorifies war as "mysterious and divine", and his decisionism passes through Donoso to Schmitt's case against parliament. His argument against the contract is correct (a promise presupposes a society in which promises bind), and the charge's own author concedes it. Grade: strong (against the source version); on balance (for the narrower affinity). Decided by: the brief's own sources (Berlin; Garrard's reply, pp. 117–131). Would be overturned by: documented reading of Maistre among the founders of Italian Fascism or National Socialism.
- `herder-and-fichte-volk-and-nation` — Claim judged: Fichte's step from Herder's plurality of peoples to rank, in which German is a living original language, the neo-Latin languages are "dead at the root", and the Germans carry the hope of mankind. Verdict: the ranking fails. Fichte's reply holds against the charges of racial nationalism and conquest, since he disclaims descent and universal monarchy. It does not answer the charge of rank. The "living/dead" criterion has no test Fichte or anyone has stated, and his own example cuts against it. *Humanität*, his specimen of a dead foreign word, is the word Herder made the end of history. *Menschlichkeit*, his living alternative, is itself formed on Latin *humanitas* [UNVERIFIED]. Herder's objection, written before Fichte spoke, stands. Grade: strong. Decided by: Herder XV.III and XVI.VI against Fichte's paras 55, 107 and 231. Would be overturned by: a measurable sense in which speakers of a continuously inherited language think more "livingly" than speakers of an adopted one. None has been shown.

### No verdict
- `locke-property-and-consent` — reconstruction. Consent is judged in Hume, property in Rousseau and the empire module, and resistance in Burke.
- `burke-reflections` — his forecasts are tested in `assignats` (the module verdict).
- `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state` — reconstruction. The historicism charge is carried to `strauss-natural-right-and-history`, Marx's inversion to `marx-manifesto`.
- `donoso-cortes-on-dictatorship` — his dilemma is tested on 1799 in `assignats` (it fits 1799, not what followed) and fully in `article-48-weimar`.

## Balance check

These four modules carry 13 lesson verdicts and 4 module verdicts. On positions the right holds or claims as its own:
- **Against:** natural slavery (`aristotle-political-animal…`), Filmer's patriarchal right, Fichte's ranking of peoples, Plato's law that democracy ends in tyranny (`decline-of-regimes`), Hobbes's and Bodin's claim that a mixed state cannot stand (module 3), Maistre's thesis that nothing designed endures (module 4), and Sparta's natural hierarchy (module 1). The "special people" explanation of Greece (`greek-miracle`) also goes against.
- **For:** Hume against the original contract, Rousseau's natural man refuted (Hobbes nearer on violence), the "Maistre as source of fascism" charge rejected, Burke's forecasts of the 1790s (module 4), Polybius's forecast of Rome's decay, and the mixed constitution (module 1).
- **Neither side's position, or both:** Thucydides (the realist description stands, the Melians' prudence is partly vindicated), Machiavelli (both sides right about different kinds of conflict), Augustine (the pagan charge and his proof both fail), Harnack (liberal Protestant history fails), the Altar of Victory, and Byzantium.

The split is roughly even. Most verdicts against right-leaning positions fall on the reactionary and absolutist strands (Filmer, Fichte, natural slavery, Maistre's strong thesis). Most verdicts for the right fall on its sceptical and conservative strand (Hume, Burke). That pattern comes from the evidence, not from a quota. I checked each verdict for shading. Two came closest. The Maistre verdict could look like a defence of the right, but it rests on the brief's own scholarly sources and keeps the narrower affinity charge. The Thucydides verdict leans on a source outside the course, Xenophon, and is graded "on balance" for that reason. No verdict was softened to balance the count.

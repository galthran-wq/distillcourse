New:
```
- `marx-manifesto` — Claim: the Manifesto's predictions. → **Round 2: Marx's distinctive forecasts failed (strong); the forecasts that held were shared with liberal economists from Smith on and are not his (established as to priority); crises are dropped from his credit.**
  - Revolution in the advanced countries failed: no proletarian revolution in an advanced country; power reached by the ballot (Labour in office 1924); several of the ten measures came by statute; the one communist revolution of the period came in an agrarian country. Grade: **strong**.
  - Immiseration failed. British real wages rose 12% in 1780–1840 while output per worker rose 46%, then 123% in 1840–1900 against output per worker's 90% (Allen 2009, on Feinstein's series). Grade: **strong** (now bound).
  - Two-class polarisation failed. The salaried middle grew: US white-collar plus service groups 24% → 75% of employment 1910–2000, professional and technical 4.4% → 23.3% (Wyatt & Hecker, *MLR* 2006); Schumpeter's family mobility. Grade: **strong**.
  - The falling rate of profit as a forecast of collapse (Capital III chs. 13–15; I ch. 32, "the knell of capitalist private property sounds") failed: no collapse. Grade: **strong**. On the narrower claim that the rate tends to fall, the British gross profit rate rose to mid-century and stabilised to 1914 (Allen 2009): one country, one period — **on balance** against Marx.
  - The forecasts that held — the world market, concentration, wage labour displacing small owners (the US self-employed share of non-farm white men fell from one in six in 1910 to one in ten in 1970, Fairlie & Meyer 1999) — **held, strong**, and were stated before him: Smith 1776 (the discovery of America and the Cape route "the two greatest and most important events recorded in the history of mankind"; "the extent of the market"; "twenty workmen serve under a master for one that is independent") and Mill 1848 ("a tendency to substitute more and more … large establishments for small ones"). Not credited to Marx: **established** (dated texts).
  - Crises dropped from the credit: panics in 1793, 1797, 1815, 1819, 1825, 1836–37 and 1847 (Thorp, *Business Annals*, 1926), and the Manifesto itself cites the "commercial crises that by their periodical return" as present fact. Cause disputed — Marx's over-production and falling profit; the Austrian account of credit below the natural rate (Mises 1912/1924; Hayek 1931/1935); others — **open** in this course.
  - Strongest objection: these are tendencies with counteracting causes (Capital III ch. 14), not predictions. Answer: the Manifesto states outcomes ("Its fall and the victory of the proletariat are equally inevitable"); a law whose counteracting causes may prevail indefinitely forecasts nothing.
  - Would be overturned by: falling long-run real wages, a shrinking salaried middle, or a proletarian revolution in an advanced country; for the priority point, a Manifesto forecast with content Smith's and Mill's lack, which came true.
  - **Objection (author's review), required in the lesson at full strength:** Marx's doctrine is ressentiment in its origin (Nietzsche, *Antichrist* 57, *Twilight* Skirmishes 34; Scheler, the labour movement's "cries for revenge" and ressentiment criticism that no improvement satisfies). Answered with the genetic fallacy stated once (a motive "neither refutes nor proves" a cause — `ressentiment-and-envy` (a)); on Scheler's own definition the diagnosis fits the Manifesto badly (it praises the bourgeoisie's achievement; Scheler places ressentiment "by no means most active in the industrial proletariat" and calls class-struggle socialism morally "highly superior" to the humanitarian kind); the doctrine is judged on its forecasts, which failed.
```

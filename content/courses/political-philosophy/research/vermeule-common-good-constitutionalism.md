# Research brief: vermeule-common-good-constitutionalism (Vermeule's common-good constitutionalism: the classical legal tradition against liberal legalism)

status: planned
module: after-liberalism
type: core
goal: Analyze Vermeule's argument that law should be read as an ordinance of reason directed to the common good rather than as a liberal charter of rights, and evaluate it against originalism and liberal constitutionalism

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `aquinas-law-and-kingship` (the backward anchor for EVERY term this lesson
  uses): law as "an ordinance of reason for the common good, made by him who has care of the
  community, and promulgated" (ST I-II q.90 a.4); the four laws, with `eternal-law` and
  `natural-law` above human law; `human-law-determination` — human law comes from natural law
  either as a conclusion (do not kill) or as a determination of what nature leaves open (the
  penalty; the craftsman's house, q.95 a.2); `unjust-law-not-law` ("a perversion of law",
  q.92 a.1 ad 4); the sovereign bound by the directive but not the coercive force of his own law
  (q.96 a.5); the besieged city whose gates are opened against the letter of the law (q.96 a.6);
  law beats judges for three reasons (q.95 a.1 ad 2). From `aristotle-faction-and-the-best-life`
  and `aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle`: the `common-good` as what separates correct from deviant
  regimes and the `six-regimes` table. From `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law`: `res-publica`
  and `natural-law` as right reason above every statute. From the module: Deneen's diagnosis that
  liberalism's two premises (`liberal-anthropology`) produce state and market as a pincer and that
  "more liberalism" cannot cure it (`deneen-post-liberalism`); Hazony's `order-of-national-states`
  and his `imperialism-of-universal-doctrine` — and Hazony's own taxonomy of the anti-liberal camps,
  in which a "neo-Catholic" camp keeps a moral minimum founded on universal reason and is drawn to
  an international regime (`hazony-virtue-of-nationalism`, Virtue ch. VII). From the course: Mill's
  `harm-principle` and the marketplace of opinion (`mill-liberty-and-its-limits`); Stephen's reply
  that the magistrate may enforce morality (`stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity`); Schmitt's
  `state-of-exception` (`schmitt-political-theology`); Oakeshott's `government-as-umpire` and
  `rationalism-in-politics` (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`); Strauss's verdict on Thomistic
  natural law (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`); Jouvenel's history of Power's growth
  (`jouvenel-on-power`) and Burnham's managers (`burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west`);
  the extended republic and Madison's faction (`federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic`,
  `faction-madison`).
- **Contributes:** One move: the post-liberal critique turned into a theory of LAW. Deneen said
  liberalism fails because of its premises and proposed no programme; Hazony said what order of
  states is best. Vermeule says what a judge or an official should do on Monday morning: read every
  enacted text (lex) inside the whole body of right (ius) — natural law, the law of nations, the
  inherited principles of the legal tradition — as a reasoned ordinance for the common good
  (justice, peace, abundance), and defer to the public authority's reasonable determinations of
  that good. The lesson introduces `common-good-constitutionalism` and `lex-and-ius`, shows that the
  originalist and the progressive are, on this account, two forms of one mistake (law as will, not
  reason), and then meets the theory from three sides: the liberal (who decides the common good,
  and what happens to the dissenter), the originalist (the classical tradition is not our law), and
  the conservative-traditionalist right itself (Burke and Oakeshott against rule by ends; Jouvenel,
  Tocqueville and Burnham against the administrative state Vermeule embraces; Strauss on whether
  Thomistic natural law survives without theology; Hazony on universal doctrine).
- **Hands off:** (i) Vermeule's subsidiarity runs from the TOP: a higher authority's power AND
  duty to aid failing lower bodies, released "in a state of exception". The next lesson
  (`de-benoist-european-new-right`) gives the same word the opposite direction — authority
  delegated upward only for what the lower level cannot do — and Hazony has already called the
  EU's version "a euphemism for empire". (ii) This lesson decides the safeguard question (see
  VERDICT): the theory supplies no check of its own against a common-good determination made in
  bad faith, and relies on the external checks of the order it works within (on balance). What
  passes on: `hungarys-fundamental-law` reads a real constitution whose Article 28 tells its courts
  to presume that laws "serve moral and economical purposes which are in accordance with common
  sense and the public good" — a text the theory's method fits, but not an application of
  Vermeule's theory, so the case is not evidence for or against this verdict.
- **Spine step:** "After liberalism: Deneen says it failed by succeeding, Hazony defends the order
  of national states, Vermeule the common good, de Benoist the right to difference — Hungary's
  constitution as the case — and the whole argument is assembled"

## VERDICT

Register, module 16, lesson verdict.

- **Claim judged:** the safeguard against abuse of the common-good power can be internal to
  officials — reasoned ordinances, good faith (bona fides), rationality review, and the maxim that
  bad rule forfeits the right to rule (l.1038).
- **Verdict: the theory supplies no check of its own against a common-good determination made in
  bad faith. Grade: on balance.** It relies on the external checks of the order it works within
  (elections, separated powers), which it does not abolish.
- **What decides it:** Madison's "Enlightened statesmen will not always be at the helm" (Federalist
  10, `C/research/texts/federalist-full.txt` l.1930–1932; `<Ref
  federalist-one-people-and-the-extended-republic>`) and Polybius's checks — each part's "power
  being checked by the others, no one part should turn the scale" (Histories VI,
  `C/research/texts/polybius/histories-book6-shuckburgh.txt` l.329, l.603–606; `<Ref
  polybius-anacyclosis-and-rome>`). A check located in the good faith of the checked is not a
  check: rationality review is performed by judges who, on the theory, defer to the authority's
  reasonable determinations, and the forfeiture maxim names no one who declares the forfeit.
- **Strongest objection, and why it does not overturn the verdict:** Vermeule's replies — (i) "abuse"
  presupposes a theory of legitimate ends, so there is no neutral baseline (l.1104); (ii) power is
  conserved, and private power abuses too (l.763); (iii) every polity, liberal ones included,
  inculcates (l.1040); (iv) positive law is disputed too ("Every June", l.810); and, beneath them,
  "who guards the guardians?" applies to every system — liberal judicial review also checks itself.
  These show the liberal state is not neutral, and that every system ends in some unchecked
  authority. They do not say who corrects a bad-faith authority under this theory; and the liberal
  order answers the guardians question with external, rival checks, not with the guardians'
  character. Why only on balance: the theory works inside, and does not propose to abolish, an
  order that keeps elections and separated powers, so in practice the external checks remain.
- **Scope:** Hungary's Fourth Amendment (2013) is **not** used as evidence here. It was not made by
  judges reading through the classical legal tradition, so it is not an application of the theory.
- **What would overturn it:** a mechanism in the theory itself, not in officials' character, that
  can block a governing majority's "determination" of the common good.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): American judges argue about whether to read the Constitution by
its original meaning or as a living document; a Harvard law professor argues in 2022 that both
share one mistake — law as the will of whoever enacted it, written to protect individuals from the
state; before 1900 lawyers understood law as reason ordered to the common good, and that tradition
still offers judges a way to read the text. Anchor (L11): Adrian Vermeule (b. 1968), professor of
constitutional law at Harvard; the book (2022) grew out of a 2020 magazine essay that drew
denunciations from both camps within days. Define at first use, before link 1: ORIGINALISM
(read the Constitution by the public meaning its words had when adopted — the method of the
conservative legal movement since the 1980s) and LIVING CONSTITUTIONALISM (read it by principles
whose application grows with the society). The newcomer's problem: two schools fight; a third man
says they are the same school — what could they possibly share?

1. **What do the two rival schools share?** Obvious attempt: nothing — one is "conservative", one
   "progressive". Vermeule: both treat law as WILL — the will of the framers frozen at 1788
   (originalist) or the will of the liberated individual, advanced by judges (progressive) — and
   both treat the whole of law as enacted rules. The originalist "denies methodologically" what the
   progressive "denies substantively" (l.785): that law contains principles of justice no one
   enacted. Answer: the dividing line is not left/right but will/reason — the progressive and the
   libertarian are "twins, mirror images" (l.1368). Seam: from Deneen (`<Ref deneen-post-liberalism>`)
   the reader has the claim that left and right are two liberalisms; here is the same claim made
   about judges.
2. **What would "law as reason" even mean?** Obvious attempt: judges impose their morality. Answer
   from the tradition, one step at a time — this is where `lex-and-ius` is introduced: LEX is the
   enacted statute; IUS is the whole body of right that contains it — natural law (`<Ref
   aquinas-law-and-kingship>` and `<Ref cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law>`), the law common to
   civilised peoples (ius gentium), and the local civil law (ius civile). French and Spanish keep
   the two words (loi/droit, ley/derecho); English has only "law" and "rights" (l.695). Rights,
   on this view, are "corollaries of justice": what is due to each (Ulpian's suum cuique). Law in
   the full sense is Aquinas's definition — the reader HAS it; Ref it and quote it once. Because:
   if law is reason, a text is read as the act of a reasoning authority aiming at an end, so its
   end is part of its meaning.
3. **What is the common good, if not the majority's preference?** Obvious attempt: the sum of
   everyone's interests (the utilitarian reading the libertarian fears). Vermeule's via negativa:
   not AGGREGATION (Papinian — "public law cannot be changed by private pacts"), not rule for
   private benefit (the six-regime table — `<Ref aristotle-regimes-and-the-middle>`, one clause),
   not Bartolus's MONSTROUS GOVERNMENT (many private tyrants over one weak public head, l.972–976 —
   a set piece). Then positively: a good that is "one in number" and shared without being
   diminished — the football team's victory (l.982) — whose content, for a lawyer, is Ulpian's
   three precepts and the conditions that let a community live by them: JUSTICE, PEACE, ABUNDANCE
   (Botero's triptych, l.1002), extended to health, safety and economic security, with SOLIDARITY
   and SUBSIDIARITY as subsidiary principles. The three-part legal definition (l.1000) is quoted.
   Because: if the common good were a sum, rights would be the only shield against it; since it is
   a shared good that is also each person's highest temporal good, rights are defined from inside
   it — "determinable", not "defeasible".
4. **If the common good does not dictate the rules, who fills them in?** Obvious attempt: the
   judges, by moral reasoning (what both camps fear). Answer: DETERMINATION (`<Ref
   aquinas-law-and-kingship>` for `human-law-determination` — the reader has the two modes and the
   craftsman). Vermeule's two extensions are the new matter: (a) determination OF the constitution —
   the common good leaves the regime form open (presidential, parliamentary, monarchy, republic;
   l.737–739; "Democracy … has no special privilege", l.1092); (b) determination WITHIN it, nested
   ever lower — constitution → statute → agency regulation → application. The architect and the
   hospital (l.735); the statute of limitations, ten years or fifteen — "rationally arbitrary"
   (l.757). Then the judicial consequence: DEFERENCE — courts check only competence, a reasonable
   public purpose, and non-arbitrary means (the "common good framework", l.1334–1338). Because:
   respecting the space of determination is respecting the authority whose task it is.
5. **A case worked through: the baker's hours (Lochner, 1905).** Set piece. New York limited
   bakers to ten hours a day; five justices struck it down as interfering with liberty of contract;
   Holmes dissented that the Constitution does not enact a particular economic theory; Harlan
   dissented that the question was "one about which there is room for debate" (l.1352). The
   standard story (taught to every American law student) makes Holmes the hero. Say the seam: one
   expects the post-liberal to side with Holmes, the critic of judicial libertarianism; Vermeule
   sides with Harlan — Holmes deferred to "dominant opinion" (positivism), Harlan to the public
   authority's reasoned pursuit of health (the framework). Then RIGGS v. PALMER (New York, 1889),
   the grandson who poisoned his grandfather to inherit (l.1446–1468): the statute said a valid will
   passes; the majority applied the principle no one may profit from his own wrong; the dissent held
   the legislature had made a valid determination. Vermeule's point: both opinions read lex inside
   ius — the disagreement is "entirely internal to the classical legal tradition" (l.1466).
   Because these two cases show the method doing what its critics say it cannot: deferring in one,
   reading a statute by principle in the other.
6. **What changes, and what does not?** Obvious attempt: this is just living constitutionalism with
   other values. Vermeule's distinction (ch. 4): DEVELOPING constitutionalism keeps principles fixed
   and unfolds their application (Euclid, 1926: the meaning "never varies", the scope "must expand
   or contract"), whereas progressive constitutionalism changes the principles in a "liturgy" of
   liberation (l.2050). Then what he says would change (l.1062 — quote short): Casey's "mystery
   passage" "stamped as abominable"; free-speech libertarianism "under the ax"; libertarian property
   rights to go; deference to an executive-centred administrative state (l.1064; ch. 5 "the living
   voice" of the law). Liberty becomes "the natural human capacity to act in accordance with
   reasoned morality ordered to the common good" (l.1054). Here the reader meets the claim that
   makes the objections inevitable: "Constraints on power are good only derivatively" (l.1038).
   Seam with `<Ref stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity>`: Vermeule invokes Stephen against Mill
   (l.2108) — the magistrate is not disabled because he may err.
7. **The OBJECTIONS at full strength (one `##` with three `###`).** The lesson's seam: the theory
   has answered "what is law?"; it now has to answer "and who keeps the rulers honest?".
   - **(a) Liberal — who determines the common good, and what happens to the dissenter?** At
     strength: "the common good" is whatever those in power prefer; a theory that says liberty is
     good "only derivatively", that blasphemy prosecutions were legitimate, that a passage about
     defining "one's own concept of existence" should be "abominable" names in advance whose
     conscience will yield (`<Ref mill-liberty-and-its-limits>`: the harm principle as the
     dissenter's only shelter). Vermeule's replies, from his text: (i) "abuse of power" presupposes
     a theory of legitimate ends — there is no neutral baseline (l.1104); (ii) power is conserved:
     liberty for one is coercion of another, and private power abuses too (l.763); (iii) every
     polity, liberal ones included, inculcates beliefs — sin taxes, schooling, jury duty (l.1040);
     (iv) disagreement exists over positive law too ("Every June", l.810). Replies (i)–(iv) show
     the liberal state is not neutral; whether they answer "who corrects a public authority that
     determines the common good in bad faith?" is the question link 9 decides. Vermeule's own
     answer is the rationality review and the maxim that bad rule forfeits the right to rule
     (l.1038) — the authority's bona fides is the hinge. State that plainly here; weigh it in link
     9.
   - **(b) Originalist — this is not our law.** At strength: American law is what was enacted and
     ratified; ius is a claim of political morality, not law, and a judge who reads lex "in light
     of" it has made himself legislator (the positivist originalists' position). Vermeule's reply:
     originalism is "illusory" — it cannot choose the level of generality at which to read a text
     without a theory of the good (ch. 3); Bostock (2020) shows its champions dividing over what
     "sex" meant in 1964 while reasoning from principle; and the founding generation were classical
     lawyers, not originalists (l.685). Work Bostock in three sentences as the course's own (the
     statute bans discrimination "because of sex"; does it cover firing a man for being gay? six
     justices said yes by the words, three said no by the expected applications).
   - **(c) From the right — rule by ends.** This is the objection the course's reader will feel
     most, and it comes from inside the tradition. (1) Burke and Oakeshott (`<Ref burke-reflections>`,
     `<Ref oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics>`): a conservative distrusts any government that
     knows the good and pursues it; "prudence is cautious how she defines" (Burke, 1791); government
     is the umpire of a game, not the coach of a team — and the football team is exactly
     Vermeule's image. (2) Tocqueville, Jouvenel and Burnham (`<Ref
     tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism>`, `<Ref jouvenel-on-power>`, `<Ref
     burnham-managers-and-the-suicide-of-the-west>`): the tutelary administrative state is the
     despotism the right warned against, and Power always presents itself as the common good's
     servant; Vermeule embraces the administrative state Deneen (`<Ref deneen-post-liberalism>`)
     calls liberalism's instrument. (3) Strauss (`<Ref strauss-natural-right-and-history>`):
     Thomistic natural law is "practically inseparable" from revealed theology — can Vermeule
     bracket "the order of grace" (l.988–992) and keep the order of nature? (4) Hazony (`<Ref
     hazony-virtue-of-nationalism>`): a doctrine of universal reason enforced across nations — the
     ius gentium, the Geneva Consensus — is his "neo-Catholic" camp, and a universal doctrine tends
     to empire. Vermeule's replies: determination IS prudence (no blueprint, l.1028); the common
     good leaves regime form open (l.737) and so does not dictate an administrative state in
     general, only in the American particular part (l.747–749 — the general/particular split);
     the developing constitution is "fundamentally 'conservative'" (l.2070). Where the lesson
     concedes: the general part (law as reasoned ordinance, lex inside ius) survives (c)(1) better
     than the particular part (deference to an executive-centred bureaucracy), which is exactly what
     (c)(2) attacks.
8. **Subsidiarity from the top (the hand-off).** Vermeule's ch. 5 reading: subsidiarity "is
   fundamentally a positive, empowering principle" — the highest authority's duty to come to the aid
   of failing lower bodies, its "full strength … released in a state of exception" (l.2376–2382);
   Isabella's giant. Ref `<Ref schmitt-political-theology>` for `state-of-exception` in one clause —
   the reader will see whose vocabulary this is. Subsidiarity from the top is the theory's own
   statement of where final power rests — which sets up the weighing.
9. **The weighing: "A check that lives in the good faith of the checked is not a check."** (The last
   body section before the Conclusion; L10e.) The claim: the safeguard can be internal to officials
   (reasoned ordinances, bona fides, rationality review, forfeiture). Set it against Madison's
   "Enlightened statesmen will not always be at the helm" (Federalist 10, l.1930–1932) and
   Polybius's mixture, in which each part's power is "checked by the others" (VI) — the course's
   two standing answers to the guardians question, both external. Then the theory's own pieces:
   rationality review is done by judges who defer to the authority's reasonable determinations;
   the forfeiture maxim states a consequence but names no one entitled to declare it; good faith is
   a property of the ruler, not a constraint on him. Then the strongest objection at full strength
   — Vermeule's replies (i)–(iv) and "who guards the guardians?", which liberal judicial review
   faces too — and why it does not overturn the verdict: it shows no system is neutral and every
   system ends somewhere, but the liberal answer places the end in rival, external powers, and
   the theory offers nothing in their place. Then the limit that keeps the grade at on balance:
   Vermeule works inside an order that keeps elections and separated powers and does not propose
   to abolish them, so the theory borrows external checks it does not supply. State what would
   overturn it (a mechanism internal to the theory that can block a governing majority's
   determination). Do not use Hungary's Fourth Amendment here: it was not an application of the
   theory.

**Conclusion** (L10): in plain words — Vermeule's account of law as reason for the common good
survives its liberal and originalist critics better than his account of what keeps the rulers
honest. On that question the verdict is on balance: the theory supplies no check of its own
against a determination made in bad faith; it relies on the elections and separated powers of the
order it works within. Hand-off: the next writer (`<Ref de-benoist-european-new-right>`) uses
"subsidiarity" for authority that rises only from below; the module's last case
(`<Ref hungarys-fundamental-law>`) is a constitution that tells its courts to presume the public
good.

Headings as claims (suggested): "Originalists and progressives share one premise"; "Lex is inside
ius"; "The common good is not a sum"; "Determination leaves the rulers a range, and the judges
must respect it"; "Harlan, not Holmes"; "Development is not progress"; "Who keeps the rulers
honest?"; "Subsidiarity from the top"; "A check that lives in the good faith of the checked is not
a check".

4. **The architect and the statute of limitations** (Intro l.735, l.757; ch. 1 l.1078–1086): Aquinas's
   craftsman (Ref) and Vermeule's hospital, then "Should the statute of limitations … be ten years or
   fifteen? Or perhaps twelve? The law is not so sophomoric as to demand a first-order reason" — the
   whole case for deference in one example.
5. **Lochner, three opinions** (ch. 2 l.1346–1362): Peckham (liberty of contract), Holmes ("dominant

6. **Riggs v. Palmer** (ch. 2 l.1446–1468): the murdering heir; both opinions inside the classical

7. **The predictions** (ch. 1 l.1062) and **the Geneva Consensus** (ch. 4 l.2118–2120): the most

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first use (L11)**, no more than three per section: originalism; living
  constitutionalism; Supreme Court "doctrine" and "precedent"; the ius commune (the common law of
  medieval and early-modern Europe, a synthesis of Roman law, Church law and local custom, taught at
  every university from Bologna to Oxford); ius civile / ius gentium / ius naturale; Ulpian (Roman
  jurist, d. 228; his sayings preserved in Justinian's Digest, 533); Papinian; Bartolus; Botero
  (Giovanni Botero, The Reason of State, 1589 — "ragion di stato"); determinatio; epikeia (Greek
  "reasonableness": reading a law by its purpose where its words would defeat it — the besieged
  city, Ref Aquinas); regnative prudence (the practical wisdom proper to a ruler); the police power
  (the general power of American states to regulate for health, safety and morals); the
  administrative state (government by agencies exercising powers delegated by statute); deference /
  Chevron (the 1984 rule that courts defer to an agency's reasonable reading of an ambiguous
  statute); Lochner (1905), Riggs (1889), Euclid (1926 — zoning), Casey (1992 — abortion), Bostock
  (2020), Obergefell (2015 — same-sex marriage); the Geneva Consensus Declaration (2020).
- **The book predates two decisions that test it** (L13 is not the slot; a Remark or an exercise):
  Dobbs (June 2022) overruled Casey — the result Vermeule wanted, by the originalist method he
  calls illusory; Loper Bright (2024) overruled Chevron — the opposite of his deference. The body
  may say this in one sentence after link 6; the research exercise does the work. Do not present
  either as refuting or confirming the theory: a theory of what law IS is not falsified by what a
  court does.
- **Do not re-teach Aquinas.** The four laws, the two modes of derivation, unjust law, the besieged
  city: each is a `<Ref aquinas-law-and-kingship>` and one clause. The new matter is determination
  of the constitution, nested determination, and deference. Likewise Mill, Stephen, Oakeshott,
  Strauss, Hazony, Deneen: Ref and one clause.
- **Vermeule's general vs particular part** (l.745–749): a reader can accept law-as-reasoned-ordinance
  and reject the executive-centred American reading. The objections in link 7(c) hit the
  particular part harder; keep the two apart or the evaluation is unfair to either side.
- **"Integralism"**: Vermeule is often called an integralist (the Catholic position that temporal
  power should be subordinate to spiritual power). The book explicitly brackets the supernatural
  end (l.988–992). Do not attach the label in the course's voice; if it appears, it appears as what
  critics call him, attributed, in References — or not at all. The label rule: The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **"Common good" in Vermeule ≠ "common good" in Aristotle/Aquinas exactly.** The concept's earlier
  definition (concepts.yaml `common-good`) is the end that separates correct from deviant regimes;
  Vermeule's is a LEGAL definition (l.1000: conditions, injunction, non-aggregation). Say that the
  lesson specialises the concept, not that it replaces it.
- **Do not present Vermeule as a judge-empowering theory.** His central institutional claim is
  deference (l.753–757, l.807, l.1068–1072). The "who decides?" section is his answer to the
  commonest misreading — handle it as the L13 slot: correct claim (method and institutional
  allocation are separate questions) → the misconception stated once (common-good constitutionalism
  lets judges impose their morality) → why it fails (the framework's three checks and deference) →
  replacement model → Riggs as the discriminating example.
- **The American material is the newcomer's hardest load.** Keep to four cases (Lochner, Riggs,
  Bostock, Casey's sentence); everything else (Curtiss-Wright, Euclid, Seila Law, Chiafalo, Alvarez,
  Lujan, Solorio) stays out of the body or goes in a folded Aside.
- **Copyright**: Polity 2022; quote briefly (a sentence or two), paraphrase the rest. Supreme Court
  opinions, Ulpian, Bartolus (as translated in the book — quote at most the two sentences) and Burke
  are free.
- **Register / balance**: do not adopt Vermeule's polemical terms ("liturgy", "illusory",
  "sophomoric", "fraudulent" l.1036) in the course's voice — they are his, inside quotation marks
  and attributed; equally, do not adopt the critics' "dangerous". Before finishing, reread for
  prejudging words in the course's own voice.
- **Dossier thin:** the critics' essays (Epps, Barnett, Baude & Sachs) are cited, not held; the
  liberal and originalist objections must be stated from the book's own rendering plus the general
  positions the course has taught (Mill; Federalist). Flag in References that the objections are
  reconstructed.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval warm-up (the only mechanical one; E3):** Give Aquinas's four-part definition of law
   (`<Ref aquinas-law-and-kingship>`) and, for each part, the element of Vermeule's "common good
   framework" (competence, reasonable public purpose, non-arbitrary means, deference) that answers
   to it. Solution maps "ordinance of reason" → non-arbitrary means, "for the common good" → public
   purpose, "by him who has care of the community" → competence, "promulgated" → the enacted text
   read first.
2. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** the Preamble of the US Constitution (1787) and Vermeule's
   re-reading of it (l.1046–1048). Metadata first (who wrote the Preamble, for whom, to do what;
   who re-reads it in 2022 and why); predict what a classical reading will find; then RECORDS /
   ARGUES / OMITS — the Preamble also says "secure the Blessings of Liberty"; how does the
   re-reading place liberty, and what can the Preamble be USED to establish about the framers'
   jurisprudence (little: a preamble states ends, not a theory of interpretation)?
3. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "Common-good constitutionalism lets judges strike down any law they
   think bad for the common good." Name the step that fails (method vs institutional allocation;
   the three-check framework; deference; Harlan, not Peckham) and state the one case in which, on
   Vermeule's own account, a court WOULD invalidate (no conceivable public purpose; plainly
   arbitrary means; outside competence).
4. **Boundary probe (E6a):** Push determination to its limit. If the common good leaves regime form
   open and democracy "has no special privilege" (l.1092), what in the theory rules out a regime in
   which the public authority determines both the law and its own review? Work it from the text:
   the nature of law as reasoned ordinance (arbitrary rule is not law; `<Ref
   aquinas-law-and-kingship>` for `unjust-law-not-law`), the forfeiture clause (l.1038), and Aquinas's
   point that the sovereign is bound by the directive, not the coercive, force of law. Conclude
   what kind of safeguard remains (internal to the rulers' reason and good faith) and what an
   objector from Tocqueville or Jouvenel (`<Ref jouvenel-on-power>`) would say it lacks.
5. **Research-shaped (E6d):** Two decisions came after the book. Read the syllabus (the official
   summary) of Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022) and of Loper Bright Enterprises
   v. Raimondo (2024) on supremecourt.gov. For each, state (a) whether the result moves toward or
   away from Vermeule's predictions (Casey; deference), (b) the method the majority used, and (c)
   what the pair shows about his claim that originalism is "illusory". Solution: Dobbs reaches the
   result by a historical test; Loper Bright ends Chevron deference on the text of the
   Administrative Procedure Act — the same Court moved with him on one, against him on the other,
   by originalist-textualist reasoning in both; a defender says this confirms that results come
   from the judges' theory of the good, a critic that originalism is a working method.
6. **Synthesis — counter-argument (E3: the right-wing objection):** "A conservative must reject
   common-good constitutionalism." Argue for or against in a claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph,
   using Burke's "prudence is cautious how she defines" (`<Ref burke-reflections>`), Oakeshott's
   umpire (`<Ref oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics>`), and Vermeule's reply that determination is
   prudence and that the triptych is "a framework orientation, not a blueprint" (l.1028). The model
   answer distinguishes the general part from the particular part.
7. **Corroboration (E3, `stephen-liberty-equality-fraternity` and `mill-liberty-and-its-limits`):**
   Set Mill's harm principle against Vermeule's l.1040 (law "teaches, habituates, and re-forms")
   and Stephen's claim that the magistrate may enforce morality. Which of Vermeule's examples (sin
   taxes, helmet laws, mandatory schooling, jury duty) would Mill himself accept, and on what
   ground? Show where the harm principle already concedes some of Vermeule's cases (harm to
   others; minors) and where it does not (Casey's sentence; blasphemy).
8. **Attack the verdict (E6b):** The lesson holds, on balance, that common-good constitutionalism
   supplies no check of its own against a determination made in bad faith. State the strongest
   objection in its best form — e.g. that liberal constitutionalism has no external check on its
   final court either, so the verdict applies a standard no system meets; or that reasoned-
   ordinance review, applied by an independent judiciary trained in the classical tradition, is an
   institutional check and not merely good faith. Then (a) say whether the objection shows a check
   internal to the theory, or only one borrowed from the order it works within; (b) say what
   evidence or textual showing would overturn the verdict — a mechanism in Vermeule's book, not in
   officials' character, that can block a governing majority's "determination" (cite where in the
   text you would look: the common good framework, l.1334–1338; forfeiture, l.1038; ch. 5); (c) say
   why Hungary's 2013 amendment cannot serve as the test either way.
9. **FORWARD (E7) → `de-benoist-european-new-right`:** Vermeule says subsidiarity is "fundamentally
   a positive, empowering principle" whose full strength is released "in a state of exception"
   (l.2376–2382). Write the opposite definition — one in which authority rises only from below and
   the higher level acts only for what the lower cannot do — and say who, under each definition,
   decides that a lower body is "failing". The solution says, with a `<Ref>`, that the next lesson's
   manifesto makes the bottom-up version "the keystone at every level" of a federal Europe, and that
   Hazony's test ("who decides?") separates the two.

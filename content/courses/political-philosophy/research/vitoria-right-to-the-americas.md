# Research brief: vitoria-right-to-the-americas (Vitoria on the right to the Americas: unjust and just titles)

status: planned
module: empire-and-guilt
type: core
goal: Analyze Vitoria's examination of every title by which Spain might hold the Americas and his conclusion that only the right of travel, trade and preaching survives — and what that right licensed

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The reader arrives with four things this lecture uses as premises and
  must never re-teach. From `cicero-res-publica-and-natural-law` (which now
  introduces `natural-law` and `just-war`): true law as right reason in
  agreement with nature, above every statute (Rep. III.22), just war only for
  honour or safety with reparation demanded first (III.23), and the Roman claim
  that rule over the servile is just (III.36–37) — the ancestor of the title
  Vitoria will reject and then leave open. From `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: the
  four kinds of law, natural law as the rational creature's participation in the
  eternal law, human law as determination, the quaestio form (objections → "on
  the other hand" → reply), and the rule that an unjust law is violence rather
  than law. From `aristotle-political-animal-and-natural-slavery`: natural
  slavery (Politics I.4–7) and Aristotle's own concession that nature often fails
  to mark the slave — that lesson's notes name Vitoria's rejection of the title
  as its forward hook. From `locke-property-and-consent` (earlier in course
  order): property derived from labour — kept dormant here; the empire lesson
  after this one turns it against Vitoria's dominium. In course order the
  reader arrives from `national-data-audited`, the application that closes the
  heredity module (not a prerequisite — the knowledge prerequisites are Aquinas
  and Aristotle). That module hands this one two things the lecture meets head
  on. (i) Cajamarca is already anchored: `geography-or-institutions` opened on
  "why did Spanish soldiers capture the Inca emperor at Cajamarca in 1532, and
  not Inca soldiers the king of Spain?" — a question about cause (guns, germs,
  steel; the Spanish model of capturing the ruler and taking tribute). Vitoria
  asks the question that lesson did not: by what right. Recall Cajamarca with a
  `<Ref>` and a clause; do not re-anchor it. (ii) The institutional account
  (`inclusive-and-extractive-institutions`; the reversal of fortune;
  `national-data-audited` on the colonial-institutions table) argued that what
  the conquerors built — tribute, forced labour, the encomienda — shaped the
  Americas' later poverty. This lesson does not weigh that claim; it asks what
  title the builders had. The seam is one sentence at the opening (L10a): the
  previous module asked what conquest caused; this one asks whether it was
  rightful, beginning with the man who asked it first.
- **Contributes:** One move: take every title Spain could claim, state each at
  full strength, strip it by natural, divine and human law, and see what is left
  — a right of travel, trade and preaching held under the law of nations — and
  then see what that remainder licensed when refused. The lesson's result is
  Vitoria's own uncomfortable sequence: persuasion → defence → redress → full
  war, and the eighth title he leaves open "for the sake of argument".
- **Hands off:** The open problem is whether a universalist doctrine (natural
  law here; consent and progress next) can justify rule over a people that has
  not consented, and whether the justification's conditions ("for their
  benefit, not Spanish profit") are restraints or a licence.
  `locke-and-mill-on-empire` takes it: Locke's "waste" land re-opens the
  discovery title Vitoria closed, and Mill's tutelage is the eighth title in
  nineteenth-century dress.
- **Spine step:** "The West judges its own conquests: Vitoria's titles, Locke's waste land, Burke's impeachment of Hastings, Mill's tutelage, the colonial balance sheet, and whether guilt is inherited"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim. Develop it before any answer: a
Dominican professor, not a courtier; the king has asked nothing; the relectio is
a public academic exercise; the Indies have been held for a generation and the
Spanish settlers, soldiers and clergy all have an interest in the answer. The
newcomer's own first attempt is the one every Spaniard gave: the Pope granted
the lands to Castile (the Bulls of Donation, 1493 — anchor: Alexander VI, a
meridian a hundred leagues west of the Azores, granted "for evangelisation").
Vitoria will reject it in Q2. But he insists on a prior question, and the
lesson's chain is his own quaestio.

1. **Why examine at all — the rule of conscience.** Question: may a Christian
   simply act on the common opinion? Obvious attempt: the matter is settled by
   the fact of possession and the king's councils. Fails: in doubtful matters
   one must consult the competent; a verdict of unlawfulness binds even if the
   act is lawful, a verdict of lawfulness secures conscience even if wrong
   (invincible ignorance) (Intro ss1–3). Because: possession is a fact, title
   is a right, and rights are judged by law, not by occupation. This is where
   the quaestio form is recalled (`<Ref>` aquinas-law-and-kingship, one clause:
   "it seems that… but on the other hand… I reply").

2. **Q1 — did the barbarians own anything?** Question: before the Spaniards,
   did the Indians have dominium (ownership and jurisdiction)? Four attempts to
   deny it, each refuted. (a) They are sinners: fails — dominion does not
   depend on grace; the Wycliff/Fitzralph thesis that it does was condemned as
   heresy (Constance 1415). (b) They are unbelievers: fails — infidels hold
   true dominion; taking their goods for unbelief alone is theft (the same
   rights "we concede to Saracens and Jews"). (c) They are irrational or mad:
   fails — dominium est ius; only a being that can suffer injury holds rights;
   brutes cannot (fencing grass from deer would be theft, shutting out the sun
   would wrong the sun), children and madmen can; and the Indians are not mad:
   cities, marriages, magistrates, laws, industry, commerce, religion — "God
   and nature never fail in things necessary"; their apparent stupidity comes
   from "evil and barbarous education", and "many of our peasants are little
   different from brute animals". (d) They are natural slaves (`<Ref>`
   aristotle lesson, one clause): fails as a title — natural slavery, properly
   read, is not a legal condition for seizing goods and persons but a natural
   deficiency needing guidance as children need parents; "slavery is a civil
   condition to which no man belongs by nature". Conclusion: true dominion,
   public and private, "as much as any Christians". Because: property follows
   reason, and reason is nature's gift, not grace's.

3. **Q2 — then by what title does Spain hold them? Seven, each at full
   strength, each refuted.** Table them (figure 2) and argue them in order:
   emperor as lord of the world (no law — natural, divine or human — makes him
   so; even if he were, jurisdiction is not property); the pope as temporal
   monarch (Christ probably had no temporal dominion; the pope none over
   unbelievers; "absurd that barbarians may refuse Christ with impunity but
   must accept Christ's vicar on pain of war"); discovery (gives title only to
   res nullius, things without an owner; the Indians were owners — "any more
   than it would if they had discovered us"); refusal of the faith (unbelief is
   no sin in those who never heard; a bare announcement obliges no one — the
   Saracen-preacher test; they must listen to peaceful persuasion and would
   have to accept preaching presented with probable argument and decent
   conduct, but "I have heard of no miracles… only provocations, savage crimes
   and unholy acts"; and even after refusal no war: belief is an act of will,
   fear impairs it); sins against nature (no jurisdiction over non-subjects; if
   princes could punish unbelievers for these, unbelievers could punish
   Christians and "France could conquer Italy" — the symmetry argument from
   Dietary Laws); voluntary acceptance (the requerimiento's consent, read to a
   frightened crowd by armed men through a notary, is void); special gift of
   God (needs miracle-confirmed prophecy; even the executor of a divine
   judgment is not guiltless). Because each fails: natural law gives rights to
   all men; divine law gives the Church no temporal sword over outsiders; human
   law cannot reach those who never consented to it.

4. **Q3 — what is left? The right of natural partnership and communication.**
   Question: if no title stands, must the Spaniards leave? Attempt: yes, the
   argument has eliminated everything. Fails: the law of nations (ius gentium),
   "enacted by the whole world, which is in a sense a commonwealth", gives
   every man the right to travel, dwell and trade where he does no harm —
   fourteen proofs: original common ownership not abolished by the division of
   property, hospitality commanded, ports, rivers and sea common, exile as
   punishment presupposes a right to go elsewhere, "man is not a wolf to his
   fellow man but a fellow". Res nullius (gold in the river, pearls in the sea)
   goes to the first taker on the same terms as to natives. The right to
   preach follows as a corollary (and the pope may assign the mission to
   Spain). The further titles: protection of converts; the pope may give
   converts a Christian prince; defence of innocents against tyranny and human
   sacrifice "even if the victims consent"; a true majority election (the
   Franks giving Childeric's crown to Pepin); aid to allies in a just war (the
   Tlaxcaltecs with Cortés against Montezuma, 1519–21 — anchor). Because: the
   rights Vitoria grants are rights of persons under a law binding all
   commonwealths, not titles of a crown.

5. **The sequence — what the just title licenses when refused.** Question: and
   if the natives obstruct travel, trade or preaching? This is the lesson's
   hinge; make the reader watch it unfold one step at a time (figure 3): first
   persuade and remove the provocation; if they still use force, defend
   (repelling force with force is natural law), build forts; if injured, seek
   redress by war on the prince's authority; the barbarians are timid, so a
   defensive victory should do little harm; a war may be just on both sides
   through invincible ignorance (the Burgundy case: France holds it in
   good-faith error, the emperor has the right); only if they persist in malice
   are they treated as treacherous enemies with the full rights of war —
   plunder, enslavement, deposing princes — "proportionate to the offence".
   Vitoria himself says of the preaching title that the affair seems to have
   "gone beyond the permissible bounds of justice and religion". Because: an
   injury is the sole just cause of war, and refusal of a right is an injury.

6. **The Law of War — the limits Vitoria puts on what he has just allowed.**
   Three non-causes: difference of religion, enlargement of empire, the
   prince's glory or convenience ("wars for the prince's convenience make
   subjects slaves"). The sole cause: harm inflicted; and not every harm. The
   prince must examine the cause with wise counsel and hear the other side; in
   doubt the possessor keeps possession. In victory he is a judge between two
   commonwealths, never a prosecutor; innocents are never killed intentionally
   (double effect: storming a fortress full of innocents only when the war
   cannot otherwise be won and the harm is not out of proportion). The three
   canons. And the period line the lesson must not hide: in a permanent war
   against pagans the innocent may be enslaved and combatants killed after
   victory — a licence Vitoria denies between Christians. Because: a war is a
   judicial act; the law that authorises it also measures it.

7. **The uncomfortable end — the eighth title and the exchequer.** Here the
   seam says what the reader expects and what the text does. Expectation: a
   lecture that has stripped seven titles will end by stripping the eighth.
   Record: Vitoria raises a title he "neither affirms nor condemns": the
   barbarians seem so near to madmen that they cannot run a commonwealth and
   "might be handed over to wiser men to govern", as princes would take orphaned
   children into their care, "supported by the requirements of charity" — then
   the two sentences that must be quoted: "merely for the sake of argument… only
   if everything is done for the benefit and good of the barbarians, and not
   merely for the profit of the Spaniards. But it is in this latter restriction
   that the whole pitfall to souls and salvation is found to lie" — and the
   sentence after it: "what was said earlier about some men being natural slaves
   might be relevant". The title rejected in Q1 as a title re-enters as a
   charitable guardianship. Then the Conclusion: were all titles to fail, trade
   need not cease (the Portuguese trade without conquering), the crown could
   still tax gold and silver at a fifth, and once many are converted the prince
   cannot abandon them — the "intolerable" loss to the exchequer as the last
   word of a theological relection. Cano's rejection (no precept of charity can
   involve coercion) goes in a footnote-like clause.

8. **The OBJECTION at full strength — the just titles licensed what the unjust
   forbade.** State it as the course's own (Pagden in References only). The
   right of travel was a right Spaniards could exercise and Indians could not
   ("who would have described Alexander as a traveller?"); the right to preach
   made every obstruction an injury; the ladder from persuasion to full war
   turns resistance itself into the just cause; the eighth title restores
   tutelage; the exchequer closes the door the argument had opened. The set
   piece is the Letter to Arcos (1534) on Cajamarca (recall, do not re-anchor:
   `<Ref>` geography-or-institutions, whose opening asked why the Spaniards
   could do it; one clause adds what that lesson did not need — Atahualpa's
   unarmed retinue massacred at the meeting, a ransom in gold taken, the Inca
   executed in 1533):
   Vitoria writes that the Indies' profits "freeze the blood in my veins", that
   even granting a just title the Indians could not know it and are innocents,
   that the conquistadors "can allege no title other than iure belli", and: "if
   the Indians are not men but monkeys, they are incapable of injury. But if
   they are men, and our neighbours… I cannot see how to excuse these
   conquistadors of utter impiety and tyranny". The reply the lesson gives him:
   the conditions are real restraints (proportion, the prince as judge, war for
   peoples not princes, the possessor's right in doubt, "no further than on
   Christians"), and he says in his own voice that the Spaniards exceeded them;
   a doctrine's content and its use are two things. Leave the reader weighing
   which the "for the sake of argument" title was.

9. **Valladolid, one paragraph.** Anchor: 1550–51, a junta at Valladolid
   convened by Charles V hears Las Casas, a Dominican who had been a colonist
   and became bishop of Chiapas, against Sepúlveda, a humanist and royal
   chronicler who argued from Aristotle's natural slavery that war on the
   Indians was just; no verdict was pronounced; the New Laws of 1542 had
   already restricted the encomienda. The point for the chain: the title
   Vitoria left open was taken up on both sides within a decade. Nothing more
   — the dossier has only the pointer.

Headings fall out as: "A verdict of conscience, not a fact of possession";
"Did the Indians own anything? Four denials"; "Seven titles, and the law each
fails"; "What is left: the right to travel, trade and preach"; "What a refused
right licenses"; "The law of war that measures the war"; "The eighth title and
the exchequer"; "Did the just titles license the conquest?"

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The quaestio worked once, as form** (`<Ref>` aquinas: the reader has seen
   the form): Q1 Art.1 natural slavery — "it seems that" (Aristotle, Mair
   1510), "on the other hand" (dominium est ius; they have cities and laws),
   reply (a deficiency needing guidance, not a title). Index 41 Q1 pp. 239,
   250–1.
2. **The deer and the sun reductio** (Q1 Arts 4–6, pp. 247–8): if brutes had
   dominion, fencing grass from deer would be theft; if the sun had rights,
   blinds would wrong it. Use it to fix "dominium est ius: only what can suffer
   injury can hold a right" — the premise the Arcos letter later turns into
   "men or monkeys".
3. **The seven unjust titles as a table**, one line each: the title at full
   strength, the law it fails under (natural / divine / human), the one-sentence
   reason, the gem ("any more than if they had discovered us"; "kingdoms could
   be exchanged every day"; the requerimiento's frightened crowd). Index 41 Q2
   pp. 252–277.
4. **The requerimiento read before a notary** (Glossary p. 380; Q2 Art.6): drawn
   up by Palacios Rubios in 1513 on the model of Deut. 20:10–14, read aloud —
   often in Spanish, from a distance — demanding recognition of the Trinity and
   of Spanish title, with war to follow on refusal; Vitoria's verdict: consent
   given in fear and ignorance "by a defenceless crowd surrounded by armed men"
   is void. The artefact's text is public domain (English in Hanke, The Spanish
   Struggle for Justice, 1949); quote two clauses at most.
5. **Perkin and Jack / the Saracen-preacher test** (Q2 Art.4, ss32–34): two men
   investigate the same matter equally; Hadrian VI says the one in grace is
   excused and the one in sin is not — "absurd"; and if Saracens preached to
   Spaniards with no more than a bare announcement, no Spaniard would be bound
   to believe. Both fix the standard the Spaniards' preaching had to meet and,
   Vitoria says, did not.
6. **The escalation ladder** (Q3 Art.1 ss6–8, pp. 281–4): persuade → defend →
   forts → redress → full rights of war, with "war just on both sides" and the
   Burgundy case alongside. Reconstruct as a `<FigureSequence>` (figure 3) and
   in prose one step per sentence.
7. **The eighth title and the Conclusion** (pp. 290–2, text l.15540–15600):
   quote the "for the sake of argument" sentence and the "pitfall" sentence;
   then the three replies to the exchequer objection (Portuguese trade, the
   fifth, the duty to converts).
8. **The Letter to Arcos** (pp. 331–3): the set piece of the objection — three
   short quotations ("freeze the blood", "no title other than iure belli", "men
   but monkeys… incapable of injury"), with Cajamarca recalled by `<Ref>`
   geography-or-institutions in one half-sentence first.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

  most two hours every Salamanca professor had to give once a year, Glossary

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Retrieval (E3, mechanical warm-up, the only one).** List the seven unjust
   titles and, for each, the kind of law it fails under; then say in one clause
   how Aquinas's "determination" (`<Ref>` aquinas-law-and-kingship) explains why
   human law cannot reach a people that never consented to it.
2. **Document reasoning — sourcing drill on the Letter to Arcos (1534).**
   Metadata first (a Dominican professor writing privately to a fellow friar
   who has been asked by a Peruvian adventurer about compounding his gains);
   predict what such a letter would claim and omit; then read the three
   quotations; records / argues / omits; closing question: what can it be USED
   to establish — that the doctrine's author judged Cajamarca unjust by his own
   doctrine; not that the doctrine restrained anyone.
3. **Boundary probe.** Vitoria's own hypothetical: a mischance kills every adult
   and leaves only children (Q3 Art.8). Run Q1 on them (dominium est ius —
   children hold rights) and Q3 (who governs them, by what title, for whose
   benefit). Then remove the condition "for their benefit" and state what the
   eighth title becomes. Solution shows why Vitoria put the whole "pitfall" in
   that restriction.
4. **Claim to refute.** "Vitoria denied Spain any right to be in America." Name
   the step that fails (Q3 Art.1 and the Conclusion) and the sentence that
   shows it ("trade would not have to cease… look at the Portuguese").
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped — the symmetry test run on the just title.**
   Vitoria's Dietary Laws argument: if Christians may punish pagans for sins
   against nature, pagans may punish Christians. Apply the same symmetry to the
   right of travel and trade: could the Indians have exercised it in Castile,
   and what would their "defence" have been when refused? State what the
   symmetry shows about a right that only one party can use, and Vitoria's
   best reply (the right is universal in form; the asymmetry is in power, not
   in law).
6. **Synthesis — counter-argument stem.** "The just titles licensed exactly
   what the unjust titles forbade." Name two facts the claim must account for
   (Vitoria's own conditions and his judgment that the Spaniards exceeded them;
   the Arcos letter) and state what would have to be true for the claim to
   survive (that a right whose refusal is an injury is a licence in any hands
   that can enforce it). Claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph, both sides.
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `locke-and-mill-on-empire`.** Take the eighth title's
   condition — rule over a people "for their benefit, not for our profit",
   until they can govern themselves — and write the rule a liberal of 1859
   would state for when a free people may govern an unfree one. Then compare
   with the sentence Mill actually wrote (the solution quotes "Despotism is a
   legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end
   be their improvement" with a `<Ref>` to the next lesson's
   civilized-community-clause and tutelage).
8. **Document reasoning — the requerimiento.** Give the metadata (1513,
   Palacios Rubios, to be read by a notary before hostilities) and ask for a
   prediction of its contents before reading two clauses; then: what does the
   document record about Spanish practice, what does it argue, what does it
   omit (any means for the hearers to understand it), and which of Vitoria's
   seven refutations it falls under (the sixth — and the fourth).

# Research brief: war-is-divine (Maistre's Seventh Dialogue: war, sacrifice and the soldier)

status: planned
module: contract-revolution-reaction
type: extra
goal: Analyze Maistre's argument that war is a divine law of expiation and compare it with Hegel's and Schmitt's accounts of war

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `maistre-and-bonald`: Providence in history (God uses the
  Revolution), the executioner as the hidden bond of society (First Dialogue),
  God as author of sovereignty and of punishment, the "no such thing as man"
  rejection of the abstract, and the charge that this is proto-fascist with its
  replies. From `rousseau-inequality-and-general-will`: the state of nature as
  solitary innocence and the Hottentot frontispiece (one paragraph there). From
  `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`: the state of nature as war, and the
  remark that sovereigns remain in it toward one another. From
  `cicero-natural-law` and `aquinas-law-and-kingship`: `just-war`. From
  `augustine-two-cities`: the earthly peace and the sacrifice of the innocent
  (reversibility is Maistre's Augustinian inheritance).
- **Contributes:** One move: the executioner's argument extended to war. The
  soldier and the executioner are the only two men society permits to kill;
  society honours the one who kills the innocent in thousands and shuns the one
  who kills the guilty by law — and this inversion needs explaining. Maistre's
  explanation: war is a law of the world under which the earth demands blood,
  innocence pays for guilt, and glory is the mark of a divine institution. Then
  the comparison the goal requires: Hegel's war as the ethical health of states
  (§§321–340) and Schmitt's "real possibility of killing" as the criterion of
  the political — three accounts that each deny war is an accident, for three
  different reasons.
- **Hands off:** This is a starred chapter; no core lesson depends on it. It
  points forward to `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state` (§§321–340 read in full
  there) and to `schmitt-concept-of-the-political` (friend and enemy, the
  "real possibility of killing"), and to `what-measures-greatness` (Spengler and
  Toynbee on war and decline) as a spiral. It also leaves the reader a test the
  historical lessons will use: Maistre's idealised ancien-régime war (campaigns
  from May to December, hospitals, enemy officers at balls) against the record.
- **Spine step:** "Government by consent (Locke) and the general will (Rousseau)
  make a revolution thinkable; Burke, Maistre and Bonald answer that no
  generation knows enough to design a society, and Hegel absorbs both sides into
  the rational state"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the course.yaml opening verbatim: why is the soldier honoured and the
executioner shunned, when both kill by law? A stranger from another world would
get it exactly backwards — and the fact that we do not needs explaining. The
lesson follows Maistre's answer, that war is a law of the world under which blood
is demanded and innocence pays for guilt, and compares it with the two other
accounts of war the course meets.

1. **Why is war possible at all?** Given what men are — reasoning, affectionate,
   afraid of death — war "is humanly inexplicable". Obvious answer: kings order
   and men march. Why it fails: sovereigns command only within "the circle of
   opinion" — Peter the Great sent thousands to die without a murmur and nearly
   had a rebellion over beards; a king cannot order a calendar changed if the
   people will not have it. So the question is not war's possibility but its
   *facility*: why men go so easily. Heading as question: *Why do men march?*
2. **Who may kill, and whom do we honour?** The stranger set piece (recall the
   executioner with a `<Ref>` maistre-and-bonald, one clause): two legal killers;
   the one who kills the guilty, rarely, after judgment, is shunned; the one who
   kills the innocent, in thousands, without judgment, is honoured; "they touch
   each other the way 1 degree touches 360 degrees". The honour has rules — a
   soldier may execute only a soldier, with a soldier's weapon, for a military
   crime, or he is degraded (the Roman vine-branch; the laurel-stick parody) —
   which shows honour is not arbitrary but a code about *what kind* of killing
   is clean. Then the paradox stated: "the most honoured thing in the world has
   always been the right to shed innocent blood innocently". Glory is a fact;
   glory must be explained.
3. **Is war a relic of a state of nature men are leaving?** The contract
   theorists' answer (`<Ref>` hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant,
   rousseau-inequality-and-general-will): nations are still in the state of
   nature toward one another; peace will come as they, too, contract (the Abbé
   de Saint-Pierre's project of perpetual peace, anchor: an early-eighteenth-
   century plan for a European league). Maistre's two replies. (a) There never
   was a state of nature: Rousseau's own frontispiece shows the Hottentot
   returning to his fellows — "society is as old as man"; the savage is not
   original man but degraded man. (b) The inference fails on its own terms:
   every argument for why individuals left the state of nature applies equally
   to nations, so if nations have never left it, something stronger than
   interest keeps them there — "an occult and terrible law demanding human
   blood". Mark the seam (L10b): from the contract story one expects war to
   recede with civilisation; Maistre's catalogue (Considerations ch. 3, and his
   own count of France's forty years of war in ninety-six) says the flow of
   blood is roughly constant.
4. **What is this law?** The widening of the lens (pp. 216–218): violence in the
   vegetable kingdom, among animals, up the chain to man — "the superb and
   terrible king" who "kills to nourish himself, to clothe himself, to adorn
   himself … to instruct himself, to amuse himself, to kill" — and then "man
   himself is charged with slaughtering man". The earth "an immense altar on
   which every living thing must be immolated without end, without measure,
   without respite, until the consummation of things, until the death of death".
   Then the link to the executioner's theodicy: human justice punishes too few,
   the age has abolished atonement, so the debt of unpunished crime is collected
   in war — the earth "cries out for blood"; "never do men disobey on the field
   of death". Define *expiation* and *reversibility* here (the innocent suffer
   for the guilty — the Augustinian and sacrificial dogma from Considerations ch.
   3, pp. 61–62, `<Ref>` augustine-two-cities for the earthly peace bought by
   sacrifice). Heading as claim: *The earth is an altar: war as the collection
   of a debt*.
5. **In what senses is war "divine"?** The sevenfold list reconstructed as a
   table, each sense with Maistre's warrant: (1) in itself, as a law of the world;
   (2) in its supernatural consequences (the soldier's character improved, not
   brutalised — "legions of atheists could not stand against burning legions");
   (3) in its mysterious glory and attraction — the gentle youth who would be
   sick at killing his sister's canary and climbs a pile of corpses tomorrow;
   (4) in the protection of its great leaders; (5) in the way it breaks out —
   no one, in the moment, chooses it; (6) in its results, which escape all
   calculation — nations degraded or exalted; (7) in the force that decides
   it. The last is the bridge to link 6.
6. **What decides a battle?** Not the big battalions: momentum is mass times
   speed, and "three parts mass and two of speed equal three of speed and two of
   mass" — 40,000 disciplined men beat 60,000; Providence keeps Europe's balance
   not by coalitions of the virtuous but by overreach and small obstacles (the
   geese of the Capitol; Rome taken by a hare under Arnulf). Then the sentence
   the lesson's title lives on: "Tell me, General, what is a lost battle? … It
   is a battle one believes one has lost"; "to win is to advance"; an army
   "slides down an inclined plane"; the same manoeuvre has two names ("I have
   split him" / "he has put himself between two fires"); "it is imagination
   that loses battles". The Spartan mother's "Step forward"; Pozharsky and the
   horse-merchant Minin delivering Russia in 1612 with a thousand men. Forward
   echo in one clause: a Russian novelist read these pages and gave the thought
   to his generals (Tolstoy; References carry the attribution). The point for
   the argument: if success is moral and not arithmetical, war is the one human
   activity whose outcome cannot be engineered — which is what "divine" means in
   sense (7).
7. **Two other accounts of war that are not Maistre's.** The comparison the goal
   asks for, two paragraphs each, as a three-column ledger. **Hegel (§§321–340;
   `<Ref>` hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state):** war is not an absolute evil but
   "the ethical moment" in which the state shows itself as an individual and the
   citizen's finite goods are revealed as finite — "the winds preserve the sea
   from the foulness of calm"; perpetual peace would corrupt; states are in a
   state of nature to each other because "there is no Praetor to judge between
   states", so disputes without a judge are settled by war; yet recognition
   survives in war (envoys, private life spared), and the judge is world history.
   Where Hegel agrees with Maistre: war is necessary, not accidental; where he
   differs: its necessity is rational (the state's self-knowledge), not
   expiatory, and he never calls it divine. **Schmitt (`<Ref>`
   schmitt-concept-of-the-political):** the political is the grouping of men
   into friends and enemies with "the real possibility of killing"; the enemy is
   public, not a private foe; war is not the aim of politics but its
   presupposition — the case that reveals the grouping; and genuine political
   theories presuppose man as dangerous (he lists Maistre and Hegel). Where he
   agrees: war cannot be abolished without abolishing politics; where he differs:
   he gives no theodicy at all — the possibility of killing is a criterion, not
   a law of expiation, and he will say "humanity" cannot wage war, where Maistre
   says the earth demands it. The ledger's rows: why war exists / what it does
   for the state / who or what judges it / what would abolish it.
8. **What the argument costs, and the charge revisited.** The idealised war of
   the old regime (campaigns May to December, soldiers fighting soldiers,
   hospitals everywhere, enemy officers at balls, pp. 214–215) is Maistre's
   evidence that war ennobles; the lesson sets it against what the reader knows
   (the Revolutionary wars he was living through; Lebrun's casualty notes) and
   lets the reader test it — the exercise does so. Then the charge from the
   previous lesson (`<Ref>` maistre-and-bonald, one clause): on war, the reply
   concedes the point — Maistre "glorifies war, declares it mysterious and
   divine", and the "vast altar" passage has no echo in Burke and few anywhere.
   The hedge the course keeps: he also condemned conquest and "the rule of the
   stick" (militarism) and never praised war for the nation's sake — his altar
   is God's, not the nation's. Close with what the reader can now do: state
   three accounts on which war is no accident and name what each puts in the
   place of the contract theorists' coming peace.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The visiting stranger (pp. 206–207)**: told in Maistre's order — two legal
   killers, who kills whom, how often, with what judgment — then the stranger's
   guess and its inversion; "1 degree touches 360 degrees". Reconstruct, then
   draw the honour code (pp. 207–208) as the rules that make a killing clean.
2. **The Hottentot frontispiece (pp. 210–211; spd.txt l.12938)**: the engraving
   from Rousseau's Discourse read against the book — the man who gives back his
   European clothes and returns to his people is, for Rousseau, nature preferred;
   for Maistre, a degraded man going home to degradation. One image, two
   readings, with the inference each draws about the state of nature.
3. **The symmetry argument against perpetual peace (pp. 211–212)**: "every
   argument against a society of nations works against a society of
   individuals" — write out the parallel (fear, interest, the common judge) and
   show where Maistre says it breaks (nations never contracted), then the
   inference to the "occult and terrible law".
4. **The chain of slaughter and the altar (pp. 216–217)**: plants → insects →
   birds, fish, quadrupeds → man → man against man; the catalogue of why man
   kills; "an immense altar … until the death of death". Then the debt argument
   (p. 217): fewer punishments, no atonement, therefore war.
5. **"War is divine" in seven senses (pp. 218–219; spd.txt l.13353–13416)** as a
   table: sense / Maistre's warrant / the anecdote that carries it (the gentle
   youth; the Te Deum; Pozharsky).
6. **Momentum against the big battalions (pp. 219–220)** and **"a battle one
   believes one has lost" (pp. 221–223)**: the mass × speed arithmetic as
   Maistre states it (bind it as his analogy, not a formula), the inclined
   plane, the two names for one manoeuvre, "Step forward". The Tolstoy echo in
   one clause.
7. **The three accounts as a ledger**: Maistre / Hegel / Schmitt × (why war
   exists; what it does; who judges; what would end it) — with the exact
   pointers (Seventh Dialogue pp. 216–219; PR §§324, 333–340; Concept of the
   Political §3, §7).
8. **The idealised war (pp. 214–215) against the record**: Maistre's picture
   (May to December, soldiers only, hospitals) set beside two facts the reader
   can check — the Revolutionary wars' levée en masse (1793) and Lebrun's
   casualty corrections (Considerations ch. 3 n.36: Joseph II's Turkish war,
   33,543 dead of disease to 80,000 killed — a figure that is Maistre's, bound
   as his) — as the exercise's raw material.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor (L11):** *the Seventh Dialogue* (the seventh evening of the
  St Petersburg Dialogues, set in 1809, published 1821 — `<Ref>`
  maistre-and-bonald for the frame); *the Abbé de Saint-Pierre* (Charles-Irénée
  Castel, whose Projet pour rendre la paix perpétuelle en Europe, 1713, Rousseau
  abridged); *Hottentot* (the Khoikhoi; recall the Rousseau lesson's anchor
  with a `<Ref>`); *expiation*, *reversibility* (define at link 4);
  *theodicy* (recall, `<Ref>`); *Te Deum* (the hymn sung for victories); *the
  geese of the Capitol* (Rome 390 BC, saved by geese that woke the garrison);
  *Arnulf* (the German king whose siege of Rome in 896 succeeded, by legend,
  when a hare ran at the walls and the pursuing soldiers found them undefended
  — Lebrun n. xiii); *Pozharsky and Minin* (the prince and the Nizhny Novgorod
  merchant who raised the army that drove the Poles from Moscow in 1612 — one
  clause); *Peter the Great's beards* (his 1698 decree); *levée en masse*
  (the Convention's conscription of August 1793, if used in the test); *Praetor*
  (Hegel's: the Roman magistrate who judged between citizens — there is none
  between states).
- **Dates to the decade** except 1612 and 1793 where the anecdote needs them.
- **Do not re-teach the executioner**; one `<Ref>` and one clause. Do not
  re-teach Providence or the charge; both are recalled, not argued.
- **Do not teach Hegel's state or Schmitt's friend–enemy here** beyond the two
  comparison paragraphs; both have their lessons. The comparison is a ledger,
  not an exposition.
- **"War is divine" means "a law of the world", not "approved by God"** —
  Maistre says so in the first of the seven senses, and the First Dialogue's
  distinction (God is the author of the evil that punishes, not of the evil that
  defiles) applies. State this at first use or the reader reads the title as
  blasphemy or as cheerleading.
- **Maistre is not a militarist**: he condemns conquest and the rule of the
  stick, and his soldier is honoured for the law he kills under, not for
  victory. Say it once (link 8), without using it to excuse the altar passage.
- **The idealised eighteenth-century war is false as history** (the Seven
  Years' War killed on a scale Maistre omits; the Revolutionary wars were
  underway as he wrote the Considerations). The lesson treats it as a claim to
  test, not as fact; the exercise does the test. Never present his casualty
  figures as the course's own.
- **Tolstoy** is an echo, one clause, attribution in References (Berlin VII;
  Garrard n.16). Do not build a section on it.
- **The momentum "formula" is an analogy**; do not typeset it as mathematics
  the reader must compute (E2: no plug-constant arithmetic in a history course).
- **Period matter**: Maistre's praise of carnage is the material; present it in
  his words at full strength and let the reader weigh; no moralising in either
  direction and no softening.
- **Names ONLY in References:** Berlin, Garrard, Lebrun, Unamuno ("the abattoir
  of the late Count Joseph de Maistre" — an epigraph in References at most),
  Görres, Stendhal (if Fabrice at Waterloo is mentioned), Tolstoy (as the
  borrower; the novel may be named as an object). Thinkers who may be named:
  Maistre, Rousseau, Hobbes, Hegel, Schmitt, Saint-Pierre, Augustine (`<Ref>`),
  La Bruyère, Frederick II (as a quoted general), Montesquieu.
- **Dossier gaps:** Hegel §§321–340 are present in full; Schmitt's Concept is
  present; Tolstoy is not cached (not needed); Strauss–Cropsey (Hassner on
  Hegel) unavailable, file empty.

1. *Retrieval, warm-up (E3).* State the executioner's argument from the
   previous lesson in three steps (`<Ref>`), then the one step the Seventh
   Dialogue adds to reach war (human justice punishes too few, so the debt is
   collected in war). Name the page for each.
2. *Boundary probe.* Suppose human justice punished every crime — Maistre's own
   counterfactual (p. 217: "if human justice struck all criminals there would be
   no war"). Does the altar passage (p. 217) still hold? The reader should find
   that the "universal law of violent death" (pp. 216–217) is argued from nature,
   not from unpunished crime, so the two grounds of war in the Dialogue are
   independent and the counterfactual removes only one — and say which one
   Hegel's account (§324) shares.
3. *Document reasoning (sourcing drill).* The idealised ancien-régime war (pp.
   214–215): author, date (written c. 1809–21, during and after the Napoleonic
   wars), audience, what it records (customs of eighteenth-century campaigning:
   seasons, hospitals, officers' courtesies), what it argues (war ennobles), what
   it omits (the wars he was living through; the Seven Years' War's scale; the
   civilians of besieged towns), and what it can be USED to establish — a
   nostalgia that is itself evidence about the reactionary mind, not a fact about
   old-regime war. Corroborate with Lebrun's note correcting his casualty figure
   (Considerations ch. 3 n.36).
4. *Plausible false claim to refute.* "Maistre says God is on the side of the
   victor, so victory proves justice." Name the failing step: pp. 219–220 deny
   that the big battalions win and say Providence keeps the balance by small
   obstacles; "war is divine in its results" (p. 219) means the results escape
   calculation, not that they are deserved; and the First Dialogue's distinction
   (God authors the evil that punishes, not the evil that defiles) forbids the
   inference.
5. *Derivation the lesson skipped.* Run Maistre's symmetry argument (pp.
   211–212) backwards: if every argument against a society of nations works
   against a society of individuals, and individuals DID form societies, what
   follows for nations? The reader should find the dilemma Maistre's "occult
   law" is invented to escape — either nations can contract (and perpetual peace
   is possible) or individuals never contracted either (his own view, from the
   Hottentot) — and state which horn he takes.
6. *Synthesis / counter-argument (the three accounts).* Someone claims that
   Hegel's "the winds preserve the sea from the foulness of calm" is Maistre's
   altar in philosophical dress. Name two facts the claim fails to account for
   (Hegel's necessity is the state's self-knowledge and keeps recognition in war,
   §§324, 338; Maistre's is expiation and requires innocent blood) and state what
   would have to be true for it to survive (that "the ethical moment" is itself a
   sacrifice demanded by something above the state — which Hegel's §340 locates in
   world history, not God).
7. **FORWARD (E7) → `schmitt-concept-of-the-political`.** Maistre says a
   stranger would misjudge our honour because he does not see that the soldier
   kills under a law. Ask: what makes the soldier's victim different from the
   executioner's — not guilt, since the soldier's victim is innocent — and the
   reader should arrive at "he is an enemy, publicly, not a criminal or a
   private foe". The solution says in one sentence that the next module's jurist
   will make exactly that distinction (hostis, not inimicus) the criterion of
   politics, with the `<Ref>`.

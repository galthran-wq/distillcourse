# Research brief: weber-only-in-the-west (Weber's 'only in the West': the Protestant ethic and rationalization)

status: planned
module: why-the-west
type: core
goal: Analyze Weber's question why rational science, law, bureaucracy and capitalism arose only in the West, and his answer from the Protestant ethic, and evaluate the evidence and the objections

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** The lesson OPENS the why-the-west module and follows
  `testing-the-clash`, which closed the civilizations module on the premise all three
  of its authors share and none explains: that the West is the civilization the
  others react to, "at an extraordinary peak of power", carrying ideas the Rest find
  of "little resonance" (that lesson's FORWARD exercise had the reader ask, of
  Huntington's list of Western ideas, what could be peculiar to the West if each has

  `what-measures-greatness` (prerequisite): the three measures of greatness
  (`measures-of-greatness`: duration, ideas others take up, power) and the lesson's
  point that a measure is a choice about what to count — which is exactly what
  Weber's parenthesis "(as we like to think)" admits. From
  `hellenization-of-christianity` (prerequisite): Christianity as a Jewish movement
  remade in Greek concepts — Weber's own list credits "the full development of a
  systematic theology … to Christianity under the influence of Hellenism"; one clause
  of recall. From `strauss-natural-right-and-history`: Weber is NOT a new name — he
  was anchored there as the German sociologist (d. 1920) whose fact–value
  distinction (`fact-value-distinction`) Strauss attacked; that lesson reconstructed
  Weber's methodology from Strauss's account and did NOT teach the Protestant Ethic
  or Strauss's critique of it (NRH ch. II pp. 59–62 and n. 22), which are new here.
  Further back, each by `<Ref>` and a clause where the material calls on it:
  `augustine-two-cities` (grace and predestination — the two cities as the
  predestined and the rest; Weber's note quoting "si non es praedestinatus, fac ut
  praedestineris"); `aquinas-law-and-kingship` (Aquinas's ordering of estates and
  occupations as natural, which Weber contrasts with Luther and Baxter);
  `bodin-sovereignty` (the wars of religion; the Huguenots as a minority);
  `english-revolution-as-test` and `carlyle-heroes` (the Puritans, Cromwell, and
  Carlyle's "the last of our heroisms", which Weber quotes); `tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`
  (New England founded by Puritans — Weber's Massachusetts case);
  `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state` (`civil-society`: the sphere of needs, labour and
  exchange — Weber's "immense cosmos" of the capitalist economy is that sphere grown
  autonomous); `marx-manifesto` (base and superstructure — the view Weber's Florence
  vs Pennsylvania case is aimed at); `gobineau-inequality-of-races` (the racial
  answer to "why here?", which Weber's heredity paragraph sets aside);
  `machiavelli-effectual-truth` and `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant` (needed for
  Strauss's alternative genealogy of the capitalist spirit).
- **Contributes:** The module's question, stated exactly and fairly, and its first
  answer. The question (Author's Introduction, 1920): to what "combination of
  circumstances" is it due that in the West, and only there, science, law,
  administration, music, the state and capitalism took a systematic, calculable form
  — "rationalization" (`rationalization`). The narrower question of the 1904–05
  essay: why the West produced not acquisitiveness (which is universal) but a sober,
  methodical, capital-accounting enterprise built on free labour, and a class of men
  who treated its endless renewal as a duty. The answer (`protestant-ethic`): ascetic
  Protestantism — Calvinist predestination and the anxiety of proof, the calling,
  worldly asceticism — attached religious sanctions to methodical work and restricted
  consumption, and the resulting conduct outlived its religious root (the iron
  cage). Then the evidence and the objections, from the left, the right and the
  data, each at full strength, and Weber's own disclaimers — so the reader leaves
  knowing what the thesis claims, what it explicitly does not claim, and the
  course's verdict on it (on balance): the Protestant ethic is not borne out as the
  main channel of the Protestant advantage, which ran through literacy; Weber's
  description of Western rationalization stands.
- **Hands off:** The lesson decides the Protestant-ethic thesis (on balance: not the
  main channel; the Protestant advantage ran through Bible-reading schooling; the
  description of rationalization stands). What it does not decide is the module's
  question — why the West at all — which stays with the module and is judged in
  `the-borrowing-ledger` (cause: open). Weber traced "only one side of the causal
  chain" — ideas acting on conduct — and left the institutions, the states and the
  rest to "a combination of circumstances". The next lesson, `landes-european-exceptionalism`, takes up the
  other side: why a divided Europe let new conduct and new inventions survive that a
  single ruler could have stopped (the FORWARD exercise arrives there by asking what
  political fact let Luther and Calvin survive at all). Further on, one clause each
  and no content: `murray-human-accomplishment` counts the West's output in science
  and art and credits Christianity among its causes; `henrich-church-and-kinship`
  moves the cause from the Reformation back to the medieval Church's family rules and
  sets out the Augsburg 1555 natural experiment at length; `pomeranz-and-goody-the-rival-thesis` answers that the
  "only in the West" list misreads Eurasia — Chinese accounting, Eurasian
  bourgeoisies, Asian cities — and that the lead came late; `the-borrowing-ledger`
  grades what the West took from others (Weber himself grants "there was printing in
  China"). The heredity paragraph of the Author's Introduction points to the
  heredity module (`hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`), which takes up the question
  Weber declined to answer.
- **Spine step:** "Why the West? Weber, Landes, Murray and Henrich argue for Western
  exceptionalism from religion, fragmentation, accomplishment and kinship; Pomeranz
  and Goody answer that the West was one link in a long chain of exchange; the
  borrowing ledger tests both"

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged.** Weber's thesis as he held it, not the one he disclaimed ("such a
  foolish and doctrinaire thesis as that the spirit of capitalism … could only have
  arisen as the result of certain effects of the Reformation", l. 2782): ascetic
  Protestantism had an elective affinity with the spirit of rational capitalism and
  accelerated it — the religious ethic was the channel through which Protestant
  populations came to outperform Catholic ones.
- **Verdict.** Not borne out as the main channel; the description of Western
  rationalization stands. Grade: **on balance**.
- **What decides it.** (1) In 272 cities of the German-speaking Holy Roman Empire,
  1300–1900, there are "no effects of Protestantism on economic growth" measured by
  city population (Cantoni 2015; staged working-paper version
  `texts/verification-2026-11/cantoni-2010-wp-protestant-reformation.txt` l. 16, l.
  73). (2) In Prussian county data of the late nineteenth century, with distance to
  Wittenberg as an instrument for Protestantism, Protestantism did raise prosperity,
  but "Protestants' higher literacy" accounts "for most of the gap in economic
  prosperity" (Becker & Woessmann 2009;
  `texts/verification-2026-11/becker-woessmann-2009-abstract.txt`). Protestantism
  mattered, through Bible-reading schooling, not through a work ethic. (3) Capital
  accounting predates the Reformation: double-entry book-keeping was printed at
  Venice in 1494 (Pacioli) and in use in Italian cities a century before — though
  Weber distinguished capitalism's technique from its rational ethic, so this narrows
  the thesis rather than refuting it. (4) What stands: the Author's Introduction's
  inventory of systematic, calculable forms is a description no objection in the
  lesson dissolves; each objection answers the question differently.
- **Strongest objection to the verdict.** The literacy channel may itself be the
  ethic at work: Bible-reading was a religious duty, and a disciplined, schooled
  population is what Weber's worldly asceticism would produce; city size and county
  income are also not the "spirit" Weber described, which was a conduct of whole
  groups, not an aggregate growth rate. Why it does not overturn the verdict:
  Weber's mechanism runs from predestination-anxiety to methodical acquisition and
  saving, not from scripture to reading; a channel that works as well for Lutherans
  (who lacked the Calvinist anxiety) as for Calvinists is not the channel he
  proposed; and the one study that measures growth over six centuries finds no
  Protestant effect at all. The objection keeps the grade at on balance rather than
  strong: neither study measures conduct directly.
- **What would overturn it.** Evidence that Calvinist conduct, not literacy, explains
  Protestant economic performance where the two can be separated — for example,
  Calvinist against Lutheran populations of equal literacy, or Protestant groups of
  low literacy outperforming Catholic groups of equal literacy.

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

The opening (course.yaml, verbatim) sets the problem: China had paper money,
printing and an examined civil service; India mathematics and logic; the Islamic world
trade from Spain to Indonesia; yet the laboratory, the systematic law code, the career
bureaucracy and the double-entry capitalist firm appeared together only in Europe; a
German sociologist in 1904 asks why and begins from the Baden statistics. The
newcomer's problem: how do you ask "why only here?" without either flattering the
West or denying a difference that, by the opening's own list, is there — and what
would count as an answer? The chain:

1. **The question, exactly as asked.** Question: what is being claimed when someone
   says a thing exists "only in the West"? Attempt (the reader's default, and the
   claim's caricature): the West had science, law, the state, and the others did
   not. Why that fails, in Weber's own text: his inventory concedes, item by item,
   that each thing existed elsewhere — astronomy in Babylon, geometry in India,
   historical scholarship in China, codified law in the Near East, polyphony
   everywhere, printing in China, officials and parties all over the world — and
   claims only that a particular FORM did not: proof, experiment, a systematic
   jurisprudence, harmony and notation, the Press, the trained official on whom
   the whole of life depends, a state "with a rational, written constitution". The
   answer: what the list has in common is a "specific and peculiar rationalism" —
   conduct made systematic, calculable and rule-bound; define `rationalization` here,
   as the answer to "what do the items share?", not as a label. The because: the
   question is not why the West had more but why its practices took one form across
   fields that look unrelated — music and accounting, law and architecture — which
   is why Weber asks for "a combination of circumstances", not a cause. Two features
   of the question sentence the lesson must show at once, because they decide what
   kind of claim it is: (a) "(as we like to think)" and "universal significance and
   value" — the selection is made by a European and says so; recall
   `strauss-natural-right-and-history` in one clause (Weber's distinction between
   a value that selects the object and a judgment of value) and let the reader see
   that the question itself carries a value — the same point `what-measures-greatness`
   made about measures (Ref, one clause); (b) Weber's printing sentence ("There was
   printing in China. But … the Press and periodicals") — he does not claim the
   West invented printing; the question of who borrowed what is
   `the-borrowing-ledger`'s (forward Ref, one clause). The rival thesis that the list
   itself misreads Eurasia is named as a later lesson's, in one sentence, without
   comment.
2. **Why not heredity: the paragraph Weber wrote and set aside.** Question: if
   rationalization appears in field after field, but only in one population, why not
   suspect the population? Attempt: heredity — the answer the reader has met as
   Gobineau's (Ref `gobineau-inequality-of-races`, one clause). Weber's own response
   (quote the heredity paragraph nearly whole — it is short): he thinks biological
   heredity's importance probably "very great", sees no way to measure "the extent
   or, above all, the form of its influence", and holds that appealing to it now
   would be "a premature renunciation of the possibility of knowledge attainable
   now". The answer is a method, not a verdict: explain first what can be explained
   by conditions that can be observed. The because, and the seam: this is a reason to
   look at religion and institutions FIRST, not a denial of heredity — state it that
   way, with no gloss in either direction; the heredity module
   (`hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`, forward Ref, one sentence) takes up the
   question with the measurement Weber lacked.
3. **Narrowing to capitalism: greed is not the thing to explain.** Question: of
   all the items, why capitalism, and what exactly about it? Attempt: the pursuit of
   gain — capitalism is greed with capital. Why it fails: greed is universal
   ("waiters, physicians, coachmen, artists, prostitutes … crusaders, gamblers, and
   beggars"); "this naïve idea of capitalism must be given up once and for all";
   adventurer capitalism — war finance, tax farming, speculation, colonial booty —
   existed in Babylon, China, India and Rome. The answer: what is peculiar is a
   sober enterprise "forever renewed", run by capital accounting (initial balance,
   calculation before each decision, final balance), separated from the household,
   on calculable law and free labour. The because: those are the conditions for
   calculation, and only one kind of capitalism needs to calculate. Seam (Ref
   `hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`, `civil-society`): the sphere of needs, labour
   and exchange the reader knows from Hegel appears in Weber as "an immense cosmos"
   that forces whoever enters it to follow its rules — so once it exists it selects
   its own people; Weber's question is where the first such people came from, since
   a selection needs something to select.
4. **The puzzle in the statistics.** Question: is there any evidence that the
   religion of a population tracks its part in this kind of capitalism? Weber's
   evidence (ch. 1, the Baden data of 1895 — a FIGURE, and bound in the sentence):
   Protestants were about 37 per cent of Baden's population but 48 per cent of the
   pupils in schools beyond the compulsory stage, and held more taxable capital per
   head (954,000 marks per 1,000 Protestants against 589,000 per 1,000 Catholics).
   The obvious explanations, each tried and each partly broken: (a) inherited wealth
   — the richest towns went Protestant in the sixteenth century, so Protestants
   started ahead; Weber grants part of this, but it does not explain the Catholic
   preference for the classical Gymnasium over the technical schools; (b) minority
   status — excluded minorities turn to business (Poles in Russia, Huguenots under
   Louis XIV, English Nonconformists, Jews), but German Catholics, a minority in
   places and the ruled in others, did not, while Protestants showed the tendency as
   rulers and as ruled (Ref `bodin-sovereignty` for the Huguenots, one clause); (c)
   Protestant worldliness against Catholic other-worldliness ("either eat well or
   sleep well") — fails because the Reformation TIGHTENED control: Calvinism in
   Geneva, Scotland, the Netherlands, New England was "the most absolutely
   unbearable form of ecclesiastical control", and the most advanced bourgeoisies
   accepted it. The answer: the cause should be sought in "the permanent intrinsic
   character" of the beliefs. The because, and an honest flag the lesson must place
   HERE, not only in the objections: Weber's own table also shows the Jewish
   population (1.5 per cent) furnishing 10 per cent of the pupils and the most
   capital per head, which his thesis sets aside as a different kind of capitalism
   (ch. 5 n. 58) — the reader should see that the data are thinner and messier than
   the argument built on them; and this is the only table in the essay.
5. **What the "spirit" is, and what it is up against.** Question: if the cause lies
   in belief, what belief — what is the attitude to be explained? Answer by contrast,
   in three steps. (i) Franklin's maxims ("time is money … credit is money … money is
   of the prolific, generating nature") against Jacob Fugger's "make money as long
   as he could": the same drive, but in Franklin it is a duty, an "ethically
   coloured maxim for the conduct of life", and from the standpoint of happiness
   "absolutely irrational". (ii) The obstacle is traditionalism: the mower whose rate
   is raised from 1 to 1.25 marks an acre and who then mows 2 acres instead of 2½,
   because "the opportunity of earning more was less attractive than that of working
   less" (FIGURE: the piece-rate diagram); "A man does not by nature wish to earn more
   and more money". The putting-out merchant's comfortable trade broken not by new
   capital but by a young man who supervised the weavers and sold direct — "the new
   spirit". (iii) The objection from the left enters here at full strength (Ref
   `marx-manifesto`): ideas are the reflection of material conditions, so the spirit
   is capitalism's product, not its cause. Weber's reply is a comparison: Florence in
   the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, "the money and capital market of all the
   great political Powers", held this attitude ethically suspect; backwoods
   Pennsylvania in the eighteenth century, barely out of barter, held it a duty — "to
   speak here of a reflection of material conditions in the ideal superstructure
   would be patent nonsense". The because: if the spirit came first where capital
   was weakest, capital did not produce it; so its source must be looked for
   elsewhere — and the medieval Church's teaching (Deo placere vix potest, the
   merchant can hardly please God) cannot be the source.
6. **From the calling to predestination: where duty and anxiety came from.**
   Question: what religious idea could make endless methodical gain a duty? Anchor
   the Reformation at first mention (L11b): Luther's theses of 1517 in Wittenberg;
   Calvin's Geneva from the 1540s; the Westminster Confession of 1647 as Calvinism's
   English-speaking creed. Attempt 1: Luther — his translation gave "calling"
   (Beruf) its sense of a worldly task set by God, so ordinary work became the place
   where duty is done, and the monk's flight from the world lost its rank. Why
   Luther is not enough: his calling stays traditionalist — accept the station God
   gave you (one clause of contrast with Aquinas's natural ordering of estates, Ref
   `aquinas-law-and-kingship`). Attempt 2: Calvinist predestination — quote the
   Confession III.3 ("some men and angels are predestinated unto everlasting life,
   and others foreordained to everlasting death"; recall `augustine-two-cities` in
   one clause: the doctrine is Augustine's, the two cities are the predestined and
   the rest). The obvious inference, and Weber's hinge (n. 66): "Fatalism is, of
   course, the only logical consequence of predestination. But on account of the
   idea of proof the psychological result was precisely the opposite." Derive it
   step by step — this is the lesson's central derivation and must not be compressed
   (L0c): (a) the believer cannot earn salvation and no priest or sacrament can give
   it — "unprecedented inner loneliness"; (b) the question "Am I one of the elect?"
   cannot be left alone in an age when the after-life was more certain than this
   life; (c) Calvin himself had no such anxiety, but the pastors who had to answer
   ordinary believers gave two counsels: consider yourself chosen, since doubt is a
   sign of weak faith; and seek certainty in tireless work in a calling; (d) works
   cannot buy salvation but can be a SIGN of it — "the technical means, not of
   purchasing salvation, but of getting rid of the fear of damnation"; (e) because a
   single lapse is a sign against you, the whole of life must be systematic — not
   the Catholic's "hand to mouth", sin and absolution, works as "a sort of insurance
   premium", but a methodical life accounted for like a business (Franklin's virtue
   tables, Puritan diaries). The because: a doctrine that logically should produce
   passivity produced, through the need for proof, the most active conduct — and the
   lesson says plainly that this step is psychological, and is where most of the
   objections later strike.
7. **Worldly asceticism and accumulation.** Question: why should methodical work
   produce CAPITAL rather than consumption? Answer: asceticism — the monk's
   discipline, which the medieval Church kept behind monastery walls (the Benedictine
   and Cistercian rule of measured time and labour), was carried into ordinary life:
   asceticism "strode into the market-place of life, slammed the door of the
   monastery behind it". Baxter's pastoral teaching: waste of time is the first and
   deadliest sin; labour is a discipline and an end in itself; profit in a lawful
   calling is God's gift to be used — "you may labour to be rich for God, though not
   for the flesh and sin" — while luxury and display are condemned. Put the two
   rules together: acquisition freed, consumption restricted; the arithmetic result
   is accumulation — "accumulation of capital through ascetic compulsion to save".
   One clause on the sects (Quakers, Baptists, Mennonites — "honesty is the best
   policy"), whose wealth was proverbial. The thesis sentence (ch. 5, quote): "rational
   conduct on the basis of the idea of the calling, was born … from the spirit of
   Christian asceticism". Define `protestant-ethic` here, as the name of this whole
   chain. FIGURE: the FigureSequence of the chain.
8. **The iron cage: the conduct outlives its root.** Question: if the spirit was
   religious, why is modern capitalism not religious? Answer: Wesley's own paradox —
   religion produces industry and frugality, which produce riches, which erode
   religion — and the cosmos, once built on machines, "needs its support no longer";
   "The Puritan wanted to work in a calling; we are forced to do so"; Baxter's "light
   cloak" became "an iron cage", binding everyone born into it "until the last ton of
   fossilized coal is burnt" (keep this clause — the coal returns in the Pomeranz
   lesson; no comment here). Then Weber's closing — the open question of new prophets
   or "mechanized petrification", and "Specialists without spirit, sensualists without
   heart" — immediately followed by his own withdrawal: this "brings us to the world
   of judgments of value and of faith", and "it is, of course, not my aim to
   substitute for a one-sided materialistic an equally one-sided spiritualistic causal
   interpretation". The because: `rationalization` is now visible as a process that
   can outrun its causes; the iron cage is a claim about the West's present, not only
   its past — and it is a verdict, which Weber both states and disowns (the Strauss
   point the reader has; one clause).
9. **The objections, at full strength, and what survives them.** Where they enter:
   after the whole thesis stands. Each objection is stated as the course's own
   position (authors in References), then Weber's reply from his own text, then what
   the exchange leaves standing.
   (a) *Capitalism before the Reformation, in Catholic Europe.* Italian merchant
   banking, bills of exchange, partnerships and double-entry book-keeping — set out
   in print at Venice in 1494, in use in the Italian cities over a century earlier —
   met Weber's own definition of rational capital accounting in Catholic cities
   before Luther was born. Weber's reply: technique is not ethos; the Italian
   moralists tolerated gain but "the feeling was never quite overcome" that it was
   dangerous — Florence against Pennsylvania again. What it leaves: Weber's
   "rational capitalism" cannot be dated by its technique; the thesis is narrowed to
   the claim that ascetic Protestantism made the conduct a duty for whole classes.
   (b) *The Baden data re-examined.* The one table is a single province, a single
   year, school enrolment and taxable capital, without controls; critics later argued
   that Protestants lived disproportionately in towns, where the technical schools
   were; the reply was that the urban location itself was no accident. The modern
   form of this objection uses Prussian county data and finds that the Protestant
   advantage is explained by literacy — Bible-reading for every believer — rather
   than by a work ethic; a different study of the German lands finds no Protestant
   effect on city growth over six centuries. State these as findings with their
   scope (Becker & Woessmann: Prussian counties, late nineteenth century, distance
   to Wittenberg as the instrument; Cantoni: 272 cities, 1300–1900, city population
   as the measure); they carry the verdict in link 10, and the Augsburg natural
   experiment stays `henrich-church-and-kinship`'s (forward Ref, one clause).
   (c) *Scotland and Holland.* Scotland, Calvinist from the 1560s, stayed poor until
   after the Union of 1707; Holland, the richest economy of the seventeenth century,
   had a merchant elite that was largely Arminian (the anti-predestinarian party)
   rather than strict Calvinist — which Weber himself concedes (strict Calvinism
   dominated "for seven years"; the moneyed interests "Arminians"), replying that the
   entrepreneurs came from the rising middle and lower bourgeoisie. The Scottish reply
   from Weber's side: the ethos can be present while capital, markets and
   institutions are lacking — which is the "combination of circumstances" clause
   doing work; the reader is asked (Exercise 3) whether that reply makes the thesis
   harder to test.
   (d) *The objection from the right: a corruption, and a philosophical source.*
   (Ref `strauss-natural-right-and-history`.) Weber's crucial link is the popular
   reading of predestination that Calvin rejected — "a carnal interpretation of a
   spiritual teaching" — so the most he proved is that "a corruption or degeneration
   of Calvin's theology" produced the capitalist spirit, a value judgment his own
   method forbade; and the spirit itself — that unlimited accumulation serves the
   common good — may come not from theology at all but from the conscious break
   with classical philosophy begun by Machiavelli and carried by Bacon and Hobbes
   (Refs `machiavelli-effectual-truth`, `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`, one
   clause each), whose books preceded the Puritan writers Weber cites, with
   Puritanism as its "carrier". With it, Tawney's point as Strauss uses it: the
   Puritanism Weber quotes had already made its peace with an existing capitalism.
   Weber's reply, from his text: he says himself that Calvin did not hold the
   doctrine of signs, and that his subject is the conduct of ordinary believers, not
   the theology of founders — the sanctions that "work in directions often different
   from the theologians' doctrine".
   (e) *Weber's own limits.* "Only one side of the causal chain"; "not … such a
   foolish and doctrinaire thesis"; ideal types, not averages; "not my aim to
   substitute … one-sided spiritualistic" for "one-sided materialistic". Where the
   lesson concedes: the essay does not establish that Calvinism caused capitalism,
   and Weber says he is not claiming it; its empirical base is thin and its strongest
   step is psychological. Where it holds: the question of the Author's Introduction
   survives every objection above, because each objection answers it differently
   rather than dissolving it — which is why the module's next five lessons exist.
10. **The weighing: the ethic is not the main channel; the rationalization stands.**
   Question: with the thesis restated as Weber held it — an elective affinity that
   accelerated rational capitalism — and the objections on the table, which way does
   the evidence point? Set the claim against its strongest defence and the two
   modern studies (all in the VERDICT section above): the six-century city panel
   finds no Protestant effect on growth; the Prussian data find a Protestant effect
   that literacy accounts for "most of"; Italian book-keeping narrows the claim to the
   ethic, not the technique. Then the strongest objection to the verdict at full
   strength — Bible-reading was itself a religious duty, so the literacy channel may be
   the ethic in another form — and why it fails as a rescue: Lutherans read too, without
   the Calvinist anxiety that is the hinge of Weber's derivation (link 6), so a channel
   both confessions share is not the one he proposed. State the verdict: on the
   evidence the course has, Protestantism mattered to Europe's economic record, but
   through schooling, not through the ethic Weber derived; the thesis is **not borne
   out as the main channel — on balance**, because neither study measures conduct
   directly. What stands is the description of Western rationalization in the Author's
   Introduction, which no objection dissolved. What would overturn the verdict:
   Calvinist conduct, not literacy, explaining Protestant performance where the two
   can be separated.

**Conclusion** (the `<Conclusion>` block): states the verdict in plain words with its
grade — Weber's question stands and his description of a West that made science, law,
administration and the firm systematic stands with it; his answer, the Protestant ethic
as the channel, is not borne out on balance, because where its effect can be measured
it runs through literacy, and over six centuries of German city growth it does not show
at all. Then the hand-off: if religion is not the main channel, the political fact that
let Luther and Calvin survive — a Europe no single ruler could close — is the next
candidate (`landes-european-exceptionalism`).

"Heredity set aside, not denied" / "Greed is everywhere; capital accounting is not" /

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Two dates, two texts.** The essay is 1904–05; the "only in the West" inventory is
  the Author's Introduction written in 1920 for the collected essays, after Weber's
  studies of China, India and ancient Judaism. The opening's "in 1904 asks why"
  compresses the two; the lesson must say once, plainly, which text is which, or the
  reader will think the list was the essay's premise rather than its later frame.
- **State the question fairly (module opener).** "Only in the West" is a claim about
  forms (systematic, calculable, rule-bound), not a claim that others lacked
  knowledge, skill, wealth or acquisitiveness — Weber concedes all four. It is also
  openly made from inside one civilization ("as we like to think"). The lesson gives
  both halves, without apology and without triumph. The rival thesis (Pomeranz,
  Goody) is named only as the later lessons' answer, and never phrased as a reproach
  (e.g. not "Eurocentric" in the course's voice; if the word is used, it is quoted
  with who used it and when).
- **Register rule.**
  The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Borrowings.** Weber's own sentence concedes printing in China; the lesson does not
  add its own claims about transfers. If the opening's "printing" is glossed, it is
  with Weber's distinction (printing existed; the Press did not), and the question of
  Gutenberg's relation to East Asian printing (most probably an independent
  invention) is left to `the-borrowing-ledger`, Ref, one clause at most. Paper money
  and the examined civil service in China are the opening's examples of things that
  existed elsewhere; do not develop them.
- **The heredity paragraph is reported exactly.** Weber says he is "inclined to think
  the importance of biological heredity very great" and that appealing to it now is
  premature. Neither half may be dropped: quoting only the first makes him a
  hereditarian; quoting only the second makes him its opponent. The paragraph is a
  methodological priority, and the lesson says so and stops.
- **Do not overstate the thesis.** Weber does not claim that Calvinism caused
  capitalism, that Protestants are richer because of their faith, or that the
  Reformation was economically motivated; he disclaims all three (l. 2782, l.
  5176–5186). A lesson that summarises him as "Protestantism caused capitalism" is the
  misreading the course must not make; the critics' strongest points (a)–(d) attack
  what he did claim.
- **Do not understate it either.** He does claim that a specific religious ethic
  attached salvation-anxiety to methodical work and so produced, in whole groups, a
  conduct that capitalism later only selected; the thesis sentence (ch. 5) says
  "born … from the spirit of Christian asceticism". The disclaimers limit the claim's
  scope, not its content.
- **Calvin vs Calvinism.** Weber's mechanism runs through the pastoral practice of the
  seventeenth century (Baxter, the Westminster divines, Bunyan), not Calvin's
  Institutes. Say "Calvinist" or "Puritan" for the mechanism, "Calvin" only for the
  founder — and that distinction is the hinge of Strauss's objection, so introduce it
  in link 6 and use it in link 9.
- **Terms to anchor at first mention (L11), no more than three per section:** *the
  Reformation* (1517 Wittenberg; Lutheran and Reformed branches); *Calvinism*,
  *Puritans* (English Calvinists who sought to "purify" the Church of England; Ref
  `english-revolution-as-test` for Cromwell's Puritans, one clause); *predestination*;
  *the elect*; *the Westminster Confession* (1647, the creed of English-speaking
  Presbyterians); *calling* / *Beruf*; *asceticism* (self-denial as discipline);
  *worldly asceticism* (Weber's "innerweltliche Askese"); *Pietism*, *Methodism*,
  *Baptists/Quakers/Mennonites* (one clause each, or cut — the sects are a sentence,
  not a section); *Arminians* (Dutch Protestants who rejected strict predestination,
  condemned at the Synod of Dort 1618–19); *Baden* (a grand duchy of south-west
  Germany, mixed Catholic and Protestant); *Gymnasium* vs *Realschule* (classical vs
  modern-technical secondary schools — Parsons's own note); *putting-out* (merchants
  supplying materials to home-working weavers and selling the cloth); *Fugger*
  (Augsburg banking family, Jacob Fugger d. 1525, the richest merchant of his time);
  *Franklin* (Benjamin Franklin, 1706–90, printer and American founder — the reader
  may know him; anchor anyway); *Baxter* (Richard Baxter, 1615–91, English Puritan
  pastor); *double-entry book-keeping* (each transaction entered twice, as debit and
  credit, so the books balance); *ideal type* (Weber's term: a deliberately sharpened
  model, not an average — needed in link 9e).
- **Numbers (L12):** keeper numbers are two: Baden's Protestants at about 37 per cent
  of the population and 48 per cent of the advanced pupils; and the mower's 2½ acres
  at 1 mark against 2 acres at 1.25. Everything else in the figures. Dates to the
  decade except 1517, 1647 and 1904/1920, where the reader needs the year.
- **Do not re-teach Strauss.** The fact–value distinction was taught in
  `strauss-natural-right-and-history`; recall it with a clause where Weber's "as we
  like to think" and his closing disclaimer call on it. Strauss's critique of the
  Protestant Ethic itself is new here.
- **Do not anticipate the module.** Landes's fragmentation, Murray's counts, Henrich's
  Church, Pomeranz's coal and Goody's Eurasia each get a forward clause at most. The
  Prussian literacy finding (Becker & Woessmann) and the city-growth finding
  (Cantoni) are this lesson's evidence for its verdict (links 9b, 10); the Augsburg
  1555 natural experiment is the Henrich lesson's to set out.
- **L8a:** Weber, Strauss, Franklin, Fugger, Luther, Calvin, Baxter, Wesley, Bunyan,
  Augustine, Marx, Machiavelli, Bacon, Hobbes, Carlyle are figures whose texts or
  lives are the material and may be named. Offenbacher's study is the source of the
  table: say "a study of Baden's statistics by one of Weber's students" in the body,
  Offenbacher in References. Giddens, Parsons (as translator), Tawney, Robertson,
  Samuelsson, Trevor-Roper, Marshall, Hamilton, Becker, Woessmann, Cantoni, Sombart,
  de Roover, Landes, Henrich, Pomeranz, Goody — References only in this lesson (the
  last four are named in the body only as the subjects of later lessons, in the
  forward clauses, or not at all). Pacioli's *Summa* and the Westminster Confession
  are named objects.
- **Period language:** Weber's notes contain judgments of nations and peoples
  (German Gemütlichkeit, Indian "traditionalism", Jewish "pariah-capitalism"); quote
  only what the lesson needs, attributed, and do not reproduce such characterisations
  in the course's voice.
- **No misconception slot** beyond the genuine one: the popular summary "Weber said
  Protestants are richer because their religion tells them to get rich" is a
  documented misreading — Weber says Puritanism condemned the pursuit of riches for
  enjoyment and that the effect was unintended. Use the refutation schema once, in
  link 7, with Baxter's "rich for God, though not for the flesh" as the

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Document reasoning (sourcing drill):** The heredity paragraph (Author's Intro,
   p. xlii). Metadata first: author, 1920, the introduction to a three-volume
   collection of comparative studies of religion, written in Germany for scholars.
   Prediction before reading: what would you expect a German professor in 1920 to say
   about heredity? Then records / argues / omits; closing question: what can the
   paragraph be USED to establish about Weber's thesis — and what can it not establish
   about heredity? Solution: it establishes a priority of method, not a finding in
   either direction.
2. **Derivation the lesson did not do (playbook-history rule 4):** From the Baden
   figures, compute each confession's share of advanced pupils divided by its share
   of population (Protestant 48/37, Catholic 42/61.3, Jewish 10/1.5), and the ratio
   of taxable capital per head. State what the ratios show, what the thesis predicts
   about them, which ratio the thesis does not explain (the Jewish one) and how Weber
   handles it (a different "pariah" capitalism, ch. 5 n. 58) — then say whether that
   move strengthens or weakens the inference from these figures. End with the
   uncertainty statement (one province, one year, no control for town residence).
3. **Boundary probe:** Scotland after 1560 — Calvinist doctrine, discipline and
   schools, and poverty until after 1707. Run the thesis on it twice: once with the
   ethic as a sufficient cause (it fails — say why), once with the ethic as one of
   "a combination of circumstances" (it survives — say what the other circumstances
   are). Then state the cost: what observation could now count against the thesis at
   all? Solution: only a case where the conditions were present, the ethic present,
   and the conduct absent — and name what that would require.
4. **Claim to refute:** "Weber showed that Protestants are richer because their
   religion taught them to seek wealth." Name the three steps that are wrong (the
   ethic condemned enjoyment of wealth; the effect was unintended; the claim is about
   a conduct in early modern groups, not about Protestants' incomes) and quote the
   sentence of Weber's that is the best evidence for each. Then say which modern
   finding gives the claim's first half a different explanation (literacy, the
   Prussian county data of link 9b).
5. **Synthesis (counter-argument stem, Strauss):** Someone claims, with Strauss, that
   the capitalist spirit came from Machiavelli, Bacon and Hobbes and only passed
   through Puritanism. First name the fact in Weber's own essay that best SUPPORTS
   the claim (his chosen specimen of the spirit, Franklin, is a document "free from
   all direct relationship to religion"). Then name two facts the claim must still
   account for (the mechanism Weber traces runs through pastors' manuals — Baxter, the
   Westminster divines — addressed to ordinary believers who read no philosophy; and
   New England, where on Weber's account the spirit was present among preachers and
   small tradesmen before any capitalist order) and state what would have to be true
   for the claim to stand (that the pastors' ethic was itself shaped by the new
   philosophy, or that being its "carrier" is all Puritanism did). Recall Hobbes's
   account of the passions in one clause (Ref `hobbes-state-of-nature-and-covenant`).
6. **Synthesis (E3, measures):** Weber's question sentence calls the West's forms
   "of universal significance and value" and adds "(as we like to think)". Using the
   three measures of greatness (Ref `what-measures-greatness`), say which measure the
   sentence uses, what the parenthesis concedes, and whether Weber's later iron-cage
   paragraph reverses the verdict on the same measure. Solution: "ideas others take
   up"; the parenthesis concedes the selection is a European valuation; the iron cage
   says the form spread and emptied — the same "failed because it succeeded" shape the
   course meets again at its end (no Ref needed beyond `what-measures-greatness`).
7. **Attack the verdict (claim to refute, aimed at the course):** The course
   concludes, on balance, that the Protestant ethic was not the main channel and that
   literacy was. State the strongest objection to that verdict in its best form (the
   duty to read scripture was itself part of the ethic; city size and county income
   are not the conduct Weber described), then say exactly what evidence would be
   needed to overturn the verdict. Solution: a design that separates Calvinist conduct
   from literacy — Calvinist against Lutheran populations of equal schooling, or
   low-literacy Protestant groups outperforming equally literate Catholic ones — with
   a measure of conduct (saving, hours worked, reinvestment) rather than growth alone;
   and the reason the Lutheran comparison bites (the anxiety of proof in link 6 is
   Calvinist).
8. **FORWARD (E7)** → `landes-european-exceptionalism`: Luther was condemned at the
   Diet of Worms in 1521 and survived under the protection of his own prince, the
   Elector of Saxony; Calvin's Geneva was a small independent city; the Dutch
   Calvinists won their own state in war against Spain. Write down what political
   fact all three cases share, and what would have happened to each movement in a
   single empire whose ruler could reach every province. The solution says in one
   sentence that Weber's ethic could only work where it was allowed to survive, that
   the next lesson makes Europe's division into competing states — rulers who could
   not stop what a neighbour tolerated — its central cause, with `<Ref>`.

# Research brief: what-conservatism-is (What conservatism is: disposition, doctrine or position)

status: planned
module: postwar-conservatism
type: core
goal: Distinguish the rival definitions of conservatism — a disposition, a positional ideology, an epistemic argument, a reaction — and test each against the thinkers the course has read

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** Eight modules of thinkers the course has called "the conservative, reactionary and anti-egalitarian line" without ever saying what makes them one line. The reader arrives with Burke's inheritance, prejudice and partnership of generations (`burke-reflections`), Maistre's Providence, "no such thing as man" and "the contrary of a revolution" (`maistre-and-bonald`), Hegel's rational state and ethical life (`hegel-ethical-life-and-the-state`), Tocqueville's equality of conditions as a providential fact (`tocqueville-equality-and-the-township`, `tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`), Carlyle's heroes and cash-nexus (`carlyle-heroes`), Schmitt's decision against discussion (`schmitt-political-theology`), and the radical right's turn from conserving to revolution — Sorel's myth, Jünger's Worker, Moeller's "Third Reich", Evola's Tradition — and its choice in 1933 (`moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich`, `the-right-in-1933`). Hume's "Of the Original Contract" is the reader's model of a sceptical, non-reactionary critic of contract (`hume-against-the-original-contract`). From this module's two opening lessons (the new prerequisite): fascism's defeat discredited the right's vocabulary — nation, people, hierarchy, authority — and the right's thinkers left by three exits (Christian democracy, liberal conservatism, emigration into philosophy); the charge of continuity, the "origins of fascism" readings of Maistre and Burke; and the right's reception of Nietzsche, concept `aristocratic-radicalism` (`the-right-after-1945`). And Jouvenel's history of Power, which grows by allying with the common man against every intermediate authority (`jouvenel-on-power`) — a conservative argument that is neither Burke's nor Maistre's and that the definitions below must also place. The question this lesson answers is the one `the-right-after-1945` forced: if a conservative must say which of the old words he still means, he must first say what makes him a conservative at all.
- **Contributes:** One move: take four candidate definitions — a disposition, a positional ideology, an epistemic argument, a reaction — and run each against the thinkers already read, keeping what survives. The result is not a definition but a map: a tradition with recurrent arguments (the three theses of reaction), recurrent assumptions, an epistemic core that has a self-application problem, and a boundary against reaction and radical conservatism that the rest of the module will test.
- **Hands off:** Whether conservatism needs a definition at all. If it is a disposition rather than a doctrine, then the attempt to state it as a doctrine is itself the error it diagnoses — Oakeshott's claim, and the next lesson (`oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`). The question whether the epistemic argument can ground anything (natural right? only prescription?) is Strauss's (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`), and that lesson also decides the self-application test raised here: the test sinks radical historicism, which offers "all thought is the product of its time" as a trans-historical truth, and leaves the epistemic argument narrowed to caution scaled to the size of a change; the question whether a "secondary ideology" is an ideology returns with Voegelin (`voegelin-gnosticism`); Kirk's six canons and his definition of conservatism as "the negation of ideology" are this module's American answer (`kirk-and-nisbet-american-conservatism`); Scruton's "dogmatics, not philosophy" comes two modules later, in the argument over equality (`scruton-meaning-of-conservatism`).
- **Spine step:** "After 1945 fascism has discredited the right, and it rebuilds in new registers: Jouvenel's history of Power, Oakeshott's disposition, Strauss's natural right, Voegelin's diagnosis of Gnosticism, Kirk's and Nisbet's American conservatism, Burnham's managers — tested on the Declaration of 1948"

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Start from the opening verbatim: twenty-three centuries of a tradition without a definition; four candidates; each includes thinkers the others exclude. The test set is fixed on the first page — Burke, Maistre, Hegel, Tocqueville, Carlyle — each recalled in one clause with a `<Ref>`. Every definition below is tried on all five.

1. **Is a conservative simply someone who defends what exists?** The obvious answer, and the one the word suggests. Attempt: conservatism as a natural attitude — habit, fear of change, Cecil's "innate conservatism". Test: it fits Burke in 1790 defending the 1688 settlement; it fails Maistre in 1796 attacking the existing French Republic, and Carlyle in 1843 attacking the existing laissez-faire England; it would make the party official defending the Soviet Union in 1990 the conservative of his day and Burke's heirs the radicals. Because: "what exists" has no direction. Answer and its "because": the word in its political sense is narrow and dated — it arose with and against the Revolution of 1789 (conservatism and Jacobinism are interdefined; a "revolution" in 1688 meant a return, in 1789 an overturning), and the name came later than the thing: Chateaubriand's journal Le Conservateur (1818), the Quarterly Review's renaming of the Tories (1830), Peel's Tamworth Manifesto (1834), Carlyle using the word in 1840 — and Burke made the tradition's founder only around 1885. So the first candidate is really two: a broad attitude every society has, and a narrow self-conscious position born of one event. The lesson keeps the narrow one.
2. **Then is it a doctrine — a list of things conservatives believe?** Attempt: the fixed list that the course has met repeatedly — human imperfection, the value of institutions ("restraints on men are among their rights"), custom and prejudice, anti-contractualism (the partnership of the dead, the living and the unborn), the social utility of religion, property, inequality, the nation. Test: Burke and Maistre tick most items and still fight over two — Maistre denies any contract at all where Burke rewrites it as a partnership, and Maistre defends religion as true where Burke defends it as useful. Hegel fails the list where it matters: his state is rational, grasped by philosophy, not merely inherited. Tocqueville fails on inequality: equality of conditions is for him a providential fact to be guided, not a decline to be resisted. Carlyle fails on institutions: he wants a hero, not a constitution. Why the attempt still yields something: the list is not a definition but a family resemblance, and one item on it does the organising work — the defence of institutions by their function and duration rather than by their truth. That is the line between conservatism and orthodoxy: a conservative can write a sentence Bentham could have signed (the best government yields the greatest happiness for the greatest number for the longest time — Maistre), and can say that political problems "do not primarily concern truth or falsehood" (Burke, against Aquinas's unjust-law-is-no-law, `aquinas-law-and-kingship`). Name this "historical utilitarianism": an institution's survival is a prima facie case that it serves a need; the burden of proof lies on the reformer. The standing objection, at full strength: this is admiration of "the bare fact of existence" (James Mill's phrase) — by this test the Inquisition and serfdom were good because they lasted. Reply: only a non-relativistic version escapes — one that can say a society held together by terror is not a functioning society at all; and Maistre's own "If you wish to conserve all, consecrate all" shows the trap, since consecration that is recommended for its usefulness is already not consecration. (This is the module's first encounter with the latent-vs-avowed-function dilemma: families that pray together in order to stay together may end up neither praying nor staying.)
3. **Then is it a position — the defence of whatever established order is under attack?** Attempt: the positional account (concept `positional-ideology`): conservatism has no content of its own; it articulates a defence only when institutions that had been taken for granted are challenged, and borrows its content from the attack (a mirror image of whatever progressives propose). Test: this explains what the fixed list could not — why Hegel in 1821, Disraeli in 1867 and Hayek in 1960 can all be claimed; why the targets shifted (religious enthusiasm, then revolution, then socialism and suffrage, then the welfare state); why the tradition moved "from substance to function" after mid-century, from defending aristocracy and Church to defending elites, culture and the state in the language of science. Where it fails: it cannot distinguish the conservative from the reactionary, or Burke from the Soviet official of 1990; and a position with no content has no direction — which is why Hayek, whose epistemology is Burke's, refused the label ("Why I am not a conservative": a position that only brakes cannot say where to go). Partial answer: the position has a characteristic grammar even if no content. Three forms of the conservative "no" recur across two centuries — the proposed change will make things worse (perversity), will change nothing (futility), will endanger something already won (jeopardy). State them as the course's own, with the retort formula ("It looks bad, indeed it is bad. But it can get much worse, for reasons you have overlooked"), and let the reader classify quotations the course has already met: Burke on the assignats (`assignats`), Tocqueville on centralisation, Carlyle on the cash-nexus, Bonald on assemblies.
4. **Then is it an argument — the one about knowledge?** Attempt: the epistemic argument (concept `epistemic-conservatism`): no individual or generation knows enough to redesign a society; inherited institutions carry more reason than any critic can see; prejudice is settled judgment with its reasons latent. Mark the distinction the newcomer will miss: this is a claim about what we can KNOW, not a denial that there is a right and wrong (not Nietzschean, not Cartesian scepticism; "prejudice" is unreasoning, not irrational). Test: it is Burke exactly (`burke-reflections`). It is only half Maistre — his thirteen theses say no constitution is made by deliberation, but his ground is Providence and the mystery of authority, not probability. It is not Hegel, who holds that reason can grasp the actual. It is Hayek — institutions "the result of human action but not of human design" — so the definition pulls in a self-declared liberal and pushes out two of the five. Then the sharp edge, the lesson's strongest objection, at full strength: the self-application problem. (a) The argument from unforeseen effects needs two probability steps — that a change will have unforeseen effects (granted) and that those effects are more likely bad than good; the second rests on no evidence about this measure, only on the general proposition "the unforeseen effects of any social change are more probably bad than good", for which there is no reason. (b) "There are no general prescriptions" is a general prescription; a plan to resist all planning belongs to the same style of politics. Where the lesson concedes: the argument cannot be stated as a doctrine without the contradiction. Where it replies: the conservative never claimed to forbid change, only revolution — prepared change is allowed (women's suffrage in 1918 after decades of partial enfranchisement, where the same measure in 1832 was not), and the asymmetry it relies on is not probabilistic but practical: destruction is fast and irreversible, growth slow ("caution in pulling down an edifice that has served for ages"). Add the second ground the epistemic argument hides: the paradox of conservative justice — if people have long lived by a practice and built their lives on it, reforming it in the name of justice is itself unjust to them ("changing the rules in the middle of the game"); so even a reformer who knows the better rule owes something to reliance on the worse one. This is a claim about justice, not knowledge, and it survives the self-application problem. State what the exchange shows about the argument, as a fact about it and not a verdict on conservatism: the reply answers (b) only by narrowing the claim, and the claim that survives is that the epistemic argument supports caution scaled to the size and irreversibility of a change, not a general presumption against change. Whether a self-application objection of this kind can be met at all is decided two lessons on, where the same test is put to historicism (`strauss-natural-right-and-history`).
5. **Conservative or reactionary — and what is the third thing?** Attempt: a conservative preserves and reforms; a reactionary restores. Test: Burke passes as conservative on his own words — "a state without the means of some change is without the means of its conservation"; history for him branches like a delta, with results no one can predict. Maistre is the hard case. The course has his sentence that the restoration will be "not a contrary revolution but the contrary of a revolution" (`maistre-and-bonald`) — he refuses the reversal. Yet the narrative he tells is the reactionary's template: a well-ordered Catholic Europe; alien ideas spread by men of letters; the betrayal of elites; a society rushing joyfully to destruction; a remnant who remember. State the thesis as the course's own: the reactionary is not a conservative but a radical of the other sign — the revolutionary hopes for a new order, the reactionary fears a new dark age, and both read history as a rupture at one date; "post hoc, propter hoc" is the reactionary's creed; nostalgia, unlike hope, cannot be disappointed. Then the dissent, also at full strength: Maistre has been called counter-revolutionary rather than reactionary precisely because he said the past cannot be re-made by will. Say what the texts settle: the two readings are about different parts of his work and both hold — his programme (restoration by Providence, not by a contrary revolution) is counter-revolutionary, his history (one rupture, alien ideas, betrayed elites, a remnant) is the reactionary's template; the dissent answers the charge against the programme and does not touch the one against the history. Carlyle forces a third category: he does not want to restore or preserve, he wants heroes to create. Name radical conservatism: the position that rejects both historical utilitarianism and humility and embraces will and myth ("too conservative not to be radical"; "conservative means creating things worth conserving"). Schmitt stands at its border (`schmitt-political-theology`); the Conservative Revolution of the 1920s is its twentieth-century form, which the reader has whole — Moeller's motto is his own (`moeller-van-den-bruck-third-reich`), the aristocratic radicalism of Nietzsche's heirs is recalled from `the-right-after-1945` — so this link only places it on the map, one clause per author. Fascism is not a point on this line but off it — the classification the reader met in `the-right-after-1945` and here only recalled: "conservative fascist" is a contradiction on this map, while right-wing authoritarianism sits at its edge; the course states the classification as a map's convention and recalls, in one clause with a `<Ref>`, the verdict that lesson reached on the continuity charge (on balance: it fails at the level of argument and holds at the level of words and persons); it does not re-run it.
6. **What survives, and what the tradition defends in common.** Answer: no one definition; four partial ones that each explain something — the narrow sense gives the date; the positional account gives the history; the epistemic argument gives the strongest philosophy and its weakest joint; the reaction boundary gives the enemies within. What all five thinkers share is an outlook rather than a creed: order is vertical, authority flows downward and is to be trusted, progress is doubted, equality is not the standard, politics can do little. Where they split: property (Burke's ballast vs a later conservatism that says it is "not capitalism" — forward to Scruton), religion (true for Maistre, useful for Burke, a political institution for Tocqueville), hierarchy (natural aristocracy vs Tocqueville's acceptance of levelling), the nation (Maistre's "no such thing as man" vs Burke's rights of Englishmen, which are a nation's rights too). Close on the fifth candidate the lesson has deliberately not tried: that conservatism is a disposition and not a doctrine at all, so that every definition above makes the Rationalist's mistake — the next lesson's claim.

Headings fall out as claims: "Defending what exists defines nothing: the word has a date"; "A list of beliefs is a family, not a definition"; "A position without content explains the history and nothing else"; "The epistemic argument and the sentence it cannot say about itself"; "Reactionaries are not conservatives"; "What the tradition shares: an outlook, not a creed".

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **Terms to anchor at first mention (L11):** "Jacobin" (the Paris club that ran the Revolution's radical phase, 1792–94 — the reader met the Terror in `burke-reflections`, recall it); "Tory/Whig" (the two English parties of 1679–81, the one for the Crown and Church, the other for Parliament and Dissent); "Tamworth Manifesto" (Peel's 1834 election address accepting the 1832 Reform Act — the reader has the Act from `british-franchise`); "orthodoxy" in Muller's sense (defending an institution because its doctrine is true); "laissez-faire" (recall from `carlyle-heroes`); "historicism" is NOT to be defined here — Strauss's lesson owns it; use "particularism" and say the general word comes two lessons on. "Fascism": a historically specific movement (Italy 1922, Germany 1933), named only to be placed off the spectrum.
- **L8a discipline, strictly:** Hamilton, Muller, Fawcett, Lilla, Berlin, Garrard, Huntington (the 1957 article's author), Hirschman, Sidgwick, Broad, Feinberg, Freeden, Cecil, Jones, Clark, Skorupski, Kekes, Beiser, Cohen, Tännsjö, Pipes — References only. State each thesis as the course's own: "the positional account", "the three theses of reaction", "the paradox of conservative justice", "the self-application problem", "the charge that a reactionary is a radical of the other sign". The thinkers whose texts are the material — Burke, Maistre, Hegel, Tocqueville, Carlyle, Hume, Hayek, Oakeshott, Schmitt — may be named. Huntington is awkward because he is also the author of the civilizations module's text: in this lesson his 1957 article is a scholar's thesis about conservatism, so the name stays in References and the concept is "the positional account" in prose.
- **Do not re-teach** Burke's prejudice, Maistre's thirteen theses, Hegel's §258, Tocqueville's providential fact, or Carlyle's heroes. Each is one clause plus a `<Ref>`; the lesson's work is the TEST, not the recall. A paragraph that re-explains the partnership of generations is a required fix.
- **The epistemic argument is not scepticism about values** (SEP 1.2): the newcomer will slide from "we cannot know enough to redesign society" to "there is no right answer". Keep the two apart in one sentence, since Strauss's lesson will accuse Burke of exactly that slide (ch. VI.B: "the seeds of historicism") — plant the sentence, do not run the argument.
- **Berlin vs Lilla on Maistre** is a real disagreement in the dossier: Berlin VI p. 137 "counter-revolutionary, not reactionary"; Lilla makes Maistre the type of the reactionary. Present both as readings; the reader has the primary text (Considerations ch. 10) to judge. Do not settle it.
- **Fawcett's politics:** he writes as a left-liberal who wants a "liberal conservatism"; his verdict that the hard right is "a chimera of populists and libertarians" is not this lesson's business. Take from him only the outlook table (vertical order), the four questions, the label history and the dilemmas — all of which conservatives themselves accept.
- **Muller's Introduction is from the 1997 edition** in the staged file; nothing about post-1997 is to be attributed to it.
- **No labs; no numbers** beyond dates — the one figure that must stay bound is "women's suffrage 1918 (from age 30), 1928 (equal)" if used, with "after 1832, 1867, 1884 had enfranchised men by stages" in the same sentence (the reader has `british-franchise`).
- **Dossier thinness:** Huntington 1957 and Hirschman 1991 are not in the dossier; their theses are reconstructed from Muller's and Fawcett's statements of them. Say so in References ("as summarised in …").
- **Register:** The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.
- **Do not re-run `the-right-after-1945`:** that lesson owns the anti-fascist consensus, the three exits and the continuity charge; this lesson uses them only as the reason a definition is needed (one paragraph at the start of link 1, with a `<Ref>`), and Jouvenel (`jouvenel-on-power`) is placed on the map in one sentence at link 3 (his argument fits the epistemic and positional accounts poorly — a liberal's fear of Power written from the right) — not re-taught.
- **Do not moralise** about any of the five. The "organised selfishness" charge (SEP 3.3) is given its strongest form — Kant's "as they are ought to read as we have made them by unjust coercion" — and answered by the non-relativistic reply, not dismissed.

## EXERCISE PLAN (E6/E7 + playbook-history mix)

1. **Document reasoning (classification; the one warm-up):** Sort four sentences the body did not sort — Bonald on assemblies, Tocqueville on centralised administration (`tocqueville-tyranny-of-majority-and-soft-despotism`), Carlyle on cash payment (`carlyle-heroes`), Adorno's "fully enlightened earth" — into perversity, futility, jeopardy, and say for each which word in the sentence decided it.
2. **Boundary probe:** A society whose established order is itself a revolutionary regime (the USSR in 1990). What does each of the four definitions say the "conservative" is there — the party official or the dissident? Which definition degenerates (the positional one), which survives (the non-relativistic epistemic one, if it can say the order never functioned as a society), and what premise did the surviving one have to add?
3. **Claim to refute:** "Burke opposed the Revolution, therefore Burke was a reactionary." Name the failing step with his own sentences: the means of change as the means of conservation; 1688 as "a revolution not made but prevented" (`burke-reflections`); the delta against the river. Then say what WOULD make a critic of 1789 a reactionary (the rupture-and-restoration story), and test Maistre's ch. 10 against it.
4. **Derivation the lesson skipped:** Run Broad's objection against Burke's prejudice passage ("the general bank and capital of nations and of ages") and construct the best reply that does not use probability — the asymmetry of destruction and growth, and the reliance argument — then state what each reply gives up (the first no longer forbids slow radical change; the second is a claim about justice, not knowledge).
5. **Sourcing drill:** Maistre's Benthamite sentence (Considerations, as excerpted in the anthology's Introduction): author, date (1796–97, in exile), audience (French royalists and the Directory's France), what it records, argues, omits; and what it can be USED to establish — that a counter-revolutionary could argue by consequences, and therefore that "conservatism is not orthodoxy" is not a modern gloss but visible in 1797.
6. **Synthesis (claim–evidence–reasoning):** Apply the paradox of conservative justice to the buyers of confiscated Church land in 1796 (`assignats`, `maistre-and-bonald` ch. 10): Maistre says the restored monarchy must not dispossess them. Is that the paradox at work, or historical utilitarianism, or mere prudence? Argue with the text and say which definition of conservatism the passage supports.
7. **Counter-argument stem:** "Someone claims conservatism is organised selfishness: 'as they are' means 'as we have made them by unjust coercion'." Name two facts the claim fails to account for (Burke's prosecution of Hastings — flagged forward to `burke-against-hastings`; the epistemic argument's indifference to who benefits) and state what would have to be true for the claim to survive (that every inherited institution's latent function is the protection of its beneficiaries).
8. **FORWARD (E7) → `oakeshott-rationalism-in-politics`:** Take the two sentences "there are no general prescriptions" and "a plan to resist all planning belongs to the same style of politics". Try to write the epistemic argument as a one-paragraph doctrine that does not fall under either. The solution shows it cannot be done as a doctrine and says in one sentence that the next lesson's answer is to deny it is a doctrine — a disposition, with knowledge that exists only in use.

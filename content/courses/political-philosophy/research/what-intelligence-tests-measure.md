# Research brief: what-intelligence-tests-measure (What intelligence tests measure: g, predictive validity and measurement invariance)

status: planned
module: heredity-and-civilization
type: core
goal: Evaluate the claim that intelligence tests measure one general ability that predicts outcomes outside the test and is measured the same way in different groups, against the evidence from factor structure, predictive validity and measurement invariance

Register rule (binding on the lesson): The course judges arguments, not people: no condemning or approving labels for any author in its own voice; a label may appear only inside a quotation, with who applied it and when. It does pass verdicts on arguments: where the lesson stages a dispute it has the evidence to settle, and in every module's closing lesson, it says which side wins, how firmly (established / strong / on balance / open) and why, as recorded in research/verdicts/. "Open" is a finding, stated with what would settle it, never a way to avoid one. The same tests apply to the left and the right.

The lesson tests premise 1 of the module's argument (the tests measure a real general ability that
matters) and the measurement half of premise 3 (the test measures the same thing in both groups). Each
of its three questions is answered the way the evidence answers it — and the evidence answers two of
them largely in the tests' favour in the main US data. That is stated plainly, with its limits stated
just as plainly; neither "the tests are biased" nor "invariance shows the gap is genetic" may survive
the lesson. Group labels (Black, White, Hispanic; Murray's "African", "European", "Latin") are the
sources' own census or self-identification categories, said once to be the authors' categories; the
course adds none. No gap sizes: the lesson says a gap exists and that its size and trend are measured
in `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment` (forward `<Ref>`), and gives no number for it.

## ARC (L10)

- **Inherits:** From `the-borrowing-ledger` (the previous module's close): its FORWARD exercise ends on
  "what kind of evidence an explanation by inherited differences would have to bring — a measure of the
  populations, not of their techniques". This lesson asks whether such a measure exists and what it is
  worth. From `the-cognitive-elite`: the NLSY and the AFQT; the positive correlations among mental
  tests and the general factor extracted from them (concept `g-factor`, with the authors' own hedge
  that its reality "was, and remains, arguable"); heritability as a population ratio (`heritability`
  — used here only in one clause, it is the next lesson's); the Part II finding that the test score
  predicts adult outcomes more steeply than the parental-background index among white NLSY members
  (11% vs 26% poverty — recalled, not re-worked); `cognitive-stratification`. That lesson promised
  that whether g is a real property or a statistical summary is contested and would be developed
  later: this lesson is where it is developed (the brief of `the-cognitive-elite` still points that
  promise at `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`; see the final report).
- **Contributes:** The one move: before anyone asks why average scores differ between groups, put the
  measure itself to three tests that could each have failed — (1) is there one ability or a bundle?
  (the hierarchical model; the reification objection in its psychometric form — the nonsense matrix
  and rotation — and the reply that a general factor returns one level up; what is common ground and
  what is open); (2) does the score predict anything outside the test room, and how much does a
  correlation of about .5 leave unexplained? (concept `predictive-validity`, with predictive bias as
  its group form); (3) does the test measure the same thing in two groups? (concept
  `measurement-invariance`, drawn for a newcomer: same pattern, same loadings, same intercepts, same
  residuals). Concepts introduced: `predictive-validity`, `measurement-invariance`, `reification`.
  Reinforced: `g-factor`, `cognitive-stratification`.
- **Hands off:** To `heritability-within-and-between-groups`: a measure that predicts and is
  invariant across groups shows that the groups differ on the same latent ability — and nothing about
  why; the next lesson asks what twin and DNA studies say about the causes of differences within
  groups, and whether that reaches the difference between them. The reification objection is
  recalled as a political charge (one number, a linear ranking of persons) in
  `gould-and-harden-on-heredity`; Spearman's hypothesis (that the gap is largest on the most
  g-loaded tests) is `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`'s; cohort non-invariance of the Flynn gains is
  `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`'s. One sentence in the `<Conclusion>` says the next lesson takes
  up heritability. What this lesson decides: the two charges against the measure (that the tests are
  biased against the lower-scoring group, and that they measure nothing outside the test room) both
  fail in the main US data (**strong**); what passes on is the cause of the measured difference, which
  this lesson does not touch, and whether g is one ability or abilities that reinforce one another,
  which stays **open**.
- **Spine step:** "Heredity as an explanation of the gaps between peoples, tested premise by premise — what the tests measure, what heritability can and cannot imply, the hereditarian case at full strength, then the US gap, adoption, admixture and polygenic scores on the step from a real gap to a genetic one — then what follows politically whatever the answer, what ancient DNA shows, the rival explanations from geography and institutions, and the key datasets audited"

## VERDICT

- **Claim judged:** the two charges that would end the dispute before it starts: (i) the tests are
  biased against Black Americans, so the measured gap is an artefact of the instrument; (ii) the score
  is a reified number that measures nothing outside the test room.
- **Verdict:** both charges **fail in the main US data**. Grade: **strong**. A sub-point stays
  **open**: whether g is one ability or several abilities that reinforce one another (the
  mutualism reading); what would settle it is a design that separates a common cause from mutual
  reinforcement during development, which the course does not hold.
- **What decides it** (dossier §2, research/heredity-evidence-review.md; sources as listed below):
  predictive validity is about .5 (Neisser et al. 1996), and the regression lines show no
  under-prediction for the lower-scoring group; if anything the common line over-predicts Black
  performance (Neisser l.~1018–1035; Bell Curve Appendix 5 and note 11). Measurement invariance is
  tenable in the main US datasets, strict in the Philadelphia cohort and NLSY97 (Lasker, Hu and the
  other studies the dossier lists). On reification: oblique rotation removes the first factor only to
  bring a general factor back one level up, which Gould concedes (Mismeasure, Afterword).
- **Strongest objection:** invariance has failed where it was tested hard: in a referred
  special-education sample a threshold was missed (Graves, ΔCFI .011), and Dolan's Dutch and South
  African analyses failed outright; and a criterion such as supervisors' ratings may carry the same
  bias as the test, so "no under-prediction" could be bias on both sides. It does not overturn the
  verdict. The failures come from a referred sample, other countries and second-language testing,
  not from representative US samples, and the South African case shows what a real failure looks
  like (broken intercepts), which the US data do not show. Criterion bias of the uniform kind
  (Einstein's traveller, Bell Curve l.3034–3038) would have to depress every test and every
  criterion (school, college, training, jobs) by the same amount; and a cause that did that would be
  a cause acting on the ability, which is the next lessons' question, not a defect of the instrument.
- **What would overturn it:** a representative US sample in which invariance fails, or in which the
  tests under-predict the lower-scoring group's outcomes.
- **What the verdict does not reach:** why the groups differ on the measured ability. Invariance is
  equally consistent with environmental and genetic causes acting on the ability (step 6).

## EXPLANATION PLAN (L0)

Opening (course.yaml, verbatim): two children sit the same test of vocabulary, number series and
picture puzzles; one scores 85, the other 115, and a school, an army or an employer may act on the
difference; before anyone can ask why average scores differ between groups, three narrower questions:
one ability or a bundle; does it predict anything outside the test room; does it measure the same
thing in a child from one group as in a child from another — each could have come out the other way.

The newcomer's problem, from the previous module (one plain sentence with a `<Ref>` to
`the-borrowing-ledger`): an explanation of the gaps between peoples by inherited differences needs a
measure of the people themselves. Intelligence tests are the measure everyone in the dispute uses. A
measure that fails any of the three tests would end the argument before it started; one that passes
all three only moves it to the next question. No anchor events beyond the 1904 paper and the 1930s
debate; the APA task force (1995–96, set up after the reception of a 1994 book — Ref
`the-cognitive-elite`) is anchored at first mention.

1. **One ability or a bundle: what the correlations show.** Problem: a test that adds up vocabulary,
   arithmetic and puzzle scores could be adding up unrelated skills, the way a "fitness score" could
   add sprinting to chess. Obvious attempt: look at each subtest separately and give up the total.
   Why it fails as a description: the subtests are not unrelated — people who do well on one tend to
   do well on all (recall, one clause and `<Ref>` to `the-cognitive-elite`: the positive correlations
   and Spearman's common factor; do not re-teach). What is new here: the correlations are not all
   equal. Verbal tests correlate more with each other than with spatial ones, memory tests with memory
   tests. The arrangement that fits the pattern is a hierarchy: dozens of narrow tests, grouped into a
   handful of broad abilities (verbal comprehension, fluid reasoning, working memory, processing
   speed, visual-spatial), and the broad abilities themselves correlated, which is what a general
   factor at the top summarises. KEEPER figure 1. State the APA task force's caution exactly: the
   hierarchy "is consistent with, but does not prove" a common cause, since abilities may correlate
   "for other reasons, such as the effects of education" (Neisser l.~290–297). Bearing: the total
   score is not an arbitrary sum; whether it is the measure of one thing is the next question.
2. **Is g a thing? The reification objection in its psychometric form.** Problem: a statistical
   summary can be computed for anything; is the general factor a summary or a cause in the head?
   Define `reification` here: treating an abstraction computed from data as a real object with causal
   power. Gould's case in three steps, each drawn (FigureSequence, figure 2), each at full strength:
   (a) *Any table has a first component.* The first principal component (one sentence: the single
   axis that captures as much of the shared variation among the scores as one axis can) exists for
   any correlation matrix — including one built from a man's age, the population of Mexico and the
   price of Swiss cheese, which all rose together (Gould l.12060). So its existence proves nothing
   about minds. (b) *Rotation.* Four tests, two verbal and two arithmetic, all positively correlated.
   The first component runs up the middle between the two clusters; rotate the axes onto the clusters
   and the general factor disappears, replaced by "verbal" and "arithmetic", with no information lost
   (l.12189–12195). The data alone do not choose. (c) *Two causal stories fit.* A child with good
   schooling, enough food and books at home will do better on every test; inherited acuity predicts
   the same pattern. Answer drawn: treating g as an organ of the mind is a choice the mathematics does
   not force.
   **Reply at full strength.** Gould's rotated axes are forced to be perpendicular, i.e. uncorrelated.
   Let them correlate — place them inside the clusters, which fits better — and the two broad factors
   correlate positively, because every test correlates positively with every other. Factor that
   correlation and a general factor returns one level up: "You can make g hide, but you can't make it
   go away" (Afterword l.5276). Gould concedes the geometry: the oblique axes "will be positively
   correlated", and "a second-order general factor must therefore exist" (l.14715–14718); Thurstone,
   the inventor of rotation, came to accept it. Then the reply's second step: a construct is judged
   by what it predicts, as "energy" is (l.5280); if g were noise it would predict nothing, if it were a
   stand-in for schooling or family background then schooling or background would predict as well —
   the next section tests exactly that.
   **Where it stands — common ground and the open part.** Both sides grant that the positive
   correlations are real and that a general factor can be extracted at some level. They disagree on
   two things, and the lesson keeps them apart. (i) *Size*: on the hierarchical reading the general
   factor carries more variance than all the broad factors together (Afterword l.5278, "three times");
   on Gould's reading second-order g is "a small percentage" (l.~14768). Part of the disagreement is
   which quantity is reported — a first principal component, or what is left to a higher-order factor
   after the broad factors take their share; the worked `<Example>` (set piece 2) shows the reader both
   numbers from one small matrix. (ii) *Cause*: a general factor fits a single underlying capacity; it
   also fits abilities that start separate and reinforce one another over a childhood, and test items
   that each draw on several independent abilities (Nisbett 2012 l.~1395–1410; References). A pattern
   of correlations cannot by itself tell these apart; evidence from development, the brain or genetics
   would have to. Bearing: premise 1's word "general" survives as a description of the correlations;
   "one ability in the head" is a hypothesis the correlations do not settle. A forward clause: the
   same objection returns as a political charge — the ranking of persons by one number — in
   `gould-and-harden-on-heredity` (`<Ref>`); it is not argued here.
3. **Does the score predict anything outside the test room?** Problem: a test can be internally tidy
   and still useless; the opening's school, army and employer want to know what the score says about
   later performance. Define `predictive-validity`: the correlation between a test score and a later
   measure of performance outside the test (grades, years of schooling, training success, job ratings).
   The evidence, each with its frame: grades about .5; years of schooling completed about .55; job
   performance .3–.5 as observed, about .5 when corrected for unreliable ratings and for the narrowed
   range of people who were hired; military training about .6 across hundreds of courses; the job
   interview, for comparison, about .14 (Neisser l.~310–390; Bell Curve l.1102–1170). Range
   restriction as a derived step, not a term dropped in: among people already hired the spread of
   ability is narrow, so the correlation inside a firm understates what the test does across
   applicants — the NFL tackles, all heavy, among whom weight hardly predicts performance (l.1080).
   The correction is disputed: a National Academy panel, making none, put the GATB's validity at about
   .25 against .45 with it (l.1118). Both numbers stated; the reader sees that the dispute is about
   the correction, not the data.
   Then **what .5 leaves unexplained** — the worked `<Example>`: r = .5 means r² = .25, a quarter of
   the variation in grades lined up with the score and three-quarters not; the spread of grades among
   pupils with the same score is still √(1 − .25) ≈ .87 of the whole spread. Among pupils one standard
   deviation above average on the test, the predicted grade is half a standard deviation above
   average, and about 28% of them are below average in grades (Φ(−.5/.87)). The same scale of
   exception holds for status (88%, Neisser l.~370). Then the test of the "surrogate" reading from
   section 2: holding parental SES constant removes only about a quarter of the score's predictive
   power for adult status, and between two brothers raised in one home the one with the higher
   adolescent score tends to reach the higher status (Neisser l.~354–366; recall the NLSY
   poverty comparison by `<Ref>` to `the-cognitive-elite`, one clause). The critic's reply at full
   strength: a regression coefficient for the score does not isolate a cause — parents' schooling,
   parents' ability, the child's schooling and the score are tangled, and the brother with the higher
   score also got more schooling (Nisbett 2009 ch. 1; the task force's "substantially mediated by
   education"). Bearing: the score predicts, moderately for individuals and strongly for group rates;
   premise 1's "matters" holds in that sense and no stronger one.
4. **Test bias in the predictive sense.** Problem: a gap exists between the average scores of US Black
   and White test-takers (stated without a number; its size and trend are measured in
   `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`, forward `<Ref>`). Is the gap a fault of the test? Obvious
   attempt: a test on which groups differ is biased by that fact. Why it fails: by that definition a
   ruler is biased against children. The task force's distinction (Neisser l.~1018–1040): "outcome
   bias" — any difference in results, like a coin that comes up heads — against "predictive bias",
   which asks whether the test predicts the performance of members of each group equally well. The
   definition, derived from what the test is used for: draw the regression line of later performance
   on score for each group (figure 4). If at the same score one group later does better than the
   line drawn for everyone, the test under-predicts that group — it is biased against it in the
   sense that matters to a school or employer; a flatter line for one group means the test predicts
   that group less accurately. The finding: in large representative samples, tests do not
   under-predict Black performance in school, college, training or jobs; where the lines differ, the
   common line slightly over-predicts (Coleman's survey .26 SD; SAT over-predicted freshman grades in
   fourteen of fifteen studies; GATB white intercept higher in 60 of 72 studies — Bell Curve
   l.6160–6166; the task force; Murray 2021 l.638, 841; dossier §2: "Murray 2021 ch. 3 [full] and
   Neisser et al. 1996 [full] agree tests do not under-predict Black performance").
   **What over-prediction does and does not mean** — the step most readers get wrong, in both
   directions. It does not mean the tests favour Black test-takers, though the Bell Curve's Appendix 5
   reads it that way ("biased in favor of blacks", l.6160). An unbiased but imperfect predictor of a
   real difference produces it automatically: if two groups differ in the ability a test measures and
   the criterion also depends on that ability, the within-group lines have the same slope (less than
   one), and the lower group's line sits lower; a single line drawn through both over-predicts the
   lower group (Bell Curve note 11, l.8682 — the authors' own statement of the mechanism). Nor does
   the absence of under-prediction prove a test is fair in every sense: if whatever depresses a
   group's scores depresses its grades and job ratings equally, no comparison of the two would reveal
   it — the authors' "uniform background bias" and Einstein's traveller (l.3034–3038), which they
   grant "might, in theory, be true" and argue is implausible because it would have to distort every
   test and every criterion by the same amount. Bearing: the predictive-bias question has a clear
   answer in the main US data — no under-prediction — and that answer is about the tests' use, not
   about what causes the gap.
5. **Does the test measure the same thing in both groups?** Problem: predictive bias compares the
   score with something outside the test; it cannot tell whether the score means the same thing inside
   it. A vocabulary subtest given to children taught in another language measures their English, not
   their verbal reasoning. How would one find out from the scores alone? Obvious attempt: compare
   item difficulties — if the same items are hard for both groups, the test is the same. That is a
   first check (items whose gap stands far out from the rest are flagged and removed in test
   construction — Murray note 14 l.2076; "the order of difficulty is virtually the same" — Bell Curve
   l.3014), but it tests items one by one, not the structure that turns subtests into ability scores.
   The answer: fit one factor model to both groups and see what has to be allowed to differ. Define
   `measurement-invariance` and draw it as a ladder (FigureSequence, figure 5), each rung with what it
   checks and what failing it would mean:
   - *configural*: the same subtests cluster under the same broad abilities in both groups (the same
     picture);
   - *metric* (same loadings): a one-point difference in the ability shows up as the same difference
     on each subtest in both groups (the arrows are equally strong);
   - *scalar* (same intercepts): two people with the same ability, one from each group, are expected
     to score the same on each subtest — the condition under which a difference in subtest means can
     be read as a difference in the ability. A subtest that is harder for one group at equal ability
     (the language example) breaks this rung;
   - *strict* (same residual variances): the part of each subtest the abilities do not explain is
     equally large in both groups.
   The method is multiple-group confirmatory factor analysis; each rung adds equality constraints and
   asks whether the fit worsens beyond a conventional threshold. Then the evidence, graded (dossier §2;
   figure 6): in the main US datasets, invariance between Black and White samples is tenable — WISC-R
   and K-ABC children [secondary], the Philadelphia cohort with strict invariance [full], NLSY97 strict
   [abstract], Woodcock-Johnson IV [abstract]. It is not automatic: in Dutch railway-job applicants
   (native Dutch against first-generation immigrants) and South African secondary pupils (White, Black,
   Indian) invariance "must be rejected" — tested, failed, with the language of the test the likeliest
   reason for the Black South African pupils. And in 1,184 children referred for special education in
   one US urban district, constraining the loadings worsened fit by .011 against a .01 cutoff: two new
   WISC-V subtests (Figure Weights, Picture Span) loaded differently by race, so only partial metric
   invariance held, and the higher rungs were not tested — a referred, not representative, sample.
   One forward clause: tested across birth cohorts rather than groups, the rise in scores over the
   twentieth century fails invariance (`us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`, `<Ref>`; no detail here).
   State the Status verbatim in substance (Pitfalls). Bearing: in the main US data, the measurement
   half of premise 3 holds — the groups differ on the same latent abilities, measured the same way.
6. **The weighing: the tests are not biased against the lower-scoring group, and they do not say
   why it scores lower.** (Heading as a claim.) Set the two charges, at full strength, against the
   evidence of steps 3–5 and against the strongest objection (VERDICT): the referred sample, the
   Dutch and South African failures, criterion bias. Reach the verdict in the course's voice: both
   charges fail in the main US data, **strong**; g as one ability or a mutualism, **open**. Then the
   limit of the verdict. Problem: if the test measures the same ability in
   the same way in both groups, has the cause of the difference been narrowed? It has, a little, and
   only in one direction. What invariance rules out: explanations that work through particular
   subtests or items — an unfamiliar language, culturally loaded vocabulary — because those break the
   intercepts (Dolan's South African case is what such a failure looks like). What it leaves open: any
   cause that acts on the ability itself. Make the reader see it with the newcomer's case: a factor
   that lowers a child's reasoning ability — poor schooling, illness, a home without books — lowers
   every subtest through the factor, exactly as an inherited difference would, and leaves all four
   rungs intact. Invariance then says the groups really differ in the measured ability; it says
   nothing about why. The hereditarian side's stronger reading at full strength (Warne l.~293–322):
   since the same model holds in both groups, the causes of the difference between them must act like
   the causes of differences within them — and within groups those causes are partly genetic. His own
   limit in the same section: invariance "does not conclusively prove" a genetic share, because a large
   enough environmental difference could produce the same picture. The objection: within-group causes
   and between-group causes acting through the same latent variable can still be different causes
   (dossier §2: Schraiber & Edge, Giangrande & Turkheimer reject the inference). The lesson stops
   there: whether the causes of the difference between groups are of the same kind as those within
   them is the hidden premise the next lesson lays bare (`heritability-within-and-between-groups`,
   forward `<Ref>`). Bearing, and the lesson's answer to its three questions: the scores reflect a
   hierarchy of correlated abilities with a general factor at the top, whose nature is contested; they
   predict outcomes moderately for persons and strongly for rates, without under-predicting Black
   performance; and in the main US datasets they measure the same abilities in the same way in Black
   and White samples. Each result could have gone the other way, and in some places it did.

Headings as claims (suggested): "The correlations fall into a hierarchy"; "Is g a thing? Rotation and
its answer"; "What the score predicts, and what it leaves out"; "Bias in the sense that matters to a
user of the test"; "Same test, same ability? Four rungs of invariance"; "The tests are not biased, and
they cannot say why the groups differ".

`<Conclusion>`: the verdict in plain words: the charge that the tests are biased against Black
Americans, and the charge that they measure nothing outside the test room, both fail in the main US
data, and the course holds this as **strong**; whether g is one ability or several that reinforce one
another is **open**. Then what the reader can now do (read a validity coefficient, a pair of regression
lines, and an invariance result, and say what each does and does not show about a group difference);
premise 1 stands in its descriptive form, premise 3's measurement half stands in the main US data; the
cause of the difference is untouched; the next lesson asks what heritability can say about it.

## Set pieces and worked examples to recreate

1. **The task force's caution** (Neisser l.~290–297): the hierarchy "is consistent with, but does not
   prove" a common cause — one sentence, quoted, as the frame for section 2. A primary statement of a
   public body, quotable as such.
2. **Rotation by hand** (Gould Figs. 6.6, 6.7, 6.11; Afterword l.5276): a four-test worked `<Example>`
   with a stated correlation matrix the course constructs — two verbal tests correlating .64, two
   arithmetic tests .64, every cross-pair .36. (a) First principal component: equal loadings of about
   .77 on all four (eigenvalue 1 + .64 + 2(.36) = 2.36, 59% of total variance — Gould's "50 to 60
   percent" reproduced; loading √(2.36/4)); second component bipolar. (b) Orthogonal rotation onto the clusters: two factors, g
   gone, the same total variance explained. (c) Oblique solution: each test loads .80 on its own
   factor, the two factors correlate .36/.64 = .5625; factoring that correlation gives each broad
   factor a loading of .75 on a general factor; each test's variance then splits as .36 general,
   .28 broad, .36 unique — so the general factor carries more than the broad factors together (the
   Afterword's claim), yet is "second-order" (Gould's term). The writer checks the arithmetic; the
   point is that both sides' numbers come from one matrix.
3. **The nonsense matrix** (Gould l.12060) — quote the list in a clause; the reply in one sentence
   (a nonsense first component does not reappear when a different set of variables is measured on the
   same objects, while g from different test batteries does — only if the Johnson et al. 2004 result
   is verified; otherwise state the reply as "judged by prediction", Afterword l.5280).
4. **What .5 leaves unexplained** (Neisser l.~252–256, l.~370): r² = .25; √.75 ≈ .87; 28% of the
   top-sixth-on-the-test below average in grades. The lesson's numeric `<Example>`; KEEPER number.
5. **Hartigan vs Hunter** (Bell Curve l.1118): .25 vs .45 for the same job tests — the range-restriction
   correction as the whole difference. One sentence each; no names in the body ("a National Academy
   of Sciences panel" is a public body and may be named).
6. **Two senses of bias** (Neisser l.~1018–1035): the coin that comes up heads — quoted in one
   sentence as the task force's own image.
7. **The three regression-line pictures** (Bell Curve Appendix 5 l.6142–6152): common line;
   higher intercept for one group (under-prediction); flatter slope. Redrawn, not copied.
8. **Over-prediction derived** (Bell Curve note 11, l.8682): a worked `<Example>` with stated numbers —
   two groups half a standard deviation apart in the ability, a test and a criterion each correlating
   .7 with the ability (no bias of any kind built in). Each group then differs by .35 SD on the test
   and by .35 SD on the criterion; within each group the line of criterion on test has slope .49; so
   the lower group's own line sits .35 × (1 − .49) ≈ .18 SD below the higher group's at every score,
   and a line fitted to a pool made up mostly of the higher group over-predicts the lower group by up
   to about .18 SD (about half that if the two groups are equal in size). The writer re-derives the
   figures; the conclusion — over-prediction is what an unbiased, imperfect test of a real difference
   produces — is the point, not the number.
9. **Einstein's traveller** (Bell Curve l.3034–3038): the authors' own statement of the bias no
   comparison could detect, and their argument that it is implausible — quote one sentence.
10. **The ΔCFI of .011** (Graves l.~218–227): the referred-sample result turns on a threshold missed by
    a thousandth — the boundary-probe exercise.
11. **Dolan's two failures** (Dolan et al. 2004 pp. 168–171): the test that failed for pupils tested in
    a language not their own, and the authors' remark that a negative result does not generalise —
    the corroboration exercise's anchor.

## Pitfalls / subtleties to flag

- **DOSSIER CONSTRAINTS.** Status lines the lesson must state, in substance:
  - §2: "the gap is real on the tests; in the main US datasets the tests measure the same latent
    abilities in both groups (moderate–strong evidence; partial non-invariance in one referred urban
    sample)." The lesson gives the first clause without a size.
  - §2, "What invariance means": "the test measures the same abilities in the same way; it says nothing
    about why the latent means differ."
  - §2: Dolan, Roorda & Wicherts 2004 — invariance "must be rejected" in the Dutch and South African
    data: "it has to be tested each time".
  - §2: "Murray 2021 ch. 3 [full] and Neisser et al. 1996 [full] agree tests do not under-predict
    Black performance; Neisser: the differential is 'apparently not due to any simple form of bias in
    the content or administration of the tests'."
  "Claims the lessons must NOT make" that bear on this lesson — both directions:
  - hereditarian direction: "'Invariance / Spearman's hypothesis shows the gap is genetic.'" The lesson
    states Warne's stronger reading as his, with his own limit and the objection, and draws no causal
    conclusion from invariance.
  - environmental direction: "'Test bias explains the gap' — main US datasets are invariant and do not
    under-predict." The lesson states this finding plainly and does not soften it with "critics say
    tests are biased" unless the critics' specific argument (uniform background bias; partial
    non-invariance in the referred sample) is named and weighed.
  - also: "'Science has shown the gap is entirely environmental' / 'genes play no role'" and "'The gap
    is X% genetic'" — neither is this lesson's subject; nothing in it may imply either.
- **Reading tags carried into the lesson:** Dolan 2000 / Dolan & Hamaker 2001 [secondary, via Wicherts
  2004]; Hu 2019 and Hajovsky & Chesnut 2022 [abstract]; Lasker 2019, Graves 2021, Neisser 1996, Murray
  2021 ch. 3, Warne 2021, Dolan 2004 [full]. No number from an [abstract] source beyond what the
  abstract says; no number from any [not read] source.
- **Where a staged text goes beyond the dossier:** (1) Murray 2021 note 14 (l.2078): "All the current
  versions of the major tests are known to be factor invariant." The dossier records partial
  non-invariance of two WISC-V subtests in a referred sample (Graves) and rejections in non-US data
  (Dolan 2004). Give both: Murray's sentence as his, and the two results it does not cover; the
  dossier's "moderate–strong" is the lesson's own grade. (2) Bell Curve l.6160 reads over-prediction
  as tests "biased in favor of blacks"; note 11 of the same book gives the mechanism that makes
  over-prediction expected without any bias — the lesson follows the mechanism, not the gloss. (3)
  Dolan 2004's group details (railway applicants; the South African White/Black/Indian pupils;
  language of the test) come from the paper fetched for this brief, not from the dossier — check them
  in the paper before writing. (4) Gould vs the Afterword on the size of g ("small percentage" vs
  "three times all other factors") — neither number is in the dossier; both are each author's claim,
  and set piece 2 shows why both can be computed from one matrix.
- **No gap sizes** — not the 14.72 points of the Philadelphia cohort, not the task force's "one
  standard deviation", not WISC-V norms. The WISC-V supplement holds no group norm table (dossier,
  "URLs and files read"); WISC-V numbers belong to `us-gap-size-trend-and-environment`. Say "a gap
  exists" and Ref forward.
- **Spearman's hypothesis is held** for `hereditarian-case-iq-and-nations`: no "the gap is largest on
  the most g-loaded tests", no Bell Curve l.3016 "wider on items that appear culturally neutral", no
  method of correlated vectors. Dolan 2004's title names it; the lesson uses only its invariance result.
- **Do not re-teach g or heritability.** The positive manifold and Spearman's factor are a clause and a
  `<Ref>` to `the-cognitive-elite`; heritability is not this lesson's at all beyond one clause in
  section 6's forward step.
- **Reification is psychometric here, political in lesson 7.** Gould's charge that ranking persons by
  one number is the political error, the 11-plus, Brigham, the army tests: none of it here. One forward
  clause only. Gould's l.~14783 passage attributing Spearman's and Burt's preference to class position
  is a motive argument — leave it out (the course judges arguments, not motives).
- **Balance on g.** Never "intelligence is g" in the course's voice; never "g is a statistical artefact"
  either. The course states: the correlations and the hierarchy are facts; a general factor can be
  extracted at some level (granted by Gould); its size depends on the model and the quantity reported;
  whether it is one capacity is an open causal question.
- **Balance on bias.** The finding is "no under-prediction in large representative samples"; it is not
  "the tests are fair in every sense" and not "the tests favour Black test-takers". Both glosses are
  refuted in the lesson (sections 4 and exercise 3).
- **Labels inside sources.** The Afterword's "no scholarly standing" (l.5272), Rushton & Jensen's or
  Murray's characterisations of opponents, critics' labels for the tests or their makers — only as
  quotations with who and when, or not at all. Provenance (who funded what, which journal) is never an
  argument here.
- **Terms a newcomer will not know (L11c, at most three per section; where each is explained):**

   .92; Φ(−.4/.92) ≈ 33%.) Then one sentence: why an employer hiring hundreds may still profit from

2. **Claim to refute (E6b):** "The test gives Black applicants a lower average score than White
   applicants, so it is biased against them." Name the sense of "bias" the claim uses (outcome), the
   sense that matters to the employer (predictive), the test that decides it (compare regression
   lines), and what the large studies found. Then the mirror claim: "Because the tests over-predict
   Black performance, they are biased in Black applicants' favour." Name the step that fails (an
   unbiased, imperfect predictor of a real difference over-predicts the lower group — note-11
   mechanism) and say what over-prediction does establish (the claim "the tests under-predict" is
   false).
3. **Boundary probe (E6a):** In the over-prediction example (set piece 8), let the test's correlation
   with the ability go to 1 and then to 0, keeping the criterion fixed. What happens to the size of
   over-prediction at each end, and why? Then the other boundary: a factor that lowers one group's
   scores *and* its later grades by exactly the same amount (the "uniform background bias"). What do
   the regression lines show, and why can no comparison of test with criterion detect it? What kind of
   evidence could?
4. **Boundary probe on a threshold (document reasoning):** In a sample of 1,184 children referred for
   special education, forcing equal loadings across Black and White children worsened fit by .011
   against a conventional cutoff of .01; two new subtests loaded differently. (a) What exactly was
   shown and not shown (metric only; scalar and strict untested; referred sample)? (b) Would the
   conclusion change at .009? What does that say about how much weight one study at the threshold can
   carry? (c) Design the study that would settle whether the two subtests behave differently in the
   population (representative sample; all four rungs; the subtests' directions tested in simplified
   language).
5. **Derivation the lesson skipped (E6c):** Take a four-test matrix with the verbal pair and the
   arithmetic pair each correlating .64, but every cross-pair correlating only .16 (instead of .36).
   Compute the first principal component's share of variance, the correlation between the oblique
   factors, and the general factor's share of each test's variance in the hierarchical solution.
   Compare with the worked example and say which side of the size dispute (Gould's "small percentage"
   vs "three times all other factors") the new matrix favours — and why neither computation says
   whether the general factor is one capacity. (Solution: first component 1.96/4 = 49%; oblique
   factors correlate .16/.64 = .25; second-order loadings .5; each test's variance .16 general, .48

   perfect test the two groups' lines coincide; with a useless one the gap between lines is the whole

8. **FORWARD (E7) → `heritability-within-and-between-groups`:** Suppose strict invariance holds between

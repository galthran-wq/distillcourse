An American option's price process must do two things at once — never fall below the payoff of immediate exercise, and admit no arbitrage — and the smallest supermartingale dominating the intrinsic value is the unique process that does both.

This is the eleventh move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the second move of its third module. <Ref
lesson="stopping-times">The previous lesson</Ref> fixed the class of exercise
rules a holder can follow, the stopping times, and proved that a martingale
or supermartingale stopped at one keeps its type; its closing exercise
listed every rule on the two-period \$4 tree and found that the \$5 put
collects at most $1.36$, against $0.96$ for the holder who must wait. What
stands open is the general case: what the seller must charge for a claim
the buyer may exercise at any stopping time, which rule the buyer should
use, and why the same right is worth nothing on a call. This lesson answers
all three with one process. A backward pass that takes a maximum at every
node computes it; it is the smallest supermartingale, after discounting,
that dominates the payoff; it is the largest value any stopping time can
collect, and the first time it meets the payoff collects exactly that; and
for a convex payoff vanishing at zero it coincides with the European price,
which is the call theorem of <Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the second
lesson</Ref> proved again inside the model. <Ref
lesson="first-passage-perpetual-put">The next lesson</Ref> takes the set of
nodes where the pass exercises, computed here date by date, and finds it in
closed form for a put with no expiry.

The route: the algorithm on the three-period \$5 put, with the exercise
region as a by-product; the three properties any price process must have
and the theorem that the pass produces the smallest process with them; the
seller's hedge, which is the European hedge with one withdrawal line; the
buyer's optimal rule, proved with optional sampling; and the convex-payoff
theorem with its one-paragraph proof.

## The algorithm: a maximum at every node, run on the \$5 put

The previous lesson found the two-period put's value by listing all five
stopping times and taking the best. Listing is not an algorithm: on the
three-period tree there are already $26$ rules, and the count squares with
every period added. The backward pass replaces the list with one
comparison per node, and the first thing to fix is what that comparison
compares.

### The three-period put, node by node

The tree is the \$4 stock with $u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$, so
$\tilde p = \tilde q = \tfrac12$ and one period's discount factor is
$\tfrac{1}{1.25} = 0.8$; prices $4$; $8, 2$; $16, 4, 1$; $32, 8, 2, 0.5$.
The claim is the \$5 put, which the holder may exercise at any date
$n \le 3$ for $G_n = (5 - S_n)^+$: $G_0 = 1$; $G_1 = 0, 3$;
$G_2 = 0, 1, 4$; $G_3 = 0, 0, 3, 4.5$. At each node the holder chooses
between two cash flows: $G_n$ now, or the claim one period on, which
<Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">one-period replication</Ref> prices at
$\tfrac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p\,V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(T)]$, the
*continuation value*. The value of the choice is the larger of the two.

### The exercise region is a by-product

The nodes where the payoff strictly beats the continuation are the nodes
where the holder acts and collects, and the pass produces them without a
second computation: $S_1 = 2$ and $S_2 = 1$, plus every in-the-money leaf.
This set is the *exercise region*, the shaded nodes of frame 4. In the
$(n, S)$ plane it lies below a boundary, and the boundary is not a fixed
price: at time $2$ the node $S_2 = 4$ is in the money and the holder
continues, so the region there starts at $1$ rather than at $4$. A second,
larger set is the *meeting set* $\{V_n = G_n\}$, every node where the
maximum is attained by $G_n$; it is what the optimal rule
$\tau^* = \min\{n : V_n = G_n\}$ of the fourth section reads, and it adds
to the exercise region the nodes where both terms are $0$, such as
$S_2 = 16$ at the top of the tree. Those trivial meetings lie outside the
drawn region because nothing is collected there: exercising for $0$ and
holding an option worth $0$ are the same cash flow. <Ref lesson="binomial-pricer-lab">The lab of this module</Ref>
computes the same region on finer trees, and <Ref
lesson="first-passage-perpetual-put">the next lesson</Ref> finds its shape
when the horizon is removed.

The rule the pass follows is "exercise when intrinsic value is at least the
continuation value", and the correction it makes to a common rule is worth
stating in full. The common rule is "exercise as soon as the option is in
the money: a sure dollar now beats a gamble later". The tree rejects it. At
$S_2 = 4$ the put is in the money by $1$, and the continuation is worth
$1.2$: the holder who exercises gives up $0.2$ per dollar of stock. The rule
fails because it compares the intrinsic value with zero, which is the wrong
alternative; the alternative to exercising now is not "nothing" but the
option one period on, and that option is worth more than $1$ whenever a
tail would take the stock to $2$ and pay $3$. Exercising is correct exactly
when the payoff now exceeds the discounted average of what the option is
worth next, which at $S_1 = 2$ it does ($3$ against $2.08$) and at
$S_2 = 4$ it does not. A holder following the common rule on this tree
exercises at the root for $1$, against $1.392$ for the pass's rule; the
closing exercises return to the comparison.

### The general contract and the general pass

The put is a special case in two ways: its payoff depends on the current
price alone, and its payoff is nonnegative. The theory needs neither.

<Definition title="American derivative security and the American algorithm (Shreve I, Definition 4.4.1, Theorem 4.4.3)">
In the $N$-period binomial model let $G_0, G_1, \ldots, G_N$ be an adapted
process with $G_n \ge 0$, the **intrinsic value process**. The
**American derivative security** with intrinsic value $G$ is the contract
that may be exercised at any one date $n \in \{0, 1, \ldots, N\}$ and pays
$G_n$ if exercised at $n$. The **American algorithm** defines a process
$V_0, \ldots, V_N$ by
$$
V_N = G_N, \qquad
V_n = \max\Bigl\{G_n,\ \frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl[\tilde p\,V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n T)\bigr]\Bigr\},
\quad n = N - 1, \ldots, 0 .
$$
</Definition>

Two conventions are folded in. First, $G_n \ge 0$ costs nothing: a payoff
that can be negative, such as $5 - S_n$ without the positive part, is never
exercised where it is negative, so replacing it by its positive part changes
no value; Shreve carries $\max\{G_n, 0\}$ through instead. Second, with
$G \ge 0$ the option is always worth exercising at expiry if it is in the
money, so "never exercise", the value $\tau = \infty$ of the previous
lesson, collects $0$ and is never better than $\tau = N$; stopping times
here take values in $\{0, \ldots, N\}$.

The pass also covers path-dependent payoffs. For $G_n$ a function of the
current price alone, $V_n = v_n(S_n)$ with one number per price, by the
Markov argument of <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the
martingale lesson</Ref>; for a payoff that reads the running maximum, the
pass runs on the path tree with $2^n$ nodes at date $n$, exactly as the
European pass does, with the maximum added at each node.

<KeyFact>
The meeting set $\{V_n = G_n\}$ is not the in-the-money set, in either
direction. $S_2 = 4$ is in the money by $1$ and lies outside it, because
the option one period on is worth $1.2$; $S_2 = 16$ is out of the money

rule that stops there gives up $0.2$. The second kind is where the rule is
indifferent, exercising for nothing and holding an option worth nothing
being the same cash flow, and it is why the optimal rule is not unique:
the theorem of the fourth section gives every rule agreeing with $\tau^*$
wherever $G_\tau > 0$ the same value $V_0$.
</KeyFact>

## Three properties any price must have, and the smallest process with them

The pass produced $1.392$. Nothing so far says that $1.392$ is the price:
that a seller can charge it and be safe against every stopping time, and
that a buyer cannot be charged less. Both statements are about arbitrage,
and each pins down one property of the process.

### Dominate, lose value only on average, and nothing more

**Dominance.** $V_n \ge G_n$ at every node. If the option traded at
$V_n < G_n$ at some node, buying it and exercising at once would collect
$G_n - V_n > 0$ with nothing at risk: an arbitrage in the sense of <Ref
lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">the first lesson</Ref>.

**Supermartingale.** The seller who holds $V_n$ at a node must be able to
deliver $V_{n+1}(H)$ or $V_{n+1}(T)$ one period on, whichever toss lands,
because the buyer may still be holding the option then. By <Ref
lesson="one-period-binomial">one-period replication</Ref> the cost of
delivering those two numbers is exactly the continuation value, so the
seller is safe only if $V_n \ge \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$.
Written for the discounted process $Y_n = V_n/(1 + r)^n$ this is
$\tilde E_n[Y_{n+1}] \le Y_n$: $Y$ is a $\tilde P$-supermartingale, the
"loses value only on average" clause. On the three-period put the
inequality is strict at exactly the two exercise nodes: at $S_1 = 2$,
$Y_1 = 3/1.25 = 2.4$ against $\tilde E_1[Y_2] = \tfrac12(1.2 + 4)/1.5625 =
1.664$; at $S_2 = 1$, $Y_2 = 2.56$ against
$\tilde E_2[Y_3] = \tfrac12(3 + 4.5)/1.953125 = 1.92$. At every other
non-terminal node it is an equality, because there $V_n$ *is* the
continuation.

**Minimality.** Any process with the first two properties is a safe price
for the seller. A buyer will not pay more than the smallest of them, and
the seller can hedge at the smallest of them, so the price is the smallest.
What the theorem adds is that a smallest one exists and that the pass
computes it.

<Theorem title="the American price is the smallest dominating supermartingale (Shreve I, Theorems 4.4.2 and 4.4.3)">
Let $V$ be the output of the American algorithm for the intrinsic value
process $G \ge 0$, and $Y_n = V_n/(1 + r)^n$. Then (i) $V_n \ge G_n$ for
all $n$; (ii) $Y$ is a $\tilde P$-supermartingale; and (iii) if $Z$ is any
adapted process with $Z_n \ge G_n$ for all $n$ and $Z_n/(1 + r)^n$ a
$\tilde P$-supermartingale, then $Z_n \ge V_n$ for all $n$. The process
with (i), (ii), (iii) is unique.

<Proof>
Roadmap: (i) and (ii) are the two arms of the maximum; (iii) is a backward
induction whose step uses that averaging preserves inequalities;
uniqueness is two applications of (iii).

**(i) and (ii).** At $n = N$, $V_N = G_N$. For $n < N$, $V_n$ is the larger
of $G_n$ and the continuation, so $V_n \ge G_n$ and
$V_n \ge \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$; dividing the second by
$(1 + r)^n$ gives $Y_n \ge \tilde E_n[Y_{n+1}]$ at every node, which is the
supermartingale clause.

**(iii).** At $n = N$: $Z_N \ge G_N = V_N$. Suppose $Z_{n+1} \ge V_{n+1}$ on
every path for some $n < N$. The supermartingale hypothesis on $Z$ reads
$Z_n \ge \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[Z_{n+1}]$, and a conditional expectation
is a weighted average with positive weights, so a pathwise inequality
between two quantities survives it:
$\tilde E_n[Z_{n+1}] \ge \tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$ on every block. Hence
$Z_n \ge \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$, the continuation value of $V$.
Together with the dominance hypothesis $Z_n \ge G_n$ this gives
$Z_n \ge \max\{G_n, \text{continuation}\} = V_n$. This is the sentence where
the argument is general: the only facts about $Z$ used are its two
hypotheses, one at time $n$ and one across the step.

**Uniqueness.** If $V'$ also has the three properties, then $V' \ge V$ by
(iii) applied to $V$ with $Z = V'$, and $V \ge V'$ by (iii) applied to $V'$
with $Z = V$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Three checks on the proof. The idea in one sentence: at each node the
process has to clear two bars, the payoff and the average of its own next
values, and the maximum clears both by the least possible amount. The step
that uses the supermartingale hypothesis on $Z$: the induction step, once,
across the fork. Where the argument would fail: for a $Z$ that dominates
$G$ but is a *sub*martingale after discounting, the step inequality runs
the wrong way, and indeed the European put's price process, a martingale,
fails (i) at $S_1 = 2$ and is not a candidate.

### The Snell envelope

<Definition title="Snell envelope">
Let $X_0, \ldots, X_N$ be an adapted process on the $N$-toss space with a
probability measure. The **Snell envelope** of $X$ is the smallest
supermartingale $U$ with $U_n \ge X_n$ for every $n$: it dominates $X$, and
every supermartingale that dominates $X$ dominates $U$. By the theorem, the
discounted American price $Y_n = V_n/(1 + r)^n$ is the Snell envelope of the
discounted intrinsic value $G_n/(1 + r)^n$ under $\tilde P$.
</Definition>

Four instances fix the boundary of the definition, all on the \$4 tree.

- **The canonical case.** The \$5 put: $Y = 1.392$; $0.384, 2.4$;
  $0, 0.768, 2.56$; $0, 0, 1.536, 2.304$ is the envelope of the discounted
  $(5 - S_n)^+$.
- **A case with a different surface.** A European claim is the American
  claim with $G_n = 0$ for $n < N$ and $G_N$ the payoff; every maximum in
  the pass is then against $0$, so the envelope is the discounted European
  price, a martingale. A martingale is a supermartingale, and the smallest
  one above a process it dominates is itself.
- **The degenerate case.** A claim paying a constant $K$ whenever exercised,
  $G_n \equiv K$. The continuation at every node is $K/(1 + r) < K$, so
  $V_n = K$ throughout and $\tau^* = 0$: the envelope is the process
  itself, and its discounted form $K/(1 + r)^n$ is a strictly decreasing
  supermartingale.
- **The near miss.** The constant process $Z \equiv 5$, the strike. It
  dominates $(5 - S_n)^+$ and its discounted form $5/(1 + r)^n$ is a
  supermartingale, so it satisfies clauses (i) and (ii); it fails the
  smallest clause, since $Z_0 = 5 > 1.392$. A seller charging $5$ is safe
  and overcharging by $3.608$.

A prompt for an instance of your own, before reading on: take the
European put's price process $0.864$; $0.48, 1.68$; $0, 1.2, 3$;
$0, 0, 3, 4.5$ and decide which clause of the Snell characterization it
fails. The clause that decides is dominance, and the node that decides it
is $S_1 = 2$.

<KeyFact>
Clauses (i) and (ii) are the seller's: dominance means the hedge can pay
whenever the buyer exercises, the supermartingale clause means the hedge
can be carried one more period whenever she does not. Clause (iii) is the
buyer's: no cheaper process is safe for the seller, so no cheaper price is
on offer. The three together are the definition of a price, not a
description of one.
</KeyFact>

## The seller's side: the European hedge with one withdrawal line

Clause (ii) was read as "the seller can carry the hedge one more period",
and the reading is now made a theorem. The hedge of a European claim in
<Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the multiperiod lesson</Ref> is a
portfolio process $\Delta_n$ with wealth obeying
$X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)$. The American
hedge is the same with one change: at a node where $V_n$ exceeds the
continuation, the seller does not need all of $V_n$ to reach $V_{n+1}$ and
withdraws the difference.

### The theorem

<Theorem title="replication with consumption (Shreve I, Theorem 4.4.4)">
Let $V$ be the American price of the intrinsic value process $G \ge 0$, and
for $n = 0, \ldots, N - 1$ define
$$
\Delta_n = \frac{V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n H) - V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n T)}{(u - d)\,S_n},
\qquad
C_n = V_n - \frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl[\tilde p\,V_{n+1}(\cdots H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(\cdots T)\bigr] \ \ge 0 .
$$
Start with $X_0 = V_0$ and let wealth evolve by
$$
X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)\bigl(X_n - C_n - \Delta_n S_n\bigr).
$$
Then $X_n = V_n$ on every path and every date; in particular
$X_n \ge G_n$, so the portfolio can pay the option at whichever stopping
time it is exercised, and the seller withdraws $C_n \ge 0$ at each date
along the way.

<Proof>
Roadmap: $C_n \ge 0$ is clause (ii); the equality $X_n = V_n$ is a forward
induction whose step is the one-period replication algebra with
$V_n - C_n$ in place of $V_n$.

**$C_n \ge 0$.** $V_n$ is the maximum of $G_n$ and the continuation, so it
is at least the continuation; $C_n$ is their difference.

**The step.** Suppose $X_n = V_n$ on a block $\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n$,
which holds at $n = 0$ by the choice of $X_0$. Then $X_n - C_n$ is the
continuation value, $\frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde q V_{n+1}(T)]$,
writing $V_{n+1}(H)$ for the value after one more head. On a head,
$$
X_{n+1}(H) = \Delta_n u S_n + (1 + r)(X_n - C_n) - (1 + r)\Delta_n S_n
= \Delta_n S_n (u - 1 - r) + \tilde p V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde q V_{n+1}(T).
$$
Since $u - 1 - r = (u - d)\tilde q$, the first term is
$\bigl(V_{n+1}(H) - V_{n+1}(T)\bigr)\tilde q$, and the sum is
$(\tilde p + \tilde q)V_{n+1}(H) = V_{n+1}(H)$. The tail case is the same
computation with $d - 1 - r = -(u - d)\tilde p$ and gives $V_{n+1}(T)$.
This is <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the multiperiod replication
step</Ref> verbatim, applied to the number the seller keeps after
withdrawing.

**Conclusion.** $X_n = V_n \ge G_n$ at every node by clause (i), so at any
stopping time $\tau$ the portfolio holds $X_\tau = V_\tau \ge G_\tau$ and
can pay.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The delta at $S_2 = 1$ is $-1$: both successors are terminal, so each pays

<Remark title="what the withdrawal is for">
The seller's hedge is uniform over the class of stopping times: the same
$\Delta_n$ and the same $X_0$ cover every exercise rule the buyer could
follow, and the seller never needs to know which one she is following. The
cost of that uniformity is that at a node where the buyer should exercise
and does not, the portfolio holds more than the option's continuation
value, and the excess $C_n$ is the seller's, not part of the hedge. Without
the withdrawal the wealth equation would carry the excess forward and the
identity $X_n = V_n$ would fail from that node on; the seller would be
over-hedged, which is safe and is not replication. A seller who instead
charged the value of the rule the buyer actually uses, $0.864$ for a buyer
who holds to expiry, would hold $2$ at node $S_1 = 2$ against a buyer who
may still exercise for $3$: the hedge would be reading the buyer's future
decision, and no portfolio process does that.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The American hedge is the European hedge, delta by delta, plus a withdrawal
of $V_n - \text{continuation}$ at each node; the withdrawals are zero
wherever the holder continues, the trivial meetings $V_n = G_n = 0$
included, and positive exactly on the exercise region, where the payoff
strictly beats the continuation, so the
supermartingale slack of the price process is, in dollars, what the buyer
leaves on the table by not exercising.
</KeyFact>

## The buyer's side: no stopping time collects more than V₀, and the first meeting of price and payoff collects exactly V₀

The seller is covered at $V_0$ against every stopping time. The buyer's
question is whether $V_0$ overcharges: whether some stopping time collects
less than $V_0$ in every case, so that the seller's safety margin is
paid for by the buyer. The theorem says no, and names the rule that
collects the full amount.

### The theorem

Write $\mathcal S_n$ for the set of stopping times with values in
$\{n, n + 1, \ldots, N\}$, the rules available to a holder who has reached
date $n$ unexercised.

<Theorem title="optimal exercise (Shreve I, Definition 4.4.1 and Theorem 4.4.5)">
Let $V$ be the American price of $G \ge 0$. For every stopping time
$\tau \in \mathcal S_0$,
$$
\tilde E\Bigl[\frac{G_\tau}{(1 + r)^\tau}\Bigr] \le V_0,
$$
and the stopping time $\tau^* = \min\{n : V_n = G_n\}$ attains equality.
Hence $V_0 = \max_{\tau \in \mathcal S_0} \tilde E[G_\tau/(1 + r)^\tau]$,
and the same holds at every date: $V_n = \max_{\tau \in \mathcal S_n}
\tilde E_n[G_\tau/(1 + r)^{\tau - n}]$, attained by
$\min\{k \ge n : V_k = G_k\}$.

<Proof>
Roadmap: $\tau^*$ is a stopping time; the upper bound is optional sampling
applied to the supermartingale $Y$; the attainment is that $Y$ stopped at
$\tau^*$ is a martingale, because $\tau^*$ stops before any node where the
supermartingale inequality is strict.

**$\tau^*$ is a stopping time with values in $\{0, \ldots, N\}$.** The event
$\{\tau^* = n\}$ is "$V_k > G_k$ for $k < n$ and $V_n = G_n$", and every
quantity in it is known by time $n$ because $V$ and $G$ are adapted. Since
$V_N = G_N$, $\tau^* \le N$ on every path.

**Upper bound.** Let $\tau \in \mathcal S_0$. $Y_n = V_n/(1 + r)^n$ is a
$\tilde P$-supermartingale by clause (ii), so by <Ref
lesson="stopping-times">optional sampling, part I</Ref> the stopped process
$Y_{n \wedge \tau}$ is a supermartingale, and iterating its one-step
inequality from $0$ to $N$ and averaging gives
$\tilde E[Y_{N \wedge \tau}] \le Y_0 = V_0$. Since $\tau \le N$,
$Y_{N \wedge \tau} = Y_\tau$, and by clause (i), $Y_\tau = V_\tau/(1 + r)^\tau
\ge G_\tau/(1 + r)^\tau$ on every path. So
$\tilde E[G_\tau/(1 + r)^\tau] \le \tilde E[Y_\tau] \le V_0$.

**Attainment.** The increment identity of the previous lesson reads
$$
Y_{(n+1) \wedge \tau^*} - Y_{n \wedge \tau^*}
= \mathbb 1_{\{\tau^* > n\}}\bigl(Y_{n+1} - Y_n\bigr).
$$
On a path with $\tau^* > n$, $V_n > G_n$, so the maximum in the algorithm
is attained by the continuation and $V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$,
that is $\tilde E_n[Y_{n+1}] = Y_n$. Take $\tilde E_n$ of the identity: the
indicator is decided by time $n$ and comes out, leaving
$\mathbb 1_{\{\tau^* > n\}}\bigl(\tilde E_n[Y_{n+1}] - Y_n\bigr)$, and this
is $0$ on every path: on $\{\tau^* > n\}$ the bracket is zero, and on
$\{\tau^* \le n\}$ the indicator is. So $Y_{n \wedge \tau^*}$ is a
martingale, $\tilde E[Y_{N \wedge \tau^*}] = Y_0 = V_0$, and
$Y_{N \wedge \tau^*} = Y_{\tau^*} = G_{\tau^*}/(1 + r)^{\tau^*}$ by the
definition of $\tau^*$. This is the sentence where the two halves meet: the
bracket may be strictly negative, but only at nodes the rule has already
stopped at.

**General $n$.** Replace $\tilde E$ by $\tilde E_n$, $\mathcal S_0$ by
$\mathcal S_n$, and $\tau^*$ by $\min\{k \ge n : V_k = G_k\}$; every step
is the same with the averages taken over the tosses after $n$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Three checks on the proof. The idea in one sentence: a supermartingale
stopped anywhere averages at most its start, and stopped before its first
strict step averages exactly its start. The step that uses the
stopping-time hypothesis on $\tau$: the upper bound, through optional
sampling; for the regret rule $\rho$ of the previous lesson the bound fails
and $\rho$ collects $1.74$ on the two-period put, above $V_0 = 1.36$. The
step that uses $\tau^*$ specifically: the attainment, where "the bracket is
zero wherever the indicator is one" is a property of no other rule that
stops later.

### One process, three descriptions

The lesson has now described the same process three ways, and the point of
the two theorems is that the descriptions are one object rather than three
facts. The pass is the computation; the Snell characterization is what the
computation produces, stated without reference to the pass; the stopping
time maximum is what the buyer gets. Theorem 2.1 reads the first two off
each other by the maximum at each node. The upper bound of Theorem 4.1
takes the second to the third through optional sampling, and the
attainment takes the first to the third through the stopped martingale.
The root value $1.392$ is the output of the pass, the smallest safe price
for the seller, and the best the buyer can collect, and each of the three
descriptions can be taken as the definition with the other two as theorems.

The American price is the smallest supermartingale dominating intrinsic

The two halves are the two parties: no stopping time collects more than

seller's diagnostic: the buyer who lets a node of $\tau^*$ pass hands the
seller the withdrawal $C_n$ of the previous section.
</KeyFact>

## Convex payoffs through the origin: the American call is the European call, proved inside the model

From the put's exercise region one would expect the call's, on the same
tree, to be a mirror image, with exercise at high prices. It is empty. The
theorem below says so for every convex payoff that vanishes at zero, and
its proof is the one inequality that <Ref
lesson="static-bounds-parity">the second lesson</Ref> used against the
market, $K/(1 + r) < K$, now run through the tree by conditional Jensen.

### The chord through the origin

<Figure
  float="right"
  width="m"
  src="./figures/convex-origin.svg"
  alt="The call payoff (s minus 5) positive part plotted against s; the chord from the origin to the point (10, 5) lies above the curve, and at s equals 6 the chord reads 3 where the curve reads 1"
  caption="Convex with g(0) = 0: the chord from the origin lies above the graph, so g(λs) ≤ λ g(s)."
/>

For a convex $g$ with $g(0) = 0$ and $0 \le \lambda \le 1$, convexity
between the points $0$ and $s$ gives
$$
g(\lambda s) = g\bigl(\lambda s + (1 - \lambda)\cdot 0\bigr)
\le \lambda\,g(s) + (1 - \lambda)\,g(0) = \lambda\,g(s).
$$
With $\lambda = \frac{1}{1 + r}$ and $s = (1 + r)S_n$ this reads
$g(S_n) \le \frac{1}{1 + r}\,g\bigl((1 + r)S_n\bigr)$: scaling the stock up
by the interest rate scales the payoff up by at least as much. For the call
$g(s) = (s - 5)^+$ at $S_n = 16$: $g(16) = 11$ against
$g(20)/1.25 = 15/1.25 = 12$.

### The theorem

<Theorem title="no early exercise for convex payoffs vanishing at zero (Shreve I, Theorem 4.5.1)">
In the $N$-period model with $0 < d < 1 + r < u$ and $r \ge 0$, let
$g : [0, \infty) \to [0, \infty)$ be convex with $g(0) = 0$, and let the
American security have intrinsic value $G_n = g(S_n)$. Then its price at
every date equals the European price of the payoff $g(S_N)$,
$V_n = \tilde E_n\bigl[g(S_N)/(1 + r)^{N - n}\bigr]$, and $\tau \equiv N$ is
an optimal exercise time.

<Proof>
Roadmap, four steps: the chord inequality; the discounted stock is a
martingale, and conditional Jensen turns the chord inequality into a
submartingale statement for the discounted payoff; optional sampling part
II bounds every stopping time by $\tau \equiv N$; the maximum of Theorem
4.1 is therefore attained at $N$.

**Step 1, the chord.** $g(S_n) \le \frac{1}{1 + r}\,g\bigl((1 + r)S_n\bigr)$
on every path, from the display above; this is the step that uses
$g(0) = 0$ and $r \ge 0$, the latter to make $\lambda \le 1$.

**Step 2, Jensen.** Under $\tilde P$ the discounted stock is a martingale
(<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the martingale lesson</Ref>),
so $(1 + r)S_n = \tilde E_n[S_{n+1}]$. Conditional Jensen's inequality
(<Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">property (v)</Ref>) for the convex $g$
gives $g(\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}]) \le \tilde E_n[g(S_{n+1})]$. Chaining with
Step 1,
$$
g(S_n) \le \frac{1}{1 + r}\,g\bigl(\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}]\bigr)
\le \frac{1}{1 + r}\,\tilde E_n\bigl[g(S_{n+1})\bigr],
$$
and dividing by $(1 + r)^n$: the discounted intrinsic value
$X_n = g(S_n)/(1 + r)^n$ satisfies $X_n \le \tilde E_n[X_{n+1}]$, a
$\tilde P$-submartingale. This is the sentence where the argument becomes
general: nothing about the tree beyond the martingale property of the
discounted stock has been used.

**Step 3, optional sampling.** For any $\tau \in \mathcal S_0$, <Ref
lesson="stopping-times">optional sampling, part II</Ref> for the
submartingale $X$ gives $\tilde E[X_{N \wedge \tau}] \le \tilde E[X_N]$, and
$N \wedge \tau = \tau$. So
$\tilde E[g(S_\tau)/(1 + r)^\tau] \le \tilde E[g(S_N)/(1 + r)^N]$.

**Step 4, the maximum.** By Theorem 4.1, $V_0$ is the maximum of the left
side over $\tau \in \mathcal S_0$; Step 3 says every value is at most the
value at $\tau \equiv N$, which is the European price, and $\tau \equiv N$
is in the class. So $V_0$ is the European price and $\tau \equiv N$ attains
it. At date $n$ the same argument runs with $\tilde E_n$ and
$\mathcal S_n$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Task>
Three checks on the proof. (1) The idea in one sentence: write it, then
compare with "a payoff that is convex through the origin gains on average
from every period of waiting, and stopping a submartingale early can only
lose". (2) Which step fails for the put $g(s) = (K - s)^+$: the chord
inequality, since $g(0) = K$ gives $g(\lambda s) \le \lambda g(s) + (1 -
\lambda)K$ and the extra term is one period's interest on $K$. (3) Which
step would fail with $r < 0$: Step 1, since $\lambda = 1/(1 + r) > 1$ puts
$\lambda s$ outside the chord.
</Task>

### The call at one node, and the put at another

<Figure
  float="right"
  width="s"
  src="./figures/call-node.svg"
  alt="The node with price 16 at time 2 for the five-dollar call: exercising pays 11; continuing leads to 32 paying 27 or 8 paying 3, worth 0.8 times one half of 30, which is 12; the gap of 1 is labelled as 5 minus 5 over 1.25"
  caption="The call at S = 16: continuing is worth 12 against 11 now, and the gap is one period's interest on the strike."
/>

On the \$4 tree the \$5 call at $S_2 = 16$ has intrinsic $11$ and
continuation $0.8\,(\tfrac12 \cdot 27 + \tfrac12 \cdot 3) = 12$. Both
successors are in the money, so the continuation is
$\frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_2[S_3 - 5] = S_2 - 5/1.25 = 16 - 4 = 12$, and the
margin over exercising, $12 - 11 = 5 - 5/1.25 = 1$, is the interest the
holder earns on the strike by paying it one period later. That is the
second lesson's argument node by node, and the theorem says it holds at
every node of every tree for every convex $g$ with $g(0) = 0$. The root
value is $2.304$, the European call's, and the closing exercises verify the
equality at all ten nodes and check it against put–call parity.

The put breaks the hypothesis, and where it breaks is visible in the same
arithmetic with the sign reversed. At a node whose successors are both
exercise nodes, $V_{n+1} = K - S_{n+1}$ on both branches, the continuation
is $\frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[K - S_{n+1}] = K/(1 + r) - S_n$, less than
the intrinsic $K - S_n$ by the same one period's interest on $K$, now lost
by the holder who waits to receive it. Such a node is always an exercise
node and its delta is $-1$. The hypothesis is that the successors are
exercise nodes, not merely in the money: substituting $K - S_{n+1}$ for
$V_{n+1}$ is wrong at a successor that continues, and an in-the-money
successor can continue, as $S_2 = 4$ does. On the three-period put the
lemma applies at $S_2 = 1$, whose successors are terminal and both in the
money, and at no other interior node; the pass found $S_1 = 2$ by the full
comparison, $3$ against $2.08$, and settled $S_2 = 4$ the same way. The
moneyness version fails outright on finer trees: with $u = 1.1$,
$d = 1/1.1$, $r = 0.001$, strike $100$ and forty periods, the node
$S = 90$ has $uS = 99 < K$ and value $25.88$ against intrinsic $10$,
because far from expiry the successors continue and the interest lost on
one period's strike is small. Convexity pushes the put's discounted payoff
up on average and the discounting of the receipt $K$ pushes it down, and
for low enough stock prices the second wins; the level at which it wins is
what the pass computes, not a function of $u$ and $K$ alone.

<Aside>
The same computation in the 1979 paper. Cox, Ross and Rubinstein's §6 runs
the pass for a three-period American put with $S = K = 80$, $u = 1.5$,
$d = 0.5$, one-period gross rate $1.1$, so $\tilde p = 0.6$, and a $5\%$
dividend paid at the end of the second period, which scales the stock at
dates $2$ and $3$ by $0.95$. Their Table 3 prints the put values with the
exercise nodes in italics: $19.108$ at the root, $8.363$ and $40$ at time
one, $0$, $23$ and $61$ at time two, and the hedge ratios $-0.396$,
$-0.192$, $-0.950$, and $-1.00$ at the deepest exercised node, whose
successors are terminal and both in the money. The dividend is the one ingredient this
lesson's tree lacks, and <Ref lesson="early-exercise-in-practice">the
lesson on real markets</Ref> adds it; it is also what restores early
exercise to the call.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
The call theorem of the first module is now a theorem about the model with
a proof that names its own hypothesis: $g(0) = 0$. A put has $g(0) = K$,
the chord inequality picks up the term $(1 - \lambda)K$, one period's
interest on the strike, and the exercise region of the three-period put is
that term winning against convexity at low prices.
</KeyFact>

What is established: the American price is computed by a backward pass
with a maximum at every node, $1.392$ for the three-period \$5 put against
$0.864$ European, and the nodes where the payoff strictly beats the
continuation form the exercise region, which contains $S_1 = 2$ and
$S_2 = 1$, not the in-the-money node $S_2 = 4$, and not the zero-payoff
meetings such as $S_2 = 16$ that the meeting set $\{V_n = G_n\}$ also
holds; the discounted price is the smallest
supermartingale dominating the discounted payoff, the Snell envelope, and
this characterization is the definition of a price rather than a
description of one; the seller replicates it with the European deltas plus
a withdrawal at every node the buyer should have exercised at; no stopping
time collects more than $V_0$, and $\tau^* = \min\{n : V_n = G_n\}$
collects exactly $V_0$, both by optional sampling; and a convex payoff
vanishing at zero is never exercised early, because its discounted value is
a submartingale, while a put's is not. What is not established is the shape
of the exercise region as a function of time and price: the pass gives it
node by node, and on the coarse tree it sits between $2$ and $4$ without
saying where. <Ref lesson="first-passage-perpetual-put">The next
lesson</Ref> removes the expiry, so that the boundary becomes a single
level, and finds that level for the put on this stock by valuing the rule
"exercise the first time the stock falls to $m$" for each candidate $m$
through the distribution of a first-passage time.

because the rule never lets a strict-supermartingale node pass.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Derive the decomposition the lesson did not write. With $Y_n = V_n/(1 + r)^n$
the discounted American price and $C_n$ the consumption of the replication
theorem, define $A_0 = 0$ and $A_{n} = \sum_{k < n} C_k/(1 + r)^k$, and
$M_n = Y_n + A_n$. Prove that $A$ is nondecreasing with $A_{n+1}$ known at
time $n$, that $M$ is a $\tilde P$-martingale, and that $M_n$ is the
discounted wealth of the seller's hedge if the withdrawals are kept in the
portfolio instead of taken out. Compute $M_3$ on the path $TTT$ of the
three-period \$5 put.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** The continuation value, written for $Y$, is
$\tilde E_n[Y_{n+1}] = Y_n - C_n/(1 + r)^n$; that one line is the
martingale property of $M$.

**$A$.** Each $C_k \ge 0$, so $A$ is nondecreasing. $A_{n+1}$ is a sum over
$k \le n$ of quantities each depending on the first $k$ tosses, so it is
decided by time $n$: $A$ is predictable, one step ahead of adapted.

**$M$ is a martingale.** $C_n = V_n - \text{continuation}$ divided by
$(1 + r)^n$ reads $C_n/(1 + r)^n = Y_n - \tilde E_n[Y_{n+1}]$. Then
$$
\tilde E_n[M_{n+1}] = \tilde E_n[Y_{n+1}] + A_{n+1}
= \bigl(Y_n - C_n/(1 + r)^n\bigr) + A_n + C_n/(1 + r)^n = M_n,
$$
taking $A_{n+1}$ out of $\tilde E_n$ because it is known at time $n$.

**$M$ is the no-withdrawal hedge.** The multiperiod lesson wrote discounted
self-financing wealth as $X_0 + \sum_{k < n}\Delta_k(S_{k+1}/(1 + r)^{k+1} -
S_k/(1 + r)^k)$. The hedge with withdrawals has discounted wealth $Y_n$;
keeping the withdrawals in the money market adds $\sum_{k < n} C_k/(1 + r)^k
= A_n$ in discounted terms, since cash kept at time $k$ and grown at $r$ is
worth $C_k/(1 + r)^k$ in time-zero dollars at every later date. So the
no-withdrawal wealth is $Y_n + A_n = M_n$, a martingale, as the discounted
wealth of every self-financing strategy must be.

**On $TTT$.** $Y_3 = 4.5/1.953125 = 2.304$;
$A_3 = 0 + 0.92/1.25 + 1/1.5625 = 0.736 + 0.64 = 1.376$; $M_3 = 3.68$. The
transferable move: a supermartingale is a martingale minus a predictable
nondecreasing process, and for the American price the subtracted process
is the accumulated consumption. This is the Doob decomposition, and the
consumption is its increment.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Set $r = 0$ on the two-period \$4 tree ($u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$), so that
$\tilde p = \tfrac13$. Price the American and the European \$5 put, show
that the discounted intrinsic value $(5 - S_n)^+$ is now a
$\tilde P$-submartingale, and identify which inequality in the put's usual
early-exercise argument needed $r > 0$.

<Solution>
**The prices.** $V_2 = 0, 1, 4$ at $16, 4, 1$. At $S_1 = 8$:
$\max(0, \tfrac13 \cdot 0 + \tfrac23 \cdot 1) = \tfrac23$. At $S_1 = 2$:
$\max(3, \tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac23 \cdot 4) = \max(3, 3) = 3$, a tie. At
the root: $\max(1, \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac23 + \tfrac23 \cdot 3) = \max(1,
2.222) = 2.222$. The European put is $\tilde E[(5 - S_2)^+] = \tfrac19 \cdot
0 + \tfrac49 \cdot 1 + \tfrac49 \cdot 4 = \tfrac{20}{9} = 2.222$: the two
prices agree at every node.

**The submartingale.** With $r = 0$ the stock itself is a
$\tilde P$-martingale, $\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}] = S_n$, and conditional Jensen
for the convex $g(s) = (5 - s)^+$ gives
$g(S_n) = g(\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}]) \le \tilde E_n[g(S_{n+1})]$ with no chord
step at all. At $S_1 = 2$: $g = 3$ against $\tfrac13 \cdot 1 + \tfrac23
\cdot 4 = 3$, equality, which is the tie.

**The inequality that needed $r > 0$.** The put's exercise argument is
"both successors exercise nodes gives continuation $K/(1 + r) - S_n < K - S_n$";
with $r = 0$ the strict inequality is an equality, and the holder is
indifferent. In the convex-payoff theorem the hypothesis $g(0) = 0$ is
what let the chord step absorb the factor $\lambda = 1/(1 + r)$; when
$r = 0$ that factor is $1$ and the hypothesis is not needed. The
transferable move: early exercise of a put is paid for by the interest on
the strike, and at zero interest a put is a convex payoff like any other,
never worth exercising strictly early.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "An American put should be exercised as soon as it is in
the money. The intrinsic value is a sure amount and the continuation is a
gamble; a sure gain beats a gamble, and the price of an American option is
in any case a supermartingale, so it tends to fall." Give the node on the
three-period \$5 put that breaks the claim, name the comparison the claim
skips, and say what is true in the supermartingale sentence.

<Solution>
**The node.** $S_2 = 4$: in the money by $1$, continuation $1.2$. The root
is a second: in the money by $1$, continuation $1.392$. A holder following
the claim exercises at the root and collects $1$ against $1.392$,
forfeiting the whole early-exercise premium and more.

**The skipped comparison.** The claim compares $G_n$ with the *risk* of
continuing; the price compares $G_n$ with the *value* of continuing, which
is a sure amount too, the cost of the portfolio that replicates the option
one period on. "Sure gain" against "gamble" is the wrong pair: both
alternatives are priced by replication, and neither price is a gamble.

**The supermartingale sentence.** The discounted price is a
$\tilde P$-supermartingale, so its average does not rise; but the
inequality is strict only on the exercise region, where $V_n > \text{continuation}$,
and on the continuation region the discounted price is a martingale. At
$S_2 = 4$, $Y_2 = 0.768$ and $\tilde E_2[Y_3] = \tfrac12(0 + 3)/1.953125 =
0.768$: no tendency to fall at all. The tendency to fall appears exactly at
the nodes the pass already marks for exercise, so it cannot be a reason to
exercise anywhere else. The transferable move: the only comparison that
decides exercise is $G_n$ against $\frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$, and
"in the money" is $G_n$ against $0$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A pricer implements
$V_n = \max\bigl(G_n,\ \tilde p\,V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(T)\bigr)$,
with no discounting in the continuation. Run it on the three-period \$3
put of the in-body exercise ($u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$,
$\tilde p = \tfrac12$), find the first node, working backward, where its
exercise decision differs from the correct pricer's, and say whether it
overprices or underprices, with the reason.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** Recompute only the nodes where the correct pricer's
comparison was close; discounting shrinks the continuation by $20\%$.

**The run.** $V_3 = 0, 0, 1, 2.5$. Time 2: at $16$, $0$; at $4$,
$\max(0, \tfrac12 \cdot 1) = 0.5$ (correct: $0.4$); at $1$,
$\max(2, \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot 2.5) = \max(2, 1.75) = 2$,
exercise, agreeing with the correct pricer. Time 1: at $8$,
$\max(0, \tfrac12 \cdot 0.5) = 0.25$; at $2$,
$\max(1, \tfrac12 \cdot 0.5 + \tfrac12 \cdot 2) = \max(1, 1.25) = 1.25$:
the buggy pricer **continues** where the correct one exercised ($1$ against
$0.96$). This is the first node where the decisions differ. Time 0:
$\max(0, \tfrac12 \cdot 0.25 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1.25) = 0.75$, against the
correct $0.464$.

**Direction.** It overprices, at every node where the continuation is
positive. By backward induction: at expiry the two pricers agree, and if the
buggy values at $n + 1$ are at least the correct ones, the buggy
continuation at $n$ is at least the correct continuation divided by $0.8$,
larger whenever positive, and the maximum at $n$ follows. It also
exercises less often, since a larger continuation wins more comparisons.
The transferable move: the pricer is computing the Snell envelope of $G$
under $\tilde p = \tfrac12$ with zero interest, which is a valid envelope of
a different market, one whose risk-neutral probability would have to be
$\tfrac13$; a pricer's decisions are only as correct as the continuation
value it compares against, and that value is a price, discount included.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the three-period \$4 tree price the American \$5 call,
$G_n = (S_n - 5)^+$, by the pass, and verify at every non-terminal node
that the maximum is attained by the continuation. Name the theorem that
predicted this before any computation, and check the root value against
the European \$5 put's $0.864$ through <Ref
lesson="static-bounds-parity">put–call parity</Ref>.

<Solution>
**The pass.** $V_3 = 27, 3, 0, 0$ at $32, 8, 2, 0.5$. Time 2: at $16$,
intrinsic $11$, continuation $0.8(\tfrac12 \cdot 27 + \tfrac12 \cdot 3) =
12$; $V_2 = 12$. At $4$: intrinsic $0$, continuation
$0.8 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 3 = 1.2$. At $1$: $0$. Time 1: at $8$, intrinsic
$3$, continuation $0.8(\tfrac12 \cdot 12 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1.2) = 5.28$;
$V_1 = 5.28$. At $2$: intrinsic $0$, continuation
$0.8 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 1.2 = 0.48$. Time 0: intrinsic $0$, continuation
$0.8(\tfrac12 \cdot 5.28 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.48) = 2.304$. At all six
non-terminal nodes the continuation is at least the intrinsic value, with
strict inequality at the two in-the-money ones ($12 > 11$, $5.28 > 3$).

European price at every node and $\tau \equiv N$ is optimal.

<Exercise>
Extend the \$4 tree to six periods ($u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$)
and run the pass for the American put with strike $4$. Tabulate, for each
number of periods remaining $m = 2, 4, 6$, the value at $S = 4$, and for
$m = 1, 3, 5$ the values at $S = 8$ and at $S = 2$; report which nodes are
exercised at each date. Say what the table suggests about the values as
$m$ grows and about the boundary, and state what would count as agreement
with the limit.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** Prices at even dates are $4 \cdot 4^k$, at odd dates
$2 \cdot 4^k$; the pass needs only one value per price per date; at each date the node
$S = 2$ is settled by the full comparison, and once it is an exercise node
every node below it is one by the interest-on-the-strike lemma, both of
its successors being exercise nodes.

**The pass, from expiry.** Time 6, at $256, 64, 16, 4, 1, 0.25, 0.0625$:
$0, 0, 0, 0, 3, 3.75, 3.9375$. Time 5, at $128, 32, 8, 2, 0.5, 0.125$:
$0, 0, 0, 2, 3.5, 3.875$, exercise at $2$ and below ($2$ against
continuation $1.2$). Time 4, at $64, 16, 4, 1, 0.25$: $0, 0, 0.8, 3, 3.75$,
exercise at $1$ and below. Time 3, at $32, 8, 2, 0.5$: $0, 0.32, 2, 3.5$,
exercise at $2$ ($2$ against $1.52$) and below. Time 2, at $16, 4, 1$:
$0.128, 0.928, 3$, exercise at $1$. Time 1, at $8, 2$: $0.4224, 2$,
exercise at $2$ ($2$ against $1.5712$). Time 0: $0.96896$.

**The table.** At $S = 4$: $0.8$ ($m = 2$), $0.928$ ($m = 4$), $0.96896$
($m = 6$). At $S = 8$: $0$ ($m = 1$), $0.32$ ($m = 3$), $0.4224$ ($m = 5$).
At $S = 2$: exercised for $2$ at every date, against continuations $1.2$,
$1.52$, $1.5712$.

**What it suggests.** Every value rises with the periods remaining and the
increments shrink: at $S = 4$ the steps are $0.128$ and $0.041$, at
$S = 8$ they are $0.32$ and $0.102$, each about a third of the last. The
sequences are converging, and the pattern $0.8, 0.928, 0.969$ at $S = 4$
and $0, 0.32, 0.42$ at $S = 8$ is consistent with limits $1$ and $0.5$,
that is $4/S$. The boundary does not move on this lattice: $S = 2$ is
exercised at every date and $S = 4$ at none, and the continuation at
$S = 2$ rises toward but stays below $2$, so the lattice places the
boundary in $[2, 4)$ at every horizon and cannot resolve it further.

**What counts as agreement.** The limit of the table as $m \to \infty$ is
the price of a put with no expiry, and <Ref
lesson="first-passage-perpetual-put">the next lesson</Ref> computes it for
this stock and this strike in closed form: $v(s) = 4/s$ for $s \ge 2$ and
$4 - s$ below, with the boundary at exactly $2$. The table is not yet close
to those numbers except at the money: $0.969$ against $1$ at $S = 4$ is
$3\%$ low, $0.4224$ against $0.5$ at $S = 8$ with five periods remaining
is $16\%$ low, and $0.128$ against $0.25$ at $S = 16$ with four remaining
is half the limit, because a node far out of the money needs many periods
for the stock to reach the boundary at all. What the finite table can be
held to is direction and rate: every value lies below its limit, since a
finite horizon only removes stopping times; the gaps to the limit,
$0.2, 0.072, 0.031$ at $S = 4$ and $0.5, 0.18, 0.078$ at $S = 8$, shrink
by a factor between two and three per two added periods; and the
exercised set at every date is $\{S \le 2\}$, consistent with a boundary
at exactly $2$. The boundary computed date by date here is the object the
next lesson studies as a single level.
</Solution>

Brownian motion is the limit object the scaled walks converge to: continuous
paths, independent stationary normal increments — and a path so rough that
its ordinary length is infinite while its squared increments accumulate at
exactly one unit per unit time.

This is the sixteenth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the second move of its fourth module. <Ref
lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">The previous lesson</Ref> scaled the
binomial walk to steps of $1/\sqrt n$, proved that the scaled walk keeps
independent increments, the martingale property and a quadratic variation
of exactly $t$ on every path at every $n$, and sent its value at any one
time to $N(0, t)$ by the central limit theorem; what it did not do was
construct a process with all of those properties at every time at once.
This lesson writes that process down by its properties, states precisely
which facts about it the course takes from measure theory without proof,
and proves from the definition alone what the rest of the module uses: the
martingale property, a first-order variation that is infinite, and a
quadratic variation $[W, W](T) = T$, the fact written $dW\,dW = dt$. What
stands open at the end is how to integrate a trading strategy against a
path of infinite length, which <Ref lesson="ito-integral">the next
lesson</Ref> takes up.

The route: the definition and its boundary cases; the fence, erected once;
the martingale property in two lines; the two variations, with the $L^2$
proof of $[W, W](T) = T$ carried through; the notation $dW\,dW = dt$
dissected; and the roughness of the path, with the volatility of a stock
read off a single price record.

## The definition: continuity plus independent normal increments

The scaled walk approaches its limit carrying three properties, and the
limit is defined by demanding them of a single process at every time.
Every term in the definition below was met in the previous lesson, so the
definition comes first and the concrete cases follow it.

### Retrieval: what the walk hands over

<Task>
Closed book, before reading on. (1) State the quadratic variation of the
scaled walk $W^{(n)}$ over $[0, t]$ and say on how many paths the statement
holds. (2) Name the two facts a martingale proof uses about an increment.
(3) State what the previous lesson's central limit theorem proved about
$W^{(n)}(t)$, and what its fence remark said it did not construct. Then
check against the fold.
</Task>

<Aside>
Answers. (1) $[W^{(n)}, W^{(n)}](t) = t$ on every path, at every $n$: $nt$
increments each squaring to $1/n$ (<Ref
lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">the scaled-walk theorem</Ref>).
(2) The increment is independent of the past, and its mean is zero. (3)
The distribution of $W^{(n)}(t)$ at one fixed $t$ converges to $N(0, t)$;
nothing was constructed with continuous paths and independent normal
increments for all $t$ at once, and Donsker's theorem, which does so, was
named and not proved.
</Aside>

<Definition title="Brownian motion (Shreve II, 3.3.1)">
Let $(\Omega, \mathcal F, P)$ be a probability space. Suppose that to each
$\omega \in \Omega$ there corresponds a continuous function
$t \mapsto W(t)$, $t \ge 0$, with $W(0) = 0$. Then $W$ is a **Brownian
motion** if for every choice of times $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_m$ the
increments
$$
W(t_1) - W(t_0),\; W(t_2) - W(t_1),\; \ldots,\; W(t_m) - W(t_{m-1})
$$
are independent, and each is normally distributed with
$$
E\bigl[W(t_{i+1}) - W(t_i)\bigr] = 0, \qquad
\mathrm{Var}\bigl[W(t_{i+1}) - W(t_i)\bigr] = t_{i+1} - t_i .
$$
</Definition>

<Figure
  src="./figures/definition.svg"
  alt="One Brownian path on the unit interval with three disjoint time windows shaded; each window's increment is labelled with its normal distribution, whose variance is the window's length"
  caption="The definition as a picture: three disjoint windows, three independent increments, each N(0, window length); the path starts at zero and never jumps."

Two things distinguish $W$ from the scaled walk $W^{(100)}$ it came from.
The walk has a natural time step of $\tfrac{1}{100}$ and is linear between
steps; $W$ has no step and no linear pieces. The walk's value at a time is
only approximately normal, to within the one percent the previous lesson
measured bar by bar; $W$'s increments are exactly normal, over every
window, however short. Four cases fix what the definition covers.

- **The canonical case.** $W$ itself, as defined: at $t = 1$, $W(1)$ is
  $N(0, 1)$; over the window $[0.10, 0.30]$ in the figure the increment is
  $N(0, 0.20)$, whatever the path did before $0.10$.
- **A case with a different surface.** Fix $c > 0$ and set
  $X(t) = W(ct)/\sqrt c$. Its paths are continuous with $X(0) = 0$; its
  increments over disjoint windows are increments of $W$ over the disjoint
  windows $[ct_i, ct_{i+1}]$, divided by $\sqrt c$, hence independent and
  normal with variance $c(t_{i+1} - t_i)/c = t_{i+1} - t_i$. So $X$ is a
  Brownian motion: compressing time by $c$ and amplitude by $\sqrt c$ gives
  back the same process, which is the self-similarity the last section
  returns to.
- **The degenerate case.** The window of length zero: $t_{i+1} = t_i$
  gives the increment $0$, which is $N(0, 0)$, and the definition's
  requirement of strict inequalities excludes it only to keep the
  independence clause from being about the constant zero.
- **The near miss.** $Y(t) = W(t) + \mu t$ for a constant $\mu \ne 0$.
  Continuous, $Y(0) = 0$, independent increments, each normal with
  variance $t_{i+1} - t_i$: every clause holds except the mean, which is
  $\mu(t_{i+1} - t_i)$. This is *Brownian motion with drift*, and the
  clause that excludes it is the zero mean alone.

A second near miss fails a different clause. Take one standard normal $Z$
and set $X(t) = \sqrt t\,Z$. The paths are continuous, $X(0) = 0$, and
$X(t)$ is $N(0, t)$ at every $t$: the marginals are Brownian. The
increment $X(t) - X(s) = (\sqrt t - \sqrt s)Z$ is a multiple of $X(s)$,
not independent of it, and its variance is $(\sqrt t - \sqrt s)^2$, not
$t - s$. Marginal distributions do not make a Brownian motion; the
increment clause is the one that carries the structure. Produce one more
case before reading on: $-W(t)$. Decide which clauses, if any, the sign
change touches.

### Covariance: the increments fix the joint law

The increments determine everything else about the process's
distribution, and the covariance of two values is the first computation
the definition permits.

<Example title="E[W(s)W(t)] = min(s, t)">
Let $0 \le s < t$. Write $W(t) = W(s) + \bigl(W(t) - W(s)\bigr)$, so
$$
E[W(s)W(t)] = E\bigl[W(s)\bigl(W(t) - W(s)\bigr)\bigr] + E[W(s)^2]
= E[W(s)]\,E[W(t) - W(s)] + \mathrm{Var}\,W(s) = 0 \cdot 0 + s .
$$
The second equality uses independence of $W(s) = W(s) - W(0)$ from the
increment $W(t) - W(s)$, the two being increments over the disjoint windows
$[0, s]$ and $[s, t]$; the third uses $E[W(s)] = 0$ and
$\mathrm{Var}\,W(s) = s - 0$. Since the roles of $s$ and $t$ can be
swapped, $E[W(s)W(t)] = \min(s, t)$ for all $s, t \ge 0$.

**Check on a sum.** $\mathrm{Var}\bigl(W(2) + W(3)\bigr)
= \mathrm{Var}\,W(2) + \mathrm{Var}\,W(3) + 2\,E[W(2)W(3)] = 2 + 3 + 2 \cdot 2 = 9$.
The direct route: $W(2) + W(3) = 2W(2) + \bigl(W(3) - W(2)\bigr)$, a sum of
independent terms with variances $4 \cdot 2 = 8$ and $1$, total $9$.
</Example>

Because the increments are independent and normal, any vector
$(W(t_1), \ldots, W(t_m))$ is a linear image of independent normals and
so is jointly normal, and a jointly normal vector is determined by its
means and covariances: all zero, and $\min(t_i, t_j)$. Shreve's Theorem
3.3.2 records this as an equivalent definition; the course uses only the
increment form.

### Existence is a theorem, and this course does not prove it

Nothing above shows that an object satisfying the definition exists. The
scaled walks converge one time at a time, and the previous lesson's fence
said so; a process defined for all $t$ with continuous paths and the
stated increments is the content of Wiener's 1923 theorem, proved by
constructing a measure on the space of continuous functions. The course
takes the theorem as given. The construction lives in Karatzas and
Shreve, §2.2–2.4, which give three routes: Kolmogorov's extension theorem
plus a continuity criterion, a series of random Haar-type functions, and
Donsker's limit of the scaled walks. This is the first entry on the fence
the next section erects.

The information structure comes with the process. A *filtration for the
Brownian motion* is a family $\mathcal F(t)$, $t \ge 0$, of collections of
events such that information accumulates ($\mathcal F(s) \subseteq
\mathcal F(t)$ for $s < t$), $W(t)$ is determined by $\mathcal F(t)$, and
every future increment $W(u) - W(t)$, $u > t$, is independent of
$\mathcal F(t)$ (Shreve II, Definition 3.3.3). The third clause is the
continuous version of <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">the coin-toss
filtration's</Ref> property that tosses after $n$ are independent of the
first $n$, and it is the clause every martingale proof below uses. A
process $\Delta(t)$ is *adapted* if $\Delta(t)$ is determined by
$\mathcal F(t)$ for every $t$; the hedging strategies of the next lessons
are adapted processes.

lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">the calibrated tree converged

## The fence: what changes when every path has probability zero

The definition moved the course from a finite set of toss sequences to a
space of paths, and four facts change in the move. Each is stated here in
the form the rest of the course uses; two are restated definitions, two
are debts.

### Almost surely, and a random variable that is finite but has no mean

On the $N$-toss space of <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">module
2</Ref>, every sequence has probability $p^{\#H}q^{\#T} > 0$, and an event
of probability zero is the empty event. On the space of infinite toss
sequences, every single sequence has probability zero: the all-heads
sequence has probability $\lim_n p^n = 0$, and so does every other
particular sequence. The sequence is nonetheless in the sample space, and
no more unlikely than any other. The vocabulary that handles this: an
event of probability one is said to occur **almost surely**, abbreviated
a.s., and the phrase concedes that the outcomes it excludes may exist,
have probability zero taken all together, and are not the empty set
(Shreve II, Definition 1.1.5). Every path-level statement about $W$ in
this lesson is an almost-sure statement, and the theorem
$[W, W](T) = T$ is one.

Expectation changes with it. On the tree, $E[X]$ is a finite sum
$\sum_\omega X(\omega)P(\omega)$; on a space where every $\omega$ has
probability zero that sum is $0$ for every $X$, and expectation is
instead the Lebesgue integral $\int_\Omega X\,dP$, built from the
probabilities of sets rather than of points. This course states the
integral's properties (linearity, monotonicity, and the convergence
theorems) and uses them as on the tree. The one consequence with no
finite analogue is that a random variable can be finite almost surely and
still have no finite mean.

<Example title="the St. Petersburg variable (Shreve II, Example 1.4.8)">
Toss a fair coin until the first head. Let $X = 2^k$ if the first head
is on toss $k$, and $X = \infty$ on the all-tails sequence. The all-tails
sequence has probability zero, so $X < \infty$ almost surely. Its
expectation is
$$
E[X] = 2 \cdot \tfrac12 + 4 \cdot \tfrac14 + 8 \cdot \tfrac18 + \cdots
= 1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots = \infty .
$$
A variable that is finite on all but a probability-zero set can have
infinite mean; "finite a.s." and "integrable" are different conditions,
and the theorems below state which one they need.
</Example>

### Conditional expectation is defined by its properties, not computed

On the tree, $E_n[X]$ was *computed*: average $X$ over the unrevealed
tosses, weighting each continuation by its probability. On path space,
the atoms that computation divides by have probability zero, and the
division cannot be performed. The way round is to promote a property the
tree's computation had to the definition.

<Definition title="conditional expectation (Shreve II, 2.3.1)">
Let $\mathcal G$ be a sub-$\sigma$-algebra of $\mathcal F$, a collection
of events representing partial information, and let $X$ be integrable or
nonnegative. The **conditional expectation** $E[X \mid \mathcal G]$ is any
random variable such that (i) it is determined by the information in
$\mathcal G$, and (ii) it agrees with $X$ on average over every event
$A \in \mathcal G$:
$$
\int_A E[X \mid \mathcal G]\,dP = \int_A X\,dP \qquad \text{for all } A \in \mathcal G .
$$
Two random variables satisfying (i) and (ii) agree almost surely.
</Definition>

Property (ii) is the *partial-averaging* property, and on the tree it is
a one-line consequence of the computation: on each atom of the first-$n$
tosses, $E_n[X]$ is the constant whose integral over the atom equals the
integral of $X$. Here it is the definition, and existence of a random
variable satisfying it is a theorem, proved in Shreve II's Appendix B
from the Radon–Nikodym theorem (its Theorem 1.6.7). That is the second
debt. What is not a debt is the toolkit: linearity, taking out
what is known, the tower property, independence, and the conditional
Jensen inequality, the five properties <Ref
lesson="coin-toss-probability">module 2 proved as finite sums</Ref>, hold
verbatim under the new definition (Shreve II, Theorem 2.3.2), and every
martingale argument in this module invokes conditional expectation only
through them. The notation changes from $E_n[\cdot]$ to
$E[\cdot \mid \mathcal F(t)]$, and nothing else does.

## The martingale property follows from independent increments in two lines

With the toolkit carried over, the first property of the walk transfers
without change. <Ref lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">The
scaled-walk theorem</Ref> proved the walk a martingale from two facts, the
increment is independent of the past and its mean is zero, and the
definition supplies both.

<Theorem title="Brownian motion is a martingale (Shreve II, 3.3.4)">
For $0 \le s \le t$, $E[W(t) \mid \mathcal F(s)] = W(s)$.

<Proof>
Write $W(t) = \bigl(W(t) - W(s)\bigr) + W(s)$ and take conditional
expectation given $\mathcal F(s)$:
$$
E[W(t) \mid \mathcal F(s)] = E\bigl[W(t) - W(s) \mid \mathcal F(s)\bigr] + E[W(s) \mid \mathcal F(s)]
= E\bigl[W(t) - W(s)\bigr] + W(s) = 0 + W(s).
$$
The first equality is linearity. In the second, the increment is
independent of $\mathcal F(s)$ by the filtration's third clause, so its
conditional expectation is its plain expectation; and $W(s)$ is
determined by $\mathcal F(s)$, so it is taken out as known. The plain
expectation of the increment is zero by definition.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The same two facts give a second martingale, the one whose compensator is
the elapsed time, and it is the first place quadratic variation appears
in a conditional expectation.

<Example title="W²(t) − t is a martingale (Shreve II, Exercise 3.2)">
For $0 \le s \le t$, expand $W(t)^2$ around $W(s)$:
$$
W(t)^2 = \bigl(W(t) - W(s)\bigr)^2 + 2W(s)\bigl(W(t) - W(s)\bigr) + W(s)^2 .
$$
Condition on $\mathcal F(s)$ term by term. The first term is a function
of an increment independent of $\mathcal F(s)$, so its conditional
expectation is $E[(W(t) - W(s))^2] = t - s$. In the second, $W(s)$ comes
out as known and the increment's conditional mean is $0$. The third is
known. So $E[W(t)^2 \mid \mathcal F(s)] = (t - s) + W(s)^2$, and
$$
E\bigl[W(t)^2 - t \mid \mathcal F(s)\bigr] = W(s)^2 - s .
$$
The process $W^2$ itself is a submartingale, drifting up at rate one per
unit time; subtracting $t$ removes exactly the accumulated variance. The
walk's version, $M_k^2 - k$, was the martingale <Ref
lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">the previous lesson's third
exercise</Ref> used to compute hitting times.
</Example>

<Exercise>
Show that for a constant $\sigma$, $Z(t) = \exp\bigl\{\sigma W(t) -
\tfrac12\sigma^2 t\bigr\}$ is a martingale. Write down the factorisation
of $Z(t)/Z(s)$ before opening the solution; the moment-generating
function $E\,e^{uX} = e^{u^2 v/2}$ of $X \sim N(0, v)$, computed in the
previous lesson, is the one external fact needed.

<Solution>
Strategy: split $Z(t)$ into a known factor and a factor built from the
increment, then use independence.

$$
Z(t) = Z(s)\,\exp\Bigl\{\sigma\bigl(W(t) - W(s)\bigr) - \tfrac12\sigma^2(t - s)\Bigr\}.
$$
Given $\mathcal F(s)$, $Z(s)$ is known and comes out; the remaining
factor is a function of the increment $W(t) - W(s) \sim N(0, t - s)$,
independent of $\mathcal F(s)$, so its conditional expectation is its
plain one:
$$
E\Bigl[e^{\sigma(W(t) - W(s))}\Bigr]\,e^{-\frac12\sigma^2(t - s)}
= e^{\frac12\sigma^2(t - s)}\,e^{-\frac12\sigma^2(t - s)} = 1 .
$$
Hence $E[Z(t) \mid \mathcal F(s)] = Z(s)$. The transferable move: for any
function of the increment, conditioning on $\mathcal F(s)$ is the same as
taking the plain expectation, and a martingale results whenever that
plain expectation is $1$. The factor $e^{-\sigma^2 t/2}$ is the
continuous form of the $1/\cosh^n\sigma$ that made $e^{\sigma M_n}$ a
martingale on the tree, and it is the same $-\tfrac12\sigma^2$ the
lognormal limit carries.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## Two variations: the path has no length, and quadratic variation exactly $T$

The martingale property transferred because it was a statement about
distributions. The walk's third property, quadratic variation, was a
statement about a single path, and it is not inherited by passing to the
limit distribution: on the walk the sum of squared increments was exact
at the walk's own mesh; $W$ has no mesh, so the sum must be taken over a
chosen partition, the partition refined, and the limit of the sums
proved to exist and to equal $T$.

### Smooth functions: finite length, zero quadratic variation

Let $f$ be a function on $[0, T]$ with a continuous derivative, and let
$\Pi = \{t_0, t_1, \ldots, t_n\}$ be a partition,
$0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = T$, with mesh
$\|\Pi\| = \max_j (t_{j+1} - t_j)$. By the mean value theorem each
increment is a slope times a width, $f(t_{j+1}) - f(t_j) =
f'(t_j^*)\,(t_{j+1} - t_j)$ for some $t_j^*$ in the cell. The
*first-order variation* is the total rise and fall,
$$
FV_T(f) = \lim_{\|\Pi\| \to 0} \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \bigl|f(t_{j+1}) - f(t_j)\bigr|
= \lim_{\|\Pi\| \to 0} \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} |f'(t_j^*)|\,(t_{j+1} - t_j)
= \int_0^T |f'(t)|\,dt ,
$$
the limit of a Riemann sum; it is finite. The *quadratic variation*
squares the increments instead:
$$
[f, f](T) = \lim_{\|\Pi\| \to 0} \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \bigl(f(t_{j+1}) - f(t_j)\bigr)^2
\le \lim_{\|\Pi\| \to 0} \|\Pi\| \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} |f'(t_j^*)|^2\,(t_{j+1} - t_j)
= \lim_{\|\Pi\| \to 0} \|\Pi\| \cdot \int_0^T |f'(t)|^2\,dt = 0 ,
$$
one factor of $(t_{j+1} - t_j)$ having been bounded by the mesh and the
other left to make a Riemann sum (Shreve II, Remark 3.4.2). A function
with a continuous derivative has finite length and zero quadratic
variation, which is why the quantity never appears in ordinary calculus.
The argument needs the derivative: the mean value theorem is the step
that turns an increment into a width times a bounded slope, and a
function without a derivative at some point in a cell gives it nothing to
work with.

### The theorem: $[W, W](T) = T$, proved in mean square

For Brownian motion there is no derivative to invoke, and the argument
runs on the increments' distribution instead. The claim is that the
sampled sum, a random variable that depends on the path, converges to
the constant $T$.

<Theorem title="quadratic variation of Brownian motion (Shreve II, 3.4.3)">
Let $W$ be a Brownian motion. For every $T \ge 0$,
$[W, W](T) = T$ almost surely: for partitions $\Pi$ of $[0, T]$ the sampled
quadratic variation
$$
Q_\Pi = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \bigl(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr)^2
$$
satisfies $E[Q_\Pi] = T$ exactly and $\mathrm{Var}(Q_\Pi) \le 2\|\Pi\|T$,
so $Q_\Pi \to T$ in mean square as $\|\Pi\| \to 0$.

<Proof>
Goal: show the mean of $Q_\Pi$ is $T$ and its variance tends to zero. Mode:
direct computation of two moments. Roadmap: one cell's mean, one cell's
variance, then add across independent cells.

**One cell's mean.** The increment $\Delta_j = W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)$ is
$N(0, t_{j+1} - t_j)$, so $E[\Delta_j^2] = \mathrm{Var}(\Delta_j) = t_{j+1} - t_j$.

**One cell's variance.** $\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_j^2) = E[\Delta_j^4] - (E[\Delta_j^2])^2$.
The fourth moment of a normal variable with mean zero is three times the
square of its variance, $E[\Delta_j^4] = 3(t_{j+1} - t_j)^2$; this is the
one computation the proof takes from outside, and the fourth-moment
exercise at the end of the lesson supplies it.
Hence
$$
\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_j^2) = 3(t_{j+1} - t_j)^2 - (t_{j+1} - t_j)^2 = 2(t_{j+1} - t_j)^2 .
$$

**Adding across cells.** The $\Delta_j$ are increments over disjoint
windows, hence independent, so means and variances both add:
$$
E[Q_\Pi] = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} (t_{j+1} - t_j) = T,
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}(Q_\Pi) = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} 2(t_{j+1} - t_j)^2
\le 2\|\Pi\| \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} (t_{j+1} - t_j) = 2\|\Pi\|T .
$$
The mean telescopes to $T$ for every partition, exactly. The variance is
bounded by the mesh times a constant and tends to zero. Therefore
$E[(Q_\Pi - T)^2] = \mathrm{Var}(Q_\Pi) \to 0$: $Q_\Pi$ converges to $T$ in
mean square, along whatever path it is computed.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

  src="./figures/l2-proof.svg"
  alt="The sampled quadratic variation drawn as a row of independent blocks, one per cell, each labelled with its mean (the cell's length) and its variance (twice the cell's length squared); below, the means telescope to T and the variances sum to at most twice the mesh times T"
  caption="The proof in one picture: independent blocks whose means telescope to T on every partition and whose variances add to at most 2‖Π‖T."

After the proof: the idea in one sentence is that each squared increment
has mean equal to its cell and a standard deviation of the same order as
its cell, and a sum of $n$ such independent terms has mean $T$ and
standard deviation of order $\sqrt n \cdot (T/n) = T/\sqrt n$. The
step that would fail for a walk with heavy-tailed increments is the
fourth-moment computation: an increment with infinite fourth moment gives
$\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_j^2) = \infty$ and the argument has nothing to bound
(the simulation exercise at the end of the lesson runs that case). For
the equal-mesh partition into $n$ cells
the variance is exactly $2T^2/n$, so the standard deviation of $Q_\Pi$
around $T$ is $T\sqrt{2/n}$: at $n = 8$ it is $0.5\,T$, which is why the
figure's frame 3 could read $0.85$; at $n = 64$ it is $0.18\,T$.

One caveat, stated precisely because the notation $[W, W](T)$ hides it.
The convergence proved is in mean square, for every sequence of
partitions whose mesh tends to zero. Mean-square convergence gives
almost-sure convergence along a subsequence (fence item 4); along a
sequence of partitions whose meshes are summable, such as the dyadic
ones with mesh $2^{-k}$, Chebyshev's inequality applied to the variance
bound and the Borel–Cantelli lemma give almost-sure convergence with no
subsequence, which is the sense in which "$= T$ almost surely" is
written. Summability is the hypothesis the almost-sure statement rests
on: along a deterministic sequence of partitions whose meshes tend to
zero without being summable, the mean-square convergence still holds and
almost-sure convergence can fail. The sharp condition is Dudley's, mesh
$o(1/\log n)$ along the sequence, and de la Vega exhibited a sequence
of partitions with meshes tending to zero but not that fast along which
the sums do not converge almost surely (Dudley 1973; de la Vega 1974).
The theorem is about partitions fixed in advance.

### First-order variation: the path has no length

The walk's first-order variation over $[0, t]$ was $t\sqrt n$ at every
refinement, on every path, and the limit inherits the divergence. The
proof uses the quadratic variation theorem and continuity together.

<Theorem title="first-order variation of Brownian motion is infinite (Shreve II, Exercise 3.4)">
Almost surely, $\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \bigl|W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr| \to \infty$
as $\|\Pi\| \to 0$ along a sequence of partitions on which $Q_\Pi \to T$.

<Proof>
Each squared increment is at most the largest absolute increment times
the absolute increment:
$$
Q_\Pi = \sum_j |\Delta_j|^2 \le \Bigl(\max_k |\Delta_k|\Bigr) \sum_j |\Delta_j| ,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\sum_j |\Delta_j| \ge \frac{Q_\Pi}{\max_k |\Delta_k|} .
$$
As $\|\Pi\| \to 0$ the numerator tends to $T > 0$ on the almost-sure
subsequence fence item 4 provides. The denominator tends to $0$: a
continuous function on the closed interval $[0, T]$ is uniformly
continuous, so the largest increment over any cell shorter than $\|\Pi\|$
tends to zero with the mesh, on every path. A quantity bounded below by
something tending to $T/0$ tends to infinity.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The two theorems together place Brownian paths outside ordinary calculus
on both counts: a function with a derivative has finite length and zero
quadratic variation, a Brownian path has infinite length and quadratic
variation $T$, and no continuous function has finite length together with
nonzero quadratic variation, because the inequality in the proof runs the
other way too: $Q_\Pi \le \max_k|\Delta_k| \cdot FV$, and finite $FV$ with
$\max|\Delta_k| \to 0$ forces $Q_\Pi \to 0$.

<KeyFact>
The Riemann–Stieltjes integral $\int \Delta\,dW$ needs an integrator of
finite length and does not exist against a Brownian path; <Ref
lesson="ito-integral">the Itô integral</Ref> is built on the quadratic
variation instead of the length, and exists.
</KeyFact>

## What $dW\,dW = dt$ says, and the two readings it does not license

The theorem is written in shorthand as $dW(t)\,dW(t) = dt$, and the
shorthand is used in every stochastic calculus from here to the end of
the course. Its correct reading is the theorem itself; two tempting
readings are false, and the second is a genuine and widespread error.

The correct statement first: **over any interval, the sum of squared
increments of $W$ converges to the interval's length**, with a mean that
is exact for every partition and a variance that shrinks with the mesh.
Over $[T_1, T_2]$ the same proof gives $Q_\Pi \to T_2 - T_1$, so Brownian
motion accumulates quadratic variation at rate one per unit time, and the
$dt$ on the right of $dW\,dW = dt$ carries an understood coefficient of
$1$. The reading to reject is the one the notation invites: that each
squared increment is approximately its time step,
$(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j))^2 \approx t_{j+1} - t_j$ for small cells. It is
rejected because it is either empty or false. Read as an approximation of
two small numbers, it says nothing: both sides tend to zero, and the
statement would remain "true" with the right side doubled or squared.
Read as the ratio being near one,
$$
\frac{\bigl(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr)^2}{t_{j+1} - t_j} \approx 1 ,
$$
it is false at every mesh: the increment is $\sqrt{t_{j+1} - t_j}\,Z$
with $Z$ standard normal, so the ratio is $Z^2$, a random variable whose
distribution does not depend on the cell at all. It has mean $1$,
standard deviation $\sqrt 2$, is below $0.1$ a quarter of the time and
above $2$ one time in six, at $\Delta t = 1$ and at $\Delta t = 10^{-4}$
alike. No single squared increment is ever close to its cell.

<Figure
  src="./figures/chi-square.svg"
  alt="Left: the density of the ratio of one squared Brownian increment to its time step, a chi-square curve with one degree of freedom that is the same at every step size, with its mean at 1 and its tail probabilities marked; right: the density of the sampled quadratic variation divided by T over n equal cells, narrowing around 1 as n grows from 4 to 256"
  caption="One term never concentrates: (ΔW)²/Δt is Z² at every Δt. The sum does: Q_Π/T has mean 1 and standard deviation √(2/n)."

The replacement model is the law of large numbers. Over $n$ equal cells,
$Q_\Pi = \sum_j (T/n)\,Z_j^2$ with the $Z_j$ independent standard normals,
so $Q_\Pi/T$ is the average of $n$ independent copies of $Z^2$, each with
mean $1$: the average converges to $1$ although no term is near $1$,
because the terms' deviations from their mean, each of order one, cancel
in the sum. That is the whole content of $dW\,dW = dt$, and the
discriminating example is the figure's left panel: a quarter of the
squared increments are below a tenth of their cell, at every mesh, and
the sum is within $\sqrt{2/n}$ of $T$ anyway.

</Task>

<Remark title="the multiplication table: dW dW = dt, dW dt = 0, dt dt = 0 (Shreve II, Remarks 3.4.4–3.4.5)">
Two more sums on the same partition go to zero, and the size arguments
are one line each. The cross variation of $W$ with $t$:
$$
\Bigl|\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \bigl(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr)(t_{j+1} - t_j)\Bigr|
\le \max_k |\Delta_k| \cdot \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} (t_{j+1} - t_j) = \max_k |\Delta_k| \cdot T \to 0 ,
$$
by the uniform continuity of the path. The quadratic variation of $t$
itself:
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} (t_{j+1} - t_j)^2 \le \|\Pi\| \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} (t_{j+1} - t_j) = \|\Pi\|T \to 0 .
$$
Recorded as $dW\,dt = 0$ and $dt\,dt = 0$, they say that in any sum of
products of increments over a refining partition, only the $dW\,dW$
products survive, and those add up to elapsed time. <Ref
lesson="ito-formula">The Itô–Doeblin formula</Ref> is the second-order
Taylor expansion with exactly these three rules applied to the
second-order terms; the notation is a bookkeeping device for the three
limits, and nothing in it refers to a single increment.

On notation: this course writes $[W, W](T)$ for the quadratic variation,
following Shreve; much of the literature writes $\langle W \rangle_T$ for
the same quantity, and the angle bracket is the form in which Girsanov's
theorem and the martingale representation theorem are usually stated.
The two symbols name one object here.
</Remark>

## Roughness at every scale, and the volatility one path reveals

The two variation theorems describe the path over an interval. Two
consequences describe it at a point and at a magnification, and a third
turns the quadratic variation into a measurement.

### Nowhere differentiable, and self-similar under zoom

A function differentiable on an interval has zero quadratic variation
there. A Brownian path accumulates quadratic variation at rate one on
every interval. So no stretch of a Brownian path, however short, is a
smooth function: if the path had a continuous derivative on some
$[a, b]$, its quadratic variation over $[a, b]$ would be zero instead of
$b - a$. This is a heuristic for the stronger fact, which is fenced: with
probability one, a Brownian path is differentiable at *no* point (Paley,
Wiener and Zygmund, 1933; Karatzas and Shreve Theorem 2.9.18). The
heuristic rules out smoothness on intervals; the theorem rules out a
derivative at even a single time, and its proof is a counting argument on
increments over three consecutive cells that the course does not carry.

The absence of a derivative is what a magnified path looks like. A smooth
curve under a microscope straightens: on a window of width $h$ around
$t_0$ its rise is about $f'(t_0)h$, and refitting the axes shows a line
whose height shrinks in proportion to the width. Brownian motion under
the same microscope does not straighten, and the scaling case of the
definition says exactly why: $W(ct)/\sqrt c$ is again a Brownian motion,
so the path on a window of width $h$, with its height divided by
$\sqrt h$, has the same distribution as the path on a window of width
$1$. Magnifying the time axis by $10$ and the value axis by
$\sqrt{10} = 3.2$ returns a statistically identical picture, at every
depth.

### The volatility of a stock, read from one price record

The quadratic variation theorem has one direct use before any calculus is
built: it turns a price record into a measurement of $\sigma$. Let
$S(t) = S(0)\exp\{\sigma W(t) + (\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t\}$ be the
geometric Brownian motion of the lognormal limit, with constant $\sigma$
and drift $\alpha$, observed at partition times $t_0 < \cdots < t_m$ of a
window $[T_1, T_2]$. The log-return over one cell is
$$
\log\frac{S(t_{j+1})}{S(t_j)} = \sigma\bigl(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr) + \bigl(\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2\bigr)(t_{j+1} - t_j),
$$
and the sum of its squares expands into three sums:
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Bigl(\log\frac{S(t_{j+1})}{S(t_j)}\Bigr)^2
= \sigma^2 \sum_j \Delta_j^2
+ 2\sigma\bigl(\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2\bigr)\sum_j \Delta_j\,(t_{j+1} - t_j)
+ \bigl(\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2\bigr)^2 \sum_j (t_{j+1} - t_j)^2 .
$$
The three sums are the three entries of the multiplication table: the
first tends to $T_2 - T_1$, the second and third to zero. So
$$
\frac{1}{T_2 - T_1}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Bigl(\log\frac{S(t_{j+1})}{S(t_j)}\Bigr)^2 \to \sigma^2 ,
$$
and the drift $\alpha$, whatever it is, has dropped out (Shreve II,
§3.4.3). The sum on the left is the *realized variance* of the record;
its square root per unit time is the *realized volatility*.

The figure below shows a geometric Brownian motion with $\sigma = 0.2$,
$\alpha = 0.08$, $S(0) = 100$, simulated over one year at $10$ ticks per
trading day, $2{,}520$ log-returns in all. The path ends at $66.8$ after

$0.04\,t$ and reaches $0.0398$ at $t = 1$, so the estimate of $\sigma$ is

ratio to $\sigma^2 T$ is one draw of $Z^2$ and says nothing. The
theorem's spread, $\sigma^2 T\sqrt{2/m}$, is $0.0011$ at $m = 2{,}520$ and
$0.016$ at $m = 12$; both observed errors are inside it.
</Example>

<Exercise>
A stock's closing prices are recorded daily for two years, $504$
log-returns, and the sum of their squares is $0.0722$. Under the geometric
Brownian motion model, estimate $\sigma$, and give the standard deviation
of the estimate of $\sigma^2$ that the quadratic variation theorem
implies. State first which quantity in the theorem the record's sum
approximates, and with what $T$.

<Solution>
Strategy: the sum approximates $\sigma^2 T$ with $T = 2$ years, and its
spread is $\sigma^2 T\sqrt{2/m}$ with $m = 504$.

$\hat\sigma^2 = 0.0722 / 2 = 0.0361$ per year, so $\hat\sigma = 0.19$. The
sampled quadratic variation of $\sigma W$ over $m$ equal cells has mean
$\sigma^2 T$ and standard deviation $\sigma^2 T\sqrt{2/m} = 0.0722 \times
\sqrt{2/504} = 0.0722 \times 0.063 = 0.0045$, so the estimate of
$\sigma^2$ per year, $0.0361$, carries a standard deviation of about
$0.0023$, six percent of its value; the estimate of $\sigma$ carries about
half that in relative terms, three percent, since a relative error in a
square is twice the relative error in its root. The transferable move:
the precision of realized volatility is set by the number of cells,
$\sqrt{2/m}$, and not by the length of the window, and the drift never
enters. In real records the cells cannot be shrunk without limit: between
trades there is no price, and at the finest scales the bid–ask bounce
adds squared increments the model does not contain, which is the point at
which <Ref lesson="realized-implied-smile">the realized-volatility
estimators of module 8</Ref> take over from the plain sum.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

What is established: Brownian motion is defined by continuous paths from
zero and independent normal increments with variance equal to elapsed
time, its existence being the first of four fenced facts, listed once
with the places their proofs live; conditional expectation on path space
is defined by partial averaging, its existence is the second fenced fact,
and its five properties carry over unchanged, so that the martingale
property of $W$ and of $W^2 - t$ follow from independent increments in
two lines each; a Brownian path has infinite first-order variation and
quadratic variation $[W, W](T) = T$, proved in mean square with mean $T$
exact on every partition and variance at most $2\|\Pi\|T$; the notation
$dW\,dW = dt$ records that convergence and not any statement about a
single increment, whose ratio to its cell is $Z^2$ at every mesh; the
path is nowhere differentiable and statistically identical under
magnification; and the quadratic variation of $\log S$ over a window is
$\sigma^2$ times the window, a measurement of volatility from one record
that the drift never touches. What is not established is any way to
integrate against $W$: a path of infinite length supports no
Riemann–Stieltjes integral, and the gain of a strategy holding
$\Delta(t)$ shares, $\int \Delta\,dW$, has no meaning yet. <Ref
lesson="ito-integral">The next lesson</Ref> constructs that integral for
adapted integrands, using the quadratic variation in place of the length,
and computes $\int_0^T W\,dW$, whose value differs from the calculus
answer by exactly $\tfrac12 T$.

<Exercise>
Three quantities from the increment structure. (a) Given $W(1) = x$,
compute $P\{W(2) \le 1 \mid W(1) = x\}$ in terms of the standard normal
distribution function $\Phi$. (b) Compute $E[W(2)W(3)]$ and
$E[W(2)\,(W(5) - W(3))]$. (c) Compute $E[W(1)W(2)W(3)]$. Name for each the
clause of the definition the computation rests on.

<Solution>
**(a)** $W(2) = W(1) + \bigl(W(2) - W(1)\bigr)$, and the increment is
$N(0, 1)$, independent of $\mathcal F(1)$ and hence of $W(1)$. Given
$W(1) = x$, $W(2)$ is $x$ plus a standard normal, so
$P\{W(2) \le 1 \mid W(1) = x\} = P\{Z \le 1 - x\} = \Phi(1 - x)$. Clause:
independence of the increment from the past, plus its distribution.

**(b)** $E[W(2)W(3)] = \min(2, 3) = 2$ by the covariance example. For the
second, $W(2)$ is an increment over $[0, 2]$ and $W(5) - W(3)$ an
increment over $[3, 5]$, disjoint windows, so they are independent and
$E[W(2)(W(5) - W(3))] = E[W(2)]\,E[W(5) - W(3)] = 0$. Clause:
independence of increments over disjoint windows.

**(c)** Write $W(3) = W(2) + \bigl(W(3) - W(2)\bigr)$ and
$W(2) = W(1) + \bigl(W(2) - W(1)\bigr)$. Then
$E[W(1)W(2)W(3)] = E[W(1)W(2)^2] + E[W(1)W(2)]\,E[W(3) - W(2)] = E[W(1)W(2)^2]$,
and expanding $W(2)^2 = W(1)^2 + 2W(1)(W(2) - W(1)) + (W(2) - W(1))^2$,
$$
E[W(1)W(2)^2] = E[W(1)^3] + 2E[W(1)^2]\,E[W(2) - W(1)] + E[W(1)]\,E[(W(2) - W(1))^2] = 0 + 0 + 0 .
$$
Every odd moment of a centred normal is zero, and every cross term
factors by independence into something containing a first moment. So
$E[W(1)W(2)W(3)] = 0$. Clause: the zero mean, used through the symmetry
of the normal. The transferable move: decompose into increments over
disjoint windows, then multiply out and let independence kill every term
with a lone first moment.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The quadratic variation proof took $E[X^4] = 3v^2$ for $X \sim N(0, v)$
from outside. (a) Prove it from the moment-generating function
$\varphi(u) = E\,e^{uX} = e^{u^2 v/2}$ by differentiating four times at
$u = 0$. (b) Use it to verify $\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_j^2) = 2(t_{j+1} - t_j)^2$
and, for the partition of $[0, T]$ into $n$ equal cells, compute
$\mathrm{Var}(Q_\Pi)$ exactly. (c) State what the same computation gives
for the sampled *cubic* variation $\sum_j |\Delta_j|^3$: its mean, and its
limit.

<Solution>
**(a)** $\varphi(u) = e^{u^2 v/2}$. Successive derivatives:
$\varphi' = uv\,\varphi$; $\varphi'' = (v + u^2v^2)\varphi$;
$\varphi''' = (3uv^2 + u^3v^3)\varphi$;
$\varphi'''' = (3v^2 + 6u^2v^3 + u^4v^4)\varphi$. Since
$\varphi^{(k)}(0) = E[X^k]$, this gives $E[X] = 0$, $E[X^2] = v$,
$E[X^3] = 0$, $E[X^4] = 3v^2$. (Shreve II, Exercise 3.3, runs the same
differentiation.)

**(b)** With $v = t_{j+1} - t_j$:
$\mathrm{Var}(\Delta_j^2) = E[\Delta_j^4] - (E[\Delta_j^2])^2 = 3v^2 - v^2 = 2v^2$.
For $n$ equal cells, $v = T/n$ for every $j$, and independence gives
$\mathrm{Var}(Q_\Pi) = n \cdot 2(T/n)^2 = 2T^2/n$, so the standard
deviation of $Q_\Pi$ is $T\sqrt{2/n}$: the bound $2\|\Pi\|T = 2T^2/n$ in
the theorem is attained with equality for equal cells.

**(c)** $|\Delta_j|^3 = v^{3/2}|Z|^3$ and $E|Z|^3 = 2\sqrt{2/\pi}$ (the
integral $2\int_0^\infty z^3 e^{-z^2/2}\,dz/\sqrt{2\pi}$, which is
$2 \cdot 2/\sqrt{2\pi}$). So
$E\sum_j |\Delta_j|^3 = 2\sqrt{2/\pi}\sum_j (t_{j+1} - t_j)^{3/2}
\le 2\sqrt{2/\pi}\,\|\Pi\|^{1/2}\,T \to 0$; a nonnegative sum with mean
tending to zero tends to zero in probability, so the cubic variation is
$0$ (Shreve II, Exercise 3.4(ii)). The transferable move: the $k$th
variation of $W$ over a cell scales like $(\Delta t)^{k/2}$, and summing
$n \approx T/\Delta t$ of them gives $(\Delta t)^{k/2 - 1}T$, which
diverges for $k = 1$, is $T$ for $k = 2$, and vanishes for $k \ge 3$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Apply the sampled quadratic variation to two processes that are not
Brownian motions. (a) $X(t) = ct$ for a constant $c$: compute
$[X, X](T)$. (b) $Y(t) = \sigma W(t) + ct$: compute $[Y, Y](T)$, showing
that the cross terms vanish and stating the rate at which their expected
absolute size goes to zero with the mesh. (c) State the general principle,
and name the formula of a later lesson it licenses.

<Solution>
**(a)** $\sum_j (c\,\Delta t_j)^2 = c^2 \sum_j (\Delta t_j)^2 \le c^2\|\Pi\|T \to 0$.
A line has zero quadratic variation, whatever its slope; this is
$dt\,dt = 0$.

**(b)** Expand the square of $\Delta Y_j = \sigma\Delta_j + c\,\Delta t_j$:
$$
\sum_j (\Delta Y_j)^2 = \sigma^2 \sum_j \Delta_j^2 + 2\sigma c \sum_j \Delta_j\,\Delta t_j + c^2 \sum_j (\Delta t_j)^2 .
$$
The first sum tends to $T$, the third to $0$ by (a). The middle sum is
bounded by $\max_k|\Delta_k| \cdot T \to 0$ by continuity, which is
$dW\,dt = 0$; for the rate, $E|\Delta_j\,\Delta t_j| = \sqrt{2/\pi}\,(\Delta t_j)^{3/2}$,
so over $n$ equal cells the expected absolute size of the middle sum is
at most $2\sigma|c|\sqrt{2/\pi}\; n\,(T/n)^{3/2} = 2\sigma|c|\sqrt{2/\pi}\,T^{3/2}/\sqrt n$,
which goes to zero like $1/\sqrt n$. Hence $[Y, Y](T) = \sigma^2 T$.

**(c)** Quadratic variation sees the noise and is blind to the drift: any
term of order $\Delta t$ per cell contributes nothing in the limit, and
only the term of order $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ survives, squared. For an Itô
process $dX = \mu\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ this is the rule
$dX\,dX = \sigma^2\,dt$ that <Ref lesson="ito-formula">the Itô–Doeblin
formula</Ref> uses for its second-order term, and it is the reason a
change of measure, which moves only the drift, cannot change the
volatility. The transferable move: to find the quadratic variation of a process, write
one increment, square it, and keep only the term whose square is of
order $\Delta t$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim. "Each squared increment $(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j))^2$ has
mean $t_{j+1} - t_j$, and its variance $2(t_{j+1} - t_j)^2$ is much
smaller than its mean when the cell is small. So for a fine partition
each squared increment is approximately equal to its cell, and
$dW\,dW = dt$ is the statement that this approximation holds term by
term." Give the correct statement, exhibit the ratio that exposes the
error, and name the step that fails.

<Solution>
The correct statement is the theorem: the *sum* of squared increments
over $[0, T]$ converges to $T$ in mean square, with the sum's standard
deviation $T\sqrt{2/n}$ over $n$ equal cells. No statement about a single
term is part of it.

The exposing ratio is $(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j))^2 / (t_{j+1} - t_j) = Z_j^2$,
with $Z_j$ standard normal. Its distribution is chi-square with one
degree of freedom at every cell size: mean $1$, standard deviation
$\sqrt 2$, below $0.1$ with probability $0.25$, above $2$ with probability
$0.16$, above $4$ with probability $0.05$. "Approximately equal to its
cell" would mean the ratio is near $1$, and it is near $1$ (within
$\pm 0.5$) only about a quarter of the time, at $\Delta t = 1$ and at
$\Delta t = 10^{-6}$ alike.

The step that fails is "variance much smaller than mean, so the variable
is near its mean". The variance $2(\Delta t)^2$ is smaller than the mean
$\Delta t$ only because both are small; the comparison that governs
concentration is the standard deviation against the mean, and
$\sqrt{2}\,\Delta t$ against $\Delta t$ is a ratio of $\sqrt 2$ at every
mesh. The claim is exactly Shreve's rejected reading (3.4.9), and what
rescues the sum is not that the terms settle but that their
order-one deviations are independent and cancel: $n$ of them, each with
standard deviation $\sqrt 2\,T/n$, add to a standard deviation of
$\sqrt{2n}\,T/n = T\sqrt{2/n}$. The transferable move: a claim that a
random variable is "near its mean" is a claim about the standard
deviation relative to the mean, never about the variance.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Three sentences on what the limit weakened, and one computation. The
scaled walk of the previous lesson has $[W^{(n)}, W^{(n)}](t) = t$
*exactly, on every path*, at its own mesh; Brownian motion has
$[W, W](T) = T$ only almost surely and in mean square. (a) Say what
property of the walk's increments made its statement exact and why the
limit process cannot have that property. (b) The walk's quadratic
variation is exact only at its own mesh. Compute the variance of the
walk's sampled quadratic variation over $[0, 1]$ when the partition
uses $m$ cells of $k = n/m$ steps each, using $E[M_k^4] = 3k^2 - 2k$ from
the previous lesson's kurtosis exercise, and compare it with the Brownian
value $2/m$.

<Solution>
**(a)** The walk's squared one-step increment is $1/n$ on every path: a
constant, with zero variance. Its sum over $nt$ steps is therefore the
constant $t$, with no probability involved. Brownian motion's increments
are normal, and a normal variable's square is not constant, so any
sampled sum is a random variable with positive variance, and the most
that can be true is that the variance goes to zero, which is a limit
statement, true almost surely rather than everywhere. The finite case was
stronger because the two-point increment distribution has a degenerate
square; the limit replaced two points by a continuum and lost the
degeneracy.

**(b)** A cell of $k$ steps has increment $M_k/\sqrt n$, squared
$M_k^2/n$, with $\mathrm{Var}(M_k^2) = E[M_k^4] - (E[M_k^2])^2
= 3k^2 - 2k - k^2 = 2k^2 - 2k$. The $m$ cells are independent, so
$$
\mathrm{Var}\bigl(Q^{\text{walk}}_\Pi\bigr) = m\,\frac{2k^2 - 2k}{n^2}
= \frac{m(2k^2 - 2k)}{(mk)^2} = \frac{2 - 2/k}{m} .
$$
At $k = 1$ (the walk's own mesh) it is $0$, the exact case. For $k \to
\infty$ it tends to $2/m$, the Brownian value computed in the
fourth-moment exercise. The walk's
sampled quadratic variation is *less* variable than Brownian motion's at
every coarse mesh, by the factor $1 - 1/k$, which is the platykurtosis
$-2/k$ of the $k$-step increment measured in the previous lesson. The
transferable move: the limit object inherits the walk's mean behaviour
exactly and its fluctuations only in the limit, and the difference is
measured by the fourth moment.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Simulate one Brownian path on $[0, 1]$ with $n = 10^4$ steps and compute
the sampled quadratic variation over $m = 10, 100, 1{,}000, 10{,}000$
equal cells. Before running, tabulate the standard deviation
$\sqrt{2/m}$ the theorem predicts at each $m$. Then replace the normal
increments by scaled Student-$t$ increments with $3$ degrees of freedom,
normalised to variance $\Delta t$, and repeat; report also the running
sample variance of the ratio (increment)$^2/\Delta t$ over the first
$10^2$, $10^3$, $10^4$ increments for both walks, and say which step of
the theorem's proof the second walk breaks.

<Solution>
**Predicted spread.** $\sqrt{2/m} = 0.447,\ 0.141,\ 0.045,\ 0.014$ at
$m = 10, 100, 1{,}000, 10{,}000$. Agreement means the sampled value lies
within about two of these of $1$.

**One Gaussian run** (fixed seed): $0.93,\ 1.21,\ 1.01,\ 0.99$. The
$m = 100$ value is $1.5$ standard deviations high, the rest within one.
The running sample variance of $Z^2$: $3.5$ at $10^2$, $2.1$ at $10^3$,
$1.98$ at $10^4$, converging to the $2$ the theorem uses.

**The Student-$t(3)$ walk** has increments with finite variance, so
$E[(\Delta X)^2] = \Delta t$ still and the mean of the sampled sum is
still $1$; but the fourth moment of $t(3)$ is infinite, so
$\mathrm{Var}((\Delta X)^2) = \infty$. One run: sampled quadratic
variations $0.82,\ 0.85,\ 0.81,\ 0.90$, drifting toward $1$ far more
slowly than the Gaussian walk; the running sample variance of the ratio
is $135$ at $10^2$, $26$ at $10^3$, $15$ at $10^4$, never settling, because
the sample variance of a variable with infinite variance is dominated by
its largest observation and grows without bound as more are drawn.

**The step that breaks.** "One cell's variance" in the proof: with
$E[\Delta^4] = \infty$ there is no bound $2\|\Pi\|T$ on the variance of
the sum, and the mean-square argument does not start. Convergence of the
sum to $1$ still holds in probability, by the weak law of large numbers
applied to the squared increments, which have a finite mean, but its rate
is no longer $1/\sqrt m$: the squared $t(3)$ increment has a tail
$P\{(\Delta X)^2/\Delta t > y\} \sim y^{-3/2}$, in the domain of a
$\tfrac32$-stable law, and the sum's fluctuations decay like $m^{-1/3}$.
The transferable move: the quadratic variation theorem's *statement* needs
only finite variance of the increments; its *rate* needs the fourth
moment, and heavy-tailed increments keep the limit while losing the rate,
which is what <Ref lesson="stylized-facts">module 8's return data</Ref>
will show in a real record.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Ordinary calculus applied to $W$. (a) Apply the chain rule as if $W$ were
differentiable: $d(W^2) = 2W\,dW$, hence
$W(T)^2 = 2\int_0^T W\,dW$. Interpret the right side as the limit of the
left-point sums $2\sum_j W(t_j)\bigl(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr)$, compute
the expectation of every such sum, and compare it with $E[W(T)^2]$.
(b) Find the algebraic identity that relates $W(T)^2$ to the same
left-point sum exactly, on every partition, and identify the extra term.

<Solution>
**(a)** Each term of the sum is $W(t_j)$ times an increment independent
of $\mathcal F(t_j)$ with mean zero, so
$E[W(t_j)(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j))] = E[W(t_j)]\,E[W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)] = 0$
and every left-point sum has expectation $0$. But
$E[W(T)^2] = \mathrm{Var}\,W(T) = T$. If the calculus identity held, a
random variable with mean $T$ would be a limit of random variables with
mean $0$; the chain rule as written is off by a quantity whose
expectation is $T$.

**(b)** On any partition, telescoping and expanding each square,
$$
W(T)^2 = \sum_j \bigl[W(t_{j+1})^2 - W(t_j)^2\bigr]
= \sum_j \Bigl[2W(t_j)\bigl(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr) + \bigl(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr)^2\Bigr]
= 2\sum_j W(t_j)\,\Delta_j + Q_\Pi ,
$$
an exact identity from $b^2 - a^2 = 2a(b - a) + (b - a)^2$. The extra term
is the sampled quadratic variation, and as the mesh goes to zero it
tends to $T$ while the first sum tends to whatever $2\int_0^T W\,dW$
means. So $W(T)^2 = 2\int_0^T W\,dW + T$: the chain rule is missing the
quadratic variation, and the missing term has exactly the expectation
that part (a) found unaccounted for. This is the doorstep of the next two
lessons: <Ref lesson="ito-integral">the Itô integral</Ref> defines
$\int_0^T W\,dW$ as the limit of the left-point sums and computes it as
$\tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 T$, and <Ref lesson="ito-formula">the
Itô–Doeblin formula</Ref> is the chain rule with the term
$\tfrac12 f''(W)\,dW\,dW = \tfrac12 f''(W)\,dt$ that the identity above
exhibits for $f(x) = x^2$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

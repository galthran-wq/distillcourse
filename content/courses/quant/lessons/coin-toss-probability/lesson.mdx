Every object the binomial model needs — random variables, filtrations,
conditional expectations — lives on a finite set of coin-toss sequences,
where every proof is a finite sum and nothing has to be taken on faith.

This is the sixth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the second move of its second module. <Ref
lesson="multiperiod-binomial">The previous lesson</Ref> priced any claim on
an $N$-period tree by the backward recursion
$V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}[\tilde p\,V_{n+1}(H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(T)]$ and
proved that the hedge it produces meets the claim at every node. What stands
open is what that recursion is: it averages the next date's value over the
toss not yet seen, given the tosses already seen, and the previous lesson had
no language for "given the tosses already seen". This lesson supplies it.
The set of toss sequences is a finite probability space; knowing the first
$n$ tosses partitions it; the recursion's average is a conditional
expectation, defined by a formula and computed on the tree; and the five
properties that every later argument in the course rests on are proved here
as manipulations of finite sums, on the one space where that is all a proof
has to be. <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">The next
lesson</Ref> uses them to say what the recursion builds: a process whose
conditional expectation goes nowhere, under one particular choice of the
coin's probability.

The route: the sample space and its measure, with the distinction between a
random variable and its distribution; information at time $n$ as a partition
of the space, and adaptedness as constancy on its blocks; $E_n[X]$ computed
at both time-one nodes of the running tree, then defined; the five
fundamental properties, each checked on the tree and then proved; and what
each property is for in the hedging argument.

## The sample space: eight sequences, and one measure among many

The previous lesson's model was a stock multiplied by $u$ or $d$ after each of
$N$ tosses, with every toss sequence given positive probability and nothing
more said about probability at all. The recursion never needed more. The
objects this lesson defines need the probability of each sequence to be a
definite number, and the first thing to fix is what those numbers are.

### Sequences, probabilities, events

Three tosses produce eight sequences, and the set of them is written
$$
\Omega = \{HHH,\ HHT,\ HTH,\ HTT,\ THH,\ THT,\ TTH,\ TTT\}.
$$
Give a head probability $p$ and a tail probability $q = 1 - p$ on every toss,
with $0 < p < 1$, and take the tosses independent; then the probability of a
sequence is the product of its toss probabilities, $p$ for each head and $q$
for each tail. Writing $\#H(\omega)$ and $\#T(\omega)$ for the numbers of
heads and tails in $\omega$,
$$
P(\omega) = p^{\#H(\omega)}\,q^{\#T(\omega)}, \qquad
P(HHH) = p^3,\quad P(HHT) = P(HTH) = P(THH) = p^2 q,\quad \ldots,\quad P(TTT) = q^3.
$$

<Figure
  src="./figures/omega-tree.svg"
  alt="A three-level binary tree whose eight leaves are the sequences HHH through TTT, each labelled with its probability p cubed, p squared q, and so on down to q cubed, and with the numerical value at p = 2/3: 8/27, 4/27, 4/27, 2/27, 4/27, 2/27, 2/27, 1/27"
  caption="The sample space for three tosses: eight sequences, one probability each, summing to (p + q)³ = 1; the blue values are for a coin with p = ⅔."
/>

An *event* is a subset of $\Omega$, and its probability is the sum of the
probabilities of its elements. "The first toss is a head" is the subset
$\{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT\}$, and its probability is
$$
p^3 + p^2 q + p^2 q + p q^2 = p^2 (p + q) + pq\,(p + q) = p\,(p + q) = p,
$$
which is what the coin was built to deliver. The computation is not a
formality: the model assigns probabilities to sequences and defines
probabilities of events by summation, and whether the event "first toss is a
head" then has probability $p$ is a consequence to be checked, not a
stipulation. The same sum with $N$ tosses collapses the same way, every
factor $(p + q)$ being $1$.

<Definition title="finite probability space (Shreve I, Definition 2.1.1)">
A **finite probability space** is a nonempty finite set $\Omega$, the
**sample space**, together with a **probability measure** $P$, a function
assigning to each $\omega \in \Omega$ a number $P(\omega) \in [0, 1]$ with
$\sum_{\omega \in \Omega} P(\omega) = 1$. An **event** is a subset
$A \subseteq \Omega$, with $P(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} P(\omega)$.
</Definition>

The coin-toss space with $P(\omega) = p^{\#H}q^{\#T}$ is the canonical
instance, and this course uses no other sample space until
<Ref lesson="brownian-motion">Brownian motion</Ref> makes $\Omega$ infinite.
Two further instances fix the definition's reach. The same $\Omega$ carries a
second measure, $\tilde P(\omega) = \tilde p^{\#H}\tilde q^{\#T}$ with
$\tilde p$ the risk-neutral probability of
<Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the one-period lesson</Ref>: the same eight
sequences, different numbers on them, and the definition does not prefer
either. The definition also admits $P(\omega) = 0$ for some $\omega$, which
the coin-toss measure never produces for $0 < p < 1$; the model's requirement
that every sequence has positive probability is a restriction on the measures
this course uses, not part of the definition. The near miss is a function
assigning $\tfrac12$ to every one of the eight sequences: it violates the
single clause $\sum P(\omega) = 1$, and rescaling it to $\tfrac18$ each gives
the measure with $p = \tfrac12$.

### A random variable is a function on Ω; its distribution is a table

A *random variable* is a real-valued function on $\Omega$. The stock prices
are the running instances: $S_0 = 4$ on every sequence, $S_1(\omega) = 8$ if
$\omega_1 = H$ and $2$ if $\omega_1 = T$, $S_2$ reads the first two tosses,
$S_3$ reads all three. The terminal payoff of any claim is a random variable
too, and so is the lookback's $\max_{k \le 3} S_k - S_3$ from the previous
lesson. The *expectation* of a random variable is its probability-weighted
sum over the sample space, $E[X] = \sum_{\omega \in \Omega} X(\omega)\,P(\omega)$;
under $p = \tfrac23$, for instance,
$E[S_3] = \tfrac{8}{27}\cdot 32 + \tfrac{12}{27}\cdot 8 + \tfrac{6}{27}\cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{27}\cdot 0.5 = 13.5$.

The correct claim first, because the wrong one is the natural reading of the
notation: a random variable is fixed by the model's rules and does not change
when the probability measure changes; what changes is the table of
probabilities with which it takes its values, its *distribution*. The
misconception, stated once: "under the risk-neutral measure the stock is a
different random variable, one that grows at the riskless rate." It is
wrong. $S_3$ is the function that sends $HHT$ to $8$ and $TTT$ to $0.50$,
and it sends them there under $P$ and under $\tilde P$ alike; the two
measures differ only in how much probability they place on $HHT$ and on
$TTT$. The replacement model is a fixed table of values with interchangeable
columns of weights.

The discriminating example is the head count $X(\omega) = \#H(\omega)$. Under
$p = \tfrac23$ its distribution is
$P(X = 0), \ldots, P(X = 3) = \tfrac{1}{27}, \tfrac{6}{27}, \tfrac{12}{27}, \tfrac{8}{27}$;
under $\tilde p = \tfrac12$ it is $\tfrac18, \tfrac38, \tfrac38, \tfrac18$.
The function $X$ is the same on both lines. Its mean is $2$ under the first
measure and $1.5$ under the second, and the stock's mean moves with it, from
$13.5$ to $7.8125 = 4 \times 1.25^3$. Nothing about the stock's values at any
node moved. The fourth exercise drills this separation on a claim made about
option prices; <Ref lesson="state-prices-change-of-measure">the
change-of-measure lesson</Ref> turns it into a single random variable
$Z = \tilde P / P$ that carries the whole difference between the two columns.

<KeyFact>
Everything this lesson defines takes the measure as an input: the same
random variable, the same partition of $\Omega$, and the same formula for
$E_n[X]$ serve for the actual coin and for the risk-neutral one, and the
course marks which is in use only by a tilde. The properties proved below
hold for every $0 < p < 1$; that they hold for $\tilde p$ in particular is
what the next lesson uses.
</KeyFact>

## Information: the first n tosses partition Ω

The recursion of the previous lesson is evaluated "at the node
$\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n$", and a hedger standing at that node knows $n$
tosses and not the remaining $N - n$. On a finite sample space that state
of knowledge is a concrete object: the set of sequences still possible.

### The filtration: partitions that refine

At time $0$ nothing has been tossed and all eight sequences remain possible.
At time $1$, if the first toss was a head, the four sequences beginning with
$H$ remain and the four beginning with $T$ are excluded; the hedger knows
which of the two blocks $[H] = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT\}$ and $[T]$ contains
the sequence being tossed, and nothing finer. At time $2$ the block has
shrunk to two sequences, and at time $3$ to one.

<Definition title="information at time n; filtration">
For $0 \le n \le N$, the **information at time $n$** is the partition
$\mathcal F_n$ of $\Omega$ into the $2^n$ blocks
$$
[\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n] = \{\omega' \in \Omega : \omega'_1 = \omega_1, \ldots, \omega'_n = \omega_n\},
$$
one block for each possible outcome of the first $n$ tosses. The sequence
$\mathcal F_0, \mathcal F_1, \ldots, \mathcal F_N$ is the **filtration** of
the coin-toss space: each $\mathcal F_{n+1}$ refines $\mathcal F_n$, every
block of $\mathcal F_n$ being the union of two blocks of $\mathcal F_{n+1}$.
</Definition>

The filtration's two ends are the two trivial partitions: $\mathcal F_0$ has
the single block $\Omega$, and $\mathcal F_N$ has $2^N$ blocks of one
sequence each. The refinement is the statement that information accumulates
and is never lost: a hedger who knows the first two tosses knows the first
one.

<Remark title="partitions and σ-algebras">
The general object in the definition's place is a *σ-algebra*, a family of
events closed under complements and countable unions; on a finite
$\Omega$ every σ-algebra is the family of all unions of the blocks of some
partition, so the two carry the same information, and this course works
with the partition until the sample space becomes infinite. The word occurs
in this lesson only here; the general definition of conditional expectation
that needs it is stated in <Ref lesson="brownian-motion">the Brownian
motion lesson</Ref>.
</Remark>

### Adapted processes: constant on the blocks

<Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">The previous lesson's definition of a
portfolio process</Ref> required $\Delta_n$ to "depend on the first $n$
tosses and on nothing later". In the language
just fixed, that phrase has an exact meaning: $\Delta_n$ takes one value on
each block of $\mathcal F_n$.

<Definition title="measurable at time n; adapted process">
A random variable $X$ **depends only on the first $n$ tosses**, or is
**$\mathcal F_n$-measurable**, if it is constant on every block of
$\mathcal F_n$: $X(\omega) = X(\omega')$ whenever $\omega$ and $\omega'$
agree in their first $n$ tosses. A sequence of random variables
$X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_N$ is an **adapted process** if $X_n$ is
$\mathcal F_n$-measurable for every $n$.
</Definition>

Four instances fix the boundary. The canonical one is the stock,
$S_n = u^{\#H(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)} d^{\,n - \#H} S_0$, which reads the
first $n$ tosses through their head count and is adapted. A structurally
different one is the running maximum $M_n = \max_{k \le n} S_k$, adapted
because each $S_k$ with $k \le n$ is constant on the blocks of
$\mathcal F_k$, hence on the finer blocks of $\mathcal F_n$; it reads the
first $n$ tosses through their order, not their count, and is adapted all
the same. The degenerate case is a constant such as $S_0$, which is
$\mathcal F_n$-measurable for every $n$ including $n = 0$, and the other
degenerate case is a terminal payoff $V_N$, measurable at time $N$ and, in
general, at no earlier time. The near miss is the previous lesson's
foresight rule, $\Delta'_1 = 1$ if $\omega_2 = H$ and $0$ if
$\omega_2 = T$: on the block $[H]$ it takes the value $1$ at $HHH$ and $0$ at
$HTH$, so it violates the single clause of constancy on blocks.

  caption="Adaptedness read off the blocks: the legal Δ₁ is one number per time-one block, the illegal one changes inside a block, which is the same as reading a toss that has not happened."

An instance of your own, before reading on: take the claim that pays $1$ at

<Aside>

THT, TTH, TTT$. It is not constant on $[H]$ ($0$ at $HHH$, $1$ at $HTH$), so
it is not $\mathcal F_1$-measurable; it is constant on each of the four

</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Adaptedness is the entire content of "no peeking": a hedge, an exercise
rule, or a trading strategy is admissible exactly when its time-$n$ decision
is constant on the blocks of $\mathcal F_n$. The wealth equation's clause on
$\Delta_n$ was this condition; <Ref lesson="stopping-times">the
stopping-time lesson</Ref> imposes it on the decision to exercise, and
<Ref lesson="ito-integral">the Itô integral</Ref> imposes it on the
integrand, where it is the difference between a definable integral and
none.
</KeyFact>

## Conditional expectation: average over the tosses not yet seen

The hedger at node $H$ of the \$4 tree knows $S_1 = 8$ and knows that
$S_3$ will be one of $32$, $8$, $8$, $2$ according to the two tosses still
to come. The natural estimate of $S_3$ from that position is the average of
the four values with the probabilities of the two remaining tosses as
weights, and that average is the object the recursion has been computing
all along.

### E₁[S₃] at both nodes

Take $p = \tfrac23$, so that two further tosses have probabilities
$p^2 = \tfrac49$ for $HH$, $pq = \tfrac29$ for each of $HT$ and $TH$, and
$q^2 = \tfrac19$ for $TT$. From node $H$ the four continuations reach
$32$, $8$, $8$, $2$; from node $T$ they reach $8$, $2$, $2$, $0.50$:
$$
E_1[S_3](H) = \tfrac49 \cdot 32 + \tfrac29 \cdot 8 + \tfrac29 \cdot 8 + \tfrac19 \cdot 2 = 18,
\qquad
E_1[S_3](T) = \tfrac49 \cdot 8 + \tfrac29 \cdot 2 + \tfrac29 \cdot 2 + \tfrac19 \cdot 0.5 = 4.5.
$$

<Figure
  src="./figures/e1-s3.svg"
  alt="Two subtrees side by side: from the node H with S1 = 8 the branches reach 16 and 4 and then the leaves 32, 8, 8, 2 with weights 4/9, 2/9, 2/9, 1/9 beside them and the sum 18 below; from the node T with S1 = 2 the leaves are 8, 2, 2, 0.5 with the same weights and the sum 4.5"
  caption="E₁[S₃] computed in place: each time-one node is the root of a two-toss subtree, and the estimate is the weighted average of its leaves with the weights of the two unrevealed tosses."
/>

The number depends on the node: $18$ at $H$, $4.5$ at $T$. So $E_1[S_3]$ is
not a number but a random variable, one value per block of $\mathcal F_1$,
and it is $\mathcal F_1$-measurable by construction. That is the general
shape, and it is what the definition records.

### The definition, and its reading as a block average

<Definition title="conditional expectation on the coin-toss space (Shreve I, Definition 2.3.1)">
Let $X$ be a random variable on the $N$-toss space and $0 \le n \le N$. The
**conditional expectation of $X$ given the information at time $n$** is
the random variable $E_n[X]$ whose value on the block
$[\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n]$ is
$$
E_n[X](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)
= \sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N} p^{\#H(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)}\,q^{\#T(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)}\,
X(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n \omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N),
$$
the sum running over the $2^{N - n}$ continuations of the fixed first $n$
tosses. For $n = 0$ the sum runs over all of $\Omega$ and $E_0[X] = E[X]$;
for $n = N$ the continuation is empty and $E_N[X] = X$.
</Definition>

Two readings of the formula are used below. The first is the one the figure
draws: $E_n[X]$ at a node is the expectation of $X$ on the subtree hanging
off that node, with the subtree's own coin. The second reads it on $\Omega$
itself. Since $P(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N) = p^{\#H(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)} q^{\#T(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)} \cdot p^{\#H(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)} q^{\#T(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)}$,
and the block $A = [\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n]$ has probability
$P(A) = p^{\#H(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)} q^{\#T(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)}$
(the sum over continuations contributes the factor $(p + q)^{N - n} = 1$),
the weight of each continuation is $P(\omega)/P(A)$, and
$$
E_n[X](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = \frac{1}{P(A)} \sum_{\omega \in A} X(\omega)\,P(\omega),
\qquad A = [\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n].
$$
So $E_n[X]$ is the average of $X$ over the block containing the
outcome, with the block's own probabilities renormalised to sum to one: the
conditional probabilities $P(\omega \mid A)$. On the running tree the
block $[H]$ holds $\tfrac{8}{27} + \tfrac{4}{27} + \tfrac{4}{27} + \tfrac{2}{27} = \tfrac{18}{27}$
of the probability under $p = \tfrac23$, and dividing each of those four
numbers by $\tfrac23$ returns the subtree weights $\tfrac49, \tfrac29, \tfrac29, \tfrac19$
of the figure above. This is the form that generalises when $\Omega$ is
infinite, and it is the form in which the independence proof below is
shortest.

<Remark title="two notations, one object">
$E_n[X]$ and $E[X \mid \mathcal F_n]$ name the same random variable; the
course writes $E_n[X]$ (Shreve's notation) throughout, and
$\tilde E_n[X]$ for the same formula with $\tilde p$, $\tilde q$ in place of
$p$, $q$. Baxter and Rennie write $E_Q(X \mid \mathcal F_i)$, with the
measure as a subscript, which makes the same point as the tilde: a
conditional expectation has two inputs, a measure and an information time,
and neither is optional.
</Remark>

The previous lesson's recursion is now a sentence. With $\tilde p$ in the
formula and one toss unrevealed,
$\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = \tilde p\,V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n H) + \tilde q\,V_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n T)$,
so backward induction reads
$$
V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}\,\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}] :
$$
the price at a node is the discounted conditional expectation of the next
date's price, under the risk-neutral coin, given the tosses so far.

<KeyFact>
The recursion is a conditional expectation under one specific measure, and
its output inherits the definition's measurability: $V_n = \frac{1}{1 +
r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$ is $\mathcal F_n$-measurable, so the price process
the previous lesson built is adapted, as the hedge that replicates it had
to be. A price that read a toss not yet made would be a hedge that could
not be executed.
</KeyFact>

### A worked example and its pair

<Example title="E₂[S₃] on the four time-two blocks, p = ⅔">
At time $2$ one toss remains, so each block's average has two terms with
weights $\tfrac23$ on the head continuation and $\tfrac13$ on the tail.

**Block $[HH]$, $S_2 = 16$.** Continuations reach $32$ and $8$:
$E_2[S_3](HH) = \tfrac23 \cdot 32 + \tfrac13 \cdot 8 = \tfrac{64}{3} + \tfrac{8}{3} = 24$.

**Block $[HT]$, $S_2 = 4$.** Continuations reach $8$ and $2$:
$E_2[S_3](HT) = \tfrac23 \cdot 8 + \tfrac13 \cdot 2 = \tfrac{16}{3} + \tfrac{2}{3} = 6$.

**Block $[TH]$, $S_2 = 4$.** The same two prices, $8$ and $2$:
$E_2[S_3](TH) = 6$.

**Block $[TT]$, $S_2 = 1$.** Continuations reach $2$ and $0.50$:
$E_2[S_3](TT) = \tfrac23 \cdot 2 + \tfrac13 \cdot 0.5 = \tfrac{4}{3} + \tfrac{1}{6} = 1.5$.

Every value is $1.5 \times S_2$, because $pu + qd = \tfrac43 + \tfrac16 = 1.5$
and the one-toss average of $S_3$ from a node with price $s$ is
$p\,us + q\,ds = (pu + qd)\,s$. The one-period average of the stock under
this coin grows it by $1.5$ per period, against the money market's $1.25$.
</Example>

all four blocks, then $E_1[X](H)$ twice: once from the four-term definition

**Strategy hint.** Same two-term averages as the example; the values come
from the payoff table, not from a formula in $S_2$, since the lookback is
path-dependent.

**Time two.** $E_2[X](HH) = \tfrac23 \cdot 0 + \tfrac13 \cdot 8 = \tfrac83$;
$E_2[X](HT) = \tfrac23 \cdot 0 + \tfrac13 \cdot 6 = 2$;
$E_2[X](TH) = \tfrac23 \cdot 0 + \tfrac13 \cdot 2 = \tfrac23$;
$E_2[X](TT) = \tfrac23 \cdot 2 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3.5 = \tfrac43 + \tfrac76 = 2.5$.
The blocks $[HT]$ and $[TH]$ share $S_2 = 4$ and carry different values, $2$
and $\tfrac23$, as the previous lesson's price tree could not.

**Time one, directly.** From $H$ the continuations $HH, HT, TH, TT$ reach the
payoffs $0, 8, 0, 6$:
$E_1[X](H) = \tfrac49 \cdot 0 + \tfrac29 \cdot 8 + \tfrac29 \cdot 0 + \tfrac19 \cdot 6 = \tfrac{16}{9} + \tfrac{6}{9} = \tfrac{22}{9}$.

**Time one, as an average of averages.**
$\tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac83 + \tfrac13 \cdot 2 = \tfrac{16}{9} + \tfrac{6}{9} = \tfrac{22}{9}$.
The two routes agree; that they must agree for every $X$ is the third
property of the next section. The transferable move: a conditional
expectation of a path-dependent payoff is computed on the sequence tree
exactly as on the price tree, one block at a time, and blocks that share a
price are not merged.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## The five properties: checked on the tree, then proved as sums

Every use of conditional expectation in the rest of the course goes through
five identities. Each is stated below, checked on the running tree with
$p = \tfrac23$, and then proved. The proof mode is the same for all five:
write the left side by the definition, as a sum over continuations, and
rearrange the sum.

Throughout, $X$ and $Y$ are random variables on the $N$-toss space with
head probability $p$, $0 < p < 1$, and $0 \le n \le N$. The one identity
used repeatedly is
$$
\sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N} p^{\#H(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)}\,q^{\#T(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)} = (p + q)^{N - n} = 1,
$$
the sum over continuations of their weights, which expands $(p + q)^{N-n}$
by the binomial theorem: a continuation with $k$ heads contributes
$p^k q^{N-n-k}$, and there are $\binom{N-n}{k}$ of them.

### (i) Linearity: the average of a sum is the sum of the averages

**Check on the tree.** At node $H$, $E_1[S_2](H) = \tfrac23 \cdot 16 + \tfrac13 \cdot 4 = 12$
and $E_1[S_3](H) = 18$ from above, so the sum of the two estimates is $30$.
Estimating $S_2 + S_3$ directly, the four continuations reach
$16 + 32$, $16 + 8$, $4 + 8$, $4 + 2$:
$E_1[S_2 + S_3](H) = \tfrac49 \cdot 48 + \tfrac29 \cdot 24 + \tfrac29 \cdot 12 + \tfrac19 \cdot 6 = \tfrac{192 + 48 + 24 + 6}{9} = 30$.

<Theorem title="linearity (Shreve I, Theorem 2.3.2 (i))">
For all constants $c_1, c_2$,
$E_n[c_1 X + c_2 Y] = c_1\,E_n[X] + c_2\,E_n[Y]$.

<Proof>
Roadmap: the definition is a weighted sum, and a weighted sum is linear in
the summand. On the block $[\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n]$, abbreviating the
weight of a continuation $\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N$ by $w$,
$$
E_n[c_1 X + c_2 Y](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)
= \sum w\,\bigl[c_1 X(\omega) + c_2 Y(\omega)\bigr]
= c_1 \sum w\,X(\omega) + c_2 \sum w\,Y(\omega)
= c_1 E_n[X] + c_2 E_n[Y],
$$
all three sums over the same continuations and each evaluated at the same
node. The node was arbitrary.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### (ii) Taking out what is known: a time-n quantity is a constant on the block

**Check on the tree.** $S_1 S_2$ at the four sequences of $[H]$ is
$8 \cdot 16$, $8 \cdot 16$, $8 \cdot 4$, $8 \cdot 4$, so
$E_1[S_1 S_2](H) = \tfrac49 \cdot 128 + \tfrac29 \cdot 128 + \tfrac29 \cdot 32 + \tfrac19 \cdot 32 = \tfrac{864}{9} = 96$,
and $S_1(H)\,E_1[S_2](H) = 8 \times 12 = 96$.

<Theorem title="taking out what is known (Shreve I, Theorem 2.3.2 (ii))">
If $X$ depends only on the first $n$ tosses, then
$E_n[XY] = X \cdot E_n[Y]$.

<Proof>
Roadmap: on a block, $X$ is one number, and a constant factor leaves a sum.
Fix the block $[\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n]$. Every continuation
$\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N$ produces a sequence in that block, and
$X$ is constant on the block, so $X(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n \omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N) = X(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)$
for every continuation. Then
$$
E_n[XY](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)
= \sum w\,X(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)\,Y(\omega)
= X(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) \sum w\,Y(\omega)
= X(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)\,E_n[Y](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n).
$$
The hypothesis was used exactly once, to replace $X(\omega)$ by a quantity
independent of the continuation; without it the factor $X(\omega)$ varies
from term to term and does not leave the sum.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### (iii) Iterated conditioning: an average of averages is the overall average

**Check on the tree.** The example gave $E_2[S_3] = 24, 6, 6, 1.5$ on the
four time-two blocks. Averaging those with one toss's weights:
$E_1[E_2[S_3]](H) = \tfrac23 \cdot 24 + \tfrac13 \cdot 6 = 18$ and
$E_1[E_2[S_3]](T) = \tfrac23 \cdot 6 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1.5 = 4.5$, which are
$E_1[S_3](H)$ and $E_1[S_3](T)$ computed directly at the start of the
previous section. One step further, $E[E_1[S_3]] = \tfrac23 \cdot 18 + \tfrac13 \cdot 4.5 = 13.5 = E[S_3]$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/tower.svg"
  alt="Four columns: the eight values of S3 grouped in pairs into boxes, arrows carrying each pair's weighted average 24, 6, 6, 1.5 into a second column, those grouped in pairs and averaged to 18 and 4.5 in a third column, and those averaged to 13.5 in the last; the averaging weights 2/3 and 1/3 are written on each arrow"
  caption="Iterated conditioning as nested averaging: averaging inside the time-two blocks and then across them lands on the same numbers as averaging inside the time-one blocks directly."
/>

<Theorem title="iterated conditioning, the tower property (Shreve I, Theorem 2.3.2 (iii))">
If $0 \le n \le m \le N$, then $E_n\bigl[E_m[X]\bigr] = E_n[X]$. In
particular $E\bigl[E_m[X]\bigr] = E[X]$.

<Proof>
Roadmap: split each continuation of the first $n$ tosses into its first
$m - n$ tosses and its last $N - m$; the weights multiply across the split
because head counts add; the inner sum is $E_m[X]$ by definition, and the
double sum over the two pieces is the single sum over the whole
continuation.

Write $Z = E_m[X]$, which depends only on the first $m$ tosses. Fix the
block $[\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n]$ and split a continuation as
$\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_m$ followed by $\omega_{m+1} \cdots \omega_N$.
Since $\#H(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N) = \#H(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_m) + \#H(\omega_{m+1} \cdots \omega_N)$
and likewise for tails, the weight of the whole continuation is the product
of the weights of its two pieces, $w = w'\,w''$. Because $Z$ does not read
the second piece,
$$
E_n[Z](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)
= \sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_m} w' \sum_{\omega_{m+1} \cdots \omega_N} w''\,Z(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_m)
= \sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_m} w'\,Z(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_m),
$$
the inner sum being $Z(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_m)$ times the sum of the
weights $w''$, which is $1$. Now substitute the definition of $Z$ at the
block $[\omega_1 \cdots \omega_m]$:
$$
\sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_m} w' \sum_{\omega_{m+1} \cdots \omega_N} w''\,X(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N)
= \sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N} w'\,w''\,X(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N)
= \sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N} w\,X(\omega)
= E_n[X](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n).
$$
The two summations over the pieces are one summation over the whole
continuation, and $w' w'' = w$. The case $n = 0$ gives
$E[E_m[X]] = E[X]$, since $E_0 = E$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### (iv) Independence: a quantity built from future tosses has the same estimate at every node

**Check on the tree.** The ratio $S_2 / S_1$ is $2$ if $\omega_2 = H$ and
$\tfrac12$ if $\omega_2 = T$, whatever the first toss. At node $H$,
$E_1[S_2/S_1](H) = \tfrac23 \cdot 2 + \tfrac13 \cdot \tfrac12 = 1.5$; at node
$T$ the same two values with the same weights give $1.5$; and
$E[S_2/S_1] = P(\omega_2 = H) \cdot 2 + P(\omega_2 = T) \cdot \tfrac12 = 1.5$,
since $P(\omega_2 = H) = p^2 + qp = p$ by the same collapse as for the first
toss.

<Theorem title="independence (Shreve I, Theorem 2.3.2 (iv))">
If $X$ depends only on tosses $n + 1$ through $N$, then $E_n[X] = E[X]$: the
conditional expectation is the same constant on every block.

<Proof>
Roadmap: the summand does not read the prefix, so the sum is the same
number at every node; then show that number is $E[X]$ by writing $E[X]$ as a
sum over prefixes and continuations and using that the prefix weights sum
to $1$.

By hypothesis $X(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N) = X(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)$
for every sequence, the value depending on the continuation alone. Hence
$$
E_n[X](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = \sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N} w\,X(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N) =: c,
$$
a sum in which $\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n$ does not appear, so $E_n[X]$
equals the constant $c$ on every block. For the value of $c$, write
$P(\omega) = v\,w$ with $v = p^{\#H(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)} q^{\#T(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n)}$
the weight of the prefix, and sum over $\Omega$ by summing over prefixes and
then continuations:
$$
E[X] = \sum_{\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n} v \sum_{\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N} w\,X(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)
= \sum_{\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n} v \cdot c = c \cdot (p + q)^n = c.
$$
</Proof>
</Theorem>

### (v) Conditional Jensen: a convex function of the estimate never exceeds the estimate of the convex function

The property that carries the most weight later is an inequality, and the
running tree shows exactly where it is strict. Take the convex function
$\phi(x) = (x - 5)^+$, the strike-$5$ call's payoff.

**Check on the tree.** At block $[HT]$, $S_3$ is $8$ or $2$ and $\phi(S_3)$
is $3$ or $0$, so $E_2[\phi(S_3)](HT) = \tfrac23 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 0 = 2$,
while $\phi(E_2[S_3](HT)) = \phi(6) = 1$: the inequality holds with a gap of
$1$. At block $[HH]$, $S_3$ is $32$ or $8$ and $\phi(S_3)$ is $27$ or $3$, so
$E_2[\phi(S_3)](HH) = \tfrac23 \cdot 27 + \tfrac13 \cdot 3 = 19$ and
$\phi(E_2[S_3](HH)) = \phi(24) = 19$: equality. At $[TT]$ both branches pay
$0$ and $\phi(1.5) = 0$: equality again.

<Figure
  src="./figures/jensen-kink.svg"
  alt="Two panels plotting the convex payoff (x − 5)⁺; left, the chord from x = 2 to x = 8 lies above the curve and at x = 6 reads 2 where the curve reads 1; right, the chord from x = 8 to x = 32 lies on the curve, and at x = 24 both read 19"
  caption="Where conditional Jensen is strict: the chord between the two branch values lies above the curve exactly when the kink sits between the branches; when both branches are on one straight piece, chord and curve coincide."
/>

The gap opens exactly where the kink of $\phi$ lies between the two branch
values, and closes where both branches are on one straight piece of $\phi$.
That is the geometric content of convexity, and the proof below uses
nothing else: a convex function lies below every chord.

<Theorem title="conditional Jensen's inequality (Shreve I, Theorem 2.3.2 (v); finite-sum proof)">
If $\phi$ is a convex function of a real variable, then
$E_n[\phi(X)] \ge \phi\bigl(E_n[X]\bigr)$.

<Proof>
Roadmap: at a node, $E_n[X]$ is a weighted average of finitely many values
with non-negative weights summing to $1$; prove that $\phi$ of such an
average is at most the average of $\phi$ by induction on the number of
terms, using only the two-point definition of convexity,
$\phi(\lambda a + (1 - \lambda) b) \le \lambda\,\phi(a) + (1 - \lambda)\,\phi(b)$
for $0 \le \lambda \le 1$.

Fix a block and list the continuations as $1, \ldots, k$ with $k = 2^{N-n}$,
weights $w_1, \ldots, w_k \ge 0$ summing to $1$, and values
$x_i = X(\omega)$ on the $i$-th continuation. Then
$E_n[X] = \sum_i w_i x_i$ and $E_n[\phi(X)] = \sum_i w_i\,\phi(x_i)$ at that
block, and the claim is
$$
\phi\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^k w_i x_i\Bigr) \le \sum_{i=1}^k w_i\,\phi(x_i).
$$
This is a statement about any finite list of weights and values; the tree
entered only in supplying them, and from here the argument is general.

**Base case, $k = 1$.** $w_1 = 1$ and both sides are $\phi(x_1)$.

**Induction step.** Assume the claim for lists of $k - 1$ terms. If
$w_k = 1$ every other weight is $0$ and both sides are $\phi(x_k)$.
Otherwise $s = 1 - w_k = \sum_{i < k} w_i$ is positive, and the numbers
$w_i / s$ for $i < k$ are non-negative weights summing to $1$. Write
$y = \sum_{i < k} (w_i / s)\,x_i$, so that
$\sum_i w_i x_i = s\,y + w_k\,x_k$ with $s + w_k = 1$. Convexity at the two
points $y$ and $x_k$ gives
$$
\phi\Bigl(\sum_i w_i x_i\Bigr) = \phi(s\,y + w_k\,x_k) \le s\,\phi(y) + w_k\,\phi(x_k),
$$
and the induction hypothesis applied to the $k - 1$ weights $w_i / s$ gives
$\phi(y) \le \sum_{i < k} (w_i / s)\,\phi(x_i)$. Multiplying that by $s$ and
adding $w_k\,\phi(x_k)$ to both sides,
$$
\phi\Bigl(\sum_i w_i x_i\Bigr) \le \sum_{i < k} w_i\,\phi(x_i) + w_k\,\phi(x_k) = \sum_{i=1}^k w_i\,\phi(x_i).
$$
The block was arbitrary, so the inequality holds for $E_n$ on every block.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Task>
Four checks on the five proofs, closed book. (1) State in one sentence what
all five proofs have in common. (2) Name the one step, in which proofs,
that uses the tosses' independence, and say what kind of coin would break
it. (3) Decide which of the five properties survive for a measure on
$\Omega$ built from arbitrary positive weights on the eight sequences,
with the block-average formula taken as the definition. (4) State what
each property says at $n = N$. Then check against the fold.
</Task>

<Aside>
Answers. (1) A conditional expectation is a finite weighted average over a
block, and the five properties are the facts that weighted averages are
linear, pass constants, nest, ignore factors that do not read the block,
and lie under convex functions. (2) The factorisation $w = w' w''$ in the
tower proof and $P(\omega) = v\,w$ in the independence proof; both fail for
a coin whose probability on toss $m$ depends on earlier tosses. (3) Every
proof except independence uses only that the weights on a block sum to
$1$, so (i), (ii), (iii) and (v) hold for any positive weights; independence
is the one that additionally needs the product structure. (4) Each block
is one sequence, every sum has one term, and all five reduce to
identities, which the third exercise examines.
</Aside>

<KeyFact>
Nothing in the five proofs distinguishes the coin an investor believes in
from the coin the prices define; what distinguishes $p$ from $\tilde p$ is
which random variables come out as martingales, and that is a computation,
not a property.
</KeyFact>

## What the hedger needs from each property

The five identities were proved for their own sake; each one is also a tool
that a specific later argument picks up, and stating those uses now fixes
what the machinery is for.

### Linearity and taking out what is known: the wealth equation collapses

A hedger holding $\Delta_n$ shares has next-date wealth
$X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)$. Conditioning on
the information at time $n$, the two properties give
$$
E_n[X_{n+1}] = \Delta_n\,E_n[S_{n+1}] + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n),
$$
because $\Delta_n$, $X_n$ and $S_n$ are all $\mathcal F_n$-measurable, so
they leave the conditional expectation as constants. On the block $[H]$ of
the running tree, <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the previous lesson's
hedge of the strike-$5$ call</Ref> has $\Delta_1 = 0.9$, $X_1 = 5.28$ and
$S_1 = 8$, one number each, while $S_2$ and $X_2$ take one value per branch;
under $p = \tfrac23$ both sides of the identity come to $8.40$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/wealth-block.svg"
  alt="A two-row table for the branches H and T out of the node H with weights 2/3 and 1/3; the columns delta-1 = 0.9, S1 = 8 and X1 = 5.28 are shaded as one block because each is the same on both rows, while S2 reads 16 and 4 and X2 reads 12 and 1.20; below, E1[X2](H) = 8.40 is rewritten as 0.9 times E1[S2](H) = 12 minus 2.40, and a muted line shows that under p-tilde = 1/2 the same rewriting gives 6.60 = 1.25 times 5.28"
  caption="The wealth equation conditioned at time one: Δ₁, S₁ and X₁ are one number each on the block, so only S₂ is averaged; under the risk-neutral coin the average is 1.25 × X₁."
/>

Under $\tilde p$,
$\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}] = (\tilde p u + \tilde q d) S_n = (1 + r) S_n$, and the
right side becomes $(1 + r) X_n$: the risk-neutral conditional expectation of
next-date wealth is this date's wealth grown at the riskless rate, for every
self-financing strategy. <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">The
next lesson</Ref> writes this as "discounted wealth is a martingale" and
derives the general pricing formula from it.

### Iterated conditioning: the recursion composes into one expectation

The recursion $V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$ applied twice gives
$V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n\bigl[\frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_{n+1}[V_{n+2}]\bigr] = \frac{1}{(1 + r)^2}\tilde E_n[V_{n+2}]$,
the inner conditional expectation absorbed by iterated conditioning and the
constant discount factor taken out by linearity; repeated to the terminal
date,
$$
V_n = \tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{V_N}{(1 + r)^{N - n}}\Bigr],
$$
the price at any node as one conditional expectation of the payoff. The
next lesson states this as its pricing theorem and proves the martingale
form of it; the sixth exercise below proves the underlying fact that every
process of the form $E_n[X]$ satisfies the one-step identity the tower gives.

### Independence: the coin and the node do not interact

The ratio $S_{n+1}/S_n$ depends only on toss $n + 1$, so $E_n[S_{n+1}/S_n] = E[S_{n+1}/S_n] = pu + qd$
at every node, and the stock's one-step conditional mean is
$(pu + qd)\,S_n$: the node fixes the level, the coin fixes the growth factor,
and the two do not interact. Under $p = \tfrac23$ that factor is $1.5$
against a money market of $1.25$, so the stock's conditional mean beats the
riskless account at every node; under $\tilde p = \tfrac12$ it is exactly
$1.25$. From that comparison one would expect the price of a call to depend
on which coin is believed. It does not: the price is $\tilde E_n$ of the
payoff whatever $p$ is, and the fourth exercise refutes the inference in
detail.

### Conditional Jensen: waiting never loses on a convex payoff

For a convex payoff $\phi$ with $\phi(0) = 0$ and $r \ge 0$, Jensen gives
$\tilde E_n[\phi(S_{n+1})] \ge \phi(\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}]) = \phi((1 + r)S_n) \ge (1 + r)\,\phi(S_n)$,
the last step by convexity through the origin; discounting,
$\frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[\phi(S_{n+1})] \ge \phi(S_n)$, so the
continuation value of a call is never below its immediate-exercise value.
The tree's node $[HT]$ is the instance: continuation $\tfrac{1}{1.25} \times$
the risk-neutral average of $3$ and $0$ is $1.20$, against immediate
exercise worth $\phi(4) = 0$. <Ref lesson="american-derivatives">The
American-option lesson</Ref> runs this argument for every $n$ at once and
concludes that an American call on a non-dividend stock is never exercised
early.

<Remark title="on infinite sample spaces">
When $\Omega$ is uncountable, as it is for Brownian motion, the block
picture fails: the events that carry the information are not built from
finitely many atoms, and the averaging formula has no blocks to average
over. The general definition (Shreve II, Definition 2.3.1) keeps two
consequences of the finite one as its axioms: $E[X \mid \mathcal F]$ is
$\mathcal F$-measurable, and its average over every event in $\mathcal F$
equals the average of $X$ over that event, the partial-averaging property
that on the coin-toss space reads
$\sum_{\omega \in A} E_n[X](\omega)\,P(\omega) = \sum_{\omega \in A} X(\omega)\,P(\omega)$
for every block $A$ of $\mathcal F_n$. That a random variable with these two
properties exists is a theorem (Radon–Nikodym), which this course cites
where it is used and does not prove. The five properties survive the change
of definition with the same statements; only their proofs change.
</Remark>

What is established: the coin-toss space $\Omega = \{H, T\}^N$ with
$P(\omega) = p^{\#H}q^{\#T}$ is a finite probability space on which the
stock, every payoff and every hedge is a function; a random variable is
that function and not its distribution, which changes with the measure;
the information at time $n$ is the partition $\mathcal F_n$ into $2^n$
blocks and a process is adapted when its time-$n$ value is constant on
them; $E_n[X]$ is the average of $X$ over the block with the block's own
probabilities, $18$ and $4.5$ for $S_3$ at the two time-one nodes of the
running tree under $p = \tfrac23$; and linearity, taking out what is known,
iterated conditioning, independence and conditional Jensen are proved for
every $0 < p < 1$ as rearrangements of finite sums. The backward recursion
is now the statement $V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$, and what is
not yet established is what kind of process that statement makes $V_n$.
<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">The next lesson</Ref> defines
a martingale as an adapted process with $E_n[M_{n+1}] = M_n$, proves that
the discounted stock, the discounted wealth of every self-financing
strategy and the discounted price of every claim are martingales under
$\tilde P$, and reads the pricing formula off that fact.

At $T$:
$\tfrac{9}{16} \cdot 8 + \tfrac{3}{16} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{3}{16} \cdot 2 + \tfrac{1}{16} \cdot 0.5 = 4.5 + 0.375 + 0.375 + 0.03125 = 5.28125$.

**Tower.** At $H$: $\tfrac34 \cdot 26 + \tfrac14 \cdot 6.5 = 19.5 + 1.625 = 21.125$.
At $T$: $\tfrac34 \cdot 6.5 + \tfrac14 \cdot 1.625 = 4.875 + 0.40625 = 5.28125$.
Both agree with the direct values.

**Growth factor.** $pu + qd = \tfrac32 + \tfrac18 = 1.625$, and indeed
$26 = 1.625 \times 16$, $6.5 = 1.625 \times 4$, $1.625 = 1.625 \times 1$,
$13 = 1.625 \times 8$. Two steps compound it: $21.125 = 1.625^2 \times 8$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Give a second proof of conditional Jensen for the call payoff
$\phi(x) = (x - K)^+$ using only properties (i) and (ii) and one further
fact you must first prove from the definition: monotonicity, that
$X \le Y$ on every sequence implies $E_n[X] \le E_n[Y]$ on every block.
Then say what additional fact about a general convex $\phi$ would let the
same argument run for it. (This is the route of Shreve's appendix; the
lesson's proof avoided it.)

<Solution>
**Monotonicity.** On a block, $E_n[Y] - E_n[X] = E_n[Y - X]$ by linearity,
and $E_n[Y - X] = \sum w\,(Y - X)(\omega)$ is a sum of non-negative weights
times non-negative values, hence non-negative. So $E_n[X] \le E_n[Y]$.

**The call payoff.** $(x - K)^+ = \max(x - K, 0)$, so on every sequence
$\phi(X) \ge X - K$ and $\phi(X) \ge 0$. Monotonicity gives
$E_n[\phi(X)] \ge E_n[X - K]$ and $E_n[\phi(X)] \ge E_n[0]$. By linearity
and taking out the constant $K$ (a constant is measurable at every time),
$E_n[X - K] = E_n[X] - K$, and $E_n[0] = 0$. Hence
$E_n[\phi(X)] \ge \max\bigl(E_n[X] - K,\ 0\bigr) = \phi\bigl(E_n[X]\bigr)$.

**General convex $\phi$.** The argument used that $\phi$ is the pointwise
maximum of two affine functions, $x - K$ and $0$, each of which passes
through $E_n$ unchanged by linearity. For a general convex $\phi$ the
needed fact is that $\phi$ is the pointwise maximum of all affine functions
lying below it, $\phi(x) = \max\{\ell(x) : \ell \text{ affine},\ \ell \le \phi\}$,
which holds because a convex function has a support line at every point.
Given it, $\phi(X) \ge \ell(X)$ for each such $\ell$, so
$E_n[\phi(X)] \ge E_n[\ell(X)] = \ell(E_n[X])$ by monotonicity and
linearity, and taking the maximum over $\ell$ on the right gives
$\phi(E_n[X])$. The transferable move: an inequality that holds for every
member of a family of affine lower bounds passes to their maximum, and
linearity is what lets each affine bound through the conditional
expectation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
The definition treats $n = 0$ and $n = N$ as extreme cases. Show that both
are consequences of the sum formula rather than separate conventions: for
$n = N$ interpret the sum over an empty continuation, and for $n = 0$
identify the sum. Then, for each of the five properties, state what it
says at $n = N$ and at $n = 0$, and identify which properties become
identities at which endpoint.

<Solution>
**$n = N$.** The continuation $\omega_{N+1} \cdots \omega_N$ is empty; there
is exactly one empty continuation, with $\#H = \#T = 0$ and weight
$p^0 q^0 = 1$. The sum has one term, $X(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_N)$, so
$E_N[X] = X$. **$n = 0$.** The block is all of $\Omega$, the continuation is
the whole sequence, and the weight $p^{\#H(\omega)}q^{\#T(\omega)}$ is
$P(\omega)$; the sum is $\sum_\omega X(\omega)P(\omega) = E[X]$.

**At $n = N$.** (i) reads $c_1 X + c_2 Y = c_1 X + c_2 Y$; (ii) says that
if $X$ depends on all $N$ tosses, which every random variable does, then
$XY = X \cdot Y$; (iii) with $m = N$ says $E_n[X] = E_n[X]$; (iv) says that
a random variable depending on no tosses, a constant $c$, has $E_N[c] = c = E[c]$;
(v) says $\phi(X) \ge \phi(X)$. All five are identities: with every toss
known there is nothing to average.

**At $n = 0$.** (i) is linearity of ordinary expectation; (ii) says that a
random variable depending on no tosses, again a constant $c$, satisfies
$E[cY] = c\,E[Y]$, which is linearity again; (iii) with $n = 0$ is the
stated particular case $E[E_m[X]] = E[X]$, a genuine statement; (iv) says
$E_0[X] = E[X]$ for every $X$, which is the definition of $E_0$; (v) is
ordinary Jensen, $E[\phi(X)] \ge \phi(E[X])$, also genuine. At the start of
the filtration the properties reduce to facts about plain expectation; at
its end they reduce to nothing. The transferable move: the endpoints of a
filtration are where the hypotheses "depends only on the first $n$ tosses"
and "depends only on tosses after $n$" become vacuous or universal, and a
property's content is measured by how much survives there.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim. "Under $p = \tfrac23$ the stock's conditional mean grows
by $1.5$ per period at every node while the money market grows by $1.25$,
so the stock is a good deal to anyone who believes $p = \tfrac23$. A call
rises when the stock rises, so to the same believer the call is a good deal
too, and the backward recursion, which uses $\tilde p = \tfrac12$,
underprices it." Locate the step that fails, using the separation between
prices and profit distributions from
<Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the one-period lesson</Ref>, and compute
one number that shows the believer is right about the bet and wrong about
the price.

<Solution>
**What is true.** Under $p = \tfrac23$, $E_n[S_{n+1}] = 1.5\,S_n > 1.25\,S_n$
at every node: an unhedged long position in the stock has a conditional
expected return above the riskless rate. The same believer, holding the
strike-$5$ one-period call bought at its price $1.20$, expects a payoff of
$\tfrac23 \cdot 3 + \tfrac13 \cdot 0 = 2$ at time one, against $1.50$ that
the $1.20$ would earn in the money market: the call is, to that believer, a
favourable bet, more favourable than the stock in proportion to its risk.
Both statements are facts about the distribution of profit and loss under
$P$, which is what $p$ governs.

**The step that fails.** "So the recursion underprices it." A price is not a
statement about a distribution of profit; it is the cost of a portfolio
that delivers the payoff on every sequence. The one-period lesson's
replication delivers the call's payoff from half a share and a loan of
$0.80$, costing $1.20$, on $H$ and on $T$ alike, and any quote above $1.20$ is sold and
hedged for a riskless profit, by a trader who need not have any opinion
about $p$. The claim conflates $E$-facts, which describe how an unhedged
position's profit is distributed, with $\tilde E$-facts, which state what a
hedged position costs. The word "underpriced" names a price that can be
arbitraged, and $1.20$ cannot be: the believer's favourable bet is available
at $1.20$ and at no other price.

**The number.** Under $p = \tfrac23$ the discounted expected payoff of the
call is $2 / 1.25 = 1.60$, against the price $1.20$. The difference, $0.40$,
is not a mispricing; it is the expected excess return an unhedged holder is
paid for carrying the call's risk, exactly as the stock's own $0.25$ per
dollar excess growth is what its unhedged holder is paid. The same
computation for the stock itself gives a discounted expected value of
$1.5 \times 4 / 1.25 = 4.80$ against a price of $4$, and no one concludes
that the stock is underpriced at $4$: the stock's price is $4$ by
definition of the model, and the call's price is $1.20$ by replication from
it. The transferable move: a measure change re-weights sequences and
changes every expectation, and leaves every price fixed, because prices are
built from portfolios, which are functions on $\Omega$ and do not read the
weights.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A table claims to list $E_2[S_3]$ under $p = \tfrac23$ on all eight
sequences:

| $\omega$ | $HHH$ | $HHT$ | $HTH$ | $HTT$ | $THH$ | $THT$ | $TTH$ | $TTT$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| "$E_2[S_3]$" | $24$ | $24$ | $6$ | $6$ | $6$ | $6$ | $1.5$ | $0.5$ |

One entry is inadmissible before any arithmetic is checked. Find it, name
the clause of the definition it violates, say what quantity the wrong entry
actually is, and give the corrected table.

<Solution>
**The inadmissible entry.** $E_2[S_3]$ depends only on the first two
tosses, so it must be constant on each block of $\mathcal F_2$. The block
$[TT] = \{TTH, TTT\}$ carries the two values $1.5$ and $0.5$; whichever is
right, the table is not $\mathcal F_2$-measurable, and the clause violated
is constancy on blocks, which the definition builds in by making $E_2[X]$
a function of $\omega_1 \omega_2$ alone.

**Which one, and what it is.** On $[TT]$ the one remaining toss reaches
$S_3 = 2$ on $TTH$ and $0.5$ on $TTT$, so
$E_2[S_3](TT) = \tfrac23 \cdot 2 + \tfrac13 \cdot 0.5 = 1.5$; the $1.5$ is
right. The entry $0.5$ is $S_3(TTT)$ itself, that is $E_3[S_3](TTT)$: the
tabulator conditioned on the third toss at that one sequence, using
information not available at time two. The other six entries are correct
($24$ on $[HH]$, $6$ on $[HT]$ and $[TH]$) and consistent with the blocks.

**Corrected table.** $24, 24, 6, 6, 6, 6, 1.5, 1.5$. The transferable move:
measurability is checkable by inspection, block by block, before any value
is verified, and a table that fails it has used a toss it was not entitled
to.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Fix a random variable $X$ on the $N$-toss space and define
$M_n = E_n[X]$ for $n = 0, 1, \ldots, N$. (a) Show that $M$ is an adapted
process with $M_0 = E[X]$ and $M_N = X$. (b) Prove from the tower property
that $E_n[M_{n+1}] = M_n$ for every $n < N$. (c) Compute the process for
$X = S_3$ on the \$4 tree with $p = \tfrac23$ at every node, and check (b)
at the root.

<Solution>
**(a)** $M_n = E_n[X]$ is a function of $\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n$ by
definition, hence $\mathcal F_n$-measurable, so $M$ is adapted. The
extreme cases of the definition give $M_0 = E_0[X] = E[X]$ and
$M_N = E_N[X] = X$.

**(b)** By iterated conditioning with $m = n + 1$,
$E_n[M_{n+1}] = E_n\bigl[E_{n+1}[X]\bigr] = E_n[X] = M_n$.

**(c)** $M_3 = S_3$: $32, 8, 8, 2, 8, 2, 2, 0.5$. $M_2 = E_2[S_3]$:
$24, 6, 6, 1.5$ from the worked example. $M_1 = E_1[S_3]$: $18$ at $H$ and
$4.5$ at $T$. $M_0 = E[S_3] = 13.5$. At the root,
$E_0[M_1] = \tfrac23 \cdot 18 + \tfrac13 \cdot 4.5 = 12 + 1.5 = 13.5 = M_0$.

A process satisfying (a) and (b), adapted with $E_n[M_{n+1}] = M_n$, is
what <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the next lesson</Ref>
defines as a *martingale*, and this exercise proves that every conditional
expectation process of a fixed random variable is one, under whichever
measure computes it. The next lesson's pricing theorem is the observation
that the discounted price process of any claim is exactly this process for
$X = V_N / (1 + r)^N$ under $\tilde P$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

In the 1979 paper that made binomial pricing standard, a call worth 34.065
trades at 36, and the authors walk the arbitrage step by step: sell the call,
hold the replicating portfolio, rebalance at every node, and bank 1.935
compounded — whichever of the eight paths the stock takes.

This is the ninth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the fifth and closing move of its second module. The module
built the machine: <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">backward induction and
the replication theorem</Ref>, which put the 80-stock call at $34.08$ with a
hedge starting at $0.719$ share; <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">the
coin-toss space</Ref> on which the recursion is a conditional expectation;
<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the pricing formula</Ref>
$V_n = \tilde E_n[V_N/(1 + r)^{N-n}]$, with the absence of arbitrage as its
corollary; and <Ref lesson="state-prices-change-of-measure">the change of
measure</Ref> as an object. What stands open is enforcement: the theorems say
a quote other than $V_0$ is an arbitrage, and an arbitrage is a sequence of
trades with a cash ledger, not a sentence in a proof. This lesson reproduces
the ledger the founding paper printed, to the cent, and reads its two
published variants as consequences of the module's theorems: a rebalance done
through the mispriced call, which loses money, and a counterparty who
exercises badly, who cannot hurt the hedger. What it hands to the next module
is that counterparty's right to exercise early, which this lesson can only
show is worthless for a call.

The route: the paper's setup and the $1.935$ the trade is built on; the ledger
along the paper's own path, date by date, with the printed numbers checked;
the theorem that makes the bottom line the same on all eight paths; the
rebalance done through the call, in dollars; what the buyer of the call can
and cannot do to the seller; and the three places where the ledger's
assumptions and a trading desk part company.

## The setup: a call worth 34.065 quoted at 36

The module's theorems were proved on Shreve's \$4 stock and computed on a
second tree, the one
[Cox, Ross and Rubinstein](https://www.unisalento.it/documents/20152/615419/Option+Pricing+-+A+Simplified+Approach.pdf)
use in "Option Pricing: A Simplified Approach", circulated as a typescript in
March 1979, revised that July and published in the *Journal of Financial
Economics* in September, the paper that gave the binomial model its name. Its
fourth section, "Riskless Trading Strategies", is the only part of it written
as instructions rather than derivation: four numbered steps, each a trade with
a dollar amount, run twice, once correctly and once wrongly. Every number in
this lesson is either printed there or reconstructed from what is.

### Retrieval: the three facts the trade uses

<Task>
Closed book, before reading on. (1) A hedger with wealth $X_n$ holds
$\Delta_n$ shares and the rest in the money market; write $X_{n+1}$. (2)
Write the hedge ratio at a node in terms of the claim's two next-date values
and the stock's. (3) Two self-financing strategies use the same $\Delta_n$
from different starting wealths $X_0$ and $X_0'$; write $X_n - X_n'$. Then
check against the fold.
</Task>

<Aside>
Answers. (1) $X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)$,
<Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the wealth equation</Ref>. (2)
$\Delta_n = \dfrac{V_{n+1}(H) - V_{n+1}(T)}{S_{n+1}(H) - S_{n+1}(T)}$, the
slope of the claim against the stock over the next period. (3)
$X_n - X_n' = (1 + r)^n (X_0 - X_0')$: the share terms cancel in the
difference of two wealth equations, so a difference in starting wealth grows
at the money market rate and is never touched by the coin. This was the
second in-body exercise of
<Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial" id="the-wealth-equation-rebalancing-paid-for-out-of-the-portfolio">the multiperiod lesson</Ref>,
and it is the whole theorem of this one.
</Aside>

### The tree, in the paper's numbers

The market is the 80-stock tree: $S_0 = 80$, $u = 1.5$, $d = 0.5$, a money
market growing by $1 + r = 1.1$ per period, and a European call with strike
$80$ expiring after three periods. The paper writes the gross rate as
$r = 1.1$; this course's $r = 0.1$ is the same number. Admissibility holds,
$0.5 < 1.1 < 1.5$, and $\tilde p = (1.1 - 0.5)/(1.5 - 0.5) = 0.6$. The call
values and hedge ratios at every node are the ones
<Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">backward induction</Ref> produced.

### The mispricing and the plan

The market quotes the call at $M = 36$. The paper's word for the gap is the
*differential*: $M - C = 36 - 34.065 = 1.935$, about $5.7$ percent of value.
Nothing in that number is a view about the stock. The actual probability of
a head entered neither the values nor the ratios, so the differential is not
a forecast that the call will finish out of the money; it is the amount by
which the quote exceeds the cost of manufacturing the call's payoff, on every
path.

The paper's Step 1 is the plan in one sentence. Sell the call for $36$. Take
$34.065$ of the proceeds and buy the replicating portfolio: $0.719$ share at
$80$ costs $57.52$, so borrow $57.52 - 34.065 = 23.455$. Put the remaining
$1.935$ in the bank. From this point the position has three accounts: the
**hedge** (shares less the loan), the **call** the hedger is short, and the
**bank**, which holds the differential and is never traded again.

<KeyFact>
The profit is taken at date 0, not at expiry: the $1.935$ is in the bank
before the first toss. Everything the hedger does afterwards is defensive.
The rebalances exist to keep the hedge worth exactly the call at every node,
so that the two cancel at settlement and the bank, grown to
$1.935 \times 1.1^3$, is all that remains.
</KeyFact>

## The ledger along the paper's path: 80, 120, 60, then 30 or 90

The paper walks one path, a head followed by a tail and then either toss, and
writes each date as a step. At every date the hedge is a stock-for-debt swap
that nets to zero cash, and the bank is untouched.

### Dates 1 and 2: two rebalances, no cash

**Date 1, heads, stock at 120.** The new ratio is $0.848$. The hedger buys
$0.848 - 0.719 = 0.129$ share at $120$, spending $15.48$, and borrows it. The
loan was $23.455$ and has grown to $23.455 \times 1.1 = 25.801$; with the new
borrowing it is $25.801 + 15.48 = 41.281$. The bank holds
$1.935 \times 1.1 = 2.129$.

**Date 2, tails, stock at 60.** The new ratio is $0.167$. The hedger sells
$0.848 - 0.167 = 0.681$ share at $60$, taking in $40.86$, and repays. The
loan has grown to $41.281 \times 1.1 = 45.409$; after repayment it is
$45.409 - 40.86 = 4.549$. The bank holds $2.341$.

### Date 3: the two endings settle to the same number

**Date 3, tails, stock at 30.** The call expires worthless. The hedger holds
$0.167$ share worth $0.167 \times 30 = 5.01$ and owes $4.549 \times 1.1 =
5.004$. Sell the shares, repay the loan, and the hedge is closed with a cent
to spare. Withdraw the bank.

**Date 3, heads, stock at 90.** The call is exercised. The paper offers two
settlements, buying the call back or buying one share and delivering it, and
prices both at the same $90 - 80 = 10$. Borrow the $10$, taking the loan to
$15.004$; sell the $0.167$ share for $15.03$; repay. The hedge is closed with
three cents to spare. Withdraw the bank.

The bank on either ending holds $1.935$ compounded for three periods,
$1.935 \times 1.1^3 = 2.575$: the differential with the interest it earned.

<Remark title="reproducing the printed 2.341">
Both circulating copies of the paper print the withdrawal at Step 4 as
$1.935\,(1.1)^3 = 2.341$. The arithmetic is not right: $1.935 \times 1.331 =
2.575$, and $2.341$ is $1.935 \times 1.1^2$, the bank's balance one period
before expiry. The paper uses that two-period figure correctly in its second
run, which liquidates at date 2 (the fourth section below), and the same
number appears to have been carried into the date-3 line. Three periods
elapse between Step 1 and Step 4; the deposit is $2.575$. In exact arithmetic
the differential is $36 - 34.080 = 1.920$ and the deposit $2.556$; the
paper's $34.065$ underfunds the hedge by $0.015$, which is the origin of the
cents left over at settlement.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Self-financing is what licenses reading the bank column as profit: nothing
was taken from the bank to feed the hedge and nothing the hedge earned was
added to it, so the $2.575$ withdrawn is the differential with its interest
and carries no part of the hedge's rounding residual.
</KeyFact>

### The theorem: a mispriced sale is a deposit that the coin cannot touch

A hedger who sells at $M$ and runs
the replicating deltas from $X_0 = M$, without splitting off a separate bank,
holds at every node the replicating wealth plus the differential compounded;
the split into hedge and bank that the paper makes is that same statement
with the two summands kept in separate accounts.

<Theorem title="the locked-in differential (corollary of Shreve I, Theorem 1.2.2)">
In an admissible $N$-period binomial model let $V_N$ be a derivative
security with price process $V_n$ and replicating deltas $\Delta_n$ from
the backward recursion. Let $X_n$ be the wealth of the self-financing
strategy that starts from $X_0 = M$ and holds $\Delta_n$ shares at every
node. Then at every node of every path,
$$
X_n - V_n = (M - V_0)\,(1 + r)^n, \qquad n = 0, 1, \ldots, N .
$$
In particular a trader who sells the claim at $M > V_0$ and follows the
strategy holds $X_N - V_N = (M - V_0)(1 + r)^N > 0$ after settling the claim
on every path: an arbitrage.

<Proof>
Direct, by induction on $n$; the only ingredient is that the wealth equation
is affine in the wealth. The base case is $X_0 - V_0 = M - V_0$. Assume the
identity at a node at date $n$. By the replication theorem, $V_n$ itself is
the wealth of the strategy started from $V_0$ with the same $\Delta_n$, so
both $X$ and $V$ obey the wealth equation with the same share holding:
$$
X_{n+1} - V_{n+1}
= \bigl[\Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)\bigr]
- \bigl[\Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(V_n - \Delta_n S_n)\bigr]
= (1 + r)(X_n - V_n),
$$
on the heads branch and on the tails branch alike, since $S_{n+1}$ appears
in both brackets with the same coefficient. With the hypothesis,
$X_{n+1} - V_{n+1} = (M - V_0)(1 + r)^{n+1}$. At $n = N$, $V_N$ is the
claim's payoff, so $X_N - V_N$ is what remains after the trader pays it; it
is the same positive number on every path, and the strategy cost nothing at
date 0 beyond the sale itself.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The coin moves the shares, and the shares are the same in both strategies,
so the coin cannot move the difference. The replication theorem enters once,
in the identification of $V_n$ with a wealth process; without it $V_{n+1}$
would not be given by a wealth equation and the brackets would not match.
The sign $M > V_0$ is used nowhere in the identity, only in the last
sentence: for $M < V_0$ the same identity says the buyer of the claim who
runs the deltas short, the paper's *reverse hedge*, holds $(V_0 - M)(1 + r)^N$
on every path.

### What the identity says about closing early

The identity holds at every node, so it also prices a close-out before
expiry. At date $n$ the hedger can buy the call back at the market's price
$M_n$, sell the shares and repay the loan, and be left with
$$
X_n - M_n = (M - V_0)(1 + r)^n - (M_n - V_n).
$$
If the market quote has come into line, $M_n = V_n$, the hedger walks away
with the differential compounded to date $n$ and is, in the paper's words,
"rid of the concern of keeping the portfolio adjusted". If the call has
become more overpriced, $M_n - V_n$ can exceed the deposit and the close-out
loses money. The paper says this plainly: the return on the position "when
evaluated at prevailing market prices at intermediate times, may be
negative", and the loss "could always be avoided by waiting until the
expiration date". Both halves are the one identity.

<KeyFact>
The locked-in profit is a statement about settlement, not about the mark:
it is guaranteed at expiry and at any node where the market price equals the
model value, and at no other node. A hedger forced to close at a market
price further from value than the compounded differential loses, and the
theorem says nothing to prevent it. Under
<Ref lesson="option-chain-bounds">real quotes</Ref> the same fact appears as
an arbitrage that is real but not collectible before the option settles.
</KeyFact>

## Rebalancing through the call hands the mispricing back

The hedge ratio rose at date 1 from $0.719$ to $0.848$ shares per call, and
the ledger raised it by buying shares. It can also be raised by reducing the
number of calls. The correct rule, stated first: adjust the position through
the instrument whose price equals its value, which on this tree is the
stock, and never through the mispriced option in the direction that pays the
mispricing. The rule needs stating because the opposite is a natural
inference. The mispricing lives in the call; every trade in the call at a
price away from value looks like another bite of the differential; so an
arbitrageur who wants more of the profit should trade the call, not the
stock, at every date. The paper runs that strategy in dollars, and it ends
with the hedger in debt.

### The second run: adjusting through the call, in the paper's numbers

**Step 1** is unchanged: sell one call at $36$, hold $0.719$ share, loan
$23.455$, bank $1.935$.

**Step 2, date 1, stock at 120.** The call, the paper supposes, "has gotten
further out of line" and trades at $75$ against a value of $60.463$: now
overpriced by $14.537$. The required ratio is $0.848$ shares per call. Keeping
$0.719$ shares, the hedger must be short $0.719/0.848 = 0.848$ calls, so buys
back $1 - 0.848 = 0.152$ calls at $75$ for $11.40$, borrowed. Loan:
$25.801 + 11.40 = 37.201$.

**Step 3, date 2, stock at 60.** The call trades at its value, $5.454$. With

$37.201 \times 1.1 = 40.921$; after repayment $2.406$ is still owed. The bank
holds $2.341$. After using it to repay, the hedger **still owes $0.065$**.

### Why the number of calls is set by value and the cost by price

The refutation is the close-out identity of the previous section, applied
to a fraction of the position. Buying back $k$ calls at market price $M_n$
at a node where their value is $V_n$ changes the ledger by
$-k\,(M_n - V_n)$, compounded from that date: it is a partial close-out at
a price further from value than the deposit can absorb. The number $k$ is
not free. It is fixed by the ratio the hedge must have next period, which
depends on the call's **value** at the two nodes ahead, while what the
hedger pays for those $k$ calls is the call's **price** now. The paper's
sentence is exact: "the number of calls we would need to buy back depends
on their value, not their price". The hedge is no longer riskless, because
$M_n$ is not something the model determines, and it can be a loss, because
$M_n - V_n$ can exceed $(M - V_0)(1 + r)^n$.

The rule the paper draws, and the replacement model for the inference above:
never buy an overpriced option or sell an underpriced one to adjust a
hedge. Its corollary runs the other way and is the discriminating case, and
the paper places it "at Step 3 in the original hedging illustration", the
correct run's date-2 node, where the hedger holds $0.848$ share against one
call. Had the call still been overpriced there, the hedger should have
lowered the ratio not by selling $0.681$ share but by selling more calls,
each sold above value and hedged with the $0.848$ share already held; that
trade adds $k\,(M_n - V_n)$ to the ledger. Trading the call is not the error. Trading it in the direction
that pays the mispricing is, and which direction the hedge demands at a node
is decided by the ratio, not by the arbitrageur.

<KeyFact>
Rebalancing through the option is the correct move under one condition: the
direction the ratio forces agrees with the sign of the mispricing, calls
sold where $M_n > V_n$, bought back where $M_n < V_n$. The $k$ calls traded
are then a fresh sale of the mispriced claim at that node, and the theorem
applied to the enlarged position locks in a further
$k\,(M_n - V_n)(1 + r)^{N - n}$ on every path, provided the hedger carries the
extra calls' deltas to expiry. When the signs disagree the stock is the only
instrument. Both cases presume a stock whose price is its value; the lesson's
last section shows that an off-lattice move removes that too, and then no
instrument rebalances exactly.
</KeyFact>

## What the buyer of the call does cannot hurt the seller

The trade sold a call to someone, and the ledger assumed that someone
exercises at expiry exactly when $S_3 > 80$. The paper asks whether a
counterparty who behaves otherwise, exercising when he should not or
failing to when he should, can damage the hedger, and answers with a
two-line dominance argument.

<Remark title="the foolish counterparty (Cox, Ross and Rubinstein 1979, §4)">
Suppose the holder exercises at a node before expiry. The hedger owes
$S_n - K$ and holds a hedge worth $V_n$, and on this tree $V_n$ exceeds
$S_n - K$ at every node: at $120$ the hedge is worth $60.463$ against $40$
owed, at $180$ it is $107.272$ against $100$; at $40$, $60$ and $20$ the
call is out of the money and nothing is owed. The hedger settles, closes the
hedge, and keeps the excess, $20.46$ or $7.27$, on top of the bank. Suppose
instead the holder fails to exercise at expiry when $S_3 = 90$. The hedger
holds a hedge worth $10$ against a call that has paid nothing, and keeps
the $10$. In the paper's words, "the longer the holder of the call goes on
making mistakes, the better off we will be".
</Remark>

The argument uses one inequality, $V_n \ge (S_n - K)^+$ at every node, and
on this tree it is checked by reading six numbers off the figure. It is not
an accident of the numbers. A call on a stock paying no dividends, with
$r \ge 0$, has a value that dominates its exercise value at every node
because the payoff is convex with $g(0) = 0$, and that dominance is what
makes the American call, whose holder may exercise at any date, worth
exactly the European one.
<Ref lesson="american-derivatives">The American-option lesson</Ref> proves
the inequality by conditional Jensen and shows where it fails, for a put,
where early exercise has value and the seller's hedge must be built to meet
it.

<KeyFact>
The protection is one-sided. The holder's exercise rule reaches the hedger
only through $V_n - (S_n - K)^+$, nonnegative at every node of this tree, so
no rule the holder can choose brings the bank below $1.935 \times 1.1^3$,
and the hedge built for exercise at expiry covers exercise at any date
without a single changed trade. Nothing in the argument protects the hedger
from the hedger's own side: a stock that leaves the lattice or a rebalance
that costs money is a loss the next section prices.
</KeyFact>

## Where the ledger and a trading desk part company

The ledger reproduces the paper, and the paper reproduces the theorem. What
the section names now is the application's other half, the assumptions the
ledger consumed silently. Three were used on every line: the stock moves
only at the tree's dates and only to the tree's two prices; shares trade at
no cost; and the counterparties on the other side settle as the model says.
The last was dealt with above. The first two are where the dollar amounts
change.

### The stock lands on the lattice: what a wrong u or d does to the hedge

The date-0 hedge is one line through two points: $0.719$ share less a loan
that has grown to $25.80$ is worth $0.719\,s - 25.80$ if the stock lands at
$s$, and it equals the call's date-1 value at exactly $s = 40$ and $s =
120$. The tree guarantees the stock lands on one of the two. A real stock
does not, and the value the call would have at any other $s$ is the same
backward induction run from $s$ with two periods left,
$v_1(s) = \bigl[0.36\,(2.25s - 80)^+ + 0.48\,(0.75s - 80)^+ + 0.16\,(0.25s -
80)^+\bigr]/1.21$, a convex function of $s$.

<Task>
Read off the gap at $s = 150$: the hedge is worth $0.719 \times 150 - 25.80 =
82.05$ and the call $v_1(150) = (0.36 \times 257.5 + 0.48 \times 32.5)/1.21
= 89.50$, a shortfall of $7.45$, nearly four times the differential. Then
read $s = 80$, a stock that did not move: the hedge is ahead by $2.0$.
</Task>

The sign is the geometry of a chord. A convex curve lies below the chord
between two points on it and above the chord's extension outside them, so
a move that overshoots the bracket, $u$ larger than $1.5$ or $d$ smaller
than $0.5$, leaves the seller of the call short, and a move that undershoots
leaves the seller ahead. The hedge ratio was computed from $u$ and $d$, and
the ledger's zero at every date is a statement about a stock that honours
them. The size of the miss grows with the curvature of $v_1$, which is the
quantity <Ref lesson="greeks-hedging-error">the hedging-error lesson</Ref>
names gamma, and its analysis of a hedge rebalanced at intervals against a
stock that moves between them is this figure with the bracket shrunk.

### Transaction costs: the turnover along each path, priced

The ledger trades shares at every date, and a desk pays for each trade. The
turnover is path-dependent. Along the paper's path the hedger buys $57.52$ of
stock at date 0, buys $15.48$ at date 1, sells $40.86$ at date 2 and sells
$5.01$ at date 3, about $119$ in all; along $H, H, H$, where the hedge builds
to a full share and then liquidates it at $270$, the total is about $370$;
along $T, T, T$ it is about $84$. At ten basis points of value traded, a
round figure for an institutional commission plus half a spread on a liquid
stock, the cost is between $0.08$ and $0.37$ against the $2.575$ banked: the
arbitrage clears on every path, thinned by a seventh at worst.

  src="./figures/turnover.svg"
  alt="A bar chart of the transaction cost at ten basis points on each of the eight paths, from 0.37 on H,H,H down to 0.08 on T,T,T, with each bar labelled by the dollar turnover it prices, 370 down to 84, and a dashed reference line at 2.575, the amount banked"
  caption="Turnover priced at 10 basis points, path by path, against the 2.575 banked: at this cost every path clears; at 1 percent the H, H, H path does not."

The estimate is an order of magnitude, not a model. It does set the
threshold: for the trade to clear on every path the differential must exceed
the worst path's cost, which is $0.395$ once each of its four legs is
compounded to date 3 (the $0.37$ in the figure is the uncompounded sum) and
$0.395/1.331 \approx 0.30$ discounted to date 0, so a quote below about
$34.36$ is not worth hedging at this cost level. At one percent per trade
every bar is ten times taller, the $H, H, H$ path costs $3.70$ before
compounding, and the trade loses on it. The fifth exercise does the count
leg by leg. Costs
also fall on the second run's calls: an option's spread is wider than its
stock's, which is one more reason the paper's rule prefers the stock.

### What the application used, and what it did not

The trade consumed four of the module's results and no others. The
replication theorem supplied the deltas and the guarantee that the hedge
meets the claim; the pricing formula supplied $34.065$ as one weighted sum
over four terminal prices, which the fourth exercise recomputes under the
actual coin; the wealth equation's linearity supplied the theorem of this
lesson; and the absence of arbitrage, proved in the martingale lesson from
the martingale property of discounted wealth, is what the ledger exhibits
in dollars. Nothing about the actual probability of a head, nothing about
the stock's expected return, and nothing about the counterparty's beliefs
entered any line.

<KeyFact>
The founding paper's own demonstration is the module's theorems run on a
ledger: a differential of $1.935$ on a $36$ quote, locked at date 0 and
grown to $2.575$ at expiry on all eight paths. Where the ledger's
assumptions fail the losses are computable, and the largest is not the
commission but the lattice: a stock that lands off the two-point bracket
moves the hedge by a chord's gap, several times the differential.
</KeyFact>

What is established: the paper's Steps 1 to 4 reproduce to the cent as a
ledger whose hedge nets to zero at every date, whose bank is never touched,
and whose withdrawal at expiry is $1.935 \times 1.1^3 = 2.575$, the printed
$2.341$ being the two-period figure; the identity $X_n - V_n = (M -
V_0)(1 + r)^n$ makes the profit path-independent and prices any early
close-out as the compounded differential less the current mispricing; a
rebalance through the mispriced call is a partial close-out at that
mispricing and can turn the profit into a debt, with the paper's rule, never
buy an overpriced option or sell an underpriced one, as the corrected
inference; the counterparty's mistakes only add to the hedger's profit
because $V_n \ge (S_n - K)^+$ at every node; and the ledger's zero is
conditional on the stock honouring the lattice and on free trading, with the
first the larger exposure.

The module is closed: replication, the coin-toss
space, martingales, the change of measure, and the founding paper's trade as
their application. What is not established is the price of the right the
counterparty holds, to exercise at a date of his own choosing, which was
shown harmless for this call and is not harmless for a put.
<Ref lesson="stopping-times">The next lesson</Ref> defines the exercise
rules that right admits, stopping times, and proves that a martingale
stopped at one is still a martingale, which is the fact the pricing of that
right rests on.

<Exercise>
The paper claims the profit is locked "no matter what the other party does"
and whichever path the stock takes. Verify the path claim without writing
eight ledgers: state the hedger's position at date 3 on an arbitrary path as
a function of $S_3$ alone, using the theorem. Then turn the theorem on the
ledger's own residuals. The hedge account was opened with $34.065$ against
an exact value of $34.080$, and the theorem, applied to the hedge account
and the exact value process, says the shortfall at date 3 is
$0.015 \times 1.1^3 = 0.020$ on every path; the ledgers settled a cent over
on $H, T, T$, three cents over on $H, T, H$, four cents short on $T, H, T$

ledger violates, write the term the violation adds to $X_{n+1} - V_{n+1}$,
and decompose the $H, H, H$ residual into the theorem's part and the
violation's part.

<Solution>
**The position.** By the theorem, on every path $X_3 - V_3 = (36 -
34.065) \times 1.1^3$ with $X_3$ the wealth of the strategy started from
$36$ and $V_3 = (S_3 - 80)^+$ the settlement. Separating the bank makes
the same statement in two accounts: the hedge started from $34.065$
satisfies $X_3^{\text{hedge}} = V_3$ on every path, so it settles to zero,
and the bank is $1.935 \times 1.1^3$ regardless of $S_3$. No path enters
the bank column.

**The hypothesis.** The induction step cancels the share terms because both
wealth equations carry the same $\Delta_n$. The ledger holds the paper's
three-decimal ratios $\Delta_n'$ while the exact value process moves with
the exact ratios $\Delta_n$, and the difference of two wealth equations with
different holdings is
$$
X_{n+1} - V_{n+1}
= (1 + r)(X_n - V_n)
+ (\Delta_n' - \Delta_n)\bigl(S_{n+1} - (1 + r) S_n\bigr) .
$$
The added term depends on the toss: $S_{n+1} - 1.1\,S_n$ is $0.4\,S_n$ on
heads and $-0.6\,S_n$ on tails. The theorem's part, $-0.015 \times 1.1^n$,
is the same on every path; the violation's part changes sign with the coin,
which is why the four residuals differ.

**The decomposition on $H, H, H$.** The exact ratios are
$\Delta_0 = (60.496 - 2.975)/80 = 0.71901$,
$\Delta_1(H) = (107.273 - 5.455)/120 = 0.84848$ and
$\Delta_2(HH) = (190 - 10)/180 = 1$; the paper's $0.719$ is $0.00001$ low,
its $0.848$ is $0.00048$ low, its $1$ is exact. Date 1: the theorem's part
is $-0.015 \times 1.1 = -0.0165$, the violation's
$-0.00001 \times 0.4 \times 80 = -0.0003$. Date 2:
$1.1 \times (-0.0168) = -0.0185$ plus
$-0.00048 \times 0.4 \times 120 = -0.0233$, together $-0.0418$, the
ledger's $180 - 72.769 - 107.272 = -0.04$ within the printed values' own
rounding. Date 3: $1.1 \times (-0.0418) = -0.046$ with nothing added, the
five cents. Of those, $0.015 \times 1.331 = 0.020$ is the underfunding the
theorem tracks and $0.0233 \times 1.1 = 0.026$ is the rounded ratio at
$120$ compounded one period. On $H, T, H$ the same rounded ratio meets a
tail, $-0.00048 \times (-0.6 \times 120) = +0.035$, and the ratio $0.167$
at $60$ adds $+0.00033 \times 0.4 \times 60 = +0.008$:
$-0.020 - 0.0004 + 0.038 + 0.008 = +0.026$, the three cents over.

The transferable move: a reproduction that does not match to the cent is a
finding, and the theorem says which part of the mismatch is
path-independent; whatever varies with the path is a violated hypothesis,
here a rounded delta, to be located at the node where it entered.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "The mispricing is in the call, so an arbitrageur who
wants more of it should keep the share count fixed and make every
intermediate adjustment through the call at the market's price; each such
trade is another bite of the differential." Split the claim, say which half
is true and under what condition, name the step that fails, and identify the
ledger line where the loss enters in the paper's second run. Then state the
trade the paper says would have enhanced the profit at Step 3 of the
original run, the correct one, where the hedger holds $0.848$ share against

<Solution>
**What is true.** Trading the call at a price away from value does change
the profit by $k\,(M_n - V_n)$ compounded, for $k$ calls sold; if the hedge
requires selling calls at a node where they are overpriced, the profit is
enhanced. The paper's corollary says exactly this.

**What fails.** The step "each such trade is another bite" assumes the
hedger chooses the direction of the trade. He does not: the ratio next
period is fixed by the call's values on the two branches ahead, and at date
1 on the paper's path it rises from $0.719$ to $0.848$, which with fixed
shares requires buying back calls. Bought at $75$ against a value of
$60.463$, each call bought returns $14.537$ of mispricing to the market.
The loss enters at Step 2: $0.152$ calls at $11.40$ that are worth $9.19$,
an overpayment of $2.21$, which compounds to $2.43$ by date 2 and exceeds
the $2.341$ in the bank, leaving $-0.065$ after the paper's rounding.

**The enhancing trade.** At Step 3 of the original run, stock at $60$, the
hedger holds $0.848$ share against one call and the ratio falls to
$0.167$. Had the call still been overpriced, the hedger should have
lowered the ratio by selling more calls at the inflated price rather than
by selling $0.681$ share: with $0.848$ shares held, being short
$0.848/0.167 = 5.08$ calls gives the required ratio, so about $4.08$
further calls sold above value, each adding its excess to the ledger. (In

transferable move: the ratio decides the direction; the arbitrageur only
decides which instrument, and picks the mispriced one exactly when the
direction is in his favour.
</Solution>

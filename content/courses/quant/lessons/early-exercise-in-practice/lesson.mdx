Exchange-traded equity options are American, yet most of the calls among them never rationally exercise early — and the exceptions, dividend captures the day before ex-date, are priced by the same backward induction this module proved.

This is the thirteenth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the fourth move of its third module. The module has now proved
the whole American theory on the tree: <Ref lesson="stopping-times">the
first lesson</Ref> fixed the admissible exercise rules as stopping times,
<Ref lesson="american-derivatives">the second</Ref> showed that the
backward pass with a maximum at every node computes the smallest
supermartingale dominating the payoff, that no stopping time collects
more than its root value, that $\tau^* = \min\{n : V_n = G_n\}$ collects
exactly that, and that a convex payoff vanishing at zero is never exercised
early, and <Ref lesson="first-passage-perpetual-put">the third</Ref> found
the \$4 put's boundary at $2$ and read it as interest on the strike
against the upside the live put keeps. What stands open is how any of this
reads on an exchange, where exercise is a notice sent to a clearing house,
assignment is a draw, stocks pay dividends, and a trader can lose money on
a position whose payoff is the same in every state of the world. This
lesson holds the module's theorems against that system: the call theorem
is obeyed daily, in the form of a quote that never falls below intrinsic
value plus interest on the strike; a dividend breaks one hypothesis of the
theorem and restores exercise at exactly one date, which the pass prices
with one extra factor in the lattice; the put's boundary dips before an
ex-date; and a documented failure, a "riskless" box spread that lost
money, is a stopping time the seller left out of the maximum. <Ref
lesson="binomial-pricer-lab">The lab</Ref> then builds the pricer these
markets assume.

The route: the exercise and assignment mechanics needed to read the
artifacts; the sell-don't-exercise decomposition of a deep-in-the-money
call; the ex-date comparison in dollars, the theorem that confines call
exercise to the date before an ex-date, and the CRR recursion that prices
it; the put's boundary with a dividend inserted; the box-spread
post-mortem; and the list of places where the exchange departs from the
tree.

## The system: exercise is an order the holder sends, assignment is a draw the writer receives

The module's theorems are statements about a holder who chooses a
stopping time and a seller who hedges against every one. On an exchange
the holder and the seller never meet, and the two clauses of the
replication theorem, "the hedge can pay whenever the buyer exercises" and
"the buyer may exercise at any stopping time", are implemented by a
clearing house.

<Figure
  src="./figures/occ-flow.svg"
  alt="A pipeline of five boxes: holder, broker, OCC, clearing member, writer; an exercise notice travels left to right, the OCC box is labelled random draw, and the writer box is labelled assigned; a note below states the $0.01 expiry threshold"
  caption="An exercise notice travels from the holder to the OCC; the OCC draws a clearing member at random, the member picks one of its writers, and that writer is assigned."
/>

Every listed equity option in the United States is cleared by the
[Options Clearing Corporation](https://www.theocc.com/), the OCC, which
guarantees every writer's obligation and keeps the record of every open
position. A holder who wants to exercise instructs a broker; the broker's
clearing member places an *exercise notice* with the OCC; the OCC selects
at random a member with an open short position in the same contract, and
that member selects, by a procedure fixed in advance, one of its own
writers. That writer is *assigned*: for a call, deliver the stock and
receive $K$; for a put, take the stock and pay $K$. The holder's $\tau$ is
a decision. The writer's is a draw from the pool of every open short
position in the contract, which is the first departure from the tree and
the last section's business.

At expiry the choice is made for the holder unless the holder objects.
Under the OCC's *exercise by exception* rule, an expiring equity option
whose intrinsic value at the closing price is at least \$0.01 is exercised
automatically unless the clearing member instructs otherwise, and an
option below that threshold lapses. A writer whose short strike sits within
cents of the close does not know, at the close, whether stock will arrive
on Monday; the name for that is *pin risk*, and it is also on the last
section's list.

The vocabulary of this section is all the artifacts need: notice,
assignment, exercise by exception. Everything else in the lesson is the
module's.

## Theorem 4.5.1 on the tape: the deep-in-the-money call is sold, never exercised

The convex-payoff theorem of <Ref lesson="american-derivatives">the second
lesson</Ref> (Shreve I, Theorem 4.5.1) says that on a stock paying no
dividends the American call's price process equals the European call's at
every node, and $\tau \equiv N$ is optimal. Its observable consequence on
an exchange is a quote: an American call on a non-dividend stock trades at
its European value, and that value never falls below intrinsic value plus
one thing the holder who exercises throws away.

### Two portfolios again: what the buyer of the call pays

<Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">Put–call parity</Ref>, proved in the
second lesson of the course for European options with no model of the
stock, reads $c = S - Ke^{-rT} + p$, with $p$ the European put at the same
strike and expiry. Since $C = c$ by the theorem, the American call's price
is
$$
C \;=\; \underbrace{(S - K)}_{\text{exercise value}}
      \;+\; \underbrace{K\bigl(1 - e^{-rT}\bigr)}_{\text{interest on the strike}}
      \;+\; \underbrace{p}_{\text{the put}} ,
$$
for a call in the money, and each of the two extra terms is nonnegative.
A holder who exercises collects the first term. A holder who sells collects
all three, because the buyer, who can carry the hedge of the module at
exactly this cost, pays no less.

<Example title="the \$70 call at \$85, 45 days, r = 3%">
The strike discounted: $Ke^{-rT} = 70\,e^{-0.03 \times 45/365} = 70 \times
0.99631 = 69.74$. The interest the strike earns over 45 days is
$70 - 69.74 = 0.26$. The alive floor of the call is $S - Ke^{-rT} = 85 -
69.74 = 15.26$, already above the exercise value $15.00$ by the $0.26$.

The \$70 put with the same expiry is quoted at $0.55$. Parity then gives
the call's price: $C = 15.26 + 0.55 = 15.81$. Exercise pays $15.00$.
Selling pays $15.81$, and the $0.81$ difference decomposes as $0.26$ of
interest on the strike plus $0.55$ of insurance against the stock falling
through \$70 in the next 45 days. Per contract of 100 shares the holder
who exercises hands the market \$81.

The holder who wants the shares rather than cash reaches the same
conclusion by a different route: keep the call, put the \$70 per share in
the money market for 45 days, and pay the strike at expiry, having earned
the $0.26$ and kept the option to walk away.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/sell-vs-exercise.svg"
  alt="Two horizontal bars: the exercise bar is 15.00 of intrinsic value in blue; the sale bar is the same 15.00 plus a thin amber segment of 0.26 interest and a green segment of 0.55 put value, with the last dollar magnified below to make the two segments legible"
  caption="Exercising collects the blue bar; selling collects all three segments, because the buyer pays S − Ke⁻ʳᵀ + p."
/>

The decomposition is the theorem's proof read as a price. The chord
inequality $g(S) \le \frac{1}{1 + r}g((1 + r)S)$ of the second lesson said
that the payoff scales up faster than the strike's interest; the put term
is the conditional-Jensen slack, the value of the branches where the stock
ends below $K$ and the exercised holder would have wished not to own it.
On the tape both terms are cents on a deep-in-the-money call and dollars
near the money, and neither is ever negative.

<KeyFact>
The early-exercise premium of an American call on a stock paying no
dividend is zero, not small: the theorem's quantifier is exact, and the
hypothesis the zero hangs on is the absence of any dividend inside the
option's life, the one the next section removes.
</KeyFact>

## Dividends: exercise just before the ex-date, and one factor in the lattice

From the theorem one would expect a dividend to shift the call's price
and leave the rule "never exercise" intact. It does the opposite: the
price moves by the dividend, and the rule acquires exactly one exception.
A dividend is paid to whoever owns the share on the *ex-dividend date*,
the first day the share trades without the right to it; on that morning
the share opens lower by the dividend, and the call holder, who does not
own a share, receives nothing. Between ex-dates nothing has changed, and
at one date something has.

### The two-date comparison in dollars

<Example title="the \$100 call at \$104, a \$0.50 dividend tomorrow, 30 days after it, r = 1%">
Two fates for the call on the last cum-dividend day.

**Exercise today.** Pay $100$, receive a share worth $104$ that will
collect the $0.50$ tomorrow: the exercise value is $104 - 100 = 4.00$.

**Hold through the ex-date.** Tomorrow the share is worth $104 - 0.50 =
103.50$ and no further dividend falls in the option's life, so the call
theorem applies from tomorrow on and the call's floor is the European
floor on the ex-dividend stock: $(S - D) - Ke^{-r\tau}$ with $\tau$ the 30
days remaining. $Ke^{-r\tau} = 100\,e^{-0.01 \times 30/365} = 99.92$, so
the floor is $103.50 - 99.92 = 3.58$, and the call's value tomorrow is
$3.58$ plus the put at \$100 on the ex-dividend stock.

**The comparison.** Exercise beats the floor by $4.00 - 3.58 = 0.42$,
which is the dividend $0.50$ the exercised holder captures minus the
interest $K(1 - e^{-r\tau}) = 0.08$ that the holder who waits keeps.
Exercise is optimal exactly when the put is worth less than $0.42$: deep in
the money, where a 30-day put 3.4% out of the money is worth cents, the
holder exercises; near the money, where the put is worth dollars, the
holder waits.
</Example>

<Figure
  src="./figures/dividend-race.svg"
  alt="A timeline with three ticks, today cum-dividend at S = 104, the ex-date where S drops to 103.50, and expiry 30 days later; above it a blue bar for the exercise value 4.00 and below it a bar for the hold value 3.58 plus an unknown green put segment; the balance line states that exercise wins when the put is worth less than 0.42"
  caption="The dividend race at the last cum-dividend date: the dividend goes to the exercised holder, the strike's interest and the put go to the holder who waits."
/>

The comparison has two consequences that hold for any stock. First, a
dividend smaller than the interest on the strike over the time to expiry,
$D \le K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$, can never make exercise optimal, whatever the
stock price: the hold floor already exceeds the exercise value. Second,
when $D > K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$ the exercise value exceeds the floor by
$D - K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$, and for a high enough stock price the put is
worth less than that. At 1% rates a \$0.50 quarterly dividend beats a
month's interest on a \$100 strike six times over; at 5% and three months
to run it does not beat it at all. The closing exercises put the same
comparison on other numbers.

<Exercise>
Same call, same stock at $104$, same strike. (a) The dividend is \$0.05
instead of \$0.50, 30 days and 1% as before. (b) The dividend is \$0.50,
but the rate is 5% and expiry is 90 days after the ex-date. In each case

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** Compare $D$ with $K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$ first; only if the
dividend is larger does the stock price matter.

**(a)** $K(1 - e^{-r\tau}) = 100(1 - e^{-0.01 \times 30/365}) = 0.08$.
The hold floor is $(104 - 0.05) - 99.92 = 4.03$, above the exercise value
$4.00$ before the put is even counted. Since $0.05 < 0.08$, no stock price
makes exercise optimal: raising $S$ raises the exercise value and the
floor by the same amount.

**(b)** $K(1 - e^{-r\tau}) = 100(1 - e^{-0.05 \times 90/365}) = 1.23$.
The hold floor is $103.50 - 98.77 = 4.73$, above $4.00$ by $0.73 = 1.23 -
0.50$. Again no stock price makes exercise optimal.

The transferable move: the dividend competes with the interest on the
strike over the remaining life, and only when it wins that comparison
does the put's value, which does depend on $S$, get a vote.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### Why only the date before an ex-date: the theorem, piecewise

The example asserted that the call theorem applies from the ex-date on.
The general statement is that a dividend restores exercise at one kind of
date and no other, and its proof is the theorem's Step 2 with the
martingale property of the discounted stock checked date by date.

<Theorem title="exercise only before an ex-date (CRR 1979 §6; Hull §15.12; Shreve II §8.5.2)">
In the $N$-period model with $r > 0$, let the stock pay a proportional
dividend $\delta S$ at each of a known set of ex-dividend dates, so that
$S_{n+1} = (1 - \delta)^{v_{n+1}}\,\omega_{n+1} S_n$ with $\omega_{n+1} \in
\{u, d\}$ and $v_{n+1} = 1$ at an ex-date and $0$ otherwise. Then the
American call with intrinsic value $(S_n - K)^+$ is never exercised at a
date $n < N$ such that $n + 1$ is not an ex-dividend date, except where its
intrinsic value is zero. Exercise, if optimal at all, occurs at a date
immediately before an ex-dividend date or at expiry.

<Proof>
Roadmap: at a date whose successor is not an ex-date, the continuation
value is bounded below by the exercise value plus one period's interest on
the strike, by dominance, conditional Jensen and the martingale step; the
bound is strict wherever the call is in the money.

Fix $n < N$ with $v_{n+1} = 0$. By clause (i) of the Snell theorem,
$V_{n+1} \ge (S_{n+1} - K)^+$, so the continuation value at $n$ satisfies
$$
\frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]
\;\ge\; \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n\bigl[(S_{n+1} - K)^+\bigr]
\;\ge\; \frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl(\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}] - K\bigr)^+
\;=\; \Bigl(S_n - \frac{K}{1 + r}\Bigr)^+ ,
$$
the second inequality by conditional Jensen for the convex function
$x \mapsto (x - K)^+$, and the equality because with no dividend at
$n + 1$ the discounted stock is a $\tilde P$-martingale,
$\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}] = (1 + r)S_n$. Since $r > 0$,
$(S_n - K/(1 + r))^+ > S_n - K = (S_n - K)^+$ whenever $S_n > K$; for
$S_n \le K$ the exercise value $(S_n - K)^+$ is zero, so exercising
collects nothing there, whatever the bound is worth (it is positive for
$K/(1 + r) < S_n \le K$, which only says the continuation is too). So the
continuation strictly exceeds the exercise value at every such node where
the call is in the money, and $V_n = G_n$ there only if $G_n = 0$, an
exercise that collects nothing. The strictness
is where $r > 0$ is spent: at $r = 0$ the chain still shows that
continuation attains the maximum, which is the form Shreve I, Theorem
4.5.1 states, but with both successors in the money Jensen is tight,
$V_n = G_n > 0$, and exercising is value-equivalent to waiting rather than
worse. This is the sentence where the argument is general: the only
property of the date used is $v_{n+1} = 0$.

At a date with $v_{n+1} = 1$ the same chain gives
$\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}] = (1 - \delta)(1 + r)S_n$ and the bound
$\bigl((1 - \delta)S_n - K/(1 + r)\bigr)^+$, which can fall below
$S_n - K$; no conclusion follows, and the pass must compare.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Three checks on the proof. The idea in one sentence: between ex-dates the
tree is the tree of the second lesson and the theorem's Step 2 runs
unchanged; at the step across an ex-date the discounted stock drops by the
dividend, the martingale equality becomes a supermartingale inequality,
and Jensen no longer delivers the floor. The hypothesis the dividend
breaks is not $g(0) = 0$, which the call still satisfies, but the
martingale property of the discounted stock. The proof also yields the
necessary condition for exercise at a pre-ex-date node: the continuation
is at least $((1 - \delta)S_n - K/(1 + r))^+$, so exercise requires
$S_n - K > (1 - \delta)S_n - K/(1 + r)$, that is
$\delta S_n > K - K/(1 + r)$: the dividend must exceed one period's
interest on the strike, the discrete form of $D > K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$.

### The pass absorbs the dividend: CRR's recursion

The theorem says where to look; the pass says what the value is. Cox, Ross
and Rubinstein's [1979 paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(79)90015-1)
handles the dividend in its §6 with one change to the lattice and none to
the comparison.

<Remark title="the dividend-adjusted lattice (CRR 1979, §6)">
With $v(n, i)$ the number of ex-dividend dates among the next $n - i$
periods, the stock price at the node reached by $j$ up-moves is
$u^j d^{\,n - i - j}(1 - \delta)^{v(n, i)} S$, and the call's value there is
$$
C(n, i, j) = \max\Bigl\{u^j d^{\,n - i - j}(1 - \delta)^{v(n, i)} S - K,\;
\frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl[\tilde p\,C(n, i - 1, j + 1) + \tilde q\,C(n, i - 1, j)\bigr]\Bigr\}.
$$
The lattice still recombines, because a proportional dividend multiplies
every node at a date by the same factor; the comparison at each node is
the American algorithm of the second lesson verbatim; and the hedge
ratio is the same difference quotient. The one operational change is in
the hedge: a seller short the stock over an ex-date owes the dividend to
the lender, and a seller long it collects it.
</Remark>

<Example title="the \$5 call on the \$4 tree with a halving dividend at n = 2">
The tree is the module's, $u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$,
$\tilde p = \tilde q = \tfrac12$, discount $0.8$, three periods, strike
$5$, and the stock pays half of itself out at time $2$: a caricature
chosen so that the lattice stays in powers of two. Prices: $4$; $8, 2$;
after the halving $8, 2, 0.5$; then $16, 4, 1, 0.25$.

**Time 3.** Payoffs $11, 0, 0, 0$.

**Time 2** (ex-dividend, no dividend ahead). At $8$: intrinsic $3$,
continuation $0.8 \times \tfrac12 (11 + 0) = 4.4$; continue, as the theorem
says. At $2$ and $0.5$: $0$.

**Time 1** (the last cum-dividend date). At $S_1 = 8$: intrinsic $3$,
continuation $0.8 \times \tfrac12 (4.4 + 0) = 1.76$; $V_1 = 3$,
**exercise**. At $S_1 = 2$: $0$.

**Time 0.** Intrinsic $0$, continuation $0.8 \times \tfrac12 (3 + 0) =
1.2$; $V_0 = 1.2$.

The European call on the same dividend-paying tree is worth $0.704$
(time 2 values $4.4, 0, 0$; time 1 values $1.76, 0$; root $0.704$), so the
right to exercise before the ex-date is worth $0.496$ at the root, against
the zero it was worth without the dividend. At the exercise node the
necessary condition holds with room: the dividend $\delta S_1 = 4$ against
one period's interest on the strike, $5 - 5/1.25 = 1$.
</Example>

### The critical price Ŝ

The two exercises show the same node deciding both ways as the dividend
changes; fixing the dividend and moving the stock gives the object CRR
name. At a pre-ex-date node the exercise value $S - K$ rises one-for-one
with $S$, while the continuation rises more slowly, because the put term
inside it shrinks as the stock climbs. The two cross once, at a
*critical price* $\hat S$: the lowest price at which the hedging portfolio
one period on is worth exactly $S - K$. Above $\hat S$ the holder
exercises, below it the holder waits, and CRR record its comparative
statics without solving for it: $\hat S$ is lower the higher the dividend
yield, the lower the interest rate, and the lower the strike. The lattice
of the example puts $\hat S$ between the exercised node $S_1 = 8$ and the
next lattice price below it, and cannot resolve it further, which is the
same coarseness the six-period put showed in the second lesson.

<KeyFact>
The module's algorithm was already general enough for dividends: one
factor $(1 - \delta)^{v}$ in the lattice, nothing in the comparison. What
the dividend changes is the theorem, not the pass: the discounted stock is
no longer a martingale across the ex-date, Jensen no longer delivers the
floor, and the pass finds an exercise node at exactly the date before the
ex-date and nowhere else.
</KeyFact>

## Puts: exercised early with no dividend, and the boundary that dips before an ex-date

The put needs no dividend to be exercised early: <Ref
lesson="american-derivatives">the second lesson</Ref> found the \$5 put's
exercise nodes on a tree with none, and <Ref
lesson="first-passage-perpetual-put">the third</Ref> read the boundary as
the level where interest lost on the strike first exceeds the upside the
live put keeps. On an exchange this is the ordinary case: deep
in-the-money puts are exercised before expiry, and the writers of those
puts are assigned.

### The dividend runs the other way for the put

For the put a coming dividend is a reason to wait, not to act: the
ex-date drop is a fall in the stock the put holder gets for free by
holding through it. CRR record the direction in §6, that the effect of
dividends yet to be paid diminishes the advantage of immediate exercise,
and the pass shows it as a boundary that dips at the date before the
ex-date and resumes after.

### The short side: assignment on the deep put, and the pin at expiry

The writer of a deep-in-the-money put on an exchange is on the wrong side
of the second lesson's optimal rule. At a node where $V_n = G_n$ the
rational holder exercises, and the replication theorem says the writer's
hedge holds exactly $G_n$ there: an assigned writer who followed the
theorem pays out of the hedge and is whole. An assigned writer who did
not, or who priced the put as European and hedged the smaller value,
is short, in cash and on a date chosen by someone else, the amount the
replication-with-consumption theorem of <Ref
lesson="american-derivatives">the second lesson</Ref> called $C_n$
(subscripted; the unsubscripted $C$ of this lesson is the call's price):
the seller's withdrawal at that node, $V_n$ minus the discounted
continuation, which is exactly what the American hedge holds above the
European one there. The box spread of the next section is that sentence
with numbers.

At expiry the draw is replaced by the threshold. A put whose strike is
\$0.01 above the close is exercised by exception; one whose strike is
\$0.01 below lapses; and a writer whose strike is the close itself learns
on the following business day whether a holder overrode the rule. The
module's model has $V_N = G_N$ and no uncertainty at $N$; the exchange
has a cent of it.

<KeyFact>
On the exchange the put's early exercise is the common case and the
call's is the exception, exactly as the module ordered them, and a coming
dividend moves the two in opposite directions: it creates a call exercise
node at the date before the ex-date and removes put exercise nodes at the
same date.
</KeyFact>

## The box spread post-mortem: a stopping time the seller left out of the maximum

From <Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">the first lesson's</Ref>
replication argument one would expect a combination of options whose
payoff is $K_2 - K_1$ in every state of the world to be a zero-coupon bond,
priced at $(K_2 - K_1)e^{-rT}$ and riskless to sell at any higher price.
Hull's Business Snapshot 12.1 records a trader who sold one above that
price and lost money, and the previous section named the reason in
advance: the writer of a deep put was short a withdrawal on a date chosen
by someone else. The combination is a *box spread*, a bull call spread
plus a bear put spread at the same two strikes $K_1 < K_2$: long the $K_1$
call, short the $K_2$ call, long the $K_2$ put, short the $K_1$ put. At
expiry the calls pay $(S_T - K_1)^+ - (S_T - K_2)^+$ and the puts pay
$(K_2 - S_T)^+ - (K_1 - S_T)^+$, and the sum is $K_2 - K_1$ in every state.
For European options the bond argument is exact; the Snapshot's legs are
American.

### The position, in Hull's numbers

A stock trades at \$50 with 30% volatility, no dividend expected, and
$r = 8\%$. A trader is offered the chance to sell a two-month 55/60 box
for \$5.10 on the CBOE, the Chicago Board Options Exchange, where the four
contracts are listed: buy the \$60 call, buy the \$55 put, sell the \$55
call, sell the \$60 put. The box pays $60 - 55 = 5$ at expiry in every
state, worth $5\,e^{-0.08 \times 2/12} = 4.93$ today. Selling it for
$5.10$ looks like $0.17$ of free money.

$5.23$ European and $5.44$ American; the \$60 put $9.46$ European and
$10.00$ American. The European box is $0.70 + 4.23 = 4.93$, the number
above. The American box is $0.70 + 4.56 = 5.26$. The trader sold for
$5.10$ something worth $5.26$.

### The diagnosis in the module's language

The \$60 put's American price is $10.00$, and $10.00 = 60 - 50$ is its
intrinsic value. In the second lesson's terms, $V_0 = G_0$ at the root:
the stopping time $\tau^* = \min\{n : V_n = G_n\}$ is $0$, and the holder
of that put exercises at once. The trader who sold it is assigned on the
first day, pays $60$ for a share worth $50$, and is left holding the share,
the \$55 put, the short \$55 call and the \$60 call, a position worth
$50 + 5.44 - 0.96 + 0.26 = 54.74$ at market. The ledger: $5.10$ received,
$60$ paid, $54.74$ held, net $-0.16$ per share, or \$16 per contract, on
a trade whose payoff was the same in every state of the world.

The module result that diagnoses it is the optimal-exercise theorem of
<Ref lesson="american-derivatives">the second lesson</Ref>: the American
price is the maximum of $\tilde E[G_\tau/(1 + r)^\tau]$ over every
stopping time, and a seller who prices at the value of one convenient
rule, $\tau \equiv N$, has priced a different contract. The European box
value $4.93$ is $\tilde E[G_N/(1 + r)^N]$ summed over four legs; the
American value $5.26$ is the same sum with the maximum over $\tau$ taken
inside two of them, and the counterparty on the \$60 put held a $\tau$ the
seller ignored. Nothing about the box's certainty at expiry protects the
seller from a date before expiry.

<KeyFact>
The failure is not the box's. Any combination with a short American leg
priced at $\tau \equiv N$, a vertical put spread, a calendar spread, a
conversion, has fixed one convenient stopping time for a counterparty who
holds all of them, and the shortfall lands on a date the seller did not
pick. The pricer that does not repeat the Snapshot's mistake takes the
maximum at every node of every leg, and that node-by-node check, with the
replicating portfolio verified beside it, is what <Ref
lesson="binomial-pricer-lab">the lab</Ref> builds.
</KeyFact>

## Where the idealisation ends: lotteries, cash dividends, costs, and the tree's size

The module's results used in this lesson are four: the convex-payoff
theorem for the call, the Snell-envelope price as a maximum over all
stopping times, the backward pass with CRR's dividend factor, and the
put's exercise region. Each holds on the exchange to the extent listed
here, and departs where listed.

### Assignment is a lottery, not a chosen stopping time

The holder's $\tau$ is a stopping time; a single writer's is a random draw from every open short
position, and the theorem's hedge is a hedge against the option, not
against the draw. The replication theorem still holds, because the hedge
carries $V_n \ge G_n$ at every node and can pay whichever writer is
picked; what the writer needs beyond the theorem is the cash and margin
to deliver on a date chosen by the OCC's random-number generator, and
the box-spread seller had neither the price nor the cash. Pin risk at
expiry is the same departure at $n = N$.

### Dividends are cash, uncertain, and declared late

The CRR factor is a proportional yield, which keeps the lattice recombining. A cash dividend
$D$ does not: $uS - D$ followed by $d$ differs from $dS - D$ followed by
$u$, and the number of nodes grows with the number of dates before the
ex-date. Hull's escrow model (§15.12 for the closed form, §21.3 for the tree)
repairs it by running the tree on $S$ minus the present value of the
dividends and adding the escrow back at each node; the amount and the date themselves are forecasts, revised when
the company declares, and the theorem's "known ex-dividend dates" is an
assumption a pricer refreshes weekly. Merton's perpetual-warrant footnote
of <Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the second lesson of the
course</Ref> is the same caveat in its oldest form.

### Exercise is not free

Assignment and exercise carry fees, exercising a
call converts an option position into a stock position with a different
margin requirement, and the sale alternative of the second section loses
the bid–ask spread, which on a deep-in-the-money option can be wider than
the interest and the put together. The theorem's inequality $C > S - K$ is
strict; the amount by which it is strict is the budget for these costs,
and near expiry on a deep-in-the-money call that budget is cents.

### Trees stop scaling

The pass is exact for a one-dimensional Markov state and cheap on a
recombining lattice; for a path-dependent payoff, a non-recombining
dividend, or several underlying assets, the node count is exponential,
and the closing exercise measures the wall. The standard
industrial answer past it is regression Monte Carlo: simulate paths,
estimate the continuation value at each date by regressing realised
payoffs on functions of the state, and exercise where intrinsic beats the
estimate. That is the method of
[Longstaff and Schwartz (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/14.1.113);
it is beyond this course's scope, and its European half,
Monte Carlo pricing with its $1/\sqrt N$ error, is <Ref
lesson="monte-carlo-pricing">the sixth module's</Ref>. Between the two
labels, a *Bermudan* option (Hull §26.3) may be exercised only on listed
dates, and the pass prices it by taking the maximum at those dates alone.

  caption="The wall: recombination keeps the lattice at n² nodes; anything that breaks it grows as 2ⁿ, and passes the one-second line before n = 30."

What is established: on a stock paying no dividends the American call
trades at the European price, and the tape enforces the theorem as a
quote of $S - K$ plus interest on $K$ plus the put, which the holder who
exercises rather than sells forfeits; a dividend breaks the martingale
property of the discounted stock across the ex-date and nothing else, so
exercise is confined to the date before an ex-date, is possible only when
the dividend beats the strike's interest over the remaining life, and is
priced by the pass with one factor $(1 - \delta)^v$ in the lattice; the
put's boundary dips before an ex-date and resumes after it; and a box of
American options sold at the European price loses money because the price
is a maximum over every stopping time and the deep short put's is
attained at $\tau = 0$. What is not established is the pricer itself:
every number in this module was computed by hand on a tree of at most six
periods, and the boundary was read off a lattice too coarse to place it.
<Ref lesson="binomial-pricer-lab">The lab</Ref> builds the pricer, verifies
its replicating portfolio at every node, and extracts the exercise
boundary from a tree fine enough to locate it.

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "Early exercise of an American call is always
irrational. The proof of the convex-payoff theorem uses only that
$(s - K)^+$ is convex and vanishes at $0$, and a dividend changes neither
fact." Give the counterexample from the lesson with its numbers, name the

<Solution>
**The counterexample.** The \$5 call on the \$4 tree with the halving
dividend at time $2$: at $S_1 = 8$ intrinsic $3$ against continuation
$1.76$, exercise strictly optimal, root value $1.2$ against European
$0.704$.

**The step.** Step 2 of the proof writes $(1 + r)S_n = \tilde E_n[S_{n+1}]$
and applies conditional Jensen to get $g(S_n) \le \frac{1}{1 +
r}\tilde E_n[g(S_{n+1})]$. Across an ex-date $\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}] =

and useless. The claim is right that convexity and $g(0) = 0$ survive; the
hypothesis violated is the one stated in the proof's Step 2 and in the
theorem's model assumptions, that the discounted stock is a
$\tilde P$-martingale.

the theorem applies date by date: an American call on a dividend-paying
stock is never exercised at a date whose successor is not an ex-date, and
never at all if every dividend is below the strike's interest to the next
exercise opportunity. The transferable move: a theorem's hypotheses
include the model's, and "the stock pays no dividends" was one of them.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Boundary probe. On the dividend-adjusted tree, let the yield $\delta \to 0$
with everything else fixed. Show directly from the continuation bound of
the theorem that the set of prices at which exercise is optimal at a
pre-ex-date node shrinks to nothing, so that the critical price
$\hat S \to \infty$, and say what the limiting tree is. Then do the same
for the continuous-time condition $D > K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$ as $D \to 0$.

<Solution>
At a pre-ex-date node the continuation is at least
$((1 - \delta)S - K/(1 + r))^+$, so exercise requires
$S - K > (1 - \delta)S - K/(1 + r)$, that is $\delta S > K r/(1 + r)$,
that is $S > K r/(\delta(1 + r))$. The right side grows without bound as
$\delta \to 0$: for every fixed $S$ there is a $\delta_0 > 0$ below which
the inequality fails and the node continues. So the exercise set at the
node is empty for small enough $\delta$ and $\hat S \to \infty$. The
limiting tree is the second lesson's, on which the theorem holds at every
node, and the exception has vanished continuously into the rule rather
than switching off: at $\delta = 0.5$ the node $S_1 = 8$ exercised, at
$\delta = 0.2$ it did not, and no $\delta$ makes a node exercise whose
dividend is below the strike's one-period interest.

In continuous time, $D \le K(1 - e^{-r\tau})$ rules exercise out at every
stock price, and for $D$ below the interest on the strike over the
remaining life the condition holds for every $S$. As $D \to 0$ the
threshold is met for every $\tau > 0$ and $r > 0$, which is the call
theorem of the course's second lesson. The transferable move: an
exception that depends on a parameter should reduce to the theorem when
the parameter is switched off, and this one does.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two lower bounds and a parity relation, from the tools of the course's
second lesson. (a) Prove $P \ge K - S$ for an American put and compare
with the European bound $p \ge Ke^{-rT} - S$, stating which is larger and
by how much. (b) Prove $S - K \le C - P \le S - Ke^{-rT}$ for American

box, which of the four legs carries the early-exercise premium, on which
side of the parity inequalities does that premium live, and what does the
\$60 put's $10.00$ say about where in the interval $C - P$ sits for that

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** (a) is a dominance argument; (b) is parity plus one
inequality per side; (c) is reading numbers against (b).

**(a)** An American put can be exercised now for $K - S$, so a price below
that is an arbitrage: buy the put, exercise, collect $K - S - P > 0$.
Hence $P \ge K - S$. The European bound is $Ke^{-rT} - S$, smaller by
$K(1 - e^{-rT})$, the interest on the strike: the American holder can have
$K$ now, the European holder only at $T$.

**(b)** Right side: $C = c$ for a call on a non-dividend stock, and
$P \ge p$, so $C - P \le c - p = S - Ke^{-rT}$ by European parity. Left
side, by the dominance lemma of the course's second lesson: portfolio A
is one European call plus $K$ in cash invested at $r$; portfolio B is one
American put plus one share. If B's holder exercises the put at some time
$t < T$, B is worth exactly $K$ then (the share is delivered for $K$),
while A is worth $c_t + Ke^{rt} \ge K$. If the put is held to expiry, B is
worth $\max(S_T, K)$ and A is worth
$(S_T - K)^+ + Ke^{rT} = \max(S_T, K) + K(e^{rT} - 1) \ge \max(S_T, K)$.
A never pays less than B at any exit
B's holder can choose, so A never costs less: $c + K \ge P + S$, and with
$C = c$ this is $C - P \ge S - K$.

**(c)** Only the put legs: $C = c$ for both calls. The premium lives on
the right side of (b): $C - P$ sits below $S - Ke^{-rT}$ by exactly
$P - p$, the early-exercise premium, which is $0.54$ for the \$60 put and
$0.21$ for the \$55. For the \$60 strike, $P = 10.00 = K - S$: the
American put sits exactly on the bound of (a). The interval in (b) is
$[S - K,\ S - Ke^{-rT}] = [-10.00,\ -9.21]$, and $C - P = 0.26 - 10.00 =
-9.74$ clears its left end by exactly $C = 0.26$, the call's whole value,
and sits below its right end $-9.21$ by $P - p = 0.54$, the early-exercise
premium just quoted, to within the cent the Snapshot's rounding allows. A
put worth no more than immediate exercise pushes $C - P$ as far left in
the interval as the call's price permits.
The transferable move: the parity interval's width $K(1 - e^{-rT})$ is
the strike's interest, and the American put's position in it measures how
close its holder is to exercising.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

doubles with every added step. No tree does it. Sampling $10^5$ paths of
$50$ steps is $5 \times 10^6$ increments, $50$ milliseconds, linear in the
number of steps whatever the dividend rule; what it gives up is exactness
and, for an American claim, the continuation value at each node, which
the tree had for free and which a simulation must estimate by regressing
realised payoffs on the state. The transferable move: the tree's economy
is recombination, and any feature that breaks it turns $n^2$ into $2^n$.
This wall is why <Ref lesson="binomial-pricer-lab">the lab</Ref> builds
the tree pricer for the claims a tree can price, and why <Ref
lesson="monte-carlo-pricing">the sixth module</Ref> prices European claims
by sampling paths, with an error that falls as $1/\sqrt N$ rather than a
node count that grows as $2^n$; Longstaff and Schwartz's regression is the
American extension, past this course's scope.
</Solution>

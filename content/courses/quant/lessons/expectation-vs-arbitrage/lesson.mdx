A bookmaker who sets odds from his own correct win probabilities can lose a
fortune on a single race; one who sets them from the money wagered breaks
even no matter which horse wins — and a stock forward is priced the second
way.

This is the first move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs about the underlying. Nothing has been established yet, so the claim
is made here in the simplest case that admits it: a contract whose payoff is
linear in the stock price. What this lesson establishes is the ground rule,
that a payoff which can be manufactured from traded assets has the cost of
the manufacture as its only admissible price, and the definition of arbitrage
that makes "only admissible" precise. What stands open at the end is every
payoff that cannot be manufactured by a buy-and-hold position, which
<Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the next lesson</Ref> bounds and the
lesson after it prices.

The route: the parable in dollars; expectation pricing where it is right and
where it stops being enforceable; the forward priced by expectation and then
by construction, with the two numbers side by side; arbitrage defined and its
near misses catalogued; and the reason a call's kinked payoff is out of reach
of the construction used here.

## The bookmaker's two books: beliefs carry risk, the book carries none

A bookmaker takes bets on a two-horse race. He studies the form and
concludes, correctly, that horse A wins with probability $0.25$ and horse B
with probability $0.75$. Bets are quoted as *odds*: "3–1 against" means a
winning \$1 stake returns \$3 profit plus the stake, and "3–1 on" means a
winning \$3 stake returns \$1 profit plus the stake. Fair odds at his
probabilities are 3–1 against A and 3–1 on B, since a \$1 bet on A at 3–1
against has expected profit $0.25 \times 3 - 0.75 \times 1 = 0$.

The public does not share his assessment. Sentiment puts \$5,000 on A and
\$10,000 on B. Two ledgers now describe his position.

**Odds from beliefs.** If A wins he pays the A-backers \$15,000 of profit and
keeps the \$10,000 staked on B: net $-5{,}000$. If B wins he pays the
B-backers $10{,}000/3 = 3{,}333$ and keeps the \$5,000 staked on A: net
$+1{,}667$. His expected profit is $0.25 \times (-5{,}000) + 0.75 \times
1{,}667 = 0$. The book is fair in expectation and exposes him to a \$5,000
loss on one race in four.

**Odds from the book.** Set the odds from the money instead: 2–1 against A
and 2–1 on B, the ratio of the two wagers. If A wins he pays $2 \times 5{,}000
= 10{,}000$ and keeps \$10,000: net $0$. If B wins he pays $10{,}000 / 2 =
5{,}000$ and keeps \$5,000: net $0$. Whichever horse wins, the money paid
out is exactly the money kept from the losing side. His correct probabilities
appear nowhere in the calculation.

<Figure
  src="./figures/bookmaker-books.svg"
  alt="Two ledgers for the same race and wagers: odds set from beliefs net −5,000 or +1,667 depending on the winner; odds set from the wagers net 0 either way"
  caption="Same wagers, same race: the beliefs ledger is fair in expectation and risks $5,000; the book ledger nets zero in both outcomes."
/>

The second bookmaker has done something the first has not: he has matched
every dollar he may owe against a dollar he will certainly hold. His payout
to A-backers is funded by B-backers and vice versa, in every outcome. The
first bookmaker's position depends on an average over races that have not
been run; the second's depends on nothing. Being right about the horses
bought the first bookmaker an expected profit of zero and a real chance of
ruin.

The break-even odds are a function of the wagers alone. For $w_A$ on A and
$w_B$ on B the riskless book quotes $w_B : w_A$ against A, and the true
probabilities never enter. The closing exercise on the bookmaker's general
odds derives this and adds the margin that a real bookmaker charges.

<Exercise>
Two horses: the first wins with probability $0.20$, the second with
probability $0.80$; \$10,000 is wagered on the first and \$50,000 on the
second. Compute the bookmaker's net in both outcomes at fair odds from his
probabilities (4–1 against the first, 4–1 on the second), then at odds from
the book. Before opening the solution, write down the four payout figures.

<Solution>
**From beliefs (4–1 against, 4–1 on).** First horse wins: pay
$4 \times 10{,}000 = 40{,}000$, keep \$50,000, net $+10{,}000$. Second wins:
pay $50{,}000 / 4 = 12{,}500$, keep \$10,000, net $-2{,}500$. Expected net:
$0.20 \times 10{,}000 + 0.80 \times (-2{,}500) = 0$, with a \$2,500 loss four
races in five.

**From the book (5–1 against, 5–1 on).** The wager ratio is $50{,}000 :
10{,}000 = 5 : 1$. First horse wins: pay $5 \times 10{,}000 = 50{,}000$, keep
\$50,000, net $0$. Second wins: pay $50{,}000 / 5 = 10{,}000$, keep \$10,000,
net $0$.

The transferable move: the odds that cancel the book are read off the
wagers, and whatever is known about the horses is discarded.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyFact>
The bookmaker's forecast, however accurate, buys him variance and no expected
gain; the riskless odds are set by the positions on his book, and the forward
price below is set the same way: by a position that cancels, not by a
forecast that averages out.
</KeyFact>

## Expectation pricing: correct for a repeated coin, unenforceable for one play

The first bookmaker's method is not foolish, and the case for it should be
stated at full strength before it is limited. Consider a game: a fair coin is
tossed and pays \$1 on heads, nothing on tails. Fifty cents is the natural
price, and the reason is a theorem.

### The strong law makes 50 cents the long-run price

<Theorem title="Strong law of large numbers (Kolmogorov)">
Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be independent random variables with a common
distribution of finite mean $m$, and let $\bar X_n = (X_1 + \cdots + X_n)/n$.
Then $\bar X_n \to m$ as $n \to \infty$ with probability one.
</Theorem>

The proof is measure-theoretic and is not carried here; Williams's
*Probability with Martingales* §12.10 gives it in full. The course uses the
statement once, in this section, and never again.

Apply it to the game bought repeatedly at price $c$ per play. The profit on
the $n$-th play is $X_n - c$ with $X_n \in \{0, 1\}$ equiprobable, so the
average profit per play converges to $\tfrac12 - c$ with probability one. At
$c < 0.50$ the buyer is certain, in the limit, to be in profit; at $c > 0.50$
the seller is. Fifty cents is the only price at which neither side can be
sure of winning in the long run, and this is what *expectation pricing*
means: price equals expected payoff, justified by repetition.

<Figure
  src="./figures/strong-law.svg"
  alt="Running average of profit per play at a 40-cent price over 400 simulated tosses, converging to the expected 0.10, with the two possible single-play outcomes marked at +0.60 and −0.40"
  caption="At a price of 40¢ the average profit per play settles at +0.10, but any single play is +0.60 or −0.40, nothing in between."
/>

The argument is sound wherever its hypothesis holds: many independent plays
of the same game, with the average being what is paid out. An insurer
pricing a million similar policies, or a casino, is in exactly this position,
and expectation pricing is the correct method there.

### One play, and the time value of money

The hypothesis fails for one play. Suppose the game is offered once at 40
cents. The buyer's expected profit is 10 cents, and the strong law says
nothing at all about a single toss: the outcome is $+0.60$ or $-0.40$, and
no amount of theory converts a favourable expectation into a guaranteed
gain. Betting the house on it would be a bet, not an arbitrage. The strong
law can suggest a price; it cannot enforce one, because enforcing a price
means having a strategy that punishes any other quote with certainty, and a
single favourable bet is not such a strategy.

One refinement is needed before stocks. If the toss happens at time $T$ and
the price is paid now, the price is 50 cents discounted. Throughout this
lesson a dollar at time $T$ is worth $e^{-rT}$ dollars now, where $r$ is the
*continuously compounded* riskless rate; equivalently a loan of $1$ now
repays $e^{rT}$ at $T$. The discounted expectation price of the deferred
game is $0.50\,e^{-rT}$, and half a unit of a bond paying $1$ at $T$
delivers the 50 cents exactly when needed.

<Remark title="compounding conventions">
The continuous-time modules of this course write growth as $e^{rT}$. The
binomial modules, starting with <Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the
one-period model</Ref>, use a per-period rate: one dollar becomes $1 + r$
after one period. The two agree when $1 + r = e^{r_c\,\Delta t}$ for a period
of length $\Delta t$; the symbol $r$ is reused for both, and each lesson
states which it means.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Expectation pricing is correct exactly where the risk is repeated,
independent, and unhedgeable, and it enforces nothing even there: the
strong law bounds an average of plays, and a single play is one sample.
</KeyFact>

## The same reasoning prices a forward, and the price is wrong

The coin game gave the expectation method its best case: the price was right
and merely unenforceable. On a stock, the same method gives a price that is
not even approximately right, and the mechanism that displaces it is the
subject of the rest of the course.

### The forward contract and its expectation price

A *forward contract* is an agreement made now to exchange one share of a
stock for a fixed amount $K$ at a fixed future time $T$. The buyer (the
*long* side) will receive the share and pay $K$; the seller (the *short*
side) will deliver the share and receive $K$. Nothing changes hands now. At
$T$ the long side holds a share worth $S_T$ for which it paid $K$, so its
payoff is $S_T - K$, positive or negative; the short side's payoff is
$K - S_T$. The *forward price* is the value of $K$ at which the contract is
worth zero to both sides at inception, so that neither pays the other to
enter it.

To apply expectation pricing, a model of $S_T$ is needed. The standard one
is *lognormal*: the log-return over $[0, T]$ is normal,
$$
\log \frac{S_T}{S_0} = X, \qquad X \sim N(\mu T,\ \sigma^2 T),
$$
where $\mu$ is the *drift* of the log-return per unit time and $\sigma$ its
*volatility*, the standard deviation per square root of unit time. The
expectation price sets $K$ so that the discounted expected payoff is zero,
$E[e^{-rT}(S_T - K)] = 0$, which gives $K = E[S_T]$.

<Example title="the lognormal mean">
$S_T = S_0 e^X$ with $X \sim N(m, v)$. Write $m = \mu T$ and $v = \sigma^2 T$.
Then
$$
E[e^X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^x \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi v}}
\exp\!\Bigl(-\frac{(x - m)^2}{2v}\Bigr)\,dx .
$$
Complete the square in the exponent:
$$
x - \frac{(x-m)^2}{2v}
= -\frac{x^2 - 2(m + v)x + m^2}{2v}
= -\frac{(x - (m + v))^2}{2v} + m + \frac{v}{2}.
$$
The remaining integrand is the $N(m + v, v)$ density, which integrates to
one, so $E[e^X] = e^{m + v/2}$ and
$$
E[S_T] = S_0\,e^{(\mu + \frac12 \sigma^2)T}.
$$
With $S_0 = 100$, $\mu = 10\%$, $\sigma = 20\%$ and $T = 1$ year:
$E[S_T] = 100\,e^{0.10 + 0.02} = 100\,e^{0.12} = 112.75$.
</Example>

Expectation pricing therefore writes $K = 112.75$ into a one-year forward on
this stock. Both parameters of the belief enter: a more bullish $\mu$ or a
larger $\sigma$ raises the number. From the coin game one would expect this
figure to be at worst a fair long-run price that a single contract cannot
enforce. It is not a fair price at all.

### The construction: borrow, buy, deliver

Take the seller's side of the contract, obliged to deliver one share at $T$.
Borrow $S_0$ now at the riskless rate, buy one share with it, and wait. At
$T$ the loan has grown to $S_0 e^{rT}$; deliver the share, receive $K$, repay
the loan. The net at $T$ is $K - S_0 e^{rT}$, a number fixed at inception
that does not depend on $S_T$.

<Task>
Step through the three frames and read the ledger's expiry column on the

This is the first instance of the course's central method.

<Definition title="replication">
A claim paying $V_T$ at time $T$ is **replicated** by a portfolio of traded
assets if the portfolio, set up now and adjusted only by rules fixed in
advance, has value exactly $V_T$ at $T$ in every state of the world. The
portfolio's cost now is the claim's **replication cost**.
</Definition>

Here the claim is the long forward's payoff $S_T - K$, and the replicating
portfolio is one share bought with a loan of $S_0$, plus a promise to pay
$K$ at $T$. Its value at $T$ is $S_T - S_0 e^{rT}$ from the share and the
loan, less the $K$ paid: $S_T - K$ exactly when $K = S_0 e^{rT}$, and the
portfolio cost nothing to set up. In this lesson the portfolio is *static*:
bought once and held, never rebalanced. The dynamic version, adjusted at
every step, is what the binomial and Black–Scholes modules build.

### The two arbitrages, in dollars

Set $S_0 = 100$, $r = 5\%$, $T = 1$: the construction's cost is
$S_0 e^{rT} = 100\,e^{0.05} = 105.13$.

<Example title="a dealer quotes the expectation price">
A dealer offers to buy or sell the one-year forward at $K = 112.75$, the
expectation price computed above. Sell him the forward, that is, take the
short side, and run the construction.

| | now | at $T$, $S_T$ high | at $T$, $S_T$ low |
|---|---|---|---|
| borrow 100 at 5% | $+100$ | $-105.13$ | $-105.13$ |
| buy one share | $-100$ | $+S_T$ | $+S_T$ |
| short forward at 112.75 | $0$ | $112.75 - S_T$ | $112.75 - S_T$ |
| **net** | $0$ | $+7.62$ | $+7.62$ |

The $S_T$ terms cancel in every column. The position costs nothing now and
pays $112.75 - 105.13 = 7.62$ at $T$ whether the stock rises, falls, or halves.
Nothing about the trade improves if the dealer's $\mu$ is right, and nothing
about it worsens if it is wrong.
</Example>

The share received under the forward returns the borrowed share, and
$105.13 - 100 = 5.13$ is collected in every state. The transferable move: a
quote above $S_0 e^{rT}$ is sold and manufactured; a quote below is bought
and the manufacture is run in reverse.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

### The theorem

The two trades together pin the forward price to a point. The statement
needs the market assumptions that made the trades possible, and they are
listed as hypotheses because later lessons weaken them one at a time.

<Theorem title="forward price of a non-dividend stock">
Suppose the stock pays no dividends before $T$, can be bought, sold and
sold short in any amount at the price $S_0$ without transaction costs, and
cash can be borrowed or lent in any amount at the continuously compounded
rate $r$, and $S_T$ exceeds and falls short of $S_0 e^{rT}$ each with
positive probability. Then the unique forward price that admits no
arbitrage by a static position in stock, cash and the forward is
$$
F_0 = S_0\,e^{rT}.
$$
No assumption on the distribution of $S_T$ is used.

<Proof>
The proof mode is direct: exhibit an arbitrage for every $K \ne S_0 e^{rT}$,
and check that $K = S_0 e^{rT}$ admits none of that form.

**If $K > S_0 e^{rT}$.** Short the forward at $K$, borrow $S_0$, buy one
share. Cost now: $S_0 - S_0 = 0$. At $T$: deliver the share against the
forward, receive $K$, repay $S_0 e^{rT}$. Net $K - S_0 e^{rT} > 0$ in every
state.

**If $K < S_0 e^{rT}$.** Long the forward at $K$, sell one share short,
lend $S_0$. Cost now: $-S_0 + S_0 = 0$. At $T$: collect $S_0 e^{rT}$ from the
loan, pay $K$ under the forward, receive the share, return it to the lender.
Net $S_0 e^{rT} - K > 0$ in every state.

**If $K = S_0 e^{rT}$.** A static position of $\alpha$ forwards, $a$ shares
and $b$ dollars of bond costs $a S_0 + b$ now and pays
$\alpha(S_T - K) + a S_T + b e^{rT}$ at $T$. Zero cost forces $b = -a S_0$,
and substituting $K = S_0 e^{rT}$ the payoff collapses to
$(\alpha + a)\,(S_T - S_0 e^{rT})$. If $\alpha + a > 0$ the position loses
whenever $S_T < S_0 e^{rT}$, if $\alpha + a < 0$ it loses whenever
$S_T > S_0 e^{rT}$, and both events have positive probability by
hypothesis; if $\alpha + a = 0$ the payoff is identically zero. No such
position is an arbitrage, so $K = S_0 e^{rT}$ admits none.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Three comprehension checks on the proof. The idea in one sentence: the
forward's payoff is a share minus a fixed sum, and both of those have prices
now. The step that uses the no-dividend hypothesis: "put the share in a
drawer and wait" loses nothing only if the share pays nothing while it sits
there; the dividend exercise at the end changes that. The step that uses short-selling: the second
direction; Hull (§5.4) shows it survives without short sales as long as
someone holds the stock for investment and can sell it.

<KeyFact>
The expectation formula $K = E[S_T]$ is not discarded by the course; it is
repaired. <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">The martingale
lesson</Ref> recovers $F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}$ as an expectation $\tilde E[S_T]$
under probabilities constructed so that the stock's expected growth rate is
$r$ in place of $\mu + \tfrac12\sigma^2$: the probabilities are read off
the replication arithmetic, as the second bookmaker's odds are read off the
wagers, so the expectation inherits this theorem's independence from any
belief about $S_T$ and is bookkeeping for a hedge, not a forecast.
</KeyFact>

### The forward price is not a forecast

The correct reading of $F_0 = S_0 e^{rT}$ is that it is the cost of carrying
the share to $T$: the spot price plus the interest on the money tied up in
it. A common misreading takes the forward price to be the market's forecast
of $S_T$, so that a stock expected to rise strongly should have a forward
price above one expected to stay flat. That reading is wrong. A forecast
enters expectation pricing at exactly one step, $K = E[S_T]$, and the
construction refuses it entry because the construction never needs to know
$S_T$: the share is bought now, at a price everyone can see, and delivered
later, at a price everyone agreed. The replacement model is the ledger in
the figure sequence, in which $S_T$ appears twice and cancels.

The distinction has a price tag. In 1994 a Kidder Peabody trader's system
booked the excess of forward over spot on each of his government-bond
trades as profit; the excess was the financing cost of holding the bond,
and rolling the trades forward kept it from ever being paid. The reported
profit reached \$100 million and the eventual loss was in the region of
\$350 million. Recording $F_0 - S_0$ as gain is the same error as recording
$e^{rT} - 1$ on a bank loan as income.

Two further things the theorem does not say. The strong law was not wrong:
if $\mu + \tfrac12 \sigma^2 > r$ then a long forward at 105.13 has positive
expected profit, and so does a share bought outright; expectation pricing
fails here because the risk is hedgeable, not because expectations are
meaningless. And the course will use expectations again, from
<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the martingale lesson</Ref>
on, but under a measure constructed from replication arithmetic, with the
expectation serving as a bookkeeping device for a hedge and never as a
forecast.

## Arbitrage defined: zero cost, no loss, a chance of gain

The trades of the previous section share a shape, and the shape is the
course's central definition.

<Definition title="arbitrage">
A trading strategy with value process $X$ is an **arbitrage** if it
satisfies all three of: **clause 1**, $X_0 = 0$, it costs nothing to set
up; **clause 2**, $P(X_T < 0) = 0$, it loses in no state; **clause 3**,
$P(X_T > 0) > 0$, it gains with positive probability.
</Definition>

The definition mentions probabilities in clauses 2 and 3, but only through
which events are possible: it asks whether a loss can happen and whether a
gain can happen, never how likely either is. Two people who disagree about
every probability but agree about what is impossible agree about what is an
arbitrage. That is what lets the theorem above hold for every distribution of
$S_T$ at once.

  caption="Each near miss satisfies two clauses and fails the third; the mispriced-forward trade satisfies all three."

### The near misses

The canonical example is the trade against a forward quoted above
$S_0 e^{rT}$: cost zero, net $K - S_0 e^{rT}$ in every state, which is
positive with probability one. A structurally different example needs no
model at all: the same share trading at \$150 in New York and £100 in
London when the exchange rate is \$1.53 per pound sells for \$153 in
London, so buying in New York and selling in London at once collects \$3
a share with nothing risked. The riskless case is the degenerate one: clause
2 holds with $X_T = K - S_0 e^{rT}$ constant, so "positive probability" in
clause 3 is probability one, and the definition covers it.

Each near miss fails exactly one clause. A lottery ticket never loses after
purchase and can win, but it costs \$2 now: clause 1 fails, and no amount of
upside rescues it. A share bought entirely with borrowed money costs nothing
and can gain, but loses if the stock falls below $S_0 e^{rT}$: clause 2
fails, and the position is a bet. A share bought and sold in the same
instant costs nothing and never loses, but never gains: clause 3 fails, and
the position is nothing.

<Task>
Produce one strategy from your own experience, financial or not, that
satisfies exactly two of the three clauses, and name the clause it fails.
Then decide whether a favourable bet made a million times, as in the
insurer's case above, becomes an arbitrage.
</Task>

The million-fold bet fails the same clause as the single one. The insurer's
aggregate of independent favourable policies has a mean far above zero and
a standard deviation that grows only as the square root of the count, so the
probability of a net loss falls toward zero as the count grows; it never
reaches zero, and clause 2 requires zero. Repetition makes a favourable bet
a better bet, and a bet is what it remains.

### Why the bound holds whether or not anyone trades it

The theorem was stated as "the unique price that admits no arbitrage", and
the phrasing matters. It is not a claim that markets are efficient, that
traders are rational, or that any particular person will take the trade. It
is the statement that at any other quote a specific, written-out strategy
collects money in every state. A dealer who quotes 112.75 is offering 7.62
per share to anyone who reads this lesson, and can be asked for it in
unlimited size; the quote cannot survive because the offer cannot be
withdrawn without changing the quote. Where an arbitrage price exists, any
other price is too dangerous to quote.

The bound is therefore a theorem about payoffs, and everything that follows
in the course is proved at the same standard: a price is established by
exhibiting the construction that punishes every alternative. Where the
construction requires assumptions the theorem lists them, and
<Ref lesson="option-chain-bounds">the last lesson of this module</Ref>
measures how far real quotes stray from the frictionless bounds and why.

<KeyFact>
Trading costs weaken the argument without breaking it: with the stock's bid
below its offer, the two trades punish only forward quotes above
$S_0^{\mathrm{offer}} e^{rT}$ or below $S_0^{\mathrm{bid}} e^{rT}$, so the
price is enforced to within the bid–offer band and no closer, and
<Ref lesson="option-chain-bounds">the last lesson of this module</Ref>
measures that band on quoted option chains.
</KeyFact>

## What static replication reaches: lines, not corners

The construction succeeded because the forward's payoff has a property the
construction can match, and naming that property shows where the method
stops. A static portfolio holds $a$ shares and $b$ dollars of riskless bond,
bought now and held. At $T$ it is worth $a S_T + b\,e^{rT}$: a straight line
in $S_T$, with slope $a$ and intercept $b e^{rT}$. The long forward's payoff
$S_T - K$ is a line with slope $1$ and intercept $-K$, so $a = 1$ and
$b = -K e^{-rT} = -S_0$ match it exactly, which is the construction.

Two more payoffs run through the entire course. A *European call* with
strike $K$ gives its holder the right, not the obligation, to buy the share
for $K$ at $T$; the holder exercises when $S_T > K$ and abandons the option
otherwise, so the payoff is $\max(S_T - K, 0)$. A *European put* is the right
to sell for $K$, with payoff $\max(K - S_T, 0)$. Unlike a forward, each costs
something to buy, since neither payoff is ever negative.

point at which any single line must fail to match them.
</Task>

The call's payoff has a corner at $K$: slope $0$ to the left, slope $1$ to
the right. No line has a corner, so no static portfolio of stock and bond
pays $\max(S_T - K, 0)$ in every state once $S_T$ can take more than two
values on both sides of $K$. The seller's dilemma is the corner in words: a
share bought and held against the option is exactly the right cover if the
option is exercised and a loss-making position if it is abandoned. Before
1973, the year the Black–Scholes and Merton papers appeared with a formula
for the call's price and the Chicago Board Options Exchange opened as the
first exchange for listed options, the usual conclusion was that options,
unlike forwards, had to be priced by expectation after all.
The conclusion is wrong, and the rest of this module and the next show why:
the corner is out of reach of a *static* position, and a position adjusted
as the stock moves reaches it.

<KeyFact>
Static replication reaches exactly the payoffs that are lines in $S_T$; the
call's corner lies outside that set, and closing the gap between a line and
a corner is the work of the next three lessons.
</KeyFact>

What is established: expectation pricing is a long-run average that can
suggest a price and enforce nothing; a forward's payoff is manufactured
from a loan and a share, so its price is $S_0 e^{rT}$ with no belief about
the stock in it; and an arbitrage is a zero-cost strategy that cannot lose
and can win, the object whose absence fixes every price in this course.
What is not established is any price for a payoff with a corner.
<Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">The next lesson</Ref> shows how much
no-arbitrage alone says about a call and a put without any model of the
stock: an interval for each, and an exact identity linking the two.

## Exercises

<Exercise>
A stock trades at $S_0 = 40$ and pays no dividends; the one-year continuously
compounded rate is $5\%$. Compute the one-year forward price. A dealer quotes
$43.50$. Write the arbitrage as a table: positions now, and cash flows at
$T$ for a high and a low value of $S_T$.

<Solution>
$F_0 = 40\,e^{0.05} = 40 \times 1.05127 = 42.05$. The quote is high, so sell
the forward and manufacture the delivery.

| | now | $S_T = 60$ | $S_T = 20$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| borrow 40 at 5% | $+40$ | $-42.05$ | $-42.05$ |
| buy one share | $-40$ | $+60$ | $+20$ |
| short forward at 43.50 | $0$ | $43.50 - 60$ | $43.50 - 20$ |
| **net** | $0$ | $+1.45$ | $+1.45$ |

The stock column cancels against the forward column in both states and
$43.50 - 42.05 = 1.45$ remains. Any $S_T$ gives the same net.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute the claim: "a one-year forward on a stock whose log-return has drift
$\mu = 20\%$ should cost more than one on an otherwise identical stock with
$\mu = 0$, because the first stock is expected to end higher." Name the
exact step at which the belief enters expectation pricing and say why the
replication argument does not contain that step.

<Solution>
Both forwards cost $S_0 e^{rT}$. The belief enters expectation pricing at the
step $K = E[S_T]$, where the expected terminal price is computed under a
model with $\mu$ in it: $E[S_T] = S_0 e^{(\mu + \sigma^2/2)T}$, so $\mu =
20\%$ gives a larger number than $\mu = 0$. Replication never computes
$E[S_T]$ or any other functional of the distribution of $S_T$. The seller
borrows $S_0$, buys the share, and delivers it; the net at $T$ is $K - S_0
e^{rT}$ whatever $S_T$ turns out to be, so the price at which that net is
zero is the same for both stocks.

If a dealer priced the two forwards differently, the trades of the theorem
would collect the difference: sell the dearer forward, buy the cheaper, and
run one construction against the other. The claim's error is the
misconception refuted in the lesson: the forward price is the cost of
carrying the share, not a forecast of where it will be.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Run the replication argument in two altered settings. (a) $r = 0$. (b) The
stock pays a known dividend $D$ at a known time $t_D < T$, which the holder
of the share receives and the holder of the forward does not. In each case
state the enforced forward price and say which leg of the construction
changed.

<Solution>
**(a)** With $r = 0$ the loan of $S_0$ repays $S_0$ and $F_0 = S_0$. No leg
changed shape; the interest on the loan leg is zero, so carrying the share
costs nothing and the forward price equals the spot price.

**(b)** The seller who borrows $S_0$ and buys the share receives $D$ at
$t_D$. That cash can repay part of the loan: borrow $S_0$ now as two loans,
$D e^{-r t_D}$ due at $t_D$, repaid by the dividend, and the remainder
$S_0 - D e^{-r t_D}$ due at $T$. At $T$ the seller owes $(S_0 - D e^{-r
t_D})\,e^{rT}$ and holds the share to deliver. The net is $K - (S_0 - D
e^{-r t_D})\,e^{rT}$ in every state, so the enforced price is
$$
F_0 = \bigl(S_0 - D e^{-r t_D}\bigr)\,e^{rT} = \bigl(S_0 - \mathrm{PV}(D)\bigr)\,e^{rT}.
$$
The leg that changed is the loan: the dividend funds part of it, so less
principal is carried to $T$. The reverse trade changes symmetrically: the
short-seller must pay $D$ to the lender of the share at $t_D$, and sets
aside $\mathrm{PV}(D)$ of the short-sale proceeds to do so. On the lesson's
stock, $S_0 = 100$, $r = 5\%$, $T = 1$, with $D = 2$ paid at $t_D = 0.5$:
$\mathrm{PV}(D) = 2 e^{-0.05 \times 0.5} = 1.95$ and
$F_0 = (100 - 1.95)\,e^{0.05} = 103.08$, against $105.13$ without the
dividend. The gap, $2.05 = D e^{r(T - t_D)}$, is the dividend carried
forward from $t_D$ to $T$: the seller collects it and the buyer of the
forward does not, so the forward is cheaper by exactly that amount.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Two horses, wagers $w_1$ on the first and $w_2$ on the second. The
bookmaker quotes odds $n_1$–1 against the first (a \$1 stake wins $n_1$)
and $n_2$–1 against the second. (a) Derive the odds that make the book
riskless with zero profit, and show they depend only on $w_1 / w_2$.
(b) Find the odds that yield the bookmaker the same profit $\pi$ whichever
horse wins, and compute them for the lesson's race ($w_1 = 5{,}000$,
$w_2 = 10{,}000$, $\pi = 1{,}000$). (c) Show that the probabilities implied
by the odds in (b), $1/(1 + n_i)$, sum to more than one, and interpret the
excess.

<Solution>
**(a)** If the first horse wins the bookmaker pays $n_1 w_1$ and keeps
$w_2$: net $w_2 - n_1 w_1$. If the second wins: net $w_1 - n_2 w_2$. Riskless
with zero profit requires both nets to vanish: $n_1 = w_2 / w_1$ and
$n_2 = w_1 / w_2$. Both are functions of the ratio $w_1 / w_2$ alone;
scaling both wagers by the same factor leaves the odds unchanged, and the
true win probabilities appear in neither.

**(b)** Set both nets equal to $\pi$: $w_2 - n_1 w_1 = \pi$ gives $n_1 =
(w_2 - \pi)/w_1$, and $w_1 - n_2 w_2 = \pi$ gives $n_2 = (w_1 - \pi)/w_2$.
For the lesson's race: $n_1 = (10{,}000 - 1{,}000)/5{,}000 = 1.8$, quoted as
9–5 against the first horse, and $n_2 = (5{,}000 - 1{,}000)/10{,}000 =
0.4$, quoted as 2–5 against, or 5–2 on, the second. Check: first wins, pay
$1.8 \times 5{,}000 = 9{,}000$, keep $10{,}000$, net $1{,}000$; second wins, pay
$0.4 \times 10{,}000 = 4{,}000$, keep $5{,}000$, net $1{,}000$.

**(c)** Implied probabilities: $1/(1 + 1.8) = 0.357$ and $1/(1 + 0.4) =
0.714$, summing to $1.071$. In general $1/(1 + n_1) = w_1/(w_1 + w_2 - \pi)$
and $1/(1 + n_2) = w_2/(w_1 + w_2 - \pi)$, so the sum is
$(w_1 + w_2)/(w_1 + w_2 - \pi) = 1 + \pi/(w_1 + w_2 - \pi)$, above one
whenever $\pi > 0$. The excess, $\pi/(w_1 + w_2 - \pi) = 1{,}000/14{,}000 =
7.1\%$, is the profit measured against the $14{,}000$ the bookmaker returns
to the winning bettors as stake plus winnings, the same sum whichever horse
wins. The same $1{,}000$ measured against the $15{,}000$ wagered is
$\pi/(w_1 + w_2) = 6.7\%$; the two figures are one profit, collected in
both outcomes, over two different denominators. The implied "probabilities"
are prices, not beliefs: they sum to more than one for the same reason a
dealer's bid is below his offer.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A bank's spot and forward quotes for the US dollar price of one pound on
May 6, 2013 (Hull, Table 1.1; bid and offer, dollars per pound):

| | bid | offer |
|---|---|---|
| spot | 1.5541 | 1.5545 |
| 1-month forward | 1.5538 | 1.5543 |
| 3-month forward | 1.5533 | 1.5538 |
| 6-month forward | 1.5526 | 1.5532 |

The forward prices are *below* spot, which the theorem of this lesson
forbids for $r > 0$. (a) Diagnose which hypothesis of the theorem fails for
a pound held in a drawer, and repair the construction. (b) From the repaired
formula, estimate the implied difference between the dollar and sterling
riskless rates from each tenor's mid-quotes, and state what would count as
the estimates agreeing with each other and with the world.

<Solution>
**(a)** The no-income hypothesis fails: a pound held for six months earns
sterling interest at some rate $r_{\mathrm{GBP}}$, as a dividend-paying stock earns
dividends. The seller who borrows dollars to buy a pound and holds it
receives interest, so as in the dividend exercise the carried principal shrinks: to
hold one pound at $T$ he needs only $e^{-r_{\mathrm{GBP}} T}$ pounds now, costing
$S_0 e^{-r_{\mathrm{GBP}} T}$ dollars, financed at the dollar rate $r_{\mathrm{USD}}$. The enforced
forward is
$$
F_0 = S_0\,e^{(r_{\mathrm{USD}} - r_{\mathrm{GBP}})T},
$$
below spot exactly when the sterling rate exceeds the dollar rate.

**(b)** Solving, $r_{\mathrm{USD}} - r_{\mathrm{GBP}} = \tfrac1T \log(F_0 / S_0)$. Spot mid $1.5543$.
One month: mid $1.55405$, $\log(1.55405/1.5543) = -0.000161$, times 12:
$-0.19\%$ per year. Three months: mid $1.55355$, $\log = -0.000483$, times
4: $-0.19\%$. Six months: mid $1.5529$, $\log = -0.000901$, times 2:
$-0.18\%$. The three tenors imply the same differential to within a
hundredth of a percent, which is the first test: a single pair of rates
explains all three quotes. Crossing bid against offer instead of using
mids, forward bid against spot offer at one end and forward offer against
spot bid at the other, gives the band inside which the theorem's
frictionless trade is not collectible, and the band is different at each
tenor: one month $-0.54\%$ to $+0.15\%$, three months $-0.31\%$ to
$-0.08\%$, six months $-0.24\%$ to $-0.12\%$. The quote spread is about
the same in price terms at every tenor, some nine to ten pips once the two
sides are crossed, so its width in $\log(F_0/S_0)$ is about $0.0006$
throughout; the annualisation factor of $12$, $4$ and $2$ then turns one
price spread into rate bands of $0.7$, $0.2$ and $0.1$ points. At one month
the band straddles zero: once costs are charged, the shortest forward
carries no information about the sign of the rate differential at all,
while the six-month quote pins it to a tenth of a point.

Agreement with the world means the sign and size match published interbank
fixings for the date: sterling short rates above dollar rates, both below
one percent, by a few tenths of a point. The forward table is therefore not
a forecast that the pound will fall; it is the interest differential, read
off the quotes.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
A European call has strike $K = S_0 e^{rT}$ and pays $\max(S_T - K, 0)$.
(a) Suppose $S_T$ can take three values $s_1 < s_2 < s_3$ with $s_1 < K <
s_3$. Show that no static portfolio of $a$ shares and $b$ dollars of bond
pays the call's payoff in all three states. (b) Suppose instead $S_T$ takes
exactly two values $s_1 < K < s_2$. Solve for the static portfolio that
replicates the call, and state its cost now.

<Solution>
**(a)** The portfolio pays $a s_i + b e^{rT}$ in state $i$. Two of the three
values lie on the same side of $K$. If $s_1, s_2 < K$, matching the call's
zero payoff in both forces $a s_1 + b e^{rT} = a s_2 + b e^{rT} = 0$, so
$a = 0$ and $b = 0$, and then the portfolio pays $0 \ne s_3 - K$ in state 3.
If $s_2, s_3 > K$, matching $s_2 - K$ and $s_3 - K$ forces $a = 1$ and
$b e^{rT} = -K$, and then the portfolio pays $s_1 - K < 0 \ne 0$ in state 1.
Either way the three equations in two unknowns are inconsistent: a line
through two points of a corner misses the third.

**(b)** Two equations, two unknowns:
$a s_1 + b e^{rT} = 0$ and $a s_2 + b e^{rT} = s_2 - K$. Subtracting,
$a = \dfrac{s_2 - K}{s_2 - s_1}$, a number strictly between $0$ and $1$, and
$b e^{rT} = -a s_1$. The cost now is $a S_0 + b = a\,(S_0 - s_1 e^{-rT})$.
With $S_0 = 4$, $s_2 = 8$, $s_1 = 2$ and $e^{rT} = 1.25$ (so $K = 5$):
$a = 3/6 = 0.5$, $b = -0.5 \times 2 / 1.25 = -0.8$, and the call costs
$0.5 \times 4 - 0.8 = 1.20$.

The two-state escape is not a curiosity: it is the entire subject of
<Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the one-period binomial model</Ref>, where
$a$ is named the delta of the call and the model's whole content is that with
two states a line always fits.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

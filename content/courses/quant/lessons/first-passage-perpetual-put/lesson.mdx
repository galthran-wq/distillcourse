For a put with no expiry on the doubling-or-halving stock, the optimal exercise rule can be found by brute honesty: value the rule "exercise when the stock first falls to level $m$" for each $m$ via first-passage probabilities, and watch one boundary dominate.

This is the twelfth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the third move of its third module. <Ref
lesson="american-derivatives">The previous lesson</Ref> proved that the
American price is the smallest supermartingale dominating the discounted
payoff, that the backward pass computes it, and that the first time price
meets payoff is an optimal rule; its closing exercise ran the pass for the
\$4 put out to six periods and found the exercised set to be $\{S \le 2\}$
at every date, with the values still rising toward something the finite
tree could not name. What stands open is the shape of the exercise region
when the horizon is removed, and the value it produces. This lesson
answers both for one put, by a route that has nothing to do with backward
induction: the distribution of the first time a fair-coin walk reaches a
level, proved twice, once by stopping a martingale and once by counting
reflected paths; the value of every rule "exercise when the stock first
falls to level $L$", computed from that distribution; the boundary
$L = 2$ read off the comparison; and, only then, the certificate that no
rule of any other shape does better, which is the previous lesson's
characterization with one limit added. <Ref
lesson="early-exercise-in-practice">The next lesson</Ref> takes the
module's results to listed options, where the expiry is finite and
dividends restore early exercise to the call.

## The symmetric walk and its first passage: a stopping time whose distribution is the question

The previous two lessons fixed what a stopping time is and what it does to
a martingale, on a tree with a last date. The object of this lesson is a
stopping time with no last date, and the first thing to settle is what
changes.

### Retrieval: what the module has fixed so far

### The walk

Toss a fair coin without end, and write $X_j = +1$ for a head on toss $j$
and $X_j = -1$ for a tail. The *symmetric random walk* is
$$
M_0 = 0, \qquad M_n = X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n, \quad n = 1, 2, \ldots:
$$
one step up or one step down per toss, each with probability
$\tfrac12$. The coin is fair by assumption in this section and the next
two; the reason the assumption costs nothing for pricing is that on the
\$4 tree the risk-neutral coin is fair, $\tilde p = \tilde q = \tfrac12$,
and the stock is $S_n = 4 \cdot 2^{M_n}$, so every statement proved here
about $M$ under a fair coin is a statement about the stock under
$\tilde P$. The fourth section makes that switch explicitly.

The walk is a martingale, since $E_n[M_{n+1}] = M_n + \tfrac12 - \tfrac12
= M_n$, and it is Markov: its future from time $n$ depends on the past
only through $M_n$. On the five tosses $T, H, H, H, T$ it visits
$0, -1, 0, 1, 2, 1$, and the third toss is the first at which it stands at
level $1$.

<Figure
  src="./figures/walk-first-passage.svg"
  alt="Five steps of the symmetric walk on the tosses T H H H T, visiting 0, minus 1, 0, 1, 2, 1; level 1 is a dashed blue line and the visit at time 3 is ringed as the first passage time tau sub 1 equals 3"
  caption="Five tosses and the first passage to level 1: the ring marks the first visit, and the later return at time 5 is not one."

<Definition title="first-passage time (Shreve I, §5.2)">
For an integer $m$, the **first-passage time** of the walk to level $m$
is
$$
\tau_m = \min\{n \ge 0 : M_n = m\},
\qquad \min \emptyset = \infty,
$$
the first date at which the walk stands at $m$, or $\infty$ on a path that
never reaches $m$.
</Definition>

Four instances fix the boundary of the definition.

- **The canonical case.** $\tau_1$, the first time the walk reaches $1$.
  On $T, H, H, H, T$ it is $3$; on $H, \ldots$ it is $1$.
- **A case with a different surface.** The stopping-times lesson's
  first-passage rule for the stock, $\min\{n : S_n \le 2\}$ on the \$4
  tree, is $\tau_{-1}$ for the walk with $S_n = 4 \cdot 2^{M_n}$: the stock
  is at or below $2$ exactly when the walk is at or below $-1$, and since
  the walk moves one unit at a time, the first time it is at or below $-1$
  is the first time it equals $-1$.
- **The degenerate case.** $\tau_0 = 0$ on every path: the walk starts at
  level $0$ and the minimum over $n \ge 0$ is attained at once.
- **The near miss.** "The last time the walk stands at level $1$" is not
  a stopping time: whether the visit at time $3$ on $T, H, H, H, T$ is the
  last one depends on every later toss, so $\{\text{last visit} = 3\}$ is
  not decided by the first three tosses. It fails the one clause a
  stopping time has.

A prompt for an instance of your own: decide whether "the first time the
walk reaches $1$ or falls to $-3$, whichever comes first" is a stopping
time, and which clause decides. It is: it equals
$\min(\tau_1, \tau_{-3})$, and <Ref lesson="stopping-times">the
stopping-times lesson's closing exercise</Ref> proved the earlier of two
stopping times is one.

### What is new: no last date

$\tau_m$ is a stopping time in the sense of the definition proved for
$N$-toss trees, applied with the same clause at every $n$: $\{\tau_m = n\}$
is the event "$M_k \ne m$ for $k < n$ and $M_n = m$", decided by the first
$n$ tosses. What is new is that no $N$ bounds it. Optional sampling was
proved on a tree with a last date, and it holds here at every finite $n$
without change, because the proof for the horizon $n$ uses the first $n$
tosses only. What it says nothing about is $X_\tau$ itself, and the
doubling strategy of <Ref lesson="stopping-times">the stopping-times
starred section</Ref> shows that $E[X_\tau]$ can differ from $X_0$ when
$\tau$ is unbounded. Every passage from $n \wedge \tau$ to $\tau$ in this
lesson is therefore a limit, taken by hand.

<Remark title="the one fenced fact">
A walk that runs without end lives on the space of infinite toss
sequences, and this course's probability was built on $N$ tosses. The
fact assumed unproved is that a probability measure exists on the
infinite sequences under which every event decided by finitely many
tosses has the probability the finite tree assigns it, and under which
$P(\tau_m < \infty) = \lim_{n \to \infty} P(\tau_m \le n)$. Shreve II,
Chapter 1, constructs it. Nothing else in this lesson is fenced: every
limit taken below is the limit of an increasing sequence of finite sums
of nonnegative terms, and such a limit is a supremum.
</Remark>

The three questions about $\tau_1$ are whether it is finite with
probability one, what its mean is, and what its distribution is. The
three-toss tree already answers the smallest cases: of the eight paths,
five reach level $1$ by time $3$, with $\tau_1 = 1$ on the four paths
beginning $H$ and $\tau_1 = 3$ on $T, H, H$. So
$P(\tau_1 = 1) = \tfrac12$, $P(\tau_1 = 3) = \tfrac18$, and
$P(\tau_1 \le 3) = \tfrac58$. The sequence $P(\tau_1 \le n)$ is
nondecreasing; the question is its limit, and how fast it gets there.

<KeyFact>
What separates $\tau_1$ from the doubling strategy is the size of the
stopped martingale on the paths still running. The doubling wealth on
$\{\tau > n\}$ is $-(2^n - 1)$ on a set of probability $2^{-n}$, a
piece worth $-1$ of expectation at every $n$, and it is that piece the
limit loses. Every martingale this lesson stops at $\tau_1$ lies between
$0$ and a bound tending to $0$ on the running paths, and the arrived
piece is a nondecreasing sum of nonnegative terms; under those two
conditions $E[X_{\tau}] = X_0$ survives the passage to the limit, and
they are what each proof below checks.
</KeyFact>

## The martingale trick: the generating function of τ₁ and the pair of facts it carries

The distribution of $\tau_1$ is an infinite list of numbers
$P(\tau_1 = 1), P(\tau_1 = 3), P(\tau_1 = 5), \ldots$, and the object that
packages the list is the *generating function*
$$
E[\alpha^{\tau_1}] = \sum_{k} \alpha^k\,P(\tau_1 = k),
\qquad 0 < \alpha < 1, \qquad \alpha^\infty := 0,
$$
a power series in $\alpha$ whose coefficients are the list. The
convention $\alpha^\infty = 0$ makes the paths that never arrive
contribute nothing, so $E[\alpha^{\tau_1}]$ increases toward
$P(\tau_1 < \infty)$ as $\alpha \uparrow 1$. What must be computed is
this function in closed form; how it is computed is by stopping a
martingale of a particular shape at $\tau_1$.

### The exponential martingale

Take the discounted \$4 stock, $S_n/1.25^n = 4 \cdot 2^{M_n}(\tfrac45)^n$,
and divide by $4$: the process $2^{M_n}(\tfrac45)^n$ is a martingale under
the fair coin, since <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the
discounted stock is</Ref>. At one step from the start it takes the values
$2 \cdot \tfrac45 = 1.6$ and $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac45 = 0.4$, with mean
$1$. The factor $\tfrac45$ is not a coincidence of the interest rate: it
is $2/(2 + \tfrac12)$, the reciprocal of the mean of $2^{X_1}$, and the
same construction works for any base.

<Theorem title="the exponential martingale (Shreve I, Lemma 5.2.1)">
Fix $\sigma > 0$ and set $\alpha = 2/(e^\sigma + e^{-\sigma})$, so that
$0 < \alpha < 1$. Under the fair coin the process
$$
Z_n = e^{\sigma M_n}\,\alpha^n, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots
$$
is a martingale, with $Z_0 = 1$.

<Proof>
$Z_{n+1} = Z_n \cdot \alpha\,e^{\sigma X_{n+1}}$. The factor $Z_n$ depends
on the first $n$ tosses and comes out of $E_n$ by <Ref
lesson="coin-toss-probability">taking out what is known</Ref>; the factor
$e^{\sigma X_{n+1}}$ depends on toss $n + 1$ alone, so by <Ref
lesson="coin-toss-probability">independence</Ref> its conditional mean is
its mean, $\tfrac12 e^\sigma + \tfrac12 e^{-\sigma} = 1/\alpha$. Hence
$E_n[Z_{n+1}] = Z_n \cdot \alpha \cdot (1/\alpha) = Z_n$. That $\alpha < 1$
is the statement $\tfrac12(e^\sigma + e^{-\sigma}) > 1$ for
$\sigma \ne 0$, which holds because $e^\sigma + e^{-\sigma} - 2 =
(e^{\sigma/2} - e^{-\sigma/2})^2 > 0$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

With $\sigma = \ln 2$ this is the discounted \$4 stock over $4$. The
proof is the martingale lesson's proof for the discounted stock with
$e^\sigma$ in place of $u$, which is why the theorem is stated for every
$\sigma$: the base is free, and the trick below chooses it to match a
given $\alpha$.

### Stopping it at τ₁

<Theorem title="the generating function of τ₁ (Shreve I, Theorem 5.2.3, m = 1)">
For the symmetric random walk and every $\alpha \in (0, 1)$,
$$
E\bigl[\alpha^{\tau_1}\bigr] = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - \alpha^2}}{\alpha},
$$
with $\alpha^{\tau_1} = 0$ on $\{\tau_1 = \infty\}$.

<Proof>
Roadmap, four steps: stop the exponential martingale at $\tau_1$ and use
optional sampling at each finite $n$; split the stopped value into the
paths that have arrived and the paths that have not; let $n \to \infty$,
bounding each piece; solve the relation between $\sigma$ and $\alpha$ for
$e^{-\sigma}$.

**Step 1, optional sampling at horizon $n$.** Fix $\sigma > 0$ and the
matching $\alpha$. $Z_{n \wedge \tau_1}$ is a martingale by <Ref
lesson="stopping-times">optional sampling, part I</Ref> applied to the
first $n$ tosses, so $E[Z_{n \wedge \tau_1}] = Z_0 = 1$ for every $n$.

**Step 2, the split.** On $\{\tau_1 \le n\}$ the frozen value is
$Z_{\tau_1} = e^{\sigma \cdot 1}\alpha^{\tau_1}$, because the walk stands
at $1$ when it is stopped. On $\{\tau_1 > n\}$ the walk has never reached
$1$, so $M_n \le 0$ and $Z_n = e^{\sigma M_n}\alpha^n \le \alpha^n$.
Therefore
$$
1 = E\bigl[e^{\sigma}\alpha^{\tau_1}\,\mathbb 1_{\{\tau_1 \le n\}}\bigr]
+ E\bigl[Z_n\,\mathbb 1_{\{\tau_1 > n\}}\bigr],
\qquad
0 \le E\bigl[Z_n\,\mathbb 1_{\{\tau_1 > n\}}\bigr] \le \alpha^n .
$$

**Step 3, the limit.** The first expectation is the finite sum
$e^\sigma\sum_{k \le n}\alpha^k P(\tau_1 = k)$, nondecreasing in $n$, and
its limit is $e^\sigma E[\alpha^{\tau_1}]$ by the definition of the
generating function, the paths with $\tau_1 = \infty$ contributing $0$ at
every $n$ and in the limit. The second expectation is squeezed between $0$
and $\alpha^n \to 0$. The left side is $1$ at every $n$. Hence
$$
e^{\sigma}\,E\bigl[\alpha^{\tau_1}\bigr] = 1,
\qquad\text{that is}\qquad
E\bigl[\alpha^{\tau_1}\bigr] = e^{-\sigma}.
$$
This is the sentence where the argument is general: it used only that
$Z$ is a martingale bounded by $e^\sigma$ on the stopped paths and by
$\alpha^n$ on the unstopped ones.

**Step 4, eliminate $\sigma$.** The relation
$\alpha(e^\sigma + e^{-\sigma}) = 2$, multiplied by $e^{-\sigma}$, is the
quadratic $\alpha(e^{-\sigma})^2 - 2e^{-\sigma} + \alpha = 0$ in the
unknown $e^{-\sigma}$, with roots $(1 \pm \sqrt{1 - \alpha^2})/\alpha$.
Their product is $1$, so one root is below $1$ and one above; $\sigma > 0$
requires $e^{-\sigma} < 1$, which selects the minus sign. Substituting
into Step 3 gives the formula.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

  caption="The trick dissected: the stopped martingale's mean is pinned at 1 for every n, the two pieces of the stopped value have limits by hand, and the base of the exponential is chosen to match the discount factor."

### The pair: certain arrival, infinite wait

<Theorem title="finite almost surely, infinite in mean (Shreve I, Theorem 5.2.2 and Corollary 5.2.4)">
For the symmetric random walk, $P(\tau_1 < \infty) = 1$ and
$E[\tau_1] = \infty$. By symmetry and by the fresh-start argument of the
closing exercises, the same holds for $\tau_m$ at every integer $m \ne 0$.

<Proof>
**Arrival.** $E[\alpha^{\tau_1}] = \sum_k \alpha^k P(\tau_1 = k)$ is a
series of nonnegative terms, each nondecreasing in $\alpha$, so as
$\alpha \uparrow 1$ it increases to $\sum_k P(\tau_1 = k) =
P(\tau_1 < \infty)$. The closed form tends to $(1 - 0)/1 = 1$. Hence
$P(\tau_1 < \infty) = 1$.

**The mean.** Differentiating the series term by term inside its radius of
convergence, $\frac{d}{d\alpha}E[\alpha^{\tau_1}] = \sum_k k\,\alpha^{k-1}
P(\tau_1 = k)$, again a series of nonnegative terms nondecreasing in
$\alpha$, whose limit as $\alpha \uparrow 1$ is $\sum_k k\,P(\tau_1 = k)
= E[\tau_1]$. Differentiating the closed form,
$$
\frac{d}{d\alpha}\,\frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - \alpha^2}}{\alpha}
= \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - \alpha^2}}{\alpha^2\sqrt{1 - \alpha^2}},
$$
whose numerator tends to $1$ and whose denominator tends to $0$ as
$\alpha \uparrow 1$. Hence $E[\tau_1] = \infty$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The two statements are about the same distribution and pull in opposite
directions. The walk reaches $1$ on every path but a set of probability
zero, and a set of probability zero is not a small set: every path on
which tails always outnumber heads, such as $T, T, H, T, T, H, \ldots$
forever, stays strictly negative, and there are uncountably many of them.
Yet the wait has no finite average, because the share of paths still
below $1$ after $n$ steps decays like $1/\sqrt{n}$, too slowly for the
tail sum $\sum_n P(\tau_1 > n)$ to converge.

  caption="The share of paths still below level 1 after n steps: it goes to zero, but only as fast as the square root of n, and the sum of these tails is the infinite mean."

<KeyFact>
Certain arrival does not price at par. A dollar paid at $\tau_1$ and
discounted at $25\%$ is worth $E[(\tfrac45)^{\tau_1}] = \tfrac12$ today,
with $(\tfrac45)^\infty = 0$ on the null set of paths that never arrive;
the fourth section values every rule "exercise at the $m$-th level below
the start" as its intrinsic value times the $m$-th power of this number.
</KeyFact>

That last number is the refutation of a claim that the first statement
invites. The claim: "the walk reaches $+1$ with certainty, so a trader who
buys the \$4 stock and sells the first time it reaches \$8 makes \$4 with
certainty, an arbitrage." The rejection: the trade is not an arbitrage,
and the reason is the second statement. The \$4 is collected at a random
date with infinite mean, and financed at $25\%$ the debt at that date is
$4 \cdot 1.25^{\tau_1}$, which exceeds \$8 on every path with
$\tau_1 \ge 5$, a set of probability $\tfrac38$. The replacement model is
the theorem just proved read at $\sigma = \ln 2$: the discounted stock is
a martingale, stopping it at $\tau_1$ keeps its mean at every horizon, and
in the limit the sale at $8$ is worth $8 \cdot \tfrac12 = 4$ today,
exactly the purchase price. Fair games stay fair at stopping times, and
the closing exercises compute the trade's loss distribution.

### The distribution itself

The generating function is a power series, and reading off its
coefficients gives every $P(\tau_1 = k)$.

<Theorem title="the distribution of τ₁ (Shreve I, (5.2.22))">
For the symmetric random walk and $j = 1, 2, \ldots$,
$$
P(\tau_1 = 2j - 1) = \frac{(2j - 2)!}{j!\,(j - 1)!}\Bigl(\frac12\Bigr)^{2j - 1},
$$
and $P(\tau_1 = k) = 0$ for even $k$.

<Proof>
Level $1$ is odd, so it is reached only at odd times, and
$E[\alpha^{\tau_1}] = \sum_{j \ge 1}\alpha^{2j - 1}P(\tau_1 = 2j - 1)$.
On the other side, the binomial series for $\sqrt{1 - x}$ with
$x = \alpha^2$ gives
$$
1 - \sqrt{1 - x}
= \sum_{j \ge 1}\frac{(2j - 2)!}{j!\,(j - 1)!}\Bigl(\frac12\Bigr)^{2j - 1} x^j,
$$
the coefficient being the $j$-th derivative of $1 - \sqrt{1 - x}$ at $0$
divided by $j!$, which is $\tfrac{1 \cdot 3 \cdots (2j - 3)}{2^j\,j!}$,
rewritten by multiplying numerator and denominator by
$2 \cdot 4 \cdots (2j - 2) = 2^{j - 1}(j - 1)!$. Dividing by $\alpha$
matches the powers $\alpha^{2j - 1}$ on both sides, and two power series
equal on $(0, 1)$ have equal coefficients.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Example title="the first three terms, and what the two factors count">
$j = 1$: $\frac{0!}{1!\,0!}(\tfrac12)^1 = \tfrac12$. The one path is $H$.

$j = 2$: $\frac{2!}{2!\,1!}(\tfrac12)^3 = \tfrac18$. The one path is
$T, H, H$: a head first would have ended the wait at time $1$.

$j = 3$: $\frac{4!}{3!\,2!}(\tfrac12)^5 = 2 \cdot \tfrac1{32} =
\tfrac1{16}$. The two paths are $T, H, T, H, H$ and $T, T, H, H, H$.

The factor $(2j - 2)!/(j!\,(j - 1)!)$ is the number of $(2j - 1)$-toss
paths that first reach $1$ on the last toss; $(\tfrac12)^{2j - 1}$ is the
probability of each. The counts $1, 1, 2, 5, 14, \ldots$ are the
[Catalan numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalan_number)
$C_{j - 1}$, and the same count with unequal coin weights gives the
asymmetric walk's distribution below.
</Example>

with $M_7 = -1$ or lower, and on seven tosses "$\tau_1 > 7$" means
$M_k \le 0$ throughout.

$j = 4$: $\frac{6!}{4!\,3!} = \frac{720}{144} = 5$, so
$P(\tau_1 = 7) = 5/128 \approx 0.039$. The paths: the first six tosses
must stay at or below $0$ and end at $0$, with the seventh a head:
$THTHTH\,H$, $THTTHH\,H$, $TTHHTH\,H$, $TTHTHH\,H$, $TTTHHH\,H$.

$j = 5$: $\frac{8!}{5!\,4!} = \frac{40320}{2880} = 14$, so
$P(\tau_1 = 9) = 14/512 \approx 0.027$.

**The sum.** $\tfrac12 + \tfrac18 + \tfrac1{16} + \tfrac5{128} +
\tfrac{14}{512} = \tfrac{256 + 64 + 32 + 20 + 14}{512} = \tfrac{386}{512}
\approx 0.754$. Through $\tau_1 = 7$ alone the sum is

**The check.** $P(M_7 = -1)$ needs three heads among seven tosses,

Then $1 - \tfrac{35}{128} = \tfrac{93}{128}$, equal to the partial sum,

or below, and on an odd number of tosses those are the paths with at most

</Solution>
</Exercise>

On the lattice the count is a constraint on the path's first $2j - 2$

draw.

## The reflection principle: the same distribution by counting paths

The distribution just proved has a factor that counts paths, and the
proof reached it through a power series rather than by counting. From
that one would expect the count to need the generating function; it does
not. A direct count exists, it produces the cumulative distribution
$P(\tau_1 \le n)$ in one line, and unlike the martingale argument it
survives unchanged into Brownian motion, where it gives the joint law of
a path's maximum and its endpoint.

### Eight paths, four reflections

Of the eight three-toss paths, five reach level $1$ by time $3$:
$HHH$, $HHT$, $HTH$, $HTT$ and $THH$. Sort the five by where they end.
One ends above $1$: $HHH$, at $3$. Three end at $1$: $HHT$, $HTH$, $THH$.
One ends below $1$ after having reached it: $HTT$, at $-1$.

Now take a path that reaches $1$ at some time $\tau_1 \le 3$ and reflect
its tail: after $\tau_1$, replace every head by a tail and every tail by
a head. The reflected path is another three-toss path, it reaches $1$ at
the same $\tau_1$, and it ends on the other side of $1$ by the same
distance, since each later step is negated. Reflecting twice gives the
original path back, so reflection pairs the paths that reach $1$ and end
below it with the paths that reach $1$ and end above it. On three tosses
the pairing is $HTT \leftrightarrow HHH$; the two paths ending at $1$ with
$\tau_1 = 1$, $HHT$ and $HTH$, are each other's reflections; and $THH$,
with $\tau_1 = 3$, has no tail to reflect.

### The theorem

<Theorem title="reflection principle for the symmetric walk (Shreve I, §5.3)">
For integers $m \ge 1$ and $n \ge m$,
$$
P(\tau_m \le n) = P(M_n \ge m) + P(M_n > m) = 2P(M_n \ge m) - P(M_n = m).
$$

<Proof>
Every $n$-toss path has probability $2^{-n}$, so probabilities of events
decided by the first $n$ tosses are path counts over $2^n$, and the claim
is a count.

**Sort.** The paths with $\tau_m \le n$ split by the endpoint: those with
$M_n \ge m$, and those with $M_n < m$. Every path with $M_n \ge m$ has
$\tau_m \le n$, because the walk moves one unit per step and must pass
through $m$ on its way to or beyond it; so the first class is exactly
$\{M_n \ge m\}$.

**Reflect.** For a path in the second class, reflect the tail after
$\tau_m$. The result has the same first $\tau_m$ tosses, so the same
$\tau_m$, and its endpoint is $m + (m - M_n) > m$. The map is its own
inverse and sends the second class into $\{M_n > m\}$; conversely every
path with $M_n > m$ has $\tau_m \le n$ and reflects to a path with
$M_n < m$. So the second class and $\{M_n > m\}$ have the same number of
paths.

**Add.** $P(\tau_m \le n) = P(M_n \ge m) + P(M_n > m)$. The second form
follows from $P(M_n > m) = P(M_n \ge m) - P(M_n = m)$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

</Task>

symmetry enters only when counts are converted to probabilities, since
for an unfair coin a path and its reflection have different
probabilities. (3) "Sort", where reaching or passing $m$ forces a visit
to $m$; a walk with jumps of size two could clear the level without
touching it, and the first class would no longer be $\{M_n \ge m\}$.
</Aside>

### The distribution again

For $m = 1$ and odd $n = 2j - 1$, the walk's parity gives
$P(M_n > 1) = P(M_n \ge 3) = P(M_n \le -3)$ by symmetry, so the theorem
reads $P(\tau_1 \le 2j - 1) = 1 - P(M_{2j - 1} = -1)$, exactly the check
of the in-body exercise. The point mass at $2j - 1$ is a difference of two
cumulative values:
$$
P(\tau_1 = 2j - 1) = P(M_{2j - 3} = -1) - P(M_{2j - 1} = -1),
$$
which at $j = 2$ is $\tfrac12 - \tfrac38 = \tfrac18$ and at $j = 3$ is
$\tfrac38 - \tfrac{10}{32} = \tfrac1{16}$, matching the generating
function's coefficients. The general identity is a binomial-coefficient
computation, folded below.

<Aside>
The algebra. $M_{2k - 1} = -1$ needs $k - 1$ heads among $2k - 1$
tosses, so $P(M_{2k - 1} = -1) = \binom{2k - 1}{k}2^{-(2k - 1)}$. Then
$$
P(\tau_1 = 2j - 1)
= \frac{(2j - 3)!}{(j - 1)!\,(j - 2)!}\Bigl(\tfrac12\Bigr)^{2j - 3}
- \frac{(2j - 1)!}{j!\,(j - 1)!}\Bigl(\tfrac12\Bigr)^{2j - 1}
= \Bigl(\tfrac12\Bigr)^{2j - 1}\frac{(2j - 3)!}{j!\,(j - 1)!}\bigl[4j(j - 1) - (2j - 1)(2j - 2)\bigr],
$$
and the bracket is $(2j - 2)(2j - 2j + 1) = 2j - 2$, so the product is
$(\tfrac12)^{2j - 1}(2j - 2)!/(j!\,(j - 1)!)$, the formula of the
previous section. Two proofs, one theorem.
</Aside>

### Why the course carries both proofs

The two arguments are not two routes to one fact but two tools with
different reach. The martingale argument needs an exponential martingale
and optional sampling, and both exist for Brownian motion, where
$e^{\sigma W(t) - \sigma^2 t/2}$ plays the role of $Z$ and the same
stopping argument gives the Laplace transform of the first passage time
(Shreve II, Theorems 3.6.1 and 3.6.2). The reflection argument needs
symmetry of the increments after the passage and nothing else; for
Brownian motion it gives $P(\tau_m \le t) = 2P(W(t) \ge m)$ and, with one
more step, the joint density of the maximum and the endpoint (Shreve II,
§3.7), which is what prices barrier options. The closing exercise that
reaches forward watches the walk's count turn into the Brownian one as
$n$ grows.

For an unfair coin the count survives and the symmetry does not. The
number of $(2j - 1)$-toss paths that first reach $1$ on the last toss is
still $(2j - 2)!/(j!\,(j - 1)!)$; each such path has $j$ heads and $j - 1$
tails, so with head probability $p$ and tail probability $q = 1 - p$,
$$
P(\tau_1 = 2j - 1) = \frac{(2j - 2)!}{j!\,(j - 1)!}\,p^j q^{j - 1}
$$
(Shreve I, Theorem 5.2.5). What changes is the sum of these terms: the
starred section states the two cases, and the closing exercises prove
that for $p < \tfrac12$ the walk reaches $1$ with probability $p/q$ only.

<KeyFact>
The reflection identity $P(\tau_m \le n) = 2P(M_n \ge m) - P(M_n = m)$ is
the statement that survives the scaling limit: for Brownian motion the
last term vanishes and the first passage to $m$ by time $t$ has
probability $2P(W(t) \ge m)$, twice a Gaussian tail. The generating
function, by contrast, packages the distribution for computing with it,
which is what the perpetual put needs next.
</KeyFact>

## The perpetual put by search: value every "exercise when the stock first falls to L" rule

The first three sections were about a walk under a fair coin. The
pricing problem is on the \$4 tree with $u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$,
$r = \tfrac14$, whose risk-neutral probabilities are
$\tilde p = \tilde q = \tfrac12$ and whose stock is
$$
S_n = 4 \cdot 2^{M_n},
$$
with $M_n$ the symmetric random walk under $\tilde P$. From here on every
expectation is $\tilde E$, every probability $\tilde P$, and every result
of the first three sections is used with that reading: the coin whose
fairness they assumed is the risk-neutral coin, whatever the actual coin
may be.

### A put with no expiry is priced by its definition, not by a pass

<Definition title="the perpetual American put">
The **perpetual American put** with strike $K$ pays $(K - S_n)^+$ when
exercised at date $n$, at any date the holder chooses, with no expiry.
Its price at $S_0 = s$ is
$$
v(s) = \sup_{\tau}\ \tilde E\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{1}{1 + r}\Bigr)^{\tau}(K - S_\tau)^+\Bigr],
$$
the supremum over all stopping times of the infinite-toss filtration, with
the term equal to $0$ on $\{\tau = \infty\}$.
</Definition>

Two things are folded in. First, the definition is the buyer's half of
the previous lesson's optimal-exercise theorem, taken as the definition
because there is no last date from which a backward pass could start; the
fifth section shows that the seller's half, the smallest dominating
supermartingale, produces the same number. Second, the price depends on
$s$ alone and not on the date, since the future of the stock from a node
at price $s$ is the same at every date; a rule that is optimal from $s$ at
time $0$ is optimal from $s$ at time $n$.

### The candidate rules and their values

From $S_0 = 4$ the natural rules are first-passage rules: exercise the
first time the stock falls to $4 \cdot 2^{-m}$, that is, at $\tau_{-m}$,
the walk's first passage to level $-m$. The rule is a stopping time; it
fires at a known price, so the payoff at exercise is the known number
$4 - 4 \cdot 2^{-m}$; and the only randomness in the discounted payoff is
the date, which the generating function handles.

<Example title="the rules exercise at 2 and exercise at 1">
**Exercise at $2$** ($m = 1$). The payoff at $\tau_{-1}$ is $4 - 2 = 2$
on every path where the rule fires, and it fires on every path but a null
set, since $\tau_{-1}$ has the distribution of $\tau_1$ and
$P(\tau_1 < \infty) = 1$. The value is
$$
\tilde E\Bigl[\Bigl(\tfrac45\Bigr)^{\tau_{-1}}\cdot 2\Bigr]
= 2\,\tilde E\Bigl[\Bigl(\tfrac45\Bigr)^{\tau_1}\Bigr]
= 2 \cdot \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - \tfrac{16}{25}}}{\tfrac45}
= 2 \cdot \frac{\tfrac25}{\tfrac45} = 2 \cdot \tfrac12 = 1 .
$$

**Exercise at $1$** ($m = 2$). The payoff is $3$, and the first passage
to $-2$ is the first passage to $-1$ followed by a fresh first passage
one level further, so $\tilde E[(\tfrac45)^{\tau_{-2}}] =
\tilde E[(\tfrac45)^{\tau_{-1}}]^2 = \tfrac14$ (Shreve I, Theorem 5.2.3
for general $m$; the closing exercises prove the squaring). The value is
$3 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac34$.

The pattern: each further level costs a factor $\tfrac12$ of discounting
and gains less than a factor $2$ of intrinsic value. Exercising at once
($m = 0$) pays $4 - 4 = 0$; exercising at $\tfrac12$ ($m = 3$) pays
$3\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac7{16}$; at $\tfrac14$ ($m = 4$),
$3\tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac1{16} = \tfrac{15}{64}$.
</Example>

### The boundary does not depend on where the stock starts

The comparison from $S_0 = 4$ generalizes without new computation. From
any lattice price $s = 2^j$ with $s \ge L$, the rule "exercise the first
time the stock reaches $L$" requires the walk to fall $\log_2(s/L)$
levels, and each level contributes a factor $\tfrac12$ of discounting; the
discount factor of the whole descent is $L/s$. So
$$
\text{value of "exercise at } L\text{" from } s
= (4 - L)\cdot\frac{L}{s},
\qquad L \le s,\ L \le 4 .
$$
The dependence on $s$ is the common factor $1/s$, and the search over $L$
is the maximization of $(4 - L)L$ over lattice prices: $0$ at $L = 4$,
$4$ at $L = 2$, $3$ at $L = 1$, $1\tfrac34$ at $L = \tfrac12$. The
winner is $L = 2$ from every starting price at or above $2$, with value
$4/s$. From a starting price at or below $2$ the rule fires at once and
the value is the intrinsic $4 - s$.

<Remark title="the conjectured value function">
$$
v(2^j) = \begin{cases} 4 - 2^j, & j \le 1, \\[2pt] 4/2^j, & j \ge 1, \end{cases}
$$
the two formulas agreeing at $j = 1$, where $4 - 2 = 4/2 = 2$. The stock
price $2$ is the *exercise boundary*: exercise at or below it, continue
above it. Compared with the finite-horizon values of <Ref
lesson="american-derivatives">the previous lesson's six-period tree</Ref>,
read at the nodes the tree reaches, $0.969$ against $1$ at $s = 4$ with
six periods remaining, $0.422$ against $0.5$ at $s = 8$ with five
remaining and $0.128$ against $0.25$ at $s = 16$ with four remaining,
every finite value sits below its limit, because a finite horizon removes
stopping times and adds none.
</Remark>

## Certification: the conjectured value is the smallest supermartingale dominating intrinsic value

The search compared level rules only, and from that one would expect a
second search over rules of other shapes to be needed. It is not. The
previous lesson's characterization says that a process which dominates
the payoff, is a supermartingale after discounting, and is attained by
some stopping time, is the price; no rule of any shape beats it. What the
perpetual case adds is one limit, in the step where a stopping time is
unbounded.

### The theorem

<Theorem title="the perpetual \$4 put on the doubling-or-halving stock (Shreve I, §5.4)">
On the tree with $u = 2$, $d = \tfrac12$, $r = \tfrac14$, let
$v(s) = 4 - s$ for lattice prices $s \le 2$ and $v(s) = 4/s$ for
$s \ge 2$. Then (i) $v(S_n) \ge (4 - S_n)^+$ at every node; (ii)
$(\tfrac45)^n v(S_n)$ is a $\tilde P$-supermartingale, a martingale on
$\{S_n \ge 4\}$ and strict on $\{S_n \le 2\}$; (iii) every stopping time
$\tau$ satisfies $\tilde E[(\tfrac45)^\tau(4 - S_\tau)^+] \le v(S_0)$, and
$\tau^* = \min\{n : S_n \le 2\}$ attains equality; and (iv) any process
$Y$ with properties (i) and (ii) satisfies $Y_n \ge v(S_n)$. Hence
$v$ is the perpetual put's price, $\tau^*$ is an optimal exercise rule,
and $2$ is the exercise boundary.

<Proof>
Roadmap: (i) and (ii) are checks at three kinds of node; (iii) is optional
sampling at a finite horizon followed by a monotone limit, and the
attainment is the search; (iv) is the same limit applied to $Y$.

**(i).** For $s \le 2$, $v(s) = 4 - s = (4 - s)^+$. For $s \ge 4$,
$v(s) = 4/s > 0 = (4 - s)^+$.

**(ii).** The one-step continuation value at price $s$ is
$\tfrac45\bigl[\tfrac12 v(2s) + \tfrac12 v(s/2)\bigr]$, and the
supermartingale clause is that it is at most $v(s)$. At $s \ge 4$, both
successors are at or above $2$, so the continuation is
$\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac12\bigl[\tfrac4{2s} + \tfrac{8}{s}\bigr]
= \tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac5s = \tfrac4s = v(s)$: equality, a martingale. At
$s = 2$: $\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac12[v(4) + v(1)] = \tfrac25(1 + 3) = 1.6
< 2 = v(2)$. At $s \le 1$: both successors are at or below $2$, so the
continuation is $\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac12[(4 - 2s) + (4 - \tfrac s2)] =
3.2 - s < 4 - s = v(s)$, the shortfall being $0.8$, one period's interest
on the strike.

**(iii), the bound.** Fix a stopping time $\tau$ and a horizon $k$. By
(ii) and <Ref lesson="stopping-times">optional sampling, part II</Ref> on
the first $k$ tosses,
$\tilde E[(\tfrac45)^{k \wedge \tau} v(S_{k \wedge \tau})] \le v(S_0)$.
On $\{\tau \le k\}$ the stopped value is $(\tfrac45)^\tau v(S_\tau) \ge
(\tfrac45)^\tau (4 - S_\tau)^+$ by (i); on $\{\tau > k\}$ it is
nonnegative. So
$$
\tilde E\bigl[(\tfrac45)^\tau (4 - S_\tau)^+\,\mathbb 1_{\{\tau \le k\}}\bigr] \le v(S_0)
\qquad\text{for every } k .
$$
The left side is a sum of nonnegative terms over the dates $n \le k$,
nondecreasing in $k$, and its limit is
$\tilde E[(\tfrac45)^\tau(4 - S_\tau)^+]$ with the $\{\tau = \infty\}$
term equal to $0$. This is the sentence where the argument becomes
general: the only facts used are (i), (ii) and that a nondecreasing
sequence bounded by $v(S_0)$ has a limit bounded by $v(S_0)$.

**(iii), the attainment.** From $S_0 = s \le 2$, $\tau^* = 0$ collects
$4 - s = v(s)$. From $s = 2^j$ with $j \ge 2$, $\tau^*$ is the walk's
first passage to $-(j - 1)$, and the search computed its value as
$(4 - 2)\cdot 2/s = 4/s = v(s)$.

**(iv).** Let $Y$ have (i) and (ii). Run the bound of (iii) for $Y$ in
place of $v$ with $\tau = \tau^*$ started at date $n$: for every $k > n$,
$(\tfrac45)^n Y_n \ge \tilde E_n[(\tfrac45)^{\tau^*}(4 - S_{\tau^*})^+
\mathbb 1_{\{\tau^* \le k\}}]$, and letting $k \to \infty$ gives
$(\tfrac45)^n Y_n \ge \tilde E_n[(\tfrac45)^{\tau^*}(4 - S_{\tau^*})^+]
= (\tfrac45)^n v(S_n)$ by the attainment, the walk from date $n$ being a
fresh symmetric walk by <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">independence
of the later tosses</Ref>.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

  alt="Three small one-step trees: at price 8 with value 0.5, successors 16 worth 0.25 and 4 worth 1, the discounted average 0.5 equals the value, a martingale; at price 2 with value 2, successors 4 worth 1 and 1 worth 3, the discounted average 1.6 is strictly less, exercise; at price 1 with value 3, successors 2 worth 2 and one half worth 3.5, the discounted average 2.2 is strictly less, exercise"
  caption="The supermartingale clause at the three kinds of node: equality above the boundary, strict inequality at it and below it."
/>

### The seller, and the equation the value solves

The seller's side needs no new theorem. <Ref
lesson="american-derivatives">Replication with consumption</Ref> was
proved one period at a time and never used the horizon: with
$\Delta_n = [v(2S_n) - v(S_n/2)]/(2S_n - S_n/2)$ and
$C_n = v(S_n) - \text{continuation}$, the seller who charged $v(S_0)$ holds
$v(S_n)$ at every node forever, withdrawing $C_n$ where the buyer should
have exercised. At $s = 4$, $\Delta = (0.5 - 2)/6 = -\tfrac14$ and
$C = 0$; at $s = 2$, $\Delta = (1 - 3)/3 = -\tfrac23$ and
$C = 2 - 1.6 = 0.4$; at $s \le 1$ both successors are exercise nodes and
$\Delta = -1$, the short-forward hedge of the previous lesson.

Properties (i), (ii) and the attainment together say that at every
lattice price one of two equalities holds, $v(s) = 4 - s$ or
$v(s) = $ continuation, which is the single equation
$$
v(s) = \max\Bigl\{4 - s,\ \tfrac45\bigl[\tfrac12 v(2s) + \tfrac12 v(s/2)\bigr]\Bigr\},
$$
the perpetual put's *Bellman equation* (Shreve I, (5.4.13)). It is
necessary and not sufficient: $w(s) = 4/s$ at every lattice price also
satisfies it, since its discounted process is a martingale and
$4/s \ge 4 - s$ everywhere, and it is not the price. The closing
exercises refute the claim that it is, and the clause that discards it
is (iv), the smallest-process clause, in the form of the boundary
condition $v(s) \to 4$ as $s \to 0$.

<KeyFact>
Discovery first, proof second: the search produced the candidate and the
boundary, and the certification confirmed them against every stopping
time, of every shape, without listing any. The entire cost of removing
the expiry is one limit, the monotone limit of a truncated expectation,
and it is the only place the argument leaves the finite tree.
</KeyFact>

## The boundary as economics: interest on the strike against the upside kept alive

The certification said where the boundary is; the arithmetic of the
continuation value says why it is there, in dollars, and the decomposition
is the one the previous lesson used to explain why puts and calls differ.

### The decomposition

Write $t(s) = v(s) - (4 - s)$, the put's value beyond the payoff of the
contract that delivers $4 - s$ at once, exercised or not. At a node with
price $s$, the continuation minus the intrinsic value is
$$
\tfrac45\,\tilde E[v(S_1)] - (4 - s)
= \underbrace{\tfrac45\,\tilde E[4 - S_1] - (4 - s)}_{= \,4/1.25 \,-\, 4 \,=\, -0.8}
+ \underbrace{\tfrac45\,\tilde E[t(S_1)]}_{\text{upside kept}},
$$
using $\tilde E[S_1] = 1.25\,s$, the martingale property of the discounted
stock. The first term is the interest lost by receiving the strike one
period later, $0.8$ at every node. The second is the discounted average
of what the live put is worth beyond $4 - S_1$ at the two successors, and
it depends on the node. The holder continues exactly where the second
term exceeds $0.8$.

At $s = 1$ both successors are exercise nodes, $t = 0$ at each, and the
upside is $0$: waiting costs $0.8$ for nothing, and the delta is $-1$. At
$s = 2$ the upper successor keeps $t(4) = 1$, the lower keeps nothing,
and the upside is $\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = 0.4 < 0.8$: exercise,
by a margin of $0.4$. At $s = 4$ the upside is
$\tfrac25[t(8) + t(2)] = \tfrac25(4.5 + 0) = 1.8 > 0.8$: continue. At
$s = 8$ it is $\tfrac25[t(16) + t(4)] = \tfrac25(12.25 + 1) = 5.3$, and
the further the stock is from the boundary, the more of the put's value is
the right not to deliver $4 - S$ when that number is negative.

### What carries forward and what does not

The same trade-off, interest on the strike against the value of keeping
the put alive, fixes the boundary in continuous time in closed form: for
the Black–Scholes stock the perpetual put's boundary is
$L^* = \frac{2r}{2r + \sigma^2}K$, which <Ref
lesson="perpetual-american-put">the module-five extra</Ref> derives by
requiring the value function to meet the payoff tangentially at $L^*$
rather than at a kink. On this tree the value function is kinked at $2$
because the lattice cannot place the boundary between prices; the smooth
version is what a continuum of prices allows. The walk's fine structure
carries forward too: <Ref lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">the next
module</Ref> rescales the symmetric walk to Brownian motion, and both
first-passage arguments proved here reappear for it, the reflection count
as $2P(W(t) \ge m)$ and the martingale trick with
$e^{\sigma W(t) - \sigma^2 t/2}$.

What does not carry forward is the instrument. No perpetual put trades;
the object is the limit of the finite-horizon puts of <Ref
lesson="binomial-pricer-lab">the module's lab</Ref>, whose boundaries
move with the date, and the limit is what those boundaries approach far
from expiry.

What is established: the first-passage time $\tau_m$ of the symmetric
walk is a stopping time with no bound, finite with probability one and
infinite in mean; its generating function $E[\alpha^{\tau_1}] =
(1 - \sqrt{1 - \alpha^2})/\alpha$ follows from stopping the exponential
martingale and taking one limit by hand, and its distribution
$P(\tau_1 = 2j - 1) = \frac{(2j - 2)!}{j!\,(j - 1)!}2^{-(2j - 1)}$
follows both from that series and from the reflection count
$P(\tau_m \le n) = 2P(M_n \ge m) - P(M_n = m)$; on the \$4 tree the rule
"exercise the first time the stock falls to $L$" is worth
$(K - L)L/s$, the boundary is the lattice maximizer of $(K - L)L$, which
is $2$ for the \$4 put, and the value $4/s$ above it is certified by the
Snell characterization with a monotone limit in place of a last date. The
boundary is where the interest lost on the strike, $0.8$ per period,
first exceeds the upside the live put keeps. What is not established is
how any of this reads on an exchange, where puts and calls expire, stocks
pay dividends, and exercise is a decision made by a clearing house's
counterparty rather than a formula. <Ref
lesson="early-exercise-in-practice">The next lesson</Ref> reads the
module's theorems against listed options and finds the one event, a
dividend the day before ex-date, that makes a rational holder exercise a
call early.

## ★ Drift changes the answer: the asymmetric walk

For a coin with head probability $p \ne \tfrac12$ the exponential
martingale is $e^{\sigma M_n}/f(\sigma)^n$ with
$f(\sigma) = p e^\sigma + q e^{-\sigma}$, since
$E_n[e^{\sigma X_{n+1}}] = f(\sigma)$, and the trick runs for every
$\sigma$ with $f(\sigma) > 1$, which is what makes the unstopped piece
vanish. For $p > \tfrac12$ that is every $\sigma > 0$, the limit
$\sigma \downarrow 0$ is available, and the walk reaches $1$ with
probability one and in finite mean time, $E[\tau_1] = 1/(p - q)$ (Shreve
I, Exercise 5.2). For $p < \tfrac12$, $f(\sigma) > 1$ only for
$\sigma > \sigma_0 = \ln(q/p)$, the smallest admissible limit is
$\sigma \downarrow \sigma_0$, and it yields
$P(\tau_1 < \infty) = e^{-\sigma_0} = p/q < 1$: a walk with downward
drift fails to reach $+1$ on a set of positive probability, and its mean
passage time is infinite for that reason rather than for the symmetric
walk's. The closing exercises carry out the $p < \tfrac12$ computation
and name the step of the symmetric proof that moves.

For pricing, the asymmetric case is the tree with $r \ne \tfrac14$, where
$\tilde p \ne \tfrac12$ and the risk-neutral walk drifts. The search of
the fourth section runs unchanged with the asymmetric generating
function in place of $\tfrac12$ per level, and the certification is the
same three checks; only the arithmetic of the discount factors changes.

<Exercise>
Prove that $E[\alpha^{\tau_2}] = \bigl(E[\alpha^{\tau_1}]\bigr)^2$ for
the symmetric walk and every $\alpha \in (0, 1)$, and deduce
$E[\alpha^{\tau_m}] = \bigl(\frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - \alpha^2}}{\alpha}\bigr)^m$
for every positive integer $m$. State which properties of the coin the
argument uses, and whether the squaring identity holds for an unfair
coin. Write the decomposition of $\tau_2$ before opening the solution.

<Solution>
**Strategy hint.** Sort the paths by the value of $\tau_1$ and use that
after $\tau_1$ the walk starts afresh from level $1$.

**The decomposition.** On $\{\tau_1 = k\}$, an event decided by the first
$k$ tosses, the walk stands at $1$ at time $k$, and
$\tau_2 - \tau_1$ is the first time the walk built from the tosses
$k + 1, k + 2, \ldots$ reaches $+1$; call that time $\tau_1'$. Those tosses
are independent of the first $k$ and are a fair coin, so $\tau_1'$ has the
distribution of $\tau_1$ and is independent of $\{\tau_1 = k\}$.

**The squaring.** With $\alpha^\infty = 0$, and using that $\tau_2 = \infty$
whenever $\tau_1 = \infty$,
$$
E[\alpha^{\tau_2}] = \sum_k E\bigl[\alpha^{k}\alpha^{\tau_1'}\mathbb 1_{\{\tau_1 = k\}}\bigr]
= \sum_k \alpha^k P(\tau_1 = k)\,E[\alpha^{\tau_1'}]
= E[\alpha^{\tau_1}]\,E[\alpha^{\tau_1}],
$$
the middle equality by independence of $\alpha^{\tau_1'}$ from the event
$\{\tau_1 = k\}$. The same argument from level $m - 1$ to level $m$ gives
$E[\alpha^{\tau_m}] = E[\alpha^{\tau_{m - 1}}]\,E[\alpha^{\tau_1}]$, and
induction gives the $m$-th power.

**What was used.** Independence of the tosses and their identical
distribution; not $p = \tfrac12$. The squaring therefore holds for an
unfair coin as well, with the asymmetric generating function of the
boundary-probe exercise in place of the symmetric one. The transferable
move: a first passage decomposes into independent, identically
distributed level crossings, which is the discrete form of the strong
Markov property that Brownian motion inherits.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Let the coin have head probability $p < \tfrac12$ and $q = 1 - p$, and
let $f(\sigma) = p e^\sigma + q e^{-\sigma}$. (a) Find $\sigma_0 > 0$
with $f(\sigma_0) = 1$ and show $f(\sigma) > 1$ exactly for
$\sigma > \sigma_0$. (b) Run the martingale trick with
$Z_n = e^{\sigma M_n} f(\sigma)^{-n}$ for $\sigma > \sigma_0$ and let
$\sigma \downarrow \sigma_0$ to compute $P(\tau_1 < \infty)$. (c) Name the
step of the symmetric proof that changes. Evaluate at $p = 0.4$.

<Solution>
**(a).** $f(\sigma) = 1$ reads $p e^{2\sigma} - e^\sigma + q = 0$, a
quadratic in $e^\sigma$ with roots $1$ and $q/p$; so
$\sigma_0 = \ln(q/p) > 0$. $f$ is convex with $f(0) = f(\sigma_0) = 1$,
so $f < 1$ on $(0, \sigma_0)$ and $f > 1$ for $\sigma > \sigma_0$. At
$p = 0.4$: $\sigma_0 = \ln 1.5 \approx 0.405$.

**(b).** $Z$ is a martingale by the same computation as the lemma, with
$f(\sigma)$ in place of $\tfrac12(e^\sigma + e^{-\sigma})$. For
$\sigma > \sigma_0$ set $\beta = 1/f(\sigma) < 1$. Optional sampling at
horizon $n$, the split into $\{\tau_1 \le n\}$ and $\{\tau_1 > n\}$, the
bound $Z_n \le \beta^n$ on the second (since $M_n \le 0$ there) and the
monotone limit give $e^\sigma E[\beta^{\tau_1}] = 1$, with
$\beta^\infty = 0$. Now let $\sigma \downarrow \sigma_0$: $\beta \uparrow 1$,
the series $\sum_k \beta^k P(\tau_1 = k)$ increases to $P(\tau_1 < \infty)$,
and $e^\sigma \to e^{\sigma_0} = q/p$. Hence
$$
P(\tau_1 < \infty) = e^{-\sigma_0} = \frac{p}{q},
$$
which is $\tfrac23$ at $p = 0.4$: one path in three never reaches $+1$.
Solving the quadratic in $e^{-\sigma}$ for general $\beta$ gives the
generating function
$E[\beta^{\tau_1}] = \bigl(1 - \sqrt{1 - 4pq\beta^2}\bigr)/(2q\beta)$,
which at $\beta \uparrow 1$ is $(1 - (q - p))/(2q) = p/q$, consistent.

**(c).** In the symmetric proof the admissible $\sigma$ were all
$\sigma > 0$, and the limit that delivered $P(\tau_1 < \infty)$ was
$\sigma \downarrow 0$, giving $e^{-0} = 1$. With downward drift the
unstopped piece $E[Z_n \mathbb 1_{\{\tau_1 > n\}}]$ is bounded by
$f(\sigma)^{-n}$, which tends to zero only when $f(\sigma) > 1$, so the
admissible $\sigma$ are bounded away from $0$ by $\sigma_0$ and the limit
lands on $e^{-\sigma_0} < 1$. In the symmetric case
$f(\sigma) = \tfrac12(e^\sigma + e^{-\sigma})$ exceeds $1$ for every
$\sigma \ne 0$, so $\sigma \downarrow 0$ is admissible; drift moves the
crossing of $1$ to $\sigma_0$. The transferable move: the value at which the exponential martingale's
normalizer crosses $1$ is the probability of ever reaching the level.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "The \$4 stock reaches \$8 with probability one, so
borrowing \$4 at $25\%$, buying the stock and selling it the first time it
reaches \$8 is an arbitrage: zero initial cost, a sure \$4 gain." Compute
the trade's profit as a function of $\tau_1$, find the probability that it
loses money, state the theorem that says the trade's present value is
zero, and name the clause of the definition of arbitrage that fails.

<Solution>
**Profit by date.** At $\tau_1$ the sale brings $8$ and the debt has
grown to $4 \cdot 1.25^{\tau_1}$: profit $8 - 4 \cdot 1.25^{\tau_1}$. At
$\tau_1 = 1$: $8 - 5 = 3$. At $\tau_1 = 3$: $8 - 7.8125 = 0.1875$. At
$\tau_1 = 5$: $8 - 12.207 = -4.207$, and the loss grows without bound
thereafter, since $1.25^{\tau_1} > 2$ for every $\tau_1 \ge 4$. On the
paths with $\tau_1 = \infty$ the stock is never sold and the debt
compounds forever.

**Probability of loss.** $P(\tau_1 \ge 5) = 1 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac18 =
\tfrac38$ under the risk-neutral coin, and positive under any coin with
$p > 0$. The mean profit under $\tilde P$ is
$8\,\tilde E[\mathbb 1_{\{\tau_1 < \infty\}}] - 4\,\tilde E[1.25^{\tau_1}]$,
and the second term is infinite by the tail: $1.25^{\tau_1}$ grows
geometrically where $\tau_1$ has an infinite mean.

**The theorem.** In present value the sale is worth
$8\,\tilde E[(\tfrac45)^{\tau_1}] = 8 \cdot \tfrac12 = 4$, the purchase
price: the discounted stock is a $\tilde P$-martingale, optional sampling
keeps its mean at every horizon, and the generating-function theorem
carries the equality to $\tau_1$ itself. A fair game stopped at a stopping
time is fair, and "sell at $8$" is a stopping time.

**The clause.** An arbitrage (<Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">the
first lesson</Ref>) has no possibility of loss; this trade loses with
probability $\tfrac38$ and its loss is unbounded. The certainty in the
claim is certainty of the *event* $\tau_1 < \infty$, and the arbitrage
definition is about the *value* $8 - 4 \cdot 1.25^{\tau_1}$, which is a
random variable with both signs. The transferable move: "with probability
one" and "in finite time" are different statements, and only the second
can be financed.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Reproduce the tail of $\tau_1$ numerically. (a) Compute
$P(\tau_1 > n)$ exactly for $n = 9$ and $n = 99$ from
$P(\tau_1 > 2j - 1) = \binom{2j}{j}4^{-j}$, and from Stirling's
approximation $\binom{2j}{j}4^{-j} \approx 1/\sqrt{\pi j}$ for
$n = 999$ and $9{,}999$. (b) Simulate $10{,}000$ symmetric walks of
$999$ steps each, record the fraction that reach $+1$ by step $99$ and the
sample mean of $\min(\tau_1, 999)$, and say what would count as agreement
with (a). (c) Explain what the truncated mean does as the truncation
grows, and why that is the experimental signature of $E[\tau_1] = \infty$.

<Solution>
**(a).** $n = 9$, $j = 5$: $\binom{10}{5}/4^5 = 252/1024 = 0.2461$.
$n = 99$, $j = 50$: $\binom{100}{50}/4^{50} = 0.0796$; Stirling gives
$1/\sqrt{50\pi} = 0.0798$. $n = 999$: $1/\sqrt{500\pi} = 0.0252$.
$n = 9{,}999$: $1/\sqrt{5000\pi} = 0.0080$. Each tenfold increase in $n$
divides the tail by $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$. (The identity
$P(\tau_1 > 2j - 1) = P(M_{2j - 1} = -1) = \binom{2j - 1}{j}2^{-(2j - 1)}
= \binom{2j}{j}4^{-j}$ uses $\binom{2j}{j} = 2\binom{2j - 1}{j}$.)

**(b).** The fraction reaching $+1$ by step $99$ estimates
$1 - 0.0796 = 0.9204$; with $10{,}000$ trials its standard error is
$\sqrt{0.92 \times 0.08/10{,}000} \approx 0.0027$, so agreement means a
sample fraction within about $0.915$ and $0.926$. The truncated mean
estimates $E[\min(\tau_1, 999)] = \sum_{n = 0}^{998} P(\tau_1 > n)$,
which by the tail formula is about $\sum_n \sqrt{2/(\pi n)} \approx
\sqrt{8 \cdot 999/\pi} \approx 50$. Its standard error is large because
the truncated variable is heavy-tailed up to the cutoff: the second moment
$\sum_n (2n + 1)P(\tau_1 > n)$ over $n < 999$ is about
$2\sqrt{2/\pi}\cdot\tfrac23 \cdot 999^{3/2} \approx 34{,}000$, so the
standard deviation is about $180$ and the standard error of the mean over
$10{,}000$ walks about $1.8$; agreement means a sample mean within roughly
$46$ and $54$.

**(c).** Truncating at $N$ gives a mean of order $\sqrt{8N/\pi}$: about
$50$ at $N = 999$, about $160$ at $N = 9{,}999$, about $500$ at
$N = 99{,}999$. The truncated mean grows without bound as the truncation
is lifted, which is what an infinite mean looks like in a finite
experiment; a random variable with a finite mean would have a truncated
mean that stabilizes. The transferable move: an infinite expectation is
never observed as a large number but as a statistic that refuses to
converge, and the growth rate, here $\sqrt N$, identifies the tail
exponent.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Price the perpetual put with strike $3$ on the \$4 tree. Value the level
rules from $S_0 = 4$, find that two of them tie, write $v(s)$, and verify
the Snell characterization by hand at $s = 2$ and at $s = 1$: dominance,
the supermartingale clause, and whether the inequality is strict. Then
say which rules are optimal and relate the tie to <Ref
lesson="american-derivatives">the previous lesson's zero-interest
exercise</Ref>. Decide first which kind of problem each part is.

<Solution>
**The kind.** The search is a first-passage computation; the verification
is the three-property check of the Snell characterization; the last part
is the interest-on-the-strike decomposition.

**The search.** "Exercise at $L$" from $4$ is worth $(3 - L)L/4$:
$L = 2$: $\tfrac{2}{4} = 0.5$; $L = 1$: $\tfrac{2}{4} = 0.5$;
$L = \tfrac12$: $\tfrac{1.25}{4} = 0.3125$. The rules at $2$ and at $1$
tie, because $(3 - L)L = 2$ at both, and $K/2 = 1.5$ lies halfway between
them on the lattice. So $v(s) = 2/s$ for $s \ge 1$ and $v(s) = 3 - s$ for
$s \le 1$, the two agreeing at $s = 1$ ($2 = 3 - 1$) and $v(2) = 1 = 3 - 2$
as well: the value equals intrinsic at both $1$ and $2$.

**At $s = 2$.** Dominance: $v(2) = 1 = (3 - 2)^+$. Continuation:
$\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac12[v(4) + v(1)] = \tfrac25(0.5 + 2) = 1 = v(2)$:
equality, so the discounted value is a martingale at $2$, and exercising
and continuing are worth the same.

**At $s = 1$.** Dominance: $v(1) = 2 = (3 - 1)^+$. Continuation:
$\tfrac25[v(2) + v(\tfrac12)] = \tfrac25(1 + 2.5) = 1.4 < 2$: strict, by
$0.6$, one period's interest on the \$3 strike.

**Optimal rules.** $\tau^* = \min\{n : S_n \le 2\}$ attains $v$, and so
does $\min\{n : S_n \le 1\}$: both collect $2/s$ from every $s \ge 2$. At
$s = 2$ the interest lost, $3 - 3/1.25 = 0.6$, exactly equals the upside
kept, $\tfrac25[t(4) + t(1)] = \tfrac25(1.5 + 0) = 0.6$, so the holder is
indifferent, as she was at every node of the previous lesson's tree with
$r = 0$, where the interest term vanished everywhere. The transferable
move: a tie in the search is a node where the supermartingale inequality
is an equality on the exercise side of the boundary, and the exercise
region is then determined only up to that node.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "The function $w(s) = 4/s$ satisfies the Bellman
equation $w(s) = \max\{4 - s, \tfrac45[\tfrac12 w(2s) + \tfrac12 w(s/2)]\}$
at every lattice price, so it is the perpetual \$4 put's price, and the
holder should never exercise." Check the claim's premise, name the clause
of the Snell characterization it fails, exhibit a price at which $w$
violates a bound from <Ref lesson="static-bounds-parity">the second
lesson</Ref>, and say what the rule "never exercise" actually collects.

<Solution>
**The premise holds.** $\tfrac45 \cdot \tfrac12[\tfrac4{2s} + \tfrac8s]
= \tfrac4s = w(s)$, so the discounted $w$ is a martingale, and
$4/s \ge 4 - s$ is $(s - 2)^2 \ge 0$, so the maximum is always the
continuation. $w$ satisfies the equation everywhere.

**The clause.** $w$ has properties (i) and (ii), a dominating
supermartingale, and fails (iv): the price is the *smallest* such process,
and $v \le w$ with strict inequality below the boundary, $v(1) = 3 <
4 = w(1)$. The Bellman equation encodes (i), (ii) and "equality in one of
them", not minimality; it has extraneous solutions, and $w$ is one.

**The bound.** A put is worth at most its strike, since exercising it
can never yield more than $K$: $w(\tfrac12) = 8 > 4$ and
$w(\tfrac14) = 16$; the claimed price grows without bound as the stock
falls, where a put's value approaches $K$. The boundary condition
$v(s) \to 4$ as $s \to 0$ (Shreve I, (5.4.15)) is the same fact stated
for the lattice.

**What "never exercise" collects.** Zero, on every path: the discounted
payoff at $\tau = \infty$ is $0$ by definition, and no finite date is
ever chosen. So $w$ is not the value of the rule it recommends; it is the
value of no rule at all. The step that fails in the claim is the passage
from "solves the equation" to "is attained by a stopping time": (iii),
attainment, is what turns a candidate into a price, and $w$ is attained by
nothing. The transferable move: a dominating supermartingale is an upper
bound on every rule's value, and only the one some rule attains is the
price.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Use the reflection identity to compute $P(\tau_m \le n)$ for $n = 16$,
$m = 4$ and for $n = 100$, $m = 10$, in both cases $m = \sqrt n$. Then
compute $2P(Z \ge 1)$ for a standard normal $Z$ and compare. Say what the
two computed values are approaching, and why the scale $m = \sqrt n$ is
the one on which the sequence has a limit at all.

<Solution>
**$n = 16$, $m = 4$.** $M_{16} = 2H - 16$ with $H$ the number of heads,
so $M_{16} \ge 4$ is $H \ge 10$ and $M_{16} = 4$ is $H = 10$. Summing
$\binom{16}{k}$ for $k = 10, \ldots, 16$: $8008 + 4368 + 1820 + 560 +
120 + 16 + 1 = 14{,}893$, so $P(M_{16} \ge 4) = 14{,}893/65{,}536 =
0.2272$ and $P(M_{16} = 4) = 8008/65{,}536 = 0.1222$. Then
$P(\tau_4 \le 16) = 2 \times 0.2272 - 0.1222 = 0.3323$.

**$n = 100$, $m = 10$.** $M_{100} \ge 10$ is $H \ge 55$, $M_{100} = 10$
is $H = 55$. $\binom{100}{50}/2^{100} = 0.0796$, and multiplying by the
ratios $\binom{100}{50 + k}/\binom{100}{50}$ gives
$P(H = 55) = 0.0485$ and $P(H \ge 55) = 0.1841$. Then
$P(\tau_{10} \le 100) = 2 \times 0.1841 - 0.0485 = 0.3197$.

**The Gaussian.** $2P(Z \ge 1) = 2(1 - 0.8413) = 0.3173$. The sequence
$0.3323, 0.3197, \ldots$ is approaching it from above. The excess is not
the point mass, which the identity subtracts; it is a continuity
correction. In its first form the identity is
$P(M_n \ge m) + P(M_n \ge m + 2)$, by parity, and each term is a binomial
tail whose Gaussian approximation sits at the half-integer below its
threshold: $P(M_n \ge m) \approx P(Z \ge (m - 1)/\sqrt n)$, which at
$n = 16$ is $P(Z \ge 0.75) = 0.2266$ against the exact $0.2272$, and
$P(M_n \ge m + 2) \approx P(Z \ge (m + 1)/\sqrt n) = P(Z \ge 1.25) =
0.1056$ against the exact $0.1051$. The two corrections sit symmetrically
about $m/\sqrt n = 1$, so their first-order parts cancel and the
second-order part survives: with $T$ the normal tail, $\varphi$ its
density and $h = 1/\sqrt n$,
$$
T(1 - h) + T(1 + h) = 2T(1) + h^2\,\varphi(1) + O(h^4),
\qquad \varphi(1) = 0.2420,
$$
which is $0.0151$ at $n = 16$ and $0.0024$ at $n = 100$: the two observed
excesses, $0.3323 - 0.3173$ and $0.3197 - 0.3173$, to the last digit. The
sequence descends to $2P(Z \ge 1)$ at rate $1/n$.

**Why $\sqrt n$.** $M_n$ has mean $0$ and variance $n$, so
$M_n/\sqrt n$ has a limit distribution, the standard normal, by the
central limit theorem; a level $m$ fixed in units of $\sqrt n$ is the
only kind of level for which $P(M_n \ge m)$ neither tends to $0$ nor to
$\tfrac12$. On that scale the reflection identity becomes
$P(\max_{k \le n} M_k \ge m) \to 2P(Z \ge m/\sqrt n)$, which is the
reflection principle for Brownian motion: the probability that a
Brownian path reaches level $a$ by time $t$ is $2P(W(t) \ge a)$. The
transferable move: the count proved on the tree survives the scaling
limit because it never used the step size, and <Ref
lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">the next module</Ref> takes that
limit of the walk itself.
</Solution>

Taylor's expansion to second order, with $(dW)^2$ replaced by $dt$ and
everything smaller discarded, is the entire content of Itô's formula — the
chain rule for functions of a path with nonzero quadratic variation.

This is the eighteenth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the fourth move of its fourth module. <Ref
lesson="ito-integral">The previous lesson</Ref> built the Itô integral as
the gain of an adapted strategy, proved it a martingale with
$E[I(t)^2] = E\int_0^t \Delta^2\,du$, and computed
$\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 T$ by an algebraic identity
specific to squares; its last exercise found the matching identity for
cubes and noticed that both corrections were $\tfrac12 \int f''(W)\,dt$.
What stands open is a method: a rule that produces the correction for
every function and every process at once, so that the hedging argument of
the next module can differentiate an option's value along a stock path
without inventing an identity each time. This lesson supplies that rule,
Itô's formula, and solves with it the two equations the course's models
are written in. What it hands on is the complete working toolkit, first
turned on a hedge that ordinary calculus says should be free in
<Ref lesson="stop-loss-paradox">the next lesson</Ref>.

The route: the size of every term of a Taylor expansion across one
Brownian increment; the theorem for $f(t, W(t))$ and the audit against
last lesson's integrals; Itô processes, $dX\,dX = \Delta^2\,dt$ and the
general formula; geometric Brownian motion solved, with the
$-\tfrac12\sigma^2$ met for the third time; the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck
equation by integrating factor, and the theorem that makes its solution
Gaussian; the product rule and the recipe every pricing argument runs.

## Taylor across one cell: the size of every term

The previous lesson closed by reading two hand-made identities as
instances of one pattern. This section shows where the pattern comes
from: a second-order Taylor expansion across one cell of a partition,
with each term's size measured against the cell's length.

### One cell of $f(W)$, expanded

Let $f$ have three continuous derivatives and fix one cell
$[t_j, t_{j+1}]$ of a partition, with increment
$\Delta W_j = W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)$. Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange
remainder says
$$
f\bigl(W(t_{j+1})\bigr) - f\bigl(W(t_j)\bigr)
= f'\bigl(W(t_j)\bigr)\,\Delta W_j
+ \tfrac12 f''\bigl(W(t_j)\bigr)\,(\Delta W_j)^2
+ \tfrac16 f'''(\xi_j)\,(\Delta W_j)^3
$$
for some $\xi_j$ between the two values. Nothing about $W$ has been used;
this is the expansion of any function across any step. For $f(x) = e^x$
and a step from $0.5$ to $1.1$, the change is $1.3555$, the tangent term
$e^{0.5} \times 0.6 = 0.9892$ accounts for $73\%$ of it, the
second-order term $\tfrac12 e^{0.5} \times 0.36 = 0.2968$ brings the
total to $1.2860$, and $0.0695$ remains, of which the cubic term is
$0.0594$.

### The size table: what $n$ cells of each term add up to

What $W$ contributes is the size of $\Delta W_j$. The increment is
$\sqrt{\Delta t_j}\,Z_j$ with $Z_j$ standard normal, a fixed distribution
at every mesh, so $|\Delta W_j|$ is of order $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, its square
of order $\Delta t$, its cube of order $\Delta t^{3/2}$. Take $n$ equal
cells of length $\Delta t = T/n$ and sum each term over all of them.

- **First order, $f'\,\Delta W$.** Each term is of order $\sqrt{\Delta t}$
  and there are $n$ of them, but the signs are random and the sum does
  not grow like $n\sqrt{\Delta t}$: it is the left-endpoint sum of the
  adapted integrand $f'(W(t))$, and the previous lesson's construction
  sends it to the Itô integral $\int_0^T f'(W)\,dW$.
- **Second order, $\tfrac12 f''\,(\Delta W)^2$.** Each term is of order
  $\Delta t$, all are positive when $f'' > 0$, and $n$ of them add up to
  something of order $T$. Replacing $(\Delta W_j)^2$ by $\Delta t_j$ turns
  the sum into a Riemann sum of $\tfrac12 f''(W(t))$, and the quadratic
  variation theorem is what licenses the replacement: the sum converges
  to $\tfrac12 \int_0^T f''(W)\,dt$.
- **Third order, $\tfrac16 f'''\,(\Delta W)^3$.** For a standard normal
  $E|Z|^3 = 2\sqrt{2/\pi} = 1.60$, so the expected size of one term is
  $1.60\,\Delta t^{3/2}$ times a bounded factor, and $n$ of them have
  expected total $1.60\,T\sqrt{\Delta t}$, which goes to zero.

For a differentiable integrator the second-order term dies the same way
the third does here: $n$ terms of size $\Delta t^2$ sum to $T\,\Delta t$.
That is the whole difference between the two calculi. The term the
ordinary chain rule drops is dropped because it vanishes; for $W$ it is
of order $\Delta t$ per cell, and $n$ such terms are worth $T$.

<KeyFact>
The Itô–Doeblin formula is the quadratic variation theorem placed inside
a Taylor expansion, and nothing else about Brownian motion enters it; the
same second-order term, $\tfrac12 f''\,(\Delta S)^2$ with $f$ an option's
value, is the gamma profit of <Ref lesson="greeks-hedging-error">the
hedging-error lesson</Ref>, which is why hedging error is a second-order
phenomenon.
</KeyFact>

## The theorem for Brownian motion, with the two fenced steps named

The size table says which terms survive; the theorem states the limit
and its proof names exactly where the size argument has to be replaced
by a convergence argument. The function is allowed to depend on time as
well, because the discounted quantities of the pricing modules do.

<Theorem title="Itô–Doeblin formula for Brownian motion (Shreve II, 4.4.1)">
Let $f(t, x)$ have continuous partial derivatives $f_t$, $f_x$ and
$f_{xx}$, and let $W$ be a Brownian motion. Then for every $T \ge 0$,
$$
f\bigl(T, W(T)\bigr) = f\bigl(0, W(0)\bigr)
+ \int_0^T f_t\bigl(t, W(t)\bigr)\,dt
+ \int_0^T f_x\bigl(t, W(t)\bigr)\,dW(t)
+ \tfrac12 \int_0^T f_{xx}\bigl(t, W(t)\bigr)\,dt .
$$
In differential notation,
$df(t, W(t)) = f_t\,dt + f_x\,dW(t) + \tfrac12 f_{xx}\,dt$.

<Proof>
Goal: the integral identity. Mode: expand across each cell of a partition
to second order, sum, and identify the limit of each of five sums.
Roadmap: the expansion; three sums that survive; two that vanish; the
two places where a size estimate is not a proof. The argument assumes
the third derivatives exist and are bounded on the range of the path;
the reduction of the theorem as stated to that case is fenced at the end.

**The expansion.** Fix a partition $0 = t_0 < \cdots < t_n = T$, write
$\Delta t_j = t_{j+1} - t_j$ and $\Delta W_j = W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)$, and
expand $f$ across each cell in both arguments:
$$
f(t_{j+1}, W(t_{j+1})) - f(t_j, W(t_j))
= f_t\,\Delta t_j + f_x\,\Delta W_j + \tfrac12 f_{xx}\,(\Delta W_j)^2
+ f_{tx}\,\Delta t_j\,\Delta W_j + \tfrac12 f_{tt}\,(\Delta t_j)^2 + R_j ,
$$
every derivative evaluated at $(t_j, W(t_j))$ and $R_j$ a remainder
bounded by a constant times $|\Delta t_j|^3 + |\Delta W_j|^3$. Summing
over $j$ telescopes the left side to $f(T, W(T)) - f(0, W(0))$, so the
right side's five sums plus the remainders converge to the theorem's
right side if each converges to its stated limit.

**The $dt$ sum.** $\sum_j f_t(t_j, W(t_j))\,\Delta t_j$ is a Riemann sum
of the function $t \mapsto f_t(t, W(t))$, continuous on $[0, T]$ because
$f_t$ and the path are, and converges to $\int_0^T f_t\,dt$ on every
path.

**The $dW$ sum.** $\sum_j f_x(t_j, W(t_j))\,\Delta W_j$ is the Itô
integral of the simple process that freezes the continuous adapted
integrand $f_x(t, W(t))$ at each left endpoint. Its convergence in mean
square to $\int_0^T f_x\,dW$ is the extension theorem of <Ref
lesson="ito-integral">the previous lesson</Ref>, whose first fenced fact
is exactly that left-endpoint staircases of a continuous integrand
approximate it.

**The $(dW)^2$ sum.** Split
$\tfrac12 \sum_j f_{xx}(t_j, W(t_j))\,(\Delta W_j)^2$ into
$\tfrac12 \sum_j f_{xx}\,\Delta t_j$, a Riemann sum converging to
$\tfrac12\int_0^T f_{xx}\,dt$, plus the difference
$\tfrac12 \sum_j f_{xx}(t_j, W(t_j))\,[(\Delta W_j)^2 - \Delta t_j]$.
Each bracket has mean zero and variance $2\Delta t_j^2$, is independent
of the coefficient in front of it, and is uncorrelated with every other
term of the sum; when $f_{xx}$ is bounded by $M$ the mean square of the
difference is at most $\tfrac12 M^2 \|\Pi\| T$, which goes to zero. This
is the step at which $dW\,dW = dt$ is used, and it is a statement about
the sum, not about any one increment. The starred section at the end of
the lesson carries the computation.

**The two vanishing sums.** By the multiplication table,
$$
\Bigl|\sum_j f_{tx}\,\Delta t_j\,\Delta W_j\Bigr|
\le \max_k |\Delta W_k| \sum_j |f_{tx}|\,\Delta t_j
\longrightarrow 0 \cdot \int_0^T |f_{tx}|\,dt = 0 ,
\qquad
\Bigl|\tfrac12\sum_j f_{tt}\,(\Delta t_j)^2\Bigr|
\le \tfrac12 \|\Pi\| \sum_j |f_{tt}|\,\Delta t_j \longrightarrow 0 ,
$$
the first by uniform continuity of the path, the second by the mesh.
The remainders are handled the same way: $\sum_j |\Delta W_j|^3 \le
\max_k|\Delta W_k| \cdot Q_\Pi \to 0 \cdot T$, and $\sum_j
\Delta t_j^3 \le \|\Pi\|^2 T$.

**The fence.** Two steps were cited, not proved. The $dW$ sum converges
by the previous lesson's fenced approximation theorem. The passage from
bounded third derivatives to the hypothesis as stated, continuity of
$f_t$, $f_x$, $f_{xx}$ alone, is a localisation: stop the path when
$|W|$ first exceeds a level $K$, on which range every derivative is
bounded and the remainder is controlled by the uniform continuity of
$f_{xx}$, then let $K \to \infty$. Karatzas and Shreve, Theorem 3.3.3,
carry it out.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

the Itô–Doeblin formula on that ground, and this course follows him. The
opened manuscript and its analysis are in Bru and Yor (2002), listed in
the references.
</Aside>

### The audit: two corrections found by hand, now by formula

The previous lesson's two integrals were computed from identities built
for the purpose. Both fall out of the theorem in one line each, with
$f$ independent of $t$ so that $f_t = 0$.

For $f(x) = \tfrac12 x^2$, $f' = x$ and $f'' = 1$:
$\tfrac12 W(T)^2 = \int_0^T W\,dW + \tfrac12 \int_0^T 1\,dt$, which is
$\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 T$. For
$f(x) = \tfrac13 x^3$, $f' = x^2$ and $f'' = 2x$:
$\tfrac13 W(T)^3 = \int_0^T W^2\,dW + \int_0^T W\,dt$, which is the
previous lesson's forward exercise. The correction is
$\tfrac12 \int f''(W)\,dt$ in both, as that exercise predicted, and it is
now available for every $f$ with no identity to find.

<Example title="the fourth moment of W(T), from the formula">
The quadratic variation proof took $E[W(T)^4] = 3T^2$ from the
moment-generating function. The formula gives it from the martingale
property of the Itô integral instead. With $f(x) = x^4$, $f' = 4x^3$ and
$f'' = 12x^2$:
$$
W(T)^4 = \int_0^T 4W(t)^3\,dW(t) + \tfrac12 \int_0^T 12\,W(t)^2\,dt
= \int_0^T 4W^3\,dW + 6\int_0^T W(t)^2\,dt .
$$
Take expectations. The Itô integral has mean zero, since its integrand
satisfies $E\int_0^T 16W^6\,dt = 16\int_0^T 15t^3\,dt < \infty$. The
time integral's expectation is computed inside the integral,
$E[W(t)^2] = t$, so
$$
E[W(T)^4] = 6\int_0^T t\,dt = 3T^2 .
$$
The route is general: for any $f$, $E[f(W(T))] = f(0) + \tfrac12
\int_0^T E[f''(W(t))]\,dt$ whenever the Itô integral's integrand is
square-integrable, and the second derivative's expectation is often
easier than the function's.
</Example>

## Itô processes: $dX\,dX = \Delta^2\,dt$ and the general formula

The theorem differentiates functions of $W$. The stock is not $W$: it is
$W$ scaled by a random volatility and pushed by a drift, and the next
module's hedge is a function of the stock. The class of processes to
which the formula extends is the one built from the two integrals the
module has defined.

<Definition title="Itô process (Shreve II, 4.4.3)">
Let $W$ be a Brownian motion with filtration $\mathcal F(t)$. An **Itô
process** is a process of the form
$$
X(t) = X(0) + \int_0^t \Delta(u)\,dW(u) + \int_0^t \Theta(u)\,du ,
$$
where $X(0)$ is a constant and $\Delta$, $\Theta$ are adapted processes
with $E\int_0^t \Delta^2\,du < \infty$ and $\int_0^t |\Theta|\,du <
\infty$ for every $t$. In differential notation,
$dX(t) = \Delta(t)\,dW(t) + \Theta(t)\,dt$; $\Delta$ is the process's
*diffusion coefficient* and $\Theta$ its *drift*.
</Definition>

Four cases fix what the definition covers.

- **The canonical case.** The stock of the next section,
  $dS = \alpha S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$: $\Theta = \alpha S(t)$ and
  $\Delta = \sigma S(t)$, both adapted because $S$ is.
- **A case with a different surface.** $X(t) = \int_0^t e^{\beta u}\,dW(u)$,
  a deterministic integrand and no drift; the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck section
  is built on it.
- **The degenerate case.** $\Delta \equiv 0$: $X(t) = X(0) + \int_0^t
  \Theta\,du$, an ordinary integral, random if $\Theta$ is, with zero
  quadratic variation by the smooth-function computation of <Ref
  lesson="brownian-motion">the Brownian motion lesson</Ref>. The near
  miss of that lesson's definition, $W(t) + \mu t$, is the case
  $\Delta \equiv 1$, $\Theta \equiv \mu$.
- **The near miss.** $X(t) = W(t) + N(t)$ with $N$ a Poisson process, which
  jumps by one at random times. Both coefficients could be adapted; the
  process still cannot be written in the form above, because both
  integrals on the right have continuous paths and $N$ does not. The
  clause that excludes it is the form itself. Processes with jumps have
  their own formula (Shreve II, Chapter 11) and are outside this course.

$W^2$ climbs on average, and subtracting it is what made $W^2 - t$ a
martingale in <Ref lesson="brownian-motion">the Brownian motion
lesson</Ref>.
</Aside>

### The quadratic variation of an Itô process

The formula's second-order term needs the quadratic variation of $X$, and
the lemma is that only the $dW$ part contributes to it.

<Theorem title="quadratic variation of an Itô process (Shreve II, Lemma 4.4.4)">
For the Itô process above, $[X, X](t) = \int_0^t \Delta(u)^2\,du$.

<Proof>
Write $I(t) = \int_0^t \Delta\,dW$ and $R(t) = \int_0^t \Theta\,du$, both
continuous in $t$. On a partition of $[0, t]$ the sampled quadratic
variation of $X = I + R$ expands into three sums:
$$
\sum_j (\Delta X_j)^2
= \sum_j (\Delta I_j)^2 + \sum_j (\Delta R_j)^2 + 2\sum_j \Delta I_j\,\Delta R_j .
$$
The first converges to $[I, I](t) = \int_0^t \Delta^2\,du$ by <Ref
lesson="ito-integral">Theorem 4.3.1 (vi) of the previous lesson</Ref>.
The second is at most $\max_k |\Delta R_k| \cdot \sum_j |\Delta R_j| \le
\max_k |\Delta R_k| \cdot \int_0^t |\Theta|\,du$, and the maximum tends
to zero because $R$ is continuous. The third is at most $2\max_k
|\Delta I_k| \cdot \int_0^t |\Theta|\,du$, which tends to zero because
$I$ is continuous. So $[X, X](t) = [I, I](t)$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

In differential notation the lemma is one line of the multiplication
table: square $dX = \Delta\,dW + \Theta\,dt$ and keep only $dW\,dW$,
$$
dX\,dX = \Delta^2\,dW\,dW + 2\Delta\Theta\,dW\,dt + \Theta^2\,dt\,dt
= \Delta^2\,dt .
$$
The drift never reaches the second-order term. <Ref
lesson="brownian-motion">The realized-volatility computation</Ref> of the
Brownian motion lesson found the same thing for the stock's log by
expanding three sums and watching the two containing $\alpha$ vanish;
the lemma is that computation for every Itô process.

### The general formula

Integrals against an Itô process are defined by splitting the
differential: for an adapted $\Gamma$,
$\int_0^t \Gamma\,dX = \int_0^t \Gamma\Delta\,dW + \int_0^t
\Gamma\Theta\,du$ (Shreve II, Definition 4.4.5). With that, the theorem
for $W$ transfers word for word.

<Theorem title="Itô–Doeblin formula for an Itô process (Shreve II, 4.4.6)">
Let $X$ be an Itô process and let $f(t, x)$ have continuous $f_t$, $f_x$,
$f_{xx}$. Then for every $T \ge 0$,
$$
f\bigl(T, X(T)\bigr) = f\bigl(0, X(0)\bigr)
+ \int_0^T f_t\,dt + \int_0^T f_x\,dX(t) + \tfrac12 \int_0^T f_{xx}\,d[X, X](t) ,
$$
that is, in differential form and then with the differentials
substituted,
$$
df(t, X(t)) = f_t\,dt + f_x\,dX + \tfrac12 f_{xx}\,dX\,dX
= \bigl(f_t + \Theta f_x + \tfrac12 \Delta^2 f_{xx}\bigr)\,dt + \Delta f_x\,dW .
$$

<Proof>
The expansion and the five sums are those of the Brownian case with
$X(t_j)$ in place of $W(t_j)$. The $dt$ sum and the two vanishing sums
are unchanged, since they used only continuity of the path and the mesh.
The $dX$ sum is the left-endpoint sum of $f_x(t, X(t))$ against $X$,
which by the definition of $\int \Gamma\,dX$ splits into an Itô integral
against $W$ and a Riemann sum against $dt$, each converging as before.
The $(dX)^2$ sum converges to $\tfrac12 \int f_{xx}\,d[X, X]$ because $X$
accumulates quadratic variation at rate $\Delta^2$ by the lemma, and
$d[X, X] = \Delta^2\,dt$ turns it into the last integral. The two fenced
steps are the same two.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Remark title="the rule behind the formula">
Expand $f$ to first order in every argument with zero quadratic
variation and to second order in every argument with nonzero quadratic
variation, then reduce the products of differentials by the table. Time
has zero quadratic variation, so $f_t$ enters once; $X$ has quadratic
variation $\int \Delta^2$, so $f_{xx}$ enters with $\tfrac12 \Delta^2$.
Every computation in the rest of the course is this rule and nothing
else, including the product rule of the lesson's final section, where
the second argument is a second process and the second-order term is
the cross variation.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Volatility is what survives squaring: $dX\,dX = \Delta^2\,dt$ contains
the diffusion coefficient and not the drift, so every second-order term
the formula produces, and every quadratic variation any strategy
accumulates, is independent of drifts. This is the property <Ref
lesson="girsanov-theorem">Girsanov's theorem</Ref> rests on when it moves
a drift by a change of measure and leaves $\sigma$ untouched.
</KeyFact>

## Geometric Brownian motion solved: the $-\tfrac12\sigma^2$ for the third time

The general formula turns a differential equation for a process into a
tool for solving it. The first equation is the stock's, and its solution
is the lognormal that <Ref lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">the
calibrated tree</Ref> converged to, now reached in three lines rather
than through a limit of binomial coins.

### Stochastic differential equations, and the fence around them

<Definition title="stochastic differential equation">
A **stochastic differential equation** for an unknown process $X$ is a
prescription
$$
dX(t) = \mu\bigl(t, X(t)\bigr)\,dt + \sigma\bigl(t, X(t)\bigr)\,dW(t),
\qquad X(0) = x_0 ,
$$
with $\mu$ and $\sigma$ given functions; a **solution** is an Itô process
whose drift is $\mu(t, X(t))$ and whose diffusion coefficient is
$\sigma(t, X(t))$, that is, a process satisfying
$X(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t \mu(u, X(u))\,du + \int_0^t \sigma(u, X(u))\,dW(u)$
for all $t$.
</Definition>

<Remark title="existence and uniqueness, fenced">
Whether a solution exists, and whether it is the only one, is a theorem
this course cites and does not prove: if $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are Lipschitz
in $x$ and grow at most linearly in $|x|$, uniformly in $t$, there is
exactly one solution, adapted to $W$'s filtration and with continuous
paths (Karatzas and Shreve, Theorem 5.2.9; Øksendal, Theorem 5.2.1). The
two equations solved below have coefficients linear in $x$ and satisfy
the hypothesis; the strategy-choice exercise at the end meets one that
does not. Where the hypothesis holds, exhibiting a process that
satisfies the equation, as the next two sections do, is exhibiting the
solution.
</Remark>

### The equation, and the substitution that solves it

The stock model is the equation
$$
dS(t) = \alpha S(t)\,dt + \sigma S(t)\,dW(t), \qquad S(0) = S_0 > 0 ,
$$
with constants $\alpha$ and $\sigma > 0$: over a short interval the
*return* $dS/S$ is $\alpha\,dt$ plus $\sigma$ times a Brownian increment,
a mean rate of return $\alpha$ and a volatility $\sigma$ per unit time.
Its diffusion coefficient $\sigma S$ carries the unknown, which is what
makes it hard to integrate directly.

Need: a function of $S$ whose differential no longer contains $S$.
Method: $f(x) = \log x$, whose derivative $1/x$ cancels the factor $S$ in
both coefficients. With $f' = 1/x$, $f'' = -1/x^2$ and
$dS\,dS = \sigma^2 S^2\,dt$ by the table,
$$
d\log S = \frac{1}{S}\,dS - \frac12\cdot\frac{1}{S^2}\,dS\,dS
= \alpha\,dt + \sigma\,dW - \tfrac12\sigma^2\,dt
= \bigl(\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2\bigr)\,dt + \sigma\,dW .
$$
Both coefficients are now constants. Integrating from $0$ to $t$ gives
$\log S(t) = \log S_0 + (\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t + \sigma W(t)$, and
exponentiating,
$$
S(t) = S_0 \exp\Bigl\{\bigl(\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2\bigr)t + \sigma W(t)\Bigr\} .
$$
The use of $\log$ required $S > 0$; the solution confirms it, since an
exponential is positive on every path.

**The check in the other direction** (Shreve II, Example 4.4.8). Write
the solution as $S = f(X)$ with $f(x) = S_0 e^x$ and
$X(t) = (\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t + \sigma W(t)$, an Itô process with
$dX = (\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2)\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ and
$dX\,dX = \sigma^2\,dt$. Since $f' = f'' = f$,
$$
dS = S\,dX + \tfrac12 S\,dX\,dX
= S\bigl(\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2\bigr)\,dt + \sigma S\,dW + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S\,dt
= \alpha S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW ,
$$
which is the equation, with the correction cancelling on the way back.
The exponential is the solution, and by the fence it is the only one.

### What the solution says: mean, median, and the gap between them

$\log S(t)$ is normal with mean $\log S_0 + (\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t$
and variance $\sigma^2 t$: $S(t)$ is lognormal, the distribution <Ref
lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">the calibrated tree's limit
theorem</Ref> reached with $r$ in the place of $\alpha$. Its **median**
is $S_0 e^{(\alpha - \sigma^2/2)t}$, the exponential of the median of the
log. Its **mean** is $S_0 e^{\alpha t}$, because
$E\,e^{\sigma W(t)} = e^{\sigma^2 t/2}$ by the moment-generating function
and this cancels the $-\tfrac12\sigma^2 t$ exactly. The stock's mean
grows at the rate $\alpha$ the equation names; its median, and with it
every typical path, grows at $\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2$.

<Example title="a driftless stock at 40% volatility over ten years">
Take $\alpha = 0$, $\sigma = 0.4$, $S_0 = 1$, $t = 10$. Then
$\sigma\sqrt t = 1.265$ and $\tfrac12\sigma^2 t = 0.8$.

**Median and mean.** The median is $e^{-0.8} = 0.45$: the typical path has
lost more than half its value. The mean is $e^{0} = 1$ at every $t$: the
stock is a martingale.

**Halving time.** The median halves when $\tfrac12\sigma^2 t = \log 2$,
at $t = 0.693/0.08 = 8.7$ years.

**Who carries the mean.** $S(10) > 1$ requires $\sigma W(10) > 0.8$, that
is, a standard normal above $0.8/1.265 = 0.632$: probability $0.26$. The
contribution of those paths to the mean is
$E[S(10)\,\mathbf 1_{\{S(10) > 1\}}] = \Phi(d_1)$ with
$d_1 = (0 + \tfrac12\sigma^2 \cdot 10)/1.265 = 0.632$, so $\Phi(0.632) =
0.74$: one path in four ends above its start, and those paths hold three
quarters of the mean. Doubling, $S(10) > 2$, has probability
$1 - \Phi((0.693 + 0.8)/1.265) = 1 - \Phi(1.18) = 0.12$.
</Example>

<Remark title="the −½σ² three times">
The constant in the exponent has now been produced by three computations
that share no steps. In <Ref lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">the
scaled-walk lesson</Ref> it was the numerator of the risk-neutral coin's
tilt: expanding $e^Y = 1 + Y + \tfrac12 Y^2$ for a one-period log-return
whose square $Y^2 = \sigma^2/n$ is the same on both branches forced
$\tilde E[Y] = (r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)/n$. In <Ref
lesson="brownian-motion">the Brownian motion lesson</Ref> it made
$e^{\sigma W(t) - \sigma^2 t/2}$ a martingale by cancelling
$E\,e^{\sigma W(t)} = e^{\sigma^2 t/2}$. Here it is
$\tfrac12 f''\,dS\,dS$ with $f = \log$. All three are one fact: the
second-order term of a Taylor expansion evaluated on a path whose
squared increments add up to $\sigma^2 t$. On the tree the term appeared
with a plus sign inside $E[e^Y]$, because $\exp$ is convex and the mean
of an exponential exceeds the exponential of the mean; here it appears
with a minus sign inside $\log S$, because $\log$ is concave. The two are
the same gap seen from its two ends.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The gap between $\alpha$ and $\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2$ is the quadratic
variation of $\log S$ per unit time, $[\log S, \log S](t) = \sigma^2 t$,
and it belongs to the path, not to the drift: <Ref
lesson="girsanov-theorem">module 6's change of measure</Ref> replaces
$\alpha$ by $r$ and leaves the $-\tfrac12\sigma^2$ where it is, so the
risk-neutral stock $S_0\exp\{(r - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t + \sigma\tilde
W(t)\}$ has the same median-below-mean fan.
</KeyFact>

<Exercise>
Compute $E[S(t)]$ and $\mathrm{Var}\,S(t)$ from the equation
$dS = \alpha S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW$ without using the closed form: integrate
the equation and take expectations to get an ordinary differential
equation for $m(t) = E[S(t)]$, then apply the formula to $S^2$ and repeat.
Evaluate both at $S_0 = 100$, $\alpha = 0.08$, $\sigma = 0.2$, $t = 1$, and
check against the lognormal. Write the two differential equations before
opening the solution.

<Solution>
Strategy: an Itô integral has mean zero, so taking expectations of an
integrated equation removes the $dW$ term and leaves an equation in the
mean alone.

**The mean.** $S(t) = S_0 + \alpha\int_0^t S\,du + \sigma\int_0^t S\,dW$.
The Itô integral has mean zero (its integrand $\sigma S$ is
square-integrable because $S(t)$ is lognormal with all moments finite).
So $m(t) = S_0 + \alpha\int_0^t m(u)\,du$, that is $m' = \alpha m$,
$m(0) = S_0$: $E[S(t)] = S_0 e^{\alpha t}$. At the given values,
$100\,e^{0.08} = 108.33$.

**The second moment.** By the formula with $f(x) = x^2$,
$d(S^2) = 2S\,dS + dS\,dS = 2\alpha S^2\,dt + 2\sigma S^2\,dW +
\sigma^2 S^2\,dt = (2\alpha + \sigma^2)S^2\,dt + 2\sigma S^2\,dW$.
Integrate and take expectations: with $q(t) = E[S(t)^2]$,
$q' = (2\alpha + \sigma^2)q$, $q(0) = S_0^2$, so
$E[S(t)^2] = S_0^2 e^{(2\alpha + \sigma^2)t}$ and
$$
\mathrm{Var}\,S(t) = S_0^2 e^{2\alpha t}\bigl(e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1\bigr) .
$$
At the given values, $10{,}000 \times e^{0.16} \times (e^{0.04} - 1) =
10{,}000 \times 1.1735 \times 0.0408 = 479$, a standard deviation of
$21.9$ around a mean of $108.3$.

**Check.** From the closed form, $S(t)^2 = S_0^2 \exp\{(2\alpha -
\sigma^2)t + 2\sigma W(t)\}$ and $E\,e^{2\sigma W(t)} = e^{2\sigma^2 t}$,
so $E[S(t)^2] = S_0^2 e^{(2\alpha - \sigma^2 + 2\sigma^2)t}$, which
agrees. The transferable move: the formula plus "Itô integrals have mean
zero" turns any moment of a solution into an ordinary differential
equation, and the route works even where no closed form exists, which is
what the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck section uses it for.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

## The Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process: an integrating factor and a Gaussian answer

Geometric Brownian motion was solved by a change of variable that removed
the unknown from the diffusion coefficient. The second solvable equation
has a constant diffusion coefficient already, and a drift that pulls the
process toward a level; what the formula supplies here is an integrating
factor, the same device that solves the corresponding ordinary
differential equation.

### The equation and its deterministic core

The *Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process* (Uhlenbeck and Ornstein, 1930, as a
model of a particle's velocity under friction and molecular bombardment)
is the solution of
$$
dR(t) = \bigl(a - \beta R(t)\bigr)\,dt + \sigma\,dW(t), \qquad R(0) = r_0 ,
$$
with constants $a$, $\beta > 0$, $\sigma > 0$. The drift is negative when
$R$ is above $a/\beta$ and positive below it, so the process is pushed
back toward $a/\beta$ from either side at a rate proportional to its
distance. With $\sigma = 0$ the equation is the ordinary one
$r' = a - \beta r$, solved by the integrating factor $e^{\beta t}$:
$(e^{\beta t} r)' = a e^{\beta t}$, hence
$r(t) = a/\beta + (r_0 - a/\beta)e^{-\beta t}$, exponential relaxation
to the level $a/\beta$ with half-life $\log 2/\beta$. Vasicek (1977)
proposed the same equation for the short-term interest rate, which is
why Shreve presents it under that name; this course uses the process
and not the rate model, and the rates fence stays in place.

### The integrating factor, by the formula

Need: the same factor $e^{\beta t}$ applied to the stochastic equation.
Method: the formula with $f(t, x) = e^{\beta t}x$, for which
$f_t = \beta e^{\beta t}x$, $f_x = e^{\beta t}$ and $f_{xx} = 0$, so the
second-order term is absent and no $dR\,dR$ is needed:
$$
d\bigl(e^{\beta t}R\bigr) = \beta e^{\beta t}R\,dt + e^{\beta t}\,dR
= \beta e^{\beta t}R\,dt + e^{\beta t}(a - \beta R)\,dt + \sigma e^{\beta t}\,dW
= a e^{\beta t}\,dt + \sigma e^{\beta t}\,dW .
$$
The $\beta R$ terms cancel, which is what the factor was chosen to do.
Integrating from $0$ to $t$,
$e^{\beta t}R(t) - r_0 = \tfrac{a}{\beta}(e^{\beta t} - 1) +
\sigma\int_0^t e^{\beta u}\,dW(u)$, and multiplying by $e^{-\beta t}$,
$$
R(t) = e^{-\beta t} r_0 + \frac{a}{\beta}\bigl(1 - e^{-\beta t}\bigr)
+ \sigma e^{-\beta t}\int_0^t e^{\beta u}\,dW(u) .
$$
The first two terms are the deterministic solution; the third is an Itô
integral with a deterministic integrand, and the previous lesson's
boundary probe found that for simple deterministic integrands such an
integral is normal. The theorem that makes this official for every
deterministic integrand is the one result of this section proved rather
than computed.

<Theorem title="an Itô integral with deterministic integrand is Gaussian (Shreve II, 4.4.9)">
Let $\Delta(u)$ be a bounded nonrandom function of time and
$I(t) = \int_0^t \Delta(u)\,dW(u)$. Then $I(t)$ is normally distributed
with mean $0$ and variance $\int_0^t \Delta(u)^2\,du$.

<Proof>
The mean is zero and the variance is $\int_0^t \Delta^2\,du$ by the
martingale property and the isometry, with no expectation left to take
on the right side because $\Delta$ is not random. What has to be shown
is the distribution, and the route is the moment-generating function:
$E\,e^{uI(t)} = \exp\{\tfrac12 u^2 \int_0^t \Delta^2\,ds\}$ for every
real $u$ is the generating function of the claimed normal, and a
distribution is determined by its generating function (the fenced step of
<Ref lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">the scaled-walk lesson's
central limit theorem</Ref>).

Fix $u$ and set $Y(t) = \int_0^t u\Delta\,dW - \tfrac12\int_0^t
u^2\Delta^2\,ds$, an Itô process with $dY = u\Delta\,dW -
\tfrac12 u^2\Delta^2\,dt$ and $dY\,dY = u^2\Delta^2\,dt$. Apply the
formula to $Z = e^Y$:
$$
dZ = Z\,dY + \tfrac12 Z\,dY\,dY
= Z\bigl(u\Delta\,dW - \tfrac12 u^2\Delta^2\,dt\bigr) + \tfrac12 Z u^2\Delta^2\,dt
= u\Delta Z\,dW .
$$
So $Z(t) = 1 + \int_0^t u\Delta Z\,dW$, one plus an Itô integral, and
$E[Z(t)] = 1$ follows once that integral is a martingale. That it is one
is the proof's fenced step, and it is not free: the integrand
$u\Delta Z$ is square-integrable exactly when $Z(t)$ has a second
moment, which is a statement about the law of $I(t)$, the thing being
proved. Localisation gets most of the way. Stop at $\tau_K$, the first
time $|I|$ reaches $K$: up to $\tau_K$ the integrand is bounded by
$|u| \sup|\Delta|\, e^{|u|K}$, so the stopped integral is a martingale
by <Ref lesson="ito-integral">the previous lesson's theorem</Ref> and
$E[Z(t \wedge \tau_K)] = 1$ for every $K$; and $Z(t \wedge \tau_K) \to
Z(t)$ almost surely as $K \to \infty$, because a continuous path is
bounded on $[0, t]$. Passing the expectation through that limit is the
step that needs a named theorem: **Novikov's criterion** (Karatzas and
Shreve, Corollary 3.5.13) says that $\exp\{\int_0^t \Theta\,dW -
\tfrac12\int_0^t \Theta^2\,ds\}$ is a martingale whenever
$E\exp\{\tfrac12\int_0^t \Theta^2\,ds\} < \infty$, and for
$\Theta = u\Delta$ the quantity inside that expectation is a number.
So $E[Z(t)] = Z(0) = 1$, and since $\int_0^t u^2\Delta^2\,ds$ is a
number, this reads
$E\,e^{uI(t)} = \exp\{\tfrac12 u^2\int_0^t \Delta^2\,ds\}$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof used the deterministic integrand at two points: Novikov's
hypothesis is met because its exponent is a number, and
$\exp\{-\tfrac12\int u^2\Delta^2\}$ leaves the expectation as a
constant. For a random integrand the first can still hold, and then
$E[Z(t)] = 1$, but the second always fails: the identity no longer
delivers a generating function, and the distribution of the integral is
not normal in general; $\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 T$ is
the previous lesson's example of a non-normal one.

### Mean reversion read off the solution

By the theorem, $\int_0^t e^{\beta u}\,dW(u)$ is normal with mean zero
and variance $\int_0^t e^{2\beta u}\,du = (e^{2\beta t} - 1)/2\beta$, so
$R(t)$ is normal with
$$
E[R(t)] = \frac{a}{\beta} + \Bigl(r_0 - \frac{a}{\beta}\Bigr)e^{-\beta t},
\qquad
\mathrm{Var}\,R(t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\beta}\bigl(1 - e^{-2\beta t}\bigr) .
$$
The mean relaxes to $a/\beta$ at the deterministic rate, from any start.
The variance grows from zero and saturates at $\sigma^2/2\beta$: the
noise accumulates, but the pull discounts old noise at rate $\beta$, so
only the last few half-lives of it are still visible. For $\beta = 1$,
$a/\beta = 0.05$ and $\sigma = 0.02$, the half-life is $0.69$, the
stationary standard deviation is $0.02/\sqrt 2 = 0.014$, and a path
started at $0.10$ has mean $0.068$ after one unit of time and $0.057$
after two. The one-sentence reading as a rate model: $R(t)$ is Gaussian,
so it is negative with positive probability at every $t$, about
$\Phi(-0.05/0.014) = 0.0002$ in the stationary state at these values,
and that flaw is what the Cox–Ingersoll–Ross model below was built to
remove.

<Exercise>
Verify the closed form by differentiating it, in Shreve's direction:
write $R(t) = f(t, X(t))$ with
$f(t, x) = e^{-\beta t}r_0 + \tfrac{a}{\beta}(1 - e^{-\beta t}) +
\sigma e^{-\beta t}x$ and $X(t) = \int_0^t e^{\beta u}\,dW(u)$, compute
$f_t$, $f_x$, $f_{xx}$ and $dX$, and show that $df$ is the
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck differential. Then compute $\mathrm{Cov}(R(s), R(t))$
for $s < t$ and read off how fast the process forgets its past. Write
$f_t$ in terms of $f$ before opening the solution.

<Solution>
Strategy: separate the randomness into an Itô process $X$ with a
deterministic integrand, and put everything else into an ordinary
function of $(t, x)$.

**The derivatives.** $f_t = -\beta e^{-\beta t}r_0 + a e^{-\beta t} -
\beta\sigma e^{-\beta t}x = a - \beta f(t, x)$, since
$\beta f = \beta e^{-\beta t}r_0 + a(1 - e^{-\beta t}) + \beta\sigma
e^{-\beta t}x$; $f_x = \sigma e^{-\beta t}$; $f_{xx} = 0$. Also
$dX = e^{\beta t}\,dW$, and $dX\,dX$ is not needed because $f_{xx} = 0$.

**The differential.** $df = f_t\,dt + f_x\,dX = (a - \beta f)\,dt +
\sigma e^{-\beta t}e^{\beta t}\,dW = (a - \beta f)\,dt + \sigma\,dW$, and
$f(0, X(0)) = r_0$. So $f(t, X(t))$ solves the equation with the right
initial value; by the uniqueness fence it is $R(t)$.

**The covariance.** For $s < t$, the random parts are
$\sigma e^{-\beta s}\int_0^s e^{\beta u}\,dW$ and
$\sigma e^{-\beta t}\int_0^t e^{\beta u}\,dW$; the second integral splits
into the first plus an increment over $(s, t]$ independent of it with
mean zero, so
$$
\mathrm{Cov}(R(s), R(t)) = \sigma^2 e^{-\beta(s + t)}\int_0^s e^{2\beta u}\,du
= \frac{\sigma^2}{2\beta}\bigl(e^{-\beta(t - s)} - e^{-\beta(t + s)}\bigr) .
$$
In the stationary state ($s$ large) this is $\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2\beta}
e^{-\beta(t - s)}$: the correlation between values $\tau$ apart is
$e^{-\beta\tau}$, and the process forgets its past with the same
half-life $\log 2/\beta$ at which its mean relaxes. The transferable
move: a solution of the form "deterministic function of $t$ and of one
deterministic-integrand Itô integral" is Gaussian, and every question
about it is a variance computation.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Remark title="Cox–Ingersoll–Ross: the same factor yields moments and no solution (Shreve II, Example 4.4.11)">
Change the diffusion coefficient to $\sigma\sqrt{R}$:
$dR = (a - \beta R)\,dt + \sigma\sqrt{R}\,dW$. The same integrating
factor gives $d(e^{\beta t}R) = a e^{\beta t}\,dt + \sigma e^{\beta t}
\sqrt{R}\,dW$, and taking expectations, $E[R(t)]$ is identical to the
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck mean; applying the formula to $(e^{\beta t}R)^2$ and
taking expectations gives the variance, which tends to
$a\sigma^2/2\beta^2$. But the integrand $\sqrt{R}$ is random, the
Gaussian theorem does not apply, and no closed form for $R(t)$ exists:
its distribution is a scaled noncentral chi-square, obtained by other
means (Cox, Ingersoll and Ross, 1985). The process never goes negative,
since at $R = 0$ the noise switches off and the drift $a\,dt$ is
positive, which is the property that motivated it. The pattern is
general: the formula produces moments for almost any equation and closed
forms for very few, and the tool for an equation with no closed form is
simulation, the Euler scheme of <Ref lesson="quadratic-variation-lab">the
module's lab</Ref> and the Monte Carlo of <Ref
lesson="monte-carlo-pricing">module 6</Ref>.
</Remark>

## The product rule and the recipe every pricing argument runs

The hedging argument of the next module differentiates a portfolio, a
position times a price, and needs the rule for a product of two Itô
processes. The rule is the two-variable case of the formula, and its
extra term is a cross variation. Stated with it is the observation that
every pricing argument of modules 5 and 6 is one computation repeated.

### Cross variation and the product rule

For two Itô processes $X$ and $Y$ driven by the same Brownian motion,
$dX = \Theta_1\,dt + \Delta_1\,dW$ and $dY = \Theta_2\,dt + \Delta_2\,dW$,
the *cross variation* $[X, Y](T)$ is the limit of
$\sum_j \Delta X_j\,\Delta Y_j$, and it is defined by polarisation:
$ab = \tfrac14[(a + b)^2 - (a - b)^2]$ applied to each term gives
$[X, Y] = \tfrac14\bigl([X + Y, X + Y] - [X - Y, X - Y]\bigr)$, which
exists because $X \pm Y$ are Itô processes with diffusion coefficients
$\Delta_1 \pm \Delta_2$. By the lemma,
$$
[X, Y](T) = \tfrac14\int_0^T \bigl[(\Delta_1 + \Delta_2)^2 - (\Delta_1 - \Delta_2)^2\bigr]\,du
= \int_0^T \Delta_1\Delta_2\,du ,
\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad dX\,dY = \Delta_1\Delta_2\,dt ,
$$
the entry the multiplication table gives by multiplying the two
differentials and keeping $dW\,dW$.

<Theorem title="two-variable Itô–Doeblin formula and the product rule (Shreve II, 4.6.2 and 4.6.3)">
Let $f(t, x, y)$ have continuous first partial derivatives and continuous
second partial derivatives in $x$ and $y$, and let $X$, $Y$ be Itô
processes. Then
$$
df(t, X, Y) = f_t\,dt + f_x\,dX + f_y\,dY
+ \tfrac12 f_{xx}\,dX\,dX + f_{xy}\,dX\,dY + \tfrac12 f_{yy}\,dY\,dY .
$$
In particular, with $f(t, x, y) = xy$,
$$
d\bigl(X(t)Y(t)\bigr) = X\,dY + Y\,dX + dX\,dY .
$$

<Proof>
The two-variable formula is the one-variable proof run with a
second-order Taylor expansion in three arguments: first order in $t$,
second order in $x$ and $y$, with the mixed term $f_{xy}\,\Delta X_j
\Delta Y_j$ converging to $\int f_{xy}\,d[X, Y]$ by the same argument
that handled $(\Delta X_j)^2$; Shreve states it and omits the
repetition, and so does this course. The product rule is the case
$f = xy$: $f_t = 0$, $f_x = y$, $f_y = x$, $f_{xx} = f_{yy} = 0$,
$f_{xy} = 1$. The derivation exercise at the end of the lesson obtains
the product rule from the one-variable formula alone, by polarisation,
without the two-variable theorem.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The extra term $dX\,dY = \Delta_1\Delta_2\,dt$ is the difference from the
classical rule, and it vanishes when either factor has zero quadratic
variation: for $Y(t) = e^{-rt}$, a smooth function of time, $d(e^{-rt}X)
= e^{-rt}\,dX - re^{-rt}X\,dt$ with no correction, which is why
discounting never produces one. When the two processes are driven by
independent Brownian motions $W_1$, $W_2$ the rule needs
$dW_1\,dW_2 = 0$, which holds because the sampled cross variation
$\sum_j \Delta W_{1,j}\Delta W_{2,j}$ has mean zero and variance
$\sum_j \Delta t_j^2 \le \|\Pi\|T \to 0$ (Shreve II, §4.6.1).

One further consequence of the formula is named here and used in module
6. **Lévy's theorem** (Shreve II, Theorem 4.6.4): a continuous martingale
$M$ with $M(0) = 0$ and $[M, M](t) = t$ is a Brownian motion. Its proof
applies the formula to $e^{uM(t) - u^2 t/2}$, whose $dt$ term vanishes
exactly because $dM\,dM = dt$, and reads off the normal generating
function; normality is a consequence of the quadratic variation, not an
extra hypothesis. <Ref lesson="girsanov-theorem">Girsanov's theorem</Ref>
uses it to recognise the drift-shifted process as a Brownian motion
under the new measure.

### The discounted stock, and the recipe

The computation the next two modules repeat is this one. Discount the
stock at the riskless rate $r$: with $f(t, x) = e^{-rt}x$, $f_t = -rf$,
$f_x = e^{-rt}$, $f_{xx} = 0$,
$$
d\bigl(e^{-rt}S(t)\bigr) = -re^{-rt}S\,dt + e^{-rt}\bigl(\alpha S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW\bigr)
= e^{-rt}(\alpha - r)S\,dt + e^{-rt}\sigma S\,dW .
$$
The $dW$ term is an Itô integral and contributes no drift. The $dt$ term
is $(\alpha - r)$ times a positive quantity, and it vanishes if and only
if $\alpha = r$: the discounted stock is a martingale exactly when its
mean rate of return is the riskless rate, which is the continuous form
of <Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">module 2's
theorem</Ref> that the discounted stock is a martingale exactly under
the coin $\tilde p = (1 + r - d)/(u - d)$.

  caption="Three candidates, one test: the dt term of each by the formula, and what setting it to zero forces; the dW term never enters the test."

The second and third columns are <Ref lesson="black-scholes-pde">module
5</Ref> and <Ref lesson="girsanov-theorem">module 6</Ref> run ahead of
themselves, and their $dt$ terms are computed by the product rule just
proved, nothing more. The test is the same in every column because an
Itô integral with a square-integrable integrand is a martingale and a
$dt$ term is not.

<KeyFact>
The discounted stock $e^{-rt}S(t)$ is a martingale exactly when
$\alpha = r$. Under the actual measure $\alpha \ne r$ and it drifts at
rate $(\alpha - r)$ times itself; making it a martingale is the job of a
change of measure, and $\alpha - r$ is the quantity that measure has to
remove. Divided by $\sigma$, it is the $\theta = (\alpha - r)/\sigma$ of
the third column, the one constant
<Ref lesson="girsanov-theorem">Girsanov's theorem</Ref> needs.
</KeyFact>

What is established: across one cell, $f(W)$ changes by
$f'\,\Delta W + \tfrac12 f''(\Delta W)^2$ up to terms of order
$\Delta t^{3/2}$, and summed over $T/\Delta t$ cells the first term
becomes the Itô integral, the second becomes $\tfrac12\int f''\,dt$ by the
quadratic variation theorem, and everything else vanishes; the resulting
formula $df = f_t\,dt + f_x\,dX + \tfrac12 f_{xx}\,dX\,dX$ holds for every
Itô process with $dX\,dX = \Delta^2\,dt$, the drift never entering the
second-order term; geometric Brownian motion is
$S_0\exp\{(\alpha - \tfrac12\sigma^2)t + \sigma W(t)\}$, with the
$-\tfrac12\sigma^2$ produced by $\tfrac12 f''\,dS\,dS$ and agreeing with
the two earlier routes to the same constant; the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck
process is Gaussian and mean-reverting in closed form because its
integrating factor leaves an Itô integral with a deterministic
integrand; the product rule carries the cross-variation term
$\Delta_1\Delta_2\,dt$; and the discounted stock is a martingale exactly
when $\alpha = r$. What is not established is what the formula does when
its hypothesis fails. Every $f$ above had a continuous second derivative;
a call's payoff $(x - K)^+$ has a kink at $K$ and no second derivative
there, and a hedge built by ordinary calculus around that kink replicates
the call at no cost on paper. <Ref lesson="stop-loss-paradox">The next
lesson</Ref> reproduces Hull's simulation of that hedge, whose cost
refuses to converge to the option's value however often it trades, and
identifies the missing term as the local time the formula cannot see.

## ★ The remainder sums, controlled

The proof of the theorem replaced $(\Delta W_j)^2$ by $\Delta t_j$ inside a
weighted sum and cited a mean-square bound. This section carries the
bound out, for the case in which the weights are bounded, and states
what a full proof adds beyond it. Nothing later in the lesson depends on
it.

### The weighted difference converges in mean square

Let $g_j = \tfrac12 f_{xx}(t_j, W(t_j))$, a random variable determined by
$\mathcal F(t_j)$ and bounded by $\tfrac12 M$, and let
$D_j = (\Delta W_j)^2 - \Delta t_j$. Then $D_j$ is a function of an
increment independent of $\mathcal F(t_j)$, with $E[D_j] = 0$ and
$E[D_j^2] = \mathrm{Var}(\Delta W_j^2) = 2\Delta t_j^2$, the variance
computed in the quadratic variation proof. The quantity to bound is
$E\bigl[(\sum_j g_j D_j)^2\bigr]$, and its expansion is the isometry
argument of the previous lesson with $D_j$ in the role of the increment.

**Cross terms.** For $i < j$, $g_i D_i g_j$ is determined by
$\mathcal F(t_j)$ and $D_j$ is independent of $\mathcal F(t_j)$ with mean
zero, so $E[g_i D_i g_j D_j] = E[g_i D_i g_j]\,E[D_j] = 0$.

**Square terms.** $E[g_j^2 D_j^2] = E[g_j^2]\,E[D_j^2] \le
\tfrac14 M^2 \cdot 2\Delta t_j^2$.

**The sum.** $E\bigl[(\sum_j g_j D_j)^2\bigr] \le \tfrac12 M^2 \sum_j
\Delta t_j^2 \le \tfrac12 M^2 \|\Pi\| T \to 0$. So
$\sum_j g_j (\Delta W_j)^2$ and $\sum_j g_j \Delta t_j$ have the same
mean-square limit, and the second is a Riemann sum of the continuous
function $\tfrac12 f_{xx}(t, W(t))$, converging on every path to
$\tfrac12\int_0^T f_{xx}\,dt$.

This is the entire content of "replace $(dW)^2$ by $dt$": not that any
squared increment is near its cell, which <Ref
lesson="brownian-motion">the Brownian motion lesson</Ref> showed is
false at every mesh, but that the weighted deviations $g_j D_j$ are
uncorrelated with mean zero and variances summing to at most the mesh
times a constant.

### What a full proof adds

Three things. First, the bound assumed $f_{xx}$ bounded; the theorem
asks only for continuity, and the gap is closed by stopping the path at
the first time $|W|$ reaches $K$, proving the identity up to that time
where all derivatives are bounded on $[-K, K]$, and letting $K \to
\infty$, using that the path is bounded on $[0, T]$ so the stopping
time eventually exceeds $T$. Second, the same localisation handles the
cubic remainder for a function with only two derivatives, through the
uniform continuity of $f_{xx}$ on $[-K, K]$: the remainder is
$\tfrac12[f_{xx}(\xi_j) - f_{xx}(W(t_j))](\Delta W_j)^2$ with the bracket
uniformly small, and $\sum_j (\Delta W_j)^2$ is bounded. Third, the
convergence of the first-order sum to the Itô integral is the previous
lesson's fenced extension theorem and is not reproved. With these, the
statement holds for every $f$ with continuous $f_t$, $f_x$, $f_{xx}$, which
is the form in which Karatzas and Shreve (Theorem 3.3.3) and Øksendal
(Theorem 4.1.2) prove it.

Prove the product rule $d(XY) = X\,dY + Y\,dX + dX\,dY$ from the
one-variable formula alone, without the two-variable theorem: apply the
formula with $f(x) = x^2$ to each of $X + Y$, $X$ and $Y$, and use

$d(W(t)^2)$, for $d(tW(t))$, and for $d(W_1(t)W_2(t))$ with $W_1$, $W_2$
independent Brownian motions.

<Solution>
Strategy: the formula for $f(x) = x^2$ applied to any Itô process $V$
gives $d(V^2) = 2V\,dV + dV\,dV$; polarise.

For $V = X + Y$, $dV = dX + dY$ and by the table
$dV\,dV = dX\,dX + 2\,dX\,dY + dY\,dY$. So
$$
d\bigl((X + Y)^2\bigr) = 2(X + Y)(dX + dY) + dX\,dX + 2\,dX\,dY + dY\,dY .
$$
Subtract $d(X^2) = 2X\,dX + dX\,dX$ and $d(Y^2) = 2Y\,dY + dY\,dY$ and
halve:
$$
d(XY) = \tfrac12\bigl[2X\,dY + 2Y\,dX + 2\,dX\,dY\bigr] = X\,dY + Y\,dX + dX\,dY .
$$
The classical terms come from the first-order parts and the correction
from the second-order parts, and the polarisation identity is the same
one that defined cross variation.

**Instances.** $d(W^2) = 2W\,dW + dt$, the first audit. $d(tW) = t\,dW +
W\,dt + dt\,dW = t\,dW + W\,dt$: no correction, because $t$ has zero
quadratic variation, and integrating gives $\int_0^T t\,dW = TW(T) -
\int_0^T W\,dt$, an integration by parts that holds with no Itô term.
$d(W_1W_2) = W_1\,dW_2 + W_2\,dW_1 + dW_1\,dW_2 = W_1\,dW_2 +
W_2\,dW_1$, since $dW_1\,dW_2 = 0$ for independent Brownian motions; so
$W_1W_2$ is a martingale, and $E[W_1(t)W_2(t)] = 0$. The transferable
move: a correction appears in a product only when both factors carry
quadratic variation from the same source.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Break this claim. "For the driftless stock $dS = \sigma S\,dW$, the mean
$E[S(t)] = S_0$ is constant, so $S(t)$ neither grows nor decays and a
typical path stays near $S_0$." Give the correct description of a typical
path, name the term responsible, and identify the step at which the
claim goes wrong. State what $S(t)$ does as $t \to \infty$.

<Solution>
The correct description: with $\alpha = 0$ the solution is
$S(t) = S_0\exp\{-\tfrac12\sigma^2 t + \sigma W(t)\}$, so $\log S(t)$ is
normal with mean $\log S_0 - \tfrac12\sigma^2 t$ and standard deviation
$\sigma\sqrt t$. The median path is $S_0 e^{-\sigma^2 t/2}$, decaying
without bound; the probability of being above $S_0$ at time $t$ is
$1 - \Phi(\tfrac12\sigma\sqrt t)$, which falls to zero. The term
responsible is $\tfrac12 f''\,dS\,dS = -\tfrac12\sigma^2\,dt$ in
$d\log S$: the mean of $S$ is held constant by the convexity of $\exp$,
while the mean of $\log S$, which is what a typical path follows, falls
at rate $\tfrac12\sigma^2$.

The step that fails is "constant mean, therefore a typical path stays
near the mean". For a symmetric distribution the mean and the median
coincide; $S(t)$ is lognormal and right-skewed, its mean lies above its
median, and as $t$ grows the mean is carried by an ever-smaller fraction
of ever-larger paths, as the ten-year example computed: one path in four
above the start, holding three quarters of the mean. The claim would
have been legitimate if the distribution of $S(t)$ stayed concentrated
around its mean, and the growth of $\mathrm{Var}\,S(t) =
S_0^2(e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1)$ says it does not.

As $t \to \infty$: the exponent is $t\bigl(-\tfrac12\sigma^2 + \sigma
W(t)/t\bigr)$, and $W(t)/t \to 0$ almost surely (the strong law of large
numbers for Brownian increments, a fenced fact), so the exponent tends to
$-\infty$ and $S(t) \to 0$ on almost every path, while $E[S(t)] = S_0$
for every $t$. A martingale can converge to zero almost surely while its
mean stays put; the mean is then held up by paths of vanishing
probability. The transferable move: in a lognormal world, read the
median for what happens and the mean for what is priced, and never
substitute one for the other.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

**The option's expansion.** With $dS\,dS = \sigma^2 S^2\,dt$,
$$
dc(t, S) = c_t\,dt + c_x\,dS + \tfrac12 c_{xx}\,dS\,dS
= \underbrace{\bigl(c_t + \alpha S c_x + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2 c_{xx}\bigr)}_{dt\text{ coefficient}}\,dt
+ \underbrace{\sigma S c_x}_{dW\text{ coefficient}}\,dW .
$$
**The portfolio's expansion.** Substituting $dS$,
$$
dX = \Delta(\alpha S\,dt + \sigma S\,dW) + r(X - \Delta S)\,dt
= \underbrace{\bigl(rX + \Delta(\alpha - r)S\bigr)}_{dt\text{ coefficient}}\,dt
+ \underbrace{\Delta\sigma S}_{dW\text{ coefficient}}\,dW ,
$$
the continuous form of the tree's wealth equation
$X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)$.

**Side by side.** The two $dW$ coefficients are $\sigma S c_x$ and
$\Delta\sigma S$; the two $dt$ coefficients are
$c_t + \alpha S c_x + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2 c_{xx}$ and
$rX + \Delta(\alpha - r)S$. Nothing has been equated. <Ref
lesson="black-scholes-pde">The next module's first lesson</Ref> demands
that the portfolio replicate the option, $X(t) = c(t, S(t))$ for all $t$,
so that the two differentials agree term by term: the $dW$ line forces
$\Delta = c_x$, and with that substituted the $\alpha S c_x$ terms cancel
between the $dt$ lines, leaving $c_t + rSc_x + \tfrac12\sigma^2 S^2 c_{xx}
= rc$, an equation in which $\alpha$ no longer appears. The two collected
lines above are the exact raw material of that derivation; the
cancellation of $\alpha$ is visible in them already, since it enters
both $dt$ coefficients through the same product $\alpha S c_x$.
</Solution>

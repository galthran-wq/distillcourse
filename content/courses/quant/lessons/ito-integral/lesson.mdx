The gain of a trading strategy that holds $\Delta(t)$ shares is
$\int \Delta\,dW$ — an integral against a path of infinite length, which
nonetheless exists because the integrand is forbidden to peek and the
increments square-sum to $dt$.

This is the seventeenth move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the third move of its fourth module. <Ref
lesson="brownian-motion">The previous lesson</Ref> defined Brownian motion,
proved it a martingale, and proved that its paths have infinite first-order
variation and quadratic variation $[W, W](T) = T$; its last exercise found
that the chain rule's value for $\int_0^T W\,dW$ is off by a term whose
expectation is $T$. What stands open is the integral itself: on the tree a
hedge's gain was a finite sum $\sum \Delta_n (S_{n+1} - S_n)$, and the
continuous hedge of the next module needs the same sum against $W$, whose
path is too rough for any integral of ordinary calculus. This lesson
constructs that integral for strategies that trade at finitely many dates,
proves for them the three properties the rest of the course uses, states
how the construction extends and which facts it takes on credit, and
computes $\int_0^T W\,dW$ from the definition. What it hands on is a
correction term that has to be found by hand for every integrand, which
<Ref lesson="ito-formula">the next lesson</Ref> mass-produces with a chain
rule.

The route: the failed audit that fixes where the integrand is evaluated;
simple integrands as trading strategies and the integral as their gain; the
martingale property, the Itô isometry and the quadratic variation of the
integral, proved; the peeking integrand that mints money; the extension to
general integrands with its fence; and $\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 -
\tfrac12 T$ from the partition sums.

## The chain rule's answer for $\int W\,dW$ fails an expectation audit

The previous lesson closed by exhibiting the failure; this section reads
the failure as an instruction about what the definition of the integral
must contain.

### Retrieval: what the previous lesson hands over

<Task>

for Brownian motion, the one every martingale proof uses. (2) Name the two
properties of conditional expectation that make $E[\Delta(s)\,(W(t) -
W(s)) \mid \mathcal F(s)] = 0$ when $\Delta(s)$ is determined by
$\mathcal F(s)$. (3) State what $Q_\Pi = \sum_j (W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j))^2$
converges to, in what sense, and why the Riemann–Stieltjes integral against
$W$ does not exist. Then check against the fold.
</Task>

<Aside>
Answers. (1) Every increment $W(u) - W(t)$ with $u > t$ is independent of
$\mathcal F(t)$ (<Ref lesson="brownian-motion">the third clause of the
filtration definition in the previous lesson</Ref>; Shreve II, Definition
3.3.3). (2) Taking out what is known removes $\Delta(s)$
from the conditional expectation; independence turns the increment's
conditional expectation into its plain expectation, which is zero (<Ref
lesson="coin-toss-probability">taking out what is known, and
independence</Ref>; Shreve I, Theorem 2.3.2 (ii) and (iv)). (3)
$Q_\Pi \to T$ in mean square as the mesh goes to zero, with $E[Q_\Pi] = T$
on every partition; a Riemann–Stieltjes integral needs an integrator of
finite length, and a Brownian path has infinite first-order variation.
</Aside>

### The audit, in four lines

If $W$ were a differentiable function, the chain rule would give
$d(W^2) = 2W\,dW$, hence $\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12 W(T)^2$. Any definition
of the left side as a limit of sums that hold a position over each cell
uses the sums
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} W(t_j)\,\bigl(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr),
$$
and every term of every such sum has expectation zero: $W(t_j)$ is
determined by $\mathcal F(t_j)$, the increment that follows is independent
of $\mathcal F(t_j)$ with mean zero, so $E[W(t_j)(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j))] =
E[W(t_j)]\,E[W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)] = 0$. The candidate answer has
$E[\tfrac12 W(T)^2] = \tfrac12 T$. A limit of mean-zero random variables
cannot have mean $\tfrac12 T$, so whatever the left-point sums converge to,
it is not $\tfrac12 W(T)^2$. The fault is located before any integral has
been defined: **the chain rule is wrong for $W$, not the sums**.

### The evaluation point is part of the definition

For a smooth integrator the position in the cell at which the integrand is
read does not matter: left-point and right-point Riemann–Stieltjes sums
converge to the same number. For $W$ the two sums differ, on every
partition and exactly, by
$$
\sum_j W(t_{j+1})\,\Delta W_j - \sum_j W(t_j)\,\Delta W_j
= \sum_j (\Delta W_j)^2 = Q_\Pi ,
\qquad \Delta W_j = W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j),
$$
and $Q_\Pi \to T$, not $0$. The two sums cannot share a limit; the choice
of evaluation point is therefore part of what "$\int W\,dW$" means, and
the previous lesson's theorem is the reason.

Finance decides the evaluation point. A position over a holding period is
chosen when the period starts, from what is known then, and held until the
period ends; a trader cannot set at 1:00 p.m. the position held since
9:00 a.m. The gain from holding $\Delta(t_j)$ shares over $[t_j, t_{j+1}]$
is $\Delta(t_j)$ times the price change over the cell, with $\Delta$ read
at the **left endpoint**. That is the only sum with a trading meaning, and
it is the sum whose limit the rest of this lesson defines.

<KeyFact>
Integration against $W$ has no evaluation-point-free definition: left and
right sums differ by $Q_\Pi \to T$ on every path. The Itô integral reads
the integrand at the left endpoint because that is where a position is
decided; every martingale property that follows is a consequence of this
choice, and the starred section shows what a different choice costs.
</KeyFact>

## Simple integrands are trading strategies, and the integral is their gain

The definition follows the finance reading exactly: fix trading dates,
choose a position at each date from the information then available, hold
it to the next date, and add up the gains. The integral of such a strategy
is a finite sum, and every proof in the next section is a computation on
that sum.

### The running strategy

Regard $W(t)$ as the price of an asset, ignoring for the illustration that
a price cannot go negative, and trade it at $t = 0, \tfrac14, \tfrac12,
\tfrac34$ by the rule: hold $2$ shares over $[0, \tfrac14)$; at
$t = \tfrac14$ hold $1$ share if $W(\tfrac14) \ge 0$ and $-1$ otherwise;
at $t = \tfrac12$ hold $3$ shares if the price rose over the second cell,
$W(\tfrac12) > W(\tfrac14)$, and $1$ share otherwise; at $t = \tfrac34$
hold nothing. Each position is fixed by prices already seen when it is
taken. Along the path with $W = 0, 0.6, 0.2, 1.0, 0.5$ at the five dates
the positions are $2, 1, 1, 0$ and the gain is
$$
2\,(0.6 - 0) + 1\,(0.2 - 0.6) + 1\,(1.0 - 0.2) + 0\,(0.5 - 1.0)
= 1.2 - 0.4 + 0.8 + 0 = 1.6 .
$$
The gain at an intermediate time is the same sum stopped early: at
$t = \tfrac38$ it is $1.2 + 1\,(W(\tfrac38) - 0.6)$, the completed first
cell plus the open second one.

### The definition

<Definition title="simple process and its Itô integral (Shreve II, §4.2.1)">
Let $W$ be a Brownian motion with filtration $\mathcal F(t)$ and let
$\Pi = \{t_0, t_1, \ldots, t_n\}$, $0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_n = T$, be a
partition of $[0, T]$. A **simple process** is an adapted process
$\Delta(t)$ that is constant on each cell $[t_j, t_{j+1})$: its value on
the cell is the random variable $\Delta(t_j)$, determined by
$\mathcal F(t_j)$. The **Itô integral** of $\Delta$ is the process
$$
I(t) = \int_0^t \Delta(u)\,dW(u)
= \sum_{j=0}^{k-1} \Delta(t_j)\bigl[W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr]
+ \Delta(t_k)\bigl[W(t) - W(t_k)\bigr]
\qquad \text{for } t_k \le t \le t_{k+1} .
$$
</Definition>

Two remarks on the clauses. $\Delta(t_0)$ is determined by
$\mathcal F(0)$, which contains no information, so the first position is a
constant, the same on every path; from $t_1$ on, positions may depend on
the path. And the value on a cell is set at the cell's left end and held
up to but not including the right end: the process is right-open on each
cell.

Four cases fix what the definition covers.

- **The canonical case.** The running strategy: positions $2$, then
  $\pm 1$ by the sign of $W(\tfrac14)$, then $3$ or $1$ by the second
  cell's direction, then $0$.
- **A case with a different surface.** $\Delta(t_j) = W(t_j)$: hold as
  many shares as the current price. Each position is a random variable
  determined at its own left endpoint, so the process is simple and
  adapted; its integral is the left-point sum of the audit, and the last
  section of this lesson computes its limit.
- **The degenerate case.** A constant position $\Delta \equiv c$ on a
  single cell $[0, T]$: $I(t) = c\,W(t)$. The integral of a constant is
  the constant times the integrator, and with $c = 1$ the Itô integral of
  $1$ is $W$ itself.
- **The near miss.** $\Delta(t_j) = \operatorname{sign}\bigl(W(t_{j+1}) -
  W(t_j)\bigr)$: constant on each cell, but determined by
  $\mathcal F(t_{j+1})$ and not by $\mathcal F(t_j)$. Every clause holds
  except adaptedness, and the fourth section computes what that one
  clause is worth.

</Task>

<Aside>

gain from holding the historic high as the position. The definition's
two clauses are the whole test: adapted, and constant on each cell. No
clause restricts how many values a position can take or how it depends
on the past.
</Aside>

<Remark title="the differential form (Shreve II, Remark 4.2.4)">
The integral $I(t) = \int_0^t \Delta\,dW$ is also written
$dI(t) = \Delta(t)\,dW(t)$. The differential form has no meaning of its
own: it abbreviates $I(t) = I(0) + \int_0^t \Delta(u)\,dW(u)$, the integral
form with an arbitrary starting value, and the two say the same thing. The
one thing the differential form drops is the initial condition $I(0) = 0$
that the definition fixes.
</Remark>

## Three theorems for simple integrands: martingale, isometry, quadratic variation

The integral is defined as the gain from trading in a martingale, and a
martingale has no tendency to rise or fall, so the gain should have none
either. The three theorems below make that exact, and each is a
computation on the finite sum of the definition using only the five
properties of conditional expectation carried over from the tree.

### The martingale property: adaptedness lets $\Delta$ out, independence kills the increment

<Theorem title="the Itô integral of a simple process is a martingale (Shreve II, 4.2.1)">
For a simple process $\Delta$ with $E|\Delta(t_j)| < \infty$ for each $j$,
the process $I(t) = \int_0^t \Delta\,dW$ satisfies
$E[I(t) \mid \mathcal F(s)] = I(s)$ for $0 \le s \le t \le T$.

<Proof>
Goal: $E[I(t) \mid \mathcal F(s)] = I(s)$. Mode: split the sum defining
$I(t)$ into the part completed by time $s$ and the part after, and
condition term by term. Roadmap: four groups of terms; the first two add
to $I(s)$, the last two have conditional expectation zero.

Suppose $s$ and $t$ lie in different cells, $s \in [t_\ell, t_{\ell+1})$
and $t \in [t_k, t_{k+1})$ with $\ell < k$; if they share a cell the same
argument runs with fewer groups. Then
$$
I(t) = \underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{\ell-1} \Delta(t_j)\,D_j}_{\text{(A)}}
+ \underbrace{\Delta(t_\ell)\bigl[W(t_{\ell+1}) - W(t_\ell)\bigr]}_{\text{(B)}}
+ \underbrace{\sum_{j=\ell+1}^{k-1} \Delta(t_j)\,D_j}_{\text{(C)}}
+ \underbrace{\Delta(t_k)\bigl[W(t) - W(t_k)\bigr]}_{\text{(D)}},
$$
with $D_j = W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)$.

**(A), completed before $s$.** Every factor is determined by
$\mathcal F(t_\ell) \subseteq \mathcal F(s)$, so the whole sum comes out
of the conditional expectation unchanged.

**(B), the cell containing $s$.** $\Delta(t_\ell)$ is determined by
$\mathcal F(s)$ and comes out; the increment splits as
$W(t_{\ell+1}) - W(t_\ell) = [W(t_{\ell+1}) - W(s)] + [W(s) - W(t_\ell)]$,
the first bracket has conditional expectation zero and the second is
known. So $E[\text{(B)} \mid \mathcal F(s)] = \Delta(t_\ell)\,[W(s) -
W(t_\ell)]$. Adding (A) gives exactly $I(s)$.

**(C), whole cells after $s$.** For each $j \ge \ell + 1$, condition first
on $\mathcal F(t_j)$ and then on $\mathcal F(s)$, which is the tower
property, $\mathcal F(s) \subseteq \mathcal F(t_j)$:
$$
E\bigl[\Delta(t_j)\,D_j \bigm| \mathcal F(s)\bigr]
= E\Bigl[\,E\bigl[\Delta(t_j)\,D_j \bigm| \mathcal F(t_j)\bigr] \Bigm| \mathcal F(s)\Bigr]
= E\Bigl[\,\Delta(t_j)\,E\bigl[D_j \bigm| \mathcal F(t_j)\bigr] \Bigm| \mathcal F(s)\Bigr]
= E\bigl[\,\Delta(t_j) \cdot 0 \bigm| \mathcal F(s)\bigr] = 0 .
$$
The second equality is the step that uses adaptedness: $\Delta(t_j)$ is
determined by $\mathcal F(t_j)$ and comes out. The third is independence
of $D_j$ from $\mathcal F(t_j)$ followed by $E[D_j] = 0$.

**(D), the open cell containing $t$.** Identical to one term of (C) with
$D_j$ replaced by $W(t) - W(t_k)$, which is likewise independent of
$\mathcal F(t_k)$ with mean zero.

Adding the four results, $E[I(t) \mid \mathcal F(s)] = I(s)$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Figure
  src="./figures/martingale-step.svg"
  alt="The equation E of delta at t j times the increment given F at t j equals delta at t j times E of the increment equals zero, with the factor delta labelled taking out what is known because the integrand is adapted, and the increment labelled independent of F at t j with zero mean; below, the peeking case where delta is the sign of the coming increment, cannot come out, and the product has positive conditional mean"
  caption="The one step every proof in this lesson turns on: adaptedness lets Δ(tⱼ) out, independence plus zero mean kills the increment. These are the same two properties module 2 proved on the tree, now carrying the continuous theory."

Every term of the gain after time $s$ is a known position times an
increment the position could not see, and such a product averages to
zero. The step that would fail for
a non-adapted integrand is the second equality in (C): if $\Delta(t_j)$
were determined only by $\mathcal F(t_{j+1})$, it could not be taken out
of the conditional expectation given $\mathcal F(t_j)$, and nothing
forces the product's conditional mean to zero.

Because $I(0) = 0$, the martingale property gives $E[I(t)] = 0$ for every
$t$, so $\mathrm{Var}\,I(t) = E[I(t)^2]$. The next theorem evaluates it.

### The Itô isometry: variance is the expected area under $\Delta^2$

<Theorem title="Itô isometry for simple processes (Shreve II, 4.2.2)">
For a simple process $\Delta$ with $E[\Delta(t_j)^2] < \infty$ for each $j$,
$$
E\bigl[I(t)^2\bigr] = E\int_0^t \Delta(u)^2\,du .
$$

<Proof>
Goal: compute $E[I(t)^2]$. Mode: expand the square of the finite sum;
cross terms vanish by conditioning, square terms give $\Delta^2$ times a
cell length. Write $D_j = W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)$ for $j < k$ and
$D_k = W(t) - W(t_k)$, so that $I(t) = \sum_{j=0}^k \Delta(t_j) D_j$ and
$$
I(t)^2 = \sum_{j=0}^k \Delta(t_j)^2 D_j^2
+ 2\sum_{0 \le i < j \le k} \Delta(t_i)\,\Delta(t_j)\,D_i\,D_j .
$$

**Cross terms.** For $i < j$ the product $\Delta(t_i)\Delta(t_j)D_i$ is
determined by $\mathcal F(t_j)$, since every factor is fixed by time
$t_j$, while $D_j$ is independent of $\mathcal F(t_j)$ with mean zero.
Hence $E[\Delta(t_i)\Delta(t_j)D_iD_j] = E[\Delta(t_i)\Delta(t_j)D_i]
\cdot E[D_j] = 0$.

**Square terms.** $\Delta(t_j)^2$ is determined by $\mathcal F(t_j)$ and
$D_j^2$ is independent of it with $E[D_j^2] = t_{j+1} - t_j$ (or
$t - t_k$ for the last term), so
$E[\Delta(t_j)^2 D_j^2] = E[\Delta(t_j)^2]\,(t_{j+1} - t_j)$.

**Assembling.** Since $\Delta$ is constant on each cell,
$\Delta(t_j)^2\,(t_{j+1} - t_j) = \int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}} \Delta(u)^2\,du$,
and summing the cells up to $t$ gives $E[I(t)^2] = \sum_j E\int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}}
\Delta^2\,du = E\int_0^t \Delta(u)^2\,du$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof repeats the previous lesson's quadratic variation computation
with the position carried along: the squared increments contribute their
cells, the cross products contribute nothing, and what was $E[Q_\Pi] = T$ for $\Delta
\equiv 1$ becomes $E\int \Delta^2$ for a strategy. The hypothesis
$E[\Delta(t_j)^2] < \infty$ is where square-integrability enters the
theory, and the extension section returns to it.

<Example title="the variance of the running strategy's gain">
The positions squared are $4$ on the first cell and $1$ on the second,
whatever the path. On the third cell the position is $3$ or $1$ according
to whether $W(\tfrac12) > W(\tfrac14)$, an event of probability
$\tfrac12$ since the increment is symmetric, so $E[\Delta(\tfrac12)^2]
= \tfrac12 \cdot 9 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = 5$. The last cell holds nothing.
By the isometry,
$$
\mathrm{Var}\,I(1) = E\int_0^1 \Delta^2\,du
= \tfrac14\,(4 + 1 + 5 + 0) = 2.5 ,
$$
so $I(1)$ has standard deviation $\sqrt{2.5} = 1.58$, and the observed
gain $1.6$ on the running path is a one-standard-deviation outcome. The
variance grows at rate $4$ per unit time on the first cell, $1$ on the
second, $5$ on the third and $0$ on the last, which is the band in the
figure.
</Example>

### Quadratic variation of the integral: rate $\Delta^2$ per unit time

Brownian motion accumulates quadratic variation at rate one per unit time.
Inside the integral it is scaled by $\Delta(u)$, and squared increments
scale by $\Delta(u)^2$.

<Theorem title="quadratic variation of the Itô integral (Shreve II, 4.2.3)">
For a simple process $\Delta$, the quadratic variation accumulated by
$I$ up to time $t$ is
$$
[I, I](t) = \int_0^t \Delta(u)^2\,du ,
$$
in the sense that the sampled sums $\sum_i (I(s_{i+1}) - I(s_i))^2$ over
partitions of $[0, t]$ converge to the right side in mean square, and
almost surely along a subsequence, as the mesh goes to zero.
</Theorem>

The idea: within one cell of the strategy's partition the integral moves
as $\Delta(t_j)$ times $W$, so its squared increments are $\Delta(t_j)^2$
times the squared increments of $W$, whose sum over the cell converges to
the cell's length by the previous lesson's theorem; the cell contributes
$\Delta(t_j)^2 (t_{j+1} - t_j) = \int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}} \Delta^2\,du$, and
the cells add. The derivation exercise at the end of the lesson carries
it through.

The two theorems together separate two quantities the tree could not.
Along the running path the positions were $2, 1, 1, 0$, so the quadratic
variation of the gain is $\tfrac14(4 + 1 + 1 + 0) = 1.5$; along a path on
which the second cell rose it is $\tfrac14(4 + 1 + 9 + 0) = 3.5$. The
quadratic variation is a property of one path and depends on the
positions taken along it; the variance, $2.5$, is the average of the
quadratic variation over all paths, a property of the ensemble, and
cannot be random. <Ref lesson="random-walk-to-brownian-motion">The
scaled-walk lesson</Ref> drew the same distinction for $W$ itself, where
the two happened to coincide because $\Delta \equiv 1$ has nothing to
vary.

<Remark title="dI dI = Δ² dt">
In differential notation the theorem reads
$dI(t)\,dI(t) = \Delta(t)^2\,dW(t)\,dW(t) = \Delta(t)^2\,dt$: square the
differential $dI = \Delta\,dW$ and apply $dW\,dW = dt$. The multiplication
table of <Ref lesson="brownian-motion">the previous lesson</Ref> applies to
integrals against $W$ with the integrand carried along, and it is the form
in which <Ref lesson="ito-formula">the Itô–Doeblin formula</Ref> computes
second-order terms.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
The integral's fluctuation is set by $\Delta^2$ alone, path by path in the
quadratic variation and on average in the isometry; nothing about the
direction of the positions enters either. This is the continuous form of
"volatility is what survives squaring", and it is why <Ref
lesson="girsanov-theorem">a change of measure</Ref>, which alters only
drifts, cannot touch the quadratic variation of any Itô integral.
</KeyFact>

## Adaptedness is load-bearing: a one-step peek mints $\sqrt{2n/\pi}$ per unit time

The martingale proof used adaptedness at exactly one equality, and the
near miss of the definition violated exactly that clause. From a
construction that is otherwise identical one would expect a small
distortion; the computation shows a gain that grows without bound as the
trading frequency rises.

### The computation

Partition $[0, T]$ into $n$ equal cells of length $T/n$ and take the
peeking position $\Delta(t_j) = \operatorname{sign}(D_j)$, where
$D_j = W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)$ is the increment about to happen. Each term of
the "integral" is $\operatorname{sign}(D_j)\,D_j = |D_j|$, so the total is
$\sum_j |D_j| \ge 0$ on every path, and it never decreases in $t$. For
$X \sim N(0, v)$, $E|X| = \sqrt{2v/\pi}$, so with $v = T/n$,
$$
E\Bigl[\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} |D_j|\Bigr] = n\,\sqrt{\frac{2T}{\pi n}}
= \sqrt{\frac{2nT}{\pi}} .
$$
At $T = 1$ and $n = 100$ trading dates the expected gain is
$\sqrt{200/\pi} = 7.98$; at $n = 10{,}000$ it is $79.8$. The spread does
not grow with it: $\mathrm{Var}|X| = v(1 - 2/\pi)$, the terms are
independent, and the variance of the total is $T(1 - 2/\pi) = 0.36\,T$,
a standard deviation of $0.60$ at $T = 1$ for every $n$. One step of
foresight, exercised $n$ times per unit time, is worth $\sqrt{2n/\pi}$
per unit time with a fixed standard deviation of $0.6$: the ratio of
mean to spread grows like $\sqrt n$, and no adapted strategy has a
positive mean at all.

### The step that fails, and its discrete twin

The martingale proof's group (C) computed
$E[\Delta(t_j)D_j \mid \mathcal F(t_j)] = \Delta(t_j)\,E[D_j \mid
\mathcal F(t_j)] = 0$, taking $\Delta(t_j)$ out as known. For the peeking
position, $\Delta(t_j)$ is determined by $\mathcal F(t_{j+1})$ and not by
$\mathcal F(t_j)$, the factor cannot come out, and the product
$\Delta(t_j)D_j = |D_j|$ has conditional expectation
$\sqrt{2(t_{j+1} - t_j)/\pi} > 0$. Every other line of the proof survives;
the theorem fails at that equality alone. The isometry fails at the same
place: its cross terms $E[\Delta(t_i)\Delta(t_j)D_iD_j]$ vanish only
because $D_j$ is independent of everything else in the product.

This is the continuous form of a computation already made. <Ref
lesson="stopping-times">The stopping-times lesson</Ref> stopped a
discounted stock at the regret rule, which reads the next toss, and found
the stopped process's mean drifting from $4$ to $2.56$ where the fair
rule's stayed at $4$; there the foresight was a stopping decision and the
leak was one toss. Here the foresight is a position and the leak is one
increment, and the value of the leak is computable: $\sqrt{2\Delta t/\pi}$
per cell, which sums to $\sqrt{2nT/\pi}$ because the cells shrink like
$1/n$ while their number grows like $n$.

that makes the integral a fair game, and it is the clause <Ref

priced by expectation, only because the position is set from the price

## General integrands: the isometry carries the limit, and three facts are fenced

Hedging strategies in the next module are continuous functions of time
and price, not staircases, so the integral must be extended beyond simple
processes. The extension is an approximation argument in which the
isometry is the mechanism and three facts are cited rather than proved.

### The hypothesis and the approximation

Let $\Delta(t)$, $0 \le t \le T$, be adapted, continuously varying or
jumping, and satisfy
$$
E\int_0^T \Delta(t)^2\,dt < \infty ,
$$
the square-integrability the isometry's hypothesis already demanded on
each cell. Freeze $\Delta$ at the left endpoint of each cell of a
partition: $\Delta_n(t) = \Delta(t_j)$ on $[t_j, t_{j+1})$. Each
$\Delta_n$ is simple and adapted, its integral is defined, and as the mesh
shrinks the staircase approaches the integrand. For the integrand of the
last section, $\Delta = W$ itself, the approximation error is computable:
$$
E\int_0^T |\Delta_n(t) - W(t)|^2\,dt
= \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}} E\bigl[(W(t) - W(t_j))^2\bigr]\,dt
= \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}} (t - t_j)\,dt
= n \cdot \frac{(T/n)^2}{2} = \frac{T^2}{2n} ,
$$
which goes to zero like $1/n$: each cell contributes the variance of a
Brownian increment integrated across the cell.

  caption="Freezing the integrand W at each left endpoint: the staircase is a simple process whose mean-square distance from W is T²/(2n), and it tends to zero as the mesh shrinks."

### The isometry makes the approximants converge

Suppose simple processes $\Delta_n$ satisfy $E\int_0^T |\Delta_n -
\Delta|^2\,dt \to 0$. The difference of two approximants is itself a
simple process on the common refinement of their partitions, and the
integral is linear on simple processes, so
$I_n(t) - I_m(t) = \int_0^t (\Delta_n - \Delta_m)\,dW$ and the isometry
gives
$$
E\bigl[(I_n(t) - I_m(t))^2\bigr] = E\int_0^t |\Delta_n(u) - \Delta_m(u)|^2\,du
\le 2\,E\int_0^T |\Delta_n - \Delta|^2\,du + 2\,E\int_0^T |\Delta - \Delta_m|^2\,du
\longrightarrow 0
$$
as $n, m \to \infty$. The sequence $I_n(t)$ is therefore Cauchy in mean
square, and the Itô integral of $\Delta$ is defined as its limit:
$$
\int_0^t \Delta(u)\,dW(u) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \int_0^t \Delta_n(u)\,dW(u)
\qquad \text{in mean square.}
$$
The isometry is what makes the definition possible: without a bound on
$E[(I_n - I_m)^2]$ in terms of the integrands, nothing would force the
integrals of two nearby staircases to be near each other.

<Remark title="the fence for this lesson">
Two facts in the extension are used and not proved here; a third is
stated with the theorem below. (1) **Approximating sequences exist**: for
every adapted $\Delta$ with $E\int_0^T \Delta^2 < \infty$ there are simple
processes with $E\int_0^T |\Delta_n - \Delta|^2 \to 0$. For continuous
$\Delta$ the left-endpoint staircase works, as the computation for
$\Delta = W$ shows; the general case is Karatzas and Shreve, §3.2. (2)
**A Cauchy sequence in mean square has a limit**: the space of
square-integrable random variables is complete (the Riesz–Fischer
theorem), which is a fact of measure theory. (3) **The limit has
continuous paths**: this needs a maximal inequality for martingales
(Doob's), Karatzas and Shreve §1.3, to pass path continuity through the
mean-square limit. Everything else about the limit is inherited from
simple processes by the continuity of expectation and conditional
expectation under mean-square convergence.
</Remark>

<Theorem title="properties of the Itô integral (Shreve II, 4.3.1)">
Let $\Delta(t)$, $0 \le t \le T$, be adapted with $E\int_0^T \Delta^2\,dt
< \infty$, and let $I(t) = \int_0^t \Delta\,dW$ be defined by the limit
above. Then: (i) the paths of $I$ are continuous; (ii) $I(t)$ is
determined by $\mathcal F(t)$; (iii) the integral is linear in the
integrand; (iv) $I$ is a martingale; (v) $E[I(t)^2] = E\int_0^t
\Delta^2\,du$; (vi) $[I, I](t) = \int_0^t \Delta^2\,du$.
</Theorem>

Properties (iv) and (v) are the theorems of the third section, transferred
along the limit; (vi) is the quadratic variation theorem transferred the
same way. The hypothesis is not decoration. For $\Delta(t) = e^{W(t)^2}$,
$E[\Delta(t)^2] = E[e^{2W(t)^2}]$ is infinite as soon as $t \ge
\tfrac14$, because the density $e^{-x^2/2t}$ cannot absorb $e^{2x^2}$
once $2 \ge 1/(2t)$; the integrand is adapted and its paths are finite, and
the isometry has no finite right side. Integrals of such integrands can
still be defined, path by path in a localised sense, and are *local
martingales* rather than martingales; the course names them here and does
not develop them.

## $\int W\,dW$ from the definition: the correction is half the quadratic variation

The audit showed that the chain rule's answer has the wrong mean; the
definition now supplies the right one, and the algebra shows where the
correction comes from without any appeal to the limit theorem beyond the
previous lesson's.

### The partition sums, exactly

Take the equal partition $t_j = jT/n$ and the approximant $\Delta_n(t) =
W(t_j)$ on $[t_j, t_{j+1})$, whose mean-square distance from $W$ is
$T^2/(2n)$. Write $W_j = W(jT/n)$. By definition,
$$
\int_0^T W\,dW = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} W_j\,(W_{j+1} - W_j) .
$$
The sum is rewritten by one algebraic identity. For any two numbers,
$b^2 - a^2 = 2a(b - a) + (b - a)^2$; put $a = W_j$, $b = W_{j+1}$ and sum
over $j$. The left side telescopes to $W_n^2 - W_0^2 = W(T)^2$, so
$$
W(T)^2 = 2\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} W_j\,(W_{j+1} - W_j) + \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} (W_{j+1} - W_j)^2 ,
\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} W_j\,(W_{j+1} - W_j) = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 Q_\Pi .
$$
This holds on every path and every partition, with no limit taken, and it
is the identity the previous lesson's forward exercise found.

  src="./figures/wdw-identity.svg"
  alt="The identity: the sum of W at t j times the increment equals one half W of T squared minus one half the sum of squared increments, each term braced and labelled: the Itô approximant, the chain rule's whole answer, and half the sampled quadratic variation which tends to half T; below, the limit as the mesh goes to zero and the expectation audit it passes"
  caption="The Itô approximant of ∫W dW is the chain rule's answer minus half the sampled quadratic variation, on every partition exactly; in the limit the correction is ½T."

### The limit, and the audit passed

As $n \to \infty$ the first term on the right does not move and the second
converges: $Q_\Pi \to T$ in mean square by <Ref
lesson="brownian-motion">the quadratic variation theorem</Ref>. Hence
$$
\int_0^T W(t)\,dW(t) = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 T ,
$$
and the same computation on $[0, t]$ gives $\int_0^t W\,dW = \tfrac12
W(t)^2 - \tfrac12 t$ for every $t$. The expectation audit that failed the
chain rule now passes: $E[\tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 T] = \tfrac12 T -
\tfrac12 T = 0$, as a martingale started at zero requires. The martingale
itself is one already met: $\tfrac12(W(t)^2 - t)$ is half the process the
previous lesson proved a martingale by direct conditioning, and Theorem
4.3.1 (iv) predicts it independently. Two routes, one process.

For a differentiable $g$ with $g(0) = 0$ the corresponding computation
gives $\int_0^T g\,dg = \int_0^T g\,g'\,dt = \tfrac12 g(T)^2$, with no
correction, because $Q_\Pi \to 0$ for a smooth function. The extra
$-\tfrac12 T$ is the nonzero quadratic variation of $W$ entering through
the algebraic identity, and the identity is what the left-endpoint
evaluation produces: a right-endpoint evaluation would have added
$\tfrac12 Q_\Pi$ instead of subtracting it.

<Example title="the quadratic variation of ∫W dW">
By property (vi) of the theorem above, quadratic variation of the general
Itô integral (Shreve II, 4.3.1), with $\Delta = W$, the process $I(t) = \tfrac12
W(t)^2 - \tfrac12 t$ has quadratic variation
$$
[I, I](t) = \int_0^t W(u)^2\,du ,
$$
a random quantity that depends on the path: large where $|W|$ has been
large, small where the path has stayed near zero. Its expectation is
$\int_0^t u\,du = \tfrac12 t^2$, which by the isometry is also
$\mathrm{Var}\,I(t)$. Check against the closed form: $\mathrm{Var}[\tfrac12
W(t)^2] = \tfrac14(E[W(t)^4] - t^2) = \tfrac14(3t^2 - t^2) = \tfrac12 t^2$,
using the fourth moment computed in the previous lesson's exercises. The
two accountings agree.
</Example>

What is established: the Itô integral of a simple process is the gain of
a strategy that sets its position at each trading date from information
then available, $I(t) = \sum_j \Delta(t_j)[W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)]$; it is a
martingale, with $E[I(t)^2] = E\int_0^t \Delta^2\,du$ and $[I, I](t) =
\int_0^t \Delta^2\,du$, each proved by conditioning one term at a time;
the proofs use adaptedness at exactly one equality, and a one-step peek
at that equality turns the integral into a gain of mean $\sqrt{2nT/\pi}$
that grows with the trading frequency; general square-integrable
integrands are reached by mean-square approximation, with the isometry
supplying the Cauchy bound and three facts fenced; and
$\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 T$, where the $-\tfrac12 T$
is half the quadratic variation and enters through an exact algebraic
identity. What is not established is a method: the correction for
$\int W\,dW$ was found by an identity specific to squares, and every new
integrand would need its own. <Ref lesson="ito-formula">The next
lesson</Ref> proves the Itô–Doeblin formula, the second-order chain rule
that produces every such correction as a single term $\tfrac12 f''\,dt$.

## ★ The midpoint rule: Stratonovich's integral obeys the chain rule and is not a martingale

The evaluation point was fixed by finance; this section computes what the
other natural choice gives. Let
$t_j^* = \tfrac12(t_j + t_{j+1})$ be the midpoints and define the
*Stratonovich integral* as the limit of the midpoint sums
$$
\int_0^T W(t) \circ dW(t) = \lim_{\|\Pi\| \to 0} \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} W(t_j^*)\,\bigl(W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)\bigr).
$$
### The midpoint sum is an Itô sum plus a half-cell quadratic variation

Split each increment at the midpoint, $W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j) = [W(t_{j+1}) -
W(t_j^*)] + [W(t_j^*) - W(t_j)]$, and split the integrand as $W(t_j^*) =
W(t_j) + [W(t_j^*) - W(t_j)]$. The midpoint sum becomes
$$
\underbrace{\sum_j W(t_j)\,[W(t_j^*) - W(t_j)] + \sum_j W(t_j^*)\,[W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j^*)]}_{\text{the Itô sum on the refined partition } \{t_0, t_0^*, t_1, t_1^*, \ldots, t_n\}}
\;+\; \underbrace{\sum_j \bigl(W(t_j^*) - W(t_j)\bigr)^2}_{Q_{\Pi/2}} .
$$
The first group is a left-endpoint sum on a partition of mesh at most
$\|\Pi\|$ and converges to the Itô integral $\tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 T$.
The second is a sum of squared increments over the left halves of the
cells only; it has mean $\sum_j \tfrac12(t_{j+1} - t_j) = \tfrac12 T$ and
variance $\sum_j 2 \cdot \tfrac14 (t_{j+1} - t_j)^2 \le \tfrac12 \|\Pi\| T
\to 0$, by the same two-moment argument as the quadratic variation
theorem, so $Q_{\Pi/2} \to \tfrac12 T$. Adding,
$$
\int_0^T W \circ dW = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 T + \tfrac12 T = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 .
$$
### What the chain rule costs: the martingale property

The midpoint rule recovers the chain rule exactly, and for this reason the
Stratonovich integral obeys the ordinary rules of calculus in general. The
price is the martingale property: $E[\tfrac12 W(T)^2] = \tfrac12 T \ne 0$,
so the Stratonovich integral of $W$ has a positive mean, and the reason is
visible in the sum. The integrand $W(t_j^*)$ is read halfway through the
cell, so the position held over $[t_j, t_{j+1}]$ uses half a cell of
foresight, and half a cell of foresight is worth $\tfrac12 T$ by the same
accounting that made a full step worth $Q_\Pi \to T$ in the audit section.
The Stratonovich integral is the right object where an integrand is a
physical quantity that varies smoothly within a cell; it is the wrong
object for a position that has to be chosen before the cell begins, and
the course uses the Itô integral throughout.

Take the simple process $\Delta = 1$ on $[0, 1)$ and $\Delta = -1$ on

<Solution>
**(a)** On $[0, 1]$, $I(t) = 1 \cdot (W(t) - 0) = W(t)$. On $[1, 2]$,

**(b)** $E[I(2)] = 2 \cdot 0 - 0 = 0$. Write $I(2) = W(1) - (W(2) - W(1))$,
a difference of two independent $N(0, 1)$ variables, so $E[I(2)^2] =
1 + 1 = 2$. The isometry's right side is $\int_0^2 \Delta^2\,du = \int_0^1
1\,du + \int_1^2 1\,du = 2$. They agree; the sign of the position is
invisible to the variance.

**(c)** $[I, I](2) = \int_0^2 \Delta^2\,du = 2$, deterministic: the
positions are constants, so the quadratic variation is the same on every
path, and here equals the variance because there is nothing to average.
The transferable move: for a deterministic integrand, quadratic variation
and variance coincide; for a random one they differ path by path and
agree on average.
</Solution>

<Exercise>
Prove the quadratic variation theorem for a simple process (Shreve II,
4.2.3): $[I, I](t) = \int_0^t \Delta(u)^2\,du$. Fix one cell $[t_j, t_{j+1}]$ of the strategy's
partition, take a partition $t_j = s_0 < s_1 < \cdots < s_m = t_{j+1}$ of
the cell, and compute the sampled sum of squared increments of $I$ over
it; then pass to the limit using the previous lesson's theorem and add
over cells. State which property of $\Delta$ each step uses.

<Solution>
Strategy: inside one cell $I$ is an affine function of $W$ with slope
$\Delta(t_j)$, so its increments are $\Delta(t_j)$ times increments of
$W$.

**One cell.** For $s_i, s_{i+1}$ in $[t_j, t_{j+1}]$, the definition gives
$I(s_{i+1}) - I(s_i) = \Delta(t_j)\,[W(s_{i+1}) - W(s_i)]$, because the
position is constant across the cell (the "constant on each cell" clause).
Hence
$$
\sum_{i=0}^{m-1} \bigl(I(s_{i+1}) - I(s_i)\bigr)^2
= \Delta(t_j)^2 \sum_{i=0}^{m-1} \bigl(W(s_{i+1}) - W(s_i)\bigr)^2 .
$$
As $\max_i (s_{i+1} - s_i) \to 0$ the sum on the right converges, in mean
square and almost surely along a subsequence, to the quadratic variation
of $W$ over $[t_j, t_{j+1}]$, which is $t_{j+1} - t_j$ (<Ref
lesson="brownian-motion">the quadratic variation theorem of the previous
lesson</Ref>, Shreve II, Theorem 3.4.3, applied to the interval). The factor $\Delta(t_j)^2$
is a fixed random variable on the path and does not interfere with the
limit. So the quadratic variation of $I$ accumulated over the cell is
$\Delta(t_j)^2\,(t_{j+1} - t_j) = \int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}} \Delta(u)^2\,du$,
the last equality again by constancy on the cell.

**The open cell containing $t$.** The same computation on $[t_k, t]$ gives
$\Delta(t_k)^2\,(t - t_k) = \int_{t_k}^t \Delta^2\,du$.

**Adding.** Quadratic variation over $[0, t]$ is the sum over the cells of
$[0, t]$, since a partition of $[0, t]$ that includes the trading dates
splits into partitions of the cells, and refining it refines each. The
total is $\sum_j \int_{t_j}^{t_{j+1}} \Delta^2\,du + \int_{t_k}^t
\Delta^2\,du = \int_0^t \Delta(u)^2\,du$.

Adaptedness was not used: the quadratic variation theorem is a statement
about a single path, and a peeking $\Delta$ would give the same formula.
What adaptedness governs is the mean, not the roughness. The transferable
move: to find the quadratic variation of an integral against $W$, square
the integrand and integrate in time.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Suppose the simple process is deterministic: each $\Delta(t_j)$ is a fixed
number, not a random variable. Show that $I(t)$ is normally distributed
with mean $0$ and variance $\int_0^t \Delta^2\,du$, building the
distribution from the independent normal increments. Then state exactly
which step of the martingale proof and which step of the isometry proof
become elementary in this case, and which hypothesis of the general
theory is being pushed to its edge.

<Solution>
Strategy: a fixed linear combination of independent normals is normal.

With $D_j = W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)$ (and $D_k = W(t) - W(t_k)$), $I(t) =
\sum_{j=0}^k \Delta(t_j) D_j$ with the coefficients constants. The $D_j$
are increments over disjoint windows, hence independent, each
$N(0, t_{j+1} - t_j)$. A linear combination of independent normals with
constant coefficients is normal, with mean $\sum_j \Delta(t_j) \cdot 0 = 0$
and variance $\sum_j \Delta(t_j)^2 (t_{j+1} - t_j) = \int_0^t
\Delta(u)^2\,du$. So $I(t) \sim N\bigl(0, \int_0^t \Delta^2\,du\bigr)$.

**What becomes elementary.** In the martingale proof, the "taking out what
is known" step in group (C) is vacuous: a constant comes out of any
expectation, conditional or not, and no filtration is needed. In the
isometry proof, the vanishing of the cross terms needs only that $D_i$
and $D_j$ are independent with mean zero, and the square terms need only
$E[D_j^2] = t_{j+1} - t_j$: the conditioning on $\mathcal F(t_j)$ that
handled a random $\Delta(t_j)^2$ is not invoked.

**The hypothesis at its edge.** Adaptedness is satisfied trivially, since
a constant is determined by every $\sigma$-algebra, so this is the
degenerate end of the adapted class: the integrand carries no information
about the path at all. What the general theory adds beyond this case is
precisely the handling of positions that depend on the past, which is
where the five properties of conditional expectation earn their place.
The normal distribution does not survive the step to random integrands:
$\int_0^T W\,dW = \tfrac12 W(T)^2 - \tfrac12 T$ is a shifted chi-square,
not a normal. Deterministic integrands recur in the next lesson, where
the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process is an integral of this kind and is
therefore Gaussian.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Solution>
Counterexample: $\Delta \equiv 1$, the most positive integrand there is,

The confusion is between integration against $dt$ and integration against
$dW$. For $\int_0^t \Delta(u)\,du$ a positive integrand does give an
increasing function: every increment is $\Delta(u)\,du$ with $du > 0$.
For $\int \Delta\,dW$ the increment over a cell is $\Delta(t_j)\,[W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)]$,
and the bracket is negative about half the time whatever the
sign of $\Delta$. A positive integrand fixes the *direction of exposure*,
long rather than short, not the direction of the gain.

What is true for every positive integrand, and every integrand that
satisfies the hypotheses: $I$ is a martingale with $E[I(t)] = 0$ and
$E[I(t)^2] = E\int_0^t \Delta^2\,du$. A positive integrand does not
even make the mean positive. The one monotone statement available is
about the quadratic variation: $[I, I](t) = \int_0^t \Delta^2\,du$ is
increasing in $t$ for every integrand, positive or not, because its
integrand is a square. The transferable move: against $dW$, sign
information in the integrand is lost to the mean and the variance and
survives only in the exposure.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Return to the $N$-period binomial model. <Ref
lesson="multiperiod-binomial">The wealth equation</Ref> is $X_{n+1} =
\Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)$ with $\Delta_n$ adapted,
and under the risk-neutral measure the discounted stock $M_n = S_n/(1 +
r)^n$ is a martingale. (a) Show that the discounted wealth is $X_0$ plus a
sum of the form $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \Delta_k (M_{k+1} - M_k)$, and identify
each ingredient of this lesson's definition in it. (b) Prove directly that
the discounted wealth is a $\tilde P$-martingale, marking the step that
uses adaptedness. (c) Compute $\tilde E_k[(M_{k+1} - M_k)^2]$ in terms of
$M_k$, $u$, $d$, $r$, and write the discrete analogue of the isometry;
say what it becomes under the calibration $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$,
$d = e^{-\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$.

<Solution>
**(a)** Divide the wealth equation by $(1 + r)^{n+1}$:
$$
\frac{X_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}} = \Delta_n \frac{S_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}}
+ \frac{X_n}{(1 + r)^n} - \Delta_n \frac{S_n}{(1 + r)^n},
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
\frac{X_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}} - \frac{X_n}{(1 + r)^n} = \Delta_n (M_{n+1} - M_n).
$$
Summing from $0$ to $n - 1$ telescopes the left side:
$X_n/(1 + r)^n = X_0 + \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \Delta_k (M_{k+1} - M_k)$. The
ingredients: the partition is the trading dates $0, 1, \ldots, N$; the
integrand $\Delta_k$ is the position set at date $k$ from the first $k$
tosses, adapted and constant over the period; the integrator $M$ is a
martingale in place of $W$; and the sum is position times increment,
left-endpoint evaluated. The discounted wealth of a self-financing
strategy is a discrete Itô integral against the discounted stock.

**(b)** For the one-step property,
$\tilde E_n[\Delta_n (M_{n+1} - M_n)] = \Delta_n\,\tilde E_n[M_{n+1} -
M_n] = \Delta_n\,(M_n - M_n) = 0$; the first equality takes $\Delta_n$ out
as known, which is legitimate because $\Delta_n$ depends on the first $n$
tosses only, and this is the step that uses adaptedness; the second is the
martingale property of $M$ under $\tilde P$. Hence
$\tilde E_n[X_{n+1}/(1 + r)^{n+1}] = X_n/(1 + r)^n$, and the multi-step property follows by the
tower property exactly as in group (C) of the continuous proof. This is
<Ref lesson="martingales-risk-neutral-pricing">the theorem that
discounted wealth is a risk-neutral martingale</Ref>, reproved as an
instance of the martingale theorem for simple integrands (Shreve II,
4.2.1).

**(c)** $M_{k+1} = M_k \cdot Y$ with $Y = u/(1 + r)$ with probability
$\tilde p$ and $d/(1 + r)$ with probability $\tilde q$, and $\tilde E[Y]
= 1$ by the choice of $\tilde p$. So $M_{k+1} - M_k = M_k (Y - 1)$ and
$$
\tilde E_k\bigl[(M_{k+1} - M_k)^2\bigr] = M_k^2\,\mathrm{Var}(Y)
= M_k^2\,\frac{\tilde p \tilde q\,(u - d)^2}{(1 + r)^2}.
$$
The cross terms of $\bigl(\sum_k \Delta_k (M_{k+1} - M_k)\bigr)^2$ vanish
by the same conditioning as in the continuous proof, and the square
terms give the discrete isometry
$$
\tilde E\Bigl[\Bigl(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \Delta_k (M_{k+1} - M_k)\Bigr)^2\Bigr]
= \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \tilde E\bigl[\Delta_k^2 M_k^2\bigr]\,
\frac{\tilde p \tilde q\,(u - d)^2}{(1 + r)^2}.
$$
Under the calibration, $u - d = 2\sinh(\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}) \approx
2\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}$, $\tilde p \tilde q \to \tfrac14$ and $1 + r \to
1$ as $\Delta t \to 0$, so the factor is $\approx \sigma^2\,\Delta t$ and
the right side is $\approx \tilde E\sum_k \Delta_k^2 M_k^2\,\sigma^2\,\Delta
t$, the Riemann sum of $E\int \Delta^2 M^2 \sigma^2\,du$: the isometry
for an integral against $\sigma M\,dW$, which is what the discounted
stock becomes in <Ref lesson="ito-formula">the geometric Brownian motion
of the next lesson</Ref>. The transferable move: every discrete
self-financing gain is a stochastic integral, and the tree's martingale
and variance results are Theorems 4.2.1 and 4.2.2 with $\Delta t$ in
place of $du$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Simulate the peeking strategy at $n = 10{,}000$ steps on $[0, 1]$ over
$2{,}000$ independent paths: at each step hold $+1$ if the coming
increment is positive and $-1$ otherwise, and record the terminal gain.
Before running, tabulate the predicted mean $\sqrt{2n/\pi}$, standard
deviation $\sqrt{1 - 2/\pi}$, and their ratio. Report the sample mean and
standard deviation of the gain, the ratio, and the fraction of paths on
which the gain was negative. Then replace the sign by the increment
itself, $\Delta(t_j) = W(t_{j+1}) - W(t_j)$, and report what the gain
converges to and with what spread. Identify in each case exactly what is
leaked and in what unit.

<Solution>
**Predictions.** Mean $\sqrt{20{,}000/\pi} = 79.79$; standard deviation
$\sqrt{1 - 2/\pi} = 0.603$; ratio $132$; negative paths: none, since every
term is $|D_j| \ge 0$.

**One run.** The gain of the sign strategy is $\sum_j |D_j|$, so the
whole simulation is four lines:

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
D = rng.standard_normal((2_000, 10_000)) / 100   # increments, dt = 1/n
gain = np.abs(D).sum(axis=1)                     # sign strategy: sum |D_j|
gain.mean(), gain.std(ddof=1), (gain < 0).mean()
```

Seed $0$, $2{,}000$ paths: sample mean $79.76$, sample standard deviation
$0.61$, ratio $130$, no negative path. Agreement means the mean lands
within about $2 \times 0.6/\sqrt{2{,}000} = 0.03$ of the prediction; the
observed gap is $0.03$, at the edge of that band, and the standard
deviation is within $2\%$ of its own. For comparison, at $n = 100$
(replace `10_000` by `100` and the divisor by `10`, same seed) the mean is
$7.99$ and the standard deviation $0.59$: the spread is the same, the mean
is ten times smaller, which is the $\sqrt n$ law.

**The increment as position.** With $\Delta(t_j) = D_j$ the gain is
$\sum_j D_j^2 = Q_\Pi$, the sampled quadratic variation, which converges
to $T = 1$ with standard deviation $\sqrt{2/n} = 0.014$ at $n = 10{,}000$;
the same seed gives `(D**2).sum(axis=1)` a sample mean of $0.9995$ and a
sample standard deviation of $0.0144$.
This leak is worth less than the sign leak, $1$ against $80$, because a
position equal to a tiny increment is a tiny position; scaling it to a
unit position, $\Delta(t_j) = D_j/\sqrt{\Delta t}$, gives $Q_\Pi/\sqrt{\Delta
t} = \sqrt n \cdot Q_\Pi \to \sqrt n$, the same $\sqrt n$ growth with
constant $1$ in place of $\sqrt{2/\pi} = 0.80$, because the sign strategy
throws away the size of the increment and keeps only its direction.

**What is leaked.** In the sign strategy, one bit per step: the direction
of the coming increment, worth $\sqrt{2\Delta t/\pi}$ per unit of position
per step. In the increment strategy, the whole coming increment. In both
the unit is one step's foresight, and the value of a step's foresight per
unit time grows like $1/\sqrt{\Delta t}$ because the information is
refreshed $1/\Delta t$ times per unit time while each refresh is worth
$\sqrt{\Delta t}$. The transferable move: the value of foresight in a
diffusion is measured in $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ per step, which is why
continuous-time models can tolerate no foresight at all.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Compute $\int_0^T W^2\,dW$ from the definition. Guess the chain rule's
answer, $\tfrac13 W(T)^3$, and audit its expectation as in the first
section: the audit passes. Then find the exact identity relating
$\sum_j W_j^2 (W_{j+1} - W_j)$ to $W(T)^3$ on a partition, pass to the
limit, and identify the correction. Finally audit the corrected answer
with the isometry: compute $E[I(T)^2]$ both from $E\int_0^T W^4\,dt$ and
from the closed form.

<Solution>
**The mean audit passes.** $E[\tfrac13 W(T)^3] = 0$ by the symmetry of the
normal, and $E[\text{Itô integral}] = 0$ always. The first section's
test cannot detect an error here; the integrand $W^2$ is even, and the
correction, if any, must have mean zero.

**The identity.** For any two numbers, $b^3 - a^3 = 3a^2(b - a) + 3a(b -
a)^2 + (b - a)^3$. With $a = W_j$, $b = W_{j+1}$, summed over $j$, the
left side telescopes to $W(T)^3$:
$$
W(T)^3 = 3\sum_j W_j^2\,\Delta W_j + 3\sum_j W_j\,(\Delta W_j)^2 + \sum_j (\Delta W_j)^3 .
$$
**The limit.** The last sum is the sampled cubic variation, which tends to
$0$ (the previous lesson's variations exercise). The middle sum is
$\sum_j W_j (\Delta W_j)^2$; replacing each $(\Delta W_j)^2$ by its cell
length $\Delta t_j$ changes the sum by $\sum_j W_j [(\Delta W_j)^2 -
\Delta t_j]$, whose terms have mean zero and are uncorrelated across
cells, with total variance $\sum_j E[W_j^2]\,2(\Delta t_j)^2 \le
2T\|\Pi\|\,T \to 0$; so the middle sum converges in mean square to
$\int_0^T W(t)\,dt$, an ordinary integral of the path. Hence
$$
\int_0^T W^2\,dW = \tfrac13 W(T)^3 - \int_0^T W(t)\,dt ,
$$
and the correction is $-\int_0^T W\,dt$, which has mean zero, which is
why the mean audit passed.

**The isometry audit.** By the isometry, $E[I(T)^2] = E\int_0^T W(t)^4\,dt
= \int_0^T 3t^2\,dt = T^3$. From the closed form, with $E[W(T)^6] =
15T^3$, $E[W(T)^3 W(t)] = 3tT$ for $t \le T$ (write $W(T) = W(t) + Y$ with
$Y$ independent, expand, and keep $E[W(t)^4] + 3E[W(t)^2]E[Y^2] = 3t^2 +
3t(T - t)$), and $E[(\int_0^T W\,dt)^2] = \int_0^T\!\!\int_0^T \min(s, t)\,
ds\,dt = T^3/3$:
$$
E\bigl[I(T)^2\bigr] = \tfrac19 \cdot 15T^3 - \tfrac23 \int_0^T 3tT\,dt + \tfrac13 T^3
= \tfrac53 T^3 - T^3 + \tfrac13 T^3 = T^3 .
$$
The two accountings agree, and they would not have without the
correction: $\tfrac13 W(T)^3$ alone has second moment $\tfrac53 T^3$.

**The pattern.** For $f(x) = x^2$ the correction was $-\tfrac12 T =
-\tfrac12 \int_0^T f''(W)\,dt$ with $f'' = 2$; for $f(x) = x^3$ it is
$-\int_0^T W\,dt = -\tfrac12 \int_0^T f''(W)\,dt$ with $f'' = 6x$. In both
cases the Itô integral of $f'(W)$ is $f(W(T)) - f(0) - \tfrac12 \int_0^T
f''(W(t))\,dt$: the identity that generated each correction is the
second-order Taylor expansion of $f$ across a cell, with $(\Delta W)^2$
replaced by $\Delta t$ and the cubic term discarded. That bookkeeping,
stated for every twice-differentiable $f$ and every Itô process, is <Ref
lesson="ito-formula">the Itô–Doeblin formula of the next lesson</Ref>.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

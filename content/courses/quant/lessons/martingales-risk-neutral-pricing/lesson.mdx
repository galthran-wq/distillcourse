The bookkeeping constants of the replication algebra assemble into a
probability measure, and under it every discounted price in the model —
stock, wealth, claim — is a martingale: a fair game whose conditional
expectation goes nowhere.

This is the seventh move of the course's argument, which claims that a
derivative's price is enforced by replication rather than estimated from
beliefs, and the third move of its second module. <Ref
lesson="multiperiod-binomial">The multiperiod lesson</Ref> priced every claim
on a tree by the backward recursion and proved that its hedge lands on the
payoff along every path; <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">the previous
lesson</Ref> built the coin-toss space, defined $E_n[X]$ as the average over
the tosses not yet seen, proved its five properties, and rewrote the
recursion as $V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$. What stands open is
what kind of process that equation makes $V_n$, and what the tilde on the
$E$ is buying. This lesson answers both: the equation says that $V_n$
discounted is a *martingale* under the coin with head probability
$\tilde p$, the stock discounted is one too, and so is the discounted wealth
of every self-financing strategy whatever its trading rule, which is why no
such strategy can be an arbitrage. Iterating the martingale property
collapses backward induction into a single conditional expectation, the
risk-neutral pricing formula; the binomial representation theorem then
turns the argument around, showing that any $\tilde P$-martingale is the
discounted wealth of some strategy, so that the hedge exists because the
price process is a martingale and not only because the recursion was run;
and the Markov property says when that price is a function of the current
stock price alone. <Ref lesson="state-prices-change-of-measure">The next
lesson</Ref> asks what a change from $P$ to $\tilde P$ is as an object, and
answers with one random variable.

## A martingale: the average of the next value is the current value, under one stated coin

The recursion $V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}]$ compares a process
at time $n$ with the conditional average of the same process one toss
later. The comparison has a name once the discount factor is moved to the
right place, and the first thing to fix is what the name means and which
coin it is measured against.

### The discounted stock on the \$4 tree, under two coins

Divide every stock price on the \$4 tree by the money market's growth,
$1.25^n$ at time $n$. The discounted prices are $4$ at the root;
$8/1.25 = 6.4$ and $2/1.25 = 1.6$ at time one; $16/1.5625 = 10.24$,
$2.56$, $0.64$ at time two; and $16.384$, $4.096$, $1.024$, $0.256$ at time
three. Under the coin with $\tilde p = \tfrac12$, the average of the two
values after any node is the value at the node:
$\tfrac12 \cdot 6.4 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1.6 = 4$ at the root,
$\tfrac12 \cdot 10.24 + \tfrac12 \cdot 2.56 = 6.4$ at $H$,
$\tfrac12 \cdot 2.56 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.64 = 1.6$ at $T$, and so on at every
node of every date. Under the coin with $p = \tfrac23$ the same averages are
$\tfrac23 \cdot 6.4 + \tfrac13 \cdot 1.6 = 4.8$ at the root and, in general,
$1.2$ times the node's value, since
$\tilde E_n$ replaced by $E_n$ multiplies by $(pu + qd)/(1 + r) = 1.5/1.25$.

The discounted stock is one process, a fixed table of numbers on the tree.
Whether the average of the next two entries equals the current entry is not
a property of the table; it is a property of the weights the average uses,
and exactly one choice of weights makes it hold. That is the content of the
definition.

<Definition title="martingale, submartingale, supermartingale (Shreve I, Definition 2.4.1)">
Let $M_0, M_1, \ldots, M_N$ be an adapted process on the $N$-toss space
with head probability $p$, $0 < p < 1$. The process is a **martingale**
under $P$ if
$$
M_n = E_n[M_{n+1}] \qquad \text{for } n = 0, 1, \ldots, N - 1,
$$
on every block of $\mathcal F_n$. It is a **submartingale** if
$M_n \le E_n[M_{n+1}]$ and a **supermartingale** if
$M_n \ge E_n[M_{n+1}]$, each for all $n$ and all blocks. The same words
with $\tilde E_n$ in place of $E_n$ define a martingale, submartingale or
supermartingale **under $\tilde P$**.
</Definition>

The definition has two inputs, the process and the measure, and no
statement of the form "$M$ is a martingale" is complete without the second.
The four cases that fix the boundary all live on the \$4 tree.

- **The canonical case.** The discounted stock $S_n/(1 + r)^n$ under
  $\tilde P$: the averages above are the check, and the theorem of the
  next subsection is the proof for every tree.
- **A case with a different surface.** Fix any random variable $X$ on the
  $N$-toss space and set $M_n = E_n[X]$. <Ref
  lesson="coin-toss-probability">The previous lesson's last exercise</Ref>
  proved $E_n[M_{n+1}] = E_n[E_{n+1}[X]] = E_n[X] = M_n$ from the tower
  property. This process reads no prices and no rates; it is a martingale
  under whichever measure computes it, and it is the object the pricing
  formula below turns out to be.
- **The degenerate case.** A constant process, $M_n = c$ for all $n$, is a
  martingale under every measure; so is the discounted money market
  account, $(1 + r)^n / (1 + r)^n = 1$.
- **The near miss.** The stock itself, undiscounted, under $\tilde P$. The
  correct statement first: $\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}] = (\tilde p u + \tilde q d)\,S_n = (1 + r)\,S_n$,
  which is the identity that defined $\tilde p$. The misconception, stated
  once: "under the risk-neutral measure the stock price is a martingale."
  It is false whenever $r > 0$: on the \$4 tree
  $\tilde E_0[S_1] = \tfrac12 \cdot 8 + \tfrac12 \cdot 2 = 5 \ne 4$. The
  clause violated is $M_n = E_n[M_{n+1}]$ itself, by the factor $1 + r$;
  the stock under $\tilde P$ is a submartingale. The reason it fails is
  that $\tilde p$ was built to make the stock's conditional growth equal
  the money market's, not zero, and a process that grows at the riskless
  rate is level only after the riskless growth is divided out. The
  replacement model: *discounted* prices, prices measured in units of the
  money market account, are the martingales; undiscounted prices grow at
  $r$ on average under $\tilde P$. The fourth closing exercise returns to
  this claim with the arithmetic reversed.

$V_n / 1.25^n$ on the three-period \$4 tree, using the values

lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the multiperiod lesson</Ref>, and decide at

### The discounted stock is a risk-neutral martingale on every tree

<Theorem title="the discounted stock under the risk-neutral measure (Shreve I, Theorem 2.4.4)">
In an admissible binomial model, $0 < d < 1 + r < u$, with
$\tilde p = \frac{1 + r - d}{u - d}$ and $\tilde q = \frac{u - 1 - r}{u - d}$,
the discounted stock price $S_n / (1 + r)^n$ is a martingale under
$\tilde P$:
$$
\tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{S_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}}\Bigr] = \frac{S_n}{(1 + r)^n}
\qquad \text{at every node, } n = 0, \ldots, N - 1.
$$

<Proof>
Roadmap: write $S_{n+1} = S_n \cdot (S_{n+1}/S_n)$, take the known factor
out, and use independence on the ratio, which reads only toss $n + 1$.

The ratio $S_{n+1}/S_n$ equals $u$ if $\omega_{n+1} = H$ and $d$ if
$\omega_{n+1} = T$, so it depends only on toss $n + 1$, while $S_n$ and the
constant $(1 + r)^{-(n+1)}$ depend only on the first $n$ tosses. Taking
out what is known, then independence,
$$
\tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{S_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}}\Bigr]
= \frac{S_n}{(1 + r)^{n+1}}\,\tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{S_{n+1}}{S_n}\Bigr]
= \frac{S_n}{(1 + r)^{n+1}}\,\tilde E\Bigl[\frac{S_{n+1}}{S_n}\Bigr]
= \frac{S_n}{(1 + r)^{n+1}}\,(\tilde p u + \tilde q d).
$$
Finally $\tilde p u + \tilde q d = \frac{(1 + r - d)u + (u - 1 - r)d}{u - d} = \frac{(1 + r)(u - d)}{u - d} = 1 + r$,
and the factor $(1 + r)$ cancels one power of the discount.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Two checks on the proof. The step that uses the specific value of
$\tilde p$ is the last line and nothing before it; with a general $p$ the
same computation gives $\tilde E_n$ replaced by $E_n$ and the factor
$(pu + qd)/(1 + r)$, which is the growth of the averages in the outer
panels of the figure. The step that uses the coin's independence across
tosses is the middle equality: for a coin whose probability on toss $n + 1$
depended on earlier tosses, $\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}/S_n]$ would vary from block
to block, and the theorem's conclusion would need $\tilde p$ to vary with
it. Admissibility is what makes $\tilde p$ a probability at all,
$0 < \tilde p < 1$; without it the identity $\tilde p u + \tilde q d = 1 + r$
still holds as algebra, and the third closing exercise examines the
boundary where it stops meaning anything.

### Multistep and constant mean: the one-step clause implies all the others

The defining clause looks one toss ahead. The tower property extends it to
any number of tosses, and that extension is what the pricing formula will
use.

<Theorem title="multistep martingale property and constant expectation (Shreve I, Remarks 2.4.2–2.4.3)">
If $M_0, \ldots, M_N$ is a martingale under $P$, then for all
$0 \le n \le m \le N$,
$$
M_n = E_n[M_m], \qquad\text{and in particular}\qquad E[M_m] = M_0 .
$$
The same holds under $\tilde P$ with $\tilde E_n$ throughout.

<Proof>
Induction on $m - n$. For $m = n$ the claim is $M_n = E_n[M_n]$, taking out
what is known applied to $M_n \cdot 1$. If $M_n = E_n[M_m]$ for some
$m < N$, then by the tower property with $n \le m$ and the one-step clause
at time $m$,
$$
E_n[M_{m+1}] = E_n\bigl[E_m[M_{m+1}]\bigr] = E_n[M_m] = M_n .
$$
The case $n = 0$ reads $M_0 = E_0[M_m] = E[M_m]$, since $M_0$ is a
constant and $E_0 = E$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<KeyFact>
A martingale is a process whose best forecast, from any node and for any
horizon, is its current value, and the forecast is made with a stated coin.
The course's tilde marks that coin and never drops: every statement below
of the form "discounted $Y$ is a martingale" carries $\tilde E_n$, and a
bare $E_n$ in its place asserts a different, generally false, fact about
the same table of numbers.
</KeyFact>

## Discounted wealth is a risk-neutral martingale for every self-financing strategy, so there is no arbitrage

The theorem just proved concerns one process, the stock. A hedger's wealth
is a different process, built from the stock by a trading rule, and the
rule can be anything adapted: buy and hold, the replicating delta of a
call, a leveraged bet on heads. Under the actual coin the average growth of
that wealth depends on the rule. Under $\tilde P$ it does not, and the
reason is one substitution into the wealth equation.

worth $7.20$ against a loan of $1.92$, which grows to $2.40$. One toss

$X_2(HT) = 0.9 \times 4 - 2.40 = 1.20$. Discounting by $1.25^2 = 1.5625$

$\tfrac12 \cdot 7.68 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.768 = 4.224$. The discounted wealth

### The theorem: any adapted rule, any starting wealth

<Theorem title="discounted wealth under the risk-neutral measure (Shreve I, Theorem 2.4.5)">
Consider an admissible $N$-period binomial model. Let
$\Delta_0, \ldots, \Delta_{N-1}$ be a portfolio process, each $\Delta_n$
depending only on the first $n$ tosses, let $X_0$ be a number, and let
$X_1, \ldots, X_N$ be generated by the wealth equation
$X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)$. Then the
discounted wealth $X_n/(1 + r)^n$ is a martingale under $\tilde P$:
$$
\tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{X_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}}\Bigr] = \frac{X_n}{(1 + r)^n},
\qquad n = 0, \ldots, N - 1 .
$$

<Proof>
Roadmap: substitute the wealth equation; split by linearity; take out the
time-$n$ quantities; apply the discounted-stock theorem to what remains.

Fix $n$ and a node. Dividing the wealth equation by $(1 + r)^{n+1}$,
$$
\frac{X_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}} = \Delta_n\,\frac{S_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}} + \frac{X_n - \Delta_n S_n}{(1 + r)^n} .
$$
Take $\tilde E_n$ of both sides. By linearity the right side splits into
two conditional expectations. In the first, $\Delta_n$ depends only on the
first $n$ tosses and comes out: $\Delta_n\,\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}/(1 + r)^{n+1}]$.
The second is the conditional expectation of a quantity that depends only
on the first $n$ tosses, hence equals that quantity:
$(X_n - \Delta_n S_n)/(1 + r)^n$. By the discounted-stock theorem the first
term is $\Delta_n S_n/(1 + r)^n$. Adding,
$$
\tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{X_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}}\Bigr]
= \frac{\Delta_n S_n}{(1 + r)^n} + \frac{X_n - \Delta_n S_n}{(1 + r)^n}
= \frac{X_n}{(1 + r)^n} .
$$
The portfolio process was arbitrary, and so was $X_0$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Three checks on the proof. The idea in one sentence: wealth is a mixture of
the stock and the money market in time-$n$ proportions, and under $\tilde P$
both ingredients grow at $r$ on average, so the mixture does too. The step
where self-financing is used: the very first line, which is the wealth
equation, and nowhere else; a strategy that injected or withdrew cash at
time $n$ would have a different first line, and the fifth closing exercise
computes what its discounted wealth does instead. The step where adaptedness
of $\Delta_n$ is used: taking $\Delta_n$ out of $\tilde E_n$, which is
legal only because $\Delta_n$ is constant on the block; the foresight rule
of <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the multiperiod lesson</Ref>, which
reads toss $n + 1$, would stay inside the expectation and the proof would
stop.

<Exercise>

**Node $H$.** $\tfrac12 \cdot 11.375/1.5625 + \tfrac12 \cdot 0.375/1.5625 = \tfrac12(7.28 + 0.24) = 3.76 = 4.70/1.25$.

**Unconditional.** The terminal values $11.375, 0.375, 0.375, 0.375$ have
$\tilde P$-weights $\tfrac14$ each, so
$\tilde E[X_2/1.5625] = \tfrac14(7.28 + 0.24 + 0.24 + 0.24) = 2.00 = X_0$,
as the constant-mean theorem requires. The excess over the hedge,
$0.24$ at time zero, is itself a self-financing strategy with $\Delta = 0$,

The transferable move: the martingale property of discounted wealth is a

### Constant mean, and the arbitrage that cannot exist

<Theorem title="constant discounted mean (Shreve I, Corollary 2.4.6)">
Under the conditions of the previous theorem,
$\tilde E\bigl[X_n/(1 + r)^n\bigr] = X_0$ for every $n = 0, \ldots, N$.

<Proof>
The discounted wealth is a $\tilde P$-martingale, and the multistep theorem
with $n = 0$ gives $\tilde E[M_m] = M_0$ for any martingale; here
$M_0 = X_0/(1 + r)^0 = X_0$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

On the mis-funded hedge of the exercise above the corollary is three
column averages. The discounted wealth is $2.00$ at the root, $3.76$ and
$0.24$ at time one, and $7.28$, $0.24$, $0.24$, $0.24$ at time two; the
fan spreads with every toss, and each column, weighted by $\tilde P$,
averages to the $2.00$ it started from.

<Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">An arbitrage</Ref> is a strategy
with $X_0 = 0$, $X_N \ge 0$ on every toss sequence, and $X_N > 0$ on at
least one. The corollary rules it out in two lines.

<Theorem title="no arbitrage in the binomial model (first fundamental theorem, forward direction)">
In an admissible $N$-period binomial model there is no self-financing
portfolio process that is an arbitrage.

<Proof>
Suppose a portfolio process with $X_0 = 0$ produced $X_N(\omega) \ge 0$
for every sequence and $X_N(\omega^*) > 0$ for some sequence $\omega^*$.
Under $\tilde P$ every sequence has positive probability, since
$0 < \tilde p < 1$ by admissibility, so
$$
\tilde E\Bigl[\frac{X_N}{(1 + r)^N}\Bigr]
= \sum_{\omega} \tilde P(\omega)\,\frac{X_N(\omega)}{(1 + r)^N}
\ \ge\ \tilde P(\omega^*)\,\frac{X_N(\omega^*)}{(1 + r)^N} \ > 0,
$$
every other term being non-negative. But the corollary says this
expectation equals $X_0 = 0$. The supposition fails.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof on the two-period \$4 tree. Borrow \$4, buy one share and hold
it for both periods: from $X_0 = 0$ the discounted wealth is $2.4$ or
$-2.4$ after one toss and $6.24$, $-1.44$, $-1.44$, $-3.36$ after two, and
the four leaves, each of $\tilde P$-weight $\tfrac14$, average to zero, as
the corollary says every zero-start fan must: the positive leaf is paid
for by three negative ones. A supposed arbitrage is a fan of the same
shape with no negative leaf and at least one positive leaf, and its
weighted sum is then positive, not zero.

The proof used two facts about $\tilde P$ and nothing else: discounted
wealth has constant $\tilde P$-mean, and $\tilde P$ gives positive
probability to every sequence that $P$ does. The second fact is what lets
"$X_N > 0$ on some sequence" become "$X_N > 0$ with positive
$\tilde P$-probability"; a measure that assigned zero to $\omega^*$ would
not see the gain. Both facts are what the general definition records.

<Definition title="risk-neutral measure">
A probability measure $\tilde P$ on the $N$-toss space is a **risk-neutral
measure** for the binomial model if (i) $\tilde P(\omega) > 0$ for every
sequence $\omega$, so that $\tilde P$ and $P$ agree on which events have
probability zero, and (ii) the discounted price of every primary asset,
here the discounted stock and the discounted money market account, is a
martingale under $\tilde P$.
</Definition>

Clause (i) is called *equivalence* of the two measures; it is the reason a
risk-neutral measure can say nothing about how likely a path is and still
decide which strategies are arbitrages, since arbitrage is a statement
about which paths are possible. The coin with head probability
$\tilde p = \frac{1 + r - d}{u - d}$ satisfies both clauses on every
admissible tree, by admissibility and the discounted-stock theorem, and the
no-arbitrage proof went through for it. The proof was written for that
coin, but it read only the two clauses, so it holds for any measure
satisfying the definition: **the existence of a risk-neutral measure
implies the absence of arbitrage.** That is the first fundamental theorem
of asset pricing in the direction that is easy, and the direction this
course can prove on the tree.

<Remark title="the converse, stated and fenced">
The converse, that a model with no arbitrage admits a risk-neutral measure,
is also true in discrete time, and it is not proved here. On a finite tree
the proof separates the convex cone of attainable discounted payoffs from
the positive orthant by a hyperplane, and the normal to the hyperplane,
normalised, is the measure; the finite-space argument is in Delbaen and
Schachermayer, Chapter 2, and the general discrete-time theorem is due to
Dalang, Morton and Willinger. For the binomial tree the converse is
cheaper than that, because <Ref lesson="one-period-binomial">the
one-period lesson</Ref> already showed that admissibility, which is
exactly no-arbitrage over one period, forces $0 < \tilde p < 1$, and the
theorem above then supplies the measure. In continuous time even the
statement changes: "no arbitrage" has to be strengthened before a measure
is guaranteed, and <Ref lesson="martingale-representation-ftap">the
fundamental-theorems lesson</Ref> states which strengthening.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Martingales cannot be beaten: a $\tilde P$-martingale started at zero that
is never negative at time $N$ is zero on every path, because its mean is
zero and every path carries positive weight. The absence of arbitrage is
that sentence applied to discounted wealth, and it needs equivalence as
much as it needs the martingale property.
</KeyFact>

## The pricing formula: backward induction is a conditional expectation

The recursion of the multiperiod lesson computes a price one date at a
time. The multistep martingale property computes the same price in one
expectation, and the two are not merely equal in value; they are the same
sum, grouped differently.

### 34.08 as one expectation

<Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">The 80-stock call</Ref> ($S_0 = 80$,
$u = 1.5$, $d = 0.5$, $1 + r = 1.1$, strike $80$, three periods,
$\tilde p = 0.6$) was priced by three backward sweeps at $34.08$. Its
terminal payoffs are $190$, $10$, $0$, $0$ at the prices $270$, $90$,
$30$, $10$, and under the $0.6$ coin the four prices have probabilities
$0.6^3 = 0.216$, $3 \times 0.6^2 \times 0.4 = 0.432$, $0.288$, $0.064$.
Then
$$
\tilde E\Bigl[\frac{V_3}{1.1^3}\Bigr] = \frac{0.216 \times 190 + 0.432 \times 10 + 0 + 0}{1.331} = \frac{45.36}{1.331} = 34.08 .
$$
The same expectation taken from node $120$, where two tosses remain with
weights $0.36$, $0.48$, $0.16$ on $270$, $90$, $30$, gives
$(68.4 + 4.8 + 0)/1.21 = 60.50$, the backward sweep's $V_1(120)$; from node
$40$ it gives $0.36 \times 10/1.21 = 2.98$, the sweep's $V_1(40)$. Every
node's price is the discounted $\tilde P$-average of the payoff over the
subtree below it.

### The theorem

<Theorem title="risk-neutral pricing formula (Shreve I, Theorem 2.4.7)">
Consider an admissible $N$-period binomial model with risk-neutral
probability $\tilde p$, and a derivative security paying $V_N$ at time $N$,
any function of the $N$ tosses. Let $V_n$ be its price at time $n$ as
defined by the backward recursion. Then for every $0 \le n \le N$,
$$
V_n = \tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{V_N}{(1 + r)^{N - n}}\Bigr],
$$
and the discounted price $V_n/(1 + r)^n$ is a martingale under $\tilde P$.

<Proof>
Roadmap: the replicating wealth is a self-financing wealth, so it is a
discounted $\tilde P$-martingale; the multistep property evaluates it at
$N$; replication identifies $X_N$ with $V_N$ and the price definition
identifies $X_n$ with $V_n$.

By <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the replication theorem</Ref> there
is an initial wealth $X_0 = V_0$ and a portfolio process
$\Delta_0, \ldots, \Delta_{N-1}$ whose wealth equation produces
$X_N = V_N$ on every sequence, and the same theorem's induction gives
$X_n = V_n$ at every node for every $n$. The discounted wealth
$X_n/(1 + r)^n$ is a $\tilde P$-martingale by the wealth theorem of the
previous section, so by the multistep property with $m = N$,
$$
\frac{X_n}{(1 + r)^n} = \tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{X_N}{(1 + r)^N}\Bigr] = \tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{V_N}{(1 + r)^N}\Bigr].
$$
Substituting $X_n = V_n$ and multiplying by $(1 + r)^n$, which is a
constant and passes through $\tilde E_n$ by linearity, gives the formula.
The martingale statement is the one-step case of the same identity, since
$V_n/(1 + r)^n = X_n/(1 + r)^n$ and the latter is a martingale.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The proof leaned on the replication theorem for the existence of the hedge
and on the wealth theorem for the martingale property. A second proof,
which needs no hedge at all, comes from the tower property alone: define
$M_n = \tilde E_n[V_N/(1 + r)^N]$, a martingale by the second case of the
definition's boundary; then
$M_n = \tilde E_n[M_{n+1}]$ is, after multiplying by $(1 + r)^n$,
$$
(1 + r)^n M_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}\,\tilde E_n\bigl[(1 + r)^{n+1} M_{n+1}\bigr],
$$
which is the backward recursion for the process $(1 + r)^n M_n$, started
from $(1 + r)^N M_N = V_N$. The recursion has one solution, so
$(1 + r)^n M_n = V_n$. The two proofs say the same thing from opposite ends:
the recursion is what the tower property looks like one step at a time.

<Remark title="notation ledger: the tilde is not optional">
$\tilde E_n[\,\cdot\,]$, spoken "risk-neutral conditional expectation given
the first $n$ tosses", is the same finite sum as $E_n$ with $\tilde p$ in
place of $p$. In the pricing formula it is the whole content: with $p$ in
its place the formula returns the discounted expected payoff under the
actual coin, which <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">the previous
lesson's fourth exercise</Ref> computed for the one-period call as $1.60$
against the price $1.20$. The common error is a formula written
$V_n = E_n[V_N/(1 + r)^{N - n}]$ with the tilde dropped; it is wrong
whenever $p \ne \tilde p$, which in any market with a risk premium is
always. Baxter and Rennie's $E_Q(\,\cdot \mid \mathcal F_n)$ carries the
measure as a subscript for the same reason.
</Remark>

<Remark title="claims that pay more than once (Shreve I, Theorem 2.4.8)">
A security paying $C_n$ at each date $n = 0, \ldots, N$, each $C_n$
depending only on the first $n$ tosses, has time-$n$ value
$V_n = \tilde E_n\bigl[\sum_{k = n}^{N} C_k/(1 + r)^{k - n}\bigr]$, the sum of
the pricing formula applied to each payment. Its hedge obeys a wealth
equation with the payment subtracted before rebalancing,
$X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - C_n - \Delta_n S_n)$, and the
discounted wealth is not a martingale, since cash leaves it at every date;
what is a martingale is discounted wealth plus the discounted payments
made so far. The second closing exercise proves the two-payment case.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
Price is a discounted conditional expectation under $\tilde P$, and the
expectation was earned by a hedge, not assumed: the formula's proof
consumed the replication theorem. The direction "the price process is a
martingale, therefore a hedge exists" is not yet available, and the next
section supplies it.
</KeyFact>

## Every risk-neutral martingale is a trading strategy: the binomial representation theorem

The pricing theorem started from a claim already known to be replicable
and read off that its discounted price is a martingale. The construction
also runs backwards. Given any $\tilde P$-martingale, a trading rule can be
built whose discounted wealth follows it node for node, and applied to the
martingale $\tilde E_n[V_N/(1 + r)^N]$ that rule is the hedge. The theorem
that says so is a statement about martingales and stock increments, with
no portfolio in its hypotheses.

### Discounted wealth is a running sum of stock increments

Write $D_n = S_n/(1 + r)^n$ for the discounted stock, the martingale of
the first section. Dividing the wealth equation by $(1 + r)^{n+1}$ and
subtracting $X_n/(1 + r)^n$ from both sides,
$$
\frac{X_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}} - \frac{X_n}{(1 + r)^n}
= \Delta_n\Bigl(\frac{S_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}} - \frac{S_n}{(1 + r)^n}\Bigr)
= \Delta_n\,(D_{n+1} - D_n),
$$
the money market term having cancelled exactly. Summing from $0$ to
$n - 1$,
$$
\frac{X_n}{(1 + r)^n} = X_0 + \sum_{k = 0}^{n - 1} \Delta_k\,(D_{k+1} - D_k):
$$
discounted wealth is the starting wealth plus the accumulated gain from
holding $\Delta_k$ units of the discounted stock over each period. Every
self-financing strategy has this form, and the representation theorem says
that every $\tilde P$-martingale does too.

### The construction at one node

Take the martingale $M_n = \tilde E_n[V_3/1.25^3]$ for the three-period
\$5 put, which by the pricing theorem is $V_n/1.25^n$: $M_0 = 0.864$,
$M_1(H) = 0.48/1.25 = 0.384$, $M_1(T) = 1.68/1.25 = 1.344$. Over the first
toss its increment is $-0.48$ on heads and $+0.48$ on tails, and the
discounted stock's increment is $6.4 - 4 = +2.4$ on heads and
$1.6 - 4 = -2.4$ on tails. Both pairs average to zero under $\tilde p = \tfrac12$,
which is what being a martingale means at this node. The ratio of the
spreads is $(-0.48 - 0.48)/(2.4 + 2.4) = -0.2$, and $-0.2$ times the stock's
increment reproduces the martingale's on both branches:
$-0.2 \times 2.4 = -0.48$ and $-0.2 \times (-2.4) = 0.48$. The number
$-0.2$ is $\delta_0(4)$, the put's root delta from the backward sweep.

### The theorem

<Theorem title="binomial representation theorem (Baxter and Rennie, §2.3)">
Let $M_0, \ldots, M_N$ be a martingale under $\tilde P$ on the $N$-toss
space of an admissible binomial model, and let $D_n = S_n/(1 + r)^n$. Then
there is a portfolio process $\phi_0, \ldots, \phi_{N-1}$, each $\phi_n$
depending only on the first $n$ tosses, such that
$$
M_n = M_0 + \sum_{k = 0}^{n - 1} \phi_k\,(D_{k+1} - D_k), \qquad n = 0, \ldots, N,
$$
namely $\phi_n = \dfrac{M_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n H) - M_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n T)}{D_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n H) - D_{n+1}(\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n T)}$.

<Proof>
Roadmap: on a two-branch fork, any two random variables that read only the
next toss are affinely related; both increments have zero conditional mean,
which kills the affine constant; induction sums the increments.

Fix $n$ and a node $\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n$, and suppress those tosses in
the notation. The increment $M_{n+1} - M_n$ takes two values, $a_H$ on the
heads branch and $a_T$ on the tails branch, and the increment
$D_{n+1} - D_n$ takes $b_H = D_n(u/(1 + r) - 1)$ and $b_T = D_n(d/(1 + r) - 1)$.
Since $d < 1 + r < u$ and $D_n > 0$, $b_H > 0 > b_T$, so
$b_H - b_T \ne 0$ and $\phi_n = (a_H - a_T)/(b_H - b_T)$ is a well-defined
number at the node, depending only on the first $n$ tosses because
$M_{n+1}(\cdot H)$, $M_{n+1}(\cdot T)$ and $D_n$ do.

**Scale and shift.** By construction $a_H - \phi_n b_H = a_T - \phi_n b_T$;
call the common value $k$. So $M_{n+1} - M_n = \phi_n(D_{n+1} - D_n) + k$
on both branches, with $\phi_n$ and $k$ constant on the block.

**The shift is zero.** Take $\tilde E_n$ of both sides. On the left,
$\tilde E_n[M_{n+1} - M_n] = M_n - M_n = 0$ by the martingale property and
taking out what is known. On the right,
$\tilde E_n[\phi_n(D_{n+1} - D_n)] = \phi_n\,\tilde E_n[D_{n+1} - D_n] = \phi_n \cdot 0$
by taking out $\phi_n$ and the discounted-stock theorem, and
$\tilde E_n[k] = k$. Hence $0 = 0 + k$, and
$M_{n+1} - M_n = \phi_n(D_{n+1} - D_n)$ on both branches.

**Summation.** The identity holds at every node of every date, and
summing it over $k = 0, \ldots, n - 1$ along any path telescopes the left
side to $M_n - M_0$.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

Two checks on the proof. The place where both processes being
$\tilde P$-martingales is used: the single line "the shift is zero", once
for each; for a submartingale $M$ the constant $k$ would be positive and
no trading rule could produce the excess. The place where the tree being
binomial is used: a fork with two branches makes any two next-toss random
variables affinely related, since two points determine a line; on a
three-branch fork the increments would in general not be proportional,
and the theorem fails for the same reason <Ref
lesson="one-period-binomial">the three-state market</Ref> could not be
replicated by two assets.

### The consequence: the hedge is a theorem about martingales

Apply the theorem to $M_n = \tilde E_n[V_N/(1 + r)^N]$, the process the
second boundary case of the definition showed is a $\tilde P$-martingale
for any payoff $V_N$. It gives a portfolio process $\phi$ with
$M_n = M_0 + \sum_{k < n} \phi_k(D_{k+1} - D_k)$. Now run the self-financing
strategy with $\Delta_k = \phi_k$ from $X_0 = M_0$: by the running-sum
identity its discounted wealth is $X_0 + \sum_{k < n} \phi_k(D_{k+1} - D_k)$,
which is $M_n$. At time $N$, $X_N/(1 + r)^N = M_N = V_N/(1 + r)^N$, so
$X_N = V_N$ on every path. The claim has been replicated, from the
starting wealth $M_0 = \tilde E[V_N/(1 + r)^N]$, by a strategy whose
existence came from the theorem and not from the recursion.

This is a second proof of <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the
multiperiod replication theorem</Ref>, and it identifies the hedge. Since
$M_{n+1} = V_{n+1}/(1 + r)^{n+1}$ and $D_{n+1} = S_{n+1}/(1 + r)^{n+1}$,
the discount factors cancel in the ratio, and
$$
\phi_n = \frac{V_{n+1}(H) - V_{n+1}(T)}{S_{n+1}(H) - S_{n+1}(T)} = \Delta_n,
$$
the delta of the backward sweep. Pricing and hedging are two readings of
one object: the martingale $\tilde E_n[V_N/(1 + r)^N]$ read at a node is
the discounted price, and read across a fork it is the hedge ratio.

<Remark title="where the theorem came from">
The reading of prices as martingales under a constructed measure, and of
hedges as the integrands that represent them, was introduced by Harrison
and Kreps in 1979, in discrete time, and extended to continuous trading by
Harrison and Pliska in 1981, whose paper contains the statement that a
market is complete exactly when its price process has the martingale
representation property. On the tree that property is the theorem above,
a two-line computation on a fork. The word "martingale" itself was
brought into probability by Doob, who credits Ville's 1939 study of
gambling systems; the fair-game meaning was the original one, and
"discounted prices are fair games under $\tilde P$" is the sentence the
whole module has been building.
</Remark>

<KeyFact>
On the binomial tree, "the discounted price is a $\tilde P$-martingale"
and "the claim can be hedged" are the same statement read in two
directions, and the representation theorem is the reverse direction. The
continuous-time twin, <Ref lesson="martingale-representation-ftap">the
martingale representation theorem</Ref>, is what makes Black–Scholes
hedges exist, and its hypothesis, one Brownian motion driving one stock,
is the continuous analogue of two branches per fork.
</KeyFact>

## The Markov property: the pricer stores prices, not paths

The pricing formula makes $V_n$ a function of the first $n$ tosses, which
is $2^n$ numbers. <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">The multiperiod
lesson</Ref> observed that for a call the $2^n$ numbers collapse to
$n + 1$, one per stock price, and that for a lookback they do not. The
Markov property is the precise form of that observation, and its proof is
one more application of independence.

### The definition, and the stock as its canonical instance

<Definition title="Markov process (Shreve I, Definition 2.5.1)">
An adapted process $X_0, \ldots, X_N$ on the $N$-toss space is a **Markov
process** under a measure if, for every $n \le N - 1$ and every function
$f$ of one variable, there is a function $g$ (depending on $n$ and $f$)
such that
$$
E_n\bigl[f(X_{n+1})\bigr] = g(X_n) .
$$
</Definition>

The clause says that the conditional distribution of the next value,
which in general depends on all $n$ tosses so far, depends on them only
through the current value. For the stock,
$$
\tilde E_n\bigl[f(S_{n+1})\bigr] = \tilde p\,f(uS_n) + \tilde q\,f(dS_n),
$$
because $S_{n+1}$ is $uS_n$ or $dS_n$ according to a toss independent of
the first $n$; the right side is $g(S_n)$ with
$g(x) = \tilde p f(ux) + \tilde q f(dx)$. The definition has the same two
inputs as the martingale's, the process and the measure, and the four
cases that fix its boundary live on the same \$4 tree, with the
quantifiers kept.

- **The canonical case.** The stock, under $\tilde P$ with the $g$ just
  displayed and under $P$ with $p$ in place of $\tilde p$. The theorem two
  subsections down is what the property buys: a pricer that stores $n + 1$
  values at date $n$ instead of $2^n$.
- **A case that separates the two properties.** A martingale that is not Markov.
  Let $X$ take the values $2, 0$ on $HTH, HTT$, the values $1, 1$ on
  $THH, THT$, and $0$ on the other four paths, and set
  $Y_n = \tilde E_n[X]$, a martingale by the second case of the earlier
  list. Then $Y_2(HT) = Y_2(TH) = 1$, but $Y_3$ is $2$ or $0$ from $HT$ and
  $1$ or $1$ from $TH$, so at $f(x) = x^2$ the conditional means are $2$
  and $1$: two nodes with the same $Y_2$ and different
  $\tilde E_2[f(Y_3)]$, and no $g(Y_2)$ returns both. The Markov clause at
  the one function $f(x) = x$ with $g$ the identity is exactly the
  martingale property; the clause has to hold for every $f$, and here it
  fails at the second one tried.
- **The degenerate case.** The money market account $B_n = (1 + r)^n$ is
  Markov under every measure, with $g(x) = f((1 + r)x)$, and for $r > 0$
  it is not a martingale. The stock under $\tilde P$ is the same failure
  with randomness restored: at $f(x) = x$ its $g(x) = (1 + r)x$ is not the
  identity. A Markov process is a martingale exactly when its $g$ at
  $f(x) = x$ is the identity, and neither property implies the other.
- **The near miss.** The running maximum $M_n = \max_{k \le n} S_k$ on its
  own. On the \$4 tree the nodes $TH$ and $TT$ both have $M_2 = 4$, with
  $S_2 = 4$ and $S_2 = 1$ respectively. From $TH$ the next maximum is
  $\max(4, 8) = 8$ on heads and $\max(4, 2) = 4$ on tails, so
  $\tilde E_2[M_3](TH) = 6$; from $TT$ it is $\max(4, 2) = 4$ and
  $\max(4, 0.5) = 4$, so $\tilde E_2[M_3](TT) = 4$. Two nodes with the same
  $M_2$ and different conditional means: no function $g(M_2)$ returns

  of $M_{n+1}$ reads is $S_n$ as well as $M_n$; the pair $(S_n, M_n)$ is
  Markov, and the definition for vector-valued processes is the same

### The independence lemma and the pricing algorithm on prices

<Theorem title="independence lemma (Shreve I, Lemma 2.5.3)">
Let $X$ depend only on the first $n$ tosses and $Y$ only on tosses
$n + 1$ through $N$, and let $f(x, y)$ be a function of two variables.
Define $g(x) = E[f(x, Y)]$, the expectation over $Y$ with $x$ held fixed.
Then $E_n[f(X, Y)] = g(X)$.

<Proof>
Fix the block $[\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n]$, on which $X$ takes one value
$x$. By the definition of $E_n$,
$E_n[f(X, Y)](\omega_1 \cdots \omega_n) = \sum w\,f\bigl(x, Y(\omega_{n+1} \cdots \omega_N)\bigr)$,
the sum over continuations with their weights $w$. This is a sum in which
the prefix enters only through the number $x$, and by the independence
property of <Ref lesson="coin-toss-probability">the previous lesson</Ref>
applied to the random variable $f(x, Y)$, which reads only the later
tosses, it equals $E[f(x, Y)] = g(x)$. The block was arbitrary, so
$E_n[f(X, Y)] = g(X)$ on every block.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

<Theorem title="path-independent claims are priced on prices (Shreve I, Theorem 2.5.8, binomial case)">
In an admissible $N$-period binomial model let the payoff be
$V_N = v_N(S_N)$ for some function $v_N$. Then for every $n$ there is a
function $v_n$ with $V_n = v_n(S_n)$, and the functions are computed by
$$
v_n(s) = \frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl[\tilde p\,v_{n+1}(us) + \tilde q\,v_{n+1}(ds)\bigr],
\qquad n = N - 1, \ldots, 0 .
$$

<Proof>
Backward induction on $n$. At $n = N$ the claim is the hypothesis. Suppose
$V_{n+1} = v_{n+1}(S_{n+1})$. The pricing theorem's martingale statement
gives $V_n = \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[V_{n+1}] = \frac{1}{1 + r}\tilde E_n[v_{n+1}(S_{n+1})]$.
Write $S_{n+1} = S_n \cdot (S_{n+1}/S_n)$ with $X = S_n$ depending on the
first $n$ tosses and $Y = S_{n+1}/S_n$ on toss $n + 1$ alone, and
$f(x, y) = v_{n+1}(xy)$. By the independence lemma
$\tilde E_n[v_{n+1}(S_{n+1})] = g(S_n)$ with
$g(s) = \tilde E[v_{n+1}(sY)] = \tilde p\,v_{n+1}(us) + \tilde q\,v_{n+1}(ds)$.
So $V_n = v_n(S_n)$ with $v_n$ as displayed.
</Proof>
</Theorem>

The theorem is the licence for the recombining-tree pricer: at date $n$ it
stores $n + 1$ values of $v_n$ rather than $2^n$ values of $V_n$, and the
whole pricing costs about $N^2/2$ evaluations instead of $2^N$. Shreve's
general version replaces the stock by any Markov process $X_n$ and any
payoff $v_N(X_N)$, with the recursion's exact form depending on the
process; the version above is the one the course's pricer implements.

### The lookback and the state it needs

A payoff that reads the path is outside the theorem's hypothesis, and the
lookback of the multiperiod lesson, paying $\max_{k \le 3} S_k - S_3$, shows
what happens. Its eight terminal values are $0, 8, 0, 6, 0, 2, 2, 3.5$ on
$HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT$; the three paths ending at $S_3 = 8$
carry $8, 0, 0$, so no function $v_3(S_3)$ reproduces them, and no
$v_n(S_n)$ can exist at any earlier date either, since the time-two values
$2.40$ at $HT$ and $0.80$ at $TH$ differ at the same price $4$.

<Task>
On the right panel, write beside each of the three paths ending at $8$ its
running maximum, $16$, $8$, $8$ for $HHT$, $HTH$, $THH$, and check that the
payoff is $M_3 - S_3$ in each case. Then group the eight paths by the pair

a single value, which is the state the pricer must carry.
</Task>

The repair is the one <Ref lesson="multiperiod-binomial">the multiperiod
lesson</Ref> used without naming it: the pair $(S_n, M_n)$ is a
two-dimensional Markov process under $\tilde P$, since
$S_{n+1} = S_n Y$ and $M_{n+1} = \max(M_n, S_n Y)$ with $Y$ the next
toss's ratio, and the independence lemma with $f$ a function of
$(s, m, y)$ gives $V_n = v_n(S_n, M_n)$ with
$$
v_n(s, m) = \frac{1}{1 + r}\bigl[\tilde p\,v_{n+1}(us, \max(m, us)) + \tilde q\,v_{n+1}(ds, m)\bigr],
$$
using $ds < s \le m$ so that a tail never raises the maximum. The general
rule: a claim is priced on the smallest state that makes its payoff a
function of the state and makes the state Markov, and the pricer's data
structure is an array indexed by that state. <Ref
lesson="binomial-pricer-lab">The pricer lab</Ref> builds both versions,
the array of $n + 1$ prices for vanilla payoffs and the table keyed by
$(s, m)$ for the lookback.

<KeyFact>
Markov is a property of the pair (process, measure) and path-independence
a property of the payoff; the pricer that stores $n + 1$ numbers per date
is correct exactly when both hold, and the replication theorem, which
stores $2^n$, is correct always. In continuous time the recursion on
$v_n(s)$ becomes a partial differential equation, and the step from the
pricing formula to that equation is <Ref
lesson="risk-neutral-valuation">the Feynman–Kac theorem</Ref>.
</KeyFact>

What is established: a martingale is an adapted process with
$E_n[M_{n+1}] = M_n$ under a stated measure; the discounted stock is a
martingale under $\tilde P$ and under no other coin, the undiscounted
stock being a $\tilde P$-submartingale that grows at $r$; the discounted
wealth of every self-financing strategy is a $\tilde P$-martingale, so
its discounted mean is its starting wealth and no strategy from zero can
end non-negative with a positive chance of gain, which is the first
fundamental theorem in the direction that a risk-neutral measure's
existence excludes arbitrage; the price of any claim is
$V_n = \tilde E_n[V_N/(1 + r)^{N - n}]$, the 80-stock call's $34.08$ being
one weighted sum over four terminal prices, and backward induction is that
expectation evaluated one toss at a time by the tower property; every
$\tilde P$-martingale is $M_0$ plus a running sum of discounted stock
increments with an adapted integrand, so the hedge of any claim exists
because its discounted price is a martingale, and the integrand is the
delta; and for a payoff $v_N(S_N)$ the price is $v_n(S_n)$, computable on
$n + 1$ prices per date, while a path-dependent payoff is priced on the
state its payoff reads. What is not established is what $\tilde P$ is in
relation to $P$ beyond agreeing on impossible events. <Ref
lesson="state-prices-change-of-measure">The next lesson</Ref> writes the
ratio $Z(\omega) = \tilde P(\omega)/P(\omega)$ on every sequence, shows
that $Z_n = E_n[Z]$ is a $P$-martingale, and prices every claim as
$E[Z\,V_N]/(1 + r)^N$ under the actual coin, which is the discrete form of
the change of measure that Girsanov's theorem performs in continuous time.

<Exercise>
A security pays $C_1$ at time $1$ and $C_2$ at time $2$ on a two-period
tree, with $C_1$ depending on the first toss and $C_2$ on both. Prove
that its time-zero price is
$V_0 = \tilde E\bigl[C_1/(1 + r) + C_2/(1 + r)^2\bigr]$, and that the
hedger who sells it must follow a wealth equation with a consumption term.
Then show that the discounted wealth of that hedger is not a
$\tilde P$-martingale but that discounted wealth plus discounted payments
made so far is.

<Solution>
**The price.** The security is the sum of two single-date claims: one
paying $C_1$ at time $1$, one paying $C_2$ at time $2$. Each is priced by
the pricing formula, $V^{(1)}_0 = \tilde E[C_1/(1 + r)]$ and
$V^{(2)}_0 = \tilde E[C_2/(1 + r)^2]$, and each has a replicating strategy
by the replication theorem. Holding both strategies from
$X_0 = V^{(1)}_0 + V^{(2)}_0$, with $\Delta_0 = \Delta^{(1)}_0 + \Delta^{(2)}_0$,
delivers $C_1 + V^{(2)}_1$ at time $1$ by linearity of the wealth
equation. The hedger pays out $C_1$ and continues with $V^{(2)}_1$ and
$\Delta^{(2)}_1$, which delivers $C_2$ at time $2$. Any other quote is
arbitraged by the usual trade against this replication, so
$V_0 = \tilde E[C_1/(1 + r)] + \tilde E[C_2/(1 + r)^2]$, which is the
claimed sum by linearity of $\tilde E$.

**The wealth equation.** The payment at time $1$ leaves the portfolio
before rebalancing, so
$X_2 = \Delta_1 S_2 + (1 + r)(X_1 - C_1 - \Delta_1 S_1)$; in general
$X_{n+1} = \Delta_n S_{n+1} + (1 + r)(X_n - C_n - \Delta_n S_n)$.

**Not a martingale.** Repeating the wealth theorem's proof with this
equation,
$\tilde E_1[X_2/(1 + r)^2] = \Delta_1 S_1/(1 + r) + (X_1 - C_1 - \Delta_1 S_1)/(1 + r) = (X_1 - C_1)/(1 + r)$,
which is $X_1/(1 + r)$ minus $C_1/(1 + r)$: the discounted wealth drops, in
conditional mean, by the discounted payment.

**The repair.** Let $A_n = \sum_{k \le n} C_k/(1 + r)^k$ be the discounted
payments made up to and including time $n$ (with $C_0 = 0$ here). Then
$A_{n+1} - A_n = C_{n+1}/(1 + r)^{n+1}$ is known at time $n + 1$, and
$$
\tilde E_n\Bigl[\frac{X_{n+1}}{(1 + r)^{n+1}} + A_n\Bigr]
= \frac{X_n - C_n}{(1 + r)^n} + A_n
= \frac{X_n}{(1 + r)^n} + A_{n-1},
$$
since $A_n - C_n/(1 + r)^n = A_{n-1}$. So the process
$X_n/(1 + r)^n + A_{n-1}$, discounted wealth plus discounted payments
already made, satisfies the martingale clause. The transferable move: a
cash flow out of a portfolio is a supermartingale correction, and adding
back the discounted cumulative flow restores the martingale, which is the
form in which coupon bonds and swaps are priced.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
On the \$4 tree hold $u = 2$ and $r = \tfrac14$ and let $d$ rise toward
$1 + r = 1.25$. Compute $\tilde p$ as a function of $d$ and its limit.
At the boundary $d = 1.25$ exactly: state which admissibility inequality
has become an equality, exhibit the trade that is an arbitrage there,
compute what the pricing formula returns for the one-period \$5 call, and
name the step of the no-arbitrage theorem's proof that fails.

<Solution>
**$\tilde p$.** $\tilde p = (1.25 - d)/(2 - d)$, which is $\tfrac12$ at
$d = \tfrac12$, $0.25$ at $d = 1$, and tends to $0$ as $d \to 1.25$; at
$d = 1.25$ it is $0$ and $\tilde q = 1$.

**The tight inequality.** $d < 1 + r$ has become $d = 1 + r$: the stock,
even on tails, keeps pace with the money market.

**The trade.** Borrow $4$ and buy one share. On tails the share is worth
$1.25 \times 4 = 5$ and the loan is $5$: net $0$. On heads the share is
worth $8$ against the loan of $5$: net $3$. Zero cost, no loss, a gain on
one of two sequences, each of positive probability under $P$: an
arbitrage.

**The formula.** $V_0 = \frac{1}{1.25}[0 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 0] = 0$. The
call is priced at zero. Buying it for nothing and holding it pays $3$ on
heads and $0$ on tails, which is the arbitrage again in another form. The
formula still returns a number; the number is not a price, because the
hedge it corresponds to, $\Delta_0 = (3 - 0)/(8 - 5) = 1$ share bought
with a loan of $4$ against $X_0 = 0$, is the arbitrage trade itself.

**The failing step.** The proof of the no-arbitrage theorem needs
$\tilde P(\omega^*) > 0$ for the sequence where the gain occurs. With
$\tilde p = 0$ the measure $\tilde P$ assigns probability $0$ to every
sequence containing a head, so $\tilde P(H) = 0$ and the inequality
$\tilde E[X_1/(1 + r)] \ge \tilde P(H)\,X_1(H)/(1 + r) > 0$ is false: the
expectation is $0$, consistent with the corollary, and the gain on $H$ is
invisible to $\tilde P$. Equivalence, clause (i) of the definition of a
risk-neutral measure, is exactly what admissibility supplies and what the
boundary removes; the martingale property, clause (ii), survives at the
boundary and is not enough on its own.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise>
Refute this claim: "Under the risk-neutral measure the stock price is a
martingale, so the expected stock price at any future date, computed with
$\tilde p$, equals today's price, and the forward price of the stock is
$S_0$." Compute $\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}]$ in general and $\tilde E[S_3]$ on
the \$4 tree, name the object that is a $\tilde P$-martingale, and
reconcile with <Ref lesson="expectation-vs-arbitrage">the forward price
$S_0(1 + r)^N$</Ref>.

<Solution>
**The general computation.** Taking out $S_n$ and using independence,
$\tilde E_n[S_{n+1}] = S_n\,\tilde E[S_{n+1}/S_n] = (\tilde p u + \tilde q d)\,S_n = (1 + r)\,S_n$.
The one-step conditional mean is the current price grown at the riskless
rate, not the current price; the stock is a $\tilde P$-submartingale when
$r > 0$, and the clause $M_n = \tilde E_n[M_{n+1}]$ fails by the factor
$1 + r$ at every node.

**The number.** On the \$4 tree, $\tilde E[S_3] = \tfrac18 \cdot 32 + \tfrac38 \cdot 8 + \tfrac38 \cdot 2 + \tfrac18 \cdot 0.5 = 4 + 3 + 0.75 + 0.0625 = 7.8125 = 4 \times 1.25^3$,
not $4$.

**The martingale.** $S_n/(1 + r)^n$, by the discounted-stock theorem;
equivalently, $\tilde E[S_N] = (1 + r)^N S_0$ by the constant-mean theorem
applied to the discounted stock.

**The forward.** The forward price is the delivery price $F$ making the
claim $S_N - F$ worth zero today. By the pricing formula,
$0 = \tilde E[(S_N - F)/(1 + r)^N] = S_0 - F/(1 + r)^N$, so
$F = (1 + r)^N S_0 = \tilde E[S_N]$: the forward price is the risk-neutral
expected stock price, which is $S_0$ grown at $r$, and this agrees with
the replication argument of the first lesson. The claim's error is the
word "martingale" applied to the undiscounted price; its conclusion
$F = S_0$ would make borrowing to buy the stock and selling it forward a
riskless gain of $rS_0$. The transferable move: "martingale under
$\tilde P$" is always a statement about prices in units of the money
market account, and translating back to dollars multiplies by
$(1 + r)^n$.
</Solution>
</Exercise>

In the proof of the discounted-wealth theorem, name the exact step where
